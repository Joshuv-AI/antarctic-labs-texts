A Law of Data Reconstruction for Random Features (and Beyond)
Leonardo Iurada, Simone Bombari, Tatiana Tommasi, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Large-scale deep learning models are known to memorize parts of the training set. In machine learning theory, memorization is often framed as interpolation or label fitting, and classical results show that this can be achieved when the number of parameters p p in the model is larger than the number of training samples n n . In this work, we consider memorization from the perspective of data reconstruction , demonstrating that this can be achieved when p p is larger than d ​ n dn , where d d is the dimensionality of the data. More specifically, we show that, in the random features model, when p ≫ d ​ n p\gg dn , the subspace spanned by the training samples in feature space gives sufficient information to identify the individual samples in input space. Our analysis suggests an optimization method to reconstruct the dataset from the model parameters, and we demonstrate that this method performs well on various architectures (random features, two-layer fully-connected and deep residual networks). Our results reveal a law of data reconstruction , according to which the entire training dataset can be recovered as p p exceeds the threshold d ​ n dn . 1 1 1 Code available at: https://github.com/iurada/data-reconstruction-law

How many parameters does a neural network need to memorize the training data?

The answer to this question depends on what one means by

72

30
]

[

,
62
,
20
]
. Notably, this reconstruction is possible in modern foundation models
[

52
,
21
]
, with consequences in terms of privacy concerns and copyright infringement
[

20

.

Understanding how many parameters a neural network needs to interpolate the dataset (or, equivalently, memorize the labels) is a classical problem
[
22
]
, with more modern literature showing that interpolation occurs as soon as the number of model parameters
p
exceeds the number of training samples

An intuition for the phenomenon is that solving

Related work

Over-parameterization and memorization.

The problem of label memorization and how it relates to the number of parameters in a neural network dates back to seminal results
[
22
,
5
]
. More recently, a line of work aimed at showing that gradient descent converges to 0 training loss (i.e., the model memorizes the labels) for networks with progressively smaller over-parameterization
[
1
,
27
,
26
,
56
,
54
,
53
,
55
,
11
]
, with
[
11
]
proving that
p\gg n
parameters are sufficient to fit any set of labels in deep neural networks.
In contrast, the problem of input data memorization and reconstruction has been much less explored from a theoretical standpoint.
Balle et al. 2022
proved impossibility results if the model is trained with differential privacy
[
28
]
, and showed that in generalized linear models it is possible to reconstruct an individual data provided all other training samples are known.
Loo et al. 2024
considered networks with an infinite number of parameters,
Smorodinsky et al. 2024
focused on partial uni-dimensional (
d=1
) training set reconstruction, and
Wang et al. 2023
assumed knowledge of model gradients, requiring
p\gg d^{2}
parameters with
n
at most of order
d^{1/4}
.
The regime
p\gg dn
was heuristically identified by
[
33
]
as enabling successful data reconstruction, while
[
13
,
31
]
studied its role as a requirement for learning certain tasks, rather than for data reconstruction.

Data reconstruction.

Recent studies have shown that training data can be extracted from generative models by carefully prompting their generation routines
[
18
,
73
,
52
,
21
]
. More generally, data reconstruction is a broader task, not limited to generative models, that aims to recover the training set directly from the learned model’s parameters
[
20
]
.
A possible approach is via model inversion attacks, which optimize over the input space to raise a class score, although this method generally recovers class prototypes rather than specific training samples
[
32
,
47
]
.
Another strategy relies on the implicit bias of gradient descent for homogeneous networks
[
46
,
40
]
:
Haim et al. 2022
proposed a reconstruction objective motivated by the observation that gradient descent converges to points satisfying the KKT conditions of a max-margin problem, and empirically showed that training samples on the classification margin can be recovered. This method was then extended by
Buzaglo et al. 2023
,
Oz et al. 2024
to the multiclass setting with general losses, and to larger scale pretrained models.

Loo et al. 2024
adapted the idea to neural networks trained with the square loss in the linear (or NTK) regime
[
39
,
44
]
. This leads to an optimization method for data reconstruction which has similarities with ours (compare Eq. (7) in
[
45
]
with Eq. (
11
) in this work).

Random features (RF) regression.

The RF model

61
]
can be regarded as a two-layer neural network with random first layer weights. Its popularity stems from its mathematical tractability and, in contrast with linear regression where the number of parameters equals the input dimension, it captures the statistical effects of over-parameterization, since the number of parameters

can scale independently from
d

[

characterized the test loss of random features, showing that it displays a double descent curve

]
. Furthermore, the RF model has been used to understand a wide family of phenomena such as feature learning
[

,

,

]
, robustness under adversarial attacks

,
12
,
34
]
, distribution shift
[

,

[

,

]
.

Problem setup

Notation.

All complexity notations
\omega,\Omega,\Theta,O,o
are understood for sufficiently large input dimension
d
and number of parameters
p
. Given a positive number
k
,
[k]
denotes the set of positive numbers from
1
to
k
. Given a vector
v
,
\left\|v\right\|_{2}
denotes its Euclidean norm. Given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, we denote by
P_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
the projector over
\Span\{\Rows(A)\}
. We say that an event holds with overwhelming probability if it holds with probability at least
1-e^{-\omega(\log d)}
.

Let
(X,Y)
be a labeled training dataset, where
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
contains the training input data (sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{P}_{X}
) on its rows and
Y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains the corresponding labels. We define the
random features
(RF) model as

f_{\text{RF}}(x,\theta)=\phi(Vx)^{\top}\theta=\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta,

(1)

where
\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the feature vector associated to the input
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are trainable parameters. The random features matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
is s.t.
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, and
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-linearity applied component-wise. We note that
f_{\text{RF}}(x,\theta)
is a generalized linear model, and it can be regarded as a two-layer fully-connected neural network, where only the second layer is trained.
We consider the supervised learning setup where a quadratic training loss (without regularization) is minimized via gradient descent. When the learning rate is sufficiently small, gradient descent converges to the interpolator which is the closest in
\ell_{2}
norm to the initialization (see Equation (33) in
[
4
]
), which we consider equal to 0 for simplicity.
Then, denoting with
\Phi:=[\varphi(x_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{n})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
the feature matrix, the trained parameters are

\theta^{*}=\Phi^{+}Y,

(2)

where
\Phi^{+}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\Phi
.

Assumption 1

(Data distribution)
.

The training samples
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
are
n
i.i.d. samples from the sub-Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{P}_{X}
, with
\left\|x\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=O(1)
, and such that
\left\|x\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
.

This assumption requires the data to have well-behaved tails (see the definition of the sub-Gaussian norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
in Eq. (
13
) in Appendix
A
for further details). This hypothesis is commonly used in the related literature, and it includes
e.g
.
Gaussian data, data respecting Lipschitz concentration
[
14
,
12
,
68
]
, and data uniform on the sphere
[
48
,
38
]
.
The normalization
\|x\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
is chosen for technical convenience, and this scaling of the norm (combined with the scaling of the random features matrix
V
) guarantees that the pre-activations of the model (
i.e
.
, the entries of
Vx
) are of constant order.

Assumption 2

(Activation function)
.

The activation function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-linear, Lipschitz continuous function such that its derivative is also Lipschitz. Letting
\mu_{l}
denote the
l
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
, we further assume that
\mu_{0}=\mu_{2}=0
,
\mu_{1}\neq 0
, and that there exist two non-zero Hermite coefficients of order
\geq 3
with different parity.

This assumption is motivated by theoretical convenience, and we expect our results to hold for a wider set of activations (as in
[
49
]
) after a more involved analysis. Except for the last condition on the parity of high-order Hermite coefficients, Assumption
2
resembles the setting considered by
[
37
]
, and it covers a wide family of odd activations, including

. The parity of high-order Hermite coefficients is used to reconstruct the correct sign of the training data, and it appears to be necessary for that, see Remark
1
for details.

Assumption 3

(Over-parameterization)
.

We consider a data dimensionality regime where
n=O(d)
, and an over-parameterized model with

p=\omega\left(nd\log^{2}d\right).

(3)

To guarantee that the RF model interpolates the data, it suffices that
p\gg n

[
49
,
70
]
. The stronger over-parameterization requirement in Eq. (
3
) was shown to be both necessary
[
14
]
and, in some settings, sufficient
[
12
]
to achieve
smooth
interpolation.
We focus on the regime
n=O(d)
, which includes the popular proportional regime
n=\Theta(d)
as well as the regime where
n
is a fixed constant (independent of
d,p
). We expect our results to be generalizable also to
n\gg d

[
49
,
38
,
58
]
.

4
Main results

In this section, we present our main theoretical results, pinpointing
p\gg dn
as the over-parameterization threshold such that data reconstruction can take place given knowledge of the subspace of the training samples in feature space.

More formally, the goal of data reconstruction is to exhibit a matrix
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that its rows
\hat{x}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are close to the rows of the original training data matrix
X
. When
\left\|\hat{x}_{j}\right\|_{2}=\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, a reconstructed sample
\hat{x}_{j}
can be considered close to a training sample
x_{i}
if
\left\|\hat{x}_{j}-x_{i}\right\|_{2}=o(\sqrt{d})
. Let us also define
\hat{\Phi}=\left[\varphi(\hat{x}_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(\hat{x}_{n})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
as the feature matrix of the reconstructed data. Then, the result below gives sufficient conditions for all rows of
\hat{X}
to be close to training samples.

Theorem 1
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
3
hold. Let
\hat{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be such that its rows satisfy
\left\|\hat{x}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, and for every
i\in[n]
,
\varphi(x_{i})\in\Span\{\Rows(\hat{\Phi})\}
.
Then, with overwhelming probability, for any
\hat{\imath}\in[n]
, there exists
i\in[n]
such that

\left\|\hat{x}_{\hat{\imath}}-x_{i}\right\|_{2}=o(\sqrt{d}).

(4)

In words, Theorem
1
states that, if the random features of the training samples are spanned by the random features of a matrix
\hat{X}
, the rows of
\hat{X}

must
be close (in input space) to the original training samples. Geometrically, this result proves that, when
p\gg dn
, in order to span the subspace generated by the training data features, one has to consider approximately the same vectors, as there is no solution to this problem obtained by non-degenerate linear combinations.
In contrast with
[
45
]
which tackles the case
p\to\infty
, our main contribution is to pinpoint the threshold
p\gg dn
constituting a sufficient amount of over-parameterization to reconstruct the data. We also highlight that, for the claim of Theorem
1
to hold, assumptions on the activation are necessary: if
\phi
is linear, picking
\hat{x}_{i}=\sqrt{d}(x_{i}+x_{i+1})/\|x_{i}+x_{i+1}\|_{2}
(

) and
\hat{x}_{n}=\sqrt{d}(x_{n}-x_{1})/\|x_{n}-x_{1}\|_{2}
gives a counterexample to (

1

B

Proof sketch.
Prior results on the RF kernel concentration
[

]

\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}(\Phi\Phi^{\top})
is bounded away from 0. This in turn gives that, if
\hat{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\varphi(\hat{x})\in\Span\{\Rows(\Phi)\}
, i.e.,

\varphi(\hat{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\varphi(x_{i}).

(5)

As argued above, the result cannot hold for linear activations, so let us focus on the non-linear component
\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})=\phi(Vx_{i})-\mu_{1}Vx_{i}
in the Hermite basis. Taking the inner product of both sides of (
5
) with
\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})
and with
\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x})
yields, with overwhelming probability,

\left|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x})-\tilde{\mu}^{2}a_{i}\right|=\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dn}{p}}\right)+o(1),\qquad\left|\left\|a\right\|_{2}^{2}-1\right|=\tilde{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{dn}{p}}\right)+o(1),

(6)

where

in its

is the sum of the squares of the Hermite coefficients of

Next, in Lemma

\textup{if \, }C=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i}\left|\frac{x_{i}^{\top}\hat{x}}{d}\right|,\qquad\textup{then \, }\left\|\frac{(X\hat{x})^{\circ l}}{d^{l}}\right\|_{2}\leq C^{l-1}+o(1),

uniformly for every

Remark 1

(Sign ambiguity)
.

The existence of two non-zero Hermite coefficients with different parity is a necessary condition to recover the sign of the training samples. In fact, if
\phi
is either even or odd, the problem is under-determined in terms of the sign of the

From the theory to a reconstruction algorithm.

Our theoretical analysis shows that, under sufficient over-parameterization (

6
Conclusions

This work studies data memorization, intended as the feasibility of reconstructing
n
data samples of dimension
d
from a trained model, focusing on the number of parameters
p
at which this becomes possible.
Our results on random features point to a
law of data reconstruction
, establishing the threshold
p\approx dn
.
Remarkably, the reconstruction method grounded in our theoretical analysis is also successful in two-layer and deep residual networks.
While our analysis assumes knowledge of the subspace of the training samples in feature space (
\Span\{\{\varphi(x_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\}
), the experimental results demonstrate that, by optimizing the loss
\mathcal{L}(\hat{X})
in Eq. (
11
), the whole training set is reconstructed when
p\gg dn
. This suggests two outstanding open problems to be tackled in future research: proving that
(i)
all the global optima of
\mathcal{L}(\hat{X})
are permutations of the training dataset, and that
(ii)
the optimization problem can be efficiently solved with gradient methods despite the non-convexity of
\mathcal{L}(\hat{X})
.
Another interesting direction is to understand what happens in the regime
n\ll p\ll dn
. On the one hand,
[
3
]
indicate how to reconstruct
any single
training sample in this regime under certain model assumptions, given access to the final parameters and, additionally, to the remaining training data. On the other hand, we cautiously suspect that, without this additional knowledge, reconstructing the entire dataset is information-theoretically impossible when
p\ll dn
: at fixed machine precision, this would require recovering
\Theta(dn)
bits from only
\Theta(p)
bits, underscoring a hard limit on reconstructability.

Additional notations and preliminaries

We use the definition of the Orlicz norm of order
\alpha

as

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}:=\inf\{t>0\;:\;\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(|X|^{\alpha}/t^{\alpha})\right]\leq 2\}.

(13)

From this definition, it follows that
\left\||X|^{\gamma}\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha/\gamma}}=\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{\alpha}}^{\gamma}
. We will say that a random variable

\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
(sub-exponential norm
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
) is

(
i.e.
it does not increase with the scalings of the problem). Notice that if

and

are scalar random variables, we have

. We use the analogous definitions for vectors. In particular, let

be a random vector, then
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{2}}
and
\left\|X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}:=\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left\|u^{\top}X\right\|_{\psi_{1}}
. We note that if
X\in\mathbb{R}
is sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential) and
\tau:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz, we have that
\tau(X)
is sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential) as well. Also, if a random variable is sub-Gaussian or sub-exponential, its
p
-th momentum is upper bounded by a constant (that might depend on
p
).

Given a matrix
A
, we indicate with

its
i
-th row, and with
A_{:j}
its
j
-th column. Given a square matrix

its smallest eigenvalue. Given a matrix
A
we indicate with

its operator (or spectral) norm, and with
\left\|A\right\|_{F}
its Frobenius (or Hilbert-Schmidt) norm (
\left\|A\right\|^{2}_{F}=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}^{2}

Lemma B.4
.

(54)

2\left|x_{1}^{\top}\left({\epsilon}_{1}-{\epsilon}_{2}\right)\right|\leq\left(\left\|{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}+\left\|{\epsilon}_{2}\right\|_{2}\right)\left\|{\epsilon}_{1}-{\epsilon}_{2}\right\|_{2}.

(82)

We will use the following notation

{\epsilon}=\frac{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits\{\left\|{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2},\left\|{\epsilon}_{2}\right\|_{2}\}}{\sqrt{d}}=O(1),

(83)

and

\delta=\frac{\left\|{\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{d}}=O(1).

(84)

Notice that, by definition, we have
\delta=O(\epsilon)
. The idea is to prove that, with overwhelming probability, there exists no solution to (
76
) such that
{\epsilon}=o(1)
. This then readily implies the claim of Theorem
2
. To do so, we state and prove a number of preliminary results.

Lemma C.1
.

We jointly have

\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=O(\sqrt{p/d}),\qquad\left\|V\right\|_{F}^{2}=O\left(p\right),\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}=O\left(p\right),

(85)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
.
Furthermore, we have that

\sup_{x\in\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{p}),\qquad\sup_{x\in\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x)\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{p}).

(86)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
.

Proof.

The first equation holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}p)
due to Theorem 4.4.5 in
[
67
]
. The second equation is a direct consequence of Theorem 3.1.1 in
[
67
]
. For the third equation, due to Theorem 3.1.1 in
[
67
]
, we have that

\left\|\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}-1\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(87)

which implies

\left\|\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=O\left(1\right).

(88)

Then, we have that the Orlicz norm
\left\|\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}\right\|_{\psi_{2/3}}=O(1)
, which also implies
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}\right]=O(1)
. Then, due to Lemma B.6 in
[
12
]
, we have that

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}=O(p),

(89)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}d\right)
, where we also used Assumption
3
.
The last statement statement can be obtained via the same argument in (
31
).
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
be sub-Gaussian random variables, not necessarily independent. Consider the random variable

Z=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(M,|\rho_{1}|\right)\left|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}\right|,

(90)

where
M=\omega(1)
. Then,
Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]
is sub-exponential with parameters
(\nu,\alpha)
(see Definition 2.7 in
[
69
]
) such that

\nu=O(1),\qquad\alpha=O(M).

(91)

Proof.

Since the
\rho
-s are sub-Gaussian, we have that both the absolute value of the mean and the second moment of
Z
(and therefore its variance) are upper bounded by positive absolute constants. Denote with
C_{1}
a constant order upper bound on the absolute value of
\mathbb{E}[Z]
. Furthermore, without loss of generality, we will consider the sub-Gaussian norms of
\rho_{1},\rho_{2},\rho_{3}
to be equal to
1
.

Consider a real number
\lambda
such that
4M|\lambda|\leq 1
. Defining
\bar{Z}=Z-\mathbb{E}[Z]
, and noting that
e^{z}\leq 1+z+z^{2}e^{|z|}/2
for every
z\in\mathbb{R}
(due to the mean-value theorem), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\bar{Z}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[1+\lambda\bar{Z}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\bar{Z}^{2}e^{|\lambda||\bar{Z}|}\right]

(92)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\bar{Z}^{2}e^{|\lambda||\bar{Z}|}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{Z}^{2}e^{\frac{M|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|+C_{1}}{4M}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(|\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|+C_{1}\right)^{2}e^{\frac{|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|+1}{4}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(|\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|+C_{1}\right)^{4}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|+1}{2}}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 1+C_{2}\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\leq e^{C_{2}\lambda^{2}}.

Here, in third line we used
Z\leq M|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|
; in the fourth line we used that
M\geq C_{1}
and
Z\leq|\rho_{1}\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|
; in the sixth line we upper bounded the expectations via an absolute constant, as
|\rho_{2}\rho_{3}|
is sub-exponential with norm 1.
Thus, we can set
\alpha=4M
, and for all
|\lambda|\leq 1/\alpha
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\lambda\bar{Z}}\right]\leq e^{C_{2}\lambda^{2}},

(93)

which gives the desired result according to Definition 2.7 in
[
69
]
.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

We have that

\displaystyle\left|({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})^{\top}V^{\top}\left(\left(\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{2})-\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)-\phi^{\prime}(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1}))\circ\left(V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\right)\right|

(94)

\displaystyle=O\left(p\delta^{3}+d\log^{2}d\delta^{2}\right),

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
.

Proof.

Since
\phi
is differentiable due to Assumption
2
, by the mean-value theorem, we have that there exists
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{2})-\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})=\phi^{\prime}(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})+\zeta)\circ(V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})),

(95)

where each entry of
\zeta
is such that
\zeta_{k}\in[0,\left[V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})\right]_{k}]
(or
\zeta_{k}\in[\left[V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})\right]_{k},0]
, if
\left[V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})\right]_{k}
is negative). Then, we have that the thesis becomes

\displaystyle\left|({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})^{\top}V^{\top}\left(\left(\phi^{\prime}(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})+\zeta)-\phi^{\prime}(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1}))\right)\circ(V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}))\right)\right|

(96)

\displaystyle=O\left(p\delta^{3}+d\log^{2}d\delta^{2}\right).

First, let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined as

\xi=\frac{{\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}}{\delta},

(97)

i.e.
the vector lying on the sphere
\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with the same direction as
{\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}
.
Then, dividing both sides of (
96
) by
\delta^{3}
and expanding the sum, the desired result can be reformulated as

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)\frac{\phi^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})+\zeta_{k})-\phi^{\prime}(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1}))}{\delta}\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)=O\left(p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right).

(98)

Due to Assumption
2
, we have both
\phi^{\prime}(z)\leq L
and
|\phi^{\prime}(z_{1})-\phi^{\prime}(z_{2})|\leq L^{\prime}|z_{1}-z_{2}|
. Thus, the previous equation is implied by

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right|\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}=O\left(p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right),

(99)

as we have
|\zeta_{k}|\leq\delta|v_{k}^{\top}\xi|
from its definition in (
95
).

Let us now consider an
\epsilon\,\sqrt{d}
-net of
\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, namely
\{x^{\epsilon}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
, such that for any
x\in\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
there exists
m\in[M]
such that
\left\|x-x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right\|_{2}\leq\epsilon\,\sqrt{d}
. Due to Corollary 4.2.13 in
[
67
]
, for
\epsilon<1
we have that the net can be chosen such that
M\leq(3/\epsilon)^{d}
. Setting
\epsilon=d^{-3/2}
, we have that there exists
m^{*}\in[M]
such that
\left\|\xi-x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1/d
, and
M\leq e^{c_{1}d\log d}
, where
c_{1}
is an absolute positive constant. Consider a generic element
x^{\epsilon}_{m}
and define

T^{(m)}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right|\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right)^{2}.

(100)

Each term
T^{(m)}_{k}
in the sum above is such that its expectation is

\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[T^{(m)}_{k}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right|\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right)^{2}\right]\leq L^{\prime}\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m}\right|^{3}\right]=O(1).

(101)

Furthermore,
T^{(m)}_{k}
is sub-exponential (in the probability space of
v_{k}
) with parameters
(\nu,\alpha)
(see Definition 2.7 in
[
69
]
) such that

\nu=O(1),\qquad\alpha=O\left(\delta^{-1}\right),

(102)

due to Lemma
C.2
. Thus, Equation (2.18) in
[
69
]
guarantees that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(T^{(m)}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[T^{(m)}_{k}\right]\right)\right|\geq p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-c_{2}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(p,d\log^{2}d\right)\right)

(103)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-c_{3}d\log^{2}d\right),

where the last step used Assumption
3
.
Next, we perform a union bound on the elements of the net, obtaining that

\sup_{m\in[M]}\left|T^{(m)}\right|=O\left(p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right),

(104)

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c_{3}d\log^{2}d+c_{1}d\log d\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}d\log^{2}d\right)
. Then, with this same probability, due to (
101
), we also have that

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right|\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right)^{2}=O\left(p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right).

(105)

Since the
\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits
function is 1 Lipschitz in any of its arguments, for every
k\in[p]
, we have

\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right|\right)-\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right|\right)\leq L^{\prime}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}\left\|\xi-x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right\|_{2}=O\left(\frac{\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}}{d}\right).

(106)

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left|\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right|\right)-\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right|\right)\right|\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right)^{2}

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{C_{2}}{d}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right\|_{2}^{2}=O(p),

where the last step used the first statement of Lemma
C.1
, and holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}d\right)
.
Furthermore, for every
k\in[p]
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right)^{2}-\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|v_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}-\xi\right)\right|\left|v_{k}^{\top}\left(x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}+\xi\right)\right|

(108)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{d}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}-\xi\right\|_{2}=O\left(\frac{\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

which yields

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right|\right)\left|\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{*}}\right)^{2}-\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}\right|\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p}\frac{C_{3}}{\sqrt{d}}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}\left\|\xi\right\|_{2}=O(p),

(109)

again due to Lemma
C.1
. Finally, applying the triangle inequality to (
105
), (
107
), and (
109
), gives

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\frac{2L}{\delta},L^{\prime}\left|v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right|\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}=O\left(p+\frac{d\log^{2}d}{\delta}\right),

(110)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}d\right)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.4
.

Suppose
{\epsilon}=o(1)
. Then, we have that

\left|({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1})^{\top}V^{\top}\left(\phi^{\prime}(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1}))\circ(V({\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}))\right)-\frac{\left\|{\epsilon}_{2}-{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{d}p\mu_{1}\right|=o(p\delta^{2}),

(111)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
.

Proof.

First, let
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be defined as in (
97
). Then, the desired result can be reformulated as

\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}-p\mu_{1}\right|=o(p).

(112)

Let us now consider an
\epsilon\,\sqrt{d}
-net of
\sqrt{d}\,\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(as we did after (
99
)).
Setting
\epsilon=d^{-2}
, we have that there exist
m^{(\xi)}
and
m^{(x)}
in
[M]
such that
\left\|\xi-x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1/d^{3/2}
,
\left\|(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})-x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right\|_{2}\leq 1/d^{3/2}
, and the size of the net is
M\leq e^{c_{1}d\log d}
, where
c_{1}
is an absolute positive constant.
Consider two generic elements of this net:
x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(1)}}
and
x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(2)}}
, and define

T^{(m^{(1)},m^{(2)})}=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(1)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(2)}}\right)^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(1)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(2)}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\right|.

(113)

Since
\left|\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(1)}}\right)\right|\leq L
, each element of the sum is sub-exponential (in the probability space of
v_{k}
). Then, due to Bernstein inequality (see Theorem 2.8.1 in
[
67
]
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(T^{(m^{(1)},m^{(2)})}>C_{1}\sqrt{dp\log d}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{2}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(C_{1}^{2}d\log d,C_{1}\sqrt{dp\log d}\right)\right)

(114)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{3}C_{1}d\log d\right),

where the second step is a consequence of Assumption
3
.
Performing a union bound, we have that, for every pair of points on the net (there are less than
e^{2c_{1}d\log d}
such pairs), including
x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}
and
x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}
, the equation above holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}C_{1}d\log d+2c_{1}d\log d\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}d\log d\right)
if
C_{1}
is chosen sufficiently large. Thus, with this probability, we have

\displaystyle T^{(m^{(x)},m^{(\xi)})}

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}\right]\right)\right|

(115)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}-p\,\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\rho_{x}^{\epsilon}\right)\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=O\left(\sqrt{dp\log d}\right)=o(p),

where we used Assumption
3
and introduced
\rho_{x}^{\epsilon}
and
\rho_{\xi}^{\epsilon}
as two standard Gaussian variables with correlation
\left(x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}/d
. Note that

\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)^{2}=1+\sqrt{2}\frac{\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)^{2}-1}{\sqrt{2}}=h_{0}\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)+\sqrt{2}h_{2}\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right),

(116)

where
h_{0}
and
h_{2}
denote the 0th and 2nd Hermite polynomials. Thus, we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\rho_{x}^{\epsilon}\right)\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]=\mu_{0}^{\phi^{\prime}}+\sqrt{2}\mu_{2}^{\phi^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\left(x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}}{d}\right)^{2},

(117)

where
\mu_{0}^{\phi^{\prime}}
is the 0th Hermite coefficient of
\phi^{\prime}
. Note that
\mu_{0}^{\phi^{\prime}}
corresponds to the 1st Hermite coefficient of
\phi
, which is
\mu_{1}\neq 0
due to Assumption
2
.
The second term on the RHS of (
117
) can be bounded via

\displaystyle\left|\left(x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right\|_{2}\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}-\xi\right\|_{2}+\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}-x_{1}-{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}\left\|\xi\right\|_{2}+\left|\left(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1}\right)^{\top}\xi\right|

(118)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{d}+\left|\frac{x_{1}^{\top}\left({\epsilon}_{1}-{\epsilon}_{2}\right)}{\delta}\right|+\left|\frac{{\epsilon}_{1}^{\top}\left({\epsilon}_{1}-{\epsilon}_{2}\right)}{\delta}\right|=O\left(\frac{1}{d}+d{\epsilon}\right),

where we used (
82
) in the last step.
Then, plugging in (
117
), we obtain

\left|\mathbb{E}\left[\phi^{\prime}\left(\rho_{x}^{\epsilon}\right)\left(\rho^{\epsilon}_{\xi}\right)^{2}\right]-\mu_{1}\right|=O\left(\frac{1}{d^{4}}+{\epsilon}^{2}\right)=o(1).

(119)

We also have

\displaystyle\left|\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}-\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}\right|

(120)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\left(\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)-\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\left(\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}-\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq L^{\prime}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}-x_{1}-{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}d+2L\sqrt{d}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left\|x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}-\xi\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\frac{\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{3}}{\sqrt{d}},

where
C_{2}
is some positive constant. This yields

\sum_{k=1}^{p}\left|\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(x)}}\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}x^{\epsilon}_{m^{(\xi)}}\right)^{2}-\phi^{\prime}\left(v_{k}^{\top}(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)\left(v_{k}^{\top}\xi\right)^{2}\right|=O\left(\frac{p}{\sqrt{d}}\right)=o(p),

(121)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}d\right)
due to Lemma
C.1
.
Then, applying the triangle inequality to (
115
), (
119
), and (
121
), the proof is complete.
∎

Lemma C.5
.

Suppose
{\epsilon}=o(1)
. Then, we have that

\left|a_{1}+a_{2}\right|=O\left(\left|a_{2}\right|\delta+\frac{\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(122)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}d\right)
over
V
and
X
.

Proof.

Consider (
78
), written as

\varphi(x_{2})=(a_{1}+a_{2})\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})+a_{2}\left(\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{2})-\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right).

(123)

Let us now take an inner product of both sides of (
123
) with
Vx_{1}
. Due to Lemma
C.1
and since
x_{1}
is a sub-Gaussian vector independent of
V
and
x_{2}
due to Assumption
1
, we have that

\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\varphi(x_{2})\right|=O\left(\frac{p}{\sqrt{d}}\log d\right),

(124)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}d\right)
over
x_{1}
and
V
.
Then, consider

\displaystyle\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\varphi(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})-\mu_{1}p\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi\left(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)-x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi(Vx_{1})\right|

(125)

\displaystyle+\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi(Vx_{1})-\mu_{1}p\right|,

and let us bound the two terms on the RHS separately. For the first one, since
\phi
is
L
-Lipschitz by Assumption
2
, we have that

\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi\left(V(x_{1}+{\epsilon}_{1})\right)-x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi(Vx_{1})\right|\leq L\left\|Vx_{1}\right\|_{2}\left\|V{\epsilon}_{1}\right\|_{2}=O\left(p{\epsilon}\right),

(126)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}d\right)
due to Lemma
C.1
and (
83
). For the second term in the RHS of (
125
), using the Hermite decomposition of
\phi
, we have that

\left|x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\phi(Vx_{1})-\mu_{1}p\right|=\left|\sum_{k=1}^{p}v_{k}^{\top}x_{1}\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{1})-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[v_{k}^{\top}x_{1}\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{1})\right]\right|.

(127)

Since
\phi
is Lipschitz, we have that
\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{1})
is a sub-Gaussian random variable (with respect to

Appendix D
Implementation details

Computational resources.
We executed all the experiments on a machine equipped with two GPUs (an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090, 24 GB VRAM and an NVIDIA RTX A5000, 24 GB VRAM), an Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-10920X CPU @ 3.50GHz and 128 GB of RAM.

Training procedure of neural networks.
By default, we train both two-layer and deep residual networks with full-batch gradient descent with step size
10^{-5}
on square loss for
10^{6}
steps, unless stated otherwise.

Optimization details for the reconstruction algorithm.
We provide precise implementation details of the reconstruction algorithm. The inputs of our procedure are the model’s optimal weights
\theta^{*}
and the structure of
f_{\text{RF}}
,
i.e
.
,
V
and
\phi
. As already mentioned in Section
5.2
, in the case of neural networks where initialization is not zero, we define
\theta^{*}\triangleq\theta^{(L)}_{*}-\theta_{0}^{(L)}
, where
\theta_{0}
are the initial parameters and
\theta^{*}
are the trained parameters after gradient descent converged on the training set. The superscript
(L)
indicates the weights of the last layer. For neural networks we also assume access to their trained weights of all layers
\{\theta^{*(1)},\dots,\theta^{*(L)}\}
, but we only require knowledge of the initialization of the last layer
\theta_{0}^{(L)}
and their internal structure.
The reconstruction problem is solved by minimizing the following objective via gradient descent with momentum:

\hat{X}^{\star}=\underset{\hat{X}:\|\hat{x}_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}}{\arg\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}\left\|P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(164)

We initialize the rows
\hat{x}_{i}
as i.i.d.
standard Gaussian vectors.
To efficiently compute the objective in (
164
) at each gradient descent iterate, we leverage the fact that, by definition,
P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}
is symmetric (
(P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}})^{\top}=P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}
) and idempotent (
(P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}})^{2}=P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}
). Expanding according to the definitions and properties above,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\hat{X})=\left\|P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=[P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}]^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=[\theta^{*}]^{\top}[P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}]^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=[\theta^{*}]^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\hat{\Phi}}\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=[\theta^{*}]^{\top}(I-\hat{\Phi}^{+}\hat{\Phi})\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=[\theta^{*}]^{\top}\theta^{*}-[\theta^{*}]^{\top}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}(\hat{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})^{-1}\hat{\Phi}\theta^{*}

\displaystyle=[\theta^{*}]^{\top}\theta^{*}-[\theta^{*}]^{\top}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}\alpha~.

Here,
\alpha
is the solution of the system of
n
linear equations in
n
unknowns
(\hat{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}^{\top})~\alpha=\hat{\Phi}\theta^{*}
which can be numerically computed via the conjugate gradient method
[
36
]
, skipping the need of inverting explicitly
\hat{\Phi}\hat{\Phi}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
at each iteration. After the gradient descent update on
\hat{X}
, we then normalize its rows
\hat{x}_{i}
to force them to lie on the
d
-dimensional sphere
\sqrt{d}~\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. More precisely,
\hat{x}_{i}\leftarrow\sqrt{d}\cdot\hat{x}_{i}/\|\hat{x}_{i}\|_{2}
, thus respecting the fact that minimization should happen on the sphere as in (
164
). Unless stated otherwise, the step size is set by default to
2\cdot 10^{3}
for the CIFAR-10 experiments and to
20
for the experiments on synthetic data. Momentum is set to
0.9
.
We consider the reconstruction optimization converged when the normalized reconstruction loss
\mathcal{L}(\hat{X})/\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
drops below
10^{-7}
. Normalizing by
\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}^{2}
makes the loss of order 1 at the beginning of the optimization so that the chosen threshold of
10^{-7}
corresponds to the effective numerical resolution in floating point representation. Unless explicitly mentioned otherwise, in every experiment we perform, the optimization is run until the reconstruction loss has converged.

]

We use “

For multiclass experiments, we use the same balanced construction; targets are one-hot encoded as

Also for ResNet runs we use the same protocol as for random features, but images are kept in tensor form (

b

B(u)=\phi(u+C_{h\rightarrow h}^{3\times 3}[\phi(C_{h\rightarrow h}^{3\times 3}[u])])~.

(165)

Given an image
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 32\times 32}
(as in the case of CIFAR-10 examples), we consider in our experiments deep residual networks with architecture

z_{0}=C_{3\rightarrow h}^{28\times 28}[x]\qquad z_{b}=B(z_{b-1})~~\text{ for }b=1,\dots,4~,\qquad f_{\text{RN}}(x)=[\theta^{(L)}]^{\top}\text{vec}(z_{4})~,

(166)

with
\theta^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{h\cdot 7\cdot 7}
the last layer’s parameters. We denote with
\text{vec}(\cdot)
the flattening of the dimensions of the 3D tensor
z_{4}
to a vector. The choice of dimensionality for
\theta^{(L)}
is motivated by the first convolution mapping the spatial size for CIFAR-10 images to
h\times 7\times 7
and the blocks preserving it. We select the number of output channels
h
based on the choice of the number of parameters in the last layer
p^{(L)}
as these two quantities are tied together, being
h=p^{(L)}/7^{2}
, eventually truncated to the nearest integer. Parameters of the first convolutional layer are initialized as
\theta^{(0)}_{i,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
i.i.d.
, while for
l>1
parameters are initialized as
\theta_{i,j}^{(l)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/\mathrm{fan\_in})
i.i.d. Here,
\mathrm{fan\_in}
equals the number of input units (for convolutional layers:
h\cdot k^{2}
; for the last linear layer:
p^{(L)}
).

As mentioned at the end of Section
4
and in the caption of Figure
2
, we test whether the features computed on the training dataset are spanned by the features of the reconstructed examples. To this end, we calculate the average per-feature orthogonal residual, formally defined as

r(\hat{\Phi})=\frac{1}{n\sqrt{p}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|P_{\hat{\Phi}}^{\perp}\,\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2},

(167)

where
P_{\hat{\Phi}}^{\perp}=I-P_{\hat{\Phi}}
projects onto the orthogonal complement of
\Span\{\Rows(\hat{\Phi})\}
. Thus
r(\hat{\Phi})=0
if and only if every
\varphi(x_{i})
lies in
\Span\{\Rows(\hat{\Phi})\}
. The normalization by
n\sqrt{p}
makes
r(\hat{\Phi})
of order 1 so that values of
r(\hat{\Phi})\ll 1
indicate numerically negligible residuals (
i.e
.
, effective span inclusion).

. Also in these settings, for
