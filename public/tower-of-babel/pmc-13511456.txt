Post-Intubation Hypotension After Titrated Induction with Ciprofol versus Propofol in Older Adults Undergoing Non-Cardiac Surgery: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Hong Bai, Jing Guo, Yumei Li, Song Zhang, Siying Chen, Zhen Wang, Kun Lu, Lizhen Zhang, Fenghua Zhong, Ruirui Gong, Jianshan Li, Jiaxin Han, Xuemei Song, Sanqing Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo compare ciprofol with propofol for titrated induction of anesthesia in older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery, with a focus on post-intubation hypotension and related hemodynamic burden.MethodsIn this prospective, randomized, double-blind trial, adults aged 65–89 years undergoing elective non-cardiac surgery were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either ciprofol or propofol, following an identical titration-to-effect induction protocol. The primary outcome was post-intubation hypotension, defined as any mean arterial pressure (MAP) ≤ 65 mmHg during the 15-minute period after successful tracheal intubation and before surgical incision. Secondary outcomes included hypotension burden expressed as area under the curve (AUC), norepinephrine requirement, cardiac output trajectories, time to loss of consciousness, and early postoperative recovery outcomes.Results160 patients were randomized. Post-intubation hypotension occurred less frequently in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (28/77 [36.3%] vs 53/79 [67.0%]; adjusted odds ratio 0.28, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.15–0.54; P < 0.001), consistent across BIS-adjusted sensitivity analyses. Ciprofol was also associated with a lower AUC for MAP ≤ 65 mmHg (0.0 [0.0–3.2] vs 2.5 [0.0–14.8] mmHg min; P = 0.047), reduced norepinephrine requirement (0.0 [0.0–10.0] µg vs 10.0 [0.0–20.0] µg; P < 0.001), and milder early cardiac output decline (group-by-time interaction β = −0.008, 95% CI −0.010 to −0.005; P < 0.001). No significant between-group differences were observed in early postoperative adverse outcomes.ConclusionIn this single-center trial of older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery, ciprofol-based titrated induction was associated with less post-intubation hypotension, lower rescue vasopressor requirement, and less cumulative hypotensive exposure than propofol, without an apparent increase in early adverse recovery outcomes. These findings suggest a favorable hemodynamic profile of ciprofol, which requires confirmation in larger multicenter studies.Keywords: ciprofol, propofol, older adults, titration-to-effect, post-intubation hypotension

Introduction

As the global population ages, an increasing number of older adults are undergoing non-cardiac surgery.1 Due to age-related reductions in cardiovascular reserve, maintaining hemodynamic stability is particularly important in this population.2 Perioperative hypotension is common among older adults and has been associated with adverse outcomes, including myocardial injury, acute kidney injury, and increased short-term mortality.3–6 Hypotensive episodes frequently occur during induction and the immediate post-intubation period.7 This period represents a clinically important transition phase, during which residual cardiovascular effects of induction agents may contribute to early hemodynamic instability. These hemodynamic changes are closely related to hypnotic exposure and may therefore be influenced by both the choice of agent and the administration strategy.

Achieving an appropriate hypnotic dose during induction remains challenging in older adults. Age-related pharmacodynamic changes, multimorbidity, and interindividual variability may increase susceptibility to excessive hypnotic exposure and hemodynamic depression.8–10 Although fixed-dose regimens are commonly adjusted according to body weight and clinical characteristics, the full hypnotic dose is administered before individual responses can be assessed. Such an approach may not fully accommodate variability in hypnotic sensitivity and may increase susceptibility to abrupt hypotension.11 Importantly, this concern is not limited to patients with advanced comorbidity or higher ASA physical status, as age-related pharmacodynamic changes may also occur in older adults with relatively preserved physiological status.12,13 A pragmatic alternative is titration-to-effect administration. With continuous monitoring of sedation and hemodynamic responses during induction, hypnotic delivery can be adjusted and terminated once predefined clinical endpoints are reached. This strategy enables individualized hypnotic exposure and may reduce excessive early anesthetic load compared with fixed bolus administration.14,15

Propofol remains the most widely used intravenous hypnotic for induction of general anesthesia; however, its dose-related cardiovascular depression remains a concern, particularly in older adults.16 Ciprofol (HSK3486), a novel 2,6-disubstituted phenol derivative, has emerged as an alternative hypnotic and exhibits approximately four-to five-fold greater potency than propofol.17–19 Previous studies have suggested that ciprofol may reduce injection pain and induction-related hypotension compared with propofol.20 Similar hemodynamic advantages have also been reported in elderly patients, including those with cardiovascular vulnerability.21,22

The favorable hemodynamic profile of ciprofol may be related to its distinct pharmacological characteristics, including higher affinity for GABAA receptors and rapid clearance into inactive metabolites, which may allow adequate hypnosis with reduced drug exposure and potentially contribute to a less pronounced cardiovascular response.17,23 However, whether these characteristics translate into a consistent hemodynamic advantage independent of dosing strategy remains uncertain.

Most previous comparisons between ciprofol and propofol have used bolus or fixed-dose administration strategies.24,25 Although ciprofol generally demonstrates a more favorable hemodynamic profile than propofol, the extent of hemodynamic depression may still be influenced by the administered dose.26,27 Therefore, fixed-dose comparisons may not fully distinguish intrinsic drug-related hemodynamic differences from the effects of unequal hypnotic exposure.

Whether ciprofol maintains a hemodynamic advantage when both agents are administered using an individualized titration-to-effect strategy remains unclear. We therefore conducted a prospective, randomized, double-blind study to compare ciprofol and propofol using an identical titration-to-effect induction protocol in older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery. We hypothesized that ciprofol would be associated with a lower incidence of post-intubation hypotension and reduced vasopressor requirements compared with propofol, even when both agents were titrated to achieve the intended hypnotic endpoint.

Material and Methods

Trial Design and Ethical Approval

This prospective, randomized, double-blind, controlled trial was conducted at a tertiary teaching hospital in China between February 20 and June 10, 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University on November 15, 2023 (approval ID: E2023191) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06258967) on February 14, 2024. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults aged 65–89 years with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III who were scheduled for elective non-cardiac surgery requiring general anesthesia.

Exclusion criteria were concurrent enrollment in another clinical trial; body mass index (BMI) ≤ 18 kg/m2 or ≥ 30 kg/m2; anticipated difficult airway or high risk of aspiration; severe hepatic dysfunction (Child–Pugh class C) or renal impairment (estimated glomerular filtration rate ≤ 30 mL/min per 1.73 m2); uncontrolled hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥ 180 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 110 mmHg); clinically significant arrhythmias or valvular disease, including high-grade atrioventricular block, prolonged QT interval, uncontrolled atrial fibrillation, or severe aortic stenosis; neurological or psychiatric disorders such as epilepsy, schizophrenia, or cognitive impairment; chronic alcohol abuse; long-term use of sedatives or opioids; known hypersensitivity to study medications; or the need for vasoactive support before induction.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the ciprofol group or the propofol group. A fixed block size of 10 was used to maintain balanced allocation. Stratification was performed according to ASA physical status (I–II vs III) to support prespecified subgroup analyses. The randomization sequence was generated using R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Allocation concealment was maintained using sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes prepared by independent personnel and opened on the day of surgery after enrollment.

To preserve blinding, both study drugs were prepared in identical syringes as visually indistinguishable white emulsions. Ciprofol (2.5 mg/mL; Haisco Pharmaceutical Group Co., Ltd., Chengdu, China) was diluted with normal saline to 2 mg/mL to allow administration at the same volume-based infusion rates used in both groups. Both study drugs were administered at an identical volume-based infusion rate (1.5 mL kg−1 h−1), with concentrations adjusted to achieve the prespecified dose rates of 15 mg kg−1 h−1 for propofol and 3 mg kg−1 h−1 for ciprofol.18,28 Study drugs were prepared by personnel not involved in anesthesia induction, intraoperative management, or postoperative outcome assessment. Participants, treating anesthesiologists, and outcome assessors remained blinded to group allocation throughout the study.

Study Procedure

Preoperative anesthetic assessments were standardized and included airway evaluation, ASA classification, and baseline hemodynamic measurements. A structured geriatric assessment was performed to enhance baseline characterization and comparability in this older cohort.29 This assessment included frailty (FRAIL questionnaire), cognitive function (Mini-Mental State Examination, MMSE), depressive symptoms (Geriatric Depression Scale–15, GDS-15), nutritional status (Mini Nutritional Assessment–Short Form, MNA-SF), functional independence (Barthel Index and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale, IADL), and comorbidity burden (Charlson Comorbidity Index). All assessments were conducted by trained personnel on the day before surgery. Baseline blood pressure was defined as the average of at least two resting measurements obtained during hospitalization.

Anesthesia Management

On the day of surgery, standard monitoring was established before induction, including noninvasive blood pressure, pulse oximetry, electrocardiography, and bispectral index (BIS). A peripheral intravenous catheter was inserted in an upper-limb vein. Midazolam 0.02 mg/kg was administered intravenously at least 30 minutes before study drug infusion as standardized premedication to reduce preoperative anxiety and provide mild sedation before arterial catheterization and anesthetic induction. All invasive monitoring and induction preparations were finished within this interval. The pressure transducer was calibrated using a square-wave test and zeroed at the level of the midaxillary line. Arterial waveforms and hemodynamic data were recorded using the LiDCOrapid system (LiDCO Ltd, Cambridge, UK). A crystalloid infusion (7–10 mL/kg) was started 20 minutes before induction, and patients remained supine and unstimulated for at least 10 minutes before anesthesia.

During induction, intravenous fluids were administered at 4 mL kg−1 h−1 in both groups, and supplemental oxygen (6 L/min) was delivered via a face mask. After 5 minutes of preoxygenation, the study drug infusion was initiated at the standardized volume rate of 1.5 mL kg−1 h−1 to maintain double-blinding. Sedation level was assessed every 30 seconds using the Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) scale. Loss of consciousness (LOC) was defined as an OAA/S score of 1, corresponding to no response to shoulder tapping.30 At LOC, the infusion rate was reduced to 0.5 mL kg−1 h−1. Cisatracurium 0.2 mg/kg and remifentanil 1 µg/kg were then administered sequentially, with remifentanil infused over 60 seconds. Tracheal intubation was performed when the BIS value decreased to below 60. Successful intubation was confirmed by two consecutive complete end-tidal CO2 waveforms.

After tracheal intubation, remifentanil was maintained at 0.01–0.1 μg kg−1 min−1 and the study drug infusion (0.3–1.6 mL kg−1 h−1) was titrated according to BIS monitoring to maintain a target range of 40–60. No additional invasive procedures were performed during the 15-minute post-intubation observation period. Mechanical ventilation was adjusted to maintain end-tidal CO2 at 35–45 mmHg and peripheral oxygen saturation at ≥ 95% by titrating FiO2, tidal volume, positive end-expiratory pressure, and respiratory rate.

Fifteen minutes after intubation, the study drug infusion was discontinued and sevoflurane was initiated. Anesthesia was subsequently maintained with sevoflurane (end-tidal concentration 1%–4%, titrated to BIS and blood pressure) and remifentanil (0.03–0.25 μg kg−1 min−1). Cisatracurium 0.05 mg/kg was administered every 30 minutes. Beyond the predefined induction and post-intubation observation periods, intraoperative blood pressure management, fluid therapy, and transfusion were guided by institutional practice at the discretion of the attending anesthesiologist.

Hemodynamic management during induction and the 15-minute post-intubation observation period followed identical predefined protocols in both groups. Norepinephrine 10 μg boluses were administered when MAP decreased to ≤65 mmHg. If MAP remained ≤65 mmHg after five boluses, continuous norepinephrine infusion was initiated at 0.05 μg kg−1 min−1. Vasopressor rescue did not constitute an automatic withdrawal criterion. Participants were withdrawn only if prespecified safety criteria were met, including refractory hemodynamic instability despite rescue therapy, serious arrhythmia, suspected allergic reaction, or any condition judged by the attending anesthesiologist to compromise patient safety.

Hypertension during the post-intubation observation period was defined as MAP ≥130 mmHg or systolic blood pressure ≥180 mmHg. Episodes were initially managed with a 20 μg remifentanil bolus and adjustment of the remifentanil infusion rate, as transient increases in blood pressure during this stimulation-free period were considered primarily related to tracheal intubation. Further management was determined by the attending anesthesiologist according to clinical circumstances.

Bradycardia was defined as heart rate ≤45 beats/min and treated with atropine 0.5 mg. When hypotension and bradycardia occurred simultaneously, dopamine 2 mg was administered.

Data Processing

Prespecified peri-induction time windows used for blood pressure and cardiac output analyses are shown in Figure 1. Beat-to-beat hemodynamic data were exported from the LiDCOrapid monitor at the end of each case as comma-separated values files for offline analysis. These files contained sequential beat-to-beat measurements of systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, MAP, and cardiac output. Prespecified artifact screening was applied, excluding values flagged on visual review, systolic blood pressure > 280 or < 30 mmHg, systolic blood pressure < diastolic blood pressure + 5 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure < 10 or >150 mmHg.31

MAP and cardiac output values were averaged over non-overlapping 10-second intervals according to the following rules: all valid data within a window were averaged; if a window contained only artifactual data, the mean value was imputed from adjacent valid windows; and if two or more consecutive windows contained artifacts, missing values were estimated using the nearest valid neighboring intervals.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was post-intubation hypotension, defined as any MAP ≤ 65 mmHg during the 15-minute period after successful tracheal intubation and before surgical incision.

Secondary hemodynamic outcomes included the burden of hypotension during the same period, expressed as the area under the curve (AUC) for MAP ≤ 65 mmHg, MAP ≤ 55 mmHg, and MAP < 70% of the baseline value. Time-weighted AUC above MAP thresholds of 100, 110, 120, 130, and 140 mmHg was also evaluated. Additional secondary outcomes included the time from the start of induction to loss of consciousness, cardiac output trajectories within the first 20 minutes after study drug initiation, and total norepinephrine dose from the start of induction to 15 minutes after intubation.

Postoperative outcomes included agitation and delirium in the postanesthesia care unit (PACU), with delirium assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU).32 During postoperative days 1–3, recovery was evaluated using the Quality of Recovery-15 (QoR-15) scale, awareness was assessed using the modified Brice questionnaire, and postoperative nausea and vomiting and delirium severity were recorded.33–35 On postoperative day 7, complications were identified by chart review or telephone follow-up and graded using the Clavien–Dindo classification, with grade ≥ II considered clinically significant.36 All postoperative outcomes were assessed by trained personnel blinded to group allocation.

Sample Size Calculation

In a pilot study conducted at our center, the incidence of post-intubation hypotension during titrated induction was 30% (9/30) with ciprofol and 57% (17/30) with propofol. Assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and 90% power, we calculated that 70 patients per group would be required to detect a statistically significant difference between groups (SAS Enterprise Guide, version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Allowing for an anticipated dropout rate of 12%, the final target sample size was 160 participants.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses followed a modified intention-to-treat principle and included all randomized participants who received the assigned study drug; participants were analyzed according to their allocated group. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables are presented as mean (SD) or median (IQR), as appropriate, and were compared using the Student t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

For prespecified adjusted analyses, skewed continuous outcomes were analyzed using median regression adjusted for age, sex, and ASA class (I–II vs III), with effects reported as adjusted median differences and 95% confidence intervals. Binary outcomes were analyzed using logistic regression accounting for the ASA stratification variable; adjusted models additionally included age and sex, and results are presented as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Baseline balance was assessed using absolute standardized differences. Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed for age, sex, and ASA class.37 Exploratory subgroup analyses considered frailty status and comorbidity burden, including hypertension and diabetes. All tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Python 3.10.

Results

Between February and June 2024, 288 patients were screened and 160 were enrolled and randomized (Figure 2). After randomization, one patient in the ciprofol group withdrew consent, and two were excluded due to frequent premature ventricular contractions prior to intervention. In the propofol group, one patient was excluded prior to intervention due to a new diagnosis of rapid atrial fibrillation. All post-randomization exclusions occurred within the ASA I–II stratum.

Sensitivity analysis adjusting for achieved BIS

Given the difference in achieved BIS values between groups, a sensitivity analysis was performed to assess whether the primary outcome remained robust after adjustment for hypnotic depth. BIS data during the 15-minute post-intubation period were available for 150 participants (75 in each group). In this complete-case population, the adjusted logistic regression model without BIS adjustment yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (adjusted OR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.13–0.55; P < 0.001). After additionally adjusting for the per-subject mean BIS during minutes 1–15 after intubation, ciprofol remained associated with a lower risk of post-intubation hypotension (adjusted OR, 0.22; 95% CI, 0.10–0.47; P < 0.001).

Secondary Outcomes

Post Hoc Analyses

To further characterize the timing of hypotension within the 15-minute post-intubation window, we performed a post hoc time-to-first-event analysis. Kaplan–Meier curves showed a higher hypotension-free probability in the ciprofol group than in the propofol group (log-rank P = 0.0001; Figure 6), with hypotensive events occurring earlier in the propofol group.

Kaplan–Meier curves for cumulative hypotension-free probability over 15 min post-intubation in ciprofol (blue) and propofol (Orange) groups.

Notes: Shaded areas: 95% CIs. Risk tables show patients at risk and events at each 3-minute interval.

Given variation in how the post-induction period is defined in prior studies,7,38 we performed a post hoc analysis using a broader peri-induction window comprising the first 20 minutes after study drug initiation. Hypotension (MAP ≤ 65 mmHg) occurred in 31.2% of patients in the ciprofol group and 55.7% in the propofol group during this interval (adjusted OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.68; P = 0.002; Supplement Table 2).

Regarding the duration of hypotensive events, the median (IQR) duration of MAP ≤65 mmHg was significantly shorter in the ciprofol group [0.0 (0.0–1.8) min] than in the propofol group [1.5 (0.0–4.2) min] (adjusted MD −1.50, 95% CI −2.48 to −0.52, P = 0.003). Similarly, the duration of MAP ≤70% of baseline MAP was significantly shorter in the ciprofol group [0.0 (0.0–2.8) min] than in the propofol group [1.3 (0.0–4.0) min] (adjusted MD −1.33, 95% CI −2.53 to −0.14, P = 0.029). Additionally, MAP ≤ 65 mmHg duration within 20 min after induction was shorter in the ciprofol group [0.0 (0.0–2.0) min] vs propofol group [1.3 (0.0–4.5) min] (adjusted MD −1.33, 95% CI −2.35 to −0.32, P = 0.010; Supplement Table 3).

Because of the floor effects observed for AUC120, AUC130, and AUC140, categorical post hoc analyses were performed for MAP elevation above predefined thresholds (Supplement Table 4). After adjustment for age, sex, and ASA physical status, the ciprofol group showed higher odds of exceeding MAP thresholds of ≥100 mmHg (adjusted OR 2.83, 95% CI 1.38–5.84; P = 0.005) and ≥110 mmHg (adjusted OR 2.69, 95% CI 1.34–5.41; P = 0.005). No significant differences were observed for higher thresholds (MAP ≥120, ≥130, or ≥140 mmHg).

Adverse Events

No serious adverse events occurred in either group. Peri-induction events, including bradycardia, oxygen desaturation, coughing, and limb movement, were uncommon and similar between groups. No cases of difficult intubation, laryngospasm, or dental trauma were recorded. No adverse events were attributed to protocol deviations or medication administration errors (Supplement Table 5).

Discussion

In this randomized double-blind trial of older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery, ciprofol was associated with a lower incidence and lower burden of post-intubation hypotension than propofol when both drugs were administered using the same titration-to-effect induction protocol. Sensitivity analyses adjusting for achieved BIS yielded consistent results. Ciprofol was also associated with lower norepinephrine requirement and a less steep decline in cardiac output, whereas early postoperative recovery outcomes were broadly similar between groups. These findings suggest that, even within a standardized titration framework designed to reduce dose-related overexposure, hypnotic choice remains clinically relevant in hemodynamically vulnerable older patients.

Anesthesia induction in older adults requires careful titration because hypnotic requirements vary substantially across individuals.28 The broad distribution of cumulative doses observed in our study further underscores the need for tailored dosing rather than standardized regimens (Supplement Figure 2). This is mainly attributed to the individual differences in patients’ age, comorbidities, and organ function, which require personalized treatment strategies rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. Although anesthetic requirements generally decrease with age, dosing decisions must also account for the combined effects of comorbidity, frailty, and vascular aging.39–41 Our previous findings demonstrated that titration-to-effect induction reduced hemodynamic instability compared with standard bolus dosing.30 Because the hemodynamic effects of both ciprofol and propofol are dose-dependent, fixed-dose designs may obscure the distinction between intrinsic drug effects and the consequences of overexposure. By applying the same titration approach to both groups, the present study minimized this source of bias and allowed a more clinically meaningful comparison of hemodynamic safety.

In this trial, post-intubation hypotension was defined within the 15-minute period after successful tracheal intubation. Although this interval lies within the early post-induction phase, it was selected to provide a consistent observation window across patients.42 Because this period precedes surgical stimulation, it allows assessment of hypotension related to the induction agents, intubation, and the onset of mechanical ventilation, with less confounding from surgical factors. This consideration is particularly important in titrated anesthesia, where the interval from drug initiation to intubation may vary substantially across individuals.43,44 Anchoring the observation window to intubation therefore improves comparability between patients. In addition, prior studies in emergency and critical care settings have identified the post-intubation period as a high-risk window for hypotension, with older age repeatedly recognized as an independent risk factor.45 Importantly, our post hoc analyses using broader peri-induction time windows yielded findings in the same direction, supporting the robustness of the main result despite variation in how the post-induction period is defined across studies.

Because the depth and duration of hypotension are clinically relevant determinants of tissue hypoperfusion and subsequent injury, we assessed hypotension burden using both AUC metrics and rescue vasopressor requirement.42,46 In addition to a lower incidence of post-intubation hypotension, ciprofol was associated with a lower AUC for MAP ≤ 65 mmHg, indicating less cumulative hypotensive exposure during this vulnerable period. We also examined MAP < 70% of baseline to account for alternative definitions of hypotension.47 Although the AUC at this threshold did not differ significantly between groups, ciprofol was associated with a shorter duration of hypotension, and the overall pattern remained directionally consistent with the primary findings.48,49 Reduced norepinephrine use with ciprofol provides complementary evidence that the between-group difference was not limited to event counting alone but was also reflected in lower rescue burden during the same observation window. This is particularly relevant in older patients, in whom vasopressor requirement often reflects limited hemodynamic reserve.

Both groups were managed within the same BIS target range (40–60); however, achieved BIS values during the post-intubation period were lower in the ciprofol group, suggesting differences in pharmacodynamic response between the two agents. Sensitivity analysis incorporating mean BIS during this period into the multivariable model yielded consistent results, indicating that the observed hemodynamic benefit was unlikely to be explained solely by differences in achieved hypnotic depth. Nevertheless, BIS primarily reflects cortical electrical activity and may not fully capture the cardiovascular effects of intravenous hypnotics. The relationship between EEG effects and hemodynamic responses across different hypnotic agents remains incompletely understood.

Although the primary focus of this study was the prevention of hypotension, perioperative hemodynamic stability also involves avoidance of excessive hypertension. In the present study, a higher unadjusted AUC for MAP ≥100 mmHg was observed in the ciprofol group; however, this difference was no longer significant after adjustment. Furthermore, the clinical interpretation of an absolute MAP threshold of 100 mmHg depends on individual baseline blood pressure, particularly in older adults with heterogeneous cardiovascular profiles. In our cohort, baseline MAP was approximately 93 mmHg in both groups (median [IQR]: 93.0 [89.0–97.0] mmHg in the ciprofol group and 93.0 [87.8–100.0] mmHg in the propofol group), indicating that MAP ≥100 mmHg represents only a modest elevation above baseline for many patients.

To further characterize these findings, we performed threshold-based analyses evaluating the proportion of patients with any exposure above predefined MAP thresholds. After adjustment for age, sex, and ASA status, ciprofol was associated with a higher proportion of patients exceeding MAP ≥100 and ≥110 mmHg; however, no significant differences were observed at higher thresholds (MAP ≥120, ≥130, or ≥140 mmHg). Therefore, the observed increase in lower-level MAP excursions should be interpreted cautiously and does not appear to represent an increased burden of clinically meaningful hypertensive events.

In addition to arterial pressure, we evaluated cardiac output trajectories to further characterize hemodynamic responses. Although baseline cardiac output and the absolute reduction at 20 minutes were similar between groups, the temporal patterns differed. Both regimens were associated with an early decline in cardiac output, consistent with previous evidence.50 However, the decline was less pronounced with ciprofol than with propofol, suggesting a more gradual early hemodynamic response. The apparent discrepancy between the trajectory analysis and the 20-minute absolute change reflects the different information provided by these approaches. The mixed-effects model captures the rate and timing of hemodynamic changes across repeated measurements, whereas a single time-point comparison may obscure transient differences, particularly in a titration-to-effect protocol where the timing of loss of consciousness and tracheal intubation varies between individuals. In addition, subsequent hemodynamic interventions, including norepinephrine rescue according to the predefined protocol, may have attenuated later between-group differences. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as evidence of different early dynamic responses rather than differences in cumulative cardiac output reduction at 20 minutes. Nevertheless, cardiac output findings should be interpreted cautiously. Cardiac output was estimated using the LiDCOrapid pulse contour algorithm, which may be influenced by acute changes in vascular tone. As norepinephrine administration differed between groups, a potential influence of vasoactive treatment on cardiac output estimates cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, these data should be considered supportive mechanistic observations rather than definitive evidence of differential cardiac effects.

Comprehensive geriatric assessment further strengthened the interpretability of this trial. By evaluating frailty, cognition, mood, nutrition, and functional status, we were able to characterize a population in whom chronological age alone does not adequately capture physiologic vulnerability. This approach improves baseline description and may also help inform future work exploring which geriatric domains are most closely related to induction-related hemodynamic instability.

Standardized midazolam premedication and cisatracurium administration may have introduced potential background effects. However, identical dosing and timing were applied in both groups. Given the low midazolam dose, minimal cardiovascular effects of cisatracurium, and the early 15-minute assessment window, these factors are unlikely to have materially affected the primary hemodynamic outcome.

Strengths and Limitations

Several strengths should be noted. First, both agents were administered using an identical titration-to-effect protocol, reducing the potential influence of excessive hypnotic exposure and allowing a more balanced comparison of their hemodynamic effects. Second, hemodynamic responses were assessed using multiple complementary measures, including hypotension burden, vasopressor requirements, and continuous cardiac output monitoring during a stimulation-free post-intubation period. Third, sensitivity analyses accounting for differences in sedation depth and prespecified subgroup analyses supported the robustness of the primary findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study with a modest sample size limits external generalizability. Four participants were excluded before study drug administration; although analyses followed the predefined modified intention-to-treat principle, the potential impact of these exclusions on generalizability cannot be completely excluded. Second, fixed block randomization carries theoretical allocation predictability toward block completion. Rigorous allocation concealment, separate drug preparation personnel and full double-blinding were applied throughout the trial, greatly minimizing practical prediction bias. Third, the 15-minute post-intubation observation window was selected to isolate early drug-related hemodynamic effects but does not reflect the entire intraoperative course. Fourth, despite BIS-adjusted sensitivity analyses supporting the primary findings, residual pharmacodynamic differences between agents and potential limitations of LiDCOrapid-derived cardiac output measurements may restrict mechanistic interpretation. Finally, secondary and post hoc analyses were exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously because no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

Conclusion

In older adults undergoing non-cardiac surgery, ciprofol-based titrated induction was associated with less post-intubation hypotension and lower norepinephrine requirement than propofol, with no apparent increase in early adverse recovery outcomes. These preliminary single-center findings suggest a favorable hemodynamic profile of ciprofol in older surgical patients, which remained consistent after sensitivity adjustment incorporating post-intubation BIS values. Generalizability to older patients with severe comorbidities or advanced organ dysfunction is limited, and larger multicenter trials are warranted for confirmation.
