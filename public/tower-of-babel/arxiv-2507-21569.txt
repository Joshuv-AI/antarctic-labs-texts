An em algorithm for quantum Boltzmann machines Preprint: APS/123-QED
Takeshi Kimura 1, Kohtaro Kato 1, Masahito Hayashi 2,3,4

Abstract
We develop a quantum version of the em algorithm for training quantum Boltzmann machines. The em algorithm is an information-geometric extension of the well-known expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm, offering a structured alternative to gradient-based methods with potential advantages in stability and convergence. We implement the algorithm on a semi-quantum restricted Boltzmann machine, where quantum effects are confined to the hidden layer. This structure enables analytical update rules while preserving quantum expressivity. Numerical experiments on benchmark datasets show that the proposed method achieves stable learning and outperforms gradient-based training in several cases. These results demonstrate the potential of information-geometric optimization for quantum machine learning, particularly in settings where standard methods struggle due to non-commutativity or vanishing gradients.

I
Introduction

Quantum machine learning is a field that aims to exploit quantum phenomena to enhance the expressivity and efficiency of learning models
schuld2014quest
;
biamonte2017quantum
;
dunjko2018machine
;
ciliberto2018quantum
;
cerezo2022challenges
. Some directions aim to enhance classical models through quantum subroutines, such as quantum kernel methods
schuld2019quantum
or quantum linear algebra
harrow2009quantum
. Others, such as variational quantum algorithms
PhysRevA.98.032309
;
benedetti2019parameterized
, rely on parametrized quantum circuits trained via classical optimization.
Although these models are actively studied, especially in light of recent advances in quantum hardware, the extent to which quantum machine learning offers concrete advantages over classical machine learning remains largely unsettled.

The quantum Boltzmann machine (QBM)
amin2018quantum
is a quantum machine learning scheme defined by a Hamiltonian that generalizes the Boltzmann machine to non-commutative settings. The aim is to leverage quantum coherence and entanglement to enhance the modeling capacity of energy-based generative models. The classical Boltzmann machine, originally proposed as a stochastic neural network based on statistical mechanics
ackley1985learning
, models data via an energy-based probability distribution and has played an influential role in the development of deep generative models.

In learning, gradient-based optimization is one of the most widely used methods in modern machine learning, particularly for deep and energy-based models. Its generality make it a powerful and flexible choice also for Boltzmann machines.
However, gradient-based methods treat the model largely as a black box and often struggle with non-convexity of the cost function when hidden variables are involved.

In such cases, the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm
dempster1977maximum
provides a structured alternative
amari1992information
. Unlike gradient-based methods, the EM algorithm exploits the hidden-layer structure by alternating between inference (E-step) and parameter optimization (M-step). This difference enable the algorithm to avoid issues such as vanishing gradients and poor convergence in highly non-convex landscapes.

Extending the EM framework to quantum settings may offer similar advantages, providing a structured approach to optimization in the presence of hidden quantum layers. However, a straightforward application of the EM algorithm to QBMs is generally infeasible due to the lack of a well-defined conditional distribution.

To address this challenge, we adopt the em algorithm, an information-geometric generalization of EM. The em algorithm reformulates the learning procedure as alternating projections in a dual statistical manifold: mixture and exponential families, which has found widespread applications in machine learning and neural network models
amari2016information
;
amari1995information
;
fujimoto2007modified
;
allassonniere2019new
. The information-geometric formulation can be naturally extended to the quantum setting by replacing classical statistical manifolds with their non-commutative analogues
hayashi2023bregman
;
hayashi2024reverse
;
hayashi2024iterative
;
hayashi2024generalized
;
hayashi2024bregman
.

Figure 1
:

This figure illustrates the em algorithm, an information-geometric generalization of the EM algorithm, proposed as an alternative approach to training QBMs. The algorithm iteratively minimizes the divergence between an exponential family (model manifold) and a mixture family (data manifold) via alternating projections. For demonstration, we apply the method to sqRBMs, where quantum effects are confined to the hidden layer.

Building on this perspective, we introduce a quantum version of the em algorithm for QBMs. To evaluate its performance and derive explicit update rules, we implement the algorithm in a semi-quantum restricted Boltzmann machine (sqRBM)
demidik2025expressive
, a hybrid model in which quantum effects appear only in the hidden layer. This setting reduces the complexity of analytical computation in each step of the em algorithm, yielding closed-form expressions for parameter updates. While sqRBMs can be simulated efficiently on a classical hardware, they are known to offer enhanced expressive power in the hidden layer compared to fully classical models
demidik2025expressive
.

We demonstrate the quantum em algorithm within the sqRBM framework for several data sets. Numerical experiments show stable learning behavior and improved average optimization compared to gradient-based methods in some cases. While the sqRBM simplifies the quantum model by confining quantum effects to the hidden layer, this study represents a first step toward a solid learning framework for QBMs and aims to provide a foundation for future extensions to fully quantum architectures.

II
Results

II.1
Model (semi-quantum restricted Boltzmann machine)

A Boltzmann machine (BM)
ackley1985learning
is an energy-based probabilistic generative model defined on an undirected graph
G=(\mathcal{V},E)
.
The vertices corresponding to binary units are classified into visible and hidden layers. The visible layer, denoted by the set of units
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{N}\}

(v_{i}=\pm 1,i\in[1,N])
, represents the inputs and outputs of the observed data. The hidden layer, denoted by
H=\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{M}\}
, represents latent features of the data.
The edges of the graph represent the interaction pattern between units, and each edge is assigned a weight indicating the strength of the coupling.

A restricted Boltzmann machine (RBM) is a BM in which no connections exist between units within the same layer, resulting in a bipartite graph structure. This ensures that units within each layer are conditionally independent given the state of the opposite layer.
The energy function of RBM is given as

\displaystyle E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})=-\sum_{v_{i}\in V}b_{i}v_{i}-\sum_{h_{j}\in H}b_{j}h_{j}-\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}v_{i}h_{j},

(1)

where
{\bf v}=(v_{1},...,v_{N})
and
{\bf h}=(h_{1},...,h_{M})
are short-hand notations,
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
are the bias strength and
w_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}
are the coupling strength between
v_{i}
and
h_{j}
. The entire state of the RBM is represented by the probability distribution:

\displaystyle P_{VH,\theta}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h}):=e^{-E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})}/Z,\quad Z=\sum_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h}}e^{-E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})}.

(2)

The goal of training BM is to reproduce a probability distribution on
V
by the marginal distribution

P_{V,\theta}({\bf v}):=\sum_{{\bf h}\in H}P_{VH,\theta}(\bf v,h)

by finding suitable parameters
\theta=(b,w)
.

A quantum Boltzmann machine (QBM)
amin2018quantum
is a model defined by replacing the binary units in the BM by qubits (1/2-spin quantum systems)
\mathbb{C}^{2}={\rm span}\{|\pm 1\rangle\}
. The energy function of BM is replaced by a local Pauli Hamiltonian on the graph that can include non-commuting interaction terms.

In the main analysis, we use semiquantum RBM (sqRBM)
demidik2025expressive
with non-commutative terms only in the hidden units. As in the classical case, the vertices are decomposed as
\mathcal{V}=V\sqcup H
, where
V
refers the visible layer and
H
refers the hidden layer. Let us denote the Pauli operators acting on the
i
-th qubit as

\displaystyle\sigma_{i}^{a}=\underbrace{I\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{i-1}\otimes\sigma^{a}\otimes\underbrace{I\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{N+M-i},\quad a=x,z.

(3)

Then, the Hamiltonian of sqRBM is given by:

\displaystyle H=-\sum_{i\in V}b_{i}\sigma_{i}^{z}-\sum_{j\in H}(b_{j}\sigma_{j}^{z}+\Gamma_{j}\sigma_{j}^{x})-\sum_{(i,j)\in E}w_{ij}\sigma_{i}^{z}\sigma_{j}^{z},

(4)

where
\Gamma_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are the additional quantum parameters.
The quantum state of the RQBM is the quantum Gibbs state

\displaystyle\rho_{VH,\theta}:=e^{-H}/Z,\quad Z=\Tr[e^{-H}],

(5)

and the reduced state on the visible layer is given by
\rho_{V,\theta}=\Tr_{H}\rho_{V,\theta}
. The probability distribution on the visible units is then

\displaystyle P_{V,\theta}(\mathbf{v})=\Tr[\Lambda_{\mathbf{v}}\rho_{V,\theta}],

(6)

where
\Lambda_{\mathbf{v}}
is a projection operator onto the computational basis of the visible units, given as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathbf{v}}=\outerproduct{\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}}\,.

(7)

The essential feature of sqRBM is its hybrid design which enables analytical derivation of output probabilities and gradients, and improves classical simulatability. Although it does not provide fully quantum enhancement, it has been theoretically shown that an RBM requires three times as many hidden units as an sqRBM to represent the same probability distribution
demidik2025expressive
.

II.2
The gradient descent

The cost function of BM is the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta})
defined as

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})\left(\log P_{V}(\mathbf{v})-\log P_{V,\theta}(\mathbf{v})\right).

(8)

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta})=0
if and only if
P_{V}=P_{V,\theta}
. The problem is minimizing the KL-divergence by updating
\theta
. The first term of the KL-divergence is independent of
\theta
, so this cost function is equivalent to the log-likelihood

Q(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta})=-\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})\log P_{V,\theta}(\mathbf{v})\,.

A common approach to minimize the KL divergence is to apply the gradient descent (GD) algorithm. In this case, the parameters are updated according to the gradient of the cost function

\displaystyle\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\cdot\partial_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}},

(9)

where
\eta>0
is the learning rate that controls the step size.

The gradient of BM is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})\sum_{\mathbf{h}}P_{H|V,\theta}(\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{v})\partial_{\theta}E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h}}P_{VH,\theta}(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})\partial_{\theta}E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})

(10)

\displaystyle=-(\overline{\langle\partial_{\theta}E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}}-\langle\partial_{\theta}E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})\rangle_{\theta})

(11)

where

\displaystyle P_{H|V,\theta}(\mathbf{h}|\mathbf{v})

\displaystyle=e^{-E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})}/\sum_{\mathbf{h}}e^{-E(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{h})},

(12)

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\theta}
and
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}
represent the average by
P_{VH,\theta}
with free and fixed visible variables, respectively, and
\overline{\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}}\equiv\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})\langle\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}
represents average by the data distribution.

The GD algorithm can be applied to QBM too, where the cost function is
given by the quantum KL divergence (quantum relative entropy) defined as

D_{KL}(\rho\|\sigma):=\Tr\rho(\log\rho-\log\sigma)\geq 0\,.

(13)

The gradient is then calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}=-\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}({\bf v})\left(\frac{\Tr[\Lambda_{\mathbf{v}}\partial_{\theta}e^{-H}]}{\Tr[\Lambda_{\mathbf{v}}e^{-H}]}-\frac{\Tr[\partial_{\theta}e^{-H}]}{\Tr[e^{-H}]}\right)\,.

(14)

In QBMs, however, the presence of transverse fields leads to non-commutativity between the Hamiltonian
H
and its parameter derivatives
\partial_{\theta}H
. This non-commutativity makes it difficult to express the gradient by the expectation values of local operators, as is possible in classical BMs. To address this issue, previous works such as Refs.
amin2018quantum
;
kieferova2017tomography
have proposed using the Golden–Thompson inequality to obtain an upper bound of the KL divergence
{\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}}
, thereby replacing the original optimization objective with a more tractable approximation. For this new cost function, the gradient can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}{\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}({\bf v})\left(\frac{\Tr[e^{-H_{\mathbf{v}}}\partial_{\theta}H_{\mathbf{v}}]}{\Tr[e^{-H_{\mathbf{v}}}]}-\frac{\Tr[e^{-H}\partial_{\theta}H]}{\Tr[e^{-H}]}\right)

(15)

\displaystyle=-(\overline{\langle\partial_{\theta}H\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}}-\langle\partial_{\theta}H\rangle_{\theta})\,,

(16)

where

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{v}}=\bra{\mathbf{v}}H\ket{\mathbf{v}}

(17)

is called the clamped Hamiltonian. In particular, for sqRBMs, the clamped Hamiltonian coincides with the model Hamiltonian, and
D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\tilde{D}_{\mathrm{KL}}
, meaning that Eq. (
16
) yields the exact update rule.

In addition, various gradient-based learning algorithms have been studied for the fully visible (i.e., no hidden layer) QBM
kappen2020learning
;
coopmans2024sample
;
patel2024quantum
;
tuysuz2024learning
;
patel2024natural
;
minervini2025evolved
, as well as for models that include hidden layers
wiebe2019generative
;
zoufal2021variational
.

GD algorithms are simple to implement and widely used, but they face fundamental difficulties when applied to highly non-convex objective functions (except the fully visible case
coopmans2024sample
). In such settings, convergence guarantees are generally weak, and the optimization process can be hindered by complex landscape features. In particular, GD may stagnate in regions where the objective function is nearly flat and the gradient becomes very small, even if the point is not a local minimum. This issue is well-known in e.g., variational quantum algorithms such as VQE
mcclean2018barren
, where barren plateaus significantly impair trainability.

II.3
The em algorithm

In the classical case, an alternative to gradient-based training is the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm
dempster1977maximum
, which optimizes the model by alternating between two steps. The E-step computes the expectation of the log-likelihood of the complete data with respect to the conditional distribution of the hidden variables, given the current parameters. The M-step then updates the model parameters by maximizing this expected log-likelihood.

Unlike gradient-based methods, the E-step for BM is easy to implement and the M-step is a convex optimization problem, which provides theoretical guarantees of convergence and helps avoid issues such as vanishing gradients away from a local minimum. This motivates us generalization of EM to the quantum domain to mitigate the barren plateau problem in certain gradient quantum learning settings
ortiz2021entanglement
.

However, extending the classical EM algorithm to QBMs is challenging due to the absence of well-defined conditional distributions and the non-commutativity of operators. Nevertheless, the EM algorithm can be interpreted as an iterative projection method in information geometry, leading to a generalization known as the em algorithm
amari1992information
.

To overcome the challenges of extending EM to QBMs, we consider applying a quantum version of the em algorithm, which is given by applying alternating projections for general exponential and mixture families
hayashi2023bregman
, to QBM by promoting its information-geometric formulation to the non-commutative setting.
As a concrete demonstration, we implement this algorithm in an sqRBM, where the visible layer remains commutative. This structural feature allows us to express the update rule analytically and efficiently, while still yielding a learning dynamics that differs fundamentally from both gradient-based quantum training and classical EM algorithms.

The em algorithm is an iterative algorithm that minimizes the KL divergence between an exponential family
\mathcal{E}
and a mixture family
\mathcal{M}

amari1995information
.
An exponential family
\mathcal{E}
for a random variable
X=\{x\}
is a set of probability distributions
p(x;\bm{\theta})
given by the exponential form:

\displaystyle p(X;\bm{\theta})=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}r_{i}(x)+k(x)-\psi(\mathbf{\theta})\right\},

(18)

where
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
n
-dimensional vector parameter,
\{r_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{n},k(x)
are functions of
x
and
\psi
is a normalization factor.
A mixture family
\mathcal{M}
is a set of distributions
q(x)
formed by a probability mixture of
m
component distributions
\{q_{i}(x)\}_{i=1}^{m}
:

\displaystyle q(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}q_{i}(x),

(19)

where
\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}=1,\quad w_{i}\geq 0
. Typically, it is given by a set of linear constraint on expectation values.

The algorithm aims to calculate the following (classical or quantum) KL divergence:

\displaystyle\min_{P\in\mathcal{M},Q\in\mathcal{E}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q).

(20)

In the em algorithm, we apply the
e
-projection (
e
-step) and the
m
-projection (
m
-step) alternately.
The
e
-projection is a projection of
Q_{t}
to
\mathcal{M}
along an exponential family to find a point
P
on a mixture family
\mathcal{M}
that minimizes the divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)
as follows:

\displaystyle P_{t}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{P\in\mathcal{M}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q_{t}).

(21)

This corresponds to the
E
step in the EM algorithm.
The
m
-projection is a projection
P_{t}
to
\mathcal{E}
along a mixture family to find a point
Q
on an exponential family
\mathcal{E}
that minimizes the divergence
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)
as follows:

\displaystyle Q_{t+1}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{Q\in\mathcal{E}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t}\|Q).

(22)

This corresponds to the
M
step in the EM algorithm.
During this process, we have

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t-1}\|Q_{t})\geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t}\|Q_{t})\geq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{t}\|Q_{t+1}).

(23)

The KL divergence decreases monotonically, and the algorithm converges at a local minima in the above process.

The em algorithm for QBM is straightforwardly defined by the exponential family

\mathcal{E}=\{\rho_{VH,\theta}\mid\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{|\theta|}\}
, the mixture family
\mathcal{M}=\{\rho_{VH}\mid\bra{\mathbf v}\rho_{V}\ket{\mathbf v}=P_{V}(\mathbf{v}),\>\forall\mathbf{v}\in V\}
. After obtaining an optimal pair
(\rho_{VH}^{*},\rho_{VH,\theta}^{*})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{E}
, the cost function of the QBM is bounded by

D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta}^{*})\leq D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho^{*}_{VH}\|\rho^{*}_{VH,\theta})

(24)

by the data-processing inequality
lindblad1975completely
, and often the equality is achieved.

For any probability distribution
P_{V}
and
\rho_{VH}
, let us denote

\displaystyle P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{{\bf v}}P_{V}({\bf v})\ket{\mathbf{v}}\bra{\mathbf{v}}\otimes\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v}}\,,

(25)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\bra{\mathbf{v}}\rho_{VH}\ket{\mathbf{v}}}{\bra{\mathbf{v}}\rho_{V}\ket{\mathbf{v}}}\,.

(26)

Any state in
\mathcal{M}
family can be written in this form for sqRBM:

\mathcal{M}=\left\{P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V}\mid\rho_{VH}\right\}.

(27)

Then, consider the objective KL divergence:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V}\|\rho_{VH,\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v}}\|\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v},\theta})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\|P_{V,\theta}).

(28)

and set the initial state to be
\rho_{VH,\theta^{(0)}}\in\mathcal{E}
. For a given
t\in\{0,1,...\}
, each step of the em algorithm for sqRBM is as follows:

e
-step
: From a given state
\rho_{VH,\theta^{(t)}}\in\mathcal{E}
, we need to find the optimal element from the mixture family
\mathcal{M}
. Since the second term in Eq. (
28
) is constant over
\mathcal{M}
, the minimization is reduced to that of the first term only. Thus, given
\theta^{(t)}
, we choose

\displaystyle\rho_{H|V}^{(t)}

\displaystyle:=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\rho_{H|V}}\sum_{\mathbf{v}}P_{V}(\mathbf{v})D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v}}\|\rho_{H|V=\mathbf{v},\theta^{(t)}}).

(29)

Since
D_{KL}\geq 0
, one can easily see that the minimum is achieved when

\displaystyle\rho_{H|V}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}.

(30)

Note that in the quantum case, the e-step becomes nontrivial due to the absence of well-defined conditional states; see
hayashi2024generalized
for further discussion. However, in the case of sqRBM, the commutativity of the visible subspace enables a straightforward implementation of the e-step, and the relative entropy can be expressed in a tractable form.

m
-step
: From given
P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}
, we find the optimal element from the exponential family
\mathcal{E}
. In other words, we calculate

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}\|\rho_{VH,\theta})

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\theta}\left(Z+\mathrm{Tr}[(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}})H]\right).

(32)

Recall that
H
is linear in
\theta=(b,w)
, and
Z=\Tr[e^{-H}]
is a convex function of
\theta
. Therefore, this is a convex optimization in terms of
\theta
.
Therefore a simple gradient descent method guarantees the convergence of the
m
-step, for which the update rules of the parameters are given as follows:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\leftarrow\theta+\eta\mathrm{Tr}_{V}P_{V}\left(\Tr_{H}[\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}\partial_{\theta}H]-\Tr[\rho_{VH,\theta}\partial_{\theta}H]\right)

(33)

\displaystyle=\theta+\eta(\overline{\langle\partial_{\theta}H\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta^{(t)}}}-\langle\partial_{\theta}H\rangle_{\theta}),

(34)

where

\displaystyle\overline{\langle\cdots\rangle_{\mathbf{v},\theta}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Tr}_{V}P_{V}\Tr_{H}[\rho_{H|V,\theta}\cdots],

(35)

\displaystyle\langle\cdots\rangle_{\theta}

\displaystyle=\Tr[\rho_{VH,\theta}\cdots].

(36)

See Appendix
A1
for the details of the calculation. Since Eq. (
31
) can be regarded as a fully visible QBM on
VH
with
P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}
as the target state, this step is the GD training of the fully-visible QBM in Ref.
coopmans2024sample
, that shows the strict convexity and
L
-smoothness of the function (
31
) (Lemmas 6, 7 in Ref.
coopmans2024sample
). Therefore, the gradient optimization in

-steps converges sufficiently fast.

While we focus on sqRBMs to facilitate analytical treatment and numerical implementation, the em algorithm is not limited to this semiquantum setting. By leveraging the generalized quantum Arimoto–Blahut algorithm proposed in Ref.
hayashi2024generalized

These are exactly the update rules of the GD algorithm (

In both algorithms, training of BM entails an inherent computational difficulty: the gradient of the KL divergence is given by the difference between two expectations, one over the data distribution (positive phase) and the other over the model distribution (negative phase). While the positive phase can often be efficiently computed, evaluating the negative phase requires sampling from the model distribution (Gibbs sampling), which involves the intractable exact calculation of partition function and becomes computationally prohibitive for large-scale networks.

A commonly used approximation is the contrastive divergence (CD) method
hinton2002training
, which estimates the negative phase by running a finite-step Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) initialized from the data distribution. For EM algorithm, one can approximate the e-step by a MCMC and the m-step by a few steps of gradient descent, the overall update procedure effectively a slight generalization of a GD algorithm with a CD method, i.e., such algorithm is a limiting case with truncated inference and partial maximization in EM algorithm
song2016learning
.

Although CD often performs well in practice, it does not guarantee convergence to the true local optimum.

We now describe the parameter settings and training methodology. To reduce sensitivity to the initial paramters and ensure statistical robustness, we perform 100 independent training runs for each experiment. The set of initial parameters is sampled uniformly from the interval
[-5,5]
in each run. For reproducibility, the random seed is fixed to 0. The training performance is evaluated by averaging the KL divergence over the 100 runs at each training step, resulting in the averaged learning curves. We use fixed hyperparameters for all models throughout this study: learning rate
\eta=0.2
and convergence threshold
\epsilon=1\times 10^{-7}
. No model-specific hyperparameter tuning is performed to ensure a fair comparison across algorithms.

We now discuss the comparative performance of the proposed em algorithm and the standard GD method. As shown in
fig.

The em algorithm outperforms or matches GD on three out of four datasets (A, B, and D), while GD performs better on dataset C. These results suggest that the relative advantage of each method depends on the structure of the data. Although the em algorithm demonstrates good general performance, it tends to converge more slowly than GD, which requires further improvement.

III
Discussion

In this study, we apply a quantum version of the em algorithm
hayashi2023bregman
to QBMs instead of extending the classical EM algorithm to QBMs.
This approach provides a principled alternative to gradient-based methods, leveraging the dual geometry of exponential and mixture families to structure the optimization process. We demonstrate its applicability by implementing the algorithm on a sqRBM, a hybrid architecture in which quantum effects are confined to the hidden layer. This structure allows the em update rule to be expressed analytically, while still preserving some of the expressive power of quantum models. Experimental results on multiple datasets indicate that the proposed method achieves effective learning performance on three out of four datasets, highlighting the potential of our framework in training QBMs under conditions where gradient-based methods often suffer from plateaus in the optimization landscape.

Despite these conceptual advantages, our experiments also reveal a practical limitation: the learning process often requires a large number of iterations to converge, particularly for certain data distributions. Addressing this slow convergence remains an important direction for future work. One promising strategy is to exploit the mathematical structure of the em algorithm’s
m
-step, whose strong convexity and
L
-smoothness make it amenable to accelerated optimization techniques such as accelerated gradient descent. Another complementary direction is to develop approximate learning schemes. For example, Ref.
song2016learning
introduces an EM-like method that generalizes contrastive divergence (CD), offering a potential foundation for constructing fast, approximate variants of the quantum em algorithm proposed in this study.

Beyond improving convergence speed, enhancing the expressive power of the model is also a key challenge. While our analysis focused on a specific class of sqRBMs, future work could extend the method to more general architectures such as those discussed in Ref.
demidik2025expressive
. Furthermore, generalizing the algorithm to fully quantum RBMs, in which the visible units are also non-commutative, represents a significant step toward broader applicability. In this context, the generalized quantum Arimoto–Blahut algorithm proposed in Ref.
hayashi2024generalized
provides a valuable framework. By combining our em approach with such tools, we aim to establish a unified and scalable learning scheme for general QBMs capable of capturing more complex data distributions.

A1
Proof of the calculation of the m-step

The
m
-step is given as follows:

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\theta}D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}\|\rho_{VH,\theta})

(61)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\theta}\Tr(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )(\log(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )-\log\rho_{VH,\theta})

(62)

Since
\Tr(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )(\log(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} ))
does not depend on
\theta
, we minimize
-\Tr(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )\log\rho_{VH,\theta}
.
We have

\displaystyle-\Tr(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )\log\rho_{VH,\theta}=Z+\Tr(P_V\times\rho_{H|V, \theta^{(t)}} )H

(63)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}:=\mathop{\rm argmin}\limits_{\theta}Z+\mathrm{Tr}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}})H.

(64)

The GD to minimize
\theta
is given as follows:

\displaystyle\theta

\displaystyle\leftarrow\theta+\eta\big(\partial_{\theta}Z+\mathrm{Tr}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}})\partial_{\theta}H\big)

(65)

\displaystyle=\theta+\eta\big(\mathrm{Tr}_{V}P_{V}\partial_{\theta}Z+\mathrm{Tr}(P_{V}\times\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}})\partial_{\theta}H\big)

(66)

\displaystyle=\theta+\eta\mathrm{Tr}_{V}P_{V}\big(\partial_{\theta}Z+\mathrm{Tr}_{H}\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}\partial_{\theta}H\big).

(67)

The derivative of
Z
with respect to
\theta
is given as follows:

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}Z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-H)}\partial_{\theta}\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-H)

(68)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-H)}\mathrm{Tr}\partial_{\theta}\exp(-H)

(69)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-H)}\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\sum_{m=1}^{n}e^{-m\delta\tau H}\partial_{\theta}H\delta te^{-(n-m)\delta\tau H}+\mathcal{O}(\delta\tau^{2})\right)

(70)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\frac{1}{\mathrm{Tr}\exp(-H)}\mathrm{Tr}\left(-\int_{0}^{1}d\tau e^{-\tau H}\partial_{\theta}He^{(\tau-1)H}\right)

(71)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{Tr}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\frac{e^{-\tau H}\partial_{\theta}He^{(\tau-1)H}}{\mathrm{Tr}\exp{(-H)}}

(72)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\frac{e^{-H}\partial_{\theta}H}{\Tr e^{-H}}

(73)

\displaystyle=-\Tr\rho_{VH,\theta}\partial_{\theta}H.

(74)

Therefore, the GD to minimize
\theta
is given as follows:

\displaystyle\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\mathrm{Tr}_{V}P_{V}\left(\Tr_{H}[\rho_{H|V,\theta^{(t)}}\partial_{\theta}H]-\Tr[\rho_{VH,\theta}\partial_{\theta}H]\right).

(75)
