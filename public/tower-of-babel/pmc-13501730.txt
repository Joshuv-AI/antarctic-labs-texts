Hypertension and its association with behavioral risk factors for non-communicable diseases: insights from the 2021 Vietnam WHO STEPS survey
Authors: Minh Van Hoang, Lan Thi Hoang Vu, Quyen Thi Tu Bui, Hoang Tu Le, Trang Thu Vu, Toan Quoc Luu, Don Nguyen, Uyen Thai Tran, Bao Quoc Tran, Lam Tuan Nguyen, Truong Duc Lai, Annie Chu, Angela Pratt, Leanne Riley, Tuan Thanh Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives and methodsTo estimate the prevalence of hypertension and examine its associations with individual and clustered behavioral risk factors among Vietnamese adults using nationally representative data from the 2021 Vietnam WHO STEPS survey. We conducted a cross-sectional, survey-weighted analysis. The hypertension prevalence analysis included 3,809 adults aged 18-69 years with valid blood pressure measurements, and multivariable regression analyses were conducted among 3,748 participants with complete data on hypertension, behavioral risk factors, BMI, and socio-demographic covariates. Behavioral risk factors included current tobacco smoking, current alcohol drinking, insufficient physical activity, and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake, examined individually and as an unweighted cumulative index ranging from 0 to 4.ResultsThe survey-weighted crude prevalence of hypertension was 26.4%, with higher prevalence among men than women and a strong age gradient. In individual-risk-factor models, alcohol drinking and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake remained associated with higher odds of hypertension after adjustment. The cumulative behavioral risk factor index showed a graded association with hypertension, with the highest adjusted odds among participants with all four risk factors. These findings provide updated evidence on hypertension and behavioral risk factor clustering in Vietnam and support integrated NCD prevention and hypertension control strategies, while causal inference cannot be made.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07909-0.

Introduction

Hypertension is a major global public health problem and one of the leading modifiable risk factors for cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, stroke, and premature mortality. It is commonly defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication. Globally, hypertension affects an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years, with approximately two-thirds living in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Awareness, treatment, and control remain suboptimal: an estimated 46% of adults with hypertension are unaware of their condition, 42% are diagnosed and treated, and only about one in five have their blood pressure controlled [2].

Vietnam, a lower-middle-income country in Southeast Asia, is also experiencing a rising burden of hypertension in the context of rapid demographic, socioeconomic, and epidemiological transitions [3, 4]. National STEPS surveys have documented an increase in hypertension among adults aged 18–69 years, from 15.3% in 2010 to 18.9% in 20153,5. The burden is substantial. In 2010, NCDs accounted for an estimated 318,000 deaths, equivalent to 72% of all deaths, and 14 million disability-adjusted life years in Vietnam. Hypertension has also been estimated to account for 91,600 deaths annually, representing 20.8% of total deaths and 7.2% of disability-adjusted life years lost, mainly through stroke and cardiovascular disease [6, 7]. Consequently, prevention and control of hypertension and other non-communicable diseases have been identified as key public health priorities in Vietnam [6, 8].

Behavioral risk factors are central to hypertension and NCD prevention because they are common, modifiable, and often socially patterned. In Vietnam, NCDs account for a substantial proportion of mortality, partly reflecting the high prevalence of behavioral risks such as tobacco smoking, alcohol use, unhealthy diets, and insufficient physical activity. In this study, we focus on four core behavioral risk factors captured in the WHO STEPS framework: current tobacco smoking, current alcohol drinking, insufficient physical activity, and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake. These behaviors are relevant to hypertension through multiple biological and behavioral pathways, including vascular dysfunction, sympathetic activation, weight gain, unhealthy dietary profiles, and broader cardiometabolic risk accumulation.

Since the previous Vietnam STEPS survey in 20153, updated national surveillance is needed to assess the current hypertension burden and the behavioral risk profile of Vietnamese adults. The 2021 Vietnam WHO STEPS survey provides standardized, nationally representative data to examine hypertension and behavioral risk factor clustering in the context of ongoing demographic, socioeconomic, and lifestyle transitions. Although these broader contextual changes were not directly tested in the present analysis, they support the need for updated population-based evidence.

Previous STEPS-based analyses in Vietnam have described hypertension prevalence and selected individual risk factors [3, 5]. However, less is known about how multiple behavioral risk factors co-occur and how their cumulative clustering is associated with hypertension. This gap is important because clustered behavioral risks may reflect shared upstream determinants and may require integrated prevention strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the prevalence of hypertension and examine its associations with individual and clustered behavioral risk factors among Vietnamese adults using nationally representative data from the 2021 Vietnam WHO STEPS survey.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study used data from the 2021 Vietnam WHO STEPwise Approach to NCD Risk Factor Surveillance survey, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey conducted across all 63 provinces/cities using standardized WHO protocols and tools [9]. The target population was Vietnamese adults aged 18–69 years residing in Vietnam at the time of the survey. This national survey provides an appropriate dataset for examining hypertension and modifiable behavioral risk factors across diverse geographic and socioeconomic populations.

Data source and sampling

The survey used a multistage, stratified cluster sampling design based on the National Sampling Master Frame developed from the 2019 Population and Housing Census [10]. Primary sampling units were selected with probability proportional to population size, households were systematically selected from updated household lists, and one eligible adult aged 18–69 years was randomly selected from each household using the Kish method. The survey included STEP 1 household interviews, STEP 2 physical measurements, and STEP 3 biochemical measurements. Participants were excluded if they were not permanent residents of Vietnam, were inpatients at health facilities, or had impaired mental health.

A total of 4738 individuals completed STEP 1. The hypertension prevalence analysis included 3,809 adults with valid blood pressure measurements. Complete data on all four behavioral risk factors were available for 3773 participants, and multivariable regression analyses included 3748 participants with complete data on hypertension, behavioral risk factors, BMI, and socio-demographic covariates.

Dependent variable

The primary outcome was hypertension. Trained health workers took blood pressure with a WHO-recommended digital automatic sphygmomanometer (BOSO device) and appropriate cuffs [11]. Participants’ right arms were measured while seated after 15 min of rest. Three 3-minute readings were taken. The second and third valid readings were averaged for analysis. If the second or third reading was invalid, the average of the two valid readings was used [11]. The survey measuring technique identified faulty readings as device error, participant movement, improper cuff positioning, or physiologically implausible levels.

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg and/or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication within 2 weeks [9].

Behavioral risk factors

Trained interviewers gave the WHO STEPS questionnaire [9] to assess behavioral risk factors. Smoking, drinking, inactivity, and fruit/vegetable intake were studied as behavioral risk factors.

Cigarettes, cigars, and pipes were considered current smoking if self-reported. Drinking at least one standard alcoholic drink in the past 30 days was considered current. These variable measures current alcohol usage according to STEPS but does not distinguish between occasional, heavy, or hazardous drinking.

Insufficient physical activity was defined as not reaching the WHO recommendation of 150 min of moderate-intensity or 75 min of vigorous-intensity exercise per week, or an equivalent combination. This study classified participants with total physical activity below 600 MET-minutes per week as having insufficient physical activity. Getting fewer than five servings of fruit and/or vegetables per day was considered inadequate. The four binary behavioral indicators were added to construct an unweighted cumulative behavioral risk factor index from 0 to 4 to assess clustering. Each participant’s co-occurring behavioral risk factors were reflected by this index. The score was unweighted to reflect behavioral risk co-occurrence for population-level surveillance, not to indicate equivalent biological effects of the four behaviors on hypertension.

Covariates

Sex/gender, age, ethnicity, education, marital status, occupation, household economic status, residence, and BMI were socio-demographic and anthropometric factors. The age groups were 18–29, 30–49, and 50–69. The ethnicity was Kinh or other. Education was divided into primary, secondary/high, and university/college. Marital status was single, married, or widowed/divorced. Senior officials/professionals, elementary, and other jobs were categorized. Low, middle, and high household economic status were evaluated using the STEPS dataset. Residence status was urban or rural. Weight and height were used to compute BMI, which was classified as < 25 kg/m² or ≥ 25 kg/m². 0.1 kg and 1 mm were the nearest weight and height measurements [11].

Data analysis

All analyses accounted for the complex survey design using the sampling weights, strata, and cluster variables provided in the STEPS dataset. Weighted proportions and 95% confidence intervals were used to estimate hypertension prevalence overall and by socio-demographic, anthropometric, and behavioral risk factor groups. Multivariable logistic regression was used to examine associations between hypertension and behavioral risk factors.

Two adjusted models were fitted separately. Adjusted Model 1 included the four individual behavioral risk factors simultaneously, whereas Adjusted Model 2 included the cumulative behavioral risk factor index as a categorical variable, with no behavioral risk factor as the reference group. Both models adjusted for age group, ethnic group, education, marital status, occupation, household economic status, residence, and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m². Sex/gender was examined descriptively but excluded from the final adjusted models because of strong collinearity with current smoking and current drinking. Covariates were selected a priori based on epidemiological relevance. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors, missing data were handled using complete-case analysis, and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were collected and managed using REDCap and analyzed using StataNow/BE 19.5.

Ethical considerations

The Ministry of Health approved WHO STEPS2021 in Vietnam. The Hanoi University of Public Health Institutional Review Board approved this study on 25 Sep 2020 under Decision No.395/2020/YTCC-HD3. All participants provided informed consent and had the right to withdraw from the study at any time.

Results

Of 5,000 selected individuals, 4,738 completed the STEP 1 household interview, corresponding to a response rate of 94.8%. Among them, 4,435 were eligible for STEPS 2–3, and 3,809 had valid blood pressure measurements for the hypertension prevalence analysis. Complete data on all four behavioral risk factors were available for 3,773 participants, and the final complete-case sample for multivariable regression analyses included 3,748 participants after excluding those with missing BMI or socio-demographic covariates.

Supplementary Table 1 shows the characteristics of triple-STEPS survey completers. Men (48.0%) smoked more than women (1.0%). Current drinking was higher among men (67.8%) than women (10.5%). Female study participants (27.1%) were less active than male participants (17.1%). 63.1% of men and 55.3% of women in the survey reported not eating fruit/vegetables. Men were more likely than women to cluster non-communicable disease risk variables.

Table 1 shows that the survey-weighted crude hypertension prevalence was 26.4% (95%CI: 24.6–28.3%). The prevalence of hypertension was higher in men (33.6%) than in women (19.3%). Hypertension prevalence increased with age (5.9%, 23.7%, and 51.7% for 18–29, 30–49, and 50–69). Higher hypertension prevalence was seen in persons with poorer education, family income, and BMI ≥ 25 kg/m².

Discussion

In this nationally representative WHO STEPS survey, the survey-weighted prevalence of hypertension among Vietnamese adults aged 18–69 years was 26.4%, higher than estimates from previous Vietnam STEPS surveys in 2015 (18.9%) and 2010 (15.3%)3,5. These findings suggest that hypertension remains a major public health concern in Vietnam. The increase in prevalence across STEPS survey rounds may reflect broader demographic, socioeconomic, and epidemiological transitions; however, these contextual factors were not directly measured in the present analysis and should therefore be interpreted as plausible background explanations rather than empirically tested mechanisms.

The strong age gradient observed in this study provides direct empirical support for the importance of demographic aging in the burden of hypertension. Hypertension prevalence increased from 5.9% among adults aged 18–29 years to more than half among those aged 50–69 years, consistent with previous evidence showing age as one of the most important correlates of hypertension [5, 12–15]. Other broader processes, including socioeconomic development, urbanization, changes in living and working environments, and shifts in dietary and physical activity patterns, may also contextualize the increasing burden of hypertension in Vietnam [2, 16, 17]. Nevertheless, because these macro-level and environmental factors were not directly assessed in the STEPS dataset, they should be viewed as contextual considerations rather than causal mechanisms demonstrated by this study.

International comparisons provide useful context but require careful interpretation. Our estimate of 26.4% is a survey-weighted crude prevalence among adults aged 18–69 years and was not age-standardized. Comparisons with age-standardized estimates from global reports or crude estimates from other country-specific surveys may therefore be affected by differences in population age structure, age range, survey year, and measurement protocols. Previous global and regional studies have documented substantial variation in hypertension prevalence across Asia, including higher estimates in some Central Asian settings and lower age-standardized estimates in several East and Southeast Asian countries [1–21]. Thus, comparisons with South Korea, Japan, China, Central Asia, and other Asian settings are used here primarily to provide a broad epidemiological context rather than direct like-for-like comparisons [18–23].

Regarding behavioral risk factors, our findings highlight the need to distinguish descriptive patterns from adjusted associations. Hypertension prevalence was higher among current smokers, current drinkers, and participants with inadequate fruit/vegetable intake, but not among those with insufficient physical activity. After adjustment, alcohol drinking and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake remained associated with higher odds of hypertension, whereas the crude association for current smoking was attenuated and no longer statistically significant. Insufficient physical activity was not associated with higher odds of hypertension; the weak inverse association observed in the primary adjusted model was not evident in the crude model or in the BMI-excluded sensitivity analysis and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The attenuation of the smoking association should not be interpreted as evidence that tobacco use is unimportant for cardiovascular health. Rather, it may reflect confounding by age, BMI, residence, socio-demographic characteristics, and residual sex/gender-related behavioral patterns. In Vietnam, smoking and alcohol drinking are strongly patterned by sex/gender, which complicates the inclusion of sex/gender in the same adjusted models as these key behavioral exposures. Similarly, inconsistent findings for insufficient physical activity may reflect limitations in self-report measurement, heterogeneity across occupational, transport-related, and leisure-time physical activity, or residual confounding. Tobacco use and physical inactivity therefore, remain important components of the broader NCD prevention agenda, although they were not robust independent correlates of higher hypertension odds in our adjusted analyses [15, 23–25].

The association between current alcohol drinking and hypertension should also be interpreted carefully. The alcohol variable captured any alcohol consumption in the past 30 days and did not measure drinking frequency, quantity, or heavy episodic drinking. This broad definition may have grouped occasional light drinkers with heavier drinkers, thereby obscured differences across exposure levels. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as a cross-sectional association with current alcohol use rather than evidence of a dose-response relationship between alcohol consumption and hypertension. Nonetheless, the persistence of this association after adjustment suggests that alcohol use is an important behavioral marker in the hypertension risk profile of Vietnamese adults.

The association between inadequate fruit/vegetable intake and hypertension is consistent with broader evidence linking healthier dietary patterns, greater fruit and vegetable intake, and comprehensive lifestyle modification with lower cardiovascular risk and reduced blood pressure [26–28]. In Asian populations, high sodium intake and the use of salty condiments are also important contextual dietary factors in hypertension epidemiology [22, 29–31]. However, salt intake was not directly measured in the present study and was not included as an exposure in the regression models. Sodium-related explanations are therefore presented as contextual background and indirect inferences from prior literature, rather than empirical findings generated by this analysis.

A key contribution of this study is the assessment of behavioral risk factor clustering using an unweighted cumulative index. The observed graded association suggests that hypertension is related not only to selected individual behaviors, particularly alcohol drinking and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake, but also to the cumulative burden of co-occurring behavioral risks. Participants with more behavioral risk factors had progressively higher adjusted odds of hypertension, with the highest odds observed among those with all four risk factors. This pattern is consistent with STEPS-based evidence showing that behavioral and metabolic risk factors frequently cluster among adults in South and Southeast Asia [32, 33].

The clustering of behavioral risk factors matters because unhealthy behaviors rarely occur in isolation. Their co-occurrence may reflect shared upstream determinants, including gendered norms around tobacco and alcohol use, occupational and living environments that influence diet and physical activity, and broader structural conditions such as alcohol availability and access to healthy foods [32, 33]. From a public health perspective, this supports integrated NCD prevention approaches that address multiple behavioral risks and their shared social and structural drivers rather than relying solely on isolated single-behavior interventions [33].

However, the cumulative index should be interpreted with caution. Because it was unweighted, it does not imply that all four behaviors contribute equally to hypertension. The individual-risk-factor model suggests that the cumulative association may be driven more strongly by alcohol consumption and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake, as these behaviors remained statistically significant after adjustment. In addition, because this was a cross-sectional analysis and interaction terms were not formally tested, we cannot determine whether the observed clustering reflects additive accumulation, synergistic interaction, or confounding by shared upstream determinants. Therefore, the clustering results should be interpreted as evidence of co-occurrence and cumulative association, rather than proof of a causal behavioral syndemic or causal synergy.

Overall, the findings have important implications for hypertension control and NCD prevention in Vietnam. The high prevalence of hypertension, strong age and BMI gradients, and graded association with clustered behavioral risks suggest the need for integrated public health strategies that combine early detection and management of hypertension with prevention approaches addressing multiple behavioral risk factors. Older adults and individuals with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² may benefit from prioritized screening and linkage to care, while alcohol use and inadequate fruit/vegetable intake represent particularly relevant behavioral targets in this analysis. These updated findings from the 2021 Vietnam STEPS survey may help inform the next phase of the Vietnam National Strategy for NCD Prevention and Control, while recognizing that causal inference cannot be made from the cross-sectional design.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design precludes assessment of temporality or causality, and reverse causality is possible if participants aware of having hypertension had modified their behaviors. Behavioral risk factors were self-reported and may be affected by recall bias, social desirability bias, and exposure misclassification. The alcohol variable captured current drinking but not frequency, quantity, or heavy episodic drinking, and self-reported physical activity may not fully distinguish occupational, transport-related, and leisure-time activity. Attrition between STEP 1 and STEPS 2–3 may have introduced selection bias, although survey weights accounted for the complex sampling design and non-response. The hypertension definition did not distinguish treated from untreated or controlled from uncontrolled hypertension. The cumulative behavioral risk factor score was unweighted, and the analysis did not formally test behavioral interactions or determine whether clustering reflected additive accumulation, synergy, or confounding by shared upstream determinants. Several relevant factors were unavailable or not included, including family history, psychological stress, comorbidity profiles, and detailed dietary sodium intake. Sex/gender was not included in the final adjusted models because of strong collinearity with smoking and drinking. The prevalence estimate was survey-weighted but not age-standardized, and some subgroup estimates had limited precision.

Conclusions

This nationally representative cross-sectional analysis suggests that hypertension remains highly prevalent among Vietnamese adults and appears higher than estimates from previous Vietnam STEPS surveys. Older age, BMI ≥ 25 kg/m², alcohol drinking, inadequate fruit/vegetable intake, and a higher number of co-occurring behavioral risk factors were associated with higher odds of hypertension. These findings highlight the public health relevance of behavioral risk clustering and support integrated strategies that combine hypertension detection, management, and treatment with NCD prevention approaches addressing multiple behavioral risks and their shared social and structural drivers. Updated evidence from the 2021 Vietnam STEPS survey may inform the evaluation of previous NCD prevention efforts and guide the next phase of Vietnam’s NCD prevention and control strategy, while causal interpretation remains limited by the cross-sectional design.
