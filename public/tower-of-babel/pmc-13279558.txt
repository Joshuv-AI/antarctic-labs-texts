Incremental Prognostic Power of Coronary Flow Reserve and Artificial Intelligence–Enabled ECG–Derived Filling Pressure in Angina With Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries
Authors: Parvin Kalhor, Matteo Manzato, Elias Hellou, Kai Nogami, Valentina Nardi, Barry A. Borlaug, Lilach O. Lerman, Amir Lerman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary flow reserve reflects microvascular function, whereas filling pressure indicates myocardial hemodynamic burden. In angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries, abnormalities in either could contribute to a supply–demand mismatch; however, their combined prognostic significance remains unclear.MethodsPatients with angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries who underwent invasive coronary reactivity tests were studied retrospectively. Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) was coronary flow reserve <2.5. An artificial intelligence–enabled ECG marker of elevated filling pressure (AIEFP) was applied as a scalable prognostic marker of myocardial hemodynamic stress. Associations with major adverse cardiovascular events were evaluated using Cox regression and propensity score overlap weighting.ResultsAmong 1957 patients with angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries (median age, 51.8 years; 67.2% women), CMD+ was identified in 26.4% and AIEFP+ in 6.6%. Over 8 years, 204 patients (10.4%) experienced major adverse cardiovascular events. In nested models, both CMD (hazard ratio [HR]=1.42 [95% CI=1.13–1.78]; P=0.002) and AIEFP (HR=2.28 [95% CI=1.67–3.11]; P<0.001) remained associated with major adverse cardiovascular events after adjustment. Adding AIEFP to the base model improved model fitness (Δχ2=23.65, P<0.001; ΔAkaike Information Criterion=−21.7) and modestly improved discrimination (ΔHarrell concordance index=0.02, P=0.02). In a 4‐group model, adjusted risks increased in a graded manner. Overlap‐weighted analysis after covariate balance demonstrated distinct temporal risk trajectories: CMD+/AIEFP+ had the highest 1‐year risk (12.7%), and AIEFP+ had the highest cumulative 8‐year risk (57% in CMD− and 51.8% in CMD+).ConclusionsCMD and AIEFP provide complementary prognostic information, with AIEFP remaining significantly associated with outcomes after multivariable adjustment. A dual‐axis framework integrating myocardial hemodynamic stress (AIEFP) and microvascular function (coronary flow reserve) may enhance risk stratification and enable identification of clinically distinct angina with nonobstructive coronary arteries phenotypes with divergent trajectories.

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is increasingly recognized as a central pathophysiological mechanism in patients with angina and nonobstructive coronary arteries (ANOCA), 1 , 2 , 3 a condition associated with a heightened risk of myocardial infarction, heart failure (HF), and mortality. 4 , 5 , 6 Contemporary guidelines advocate systematic phenotyping that extends beyond epicardial assessment to direct evaluation of the coronary microcirculation. 7 , 8 , 9 Within this framework, invasively measured coronary flow reserve (CFR) remains the reference standard to quantify microvascular vasodilatory capacity and is independently associated with outcomes. 1 , 7 , 9 , 10

A growing paradigm suggests that ANOCA outcomes are shaped by a bidirectional interaction between microvascular function and left ventricular (LV) hemodynamics. 11 , 12 Impaired vasodilatory capacity promotes subendocardial ischemia, triggering profibrotic signaling, myocardial stiffening, and elevated LV filling pressures. 13 , 14 CMD is highly prevalent in patients with diastolic dysfunction and HF and independently associates with disease severity and adverse outcomes. 15 Conversely, elevated filling pressure increases wall stress and intramyocardial compressive forces, thereby impeding microvascular flow. 16 This reduces coronary perfusion pressure (≈aortic diastolic pressure—LV filling pressure) 17 , 18 and shortens the diastolic perfusion window because of persistent extravascular compression. 19 These alterations preferentially compromise subendocardial flow, which represents the distal myocardial perfusion territory and is most vulnerable to extravascular compressive forces. 20 The result is elevated microvascular resistance and lower CFR, reinforcing a vicious cycle of vascular and myocardial dysfunction that culminates in capillary rarefaction, fibrosis, 21 , 22 as well as impairments in exercise capacity. 18

Although the myocardial hemodynamic domain is mechanistically intertwined with coronary microvascular function, its clinical assessment remains challenging. LV filling pressures can be estimated from diastolic dysfunction grades on echocardiography, 23 but these are subject to technical variability and often yield indeterminate findings. 24 A recently developed and externally validated artificial intelligence–enabled ECG (AI‐ECG) tool has been shown to estimate diastolic dysfunction and elevated filling pressures. This model was trained and validated against echocardiographic measures of diastolic function, 23 which was validated based on the “Mayo Clinic Unified Data Platform,” in which our data set is also derived from this population. The AI‐ECG tool provides a simple, scalable, and widely available estimate of filling pressure from a standard 12‐lead ECG. 25 , 26 This readily available marker of myocardial load may complement the vascular information captured by CFR, enabling a more comprehensive and practical framework for prognostic assessment in ANOCA.

Therefore, we hypothesized that the artificial intelligence–enabled ECG marker of elevated filling pressure (AIEFP) captures a complementary dimension of myocardial vulnerability, providing prognostic information beyond CFR. Integration of this easily available upstream myocardial stress signal with invasive coronary microvascular assessment would enhance risk discrimination and help identify biologically coherent ANOCA phenotypes. Importantly, this approach is not intended as a substitute for echocardiography, but as a complementary use of a validated, scalable ECG‐derived marker to identify myocardial stress associated with more adverse outcomes and refine prognostic stratification.

METHODS

Transparency and Openness Statement

This study adheres to the AHA Journals' implementation of the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines. The data supporting this study's findings are not publicly available because of institutional policies and restrictions related to patient privacy. The data may be made available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and with appropriate institutional approvals. The analytic methods used in this study are described in sufficient detail within the article and Supplemental Materials to allow replication of the results.

Study Design and Population

This retrospective, single‐center, cohort study included all patients aged ≥18 years with symptoms of stable angina who were found to have nonobstructive coronary artery disease (<50% stenosis in all epicardial vessels) on clinically indicated elective coronary angiography and invasive coronary reactivity test (CRT) at the Mayo Clinic between 1993 and 2023. Exclusion criteria were as follows: LV ejection fraction <50%, the absence of a research‐quality 12‐lead ECG within 2 weeks of the CRT procedure, an incomplete physiological assessment, or insufficient follow‐up data. The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (No. 05‐004191) approved the study protocol. The present study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki for the investigation of human subjects, and all participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in the registry.

Coronary Angiography and CRT

Patients presented to the cardiac catheterization laboratory in a fasting state, with cardiovascular medications withheld for at least 48 hours. All participants underwent diagnostic coronary angiography following standard protocols. Subsequently, CRT was performed to evaluate coronary microvascular function.

From 1993 through 2021, CFR was measured using a Doppler flow wire system (FloWire, Philips/Volcano) positioned within a 2.2‐F coronary infusion catheter (Transit Microcatheter, DePuy Synthes Companies) advanced into the mid left anterior descending artery. Incremental intracoronary bolus injections of adenosine (18–72 μg) were administered through the guiding catheter to achieve maximal hyperemia. CFR was calculated as the ratio of the average peak coronary flow velocity during hyperemia to baseline average peak velocity. 27 , 28 Beginning in 2022, CFR assessment was performed using a thermodilution‐based technique with a dual‐sensor PressureWire (PressureWire X; Abbott Vascular Inc) connected to a CoroFlow console. The wire, positioned in the mid left anterior descending artery, incorporates a pressure transducer and 2 thermistors that measure the mean transit time of a rapidly injected cold saline bolus at rest and during adenosine‐induced hyperemia. Because mean transit time decreases as coronary flow increases, CFR was derived as the ratio of mean transit time at baseline/that at hyperemia. 29 , 30 CMD was defined as CFR <2.5. 31 , 32

AI‐ECG for Elevated Filling Pressure

To noninvasively index myocardial hemodynamic burden, we applied a validated, officially released AI‐ECG model (ResNet‐18 convolutional neural network) to standard 10‐second 12‐lead ECGs acquired within 2 weeks before CRT. The model outputs probabilities for 4 grades of diastolic function (0–3). 25 Consistent with the model specification, AIEFP was defined as the sum of the predicted probabilities for diastolic dysfunction grades 2 and 3. A specified probability threshold of ≥0.264, corresponding to the optimal operating point identified in the development and validation study, was used to classify increased filling pressure. This binary construct demonstrated robust discrimination for elevated filling pressure (area under the curve, 0.911), with a high negative predictive value (94.5%) and prognostic performance comparable to echocardiographic assessment. Accordingly, AIEFP was applied as a predefined binary marker (+/−) in all analyses and used for patient stratification alongside CFR. Additionally, we performed an internal assessment of model performance within our cohort with ANOCA.

Clinical Assessment and Follow‐Up

Baseline demographic data, cardiovascular risk factors, medical history, and laboratory results were extracted from the electronic health record by trained investigators blinded to the study's primary hypotheses.

The primary end point was major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs), a composite of all‐cause mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, or hospitalization for HF. Mortality was ascertained through a systematic review of hospital records and linkage to the Minnesota Death Index. Nonfatal myocardial infarction was defined according to the Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, requiring a rise and/or fall in cardiac troponin with at least 1 value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit, accompanied by evidence of myocardial ischemia (eg, symptoms, new ischemic ECG changes, or imaging evidence). 33 HF was diagnosed with any cardinal symptoms and abnormal findings of echocardiography, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) level, and HF hospitalization was defined as an unplanned admission for worsening symptoms of HF requiring intravenous diuretic therapy. 34 All events were independently adjudicated by at least 2 physician investigators blinded to biomarker data, using a combination of institutional databases, death certificates, and detailed medical record review to ensure rigor and consistency. Time to event was calculated as the period between the date of procedure and the first occurrence of an MACE that documented a clinical event in the medical record or registry. Patients without an MACE were censored at their last follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1) and SPSS (version 29, IBM Corp). A 2‐sided P<0.050 was considered statistically significant. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Kruskal‐Wallis test for 4‐group analyses or the Mann–Whitney U test for 2‐group analyses. Categorical variables were presented as counts and valid percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. To account for imbalanced group sizes and provide scale‐independent estimates of group differences, effect sizes (η2 for continuous data and Cohen h or Cramer V for categorical data) were calculated in addition to P values and are reported in the Supplemental Materials. All survival analyses were performed using complete‐case data, as all patients in the analytic cohort had complete information on the primary exposures (CFR and AIEFP), outcome (MACE), and the prespecified covariates used in the multivariable models. Missing data were limited to descriptive characteristics not used in modeling and represented <20% of observations.

Univariate and Multivariable Prognostic Modeling

Unadjusted MACE‐free survival distributions were evaluated with Kaplan–Meier curves and global/pairwise log‐rank tests with Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment. Multivariable Cox proportional‐hazards models were constructed hierarchically using prespecified clinically relevant covariates. Nested models were used to evaluate the incremental association of CMD, AIEFP, and their interaction with outcomes. Akaike Information Criterion was used as a secondary index of relative model parsimony, whereas likelihood‐based testing and discrimination metrics were used to assess incremental prognostic information. To ensure model stability, the variance inflation factor was calculated for all predictors; models containing any variable with a variance inflation factor ≥10 were excluded to prevent multicollinearity. Incremental model performance was assessed using complementary metrics reflecting distinct aspects of prognostic modeling. Improvement in model fit was evaluated with likelihood‐ratio testing of nested Cox models and change in Akaike Information Criterion, with Akaike Information Criterion interpreted as a measure of relative fit and parsimony rather than discrimination. Prognostic discrimination was assessed using Harrell concordance index, and change in discrimination with addition of AIEFP was quantified using both ΔC and the integrated discrimination improvement. A final model incorporating the 4 predefined CMD/AIEFP phenotypes generated adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) for MACE. Proportional‐hazards assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and precision was validated by 1000 bootstrap resampling. Prespecified sensitivity analyses were performed stratified by CFR measurement technique and additionally adjusted for enrollment year to assess the robustness of the prognostic association of AIEFP.

Confirmatory Analysis With Propensity Score Weighting

To verify robustness of the phenotype‐based findings and minimize residual confounding, propensity‐score overlap weighting was applied to achieve covariate balance across 4 groups. Propensity scores for phenotype membership were estimated using generalized boosted models. Covariate balance was assessed with standardized mean differences (<0.10 indicating adequate balance). Weighted Cox regression estimated aHRs for MACE, and weighted Kaplan–Meier estimators provided 1‐ and 8‐year absolute risks, yielding clinically interpretable short‐ and long‐term risk trajectories in a balanced pseudopopulation.

RESULTS

Study Population and Grouping Distribution

From an initial cohort of 2307 patients with ANOCA, 1957 met the eligibility criteria and were ultimately included (Figure 1). The individuals were predominantly women (67.2%, N=1316) with a median age of 51.8 (43.3–60.6) years. Overall, CMD was present in 26.4% (N=516) of patients, and AIEFP in 6.6% (N=129) of patients with ANOCA.

Among AIEFP+ patients, 51.9% had concurrent CMD+, whereas only 12.7% of CMD+ patients had AIEFP+, underscoring the partial but clinically relevant overlap between microvascular dysfunction and elevated filling pressure. Baseline characteristics of the total cohort, along with stratification by AIEFP status, are presented in Table 1. Patients with AIEFP+ were older (65.3 versus 51.1 years) and more frequently women (79.8% versus 66.4%). The cardiometabolic comorbidity burden was higher in the AIEFP+ group, with a greater prevalence of hypertension (67.4% versus 42.2%) and diabetes (21.7% versus 10.9%). Laboratory profiles were consistent with increased myocardial and systemic stress among AIEFP+ patients, characterized by markedly higher NT‐proBNP levels (252 [150–488] versus 66.5 [37–123] pg/mL), modestly higher renal indexes, higher glycemic markers, and lower hemoglobin concentrations. Inflammatory burden assessed by hs‐CRP (high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein) was comparable between groups.

Medication use reflected a greater cardiovascular disease burden in AIEFP+ patients, including higher use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers (25.6% versus 14.2%) and β‐blockers (51.2% versus 32.1%), whereas the use of aspirin and calcium‐channel blockers was similar.

Cardiac structure, coronary physiology, and invasive hemodynamics differed substantially by AIEFP status. AIEFP+ patients had higher left atrial volume index (34 [28–42] versus 27 [23–34] mL/m2), lower CFR (2.4 [2.1–2.9] versus 2.9 [2.5–3.4]), higher prevalence of CMD (51.9% versus 24.6%), modestly lower left ventricular ejection fraction (60% [54%–65%] versus 62% [59%–65%]), and higher left ventricular end‐diastolic pressure (20 [15–24] versus 16 [12–20] mm Hg). AI‐ECG derived atrial fibrillation probability was markedly higher in the AIEFP+ group (18.1% [3.9%–35.5%] versus 0.9% [0.3%–3.1%]), supporting internal biological coherence of the AIEFP phenotype (Tables S1 and S2).

Prognostic Analyses

In up to 8 years’ follow‐up (median, 6.7 years), MACEs occurred in 204 patients (10.4%), comprising 93 all‐cause deaths (4.8%), 50 nonfatal myocardial infarctions (2.6%), and 61 HF hospitalizations (3.1%). The absolute numbers and proportions of MACE events across CMD/AIEFP phenotypes were as follows: CMD−/AIEFP− (111 events among 1379 patients; 8.0%), CMD+/AIEFP− (57/449; 12.7%), CMD−/AIEFP+ (16/62; 25.8%), and CMD+/AIEFP+ (20/67; 29.9%).

In unadjusted Kaplan–Meier analyses, both CMD+ and AIEFP+ were individually associated with significantly lower MACE‐free survival (log‐rank P<0.001 for both; Figure 2). When stratified into the 4 groups, a graded, stepwise decline in MACE‐free survival was observed (global log‐rank P<0.001; Figure 3). All pairwise phenotype comparisons remained significant after Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment (P<0.001), except for CMD−/AIEFP+ versus CMD+/AIEFP+ (P=0.545), where wide 95% CIs were driven by few patients and higher event rates. Nested model analysis demonstrated that addition of AIEFP to the base model containing clinical covariates and CMD significantly improved model fit (likelihood‐ratio test Δχ2=23.65, P<0.001; ΔAkaike Information Criterion=−21.7; Tables S3–S7). This was accompanied by a modest but statistically significant improvement in discrimination, with Harrell concordance index increasing from 0.66 (95% CI, 0.63–0.69) to 0.68 (95% CI, 0.64–0.70) (ΔC=0.02, P=0.02). The integrated discrimination improvement further supported incremental discrimination when AIEFP was included. The CMD×AIEFP interaction was not statistically significant (P=0.697), suggesting no evidence of multiplicative interaction between CMD and AIEFP. These findings indicate that AIEFP provides prognostic information not captured by clinical covariates and invasive microvascular measures.

In a 4‐group multivariable Cox model, risk increased progressively and in a stepwise manner across the phenotypes. Compared with the CMD−/AIEFP− as a reference group, the aHR for MACE was 1.45 (95% CI, 1.13–1.86) for CMD+/AIEFP−, 2.42 (95% CI, 1.59–3.69) for CMD−/AIEFP+, and 3.12 (95% CI, 2.05–4.74) for CMD+/AIEFP+ (Table 2). These results, consistently reproduced across 1000 bootstrap resampling validations, underscore the robustness of the proposed 4‐tier risk stratification scheme (Table S8). Sensitivity analyses accounting for enrollment timing, modeled continuously and by study era (pre‐2010 versus post‐2010), demonstrated no association between enrollment period and outcomes, with no impact on covariate effect estimates. Formal interaction testing in the full cohort revealed no evidence of effect modification by CFR measurement technique (interaction P=0.990), supporting the robustness of the findings across CFR method.

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

The current study demonstrated that integrating a noninvasive AI‐derived ECG marker of elevated filling pressure (AIEFP) with invasive CFR could refine risk stratification in ANOCA. This dual framework assessment of myocardial hemodynamic stress and microvascular function identified distinct phenotypes with divergent clinical trajectories, demonstrating that myocardial load and microvascular capacity contribute in complementary ways to adverse outcomes. These findings highlight this signal as a potential practical tool to improve prognostic evaluation.

Myocardial Vulnerability and Microvascular Capacity: An Integrative Perspective

In ANOCA, symptoms and outcomes often reflect a bidirectional coupling between the coronary microvasculature and the myocardium rather than microvascular dysfunction alone. 35 , 36 Prior studies suggest that myocardial hemodynamic stress and microvascular dysfunction frequently coexist in ANOCA‐related syndromes. 37 , 38 The present study extends these observations by integrating invasive coronary physiology with an ECG‐derived myocardial stress signal applied for prognostic stratification.

In our cohort, just over half of patients with AIEFP positivity also exhibited CMD, consistent with prior evidence that microvascular dysfunction is common in clinical states characterized by elevated filling pressures, including HF with preserved ejection fraction. 14 , 39 Meta‐analytic data indicate CMD in up to 71% of HF with preserved ejection fraction cases, with impaired CFR present in ≈62%. 40 , 41 The observed overlap is consistent with prior reports linking CMD and diastolic dysfunction across related clinical syndromes. Conversely, only a minority of patients with CMD were AIEFP positive in the present study, a proportion lower than the reported prevalence of echocardiographic diastolic dysfunction in comparable CMD populations. 37 , 42 This distinction, compared with the broader prevalence of any grade of diastolic dysfunction among patients with CMD, suggests that AIEFP identifies the subset in whom myocardial stress has progressed to clinically meaningful hemodynamic burden and worse outcome, thereby refining prognostic prediction within CMD.

The resulting partial overlap is therefore expected and biologically plausible. Impaired coronary flow reserve does not uniformly translate into elevated myocardial filling pressures, and the ECG‐derived signal should not be interpreted as a surrogate for comprehensive diastolic assessment. Instead, AIEFP identifies a smaller, higher‐risk subset characterized by sustained myocardial hemodynamic burden, thereby refining prognostic stratification within CMD using a widely available tool.

Complementary Prognostic Contributions of Myocardial Stress and Microvascular Function

A central finding of this study is the additive prognostic value of the ECG‐derived myocardial hemodynamic stress marker (AIEFP) when considered alongside invasive coronary flow reserve. In adjusted models, the CMD−/AIEFP+ phenotype conferred a significantly higher risk of MACEs than the CMD+/AIEFP− phenotype. This suggests that in the population with ANOCA, a noninvasive signal of myocardial hemodynamic burden may identify clinical risk not necessarily captured by microvascular vasodilatory capacity alone. 18 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 The observed complementary behavior of these markers is consistent with established physiological principles describing a bidirectional coupling between the myocardium and the microcirculation. Mechanistically, chronically increased wall stress and elevated filling pressures have been shown to decrease microvascular perfusion by shortening the diastolic perfusion window and increasing extravascular compressive forces, particularly in the subendocardium.

Our results are consistent with prior translational pressure‐overload models, which demonstrate that chronically increased wall stress impairs subendocardial perfusion, potentially leading to a secondary microvascular dysfunction superimposed on, or independent of, primary endothelial disease. 48 , 49 Conversely, primary microvascular dysfunction may trigger subendocardial ischemia, which promotes myocardial stiffening and further elevates filling pressures. 18 , 50 However, we cannot exclude shared underlying mechanisms that contribute to each independently.

Unmasking Distinct Clinical Trajectories

Although recent registry data suggest that the average patient with ANOCA may have a prognosis similar to the general population, 51 our findings highlight that invasive and AI‐enhanced phenotyping can identify high‐risk biological subsets, specifically those with CMD and elevated filling pressures, who experience higher rates of HF hospitalization and mortality. These observations indicate that clinical risk in ANOCA is not uniformly distributed but is concentrated within physiologically defined phenotypes with divergent trajectories.

After overlap weighting, integration of CMD and AIEFP status revealed distinct risk trajectories. The CMD+/AIEFP+ group had the highest early event rate (12.7% at 1 year), consistent with a brittle hemodynamic state combining impaired microvascular reserve and elevated diastolic load. In contrast, CMD−/AIEFP+ exhibited the steepest long‐term risk accumulation (57% at 8 years), reflecting progressive myocardial remodeling despite preserved perfusion. CMD+/AIEFP− showed intermediate risk (37%), suggesting that isolated microvascular dysfunction primarily drives ischemic vulnerability.

Collectively, these patterns suggest that myocardial hemodynamic stress is associated with earlier and more sustained risk accumulation than impaired CFR alone. This aligns with prior studies linking low CFR to subclinical diastolic dysfunction and with evidence that combined CMD and diastolic dysfunction markedly increase HF with preserved ejection fraction hospitalization risk. 3 , 41 , 52 , 53 Together, AIEFP and CFR delineate complementary axes of vulnerability: AIEFP captures resting diastolic load, whereas CFR reflects cumulative ischemic burden. Their integration defines temporally distinct yet additive pathways of risk, supporting a unified framework for precision phenotyping and mechanism‐guided investigation in ANOCA.

Clinical Implications: A Scalable Prognostic Layer, Not a Diagnostic Substitute

From a clinical perspective, the primary value of the AI‐ECG–derived filling pressure signal lies in its role as a scalable prognostic layer rather than a diagnostic alternative to echocardiography. Although echocardiography remains the reference standard for structural and functional assessment, it is resource intensive, is subject to interobserver variability, and often yields indeterminate diastolic classifications. In contrast, the ECG‐derived signal evaluated here provides a widely available, low‐cost marker of myocardial hemodynamic vulnerability that can be applied longitudinally and across care settings. When integrated with invasive coronary physiology, this signal may offer a scalable adjunct for prognostic enrichment by identifying patients in whom myocardial stress contributes meaningfully to adverse outcomes, even in the absence of overt systolic dysfunction or microvascular impairment.

Clinical Perspectives

Competency in Medical Knowledge

Integration of invasive CFR and an AI‐ECG marker for elevated filling pressure (AIEFP) provides a complementary framework for prognostic assessment in ANOCA, capturing both vascular and myocardial stress domains. The presence of this ECG‐derived signal may indicate a higher‐risk myocardial phenotype and help prioritize patients for closer evaluation of myocardial hemodynamic burden and individualized longitudinal management, rather than serving as a diagnostic or therapeutic determinant. 54 , 55 , 56 , 57 , 58

Translational Outlook

AI‐ECG–derived myocardial stress signals may provide a scalable adjunct for prognostic risk stratification in ANOCA. Prospective studies will be required to assess whether such risk stratification meaningfully informs clinical evaluation pathways or improves long‐term outcomes.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the large, well‐phenotyped cohort with invasive coronary physiology measurements, long‐term follow‐up with adjudicated end points, and a confirmatory propensity score–based analysis. Nevertheless, several limitations merit consideration. This was a single‐center, retrospective study; a prospective, multicenter study with standardized echocardiographic acquisition is required to generalize prognostic performance for clinical deployment. Two CFR assessment techniques (Doppler and thermodilution) were used; however, sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent results. The AI‐ECG–derived filling pressure marker, although previously validated against echocardiographic measures of diastolic function, represents an indirect, resting surrogate and does not capture instantaneous invasive or exercise‐induced change in hemodynamics. However, its role in identifying a high‐risk phenotype remains clinically significant. Longitudinal medication data were incomplete, limiting assessment of time‐varying treatment effects. Finally, the confirmatory analysis yielded a reduced effective sample size in the highest‐risk strata, underscoring the need for larger studies to support clinical generalizability.

CONCLUSIONS

Integrating invasive coronary flow reserve and an AI‐derived ECG marker of elevated filling pressure distinguishes clinically meaningful ANOCA subgroups with divergent short‐ and long‐term prognoses. Leveraging AI‐ECG, a routinely available test, offers a practical means of identifying a higher‐risk myocardial phenotype. Together, this dual‐axis approach provides a scalable framework for refined prognostic stratification and exploratory phenotypic classification in ANOCA.
