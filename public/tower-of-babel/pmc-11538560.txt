Machine learning-enhanced band gaps prediction for low-symmetry double and layered perovskites
Authors: Alireza Sabagh Moeini, Fatemeh Shariatmadar Tehrani, Alireza Naeimi-Sadigh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDensity functional theory (DFT) calculations are widely used for material property prediction, but their computational cost can hinder the discovery of novel perovskites. This work explores machine learning (ML) as a faster alternative for predicting band gaps in complex perovskites, focusing on low-symmetry double and layered structures. We employ Support Vector Regression (SVR), Random Forest Regression (RFR), Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) to predict both direct and indirect band gaps. Model performance is evaluated using Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Squared Error (MSE), and R-squared (R²) metrics. Our results reveal SVR as the most effective general model for predicting band gaps in both double and layered perovskites. Interestingly, for double perovskites specifically, XGBoost achieves even higher accuracy when incorporating derivative discontinuity as a feature. Feature importance analysis identifies the standard deviation of valence charges (“Valence (std)”) as the most critical factor for band gap prediction across all studied perovskites. This research demonstrates the potential of ML for efficient and accurate band gap prediction in complex perovskites, accelerating material discovery efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77081-7.Keywords: Double perovskites, Layered perovskites, Band gap prediction, Machine learning, Feature ranking, Elemental features, ValenceSubject terms: Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Nanoscience and technology, Physics

Introduction

In general, the “perovskite” term describes a class of minerals known as calcium titanate, with the molecular formula CaTiO3. The Ural is where the mineral was found. Mountains of Russia was written by Gustav Rose in 1839 and is named after Lev Perovski, a Russian mineralogist1,2. The intriguing electrical and magnetic properties of perovskites in the form of ABX3, along with their compositional and structural complexity such as piezoelectricity3, optical properties4, high-temperature superconductivity5,6, and ferroelectricity7. Also, their ability to perform band gap tuning makes them desirable for various engineering applications, particularly in solar cells8that use photovoltaics9,10. Perovskites are inexpensive to create because, despite the silicon-based devices, they can be made at lower temperatures using simple solution-processed8,11or mechanochemical methods12,13.

The general chemical formula for ideal perovskites is ABX3, where A is the larger cation, B is the smaller cation, and anion X is often oxygen or halogen. Double perovskites, denoted by the formulae AA′B2O6 (double A-site) or A2BB′′O6(double B-site), are perovskites in which the A or B sites are occupied by two distinct types of cations14,15. Also, hybrid organic-inorganic perovskites (HOIPs) are crystals with the structural formula ABX3, where A, B, and X are organic and inorganic ions, respectively16,17.

Low symmetry perovskite refers to a perovskite material that deviates from the ideal cubic structure with high symmetry. This deviation can occur due to several factors and can have significant implications for the material’s properties such as electronic properties, optical properties, functional properties, etc. Various methods, such as titling, atom shifting18, mixed-cation perovskites, lower temperature synthesis process19, and cation selection layered perovskites20or double perovskites21, are employed to reduce the symmetry of perovskites. A layered perovskite is made up of two-dimensional (2D) ABO3cubic perovskite slabs divided by a metal atom pattern. Layered perovskites come in a variety of phases, with differences in the cubic perovskite slab patterns, thickness, and relative displacement. The low symmetry and consequent availability of newer and more stable properties, along with the ability to find a wide variety of combinations in perovskites20–22, make these materials an excellent choice for use in various industrial applications, including solar cells23–27, LEDs28–31, lasers32–35, and more.

In the field of material science, the idea of the band gap is fundamental to understanding how different materials behave when exposed to light and electricity. The electrical conductivity, light absorption capabilities, and final classification of a material as an insulator, conductor, or semiconductor depend on the size of the band gap. Perovskites do have the ability to operate as semiconductors, especially when used in photovoltaic (solar cell) applications. The perovskites are a class of minerals that possess a particular crystal structure. Their extraordinary features and prospective uses in multiple technologies have made them highly sought-after in recent times36–39. It is expected that numerous theoretical and practical investigations on their electronic characteristics have been carried out as a result of this desire. Experiments on these materials are challenging because of experimental limitations, even though the experimental data are more trustworthy40,41. Density functional theory (DFT)-based ab initio computations are an effective computing technique widely used in the condensed matter physics and material science sectors42,43. The well-known methods for estimating the ground state electronic features of materials, the Generalized Gradient Approximation (GGA) and the Local Density Approximation (LDA), are based on uniform and non-uniform electron density distributions, respectively, but they are not able to yield reliable results for excited state DFT calculations44,45. Conversely, hybrid approaches aim to combine enhanced DFT mathematics and computational codes with elements of first principles approaches, including Hartree-Fock techniques. The GW method, which is based on the Many-Body Perturbation Theory, can be applied consistently and yield accurate results, particularly when examining the electrical and optical characteristics of materials46. Band offsets, band gaps, and other calculations can be made using another DFT calculation method that uses the hybrid functional HSE06; nevertheless, it was found to have a number of shortcomings, especially when dealing with direct-indirect crossover, as the alloy GaAsP illustrates44. The Gritsenko, van Leeuwen, van Lenthe, and Baerends (GLLB) potential is further altered by adding correlation and switching out the energy density functional for one that is better suited for solids. This yields a potential that we refer to as GLLB-SC (solid, correlation)47. It is commonly known that due to the approximate attributes of the exchange-correlation functional and the absence of the derivative discontinuities, the Kohn-Sham eigenvalues from DFT consistently underestimate band gaps of extended semiconductors48. Though many-body methods, such as the GW approximation, yield better band gaps, their computational expense limits their use for hundreds of compounds. So, a better choice is to employ the GLLB potential enhanced for solids (-SC) potential, which has a discrete derivative discontinuity estimate element.

When compared to conventional first-principles methods, machine learning (ML) techniques can effectively speed up the search for materials and reduce computation costs while preserving accuracy. Big data, artificial intelligence, and materials modeling are now tightly intertwined in materials design, which has resulted in a paradigm change49,50. Among them are the so-called ML approaches that the statistical learning community has defined, and which are presently guiding research toward a new paradigm of data-driven science51. ML has also shown great success in the problem of predicting the band gap of different types of perovskites and double perovskites52–57. A crucial step in the ML process is feature engineering, which includes feature construction and feature selection. The maximum performance limit of the model is often determined by the validity of the features, sample size, and feature dimensionality quality of the data in most ML procedures58. Some feature calculations are expensive and laborious to extract using experimental studies or DFT calculations. However, it is possible to prepare features for ML by using the features of the constituent elements of the compound. With the neural networks model, Heng et al. have predicted the band gap of some III-V and II-VI semiconductors from the constituent’s elemental features. They have proposed that, throughout the compound formation process, the band gap energy and lattice constant depend on the outcome of the struggle between attractive and repulsive forces and, accordingly, on how easily the atoms may disperse while considering their mass59. For Kesterite I2-II-IV-V4 semiconductors, L. Weston and C. Stamp have used the ionic radius, electronegativity, and row in the periodic table for each element in the I2-II-IV VI4compound; this provides these features overall for each compound to determine whether has direct or indirect band gaps60. For wurtzite nitride semiconductors, Huang et al. have chosen eight elemental features including covalent radius, first ionization energy, atomic weight, atomic number, periodic number, valence, covalent radius, and melting point. The feature space, as a subset of the 58-dimensional space based on all conceivable combinations of the eight elemental features, has been used to train and test the models. Covalent radius, electronegativity, and valence have been shown as element’s most significant electronic features from a physically naturally inclined point of view, and they are thought to be the most useful and applicable descriptors for electronic band gap and alignment predictions61. With artificial neural networks and features as an electrochemical factor, atomic number factor, cohesive energy factor, size factor, valence-electron factor, and Mendeleev number factor Zeng et al. have predicted the chalcopyrite semiconductors of I-III-VI2 and II-IV-V2types and finally find out that the correlation technique in this study has served as an efficient tool to investigate semiconductor properties from a large number of compounds and narrow down the number of potential candidates62. Chenebuah et al. have employed a wide range of combinational features, including the periodic table properties, element size, electrical and physical features, thermochemistry, Zunger pseudopotential, developed stability features, and crystallographic features, to study the electronic properties of ABX3 and A2BB’X6perovskites. The energy above the hull and standard-deviated electronegativity have been determined to be the most significant characteristics57. ML models that explain the relationship between the elemental characteristics of the element ions and the band gap of ABX3perovskites have been presented by Gladkikh et al. They have demonstrated that the atomic radii, ionization energies, electro-negativities, and electron affinities of the components primarily control the band gap. The band gap has a non-linear relationship with the descriptions56. The chemical interpretation of such dependence requires more research. To anticipate band gaps of double perovskites materials, Zhang et al. have used 20 physical characteristics, they found that, by the physics of the underlying electronic structure, the bulk modulus, superconductivity temperature, and cation electronegativity show the highest relevance scores53. To predict band gap in the ABO3perovskite materials, Li et al. have employed physical and chemical characteristics (such as the atomic mass, atomic number, Pauling’s electronegativity, melting point, etc.) as well as electronic structural characteristics (such as the number of electrons in the valence orbitals of the s-, p-, and d-atoms, etc.). Calculating the mean and standard deviation of all element data ensures that every compound receives an equal quantity of features22. To predict the band gap of inorganic solids, Zhuo et al. have used five different ML models and their features based solely on the elemental features of the constituent elements. These features include but are not limited to, the atom’s relative position on the periodic table, its electronic structure, and its physical characteristics63.

In this study, we utilize the open low-symmetry perovskites database in the Computational Material Repository (CMR) to predict the band gaps (Eg) of double perovskites and layered perovskite materials using support vector regression (SVR), random forest regression (RFR), gradient boosting regression (GBR), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). We first selected 40 features to train our machine learning models.

The SVR model outperformed RFR, GBR, and XGBoost for both double perovskites and layered perovskites datasets, achieving a prediction accuracy exceeding 95% and demonstrating the best values for mean absolute error (MAE) and mean squared error (MSE) among the models. Furthermore, incorporating the derivative discontinuity as a feature resulted in all four models exceeding 98% prediction accuracy. This clearly demonstrates the benefit of including additional relevant criteria.

While previous studies haven’t identified a single most impactful feature consistently, our investigation reveals that “Valence (std)” holds the most significant influence across two datasets, four machine learning models, and three feature importance models. It’s important to note that this is a relative finding, but not unrealistic.

Data and features

All of our data, including both double and layered perovskites, were extracted from literature sources20,21 that were also uploaded to the CMR. The double perovskite dataset contains 1493 samples, while the layered perovskite dataset consists of 491 samples. Layered perovskites, in the Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) and Dion-Jacobson (DJ) phases, are known as efficient solar cell materials. These materials deviate from their ideal, high-symmetry form, resulting in the following chemical formulae: A2B4 × 8, A4B6 × 14, A2B2B′X6, A2B2B′4 × 14, and A4B4B′8 × 16. Similar to layered perovskites, double perovskites also deviate from their ideal state and adopt the following chemical formulae: A2 A′2B2B′2 × 12, A2B2B′4 × 12, A4B4 × 12. In these formulae, A represents the larger cation, B the smaller cation, and X is the anion (always oxygen). All features, except derivative discontinuity, are derived from the properties of the constituent atoms in each perovskite. No specific features of the perovskite itself require measurement. We calculate the mean and standard deviation (std) of each relevant atomic property, such as mass, number, valence, etc., and report these values as features. The number of atoms and number of unique atomic species are directly obtainable from the perovskite formula, eliminating the need for additional computational features. By calculating the mean and standard deviation of all elemental data, we ensure that every compound has the same number of features.

Our goal is to predict the direct and indirect band gaps of both double and layered perovskites. We achieve this by utilizing data computed using the GLLB-SC approach, which incorporates a derivative discontinuity for improved accuracy. In the GLLB-SC technique, a derivative discontinuity is a modification to the Kohn-Sham band gap that yields a more accurate representation of a material’s basic band gap47,64.

The process entails folding the dataset into several subsets, training the model on a portion of the data, then assessing its performance on the remaining data that has not yet been viewed. Several iterations of this process are carried out, using various subsets for testing and training. In this study, we use Cross-Validation (CV) criteria. CV in ML is a technique used to assess the performance and generalization ability of a predictive model. Cross-validation’s primary objective is to predict a model’s performance on unseen data, which aids in evaluating the model’s capacity for generalization and identifying overfitting or under-fitting problems. K-fold cross-validation is the most often used type of CV. In this technique, the data is divided into k subsets or folds, and the model is trained and assessed k times, using the remaining folds as the training set and a different fold as the test set each time. When we compared it to a single train-test split, CV aids in obtaining a more accurate estimate of a model’s performance. It helps inform choices about model selection and hyper-parameter optimization by offering insights into how effectively the model generalizes to fresh, untested data. CV in this study shows that SVR for direct and indirect band gap prediction of double perovskites has excellent outcomes and reliable procedures for this content and this amount of data. But for layered perovskites with CV criteria, better results are achieved by using XGBoost model.

To assess the importance of features several feature ranking tools are used including Local Interpretable Model-Agnosti Explanations (LIME), SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), and Permutation Importance (PI). To explain why a given data point led to a particular prediction, LIME works by approximating the complex model locally around that data point with a simpler, interpretable model, such as a linear regression. You can learn more about how the model is behaving with the current hyper-parameters by examining the LIME explanations for different data points and predictions. If the explanations don’t make sense or don’t match the understanding of the data, now need to adjust the hyper-parameter70. The acronym SHAP refers to a technique in the explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) field that attempts to make complex ML model predictions more comprehensible and interpretable. It is predicated on the idea of Shapley values and game theory. Within the field of ML, SHAP offers a means of elucidating the relative contributions of every feature or input variable to the final output or prediction of a model. Using a Shapley value, which is the average marginal contribution of a feature across all possible feature combinations, this method quantifies the importance of each feature71. Using a single feature in the data and rearranging its values, PI breaks the feature’s association with the target variable. The significance of the feature is indicated by the decline in model performance following shuffling. PI provides a ranking of features based on their influence on model performance. Features that exhibit persistently high-performance declines during shuffling are probably more significant. Features that show little to no performance loss after rearranging may be superfluous or unnecessary. These might be good candidates to be eliminated, which would result in a more narrowly chosen set of traits for additional analysis72. These three models are used to recognize the top ten most significant input features for direct and indirect band gaps. When discussing feature importance, we naturally do not consider the derivative discontinuity features, because in the presence of a derivative discontinuity, none of the other features are very significant, and the derivative discontinuity itself has the highest impact coefficient as a feature. Our discussion is about the case where we are evaluating the importance of features while considering the other forty features. The total number of possible cases for finding the top ten is 48. This is because we have two different targets (direct and indirect band gap) × two data models (double and layered) × four different ML models and × three methods for finding the top ten features. It is clear from analyzing the feature importance data that the “Valence (std)” feature is essential for predicting both direct and indirect band gaps. Its remarkable appearance in 47 of the 48 potential cases highlights its high impact in a range of situations. Its noteworthy relevance is further highlighted by its average score of 1.5 since lower values denote higher importance (a “1” score is the best importance and a “10” score is the least importance). The importance of the other features for double and layered perovskites decreases in terms of their presence in influencing band gap prediction and their rankings, indicating a lesser impact on the band gap prediction. Also, since they do not exhibit any specific importance order like “Valence (std)”, it is therefore not possible to provide an accurate conclusion regarding the determining role of other features. As shown in Fig. 4, the next four important features include “Heat of Vaporization (std)” with 44 occurrences and an average score of 4.7, “Ionic radius (std)” with 37 occurrences and an average score of 6, “Group (std)” with 35 occurrences and an average score of 3.6, and ”Valence (mean)” with 31 occurrences and an average score of 5.5, ranking subsequently. Remarkably the mean and standard deviation of Valence are the two most important features. Derivative discontinuity shows its predicted influence with a coefficient of 0.95, as expected. After that, the two expected features, valence (std) and valence (mean) show coefficients of 0.41 and 0.34, respectively. Features pertaining to the data’s electronic properties have the highest coefficients, as would be predicted, whereas features pertaining to the data’s physical properties, like atomic mass, have the lowest coefficients. The relationship between the properties of the constituent elements of a material, such as the studied perovskites, and the band gap is by no means straightforward; rather, it is a complex relationship. Using three feature importance methods and one Pearson correlation method, we were able to identify a general and reasonable indication of the existence of a strong relationship between electronic properties (such as valence, group, electronegativity, and ionic radius) and the band gap. The interesting point here is that machine learning, without any knowledge of our features and just by having a few numbers, is able to demonstrate this claim. However, to further confirm which electronic property might have the most significant impact, more extensive studies are required.

Conclusion

The abilities of four ML models to predict both direct and indirect band gaps across a wide variety of double and layered perovskite structures, as important solar cell materials are examined. For use as perovskite descriptors, 40 generalized input features were first created based on the fundamental elemental features. Next, a new feature known as derivative discontinuity is added as a parameter, and its implications are examined. The study’s implementation of the Support Vector Regression (SVR) model confirms that it is the most effective model for band gap prediction. However, for the band gap, the SVR model demonstrated good performance in the preliminary set of results, and the other three models with the best overall standardized error scores, Gradient Boosting Regression (GBR), Random Forest Regression (RFR), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) perform best over a range of sample sizes and streamline feature selection. The 10 most crucial features for both direct and indirect band gaps are finally determined by running SHAP, LIME, and PI on all input features. Consequently, feature importance revealed a robust correlation between the standard-deviated Valence of atoms and the band gap of double and layered perovskites.
