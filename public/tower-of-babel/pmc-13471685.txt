Cavernous venous malformation of the frontal sinus: CT and MRI imaging findings
Authors: Fengyu Wang, Man Chong, Jinghan Sun, Wei Li, Quan Zhang, Jiayuan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cavernous venous malformations (CVMs) of the paranasal sinuses are rare benign vascular lesions that can mimic aggressive neoplasms. Here, we report a 24-year-old male presenting with progressive nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, and visual impairment. Preoperative CT demonstrated an expansile, soft-tissue mass within the left frontal sinus, characterized by a delicate osseous shell, internal radial bony ridges, and a pedunculated attachment to the anterior sinus wall. Dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI revealed progressive centripetal enhancement with a characteristic “slow-filling” pattern. The lesion was successfully excised via an endoscopic approach. Histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of CVM, demonstrating multiple irregular vascular spaces lined by flattened endothelial cells within the mucosal stroma. Postoperative outcomes were favorable without recurrence. CVM should be included in the differential diagnosis of unilateral frontal sinus masses when bony remodeling, internal radial ridges, and progressive centripetal enhancement are present. Combining CT and MRI findings is indispensable for distinguishing CVM from other sinonasal lesions and guiding minimally invasive endoscopic resection.

Background

Hemangiomas are benign vascular neoplasms that can occur in nearly every tissue in the human body. Although these lesions are fairly common in the head and neck, they occur infrequently in the sinonasal cavity [1]. Nasal hemangiomas are typically capillary in nature and often congenital, whereas hemangiomas involving the paranasal sinuses are predominantly cavernous in nature [2]. Although clinically referred to as “cavernous hemangiomas,” these lesions are characterized by dilated vascular spaces and normal endothelial turnover. Therefore, they are biologically distinct from true proliferative neoplasms and are more accurately classified as cavernous venous malformations (CVMs) [3]. It can present with recurrent epistaxis, nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, facial pain or central facial deformity [2]. Regarding their growth pattern, they exhibit a slow clinical course. Due to their compressive effect within the confined sinus space, they tend to cause expansile bone remodeling and thinning, rather than the aggressive destruction seen in malignancies [2,4].

While isolated cases involving the maxillary sinus have been described [2,5], CVM of the frontal sinus is exceptionally rare. We report a case of a giant frontal sinus CVM causing orbital compression, and discuss the radiological findings, differential diagnosis, and management of this lesion.

Case report

A 24-year-old male patient presented with nasal obstruction and rhinorrhea for 2 years, accompanied by decreased visual acuity in the left eye for 1 year. He denied a family history of hereditary diseases.

Given the patient's primary complaints of progressive nasal obstruction and visual impairment, an initial non-contrast orbital MRI was performed. The examination revealed an enlarged left frontal sinus occupied by a lobulated mass that extended inferiorly into the ipsilateral anterior ethmoid sinus (Fig. 1). The lesion appeared predominantly isointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), intermixed with multiple cystic-like areas within the locules (Fig. 1B), and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed no restriction (Fig. 1C). Clinically, the lesion caused significant mass effect, compressing the superior rectus muscle (Fig. 1D) along with secondary obstructive inflammatory changes in the left frontal sinus manifested as fluid accumulation within the sinus cavity. Axial non-contrast sinus CT (bone window) demonstrates a localized, expansile lesion involving the left frontal bone and the left frontal sinus. The lesion exhibits a characteristic ground-glass opacity with scattered, irregular punctate and linear calcification, resulting in a “cotton-wool” appearance. Notably, radial bony ridges are observed along the wall of the left frontal sinus, extending toward the center of the lesion (Fig. 2). The lesion causes marked expansion of the bone with associated cortical thinning, though the cortex remains intact without evidence of frank bone destruction or an associated soft tissue mass. The patient underwent surgical resection of the lesion. Intraoperatively, the frontal sinus tumor was found to displace and compress the globe, associated with a bony defect in the orbital roof and extension into the retrobulbar space. The dura mater remained uninvolved and intact. The tumor was meticulously dissected along the orbital and skull base margins; further exploration confirmed that the lesion originated from the anterolateral wall of the frontal sinus. The tumor tissue, which was polypoid and prone to bleeding, was resected in pieces. The intraoperative blood loss was minimal, and the patient recovered smoothly after surgery. Microscopic examination was performed on the resected lesion specimen from the left frontal sinus. Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining demonstrated multiple irregular vascular spaces lined by flattened endothelial cells within the mucosal stroma. Focal fibrous hyperplasia with lymphocytic infiltration was noted, along with scattered areas of calcification (Fig. 4). The lining endothelial cells were positive for CD31 and CD34, which are highly specific and sensitive markers for endothelial cells, thereby confirming the vascular origin of the lesion; stromal cells expressed Vimentin and β-Catenin diffusely, with focal PR positivity; smooth muscle cells were SMA-positive, indicating perivascular smooth muscle components. Additionally, AR, ER, and S-100 were negative, and the Ki-67 proliferation index was low, supporting the benign biological behavior of the lesion. Based on the above morphological and immunohistochemical findings, the lesion was pathologically diagnosed as CVM.Fig. 4HE-stained sections of the frontal sinus lesion: Irregular, dilated thin-walled vascular spaces with flattened endothelium (red arrows) are embedded in the mucosal stroma. Following surgery, the patient was scheduled for regular clinical and radiological follow-up to monitor recovery and screen for recurrence. Two months after discharge, the patient's preoperative symptoms had significantly improved. Notably, regarding his visual symptoms, the globe displacement was fully corrected, and the restricted ocular motility and retrobulbar pressure were completely resolved, with his visual acuity returning to baseline. At this 2-month postoperative interval, a follow-up maxillofacial CT showed a focal bony defect at the anteroinferior wall of the left frontal sinus, with adjacent wall thickening and heterogeneous osteosclerosis. Soft-tissue opacification containing scattered punctate calcifications were observed within the sinus, which was considered to represent postoperative mucosal reactive changes rather than tumor recurrence (Fig. 5). Discussion

CVMs are benign vascular lesions commonly found in the head and neck. While isolated cases involving the maxillary sinus have been described [2,5], involvement of the paranasal sinuses is uncommon. A 21-year retrospective study of 1479 CVM patients identified 300 cases in the head and neck, with a mere 37 involving the sinonasal mucosa. These vascular lesions exhibited a marked predilection for the nasal septum (40.5%), inferior turbinate (29.7%), the maxillary sinus (8.1%), and the uncinate process (8.1%); notably, not a single case originated within the frontal sinus [6]. This makes primary origin from the frontal sinus, as observed in our case, exceptionally rare. Furthermore, histopathologically, CVMs in the sinonasal tract are predominantly of the capillary type, which are mainly encountered in children [7].

In our case, the patient presented with chronic nasal obstruction and purulent rhinorrhea for 2 years. Literature on sinonasal CVMs indicate that these lesions may result in chronic epistaxis and nasal obstruction [8]; however, due to the non-specific nature of such clinical findings, these entities can be confused with other benign inflammatory diseases. In our case, the progressive loss of vision in the left eye served as a critical “red flag,” suggesting potential close or damage to the optic nerve. These progressive symptoms necessitated radiological evaluation to clarify the underlying pathology, as these lesions can cause compression of adjacent orbital structures as they expand. Accurate preoperative diagnosis, therefore, relies heavily on characteristic imaging findings to differentiate CVMs from more frequent sinonasal pathologies.

The differential radiological diagnosis of a unilateral frontal sinus mass should be approached based on histological origins:

Epithelial-derived lesions: Inverted papillomas (IP) are common benign tumors that typically demonstrate a characteristic “convoluted cerebriform pattern” (CCP) on MRI. It has been reported in the literature that the presence of CCP exhibits an exceedingly high sensitivity and negative predictive value for the diagnosis of IP, especially on contrast-enhanced T1WI [9]. The complete absence of this morphology in our case strongly suggests an alternative diagnosis. Furthermore, considering that IP has a documented potential for malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), radiological evidence of aggressive, permeative bone destruction is often a key indicator of such progression [9,10]. In contrast, the well-defined osseous shell and pressure-induced remodeling observed in our patient are classical imaging hallmarks of a slow-growing, benign lesion.

Osteogenic, chondrogenic, or fibro-osseous origin lesions: Osteomas are the most frequent tumors of the frontal sinus. Although the irregular calcifications observed in our case made the initial differentiation from an osteoma somewhat challenging, the soft-tissue density and specific enhancement pattern were inconsistent with the dense, compact bone typical of an osteoma. Fibrous dysplasia (FD) should be considered given its classic ground-glass appearance on CT; however, FD typically presents as a poorly-defined, expansile bony lesion that preserves the overall shape of the bone [11], rather than an isolated soft-tissue mass with irregular matrix calcification as seen in our patient. Additionally, primary intraosseous hemangioma (PIH) can manifest with bony expansion and honeycombing or “sunburst” trabeculation [12]; unlike sinonasal CMVs, which may arise from osseous, mucosal, or submucosal tissues of the nasal cavity or sinuses [8], this specific lesion originates from vessels within the diploic space [13]. Nonetheless, the close anatomical relationship between these structures makes differentiating a frontal bone PIH with secondary sinus involvement from a primary frontal sinus CVM with bone invasion clinically challenging. Furthermore, chondrosarcomas or chondromas must be considered when calcified matrices are present. These cartilaginous tumors typically exhibit characteristic “ring-and-arc” chondroid matrix mineralization on CT and very high signal intensity on T2WI [14]. However, the progressive centripetal enhancement observed in our patient is not a feature of cartilaginous lesions, which more commonly show peripheral or septal enhancement patterns matching their lobular architecture.

Fibrous, mesenchymal, or reactive/vascular lesions: Furthermore, although vascular tumors like juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (JNA) can exhibit intense enhancement, JNA characteristically originates from the sphenopalatine foramen and preferentially expands into the pterygopalatine fossa [15]. The anatomical location of the lesion in our patient, isolated within the frontal sinus, and the lack of involvement of the posterior sinonasal structures effectively exclude JNA as a diagnostic possibility. Similarly, organized hematoma (OH) of the frontal sinus can mimic vascular tumors, often presenting as an expansile mass with heterogeneous, progressive enhancement on MRI due to its recurrent hemorrhage and neocapillary proliferation [16]. However, OH typically displays a characteristic T2 hypointense rim corresponding to a fibrous capsule or hemosiderin deposition, and is clinically associated with repeated minor epistaxis or a history of trauma, which contrasts with the imaging and clinical findings of our patient.

This case is educationally distinctive for 3 reasons. First, to our knowledge, this represents the first documented isolated primary CVM confined to the frontal sinus, expanding the known anatomical distribution of this entity. Second, the CT and MRI imaging constellation, a delicate osseous shell with internal radial bony ridges and pedunculated attachment on CT, coupled with progressive centripetal “slow-filling” enhancement on dynamic MRI, constitutes a previously uncharacterized radiological signature for sinonasal CVMs. This pattern helps distinguish CVM from aggressive neoplasms exhibiting frank bone destruction and rapid homogeneous enhancement. Third, successful endoscopic excision guided by these preoperative imaging features validates that accurate radiological characterization can enable minimally invasive management, preventing unnecessary open surgical morbidity.

In summary, while sinonasal CVM is rare, it should be included in the differential diagnosis of any slow-growing, expansile frontal sinus mass with radial bony ridges. In our case, although radiological evidence showed no direct involvement of the optic nerve, the progressive visual loss served as a critical clinical “red flag.” This underscores the fact that even benign, slow-growing lesions can lead to significant orbital or ocular symptoms through local mass effect and compression of adjacent structures as they expand. Given the high vascularity of these lesions, an accurate preoperative diagnosis based on the aforementioned imaging characteristics, specifically the centripetal enhancement, is paramount to anticipate potential intraoperative hemorrhage. Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice to prevent recurrence and relieve compressive symptoms. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when encountering similar radiological patterns to ensure optimal patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Primary sinonasal CVM is an exceptionally rare case that can easily be misidentified as a locally aggressive neoplasm due to its expansile nature. A comprehensive understanding of its clinicopathological profile is essential to include this benign lesion in the differential diagnosis of sinonasal masses, particularly in cases presenting with persistent nasal symptoms or atypical orbital manifestations. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), specifically dynamic contrast enhancement, are indispensable diagnostic tools for characterizing the lesion, assessing its anatomical extension, and guiding the selection of the most appropriate surgical approach. Whether via exclusive endoscopic sinus surgery or a combined external approach, complete surgical excision remains the definitive treatment, ensuring both effective symptom relief and a favorable prognosis.
