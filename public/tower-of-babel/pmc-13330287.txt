Integrative proteogenomic and observational analysis identifies potential biomarkers for latent autoimmune diabetes in adults
Authors: Yuanqin Yang, Jiawen Lu, Zhenqian Wang, Tianyuan Lu, Jin Ding, Ruofan Wang, Sirui Zhou, Zhiguang Zhou, Jingyi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) shares core genetic and immunological features with type 1 diabetes (T1D) but is frequently misdiagnosed as type 2 diabetes (T2D). With few biomarkers for its timely diagnosis and management, this study integrated proteome-wide Mendelian randomisation (MR) and observational clinical analysis to identify potential LADA biomarkers.MethodsWe performed proteome-wide MR using cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTLs) for 1,389 plasma proteins from the deCODE study (n = 35,559) and genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for LADA (2,634 cases and 5,947 controls, European ancestry). Robustness was enhanced via multiple sensitivity analyses. Pathway enrichment analysis, druggability evaluation, phenome-wide MR, and interaction analyses were performed to investigate the clinical relevance and biological context of candidate proteins. Candidate proteins were further evaluated using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays in a matched Chinese clinical study (n = 241) to assess their discriminative ability for LADA.ResultsProteome-wide MR and colocalisation analyses indicated associations between genetically predicted plasma levels of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10; OR [95% CI] per 1-SD increase in protein levels: 5.49 [1.74,17.32]), serum amyloid A1 (SAA1; 1.28 [1.14,1.45]), and SAA2 (1.22 [1.11,1.34]) with LADA risk. Replication, multi-tissue eQTL, and multivariable MR supported CXCL10’s association. Druggability evaluation suggested CXCL10 as a drug target under investigation, and phenome-wide MR of 1,006 diseases and traits indicated no major safety concerns for CXCL10 as a potential biomarker. In the observational clinical study, CXCL10 differentiated LADA from healthy controls (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [ROC-AUC]: 0.889; precision-recall area under the curve [PR-AUC]: 0.919) and T2D (ROC-AUC: 0.838; PR-AUC: 0.921), with both models showing adequate calibration.ConclusionsThis study suggests that CXCL10 is a putative biomarker associated with LADA, demonstrating discriminative ability to distinguish LADA from T2D in an observational clinical cohort. These findings contribute to understanding the autoimmune molecular aetiology of LADA and support its diagnostic potential in resolving the clinical ambiguity between LADA and T2D.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03203-2.

Introduction

Latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) is the most common form of adult-onset autoimmune diabetes, with clinical and genetic features intermediate between type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D), accounting for approximately 12% of all patients with adult-onset diabetes [1]. It is characterized by the presence of islet autoantibodies and progressive but milder β-cell destruction compared with T1D. This allows patients to remain non-insulin-dependent for at least six months post-diagnosis, providing a therapeutic window to preserve β-cell function [1]. LADA exhibits substantial heterogeneity, and its pathogenesis remains unclear. In addition, because it is typically diagnosed at an older age and shares metabolic characteristics with T2D, misdiagnosis is common—approximately 5%–10% of presumed T2D cases are actually LADA [2]. Such misclassification can lead to suboptimal treatment regimens and accelerated β-cell failure, consequently raising the risk of complications such as cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [3]. Given the limited sensitivity and specificity of current diagnostic strategies [4], identifying biomarkers of LADA is crucial for differentiating the condition from other forms of diabetes, particularly T2D, and for enabling early, targeted intervention.

Plasma protein profiling represents a readily accessible approach for immune status assessment, with plasma proteins constituting ~ 42% of routine blood-based diagnostic tests [5]. The immune profile of LADA includes islet inflammation, the presence of islet autoantibodies, and T-cell reactivity to islet antigens [6]. Current evidence substantiates the involvement of both innate and acquired immune mechanisms in the pathogenesis of LADA [7]. However, cross-sectional studies are constrained by their observational design and are more susceptible to unmeasured confounders. Comprehensive genetic and proteomic analyses have advanced biomarker identification and the evaluation of therapeutic candidates [8]. Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified genetic loci associated with LADA and its overlap with T1D and T2D, thereby informing genetic diagnostic strategies [9]. Proteome-wide Mendelian randomisation (MR) studies using genetic instruments have implicated proteins such as signal regulatory protein gamma and chymotrypsinogen B1 as promising targets for drug development in T1D [10]. Despite these advances, integrated proteogenomic and clinical analyses to identify biomarkers of LADA remain lacking.

This study seeks to enhance biomarker identification and therapeutic candidate evaluation for LADA using MR and population-based analyses. Our objectives are threefold: (1) to estimate the effects of genetically predicted plasma protein levels on LADA risk in individuals of European ancestry using cis-protein quantitative trait loci (cis-pQTLs) as instrumental variables (IV); (2) to assess these proteins as candidates for LADA therapeutic exploration via sensitivity and exploratory analyses; and (3) to evaluate their discriminative ability for LADA in a cross-sectional Chinese study.

Methods

Study design

The study framework is depicted in Fig. 1. It includes three main components: (1) integration of two-sample MR and colocalisation analyses to decipher the associations of non-major histocompatibility complex (non-MHC) proteins and the risk of LADA; (2) follow-up analyses for proteins undergoing clinical trials through MR sensitivity, replication, multi-tissue expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) validation, multivariable MR (MVMR), phenome-wide MR, and interaction analyses with antidiabetic drug targets; and (3) validation of the association between prioritised proteins and LADA risk, as well as their ability to distinguish LADA from T2D and healthy controls (HCs) in a Chinese cohort.

The work flow of study

Ethics statement

Summary-level GWAS datasets used in this study are publicly available. All original GWAS studies were approved by the ethics committee with informed consent from all participants. The observational clinical study of LADA was conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the ethics committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University, China (Approval No. 2021-709).

Exposure data sources

Discovery datasets for cis-pQTLs associated with protein levels were derived from a proteome-wide GWAS from the deCODE study (n = 35,559), identifying 1,881 sentinel cis-pQTLs linked to 1,809 proteins [11]. Replication datasets of prioritised protein levels were sourced from GWASs in the AGES Reykjavik study (n = 5,457) and the Fenland study (n = 10,708) [12, 13]. These proteins were measured using the SomaScan platform. To evaluate different proteomics platform effects, we replicated the associations for prioritised proteins using cis-pQTLs from the UK Biobank Pharma Proteomics Project (UKB-PPP; n = 34,557), with protein levels quantified using the Olink platform [14]. Protein levels were normalized within each study. Further details and multi-tissue eQTL validation data are provided in Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 1.

Outcome data sources

Summary statistics for LADA were obtained from the largest meta-analysis of 12 GWASs in 2,634 cases and 5,947 controls (Supplementary Table 1) [9]. LADA cases were defined by the presence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies, particularly GAD65 autoantibody positivity, and the absence of insulin requirement for at least 6 months or one year post-diagnosis [9].

Summary statistics for T1D were derived from a GWAS of 18,942 cases and 501,638 controls [15]. T2D associations were derived from a meta-analysis of GWASs (P for heterogeneity < 0.05), including 74,124 cases and 824,006 controls (Supplementary Table 1) [16]. All participants in these exposure and outcome GWASs were of European ancestry.

Clinical cohort

A total of 81 participants with LADA, 48 with T1D, 41 with T2D and 71 HCs were enrolled at the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University from July 2021 to December 2024. Age and sex were frequency-matched across the four groups (Supplementary Table 1). The inclusion and exclusion criteria for participants, anthropometric measurements, and measurements of blood glucose, C-peptide, HbA1c, and prioritised proteins are shown in the Supplementary Methods.

Genetic IVs selection

IVs were selected under three key assumptions—(i) the instruments are strongly associated with the protein of interest; (ii) the instrument-disease association is unconfounded; and (iii) the instruments influence the disease only via the protein [8]. Cis-pQTLs located near (± 1Mb) or within the genes encoding corresponding proteins were selected as IVs to proxy protein levels. Eligible cis-pQTLs had to be common (minor allele frequency > 0.01), genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10−8), and independent (linkage disequilibrium (LD) r2 < 0.001, clumped using the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European panel). Variants with F-statistics < 10 were excluded to minimize weak instrument bias. Proteins within the MHC region (GRCh38 coordinates CHR6:28510120-33480577) were excluded due to their high pleiotropic nature [10]. For missing SNPs in outcome GWASs, proxy SNPs in high LD (r2 > 0.8) were substituted. To minimize errors related to strand issues, palindromic alleles with MAF > 0.42 were excluded. Following these stringent criteria, 2,218 cis-pQTLs were selected as IVs for 1,389 proteins (Supplementary Table 3). The same procedures were applied in replication, reverse, and cis-eQTL MR analyses.

Primary MR and colocalisation analyses

To explore associations between 1,389 proteins and LADA risk, we employed the Wald ratio method for single-IV proteins and inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method for those with ≥ 2 IVs. Fixed-effects IVW was applied when no heterogeneity was detected; otherwise, random-effects IVW was used, as assessed by Cochran's Q test. Multiple testing was controlled using Bonferroni correction (P = 3.60 × 10−5 [0.05/1,389]). For proteins reaching significance, Bayesian colocalisation analysis was performed to test whether associations were driven by LD, using standard settings and default prior probabilities. For each protein, ± 250 kb windows around the lead cis-pQTL were analysed. We focused on the posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PP.H4), which suggests that both the protein and LADA are influenced by the same variants in this region. A PP.H4 ≥ 80% was deemed as high support of colocalisation, while 50% < PP.H4 < 80% suggested medium support of colocalisation [17]. Pair-wise conditional colocalisation (PWCoCo) method was used to address potential presence of multiple shared variants [18]. Detailed information of PWCoCo and hypotheses of colocalisation are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Enrichment, replication, reverse MR, Multi-locus MR, and eQTL validation analyses

For proteins significantly associated with LADA after Bonferroni correction and supported by colocalisation, we conducted KEGG enrichment analyses using all 1,389 genes as the background set. Pathways with false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected P < 0.05 were considered significant.

We then investigated associations between these proteins and LADA in independent replication datasets using Wald ratio or IVW methods. Reverse MR was conducted to assess whether genetic predisposition to LADA influenced these protein levels. As validation, we repeated MR using both cis- and trans-pQTLs as IVs for these proteins. This approach enabled us to examine whether including multiple IVs per protein, which capture more variance, affected the results of main MR analysis. In the reverse MR and multi-locus analyses, IVW, radial IVW, weighted median, and MR-robust adjusted profile scores (MR-RAPS) methods were applied.

Additionally, cis-eQTLs for genes encoding candidate proteins across various tissues and cell types were used as IVs to test associations between their mRNA expressions and LADA using available data. Results with FDR-corrected P < 0.05 were considered significant. Steiger filtering was applied to ensure IVs explained more variance in the exposure than in the outcome.

Evaluation of druggability

We evaluated the druggability of the candidate proteins by querying a list of 4,479 druggable genes [19], along with ChEMBL (release 27) [20] and DrugBank databases (release 5.1.11) [21]. Detailed information on the 3 resources is provided in Supplementary Methods. Proteins that were targets of approved drugs or drugs in clinical development were prioritised for further analysis.

MVMR and interaction analysis between prior proteins and antidiabetic drug targets

MVMR was conducted to assess whether the prior druggable protein has a direct effect on LADA independent of related proteins, using genome-wide significant pQTLs for at least one of these proteins, with other instrument selection criteria unchanged. The primary analysis used random-effect MVMR-IVW, while MVMR-weighted median, MVMR-Egger, and MVMR-LASSO served as sensitivity analyses. For all two-sample MR analyses, the GWAS samples for exposures and outcomes were non-overlapping.

Drug targets for 8 approved antidiabetic drug classes were included in the interaction analysis, comprising 41 drug targets and one immune intervention target (Supplementary Table 4) [1, 21]. Potential interactions between the prior druggable proteins and these targets were assessed using STRING (v12.0) and GeneMANIA, considering only interactions identified by both databases.

Phenome-wide MR of the druggable protein

To better understand the association between prior proteins and LADA, we performed MR to evaluate their effects on autoimmune diseases and protein levels of T-cell transcriptomic traits. We analysed 9 autoimmune diseases, including Crohn’s disease, multiple sclerosis (MS), ulcerative colitis (UC), and others (Supplementary Table 1). T-cell markers were obtained from the Cell Marker 2.0 database (Supplementary Table 5) and cross-referenced with summary GWAS data for 2,923 plasma proteins in UKB-PPP, yielding 107 proteins for analysis (Supplementary Table 1) [14, 22]. Effects of protein levels on the 9 autoimmune diseases and 107 proteins were estimated using the Wald ratio, and results with FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. To explore potential side effects of the druggable protein, we conducted MR on other diseases and traits using summary-level outcome GWAS datasets from IEU OpenGWAS project with European ancestry [23]. After excluding small studies (n < 10,000), sex-specific or redundant datasets, socioeconomic or behavioural traits, and outcomes previously analysed (Supplementary Methods), 997 outcomes remained in phenome-wide MR analysis (Supplementary Table 6). Associations with a P < 0.05/997 were interpreted as potential side effects.

Observational clinical analysis in Chinese population

A series of clinical analyses were conducted to further investigate the effects of prioritised protein on LADA, extending the findings from the primary analysis to a matched Chinese cohort. Prioritised protein levels were compared across LADA, T1D, T2D, and HCs using the Kruskal-Wallis test, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Next, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships between prioritised protein levels and relevant clinical parameters. Logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), fasting blood glucose (FBG), prescription of DPP4 inhibitors and Metformin, and the history of autoimmune disorders were used to evaluate the association between prioritised protein and LADA. For groups that exhibited significantly different protein levels compared to LADA, the ability of these proteins to distinguish LADA from each group was evaluated using thearea under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) and precision-recall curve (PR-AUC) using logistic models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap with 10,000 resampling method was used to estimate 95% CI of AUCs. The model’s goodness of fit and calibration were evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and Brier score.

Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) for LADA per one standard deviation (SD) increase in levels of proteins and gene expressions. All analyses were two-sided and conducted using TwoSampleMR (v0.6.4), pROC (v1.18.5), and coloc (v5.1.0.1) packages in R software (v4.2.2).

Results

Proteome-wide MR and colocalisation studies identified 3 candidate proteins for LADA

After Bonferroni correction, the proteome-wide MR study identified 4 proteins significantly associated with LADA (Fig. 2A; Supplementary Table 7). Specifically, genetically predicted higher levels of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10; OR [95%CI] per 1-SD increase in plasma protein levels: 5.49[1.74, 17.32]; P = 2.06 × 10−7), MIA SH3 domain containing (MIA; 1.54 [1.27, 1.88]; P = 1.13 × 10−5), serum amyloid A2 (SAA2; 1.28 [1.14, 1.45]; P = 3.05 × 10−5) and serum amyloid A1 (SAA1; 1.22 [1.11, 1.34]; P = 3.48 × 10−5) were associated with a higher risk of LADA (Fig. 2B). Colocalisation analyses showed the PP.H4 values for CXCL10, SAA2, SAA1, and MIA were 72.86%, 63.46%, 63.52%, and 4.08%, respectively (Supplementary Table 8; Supplementary Figs. 1–4). PWCoCo further supported colocalisation for CXCL10, SAA1, and SAA2 (all PP.H4 > 50%), but not MIA (Supplementary Table 9). Steiger filtering test showed cis-pQTLs of CXCL10, SAA1, and SAA2 explained more variance in protein levels than in LADA, though the Steiger P for CXCL10 was > 0.05 (Supplementary Table 10). Given that the post hoc use of F-statistics to choose IVs may introduce more bias than it prevents, we repeated two-sample MR of proteins on LADA using IVs without applying F-statistics criteria. No substantial changes were observed (Supplementary Table 11). To mitigate potential winner’s curse bias in the LADA outcome, a conditional maximum likelihood method was applied [24]. Enrichment and comparison analysis of LADA-associated proteins

Based on 3 LADA-associated proteins, KEGG enrichment analysis of the 3 LADA-associated proteins identified seven significant biological pathways, with top three being RIG-I-like receptor signalling pathway, IL-17 signalling pathway, and TNF signalling pathway (Supplementary Table 12).

Two-sample MR IVW or Wald ratio method was employed to assess the effects of 3 LADA-associated proteins on T1D and T2D, compared to LADA. Genetically predicted higher levels of CXCL10 were associated with higher risks of T1D (1.81 [1.20, 2.71]; P = 0.004) and LADA, but not T2D (1.08 [0.95, 1.24]; P = 0.251) (Supplementary Table 13). Additionally, SAA1 and SAA2 levels were only associated with LADA (Supplementary Table 13). The PWCoCo analysis indicated that the IV for CXCL10 (rs6532086) is likely a shared variant with T1D (PP.H4 = 70.36%; Supplementary Table 9).

MR sensitivity analyses of candidate proteins

IVs for CXCL10, SAA1, SAA2 in multi-locus, replication, and eQTL validation analyses, as well as for LADA in reverse MR analysis, are listed in Supplementary Table 14. Multiple locus MR analysis consistently showed that higher levels of CXCL10, SAA1, and SAA2 were linked to increased LADA risk (Supplementary Fig. 5A). These associations were supported by radial IVW, weighted median, and MR-RAPS methods (Supplementary Table 15). Conversely, reverse MR analysis provided no evidence that genetic predisposition to LADA influenced CXCL10, SAA1, and SAA2 levels (Supplementary Fig. 5B; Supplementary Table 16). Replication analysis validated these findings: increased levels of CXCL10 (OR [95% CI]: 2.28 [1.26, 4.11]; P = 0.006), SAA2 (1.22 [1.06, 1.41]; P = 0.005), and SAA1 (1.08 [1.01, 1.16]; P = 0.029) were associated with increased LADA risk (Supplementary Fig. 5C; Supplementary Table 17) [12, 13]. Of the three proteins, data were available only for CXCL10 in the UKB-PPP, where it was also found to be associated with LADA risk (6.14 [1.81,20.87]; P = 0.004) [14].

Using cis-eQTLs as instruments, genetically predicted higher CXCL10 mRNA expression was associated with an increased risk of LADA in both blood tissues (OR [95% CI]: 1.45 [1.09, 1.93]; P = 0.011) and lymphoma cell lines (1.37 [1.13, 1.65]; P = 0.001; Supplementary Table 18). Additionally, higher expression levels of SAA1 and SAA2 in tibial artery and higher SAA1 expression in lung were linked to higher LADA risk (Supplementary Table 18). All IVs passed the MR-Steiger test (Supplementary Table 10).

Evaluation of candidate proteins with pharmacological pathways

A search of druggable gene lists, the ChEMBL, and the DrugBank databases identified two CXCL10-targeted drugs, ELDELUMAB and NI-0801. While these compounds are currently entering phase I/II clinical trials for distinct autoimmune disorders, such as Crohn’s disease, UC, and allergic contact dermatitis, they are not currently being evaluated for LADA (Table 1). Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as highlighting the CXCL10 inflammatory axis as a biologically relevant and potentially targetable pathway, rather than providing immediate therapeutic candidates. MVMR of CXCL10 on LADA

A total of 11 IVs were included in the MVMR analysis (Supplementary Table 19). MVMR-IVW showed that genetically predicted CXCL10 protein levels were directly associated with the risk of LADA (6.95 [2.71,17.81]; P < 0.001), conditioning on CXCL9 and CXCL11. MVMR-LASSO and MVMR-weighted median methods supported the results, while MVMR-Egger suggested no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Table 20).

Effect of CXCL10 on other diseases and traits

Phenome-wide MR analyses across 997 outcomes were conducted to evaluate potential side effects of CXCL10 target. After applying Bonferroni-correction, genetically predicted CXCL10 levels were only associated with decreased levels of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG; β [95% CI]: − 0.069 [− 0.096, − 0.043]; P = 2.62 × 10−7; Fig. 3C). No other significant adverse outcomes were identified, supporting the overall safety of CXCL10-targeted drugs (Fig. 3C; Supplementary Table 23).

Interactions between CXCL10 and antidiabetic drug targets

The STRING database indicated that CXCL10 may have co-expression and experimentally determined interactions with dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP4), with the highest combined score of 0.777. GeneMANIA also showed that CXCL10 has co-expression and physical interactions with DPP4 (Supplementary Table 24).

Clinical study on the relationship and differential ability of CXCL10

Anthropometric and biochemical characteristics of HCs, participants with LADA, T1D and T2D are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Compared to HCs, patients with LADA exhibited significantly higher fasting blood glucose (FBG), HbA1c, and CXCL10 levels. Patients with LADA exhibited the highest levels of CXCL10, which was significant compared to T2D, but not significant compared to T1D (Fig. 4A). Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated positive associations of CXCL10 levels with FBG (r = 0.406; P < 0.001; Fig. 4B) and HbA1c (r = 0.464; P < 0.001; Fig. 4C; Supplementary Table 25). Multivariable logistic regression confirmed that elevated CXCL10 levels were significantly associated with increased LADA risk (OR [95% CI]: 1.06 [1.03,1.12]; P = 0.001). CXCL10 levels demonstrated discriminative accuracy for distinguishing LADA from HCs (ROC-AUC: 0.889 [0.838,0.939]; Fig. 4D; PR-AUC: 0.919 [0.917,0.921]; Fig. 4E) and from T2D (ROC-AUC: 0.838 [0.768,0.908]; Fig. 4F; PR-AUC: 0.921 [0.914,0.928]; Fig. 4G). Both logistic regression models showed good calibration, as indicated by non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow tests (LADA vs. HC: P = 0.795; LADA vs. T2D: P = 0.297) and low Brier scores (0.163 and 0.164, respectively). To enhance the robustness of these findings, a series of sensitivity analyses were performed. Firstly, we used a full linear regression model to investigate the associations between CXCL10 levels and GADA titers. It suggested that increased CXCL10 was associated with higher GADA titers (β [95% CI]: 0.002 [0.001, 0.003]; P = 0.004). Secondly, the abilities of CXCL10 to differentiate atypical LADA cases with low (< 180 units/ml) or negative GADA titers (n = 24) from HCs and T2D were evaluated using logistic models adjusted for age, sex, and BMI. Results showed CXCL10 maintained a potential ability to distinguish this atypical LADA subgroup from T2D (ROC-AUC: 0.777 [0.653, 0.883]; PR-AUC: 0.749 [0.636, 0.873]; Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). Furthermore, CXCL10 demonstrated potential discriminative ability between atypical LADA cases and HCs (ROC-AUC: 0.851 [0.754, 0.931]; PR-AUC: 0.776 [0.668, 0.885]; Supplementary Fig. 6C and D). Finally, the relationship between LADA disease duration and CXCL10 levels was investigated. Discussion

This study leveraged plasma cis-pQTL data and the largest available LADA GWAS to investigate associations between 1,389 circulating proteins and the risk of LADA. We identified four proteins potentially linked to LADA, of which CXCL10, SAA1, and SAA2 showed evidence of colocalisation. These findings were corroborated through replication studies, multi-locus MR, reverse MR, and eQTL validation analyses. Notably, MVMR analysis demonstrated an independent effect of CXCL10 on LADA, and phenome-wide MR analysis indicated good safety with no significant adverse outcomes. Aligned with European-ancestry MR results, the external Chinese clinical study further suggested that elevated serum levels of CXCL10 were associated with increased LADA risk and showed potential for distinguishing LADA from HCs and T2D.

CXCL10 is recognised as a key mediator in the immune-mediated pathogenesis of various autoimmune disorders [25], yet its role in LADA remains incompletely characterized. In our study, higher CXCL10 levels were associated with increased risks of multiple autoimmune diseases, including T1D, MS, and UC, consistent with prior evidence [26–28]. Notably, epidemiological evidence links both MS and inflammatory bowel disease to a higher risk of LADA [29]. Moreover, we observed no evident difference in CXCL10 levels between LADA and T1D groups. This concerted evidence suggests that CXCL10 may constitute a shared immunopathological component underlying the autoimmune susceptibility observed across these conditions. The mechanistic role of CXCL10 is supported by studies showing that tissue-resident memory T cells can produce CXCL10, thereby activating adaptive immunity in individuals with T1D [26]. Furthermore, a recent single-cell RNA sequencing study in patients with LADA showed elevated CXCL10 expression in specific monocyte subpopulations, suggesting its involvement in LADA pathogenesis may be through monocyte activity [30]. Collectively, these findings provide evidence that CXCL10 may contribute to the development of LADA through immune pathways shared with T1D, MS, and UC. Meanwhile, the pronounced immunological overlap with T1D highlights the need for further study to delineate the specific role of CXCL10. Interestingly, we found genetically predicted CXCL10 levels were associated with lower circulating SHBG. SHBG is closely linked to metabolic health: prior studies report reduced SHBG in metabolic syndrome and in T1D with insulin resistance [31, 32]. Therefore, the CXCL10-SHBG association may point to potential metabolic-hormonal correlates of the CXCL10 axis, but this observation requires further validation.

Given this potential role, we sought to elucidate the underlying mechanism by investigating associations between CXCL10 and T-cell transcriptomic trait-encoded proteins. Although no associations survived FDR correction, nominal signals showed that higher CXCL10 was linked to lower ITGB1 and KIT, and higher APEX1, STX3, CD3G, and CD2. Studies have reported that ITGB1-deficient mice exhibit reduced pancreatic β-cell expansion, and overexpression of KIT has been shown to enhance β-cell function, improve insulin secretion [33, 34]. In contrast, APEX1, STX3, CD3G, and CD2 are well-known proteins of pro-inflammatory processes [35–38]. Based on these findings, higher CXCL10 levels may contribute to an increased risk of LADA by downregulating ITGB1 and KIT—potentially impairing β-cell function and insulin secretion, while simultaneously upregulating pro-inflammatory proteins such as APEX1, STX3, CD3G, and CD2, thereby exacerbating immune-mediated β-cell dysfunction. Our protein–protein interaction analysis also identified CXCL10-DPP4 interactions. DPP4 inhibitors (e.g., teneligliptin and sitagliptin) effectively improve glycaemic control and β-cell function in LADA [1]. A previous study showed that teneligliptin treatment significantly reduced plasma levels of CXCL10 in patients with T2D [39]. Additionally, another study showed that sitagliptin inhibits the NF-κB pathway, reducing liver inflammation by downregulating the mRNA expression of Cxcl10 in diabetic mice [40]. These results expand our understanding and interpretation of the protective role of lowering CXCL10 in LADA pathogenesis.

In addition to CXCL10, we identified two other proteins, SAA1 and SAA2, that showed strong associations with LADA. These proteins are members of the SAA family, key components of the acute-phase response. SAA proteins have been shown to be associated with the pathogenesis of gut-related inflammatory diseases and T2D [41, 42]. In gut-related inflammatory diseases, SAA1 and SAA2 proteins were secreted by intestinal epithelial cells, where they play roles in regulating the activity of helper T-cell 17 and stimulating the production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-17 [42]. However, because SAA1 and SAA2 act as broad acute-phase reactants and have roles in lipid metabolism, they are highly sensitive but non-specific markers of systemic inflammation and metabolic traits. Their elevation is common across a wide spectrum of autoimmune and metabolic conditions, such as gut-related inflammatory diseases, cardiovascular disease, and T2D [42, 43]. Consequently, while they may reflect a generalized inflammatory burden or shared metabolic dysfunction in these patients, they are likely less specific to the hallmark autoimmune beta-cell destruction that defines the LADA phenotype. Therefore, the exact mechanisms of SAA proteins in the pathogenesis of LADA and T2D remain unclear and warrant further dedicated investigation.

Strengths and limitations

The key strength of this study is that consistent positive associations between CXCL10 and LADA risk were observed across multiple analyses: MR findings in European cohorts using two distinct affinity platforms, and observational associations in an independent Chinese cohort with ELISA measurements. This triangulated analytical approach, combined with gene-phenotype evidence, strengthens the robustness of our findings. This study also has several limitations. First, we only assessed proteins located in non-MHC region, as the high risk of horizontal pleiotropy associated with the MHC region may violate MR assumptions and introduce bias. Second, although this research incorporated a broad range of plasma proteins, some relevant proteins may have been missed due to a lack of appropriate IVs. Third, the winner’s curse in the LADA GWAS may inflate the effect sizes of CXCL10 on LADA in our MR estimates, owing to the relatively small LADA sample size. However, the minimum P-value for IVs of CXCL10 was < 10−13, and the result was replicated using IVs from the deCODE study and UKB-PPP study, substantially mitigating this issue in practice [44]. Moreover, although the LADA GWAS data we used represents the most up—to-date and largest available dataset, further larger and multi-ancestry GWASs of LADA are warranted. Fourth, while controlling for CXCL9 and CXCL11 in MVMR analyses helps mitigate the risk of horizontal pleiotropy for CXCL10 on LADA, the possibility of bias via shared inflammatory networks or direct immunomodulation cannot be entirely excluded. Fifth, although LADA-associated human leukocyte antigen (HLA) risk alleles vary between European and East Asian populations, our study observed the CXCL10-LADA association remained consistent across both. While downstream CXCL10 upregulation may represent a convergent pathway in established autoimmunity, our clinical cohort lacked HLA genotyping, precluding specific allele subgroup analyses. Future multi-ancestry studies with detailed HLA genotyping are warranted to fully evaluate this. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the clinical study cannot rule out the possibility of reverse causation, while the modest sample size may result in restricted overall statistical power. Larger prospective follow-up studies and randomized clinical trials are required to fully explore its role in LADA. Additionally, these associations have yet to be evaluated in African, South Asian, or Admixed American populations. Future multi-ancestry studies are essential to fully understand how diverse genetic backgrounds influence this biomarker’s performance.

Conclusions

In summary, this study integrates genetic association data with observational analyses to identify CXCL10 as a putative biomarker for autoimmune diabetes, demonstrating high discriminative ability in distinguishing LADA from T2D. Our multi-omics approach further elucidates potential mechanistic pathways underlying its pathogenic role, providing a theoretical basis for targeting CXCL10 in LADA treatment. Further clinical and basic studies are warranted to validate and explore the feasibility and effectiveness of targeting CXCL10 for the prevention and treatment of LADA.
