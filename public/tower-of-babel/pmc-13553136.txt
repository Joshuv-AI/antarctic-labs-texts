Why Parity is Not the Goal: Increasing Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Representation in Non‐General Practice Specialties
Authors: Ryan Dashwood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors remain under‐represented in non‐general practice specialties. Although parity is often used as a benchmark for workforce reform, targets alone cannot address the structural challenges that constrain entry, progression and retention. Specialist training systems must be redesigned through Indigenous leadership, shared authority, culturally safe workplaces, protected mentorship and recognition of cultural labour. Parity is not the goal; it is the consequence of systems that are equitable, accountable and grounded in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander self‐determination. Building such systems will strengthen specialist care and improve outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients and communities.

Positionality Statement

As a Budawang man of the Yuin Nation, Aboriginal emergency physician, researcher, doctoral student and medical workforce advocate, I write from the interface of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledges, Western biomedical systems, specialist medical training and health policy. These experiences shape my perspective, while maintaining an acute awareness that I remain a lifelong learner in this space and that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives are diverse, dynamic and equally valid.

I recognise that the current voice of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Fellows has been made possible by the work of many who came before us: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people outside medicine, Elders, community leaders, health workers, educators, researchers, advocates and non‐Indigenous allies who have advanced Indigenous medical education, workforce reform and cultural safety over many years. This perspective article is offered as one Aboriginal Fellow's contribution, grounded in self‐determination, accountability to community and continued reflection on how my own experiences shape this work.

Introduction

Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health professionals improve access, experience and outcomes for Indigenous patients and strengthen the cultural safety of the broader health system [2, 3]. Their contributions extend beyond clinical care to leadership, policy, education and advocacy, often while still early in their careers [4, 5].

Despite steady growth in medical school enrolments, representation within non‐general practice specialties remains extremely low. The ‘Growing the number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander medical specialists 2021’ report from the Australian Indigenous Doctors' Association (AIDA) states that fewer than 0.2% of Australia's medical specialists identify as Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander [6].

Parity is frequently invoked as the benchmark for Indigenous workforce development [7]. However, when specialist medical colleges treat parity as the primary goal, the deeper issue is obscured: Australia's specialist training systems were not designed with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors in mind. A focus on numbers can encourage reactive, target‐driven responses, often triggered by accreditation pressures, rather than the structural transformation required for Indigenous doctors to enter, remain and thrive [8].

This challenge is compounded by persistent misunderstandings about the role and capacity of Indigenous organisations. Colleges may assume Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander bodies can absorb advisory or cultural safety responsibilities without resourcing or partnership [8]. These assumptions reproduce cultural load, placing disproportionate expectations on Indigenous doctors and organisations to repair systems they did not design. Combined with racism, culturally unsafe environments and limited access to Indigenous mentorship, these dynamics undermine recruitment, progression and retention [9, 10].

Parity should therefore be considered as a byproduct of systems that are culturally safe, structurally accountable and grounded in Indigenous self‐determination. Until training environments (the combination of both workplace and college professional development oversight) support Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors to enter their domains willingly and flourish, parity will remain elusive, not because Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors are incapable, but because systems remain misaligned with their needs. These are not exceptional requests. They are the same conditions all doctors are entitled to; that is, a safe workplace free of racism, access to appropriate support, flexible and contemporary educational delivery and recognition, resourcing and remuneration for work undertaken.

Structural Challenges Embedded in Specialist Training Systems

Specialist training in Australia reflects colonial histories, hierarchical cultures and dominant biomedical worldviews. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors continue to report racism, cultural unsafety, isolation and limited access to Indigenous mentorship [10]. These experiences are not incidental; they are consequences of systems that have never centred Indigenous knowledge and priorities.

A defining barrier is the disproportionate cultural and professional load carried by Indigenous trainees. Many are expected to provide cultural advice to the workplace and/or college, mentor students and contribute to institutional reform, often without recognition or protected time. This invisible labour compounds already demanding training requirements and contributes to burnout and attrition [4, 6, 10].

These pressures are reinforced by the reactive posture of many colleges and training sites. Initiatives are frequently introduced only when accreditation requirements demand action, rather than through proactive strategies grounded in Indigenous leadership [6, 8]. Cultural safety is therefore treated as compliance rather than a core professional obligation.

Uninformed assumptions about Indigenous organisations further entrench these challenges. Colleges rely on Indigenous bodies to provide expertise without ensuring appropriate resourcing, authority or partnership [8]. This reflects patterns of extraction in which Indigenous knowledge is relied upon but not structurally embedded.

Access to training opportunities also remains inequitable. Informal networks, subjective selection processes and unspoken expectations continue to advantage trainees aligned with dominant norms [6, 10]. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors, these challenges are cumulative, producing disadvantage that cannot be attributed to individual deficit.

Reframing Parity: From Endpoint to Byproduct

Parity is often positioned as the ultimate measure of success in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander medical workforce development [6, 7]. Although representation matters, treating parity as the goal risks reinforcing a deficit narrative in which Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors are expected to catch up to systems that remain unchanged. This framing obscures the structural conditions constraining entry, progression and retention within non‐general practice specialties.

A more meaningful question is why parity has not occurred despite growth in medical school enrolments [11]. The answer lies in the misalignment between the needs of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors and the systems responsible for training and employing them. Selection processes, assessment structures and cultural norms continue to privilege dominant worldviews and disadvantage those who do not conform [6]. These dynamics are compounded by racism, cultural load and limited Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander representation in governance [6, 8, 10].

Parity should be understood as a byproduct of systems that are culturally safe, equitable and accountable. When training environments support Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors to thrive, numerical growth follows. When systems remain unchanged, parity targets risk becoming symbolic gestures that mask ongoing exclusion. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors are not lacking capability; they are too often navigating systems that expose them to racism, cultural unsafety and cumulative cultural and colonial load. When these systems drive disengagement from specialist pathways, patients, public hospitals and the broader health system lose the clinical, cultural and leadership contributions that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander specialists bring.

The Role of Colleges: From Consultation to Shared Authority

Specialist colleges shape who enters (and completes) training. Their policies, governance structures and cultures determine whether Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors experience training as a pathway or as a challenge. Although many colleges express commitments to cultural safety, progress remains uneven and often symbolic [6].

A persistent challenge is reliance on consultation rather than shared authority. Colleges may seek advice from Indigenous organisations while retaining decision‐making control. This reinforces colonial patterns in which Indigenous expertise is acknowledged but not embedded. Inclusion of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander leadership in governance is therefore essential [8, 10].

Saunders' analysis highlights that cultural safety cannot be achieved through policy statements or training modules alone [8]. It requires accountability mechanisms, measurable indicators and consequences for failing to meet standards. Colleges must also address internal cultures, including racism, exclusion and expectations that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander trainees will carry cultural responsibilities without recognition [8, 10].

Training workplaces are also significant contributors to unsafe clinical and educational environments for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors. Colleges should use their accreditation authority to enforce culturally safe training conditions. The risk of losing accreditation, and therefore workforce, is a powerful driver for change. Accreditation standards must make clear that workplaces are responsible for creating environments where Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors can thrive and focus on delivering and developing patient care.

Enabling Growth: Building Systems That Allow Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Doctors to Thrive

Increasing the number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander specialists is essential for improving health outcomes and strengthening Australia's health system [4, 6, 10]. However, growth will only be sustained if training systems are redesigned to support Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors to thrive. Targets alone cannot achieve this and the following four domains are critical: (i) building internal Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander capacity; (ii) building desirability; (iii) building culturally safe training programmes; and (iv) building continuing professional development systems. Building internal Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander capacity: Colleges must invest in Indigenous governance and leadership, including funded roles with decision‐making authority across accreditation, assessment and curriculum [6, 8, 10]. Building desirability: Specialist training must be culturally safe and, therefore, a desirable environment to enter. This requires addressing racism, reducing cultural load, providing Indigenous mentorship and accommodating cultural responsibilities [10]. Building culturally safe training programmes: Training programmes must reflect Indigenous priorities, including equitable selection processes, transparent pathways and flexible structures [6, 10]. Building continuing professional development systems: Sustaining Indigenous specialists requires ongoing support through leadership development and safe professional environments [6, 8].

Strong, reciprocal partnerships with Indigenous organisations must become standard. Initiatives such as AIDA's self‐reporting standards for specialist medical colleges provide mechanisms for accountability, but require resourcing, trust and shared authority to be effective [6, 8].

AIDA's ongoing expansion of the Specialist Trainee Support Program is one example of Indigenous self‐determination and leadership supporting Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors to enter and progress through non‐general practice specialist training [12]. However, the impact of such programmes depends on health services and training workplaces enabling participation, including through protected time, release from clinical duties and recognition of professional development as core workforce reform. Without these integrated system changes, parity risks remaining an aspiration rather than a meaningful structural commitment.

Conclusion

Numerical growth in the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander specialist workforce is essential, but parity cannot be achieved through targets alone. It will remain elusive while training systems are shaped by colonial assumptions and governance structures that exclude Indigenous leadership.

When training environments are culturally safe, equitable and grounded in Indigenous self‐determination, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander doctors will not only enter specialist pathways in greater numbers but also will thrive and contribute to sustained system change [4, 6, 8, 10].

Parity is not the goal. It will be the inevitable consequence of specialist training systems that are equitable, culturally safe and accountable. Most importantly, increasing the number of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander specialists will improve the cultural safety, accessibility and quality of specialist care, contributing to better health outcomes for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander patients, families and communities.
