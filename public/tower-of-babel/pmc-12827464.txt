Characteristics of crack network catastrophe in highly weathered mudstone under hydro-mechanical disturbance: a cross-scale damage constitutive framework
Authors: Honghuan Cui, Wei Wan, Biao Ma, Hongyan Guo, Huizhen Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To thoroughly investigate the patterns of crack development and the mechanisms of degradation damage in shallow, highly weathered mudstone subjected to repeated dry-wet cycles, a series of laboratory tests were conducted. Initially, the progression of surface cracks on the specimens was documented through fixed-point photography. Image processing techniques using ImageJ software were employed, with crack ratio and fractal dimension as evaluation indices, to quantitatively analyze the relationship between fracture development and the dry-wet effect at both macro and mesoscales. Secondly, stress-strain characteristics of mudstone under different numbers of dry-wet cycles were obtained through direct shear tests. Mesoscopic parameters, crack ratio and fractal dimension, were introduced to establish a damage constitutive model for mudstone under the coupled action of dry-wet cycles and loading. The results indicate that both cohesion and internal friction angle exhibit a stepwise degradation pattern with increasing dry-wet cycles. After 9 cycles, the loss rate of cohesion is significantly higher than that of the internal friction angle. Both crack ratio and fractal dimension increase with the number of dry-wet cycles but decrease with increasing compaction degree. After 3 dry-wet cycles, the basic skeletal structure of the cracks has been preliminarily formed, gradually developing into a reticulated crack network. The stress-strain characteristics can be roughly divided into elastic deformation, elastoplastic deformation, and plastic deformation stages. Furthermore, the stress state of mudstone gradually transitions from strain hardening to strain softening with increasing dry-wet cycles. The established meso-damage constitutive model demonstrates a strong correlation with experimental data, effectively capturing the deformation and failure processes of shallow, highly weathered mudstone subjected to dry-wet cycles. Notably, the model exhibits the highest fitting accuracy under low-stress conditions, underscoring its robust applicability in characterizing the deformation and failure characteristics of shallow weathered mudstone in such environments.The research findings provide theoretical references for the optimal design of reinforcement for shallow weathered mudstone cut slopes in practical engineering.

Introduction

Mudstone is a category of sedimentary rock that forms through the compaction and accumulation of fine mineral particles resulting from weathering and erosion. Rich in various clay minerals, such as illite and montmorillonite, mudstone exhibits significant water sensitivity. This characteristic is manifested by swelling upon water absorption, shrinkage during dehydration, susceptibility to cracking, and low mechanical strength. In addition to its unique engineering properties, mudstone is widely distributed across China, spanning more than twenty provinces and regions1. Especially in shallow, easily weathered zones near the surface, under the combined influence of natural environments and engineering disturbances, weathered mudstone undergoes repeated swelling and shrinkage processes. Internal micro-cracks continuously initiate, propagate, and interconnect as these cycles progress, ultimately compromising the structural integrity of rock masses and leading to a significant deterioration in their physical and mechanical properties. The typical hazards that arise from this process—such as slope instability, collapse, and pavement cracking—pose serious threats to the traffic safety of highways.Therefore, it is crucial to investigate the mechanisms of degradation damage affecting the mechanical properties and to understand the evolution patterns of fracture development in shallow highly weathered mudstone subjected to dry-wet cycles. This research is essential for ensuring the stability of cut slopes.

The cycles of drying and wetting, resulting from the frequent alternations between precipitation and evaporation in natural environments, further complicate and introduce uncertainty into the engineering properties of mudstone2–4. In recent years, the expansion of infrastructure construction into regions characterized by complex geological conditions has resulted in an increased utilization of mudstone within engineering applications. Consequently, research focusing on the evolution of engineering properties under dry-wet cycles has gradually emerged as a central theme in the field of geotechnical engineering. For instance, Zhang et al.5 revealed the relationship between swelling-shrinkage deformation and moisture content changes in weathered mudstone through laboratory dry-wet cycle tests. Cao et al.6 conducted direct shear tests on carbonaceous mudstone weak interlayers subjected to dry-wet cycles. The results indicated a significant impact of these cycles on the specimens, with cohesion experiencing a substantial decrease as the number of cycles increased, while the internal friction angle remained largely unchanged.Tao et al.7 investigated the shear behavior of weak structural planes in landslides induced by groundwater infiltration, revealing that mudstone transitions from strain hardening to strain softening following dry-wet cycles. Yin et al.8 examined the crack propagation patterns of red-bed mudstone subjected to dry-wet cycles, noting that cracks primarily develop along the bedding direction during the initial stage of water immersion. Huang et al.9 utilized multi-scale analytical techniques, including digital image correlation, acoustic emission monitoring, 3D scanning, and scanning electron microscopy, to study red-bed mudstone. Their findings indicated that dry-wet cycles facilitate the extension and interconnection of pre-existing micro-cracks, with an increasing distribution of these micro-cracks observed as the number of cycles rises. Furthermore, Huang et al.10 explored the development patterns of surface cracks in compacted expansive soil and dynamically assessed crack progression under varying dry-wet cycles through image processing techniques; they identified a significant correlation between soil cohesion, internal friction angle, and crack parameters.

Existing research on the deterioration of mudstone engineering properties under dry-wet cycles has predominantly focused on macro-strength parameters, with relatively limited attention paid to meso- and micro-scale influencing factors. It is crucial to recognize that the dry-wet cycle process can induce multi-scale deterioration effects in mudstone at macro-, meso-, and micro-levels. Extracting and quantitatively analyzing typical parameters from meso- and micro-perspectives can provide deeper insights into its degradation mechanisms, thereby systematically elucidating the complete process of mechanical property decay in mudstone.

Constructing a damage constitutive model for mudstone subjected to dry-wet cycles is a fundamental aspect of investigating its environmental degradation mechanisms. Such models are essential for quantitatively characterizing the evolution of damage in mudstone during these cycles, as well as for scientifically assessing its long-term stability and cumulative degree of damage. This research holds significant theoretical and engineering value. Current research trends in rock and soil mechanics primarily focus on three categories of constitutive models: damage constitutive models, continuum mechanics models, and empirical formula-based constitutive models.

Scholars worldwide have conducted extensive and in-depth research in this area, proposing hundreds of constitutive models for geomaterials that have yielded significant results11–18. In the development of damage theory, Kachanov et al.19 were the first to introduce the core concepts of “effective stress” and “continuous damage factor.” Building on this foundation, Rabotnov et al.20 further advanced the concept by introducing the “damage factor.” Subsequently, Lemaitre et al.21 proposed the strain equivalence hypothesis, which established a quantitative relationship between nominal stress and effective stress. Many subsequent researchers, both domestically and internationally, have expanded upon this theory within the realm of dry-wet cycle damage constitutive models and other damage-based constitutive frameworks, significantly advancing the systematic development of damage theory. Notably, Li et al.22 investigated the meso-structural damage behavior of carbonaceous mudstone; based on their experimental findings, they categorized stress-strain characteristics into four distinct stages and developed corresponding damage evolution constitutive models for each stage utilizing different damage variables.Tang et al.23 treated the Weibull distribution as a representation of the damage variable and subsequently established a corresponding damage evolution model. However, employing axial strain as the micro-element strength does not accurately capture the deformation mechanical behavior of rock under complex stress states. Jian et al.24 examined the coupled effects of dry-wet cycles and loading on sandstone. Utilizing the strain equivalence hypothesis and characterizing micro-damage in sandstone through the Weibull probability density function, they proposed a damage constitutive relationship for sandstone subjected to these coupled actions. Cheng et al.25 developed a damage constitutive model for mudstone that accounts for various dry-wet cycle paths to analyze the shallow instability mechanisms associated with mudstone slopes. Ju et al.26 investigated the degradation patterns of shallow mudstone during landslides influenced by dry-wet cycles. By analyzing stress-strain curve characteristics, they conducted segmented research and established a piecewise statistical damage constitutive model specifically for mudstone.

Globally, scholars have introduced numerous theories regarding mudstone’s response to dry-wet cycles, thereby providing theoretical support for elucidating its damage degradation mechanisms. Nevertheless, current constitutive models predominantly define dry-wet cycle damage variables based solely on macro-parameters while overlooking meso- and micro-factors that contribute to mudstone’s dry-wet damage degradation process. This often leads to constitutive models yielding results slightly higher than laboratory data or producing unsatisfactory predictive outcomes, revealing significant limitations in their applicability. Consequently, further investigation into this area is urgently warranted.

Based on the aforementioned investigation, this study conducted laboratory direct shear tests on shallow weathered mudstone specimens subjected to varying numbers of dry-wet cycles. The development of cracks during these cycles was recorded and analyzed at both macro and meso scales to elucidate the strength degradation damage experienced by the mudstone specimens under different cycle counts. Crack development indices, including crack ratio and fractal dimension, were extracted and quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software. Finally, taking into account the initial damage sustained by the mudstone specimens, a damage constitutive model was developed to describe their behavior under the combined effects of dry-wet cycles and loading conditions. The applicability of this model was validated against experimental data, providing valuable insights for future in-depth research endeavors.

Crack image processing and calculation

The images obtained from the tests were surface images of the mudstone specimens after each dry-wet cycle. To ensure image quality, a consistent shooting distance, angle, and lighting were maintained. The captured images were processed using ImageJ software, including: (1) Cropping the image size to remove surrounding environmental influences; (2) Converting the cropped image to an 8-bit grayscale image; (3) Adjusting the image threshold; (4) Denoising and removing impurities from the binarized image, eliminating extraneous noise outside the main crack body; finally, extracting the skeleton contour of the specimen cracks. Direct shear test

To investigate the attenuation law of soil strength in mudstone specimens under different dry-wet cycles, the samples were divided into three groups based on compaction degrees of 95%, 90%, and 85%. Each group consisted of four samples corresponding to the four vertical stresses set, and the number of dry-wet cycles was 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9, for a total of 72 samples. The test instrument was a ZJ-type electric strain-controlled direct shear apparatus. Specimen dimensions were 61.8 mm in diameter and 20 mm in height. The quick shear method was employed. Four specimens were prepared under identical conditions for each group. Four levels of vertical load were applied according to test conditions: 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 kPa. Results and analysis

Shear stress-shear displacement relationship curves

Taking a compaction degree of 95% as an example, the shear stress-shear displacement curves of mudstone specimens subjected to varying numbers of dry-wet cycles are illustrated in Fig. 8. The analysis indicates that, under different numbers of dry-wet cycles, the stress-strain characteristics of the specimens progressively transition from strain hardening to strain softening. Furthermore, while shear strength increases with rising overburden load, it diminishes with an increasing number of dry-wet cycles.In strain hardening-type curves, shear stress increases with shear displacement until it stabilizes, without exhibiting a peak stress. In strain softening-type curves, shear stress rises to a certain peak with increasing shear displacement and then begins to decrease slowly, stabilizing after reaching the residual strength. Based on the stress-strain state during the shear process of mudstone specimens, as shown in Fig. 9, it can be roughly divided into 3 stages: Stage I is the linear elastic stage, where for shear strains less than 1%, the specimen’s stress-strain relationship exhibits linear growth. Stage II represents the elastoplastic deformation phase. During this stage, as shear strain continues to increase, the rate of growth in shear stress begins to decelerate, leading the specimen to gradually approach a yield state; at this point, some cementitious materials start to experience deformation and damage. Stage III corresponds to the plastic deformation phase. In cases characterized by hardening-type curves, shear stress persists in its slow upward trajectory, while the cohesion among internal mudstone particles progressively diminishes.For softening-type curves, after reaching the peak point, shear stress begins to decline, connections between particles are destroyed, friction is weakened, and the specimen exhibits significant degradation damage. During multiple dry-wet cycles, the internal structure of mudstone undergoes repeated swelling and shrinkage deformation, causing certain damage to the mudstone structure (including particle arrangement, contact surfaces, pore structure, etc.)31. The accumulated damage leads to strength reduction.

Additionally, during dry-wet cycles, numerous micro-cracks form inside and on the surface of the mudstone. As the number of cycles increases, these cracks gradually propagate and connect. The presence of cracks significantly reduces the overall strength of the mudstone structure, causing stress concentration at crack tips, thereby making the mudstone structure more susceptible to softening.

Analysis of shear strength indices under different dry-wet cycles

Based on test calculation results, the relationship curves between cohesion (c), internal friction angle (φ), and the number of dry-wet cycles for mudstone specimens at different compaction degrees are shown in Fig. 10. The numerical values marked in the dashed boxes represent the loss rate of parameters after different numbers of dry-wet cycles32, characterizing the change in mechanical properties of the specimens after undergoing the dry-wet cycle process. From Fig. 10a,b, it can be seen that both cohesion (c) and internal friction angle (φ) of mudstone specimens exhibit a stepwise attenuation pattern with increasing number of dry-wet cycles. For a single step, the specimen with 85% compaction degree reached its maximum cohesion (c) loss rate of 35.010% after just 1 dry-wet cycle, while the maximum internal friction angle (φ) loss rate of 24.814% occurred after 3 cycles. Comparing the loss rates, after 9 dry-wet cycles, the cohesion (c) loss rates for specimens at the three compaction degrees were 88.262%, 91.488%, and 91.358%, respectively, while the internal friction angle (φ) loss rates were 54.983%, 66.604%, and 77.846%, respectively. This indicates that the loss rate of cohesion (c) is significantly higher than that of internal friction angle (φ) after dry-wet cycles. The reason is that during the desiccation process, a large amount of water evaporates, leaving insufficient moisture to maintain the bonds between particles, thus significantly weakening cohesion (c). Internal friction angle (φ) is relatively less affected by moisture. Simultaneously, the reduction in soil cohesion (c) is highly prone to causing cracking, which is also an important reason for crack generation in practical engineering33.

Crack evolution law of mudstone

Extraction and calculation of crack indices

After desiccation was completed, surface crack indices of mudstone specimens were extracted. The captured images were binarized and calculated using ImageJ software. The crack development process under different dry-wet cycles for mudstone specimens at the three compaction degrees after binarization is shown in Fig. 11. The calculated results for crack development indices, crack ratio (S) and fractal dimension (Z), are listed in Table 3. It can be observed that the crack ratio of mudstone specimens increases with the number of dry-wet cycles but decreases with increasing compaction degree. Observing the crack development images in Fig. 11, crack development becomes more complex with increasing cycles.After the first dry-wet cycles, the surface cracks appear as short, scattered, isolated microcracks that have not yet formed a continuous network, lacking overall connectivity. After the third cycle, the basic skeleton structure of cracks forms, with crack patterns spreading from the specimen edges towards the center. As cycles progress further, a grid-like crack structure gradually forms. The reason is that as dry-wet cycles progress, the integrity of the soil structure is destroyed, opening pathways for crack connectivity. Throughout the dry-wet process, while new cracks generate, some old cracks may disappear. New crack development deepens and expands based on existing cracks. Once cracks form, the bonding force between particles at the crack interface is damaged. Although crack healing may occur during the wetting process due to water flushing, the soil structure is already damaged. During the next dry-wet cycle, these weakened areas become the starting points for new crack development. This damage is irreversible throughout the entire dry-wet cycle process.

Quantitative analysis of crack indicators

In Fig. 12, the distribution patterns of crack rates in relation to dry-wet cycle counts for specimens at three different compaction degrees can be analyzed across four distinct stages. Specimens with compaction degrees of 90% and 85% primarily exhibited crack evolution and development concentrated in Stages I and II. Following three dry-wet cycles, the growth rate of crack rates significantly slowed down. In contrast, specimens with a compaction degree of 95% demonstrated that crack evolution was predominantly focused on Stages II and III. Although the growth rate of the crack ratio decelerated after the fifth dry-wet cycle, it continued to exhibit a relatively rapid upward trend. After the seventh cycle, there was a gradual increase in the growth rate of the crack ratio.

This phenomenon occurs because surface cracks in specimens lack connectivity necessary for forming a continuous network following just one wet-dry cycle. After three cycles, however, a skeletal framework for crack development is largely established in specimens with 90% and 85% compaction levels, leading to more extensive cracking that covers a broader surface area. For specimens compacted to 95%, smaller pore ratios and reduced inter-particle spacing limit available contraction space during early cycles, thereby hindering fissure development. As cycling progresses, repeated moisture action induces continuous expansion and contraction deformations that disrupt particle cohesion; this process opens closed pores and facilitates gradual fissure development and stabilization over time.

Based on the test results, it can be observed that the fractal dimension of the specimens exhibits a decreasing rate of increase and gradually stabilizes after undergoing three dry-wet cycles. Specimens with compaction degrees of 90% and 85% demonstrate the most significant increase in fractal dimension during Phase I, whereas specimens with a compaction degree of 95% exhibit the greatest increase in Phase II. This finding indicates that during the dry-wet cycles, crack development in the specimens is influenced by their compaction degree; specifically, specimens at lower compaction levels tend to show a greater propensity for more complex crack formation during the initial stages of the cycle.

The meso-scale parameter f is proportional to crack ratio and fractal dimension. Combined with the experimental data in Table 3, it is evident that the meso-scale parameter f of mudstone specimens increases with the number of dry-wet cycles. Development of damage constitutive models

This section builds upon the preceding experimental content and considers the presence of initial damage in the specimens. It introduces two key indicators that describe the evolution characteristics of crack development in specimens during dry-wet cycles: crack ratio and fractal dimension. Additionally, the degradation damage of the specimen is influenced by loading effects. Under varying durations of dry-wet cycles, these factors exhibit complex coupled interactions that collectively impact the mudstone, thereby exacerbating its damage severity. This damage demonstrates a degree of randomness during shear processes. Following exposure to dry-wet cycles, significant degradation occurs in the mechanical properties of the specimen. This degradation fundamentally arises from a reduction in the effective bearing area and alterations in pore structure among internal particles within the specimen.

Damage variables

The mechanical properties of mudstone specimens experience significant degradation following dry-wet cycles, primarily due to the reduction in effective bearing area and alterations in the pore structure among internal particles. To more accurately characterize the degradation process induced by the dry-wet effect, meso-scale parameters related to crack evolution during these cycles are employed. Crack ratio reflects the degradation degree of the effective bearing area during dry-wet cycles, while fractal dimension reflects the complexity of crack development caused by dry-wet cycle damage; greater complexity corresponds to a larger fractal dimension. This paper defines the dry-wet cycle damage variable for mudstone by selecting the loss rate of the specimen’s tangent modulus. However, previous studies have often overlooked initial damage when establishing damage variables for geomaterials, treating specimens that have not undergone dry-wet cycles as undamaged. Under the combined effects of dry and wet cycles, crack development in mudstone propagates from the periphery toward the center. Upon loading, the effective bearing area of the specimen diminishes under stress. Microcracks generated internally initially propagate along the edges of the specimen and gradually converge toward the center. The progression of microcracks is irregular and exhibits a degree of randomness. Currently, the distribution of micro-damage within rock and soil bodies subjected to loading is typically characterized using both the Weibull probability density function and the normal distribution function. While both effectively describe micro-damage in mudstone, it is noteworthy that the Weibull distribution is simpler to compute and more widely applied than its normal counterpart. To verify the applicability of the established damage constitutive model for mudstone under the coupling action of dry-wet cycles and loading, specimens with a compaction degree of 95% are taken as an example. The stress-strain curves obtained from experiments are compared with the model curves. Figure 14 shows the comparison between the model’s theoretical calculation results and the measured data curves. The model curves show high agreement with the strain hardening-type curves in the stress-strain plots. For strain softening-type curves, some deviation exists in the agreement. The reason is that after shear failure, the stress within the specimen is non-uniform, particularly on the shear plane, exhibiting complexity and randomness.

Analysis of degradation mechanism of mudstone under Dry-Wet cycles and loading coupling

Under dry-wet cycles, the physical and chemical properties of mudstone undergo multi-scale changes: macroscopically, mainly degradation of mechanical properties, while meso- and microscopically, mainly movement of mineral particles and structural deformation. Mudstone exhibits a typical rock-like structure under dry conditions. Its original dense structure contains a large amount of highly reactive clay minerals (illite, montmorillonite, etc.). Due to their strong inter-crystalline cation exchange capacity, water molecules can penetrate the crystal interior, causing crystal structure expansion (increased interlayer spacing), resulting in significant volumetric swelling. Under unrestricted conditions, during expansion, fine particles at aggregate boundaries lose cohesion and undergo spalling, generating a micro-crack network. During water loss and contraction, cracks partially close. Under repeated wet-swell/dry-shrink cycles, cracks continuously accumulate, expand, and eventually lead to complete mudding disintegration. When subjected to external constraints, restricted expansion transforms into non-uniform stress, causing micro-crack generation within the material. Under the influence of dry-wet cycles, progressive mudding damage occurs inside the aggregates, while the macro-morphology remains largely intact.

Simultaneously, the redistribution of local pore space and density heterogeneity exacerbate microstructural degradation. As the number of dry-wet cycles increases, pore water content rises due to crack network extension. Swelling-shrinkage forces repeatedly act on the internal particle skeleton of the mudstone, ultimately forming a cumulative damage effect. Conclusions and discussion

Discussion

In Fig. 14 above, under a normal stress of 12.5 kPa, the measured data and model curves for specimens subjected to different dry-wet cycles exhibit a relatively consistent trend. This observation indicates that the constitutive model developed in this study demonstrates strong applicability in characterizing the stress-strain behavior of shallow highly weathered expansive mudstone. The findings validate the model’s effectiveness in simulating the mechanical response of mudstone under low-stress conditions, particularly its capacity to capture the material’s nonlinear response and stress transformation process when accounting for meso-structural factors. As normal stress increases to 100 kPa, specimens transition into the strain softening stage following dry-wet cycles; during this phase, the rate of shear stress growth diminishes and rapidly declines into the residual stress zone after reaching peak strength.This phenomenon highlights the non-uniform failure characteristics of expansive mudstone under high-stress conditions, where imbalances in local stress distribution result in discrepancies between the model predictions and measured data. This suggests that while the current model effectively captures the overall mechanical properties of the material, it inadequately addresses meso-damage mechanisms under extreme stress and complex loading scenarios. Future research should focus on enhancing the modeling of local failure processes by taking into account the non-uniform distribution of stress concentrations and their influence on the overall mechanical response, thereby improving model accuracy under more intricate working conditions. This study proposes a constitutive model that incorporates meso-factors based on a simple and widely utilized direct shear test commonly employed in engineering practice. The approach simplifies the complex three-dimensional strain problem to a plane strain scenario.This model not only provides theoretical support for the mechanical behavior of expansive mudstone but also offers an effective theoretical basis for the construction and treatment of cut slopes in this region. Although the existing model still requires further refinement, it provides an important theoretical framework for risk assessment and treatment optimization of expansive mudstone slopes and serves as a reference for similar future projects.

Conclusions

This paper conducted laboratory dry-wet cycle tests and direct shear tests on shallow highly weathered mudstone from northwestern Hebei Province. Image processing techniques were utilized to extract and quantitatively analyze crack development indices of desiccated mudstone specimens. Considering the influence of meso-factors, a damage constitutive model for mudstone under the coupling action of dry-wet cycles and loading was established and validated against experimental data. The main conclusions are as follows: Both cohesion (c) and internal friction angle (φ) of mudstone specimens exhibit a stepwise attenuation pattern with increasing number of dry-wet cycles. After 9 repeated cycles, the loss rate of cohesion (c) is significantly higher than that of the internal friction angle (φ).The crack ratio of mudstone specimens increases with the number of dry-wet cycles, while it decreases as the degree of compaction rises. Following the initial dry-wet cycles, crack development is characterized by a relatively singular and fragmented nature. After the third cycle, a fundamental skeleton structure of cracks begins to form, with crack patterns extending from the edges of the specimen towards its center. As additional cycles progress, these patterns gradually evolve into a grid-like crack structure.Mudstone specimens at three different degrees of compaction demonstrate a transition in stress-strain characteristics from strain hardening to strain softening as the number of dry-wet cycles increases. Strain hardening-type curves do not exhibit a peak stress. The shear processes associated with both types of stress-strain curves can be broadly categorized into three stages: linear elastic deformation, elastoplastic deformation, and plastic deformation.The damage constitutive model developed in this paper, which accounts for the combined effects of dry-wet cycles and loading, effectively captures the deformation characteristics of the stress-strain curve associated with shallow weathered mudstone. The theoretical curve produced by the model exhibits high accuracy and an excellent fit under low-stress conditions. This finding suggests that the proposed constitutive model is particularly well-suited for analyzing degradation damage in shallow weathered mudstone subjected to low stress.
