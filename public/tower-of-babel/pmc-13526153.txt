Applications and Emergent Solutions for the Nanofood Industry and Nanoagriculture: Technology Landscape, Market Analysis, and Patent Trends
Authors: Malgorzata Kus-Liskiewicz, Justyna Gawel, Paula Coutinho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanotechnology is increasingly recognized as a key enabling technology for addressing critical challenges in the global agri-food system, including food security, sustainability, and resource efficiency. As the industry seeks to enhance productivity while reducing environmental impact, nano-enabled solutions such as nanofertilizers, nanopesticides, and veterinary delivery systems are becoming pivotal in driving agricultural innovation. This review provides a comprehensive literature narrative review of current and emerging nano-enabled applications in food systems and agriculture, spanning nanoencapsulation, nanoemulsions, nanocomposites, and nanosensors designed for improved bioavailability, stability, and safety. Beyond purely technological developments, the work integrates a market analysis and a patent landscape evaluation (using Espacenet) to identify innovation trajectories and commercialization trends. The most recent developments highlights the emerging convergence of nanotechnology with digitalization, including Artificial Intelligence (AI) and the Internet of Things (IoT), highly relevant for precision agriculture, and smart food systems, which is expected to play a key role in translating scientific advances into socially accepted and commercially viable solutions. Nevertheless,despite promising technological and commercial indicators, a significant finding is that most nano-enabled solutions remain incremental formulation-based innovations. Scalability, safety concerns, and the lack of regulatory harmonization represent the primary barriers to widespread adoption. Ultimately, this work emphasizes that a responsible and scalable transition to nanofood and nanoagriculture systems requires integrated, interdisciplinary approaches and a clear focus on overcoming existing commercial and safety hurdles, namely regarding harmonized risk assessment frameworks, standardized characterization methods, and life-cycle-based evaluations.Keywords: nanofood, nanoagriculture, patent landscape, food safety regulation, technological trends, Internet of Things

Introduction

Nanotechnology is increasingly recognized as a transformative driver of innovation in the agri-food sector, addressing critical global challenges including population growth, climate change, resource scarcity, and the demand for sustainable food production systems.1,2 The global food system is under mounting pressure to deliver higher productivity while minimizing environmental impact and ensuring food safety and nutritional quality. In this context, nanotechnology offers unique opportunities to engineer materials with enhanced physicochemical properties, enabling novel functionalities across the entire food supply chain.3,4 At the nanoscale (1–100 nm), materials exhibit distinct optical, chemical, and biological behaviors resulting from their high surface area-to-volume ratio and quantum effects. These properties enable the development of advanced systems for targeted delivery, controlled release, sensing, and structural reinforcement. Consequently, nanotechnology has found applications in food processing, packaging, and agriculture, ranging from nanoencapsulation of bioactive compounds and nanoemulsions to nanocomposite packaging and nanosensors for food safety monitoring.5–14 Beyond individual applications, a paradigm shift is emerging toward the integration of nanotechnology with digital technologies, including artificial intelligence (AI), Internet of Things (IoT), and precision agriculture platforms. This convergence enables the transition from passive materials to intelligent, responsive systems capable of real-time monitoring and adaptive functionality.8–11 At the same time, increasing attention is being given to sustainability, regulatory compliance, and consumer acceptance, which are critical determinants of large-scale implementation.12,13

Despite rapid scientific progress, the translation of nano-enabled solutions into commercially viable products remains uneven. Challenges related to scalability, safety assessment, regulatory harmonization, and market readiness continue to limit widespread adoption.3,14 Therefore, a comprehensive evaluation that integrates technological advances with market trends and innovation indicators is essential.

This review provides a structured overview of nanotechnology applications across the agri-food value chain, including food systems and agricultural production based on a systematic literature and patent review. The analysis primarily focused on recent publications and data from the last 10 years to capture the latest technological advancements. In addition to summarizing current technologies, an analysis of the market dynamics and patent landscapes outlines emerging trends and innovation trajectories. Particular emphasis is placed on bridging the gap between research and application, highlighting future directions that may enable responsible and scalable implementation of nanotechnology in the agri-food industry.

Application of Nanomaterials in Food Chain

With the increasing world population, global climate change, energy shortages, and loss of cultivable land, new technologies are crucial to increasing and enhancing food production and quality. Nanotechnology nowadays is considered as the effective approach to develop production of the functional food. Indeed, the nanomaterials can be used as the food additives and during the food processing at any level.15 The definition of “nanofood” refers to “food that has been cultivated, produced, processed or packaged using nanotechnology techniques or tools, or to which manufactured nanomaterials have been added”.16 It is limited to the defined category of nanomaterials that are not naturally present in the food products (ie carbohydrates, protein, fats), but nanomaterials which are intentionally manufactured or engineered.14 The applications of nanotechnology in food sector can be summarized in two main groups that are food processing and food packaging.

The most relevant applications include the (nano)encapsulation of bioactive ingredients and nutraceuticals, nano-enhanced food additives, nanobiosensors, and the development of smart and active packaging. Specifically, nanoencapsulation is widely employed to enhance the bioavailability of compounds such as polyphenols, phytosterols, carotenoids, vitamins, and minerals, thereby maximizing the health benefits of fortified functional foods.17,18 The polymeric (Polyglycolic acid, poly(lactic acid), chitosan, and sodium alginate) and lipid-based nanocarriers, namely nanoemulsions, are the most commonly used to encapsulate active ingredients, ensuring precise delivery and stability and as flavor and texture modifiers of foods. Moreover, metals and metal oxides such as iron, silver, titanium dioxide, magnesium, calcium, selenium, and silicates are used to enhance bioavailability, and provide unique benefits such as flavor optimization and improved preservation.12 As for smart and active packaging the antimicrobial coatings based on nanomaterials emerge as a promising trend in food industry, with the use of nanoparticles to extend shelf life and incorporating nanosensors into packaging to detect toxins, pathogens, and pesticides or gases released due to food spoilage, ensuring food.19

In sum, post-harvest processing encompasses nanostructure used as food additives, delivering system of nutrients, antimicrobial agents, while some nanosensor or filler material for improving the shelf-life can be used for packaging system.20 Recent advancements in nanotechnology have also revolutionized agricultural sector, offering novel approaches to increase productivity and crop improvement (Figure 1).

Application of Nanotechnology in Food Manufacturing and Packaging

The main developments in food nanotechnology application have been aimed at altering the texture and sensory attributes of food components, encapsulating nutrients or additives, developing new tastes, controlling the release of flavours, increasing the bioavailability of nutritional components, improving the food stability by adding the nanopreservatives. Among the various nanotechnological strategies applied in food manufacturing, nanoencapsulation represents one of the most extensively investigated approaches (Figure 2).

Mechanism of protecting sensitive nutraceuticals via encapsulation. The pH-responsive and moisture-resistant nanoformulation shields bioactives from thermal, photolytic, and gastrointestinal degradation to achieve targeted intestinal release. This encapsulation approach prevents oxidative degradation and enables controlled release governed by temperature, pH, and specific enzymes. Furthermore, nanomaterial surface modification improves bioavailability through enhanced transport, while mucoadhesive properties prolong intestinal residence time for superior nutrient release. Finally, the system ensures better texture alongside effective taste and odor masking of sensitive bioactives.22–27

In order for the body to utilize a specific bioactive, the compound must be released from the food matrix and absorbed through the epithelial layer in the gastrointestinal tract (GIT).28 Nanoencapsulation can enhance the bioavailability of bioactive compounds after oral administration while providing precise control over their release. It also improves the stability of the bioproduct and prevents degradation during processing.29 There has been growing interest in developing nanoemulsion-based delivery system designed to encapsulate, protect, and deliver non-polar bioactive agents.5 Various components, such as lutein, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamins, which are unstable and sensitive to acidic environment of the stomach, can be delivered after their encapsulation.30–32 An additional advantage of this technology is the improved bioavailability of nutraceutical compounds, achieved through their subcellular size and enhanced cellular uptake, which together result in higher overall drug bioavailability.33–35 However, this enhanced bioavailability raises significant toxicological concerns, as the markedly increased cellular uptake may inadvertently result in elevated systemic exposure and unanticipated tissue accumulation. Consequently, such effects may trigger localized cellular stress responses or cytotoxicity, particularly when the degradation kinetics and biodistribution profile of the nanocarrier remain insufficiently characterized.

Beyond delivery efficiency and stability, nanomaterials may also influence the sensory perception of food products. According to the phenomenon as the size of nanomaterials (NMs) decreases, the surface to volume ratio increases, it suggests that smaller particle can evoke greater intensity than its bulk product.36 Salminen et al reported on nano spray-dried sodium chloride which replace the regular size of NaCl when applied onto the surface of products The authors achieved the reduction of salt content, and simultaneously provide the same saltines taste.37 That technology may be of great importance for various components in human diets which should be sparingly consumed.36 Hoseini et al reported on the effects of free and nano-formulated essential oils on the sensory properties of cheese, which the latter identified as the most preffered option.38 Nevertheless, a critical bottleneck in the widespread adoption of sensory-modifying nanomaterials is their long-term stability within complex food matrices, where unpredictable particle aggregation can negate the benefits of the high surface-to-volume ratio, resulting in inconsistent taste delivery.

Nanoparticles are being often used in the food production to enhance food colour, as the anticaking agent. In addition to organic nanocarriers, inorganic nanoparticles, namely metal and metal oxide-based materials have attracted attention due to their multifunctional roles. Among them, TiO2 and SiO2, are the most implemented as the colouring of the sugar coatings on the doughnuts or as the additive to keep the flow characteristics in powdered products, respectively.15,39 Although, the titanium dioxide has been forbidden as a food additive in EU since August 2022, its use as a component in food packaging material remains permitted.40 Rompelberg et al reported on the intake of TiO2 through several dietary and oral sources in the Dutch population. He found that chewing gum, coffee creamer, milk, and sauces were the main dietary contributors for the intake of TiO2 for the adult, while for the children the toothpaste was the main contributor.41 Metal and metal oxide nanoparticles can be used as a source of functional foods and act as a reservoirs for essential bioactive compounds. Unlike conventional zinc compounds, ZnO nanoparticles (NPs) demonstrate greater solubility under the acidic conditions of the stomach. This increased solubility ensures efficient absorption of zinc ions into the bloodstream, facilitating better bioavailability and utilization by the body.42–44 Potential applications of iron nanoparticles in foods in the bakery and dairy products, or in juices and beverages, include their use as iron fortifiers, and antimicrobial agents.45–47 Supplementation with iron nanoparticles has been reported as a promising approach for the treatment of iron-deficiency anemia.48 Despite these physiological advantages, the application of inorganic nanoparticles remains highly controversial. Comprehensive toxicological assessments have demonstrated that persistent metal oxide nanoparticles may traverse biological barriers, including the blood–brain barrier, while their long-term biopersistence and accumulation within vital organs remain insufficiently investigated. Moreover, the prohibition of TiO2 in the European Union underscores substantial regulatory discrepancies across global markets, thereby generating considerable compliance challenges for international food supply chains.

While many nanotechnological applications focus on modifying food composition, a parallel and rapidly advancing area concerns food packaging systems. The application of nanotechnology to food packaging has been introduced to improve food quality, durability, safety, mechanical and barrier properties. Especially nanocomposites, materials composed of a matrix and nanostructures, play a pivotal role in development of food packaging sector. These nanocomposites use of various nanoparticles as an antimicrobial agent to extend the freshness of food and prevent contamination and spoiled food. The application of hybrid inorganic-organic structure in packaging materials supports the advancement of the packaging industry by enhancing mechanical strength and barrier performance, while enabling the production of more stable and biodegradable alternatives to conventional materials.49 Food packaging materials can also protect the sensitive bioactive components of food from harsh physical and environmental conditions.50,51 Nanotechnology in the food industry provides not only new solutions for improving the taste and quality of food, but also enhances its safety and health benefits. Intelligent or smart packaging use of nanosensors to monitor the condition of packaged food or the environment surrounding the food.52 Barcodes included into that smart material, may monitor, record, sense, track, and indicate the information about food safety, and history during the supply chain.8 From a critical perspective, however, the growing interest in nanostructured packaging must be carefully balanced against potential environmental and health risks. The migration of nanoparticles, such as silver or zinc oxides, from packaging into food may pose direct ingestion hazards to consumers. Moreover, the incorporation of nanomaterials into biodegradable plastics raises end-of-life concerns, as their ecotoxicological effects in soil and aquatic environments remain poorly understood. In addition, public skepticism toward “nano-engineered” foods, combined with the lack of transparent labeling and standardized risk-assessment frameworks, may significantly hinder the commercial adoption of these technologies.

Nanotechnology in Agriculture

While nanotechnology in food manufacturing and packaging primarily targets product quality, stability, and consumer-facing attributes, its application in agriculture focuses more strongly on input efficiency, environmental sustainability, and productivity at the primary production level. It should be mentioned that nanotechnology has significant potential beyond food preservation to impact the agriculture sector, particularly by offering technology-based solutions in crop and livestock production (Table 1). Controlled release of pesticides, herbicides and plant growth regulators can be achieved via the usage of nanocarriers.53,54 Encapsulation of chemicals used as the delivery system lower the dosage of the herbicide simultaneously, without any loss of efficiency and without damage soil and water.54 Likewise, nanofertilizers are applied to improve nutrient uptake efficiency. On the other hand, nanotechnology has also been used to treat water pollution, which is one of the main problems in aquaculture.55

Food Nanotechnology Market Segmentation Overview

Projections indicate that global demand for animal feed will nearly double beyond the year 2050. It is estimated that approximately 1.3 billion metric tons of grain will be required exclusively for livestock nutrition.56,57 The application of nanotechnology-based feed additives in animal husbandry represents a promising field with substantial potential for further development. Peters et al in 2016 reported on the limited number of nanomaterial applications as feed additives, most of which were in the development phase.14 However, the deployment of innovative nanotechnological solutions should be approached with prudence until a comprehensive regulatory and legal framework governing their application in the agri-food sector is established. Concurrently, the challenge of enhancing the nutritional quality of animal feed to ensure adequate food production capable of sustaining the global human population is widely acknowledged. To address this, the European Unions’s Horizon 2020 programme funded a project entitled: Nanostructured carriers for improved cattle feed (NanoFEED), which was focused on development of novel feed additives enhancing the efficacy of cattle production. Indeed, the results of the latest research show that incorporating nanomaterials into animal feed can lead to improved animal growth, better-feed utilization and more efficient nutrient conversion.56,58,59 The integration of NMs within the animal feed industry is not limited to their role as nutritional additives but also includes their emerging applications in development and/or delivery of vaccines and innovative veterinary therapeutics.

Overall, nanotechnology-based solutions are being explored across multiple stages of the food production chain, from primary agricultural inputs to post-harvest processing, packaging, and quality monitoring. However, the nature, maturity, and objectives of these applications differ substantially between food manufacturing and agricultural production systems. Their translation from laboratory-scale research to large-scale industrial and commercial implementation remains uneven. Many nano-enabled agri-food technologies remain expensive to manufacture and difficult to scale industrially. High production costs may limit accessibility for small and medium-sized agricultural producers, particularly in developing economies.3 The degree of technological maturity, regulatory acceptance, and economic feasibility varies considerably across application domains. These factors are reflected not only in scientific output but also in market dynamics and innovation indicators, which are examined in the following section.

Innovation and Commercial Landscape of Agri-Food Nanotechnology

Market Analysis

The global food nanotechnology market is projected to expand at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 11.6%, reaching an estimated value of USD 49.1 billion by 2030, compared with approximately USD 22.6 billion in 2024, according to internal analysis.21 A principal driver of growth in the global food nanotechnology market is the increasing adoption of advanced nanotechnological tools aimed at enhancing agricultural productivity, enabling the development of smart food- packaging system, supporting the production of precision nutrition and functional food products, and addressing the rising food demand associated with rapid population growth. On the other hand, there is a lack adequately trained laboratory professionals, especially in developing countries, as those technology demands precise analytical capabilities and careful interpretation to ensure reliable outcomes.60

The food nanotechnology market encompasses diverse applications and materials, but is commonly segmented by technology type, application area, end-user group, and geography (Table 1). These categories provide a structured view of technological maturation and emerging commercial opportunities.

In parallel with market growth, innovation in agri-food nanotechnology is increasingly reflected in industrial property activity, which provides additional insight into technological focus areas, maturity levels, and commercialization strategies. A patent landscape analysis was therefore conducted to complement the scientific and market perspectives discussed above.

Patent Landscape in Nanofood Technologies

A patent analysis was conducted focused on patent documents retrieved from the Espacenet database (European Patent Office), covering inventions developed over the last decades that address nanotechnology applications in agriculture and food-related systems, including nanoencapsulation, nanocomposites, and nanostructured delivery systems. The first query targeted titles containing the term “nano” combined with International Patent Classification (IPC) codes related to agriculture and food systems (IPC = A01 AND IPC = A23), The final categorization included the domains of agriculture (A01), including nanopesticides, nano-sized feed additives, and plant protection-related systems; food manufacturing and processing (A23), including nanoencapsulation and delivery of functional/bioactive ingredients, nanocomposite and smart monitoring systems, identified through additional IPC/CPC classes (eg, B82Y) associated with active and intelligent packaging materials and nanocomposites; and sensors/smart systems regarding the application of nanosensores in quality, safety, and environmental monitoring.

This complement the scientific literature review and allows the identification of technological trends, innovation strategies related to the use of nanoparticles in the agri-food sector. The analysis Indeed, patent analysis is a pivotal tool for the evaluation of trends and advancements in specific research fields, supporting the identification of opportunities that could be used by the industry as well as researchers, in order to adjust investment strategies, improve and potentially commercialize the corresponding patented applications.61

The temporal distribution of patent families indicates a progressive increase in filings, with a more pronounced growth phase observed from the mid-2010s. This reflects the broader expansion of applied nanotechnology and the increasing scientific, industrial, and policy-driven interest in sustainable agriculture, food security, and advanced food processing technologies. Nonetheless, the recent stabilization in the number of newly filed patent families suggests a possible transition from an exploratory phase dominated by technological proof-of-concept developments towards a stage characterized by incremental optimization, consolidation, and increasing regulatory scrutiny.62,63 Representative patent families illustrating the main application domains, innovation maturity levels, and associated regulatory challenges are summarized in Table 2.

Among the analyzed patent families, IPC A01-related inventions represented a substantial proportion of the dataset, with a marked increase observed after 2010s’, reflecting growing interest in sustainability-oriented agricultural inputs. Within this category, the dominant technological focus involved nanopesticides and nanoherbicides designed to enhance bioavailability, reducing active ingredient dosage, and improving targeted delivery efficiency. For example, CN118177113A, exemplify this trend by focusing on high-efficiency formulations with enhanced leaf adhesion. Furthermore, the scope of A01 patents in the dataset extends to livestock-realted applications, as seen in CN111357879A, which covers nano-sized fermented additives designed to improve gut health in animal production. Additionally, controlled release nanofertilizers and antimicrobial nanoparticles were also recurrent innovations, and mainly dedicated to the introduction of new formulations enabling controlled or stimuli-responsive nutrient release, as well as plant protection systems based on nanocarriers for biocontrol agents (DE102012000707A1). Overall, the predominance of efficiency-enhancing technologies suggests that innovation in agricultural nanotechnology is largely driven by sustainability goals, regulatory requirements and increasing pressure to reduce agrochemical burden while maintaining productivity.64 In contrast, patents classified under IPC A23 predominantly address nanoencapsulation strategies. Representative examples include CN116616428A, describing nano-complexes of selenium and lycopene with enhanced antioxidant stability and bioaccessibility. Other A23-related inventions focus on functional food ingredients, where nanoparticles are used to modulate texture, solubility, or release kinetics, as well as food preservation technologies that incorporate antimicrobial nanoparticles into coatings or packaging systems. The findings indicate a shift in the application of nanotechnology in the food sector. Inventions like CN118182761A further demonstrate the use of polymeric systems to protect volatile flavors, reflecting a move towards sophisticated nutrient carrier systems. Thus, delivery systems innovations rather than novel active substances indicates a technological shift toward performance optimization and value-added food products rather than purely processing-related applications, consistent with recent developments in food nanotechnology.19

A third technological subset of patent families are related to nanocomposite materials and are often classified under additional IPC/CPC codes related to materials science and nanotechnology (eg, B82Y). This subset includes active and intelligent packaging solutions that incorporate nanoparticles with antimicrobial or barrier properties, as demonstrated in US11612179B2 (reinforced food contact materials) and US2011123589A1 (metallic-polysaccharidic antimicrobial composites), as well as nanosensors integrated into packaging or agricultural systems, exemplified by CN118182759A, which introduces nanomaterial-based electrochemical sensors for real-time monitoring of food freshness. The increasing trend of new patents in the last decade suggests a growing convergence between nanotechnology, digitalization, and food safety systems, supporting the emergence of smart agri-food technologies. Furthermore, an analysis of the applicant base shows that innovation in this field is driven by a diverse array of stakeholders, including industrial companies, academic institutions and public research organizations. A higher patenting activity is observed for entities based in Asia, particularly China, reflecting strong governmental support for applied nanotechnology and strategic patenting policies. This is evidenced by the high volume of filings from institutions like Jiangnan University and Liaoning A Plus Feed Co., which dominate the recent patent landscape in the analyzed dataset. In contrast, patenting activity in the United States and Europe appears to be more selective and is often aligned with clearer regulatory pathways and commercialization prospects. This geographical distribution suggests the coexistence of different innovation models, ranging from volume-driven patenting strategies in emerging economies to market-oriented intellectual property protection in more established regulatory environments.

Overall, the patent landscape reveals a predominance of incremental innovation, with a focus on formulation strategies, encapsulation efficiency, or delivery performance over the development of fundamentally new materials. Furthermore, although patent claims frequently emphasize enhanced efficacy and functionality, relatively limited information is provided regarding industrial scalability, environmental fate, or life-cycle assessment. As outlined in analogous patent landscape analyses, this discrepancy underscores the challenges in translating patented nanotechnologies into commercially viable and socially acceptable products.20,62 Ultimately, the effective and successful commercialization will likely depend not only on technical performance but also on regulatory acceptance, safety validation, and consumer trust.

Emergent Solutions and Future Outlook

Emerging nano-enabled agri-food systems are increasingly shaped by the convergence of nanotechnology with digital infrastructures, sustainability requirements, and translational constraints. A key direction is the integration of nanosensors with Internet of Things (IoT) platforms and artificial intelligence (AI), enabling real-time monitoring of food quality, environmental conditions, and agricultural inputs, and supporting predictive decision-making across supply chains.8,52 Simultaneously, next-generation systems are evolving toward smart and stimuli-responsive nanomaterials capable of adapting to environmental triggers such as pH, temperature, or microbial activity.5,17 Sustainability considerations are increasingly embedded at the design stage, with growing emphasis on bio-based and biodegradable nanomaterials, green synthesis approaches, and circular economy integration, particularly in packaging applications.12,49 In parallel, nanotechnology is advancing precision agriculture through targeted delivery systems that enhance nutrient use efficiency and reduce environmental losses, with future developments expected to integrate sensing and delivery into closed-loop systems.54,64 Despite these advances, a significant translational gap persists. Many nano-enabled solutions remain at intermediate Technology Readiness Levels (TRL 3–6), constrained by scalability, cost, regulatory uncertainty, and limited long-term safety data.14,63 Indeed, Hoffman et al identified key opportunities for engineered nanomaterials in agriculture by evaluating their efficacy and Technology Readiness Level (Figure 3).65 TRL scores progressed from limited laboratory tests on model crops up to multi-species greenhouse and field scales, with commercial solutions receiving the highest rating. Their findings highlight that while laboratory proof-of-concepts (TRL 3–4) are abundant, scaling up to relevant environments (TRL 5–6) and achieving full commercialization (TRL 7–9) remain bottlenecked by regulatory frameworks and high costs. Regulatory harmonization and the implementation of safety-by-design approaches will therefore be critical for commercialization.

Nanotechnology ensures precise delivery of bio-stimulating microorganisms to optimize plant health and fix deficiencies. Seed nanocoating provides high-tech micro-shields that protect, nourish, and boost germination performance. Based on nanotechnology, various genetic engineering systems are also supporting agriculture. Nanofertilizers provide targeted nutrient delivery to correct plant deficiencies more efficiently, while nanosensors are developed to sense and analyze different parameters such as environmental and biomolecular at the nanoscale. Nanopesticides utilize nanomaterials either directly as active ingredients or as smart carriers to encapsulate them. Nano-based soil conditioners can help and eradicate micronutrient deficiency. Nanomaterials improve both cold and heat stress tolerance by stabilizing cell structures, enhancing antifreeze mechanisms, and boosting antioxidant activity. Nano-agrochemicals, such as nanofungicides, are increasingly utilized to prevent plant diseases. Furthermore, nano-based technologies offer cost-effective, economically feasible solutions for water conservation, decontamination, and agricultural bioremediation to prevent the chemical pollution of groundwater systems.66–74

Market and patent trends indicate a transition from exploratory research toward incremental, application-driven innovation, particularly in smart packaging, functional foods, and precision agriculture. Regional differences in innovation strategies persist, with Asia leading in patent activity and Europe and North America emphasizing regulatory alignment and market readiness.

Conclusion

This review highlights the expanding role of nanotechnology across the agri-food chain, from primary agricultural inputs to food processing, packaging, and quality and safety monitoring systems. While significant progress has been made in nanoencapsulation, nanocomposites, and nanosensors, the level of technological maturity and market readiness varies considerably among application domains.

The combined analysis of literature review, market and patent data provides a comprehensive delineation of innovation dynamics in the agri-food nanotechnology sector. Patent trends indicate a predominance of incremental formulation-based innovations, aligning with market growth driven by functional foods, active packaging, and sustainability-oriented agricultural solutions.

Despite promising technological and commercial indicators, the large-scale deployment of nanotechnology-based solutions remains constrained by regulatory uncertainty, limited long-term safety data, scalability challenges, and consumer perception issues, particularly in food-related applications.

Future research and innovation efforts should prioritize harmonized risk assessment frameworks, standardized characterization methods, and life-cycle-based evaluations, alongside the development of scalable, safe-by-design nanomaterials. The convergence of nanotechnology with digitalization, precision agriculture, and smart food systems is expected to play a key role in translating scientific advances into socially accepted and commercially viable solutions.
