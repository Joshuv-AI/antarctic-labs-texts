Clinical risk-aware reinforcement learning for latency-constrained healthcare IoT scheduling
Authors: Jawaher Abdullah Bin Jumah, Hyder Osman Mirghani, Saad Alateeq, Muidh Awadh Algahtani, Bushra Yousef Althubyani, Naif Alsaadi, Aisha Abdallah, Malik Bader Alazzam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid growth of the Healthcare Internet of Things (HIoT) has enabled continuous, real-time patient monitoring through wearable and bedside devices. These systems generate time-sensitive physiological data that are essential for the early detection of critical conditions such as arrhythmias and hypoxia. However, conventional cloud-centric architectures introduce significant end-to-end latency, which can compromise timely clinical response in safety-critical scenarios. Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) mitigates this limitation by bringing computation closer to data sources; yet, existing scheduling approaches remain largely system-centric and do not adequately incorporate patient-specific clinical risk into their decision-making. To address this gap, this paper presents CRAI-LCS, a clinical risk-aware Reinforcement learning (RL) framework for latency-constrained scheduling in HIoT systems. Unlike prior simulation-driven studies, CRAI-LCS integrates real physiological data from the PhysioNet MIT-BIH Arrhythmia database to construct realistic, data-driven workloads. Specifically, electrocardiogram (ECG) signals are segmented into time-windowed tasks with clinically grounded characteristics, including input size, computational demand, and urgency-aware deadlines. The framework combines data-driven clinical risk estimation, deadline-violation prediction, and RL-based scheduling to dynamically prioritize high-risk tasks while efficiently managing system resources. Experimental results demonstrate that CRAI-LCS consistently outperforms baseline approaches in terms of latency, deadline compliance, and resource utilization under realistic workload conditions. Ablation studies further confirm the individual contributions of the clinical risk-awareness and predictive scheduling components. Overall, these findings highlight the importance of incorporating real physiological data into scheduling design, providing a more reliable and clinically relevant foundation for next-generation healthcare edge intelligence systems.

Introduction

The rapid evolution of Internet of Things (IoT) and edge intelligence has significantly transformed healthcare systems by enabling continuous monitoring, real-time data acquisition, and intelligent clinical services. Healthcare IoT platforms generate heterogeneous physiological information from wearable sensors, medical devices, and remote monitoring infrastructures, requiring reliable computational mechanisms for timely analysis and decision-making. Mobile Edge Computing (MEC) has emerged as an effective paradigm for addressing the latency and scalability limitations of cloud-centric healthcare architectures by bringing computation closer to data sources and improving responsiveness in delay-sensitive applications1. However, healthcare environments require not only efficient computation but also secure, reliable, and adaptive intelligence for managing sensitive medical data. Federated learning approaches have been investigated to preserve privacy while enabling collaborative intelligence across distributed IoT healthcare devices2, whereas security analyses of MEC-enabled 5G applications have highlighted vulnerabilities that must be considered in practical deployments3.

Recent advances in healthcare intelligence have further emphasized the integration of advanced technologies such as blockchain and artificial intelligence to enhance trust, interoperability, and data-driven decision-making4. Nevertheless, efficient task scheduling in healthcare edge environments remains challenging due to dynamic workloads, limited resources, strict latency requirements, and heterogeneous clinical priorities. Existing scheduling strategies mainly optimize computational objectives, while healthcare tasks may have significantly different levels of clinical importance despite similar resource requirements. Recent adaptive scheduling approaches for fog-cloud IoT systems demonstrate improvements in resource utilization and optimization performance5, while risk-aware scheduling mechanisms have shown the importance of incorporating task urgency and deadline constraints in heterogeneous computing platforms6.

Artificial intelligence-based healthcare frameworks have demonstrated promising capabilities for extracting meaningful clinical information from medical data. For example, learning-driven healthcare systems have employed federated learning and deep neural networks for medical prediction and diagnosis7, while biomedical data analysis approaches have highlighted the importance of intelligent processing for extracting valuable health-related knowledge8. However, these approaches mainly focus on prediction and analytics rather than integrating clinical risk information into real-time computational scheduling decisions.

Reinforcement learning (RL) has recently attracted considerable attention for adaptive resource management in dynamic edge-cloud environments. Advanced RL strategies have improved decision-making efficiency by learning optimal policies under uncertain conditions and limited observations9. Moreover, sustainable fog-cloud infrastructures have investigated intelligent resource management and secure operation in industrial environments10. In healthcare scenarios, intelligent scheduling frameworks have explored fairness-aware hospital resource allocation11 and secure healthcare architectures combining lightweight intelligence with blockchain mechanisms12. However, existing solutions generally do not establish a closed-loop relationship between patient physiological conditions, clinical risk estimation, workload generation, and edge scheduling decisions.

This limitation becomes more critical in real-time healthcare applications, where delayed processing of high-risk physiological events may directly affect clinical outcomes. Medical service planning and remote healthcare optimization further demonstrate the need for intelligent resource coordination under geographically distributed conditions13, while real-world RL studies emphasize challenges related to adaptability, constraints, and sample efficiency in practical decision-making systems14. Although mobile health task offloading strategies based on deep reinforcement learning have been investigated15,16, they commonly prioritize system-level metrics rather than patient-specific urgency. Furthermore, emerging healthcare delivery models require adaptive computational support for remote and resource-limited regions17, and future MEC-enabled 6G networks introduce additional mobility and orchestration challenges for latency-sensitive services18. Data-driven intelligent frameworks also demonstrate the potential of advanced learning models for extracting reliable information from limited and complex datasets19.

Motivated by these challenges, this study proposes CRAI-LCS, a clinical risk-aware RL framework that establishes a patient-centered scheduling paradigm for healthcare IoT edge environments. Unlike conventional approaches that separately employ risk estimation and scheduling optimization, CRAI-LCS integrates clinical risk, predicted deadline-violation probability, ECG-derived workload characteristics, and dynamic edge–cloud states directly into the RL decision process. In this formulation, clinical risk is not treated as an external classification output or a predefined priority label; rather, it becomes an intrinsic decision variable within the Markov Decision Process (MDP), enabling the scheduler to adapt resource allocation according to both patient condition and system dynamics. The key motivation behind CRAI-LCS is to bridge the gap between clinical intelligence and computational resource management. By utilizing authentic physiological workloads derived from ECG signals rather than purely synthetic task models, the proposed framework enables scheduling decisions that jointly consider computational requirements and medical urgency. This risk-conditioned formulation advances healthcare IoT scheduling beyond infrastructure-centric optimization toward patient-centric intelligent resource management, where high-risk medical events receive adaptive priority while latency constraints and efficient resource utilization are maintained. Related work

The rapid proliferation of IoT devices and latency-sensitive healthcare applications has intensified research on edge computing paradigms capable of delivering timely, reliable, and intelligent services. The existing literature can be broadly categorized into four interrelated research streams: (i) foundational principles and orchestration of edge computing for delay-sensitive IoT applications, (ii) latency-constrained and deadline-aware scheduling in fog–cloud environments, (iii) RL–based resource allocation and task offloading, and (iv) patient-centric, risk-aware healthcare scheduling frameworks. A systematic examination of these research streams reveals the absence of a unified framework that jointly integrates clinical risk awareness with latency-constrained adaptive scheduling.

Edge computing foundations and orchestration for delay-sensitive IoT

Edge computing has become a fundamental paradigm for supporting delay-sensitive IoT applications by reducing communication latency, enabling distributed processing, and overcoming the limitations of centralized cloud infrastructures. The evolution of edge computing architectures and their ability to satisfy stringent IoT requirements have been extensively investigated, highlighting challenges related to resource management, scalability, and efficient computation placement in heterogeneous environments20. However, most existing edge computing solutions primarily optimize infrastructure-level objectives and do not consider application-specific requirements, particularly in safety-critical healthcare scenarios.

Recent developments in AI-driven healthcare ecosystems have further emphasized the importance of integrating edge intelligence with medical applications. The growing collaboration among artificial intelligence, medical devices, and healthcare infrastructures demonstrates the need for intelligent computational frameworks capable of supporting reliable and timely medical services21. Nevertheless, effective orchestration of healthcare workloads remains challenging due to diverse task characteristics, dynamic resource availability, and varying levels of clinical importance.

Resource scheduling in edge environments has been systematically studied through architectural models, taxonomies, and optimization strategies addressing task allocation, resource provisioning, and workload balancing22. Although these approaches provide valuable insights into edge resource orchestration, they generally focus on computational efficiency rather than incorporating clinical urgency or risk-aware prioritization. In healthcare applications, medical tasks with similar computational requirements may have significantly different consequences when delayed.

Recent medical AI approaches, such as lightweight deep learning models for precise lesion detection, demonstrate the potential of edge-enabled intelligence for real-time healthcare analysis23. Furthermore, real-time medical signal processing frameworks highlight the necessity of reliable and efficient inference mechanisms for continuous physiological monitoring24. Beyond healthcare-specific applications, collaborative edge inference strategies have also investigated efficient distribution of AI workloads across edge resources to reduce latency and improve scalability25. However, existing studies still lack a unified framework that integrates clinical risk estimation with adaptive edge scheduling decisions. Therefore, developing risk-aware reinforcement learning-based scheduling mechanisms remains essential for next-generation healthcare IoT systems.

Latency-constrained and deadline-sensitive scheduling

Adherence to stringent deadlines in fog–cloud environments is essential for healthcare monitoring and emergency response systems. An energy-efficient resource allocation technique that provides latency guarantees is introduced in26, employing Lyapunov optimization to minimize power consumption while satisfying delay constraints. Despite achieving ultra-reliable low-latency performance, the resulting scheduling policies remain indifferent to task-level clinical risk. A bag-of-tasks scheduling framework for deadline-sensitive IoT applications in fog–cloud environments is presented in27. The system promptly redirects tasks to the cloud when local deadlines are unachievable, thereby preventing deadline violations. Nonetheless, deadlines are applied uniformly to all tasks, failing to distinguish between high-risk clinical events and routine monitoring data.

These studies demonstrate significant advances in deadline enforcement and latency-aware optimization. Nevertheless, none of them integrates risk classification, predictive violation modeling, or adaptive learning into healthcare IoT scheduling.

Reinforcement learning-based resource allocation and offloading

Reinforcement learning has been extensively employed to manage dynamic and stochastic edge environments. A multi-objective DRL framework for mobile edge computing is proposed in28, simultaneously minimizing delay and energy consumption without explicit preference weights. Although Pareto efficiency is improved, task significance remains at the system level rather than reflecting application-specific criticality. Hierarchical (DRL) for integrated service caching and compute offloading in edge–cloud systems is presented in29, decomposing caching and offloading decisions into cooperative agents. This approach significantly reduces average latency but does not provide differentiated latency guarantees for safety-critical tasks. The study in30 investigates adaptive RL-based task offloading in vehicular edge computing, focusing on mobility and fluctuating network conditions. In31, an energy–delay-aware microservice deployment and routing optimization approach is introduced, employing RL and dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) to achieve a trade-off between delay and energy consumption in edge microservices. The study in32 further examines DRL-based resource orchestration in multi-edge vehicular environments, aiming to minimize processing costs through advanced policy gradient techniques.

Risk-aware and patient-centric frameworks

Beyond system-level optimization, recent efforts have emphasized risk awareness and patient-centric design. A patient-centric healthcare system integrating blockchain, cloud computing, and IoT for semantic interoperability is proposed in33, emphasizing secure data sharing and a layered architectural design. Although interoperability and security are enhanced, real-time scheduling under latency constraints remains unaddressed.

A risk-aware, privacy-preserving federated learning system for remote patient monitoring is introduced in34, incorporating adaptive security policies conditioned on patient state. The system dynamically adjusts privacy and robustness parameters, demonstrating enhanced resilience under adversarial conditions. Nonetheless, scheduling and resource allocation are not jointly optimized with clinical risk under stringent timing constraints.

The study in35 investigates secure explainable AI frameworks for risk-sensitive healthcare analytics, employing swarm-based Quality of Service (QoS) control and incorporating layered security, transparency, and adaptive QoS management within cloud-native environments. Despite addressing risk-sensitive analytics and QoS adaptation, the integration of RL for real-time edge scheduling remains largely unexplored.

Research gap and positioning of this work

The reviewed studies demonstrate substantial progress in edge computing, deadline-aware scheduling, RL-based resource management, and healthcare-oriented intelligent systems. However, existing research remains fragmented across these domains. Edge computing and scheduling approaches primarily focus on reducing latency, improving energy efficiency, and optimizing resource utilization, while the clinical significance of healthcare tasks is generally represented through predefined priorities or application-level constraints. Consequently, these methods may treat heterogeneous medical workloads with similar computational characteristics but substantially different clinical consequences in the same manner.

Recent healthcare-oriented frameworks have improved patient awareness and risk management; however, clinical risk is often considered separately from the computational decision-making process. In particular, most existing approaches do not establish a direct relationship between physiological conditions, workload generation, and adaptive scheduling decisions. Furthermore, many evaluation frameworks rely on generic or synthetic workloads, limiting their ability to represent realistic healthcare IoT scenarios where patient conditions dynamically influence task urgency.

CRAI-LCS addresses this research gap by introducing a clinical-to-computational decision paradigm that integrates physiological risk estimation, ECG-derived workload modeling, and RL-based scheduling within a unified framework. Unlike conventional approaches that optimize infrastructure-level objectives independently of patient conditions, CRAI-LCS incorporates clinical risk as an intrinsic decision variable within the scheduling process. This enables adaptive resource allocation that simultaneously considers patient urgency, deadline constraints, and dynamic edge resource availability.

System architecture and proposed framework

This section presents the comprehensive framework of the proposed Clinical Risk-Aware AI-Enhanced Latency-Constrained Scheduling (CRAI-LCS) system for real-time healthcare IoT environments. The primary objective is to enable low-latency, reliable task execution while integrating clinically significant information into scheduling decisions. In healthcare IoT environments such as continuous patient monitoring, the timely processing of physiological data is essential for the early detection of critical conditions. Current scheduling methodologies predominantly optimize system-level metrics such as latency and resource utilization, frequently overlooking patient-specific clinical urgency. The proposed system addresses this limitation by incorporating data-driven clinical risk assessment into the scheduling process.

The CRAI-LCS system operates within a hierarchical edge–cloud architecture and integrates three principal components: (i) a CNN–LSTM-based clinical risk assessment module, (ii) a deadline-violation prediction module, and (iii) a RL–driven scheduling module. These components collectively enable adaptive, risk-sensitive, and latency-aware task allocation in dynamic system environments.

System architecture

Figure 2 illustrates the overall architecture of the CRAI-LCS framework, which follows a hierarchical edge–cloud architecture composed of three main layers: the IoT sensing layer, edge computing layer, and AI-enhanced cloud layer. The objective of this architecture is to establish a direct clinical-to-computational decision pathway by connecting physiological information, healthcare workload generation, and adaptive scheduling decisions.

The IoT sensing layer consists of wearable devices and medical sensors that continuously acquire physiological signals, including ECG measurements and other patient-related information. The collected signals are transmitted to the edge gateway, where preprocessing and feature extraction operations are performed. Instead of directly treating sensed data as generic computational requests, CRAI-LCS transforms physiological information into clinically meaningful healthcare tasks characterized by computational requirements, latency constraints, and patient-specific urgency.

At the edge layer, the extracted ECG features are processed by the CNN-LSTM-based clinical risk estimation module to generate a dynamic clinical risk score. This score represents the severity level associated with each healthcare event and provides patient-specific urgency information for subsequent scheduling decisions. The estimated clinical risk is then integrated with ECG-derived workload characteristics to construct a risk-aware task representation. Specifically, each generated healthcare task is described by four main attributes: task size, which indicates the amount of data requiring processing; computational demand, which represents the required processing resources; deadline constraint, which defines the maximum acceptable response time for task execution; and clinical risk score, which reflects the urgency level estimated from physiological information. Therefore, the workload generation process in CRAI-LCS does not rely on purely synthetic tasks; instead, it preserves the relationship between patient condition and computational workload characteristics.

The generated risk-aware task representation is combined with dynamic edge–cloud system states to construct the RL state space. These states include queue conditions, available computational resources, network delay, and current workload conditions. The risk-guided RL scheduler utilizes this integrated state representation to determine appropriate execution decisions, including local processing or cloud offloading. Consequently, the scheduler does not treat clinical risk as an external classification output; rather, it incorporates the estimated risk information as an intrinsic decision factor during resource allocation. The scheduling policy is continuously improved through feedback signals derived from latency, deadline violations, resource utilization, and task completion outcomes.

The cloud layer provides additional computational resources and hosts predictive intelligence modules, including latency estimation and deadline violation prediction mechanisms, which support proactive scheduling decisions under dynamic workload conditions. Through this integrated architecture, CRAI-LCS establishes a closed-loop clinical-to-computational framework that connects physiological monitoring, ECG-derived workload generation, clinical risk assessment, and adaptive RL-based scheduling.

Reinforcement learning framework

The dynamic and stochastic characteristics of real-time healthcare IoT systems render static scheduling policies inadequate for ensuring continuous optimal performance. Task arrivals are erratic, network conditions vary, and clinical risk levels may alter suddenly. The proposed methodology tackles these problems by modeling the scheduling process as a sequential decision-making problem and employing a RL technique to develop an adaptive, risk-aware, and latency-constrained scheduling policy.

The scheduling environment is represented as a MDP, characterized by the tuple (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:S,\:A,\:P,\:R$$\end{document}). At each decision epoch t, the scheduler observes the system state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{t}\in\:\:S$$\end{document} and selects an action \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{a}_{t}\in\:\:A$$\end{document}. Subsequently, the environment changes to a new state \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{\mathrm{t}+1}$$\end{document} based on the transition probability P, and a reward \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{t}$$\end{document} is obtained. State Representation: The system state must encompass both computational conditions and clinical urgency. The state vector at time t is defined as follows:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{s}_{t}=\:\left\{\:{Q}_{t},\:{U}_{t},\:{R}_{i},\:{P}_{miss,i},\:{D}_{i}\right\}$$\end{document}

where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Q}_{t}$$\end{document} signifies the present queue lengths at edge nodes, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{t}$$\end{document} indicates the resource utilization levels of edge servers, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{i}$$\end{document} refers to the clinical risk score of the incoming task, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{miss,i}\:$$\end{document}represents the anticipated likelihood of deadline violation, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{D}_{i}$$\end{document} defines the latency constraint linked to the task. This approach allows the learning agent to concurrently evaluate system congestion, resource availability, and medical urgency.

Integrating clinical risk directly into the state space enables the scheduler to respond to patient severity instead of depending on fixed binary priority labels. Action Space: For each incoming task, the agent chooses an execution site among the available processing nodes. The action space is hence delineated as:28\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ a_{t} ~ \in ~\left\{ {E_{1} ,~E_{2} ,~ \ldots ,~E_{M} ,~C} \right\} $$\end{document}

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{E}_{j}$$\end{document} denotes an edge node and C represents the cloud server. The action dictates whether the task is executed locally at a designated edge node or transferred to the cloud. This approach enables the agent to dynamically optimize latency reduction and resource efficiency.

incentive Design: To maintain alignment with the unified optimization target outlined in Section “Problem formulation”, the immediate incentive is defined as the negative instantaneous cost:29\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{t}=\:-\:\left(\:\alpha\:\:{L}_{i}+\:\beta\:\:{P}_{mis{s}_{i}}-\:\gamma\:\:{R}_{i}\right)+\:\eta\:{U}_{edge}$$\end{document}

In this formulation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{i}$$\end{document} defines the actual delay of the chosen action, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{miss,i}\:$$\end{document}signifies the anticipated chance of deadline infringement, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{R}_{i}$$\end{document} represents the clinical risk score, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{U}_{edge}$$\end{document} indicates system-wide resource consumption. The weighting parameters alpha, beta, gamma, and eta govern the balance of latency reduction, violation avoidance, risk prioritization, and efficiency.

This reward framework guarantees that high-risk jobs yield greater rewards when executed with low latency, whereas actions resulting in deadline breaches incur penalties. Consequently, the acquired policy inherently emphasizes clinically essential tasks amid limited resources.

Learning algorithm: Utilizing a discrete action space and continuous state data, a Deep Q-Network (DQN) architecture is implemented to approximate the optimal action-value function Q(s,a). The neural network characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} predicts the anticipated cumulative discounted reward. 30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Q\left({s}_{t},\:{a}_{t};\:\theta\:\right)\approx\:\mathbb{\:}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{\:}\left[\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty\:}{\lambda\:}^{k}{R}_{t+k}\:\right]$$\end{document}

where the target value is defined as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{y}_{t}=\:{R}_{t}+\:\lambda\:\underset{{a}^{{\prime\:}}}{\mathrm{max}}Q\left({s}_{t+1},\:{a}^{{\prime\:}};\:{\theta\:}^{-}\right)$$\end{document}

and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{-}$$\end{document} denotes the target network parameters used to stabilize training. Experience replay is employed to reduce correlation between consecutive samples and improve convergence stability.

Policy optimality: The optimal scheduling policy is obtained by maximizing the expected cumulative discounted reward. 32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\pi\:}^{*}=\:arg\underset{\pi\:}{\mathrm{max}}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{\:}\left[\sum\limits_{t=0}^{\infty\:}{\lambda\:}^{t}{R}_{t}\right]$$\end{document}

Since the reward function corresponds with the unified purpose outlined in Section “Problem formulation”, maximizing long-term reward equates to minimizing latency, decreasing violation probability, prioritizing clinical risk, and enhancing resource usage over time. The proposed RL-based scheduler adapts to variations in workload, network dynamics, and patient risk through ongoing interaction with the environment, facilitating robust and clinically informed real-time decision-making.

Formal constraint definition: The scheduling decision is subject to the following system constraints. 33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C1:\:{L}_{i,j}\le\:{D}_{i},\forall\:i,j$$\end{document}34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C2:\:Loa{d}_{j}+{S}_{i}\le\:Capacit{y}_{j},\forall\:j$$\end{document}35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C3:i\in\:T\sum\limits_{i\in\:\mathcal{T}}{E}_{i}\le\:{E}_{max}$$\end{document}36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C4:\:{R}_{i}^{served}\ge\:\forall\:i,{R}_{i}^{served}\in\:{T}_{critical}$$\end{document}

This study approximates the scheduling strategy via a Deep Q-Network (DQN), with the action-value function depicted by a fully connected neural network. Here, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\theta\:}^{-}$$\end{document} denote the online and target network parameters, respectively.

Integrated RL-based scheduling and system implementation

The proposed CRAI-LCS framework integrates clinical risk assessment, deadline-violation prediction, and RL–driven scheduling into a unified decision-making process. In contrast to traditional methods that employ separate rule-based modules for priority assignment, load balancing, and resource allocation, the proposed approach incorporates all decision factors into the state representation and reward formulation of the RL agent, as detailed in Section “Reinforcement learning framework”. Specifically, clinical risk, task urgency, system load, and resource availability are encoded within the system state, enabling the agent to implicitly learn strategies for dynamic priority adjustment and adaptive resource allocation. Task urgency is determined by the clinical risk score and deadline constraints, while system congestion and resource consumption are assessed through queue length and utilization metrics. Moreover, predictive indicators such as the probability of deadline violations are incorporated into the state space, enabling the agent to anticipate infeasible scheduling decisions during high-load scenarios.

Rather than relying on predefined heuristic rules, the proposed method allows the scheduling policy to evolve through interaction with the environment. The overall scheduling workflow is summarized in Algorithm 1. At each decision step, the agent observes the current system state, selects an action using an exploration–exploitation strategy, executes the scheduling decision, and updates its policy based on the observed system feedback. This mechanism enables continuous adaptation to dynamic workloads and varying resource conditions. Energy-aware behavior and load balancing are also implicitly learned through the reward structure. By imposing penalties for inefficient resource usage and excessive latency while rewarding the timely completion of high-risk tasks, the agent learns to balance scheduling performance and resource efficiency. Consequently, strategies such as dynamic priority adjustment, proactive load balancing, and energy optimization emerge naturally from the RL process rather than operating as separate, hand-crafted modules.

Computational complexity analysis

The computational complexity of the proposed CRAI-LCS framework is mostly determined by the RL-based scheduling approach outlined in Section “Reinforcement learning framework”. At each decision point, the agent assesses the system status and chooses an action that assigns a task to an accessible processing node.

Let N represent the quantity of tasks and M signify the number of edge nodes. The system assesses probable execution of each incoming job across prospective nodes to predict latency and feasibility. This yields a computational expense commensurate with:38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{allocation}=O(N\times\:M)$$\end{document}

which corresponds to the task-to-resource evaluation process.

Besides the scheduling choice, the RL element incurs extra processing overhead related to neural network updates. Let W represent the quantity of trainable parameters in the Q-network and B the mini-batch size utilized during training. The intricacy of each learning update can be articulated as:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{learning}=O(B\times\:W)$$\end{document}

Given that W is constant once the network design is established and does not vary with the number of tasks or edge nodes, the primary consideration in large-scale contexts is the task-resource assessment.

Consequently, the total computational complexity of the CRAI-LCS framework for each scheduling horizon can be estimated as:40\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{total}=O(N\times\:M+B\times\:W)$$\end{document}

Results and discussions

To thoroughly evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed CRAI-LCS architecture, a hybrid data-driven edge–cloud simulation environment was established, combining real physiological signals with system-level modeling. In contrast to traditional studies that rely exclusively on synthetic workload generation, this study integrates ECG-derived tasks to simulate realistic healthcare monitoring scenarios.

The experimental testbed models a modern hospital IoT ecosystem comprising diverse medical sensors (both wearable and bedside devices), distributed edge computing nodes, and cloud infrastructure. Edge nodes operate as intelligent gateways that perform local inference, clinical risk assessment via the CNN–LSTM model, and initial task scheduling, whereas the cloud layer manages overflow workloads and computationally intensive tasks. The system setup includes 200 IoT devices, 25 edge nodes with heterogeneous processing capabilities, and 2 high-performance cloud instances. Task arrivals follow a stochastic process with rates ranging from 8 to 25 tasks per second, reflecting both normal operational conditions and peak demand scenarios encountered in real-world healthcare settings.

Unlike previous simulation-based approaches, task characteristics are derived from real ECG data, as described in Section “Problem formulation”. Input data volumes are determined by signal segmentation, computational requirements depend on the inference complexity of the clinical risk model, and task deadlines are assigned based on clinically informed urgency levels. Clinical risk scores are produced by the CNN–LSTM-based risk estimator, ensuring that scheduling decisions are grounded in real physiological data rather than artificially generated values.

Average latency

Latency is a critical performance metric in real-time healthcare IoT systems, as delayed processing of time-sensitive physiological signals can compromise timely clinical response. In this context, maintaining low and stable end-to-end latency is essential, particularly for high-risk tasks associated with abnormal physiological conditions.

The proposed CRAI-LCS methodology addresses this challenge by incorporating data-driven clinical risk assessment and predictive deadline-violation modeling into the scheduling process. In contrast to traditional methods that rely exclusively on system-level indicators, CRAI-LCS prioritizes tasks based on clinically derived urgency from real ECG signals, enabling more informed scheduling decisions.

Figure 3 illustrates that CRAI-LCS consistently achieves the lowest average end-to-end latency across all task arrival rates (λ = 8–25 tasks/s). Latency increases progressively with workload intensity; however, the proposed approach maintains a clear performance advantage over all baseline methods. At high load (λ = 20 tasks/s), CRAI-LCS achieves an average latency of approximately 148 ms, remaining within clinically acceptable limits for high-risk workloads. In contrast, baseline approaches exhibit significantly higher latency values (182–228 ms), indicating reduced responsiveness under comparable conditions.

The observed performance improvements can be attributed to three key mechanisms. First, risk-informed task prioritization ensures that tasks with higher clinical risk scores Ri are prioritized for immediate execution, thereby minimizing transmission delays and improving response time. Second, predictive deadline-violation estimation allows the scheduling strategy to incorporate predicted violation probabilities, enabling proactive decision-making in dynamic system environments. Third, the adaptive RL optimization balances latency, clinical risk, and resource consumption through a carefully designed reward function, ensuring consistent performance despite increasing workload demands.

Energy consumption

Energy efficiency is a critical factor in healthcare IoT systems, as many devices operate under stringent power constraints, including wearable sensors, bedside monitoring units, and edge gateways. Inefficient energy usage can reduce device longevity and compromise continuous monitoring, particularly in prolonged patient care scenarios.

The proposed CRAI-LCS framework integrates energy awareness into the scheduling process through a reward formulation that balances latency, clinical risk, and energy consumption. Unlike traditional methods that improve energy efficiency through a purely system-centric approach, CRAI-LCS leverages clinically informed decision-making to minimize redundant computation and communication overhead. Figure 6 shows that CRAI-LCS consistently exhibits the lowest energy consumption across all workload levels, ranging from 60 to 200 incoming requests. Energy usage increases with workload across all methods; however, the proposed framework demonstrates a more gradual growth rate compared to baseline approaches. Under moderate load (100 requests), CRAI-LCS consumes approximately 285 J, whereas baseline approaches exhibit significantly higher consumption levels. At maximum capacity (200 requests), CRAI-LCS maintains energy usage between 410 and 420 J, while alternative approaches exhibit considerably higher values, reflecting poorer energy efficiency.

The observed improvements can be attributed to three key factors. First, clinically guided task allocation ensures that high-risk tasks are prioritized for local edge execution, thereby reducing costly data transmission and cloud processing overhead. Second, predictive scheduling decisions based on deadline-violation prediction help avoid unnecessary offloading attempts that would fail to meet latency requirements, thereby conserving energy. Third, the balanced reward optimization integrates energy consumption as a penalty term in the RL objective, enabling the system to identify efficient trade-offs between performance and resource utilization.

Resource utilization efficiency

Resource utilization efficiency indicates the degree to which available edge computing resources are effectively utilized for processing incoming tasks, hence minimizing idle capacity and preventing system overload. In healthcare IoT settings, it is essential to strike a balance between underutilization and overutilization, since improper resource allocation might result in wasted infrastructure or hinder the timely execution of key clinical procedures. The proposed CRAI-LCS framework optimizes resource utilization by including risk-aware and load-aware scheduling methods. The system utilizes clinical risk rankings based on actual physiological signals to dynamically prioritize high-risk tasks and allocate lower-priority workloads among available edge nodes. This method facilitates equitable resource distribution and mitigates localized congestion amid fluctuating workload scenarios. Figure 7 demonstrates that CRAI-LCS constantly achieves elevated resource utilization despite rising quantities of incoming requests. Utilization progressively escalates with workload intensity, attaining roughly 90–92% during peak load conditions (200 requests), while demonstrating stable performance without notable fluctuations. This signifies that the framework adeptly adapts to fluctuating task arrivals and diverse workload attributes. Conversely, baseline methods exhibit diminished utilization rates and increased variability, especially when the system nears high-load circumstances. This pattern indicates inefficient load distribution and diminished adaptability to fluctuating system conditions, perhaps resulting in underutilized resources or localized bottlenecks.

The enhanced efficacy of CRAI-LCS is due to its capacity to integrate predictive system awareness and clinically informed prioritizing into the scheduling procedure. By evaluating both the expected system status and the urgency of incoming activities, the framework sustains elevated resource utilization while guaranteeing that essential workloads are executed within their latency parameters. The results demonstrate that CRAI-LCS offers steady and efficient resource use compared to baseline approaches, especially in situations with high workload intensity and varied task needs.

An ablation study is performed under maximum workload conditions (λ = 20 tasks/sec) to assess the contribution of specific components inside the CRAI-LCS architecture. This result highlights the significance of flexible decision-making in settings marked by random task arrivals and diverse workloads.

The ablation results indicate that integrating clinical risk estimation, predictive scheduling, and multi-objective optimization enhances the efficacy of the CRAI-LCS system. The observed performance decline with the removal of individual components indicates that these factors function synergistically rather than individually.

This non-parametric test was selected because RL-based scheduling approaches may exhibit performance variations due to stochastic policy updates and environmental interactions. The statistical analysis was performed on the main evaluation metrics, including latency, deadline violation rate, clinical risk coverage, edge resource utilization, and energy consumption.

Impact of IoT data reliability on CRAI-LCS performance

The effectiveness of healthcare IoT intelligence systems strongly depends on the reliability and trustworthiness of sensed physiological data. Since CRAI-LCS utilizes ECG-derived information to estimate clinical risk and guide scheduling decisions, inaccurate or unreliable sensor measurements may propagate errors into the risk estimation process and consequently affect task prioritization. Recent studies have highlighted that IoT data reliability, sensor uncertainty, and trust assessment are critical factors for reliable healthcare IoT decision-making, particularly when AI-based methods are applied to safety-sensitive applications40.

In practical healthcare IoT environments, sensing errors may arise from several factors, including sensor noise, signal degradation, missing measurements, communication disturbances, and device-level failures. Such uncertainties can influence the quality of physiological information and may lead to inaccurate estimation of patient conditions. Therefore, ensuring data reliability is an important consideration for deploying intelligent scheduling frameworks in real-world healthcare scenarios.

In the current study, CRAI-LCS assumes that the acquired ECG signals are obtained from validated healthcare datasets with acceptable signal quality. This assumption enables a controlled evaluation of the proposed clinical risk-aware scheduling mechanism. However, in real deployments, integrating data quality indicators, sensor confidence scores, and signal reliability measures into the clinical risk representation can further improve scheduling robustness.

A reliability-aware extension of CRAI-LCS could jointly consider patient clinical risk and sensing confidence within the RL state representation. Such an approach would enable the scheduler to distinguish between truly critical physiological events and potentially unreliable observations, leading to more robust resource allocation decisions in dynamic healthcare IoT environments. Investigating joint clinical risk and sensor reliability-aware scheduling is considered an important direction for future research.

Discussion

The experimental results demonstrate that the proposed CRAI-LCS framework consistently improves the performance of healthcare IoT scheduling compared with baseline (DRL)approaches across multiple evaluation metrics, including end-to-end latency, deadline violation rate, clinical risk coverage, task execution efficiency, energy consumption, and resource utilization. These improvements are observed under different workload intensities, indicating the robustness of the proposed approach under dynamic edge–cloud conditions. In particular, CRAI-LCS achieves lower latency and fewer deadline violations while maintaining higher clinical risk coverage for time-sensitive healthcare tasks. Moreover, the framework provides more stable resource utilization and improved energy efficiency as workload conditions become more challenging. The magnitude of improvement varies depending on the considered metric and system configuration; however, the overall performance trend consistently demonstrates the advantage of incorporating clinical awareness into healthcare IoT scheduling.

The observed performance gains can be mainly attributed to the joint integration of clinical risk estimation, predictive scheduling, and adaptive RL-based resource optimization. Unlike conventional scheduling approaches that primarily rely on computational states, network conditions, or predefined priorities, CRAI-LCS incorporates ECG-derived clinical risk information into the scheduling decision process. This enables the scheduler to differentiate between healthcare tasks with similar computational characteristics but different clinical consequences, allowing high-risk tasks to receive appropriate priority under limited edge resources. Furthermore, the deadline violation prediction module enhances proactive decision-making by estimating potential execution failures before scheduling actions are selected, thereby improving deadline compliance and system responsiveness.

Another important factor contributing to the improved performance is the risk-aware RL formulation, where clinical urgency is jointly considered with latency, energy consumption, and resource availability. Instead of optimizing a single system-level objective, the proposed reward mechanism enables the scheduler to achieve a balanced trade-off between patient-centric requirements and infrastructure constraints. Therefore, the reported improvements are not solely attributed to the learning algorithm itself but result from the integration of physiological intelligence, workload-aware task representation, and adaptive resource management within a unified framework.

The effectiveness of CRAI-LCS should be interpreted within the scope of the conducted evaluation. The current framework relies on validated ECG datasets and a simulated edge–cloud environment to provide controlled and reproducible performance analysis. Consequently, factors such as sensor reliability, network instability, large-scale multi-patient interactions, and heterogeneous deployment conditions may influence performance in real-world scenarios. Although the proposed approach demonstrates promising results under the evaluated healthcare IoT settings, further validation using real clinical environments and diverse healthcare data sources is required to fully assess its practical applicability.

Overall, the findings indicate that incorporating data-driven clinical awareness into edge scheduling can improve the reliability and efficiency of healthcare IoT resource management. The results demonstrate the effectiveness of CRAI-LCS in the evaluated scenarios and highlight its potential as a foundation for future real-world deployment and validation.

Conclusion and future work

This study presents CRAI-LCS, a Clinical Risk-Aware AI-Enhanced Latency-Constrained Scheduling architecture designed for real-time healthcare IoT systems. By integrating a lightweight clinical risk estimator, a deadline-violation predictor, and a constrained (DRL)scheduler with a clinically informed reward function, CRAI-LCS shifts from traditional system-centric optimization to patient-centric decision-making. The system explicitly prioritizes high-risk physiological events, enforces stringent latency constraints, and optimizes energy and resource utilization in resource-limited edge–cloud scenarios.

Comprehensive simulations demonstrate that CRAI-LCS achieves consistent improvements over recent DRL-based benchmarks under the evaluated healthcare IoT scenarios. Specifically, it achieves a 23–48% reduction in end-to-end latency, a 55–81% reduction in deadline violation rates, and 96.4% clinical risk coverage for high-risk tasks, while simultaneously reducing energy consumption by 32–55% and maintaining stable resource utilization at 91–93%. The ablation study further confirms that each component; clinical risk awareness, violation prediction, and constraint enforcement; plays a critical role in achieving these improvements, particularly in preserving timeliness and safety during unpredictable, high-risk workloads.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the proposed framework leverages ECG-derived workloads from a real-world dataset, the current evaluation is conducted within a simulated edge–cloud environment, which may not fully capture real-world deployment complexities such as network instability, sensor noise, and multi-patient interference. Furthermore, system performance depends on the accuracy and generalizability of the clinical risk estimation model, which is trained on a single publicly available dataset. The computational overhead associated with DRL policy updates, although mitigated through lightweight model design, may still pose challenges for deployment on highly resource-constrained edge devices.

Future work will pursue several directions. First, the proposed framework will be implemented and validated in clinical test environments, incorporating diverse wearable devices and bedside monitoring systems. Second, federated learning will be adopted to enable privacy-preserving collaboration among multiple hospitals without centralizing sensitive patient information. Third, uncertainty quantification techniques, such as Bayesian (DRL)or conformal prediction, will be integrated to provide confidence-aware risk assessments and improved decision boundaries. Fourth, energy-harvesting-aware scheduling will be explored for battery-free or self-powered IoT devices in remote monitoring scenarios. Finally, explainable AI modules will be developed to generate clinically interpretable rationales for scheduling decisions, enhancing physician trust and facilitating regulatory compliance.
