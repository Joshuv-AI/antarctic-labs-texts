Total Syntheses and Structural Reassignment of Ugonin T and Neougonin B: Anti-inflammatory Flavonoids Related to Helminthostachys zeylanica
Authors: Yuh-Chiang Shen, Chang-Hui Chen, San-Yao Su, Yi-Jyung Lin, Zhong-You Lai, Cheng-Ming Chen, Chun-Wei Chen, Fei-Pei Kao, Ming-Jaw Don, Yea-Hwey Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study reports the first total syntheses of the naturally occurring flavonoids ugonin C (1), ugonin F (2), and, based on the originally proposed structures, compounds 3 and 4 (corresponding to the structures first assigned to ugonin T and neougonin B, respectively), all featuring a dihydrofuran ring fused to a phenolic moiety. In addition, a series of analogues (18, 19, 22, 23a, 23b, 27a, 27b, and 28) were synthesized to explore their biological activities. On the basis of synthetic strategy and detailed NMR analyses, the structures of naturally occurring ugonin T and neougonin B were reassigned to correspond to compounds 23a and 19, respectively. Biological evaluation revealed that several synthesized compounds exhibited potent anti-inflammatory activity, particularly compound 27a, which showed the strongest inhibition of NF-κB associated nitric oxide production in BV2 microglial cells.

The rhizomes of Helminthostachys zeylanica (L.) Hook. (Ophioglossaceae), also known as “Daodi-Ugon”, are used in traditional Chinese medicine as an antipyretic and antiphlogistic agent. Ugonins C (1), F (2), T (originally proposed as 3), and neougonin B (originally proposed as 4) were isolated and characterized as four minor components from H. zeylanica. − (Figure ). These natural products 1–4 possess a flavonoid skeleton, and a dihydrofuran ring is connected to the phenolic moiety. Compound 3 showed anti-inflammatory activity. Until recently, total syntheses of other ugonins from this plant, including ugonstilbenes A–C and ugonins L, S, U, and Z, had been reported; , however, no total syntheses of 1–4 had previously been reported. Naturally occurring flavonoids and synthesized analogues have been reported to have various biological activities including antioxidant, , anti-inflammatory, ,− antibacterial, and cytotoxic effects. ,,, They have potential as novel therapeutic agents. This study aimed to synthesize ugonins C, F, T, and neougonin B in order to further study their biological activities. The structures of 1–4 were determined on the basis of their NMR spectral data. − In addition, analogues 18, 19, 22, 23a, 23b, 27a, 27b, and 28 were also synthesized. The synthesized compounds were evaluated for their anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities.

Structural Reassignment of Ugonin T and Neougonin B

The NMR data of synthesized compounds 1 and 2 in acetone-d 6 were summarized in Table . The NMR data of synthesized compound 2 was consistent with that of reported naturally occurring ugonin F. The NMR data of reported ugonin T, neougonin B, synthesized compounds 3, 4, 19, and 23a in CD3OD were summarized in Tables and . The 1H NMR results indicated that the structure of the synthesized compound 23a was more than likely to be consistent with that of reported naturally occurring ugonin T, showing a singlet proton signal at C-6 (δH 6.18). The structure of the synthesized compound 19 was more than likely to be consistent with that of reported naturally occurring neougonin B, showing a singlet proton signal at C-6 (δH 6.15). In addition, in the HMBC spectrum (acetone-d 6) of 19, a hydroxy group at C-5 (δH 12.5) showed correlations with C-4a (δC 104.7, C), C-5 (δC 162.8, C), and C-6 (δC 94.3, CH) (Figure S109). It was indicated that the dihydrofuran ring should be located at C-7/C-8 positions like ugonins C and F instead of located at C-6/C-7 positions. Taken together, the synthetic and spectroscopic data strongly support reassignment of naturally occurring ugonin T and neougonin B to compounds 23a and 19, respectively.

It is noteworthy that the reported melting point of natural ugonin T (202–204 °C) differs considerably from that of synthesized 3 (258–260 °C) and its regioisomer 23a (251–252 °C) alike, and the reported melting point of natural ugonin C (236–237 °C) likewise differs from that of synthesized 1 (229–231 °C). No melting point or IR data have been reported for natural neougonin B. These discrepancies most likely reflect impurity or partial decomposition of the small quantities of natural material originally isolated, rather than a structural difference; melting point and IR are therefore not reliable criteria for distinguishing among these closely related regioisomers. In contrast, the full 1H and 13C NMR data, including the diagnostic C-6 chemical shifts and HMBC correlations described above, unambiguously distinguish the regiochemistry and provide the basis for the structural reassignment reported here.

The inhibitory effects of synthesized compounds 1–4, 18, 19, 22, 23a, 23b, 27a, 27b, and 28 on nitric oxide (NO) production were evaluated in BV2 microglial cells, as shown in Table . PDTC (pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate), an NF-κB inhibitor, was used as the positive control. The half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) for NO inhibition was determined based on nitrite accumulation in the culture medium. No obvious cytotoxicity was observed under the tested condition, indicating that the observed NO inhibitory effects were not attributable to cytotoxicity. As summarized in Table , most synthesized compounds exhibited potent NO inhibitory activity, with several compounds outperforming the positive control PDTC (EC50 = 12.1 μg/mL). Notably, compounds 3, 4, and 27a demonstrated the strongest activity, with EC50 values ranging from 1.81 to 2.38 μg/mL, whereas the remaining compounds showed moderate activity (EC50 = 3.23–9.47 μg/mL). Structure–activity relationship analysis suggested that the position of the dihydrofuran ring is critical for anti-inflammatory activity, with substitution at C-6/C-7 positions significantly enhancing NO inhibitory potency. In addition, the presence of dihydroxy groups on the B-ring further contributed to the observed activity, highlighting their importance in modulating anti-inflammatory effects. Notably, compound 27a demonstrated the strongest inhibition of nitric oxide production in BV-2 microglial cells, which may be associated with suppression of NF-κB activation and subsequent downregulation of iNOS expression (Figure ).

Additionally, the antioxidant potential of synthesized compounds was assessed using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. Based on the DPPH radical scavenging assay (Table ), distinct structure–activity relationships (SAR) could be identified among the synthesized flavonoid derivatives. First, compounds bearing catechol-type dihydroxy substitution on the B-ring (3′,4′-dihydroxy) exhibited significantly enhanced radical scavenging activity. For instance, compounds 4 (EC50 = 7.72 μg/mL), 18 (9.75 μg/mL), and 19 (7.20 μg/mL) demonstrated stronger antioxidant effects compared to mono-hydroxylated analogues such as 1, 2, 22, 23b, and 27b (EC50 > 50 μg/mL). This observation suggests that the ortho-dihydroxy (catechol) moiety plays a critical role in stabilizing DPPH radicals. The position of the dihydrofuran ring appears to exert a modulatory but less dominant effect compared to B-ring substitution. Compounds with similar catechol substitution but different ring fusion patterns (e.g., 3, 23a, and 27a) still retained moderate to strong activity, suggesting that electronic properties of the phenolic B-ring appear to be a major determinant, while the fused dihydrofuran system may influence activity through conformational or steric effects.

In summary, a series of 12 flavonoids bearing a dihydrofuran ring at different positions were successfully synthesized. Based on comprehensive NMR analysis, the structures of compounds 23a and 19 were confirmed to correspond to the naturally occurring ugonin T and neougonin B, respectively. Among the synthesized compounds, 27a exhibited the most potent anti-inflammatory activity, highlighting the importance of specific structural features in modulating biological effects. These findings provide valuable insights into the structure–activity relationships of this class of flavonoids and suggest that compound 27a represents a promising lead for further development in the treatment of inflammation-related neurological disorders.

Experimental Section

General Experimental Procedures

Melting points were measured on a Yanaco MP-S9 micro melting point apparatus, and the thermometer was used without correction. IR spectra were obtained on a Nicolet Avatar 320 FTIR spectrophotometer. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectra were recorded on Varian VNMRS 600 MHz and Unity Inova 500 MHz FT-NMR spectrometers. Chemical shifts are reported in parts per million (δ) units relative to internal deuterium solvent. The high-resolution ESIMS spectra were measured on a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Focus Orbitrap mass spectrometer. Microwave irradiation was performed on CEM Discover apparatus. Column chromatography was performed with E. Merck 230–400 mesh silica gel.

Microglial Cell Culture and Measurements of NO33

The murine microglial cell line (BV2) was cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone). BV-2 cells were pretreated with compound 27a (0.5, 1.0, or 1.5 μM) for 3 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (1 μg/mL) for an additional 4 h. Production of NO was measured by the accumulation of nitrite in the culture medium 24 h after stimulation with LPS by the Griess reagent. Pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC), an established inhibitor of NF-κB signaling, was used as a mechanistic positive control to validate NO suppression via the NF-κB/iNOS axis. Data were calculated as 50% inhibitory concentration (EC50) and expressed as the mean ± SEM from 3–5 experiments performed on different days.

Measurement of DPPH Radical-Scavenging Capacity

DPPH radical-scavenging capacity assay was performed as in our previous report. Briefly, drugs were diluted in MeOH to a range of concentrations (10–50 μM). DPPH solution (200 μL; 200 μM in MeOH) was added to 10 μL of each diluted sample in a 96-well microplate, and the resulting solution was allowed to react for 30 min in the dark at ambient temperature. The absorbance caused by the DPPH radical at 517 nm was determined by a microplate-spectrophotometer. The radical scavenging capacity was expressed as delta OD517 (ΔOD517), and values were the means of three replicates. Trolox, an antioxidant, was included as a reference.

Immunofluorescence Staining

To evaluate the potential anti-inflammatory activity of compound 27a, an immune-fluorescence labeling assay targeting NF-κB and iNOS was performed. Microglial cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/well and treated with LPS (1.0 μg/mL) in the presence or absence of compound 27a (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 μg/mL) for 3 h. Following treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PBS for 30 min, blocked with 1% BSA in 0.1 M PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min, and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against phospho-pP65NF-κB (pP65; 1:100) and iNOS (1:400). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and samples were subsequently incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies. Images were acquired using a Carl Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope. Quantitative analysis of colocalization (double positive stain) of pP65 with iNOS was performed by counting 20–30 cells across 3 fields in control and LPS-treated cultures, with or without compound 27a (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 μg/mL). Each experiment was conducted in duplicate, and the number of double-stained cell of pP65 and iNOS was determined from three randomly selected microscopic fields (400×) per section; the mean value from these fields was used for statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The mean value ±S.E.M was used to represent all data in the text, tables, and figures. Data analysis was performed using a two-tailed t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by the post-hoc Student-Newman-Keuls (S–N–K) t-test for multiple comparisons dose–response relationships were evaluated by linear regression of response levels against drug concentration, and the slope was tested against zero using Student’s t-test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
