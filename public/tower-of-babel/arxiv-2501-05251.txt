Direct coupling of continuum and shell elements in large deformation problems
Astrid Pechstein, Michael Neunteufel

Abstract
. In many applications, thin shell-like structures are integrated within or attached to volumetric bodies. This includes reinforcements placed in soft matrix material in lightweight structure design, or hollow structures that are partially or completely filled. Finite element simulations of such setups are highly challenging. A brute force discretization of structural as well as volumetric parts using well-shaped three-dimensional elements may be accurate, but leads to problems of enormous computational complexity even for simple models. One desired alternative is the use of shell elements for thin-walled parts, as such a discretization greatly alleviates size restrictions on the underlying finite element mesh. However, the coupling of different formulations within a single framework is often not straightforward and may lead to locking if not done carefully. Neunteufel and Schöberl proposed a mixed shell element where, apart from displacements of the center surface, bending moments are used as independent unknowns. These elements were not only shown to be locking free and highly accurate in large-deformation regime, but also do not require differentiability of the shell surface. They can directly be coupled to classical volume elements of arbitrary order by sharing displacement degrees of freedom at the center surface, thus achieving the desired coupled discretization. As the elements can be used on unstructured meshes, adaptive mesh refinement based on local stress and bending moments can be used. We present computational results that confirm exceptional accuracy for problems where thin-walled structures are embedded as reinforcements within soft matrix material.

1.
Introduction

As lightweight structures are designed, one often meets with stiff thin-walled structures (the
reinforcement
) in combination with a soft matrix. Reinforcements may be submerged within the matrix, as well as form the surface of the lightweight structure
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Finite element simulations of these combined structures prove to be computationally expensive due to their adverse geometric dimensions. As thin-walled structures are resolved by conventional elements, the thickness of the reinforcements makes small mesh sizes in these regions necessary, which leads to an excessive growth in the number of degrees of freedom.

To alleviate the restrictions on the mesh size, solid shell elements have been developed especially for the discretization of thin structures. Optimally, the reinforcement can then be discretized using only one layer of solid shell elements, where the mesh size in thickness direction is independent of the in-plane size of these elements. This greatly reduces the number of elements, and thus the number of unknowns to be computed. One well-known approach is the use of assumed natural strains as additional, element-internal degrees of freedom in the solid-shell elements, as was proposed by Sze and Yao
[
4
]
, and extended to the large deformation case in
[
5
]
. Another large deformation element based on the Hu–Washizu three-field principle is presented by Klinkel et al.
[
6
]
.

As these elements are constructed in such a way that degrees of freedoms and shape functions coincide with conventional elements on the reinforcement/matrix interface, coupling of the different element types in a combined structure is straightforward when using a matching pair of solid shell and standard elements. Limitations of this approach are found at the boundaries of flat reinforcement structures: here, the thickness still limits the mesh size (although much less so than in the case of standard elements), and the connection of several shells along a common line (e.g. a T-joint) is not straightforward, especially if the shells do not meet at an angle of
90\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolDegree}
.

A different approach is the modeling of thin-walled reinforcements as shells, which are included as surface or interface within the soft matrix. This approach shows great advantages from the point of view of mesh generation, as then the thickness does not appear in the finite element mesh at all. Reinforcements are modelled as shell, only its mid-surface is discretized.
Under the kinematic assumptions of Kirchhoff–Love theory, elastic energy on the shell mid-surface can be derived analytically, leading to fourth order partial differential equations for the displacement, see e.g. the monograph
[
7
]
. Finite elements have to satisfy
\mathcal{C}^{1}
continuity, where no kinks in the displacement are allowed. For the geometrically linear case, one well-known example is the Argyris triangle from the TUBA family
[
8
]
. Extensions to large deformations were, however, of limited success
[
9
]
. A quadrilateral element suitable for large deformations was proposed by Vetyukov
[
10
]
. It is an element from the family of absolute nodal coordinate formulation based elements, and uses positions and assorted slope vectors as degrees of freedom. Additionally, we cite the triangular six-node element by Dau et al.
[
11
]
and the monograph by Bernadou and James
[
12
]
for different Kirchhoff–Love shell element formulations. Common to all these specific shell elements is the fact that the degrees of freedom for the mid-surface displacement do not match the nodal degrees of freedom of elements used in the matrix. Thus, coupling of these discretizations is not straightforward, and leads to locking or loss of accuracy if not done carefully.

In the field of isogeometric analysis (IGA), splines of higher smoothness are used as ansatz functions in the discretization, see the monograph by Cottrell, Hughes, and Bazilevs
[
13
]
for an introduction. Splines lend themselves naturally to the discretization of Kirchhoff–Love shells, as the
\mathcal{C}^{1}
differentiability can be ensured through the choice of order and multiplicity of knots in the discretization. Bazilevs et al.
[
14
]
proposed to use T-splines for volumes as well as plate domains, for solids as well as fluid mechanics. Interpolation by non-uniform rational B-splines (NURBS) was proven to be effective for modeling classical shells by Kiendl et al.
[
15
]
, and extended to compressible and incompressible hyperelastic materials in
[
16
]
. Regarding reinforcements, shell elements for fibre-reinforced composites were designed by Schulte and co-workers
[
17
]
. When the same class of IGA elements can be used for reinforcement and matrix, a direct coupling of the approaches is possible, see Liu et al.
[
18
]
. However, the discretization of complex reinforced structures requires a discretization consisting of multiple patches. For these multiple patches,
\mathcal{C}^{1}
continuity is lost, and needs to be re-established through additional constraints. Schuß et al.
[
19
]
presented a methodology applicable to Kirchhoff–Love shells. However, the joint discretization of matrix volume and shell structure using IGA splines is certainly not straightforward. Also, the treatment of kinks in the surface or joints of more than one shell along a common line is to be done with care. Last, correct representations of the coupling of trimmed surfaces and volumes has not been treated in the literature to the best knowledge of the authors.

Pechstein and Schöberl introduced the
Tangential-Displacement-Normal-Normal-Stress
(TDNNS) method
[
20

and proved that it is locking-free in the sense of solid-shell elements for the small deformation case in
[
21
]
. An extension to the large deformation case could be established by Neunteufel et al.
[
22
]
by way of a three-field Hu–Washizu formulation. Concerning structural elements, a plate formulation for Reissner–Mindlin plates based on these techniques was presented in
[
23
]
. These ideas were generalized to large-deformation shells by Neunteufel and Schöberl in
[
24
]
, where a family of arbitrary order mixed shell elements was proposed. When reducing this shell formulation to linear Kirchhoff–Love plates, the mixed Hellan–Herrmann–Johnson method is recovered
[
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
]
. Membrane locking of the original method
[
24
]
was treated efficiently in
[
29
]
. Coupling of these shell elements with electromechanics for dielectric elastomers has been successfully performed by Pechstein and Krommer in
[
30
]
. Essential for all these formulations based on the original contribution
[
24
]
is that in this formulation
\mathcal{C}^{1}
continuity for the displacement could be alleviated by the introduction of an independent moment tensor. Nodal elements without additional smoothness can directly be used to discretize the shell mid-surface displacement. Thus, a coupled discretization of reinforcements and matrix is possible in a straightforward manner.

The coupling of shear deformable Reissner–Mindlin shells to solid domains has been treated in
[
31
,
32
]
. Coupling of Cosserat shells in the framework of tangential calculus is discussed by Sky et al. in
[

The additional degrees of freedom in these shell formulations alleviate the mid-surface smoothness constraint, but can lead to (shear) locking, which has to be taken into consideration. The extension to shear deformable shell models is, however, out of scope of this work.

The present paper is organized as follows: In the next section we will describe the original elasticity problem involving structure and bulk domains. Section
3
is devoted to the geometric and kinematic reduction of the full elasticity problem corresponding shell and a bulk part. Therein the continuous shell equations are derived. In Section
4
we present a consistent discretization by mixed shell elements. We discuss involved finite element spaces, hybridization, and the applicability to structures with kinks and branches such as T-joints. Local equilibrium conditions are derived in Section
5

6

2.
Definition of the original problem

Geometry and kinematics of the original problem

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the domain associated to the so-called reference configuration of the body of interest. In the following, we assume that this domain can be subdivided into two parts: the shell domain, which is of small thickness, but otherwise arbitrary topology concerning its mid-surface, and the bulk domain, which denotes the remaining part of
\Omega
. To be concise, let
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be an oriented surface, i.e. a two-dimensional manifold with normal vector
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Then, for given thickness
t_{\mathcal{S}}
, we define the shell domain
\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}
as

(1)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\{\boldsymbol{X}+\zeta\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}:\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{S},\zeta\in[-t_{\mathcal{S}}/2,t_{\mathcal{S}}/2]\}.

It is assumed that the mid-surface
\mathcal{S}
and thickness
t_{\mathcal{S}}
correspond insofar as that
\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}\subset\overline{\Omega}
; note that for the current definition, the shell domain may be immersed within
\Omega
, extend to the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and may also coincide with the original domain’s surface. In all cases, the bulk domain is defined as the remaining part of
\Omega
,

(2)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\Omega\backslash\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}.

Let now
\boldsymbol{u}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
denote the displacement vector describing the deformation of the body of interest. Using standard notation, we introduce
\nabla=\partial/\partial\boldsymbol{X}
as the derivative with respect to material coordinates
\boldsymbol{X}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}=(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{T}+{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}
the deformation gradient. Its determinant is denoted as
J=\det{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}
, while right Cauchy-Green tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}^{T}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}
and Green-Lagrange strain
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}}=1/2({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}})
describe local deformations of the body. Last, any surface normal vector is mapped through the contravariant transformation,

(3)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{Cof}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}}{|\operatorname{Cof}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}|},

with
\operatorname{Cof}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}=J{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}^{-T}
the cofactor matrix. Above and in the following, we adhere to the convention to use upper-case letters for quantities associated to reference configuration, and lower-case letters for the corresponding quantities in deformed configuration.

Hyperelastic materials

We assume that the elastic behavior of the solid is defined in the way of elastic energy densities. Let
\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})
denote the objective elastic energy density for the unreduced, original body. For presentation of the proposed method, we assume that all external forces are conservative body loads, such that there exists an energy density representation
\psi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{u})
. Of course, the generalization to surface loads as well as non-conservative loads is straightforward. Then, the static solution
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfies the minimization problem

(4)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})-\psi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)\,dV\to\min_{\boldsymbol{u}\text{ adm.}}.

Above, any displacement field is
admissible
if it corresponds to a finite elastic energy,
\int_{\Omega}\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})\,dV<\infty
, and the kinematic boundary conditions on
\boldsymbol{u}
are satisfied. In the context of linear elasticity, these requests usually result in the common choice
\boldsymbol{u}\in[H^{1}(\Omega)]^{3}
with
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{0}
on the Dirichlet boundary
\Gamma_{\text{fix}}\subset\partial\Omega
.
The principle of virtual work yields a variational formulation; let the solution
\boldsymbol{u}
satisfy

(5)

\displaystyle\delta W_{\text{int}}=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\right)\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right)\,dV=\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}},

for
admissible
virtual displacements
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
satisfying homogeneous kinematic boundary conditions. Last, we state that the second and first Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensors are defined through the hyperelastic potential,

(6)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}}

\displaystyle=2\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}}.

The case of small deformations

If all deformations are small, reference and spatial configuration of the body of interest are commonly identified, and the Green strain tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{E}}}
is replaced by its linearization

{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}=1/2(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{T})
. Cauchy stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}
and Piola–Kirchhoff stresses
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{S}}}
,
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}}
are no longer distinguished, and a linear relationship between stress tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}
and strain
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}
is assumed, such that with the fourth order elasticity tensor
\mathbb{C}
,

(7)

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}:\mathbb{C}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}}=\mathbb{C}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}.

Kinematic reduction

For thin shells, Kirchhoff–Love kinematic assumptions are often used to describe the through-the-thickness deformation in terms of the shell’s mid-surface deformation only
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
. Similar assumptions on the displacement field in the shell part
\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}
are posed in the following. Beforehand, we state
the general representation of a shell surface in the framework of differential geometry, as well as the relevant surface measures.

The shell’s mid-surface
\mathcal{S}
is assumed to be sufficiently smooth to allow for the following definitions of the surface gradient and metric tensors in a classical sense; the extension to surfaces with kinks and T-joints is discussed later in Section
4
. Let
\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}
denote the surface gradient, which acts in the tangential plane of the surface only.
The first and second metric tensor of the shell’s mid-surface describe the projection to its tangential plane and its curvature, respectively.
We use

(9)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}-\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}.

It is noted that, for any smooth field
f
defined on any three-dimensional superset
\Omega\supset\mathcal{S}
, the restriction of its gradient to the surface and projection onto the tangential plane yields the surface gradient, such that

(10)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}f

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}\cdot(\nabla f)|_{\mathcal{S}}.

Above, the shell’s mid-surface displacement in the geometrically reduced problem was introduced as
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
. The surface deformation gradient evaluates to,

(11)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=(\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}})^{T}+{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}.

The surface normal to the deformed shell
\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}
is connected to
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}
by (
3
), where
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}
is to be replaced by
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
.

Kirchhoff–Love kinematic assumptions on
\boldsymbol{u}
in
\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}
are now made to connect volumetric and mid-surface displacement; first, it is assumed that the restriction
\boldsymbol{u}|_{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
holds in the sense of a trace operation. Additionally, any material point
\boldsymbol{X}+\zeta\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}
is mapped under deformation to the according point on the deformed surface normal,

(12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}+\zeta\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\to\boldsymbol{X}+\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{X})+\zeta\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\text{ for }\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{S}.

The above restriction (
12
) represents the classic conditions of no shear and no thickness deformation.
Note that, in such a regime, the surface deformation gradient
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
can equivalently be defined as the projection of the volumetric deformation gradient
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}
to the tangential plane,

(13)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}})|_{\mathcal{S}}.

Finally, surface measures of the deformed surface are given by

(14)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{I}}}-\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{T}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}.

The Green strain measures, membrane strain
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}
and curvature
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}
, are defined as the respective changes of the metric tensors after pull-back to the reference configuration. They are symmetric and in the tangential plane, defined through

(15)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{T}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}

\displaystyle=-({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}^{T}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{b}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}})=\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}.

Under the Kirchhoff–Love kinematic assumptions (
12
), the in-plane part of the Cauchy-Green tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{\mathcal{S}}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}
can be expressed in terms of the strain measures (
15
) when neglecting higher order terms in the thickness coordinate
\zeta
,

(16)

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{A}}}+2{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}+2\zeta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}+O(\zeta^{2}).

Reduction of energy densities

Let us now assume that the free energy of the full problem composes additively into a shell and a bulk part, such that

(17)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\psi({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})\,dV

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}}\psi_{\mathcal{B}}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})\,dV+\int_{\Omega_{\mathcal{S}}}\psi_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})\,dV.

These respective contributions are to be replaced by approximate energy representations on
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{S}
. For the bulk, it is proposed to use the extended domain
\mathcal{B}
instead of
\Omega_{\mathcal{B}}
, amounting to

(18)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\psi_{\mathcal{B}}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}})\,dV,

\displaystyle\delta W_{\text{int},\mathcal{B}}=\delta\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\right)\,dV.

The reduction is less straightforward for the shell domain.
For certain volume energy densities
\psi_{\mathcal{S}}
, it is possible to derive the in-plane strain-stress relation under plane stress conditions analytically in terms of
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{\mathcal{S}}
, and thereby the Green strain measures
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}
. Let
\tilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{\mathcal{S}})
denote such a plane-stress continuum energy density for the shell domain. Explicit integration through the thickness then yields a surface energy density
\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}
,

(19)

\displaystyle\int_{-t/2}^{t/2}\tilde{\psi}_{\mathcal{S}}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}_{\mathcal{S}})\,dV

\displaystyle=\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}).

In the case of St. Venant–Kirchhoff materials, one finds

(20)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}})&=\frac{t_{\mathcal{S}}\,E}{2(1-\nu^{2})}\left((1-\nu){\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}+\nu(\operatorname{tr}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}})^{2}+\frac{t_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}}{12}\left((1-\nu){\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}+\nu(\operatorname{tr}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}})^{2}\right)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}:\mathbb{C}_{m}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}+{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}:\mathbb{C}_{b}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}\right).\end{split}

Above,
\mathbb{C}_{m}
and
\mathbb{C}_{b}
denote the fourth order tensors representing membrane and bending stiffness for the plane stress case, respectively. It is noted that membrane stiffnesses grow linearly with the thickness parameter,
\mathbb{C}_{m}\in O(t_{\mathcal{S}})
, while bending stiffnesses are of third order,
\mathbb{C}_{b}\in O(t_{\mathcal{S}}^{3})
.

We propose to use expression (
19
) for the shell contribution to the virtual work of internal forces,

(21)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}},{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}})\,d\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle\delta W_{\text{int},\mathcal{S}}=\delta\Psi_{\mathcal{S}}=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}+\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}\right)\,d\mathcal{S}.

It is noted that the energy density
\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}
was derived under the assumption of a plane-stress state, i.e. for vanishing shear and thickness stresses. This assumption is not necessarily met when structures are immersed in bulk material; for many applications it is valid to assume that shear and thickness stresses are small compared to the in-plane stresses met with in the structural reinforcements. Computational results in Section
6
support this assumption.

Combining (
18
) and (
21
) into (
5
), we pose the variational problem to find
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{B}}
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
satisfying the compatibility constraint (
8
), such that for all admissible virtual displacements
\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
and dependent virtual membrane strains
\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}
and virtual curvatures
\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}
, equilibrium is achieved;

(22)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{B}}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{C}}}\right)\,dV+\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}+\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}}{\partial{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}\right)\,d\mathcal{S}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right)\,dV.

Let us shortly discuss the notion of
admissibility
of displacement fields in the current setting employing classical shell theory. For the second metric tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{B}}}
and curvature tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}
to be well-defined in the sense of classical derivatives, it is necessary to have the normal vector
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}
piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{1}
and globally continuous, and
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
to be continuously differentiable and piecewise
\mathcal{C}^{2}

at least
. This is a severe restriction on the underlying geometry – kinks are not allowed – as well as on any subsequent finite element discretization. A strategy to alleviate these constraints, and thereby enable the usage of standard nodal elements, is discussed using mixed shell elements.

The case of small deformations and plates

The equations simplify greatly when restricted to the small deformation case and plates. Assume that not only the usage of the geometrically linear theory of elasticity using the linearized strain
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}
and stress
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}
according to (
7
) can be rectified, but also that
\mathcal{S}
is non-curved. Under these assumptions, we shortly provide the ensuing formulation. For the case of a linear Kirchhoff–Love plate, we assume without restriction of generality that the unit normal is directed in
z
direction, such that
\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}=\boldsymbol{e}_{z}
. Consistently, we address the deflection component of

Consistent discretization using mixed shell elements

A consistent discretization of (

Low-regularity shell elements

Neunteufel and Schöberl
[

proposed to discretize the moment tensor

is convenient, see the recent work
[

. In short, let

denote the set of element edges. Then, three independent fields
\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}
,
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}
, and

are discretized through

(30a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}},\delta\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle\in\{\boldsymbol{v}\in[\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{S})]^{3}:\boldsymbol{v}|_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}\in[P^{k}(T_{\mathcal{S}})]^{3}\},

(30b)

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}},\delta\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}

\displaystyle\in\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}:\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}|_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}\in P_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1}(T_{\mathcal{S}})\},

(30c)

\displaystyle\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}},\delta\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}

\displaystyle\in\{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{sym}}:\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}|_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}\in P_{\mathrm{sym}}^{k-1}(T_{\mathcal{S}}),[\![\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}]\!]_{E}=0\text{ on }E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}\}.

\boldsymbol{\nu}_{r}
and

in undeformed configuration, see also Figure

[

.

While the tensor-valued independent moment tensor

\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}=\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}

(31)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}):=&\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}})\,d\mathcal{S}+\sum_{T_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}(-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}+\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}):\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}\,d\mathcal{S}\,+\\

as in (

) and (

\displaystyle\delta\Psi_{\mathcal{B}}+\delta\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathcal{B}}\left(\frac{\partial\psi_{\mathrm{ext}}}{\partial\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\right)\,dV.

Hybridization and structures with kinks and branches

The shell formulation (
31
) has a saddle-point structure, which leads to an indefinite stiffness matrix after assembling. To overcome this issue, hybridization techniques can be used, see e.g.
[
44
,
45
]
and specifically for the TDNNS method
[
40
,
22
]
for details. The idea is to break the normal-normal continuity of the moment tensor elements (
30c
). By adding an additional unknown as Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, we reinforce the broken continuity in weak sense. Therefore, the moment tensor
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}
(and the curvature tensor
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}
) can be efficiently eliminated from the system matrix element-wise, and the resulting system involving only the displacement fields
\boldsymbol{u}
and the new Lagrange multiplier
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a minimization problem.

The additional unknowns live only on the skeleton
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
, the edges, of the shell triangulation
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}
and will be denoted by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
.
The set of all admissible Lagrange functions is given by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in[\mathcal{C}(\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}})]^{3}:\boldsymbol{\beta}|_{E}\in[P^{k}(E)]^{3},\,[\![\boldsymbol{\beta}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]\!]_{E}=\boldsymbol{0},\,\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{t}_{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}=0,\boldsymbol{\beta}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma_{\text{fix}}\}.

This choice implies that for any
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
as above, the in-plane normal component
\alpha_{\nu}:=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{l}=-\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{r}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}_{r}
is unique. The hybridized version of (
31
) reads

(33)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Pi_{\mathcal{S}}(&\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}):=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi_{\mathcal{S},t}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}})\,d\mathcal{S}+\sum_{T_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}(-\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}}+\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{n}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{F}}}_{\mathcal{S}}-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\boldsymbol{N}_{\mathcal{S}}):\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}\,d\mathcal{S}\,+\\
&\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{E}(\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{n}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{r})-\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{r}))\,\{\!\{\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}\}\!\}_{E}+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\nu}[\![\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}]\!]\,ds\to\min_{\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathcal{S}},\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}},\boldsymbol{\alpha}\text{ adm.}}\max_{\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}\text{ adm.}}\,,\end{split}

where jump and average of the in-plane normal-normal component
\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}
are defined in (
28
)–(
29
).

For structures with kinks the shell method intrinsically preserves the angle of the kink and the inflow of the normal-normal component of the moment tensor
\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}
equals the outflow. The moment conservation follows by the construction of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}
. To see that the angle is preserved in a weak sense, consider on edge
E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
. Then the variation of
\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}
at the edge terms of (
31
) reads

(34)

\displaystyle\int_{E}(\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{n}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{r})-\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{r}))\,\delta\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}\,ds=0

and thus
(\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{r})-\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{n}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{r}))=0
.

Branched shells, where more than two elements
\{T_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
share a common edge
E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}
, are more delicate
[
40
]
. First, there is no notation of a “left” or “right” element, such that we have to rewrite the boundary terms into an equivalent form applicable for those structures. To this end, we define the averaged normal vector
\{\!\{\boldsymbol{N}\}\!\}=\frac{\boldsymbol{N}_{l}+\boldsymbol{N}_{r}}{|\boldsymbol{N}_{l}+\boldsymbol{N}_{r}|}
and analogously
\{\!\{\boldsymbol{n}\}\!\}
. Then, one can readily show that

\displaystyle\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{n}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}_{r})-\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{l}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{r}))\,\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}(\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{n}_{T}\cdot\{\!\{\boldsymbol{n}\}\!\})-\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{T}\cdot\{\!\{\boldsymbol{N}\}\!\}))\,\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}.

This concept, however, can now be easily extended to branched shells by defining the averaged normal vector
\{\!\{\boldsymbol{N}\}\!\}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{N}_{T_{i}}}{|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\boldsymbol{N}_{T_{i}}|}
(to avoid cancellation of the normals one can swap the orientation of one branch if necessary). One important difference to kinked shells is that we require to use the hybridized version, the other method fails to handle branched shells, which can be seen as follows: If we keep
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}
to be normal-normal continuous we would analogously to (
34
) preserve the
sum
of all angles. Breaking the continuity of
\hat{m}_{\nu\nu}
yields the constraints that the angle
\operatorname{acos}(\boldsymbol{N}_{T_{i}}\cdot\{\!\{\boldsymbol{N}\}\!\})
is preserved in a weak sense for all
i=1,\dots,k
, i.e., each individual angle is conserved. The hybridization variable
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is still single valued on
E
. Instead of the normal-normal continuity of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}}
at the branched edge, it now enforces the
balance of moments
: the sum of all inflow and outflow moments at the edge are equal.

5.
Implications on local equilibrium

In this section, we restrict the analysis to the case of linear elasticity, small deformation, and non-curved plates.

Under the above assumptions on the energy approximations and kinematic restrictions for admissible displacement fields, the principle of virtual work can be used to deduce local equilibrium equations. Within this section, we compute the local equilibrium conditions at the shell/plate mid-surface, and discuss their validity.

In classical continuum mechanics, local equilibrium of forces at small deformations is characterized in differential form,
\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}+\boldsymbol{f}=0
for volume forces
\boldsymbol{f}
, and on the boundary
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}=\boldsymbol{t}
for the surface traction forces
\boldsymbol{t}
. The stress tensor is not necessarily differentiable everywhere in classical sense; however, on any interface within the body, with
\boldsymbol{N}
its normal vector, continuity of the stress vector
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}
goes back to Cauchy. In any classical displacement-based finite element discretization, continuity of the stress vector
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}
is enforced in weak sense on element interfaces, such that any discontinuity does not perform work on the discrete virtual displacements.
This is usually derived from the principle of virtual work, performing integration by parts on each element separately. As similar considerations will be needed for the much more complex case of the coupled continuum/shell model, we will perform this analysis in detail for the well-understood case in classical continuum theory.

Local equilibrium in linear elasticity problems

Let now
\mathcal{T}=\{T\}
be a compatible finite element mesh of a domain
\Omega
.
Element-wise integration by parts in the virtual work of internal forces leads to

(37)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}\,dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\left(\int_{T}-\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dV+\int_{\partial T}({\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}_{T})\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dA\right).

Reordering the surface integrals face by face, using definition (
25
) of the jump of
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}
across
F\in\mathcal{F}
, where
\mathcal{F}
denotes the set of all facets of
\mathcal{T}
, this is equivalent to

(38)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}\,dV

\displaystyle=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{T}-\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dV+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{F}[\![{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}]\!]_{F}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dA.

For simplicity, assume that only volume forces
\boldsymbol{f}
, no surface tractions
\boldsymbol{t}
are acting on the domain. Then, the principle of virtual work states

(39)

\displaystyle\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}}\int_{T}(-\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}+\boldsymbol{f})\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dV+\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}}\int_{F}[\![{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}]\!]_{F}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,dA=0.

In other words, equilibrium of forces
-\operatorname{div}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}+\boldsymbol{f}
and continuity of the stress vector
[\![{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}]\!]_{F}=\boldsymbol{0}
are satisfied in weak sense.

Local equilibrium in coupled plate problems

In the following, a similar derivation will be done for the coupled linearized problem as stated in (
35
). To simplify the derivation, we first assume that
the displacement field’s derivative is globally continuous. Then the edge terms in the linear plate formulation (
36
) vanish. Further, we assume that
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
is continuous at vertices and that no external forces are acting, such that the work of external forces equals zero,
\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}=0
. Otherwise, external forces enter the equilibrium conditions in a well-known manner.

Define, for ease of presentation
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}:=\mathbb{C}_{m}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
as the membrane stress tensor and use the symmetric stress tensor
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}
for
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{P}}}
, as we are in the linear regime. Considering some general virtual displacement field
\delta\boldsymbol{u}
while setting
\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\kappa}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
and
\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}={\boldsymbol{\mathrm{0}}}
, the principle of virtual work (
35
) reduces to

(40)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathcal{B}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\sigma}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\varepsilon}}}\,dV+\int_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}\,d\mathcal{S}-\int_{\mathcal{S}}\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}\delta u_{z}:{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}\,d\mathcal{S}=0.\end{split}

The variation of the bulk energy has already been discussed above. We continue with the variation of the membrane part. Here, integration by parts is conducted on each surface element, where again the in-plane edge normal vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
enters the boundary expression. A reordering of edge terms leads to the following formula, making use of the jump of the membrane stress vector as defined in (
27
),

(41)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\int_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}:\delta{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{e}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}\,d\mathcal{S}&=\sum_{T_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}\left(-\int_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}\operatorname{div}_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,d\mathcal{S}+\int_{\partial T_{\mathcal{S}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,ds\right)\\
&=\sum_{T_{\mathcal{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{T_{\mathcal{S}}}(-\operatorname{div}_{\mathcal{S}}{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u})\,d\mathcal{S}+\sum_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{S}}}\int_{E_{\mathcal{S}}}[\![{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\nu}]\!]_{E_{\mathcal{S}}}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{u}\,ds.\end{split}

Note that, as
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{\tau}}}
as well as
\boldsymbol{\nu}
lie in the tangential plane, only tangential mid-surface displacement components are affected.

Next, the variation of the bending terms are considered. We start by performing integration by parts, using again that
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
acts in the tangential plane only, and that
{\boldsymbol{\mathrm{m}}}_{\mathrm{lin}}
is normal-normal continuous

6.3.
Wrinkling problem

In
[
51

, Knapp and co-workers considered a problem of generating and numerical modeling of microscopic wrinkling surfaces on polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) elastomers. In their experiment, a block of PDMS is pre-stretched and subsequently part of its surface is plasma-treated. Consequently, a stiffer surface layer evolves, where stiffness and thickness depend on the length of plasma treatment. On release, the bulk material contracts to the original stress-free length, which leads to the formation of wrinkles on the treated surface. Knapp et al.
[
51

provide not only measurements for the different wrinkle wavelengths observed, but also data for the thickness and mechanic properties of the surface and bulk material, as well as simulation results. In the current contribution, we set up a similar experiment based on their material data, and test the proposed shell formulation in this demanding setup. Not only do the observed wavelengths make a fine discretization of the treated area necessary; additionally, singularities at the boundaries of the treated surface patches are expected, and the near incompressibility of the bulk material PDMS makes a displacement-pressure formulation in the bulk material necessary.

Summary

In this paper we presented a novel approach for the coupling of continuum and shell elements in large deformation problems. The proposed method allows for the efficient simulation of structures where thin-walled reinforcements are embedded within or attached to a bulk material. By using a mixed shell formulation, we alleviate the need for higher smoothness in the displacement field, enabling the use of standard nodal elements. Local equilibrium conditions were derived for the coupled problem in the linear case for plates, showing consistency. Several numerical examples were provided to demonstrate the performance and efficiency of the proposed method. Overall, the proposed method provides a powerful tool for the simulation of complex structures involving thin-walled reinforcements, offering significant computational savings while maintaining high accuracy.
