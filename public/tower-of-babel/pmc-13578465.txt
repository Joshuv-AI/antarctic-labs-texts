Azithromycin-induced hepatocellular liver injury in a healthy young male: A case report
Authors: Ghaleb Alharbi, Mabrouk AL-Rasheedi, Hind Khalid Goresh, Faisal Abdullah Alharbi, Jayiz S Alharbi, Abrar Alqurashi, Yara Alromaih, Norah Alsuhaibani, Kholood S Aldmasi, Haya S Alharbi, Khawaja Husnain Haider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrug-induced liver injury (DILI) is an important and frequently under-recognized cause of acute hepatitis. Antibiotics represent one of the most commonly implicated drug classes. Azithromycin, a widely used macrolide antibiotic, is generally well tolerated; however, rare cases of idiosyncratic hepatotoxicity have been reported in the literature. This report describes a case of azithromycin-induced acute hepatocellular liver injury in a previously healthy 20-year-old male who developed jaundice, elevated aminotransferases, and hyperbilirubinemia within three days of initiating a standard three-day course of oral azithromycin 500 mg once daily (cumulative dose 1500 mg) prescribed for an upper respiratory tract infection. Biochemical analysis demonstrated a hepatocellular pattern of injury, confirmed by an R-value of 10.6 (ALT/ULN ÷ ALP/ULN), which exceeds the threshold of 5 required for this classification according to international guidelines. Viral hepatitis and autoimmune etiologies were excluded, and the patient specifically denied the use of dietary supplements, vitamins, bodybuilding or protein powders, and herbal remedies. Causality was assessed using the Roussel Uclaf Causality Assessment Method (RUCAM); on recalculation using the 2016 updated criteria, the score is 6, consistent with a probable causal relationship. The patient recovered fully with supportive care following prompt drug discontinuation, with normalization of liver function tests within eight weeks. This case highlights the importance of accurate biochemical classification of DILI, rigorous causality assessment, and heightened clinical vigilance when prescribing azithromycin even under standard guideline-recommended dosing regimens in young patients without pre-existing liver disease.Keywords: Drug-induced liver injury, Azithromycin, Hepatocellular injury, R-value, DILI, RUCAM, Macrolide hepatotoxicity

Introduction

Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) represents a significant cause of hepatic morbidity, accounting for approximately 5% of cases of jaundice and up to 10% of hospital admissions for acute hepatitis [1]. Antibiotics are among the most frequently implicated drug classes, although antibiotic-induced hepatotoxicity remains under-recognized, occurring in approximately one in 10,000 exposed individuals [2]. Population-based studies estimate an incidence ranging from 1 to 10 cases per 100,000 antibiotic prescriptions [3].

Azithromycin, a semisynthetic macrolide antibiotic, has been widely prescribed for more than two decades owing to its broad antimicrobial spectrum, favorable pharmacokinetic profile, and generally good tolerability [4]. Despite its widespread use, azithromycin-associated liver injury is rare. Published literature has historically described this injury predominantly as cholestatic or mixed-pattern hepatitis; however, accumulating evidence from the Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) and more recent multicenter data indicates that hepatocellular injury may represent the predominant pattern when classified using standardized biochemical criteria [5,6].

The pattern of liver injury in DILI is formally determined using the R-value, calculated as (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN), where ULN denotes the upper limit of normal. An R-value greater than 5 defines a hepatocellular pattern, less than 2 defines a cholestatic pattern, and values between 2 and 5 define a mixed pattern [7]. Accurate pattern classification is clinically important, as it guides differential diagnosis, informs prognosis, and is required for rigorous causality assessment.

Herein, we report a case of azithromycin-induced hepatocellular liver injury in a previously healthy 20-year-old male following a standard three-day regimen, confirmed by R-value calculation and updated RUCAM scoring, with discussion of the differential diagnosis and a review of the relevant literature.

Case presentation

A 20-year-old male with no prior medical history presented to Al-Bukayriah General Hospital in September 2025 with a three-day history of upper abdominal pain, recurrent hematemesis, and intermittent fever with chills. On examination, he was noted to be jaundiced with dark-colored urine.

Three days prior to admission, the patient had consulted his general practitioner for symptoms of an upper respiratory tract infection, including dry cough and sore throat, and was prescribed a standard three-day course of oral azithromycin 500 mg once daily (cumulative planned dose 1500 mg). He denied alcohol consumption, illicit drug use, and the concurrent use of any other potentially hepatotoxic medications, including acetaminophen and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). On directed questioning, he also explicitly denied the use of dietary supplements, vitamins, bodybuilding or protein powders, and herbal or traditional remedies in the weeks preceding admission. He had no personal or family history of liver disease, autoimmune conditions, or hereditary metabolic disorders.

On Day 3 of initiating azithromycin therapy, having completed the full prescribed course and ingested the entire cumulative dose of 1500 mg across three consecutive days, the patient developed progressive nausea, vomiting, hematemesis, and fever, prompting acute hospital admission. Azithromycin was immediately discontinued upon presentation.

The recurrent hematemesis noted at presentation was evaluated clinically. Given the patient's spontaneous clinical stabilization, stable and normal serial hemoglobin values (admission Hb 14.6 g/dL; discharge Hb 14.2 g/dL), and the absence of coagulopathy (INR 1.17), upper endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy, EGD) was deferred; the presentation was judged most consistent with a self-limiting Mallory-Weiss mucosal tear secondary to forceful retching, rather than ischemic hepatitis or variceal hemorrhage (see Diagnostic Assessment and Discussion).

Diagnostic assessment

Laboratory investigations

Laboratory investigations on admission revealed marked hyperbilirubinemia with significantly elevated aminotransferases (ALT and AST), while alkaline phosphatase (ALP) remained only mildly elevated, not exceeding 1.5 times the upper limit of normal at any point during hospitalization. Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) was elevated. Coagulation parameters, including the international normalized ratio (INR), were within acceptable limits throughout the admission. A complete blood count on admission showed a normal total white-cell count of 7.4 × 10 ³ /µL, with a normal differential and no atypical lymphocytosis or eosinophilia, arguing against infectious mononucleosis or an eosinophilic drug-hypersensitivity reaction as the underlying process. R-value calculation and injury pattern classification

To formally classify the pattern of liver injury, the R-value was calculated at peak aminotransferase elevation (Day 1 of admission) using the standard formula: R = (ALT/ULN) ÷ (ALP/ULN) [7]. With peak ALT = 478 U/L (11.95 × ULN) and ALP = 135 U/L (1.13 × ULN), the calculated R-value was 10.6 (Table 2). An R-value of 10.6 clearly exceeds the threshold of 5, confirming a hepatocellular pattern of liver injury [7].

Management and clinical course

Azithromycin was discontinued immediately upon hospital admission. The patient was managed conservatively with supportive therapy, including intravenous omeprazole for gastrointestinal protection and metoclopramide for symptom control. No corticosteroids or specific hepatoprotective agents were administered. N-acetylcysteine (NAC) was considered, in keeping with ACG guidance that NAC may be considered in early or evolving DILI [9]; however, it was withheld given the patient's prompt spontaneous clinical and biochemical improvement, preserved synthetic liver function (normal albumin and INR throughout the admission), and absence of features of acute liver failure. Liver function tests were monitored serially throughout the admission.

Follow-up and outcomes

The patient demonstrated progressive clinical and biochemical improvement following drug discontinuation. Serial liver function tests showed a consistent decline in aminotransferases and bilirubin levels from Day 2 onward. The patient was discharged in a clinically stable condition. Complete normalization of liver function tests was achieved within eight weeks of the initial presentation. The patient remained asymptomatic on follow-up, with no evidence of recurrence or chronic liver injury.

Discussion

Azithromycin is one of the most widely utilized macrolide antimicrobials globally due to its convenient single-daily dosing, high intracellular tissue accumulation, and broad clinical efficacy [4]. Although early case reports predominantly described cholestatic or mixed-pattern hepatitis [12,13], more recent and larger studies indicate that hepatocellular injury may be the most prevalent pattern when formally classified using the R-value [5,6].

The DILIN prospective study, which represents one of the largest systematic analyses of azithromycin-induced hepatotoxicity, identified hepatocellular injury in 10 of 18 patients (55.6%), cholestatic injury in 6 (33.3%), and mixed injury in 2 (11.1%) [5]. This has since been corroborated and extended by the more recent multicenter DILIN study of Conlon et al., who reported a hepatocellular pattern in approximately 60% of 30 azithromycin-associated DILI cases, worse outcomes among younger patients, and a significant association with the HLA-DQA1*03:01 allele — observations that may be relevant to the young age and severity of presentation seen in the present patient [6]. These findings underscore the importance of biochemical pattern classification using the R-value rather than relying on clinical impression alone.

In the present case, the R-value of 10.6 at peak enzyme elevation unequivocally classifies the injury as hepatocellular. This is consistent with published case reports of azithromycin-induced hepatocellular injury, including those reported by Ellison and Blackwell [14] and Wong et al. [15], and the cholestatic case described by Koffas et al., in which liver biopsy confirmed drug-induced hepatic injury [16]. The present case is notable for the exceptionally young age of the patient (20 years), which is well below the mean age of 37 years reported in the DILIN series [5], and is consistent with the observation by Conlon et al. that younger age was associated with more severe outcomes in azithromycin-induced DILI [6].

The prescribed regimen in this case was a standard three-day azithromycin regimen (500 mg once daily for 3 days; total cumulative dose 1500 mg) [4], a recognized alternative to the conventional five-day tapering course. The patient completed the full prescribed course, with symptom onset coinciding with the third and final dose, having ingested the entire cumulative dose of 1500 mg. This demonstrates that acute DILI can develop rapidly even during the course of a standard, guideline-recommended outpatient regimen without supratherapeutic dosing. Azithromycin exhibits unique pharmacokinetics characterized by rapid cellular uptake, extensive tissue sequestration, and a prolonged elimination half-life (~68 h), resulting in sustained hepatic tissue concentrations [4]. In genetically susceptible individuals—such as those harboring predisposing HLA alleles [6]—these concentrations may trigger idiosyncratic immune-mediated hepatocellular injury with a remarkably short latency period.

The clinical presentation in this case merits specific discussion. The occurrence of hematemesis is an atypical feature not commonly described in azithromycin-induced DILI. Ischemic hepatitis secondary to hematemesis-related hemodynamic compromise was considered; however, this diagnosis was excluded on clinical grounds, as the patient demonstrated no evidence of hemodynamic instability, hypotension, or shock. Furthermore, the aminotransferase elevation pattern — peaking at 478 U/L for ALT — is substantially lower than the dramatic elevations (often >1000 U/L) characteristic of ischemic hepatitis [8]. Given the patient's spontaneous clinical stabilization, stable serial hemoglobin values, and normal coagulation parameters (INR 1.17), upper endoscopy was not pursued, and the hematemesis was instead attributed to a self-limiting Mallory-Weiss tear secondary to retching. The LiverTox database similarly notes that the hepatocellular form of azithromycin-induced liver injury is characterized by markedly elevated aminotransferases with alkaline phosphatase values usually less than twice the upper limit of normal, consistent with the biochemical profile observed in this patient [17].

The favorable clinical outcome in this patient, with complete biochemical recovery within eight weeks of drug discontinuation and without the need for advanced therapeutic intervention, is consistent with published reports indicating that most patients with azithromycin-induced DILI achieve full recovery following cessation of the offending drug [5,17]. However, clinicians should remain alert to the potential for severe outcomes, including acute liver failure and, in rare cases, the need for liver transplantation, as illustrated by the case reported by Park et al., in which a patient with azithromycin-induced liver failure required emergency living-donor liver transplantation [18].

Limitations

Several limitations of this report warrant acknowledgment. First, in keeping with current ACG [9] and AASLD [10] guidance on the recommended diagnostic workup for suspected DILI, serologic testing for hepatitis E virus (anti-HEV IgM), Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), and cytomegalovirus (CMV), as well as serum ceruloplasmin for Wilson disease, were not performed, as these investigations were not available at our community hospital at the time of the patient's presentation. This is acknowledged as a recognized limitation of the present report. While the clinical presentation was not suggestive of infectious mononucleosis — with no prominent pharyngitis, no splenomegaly, and no atypical lymphocytosis on the admission complete blood count — formal serological exclusion of these pathogens, together with Wilson disease testing, would have further strengthened the diagnostic workup. Second, liver biopsy was not performed; however, the diagnosis was well supported by the clinical course, biochemical pattern, exclusion of major alternative etiologies, and a RUCAM score of 6 (probable). Third, certain laboratory parameters — including GGT, serum albumin, and INR — were not recorded consistently throughout the admission, limiting the completeness of serial data. Fourth, re-challenge with azithromycin was not performed, in keeping with standard clinical practice given the ethical considerations and potential risks involved. Fifth, upper endoscopy was not performed to directly visualize the source of the hematemesis; this diagnosis was therefore inferred on clinical and laboratory grounds rather than confirmed endoscopically.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates azithromycin-induced hepatocellular liver injury in a previously healthy young male, confirmed by R-value calculation (R = 10.6) and RUCAM causality scoring (score = 6, probable). Accurate biochemical classification of DILI using the R-value is essential and should not be overlooked in clinical practice. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for DILI — including a hepatocellular pattern — in patients presenting with acute liver injury shortly after azithromycin exposure, even under standard guideline-recommended dosing regimens (500 mg once daily for 3 days) and in the absence of pre-existing liver disease or identifiable risk factors. Prompt drug discontinuation and supportive management are the cornerstones of treatment, and prognosis is generally favorable with early recognition.
