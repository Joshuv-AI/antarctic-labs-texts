Geochemical study of trace metals and health risk assessment in Indian geothermal aquifer using health indices, MCS and APCS-MLR
Authors: Tirumalesh Keesari, Bhumika Kumari, Saha Dauji, Annadasankar Roy, Asmita Maitra, Saibal Gupta, Banajarani Panda, Diksha Pant, Diana Anoubam Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fresh water aquifers adjoining the geothermal resources are often vulnerable to trace metal contamination and associated risks to human health. Realistic assessment of health hazard as well as source apportionment play a vital role in designing suitable remedial actions, which can be better achieved through application of probabilistic methods using Monte Carlo Simulations (MCS) and multivariate based Absolute Principal Component Score-Multiple Linear Regression (APCS-MLR) methods. In this study, a comprehensive analysis of groundwater quality was performed using multiple pollution indices (HPI, HEI, Cd, IWPI), MCS and APCS-MLR methods. Chemical results indicate that TDS, F− and NO3− showed exceedances in 19%, 38% and 23% of the samples respectively while trace metals (Fe, Mn, Pb, and As) showed higher exceedances compared to WHO limits. Pollution indices suggest that 73% of the samples fall under low contamination and the rest (27%) in high risk category. MCS infers both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks to different age groups mainly due to arsenic and lead. Sensitivity analysis indicates body weight, ingestion rate as most influential followed by arsenic concentration. High geochemical mobility is noticed for Zn and Co while Al and Ni are largely immobile. Both relative mobility index and APCS-MLR model output point to rock weathering and geothermal sources as the key contributors accounting for 19.8% of the trace metal load in this region. This integrative approach underscores the need for regular monitoring and implementation of policies for safeguarding public health in this region.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49022-z.

Introduction

Volcanic regions and geothermal systems act as a natural source for potentially toxic elements and may pose serious problem for communities depending on local aquifers for potable supply. Due to intense hydrochemical processes, the circulating waters get enriched in a broad spectrum of inorganic elements including trace metals. Furthermore, high temperature and pressure also enhance the formation and transport of a wide array of organic pollutants as well as increasing the potential of environmental contamination if not managed judiciously 48. Besides direct influence of geothermal fluids, mixing of thermal spouts with shallow groundwater may also lead to elevated levels of trace metal in adjoining groundwater and surface water sources. Geothermal activity leading to contamination of drinking water wells with high As was reported by Rodríguez-Cantú et al. 40, where As concentration exceeded 5.9 times the WHO limit. Morales-Simfors and Bundschuh 27 also reported As levels up to 126 mg/L in groundwater from Southern Tibet, China Continental Zone. Another study related to the impact of industrial geothermal operations in Nicaragua on large surface water bodies revealed elevated As concentrations in lake due to discharge of residual water 27.

Similarly, high Mo and U were reported in Mt. Meru Tanzania Continental Rift, Volcanic region 51, Pb and Cd in Lake Bogoria, Kenya Continental Rift, Volcanic region 49, Thallium and Cesium (776 µg/L) in thermal springs of Nagqu geothermal field, Tibet. The hydrochemical characteristics of these contaminated waters indicate distinct types and point to source rocks. For instance, studies at Mt. Meru, Tanzania, directly linked high concentrations of Mo and U in water to interactions with reactive debris, avalanche and volcanic ash deposits 51. In Tengchong geothermal field in China, the high fluoride levels are sourced from the dissolution of fluoride bearing minerals such as mica, fluorite, and apatite, which are abundant in the region’s sandstones, granites, and volcanic rocks 61. Studies also indicated long distance contaminant transport in the downstream of the Zangbo River in Tibet, China adjoining the Yangbajing geothermal plant 62(Guo and Wang 2009).

Visible health effects related to heavy metals and arsenic toxicity from several geothermal fields are reported worldwide 40 64. Health risk assessments conducted in Tunisia and Kenya revealed that dermal exposure posed minimal risk, however, ingestion of geothermal water containing elevated Pb and Cd presented a significant non-carcinogenic risk (Hazard Index, HI > 1), particularly for children. Sunguti et al. 49 reported Pb (mean 0.06 ppm) and Cd (mean 0.05 ppm) levels exceeding WHO limits and inferred that the non-carcinogenic risk associated with Pb and Cd to the local residents from using the geothermal springs of Lake Bogoria, Kenya exceeded the acceptable limits with HQ values (> 1) for both Pb and Cd. In a study by Zhang et al. 63, 60% of sampled geothermal sites showed that children were found to be at a higher health risk due to F contamination compared to adults in North China Plain. The HI exceeded 1 for 12% of children and 7% of adults indicating a significant risk of dental and skeletal fluorosis in Guang’an, China 53.

Studies on health risks due to heavy metals in regions around geothermal sites in India have not been comprehensive. Especially in Eastern India, the effect of heavy metals on human health may be more pronounced due to limited water availability and higher dependency on groundwater. In addition, extensive use of pesticides and fertilizers may further contribute to heavy metal loads to local groundwater since agriculture is the main occupation in this region 60. Several studies have reported presence of high fluoride in the groundwater of this region 3,17,21,30,22. In addition, Barik and Biswal , 4 in their study highlighted groundwater with higher levels of toxic metals like Cr (VI), Se, Mn, Zn, and Cu. Similar studies on toxic elements such as Pb, Cu, Ni, Cd, Fe, and Cr in this region was conducted by Pradhan et al. 37. Health risk assessment studies have suggested ingestion of contaminated water as the exposure pathway and children are identified as the most vulnerable population group due to their lower body weight and developing metabolic systems. Therefore, understanding the presence of contaminants, their impact on human health and geochemical mobility is very important for transparent risk communication and active community engagement to ensure the sustainable and safe management of water resources adjoining geothermal systems.

Multivariate statistical methods have gained popularity in recent times due to their convenience and efficiency for handling regional scale problems. Among the reported multivariate tools, Absolute Principal Component Score-Multiple Linear Regression (APCS-MLR) receptor method developed by Thurston and Spengler 50 has been widely used by researchers as an effective and practical tool for identifying the impact of geogenic processes and anthropogenic activities to groundwater quality and pollution sources 25,56. Groundwater pollution sources and relative contributions were estimated in the Hutuo River basin, China using APCS-MLR 58. Similarly, sources of nitrate pollution in groundwater were quantified and later verified using Bayesian isotope mixing model by 57. Authors have also used APCS-MLR model for source apportionment of heavy metal pollution 44 and demarcate the agricultural drainage factors from discharge of domestic effluent in river streams 11. The PCA-APCS-MLR model offers the advantage of not requiring detailed information on pollution sources. It relies solely on hydrochemical indicators to extract principal components (PCs) and infer pollution sources, thereby reducing both time and cost associated with data collection and analysis.

In this study, geochemical mechanisms responsible for the mobilization and enrichment of trace metals from source rocks into the aqueous phase were assessed along with a comprehensive evaluation of possible human health risks using various pollution indices and simulations. This objective was achieved by analyzing the major ions and trace element concentrations and applying Monte Carlo Simulation (MCS) for probabilistic and sensitivity analyses of carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health risks across different age groups along with estimation of contemporary pollution indices. In addition, we have used APCS-MLR method to quantify the contributions of different sources towards trace metals. The research outcome from this study will be helpful in advancing the understanding of origin of heavy metal elements in aquifers adjoining geothermal sites as well as applicability of integration of conventional hazard indicators and probabilistic approaches to achieve a holistic assessment of the health hazard and planning of sustainable water supplies.

Study area

Climate

The study area is located in the Nayagarh and Khordha districts of Odisha, an eastern state of India, between longitudes 85°18’34.6"E and 85°31’8.4"E and latitudes 20°11’46.3"N and 20°16’43.1"N (Fig. 1). Nayagarh is a landlocked district while Khordha is bordered by a lagoon on one side. These districts lie in regions prone to cyclones and floods. The climate of the study area is classified as subtropical monsoon, with approximately 85% of the annual rainfall occurring during the southwest monsoon. The average annual rainfall is 1,451 mm, with maximum precipitation received during July - August. Recent data indicated a rise in groundwater levels ranging from 0.69 to 5.21 m in Nayagarh and 0.05 to 6.15 m in Khordha while groundwater levels declined in some parts of Khorda from 0.05 to 1.49 m 5. The average temperatures during summer and winters are ~ 45 °C and ~ 22 °C respectively.

Geology

The study area comprises three major geological formations, (i) Precambrian consolidated formations (found in Khordha and parts of Nayagarh), (ii) Semi-consolidated Gondwana and Tertiary formations (in Khordha), and (iii) Unconsolidated Quaternary formations (along the coastal tract of Khordha). The Precambrian consolidated formations consist of hard crystalline and metamorphosed rocks from the Precambrian era. Common rock types in this region include granite gneisses, schistose rocks, chondrites, shale, quartzites, calc-silicates, phyllite, sandstone, and charnockites. The semi-consolidated formations primarily consist of sediments from the Gondwana and Baripada beds. These include rocks such as sandstones, siltstones, shale, conglomeratic beds, stratified semi-consolidated sand beds with intercalated shale, and fossiliferous limestone (Fig. 1a). The unconsolidated formations consist mostly of alluvium. The study area falls within the Mahanadi geothermal province, which is part of the non-orogenic belt. Notable geothermal features in the region include the Atri Hot Spring in Khordha district and the Tarabalo Hot Spring in Nayagarh district. The Atri Hot Spring is situated in a lateritic terrain within the Eastern Ghats rock province, while the Tarabalo Hot Spring lies in a granitic terrain overlain by soil and alluvium 5.

Methodology

Sample collection

All the sampling and measurements are performed with the standard pre-defined methods for this study 1. A total of twenty-six representative samples were collected from the study area and measured for physicochemical parameters (pH, EC, TDS, Dissolved Oxygen and Temperature) using a multiparameter kit (Hanna Make, HI 9829–11042) (Fig. 1b). The maps in Fig. 1 are prepared using QGIS software version 3.24.3 (https://qgis.org) from data source https://indiawris.gov.in/wris/#/geoSpatialData (accessed on 9th April 2026). The multiparameter kit was calibrated using a HANNA quick calibration solution before the commencement of sampling and after every ten samples during field measurements. Samples were filtered through the 0.45 μm filter to remove the suspended materials and stored in clean and dry Tarson bottles for laboratory measurements. Samples were kept at ~ 4 °C in the laboratory to inhibit microbial activity.

Major ion measurements were carried out using ion chromatography (DX-500). Analytical column AS-11 was used for separating anions while CS-12 was used for cation separation. The eluted ions were detected by conductivity detector. A loop of 25µL was used for sample injection and multi-level mixed ion standards bracketing the ion concentrations in samples were used for calibration purpose. The analytical precision of the measurements is 2–5%.

Bicarbonate (HCO3−) was estimated from field alkalinity measured by Gran titration method. The accuracy of the major ion measurements was also verified using charge balance error (CBE, Eq. 1). The CBE was found to be within the allowed limits of ± 5%. Samples were measured for trace elements and heavy metals such as Al, As, Zn, Li, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb using Quadrupole ICP-MS (ThermoFisher iCAP RQ model). For quantification, a multi-element standard was used. The plasma power was operated at 1300 W and the mass resolution was 6000. Pollution level assessment

Heavy Metal Pollution Index (HPI) is adopted in this study to evaluate overall metal pollution in water. HPI provides a comprehensive index taking in to account the uncertainty in the weight assignment for individual ions based on the persistent hazard and toxicity effects. These factors underline the magnitude of the heavy metal pollution in water sources and associated human health risks. The HPI is calculated using Eq. 2 26.2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:HPI=\frac{\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{W}_{i}{Q}_{i}}{\sum\:_{i=1}^{n}{W}_{i}}$$\end{document}

Where, Wi is assigned weight of the ith parameter, Qi is the sub-index value of the ith parameter and n is total number of parameters used for indexing. The sub-index (Qi) is calculated for each parameter using Eq. 3 26.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Q}_{i}=\:\frac{(\:{C}_{i}-{L}_{i})}{(\:{P}_{i}-{L}_{i})}$$\end{document}

In the above equation, Ci represents measured concentration of the ith parameter. Li and Pi are desirable limits and maximum permissible limits respectively for ith parameter. There are three categories defined based on the calculated HPI, viz., low, medium, and high heavy metal pollution for HPI values < 40, 40–80, and > 80 respectively.

Heavy Metal Evaluation Index (HEI) is also used to estimate the level of heavy metal in water, potential human health risks, and toxicity 8; (Eq. 4).4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:HEI = \:\sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {\frac{{H_{c} }}{{H_{{max}} }}}$$\end{document}

Here, Hc (µg/L) is the metal concentration, and Hmax (µg/L) is the maximum permissible limit of metal. The level of contamination is categorized into low, medium and high for HEI values < 28, 28–56, and > 56 respectively.

The Contamination Degree Index (Cd) is another indicator to appraise the quality of water samples and heavy metal-led contamination 8; (Eq. 5).5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:C_{d} = \:\sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {C_{{Fi}} }$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{Fi}=\:\frac{{C}_{Ai}}{{C}_{Si}}-1$$\end{document}

Where, CFi is the contamination fit index for the ith parameter. CFi is calculated using Eq. 6 where CAi represents the measured concentration of the metal and CSi is the standard value i.e., maximum permissible limit. All the enumerated values of the contamination fit index for each parameter are summed into one single value i.e., Cd, for water samples. Here, the contamination level is categorized into low, medium, and high for Cd values < 23, 23–46, > 46 respectively.

Integrated Water Pollution Index (IWPI), a new pollution index, is also applied in this study 14,19. This index can be used for a large number of samples and for diverse pollutants, like biological, chemical, and physical responsible for the major alteration of water quality. Here, pollution load (PL) is estimated (Eq. 7) and evaluated using measured values (Ic) of each parameter and its standard limits (Sd).7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:PL=1+\left[\frac{{{I}_{c}-S}_{d}}{{S}_{d}}\right]$$\end{document}

All the calculated PL values of parameters are integrated into a single value to develop IWPI for each sample (Eq. 8).8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:IWPI = \:\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {PL}$$\end{document}

There are four categories based on the IWPI values, viz., excellent (< 0.5), good (0.5–0.75), moderately polluted (0.75–1) and highly polluted water (> 1).

Carcinogenic analysis

Carcinogenic risk (ILCR) is evaluated using Eq. 1047.10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ILCR=ADD\:\times\:CSF$$\end{document}

In the above equation, ILCR represents the Incremental Lifetime Carcinogenic Risk, ADD is defined in Sect. 3.3 and CSF represents the Cancer Slope Factor, related to lifetime exposure to the average amount of one mg/kg/day of carcinogenic heavy metals. The CSF for heavy metals is pre-determined by extensive scientific research on nature of the metals and their impact on the water quality.

The evaluated ILCR values for each metal are summed and expressed as a single value known as total carcinogenic risk, CRT (Eq. 11).11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{CR}_{T}={ILCR}_{Ni}{+ILCR}_{As}{+ILCR}_{Pb}$$\end{document}

Here, the CRT accounts for Ni, As and Pb values and values between 10− 6 and 10− 4 are safe with regard to carcinogenic risk52.

Hazard quotient (HQ)

The Hazard Quotient (HQ) is defined as the ratio of the mean dose of carcinogenic or non-carcinogenic metal in daily life (ADD) to the reference dose (RfD) of that metal 35(Eq. 12).12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:HQ=\:\frac{ADD}{{R}_{f}D}$$\end{document}13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$THQ = \sum\limits_{{i = 1}}^{n} {HQ_{i} }$$\end{document}

Total Hazard Quotient (THQ) is the summation of all the evaluated HQ values of metals for water samples (Eq. 13). THQ = 1 is assigned as the threshold value and THQ < 1 is acceptable and > 1 is considered as unsuitable for drinking purposes.

Probabilistic hazard/ risk analysis and sensitivity analysis

A probabilistic approach was also employed in this study, to account for uncertainties in estimating both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks due to consumption of contaminated water. The calculation of the ADD involves multiple variable parameters, which can differ across various population groups or individuals and the resulting Incremental Lifetime Cancer Risk (ILCR) and Target Hazard Quotient (THQ) values derived from specific ADD inputs are used to evaluate uncertainty7. Two primary simulation methods exist for such uncertainty assessments: parametric and non-parametric. Here, a parametric method is utilized and Monte Carlo Simulation (10000 MCS) is conducted.

The variables involved in the analysis - such as contaminant concentration, Body Weight (BW), Ingestion Rate (IR), and Exposure Frequency (EF) - are assumed to be largely uncorrelated. As a result, a computationally efficient local sensitivity analysis technique28, was applied for assessing the influence of these input factors on THQ and ILCR outcomes.

Source apportionment using APCS-MLR receptor model

To investigate the contributions of latent hydrogeochemical processes to specific water quality parameters, a source apportionment study was conducted using Absolute Principal Component Scores-Multiple Linear Regression (APCS-MLR) model50. The study aimed to quantify the influence of various processes on ions such as Al, As, Zn, Li, Co, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb.

The numeric columns were standardized to eliminate any scaling bias, transforming them into z-scores (mean = 0, standard deviation = 1). The standardized data matrix Z is represented as;14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Z=\frac{X-\mu\:}{\sigma\:}$$\end{document}

Where, X is raw data matrix, µ is variable wise mean and σ is variable wise standard deviation. The unrotated principal components were subjected to an orthogonal Varimax rotation to improve interpretability. The rotation is mathematically represented as:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{L}_{rot}={L}_{unrot}\cdot\:R$$\end{document}

Where, R is the rotation matrix. The rotated loadings were used to calculate the Absolute Principal Component Scores (APCS) for each sample, representing the contribution of each principal component to the specific water quality parameters. This step was essential for estimating the contributions of latent sources and was performed using the following equation:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:APCS=Z\cdot\:{W}_{rot}-{A}_{0}$$\end{document}

Where, Wrot is score coefficient matrix. For each target ion, a Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) model was fit using the APCS scores as predictors. The MLR model can be expressed as:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:y={\beta\:}_{0}+{\beta\:}_{1}AP{CS}_{1}+{\beta\:}_{2}AP{CS}_{2}+\dots\:+{\beta\:}_{k}AP{CS}_{k}+ϵ$$\end{document}

The analysis also involved calculating the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) for each principal component to assess multicollinearity. The percent contribution of each principal component was determined for each ion, which was calculated as:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{Pct}}_{i}=\frac{\left|APC{S}_{ij}\cdot\:{\beta\:}_{j}\right|}{{\sum\:}_{j}\left|APC{S}_{ij}\cdot\:{\beta\:}_{j}\right|}\times\:100$$\end{document}

A stacked bar plot was generated to visualize the mean percentage contributions of the principal components for each ion, providing a representation of the sources influencing the water quality parameters. R², Adjusted R², and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) were calculated to assess the model’s performance, with R² measuring the proportion of variance explained, Adjusted R² adjusting for the number of predictors to prevent overfitting, and RMSE providing a measure of the average prediction error. The scikit-learn36, statsmodels12,24,34,43, and matplotlib python packages13 were used in Google Colab® environment for PCA, regression analysis, statistical diagnostics, data manipulation, and visualization in the APCS-MLR method.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical parameters

The analytical data is provided in the Supplementary Information (Table S-1). The release of carbon dioxide from aquatic fauna and flora influences the pH of groundwater, which may impact the taste and odor of groundwater. The pH values of the water samples fall between 6.25 and 8.95 with a mean value of 7.51 and all of the samples have pH within the guideline values of 6.5–8.5 55. The EC values ranged between 297 and 3516 µS/cm and have a mean value of 612 µS/cm while TDS varied from 150 to 1759 mg/L (mean value: 612 mg/L). Changes in the values of both EC and TDS are directly linked with the concentration of dissolved ions. High TDS is responsible for the hardness, bitter taste, and may lead to constipation and laxative effects in humans 55. About 19% of samples have TDS values above the permissible limit 55. The water temperature changes the biological structure of the water sources. High temperature reduces the dissolved oxygen and causes fatality of bio-aquatic components. In this study area, the temperature was from 24 to 31.7 °C (mean value: 29 °C) for shallow aquifer and 47–58 °C (mean value: 52 °C) for thermal sources. The dissolved oxygen ranges from 0.28 to 4.3 mg/L with an average value of 1.47 mg/L.

Chemical parameters

The major ion data is provided in Supplementary Information (Table S2). Alkali (Na+ and K+) and alkaline earth metal (Ca2+and Mg2+) ions are reviewed for water quality and human-related health threats. Na+ in water samples is in the range of 18–296 mg/L with a mean value of 103 mg/L and K+ is in the range of 0.41–152 mg/L with a mean value of 12 mg/L. The presence of these alkali metal ions is imperative, but excessive amounts may cause minor health effects. Na+ concentration exceeding 200 mg/L will execute bitter taste of water and in another case, the concentration of K+ in water more than the purview of 82 mg/L results in abdominal malfunctioning. For both alkali metal ions, only 4% of samples are exceeding the guideline limit set by WHO 55.

In the case of alkaline earth metal ions, Ca2+ in water samples lie within the range of 5.9–246 mg/L with a mean value of 82 mg/L. All the water samples contain Ca2+ within the guideline value set by WHO 55 i.e., 300 mg/L. The Mg2+ varies from 0.17 to 101 mg/L having a mean of 26 mg/L. Only 4% of the water samples exceeded the Mg2+ ion concentration guideline value set by WHO 55. Although both Ca2+and Mg2+ are vital for human health as they play important roles in biological systems both are also liable for health-related risks. The Ca2+ ion rich water may lead to kidney dysfunction and Ca2+ deficit water results in hypertension. The consumption of Mg2+ rich water has laxative, cathartic, and diuretic effects on human beings 55.

Fluoride (F−) concentrations range from 0.29 to 13 mg/L with a mean of 3.6 mg/L and 38% of water samples exceed the guideline value of 1.5 mg/L set by WHO 55. The NO3− in water samples varies from 0.24 to 134 mg/L with a mean of 26 mg/L where about 23% of samples exceeded the guideline value set by WHO 55 (50 mg/L). The ingestion of nitrate rich water may lead to serious health hazards like blue-baby syndrome in infants 55. Previous studies indicate that 30% and 34.9% of the water samples are contaminated with F− and NO3− ions respectively 30. Nitrate and fluoride contamination in groundwater originates from distinct sources and follow different mobilization pathways. Elevated nitrate levels are mainly linked to anthropogenic activities such as fertilizer intensive agricultural; through irrigation return flow, sewage infiltration, and septic leachate, which facilitate rapid nitrate leaching through soil into aquifers. High nitrate levels in groundwater are reported across many Odisha districts 6. In contrast, high fluoride levels can be attributed to intensified water–rock interaction under elevated temperatures, which enhances the dissolution of fluoride-bearing minerals such as fluorite, biotite, apatite, hornblende, and mica 20. Elevated fluoride in drinking water can lead to dental and skeletal fluorosis, causing mottled teeth, joint pain, bone deformities, and mobility issues 6.

The Cl− in water samples is found to range between 16 and 694 mg/L with a mean of 162 mg/L. About 19% of water samples have higher Cl− above the guideline value set by WHO 55 (250 mg/L). Laxative effects and inhibition of microbial activity essential for humans are some of the health hazards related to ingestion of Cl− rich water 55. HCO3− is responsible for alkalinity in water and it ranges from 23 to 587 mg/L with a mean value of 236 mg/L. About 12% of the samples exceeded the acceptable value set by WHO 55 for HCO3−, i.e., 500 mg/L. The SO42− in water samples is in the range of 3.44–75 mg/L with a mean of 28 mg/L. The SO42− rich water causes dehydration and bitter taste to water 55. All the water samples show SO42− values within the permissible limit set by WHO 55 i.e., 250 mg/L.

Trace metals and associated health risks

The trace metal data is provided in Supplementary Information (Table S-3). Aluminium is the third most abundant trivalent element in the environment and in the present study it is found to range from 0.009 to 0.31 mg/L (mean value: 0.083 mg/L), only two samples marginally exceeded the WHO 55 limit (0.2 mg/L). There are some health effects like diarrhoea, nausea, arthritic pain, and mouth ulcers due to an increase in the concentration of Al in the human body. Alzheimer’s disease is the most serious issue of exposure to Al 55. The Fe content in the water samples ranged between 0.16 and 15.9 mg/L (mean value: 2.76 mg/L). About 77% of water samples contain excess iron content above the guideline value WHO 55 i.e., 0.3 mg/L. Fe is an essential element responsible for binding and carrying oxygen within the human body, but an excess amount results in a metallic taste and brownish colour of the water. Zinc ranges from 0.064 mg/L to 2.65 mg/L with a mean value of 0.558 mg/L. All the samples show Zn concentration within the guideline value set by WHO 55 for Zn i.e., 5 mg/L. Weak metabolism and haematological disorder are few of the health effects due to high Zn content in the human body. Arsenic can lead to acute poisoning in infants/children and malfunctioning of cell respiration 55. The As content in the water samples ranged between 0.04 and 61 µg/L (mean value: 15.2 µg/L). About 50% of the water samples show high concentration of As greater than 10 µg/L. Arsenic enrichment in groundwater is governed by a combination of natural geochemical processes and anthropogenic activities. With the dissolution of Fe and Mn oxyhydroxides its mobilization increases. In alluvial aquifers like present study area, groundwater chemistry indicates that carbonate weathering and the breakdown of Fe–Mn oxyhydroxides facilitate the release of adsorbed arsenic into the aqueous phase. Additionally, human-induced factors such as the infiltration of organic matter–rich surface water and agricultural fertilizer inputs can further dislocate sediment-bound arsenic, enhancing its mobility 18,41. The dissolved oxygen has inverse relation with As concentrations 59. Overall, arsenic release is influenced by both rock–water interactions and external inputs that modify redox conditions and sediment surface chemistry.

The Cu concentration in the water samples ranged between 0.0013 and 0.204 mg/L (mean value: 0.085 mg/L). All of the samples are under the acceptable value set by WHO 55 for the Cu content in water.Mn takes part in many physiological processes and is also essential for photosynthesis The Mn content in the water samples ranged between 0.0041 and 41.8 mg/L (mean value: 0.513 mg/L), and about 69% of the samples exceeded the guideline value set by WHO 55 i.e., 0.08 mg/L. Mn takes part in many physiological processes and is also essential for photosynthesis, however, Mn toxicity may lead to difficulty walking, and facial muscle spasms .

The concentration of Ni in water samples ranged from 0.0005 to 0.0032 mg/L with a mean value of 0.002 mg/L. The guideline value for Ni content in water is 0.07 mg/L, which is set by WHO 55 and all the samples fall within the guideline value. Long-term exposure can lead to loss of body weight, cardiovascular disease, and liver failure (USEPA 1995). The Pb content in the water samples ranged between 0.004 and 0.035 mg/L (mean value: 0.014 mg/L). About 69% of water samples show marginal contamination due to excess Pb (WHO limit 0.01 mg/L). Lead in groundwater originates from the weathering of Galena, with secondary minerals such as Cerussite and Anglesite forming during oxidation. Unlike As, Pb mobility is more sensitive to pH and adsorption processes, being more soluble under acidic conditions and strongly adsorbed onto clay minerals, iron–manganese oxides, and organic matter under neutral to alkaline pH. Ion exchange, high TDS, and changes in groundwater chemistry can remobilize Pb, while anthropogenic sources such as industrial effluents, urban runoff, sewage leakage, and legacy mining activities often contribute additional lead to shallow aquifers. Lead is an extremely toxic heavy metal and it affects the photosynthesis process of plants. Pb replaces essential bi-valent (Ca2+) and monovalent (Na+) elements in the human body, which alters the biological processes like enzyme regulation, maturation, cell adhesion, inter- and intra-cellular signalling, and ionic transportation. Lead toxicity may result in memory loss and neurological disorders 10. Increasing contamination levels of Pb and Ni leading to water degradation was noted in a study by Muduli et al. 29. Sr2+ is essential for healthy bones and teeth but may result in toxicity. Sr2+ content in the water samples ranged between 0.027 and 1.67 mg/L (mean value: 0.398 mg/L). About 41% of water samples contain Sr2+ concentration greater than 1.5 mg/L, which is set by WHO 55.

Health risk assessment using pollution indices

HPI, HEI, and Cd are calculated for metals viz., Li, Al, Pb, Mn, Fe, Zn, and Cu. The HPI ranges from − 6 to 110 (mean value: 39.8). In this interpretation, positive values indicate exceedance of permissible limits, whereas negative values represent water quality that is lower than the prescribed standards. Several studies have treated pollution or water quality indices in this manner, allowing values to vary freely instead of truncating them to zero 8,38. The level of heavy metal contamination is distributed into three categories based on the HPI values. About 76.92% of the water samples are less contaminated with heavy metals (HPI: < 40%) while 11% each have medium (HPI: 40–80) and high contamination levels (HPI: > 80%) with heavy metals. Similar observations were drawn by Reza and Singh 39, reporting elevated levels of heavy metals in water samples from Odisha, with mean HPI value of 52.2 during the pre-monsoon and 41.5 in the post-monsoon seasons. Other studies also reported heavy metal contamination in about 16% of the collected samples from the coastal regions of Odisha 31.

Water quality affected by heavy metal contamination was also classified based on the HEI values, viz., low (HEI < 28), medium (HEI 28–56) and high contamination (HEI > 56). In the study area, the water samples showed HEI values from 1.7 to 56.1 with an average value of 12.8. 19 out of 26 samples (73%) fall in the low contamination category (HEI < 28) while 4% and 23% of the samples fall in medium (28–56) and high (> 56) contamination categories respectively. Degree of contamination (Cd) values ranged from − 5.3 to 49.1 (mean value: 5.8) and most samples (73%) fall under safe category (Cd < 23) while 27% fall under high contamination category. None of the samples fall within the medium contamination category (23–46) with regard to Cd. Based on the outcome of all these three indices, it is evident that majority of the samples (~ 73%) exhibit low levels of heavy metal contamination and only ~ 20% of the samples are affected by heavy metal contamination.

IWPI is employed to understand the direct relationship among the concentration of chemical parameters and their impact on the water quality. The water quality is categorized into four different classes, excellent (IWPI < 0.5), good (0.5–0.75), moderately polluted (0.75–1) and highly polluted (> 1). For this purpose, a total of 19 input parameters were examined viz., TDS, cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Na+ and K+), anions (F−, Cl−, NO3−, SO42− and HCO3−), metals (Al, Fe, Zn, As, Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb and Sr). The order of pollution load by these parameters is found to be: Fe > Mn > F−> As > Pb > Cl−> TDS > NO3−> Na+> HCO3− > Ca2+> Sr > Mg2+> K+ > SO42−> Zn > Al > Cu > Ni. In the metal class; Fe, Mn, As, and Pb are responsible for the pollution load in the water samples, while other parameters viz., F−, Cl−, TDS, and NO3− also contribute to pollution. IWPI for the water samples ranged from 0.42 to 4.14 (mean value: 1.31). About 12% of the samples were found to be in an excellent category followed by 15% samples of good, 27% samples moderately polluted (0.75–1) and the rest (46%) fall under highly polluted category. Similar trends were reported by Nazneen et al. 32 with regard to heavy metal contamination (Fe > Mn > Cu > Pb > Zn > Ni) in water samples from Odisha.

Carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic health risk

In this study, ADD is calculated for Zn, As, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb and these values were used to estimate both carcinogenic risk (CRT) and non-carcinogenic risk (HQ). Among these metals, Ni, As, and Pb are evaluated for carcinogenic vulnerability in infants, females, and males. The CRT value for infants ranged from 0.17 to 7.84, with a mean value of 2.14. For females, CRT varied between 0.13 and 6.26 (mean value: 1.71) while for males, it ranged from 0.15 to 6.95 with an average value of 1.90. Adults (females and males) are at possible risk of cancer due to higher exposure and consumption rates, while infants remain susceptible to early-age cancer-related risks due to lower body weight, boiled water intake, and higher water intake for unit bodyweight. A critical and recurring conclusion from numerous independent studies is that infants are the most vulnerable population group2,40(Zhang et al. 2025). Due to their lower body weight, higher water intake relative to their weight, and developing physiology, infants consistently exhibit higher CRT values compared adults exposed to the same contaminated water. Nayak and Nandimandalam31 also reported ILCR values exceeding 1.0 × 10− 3, indicating the possibility of carcinogenic risks associated with Ni and As in the water samples.

The THQ is calculated to assess the non-carcinogenic health risk associated with Zn, As, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Pb across the three groups: infants, females and males. The THQ values ranged from 0.75 to 17.69 (mean value: 5.20) for infants and 92% may pose non-carcinogenic risk (THQ < 1). In the case of female, the THQ values ranged from 0.60 to 14.13 (mean: 4.15) and ~ 77% of the samples suggest non-carcinogenic risk (THQ > 1). Whereas for the male group, the estimated THQ ranged from 0.67 to 15.69 (mean: 4.61), with 85% samples pose possible risk. Similar observation was reported by Sahu et al.42 and they found order of THQ as: As > Pb > Zn > Cu.

Probabilistic and sensitivity analysis

Variability and uncertainties in the body weight (BW), ingestion rate (IR), and exposure frequency (EF) of each age group as well as sampling heterogeneity was addressed by adopting probabilistic risk analysis. This helps in robust assessment of carcinogenic and non-carcinogenic risks due to drinking contaminated water. In this study, THQ and ILCR for infants, females, and males are examined with probabilistic estimates for both non-carcinogenic and carcinogenic risks. In addition, sensitivity analysis is also performed to identify the most sensitive parameter responsible for the health hazard in infants, females and males.

Risk analysis for infants showed ILCR values; 1.535 × 10− 1, 1.5018, and 7.6038 for the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles respectively indicating a wide range of potential carcinogenic risk (Fig. 2). On the basis of sensitivity data, BW, IR and As together contribute to ~ 97% of total carcinogenic risk. The sensitivity trend for infants follows the order: BW (52.97%) > As (29.56%) > IR (14.49%) > Pb (2.53%) (Fig. 2a). For females, the probability of carcinogenic risk shifts from 1.368 × 10− 1 at 5th percentile to 1.2137 at 50th percentile, and further to 5.770 at 95th percentile (Fig. 2b). The sensitivity analysis indicates the order as As (44.55%) > BW (25.98%) > IR (24.99%) > Pb (3.82%) for female group. The carcinogenic risk for male group varies with ILCR values showing 1.8 × 10− 1, 1.4267 and 6.1858 at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 2c). The sensitivity analysis for males followed the order As (49%) > BW (28.06%) > IR (18.01%) > Pb (4.2%). Results indicate that BW is sensitive factor for infants as compared to females and males, where As is more sensitive factor. IR is the third-most sensitive parameter followed by Pb, which together contribute to < 5% for all the age groups.

Probabilistic risk analysis of THQ ranged from 0.5223 to 17.4438 for 5th to 95th percentiles with the median value of 3.8744. This indicates the varying potential of non-carcinogenic risk to the infants. On the basis of sensitivity data, BW, IR and As together contribute to ~ 95% and the order is found to be BW (52.81%) > As (29.47%) > IR (14.44%) > Pb (2.53%) for infants towards non-carcinogenic risk (Fig. 3a). For females, probabilities of non-carcinogenic risk were found to be 0.4703, 3.1505, and 13.1424 in the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles respectively (Fig. 3b). Sensitivity analysis indicated the order As (44.34%) > BW (25.85%) > IR (24.87%) > Pb (3.8%) towards non-carcinogenic risk for females. In case of males, the non-carcinogenic risk was found to be 0.6595, 3.7152, and 13.9374 at 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles, respectively (Fig. 3c) and the order of sensitivity was As (48.74%) > BW (27.92%) > IR (17.91%) > Pb (4.18%). BW and As were found to be most critical parameters that may influence the health risks.

Trace metal geochemistry

The presence of trace metals in groundwater mainly depends on the solubility of minerals and hydrochemical characteristics such as pH, TDS, DO and other persisting ions. Alkali and alkaline earth metals exhibit high solubility and are commonly derived from geological formation such as Sandstone, Limestone, Shale, Granite, Khondalites, Charnockites, Quartzites, Calcsilicates, Phyllite, and Marble, etc. 5. On the other hand, trace elements like As, Ni, and Si are moderately soluble and trace metals like, Pb, Zn, Cu, Mn, Co, Al, and Fe exhibit very limited solubility. These metals often release into the groundwater mainly from the secondary minerals. From the plot of LogƮ versus Log K values it can be clearly indicated that alkali and alkaline earth metals fall in the most soluble group, whereas other trace metals show moderate to insoluble nature (Fig. 4a). The mobility of the trace metals was assessed by normalization and comparing with corresponding values of Upper Continental Crust (UCC). From the plot of elemental concentration in UCC versus the collected groundwater, it can be observed that the majority of the trace metals of interest fall in intermediate mobility zone (Fig. 4b). Elements such as Zn and Co are mobile while Co, Cu, Sr, Mn, Pb, Li, and As exhibit intermediate mobility. In contrast, Al, Fe and Ni are largely immobile. The mobility of Zn and Co, along with their positive correlation between groundwater and UCC, indicates the continental flow of these elements from the surface into the aquifers. The immobility of Al, Fe, and Ni reflects their depleted presence in groundwater.

Trace elements such as Zn, Sr, Cu, Co, Pb, and As showed less variability in their RM values. It is also observed that the order RM values are similar to the order of ion concentrations in groundwater, irrespective of the host rock composition. High RM variation is mainly exhibited by most of the studied trace metals suggesting the influence of various natural processes such as precipitation, sorption, preferential release, dissolution, and meteoric alteration. From the plot, it can be observed that Sr, Zn, Cu, Co, Pb andAs were found to demonstrate high RM while Fe, Mn, Ni, Al display low RM (Fig. 5a). Based on the earlier research on the hydrogeochemical behavior of trace metals, it can be suggested that Al, Ni, Fe, and Mn concentrations are mainly linked to congenial weathering processes and aquifer mineralogy 15. In addition, pH and DO exerts a strong control on the mobility of many trace elements due to formation of oxyanions under oxic (oxygen-rich) and alkaline (high pH) conditions. On the other hand, Fe and Mn levels in groundwater strongly correlate with climatic factors like precipitation and temperature. Besides, acidification also enhances the dissolution of trace metals such as Fe, Co, and Ni from aquifer sediments 23. Collectively, these findings underscore that the high variability in RM values of trace metals in groundwater reflects the combined influence of dissolution and weathering, oxidation and precipitation, sorption and desorption, and recharge driven geochemical evolution.

Among the trace elements, Zn and Co showed high mobility, i.e., RM > 1. Seven elements viz., Fe, Mn, Pb, Sr, Cu, and As fall under the moderate mobile category (RM 0.01 to 1) while two elements Ni and Al fall under the immobile category with RM < 0.01, indicating that they are more readily attenuated, or removed from the water, through chemical reactions like precipitation or adsorption (Fig. 5b). The immobility of Ni can be attributed to its divalent nature and its strong adsorption onto oxyhydroxides or clay minerals. Al is immobile or least mobile with RM < 0.001 due to the formation of oxyhydroxides under oxic conditions, on the contrary, Zn and Co exhibit better mobility than the other trace metals and have almost equal values of RM. Higher mobility of Zn and Co could be due to the formation of carbonate complexes in the low pH condition 9. The low pH of the groundwater samples prevents the selective scavenging of Zn by lowering its sorption affinity onto secondary minerals such as iron oxides and carbonates. Low alkalinity results in low mobility, whereas low pH lowers the sorption rate of the elements by promoting high mobility of the trace metals 33. Fe forms less soluble and stable products such as iron oxides and carbonates in oxic conditions, leading to precipitation of Fe and reducing Fe concentration entering groundwater. In oxidizing conditions, iron is immobilized as Fe(III) hydrous oxide. Mobility of Pb, Sr, and Cu are majorly affected by reduced dissolution of Fe-Mn oxides as well as ion exchange mechanisms. Arsenic can be immobilized through adsorption onto hydrous oxide surfaces under oxic conditions. Under strong reducing conditions, As can be removed by forming sulfide mineral precipitate. The moderate behavior of As is due to the limiting redox potential and presence of adsorbing species like Fe2O3. As (III) is trapped by the Fe (III), whereas As (V) is not efficiently scavenged, resulting in As showing moderate mobility.

The PCA varimax rotated loadings for the trace metals in both thermal and non-thermal samples is shown in Fig. 6a. Five principal components (PCs) are found to be significant with eigen values more than one. These five PCs 1 to 5, before varimax rotation explain a variance of 31.2%, 19.8%, 13.5%, 10.3% and 7.7% respectively explaining total variance of 82.5%. The factor loadings show that PC1 has high loadings of Temp, Li and Ni, but also show marginal loadings of Si and Ni. This trend indicates both lithogenic influences and anthropogenic enrichment. In addition, it can be suggested that thermal water mixes with shallow groundwater leading to increased Li concentration. Studies on mineral-water interactions on enriched trace metal concentration have indicated thermal water influence on dissolution of specific minerals 53,54. PC2 has high loadings for Co, Cu and Ni and shows strong anthropogenic inputs, likely from agricultural practices, industrial activities, and surface contamination. Negative loading of Pb suggests that ion-exchange or adsorption processes governing the trace metal migration in the aquifers. PC3 showed high loadings for Al and Zn and low for Mn while negative for pH. These trends suggest a combination of industrial discharge, corrosion-related metal release, and redox-sensitive mobilization processes in groundwater. PC4 showed high loadings for Mn and Fe and low for Si whereas negative loadings for Al and As. Oxidation-reduction reactions alter the mobilization of redox-sensitive elements such as Fe, Mn and As. This observation points to different various geochemical processes controlling trace metal mobilization. PC5 show high loadings of Sr, TDS, Mn and As. This component though has lower contribution, but remains consistent with As and Mn along with TDS and Sr. This could be due to higher TDS aiding in mobilization of trace metals as well as alkaline earth metal. From the PCs it can be observed that factors are inter-related and mainly indicate contribution of water-rock interactions along with thermal water influence, ion exchange and redox chemistry. Thus, multivariate statistical analysis indicates that trace metal concentrations in both thermal and non-thermal groundwater are controlled by a combination of geogenic and anthropogenic sources. A major geogenic contribution arises from water–rock interaction and geothermal mixing, particularly reflected by elevated temperature. The concentration of Li and Ni may be due to mineral dissolution and thermal fluid influence. Anthropogenic inputs are linked to agricultural practices, sewage intrusion, industrial discharge, and surface contamination, contributing to metals such as Co, Cu, Zn, As, and Pb. In addition, redox reactions and ion-exchange processes play an important role in regulating the mobility of Fe, Mn, and As. Overall, the results demonstrate integrated effects of lithogenic weathering, geothermal processes, and human activities govern groundwater chemistry.

To further substantiate the understanding on the contribution of each of these components towards trace metal concentration in groundwater, APCS-MLR was performed and the result output is shown in Fig. 6b (Table S4). The analysis revealed that Co exhibits the best model fit with an R² of 0.941 and a low RMSE of 12.26, indicating accurate predictions. Mn shows the weakest fit with an R² of 0.682, an Adjusted R² of 0.602, and a high RMSE of 559.95, reflecting large prediction errors. Other ions, such as Ni and Pb, demonstrate good model performance with relatively high R² values and low RMSE values, suggesting relatively better prediction accuracy. Zn, however, has a notably high RMSE of 259.77, indicating substantial prediction errors likely due to variability, outliers or unresolved processes explained by PCA. The factor contributions of various ions to the principal components (PCs) reveal distinct geochemical patterns. Al shows a strong contribution from PC3 (29.4%), suggesting influence of industrial discharge and redox processes. As is highly influenced by PC2 (28.5%), reflecting anthropogenic source contributions like agriculture as well as industrial activities. Zn is predominantly explained by PC3 (37.9%), suggesting complex sources influencing mobilisation. Li is largely contributed by PC1 (57%), suggesting primarily thermal water influence. Co is mostly explained by PC2 (62.4%), highlighting strong anthropogenic inputs. Cu is also highly influenced by PC2 (55.6%), pointing to industrial and agricultural source contributions. Mn shows a mixed influence, with considerable contributions from PC4 (15.1%) and PC5 (12.2%). Ni has a strong association with PC1 (51.4%), indicating lithogenic and thermal water influences, while Pb shows a prominent contribution from PC2 (32%), reflecting impact of ion-exchange and surface contamination processes. Overall, APCS-MLR estimated 19.8% of the trace metal load is derived from rock weathering and geothermal water contribution, based on average value of mean PC1 contributions across all trace metal ions.

The unidentified portions in the APCS-MLR model represents the variability in the ion concentrations that is not accounted for by the principal components (PCs) in the analysis. For several ions, such as Zn (52.2%), Mn (50.1%), and Pb (54.9%), a significant proportion of their variability remains unexplained, suggesting the influence of additional factors or sources/processes not captured by the PCs. This could be due to complex and heterogenic geochemical processes, unmeasured factors, or interactions that are not directly linked or not resolved to the key components identified in the APCS-MLR receptor model.
