On the density version of quadratic Waring’s problem and the quadratic Waring–Goldbach problem
Zi Li Lim

Abstract
. We prove a sharp density theorem for quadratic Waring’s problem over cyclic groups, when the number of variables is at least 5 5 . Also, we obtain some new improvements on the density version of the quadratic Waring–Goldbach problem over integers.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Introduction

Denote the set of all prime numbers by
\mathcal{P}
. Let
A
be a subset of primes, recall that the relative lower density
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)
is defined to be

\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)=\liminf_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|A\cap[N]|}{|\mathcal{P}\cap[N]|}.

Li and Pan
[
LP10
]
proved a density version of Vinogradov three primes theorem, which states that if
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)>2/3
, then every sufficiently large odd integer can be represented as a sum of three elements in
A
. In this paper, we refer to quantities such as the
2/3
in Li and Pan’s result as a “density threshold”.

In a breakthrough paper, Shao
[
Sha14
]
proved the sharp density version of Vinogradov three primes theorem, lowering the density threshold from
2/3
to
5/8
, which led to many interesting subsequent developments, such as
[
Sha14a
]
,
[
MS17
]
and
[
MMS17
]
. Later, density type theorems were considered in other arithmetic settings, for example, Waring’s problem
[
Sal21
]
, or the Waring–Goldbach problem
[
Gao25
]
,
[
Zha25
]
, but the optimal densities are unknown.

In this paper, we present some refinements of density type theorems for quadratic Waring’s problem and the quadratic Waring–Goldbach problem. Our first theorem is a sharp density theorem for quadratic Waring’s problem over cyclic groups. Informally, it gives a density threshold of
1/s
of the Waring problem for sums of
s
squares in a cyclic group, provided that the order of the group is not divisible by small primes, and
s
is at least
5
. For any positive integer
W
, denote the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
by
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer and
\theta\in(1/s,1]
. There exists a constant
M(s,\theta)
depending only on
s
and
\theta
such that the following holds.

Suppose
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
is an integer with
p_{i}\geq M(s,\theta)
for all
i
, and
A
is a subset of the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
with relative density greater than or equal to
\theta
, i.e.
|A|\geq\theta|(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}|
. Then, for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, there exist
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in A
such that
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
. Moreover, one has the following quantitative estimate

|\{(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s})\in A^{s}:y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\}|\geq c(s,\theta)W^{-1}|(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}|^{s}

for some constant
c(s,\theta)>0
depending only on
s
and
\theta
.

Remark 1.2
.

For Theorem
1.1
, it is necessary to exclude small prime divisors. Let
W
be an integer coprime to
3
, and
A=\{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/3W)^{(2)}:x\equiv 0\;\mathrm{mod}\;3\}
, then the relative density of
A
is
1/2
, but the
s
-fold sumset of
A
can only contains the elements congruent to
0\;\mathrm{mod}\;3
.

It might be worth to point out that the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
are fairly sparse. Suppose
W=\prod_{1\leq i\leq r}p_{i}
is a product of
r
distinct odd primes, then the absolute density of
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}
in the ambient space
\mathbb{Z}/W
is at most
\prod_{1\leq i\leq r}(p_{i}+1)/(2p_{i})\leq(2/3)^{r}
, which tends to zero when
r\rightarrow\infty
. Hence, a subset of
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}
with relative density greater than
1/s
can have absolute density arbitrarily close to zero.

The next theorem confirms that the density threshold
1/s
is indeed sharp, except possibly at the endpoint.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer and
\tau\in[0,1/s)
. For every sufficiently large (depending only on
s
and
\tau
) prime
p
, there exists a subset
A_{p}
of the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/p
with relative density greater than or equal to
\tau
, i.e.
|A_{p}|\geq\tau|(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{(2)}|
, but the
s
-fold sumset
sA_{p}
is not equal to
\mathbb{Z}/p
.

Now, we turn our attention from cyclic groups to integers. Salmensuu
[
Sal21
]
proved a density theorem for Waring’s problem. In quadratic case of Salmensuu’s theorem, the number of squares needed is at least
44
and the density threshold is approximately
0.9206
. Gao
[
Gao25
]
and Zhao
[
Zha25
]
generalized Salmensuu’s theorem to the Waring–Goldbach problem.

We choose to work with the Waring–Goldbach problem to demonstrate our method for technical reasons (it is easier to work with reduced congruence classes for corresponding local problem, see Theorem
1.6
). We obtain some new improvements, but not the optimal density. For any integer
s\geq 5
, define

D_{s}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{59}{60}&\text{if $s=5$}\\
\frac{7}{8}&\text{if $s=6$}\\
\frac{3}{4}&\text{if $s=7$}\\
\frac{s+13}{4s}&\text{if $8\leq s\leq 12$}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if $s\geq 13$}\end{cases}

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer, and
A
be a subset of primes with relative lower density
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)>\sqrt{D_{s}}
. Then, for all sufficiently large integers
y
that are congruent to
s\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
, there exist primes
p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{s}\in A
such that
y=p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}+...+p_{s}^{2}
.

Remark 1.5
.

The congruence condition in Theorem
1.4
is necessary, since the square of any prime greater than
3
is congruent to
1\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
.

For comparison, Gao
[
Gao25
]
proved a density theorem for the quadratic Waring–Goldbach problem for
s\geq 44
and density threshold
3/4
, and Zhao
[
Zha25
]
improved the result to
s\geq 8
and density threshold
\sqrt{1-\min\{s,16\}/32}
. (Gao also considered the Waring–Goldbach problem for higher powers, see relevant discussions later.) Qualitatively, Theorem
1.4
allows larger range of
s
. Quantitatively, the density thresholds given by
\sqrt{D_{s}}
improve upon previous record
\sqrt{1-\min\{s,16\}/32}
by Zhao. The table below is a detailed numerical comparison between the density thresholds in Theorem

The key to Theorem
1.4
is the following local result. For any integer
s\geq 5
, define

d_{s}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{9}{10}&\text{if $s=5$}\\
\frac{5}{6}&\text{if $s=6$}\\
\frac{s+13}{4s}&\text{if $7\leq s\leq 12$}\\
\frac{1}{2}&\text{if $s\geq 13$}\end{cases}

Note that
D_{s}\geq d_{s}\geq 1/2
and
d_{s}\geq(s+13)/(4s)
for all
s
. Similar as before, for any positive integer
W
, denote the squares in
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times}
by
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer, and
W
be a square free integer. Assume
(2\cdot 3\cdot 5,W)=1
when
s\geq 6
, and
(2\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7,W)=1
when
s=5
. Let
f_{j}:(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
be functions for
1\leq j\leq s
with

\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{s}])>d_{s}
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}]\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Then, for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, we have
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})+...+f_{s}(x_{s}))>d_{s}
and
f_{j}(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

The essential distinction between Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.6
is Theorem
1.1
excludes considerably more amount of small primes. Due to this distinction, their proofs are fundamentally different.

1.2.
Sketch of the proofs

All density type theorems, including ours, crucially rely on the transference principle pioneered by Green and Tao in their celebrated proof that primes contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions
[
Gre05
]
,
[
GT08
]
.

One of the key ingredients needed for the transference principle is restriction estimates. For Theorem
1.1
, since we are working over cyclic groups
\mathbb{Z}/W
, we need restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
instead of the classical Bourgain’s restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}

[
Bou89
]
. Surprisingly, the proofs are different in the cyclic group setting and in the integer setting—the squares are denser in cyclic groups, but one has extra product structure of the groups.

For Theorem
1.4
, our proof is based on a variant of the combinatorial inequalities developed by Shao
[
Sha14
]
and by Lacey, Mousavi, Rahimi and Vempati
[
Lac+24
]
. The combinatorial inequality is inductive in nature, and the base cases are handled by linear programming with computer assistance.

1.3.
Future directions

The main purpose of this paper is demonstrating the methods as a proof of concept, rather than pursuing the best density that can be obtained by the methods. There are certainly rooms for further improvements. First, we expect the density threshold
\sqrt{D_{s}}
can be improved to
D_{s}
by not using certain smooth weights for majorants (see Appendix
B
). Second, the numerical linear programming computations in Section
7
can be scaled up to lead to better density.

In another direction, we believe Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.4
can be generalized to higher powers. We also expect the techniques for Theorem
1.4
are applicable for the setting of Waring’s problem.

Remark 1.7
.

To prove results like Theorem
1.6
, in principle, one can use Theorem
1.1
to handle large primes, formulate the problem for medium primes in linear programming, and deal with small primes by hand. However, the number of “medium” primes here is astronomical, even for computers.

Remark 1.8
.

By classical estimates in number theory and union bounds, it is possible to prove Theorem
1.4
for density threshold extremely close to
1
.

Road map

in Section

. Section

The key combinatorial inequality for Theorem

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we will clarify our notation and review some basic facts about the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
. A comprehensive discussion on this subject can be found in the excellent book
[
IK04
, Chapter 3]
.

2.1.
Squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W

Let
W
be a positive integer. Denote the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
by
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}
, more formally

(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W:x=y^{2}\,\text{for some $y\in\mathbb{Z}/W$}\}

Similarly, we define

(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W:x=y^{2}\,\text{for some $y\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times}$}\}

Let
S(W)
be the number of squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
, that is

S(W)=|(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}|.

We will use the convenient notation
\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}
to denote the summation over the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
, so
\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}
and
\sum_{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}}
are interchangeable.

Now we recall some classical and well-known results regarding the squares. Here and throughout,
p
will always be a prime number. We start with the number of sqaures in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
p\geq 3
and
n\geq 1
, then

S(p^{n})=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{p^{n+1}-1}{p+1}+1&\text{when $n$ is odd}\\
\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{p(p^{n}-1)}{p+1}+1&\text{when $n$ is even}\end{cases}

In particular, we have
S(p^{n})\asymp p^{n}
.

Interested readers can find a proof of Proposition
2.1
in
[
Sta96
]
. In fact, instead of a closed form formula, we will only need
S(p^{n})=\frac{1}{2}p^{n}+O(p^{n-1})
, which can be derived more straightforwardly by looking at the squares in
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}
. Intuitively, Proposition
2.1
says that roughly half of the elements of
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
are squares. By Chinese remainder theorem,
(\mathbb{Z}/W_{1}W_{2})^{(2)}
can be identified with
(\mathbb{Z}/W_{1})^{(2)}\times(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}
if
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are coprime, hence Proposition
2.1
also provides a formula for
S(W)
for general modulus
W
. The next proposition is a Gauss sum type result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
p\geq 3,n\geq 1
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
, then

\bigg\lvert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rvert\ll(t,p^{n})p^{1/2}

where the implied constant does not depend on
p,n
or
t
.

The formulation of Proposition
2.2
is slightly different than the Gauss sum type results in literature. To save readers’ effort, we briefly explain how to derive Proposition
2.2
from standard references in Appendix
A
. Intuitively, the term
(t,p^{n})
corresponds to the repetition of some elements in the sum, and the term
p^{1/2}
corresponds to the cancellation.

2.2.
Other notation

We will use asymptoptic notation
\ll,\gg,\asymp,O,o
, and all the implied constants are allowed to depend on
s,\theta
.

Let
W
be a positive integer. We will denote
e^{\frac{2\pi i}{W}\cdot}
by
e_{W}(\cdot)
, and
e^{2\pi i\cdot}
by
e(\cdot)
. For functions on
\mathbb{Z}/W
, all
l^{q}
-norms, Fourier transforms and convolutions are taken with respect to the counting measure. Hence, if
f,g:\mathbb{Z}/W\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
are functions, then the
l^{q}
-norm is

\lVert f\rVert_{q}=\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\lvert f(x)\rvert^{q}\right)^{1/q},

the Fourier transform is

\widetilde{f}(\xi)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W}f(x)e_{W}(-x\xi),

and the convolution is

f*g(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y+z=x\end{subarray}}f(y)g(z).

For functions on
\mathbb{Z}
, the
l^{q}
-norms are taken with respect to counting measure. For functions on
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, the
L^{q}
-norms are taken with respect to Lebesgue measure. If
f,g
are finitely supported functions on
\mathbb{Z}
, then the Fourier transform is defined to be

\widehat{f}(\alpha)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f(n)e(n\alpha)

and the convolution is defined to be

f*g(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,z\in\mathbb{Z}\\
y+z=x\end{subarray}}f(y)g(z)

Finally, we will use
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}
to denote the distance to the nearest integer.

3.
Restriction Estimates

In this section, we formulate and prove restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
, which is an ingredient needed for the transference principle. It can be viewed as an analog of Bourgain’s restriction estimates for squares in integers
[
Bou89
]
over
\mathbb{Z}/W
. However, the proof of the cases over
\mathbb{Z}/W
and that of the cases over integers are somewhat different, since one can explore the product structure of
\mathbb{Z}/W
via Chinese remainder theorem.

Recall that we denote the number of squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
by
S(W)
, and the notation
\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}
means the summation over the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
. See Section
2
for more on our notation.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
W
be a positive integer and
q\geq 2
be a real number. Define
C_{q}(W)
to be the best constant such that

\left(\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}a_{x}e_{W}(tx)\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H}\right)^{1/q}\leq C_{q}(W)S(W)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}\lVert a_{x}\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)^{1/2}

for all finite dimensional Hilbert space
H
with norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{H}
and all vectors
a_{x}\in H
.

Remark 3.2
.

The constants
C_{q}(W)
are defined for vector-valued restriction estimates. When specializing to scalars, they give more “natural” inequalities:

\left(\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}\bigg\lvert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}a_{x}e_{W}(tx)\bigg\rvert^{q}\right)^{1/q}\leq C_{q}(W)S(W)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}\lvert a_{x}\rvert^{2}\right)^{1/2}

for all complex numbers
a_{x}
. Indeed, only this form of restriction estimates would be needed for the proof of our main theorem. However, the vector-valued formulation is crucial for the multiplicativity of
C_{q}(W)
(Lemma
3.4
).

The purpose of this section is to prove the following theorem, which says that one has satisfactory restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
for
q>4
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
q>4
be a real number. There exists a constant
M(q)
depending only on
q
such that the following holds.

Suppose
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
is a positive integer with
p_{i}\geq M(q)
for all
i
, then

C_{q}(W)=O_{q}(1).

As a remark, the requirement that
s\geq 5
in Theorem
1.1
and Theorem
1.4
is due to the critical exponent of restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
. The proof of Theorem
3.3
would be deferred to the end of the section. First, we need the following lemma to reduce to the cases of prime power moduli.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
W_{1},W_{2}
be two coprime positive integers, then
C_{q}(W_{1}W_{2})=C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})
.

Proof.

First, we will show that
C_{q}(W_{1}W_{2})\leq C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})
. Since
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are coprime, we have
\beta_{2}W_{1}+\beta_{1}W_{2}=1
for some integers
\beta_{1},\beta_{2}
with
(\beta_{1},W_{1})=(\beta_{2},W_{2})=1
. Hence, we have multiplicative property
e_{W}(u)=e_{W}((\beta_{2}W_{1}+\beta_{1}W_{2})u)=e_{W_{1}}(\beta_{1}u)e_{W_{2}}(\beta_{2}u)
for all
u
.

Furthermore, by Chinese remainder theorem, we can identify
\mathbb{Z}/W
with
\mathbb{Z}/W_{1}\times\mathbb{Z}/W_{2}
and
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}
with
(\mathbb{Z}/W_{1})^{(2)}\times(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}
. Under these identifications, we will write
x=(x_{1},x_{2})
for
x\in\mathbb{Z}/W
as our notation. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}a_{x}e_{W}(tx)\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H}\right)^{1/q}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{t_{1}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\sum_{t_{2}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}a_{(x_{1},x_{2})}e_{W_{1}}(\beta_{1}t_{1}x_{1})e_{W_{2}}(\beta_{2}t_{2}x_{2})\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H}\right)^{1/q}.

By definition of
C_{q}(W_{2})
, the expression above is bounded by

(3.1)

C_{q}(W_{2})S(W_{2})^{1/2}\left(\sum_{t_{1}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\left(\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}a_{(x_{1},x_{2})}e_{W_{1}}(t_{1}x_{1})\bigg\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)^{q/2}\right)^{1/q}.

To proceed, we will work over the vector space
H^{(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}}
. Let
b_{x_{1}}=(a_{(x_{1},x_{2})})_{x_{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}}\in H^{(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}}
. Note that

\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}b_{x_{1}}e_{W_{1}}(t_{1}x_{1})\bigg\rVert_{H^{(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}}}=\left(\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}a_{(x_{1},x_{2})}e_{W_{1}}(t_{1}x_{1})\bigg\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)^{1/2}.

Hence, we deduce that (
3.1
) is bounded by

\begin{split}&C_{q}(W_{2})S(W_{2})^{1/2}C_{q}(W_{1})S(W_{1})^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\lVert b_{x_{1}}\rVert^{2}_{H^{(\mathbb{Z}/W_{2})^{(2)}}}\right)^{1/2}\\
&=C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})S(W)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\lVert a_{(x_{1},x_{2})}\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)^{1/2},\end{split}

and this concludes that
C_{q}(W)\leq C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})
.

Next, we will show that
C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})\leq C_{q}(W)
. Suppose the worst examples for
C_{q}(W_{1})
and
C_{q}(W_{2})
are given by some vectors
a_{x_{1}}^{(1)}
and
a_{x_{2}}^{(2)}
in some vector spaces
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
respectively. Considering
a_{x_{1}}^{(1)}\otimes a_{x_{2}}^{(2)}
in the tensor product
H_{1}\otimes H_{2}
, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{t_{1}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\sum_{t_{2}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}a_{x_{1}}^{(1)}\otimes a_{x_{2}}^{(2)}e_{W_{1}}(t_{1}x_{1})e_{W_{2}}(t_{2}x_{2})\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H_{1}\otimes H_{2}}\\
&=\left(\sum_{t_{1}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}a_{x_{1}}^{(1)}e_{W_{1}}(t_{1}x_{1})\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{t_{2}\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}a_{x_{2}}^{(2)}e_{W_{2}}(t_{2}x_{2})\bigg\rVert^{q}_{H_{2}}\right)\end{split}

since
\lVert v_{1}\otimes v_{2}\rVert_{H_{1}\otimes H_{2}}=\lVert v_{1}\rVert_{H_{1}}\lVert v_{2}\rVert_{H_{2}}
for all
v_{1}\in H_{1},v_{2}\in H_{2}
. This implies that
C_{q}(W_{1})C_{q}(W_{2})\leq C_{q}(W)
.

∎

The next proposition is the key to the proof of Theorem
3.3
. It is about restriction estimates for squares in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
q\geq 4
be a real number, we have

C_{q}(p^{n})\leq 1+O_{q}\left(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{q}{2}-1}}\right)

for all sufficiently large (depending on
q
) primes
p
and all positive integers
n
. The implied constant for big
O
only depends on
q
but not
p
and
n
.

Proof of Proposition
3.5
for
q=4
.

Throughout the proof, we will assume
p
is sufficiently large. Expanding the powers, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert^{4}_{H}&=\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\sum_{x,y,z,w=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\langle a_{z},a_{w}\rangle_{H}e_{p^{n}}(t(x-y+z-w))\\
&=p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y,z,w=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=w-z\end{subarray}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\langle a_{z},a_{w}\rangle_{H}.\end{split}

Rewrite the expression above as

p^{n}\sum_{l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y,z,w=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l=w-z\end{subarray}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\langle a_{z},a_{w}\rangle_{H}=p^{n}\sum_{l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\bigg\rvert^{2},

and we will divide into two cases according to the invertibility of
l
.

First, we consider the summation over
l
that are not invertible in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. Note that

p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\notin(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\bigg\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\bigg\rvert^{2}\leq p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\notin(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lvert\langle a_{x},a_{x-l}\rangle_{H}\rvert\right)^{2}

if we conveniently denote
a_{u}=0
for
u
that is not a square mod
p^{n}
.

Applying Cauchy–Schwarz inequality twice, the expression above is bounded by

(3.2)

\begin{split}p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\notin(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}\lVert a_{x-l}\rVert_{H}\right)^{2}&\leq p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\notin(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}^{2}\right)^{2}\\
&=p^{2n-1}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}^{2}\right)^{2}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{p}S(p^{n})^{2}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}^{2}\right)^{2}\end{split}

where the last line follows from Proposition
2.1
.

Next, we consider the summation over
l
that are invertible in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. To proceed, we need a little digression on number of the solutions to certain equation over
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. For any
l\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}
, define

T(l,p^{n})=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}:x\;\text{and}\;y\;\text{are squares, and}\;x-y=l\}

We claim that
|T(l,p^{n})|\leq\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2
for
p\geq 3
. To see this, let

\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})=\{(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}:a,b\neq 0\;\text{and}\;a^{2}-b^{2}=l\}

Note that for any
(a,b)\in\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})
, we have
(a+b)(a-b)=l
. Since
p\neq 2
, we can perform change of variables
u=a+b,v=a-b
and rewrite the equation as
uv=l
. The assumption that
l
is invertible implies that
u,v
are invertible. Thus, there are at most
|(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}|=p^{n}-p^{n-1}
choices of
(u,v)
and hence
|\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})|\leq p^{n}-p^{n-1}
.

The relation between
\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})
and
T(l,p^{n})
is not exactly one-to-one. For any
(x,y)\in T(l,p^{n})
with
x
and
y
both nonzero, there are at least four different elements in
\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})
corresponding to it, since
(\pm a,\pm b)
will correspond to the same
(x,y)
. Also, we need to consider the special case that
(l,0)
and
(0,-l)
might belong to
T(l,p^{n})
. Hence, we have

|T(l,p^{n})|\leq\frac{|\mathcal{A}(l,p^{n})|}{4}+2\leq\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2.

Now, we can continue our previous estimates. Note that

p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\bigg\lvert\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\bigg\rvert^{2}\leq p^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}|T(l,p^{n})|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\lvert\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\rvert^{2}

by Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.

Invoking the estimates for
|T(l,p^{n})|
, the expression above is bounded by

(3.3)

\begin{split}&p^{n}\left(\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\lvert\langle a_{x},a_{y}\rangle_{H}\rvert^{2}\\
&\leq p^{n}\left(\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
l\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
x-y=l\end{subarray}}\left(\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}\lVert a_{y}\rVert_{H}\right)^{2}\\
&\leq p^{n}\left(\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2\right)\sum_{x,y=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\left(\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}\lVert a_{y}\rVert_{H}\right)^{2}\\
&=p^{n}\left(\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2\right)\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}^{2}\right)^{2}.\end{split}

Finally, by Proposition
2.1
, we have

(3.4)

p^{n}\left(\frac{p^{n}-p^{n-1}}{4}+2\right)S(p^{n})^{-2}\leq\frac{p^{2n+2}+O(p^{2n+1})}{p^{2n+2}-O(p^{2n+1})}\leq 1+O\left(\frac{1}{p}\right).

Combining (
3.2
), (
3.3
) and (
3.4
), we conclude the desired inequality
C_{4}(p^{n})\leq 1+O(1/p)
for sufficiently large
p
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.5
for
q>4
.

Throughout the proof, we will assume
p
is sufficiently large depending on
q
, and all implied constants are allowed to depend on
q
. We may also assume
\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert_{H}^{2}=S(p^{n})
by normalization.

We would like to reduce to the case that

(3.5)

\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}\geq S(p^{n})-O(p^{n-1})

for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. To carry out the reduction, let
A
be a large constant (depending only on
q
) chosen later, and suppose

\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}\leq S(p^{n})-Ap^{n-1}

for all
t\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. Then, by restriction estimates for
q=4
(Proposition
3.5
), we have

\begin{split}\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}&\leq(S(p^{n})-Ap^{n-1})^{q-4}\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{4}\\
&\leq(S(p^{n})-Ap^{n-1})^{q-4}(1+O(1/p))S(p^{n})^{4}\\
&=S(p^{n})^{q}(1-Ap^{n-1}/S(p^{n}))^{q-4}(1+O(1/p)).\end{split}

Since
p^{n-1}/S(p^{n})\gg 1/p
, choosing sufficiently large
A
depending on
q
will make
(1-Ap^{n-1}/S(p^{n}))^{q-4}(1+O(1/p))\leq 1
. Hence, we can reduce to that case that (
3.5
) holds for some
t\in\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
. By multiplying
e_{p^{n}}(tx)
to
a_{x}
, we may further assume that
t=0
and hence

(3.6)

\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}\bigg\rVert_{H}\geq S(p^{n})-O(p^{n-1}).

Now, let
c=\frac{1}{S(p^{n})}\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}
be the average of
a_{x}
and decompose
a_{x}=b_{x}+c
, where the vectors
b_{x}
have mean zero. One has good lower and upper bounds for
\lVert c\rVert_{H}
, that is

1-O(1/p)\leq\lVert c\rVert_{H}\leq 1

by (
3.6
) and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality respectively. Moreover, Pythagoras’s theorem says that
\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert a_{x}\rVert^{2}_{H}=\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert b_{x}\rVert^{2}_{H}+S(p^{n})\lVert c\rVert^{2}_{H}
. Combining this and the lower bound for
\lVert c\rVert_{H}
, we deduce a good
l^{2}
-bound for
b_{x}
, which is

(3.7)

\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert b_{x}\rVert^{2}_{H}\ll\frac{1}{p}S(p^{n}).

Note that

(3.8)

\begin{split}&\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\\
&=\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}b_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)+\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}ce_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\\
&=\lVert S(p^{n})c\rVert^{q}_{H}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}b_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)+\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}ce_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\\
&\leq S(p^{n})^{q}+\left\{\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}b_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\right)^{1/q}+\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}ce_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\right)^{1/q}\right\}^{q}\end{split}

where the last line follows from Minkowski inequality. We will estimate the sums involving
b_{x}
and
c
separately.

For the sum involving
b_{x}
, we will use (
3.7
) and restriction estimates for
q=4
. We have

(3.9)

\begin{split}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}b_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\right)^{1/q}&\leq\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}b_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{4}\right)^{1/4}\\
&\leq C_{4}(p^{n})S(p^{n})^{1/2}\left(\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\lVert b_{x}\rVert^{2}_{H}\right)^{1/2}\\
&\ll p^{n-1/2}.\end{split}

For the sum involving
c
, we will use Gauss sum estimates (Proposition
2.2
). We have

(3.10)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}ce_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}\\
t\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lvert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rvert^{q}\ll\sum_{1\leq t<p^{n}}(t,p^{n})^{q}p^{q/2},

which is

(3.11)

\begin{split}&\sum_{0\leq e\leq n-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq t<p^{n}\\
p^{e}||t\end{subarray}}p^{qe}p^{q/2}\\
&\leq\sum_{0\leq e\leq n-1}p^{n-e}p^{qe+q/2}\\
&=p^{n+q/2}\sum_{0\leq e\leq n-1}p^{(q-1)e}\\
&\ll p^{n+q/2}p^{(n-1)(q-1)}\\
&=p^{qn+1-q/2}.\end{split}

Combining (
3.8
), (
3.9
), (
3.10
) and (
3.11
), we obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{t\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}\bigg\lVert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p^{n}}a_{x}e_{p^{n}}(tx)\bigg\rVert_{H}^{q}&\leq S(p^{n})^{q}+O(p^{qn-q/2}+p^{qn+1-q/2})\\
&\leq S(p^{n})^{q}(1+O(p^{-\frac{q}{2}+1})).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we have all the ingredients needed for the proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

Let
q>4
, by Proposition
3.5
, there exists a constant
M=M(q)
such that

C_{q}(p^{n})\leq 1+O_{q}\left(\frac{1}{p^{\frac{q}{2}-1}}\right)

for all
p\geq M
and
n\geq 1
.

Let
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
be a modulus with
p_{i}\geq M
for all
i
. By Lemma
3.4
, we have

C_{q}(W)=\prod_{i}C_{q}(p_{i}^{n_{i}})\leq\prod_{p\geq M}(1+O_{q}(p^{-q/2+1}))\leq\exp\left(\sum_{p\geq M}O_{q}(p^{-q/2+1})\right)\ll_{q}1

since
q/2-1>1
when
q>4
.
∎

4.
Sumsets of Dense Subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/W

With the transference principle, one can treat relatively dense subsets of squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
as if they are actually dense in
\mathbb{Z}/W
. Therefore, we would like a quantitative sumset estimates for dense subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/W
. Li and Pan
[
LP10
, Lemma 3.3]
proved the desired sumset estimates for prime moduli, and we will generalize their results to general moduli.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
0<\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}\leq 1
with
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+...+\theta_{s}>1
. There exist positive constants
c(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})
and
M(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})
depending only on
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}
such that the following holds.

Suppose
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
is an intger with
p_{i}\geq M(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})
for all
i
, and
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{s}
are subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/W
with
|A_{j}|\geq\theta_{j}W
for all
1\leq j\leq s
. Then,

1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}*...*1_{A_{s}}(x)\geq c(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})W^{s-1}

for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}/W
.

Our proof of Theorem
4.1
is similar to Li and Pan’s proof of
[
LP10
, Lemma 3.3]
, just that we need the following more general Pollard–Kneser type result due to Green and Ruzsa
[
GR05
, Proposition 6.1]
.

Theorem 4.2

(Green–Ruzsa)
.

Let
Z
be a finite abelian group, and
D(Z)
be the size of the largest proper subgroup of
Z
. Suppose
A,B
are subsets of
Z
, then, for any
1\leq t\leq\min\{|A|,\,|B|\}
, we have

\sum_{x\in Z}\min\{t,\,1_{A}*1_{B}(x)\}\geq t\min\{|Z|,\,|A|+|B|-t-D(Z)\}.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We will induct on
s
, the number of the subsets. The base case would be
s=2
. Note that

\begin{split}1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}(x)&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a_{1}\in A_{1},a_{2}\in A_{2}\\
a_{1}+a_{2}=x\end{subarray}}1\\
&=|A_{1}\cap(x-A_{2})|\\
&=|A_{1}|+|x-A_{2}|-|A_{1}\cup(x-A_{2})|\\
&\geq\theta_{1}W+\theta_{2}W-W\\
&=(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-1)W\end{split}

for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}/W
. This completes the proof for base case.

Assume the Theorem
4.1
holds for
s-1
subsets. Let
\epsilon
be a small parameter and
M^{\prime}
be a large parameter chosen later. We will assume that we are working with moduli
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
with
p_{i}\geq M^{\prime}
for all
i
, in particular,
W
will be sufficiently large. Let
A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{s}
be subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/W
with
|A_{j}|\geq\theta_{j}W
for all
1\leq j\leq s
. Due to Theorem
4.2
, we have

\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\min\{\lceil\epsilon W\rceil,\,1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}(x)\}\geq\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\min\left\{W,\,\theta_{1}W+\theta_{2}W-\lceil\epsilon W\rceil-\frac{1}{\min_{i}p_{i}}W\right\}

by noting that
D(\mathbb{Z}/W)\leq\frac{1}{\min_{i}p_{i}}W
since
D(\mathbb{Z}/W)
divides
W
.

Hence, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}(x)>\lceil\epsilon^{2}W\rceil\end{subarray}}\min\{\lceil\epsilon W\rceil,\,1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}(x)\}\\
&\geq\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\min\left\{W,\,\theta_{1}W+\theta_{2}W-\lceil\epsilon W\rceil-\frac{1}{\min_{i}p_{i}}W\right\}-\lceil\epsilon^{2}W\rceil W\\
&\geq\min\left\{(\epsilon-2\epsilon^{2})W^{2},\,\epsilon\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\right)W^{2}\right\}\end{split}

where the last line follows from assuming
M^{\prime}
is sufficiently large so
W
is sufficiently large and hence
\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\leq 2\epsilon W
and
\lceil\epsilon^{2}W\rceil\leq 2\epsilon^{2}W
for notational convenience.

Denote
B=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W:1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}(x)>\lceil\epsilon^{2}W\rceil\}
, then we conclude that

(4.1)

\begin{split}|B|&\geq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon-2\epsilon^{2}}{\lceil\epsilon W\rceil}W^{2},\,\frac{\epsilon\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\right)}{\lceil\epsilon W\rceil}W^{2}\right\}\\
&\geq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon-2\epsilon^{2}}{\epsilon W+\epsilon^{2}W}W^{2},\,\frac{\epsilon\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}\right)}{\epsilon W+\epsilon^{2}W}W^{2}\right\}\\
&\quad\text{(Assuming $W$ is large enough so $\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\leq\epsilon W+\epsilon^{2}W$)}\\
&=\min\left\{\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}W,\,\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}}{1+\epsilon}W\right\}.\end{split}

Now, we will choose our parameters carefully. We will choose sufficiently small
\epsilon
depending on
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}
and sufficiently large
M^{\prime}
depending on
\epsilon,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}
so that we have the followings:

•

\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\leq\min\{\theta_{1}W,\,\theta_{2}W\}
so that the assumption of Theorem
4.2
is met.

•

\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\leq 2\epsilon W
,
\lceil\epsilon^{2}W\rceil\leq 2\epsilon^{2}W
and
\lceil\epsilon W\rceil\leq\epsilon W+\epsilon^{2}W
for notational convenience.

•

\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}
is close enough to
1
and
\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}}{1+\epsilon}
is close enough to
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}
so that

\min\left\{\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1+\epsilon},\,\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-4\epsilon-\frac{1}{M^{\prime}}}{1+\epsilon}\right\}+\theta_{3}+...+\theta_{s}>1.

•

Thus, by (
4.1
), we have
|B|\geq\eta W
for some
\eta
depending on
M^{\prime},\epsilon,\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}
with
0<\eta\leq 1
and
\eta+\theta_{3}+...+\theta_{s}>1
.

•

By induction hypothesis, we may apply Theorem
4.1
for
s-1
densities
\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s}
to obtain two constants
c(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})>0
and
M(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})
. We claim that for
s
densities
\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s}
, we can pick
c(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})=\epsilon^{2}c(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})
and
M(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},...,\theta_{s})=\max\{M^{\prime},\,M(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})\}
.

To finish the proof, note that for all
x\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, we have

\begin{split}1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}}*...*1_{A_{s}}(x)&\geq(1_{A_{1}}*1_{A_{2}})|_{B}*(1_{A_{3}}...*1_{A_{s}})(x)\\
&\geq\epsilon^{2}W(1_{B}*1_{A_{3}}...*1_{A_{s}})(x)\\
&\geq\epsilon^{2}Wc(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})W^{s-2}\quad\quad\text{(By induction hypothesis)}\\
&=\epsilon^{2}c(\eta,\theta_{3},...,\theta_{s})W^{s-1}.\end{split}

∎

5.
Transference Principle

In this section, we will use the transference principle pioneered by Green and Tao
[
Gre05
]
,
[
GT08
]
to prove Theorem
1.1
. We will closely follow the arguments in Section 6 in
[
Gre05
]
. As a remark, one can view the assumption of Theorem
1.1
that
W
does not have small prime divisors as an analog of the
W
-trick.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer and
\theta\in(1/s,1]
. Let
M
be a large constant chosen later, and we will be working with moduli
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
with
\min_{i}p_{i}\geq M
so that we can freely apply Gauss sum estimates, restriction theorem, quantitative sumset estimates, etc. Let
A
be a subset of the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
with
|A|\geq\theta S(W)
.

We define the following objects for the transference principle:

•

Let
\nu=\frac{1}{S(W)}1_{(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{(2)}}
be a probability measure supported on the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/W
.

•

Define the normalized indicator function of
A
by setting
a=1_{A}\nu
. Note that the
l^{1}
-norm of
a
is the relative density of
A
.

•

Let
\delta,\epsilon>0
be small parameters chosen later. Let

R=\{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W:\lvert\widetilde{a}(\xi)\rvert>\delta\}

be the large spectrum of
a
and

B=\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W:\bigg\lVert\frac{x\xi}{W}\bigg\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}<\epsilon\;\text{for all $\xi\in R$}\right\}

be the Bohr neighborhood of
R
.

•

Let
\beta=\frac{1}{|B|}1_{B}
and
a^{\prime}=a*\beta*\beta
. The function
a^{\prime}
can be thought of as the “transferred” version of
a
. Note that
a^{\prime}
and
a
have the same
l^{1}
-norm.

First, we show that
A
and the transferred version of
A
are close in finding certain additive configurations.

Proposition 5.1
.

For all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\end{subarray}}a(x_{1})a(x_{2})...a(x_{s})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\end{subarray}}a^{\prime}(x_{1})a^{\prime}(x_{2})...a^{\prime}(x_{s})+O\left(\frac{1}{W}(\epsilon\delta^{-4.5}+\delta^{1/2})\right)

Proof.

By Fourier expansions, we obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\end{subarray}}a(x_{1})a(x_{2})...a(x_{s})-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\end{subarray}}a^{\prime}(x_{1})a^{\prime}(x_{2})...a^{\prime}(x_{s})\\
&=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\widetilde{a}(\xi)^{s}e_{W}(\xi y)-\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\widetilde{a^{\prime}}(\xi)^{s}e_{W}(\xi y)\\
&=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\widetilde{a}(\xi)^{s}e_{W}(\xi y)(1-\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)^{2s}).\end{split}

We will divide the sum above into two cases, according to whether
\xi
is in the large spectrum
R
.

Note that by construction of
B
, we have
\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)=1+O(\epsilon)
if
\xi\in R
. Also, by restriction estimates (Theorem
3.3
), we obtain
\lVert\widetilde{a}\rVert_{q}=O_{q}(1)
when all the prime factors of
W
are sufficiently large depending on
q
. In particular, we have
\lVert\widetilde{a}\rVert_{4.5}=O(1)
and hence
|R|=O(\delta^{-4.5})
. Therefore, we deduce

\bigg\lvert\sum_{\xi\in R}\widetilde{a}(\xi)^{s}e_{W}(\xi y)(1-\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)^{2s})\bigg\rvert\ll\epsilon|R|\ll\epsilon\delta^{-4.5}.

On the other hand, we have

\bigg\lvert\sum_{\xi\notin R}\widetilde{a}(\xi)^{s}e_{W}(\xi y)(1-\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)^{2s})\bigg\rvert\ll\delta^{1/2}\sum_{\xi\notin R}\lvert\widetilde{a}(\xi)\rvert^{s-1/2}\ll\delta^{1/2}

by applying the restriction estimates for
q=s-1/2
or
q=4.5
.
∎

The next proposition is about the pseudorandomness of the measure
\nu
.

Proposition 5.2
.

For all
\xi\neq 0
, we have

\lvert\widetilde{\nu}(\xi)\rvert\ll\frac{1}{(\min_{i}p_{i})^{1/2}}.

Recall that the minimum is taken among all prime factors of
W
.

Proof.

First, we recall the multiplicativity of Gauss sums. Let
W=W_{1}W_{2}
with
(W_{1},W_{2})=1
, then
\lambda_{2}W_{1}+\lambda_{1}W_{2}=1
for some integers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
with
(\lambda_{1},W_{1})=(\lambda_{2},W_{2})=1
. We can decompose

\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}e_{W}(\xi x)=\left(\sum_{x_{1}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{1}}e_{W_{1}}(\lambda_{1}\xi x_{1})\right)\left(\sum_{x_{2}=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W_{2}}e_{W_{2}}(\lambda_{2}\xi x_{2})\right).

Now, if we write
W=\prod_{i}p_{i}^{n_{i}}
, then there exists a prime
p_{j}
such that
p_{j}^{n_{j}}\nmid\xi
since
\xi\neq 0
. By Gauss sum estimates (Proposition
2.2
) and the multiplicativity of Gauss sums, we have

\begin{split}\lvert\widetilde{\nu}(\xi)\rvert=\frac{1}{S(W)}\bigg\lvert\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,W}e_{W}(\xi x)\bigg\rvert&\ll\frac{1}{S(W)}p_{j}^{n_{j}-1}p_{j}^{1/2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}S(p_{i}^{n_{i}})\\
&=\frac{1}{S(p_{j}^{n_{j}})}p_{j}^{n_{j}-1/2}\\
&\ll\frac{1}{p_{j}^{1/2}}\\
&\leq\frac{1}{(\min_{i}p_{i})^{1/2}}.\end{split}

∎

Next, we show that
a^{\prime}
is set-like in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.3
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, we have

a^{\prime}(x)\leq\frac{1}{W}\left(1+O\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{O(\delta^{-4.5})}(\min_{i}p_{i})^{1/2}}\right)\right)

Proof.

Note that

\begin{split}a^{\prime}(x)&\leq\nu*\beta*\beta(x)\\
&=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W}(\nu*\beta*\beta)\widetilde{\;\;}(\xi)e_{W}(\xi x)\quad\quad\text{by Fourier expansion}\\
&=\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W}\widetilde{\nu}(\xi)\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)^{2}e_{W}(\xi x)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{W}+\frac{1}{W}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\lvert\widetilde{\nu}(\xi)\rvert\lvert\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\end{split}

By Proposition
5.2
and Parseval’s identity, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\lvert\widetilde{\nu}(\xi)\rvert\lvert\widetilde{\beta}(\xi)\rvert^{2}\ll\frac{W}{(\min_{i}p_{i})^{1/2}|B|}.

The classical lower bound for the size of Bohr sets
[
TV06
]
[Lemma 4.20] states that
|B|\geq\epsilon^{|R|}W
. To complete the proof, we observe that
|R|=O(\delta^{-4.5})
since
\lVert\widetilde{a}\rVert_{4.5}=O(1)
due to restriction estimates (Theorem
3.3
) for
q=4.5
.
∎

Now, we have all the ingredients to prove our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer and
\theta\in(1/s,1]
. We will choose a list of parameters carefully depending on
s,\theta
as follow:

•

Choose
\gamma>0
sufficiently small such that
\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta s>1
.

•

Let
c^{\prime}=c^{\prime}(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta,\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta,...,\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta)>0
and
M^{\prime}=M^{\prime}(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta,\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta,...,\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta)
be the constants from the quantitative sumset estimates for dense subsets of cyclic groups (Theorem
4.1
) for the density
\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta
.

•

Let
M^{\prime\prime}
be the constant from the restriction estimates (Theorem
3.3
) for
q=4.5
.

•

Choose
\delta
and then
\epsilon
sufficiently small such that the error term in Proposition
5.1
is less than
\frac{\gamma^{s}\theta^{s}c^{\prime}}{2W}
.

•

By Proposition
5.3
, we can choose sufficiently large
M^{\prime\prime\prime}
such that
\lVert a^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{W}(1+\gamma)
when
\min_{i}p_{i}\geq M^{\prime\prime\prime}
.

•

For the proof of main theorem (Theorem
1.1
), we claim that we can choose
c=\frac{\gamma^{s}\theta^{s}c^{\prime}}{2}>0
and
M=\max\{M^{\prime},\,M^{\prime\prime},\,M^{\prime\prime\prime}\}
.

Let

A^{\prime}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}/W:a^{\prime}(x)\geq\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}\right\}

Since
\lVert a^{\prime}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\frac{1}{W}(1+\gamma)
and
\lVert a^{\prime}\rVert_{1}\geq\theta
, by pigeonhole principle, we have
|A^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\theta W
.

By the quantitative sumset estimates for dense subsets of
\mathbb{Z}/W
(Theorem
4.1
), we have

\begin{split}\underbrace{a^{\prime}*a^{\prime}*...*a^{\prime}}_{s-\text{times}}(y)&\geq\left(\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}1_{A^{\prime}}\right)*\left(\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}1_{A^{\prime}}\right)*...*\left(\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}1_{A^{\prime}}\right)(y)\\
&=\left(\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}\right)^{s}(1_{A^{\prime}}*1_{A^{\prime}}*...*1_{A^{\prime}})(y)\\
&\geq\left(\frac{\gamma\theta}{W}\right)^{s}c^{\prime}W^{s-1}\\
&=\frac{\gamma^{s}\theta^{s}c^{\prime}}{W}\end{split}

for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/W
. Combining this and Proposition
5.1
, we conclude that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}/W\\
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}\end{subarray}}a(x_{1})a(x_{2})...a(x_{s})\geq\frac{\gamma^{s}\theta^{s}c^{\prime}}{2W}

and complete the proof.
∎

6.
Counterexamples

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
to complement Theorem
1.1
. By identifying the representatives of the elements of
\mathbb{Z}/p
with the integers in
[1,p]
, we can speak of “intervals” in
\mathbb{Z}/p
. We will use squares in intervals in
\mathbb{Z}/p
to construct subsets of squares with relative density close to
1/s
, but their
s
-fold sumsets are not entire
\mathbb{Z}/p
.

The next proposition, which states that the quadratic residues are equidistributed among intervals, is a classical result that can be proved in many different ways, say, by Polya-Vinogradov inequality. For self-containedness, we still include a short proof based on Gauss sums.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
0<\eta<1
, then

\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:1\leq x\leq\eta p\;\;\text{and $x$ is a square mod $p$}\}=\eta\frac{p+1}{2}+O(\sqrt{p}\log p)

for all primes
p
.

Proof.

By abuse of notation, we will denote the characteristic function of the interval
[1,\,\eta p]
in
\mathbb{Z}/p
by
1_{[1,\,\eta p]}
. By Fourier expansion, we have

\begin{split}&\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}1_{[1,\,\eta p]}(x)\\
&=\frac{1}{p}\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}\sum_{\xi\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}\widetilde{1}_{[1,\,\eta p]}(\xi)e_{p}(\xi x)\\
&=\frac{\lfloor\eta p\rfloor}{p}\cdot\frac{p+1}{2}+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\,\mathrm{mod}\,p\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\widetilde{1}_{[1,\,\eta p]}(\xi)\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}e_{p}(\xi x)\\
&=\eta\frac{p+1}{2}+O(1)+\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\,\mathrm{mod}\,p\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\widetilde{1}_{[1,\,\eta p]}(\xi)\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}e_{p}(\xi x).\end{split}

To bound the error term, we calculate the Fourier coefficients

\lvert\widetilde{1}_{[1,\,\eta p]}(\xi)\rvert=\bigg\lvert\sum_{1\leq x\leq\lfloor\eta p\rfloor}e_{p}(-x\xi)\bigg\rvert\ll\bigg\lVert\frac{\xi}{p}\bigg\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}^{-1}

for all
\xi\neq 0
. Combining this and the Gauss sum estimates (Proposition
2.2
), we deduce that

\begin{split}\bigg\lvert\frac{1}{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\,\mathrm{mod}\,p\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\widetilde{1}_{[1,\,\eta p]}(\xi)\sum_{x=\square\,\mathrm{mod}\,p}e_{p}(\xi x)\bigg\rvert&\ll\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\xi\,\mathrm{mod}\,p\\
\xi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\bigg\lVert\frac{\xi}{p}\bigg\rVert_{\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}}^{-1}\\
&\ll\sqrt{p}\log p.\end{split}

∎

Now, we will apply Proposition
6.1
to prove Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer, and
\tau\in[0,1/s)
. Choose
\eta
with
\tau<\eta<1/s
, by Proposition
6.1
, we have

\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:1\leq x\leq\eta p\;\;\text{and $x$ is a square mod $p$}\}\geq\tau\frac{p+1}{2}

for sufficiently large
p
. However, the
s
-fold sumset of
\{x\in\mathbb{Z}:1\leq x\leq\eta p\;\;\text{and $x$ is a square mod $p$}\}
cannot be
\mathbb{Z}/p
since
\eta s<1
.
∎

7.
A Combinatorial Lemma

One of the key ingredients in Shao’s
[
Sha14
]
proof of the sharp density version of Vinogradov three primes theorem is a combinatorial inequality. Subsequently, Lacey, Mousavi, Rahimi and Vempati
[
Lac+24
]
developed a variant of Shao’s inequality to generalize the density version of Vinogradov theorem to sum of more primes. In this section, we will use variants of Shao’s and Lacey, Mousavi, Rahimi and Vempati’s ideas to prove a combinatorial inequality that suits our purpose.

The combinatorial inequality is inductive in nature, but our base cases are more complicated, hence we handled them by linear programming with computer assistance. As a remark, Shao also used linear programming techniques in his proof, but the linear programming problems we dealt with are different due to different formulations of the combinatorial inequalities.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
s\geq 6,n\geq 3
or
s=5,n\geq 5
. For each integer
1\leq j\leq s
, let
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n}
be a decreasing sequence in interval
[0,1]
. Suppose

\frac{1}{ns}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}>d_{s}
and
a_{0,j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Then, there exist indices
0\leq i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{s}<n
with
i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{s}\geq 2n
such that

\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leq j\leq s}a_{i_{j},j}>d_{s}
and
a_{i_{j},j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

The proof of Lemma
7.1
is a bit long, so we separate it into inductive step and base cases. Along the proof, we will see explanations for the somewhat awkward definition of
d_{s}
. The reason why we choose
d_{s}
to be greater than or equal to
(s+13)/4s
is to make the inductive step work.

Proof of Lemma
7.1
assuming the base cases.

We will prove Lemma
7.1
by induction on
n
. Let
s\geq 6,n\geq 4
or
s=5,n\geq 6
. Suppose the lemma holds for
n-1
.

There are at least two nonzero elements among
a_{1,j}
where
1\leq j\leq s
, otherwise the total average is not large enough, that is

\frac{1}{ns}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}\leq\frac{1}{ns}(n+(s-1))\leq\frac{s+3}{4s}

since
n\geq 4
. However, the total average is assumed to be greater than
d_{s}
, which is at least
(s+13)/(4s)
. Thus, by relabeling if needed, we may assume
a_{1,1},a_{1,2}\neq 0
.

Suppose we have

\frac{1}{s}(a_{0,1}+a_{0,2}+a_{i_{3},3}+a_{i_{4},4}+...+a_{i_{s},s})\leq d_{s}\;\text{for some indices}\;0\leq i_{3},i_{4},...,i_{s}<n.

By monotonicity of
a_{i,j}
in
i
, we may further assume

\frac{1}{s}(a_{0,1}+a_{0,2}+a_{i_{3},3}+a_{i_{4},4}+...+a_{i_{s},s})\leq d_{s}\;\text{for some indices}\;1\leq i_{3},i_{4},...,i_{s}<n.

Remove the elements
a_{0,1},a_{0,2},a_{i_{3},3},a_{i_{4},4},...,a_{i_{s},s}
from the sequences
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s}
and denote the new sequences after removal by
\{b_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n-1,1\leq j\leq s}
. Note that by construction, we have

(7.1)

\begin{split}b_{i,j}=a_{i+1,j}&\;\;\text{if}\;j=1,2\\
b_{i,j}\leq a_{i,j}&\;\;\text{if}\;j\geq 3.\end{split}

Observe that the total average of
b_{i,j}
is greater than or equal to the total average of
a_{i,j}
since the average of the removed elements is less than the total average of
a_{i,j}
, that is

\frac{1}{(n-1)s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n-1\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}b_{i,j}\geq\frac{1}{ns}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}>d_{s}.

Also, the elements
b_{0,j}
are nonzero for
1\leq j\leq s
because
b_{0,j}=a_{1,j}\neq 0
for
j=1,2
and
b_{0,j}=a_{0,j}\neq 0
for
j\geq 3
. In short, the sequences
\{b_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n-1,1\leq j\leq s}
satisfy all assumptions in Lemma
7.1
for
n-1
. By induction hypothesis, there exist indices
i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{s}
with
i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{s}\geq 2(n-1)
such that

\frac{1}{s}\sum_{1\leq j\leq s}b_{i_{j},j}>d_{s}
and
b_{i_{j},j}\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Note that by equations (
7.1
), we conclude that

\frac{1}{s}(a_{i_{1}+1,1}+a_{i_{2}+1,2}+a_{i_{3},3}+...+a_{i_{s},s})>d_{s}.

Furthermore, the sum of indices is
(i_{1}+1)+(i_{2}+1)+i_{3}+...+i_{s}\geq 2(n-1)+2=2n
, hence the elements
a_{i_{1}+1,1},a_{i_{2}+1,2},a_{i_{3},3},...,a_{i_{s},s}
are the desired collection of the elements for Lemma
7.1
.

Now, we consider the other possibility, namely

\frac{1}{s}(a_{0,1}+a_{0,2}+a_{i_{3},3}+a_{i_{4},4}+...+a_{i_{s},s})>d_{s}\;\text{for all indices}\;0\leq i_{3},i_{4},...,i_{s}<n.

For each
1\leq j\leq s
, let
t_{j}
be the number of nonzero elements among
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n}
. In particular, we have

\frac{1}{s}(a_{0,1}+a_{0,2}+a_{t_{3}-1,3}+a_{t_{4}-1,4}+...+a_{t_{s}-1,s})>d_{s}.

It remains to check that the sum of indices is large enough. Since
a_{i,j}
are all bounded by
1
, we have

2n+(t_{3}+t_{4}+...+t_{s})\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}>nsd_{s}

Hence, we deduce that

\begin{split}(t_{3}-1)+(t_{4}-1)+...+(t_{s}-1)&>n(sd_{s}-2)-(s-2)\\
&\geq n\left(\frac{s+13}{4}-2\right)-(s-2)\;\;\left(\text{since $d_{s}\geq\frac{s+13}{4s}$ for all $s$}\right)\\
&=n\left(\frac{s+5}{4}\right)-(s-2).\end{split}

Note that
n(s+5)/4-(s-2)\geq 2n-1
when
n\geq 4
, thus we have

(t_{3}-1)+(t_{4}-1)+...+(t_{s}-1)>2n-1.

Since
(t_{3}-1)+(t_{4}-1)+...+(t_{s}-1)
is an integer, we conclude that

(t_{3}-1)+(t_{4}-1)+...+(t_{s}-1)\geq 2n

and this completes the inductive step.
∎

For the base cases of Lemma
7.1
when
5\leq s\leq 11
, we will handle them via linear programming with computer assistance. For certain linear programming problems to have expected optimal values, we choose
d_{s}=5/6
instead of
(s+13)/4s
when
s=6
. This is also the reason why the base case for
s=5
corresponds to
n=5
as opposed to
n=3
.

Proof of base cases of Lemma
7.1
when
5\leq s\leq 11
.

The crucial observation is for any fixed
s,n
, the Lemma
7.1
can be approached by linear programming. Let
s,n
be fixed, for any integral indices
1\leq t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s}\leq n
, we can define the following linear programming problem
LP
(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{2})
.

LP
(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s})
:

Variables
:
a_{i,j}
for
0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s

Constraints
:

(1)

For all
0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s
, we require that
0\leq a_{i,j}\leq 1
.

(2)

For all
1\leq j\leq s
and
i\geq t_{j}
, we require that
a_{i,j}=0
.

(3)

For all
0\leq i<n-1,1\leq j\leq s
, we require that
a_{i,j}\geq a_{i+1,j}
.

(4)

For all indices
0\leq i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{s}<n
with
i_{1}+i_{2}+....+i_{s}\geq 2n
and
i_{1}<t_{1},i_{2}<t_{2},...,i_{s}<t_{s}
, we require that

\frac{1}{s}(a_{i_{1},1}+a_{i_{2},2}+...+a_{i_{s},s})\leq d_{s}.

Objective
: Maximize

\frac{1}{ns}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}

We claim that if the maxima for linear programming problems

(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s})
are upper bounded by

for all indices
1\leq t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s}\leq n
, then Lemma
7.1
is true for the parameters

.

To see why the claim is true, we suppose the contrary, that is, the maxima for linear programming problems
LP
(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s})
are upper bounded by
d_{s}
for all indices
1\leq t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s}\leq n
, but Lemma
7.1
is false for the parameters
s,n
. Let
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s}
be the hypothetical counterexample and
T_{j}
be the number of nonzero elements among
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n}
for every
1\leq j\leq s
. Note that the numbers
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s}
satisfy all constraints of the linear programming problem
LP
(T_{1},T_{2},...,T_{s})
, hence the total average of
\{a_{i,j}\}_{0\leq i<n,1\leq j\leq s}
is upper bounded by the maximum for this linear programming problem, which is less than or equal to
d_{s}
. This leads to a contradiction.

Now, the coefficients involved in the linear programming problems
LP
(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s})
are rational numbers, hence the maxima will be rational numbers. Therefore, these linear programming problems can be solved by computers without numerical errors in finitely many steps. We used a computer program to verify that in the cases

(s,n)=(5,5),(6,3),(7,3),(8,3),(9,3),(10,3),(11,3),

the maxima for linear programming problems

(t_{1},t_{2},...,t_{s})
are indeed upper bounded by
d_{s}
. For those
(s,n)
, there are
126,28,36,45,55,66,78
linear programming problems for each
(s,n)
respectively. The codes we used were initially generated by ChatGPT and then modified by the author. The codes run in Sage and took about one hour to run on a personal computer.
∎

Now, we will apply Lemma
7.1
to prove Theorem
1.6
. The method is similar to that of Shao
[

, Proposition 3.1]

[

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

We will prove Theorem
1.6
by induction on the number of prime divisors of
W
. The base case is
W
being a single prime
p
. Note that by coprime assumptions in Theorem
1.6
, we have
p\geq 7
when
s\geq 6
and
p\geq 11
when
s=5
.

For every
1\leq j\leq s
, write the image of
f_{j}
as a decreasing seqeunce
a_{0,j}\geq a_{1,j}\geq a_{2,j}\geq...\geq a_{n-1,j}
in interval
[0,1]
, where
n=(p-1)/2
. Note that by lower bounds for
p
, we have
n\geq 3
when
s\geq 6
and
n\geq 5
when
s=5
. Moreover, we have lower bound for the total average of
a_{i,j}
, that is

\frac{1}{ns}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i<n\\
1\leq j\leq s\end{subarray}}a_{i,j}=\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{s}])>d_{s}.

Also, the leading terms
a_{0,j}
are nonzero since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}]\neq 0
for
1\leq j\leq s
. Therefore, we can apply Lemma
7.1
and conclude that there exist indices
i_{1},i_{2},...,i_{s}
with
i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{s}\geq 2n
such that

\frac{1}{s}(a_{i_{1},1}+a_{i_{2},2}+...+a_{i_{s},s})>d_{s}\;\text{and}\;a_{i_{j},j}\neq 0\;\text{for all}\;1\leq j\leq s.

Let
I_{j}=\{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)}:f_{j}(x)\geq a_{i_{j},j}\}
for
1\leq j\leq s
. The size of
I_{j}
is at least
i_{j}+1
for
1\leq j\leq s
. By the Cauchy–Davenport inequality, we have

\begin{split}|I_{1}+I_{2}+...+I_{s}|&\geq\min\{p,\;|I_{1}|+|I_{2}|+...+|I_{s}|-(s-1)\}\\
&\geq\min\{p,\;i_{1}+i_{2}+...+i_{s}+1\}\\
&\geq\min\{p,\;2n+1\}\\
&=p\end{split}

hence the sumset
I_{1}+I_{2}+...+I_{s}
is
\mathbb{Z}/p
. Thus, for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/p
, there exist
x_{j}\in I_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq s
such that
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
. By constructions, we have
f_{j}(x_{j})\geq a_{i_{j},j}>0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
and

\frac{1}{s}(f_{1}(x_{1})+f_{2}(x_{2})+...+f_{s}(x_{s}))\geq\frac{1}{s}(a_{i_{1},1}+a_{i_{2},2}+...+a_{i_{s},s})>d_{s}.

This completes the proof of base case.

Now, for general modulus
W
, suppose Theorem
1.6
is true for all moduli whose numbers of prime divisors are strictly less than the number of prime divisors of
W
. Write
W=pW^{\prime}
for some prime
p
and square free integer
W^{\prime}
, where
W^{\prime}
has one less prime divisors than
W
. By Chinese remainder theorem, we can identify
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
with
(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)}\times(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
. The elements in
(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
can be conveniently written as
(x,x^{\prime})
where
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)},x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
.

For each
1\leq j\leq s
, define
g_{j}:(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
by setting

g_{j}(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}}f_{j}(x,x^{\prime})

for all
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)}
. Note that we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[g_{j}]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}]\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
and

\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[g_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[g_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[g_{s}])=\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{s}])>d_{s}.

Since we know that the base case is true, for all
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}/W
, we have
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(g_{1}(x_{1})+g_{2}(x_{2})+...+g_{s}(x_{s}))>d_{s}
and
g_{j}(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

For each
1\leq j\leq s
, define
h_{j}:(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
by setting

h_{j}(x^{\prime})=f_{j}(x_{j},x^{\prime})

for all
x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
. Note that we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{j}]=g_{j}(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
and

\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{s}])=\frac{1}{s}(g_{1}(x_{1})+g_{2}(x_{2})+...+g_{s}(x_{s}))>d_{s}.

By induction hypothesis, we have
y^{\prime}=x_{1}^{\prime}+x_{2}^{\prime}+...+x_{s}^{\prime}
for some
x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{s}^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(h_{1}(x_{1}^{\prime})+h_{2}(x_{2}^{\prime})+...+h_{s}(x_{s}^{\prime}))>d_{s}
and
h_{j}(x_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

which is the same as

\frac{1}{s}(f_{1}(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})+f_{2}(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})+...+f_{s}(x_{s},x_{s}^{\prime}))>d_{s}
and
f_{j}(x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Hence,
(y,y^{\prime})=(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})+(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})+...+(x_{s},x_{s}^{\prime})
is the desired decomposition.
∎

Proposition 8.1
.

Let

or

. Let

be a function with

. For all

, we have

for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+...+f(x_{s}))>d_{s}
and
f(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Proof.

We will divide into four different cases according to the values of
s
.

When
s\geq 8
:

There are two elements in
(\mathbb{Z}/5)^{\times(2)}
, which are
1
and
4
. Note that
f(1)
and
f(4)
are nonzero since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>D_{s}\geq 1/2
.

Observe that for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, there are two ways to write
y
as a sum of
1
’s and
4
’s, so that the number of
1
’s is not less than the number of
4
’s in one way, and the number of
4
’s is not less than the number of
1
’s in the other way. This is because we can consider

\underbrace{1+1+...+1}_{r\;\text{times}}+\underbrace{4+4+...+4}_{s-r\;\text{times}}=r+4(s-r)=4s-3r

for
0\leq r\leq 4
and
s-4\leq r\leq s
. Note that
4s-3r
ranges through all congruence classes
\mathrm{mod}\;5
when
r
ranges through all congruence classes
\mathrm{mod}\;5
.

Thus, for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, we can pick a way to write
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
where
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/5)^{\times(2)}
so that

\frac{1}{s}(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+...+f(x_{s}))\geq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>D_{s}\geq d_{s}.

When
s=7
:

By enumerating all possibilities, we observe that for
0,2,3\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, there are two ways to write them as a sum of
1
’s and
4
’s, so that the number of
1
’s is not less than the number of
4
’s in one way, and the number of
4
’s is not less than the number of
1
’s in the other way. Hence, the elements
0,2,3
can be handled by the same reasoning in previous case
(s\geq 8)
.

For
1,4\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, we have
1=1+1+1+1+4+4+4\;\mathrm{mod}\;5
and
4=1+1+1+4+4+4+4\;\mathrm{mod}\;5
. The analysis for elements
1
and
4
is similar and we will demonstrate it for the element
1
. First, note that
f(1),f(4)>2D_{7}-1
since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]=(f(1)+f(4))/2>D_{7}
. To proceed, we compute

\frac{1}{7}(4f(1)+3f(4))>\frac{1}{7}(6D_{7}+(2D_{7}-1))=\frac{5}{7}=d_{7}.

When
s=6
:

By enumerating all possibilities, we observe that for
0,1,4\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, there are two ways to write them as a sum of
1
’s and
4
’s, so that the number of
1
’s is not less than the number of
4
’s in one way, and the number of
4
’s is not less than the number of
1
’s in the other way. Hence, the elements
0,1,4
can be handled by the same reasoning in previous cases.

For
2,3\in\mathbb{Z}/5
, we have
2=1+1+1+1+4+4\;\mathrm{mod}\;5
and
3=1+1+4+4+4+4\;\mathrm{mod}\;5
. The analysis for elements
2
and
3
is similar and we will demonstrate it for the element
2
. First, note that
f(1),f(4)>2D_{6}-1
since
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]=(f(1)+f(4))/2>D_{6}
. To proceed, we compute

\frac{1}{6}(4f(1)+2f(4))>\frac{1}{7}(4D_{6}+2(2D_{6}-1))=\frac{5}{6}=d_{6}.

When
s=5
:

Since the size of
(\mathbb{Z}/35)^{\times(2)}
is
6
and
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>D_{5}
, we have
f(x)>6D_{5}-5
for any
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/35)^{\times(2)}
. Note that the
5
-fold sumset of
(\mathbb{Z}/35)^{\times(2)}
is
\mathbb{Z}/35
, hence, for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/35

y=x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}+x_{5}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\in(\mathbb{Z}/35)^{\times(2)}
. To complete the proof, we compute

\frac{1}{5}(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+f(x_{3})+f(x_{4})+f(x_{5}))>\frac{1}{5}(5(6D_{5}-5))=\frac{9}{10}=d_{5}.

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>D_{s}
. For all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/24W
with
y\equiv s\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
, we have
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/24W)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+...+f(x_{s}))>d_{s}
and
f(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we may assume
5|W
when
s\geq 6
and
35|W
when
s=5
. This is because if
W_{1}|W_{2}
and Theorem
8.2
holds for modulus
W_{2}
, then Theorem
8.2
is also true for modulus
W_{1}
.

Write
W=mW^{\prime}
where
m=5
if
s\geq 6
, and
m=35
if
s=5
. By Chinese remainder theorem, we can identify
(\mathbb{Z}/24W)^{\times(2)}
with
(\mathbb{Z}/24)^{\times(2)}\times(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)}\times(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
. The elements in
(\mathbb{Z}/24W)^{\times(2)}
can be conveniently written as
(1,x,x^{\prime})
where
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)},x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
, since
1
is the only element in
(\mathbb{Z}/24)^{\times(2)}
.

Define
g:(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
by setting

g(x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}}f(1,x,x^{\prime})

for all
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)}
. Note that we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[g]=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>D_{s}
. By applying Proposition
8.1
, for all
(s,y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{Z}/24W
, we have
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/m)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(g(x_{1})+g(x_{2})+...+g(x_{s}))>d_{s}
and
g(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

For each
1\leq j\leq s
, define
h_{j}:(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
by setting

h_{j}(x^{\prime})=f(1,x_{j},x^{\prime})

for all
x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
. Note that we have
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{j}]=g(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
and

\frac{1}{s}(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[h_{s}])=\frac{1}{s}(g(x_{1})+g(x_{2})+...+g(x_{s}))>d_{s}.

By applying Theorem
1.6
, we have
y^{\prime}=x_{1}^{\prime}+x_{2}^{\prime}+...+x_{s}^{\prime}
for some
x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime},...,x_{s}^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W^{\prime})^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(h_{1}(x_{1}^{\prime})+h_{2}(x_{2}^{\prime})+...+h_{s}(x_{s}^{\prime}))>d_{s}
and
h_{j}(x_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

which is the same as

\frac{1}{s}(f(1,x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})+f(1,x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})+...+f(1,x_{s},x_{s}^{\prime}))>d_{s}
and
f(1,x_{j},x_{j}^{\prime})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Hence,
(s,y,y^{\prime})=(1,x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})+(1,x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})+...+(1,x_{s},x_{s}^{\prime})
is the desired decomposition.
∎

Now, we have all ingredients needed to deduce Theorem
1.4
from the transference principle. Roughly speaking, Theorem
8.2
is essential for completing the
W
-trick. We state the general transference mechanism as follow.

Hypothesis
(s,\mu)
:

Let
W
be a square free integer with
(24,W)=1
, and
f:(\mathbb{Z}/24W)^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
be a function with
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f]>\mu
. For all
y\in\mathbb{Z}/24W
with
y\equiv s\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
, we have
y=x_{1}+x_{2}+...+x_{s}
for some
x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/24W)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(f(x_{1})+f(x_{2})+...+f(x_{s}))\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
f(x_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
s\geq 5
be an integer,
0<\mu<1
be a real number, and
A
be a subset of primes with relative lower density
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)>\sqrt{\mu}
. Suppose the Hypothesis
(s,\mu)
is true, then, for all sufficiently large integers
y
that are congruent to
s\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
, there exist primes
p_{1},p_{2},...,p_{s}\in A
such that
y=p_{1}^{2}+p_{2}^{2}+...+p_{s}^{2}
.

Theorem
1.4
indeed follows from Proposition
8.3
since Theorem
8.2
implies that Hypothesis
(s,D_{s})
is true for
s\geq 5
. Proposition
8.3
is quite close to Section 3 in
[
LP10
]
, where Li and Pan applied the transference principle to deduce their density version of Vinogradov three primes theorem from local results. Hence, the proof of Proposition
8.3
only requires slight modifications to the proof in Section 3 in
[
LP10
]
, and it will be deferred to Appendix
B
.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
2.2

For any odd prime
p
, let

\epsilon_{p}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $p\equiv 1$ mod $4$}\\
i&\text{if $p\equiv 3$ mod $4$}\end{cases}

First, we recall the following result on Gauss sums with reduced residue classes.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
p\geq 3,n\geq 1
and
t\in\mathbb{Z}
with
(t,p)=1
, then

\sum_{y\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}}e_{p^{n}}(ty^{2})=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{t}{p}\right)\epsilon_{p}\sqrt{p}-1&\text{when $n=1$}\\
0&\text{when $n\geq 2$}\end{cases}

where
\left(\frac{\cdot}{p}\right)
is the Legendre symbol.

Proof.

The case that
n=1
is more well-known, see
[
IK04
, Chapter 3, Section 3.5, Theorem 3.3]
. The cases that
n\geq 2
can be derived from
[
IK04
, Chapter 3, Section 3.4, Lemma 3.1]
. Alternatively, one can calculate directly

\begin{split}\sum_{y\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{\times}}e_{p^{n}}(ty^{2})&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-1})^{\times}\\
w\in\mathbb{Z}/p\end{subarray}}e_{p^{n}}(t(z+p^{n-1}w)^{2})\\
&=\sum_{z\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-1})^{\times}}e_{p^{n}}(tz^{2})\sum_{w\in\mathbb{Z}/p}e_{p^{n}}(2tp^{n-1}zw)\\
&=0\end{split}

since
z,t
are coprime to
p
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2
.

We will partition the squares in
\mathbb{Z}/p^{n}
according to the divisibility of
p
powers. Let
0\leq e<n
, if
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n})^{(2)}
and
p^{e}||x
, then
e
must be even and we can write
x=p^{e}x^{\prime}
for some
x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-e})^{\times(2)}
.

Write
t=p^{v}t^{\prime}
with
(t^{\prime},p)=1
. We may assume
v<n
otherwise the results are trivial. When
e<n-v
, we have

\begin{split}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-e})^{\times(2)}}e_{p^{n}}(tp^{e}x^{\prime})&=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-e})^{\times(2)}}e_{p^{n-v-e}}(t^{\prime}x^{\prime})\\
&=p^{v}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-v-e})^{\times(2)}}e_{p^{n-v-e}}(t^{\prime}x^{\prime})\\
&=p^{v}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{y\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-v-e})^{\times}}e_{p^{n-v-e}}(t^{\prime}y^{2})\\
&=\begin{cases}O(p^{v+1/2})&\text{when $n-v-e=1$}\\
0&\text{when $n-v-e\geq 2$}\end{cases}\end{split}

by Lemma
A.1
. When
e\geq n-v
, we have

\bigg\lvert\sum_{x^{\prime}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{n-e})^{\times(2)}}e_{p^{n}}(tp^{e}x^{\prime})\bigg\rvert\leq p^{n-e}.

Ranging through all
e
such that
e\geq n-v
, the contribution is at most

p^{v}+p^{v-1}+...+p+1=O(p^{v})

Combining the contributions from
e<n-v
and
e\geq n-v
completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
8.3

In this section, we will closely follow Section 3 in
[
LP10
]
to prove Proposition
8.3
.

First, we will define majorants that parametrize prime squares. These majorants have appeared in
[
Cho18
]
,
[
Gao25
]
and
[
Zha25
]
. Let
N,W\geq 2
be integers with
W\leq\log N
, and
b\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
. Denote the number of square roots of
b
by
\sigma_{W}(b)
, that is

\sigma_{W}(b)=|\{x\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times}:x^{2}=b\}|

In fact, the value of
\sigma_{W}(b)
is independent of
b
and equal to
\varphi(W)/|(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}|
, so we will often abbreviate it as
\sigma_{W}
. Define a function
\nu_{b}:[N]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by setting

\nu_{b}(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}(b)}(2p\log p)&\text{if $Wn+b=p^{2}$ for some prime $p$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for all
n\in[N]
. Roughly speaking, the majorant
\nu_{b}
parametrizes weighted prime squares that are
b\;\mathrm{mod}\;W
in
[WN]
.

Before proceeding further, we record the following well-known asymptotic formulae which would be useful later:

(B.1)

\sum_{x\leq X}x\Lambda(x)=\frac{X^{2}}{2}+O(X^{2}\exp(-c\sqrt{\log X}))

where
\Lambda
is von Mangoldt function and
c
is a positive absolute constant. Also, let
Q>0,X\geq 1
, and
a,r
be positive integers with
r\leq(\log X)^{Q}
and
(a,r)=1
, then

(B.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\leq X\\
x\equiv a\;\mathrm{mod}\;r\end{subarray}}x\Lambda(x)=\frac{X^{2}}{2\varphi(r)}+O(X^{2}\exp(-c_{Q}\sqrt{\log X}))

where
c_{Q}
is a positive absolute constant depending on
Q
. These asymptotic formulae can be proved by Siegel-Walfisz theorem and summation by parts.

By applying formula (
B.2
), one can show that

\sum_{n\in[N]}\nu_{b}(n)=N+O(N\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N}))

where
c>0
is a constant. (The constant
c
here might be different from the constant
c
in formula (
B.1
), however, we will abuse the notation here and throughout.)

Now, let
A
be a subset of primes with relative lower density
\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)>\sqrt{\mu}
. Let
N,W\geq 2
be integers with
W\leq\log N
, and
b\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
, we will define a function
f_{A,b}:[N]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by setting

f_{A,b}(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}}(2p\log p)1_{A}(p)&\text{if $Wn+b=p^{2}$ for some prime $p$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

for all
n\in[N]
. Roughly speaking, the function
f_{A,b}
can be thought as a weighted,
W
-tricked version of characteristic function of
A
.

Let
y
be a sufficiently large (depending on
A
and
s
) integer such that
y\equiv s\;\mathrm{mod}\;24
. Let

w=\log\log\log y,\;\;W=8\prod_{3\leq p<w}p,\;\;N=\left\lfloor\frac{2y}{sW}\right\rfloor.

Note that we have
w=O(\log\log\log N)
and hence
W\leq\log N
. We will work with
\nu_{b},f_{A,b}
defined as before based on this choice of
N,W
.

Let
\kappa>0
be a sufficiently small parameter depending on
A
, for the sake of the definiteness, say
\kappa=(\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)-\sqrt{\mu})^{2}/10^{10}
. Define a function
f:(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}\longrightarrow[0,1]
by setting

f(b)=\max\left\{0,\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{n\in[N]}f_{A,b}(n)-\kappa\right\}

for all
b\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
. Note that

\begin{split}&\sum_{b\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}}\sum_{n\in[N]}f_{A,b}(n)\\
&=\frac{2\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in A\\
W\leq p^{2}\leq W(N+1)\\
p^{2}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}\end{subarray}}p\log p\\
&\geq\frac{2\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in A\\
p\leq\sqrt{W(N+1)}\end{subarray}}p\log p-O(W)\Bigg)\\
&\geq\frac{2\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}}\Bigg(\sum_{p\leq(\sqrt{\mu}+2\sqrt{\kappa})\sqrt{W(N+1)}}p\log p-O(W)\Bigg)\;\;\;\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{(since $\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(A)>\sqrt{\mu}$)}\\
&\geq\frac{2\varphi(W)}{W\sigma_{W}}\left(\frac{(\sqrt{\mu}+2\sqrt{\kappa})^{2}WN}{2}-O(WN\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N}))\right)\;\;\quad\text{(by formula \eqref{eqs: prime 1})}\\
&\geq\frac{\varphi(W)}{\sigma_{W}}\left((\sqrt{\mu}+2\sqrt{\kappa})^{2}N-O(N\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N}))\right)\end{split}

Thus, we have

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{b\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}}f(b)\geq\mu+\kappa

Even though the relative lower density of
A
is greater than
\sqrt{\mu}
, the
\nu
-weighted density of
A
is only greater than
\mu
, and this is the reason why the threshold in Theorem
1.4
is
\sqrt{D_{s}}
instead of
D_{s}
.

By Hypothesis
(s,\mu)
, we have
y=b_{1}+b_{2}+...+b_{s}\;\mathrm{mod}\;W
for some
b_{1},b_{2},...,b_{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W)^{\times(2)}
such that

\frac{1}{s}(f(b_{1})+f(b_{2})+...+f(b_{s}))\geq\frac{1}{2}
and
f(b_{j})\neq 0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

In particular, this implies that

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{A,b_{1}}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{A,b_{2}}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{A,b_{s}}]>\frac{s}{2}(1+\kappa)\;\text{and}\;\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{A,b_{j}}]>\frac{\kappa}{2}\;\text{for all}\;1\leq j\leq s.

Let
y^{\prime}=(y-b_{1}-b_{2}-...-b_{s})/W
. The goal is to find
n_{1},n_{2},...,n_{s}\in[N]
such that

y^{\prime}=n_{1}+n_{2}+...+n_{s}
and
f_{A,b_{j}}(n_{j})>0
for all
1\leq j\leq s

since
Wn_{j}+b_{j}
will be a square of prime in
A
when
f_{A,b_{j}}(n_{j})>0
for all
1\leq j\leq s
.

Now that we have completed the
W
-trick, for the rest of the details of the transference principle, we are able to find a well-formulated proposition in literature to cover them. The following proposition due to Salmensuu
[
Sal21
, Proposition 3.9]
is what we need.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
N\geq 1,s\geq 2
be integers and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
f_{j}:[N]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\nu_{j}:[N]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be functions with
f_{j}\leq\nu_{j}
for
1\leq j\leq s
. Assume the following are true.

(1)

(Pseudorandomness)
\lVert\widehat{\nu}_{j}-\widehat{1}_{[N]}\rVert_{\infty}\leq\eta N
for some
\eta>0
and all
1\leq j\leq s

(2)

(Restriction at
q
)
\lVert\widehat{f}_{j}\rVert_{q}\leq KN^{1-1/q}
for some
K>0,s-1<q<s
and all
1\leq j\leq s

(3)

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{1}]+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{2}]+...+\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{s}]>\frac{s}{2}(1+\epsilon)

(4)

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[f_{j}]>\frac{\epsilon}{2}
for all
1\leq j\leq s

Suppose
\eta
is sufficiently small depending on
s,\epsilon,K,q
. Then, for any integers
y
with

y\in\left((1-(\epsilon/32)^{2})\frac{sN}{2},(1+(\epsilon/32))\frac{sN}{2}\right)

we have

f_{1}*f_{2}*...*f_{s}(y)\geq c(\epsilon,s)N^{s-1}

where
c(\epsilon,s)>0
is a constant that only depends on
\epsilon,s
.

The proof of Proposition
B.1
essentially involves embedding
[N]
in
\mathbb{Z}/N^{\prime}
for some prime
N^{\prime}\in\left[(1+(\kappa^{2}/100))\frac{y}{W},(1+(2\kappa^{2}/100))\frac{y}{W}\right]
. Passing from
N
to
N^{\prime}
loses density by a factor of
2/s
, and this is why the lower bound
1/2
appears in Hypothesis
(s,\mu)
.

To apply Proposition
B.1
to
f_{A,b_{j}},\nu_{b_{j}}
for
1\leq j\leq s
and complete the proof, it remains to verify the pseudorandomness and restriction at
q
. These types of estimates have been proved by Chow
[
Cho18
]
, Gao
[
Gao25
]
and Zhao
[
Zha25
]
. The version we need is in
[
Zha25
, Proposition 3.2, 3.3]
, which states that

\lVert\widehat{\nu}_{b}-\widehat{1}_{[N]}\rVert_{\infty}\ll_{\epsilon}\frac{N}{w^{1/2-\epsilon}}

for all
\epsilon>0
, and

\lVert\widehat{f}_{A,b}\rVert_{q}\ll_{q}N^{1-1/q}

for all
q>4
.
