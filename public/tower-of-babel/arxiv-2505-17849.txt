A sparse h ​ p hp -finite element method for piecewise-smooth differential equations with periodic boundary conditions
Daniel VandenHeuvel, Sheehan Olver

Abstract
We develop an efficient h ​ p hp -finite element method for piecewise-smooth differential equations with periodic boundary conditions, using orthogonal polynomials defined on circular arcs. The operators derived from this basis are banded and achieve optimal complexity regardless of h h or p p , both for building the discretisation and solving the resulting linear system in the case where the operator is symmetric positive definite. The basis serves as a useful alternative to other bases such as the Fourier or integrated Legendre bases, especially for problems with discontinuities. We relate the convergence properties of these bases to regions of analyticity in the complex plane, and further use several differential equation examples to demonstrate these properties. The basis spans the low order eigenfunctions of constant coefficient differential operators, thereby achieving better smoothness properties for time-evolution partial differential equations.



1
Introduction

In this work, we develop a periodic piecewise smooth basis for solving differential equations with periodic boundary conditions in one dimension. The basis can either be viewed as a re-orthogonalised variant of Fourier series, or via the change of variables
x=\cos\theta
,
y=\sin\theta
, as bivariate orthogonal polynomials defined over arcs of the unit circle. The construction leads to sparse banded operators for the weak Laplacian and mass matrices, with bandwidth independent of the truncation of the basis, hence the complexity of numerical solutions matches that of Fourier series. Since our basis is periodic, the numerical solution remains periodic and, unlike a standard Fourier approach
[
1
,
2
]
, we can easily handle discontinuities by placing element nodes at the points of discontinuity. While in this work we only consider one-dimensional problems, the basis we develop can be extended to two-dimensional problems such as radial problems, for example differential equations in a sector.

Differential equations with periodic boundary conditions arise in a variety of applications, such as molecular dynamics
[
3
]
, quantum mechanics
[
4
]
, and fluid mechanics
[
5
]
. Applying orthogonal polynomials for solving these differential equations with a spectral element method can be traced back to the hierarchical
p
-finite element method (FEM) basis using integrated Legendre polynomials as introduced by Babuška and Szabó
[
6
]
, where high-order orthogonal polynomials can be utilised together with sparse operators for the efficient numerical solution of such problems. While other classical bases such as the Fourier or the integrated Legendre basis could be applied to these periodic problems
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
8
]
, they all have certain limitations. The Fourier basis is not able to handle discontinuities, and other orthogonal bases do not have periodicity as a natural property. For example, the integrated Legendre basis can be used for the spectral element method by making the shape functions themselves periodic
[
9
]
, but the periodicity in the resulting solution’s derivatives may be lost numerically at the element junctions, in particular at
\theta=\pm T
for
2T
-periodic problems, when solving problems over time. We call this numerical difference in periodicity the
periodic drift
and find that solutions using bases such as the integrated Legendre basis may display growing periodic drift over time, particularly in the solution’s derivatives. The basis we develop in this work gets around this property by, instead of defining the polynomials on the interval in the
\theta
coordinate, we define the polynomials on circular arcs instead, meaning in the
x
and
y
coordinates. Thus, each individual basis polynomial is itself periodic when extended to the whole interval and we obtain a sparse and efficient solver.

We find that this basis helps to reduce the effects of periodic drift, an advantage over bases such as the integrated Legendre basis. A key contribution of this paper is the analysis and comparison of these different bases, that is, the Fourier basis (which we denote
\mathbf{F}
), the integrated Legendre basis (which we denote
\mathbf{W}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
and is polynomial in
\theta
) and the new arc polynomial basis (which we denote
\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
and is polynomial in
x
and
y
). A similar basis was previously introduced in
[
10
]
, which defines
half-range Chebyshev polynomials
for the purpose of Fourier extensions, which are in fact a special case of our arc polynomials corresponding to a semicircular arc. Orthogonal polynomials on arcs were also considered by
[
11
]
; however an important difference — and a primary contribution — of our work is the embedding of such a basis within an
hp
-FEM framework, together with a subsequent convergence analysis. The bases we consider in this work are summarised in Table
1
.

Our introduction of the basis

In this sense, our work can be viewed as having a similar goal: we replace the classical Fourier basis by a more flexible piecewise polynomial basis

The paper is structured as follows. In Section
2
we introduce the semiclassical Jacobi polynomials from
[
13
,
14
]
and generalise them to the non-integrable weight
x^{a}(t-x)^{c}/(1-x)
, serving as a fundamental component of our spectral element method for their use as bubble functions, basis elements which are supported on a single element. Section

\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}=1,\,x\geq h\}

3

5

[
7

Definition 3
.

The upper triangular

Proposition 2
.

The matrix

The matrix

(3.14)

When convenient, we will omit the dependence on

Proposition 5
.

The matrices

\displaystyle D_{(b),\mathbf{p}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}p_{n}^{(b)}(\theta)

with the coefficients coming from the differentiation matrices. The matrix

is defined from interlacing

Proof.

Differentiating

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{p}^{(b)}=x\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}y}\mathbf{P}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}(\sigma)-y\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}x}\mathbf{P}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}(\sigma)=\mathbf{q}^{(b+1)}\frac{1}{1-h}D_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b+1,1/2)},

(3.19)

and we let

(3.20)

The

(3.21)

Subtracting (

) from (

∎

3.3
Mass matrix

We now consider computing the mass matrix
M^{(b)}:=[\mathbf{P}^{(b)}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}^{(b)}
. We can assume here that
b=0
since, for
b\neq 0
, we can write
\mathbf{P}^{(b)}=\mathbf{P}^{(0)}R_{(0)}^{(b)}
so that

\displaystyle M^{(b)}

\displaystyle=\left[R_{(0)}^{(b)}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0)}R_{(0)}^{(b)},

(3.22)

thus all we need is the ability to compute
M^{(0)}
. The entries of

are defined by

\displaystyle M_{ij}^{(0)}=\int_{-\varphi}^{\varphi}P_{i}^{(0)}(\theta)P_{j}^{(0)}(\theta)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta},

where
P_{k}^{(0)}
denotes the
k
th polynomial in
\mathbf{P}^{(0)}
. This is exactly the inner product defining orthogonality for the
\mathbf{P}^{(0)}
polynomials, hence
M^{(0)}
is diagonal. The entries come from interlacing the mass matrices for the
p_{n}^{(0)}
and
q_{n}^{(0)}
polynomials, in particular their squared norms. Using the formula for the norms of the semiclassical Jacobi polynomials from (
A.1
) in

, we can show that

\displaystyle\|p_{n}^{(0)}\|^{2}=4\arccsc(\!\!\sqrt\tau),\quad\|q_{n}^{(0)}\|^{2}=\frac{(1-h)^{2}}{2}\left[\tau^{2}\arccsc(\!\!\sqrt\tau)+(2-\tau)\sqrt{\tau-1}\right].

(3.23)

Interlacing these expressions gives us
M^{(0)}
. We have therefore proven the following.

Proposition 6
.

Define the mass matrix
M^{(b,h)}:=[\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}
. The matrix
M^{(0,h)}
is given by

M^{(0,h)}=\diag(m_{p},m_{q},m_{p},m_{q},\ldots),

(3.24)

where

and

is given by

(3.25)

When convenient, we will omit the dependence on
h
, writing

.

3.4
Transforms

To finish this section, we are interested in computing transforms that approximate a given function
f(x,y)
by a polynomial in the arc polynomial basis. In particular, define the polynomial

by

f_{n}(x,y):=\hat{f}_{0}p_{0}(x,y)+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[\hat{f}_{j1}q_{j}(x,y)+\hat{f}_{j2}p_{j}(x,y)\right].

The aim is to find coefficients

,
\hat{f}_{j1}
, and
\hat{f}_{j2}
such that

at some set of nodes. The following lemma tells us that these nodes should be the Gauss–Radau quadrature nodes.

Lemma 1
.

Assume

. Let
\{(\xi_{j},w_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
denote the Gauss–Radau quadrature nodes and weights with respect to the weight

and

\langle f,g\rangle_{n}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}\left[f(x_{j},y_{j})g(x_{j},y_{j})+f(x_{j},-y_{j})g(x_{j},-y_{j})\right],

(3.27)

where
x_{j}=(h-1)\xi_{j}+1
and
y_{j}=\sqrt{1-x_{j}^{2}}
.

Proof.

This is a simple extension of Theorems 1.22 and 1.23 in
[
19
]
adapted to Gauss–Radau quadrature and is similar to the quadrature rules developed in
[
11
]
. The Gauss–Radau quadrature nodes and weights can be computed using Theorem 3.2 in
[
19
]
. Note that the summand for

is
2w_{1}f(1,0)g(1,0)
.
∎

Lemma
1
can be used to compute the coefficients in (

[

]
, we can derive Proposition
7

4
Spectral element method

We now use the arc polynomials to derive a spectral element method. This will require that we first extend the arc polynomials in a piecewise basis over multiple intervals, and then we need to derive formulas for the mass matrix and the weak Laplacian. The bases we define are analogous to the bases defined in terms of the integrated Legendre functions introduced in
[
6
,
7
]
. In what follows, we need the following definitions.

Definition 8
.

We define the interval
I:=[-\pi,\pi]
and associate with
\bm{\theta}:=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n+1})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
the partition
-\pi=\theta_{1}<\cdots<\theta_{n}<\theta_{n+1}=\pi
, where
n\geq 2
. The
i
th element is
E_{i}:=[\theta_{i},\theta_{i+1}]
with length
\ell_{i}:=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. We further define
E_{0}=E_{n}
,
\ell_{0}=\ell_{n}
, and
\theta_{0}=\theta_{n+1}
.

Definition 9
.

The mapping
a_{i}\colon E_{i}\to E_{i}^{\prime}
is defined by

a_{i}(\theta):=\theta-\frac{\theta_{i}+\theta_{i+1}}{2},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(4.1)

where
E_{i}^{\prime}:=[-\varphi_{i},\varphi_{i}]
and
\varphi_{i}:=\ell_{i}/2
. We define
h_{i}:=\cos\varphi_{i}
and
\tau_{i}:=2/(1-h_{i})
.

It is a crucial property that the mapping (
9
) is defined only as a translation, instead of using, for example,
b_{i}(\theta)=(\pi/2\ell_{i})(2\theta-\theta_{i}-\theta_{i+1})
which maps
E_{i}
into
[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, since scaling implies that trigonometric polynomials on a single element will no longer be trigonometric polynomials with the same period on the entire interval
I
. This property is what will allow our bases to preserve periodicity, as we discuss later. As an example of this issue with scaling, consider
f(\theta)=\cos\theta
on
E_{i}
. We find
f(a_{i}(\theta))=\sin[(\theta_{i}+\theta_{i+1})/2]\sin(\theta)+\cos[(\theta_{i}+\theta_{i+1})/2]\cos\theta
which is still
2\pi
-periodic, while
f(b_{i}(\theta))=\sin[\pi(\theta_{i+1}-\theta)/\ell_{i}]
is
2\ell_{i}
-periodic.

The first polynomials we define are related to the arc polynomials where
b=0
. These are simply translated versions of
\mathbf{P}^{(0)}
that are repeated over each interval. Formally, we have the following definition.

Definition 10

(Piecewise arc polynomials with
b=0
)
.

The polynomial
P_{mj;i}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}\colon I\to\mathbb{R}
is given by

P_{mj;i}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}(\theta):=\begin{cases}P_{mj;i}^{(b)}\left(a_{i}(\theta)\right)&\theta\in E_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4.2)

where
m=0,1,\ldots
denotes the polynomial degree,
i=1,2,\ldots,n
indexes the element, and
j=1,2
distinguishes the two families of arc polynomials.
The polynomials
P_{mj;i}^{(b)}
are defined by

\displaystyle P_{m1;i}^{(b)}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=q_{m}^{(b,h_{i})}(\theta),\quad m=1,2,3,\ldots,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(4.3)

\displaystyle P_{m2;i}^{(b)}(\theta)

\displaystyle:=p_{m}^{(b,h_{i})}(\theta),\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots,\quad i=1,\ldots,n.

(4.4)

With these polynomials, we define the basis
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
by

\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{02}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{11}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{12}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{21}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{22}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}&\cdots\end{bmatrix},

(4.5)

where

\mathbf{P}_{mj}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}:=\begin{bmatrix}P_{mj;1}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}&P_{mj;2}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}&\cdots&P_{mj;n}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.6)

The
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
basis is not sufficient on its own for our spectral element method as to obtain sparsity we need a basis comprised of hat and bubble functions, also known as internal and external shape functions, respectively
[
7
,
22
]
. In order to define an analogous basis
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
, let us first determine what the hat functions should look like in such a basis. Since we will be working with trigonometric polynomials, it would not be appropriate to consider linear hat functions in
\theta
. In particular, we should assume that the hat functions are represented as trigonometric polynomials, and that we have
n
of them instead of
n+1
since
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{n+1}
represent the same point on a periodic interval.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\phi_{i}
be a hat function satisfying
\phi_{i}(\theta_{j})=\delta_{ij}
where
\delta_{ij}=1
if
i=j
and
\delta_{ij}=0
otherwise, and
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then
\phi_{i}
can be written as

\phi_{i}(\theta)=\begin{cases}\psi_{i}^{(1)}(\theta)&\theta\in E_{i-1},\\
\psi_{i}^{(2)}(\theta)&\theta\in E_{i},\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(4.7)

where
\psi_{i}^{(1)}(\theta)
and
\psi_{i}^{(2)}(\theta)
are linear trigonometric polynomials of the form

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{(1)}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{1-\csc(\ell_{i-1})\left[\left(\sin\theta_{i-1}+\sin\theta_{i}\right)\cos\theta-\left(\cos\theta_{i-1}+\cos\theta_{i}\right)\sin\theta\right]\right\},

(4.8)

\displaystyle\psi_{i}^{(2)}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{1+\csc(\ell_i)\left[\left(\sin\theta_{i}+\sin\theta_{i+1}\right)\cos\theta-\left(\cos\theta_{i}+\cos\theta_{i+1}\right)\sin\theta\right]\right\}.

(4.9)

Proof.

This can be easily proven by simply substituting
\theta=\theta_{j}
into (
4.8
)–(
4.9
) and considering the cases
i=j
and
i\neq j
separately.
∎

Figure 3
:

\phi_{1}(\theta)
(blue),
\phi_{2}(\theta)
(red), and
\phi_{3}(\theta)
(green) for the equally spaced grid
\bm{\theta}=(-\pi,-\pi/3,\pi/3,\pi)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
. The vertical lines show the grids. These hat functions are piecewise affine functions in
x
and
y
.

This lemma gives us the definition that we need for our hat functions
\phi_{i}
, and thus for our
b=-1
basis. An example of what these hat functions look like in the case
n=3
is shown in Figure
3
. This basis will be what we use to define our trial and test functions for our spectral element method.

Definition 11

(Piecewise arc polynomials with
b=-1
)
.

The
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis is defined by

\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{H}^{\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{B}^{\bm{\theta}}\end{bmatrix},

(4.10)

where
\mathbf{H}^{\bm{\theta}}
are the hat functions
\mathbf{H}^{\bm{\theta}}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n})
from Lemma
2
, and
B
are the bubble functions

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}^{\bm{\theta}}:=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{12}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{21}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{22}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}&\mathbf{P}_{31}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}.

(4.11)

We will typically suppress the dependence of
\mathbf{H}^{\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{B}^{\bm{\theta}}
on
\bm{\theta}
.

We note that a key difference between the
b=0
and
b=-1
bases is that the
b=0
basis can represent discontinuous functions while the
b=-1
basis cannot, with both bases giving the same convergence rate as we eventually show in Theorem
6
. We see the difference between these two bases later in Section
6.1
, where we expand the solution to a differential equation in the
b=-1
basis while expanding a discontinuous right-hand side in the
b=0
basis.

4.1
Mass matrix

The mass matrix for these piecewise bases can be easily computed in terms of the arc polynomials, where we define the mass matrix
M^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
by
M^{(b),\bm{\theta}}:=[\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
. Let us start by considering
M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
. To compute the entries, note that since the
b=0
arc polynomials are orthogonal with respect to the unit weight,
M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
must be a diagonal matrix. These entries can be found by noting that, for a given function
f(\theta)=g(a_{i}(\theta))
supported over an element
E_{i}
,

\int_{I}f(\theta)^{2}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}=\int_{E_{i}}g(a_{i}(\theta))^{2}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}=\int_{-\varphi_{i}}^{\varphi_{i}}g(\theta)^{2}\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}.

(4.12)

Thus,
M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
can be obtained by simply taking the corresponding entries of the mass matrix for the associated arc polynomials. The
b=-1
case can be obtained using the connection matrix defined in the next section, similarly to (
3.22
), so that
M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
. These findings are summarised below.

Proposition 8
.

The mass matrix
M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
is given by

M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=\diag\left(\|\mathbf{p}_{0}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\|^{2},\|\mathbf{q}_{1}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\|^{2},\|\mathbf{p}_{1}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\|^{2},\ldots\right),

(4.13)

where

\|\mathbf{p}_{m}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}:=\diag\left(\|p_{m}^{(0,h_{1})}\|_{(0,h_{1})}^{2},\ldots,\|p_{m}^{(0,h_{n})}\|_{(0,h_{n})}^{2}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

(4.14)

and similarly for
\|\mathbf{q}_{m}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\|^{2}
. The mass matrix
M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
is given by
M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
, where
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
.

4.2
Connection matrix

The

Let

Proposition 10

The matrices

4.2.1
Factorising
CB^{3}
-arrowhead matrices

Similar to
[
7
]
, it will be useful to know how to compute the reverse Cholesky factorisation of a
CB^{3}
-arrowhead matrix. In particular, given a
CB^{3}
-arrowhead matrix
A
, we are interested in computing a lower triangular matrix
L
such that
A=L^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L
. The reverse Cholesky factorisation is preferable to the standard Cholesky factorisation because it leads to less fill-in and instead has a similar structure to a
CB^{3}
-arrowhead matrix, as the following results show.

Lemma 4
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a symmetric positive definite matrix with sub-bandwidths
(\ell,\ell)
. Then
A=L^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L
where

A=\begin{bmatrix}a_{11}&\mathbf{a}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\\
\mathbf{a}&A_{0}\end{bmatrix}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad L=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{a_{11}-\mathbf{v}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{v}}\\
\mathbf{v}&L_{0}\end{bmatrix},

(4.22)

where
\mathbf{v}=L_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{a}
,
A_{0}=L_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{0}
, and
L_{0}
is a
(\ell,0)
banded matrix. In particular,
L
is an
(\ell,0)
banded matrix except for the first column. This matrix
L
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n\ell)
complexity.

Proof.

The form of
L
can be verified directly by multiplying out
L^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L
. Note that
A_{0}
is a
(\ell,\ell)
banded matrix which gives the banded structure in
L_{0}
. To obtain the complexity in computing
L
, first see that
L_{0}
can be computed from
A_{0}
in
\mathcal{O}(n\ell)
operations since
A_{0}
is banded. Computing
\mathbf{v}=L_{0}^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{a}
then takes
\mathcal{O}(n\ell)
operations also
[
24
]
, and thus
L
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(n\ell)
complexity.
∎

Theorem 2
.

If
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+pm)\times(m+pm)}
is a symmetric positive definite
CB^{3}(\ell,\ell;1,0)
or
CB^{3}(\ell,\ell;0,1)
matrix, then the reverse Cholesky factor
L
of
A
can be decomposed as

L=L_{1}+L_{2},

(4.23)

with
L_{1}
a
CB^{3}(\ell,0;1,1)
matrix and

L_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{L}_{2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix},

(4.24)

where
\widetilde{L}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a matrix whose only non-zero entries are in the first column.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem 4.2 of
[
7
]
. We only consider the
CB^{3}(\ell,\ell;1,0)
case since the second case then follows after transposing. Write

A=\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}&B\\
B^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&D\end{bmatrix}

(4.25)

where, as in Definition
13
,
D=D_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus D_{m}
. The reverse Cholesky factor of
D
is given by
\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{L}_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widetilde{L}_{m}
, where
D_{i}=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m

[
7
]
. Now, write

\begin{bmatrix}A_{0}&B\\
B^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&D\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\\
&\widetilde{L}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}L_{0}\\
L_{B}&\widetilde{L}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{0}+L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{B}&L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}\\
\widetilde{L}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{B}&\widetilde{L}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.26)

We see that
B=L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}
. Letting
L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=(M_{1},M_{2},\ldots,M_{\ell},\mathbf{0},\ldots,\mathbf{0})
and writing

\widetilde{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{L}_{11}\\
\vdots&\ddots\\
\widetilde{L}_{p1}&\cdots&\widetilde{L}_{pp}\end{bmatrix},

(4.27)

multiplying out
L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}
leads to

M_{\ell}=B_{\ell}\widetilde{L}_{\ell\ell}^{-1}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad M_{k}=\left(B_{k}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}M_{j}\widetilde{L}_{jk}\right)\widetilde{L}_{kk}^{-1},\quad k=1,\ldots,\ell-1.

(4.28)

Since the
\widetilde{L}_{jk}
are all diagonal, the
M_{k}
blocks all have the same cyclic banded structure as
B_{k}
,
k=1,\ldots,\ell
. Next, we see that

L_{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{0}=A_{0}-L_{B}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}L_{B}=A_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}M_{j}M_{j}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

(4.29)

thus
L_{0}
is the reverse Cholesky factor of
A_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}M_{j}M_{j}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
. Since
M_{j}
is a cyclic banded matrix with sub-bandwidths
(1,0)
,
M_{j}M_{j}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
is a cyclic banded matrix with sub-bandwidths
(1,1)
, and thus has the same structure as
A_{0}
. Therefore, from Lemma
4
,
L_{0}
is a
(1,0)
banded matrix except for its first column. These results lead to the decomposition given in the theorem.
∎

We could give a more general result for matrices with larger sub-bandwidths, but the cases covered by Theorem
2
are sufficient for the differential equations covered in this work. The only difference in the proof would be in the structure of
L_{0}
. The structure of the reverse Cholesky factor in Theorem
2
leads to the following result for solving symmetric positive definite linear systems with these matrices in optimal complexity.

Corollary 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+pm)\times(m+pm)}
be a symmetric positive definite
CB^{3}(\ell,\ell;1,0)
or
CB^{3}(\ell,\ell;0,1)
matrix and let
A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}
, where
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+pm}
. The solution
\mathbf{x}
can be computed in optimal complexity, namely in
\mathcal{O}(N)
operations where
N=m+pm
.

Proof.

The proof of Theorem
2
gave the following algorithm for computing the reverse Cholesky factor
L
. Using the same decomposition (
4.25
), the steps are:

1.

Compute
\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
such that
D_{i}=\widetilde{L}_{i}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\widetilde{L}_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m
.

2.

Compute
M_{k}=\left(B_{k}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}M_{j}\widetilde{L}_{jk}\right)\widetilde{L}_{kk}^{-1}
for
k=\ell,\ell-1,\ldots,1
.

3.

Compute
\widetilde{A}_{0}=A_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}M_{j}M_{j}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
.

4.

Compute the reverse Cholesky factor
L_{0}
of
\widetilde{A}_{0}
.

The reverse Cholesky factors
\widetilde{L}_{i}
can be computed in
\mathcal{O}(p)
operations, thus the first step takes
\mathcal{O}(mp)
operations. For the second step, each
\widetilde{L}_{jk}
block is diagonal, and so computing
(B_{k}-\sum_{j=k+1}^{\ell}M_{j}\widetilde{L}_{jk})\widetilde{L}_{kk}^{-1}
for each
k=1,\ldots,\ell
takes
\mathcal{O}(m)
operations in total. The third step similarly takes
\mathcal{O}(m)
operations, as does the final step due to Lemma
4
. Thus, the factor overall takes
\mathcal{O}(m)+\mathcal{O}(mp)=\mathcal{O}(N)
operations to compute. The remaining complexity comes from applying the inverse of
L
to vectors for computing
\mathbf{x}
. Using a similar argument to the proof of Corollary 4.3 in
[
7
]
shows that this complexity is also
\mathcal{O}(N)
, noting that the additional non-zero column in
L_{0}
only adds
\mathcal{O}(m)
additional operations.
∎

We apply these results to the examples discussed in Section
6
, where any symmetric positive definite system with a
CB^{3}
-arrowhead matrix will be solved in optimal complexity using the reverse Cholesky factors.

. Similar to the proof of Proposition

, the computation of

relies on the identity

D_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=\left[M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{-1}\left[\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\right).

(4.35)

The remaining details for this computation are given in
D
.
∎

4.4
Computing expansions

In order to solve differential equations with our basis, we need to know how to expand functions in this basis, as we will need to be expanding right-hand sides and initial conditions for time-evolution problems. In particular, consider expanding a function
f(x,y)
in the
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
or
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
bases, meaning
f=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
or
f=\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
. In the
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
case, we compute the expansion coefficients
\mathbf{f}^{(0,h_{i})}
for
f
restricted to each element
E_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, meaning
\left.f\right|_{E_{i}}=\mathbf{P}^{(0,h_{i})}\mathbf{f}^{(0,h_{i})}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Writing
\mathbf{f}^{(0,h_{i})}=(f_{0}^{(0,h_{i})},f_{1}^{(0,h_{i})},f_{2}^{(0,h_{i})},\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{j}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=(f_{j}^{(0,h_{1})},\ldots,f_{j}^{(0,h_{n})})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots
,
\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
is found by interlacing these coefficients:

\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\left[\mathbf{f}_{1}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\left[\mathbf{f}_{2}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(4.36)

The coefficients
\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
are computed using
\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
and
R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
:

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\left[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{-1}\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}},

(4.37)

thus
\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
is the solution to
R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
. The
CB^{3}
-arrowhead structure of
R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
allows this linear system to be solved efficiently. These results are summarised below.

Proposition 12
.

Define
\mathbf{f}^{(0,h_{i})}=(f_{0}^{(0,h_{i})},f_{1}^{(0,h_{i})},f_{2}^{(0,h_{i})},\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{j}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=(f_{j}^{(0,h_{1})},\ldots,f_{j}^{(0,h_{n})})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots
. The coefficients
\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
such that
f=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
are given by

\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\left[\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\left[\mathbf{f}_{1}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\left[\mathbf{f}_{2}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

(4.38)

The coefficients
\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
such that
f=\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
are given by the solution to
R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
.

5
Analysis

In this section we consider some theoretical results for our bases. We start by rigorously demonstrating that we can expand any trigonometric polynomial
f(\theta)=a_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_{n}\cos n\theta+b_{n}\sin n\theta)
with a finite expansion in
\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
basis,
b\in\{-1,0\}
, using
M\max\{2N,1\}
coefficients where
M
is the number of elements. We then finish with some convergence properties for our basis.

5.1
Expanding trigonometric polynomials

A fundamental property of our
\mathbf{P}^{(-1)}
basis is that it can be used to exactly represent trigonometric polynomials, meaning with a finite expansion. We prove this fact in this section, starting with a lemma demonstrating that we can represent
\cos n\theta
and
\sin n\theta
over an arc as finite expansions in the
\mathbf{p}^{(0)}
and
\mathbf{q}^{(0)}
bases, respectively.

Lemma 5
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and assume
\theta\in\Omega_{h}
,
|h|<1
. We can expand
\cos n\theta=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\hat{\mu}_{jn}p_{j}^{(0,h)}(\theta)
and
\sin n\theta=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\eta}_{jn}q_{j}^{(0,h)}(\theta)
for some coefficients
\{\hat{\mu}_{jn}\}
and
\{\hat{\eta}_{jn}\}
.

Proof.

We give the proof of this lemma in
E
, where we also derive a recurrence relationship for computing the coefficients
\hat{\mu}_{jn}
and
\hat{\eta}_{jn}
. In particular, the
\cos n\theta
expansion is considered in Lemma
E1
, and the
\sin(n\theta)
expansion is considered in Lemma
E2
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Given a trigonometric polynomial
f(\theta)=a_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_{n}\cos n\theta+b_{n}\sin n\theta)
for
\theta\in\Omega_{h}
,
|h|<1
, we can write
f(\theta)=\mathbf{P}^{(b)}(\theta)\mathbf{f}
for
b\geq-1
, where
\mathbf{f}=(\mathbf{f}_{2N+1}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{2N+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2N+1}
. In particular,
f(\theta)
can be represented as an expansion in the
\mathbf{P}^{(b)}
basis with
2N+1
terms.

Proof.

For
b=0
this follows from simply expanding each of the terms
\cos(n\theta)
and
\sin(n\theta)
in
f(\theta)
using Lemma
5
. When
b\neq 0
, the result follows from first expanding in the
\mathbf{P}^{(0)}
basis and then transforming using the connection matrix
R_{(0)}^{(b)}
, noting that this matrix is upper triangular.
∎

Lemma
5
and Corollary
2
lead us to the following theorem that extends these results to the entire circle. Note also in the following theorem that the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis requires
M
fewer coefficients than the
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
basis when the trigonometric polynomial is not constant.

Theorem 3
.

Given a trigonometric polynomial
f(\theta)=a_{0}+\sum_{n=1}^{N}(a_{n}\cos n\theta+b_{n}\sin n\theta)
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, we can write
f(\theta)=\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
for
b\in\{-1,0\}
, where
\mathbf{f}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}=([\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
and
\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M(2N+1)}
and
\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\max\{2N,1\}}
, using the grid
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M+1})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
with
M\geq 2
.

Proof.

The case for
N=0
is obvious since
\mathbf{f}_{0}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}=(a_{0},\ldots,a_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
for both
b\in\{-1,0\}
. The
b=0
case follows from combining Corollary
2
and Proposition
12
since each element contributes
2N+1
terms. The
b=-1
case can be derived from
b=0
using
R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{f}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
, leading to the system
\mathbf{f}_{i}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}=C_{i}\mathbf{f}_{i-1}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}+D_{i}\mathbf{f}_{i+1}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
, where
C_{i}
and

are invertible diagonal matrices. Sparing the details, we can show that we obtain a valid solution when
\mathbf{f}_{i}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{0}
for

which leads to the result.
∎

5.2
Convergence

Now we consider the convergence of expansions in our basis to a given function
f
, which is no longer assumed to be a finite trigonometric polynomial. To start, we give some definitions followed by a result analogous to Theorem 3.2 of
[
11
]
, stating that if we can expand the even and odd parts of a function in a semiclassical Jacobi expansion then the expansion also converges in the arc polynomial basis. This then implies that, provided a function

has a convergent series over each element in a given grid,

has a convergent series in the
\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
basis for
b\in\{-1,0\}
.

Definition 15
.

Given a function
f(x,y)
on an arc
\Omega_{h}
,
|h|<1

and
odd
parts of

to be, respectively,

f_{e}(x):=\frac{f\left(x,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)+f\left(x,-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2},\qquad f_{o}(x):=\frac{f\left(x,\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)-f\left(x,-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)}{2\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}.

(5.1)

We may also slightly misuse this notation to define

and

Definition 16
.

Given a function

on an arc
\Omega_{h}
,
|h|<1

omitting the dependence of the functions and coefficients on

and

If needed, we include superscripts to highlight the

dependence. Similarly, for a function

on

(5.3)

Proposition 13
.

Let

be a function on an arc

,
|h|<1
and assume

. If

and

We can assume that

in what follows since, for

. We omit superscripts showing the dependence on

and

(5.5)

\displaystyle\langle f,q_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=2(1-h)^{b+2}\left\langle P_{j-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}(\sigma),f_{o}(\zeta)\right\rangle^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)},\quad j=1,2,3,\ldots,

(5.6)

5.2.1
Rate of convergence

Now let us examine the rate of convergence of our series. In particular, if

Definition 17

We use

[

The arguments we apply in what follows mirror those used for the half-range Chebyshev polynomials of
[

Lemma 6

Let

be a sequence of orthogonal polynomials on

Proof.

This is a restatement of

(5.16)

Similarly, all the coefficients in the expansion are

Proof.

This is a simple corollary of Theorem

6
Solving differential equations

The primary motivation for this work is the solution of piecewise-smooth differential equations with periodic boundary conditions. The Fourier basis, as is typically used for problems with periodic boundary conditions
[
1
]
, is not appropriate for these problems. In particular, for solutions not analytic in a sufficiently large strip around the real axis, the number of coefficients needed to resolve the solution to machine precision is large. Piecewise bases such as our
\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}
are more appropriate for such equations since the elements can be defined to align with the discontinuities, giving a smooth function inside each element. For this reason, in what follows, we compare results when using the piecewise arc polynomial basis
\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
, the periodic piecewise integrated Legendre basis
\mathbf{W}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
, and the Fourier basis
\mathbf{F}
.

Code availability

The code that implements our bases and produces the results in the examples that follow is provided in
ArcPolynomials.jl

[
27
]
.

6.1
Screened Poisson equation

Our first example considers the screened Poisson equation in one dimension
[
7
]

-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{{}^{\prime}}u(\theta)}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}+\omega^{2}u(\theta)=f(\theta)

(6.1)

with periodic boundary conditions, where
f
is
2\pi
-periodic and
\omega>1
is a frequency parameter. In this case, the Fourier basis is preferable when
f
is analytic in a sufficiently large horizontal strip centred on the real axis, otherwise the piecewise bases will be preferable. We consider general
f
to start, but use a discontinuous initial condition
f(\theta)=2+\sgn(|\theta|-\pi/3)
for a practical example afterwards. To solve (
6.1
), we first put it into weak form. Taking
v\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}(I)
, where
I:=[-\pi,\pi]
,
H_{\mathrm{per}}^{s}(I):=\{v\in H^{s}(I):v~\textnormal{is}~2\pi\textnormal{-periodic}\}
,
H^{s}(I):=W^{s,2}(I)
is the typical Sobolev space
[
28
,
7
]
, the weak form of (
6.1
) is: find
u\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}(I)
such that

\langle v^{\prime},u^{\prime}\rangle+\omega^{2}\langle v,u\rangle=\langle v,f\rangle\quad\forall v\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{1}(I),

(6.2)

where
f\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}(I)
and
\langle f,g\rangle:=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(\theta)g(\theta)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}
. Focusing on the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis first for a given grid
\bm{\theta}
, we expand
v(\theta)=\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{v}
and
u(\theta)=\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{u}
. Since
f\in H_{\mathrm{per}}^{-1}(I)
, allowing for discontinuities in
f
, we expand
f
in the
b=0
basis rather than the
b=-1
basis, giving
f(\theta)=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}
so that (
6.2
) becomes

\mathbf{v}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(-\Delta^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}+\omega^{2}M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\right)\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{v}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}.

(6.3)

Enforcing (
6.3
) for all
\mathbf{v}
gives

\left(-\Delta^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}+\omega^{2}M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\right)\mathbf{u}=\left[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}.

(6.4)

A similar equation to (
6.4
) holds for the
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis. In the Fourier basis we work with the strong form (
6.1
), giving

\left(-D^{2}+\omega^{2}I\right)\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{F}}=\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{F}},

(6.5)

where
\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\mathbf{F}/\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\theta=\mathbf{F}D
,
u(\theta)=\mathbf{F}(\theta)\mathbf{u}_{\mathbf{F}}
, and
f(\theta)=\mathbf{F}(\theta)\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{F}}
. To solve these infinite dimensional systems, we need to project them into a finite dimensional space. To this end, define the
n\times\infty
projection operator
\mathcal{P}_{n}=(I_{n},\mathbf{0})
so that, given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty\times\infty}
,
\mathcal{P}_{n}A\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the
n\times n
principal finite section of
A
. We call
n
the
truncation size
. The solution
\mathbf{u}
to (
6.4
) is thus approximated by the solution to the projected problem, namely
\mathbf{u}\approx(\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},\mathbf{0})^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
where

\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=\left[\mathcal{P}_{n}\left(-\Delta^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}+\omega^{2}M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\right]^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{n}\left(\left[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{f}\right)\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}},

(6.6)

and similarly in the
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis. Note that, in practice,
\mathbf{f}
is finite dimensional since we only compute as many coefficients as are needed to resolve
f
to machine precision; the same holds for other function expansions in the examples that follow. The system (
6.5
) does not require such a projection since
D
is block diagonal, allowing the system to be solved exactly as
\mathbf{f}_{\mathbf{F}}
is finite dimensional. As discussed in Section
4.2.1
, we compute
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
in (
6.6
) by using the reverse Cholesky factorisation of
\mathcal{P}_{n}(-\Delta^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}+\omega^{2}M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}})\mathcal{P}_{n}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, and similarly for the truncated solution in the
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis; note that, for problems where the resulting system is not symmetric positive definite, we can not apply the reverse Cholesky factorisation and so cannot solve the problem in optimal complexity.

To now make this discussion concrete, we fix
\omega=3/2
and use the discontinuous function

f(\theta)=2+\sgn\left(|\theta|-\frac{\pi}{3}\right),

(6.7)

and our grid is
10
equally spaced points between
-\pi
and
\pi
. To machine precision,
f
requires
9
coefficients in the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
bases, and the discontinuity in
f
means that we cannot expand
f
in the
\mathbf{F}
basis, hence we only consider
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
in what follows. Using these expansion lengths, we use the truncation size
n=144
for both bases, where this
n
is the number of coefficients needed plus
15M
where
M
is the number of elements; this extra
15M
comes from needing to account for the block bandwidths from the products in (
6.6
).

Remark 1
.

The next examples in this section that do not involve a discontinuity will only need a correction of
2M
due to Theorem
3
since, as a consequence, the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
solution should map trigonometric polynomials back into trigonometric polynomials, although the same cannot necessarily be said for the
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis.

We show the solution in each basis in Figure

, as well as the derivatives for each solution up to the second derivative, using

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\mathrm{d}\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}D_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\left[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right]^{-1}D_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}},

(6.8)

with a similar result for
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
. We truncate the matrices in (

) using
\mathcal{P}_{15M}
since, for larger

6.2
Heat Equation

The second problem we consider is the heat equation,

\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}u(\theta,t)}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}t}=\frac{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}^{{}^{\prime}}u(\theta,t)}{\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}\theta^{{}^{\prime}}}.

(6.9)

The solution to (
6.9
) is governed by, using a similar argument to the screened Poisson example,

M^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\Delta^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}\mathbf{u}

(6.10)

in the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis, with a similar result for the
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis. To avoid discussing complications related to timestepping, we solve these systems using the matrix exponential, for example
\mathbf{u}(t)=\exp([M^{(-1), \bm\theta}]^{-1}\Delta^{(-1), \bm\theta}t)\mathbf{u}(0)
, computing
\mathbf{u}(0)
from the initial condition. We compute the matrix exponential using ExponentialUtilities.jl
[
29
]
. The initial condition over
[-\pi,\pi]
is given by

u(\theta,0)=\begin{cases}0&-\pi\leq\theta<-\pi/4-\varepsilon,\\
1+(\theta-\varepsilon+\pi/4)/(2\varepsilon)&-\pi/4-\varepsilon\leq\theta<-\pi/4+\varepsilon,\\
2+(\theta+\varepsilon-\pi/4)/\pi&-\pi/4+\varepsilon\leq\theta<\pi/4-\varepsilon,\\
2-(\theta+\varepsilon-\pi/4)/\varepsilon&\pi/4-\varepsilon\leq\theta<\pi/4+\varepsilon,\\
0&\pi/4+\varepsilon\leq\theta\leq\pi,\end{cases}

(6.11)

with
\varepsilon=0.005
, which is a continuous approximation to the function
\chi
that is zero outside of
[-\pi/4,\pi/4]
and linearly increases from
1
to
2
over
[-\pi/4,\pi/4]
. We do not use the Fourier basis in this example as (
6.11
) requires too many coefficients to approximate. We use the grid
\bm{\theta}=(-\pi,-\pi/4-\varepsilon,-\pi/4+\varepsilon,\pi/4-\varepsilon,\pi/4+\varepsilon,\pi)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, leading to truncation sizes of
n=55
and
n=15
for the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
bases, respectively.

Remark 2
.

Instead of using an approximation to
\chi
, we could expand
\chi
in the
b=0
basis so that instead of (
6.10
) we have

M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{u}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)\mathbf{u}.

(6.12)

The complication with this approach is in the computation of the derivatives, since we have not defined a
b=1
basis to represent the derivatives in; this basis could be defined using
b=1
polynomials together with delta functions, but we do not consider defining
\mathbf{P}^{(1),\bm{\theta}}
here. We can use

-\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\mathbf{P}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)=-\left(R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)^{-\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\left(D_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}M^{(0),\bm{\theta}}D_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\left(R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}\right)^{-1},

(6.13)

however we end up needing many coefficients to resolve the solution accurately due to the need to compute
[R_{(-1)}^{(0),\bm{\theta}}]^{-1}
for the matrix exponential. One way around this would be to take a single backward Euler step so that the discontinuity is smoothed out, followed by the matrix exponential for the remaining steps, or to simply use timestepping for the entire integration. We do not consider these alternatives here.

Rather than only look at the solution, we use this example to analyse the differences between the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
bases. For a given numerical solution
\hat{u}(\theta,t)
, define the

for its

\theta

\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{per}}^{(d)}(t):=|\hat{u}^{(d)}(\pi,t)-\hat{u}^{(d)}(-\pi,t)|

\hat{u}^{(d)}(\theta,t)
denotes an estimate for the
d
th derivative with respect to
\theta
at
(\theta,t)
. The drift
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{per}}^{(d)}(t)
measures how
\hat{u}^{(d)}(\theta,t)
loses

\mathbf{W}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis remain large as time increases, showing that this basis fails to preserve periodicity unlike in the
\mathbf{P}^{(-1),\bm{\theta}}
basis.

7
Conclusion

In this work we introduced a one-dimensional periodic basis for solving differential equations with periodic boundary conditions. The operators derived with this basis have a special banded structure that makes them efficient to work with. The basis can be used to solve problems in optimal complexity, even those with discontinuities that a standard approach with a Fourier basis cannot handle, and has better numerical and convergence properties than other orthogonal polynomial approaches such as with a piecewise integrated Legendre basis from
[
6
,
7
]
. Our numerical examples demonstrated these differences, and also highlighted that our solution is able to better preserve periodicity when integrating overtime.

Our arc polynomial basis, and its piecewise variant, could be used to solve more complicated problems than those considered here. Nonlinear equations such as Burgers’ equation or the nonlinear Schrödinger equation could be considered, using a pseudospectral approach by efficiently transforming from values on a grid to polynomial coefficients, and from polynomial coefficients to values on a grid, similar to the method used in Section 6 of
[
7
]
for solving Burgers’ equation with the integrated Legendre basis. The basis also generalises simply to problems other than one-dimensional differential equations. Using a tensor product basis, we could apply our basis to problems over domains such as tensor product domains, periodic strips, and a torus. Problems that periodic in both dimensions would also be simple, applying a tensor product basis with our arc polynomials. Similar to
[
7
]
, these higher dimensional problems could be combined with an alternating direction implicit iteration for solving the associated linear systems to achieve quasi-optimal complexity. A subject of future work will be the consideration of problems in sectors of the form
\{0<r<1,\,|\theta|<\varphi\}
, where the arc polynomials can be used to generalise Zernike polynomials effectively to this domain. One complication with this approach is that, while one may form tensor-product bases such as
C_{ij}^{(h)}(r,\theta)=P_{i}^{(0,1)}(2r-1)p_{j}^{(0,h)}(\theta)
and
S_{ij}^{(h)}(r,\theta)=P_{i}^{(0,1)}(2r-1)q_{j}^{(0,h)}(\theta)
, these are not polynomials in Cartesian coordinates (
x
and
y
). As a consequence, the associated operators may not be sparse, particularly when used in an
hp
-FEM framework, and there may be regularity issues at
r=0
that require special care. Instead, an approach similar to that used in
[
30
]
is necessary if one wants polynomials in Cartesian coordinates, using the Gram–Schmidt process on
C_{ij}^{(h)}
and
S_{ij}^{(h)}
to construct the orthogonal polynomials on the sector. Similar extensions could be done to annuli, with generalisations similar to those in
[
13
,
31
]
.

Computing

In this appendix we consider the computation of the coefficients
\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}
and
\beta^{t,(a,b,c)}
defining
P_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)=\beta^{t,(a,b,c)}(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}
. To start, we denote by
\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}
the integral

\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}=\int_{0}^{1}w^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{x}=\mathrm{B}(a+1,b+1)t^{c}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(a+1,-c;a+b+2,t^{-1}\right),

(A.1)

where
\mathrm{B}
is the beta function and
{}_{2}F_{1}
is the Gaussian hypergeometric function. This identity (
A.1
) could be derived by relating the integral to the generalised beta distribution
[
32
]
.

Using the Gram–Schmidt procedure with the inner product (
2.2
),
we obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{P}_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)

\displaystyle=x-\frac{\langle x,1\rangle^{t,(a,b,c)}}{\|1\|_{t,(a,b,c)}^{2}}=x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}=\frac{\Gamma^{t,(a+1,b,c)}}{\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}.

We can obtain
P_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}
from this by noting that all the polynomials have the same norm, meaning
\|P_{n}^{t,(a,b,c)}\|_{t,(a,b,c)}^{2}=\|1\|_{t,(a,b,c)}^{2}=\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}
for all
n\geq 0

[
13
]
. Thus,

\displaystyle P_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\|1\|_{t,(a,b,c)}^{2}}{\left\|\tilde{P}_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}\right\|_{t,(a,b,c)}^{2}}}\tilde{P}_{1}^{t,(a,b,c)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}{\int_{0}^{1}\left(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)^{2}w^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{x}}}\left(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}{\int_{0}^{1}\left[x^{2}-2\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}x+\left(\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)^{2}\right]w^{t,(a,b,c)}(x)}}\left(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}{\Gamma^{t,(a+2,b,c)}-2\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\Gamma^{t,(a+1,b,c)}+\left(\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)^{2}\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}}\left(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)

\displaystyle=\beta^{t,(a,b,c)}\left(x-\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)~~\text{where}~~\beta^{t,(a,b,c)}=\sqrt{\frac{\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}{\Gamma^{t,(a+2,b,c)}-2\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\Gamma^{t,(a+1,b,c)}+\left(\alpha^{t,(a,b,c)}\right)^{2}\Gamma^{t,(a,b,c)}}}.

Appendix B
D_{(b),\mathbf{q}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{p}}
is lower bidiagonal

In this appendix we complete the details of the proof of Proposition
5
. We need to show that
D_{(b),\mathbf{q}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{p}}
is a lower bidiagonal matrix, which is not immediately obvious since the individual operators for
x\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}_{y}
and
y\mathinner{\partial}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}_{x}
are dense. There is likely a simpler way to express
D_{(b),\mathbf{q}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{p}}
than (
3.16
) that makes this bidiagonal structure clear, but here we prove it using inner products. In particular, consider

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}q_{n}^{(b)}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}d_{nj}p_{j}^{(b+1)}(x,y).

The numerator of
d_{nj}
, denoted
\gamma_{nj}
, is given by

\displaystyle\gamma_{nj}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}q_{n}^{(b)},p_{j}^{(b+1)}\right\rangle^{(b+1,h)}

\displaystyle=2q_{n}^{(b)}(\varphi)p_{j}^{(b+1)}(\varphi)(\cos\varphi-h)^{b+1}-\int_{-\varphi}^{\varphi}q_{n}^{(b)}(\theta)\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\left[p_{j}^{(b+1)}(\theta)(\cos\theta-h)^{b+1}\right]\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}.

(B.1)

If
b\neq-1
, the first term in (
B.1
) is zero since
\cos\varphi-h=\cos\varphi-\cos\varphi=0
. Otherwise, see that

\displaystyle q_{n}^{(-1)}(\varphi)

\displaystyle=\sin\varphi P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,-1,1/2)}(1)=\begin{cases}\sin\varphi&n=1,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We will consider the
(b,n)=(-1,1)
case last and assume that this first term is zero in what follows. To evaluate the integral we first note that, using
\cos\theta-h=(1-h)(1-\sigma)
, we have the weighted derivative formula

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\left[\left(\cos\theta-h\right)^{b+1}\mathbf{p}^{(b+1)}\right]=\left(\cos\theta-h\right)^{b}\mathbf{q}^{(b)}D_{\mathrm{b},(-1/2,b+1,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)},

(B.2)

and this matrix
D_{\mathrm{b},(-1/2,b+1,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}
is an upper bidiagonal matrix
[
13
]
. Thus,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\theta}\left[\left(\cos\theta-h\right)^{b+1}p_{j}^{(b+1)}(\theta)\right]=\left(\cos\theta-h\right)^{b}\left[\xi_{j-1,j}q_{j}^{(b)}(\theta)+\xi_{jj}q_{j+1}^{(b)}\right],

where
\xi_{j-1,j}
and
\xi_{jj}
are the non-zero entries in the
j
th column of
(1-h)^{b}D_{\mathrm{b},(-1/2,b+1,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}
. Putting this relationship into (
B.1
), assuming
(b,n)\neq(-1,1)
,

\gamma_{nj}=-\xi_{j-1,j}\langle q_{n}^{(b)},q_{j}^{(b)}\rangle^{(b,h)}-\xi_{jj}\langle q_{n}^{(b)},q_{j+1}^{(b)}\rangle^{(b,h)}.

(B.3)

Thus,
\gamma_{nj}
is zero whenever
n\neq j
or
n\neq j+1
. In particular,
d_{nj}\neq 0
only for
n\neq j
or
n\neq j+1
, showing that
D_{(b),\mathbf{q}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{p}}
has this lower bidiagonal structure.

We still need to consider the
b=-1
and
n=1
case. Here,
\mathrm{d}q_{1}^{(-1)}/\mathrm{d}\theta=\cos\theta
, so

\displaystyle\gamma_{1j}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\varphi}^{\varphi}\cos\theta p_{j}^{(0)}(\theta)\mathinner{\mathrm{d}}\mathchoice{\mskip-3.0mu}{\mskip-3.0mu}{}{}{}{\theta}.

Writing out
\cos(\theta)
as a linear combination of
p_{0}^{(0)}(\theta)=1
and
p_{1}^{(0)}(\theta)=P_{1}^{\tau,(-1/2,0,-1/2)}[(\cos\theta-1)/(h-1)]
, it is easy to show that

\cos\theta=\left[1+(h-1)\alpha^{\tau,(-1/2,0,-1/2)}\right]p_{0}^{(0)}(\theta)+\frac{h-1}{\beta^{\tau,(-1/2,0,-1/2)}}p_{1}^{(0)}(\theta),

(B.4)

which implies that
\gamma_{1j}=0
whenever
j>1
; note that
\beta^{\tau,(-1/2,0,-1/2)}\neq 0
since that would otherwise imply that
p_{1}^{(0)}
is identically zero. We therefore see that
d_{1j}
is non-zero only for
j=1
and
j=2
when
b=-1
and
n=1
. Thus,
D_{(b),\mathbf{q}}^{(b+1),\mathbf{p}}
is also a lower bidiagonal matrix in this case, and this completes the proof.

Lemma E1
.

Let

and

\cos(n\theta)=\frac{1}{\mu_{00}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\mu_{jn}p_{j}^{(0,h)}(\theta),\quad|\theta|<\varphi,

1

or

\mu_{jn}=0
. The initial values for the recurrence (

Proof.

In what follows, we will denote

Now consider

Definition F1

In this appendix we consider the problem of expanding

Definition G2

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\mathbf{P}
be a basis of orthogonal polynomials. The

is the matrix

such that

\displaystyle xp_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{n}p_{n-1}(x)+a_{n}p_{n}(x)+b_{n}p_{n+1}(x),

where
c_{0}=0
. We will use superscripts to refer to Jacobi matrices for the semiclassical Jacobi polynomials, i.e.

Let’s now work on computing
J_{a}
. First, the following lemma concerning weighted conversion between semiclassical Jacobi polynomials both with

Lemma G3
.

The matrix
L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(a,-1,c)}^{t,(a-1,-1,c-1)}
is a
(2,0)
banded matrix given by

L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(a,-1,c)}^{t,(a-1,-1,c-1)}=\begin{bmatrix}t-1&\mathbf{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\\
\ell_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\ell_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}&L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(a,1,c)}^{t,(a-1,1,c-1)}\end{bmatrix},

(G.1)

where
\ell_{1}=1+\alpha^{t,(a-1,1,c-1)}-t
,
\ell_{3}=1/\beta^{t,(a-1,1,c-1)}
,
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(1,0,\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, and
\mathbf{e}_{2}=(0,1,0,\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}

Proof.

Writing

L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(a,-1,c)}^{t,(a-1,-1,c-1)}=\begin{bmatrix}\ell&\mathbf{0}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}\\

\displaystyle w^{t,(a,0,c)}

\displaystyle=w^{t,(a-1,0,c-1)}\ell+w^{t,(a-1,1,c-1)}\mathbf{P}^{t,(a-1,1,c-1)}\bm{\ell},

(G.9)

\displaystyle q_{n}^{(b,h)}(x,y)\mathbf{p}^{(b,h)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M^{(b,h)}_{n,(1,2)},\qquad\qquad M^{(b,h)}_{n,(1,2)}:=R_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}\left(J^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}\right),

(G.10)

\displaystyle q_{n}^{(b,h)}(x,y)\mathbf{q}^{(b,h)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M^{(b,h)}_{n,(1,1)},\qquad\qquad M^{(b,h)}_{n,(1,1)}:=(1-h)^{2}L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(1/2,b,1/2)}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}\left(J^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}\right).

(G.11)

Now let’s consider
a(x,y)\mathbf{P}(x,y)
. Given
\mathbf{a}:=(a_{02},a_{11},a_{12},\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
such that
a(x,y)=\mathbf{P}(x,y)\mathbf{a}
, we can write

\displaystyle a(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}q_{n}(x,y)+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}p_{n}(x,y).

(G.12)

Then, using Lemma
G4
,

\displaystyle a(x,y)\mathbf{p}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}(x,y)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M_{n,(1,2)}+\mathbf{p}(x,y)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M_{n,(2,2)}.

(G.13)

\displaystyle a(x,y)\mathbf{q}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}(x,y)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M_{n,(1,1)}+\mathbf{q}(x,y)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M_{n,(2,1)}.

(G.14)

Next, define
M_{(1,2)}:=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M_{n,(1,2)}
and similarly for
M_{(2,2)}
,
M_{(1,1)}
, and
M_{(2,1)}
. To evaluate these matrices requires some more work. First,

\displaystyle M_{(2,2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M_{n,(2,2)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}P_{n}^{-}(J^{-})=b_{2}(J^{-}),

(G.15)

where
b_{2}(\sigma):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}P_{n}^{-}(\sigma)
. Next,

\displaystyle M_{(2,1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M_{n,(2,1)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}P_{n}^{-}(J^{+})=b_{2}(J^{+}).

(G.16)

For
M_{(1,2)}
we have

\displaystyle M_{(1,2)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M_{n,(1,2)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}R_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}P_{n-1}^{+}(J^{-})=R_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}b_{1}(J^{-}),

(G.17)

where
b_{1}(\sigma):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}P_{n-1}^{+}(\sigma)
. Finally,

\displaystyle M_{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M_{n,(1,1)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}(1-h)^{2}L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(1/2,b,1/2)}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}P_{n-1}^{+}(J^{+})=(1-h)^{2}L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(1/2,b,1/2)}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}b_{1}(J^{+}).

(G.18)

The only remaining complication involved in (
G.15
)–(
G.18
) is in the computation of
b_{1}(J^{\pm})
and
b_{2}(J^{\pm})
. This computation is done using Clenshaw’s algorithm
[
18
]
together with the three-term recurrence relationships defining
\mathbf{P}^{\pm}
, making use of the
Clenshaw
function in RecurrenceRelationshipArrays.jl
[
33
]
. We summarise these results and give further properties of these
M
matrices below.

Lemma G5
.

Given
b\geq-1
and
|h|<1
, suppose
a(x,y)
has a convergent
\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}
-series so that

a(x,y)=b_{1}(\sigma)+yb_{1}(\sigma)\equiv\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}\mathbf{a},

(G.19)

where
\sigma:=(x-1)/(h-1)
,
\tau:=2/(1-h)
,
b_{2}(\sigma):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}P_{n}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}(\sigma)
,
b_{1}(\sigma):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}(\sigma)
, and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{02},a_{11},a_{12},\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
. Then, using the same notation as in Lemma
G4
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M^{(b,h)}_{n,(2,2)}

\displaystyle=b_{2}(J^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)})=:M^{(b,h)}_{(2,2)},

(G.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n2}M^{(b,h)}_{n,(2,1)}

\displaystyle=b_{2}(J^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)})=:M^{(b,h)}_{(2,1)},

(G.21)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M^{(b,h)}_{n,(1,2)}

\displaystyle=R_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}b_{1}(J^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)})=:M_{(1,2)}^{(b,h)},

(G.22)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n1}M^{(b,h)}_{(1,1)}

\displaystyle=(1-h)^{2}L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(1/2,b,1/2)}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}b_{1}(J^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)})=:M_{(1,1)}^{(b,h)},

(G.23)

and

\displaystyle a(x,y)\mathbf{p}^{(b,h)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M^{(b,h)}_{(1,2)}+\mathbf{p}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M^{(b,h)}_{(2,2)},

(G.24)

\displaystyle a(x,y)\mathbf{q}^{(b,h)}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M_{(1,1)}^{(b,h)}+\mathbf{q}^{(b,h)}(x,y)M_{(2,1)}^{(b,h)}.

(G.25)

Let
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
be such that
a_{n1}=0
for
n>\alpha_{1}
and
a_{n2}=0
for
n>\alpha_{2}
, in particular
b_{1}(\sigma)\equiv\sum_{n=1}^{\alpha_{1}}a_{n1}P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}(\sigma)
and
b_{2}(\sigma)\equiv\sum_{n=0}^{\alpha_{2}}a_{n2}P_{n}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}(\sigma)
. Then
M_{(2,2)}^{(b,h)}
and
M_{(2,1)}^{(b,h)}
are
(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2})
banded matrices,
M_{(1,2)}^{(b,h)}
is a
(\alpha_{1}-1,\alpha_{1}+1)
banded matrix, and
M_{(1,1)}^{(b,h)}
is a
(\alpha_{1}+1,\alpha_{1}-1)
banded matrix.

Proof.

The only unproven part of this result is the last statement concerning the bandwidths of these matrices. To prove these bandwidths, suppose we have a polynomial
h(\sigma)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}h_{n}\sigma^{n}
and consider
h(J)
where
J
is a tridiagonal matrix. Since multiplying matrices adds their bandwidths together, the largest bandwidth matrix in the sum associated with
h(J)
comes from
J^{d}
which is a
(d,d)
banded matrix (since tridiagonal matrices are
(1,1)
banded matrices). Since
b_{1}(\sigma)=\sum_{n=1}^{\alpha_{1}}a_{n1}P_{n-1}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}
, we see that
b_{1}(J^{\tau,(\pm 1/2,b,\pm 1/2)})
must be a
(\alpha_{1}-1,\alpha_{1}-1)
banded matrix and similarly for
b_{2}(\sigma)=\sum_{n=0}^{\alpha_{2}}a_{n2}P_{n}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}(\sigma)
. The result for
M_{(1,2)}^{(b,h)}
and
M_{(1,1)}^{(b,h)}
follows from this argument together with noting that
R_{(-1/2,b,-1/2)}^{\tau,(1/2,b,1/2)}
and
L_{\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c},(1/2,b,1/2)}^{\tau,(-1/2,b,-1/2)}
are
(0,2)
and
(2,0)
banded matrices, respectively.
∎

Using this result, we can finally derive
J_{a}
. We see that

\displaystyle a(x,y)p_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{q}(x,y)M_{(1,2)}\mathbf{e}_{n}+\mathbf{p}(x,y)M_{(2,2)}\mathbf{e}_{n},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots,

\displaystyle a(x,y)q_{n}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{p}(x,y)M_{(1,1)}\mathbf{e}_{n-1}+\mathbf{q}(x,y)M_{(2,1)}\mathbf{e}_{n-1},\quad n=1,2,\ldots.

Thus, letting
\mathbf{e}_{0}:=(1,0,\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
,
\mathbf{e}_{1}=(0,1,0,\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, and so on, and letting
M_{(u,v)}=\{m_{i,j,(u,v)}\}_{i,j=0,1,2,\ldots}
, we can write, omitting the
(x,y)
arguments,

\displaystyle a\mathbf{P}

\displaystyle=a\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&q_{1}&p_{1}&q_{2}&p_{2}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}=a\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}M_{(1,2)}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{p}M_{(2,2)}\mathbf{e}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}M_{(1,1)}\mathbf{e}_{0}+\mathbf{q}M_{(2,1)}\mathbf{e}_{0}\\
\mathbf{q}M_{(1,2)}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{p}M_{(2,2)}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{p}M_{(1,1)}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathbf{q}M_{(2,1)}\mathbf{e}_{1}\\
\mathbf{q}M_{(1,2)}\mathbf{e}_{2}+\mathbf{p}M_{(2,2)}\mathbf{e}_{2}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,0,(1,2)}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,0,(2,2)}\\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,0,(1,1)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,0,(2,1)}\\
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,1,(1,2)}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,1,(2,2)}\\
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,1,(1,1)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,1,(2,1)}\\
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,2,(1,2)}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,2,(2,2)}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix}^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}.

We can write this first entry and second entry as

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,0,(1,2)}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,0,(2,2)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&q_{1}&p_{1}&q_{2}&p_{2}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m_{0,0,(2,2)}\\
m_{0,0,(1,2)}\\
m_{1,0,(2,2)}\\
m_{1,0,(1,2)}\\
m_{2,0,(2,2)}\\
\vdots\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}m_{n,0,(1,1)}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}q_{n}m_{n-1,0,(2,1)}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}p_{0}&q_{1}&p_{1}&q_{2}&p_{2}&\cdots\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}m_{0,0,(1,1)}\\
m_{0,0,(2,1)}\\
m_{1,0,(1,1)}\\
m_{1,0,(2,1)}\\
m_{2,0,(1,1)}\end{bmatrix},

respectively, and this pattern continues for the remaining entries. Thus, we finally obtain the following result. Note that the bandwidth of
J_{a}
in the result below is simply the number of non-zero entries in
\mathbf{a}=(a_{02},a_{11},a_{12},a_{21},a_{22},\ldots)^{\mkern-1.5mu\mathsf{T}}
, with the exception that if the last non-zero is an
a_{n1}
term instead of an
a_{n2}
term we need to add
1
.

Proposition G1
.

Given
b\geq-1
and
|h|<1
, suppose
a(x,y)
has a convergent
\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}
-series so that
a(x,y)=b_{1}(\sigma)+yb_{1}(\sigma)\equiv\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}A
, using the same notation as in Lemma
G5
. Then the matrix
J_{a}^{(b,h)}
defined by
a(x,y)\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}(x,y)=\mathbf{P}^{(b,h)}(x,y)J_{a}^{(b,h)}
is given by

J_{a}^{(b,h)}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|cc|cc|c}m_{0,0,(2,2)}&m_{0,0,(1,1)}&m_{0,1,(2,2)}&m_{0,1,(1,1)}&m_{0,2,(2,2)}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr m_{0,0,(1,2)}&m_{0,0,(2,1)}&m_{0,1,(1,2)}&m_{0,1,(2,1)}&m_{0,2,(1,2)}&\cdots\\
m_{1,0,(2,2)}&m_{1,0,(1,1)}&m_{1,1,(2,2)}&m_{1,1,(1,1)}&m_{1,2,(2,2)}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr m_{1,0,(1,2)}&m_{1,0,(2,1)}&m_{1,1,(1,2)}&m_{1,1,(2,1)}&m_{1,2,(1,2)}&\cdots\\
m_{2,0,(2,2)}&m_{2,0,(1,1)}&m_{2,1,(2,2)}&m_{2,1,(1,1)}&m_{2,2,(2,2)}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr m_{2,0,(1,2)}&m_{2,0,(2,1)}&m_{2,1,(1,2)}&m_{2,1,(2,1)}&m_{2,2,(1,2)}&\cdots\\
m_{3,0,(2,2)}&m_{3,0,(1,1)}&m_{3,1,(2,2)}&m_{3,1,(1,1)}&m_{3,2,(2,2)}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr m_{3,0,(1,2)}&m_{3,0,(2,1)}&m_{3,1,(1,2)}&m_{3,1,(2,1)}&m_{3,2,(1,2)}&\cdots\\
m_{4,0,(2,2)}&m_{4,0,(1,1)}&m_{4,1,(2,2)}&m_{4,1,(1,1)}&m_{4,2,(2,2)}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right]

(G.26)

using the notation
M_{(u,v)}^{(b,h)}=\{m_{i,j,(u,v)}\}_{i,j=0,1,2,\ldots}
. This matrix
J_{a}^{(b,h)}
is an
(\alpha,\alpha)
banded matrix, where
\alpha=2\max\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\}+\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}]
,
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are as in Lemma
G5
, and
\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}]=1
if
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}
and
\mathbf{1}[\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}]=0
otherwise.

The analogous result for
\mathbf{P}^{(b),\bm{\theta}}
can be derived by applying Proposition
G1
over each element.
