Multi-omics analysis reveals MICB promotes cerebral atherosclerosis through FTMT suppression in NK and plasma cells
Authors: Yonggang Dai, Ye Liu, Yunjun Yan, Haoran Ji, Hongya Wang, Xuewei Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFerroptosis, an iron-dependent form of regulated cell death, is implicated in vascular injury, but how immune-related proteins modulate ferroptosis and cerebral atherosclerosis (CeA) remains unclear.MethodsWe performed multi-step Mendelian randomization (MR) integrating plasma proteomics, single-cell eQTL, and FinnGen CeA GWAS data. Mediation MR quantified the role of the ferroptosis regulator FTMT. Cell-type–specific MR was conducted across 14 immune subtypes. Functional enrichment and RT-qPCR of clinical blood samples validated findings.ResultsFour immune proteins (KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, MICB) were identified as causal risk factors for CeA, acting primarily by downregulating FTMT (all β1 < 0, P < 0.001). Mediation analysis revealed FTMT explained up to 53% of the immune protein effect. Single-cell MR demonstrated striking cell-type specificity for MICB: higher MICB in NK cells and IgJ+ B cells was strongly associated with CeA risk (e.g., β = 0.90, P = 4.3 × 10−6), while higher MICB in dendritic cells was protective (β = − 0.57, P = 2.1 × 10−30). In clinical samples, RT-qPCR confirmed reduced FTMT expression, and plasma ELISA showed elevated circulating LTA, soluble MICB, and soluble HLA-E in CeA patients. Network analysis highlighted NK cell cytotoxicity as a central pathway.ConclusionsIn summary, our study demonstrates that four immune-related proteins, including MICB, promote cerebral atherosclerosis by downregulating the ferroptosis-protective factor FTMT. Importantly, single-cell MR reveals that MICB acts as a risk factor for CeA specifically in NK cells and plasma cells, providing new mechanistic insight into cell-type–dependent immune regulation of vascular disease.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13148-026-02212-y.

Introduction

Cerebral atherosclerosis (CeA) is a leading cause of ischemic stroke and cognitive decline worldwide, imposing a substantial health and socioeconomic burden. While classical risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking are well recognized, increasing evidence implicates non-traditional biological pathways—including immune dysregulation and regulated cell death—in the pathogenesis of CeA [1–3].

Ferroptosis, a distinct form of iron-dependent, lipid peroxidation-driven cell death, has garnered considerable attention for its role in cardiovascular and neurovascular diseases [4–6]. Unlike apoptosis or necrosis, ferroptosis is characterized by the accumulation of lipid hydroperoxides and iron overload, culminating in mitochondrial dysfunction and cell injury. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of ferroptosis regulators such as FTMT (ferritin mitochondrial), which functions as a key mitochondrial iron buffer, thereby protecting against oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [7–10]. Disruption of FTMT homeostasis has been increasingly associated with vascular injury and atherogenesis.

Despite these advances, the upstream molecular and genetic mechanisms regulating ferroptosis in CeA remain incompletely elucidated. Immune system components, particularly natural killer (NK) cells and their associated proteins (e.g., KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, MICB), play crucial roles in both innate immunity and vascular homeostasis. Emerging data suggest that immune mediators can modulate ferroptotic pathways, either by influencing antioxidant gene expression or by altering mitochondrial iron metabolism [11–14]. However, the causal relationships and intermediate mechanisms by which immune-related proteins regulate ferroptosis—and their subsequent contribution to CeA—are not well defined.

Mendelian randomization (MR) has emerged as a powerful approach for causal inference using genetic variants as instrumental variables, minimizing confounding and reverse causality. Furthermore, mediation MR frameworks now allow quantification of indirect (mediated) effects and the disentanglement of complex biological pathways [15–18]. Nevertheless, most existing MR studies have relied on bulk-tissue or plasma data, which do not resolve the cellular heterogeneity of the immune system and may mask cell-type–specific regulatory mechanisms.

Recent advances in single-cell genomics and eQTL analysis have enabled causal inference at the level of individual immune cell subtypes, revealing a new layer of complexity in the genetic regulation of vascular disease [19]. Single-cell eQTL(sc-eQTL) analyses now allow the dissection of context-dependent gene regulation, uncovering novel disease-relevant signals that may be obscured in conventional, mixed-cell analyses. Leveraging these innovations, our study systematically interrogates the cell-type–specific effects of key immune genes—especially MICB—on ferroptosis and CeA pathogenesis at single-cell resolution, providing a precise mechanistic framework for understanding immune–ferroptosis crosstalk in vascular disease.

In this study, we implemented a multi-step mediation MR approach to systematically dissect the causal cascade from immune-related plasma proteins to CeA, focusing on the mediating role of FTMT-driven ferroptosis [20, 21]. By integrating large-scale proteomic and genomic data from European cohorts, functional enrichment analyses, sc-eQTL Mendelian randomization, and clinical validation in patient samples, we aim to uncover a novel immune–ferroptosis–atherosclerosis axis [22]. This integrative strategy not only clarifies upstream immune mechanisms and their cell-type specificity, but also provides mechanistic insights that may inform future precision therapeutic strategies for cerebral atherosclerosis.

Materials and methods

To improve readability, the Methods in the main text are intentionally concise and focus on information required for reproducibility at the journal-article level. Expanded procedural details (including complete MR QC and diagnostics, full RT-qPCR/ELISA protocols and QC criteria, and MIQE reporting items) are provided in Supplementary Methods (Sections S1–S10) and the Supplementary Tables/Figures, and are cross-referenced below.

Data sources

We integrated European-ancestry summary statistics from large-scale pQTL resources (Icelandic SOMAscan v4 serum pQTLs and UK Biobank–based plasma pQTLs), a curated ferroptosis gene/protein list (FerrDb v2), and FinnGen Release 12 GWAS summary statistics for cerebral atherosclerosis (CeA). For cell-type–specific inference, we leveraged an immune single-cell eQTL (sc-eQTL) resource covering 14 immune cell types/subtypes. Data-source details and accessions are provided in Supplementary Methods S2 and Supplementary Tables S1–S2 and S11.

Instrument selection, LD clumping, and harmonization

Genetic instruments were defined as variants associated with each exposure at prespecified thresholds and pruned for LD using a European reference panel. For bulk pQTL MR, instruments were clumped at r2 < 0.001 within 10,000 kb. For sc-eQTL instruments (cis window), where candidate genes include MHC-region loci with dense local LD, we used a more permissive clumping threshold (r2 < 0.3 within 100 kb) to retain ≥ 2 informative cis instruments per gene–cell-type model; instrument selection details are provided in Supplementary Table S11. Instrument strength was quantified using F-statistics (F = (beta/SE)2), with weak instruments excluded (SNP-level F > 10). For transparency, we report nSNP, mean F, and minimum F for each exposure and each gene–cell-type MR analysis (Supplementary Table S14 for pQTL exposures; Supplementary Table S16 for sc-eQTL models), and full sc-eQTL MR results are summarized in Supplementary Table S12. As a sensitivity analysis addressing LD pruning stringency, we re-clumped sc-eQTL instruments at r2 < 0.05 and re-estimated key MICB models; effect directions and the overall significance framework were preserved (Supplementary Table S17). pQTL variant coordinates are reported on the GRCh37/hg19 build in the source datasets (Supplementary Tables S1–S2), and harmonization across datasets was performed by rsID and allele alignment; palindromic variants were handled using allele-frequency–based strand-ambiguity rules (Supplementary Methods S3).

Two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses

We used two-sample MR to estimate causal effects between circulating proteins (or gene expression in sc-eQTL analyses) and CeA, and to map upstream regulators of the ferroptosis-protective factor FTMT within a stepwise framework (schematized in Fig. 1). The primary estimator was inverse-variance weighted (IVW) MR; weighted-median and MR-Egger were applied as sensitivity estimators. Step-specific multiple-testing control across the bulk MR, mediation MR, and sc-eQTL MR components is summarized in Supplementary Table S15 (see also Supplementary Methods S4).

Mediation Mendelian randomization

To quantify whether the effect of immune-related proteins on CeA is mediated through FTMT, we performed mediation MR by combining the exposure-to-mediator (beta1) and mediator-to-outcome (beta2) estimates and computing the indirect effect as beta1 × beta2. Uncertainty for mediated effects was derived using the delta method. Assumptions and limitations of mediation MR in this biological context are detailed in Supplementary Methods S5 and discussed in the main Discussion.

Sensitivity, heterogeneity, and pleiotropy analyses

For primary MR findings we evaluated heterogeneity and potential horizontal pleiotropy using Cochran’s Q and I^2, the MR-Egger intercept test, MR-PRESSO global/outlier tests, and leave-one-out analyses. Diagnostic outputs and decision rules are provided in Supplementary Methods S4.2 and Supplementary Table S9.

Functional enrichment and network analysis

To contextualize FTMT-centered pathways, we constructed protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks using STRING (minimum interaction score 0.4) and performed Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler with FDR control (adjusted P < 0.05). Full parameters and gene sets are provided in Supplementary Methods S4 and S6.

sc-eQTL processing and cell-type–specific MR

sc-eQTL instruments were curated per gene and immune cell type under QC criteria described in Supplementary Methods S6, including exclusion of gene–cell-type combinations with fewer than two valid instruments after clumping and strength filtering. Cell-type labels followed the source annotations and marker-based validation (Supplementary Table S11). Cell-type–specific MR was performed analogously to bulk MR. Instrument metrics (nSNP, mean F, min F) for each gene–cell-type model are reported in Supplementary Table S16, and causal estimates for each gene–cell type pair are reported in Supplementary Table S12.

Clinical validation (RT-qPCR and ELISA)

Whole blood and plasma were collected from CeA patients (n = 20) and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (n = 20) at Shandong Provincial Third Hospital. CeA diagnosis was established by neurologists based on clinical assessment and cerebrovascular imaging (e.g., CTA/MRA) consistent with intracranial atherosclerotic lesions. Eligibility criteria, baseline risk factors, and available medication information are summarized in Supplementary Table S7. Additional biospecimen processing details are provided in Supplementary Methods S7.

Gene-expression validation for FTMT and KIR2DL3 was performed using whole-blood RT-qPCR (LightCycler 480 II; SYBR chemistry) with GAPDH as the reference gene and relative expression calculated by the 2^-ΔΔCt method. Because circulating FTMT and KIR2DL3 were below the lower limit of detection in plasma, they were not pursued by ELISA in this cohort. Plasma proteins LTA, soluble MICB, and soluble HLA-E were quantified by ELISA with duplicate measurements and standard-curve interpolation. Complete RT-qPCR/ELISA protocols, primer sequences, MIQE items, assay QC criteria, and raw outputs are provided in Supplementary Methods S8–S9, Supplementary Table S8, and Supplementary Figure/Table S10.

Statistical analysis and software

All analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.1). TwoSampleMR was used for harmonization and MR estimation, and MR-PRESSO was used for pleiotropy/outlier testing. Unless otherwise specified, two-sided P values were used; adjusted significance thresholds for each analysis layer are summarized in Supplementary Table S15.

This comprehensive, stepwise analytic workflow (Fig. 1B) enables systematic evaluation of both bulk and cell-type–specific genetic regulation along the immune–ferroptosis–CeA axis. In the following sections, we present the results from each analytic stage.

Step 1: causal effect of ferroptosis-related proteins on CeA (β2)

To explore potential causal associations between ferroptosis-related proteins and cerebral atherosclerosis, we performed two-sample MR using pQTL summary statistics for 165 ferroptosis-related proteins from the Icelandic cohort (Fig. 2A). IVW MR was used as the primary estimator. In total, 38 proteins showed nominal associations (P < 0.05) with cerebral atherosclerosis risk. After correcting for multiple testing across 165 proteins using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR q < 0.05), three proteins remained statistically significant: BECN1 (OR = 0.330, 95% CI 0.194–0.560; P = 3.96 × 10−5), MDM4 (OR = 0.608, 95% CI 0.466–0.794; FDR = 2.52 × 10−4), and FTMT (OR = 0.829, 95% CI 0.769–0.894; P = 1.08 × 10−6) (Table 1; Supplementary Table S3). Notably, all three also met the Bonferroni threshold (0.05/165 = 3.03 × 10−4). Sensitivity diagnostics (funnel, scatter, and leave-one-out analyses) are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Together, these results implicate ferroptosis biology in CeA and highlight FTMT as a candidate protective factor for downstream analyses (Fig. 2B).

Step 2: identification of circulating plasma proteins associated with CeA (β_all)

To further identify circulating proteins associated with cerebral atherosclerosis, we conducted MR analysis using pQTL data from 2,619 plasma proteins in the UK Biobank cohort. IVW was again applied as the primary method. A total of 305 proteins were significantly associated with cerebral atherosclerosis at P < 0.05. After FDR correction (0.05/2619 ≈ 1.91 × 10−5), 18 proteins remained significant. Among them, IL6, EXTL1, KLRK1, CFHR5, TOP1, and TDGF1 demonstrated protective effects, whereas MICB, KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, IGF2R, DXO, FBLN2, TNF, and EDDM3B (Table 2) were identified as risk factors promoting cerebral atherosclerosis (Fig. 2C). Detailed forest plots, funnel plots, scatter plots, and leave-one-out analyses for the MR of plasma proteins are shown in Supplementary Figures S2–S5. Comprehensive MR results for all 2,619 UKB plasma proteins are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Step 3: causal effects of circulating plasma proteins on ferroptosis regulators (β1)

To investigate whether CeA-associated immune proteins act upstream of ferroptosis biology, we conducted a second round of MR analysis using the seven plasma proteins highlighted in Step 2 (CFHR5, DXO, HLA-E, KIR2DL3, LTA/TNF-β, sMICB, and TNF) as exposures and the three robust ferroptosis-related proteins identified in Step 1 (BECN1, MDM4, and FTMT) as outcomes. IVW MR was used as the primary estimator. At the nominal level (P < 0.05), 23 exposure–outcome associations were observed. After Bonferroni correction for 21 primary IVW tests (0.05/(7 × 3) = 2.38 × 10−3), 11 associations remained statistically robust (Table 3; Supplementary Table S5). Comprehensive forest, funnel, scatter, and leave-one-out plots for significant associations are provided in Supplementary Figures S6–S9. These results indicate that a subset of circulating immune proteins may act as upstream regulators of ferroptosis-related molecular pathways (Fig. 2D).

Step 4: mediation MR analysis of immune proteins, FTMT, and CeA (β1 × β2)

To explore whether ferroptosis mediates the causal pathway from circulating proteins to cerebral atherosclerosis, we performed a two-step MR-based mediation analysis. First, the causal effect of UKB-identified proteins on ferroptosis-related proteins (β1) was estimated, followed by the effect of ferroptosis-related proteins on cerebral atherosclerosis (β2). The total effect (β_total) from the UKB protein to the outcome was also evaluated. Associations were considered significant if β1 × β2 > 0 and the mediation P-value passed the FDR threshold (0.05 / (3 × 11) ≈ 2.8 × 10−3).

Four significant mediation effects were observed (Table 4), all converging on FTMT, a ferroptosis regulator shown previously to be protective against cerebral atherosclerosis (Figs. 3A–B), accounting for 23.8% to 53.4% of the total effect (Fig. 3C). The comprehensive mediation MR results, including all tested protein–mediator–disease pathways, are provided in Supplementary Table S6.

Step 5: functional exploration of the FTMT regulatory network

Given the consistent mediation of FTMT by the four identified proteins, we constructed a PPI network using the STRING database, applying a minimum interaction score threshold of 0.400 (medium confidence). The analysis revealed a high degree of co-expression and interaction among KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, and MICB.

To further understand the functional implications, we expanded the network by including the top 35 proteins most closely connected to the four mediators. GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses indicated significant enrichment in immune-related processes, particularly: NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity; immune receptor activity; external side of plasma membrane.

Notably, HLA-E, KIR2DL3, and MICB are directly involved in NK cell signaling pathways. These results imply that NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity may play a pivotal role in modulating FTMT expression and ferroptosis, thereby influencing cerebral atherosclerosis pathogenesis.

Step 6: experimental validation of candidate biomarkers at the transcript and protein levels (RT-qPCR and plasma ELISA)

Because soluble FTMT is extremely low in plasma and fell below the detection limit in our feasibility screening, we quantified FTMT at the transcript level in whole blood by RT-qPCR. As shown in Fig. 4A, FTMT mRNA was significantly reduced in CeA patients (CeA, n = 20) compared with healthy controls (Control, n = 20) (P = 0.0024).

Step 7: cell-type–specific selection of instrumental variables for immune genes

To evaluate the cell-specific regulatory effects of key immune genes, we conducted cis-eQTL screening across 14 immune cell subtypes using single-cell resolution datasets. Specifically, we extracted SNPs significantly associated with the expression of four candidate immune genes (KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, MICB) within each cell type (e.g., NK cells, B cells, dendritic cells). Only SNPs meeting the following instrumental variable criteria were retained: F-statistic > 10, Pval < 0.05, and LD clumping thresholds of r2 < 0.3 within 100 kb windows (Supplementary Table S11).

Step 8: cell-type–specific Mendelian randomization reveals MICB and LTA as context-dependent determinants of cerebral atherosclerosis risk

To systematically evaluate the context-dependent causal effects of MICB and LTA in immune cells, we performed cell type–resolved MR analyses using sc-eQTL–derived instruments. For each gene–cell type pair, causal estimates were derived using the inverse variance weighted (IVW) method, with detailed results summarized in Supplementary Table S12 and visualized in Supplementary Figure S11–S14.

Among all candidate genes, MICB demonstrated the most striking cell-type–specific effects. Elevated MICB expression in NK cells (β = 0.90, 95% CI not shown, P = 4.26 × 10−6), NKR (β = 0.72, P = 1.04 × 10−9), and plasma (β = 0.28, P = 1.06 × 10−14) was significantly associated with increased risk of cerebral atherosclerosis (see Fig.5 and IVW_ForestPlot_ImmuneProtein_Cell.png). In contrast, higher MICB expression in dendritic cells (DC) showed a robust protective association (β = − 0.57, P = 2.05 × 10−30).

For LTA, protective effects were identified in NKR (β = − 0.54, P = 3.47 × 10−24) and CD4+ SOX4+ T cells (β = − 0.29, P = 2.09 × 10), with no significant associations observed in other cell types (Fig. 5A; Table 5).

MICB as a central mediator linking immune regulation, ferroptosis, and cerebral atherosclerosis

Our integrative Mendelian randomization analyses, leveraging both bulk-tissue and sc-eQTL data, identified MICB as a key immune-related gene causally promoting the development of CeA. Bulk MR analysis demonstrated that higher circulating MICB levels are associated with increased CeA risk. Further, single-cell MR revealed that this effect is highly cell-type–specific: MICB acts as a pro-atherogenic factor in NK cells and B cells, while showing a protective association in dendritic cells.

Mechanistically, mediation analyses support that MICB promotes CeA at least in part by downregulating FTMT, a ferroptosis-protective protein, thus linking immune signaling to ferroptosis and vascular injury. These findings are reinforced by functional network and pathway analyses, as well as RT-qPCR validation of gene expression differences in clinical samples.

Collectively, our results establish MICB as a critical mediator of immune–ferroptosis crosstalk in the pathogenesis of cerebral atherosclerosis and highlight the importance of cell type–resolved genetic analysis for the discovery of precision immunotherapeutic targets.

Discussion

This study reveals a previously unrecognized mechanistic axis linking immune-related plasma proteins to cerebral atherosclerosis (CeA) via the ferroptosis regulator FTMT. Using a comprehensive, multi-step Mendelian randomization (MR) framework that incorporates both bulk-tissue and single-cell eQTL datasets, along with clinical validation, we provide compelling evidence that immune signaling—particularly through natural killer (NK) cell–associated proteins—suppresses FTMT expression, thereby promoting ferroptosis and vascular injury [23, 24].

FTMT is a mitochondrial ferritin protein that sequesters iron, preventing its participation in Fenton reactions and thereby inhibiting lipid peroxidation, a hallmark of ferroptosis. It serves as a dynamic buffer for mitochondrial iron, safeguarding iron-sulfur cluster biogenesis and limiting oxidative damage [25–27]. FTMT also interacts with antioxidant defense enzymes, such as GPX4, further enhancing resistance to lipid peroxidation. Under mild oxidative stress, FTMT is upregulated to preserve mitochondrial function; under severe stress, however, it can be degraded by mitochondrial proteases (e.g., LONP1), causing a surge in labile iron and triggering ferroptosis—a dualistic response reflecting cellular context and stress thresholds.

Our initial MR and mediation analyses identified four immune-related plasma proteins—KIR2DL3, HLA-E, LTA, and MICB—that consistently and causally downregulate FTMT, with these proteins being central mediators of NK cell cytotoxicity signaling [28]. Mediation MR quantified that over 50% of the effects of KIR2DL3 and HLA-E on CeA were mediated by FTMT suppression, confirming its pivotal position within the immune–ferroptosis–vascular axis. Pathway enrichment further emphasized NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity, immune receptor signaling, and mitochondrial stress as major mechanistic themes [29]. These findings suggest that innate immune effectors can drive vascular disease not only through classical cytotoxicity but also by tipping the balance toward ferroptotic cell death, particularly via persistent repression of FTMT and mitochondrial iron overload [30–32].

To substantiate these genetic findings, we quantified FTMT expression in clinical whole-blood samples and observed significantly reduced FTMT mRNA in CeA patients relative to controls (Fig. 4A). Because transcript abundance in blood does not necessarily mirror circulating protein levels, we further performed plasma ELISA as an orthogonal validation layer for three measurable immune proteins. This revealed significant increases in LTA/TNF-β, sMICB, and sHLA-E in CeA (Fig. 4B–D), aligning with the direction of effect inferred from the pQTL-based MR. Collectively, the convergence of MR-based causal inference, transcript-level validation of FTMT, and protein-level phenotyping of immune ligands provides clinical molecular support for our model and highlights dysregulation of the immune–ferroptosis–FTMT axis as a plausible contributor to CeA pathogenesis.

Crucially, by integrating single-cell eQTL MR, we demonstrate that the pathogenic effect of MICB is highly cell-type–dependent. MICB acts as a potent pro-atherogenic factor in NK cells (including both XCL1+ and XCL1− subsets) and IgJ+ B cells, while showing a protective association in dendritic cells. This dichotomy not only underscores the necessity of immune cell stratification in mechanistic studies but also aligns with recent advances in single-cell genomics revealing the context-dependence of immune–ferroptosis regulation. Mechanistically, MICB-driven FTMT downregulation in NK and B cells appears to facilitate ferroptosis-mediated vascular injury, whereas in dendritic cells, MICB may participate in protective responses.

Mechanistically, MICB is a stress-inducible ligand for the activating receptor NKG2D (encoded by KLRK1) on NK cells and subsets of T cells [33]. In inflammatory milieus, increased membrane MICB and/or shedding into soluble MICB can chronically engage NKG2D, which may promote sustained NK-cell activation and immunometabolic stress, thereby predisposing to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation when mitochondrial iron buffering (via FTMT) is compromised [34–36]. Conversely, MICB signaling in dendritic cells may reflect a compensatory immune-surveillance program that supports antigen presentation and resolution of inflammation; this could explain the protective association observed in dendritic cells despite the pro-atherogenic signal in NK-lineage models [37]. We note that MR estimates capture genetically proxied expression levels and do not directly distinguish membrane-bound versus soluble MICB; therefore, the MICB–NKG2D interpretation is presented as a biologically plausible framework that warrants targeted validation in purified cell populations [34].

The cell-specific MR findings suggest that future immunotherapeutic strategies targeting MICB or the broader FTMT–ferroptosis axis should be tailored to particular immune cell subsets—such as NK and B cells—to optimize efficacy while minimizing unwanted effects on protective dendritic cell mechanisms. Our integrative multi-omics approach, combining bulk and single-cell MR, mediation analysis, and clinical validation, thus offers a comprehensive blueprint for uncovering the cell context–dependent regulation of vascular disease and advances the prospect of precision immunotherapy for CeA.

From a translational perspective, targeting FTMT expression or stability may offer therapeutic opportunities. Pharmacological stabilizers of FTMT could mitigate vascular damage by buffering iron and preserving mitochondrial integrity, while in oncology, enhancing FTMT degradation may sensitize cancer cells to ferroptosis-inducing agents.

Despite the robust multi-layered design, our study has limitations. The use of blood-based pQTL and eQTL may not fully capture FTMT dynamics in cerebrovascular tissues; experimental validation in endothelial and smooth muscle cell models is needed to clarify tissue specificity. The clinical validation cohort was modest in size, and medication histories beyond statin use were not consistently available (Supplementary Table S7), which may have influenced bulk blood expression measurements. In addition, whole-blood RT-qPCR reflects averaged signals across leukocyte subpopulations and may be affected by disease-related shifts in cell composition. While MR analysis reduces confounding and reverse causality, residual pleiotropy cannot be completely excluded despite comprehensive sensitivity checks. Future studies should further dissect the cell-type–specific MICB–FTMT axis and explore targeted interventions within defined immune cell niches.

Conclusions

In summary, this study systematically elucidates a previously uncharacterized immune–ferroptosis–atherosclerosis axis in CeA. By integrating multi-step mediation MR, sc-eQTL analysis, and clinical validation, we demonstrate that immune-related proteins—particularly MICB—promote CeA development by downregulating the ferroptosis-protective protein FTMT. Notably, MICB’s pro-atherogenic effects are highly cell-type specific, acting predominantly through NK cells and B cells, but exhibiting a protective role in dendritic cells. These findings highlight FTMT as a critical molecular checkpoint and support the need for precision immunotherapeutic strategies targeting specific immune cell populations. Our results lay a foundation for future research into immune–mitochondrial crosstalk and suggest novel therapeutic targets for the early intervention and prevention of CeA.
