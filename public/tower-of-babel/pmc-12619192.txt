Beyond the pill: contextual effects of community-based PrEP delivery on pregnancy risk among FSWs in Cameroon – a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Jean-Pierre Yves Awono Noah, Francis Ateba Ndongo, Justin Ndié, Martial Gaël Bonyohe, Rogacien Kana Dongmo, Rodrigue Bile Komokcheua, Félicité Naah Tabala, Jérôme Ateudjieu, Anne Cécile Zoung-Kanyi Bissek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale sex workers (FSWs) face elevated risks of both HIV acquisition and unintended pregnancies. While oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is an established HIV prevention tool, its broader impact on sexual and reproductive health (SRH) outcomes remains unclear in real-world settings. In Cameroon, PrEP is delivered via community-based platforms where SRH services are opportunistically offered alongside HIV prevention.ObjectiveTo assess how exposure to an integrated, community-based oral PrEP–SRH programme influences the occurrence of pregnancy among FSWs in Cameroon.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis using propensity score–matched data from FSWs enrolled in a national PrEP programme. Age was centred at the median (24 years), and robust Poisson regression models were fitted to estimate adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) for pregnancy occurrence. An interaction term between age and PrEP exposure was introduced, and model stability was assessed via bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations).ResultsPregnancy occurred in 28.4% of FSWs not on PrEP compared with 20.5% among PrEP users. No significant association was observed in unstratified models (aRR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.44–1.52; p = 0.522). A significant interaction with age was detected (aRR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.05–1.82; p = 0.021), but bootstrap validation showed that the 95% confidence interval for the regression coefficient (B = 0.323; 95% CI: − 0.083 to 0.610; p = 0.024) included the null, raising concerns about robustness.ConclusionCommunity-based delivery of PrEP within integrated SRH platforms showed no overall association with pregnancy occurrence among FSWs, although a possible age-related effect was suggested. These findings underline the importance of delivery context and highlight the need for larger, prospective studies to confirm whether integrated models can influence reproductive outcomes and inform the development of future multipurpose prevention technologies (MPTs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25354-9.Keywords: Pre-exposure prophylaxis, Sex workers, Pregnancy, Community health services, Cameroon, Propensity score

Introduction

The implementation of oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) among female sex workers (FSWs) in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) offers a unique opportunity to strengthen routine follow-up of HIV-negative FSWs and expand the scope of preventive services. While PrEP initiation should not depend on access to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, it may conversely serve as an entry point to such care [1]. In Cameroon, community-based organisations (CBOs) delivering HIV prevention services to FSWs are also mandated to provide SRH services, including contraceptive counselling and access to modern methods. National PrEP guidelines promote the principle of dual protection, referring to the combined use of PrEP and condoms to prevent HIV and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs), rather than pregnancy specifically. Nonetheless, concerns persist that PrEP use may lead to decreased condom or contraceptive use through behavioural risk compensation, normalisation of condomless sex, or limitations in the integration of PrEP into broader SRH services. In several trials, pregnancy rates remained relatively high among FSWs on PrEP, and modelling has suggested a potential two-fold increase in condomless sex [2]. Recent national evidence further illustrates the dual burden of HIV and reproductive health risks faced by FSWs in Cameroon. The country has a concentrated HIV epidemic, with a national incidence of 0.24% among adults [3]. By contrast, HIV prevalence among FSWs remains disproportionately high. In 2016, 24.3% of FSWs tested positive, nearly half of whom (48.3%) were newly diagnosed [4]. More recent data from 2023 confirm this persistent vulnerability, with an estimated prevalence of 14.9% (95% CI: 14.6–15.2), of which 39% represented new diagnoses [5]. These levels are striking compared with the adult general population, where prevalence declined from 3.4% (95% CI: 3.1–3.8) in 2017 to 2.1% in 2023 [3, 6].

Alongside this HIV burden, unintended pregnancies remain frequent, with more than one-third of recent pregnancies among FSWs reported as unplanned [7]. Contraceptive use is inconsistent, as most women rely primarily on condoms, while uptake of longer-acting reversible methods remains limited [7]. Since 2019, oral PrEP has been introduced through CBO-led, peer-to-peer platforms targeting key populations, including FSWs. However, uptake and continuation have been modest: of 27,750 individuals sensitised for PrEP, 3,138 were eligible and 1,409 (45%; FSWs: 691; Men who have Sex with Men: 718) initiated treatment. Continuation rates declined steeply, with 37% remaining at 3 months, 28% at 6 months, and only 19% at 12 months. Discontinuation was significantly higher among FSWs than Men who have Sex with Men [8]. This convergence of high HIV prevalence, frequent unintended pregnancies, inconsistent contraceptive uptake, and limited PrEP retention raise important questions about how PrEP implementation may influence real-life pregnancy outcomes among female sex workers (FSWs). This is particularly relevant in contexts where HIV prevention and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services are delivered through an opportunistic integration approach, whereby contact with HIV prevention platforms is used as an entry point for broader reproductive health support. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), empirical data assessing the impact of such integrated models on pregnancy rates remain limited. This study aims to address that gap by examining the influence of the national oral PrEP programme on pregnancy occurrence among FSWs in Cameroon, where SRH services are offered alongside PrEP within community-based platforms.

PrEP intervention in Cameroon

As part of its 2018–2022 National HIV Strategic Plan, Cameroon piloted the introduction of oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for key populations, including female sex workers (FSWs) and men who have sex with men (MSM). Funded by PEPFAR, the programme began in June 2019 in Yaoundé and Douala and expanded to Bafoussam, Bertoua, and Bamenda in August 2020. Eligible individuals aged 21 or older with confirmed HIV-negative status and adequate renal function were enrolled. This age threshold was set in accordance with national legal constraints, as both sex work and same-sex sexual activity remain criminalised in Cameroon [9]. PrEP services were delivered through community-based organisations using a continuous delivery model, with monthly follow-up during initiation and quarterly follow-up thereafter. Comprehensive packages included HIV testing, STI screening, counselling, and free laboratory tests. Adherence support was provided through home visits and peer-led follow-up. The PrEP regimen consisted of a daily oral dose of tenofovir disoproxil fumarate and emtricitabine (TDF/FTC).

Methods

Study design

This study is a secondary analysis drawing on two cross-sectional surveys conducted to evaluate community-based PrEP delivery in Cameroon. Both were implemented in five urban sites (Douala, Yaoundé, Bertoua, Bamenda, and Bafoussam) through community-based organisations (CBOs) providing HIV and SRH services to FSWs. The satisfaction survey enrolled FSWs aged 21 years or older who had used PrEP for at least 12 months (minimum sample size: 245), whereas the acceptability survey targeted HIV-negative FSWs aged 18–24 years not yet using PrEP (minimum sample size: 223). Voluntary sampling [10, 11] was employed in both surveys, with eligibility requiring engagement in sex work for at least 12 months. Each survey incorporated a harmonised reproductive health module covering pregnancy history, intentions, and outcomes. In a previous study [7], the two datasets were merged and variables aligned, with participants with missing values excluded to ensure comparability and internal validity. The present secondary analysis therefore draws on this harmonised database, which has been described in detail elsewhere [7].

Participants and matching

Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was employed to mitigate the risk of selection bias resulting from the non-randomised assignment of PrEP [12–15]. From the merged dataset of two cross-sectional surveys, a total of 176 participants (88 PrEP users and 88 non-users) were matched based on the described procedure. Matching was performed using a 1:1 optimal algorithm based on the Mahalanobis distance and propensity scores, with calipers set to 0.10 × the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score [16]. The propensity score was estimated using 15 baseline covariates: age, place of residence, education, cohabiting with sexual partner, a source of income other than prostitution, affiliation with a sex workers’ association, disclosure of FSW status to close contacts, HIV test within the last 12 months, sexual intercourse while intoxicated, abuse of one or multiple psychoactive substances, symptoms of STIs over the past 12 months, perceived risk of HIV cinfection, intention to become pregnant, and use of modern contraception. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank test with Hollander and Wolfe correction, and a standardized effect size of 0.1 or less (≤ 0.1) was interpreted as negligible [12, 17].

Variables

The primary outcome of interest was the occurrence of at least one pregnancy within the past 12 months, assessed through a retrospective question: “Have you been pregnant in the past twelve months?” (yes/no), as previously described [7]. The main exposure variable was oral PrEP status (user vs. non-user). PrEP users were FSWs who had participated in the PrEP satisfaction survey after at least 12 months of use, while non-users were FSWs who had participated in the PrEP acceptability survey and had not initiated PrEP. All covariates included in the propensity score model were considered potential confounders and were collected through the structured questionnaire used in the PrEP satisfaction and acceptability surveys previously published elsewhere [7].

Data management and analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27) and XLStat premium (version 2019). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies for categorical variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables.

A stepwise hierarchical modelling strategy was implemented using Robust Poisson Regression (RPR) to estimate adjusted risk ratios (aRRs) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for pregnancy occurrence. This approach was preferred over logistic regression to obtain more interpretable risk estimates [18, 19]. Its robustness allows for use even in relatively small sample sizes as low as 100 participants [20] and it does not require the Poisson distribution assumption [21].

Given the potential effect modification role of age, being associated both with pregnancy risk [7] and PrEP uptake [22], and its residual imbalance after matching, we first examined its standalone contribution before introducing the main exposure variable (PrEP use). Three models were therefore sequentially fitted (Fig. 2): Model 1, included “age centred” only (age centred around the median) to evaluate its independent association with pregnancy occurrence. Model 2 added “PrEP use” as the main independent variable to Model 1, allowing estimation of the adjusted effect of PrEP on pregnancy while controlling for age. Model 3 included an interaction term between “PrEP use” and “age centred” to assess whether the effect of PrEP varied across age strata.The use of “age centred” instead of raw age reduces improves interpretability when testing interaction effects [23, 24].

Hierarchical modelling strategy and adjusted robust Poisson regression models for pregnancy occurrence

Having done this, we refit the model that we chose and inspect them to look for signs of bias like outliers and influential cases (Cook’s distance, Leverage “hat” value) (Supplementary file 1); we then check for linearity of the log link function and multicollinearity [25, 26]. We confirmed that the assumption of linearity of the log link function was met and found no evidence of multicollinearity (Supplementary file 1). Residual diagnostics indicated acceptable model fit. The analysis of standardised residuals (ZRE) revealed that 4.5% of observations fell outside the ± 1.96 range, and only 0.6% exceeded ± 2.58. No extreme outliers were detected, as no cases had ZRE values greater than ± 3. Additionally, influence statistics showed that the absolute values of DFBeta for all predictors remained below the conventional threshold of 1, except for the intercept and the PrEP exposure variable (Supplementary file 1). These preliminary analyses suggest that individual observations exerted minimal influence on the estimated model parameters and that the regression estimates are robust to potential outliers. Finally, a 1000-sample bootstrap RPR was applied to improve the stability and reliability of the estimates for the retained model [27]. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The primary study was approved by the National Human Health Research Ethics Committee in Cameroon (CNERSH) under reference number 2023/02/1945/L/CNERSH [7]. This secondary analysis involved fully de-identified data and posed no additional risk to participants. According to international guidelines, including the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki [28] and the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS) Guidelines [29], no further ethical approval was required.

The median age was 24 years (IQR: 23–24). Notably, 98.9% of participants in the control group were aged 20–24, compared to 61.4% in the intervention group, resulting in an overall proportion of 80.1% in this age group (Table 1). Most participants were recruited from Yaoundé (38.1%) and Douala (34.1%). Regarding education, 60.8% had completed secondary school, 13.6% had primary education, 23.9% had tertiary education, and only 1.7% reported no formal education. A small proportion (1.1%) lived with a sexual partner, and 37.5% had a source of income other than sex work (Table 1).

As shown in Tables 2, 51.1% of participants were affiliated with a sex worker organisation, and 54.0% reported that relatives were aware of their sex work. Nearly all participants (96.0%) had undergone HIV testing in the past 12 months. Substance use was highly prevalent: 64.2% reported engaging in sex under the influence of psychoactive substances or alcohol, 55.7% had abused one psychoactive substance in the last 12 months, and 31.8% had abused two or more. In total, 69.3% reported experiencing at least one STI-related symptom during the same period, yet only 25.0% perceived themselves to be at high risk of HIV infection (Table 2).

Table 3 presents reproductive health indicators. One-third of participants (32.4%) reported an intention to become pregnant in the previous year. Contraceptive use was high, with 64.8% reporting use of at least one modern method, condoms being the most common (62.3%), followed by pills, implants, and injectables. Overall, 24.4% (43/176) of participants reported at least one pregnancy in the previous year. Although pregnancy prevalence was higher among non-PrEP users (28.4%) than among PrEP users (20.5%), the difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 3).

Hierarchical and bootstrap regression analysis of influence of PrEP intervention on pregnancy occurrence

Table 4 presents the results of a hierarchical robust Poisson regression assessing the effects of age and PrEP exposure on pregnancy risk among FSWs. In Model 1, centred age alone was not significantly associated with pregnancy occurrence (aRR = 1.02; 95% CI: 0.88–1.18; p = 0.836). In Model 2, neither age (aRR = 1.05; 95% CI: 0.89–1.23; p = 0.561) nor PrEP exposure (aRR = 0.69; 95% CI: 0.40–1.17; p = 0.165) showed a significant association with pregnancy risk. In Model 3, the main effects of age (aRR = 0.81; 95% CI: 0.65–1.02; p = 0.078) and PrEP exposure (aRR = 0.82; 95% CI: 0.44–1.52; p = 0.522) were not significant, whereas the age-by-PrEP interaction reached significance (aRR = 1.38; 95% CI: 1.05–1.82; p = 0.021). Given that almost all non-PrEP participants were aged 20–24 years (87/88), age variability was minimal in the unexposed group; thus, the interaction likely reflects patterns among PrEP users rather than a generalisable age effect (Table 4).

Discussion

Contextual effect of an integrated PrEP–SRH model on pregnancy risk

This study assessed whether exposure to a community-based, opportunistically integrated PrEP–SRH service model influenced pregnancy occurrence among FSWs in Cameroon. Overall, 24.4% of participants reported at least one pregnancy in the past 12 months, with comparable rates among non-PrEP users (28.4%) and PrEP users (20.5%). In adjusted analyses, no significant association was found between PrEP exposure and pregnancy risk. However, a significant age-by-PrEP interaction was detected, suggesting that younger women may derive greater benefit from the integrated service model. Given the limited variability in age among non-PrEP participants, this result should be interpreted cautiously. Our findings therefore indicate that the potential influence of integrated PrEP–SRH models on fertility outcomes reflects not only the biomedical effect of PrEP but also the delivery context—particularly the ability of peer-led, community-based platforms to address age-specific reproductive health needs.

These results should also be interpreted in light of the Cameroonian context, where CBOs deliver services largely through peer-to-peer models. In such settings, barriers such as conscientious objection to emergency contraception are less likely to arise, since peers are generally supportive and not constrained by restrictive professional codes of practice. Nevertheless, such dilemmas remain highly relevant internationally, where provider objection continues to limit access to emergency contraception and may conflict with efforts to prevent unintended pregnancies among FSWs [30–32]. Framing our findings within this perspective underscores that integrated PrEP–SRH models must engage not only with biomedical outcomes but also with ethical, social, and structural determinants of reproductive autonomy across diverse contexts.

Assessing the stability of findings through bootstrap validation

To assess the stability of this observed interaction, a bootstrap analysis was conducted using 1,000 resampled datasets. While the interaction term between age and programme exposure remained statistically significant (p = 0.024), its 95% confidence interval included the null (–0.083 to 0.610), raising concerns about the robustness of the finding. Similarly, the main effect of PrEP exposure remained non-significant in the bootstrap-adjusted model, suggesting limited evidence for a direct influence of programme participation on pregnancy occurrence, after accounting for age. These inconsistencies between the conventional regression and bootstrap estimates—particularly the divergence between significant p-values and confidence intervals encompassing the null—highlight potential model instability. This reinforces the importance of triangulating classical regression with resampling techniques, especially in moderately sized samples, and cautions against overinterpreting isolated significant results in the absence of stable confidence bounds [33]. Taken together, the results suggest that while an age-related differential effect may exist, the protective influence observed may be context-dependent rather than inherently attributable to PrEP exposure itself. Future studies should seek to confirm these associations using larger, prospectively collected cohorts with more statistical power.

Our results are consistent with broader evidence from integrated FP–HIV services in sub-Saharan Africa. A recent meta-analysis reported that integration increased dual contraceptive use by at least 8% in five studies and modern contraceptive use by at least 8% in four studies, and reduced unmet need for contraception in two studies, but did not show a clear reduction in unintended pregnancies [34]. This apparent disconnect may reflect the complexity of reproductive intentions among FSWs, as well as structural and programmatic barriers that limit contraceptive choice and continuity.

Evidence from regional programme evaluations further supports this interpretation. A synthesis of integration models across sub-Saharan Africa highlighted that, while integration can reduce costs, increase client knowledge, and improve uptake of modern contraceptives, its effect on unintended pregnancies remains difficult to demonstrate due to methodological limitations and implementation gaps [35]. Similarly, the ANRS 12,381 PRINCESSE project in Côte d’Ivoire showed that community-based, peer-led platforms can simultaneously address HIV prevention and reproductive health needs, reinforcing the view that any protective effect observed is more likely attributable to delivery context than to PrEP’s pharmacological action alone [36, 37].

Overall, our findings are consistent with international evidence suggesting that integrated HIV–SRH models can strengthen reproductive autonomy by expanding contraceptive uptake, even if they do not directly reduce unintended pregnancies. Strengthening CBO-led platforms with comprehensive reproductive health components—including safer conception and fertility planning—while ensuring system-level investments in commodities, infrastructure, and provider training, will be critical to realise the full promise of integration in LMICs [35].

Strengthening internal validity through propensity score matching

Covariate balance was assessed by comparing standardised effect sizes before and after matching. Before matching, several covariates exhibited substantial imbalance between PrEP users and non-users, with effect sizes ranging from 0.008 to 0.785. After matching, all covariates except for age achieved a standardised effect size ≤ 0.1, which is generally considered to indicate negligible imbalance [12]. By improving group comparability through matching, this analysis strengthens the plausibility that observed differences in pregnancy occurrence may be attributed to programme exposure rather than underlying confounding factors.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study represents one of the first efforts to quantitatively assess the association between exposure to a national oral PrEP programme and pregnancy occurrence among female sex workers (FSWs) in sub-Saharan Africa. It leveraged a robust analytical strategy that combined propensity score matching (PSM) and hierarchical Poisson regression to reduce confounding and test for effect modification. The integration of bootstrap resampling further enhanced the rigour of inference by testing the stability of the model estimates, an approach rarely applied in this context. Additionally, the study explored contextual interpretations of programme effects, informed by triangulation with qualitative and programmatic data from the region.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the analysis limits causal inference and does not allow for temporality between PrEP exposure and pregnancy. Second, residual confounding may persist despite PSM. Third, although the bootstrap analysis improves confidence in the findings, the modest sample size limited the statistical power to detect small effect sizes and contributed to wide confidence intervals. Fourth, the study relied on self-reported data for some variables, which may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. Lastly, while the findings align with contextual evidence from the ANRS PRINCESSE project, they may not be generalisable beyond the specific implementation settings in Cameroon.

Conclusion and perspectives

This study provides new insight into the influence of a community-based, opportunistically integrated PrEP–SRH service model on pregnancy occurrence among FSWs in Cameroon. While a nominally significant age-by-PrEP interaction was detected, bootstrap validation indicated limited robustness, and no overall association was found between PrEP exposure and pregnancy risk. These results suggest that reproductive outcomes are shaped less by the biomedical properties of PrEP than by the delivery context, particularly the ability of peer-led, community-based platforms to address age-specific reproductive health needs. Strengthening such platforms to provide integrated HIV prevention and reproductive health services remains a priority. Future research should confirm these findings in larger, prospectively designed cohorts and explore how integration can best enhance reproductive autonomy in this population.
