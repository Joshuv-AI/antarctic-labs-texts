Abstract
. Let Σ g , r \Sigma_{g,r} denote the r r -punctured closed Riemann surface of genus g g . For every g ≥ 0 g\geq 0 , we determine the four-variable generating function for the mixed Hodge numbers of the unordered configuration spaces of Σ g , 1 \Sigma_{g,1} . The cases where g ≥ 2 g\geq 2 are new. Combining a result of [ 11 ] , this determines the analogous generating function for Σ g , r \Sigma_{g,r} for all r ≥ 1 r\geq 1 . As an application of our formula we illustrate how classical homological stability results, as well as so-called secondary stability results of [ 16 ] can be interpolated to illustrate stable behaviors in the mixed Hodge numbers of these spaces which have been thus-far undiscovered.



1.
Introduction

For
n\geq 0
and any smooth connected complex variety
\mathcal{M}
, let

(1.1)

\PConf_{n}(\mathcal{M}):=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}:x_{i}\neq x_{j}\}

be the ordered configuration space of
\mathcal{M}
, and

(1.2)

\Conf_{n}(\mathcal{M}):=\PConf_{n}(\mathcal{M})/S_{n}

be the unordered configuration space of
\mathcal{M}
. As our primary focus in this work will be on the unordered configuration spaces, we will often just say configuration space to indicate unordered configuration space.

For
p,q,i\geq 0
, define the mixed Hodge number
h^{p,q;i}(\mathcal{M})
as the dimension of the
(p,q)
-part of the mixed Hodge structure of
H^{i}(\mathcal{M};{\mathbb{Q}})
. Our primary concern in this work will be with the generating function,

(1.3)

f_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y,u,t):=\sum_{p,q,i,n\geq 0}(-1)^{i}h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(\mathcal{M}))\,x^{p}y^{q}u^{i}t^{n},

that records (up to sign) all mixed Hodge numbers of all configuration spaces of
\mathcal{M}
.

Generating functions similar to
f_{\mathcal{M}}
have already seen a tremendous amount of attention in works such as
[
4
,
8
,
27
]
, for instance. In keeping with these preceding works, a few words may be necessary to justify the decision to use a signed Hilbert series. Firstly, one should note that nothing is actually lost by making this choice since
\sum_{p,q,i,n\geq 0}h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(X))\,x^{p}y^{q}u^{i}t^{n}=f_{X}(x,y,-u,t)
. As for why we have chosen to do this, there is quite a bit of precedent going back to the seminal work of Macdonald
[
14
]
and later work of Cheah
[
3
]
. All of these previous works contribute to the following natural question, which we aim to resolve in part during the course of the present paper.

Question 1.1
.

Specialize
\mathcal{M}
to be a smooth connected complex variety. Is
f_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y,u,t)
always rational? If yes, what can we say about its denominator in general? For specific examples of
\mathcal{M}
, can we find an exact formula?

We note that Question
1.1
can be easily answered for
f_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y,1,t)
because of the motivicity of the
E
-polynomial and a result of Vakil and Wood
[
27
, Proposition 5.9]
paired with some results of
[
14
]
. On the other hand, Question
1.1
for
f_{\mathcal{M}}(1,1,u,t)
concerns the rationality of the generating function for the Betti numbers of the configuration spaces of
\mathcal{M}
, which can be checked for any Riemann surface using
[
8
]
. The rationality question for
f_{\mathcal{M}}(1,1,u,t)
for manifolds more general than Riemann surfaces remains open. Loosening our requirement that
\mathcal{M}
be a manifold, and considering the (bivariate) generating function encoding the Betti numbers with varying homological index and number of points, it follows from
[
20
,
1
]
that the associated generating function is rational whenever
\mathcal{M}
is a one-dimensional CW-complex (i.e. a graph).

We investigate
f_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y,u,t)
as a common refinement of the extensively studied
f_{\mathcal{M}}(1,1,u,t)
and the “easy”
f_{\mathcal{M}}(x,y,1,t)
. The primary result of this paper (Theorem
1.5
, below) will answer all of the above in the affirmative in the cases where
\mathcal{M}
is a multi-punctured Riemann surface.

For
g\geq 0,r\geq 1
, we denote by
X=\Sigma_{g,r}
the
r
-punctured closed Riemann surface of genus
g
, equipped with any structure as a complex variety. We provide a clean explicit rational formula for
f_{X}(x,y,u,t)
. The case of
r=0
is fundamentally different, and is treated by Pagaria in
[
19
]
. It is notable that our methods cannot be used to derive a closed form for the Hilbert Series in this case. Pagaria provides formulas
[

f_{X}(x,x,u,t)
, though the resulting expression is not in any obvious way related to the ones given here for the

Theorem A
.

For
g\geq 0
and
r\geq 1
, we have

(1.4)

f_{X}(x,y,u,t)=\frac{1}{(1+xyut)^{r-1}}\frac{\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}}{(1-t)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}},

where
\Phi_{g}
is any
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
-linear map satisfying

(1.5)

\Phi_{g}(z^{j})=\begin{cases}u^{j}t^{j},&0\leq j\leq g;\\
u^{j-1}t^{j},&g+2\leq j\leq 2g+2.\end{cases}

Remark 1.2
.

For
p+q=n
, the coefficient of
x^{p}y^{q}z^{n}
in
(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}
is given precisely by

\displaystyle(-1)^{n}\left\lparen\binom{g}{p}\binom{g}{q}-\binom{g}{p-1}\binom{g}{q-1}\right\rparen.

By the log-concavity of the binomial coefficeint sequence
\{\binom{g}{j}\}_{j}
and the property that
\binom{g}{j}=\binom{g}{g-j}
, we have

\mathrm{sgn}\left\lparen\binom{g}{p}\binom{g}{q}-\binom{g}{p-1}\binom{g}{q-1}\right\rparen=\begin{cases}+,&0\leq p+q\leq g,\\
0,&p+q=g+1,\\
-,&g+1\leq p+q\leq 2g+2.\end{cases}

As a result, the
x^{p}y^{q}z^{g+1}
-coefficient of
(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}
vanishes, so
\Phi_{g}(z^{g+1})
need not be specified. Furthermore,
\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}
has nonnegative coefficients in
x,y,-u,t
, so the coefficients of
f_{X}(x,y,-u,t)
are nonnegative. While this positivity of
f_{X}(x,y,-u,t)
is guaranteed by the fact that
h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(X))\geq 0
, the positivity of its
numerator

\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}
is a stronger statement; in other words, (
1.4
) gives a cancellation-free formula to compute
h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(X))
.

Relevant to this discussion, the aformentioned rational formula in the
r=0
case given by Pagaria
[
19
]
also has a manifestly positive numerator. It is the belief of the authors that this suggests a deeper topological or representation theoretic structure interacting with the mixed-Hodge numbers, such as the action of the mapping class group of

[

]
.

Remark 1.4
.

The equation (
1.4
) can also give one a sense of how things look when we also allow the parameters

The study of regular behaviors in configuration spaces that manifest when one varies not only the number of points being configured but also the underlying space, has become very common in recent years, though largely in the context of configuration spaces of graphs. See
[
13
,
17
,
21
,
12
]
for a small sampling of these kinds of results. Notably, in those contexts, it has been observed that the corresponding generating functions encoding Betti numbers with varying underlying graphs can be rational, combining
[

]
with
[

]
, or even algebraic
[
22
]
. It would be interesting to see more results on generating functions associated with configuration spaces which account not only for the homological index and the number of points, but also variation in the base space.

As an immediate application, substituting
x=y=1
, Theorem
1.5
provides a clean formula for the generating function for the Betti numbers up to sign. These Betti numbers were originally calculated in
[
8
]
. The necessity of the degree shift
\Phi_{g}
to convert the numerator into a factored form is probably one reason why a simple formula for the numerator was not known before.

As another application, setting
x,y,
and
u
all equal to 1 turns our main theorem into a statement about the generating function for the Euler characteristic of these spaces. Generating functions of this sort are the primary consideration of the famous theorem of Gal
[
10
]
, which provided a beautifully clean rational description of the generating function for the Euler characteristic

\sum_{n\geq 0}\chi(\Conf_{n}(S))t^{n},

whenever
S
is a simplicial complex. The main result of this paper therefore recovers and expands on
[
10
]
in the specific case of configuration spaces of punctured surfaces.

Perhaps the most mysterious seeming part of the formula in Theorem
1.5
is the combinatorial shifting operator
\Phi_{g}
. Originally, the necessary presence of such a shift was observed in an entirely “brute force” fashion by the first author while he studied the Betti numbers found in
[
8
]
. Having proven Theorem
1.5
, however, we are now able to give a better explanation in terms of the geometry of the situation. As we will see in the proof of Proposition
3.7
, the shift is a direct manifestation of the hard Lefschetz property of the operator
\omega\wedge:\bigwedge^{*}V\to\bigwedge^{*}V
, where
V={\mathbb{Q}}^{2g}
with basis
x_{1},y_{1},\dots,x_{g},y_{g}
, and

(1.6)

\omega=x_{1}\wedge y_{1}+\dots+x_{g}\wedge y_{g}.

(See Lemma
3.5
.) The reason why this operator shows up is because an isomorphic copy of it appears in the explicit description of the differential map of our spectral sequence, as explained in Section
3.2

To highlight the difficulty of Theorem
1.5
, we note that one of its consequences is that the mixed Hodge structure for
H^{i}(\Conf_{n}(\Sigma_{g,r}))
is not pure for certain
i,n
if
g,r>1
. This is in contrast to the
g=r=1
case, where one has purity of weight
\lfloor 3i/2\rfloor
due to
[

]
. This purity was crucial in the determination of the
g=r=1
case in
[
4
]
.

To illustrate the power of Theorem
1.5
, one can consider its implications in the asymptotic behavior in the parameters
i
and
n
of the Betti numbers, or mixed-Hodge numbers more generally. Classically speaking, homological stability in configuration spaces of manifolds considered the stable behavior of Betti numbers when
i
was fixed and
n
allowed to vary (See
[
23
]
[
15
]
[
5
]
and the references therein). More recently it has been noted that regular behaviors in the Betti numbers also appear in ordered configuration spaces of orientable non-compact manifolds when one travels along any line of slope
\frac{1}{2}
,
i=\frac{1}{2}n

[
16
]
. Our Theorem
1.5
can be viewed as providing a stability theorem that is all encompassing, at least in the case of punctured Reimann surfaces. In other words, it’s a version of stability for the Betti numbers that does not require one to look along linear slices of
i
and
n
. Conversely, our main theorem implies the presence of “good behavior” of the Betti numbers along any (quasi) linear relationship between
i
and
n
. We will show the following during the course of this work as a direct corollary to our main theorem.

Corollary 1.5
.

For
X=\Sigma_{g,r},

g\geq 0,

r\geq 1,
we have

(a)

for all
p,q,i,n
,
h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(X))=0
whenever
n<i
and
h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n}(X))=h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{n+1}(X))
whenever
n\geq i+1
.

(b)

\sum_{p,q,i}(-1)^{i}h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{i}(X))x^{p}y^{q}z^{i}=\frac{T_{\leq g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}}{(1+xyz)^{r-1}(1-x^{2}yz^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}z^{2})^{g}},

where the operator
T_{\leq g}
means keeping only the terms with
z
-degree at most
g
.

(c)

\sum_{p,q,i}(-1)^{i}h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{i+1}(X))x^{p}y^{q}z^{i}=\frac{\Psi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}}{(1+xyz)^{r-1}(1-x^{2}yz^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}z^{2})^{g}},

where
\Psi_{g}
is any
{\mathbb{Q}}[x,y]
-linear map satisfying

(1.7)

\Psi_{g}(z^{j})=\begin{cases}z^{j},&0\leq j\leq g;\\
z^{j-1},&g+2\leq j\leq 2g+2.\end{cases}

Breaking Corollary
1.5
into its parts,
(a)
tells us that the mixed Hodge numbers of
\Conf_{n}(X)
vanish above the line
i=n
. While this fact was already known (see
[
16
]
, for instance) our generating function explicitly observes the behavior by consequence of the fact that the denominator of (
1.4
) is a univariate polynomial with coefficieints in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
in the variable
ut
. Part
(a)
also tells us that homological stability in homological index
i
starts precisely at
n=i+1
. This is a vast improvement on the representation stable range for the spaces
\PConf_{n}(X)
, which is known to start at
n=2i
.

The later two parts of Corollary
1.5
tell us that if one were to look along either of the two linear strands
i=n
or
i=n+1
, the generating function for the mixed Hodge numbers remains rational. Note that while it is always the case that rational multi-variate generating functions produce
algebraic
generating functions along linear strands
[
24
, Chapter 6]
, our generating function (
1.4
) is special in that these associated generating functions are rational.

One also sees that applying
(a)
together with
(b)
and
(c)
, one obtains rational formulas for the generating functions of the mixed Hodge numbers along any linear strand
i=k_{1}m,n=k_{2}m
.

Finally, combining
(a)
and
(c)
also computes the stable mixed Hodge numbers
h^{p,q;i}(\Conf_{\infty}(X))
. We will prove Corollary
1.5
in Section
4
.

It is a simple algebraic computation to apply Corollary
1.5
to recover the explicit formulas for the Betti numbers of configurations of punctured surfaces given by Drummond-Cole and Knudsen in
[
8
, Proposition 3.5]
. We note that Corollary
1.5
specialized to the Betti numbers by setting
x=y=1
and replacing
z
with
-z
tells us more than just a formula for these numbers. Indeed, as noted in Remark
1.2
, it also reveals that the rational generating function for the Betti numbers has a numerator with positive coefficients!

To end this introduction, we take a moment to say a few words about what might be going on in the case of
\PConf_{n}(X)
. Much of the modern literature on ordered configuration spaces of manifolds and other spaces has put considerable stress on the fact that one must incorporate the natural actions of the symmetric groups into any computation one may perform on their cohomology groups. The language of Representation Stability (see
[
7
,
6
,
16
]
for a small sampling) was essentially designed to contend with the kinds of stable behaviors that one finds when studying the representations which appear in these cohomology groups. Parallel to these developments, there has also been considerable work trying to understand equivariant generalizations of Hilbert series in a number of circumstances wherein one has a family of spaces each carrying some kind of naturally compatible symmetry (see
[
25
,
9
]
, for some instances of this). It is our current belief, due to the fact that mixed Hodge structures on these ordered configuration spaces fully respect all permutation actions as well as the morphisms which introduce (or forget) points into the configuration space, that one can define an equivariant version of
f_{X}
, which carries a similar rationality property as in our main theorem. We leave this question as a possible interesting follow-up to the present work.

2.
Computational background and setup

2.1.
Computations in the cohomology of
\Sigma_{g,1}

Fix
g\geq 0
and
X=\Sigma_{g,1}
. Then the cohomology ring of
X
is given by

\ H^{*}(X)={\mathbb{Q}}\langle 1,x^{(1)},\dots,x^{(g)},y^{(1)},\dots,y^{(g)}\rangle,

where
\deg x^{(a)}=\deg y^{(a)}=1
,
x^{(a)}
has Hodge type
(1,0)
, and
y^{(a)}
has Hodge type
(0,1)
for each
1\leq a\leq g
.

For
n\geq 1
, the Künneth formula gives

H^{*}(X^{n})=\bigwedge[x^{(a)}_{i},y^{(a)}_{i}:1\leq a\leq g,1\leq i\leq n]/(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}:\alpha,\beta\in H^{1}(X)),

where for any
1\leq i\leq n
and
\alpha\in H^{1}(X)
, we denote by
\alpha_{i}
the pullback of
\alpha
via the
i

\pi_{i}:X^{n}\to X
.

Let
\delta:X\to X^{2}
be the diagonal map. We define the
diagonal class

[\Delta]\in H^{2}(X^{2})
to be the image of
1
under the Gysin map
\delta_{*}:H^{0}(X)\to H^{2}(X\times X)
, which we now take the time to explicitly construct.

Let
\Sigma=\Sigma_{g}
, and choose a volume form
\omega
of
H^{2}(\Sigma)
. Let
x^{(a)}
and
y^{(a)}
, where
1\leq a\leq g
, be chosen such that
x^{(a)}y^{(a)}=\omega
and
x^{(a)}x^{(b)}=y^{(a)}y^{(b)}=x^{(a)}y^{(b)}=0
in
H^{2}(\Sigma)
for
a\neq b
. The compactly supported cohomology of
X
is given by
H^{0}_{c}(X)=0
,
H^{1}_{c}(X)=H^{1}(X)=H^{1}(\Sigma)
and
H^{2}_{c}(X)=H^{2}(\Sigma)
. Under the above identification, the Poincaré duality pairing
H^{1}(X)\times H^{1}_{c}(X)\to H^{2}_{c}(X)
is just the cup product
H^{1}(\Sigma)\times H^{1}(\Sigma)\to H^{2}(\Sigma)

By Künneth’s formula, the cohomology of
\Sigma^{2}
is given by
H^{*}(\Sigma^{2})=H^{*}(\Sigma)\otimes H^{*}(\Sigma)
. For
\gamma\in H^{*}(\Sigma)

\gamma_{1}=\gamma\otimes 1
and
\gamma_{2}=1\otimes\gamma
. There are natural maps
H^{*}_{c}(X^{2})\hookrightarrow H^{*}(\Sigma^{2})\twoheadrightarrow H^{*}(X^{2})

H^{4}_{c}(X^{2})\cong H^{4}(X^{2})

\omega_{1}\omega_{2}

\alpha,\beta\in H^{1}(X)

\alpha_{j}\beta_{j}=0
for
\alpha,\beta\in H^{1}(X)
and

We are now ready to define the Gysin map. Denote by
\int
the isomorphism that sends the volume form to

\int:H^{2}_{c}(X)\to{\mathbb{Q}}

\omega

(2.1)

\int_{X}\epsilon\cdot\delta^{*}(\gamma)=\int_{X^{2}}\delta_{*}(\epsilon)\cdot\gamma,\text{ for all }\epsilon\in H^{i}(X)\text{, }\gamma\in H^{2-i}_{c}(X^{2}),

where
\delta^{*}

\delta^{*}
is induced from

\delta^{*}(\gamma_{j})=\gamma

The above alternative construction for the Gysin map now gives us a means to define the diagonal class

(2.2)

\int_{X}\delta^{*}(\gamma)=\int_{X^{2}}[\Delta]\cdot\gamma,\text{ for all }\gamma\in H^{2}_{c}(X^{2}).

Our next lemma uses the above characterization of the diagonal class to provide a slightly more useful expression for

Lemma 2.1
.

We have

(2.3)

[\Delta]=-\sum_{a=1}^{g}\left\lparen x^{(a)}_{1}y^{(a)}_{2}+x^{(a)}_{2}y^{(a)}_{1}\right\rparen.

Proof.

It suffices to verify (
2.2
) for
\gamma=x^{(a)}_{1}x^{(b)}_{2},y^{(a)}_{1}y^{(b)}_{2}
,
x^{(a)}_{1}y^{(b)}_{2}
, and
y^{(a)}_{1}x^{(b)}_{2}
, assuming that
[\Delta]
is given by (
2.3
).

We demonstrate two examples only, and the rest are analogous. For
\gamma=x^{(a)}_{1}x^{(b)}_{2}
, the left-hand side of (

Proposition 3.3
.

The distinguished generators,

Proof.

Fix
\mathbf{t}

To start, the polynomial

is, by definition, comprised of the sum of all monomials obtained by applying permutations to

Critically, in so far as the monomials are concerned, permuting the size two blocks within a given color, say

\alpha_{\bullet,\bullet}
in

Having proven our claim, we next observe that

by our observation above that

3.2.
Concluding the proof using the distinguished basis

Having proven that our distinguished generators form a basis, we will now be able to prove our main theorem as a consequence. In particular, we compute dimensions of the components of the graded vector space
{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g}:=\bigoplus_{n}{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g,n}

Consider the exterior algebra

(3.2)

\omega=x_{1}\wedge y_{1}+\dots+x_{g}\wedge y_{g},

and consider the operator

defined by
\iota\alpha=\omega\wedge\alpha
. Then

has matrix coefficients

(3.3)

\iota\alpha_{(S,T)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S^{\prime},T^{\prime}\\
\lvert S^{\prime}\rvert+\lvert T^{\prime}\rvert=\lvert S\rvert+\lvert T\rvert+2\end{subarray}}c^{S,T}_{S^{\prime},T^{\prime}}\alpha_{(S^{\prime},T^{\prime})}

with the
same

as in (
2.24

Lemma 3.5

The operator

Proof.

This follows from the Hard Lefschetz Theorem for any abelian variety of dimension

∎

Returning to

H_{g}=\ker(d|{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g})/\im(d|{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g}),\;H_{g,n}=\ker(d|{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g,n})/\im(d|{\mathbb{Q}}\Gamma_{g,n}).

Proposition 3.6

We have

\sum_{p,q,w_{1},w_{2},n}\dim H_{g,n}^{(p,q,w_{1},w_{2})}x^{w_{1}}y^{w_{2}}(-u)^{p+q}t^{n}=\frac{\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}}{(1-t)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}},

(3.6)

\Phi_{g}(z^{j})=\begin{cases}u^{j}t^{j},&0\leq j\leq g;\\

Given

V_{(\rho,\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v})}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{Q}}\{f_{(\rho,S,T,\vec{u},\vec{v})}:\lvert S\rvert=\sigma,\lvert T\rvert=\tau\}

(3.7)

(3.8)

Then

(3.9)

H_{g,n}^{(p,q,w_{1},w_{2})}=\bigoplus_{\rho,\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v}}H_{(\rho,\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v})}

where the sum ranges over all

such that (

By Lemma

(3.10)

\dim H_{(1,\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v})}=\begin{cases}0,&\sigma+\tau\leq g-1,\\

\displaystyle\sum_{p,q,w_{1},w_{2},n}\dim H_{g,n}^{(p,q,w_{1},w_{2})}x^{w_{1}}y^{w_{2}}(-u)^{p+q}t^{n}

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v}}\dim H_{(1,\sigma,\tau,\lvert\vec{u}\rvert,\lvert\vec{v})\rvert}x^{1+\sigma+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}y^{1+\tau+\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}(-u)^{1+\sigma+\tau+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}t^{2+\sigma+\tau+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}/(1-t)+

(3.14)

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v}}\dim H_{(0,\sigma,\tau,\vec{u},\vec{v})}x^{\sigma+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}y^{\tau+\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}(-u)^{\sigma+\tau+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}t^{\sigma+\tau+2\lvert\vec{u}\rvert+2\lvert\vec{v}\rvert}/(1-t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(1-t)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}\left\lparen-xyut^{2}P(-xut,-yut)+Q(-xut,-yut)\right\rparen,

and

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
be as in the proof above. Then

(3.16)

-xyut^{2}P(-xut,-yut)+Q(-xut,-yut)=\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}

where
\Phi_{g}
is as in (
3.6
) above.

Proof.

This is ultimately an exercise in expanding the relevant polynomials and performing simple algebraic manipulations. Instead of mechanically showing that the two sides of our desired equality are equal, we approach the problem from a more algebraic perspective. This argument hopefully stresses and elucidates how
\Phi_{g}
arises in context.

To start, note that in the sums defining
P(x,y)
and
Q(x,y)
, the constraint
\sigma,\tau\geq 0
on the summands is unnecessary. This is obvious for
Q(x,y)
, whereas for
P(x,y)
,
\sigma
being negative, for instance, forces
\tau
to be larger than
g
, thereby killing the summand because of the
\binom{g}{\tau+1}
factor.

Now, we rewrite the binomial partial sums using the truncation operator: for
J\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}
and a Laurent series

, define

T^{x,y}_{J}f(x,y):=\sum_{\sigma+\tau\in J}x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}[x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}]f(x,y),

where
[x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}]f(x,y)
is the
x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}
-coefficient of
f(x,y)
. In particular, one can think of
T^{x,y}_{J}f(x,y)
as removing all terms in
f(x,y)
whose total degree is outside of
J
.

Then

\displaystyle P(x,y)

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\geq g-1}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in{\mathbb{Z}}}x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}\left\lparen\binom{g}{\sigma}\binom{g}{\tau}-\binom{g}{\sigma+1}\binom{g}{\tau+1}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\geq g-1}\left\lparen(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}-x^{-1}y^{-1}(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\geq g-1}\left\lparen-x^{-1}y^{-1}(1-xy)(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=-x^{-1}y^{-1}T^{x,y}_{\geq g+1}\left\lparen(1-xy)(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}\right\rparen,

and

\displaystyle Q(x,y)

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\leq g}\sum_{\sigma,\tau\in{\mathbb{Z}}}x^{\sigma}y^{\tau}\left\lparen\binom{g}{\sigma}\binom{g}{\tau}-\binom{g}{\sigma-1}\binom{g}{\tau-1}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\leq g}\left\lparen(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}-xy(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=T^{x,y}_{\leq g}\left\lparen(1-xy)(1+x)^{g}(1+y)^{g}\right\rparen.

Substituting to the left-hand side of (
3.16
), and noting that the substitution
x\mapsto-xut,y\mapsto-yut
does not affect the total degree in
x,y
, we get

\displaystyle-xyut^{2}P(-xut,-yut)+Q(-xut,-yut)

\displaystyle=\left\lparen-xyut^{2}(-(xut)^{-1}(yut)^{-1})T^{x,y}_{\geq g+1}+T^{x,y}_{\leq g}\right\rparen\left\lparen(1-xyu^{2}t^{2})(1-xut)^{g}(1-yut)^{g}\right\rparen

\displaystyle=\left\lparen u^{-1}T^{x,y}_{\geq g+1}+T^{x,y}_{\leq g}\right\rparen\left\lparen(1-xyu^{2}t^{2})(1-xut)^{g}(1-yut)^{g}\right\rparen,

which clearly agrees with
\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}.

∎

By

]
, it suffices to prove Theorem

4.
The Proof of Corollary

In this final section, we expand out the algebra necessary to prove Corollary

.

Proof of Corollary
1.5
.

Throughout the proof, we regard
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
as the coefficient ring, so polynomials in
x,y
are treated as constants.

By the definition of
\Phi_{g}
, there exist sequences
\{a_{i}(x,y)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
\{b_{i}(x,y)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of polynomials in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y]
such that

\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}a_{i}u^{i}t^{i}+b_{i}u^{i}t^{i+1}.

Note that
a_{i}=0
unless
0\leq i\leq g
and
b_{i}=0
unless
g+1\leq i\leq 2g+1
.

Since
1/((1+xyut)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g})
is a power series in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x,y][[ut]]
, there exist sequences
\{c_{i}(x,y)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
and
\{d_{i}(x,y)\}_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
such that

\frac{1}{(1+xyut)^{r-1}}\frac{\Phi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}}{(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}=\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{Z}}}c_{i}u^{i}t^{i}+d_{i}u^{i}t^{i+1}.

Multiplying by
1/(1-t)=1+t+t^{2}+\dots
, we conclude that the
u^{i}t^{n}
-coefficient of
f_{X}(x,y,u,t)
is

[u^{i}t^{n}]f_{X}(x,y,u,t)=\begin{cases}0,&n<i;\\
c_{i}(x,y),&n=i;\\
c_{i}(x,y)+d_{i}(x,y),&n\geq i+1,\end{cases}

proving the stability
(a)
.

To prove
(b)
, we extract

\displaystyle\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i}]f_{X}(x,y,u,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i}]\left\{\frac{\sum_{j}a_{j}u^{j}t^{j}+b_{j}u^{j}t^{j+1}}{(1+xyut)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}\frac{1}{1-t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i}]\left\{\frac{\sum_{j}a_{j}u^{j}t^{j}}{(1+xyut)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}z^{i}}{(1+xyz)(1-x^{2}yz^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}z^{2})^{g}},

where the second equality is because
b_{j}u^{j}t^{j+1}
term cannot contribute and the only term in
1+t+t^{2}+\dots
that contributes is the constant term
1
. Finally, we note that
\sum_{i}a_{i}z^{i}=T_{\leq g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}
. This finishes the proof of
(b)
.

To prove
(c)
, a similar inspection yields

\displaystyle\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i+1}]f_{X}(x,y,u,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i+1}]\left\{\frac{\sum_{j}a_{j}u^{j}t^{j}+b_{j}u^{j}t^{j+1}}{(1+xyut)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}\frac{1}{1-t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}z^{i}[u^{i}t^{i+1}]\left\{\frac{(\sum_{j}a_{j}u^{j}t^{j})\cdot t+(\sum_{j}b_{j}u^{j}t^{j+1})\cdot 1}{(1+xyut)(1-x^{2}yu^{2}t^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}u^{2}t^{2})^{g}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i}a_{i}z^{i}+\sum_{i}b_{i}z^{i}}{(1+xyz)(1-x^{2}yz^{2})^{g}(1-xy^{2}z^{2})^{g}}.

Again, we note that
\sum_{i}a_{i}z^{i}+\sum_{i}b_{i}z^{i}=\Psi_{g}\{(1-xyz^{2})(1-xz)^{g}(1-yz)^{g}\}
. This finishes the proof of
(c)
.
