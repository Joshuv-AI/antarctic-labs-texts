Clinical characteristics, risk factor analysis, and outcomes of graft rejection after Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty
Authors: Shalini Singh, Md Hasnat Ali, Sunita Chaurasia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To report the clinical characteristics, risk factors, and outcomes of graft rejection after Descemet membrane endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK).Methods:Of the total of 1026 eyes of 935 patients that underwent DMEK, 25 eyes of 25 patients had rejection. The risk factors in 25 eyes were compared with those of control eyes without rejection during the same study period. The control group consisted of 98 eyes of 98 patients, who underwent DMEK during the same period and had no episode of rejection at a minimum and regular follow-up period of 2 years from the time of surgery.Results:Overall, the incidence of rejection was 2.5%. The median age of patients was 53 (interquartile range, IQR, 45–62) years. In the control group, the median age was 65.5 (IQR 56.2–70.8) years. Most (88%) rejection episodes occurred within the first 2 years of surgery. Multivariate analysis showed younger age at surgery, male gender, rebubbling, cessation of topical steroids, and indication of DMEK (failed graft, corneal edema post-cataract surgery, and corneal edema due to inflammatory causes) as significant risk factors for graft rejection. No significant temporal correlation was observed with COVID-19 vaccination in the majority (92%). The mean endothelial cell loss following rejection was 72.5%. Kaplan–Meier survival plot showed the probability of graft survival was 90% at 1 year, 82% at 2 years, 80% at 3 years, 80% at 4 years, and 78% at 5–8 years.Conclusion:Our study suggests that the risk of rejection is higher for indications such as failed previous graft, post-cataract surgery edema, and edema postinflammatory causes, when compared to Fuchs endothelial dystrophy. Topical steroids should be continued at least up to 2 years after surgery as this is the most common time frame of the rejection episode. The majority (68%) of the rejection episodes were reversible and resulted in a favorable outcome.

Descemet stripping endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK) and Descemet endothelial keratoplasty (DMEK) are the two popular techniques of endothelial keratoplasty (EK). One of the many reasons for the rapid adoption of the EK techniques was due to the lowered risk of rejection when compared to penetrating keratoplasty (PK).[123456]

Some of the risk factors that have been identified for graft rejection in EK include young age, previous keratoplasty, corneal vascularization, coexisting glaucoma, sudden discontinuation of topical steroids, systemic comorbidities, peripheral anterior synechiae, and African American ancestry.[7] The risk of rejection after DMEK is believed to be lower than that after DSEK. The rate of rejection after DMEK is variably reported, with a mean rate of 1.9% and a range of 0–6%.[8] This broad range is attributed to several reasons such as divergence in ethnicity, indications of the DMEK, differences in the postoperative steroid regimen, variable period of follow-up after surgery, and sample sizes.[9] Anshu et al.[10] reported a rejection rate of <1% with a median follow-up of 13 months. Hos et al.[11] reported a risk of 0.9% after 1 year and 2.3% after 2 and 4 years. Birbal et al.[12] reported 5-year outcomes of 500 DMEK and a graft rejection rate of 2.8%. Basak et al.[13] reported a rejection rate of 1.2%, with a median follow-up of 17 months.

In the present study, we report the demographics, clinical profile, and risk factors of graft rejection after DMEK performed at a tertiary care institution.

Methods

During the study period between January 2017 and March 2024, a total of 1026 eyes of 935 patients had undergone DMEK, of which 25 eyes of 25 patients were diagnosed with allograft rejection after DMEK. The analysis of 25 eyes with rejection episode was conducted retrospectively and was compared with a control group that comprised 98 eyes of 98 patients, who underwent DMEK during the same period and had no episode of rejection at a minimum and regular follow-up period of 2 years from the time of surgery. Data captured included demographic details, indication for DMEK, associated ocular comorbidities, clinical presentations of graft rejection, recovery of graft clarity, and endothelial cell loss after rejection episode in those eyes where graft clarity was restored following rejection episode. We also collected data related to COVID-19 vaccination in the study and the control group. Overall, out of 123 patients, 25 patients had a history of COVID-19 vaccination.

Of the 1026 eyes that had DMEK, 45 eyes of 45 (4.38%) patients had primary graft failure and these were excluded from the analysis of rejection rate. The exact cause of primary graft failure could not be discerned and most likely was attributed to multitude factors such as complexity associated with the indication of surgery, iatrogenic causes, and donor-related factors.

DMEK was performed by a team of experienced corneal surgeons and trained senior corneal fellows using DMEK scroll prepared by the surgeon in the operating room until 2022 and thereafter using prestripped DM scroll with a stromal window, received from the institute affiliated eye bank. The surgery was performed under peribulbar anesthesia. The patients were treated with topical broad-spectrum antibiotic four times daily for 1–2 weeks, prednisolone acetate 1% eyedrops (Pred Forte, Allergan) in weekly tapering dosage. Typically, the patients received topical steroids 6–8 times daily, which was tapered weekly to a maintenance dose of twice daily for 6 months, once daily till 2 years. In eyes with a steroid response, loteprednol etabonate 1% was advised after 1 year. Additional treatment with antiglaucoma medications was considered as in eyes with steroid response or patients with pre-existing or secondary glaucoma. In general, the protocol for follow-up after DMEK was day 1, day 3, 1 week, 4–6 weeks, 3–6 months, and 6 monthly thereafter for 2 years and yearly thereafter. In the eyes with significant graft detachments, rebubbling was performed with 100% air injection. At each visit, visual acuity, slit-lamp examination, and intraocular pressure (IOP) were documented. Specular microscopy was performed whenever possible.

Primary graft failure referred to the grafts where the corneal clarity failed to improve even at 6–8 weeks from surgery.

The definitions of graft rejection and graft failure are described as below:

a)Graft Rejection: The graft rejection was diagnosed based on the previously described clinical features such as a decrease in visual acuity with evidence of edema in a previously clear and compact cornea with/without additional signs and symptoms such as keratic precipitates, anterior chamber inflammation, ocular congestion, pain, discomfort, and photophobia.[1415]b)Graft Failure: Graft failure was diagnosed when there was no improvement in corneal clarity for 3 months from the time of diagnosis of rejection.

Graft rejections were treated with topical prednisolone acetate 1% in an hourly dosage for 4–6 days, followed by two hourly dosages for next 4–6 days, with a weekly tapering dosage to a maintenance dose of twice daily till 6 months and once a day thereafter. In those with severe rejection episodes (defined clinically as diffuse severe corneal edema), and with a short duration of history (<3 days), systemic steroid intravenous methyl prednisolone acetate 500 mg–1 g single dose was also given additionally.

The various parameters that were studied as potential risk factors for rejection were recipient’s age, recipient’s gender, donor’s age, donor’s gender, nonusage of steroids prior to rejection episode, indication of surgery, peripheral anterior synechiae, glaucoma, and rebubbling. Comparison of these parameters was done with the 98 eyes that had DMEK within the study period without the rejection episode.

The clinical characteristics of eyes with a successful reversal of graft clarity following rejection were compared with the eyes with persistent graft edema and failure after rejection. The various parameters studied were recipient’s age and gender, concomitant procedure with DMEK, glaucoma, rebubbling, duration of rejection at the time of presentation in clinic, nonuse of steroids at initial presentation, donor’s endothelial cell density (ECD), and age.

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistical software R: a language and environment for statistical computing, Vienna, Austria. The presentation of the categorical variables was done in the form of number and percentage (%). The quantitative data were presented as the means ± SD and median with 25th and 75th percentiles (interquartile range). For the cases in which the data were not normal, nonparametric tests were used. To study the impact of potential risk factors for rejection episode, logistic regression analysis was performed. Multivariable models were used to explore confounding risk factors where appropriate. A two-tailed probability, 5%, was considered statistically significant.

Clinical Profile and outcomes of 25 eyes with Rejection after DMEK

The indications for DMEK in eyes with rejection were failed previous graft in 11 (44%) eyes, post-cataract surgery corneal edema in seven (28%) eyes, endothelial dystrophy in five (20%) eyes, and corneal edema postinflammatory condition (viral endothelitis and iridocorneal endothelial syndrome) in two (8%) eyes. Among the 11 failed grafts, 10 had failed after prior DSEK and one after prior penetrating keratoplasty (PK), and in sequential order, DMEK was the second graft in eight eyes and third graft in three eyes. Of the 11 failed previous grafts, eight (7 DSEK and one PK) had failed following graft rejection and other three were non-rejection graft failures. The baseline diagnosis in 11 eyes with failed grafts was post-cataract surgery corneal edema in six eyes, adherent leucoma in three eyes, and corneal ectasia in two eyes.

The majority of 24 (96%) patients were symptomatic at the clinic visit, and one patient was diagnosed incidentally during the clinic visit. The symptoms reported were decreased vision in 24 eyes, redness with pain/discomfort in four, and photophobia in 12 eyes. The mean duration of symptoms was 11 (range 7–14) days. The slit-lamp evaluation revealed diffuse cornea edema in 22 eyes, keratic precipitates in 20 eyes, anterior chamber reaction in 12 eyes, and Khodadoust line in one eye.

At the time of diagnosis of rejection, 19 (76%) eyes were using topical steroids in a dosage of 1–2 times a day; the remaining 6 (24%) eyes were not on steroid medications. Eight (32%) eyes had comorbidities that included secondary glaucoma in eight eyes, and seven eyes had peripheral anterior synechiae. Two out of 25 (8%) eyes had a history of rejection episode within 1 month of COVID-19 vaccine.

The median time of rejection episode from the surgery was 1 year (IQR 0.6, 1.3). Rejection episode occurred within 1 year after surgery in 13 (52%) eyes, between 1 and 2 years in 9 (36%) eyes, and three (12%) eyes had rejection beyond 2 years. The majority, 24 (96%) eyes, had one episode of rejection, and one eye had another second episode of rejection.

All eyes received intensive topical steroids; 2 (8%) patients additionally received one dose of IV methylprednisolone at the initial presentation. Of the 25 eyes, 17 (68%) eyes had improvement in graft clarity; the remaining 8 (32%) eyes failed to recover following the episode of rejection. Of the 17 eyes with complete recovery after rejection episode, 7 (41.1%) remained clear till the last follow-up. The mean endothelial cell loss (ECL) after rejection episode resolved was 1786 (1530–2019) cells/mm2, 72.5% compared to ECD documented at the clinic visit prior to rejection episode (n = 14 eyes). The median survival of the graft after episode of rejection was 1.1 (IQR 0.6–1.5) years. The mean duration of follow-up from the time of surgery was 2.56 (range 2–8) years in 25 eyes.

In the control group of without rejection episode, the median age was 65.5 (IQR 56.2–70.8) years. Fifty-six (57%) were males. The most common indication for DMEK was endothelial dystrophy in 53 (54) eyes, followed by post-cataract surgery corneal edema in 22 (22.4%) eyes, failed graft in 14 eyes (12 eyes with failed DSEK and two eyes with failed PK), and corneal edema postinflammatory etiology in 9 (9.2%) eyes. Ninety-three (96%) were on topical steroids at a dosage of 1–2 times daily till the last follow-up. The median survival time of the grafts in the control group was 5 (IQR 4–5.9) years.

Younger age, rebubbling, nonusage of topical steroids, failed prior graft, and corneal edema post-cataract surgery were identified as significant risk factors for graft rejection in univariate analysis. In the multivariate analysis, surgery at a younger age, male gender, rebubbling, cessation of topical steroids and indication of DMEK comprising failed graft, corneal edema post cataract surgery, and corneal edema due to inflammatory causes emerged as significant risk factors for graft rejection.

Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curve [Fig. 2] describes the cumulative success probability of DMEK over the follow-up period. The probability of graft survival was 89.7% at 1 year, 81.9 at 2 years, 80% at 3 years, 79.9% at 4 years, and 77.9% at 5–8 years. There is a noticeable higher probability of graft rejection leading to failure in the initial period up to 2 years. KM plot also illustrates that after this initial period, the success rate remained relatively stable, with no additional failure up to 8 years. The 6–12 months probability of graft survival after rejection episode was 70.8%, and 65% at 1–3 years after graft rejection episode.

Discussion

Most studies have consistently indicated a lower risk of rejection in DMEK compared to PK and DSEK. Although the exact reasons for the low risk of rejection remain unclear, it is most likely due to lesser antigenic load in DMEK. We found that the risk of rejection was 2.54% within a mean follow-up duration of 2.6 (range 2–8) years, which is comparable to the previous studies.

The clinical presentation of DMEK graft rejection is diverse. The common symptoms reported are pain, discomfort, photophobia, and a decrease in vision, but asymptomatic nature with incidental diagnosis at clinic visits has been well documented. The various signs that have been noted in the clinic visits include congestion, anterior chamber reaction, keratic precipitates, and edema.[813] Dapena et al.[15] reported one patient of DMEK graft rejection who had a rejection line. Hos et al.[11] reported that most of the patients with DMEK graft rejection were clinically asymptomatic at presentation and were diagnosed on a routine clinical visit. In our study, all except one were symptomatic and complained of reduced vision at presentation. The most common clinical feature was corneal edema, followed by keratic precipitates and Khodadoust line.

In view of lower risk of rejection in DMEK, one clinical question is when the topical steroids can be discontinued after surgery. Price et al. studied incidence of rejection after discontinuing topical steroids and found it to be as high as 6% in the group that discontinued topical steroids within 1 year of DMEK.[14] Hos et al.[11] reported that the probability of graft rejection was the highest in the first 2 years (3.2%) after DMEK and slightly less thereafter for the next 2 years (2.3%). We observed that most (22/25 eyes) of the rejections occurred within the first 2 years after surgery. These observations coupled with asymptomatic or often subtle initial symptoms of graft rejection in DMEK in many eyes underscore the importance of topical steroid therapy up to 2 years and beyond after the surgery.

Although the risk of rejection in DMEK is lower than that in PK and DSEK, several situations may be associated with an increased risk. In this study, we analyzed some of the risk factors for rejection after DMEK and compared these with the control eyes that had minimum follow-up of 2 years and did not have rejection episode. We found that the risk of graft rejection was significantly higher in failed prior grafts, post-cataract surgery corneal edema, and eyes with inflammatory causes of corneal edema. The other significant risk factors identified were younger age, rebubbling, and nonuse of steroids at presentation. Younger recipient age has been a well-recognized risk factor for rejection after keratoplasty.[16] Rebubbling as a risk factor could be indirectly explained because of another intervention after primary surgery leading to additional inflammation and increased immunological activity. Glaucoma has been reported to be an important risk factor for both immunological graft rejection and nonimmunogenic graft failure in penetrating keratoplasty and endothelial keratoplasty. Anshu et al.[17] identified that the alteration in the proteomic composition of aqueous humor could be a cause for increased endothelial cell attrition and poor graft survival in post-glaucoma drainage eyes. Our study did not show glaucoma as a strong risk factor for graft rejection. Glaucoma as a risk factor for graft rejection is a complex parameter for assessment. The eyes with coexisting glaucoma also have a higher chance of nonimmunogenic graft failure, which might precede or overlap with allograft rejection episodes. Although we could not analyze the absolute numbers of nonimmunogenic graft failures in glaucoma eyes during the study period, this information would be analyzed in future studies. Post COVID-19 pandemic, there were several reports of graft rejection after DMEK during active infection and associated with COVID-19 vaccination.[6] We did not find any significant temporal association with COVID-19 vaccination in our study.

The majority (17/25 eyes, 68%) of grafts recovered after the rejection episode; however, only 7/17 (41.1%) eyes remained clear till the last follow-up. The mean endothelial cell loss at 6–12 months after rejection was 73% in comparison to the study by Hos et al., which reported that the ECL post rejection was 50% and 53% at 3 months and 1 year follow-up.[11] The higher ECL in our study could be due to differences in the indications of surgery, and severity of the rejection episodes. Additionally, most cases of rejection in the latter study were in FECD, while the indications were heterogeneous in our study. Higher ECL after the rejection episode may be the reason for decreased survival of the grafts post recovery of the rejection in our study.

On comparing with our previous study,[18] where we analyzed the graft rejection and the risk factors of rejection after DSEK, we found that similar indications (failed prior graft and corneal edema postinflammatory cause) and the nonuse of steroids were the risk factors common to both DSEK and DMEK. The risk of graft rejection was 3.6% after DSEK, in comparison to 2.54% in this study. We noted that the 6–12 months graft survival rate following rejection after DSEK was higher than DMEK (90% vs 70.8%). A possible reason could be the higher ECL postrejection in DMEK versus DSEK in the previous study (73% vs 55%).

One of the limitations of the study is that we could not compare with the entire cohort of eyes that underwent DMEK during the study period. Even though the control group included the 98 eyes without rejection, they had a minimum follow-up of 2 years, which is the most sensitive time frame of rejection after DMEK, making the comparative analysis of risk factors insightful.

In the current literature, there is a lack of prospective randomized study after DMEK with a defined topical steroid application regime, follow-up scheme documenting endothelial cell loss, and the frequency of asymptomatic graft rejections after DMEK. Additionally, the influence of HLA incompatibility in DMEK is unclear. Future studies may be planned prospectively specifically to address these parameters as risk factors for graft rejection after DMEK.

Conclusion

The study highlights the important risk factors associated with rejection after DMEK. Based on an institutional practice set up with electron medical record systems, it represents the real-world data and a comprehensive analysis of graft rejections after DMEK.
