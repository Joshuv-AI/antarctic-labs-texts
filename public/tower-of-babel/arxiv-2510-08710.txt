Abstract
. Case-based reasoning is a cornerstone of U.S. legal practice, requiring professionals to argue about a current case by drawing analogies to and distinguishing from past precedents. While Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities, their proficiency in this complex, nuanced form of reasoning needs further investigation. We propose a formal framework that decomposes the process of identifying significant distinctions between cases into three-stage reasoning tasks. Our framework models cases using factual predicates called factors, organizes them into a legal knowledge hierarchy, and defines verifiable rules for identifying distinctions, analyzing their argumentative support, and evaluating their significance. Through comprehensive evaluation of modern reasoning LLMs, we reveal a paradox: while models achieve high accuracy on surface-level reasoning (Task 1), performance degrades on hierarchical reasoning (Task 2: 64.82%-92.09%) and collapses on integrated analysis (Task 3: 11.46%-33.99%). Most strikingly, we find that models consistently expend more computational resources on incorrect responses than correct ones, suggesting that “thinking longer” does not always mean “thinking smarter.” Our work provides a methodology for fine-grained analysis of LLM reasoning capabilities in complex domains and reveals fundamental limitations that must be addressed for robust and trustworthy legal AI.



1.
Introduction

A core component of legal expertise is the ability to reason from precedent
(
Lamond 2006
;
Ashley 2017
)
. In the common law system, the doctrine of stare decisis obligates courts to follow the rulings of prior cases with similar factual circumstances
(
Douglas 1949
)
. Attorneys, therefore, build arguments by drawing analogies to favorable precedents and distinguishing their current case from unfavorable ones
(
Gerhardt 2011
)
. This process is not a simple pattern-matching task
(
Schauer and Spellman 2017
;
Horty 2011
;
Schauer 2008
)
; its reasoning process involves abstracting specific facts into broader legal concepts, weighing conflicting evidence, and understanding how different facts contribute to an overall legal argument.

The recent success of LLMs has raised the question of whether they can assist in these sophisticated reasoning tasks
(
Smith 2025
;
Atkinson and Bench-Capon 2019
)
. Reasoning LLMs have shown remarkable capabilities in various domains
(
Ke et al. 2025
)
, including planning
(
Wei et al. 2025
)
and mathematical reasoning
(
Wang et al. 2025b
)
. However, their capabilities in the complex, nuanced form of reasoning required in legal practice needs further investigation. This gap is particularly concerning given the increasing interest in deploying AI systems in legal contexts, where accuracy and reliability are paramount.

We introduce a formal framework that deconstructs one of the central arguments in case-based reasoning: identifying significant distinctions. A significant distinction is a factual difference between a current case and a precedent that is critical enough to justify a different outcome. Our framework operationalizes this concept through a sequence of three increasingly complex reasoning tasks, each designed to integrate specific aspects of hierarchical legal reasoning.

We build a decomposed framework grounded in the CATO tradition of case-based legal reasoning
(
Aleven 1997
;
Ashley and Aleven 1997
;
Aleven and Ashley 1995
)
. Each problem includes a factor hierarchy, a current case (
C_{1}
), and a precedent case (
C_{2}
). A symbolic solver computes ground truth using formal rules about distinctions, support, blocking, and emphasis/downplay. We then prompt LLMs to perform the same analysis in three increasingly challenging tasks. Our overall framework, shown in Figure
1
, provides a comprehensive view of how cases are processed from their linear representations to the identification of significant distinctions through hierarchical legal reasoning.

Figure 1.
The decomposed framework for identifying significant distinctions, which consists of three steps: (1) identify distinctions, (2) analyze argumentative roles of a distinction via legal knowledge hierarchy, and (3) identify significant distinctions. Red and blue presents the favoring side of the factors.

Our evaluation reveals a paradox that challenges conventional assumptions about LLM reasoning. While models achieve high accuracy on surface-level reasoning (Task 1), performance degrades significantly on hierarchical reasoning (Task 2: 64.82%-92.09%) and collapses on integrated analysis (Task 3: 11.46%-33.99%). Most remarkably, we find that models consistently expend more computational resources on incorrect responses than correct ones, suggesting that “thinking longer” does not always mean “thinking smarter.” This finding has profound implications for the development of reasoning LLMs.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we operationalize the CATO-style hierarchy into three decomposed tasks: (1) identify distinctions, (2) assess emphasizing vs. downplaying specific distinctions, and (3) integrate all steps to identify all significant distinctions. Second, we formalize the process of identifying significant distinctions using a symbolic representation of cases, factors, and a conceptual hierarchy. This model bridges abstract legal theory and computational implementation. Third, we present an empirical study that reveals limitations in current LLM architectures for complex legal reasoning, providing insights for the development of more effective and trustworthy legal AI systems.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
AI & Law: Case-Based Reasoning Systems

Early AI and law research established the foundation for computational legal reasoning. The HYPO system
(
Ashley 1991
;
Ashley 1992
)
pioneered factor-based case representation and distinguishing cases through factual differences and explored hypothetical reasoning. The CATO system
(
Aleven 1997
;
Ashley and Aleven 1997
)
introduced hierarchical organization of legal knowledge, recognizing that factors serve as evidence for abstract legal concerns, which support higher-level legal issues. CATO’s framework for identifying distinctions, analyzing their argumentative roles, and determining significance provided the theoretical foundation for our decomposed evaluation approach.

Subsequent work extended these systems through CABARET
(
Rissland and Skalak 1991
)
, which integrated case-based with rule-based reasoning, IBP
(
Brüninghaus and Ashley 2003
)
which employed machine learning to identify factors and case-based argumentation to predict outcomes, and VJAP
(
Grabmair 2017
;
Grabmair 2016
)
, which developed a value-based legal argumentation system. Recent research has focused on scaling these approaches to larger case databases and more complex legal domains
(
Chau and Livermore 2024
;
Zhang and Ashley 2025
;
Gray et al. 2025
)
.

These traditional systems required extensive manual knowledge engineering and operated on structured representations with explicit rule-based reasoning. The emergence of LLMs has created new opportunities for legal AI, but also new challenges in evaluating and understanding their reasoning capabilities. Our work bridges this gap by providing a framework for evaluating how well modern LLMs can perform the sophisticated reasoning tasks that these early systems were designed to handle.

2.2.
Hierarchical Reasoning in LLMs

Hierarchical reasoning is crucial for complex reasoning tasks like legal analysis
(
Wang et al. 2025a
;
Lyu et al. 2023
;
Canavotto and Horty 2023
)
. It involves moving between different levels of abstraction and integrating information across multiple levels. For example, in legal case analysis, a lawyer needs to reason from specific facts to intermediate concepts and ultimately to high-level legal issues.

Chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
demonstrated that explicitly encouraging models to generate intermediate reasoning steps can significantly improve performance on complex tasks. However, chain-of-thought approaches typically focus on linear reasoning chains rather than the hierarchical structures found in legal reasoning
(
Wang et al. 2025a
)
. Tree-of-thoughts approaches
(
Yao et al. 2023
)
have extended this work to explore multiple reasoning paths simultaneously, allowing models to consider alternative approaches and backtrack when necessary. Recent work on reasoning with external tools
(
Mialon et al. 2023
;
Ke et al. 2025
)
has explored how models can leverage specialized reasoning modules for different types of analysis.

Most existing work on hierarchical reasoning in LLMs has focused on mathematical or logical reasoning tasks. The legal domain presents unique challenges due to the need to reason about abstract legal concepts, weigh conflicting evidence, and integrate multiple types of analysis. Our work addresses this gap by providing a specific framework for evaluating hierarchical reasoning in the legal domain.

Alignment and Evaluation Methodologies for LLMs

The deployment of AI systems in legal contexts raises questions about trustworthiness, reliability, and accountability
(
Afroogh et al. 2024
;
Kattnig et al. 2024
)
. Legal professionals need to understand not just what AI systems can do, but how they reason and where they may fail
(
Zhang et al. 2025
;
Zhang and Ashley 2025
)
.

The evaluation of AI systems in legal contexts presents unique challenges due to the complexity and nuance of legal reasoning
(

)
. Traditional evaluation approaches
(
Aletras et al. 2016
;
Chalkidis et al. 2018
;
Kim and Goebel 2017
)
focus on accuracy on specific tasks like judical decision prediction, element extraction and question answering, which may not capture the full range of capabilities required for legal practice
(
Kattnig et al. 2024
)
. Recent work has developed benchmarks such as CUAD
(
Hendrycks et al. 2021
)
, LegalBench
(
Guha et al. 2023
)
and the COLIEE competition
(
Goebel et al. 2025
)
, but these focus primarily on isolated skills rather than the integrated application of the reasoning chain.

Recent work on explainable AI has focused on providing interpretable explanations for model decisions
(
Bilal et al. 2025
)
, but most work has focused on tasks like text classification
(
Chhatwal et al. 2018
;
Collenette et al. 2023
)
and judgement prediction
(
Collenette et al. 2020
;
Sesodia et al. 2025
)
and may not be applicable to the reasoning required in legal analysis. Work on AI safety and alignment
(
Irving et al. 2018
;
Buhl et al. 2025
;
Hubinger et al. 2024
)
has explored ensuring AI systems behave consistently with human values and expectations, which is particularly relevant to legal AI where systems must reason consistently with legal principles and professional standards.

Our work addresses these challenges by providing a decomposed evaluation framework on legal reasoning. We structure the evaluation around a principled reasoning process instead of atomic components, enabling detailed analysis of where models succeed and fail, and guiding the development of more capable and trustworthy legal AI systems.

3.
Modeling Hierarchical Case-Based Legal Reasoning

To computationally model case-based reasoning, we first formalize its core components: the cases themselves and the hierarchical structure of legal concepts they relate to.

3.1.
Case Representation

A legal case can be distilled into a set of legally relevant facts, represented as factors, and an outcome. Factors are stereotyped fact patterns that tend to favor either the plaintiff (p) or the defendant (d).

Definition 1 (Factor)
: A factor
f
is a predicate representing a legally relevant fact. The set of all possible factors is denoted by
\mathcal{F}
. Each factor
f\in\mathcal{F}
has a favored party,
s(f)\in\{p,d\}
.

Definition 2 (Case)
: A case
C
is a pair
F,o
, where
F\subseteq\mathcal{F}
is the set of factors present in the case, and
o\in\{p,d\}
is the outcome (i.e., the winning party).

In our analysis, we consider a current case,
C_{1}=(F_{1},o_{1})
, where the outcome
o_{1}
is unknown, and a precedent case,
C_{2}=(F_{2},o_{2})
, where the outcome
o_{2}
is known.

3.2.
Hierarchy of Legal Knowledge

Factors do not exist in isolation. They serve as evidence for more abstract legal concerns, which in turn inform the ultimate legal issues in a case. This multi-level abstraction is central to legal argument, allowing legal professionals to argue that, while the surface-level facts may differ, the underlying legal principles might be the same. We model this as a hierarchy.

Definition 3 (Hierarchy and Nodes)
: The hierarchy is a directed acyclic graph (DAG)
G=(V,E)
where:

-
V=\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{C}\cup\mathcal{I}
is the set of nodes, comprising base-level factors
\mathcal{F}
, intermediate legal concerns
\mathcal{C}
, and top-level legal issues
\mathcal{I}
.

-
E
is the set of edges, representing support relationships. An edge from node
u
to
v
means
u
provides evidence for
v
.

Not all support is equal. For instance, a factor can provide strong, direct evidence for a concern, or weak, circumstantial evidence.

Definition 4 (Edge Strength)
: Each edge
e\in E
has a strength,
\sigma(e)\in\{\text{strong},\text{weak}\}
.

This hierarchy forms the backbone of our reasoning framework, allowing us to model the nuanced connections between concrete facts and abstract legal concepts.

4.
A Decomposed Framework for Identifying Significant Distinctions

Different Levels of Difficulty

Task 1: Identify Distinctions:
Given a current case, a precedent and the favored side for each factor, identify all distinctions that could potentially make the precedent a poor analogy for the current case.

Task 2: Analyze Argumentative Roles of A Distinction:
Given a current case, a precedent, the hierarchy and a distinction, analyze the argumentative roles of the distinction.

Task 3: Identify All Significant Distinctions:
Given a current case, a precedent, and the hierarchy, identify all significant distinctions.

Figure 2.
Modifying the difficulty level of the tasks by increasing the reasoning complexity.

We now present our core contribution: a decomposed framework that formalizes the identification of significant distinctions. As illustrated in Figure
1
, our framework transforms the complex process of hierarchical legal reasoning from linear case representations through the systematic extraction of distinctions, incorporating legal knowledge hierarchies and formal rules to ultimately identify significant distinctions. The framework consists of three sequential tasks, each with increasing complexity as demonstrated in Figure
2
. A step-by-step application of this framework is detailed in Appendix
B
.

4.1.
Task 1: Identify Distinctions

The first step is to identify all factual differences that could potentially make the precedent a poor analogy for the current case. These are called distinctions.

Definition 5 (Distinction)
: Given a current case
C_{1}=(F_{1},o_{1})
and a precedent
C_{2}=(F_{2},o_{2})
, a factor
f\in\mathcal{F}
is a distinction if it satisfies one of the following conditions:

(1)

The factor is a strength for the precedent’s winner that the current case lacks:

f\in F_{2}\land f\notin F_{1}\land s(f)=o_{2}

(2)

The factor is a weakness for the precedent’s loser that is present in the current case:

f\in F_{1}\land f\notin F_{2}\land s(f)\neq o_{2}

Input
: A current case
C_{1}=(F_{1},o_{1})
, a precedent
C_{2}=(F_{2},o_{2})
and the favored side
s(f)
for each factor
f\in F_{1}\cup F_{2}
.

Goal
: To produce the set of all distinctions,
D=\{f\in F_{1}\cup F_{2}\mid f\text{ is a distinction}\}
.

Metrics
:

•

Accuracy: For a model-generated set of distinctions
D_{model}
, accuracy is 1 if
D_{model}=D_{gt}
(exact set match with the ground truth set), and 0 otherwise. Overall accuracy is the average over all test instances.

•

Reasoning Token: The average reasoning token cost for the model to generate a response for a single instance.

4.2.
Task 2: Analyzing Argumentative Roles of A Distinction

A factor’s effectiveness depends on how it supports the legal concerns in the hierarchy. This support can be direct and strong, or it can be weak and potentially neutralized by countervailing evidence. This leads to the concepts of “effective support” and “blocking.”

Definition 6 (Path)
: A path
\pi(f,P)
from a factor
f
to an ancestor concern
P
is a sequence of edges in the hierarchy. The path is strong if all edges in it are strong. If any edge is weak, the entire path is weak.

Definition 7 (Blocking)
: In a case
C_{i}
, the support from a factor
(f\in F_{i})
for a concern
P
is blocked if and only if:

(1)

The path
\pi(f,P)
is weak.

(2)

There exists an opposing factor
f_{\text{con}}\in F_{i}
(where
s(f_{\text{con}})\neq s(f)
) that has a strong path to
P
.

Definition 8 (Effective Support)
: A factor
f
provides effective support for a concern
P
if its path to
P
is strong, or if its path is weak and not blocked.

This task assesses a model’s ability to trace dependencies in a hierarchy and apply a rule-based system for weighing conflicting evidence, which simulates the real-world case-based legal reasoning.

Once a distinction is identified, its argumentative power can be modulated. An opponent may downplay it by pointing to other facts that serve the same argumentative purpose. Conversely, one can emphasize it by showing it creates a crucial argumentative gap.

Let
D
be a distinction. Assume
D
is a pro-plaintiff factor present in the precedent
C_{2}
but not the current case
C_{1}
. Let
P
be an ancestor concern of
D
.

Definition 9 (Downplaying a Distinction)
: The distinction
D
can be downplayed with respect to
P
if, in the current case
C_{1}
, there exists an alternative pro-plaintiff factor
f_{\text{alt}}\in F_{1}

(f_{\text{alt}}\neq D)
that provides effective support for
P
.

Definition 10 (Emphasizing a Distinction)
: The distinction
D
can be emphasized with respect to
P
if the current case
C_{1}
lacks any pro-plaintiff factor that provides effective support for
P
.

This task requires reasoning about hypotheticals and alternative arguments, a sophisticated cognitive skill.

Input
: A current case
C_{1}
, a precedent
C_{2},
the hierarchy
G
, and a specific distinction
D
to analyze.

Goal
: To produce a boolean pair,
(\text{can\_be\_emphasized},\text{can\_be\_downplayed})
, indicating if
D
can be emphasized and if it can be downplayed with respect to any of its ancestor concerns, respectively.

Metrics
:

•

Accuracy: An instance is correct only if both boolean values match the ground truth pair.

•

Reasoning Token: The average reasoning token cost for the model to generate a response for a single instance.

4.3.
Task 3: Identify Significant Distinctions

The final step synthesizes the previous analyses to determine all significant distinctions.

Definition 11 (Significant Distinction)
: A distinction
D
is significant if and only if it can be emphasized and it cannot be downplayed.

A significant distinction represents a fundamental difference between the cases that is not easily explained away. It points to a core weakness in the analogical argument and provides a strong basis for arguing for a different outcome.

Input
: A current case
C_{1}=(F_{1},o_{1})
, a precedent
C_{2}=(F_{2},o_{2})
, and the hierarchy
G
.

Goal
: To produce the set of all significant distinctions,
D^{sig}=\{f\in F_{1}\cup F_{2}\mid f\text{ is a significant distinction}\}
.

Metrics
:

•

Accuracy: For a LLM-generated set
D_{model}^{sig}
, accuracy is 1 if
D_{model}^{sig}=D_{gt}^{sig}
(exact set match with the ground truth set), and 0 otherwise.

•

Reasoning Token: The average reasoning token cost for the model to generate a response for a single instance.

5.
Experimental Setup

5.1.
Evaluation Pipeline

Figure 3.
The evaluation pipeline, which consists of scenario generation, ground truth creation, LLM inference, and evaluation.

To evaluate LLMs against our framework, we implemented an evaluation pipeline that operationalizes the three tasks described in Section
4
. This pipeline as shown in Figure
3
allows us to test models in a controlled and reproducible manner.

•

Scenario Generation: We developed a scenario generator that creates pairs of a current case and a precedent in factor-based representation by sampling factors from the CATO framework. The generator is designed to produce scenarios that are tailored to test each of the three tasks, creating cases with specific instances of blocking, opportunities for downplaying/emphasizing, etc.

•

Ground Truth Creation: For each generated scenario, a deterministic solver applies the formal rules defined in Section
4
to produce a ground-truth analysis for each task.

•

LLM Inference: The scenarios are then presented in a structured format to LLMs, which are prompted to perform each of the three reasoning tasks. The detailed prompt templates used for each task are provided in Appendix
C
.

•

Evaluation: The LLM-generated responses are compared against the ground truth to calculate accuracy for each task. This allows us to measure performance at a granular level.

5.2.
Case Complexity

Our experimental setup employed calibrated parameters across different model families to ensure fair comparison while optimizing for each model’s capabilities. The evaluation framework used 253 test instances on each task, where each case pair consisted of a current case with 4 factors and a precedent case with 4 factors, representing moderate complexity scenarios. The legal knowledge hierarchy was represented using the CATO framework in Mermaid diagram format (see Figure
7
), providing the relationships between factors, legal concerns, and legal issues.

6.
Empirical Results

Figure 4.
Model performance across Tasks 1–3. The left panel illustrates accuracy, showing a decline as tasks become more complex. The right panel displays the average number of thinking tokens used, which increases with task difficulty.

Our experimental evaluation, with results summarized in Figure
4
and Table
1
, reveals findings on the capabilities and limitations of current reasoning LLMs in hierarchical legal reasoning tasks.

6.1.
Performance Degradation Across Task Complexity

We observe systematic decline in model accuracy as task complexity increases. All models achieve perfect performance (100% accuracy) on Task 1, which requires identifying surface-level distinctions between cases. This suggests that the LLMs evaluated in this study excel at simple pattern recognition and basic comparative analysis when the reasoning requirements are relatively straightforward.

Performance degrades when models must engage in hierarchical reasoning about argumentative roles (Task 2), with accuracy ranging from 64.82% (gemini-flash) to 92.09% (gpt-5). The performance collapse occurs in Task 3, where models must synthesize all previous analyses to identify all significant distinctions. Accuracy of reasoning LLMsplummets to a range of 11.46% (gemini-flash) to 33.99% (qwen3-thinking), with most models achieving less than 25% accuracy. This suggests that the integration of multiple reasoning steps represents a challenge for current reasoning LLMs.

6.2.
The Importance of Intermediate Reasoning

The comparison between thinking and non-thinking models reveals the performance gain from the intermediate reasoning process. The qwen3-thinking model achieves 78.66% accuracy on Task 2 compared to 30.04% for its non-thinking counterpart, representing a 2.6-fold improvement. The advantage of thinking models becomes even more pronounced in Task 3, where the non-thinking model fails completely (0.00% accuracy) while the thinking model maintains some capability (33.99%).

This difference demonstrates that reasoning capabilities enabled by the reinforcement-learning-based post-training strategy
(
Guo et al. 2025
)
are beneficial for navigating the hierarchical structure of legal knowledge. However, the diminishing returns observed in Task 3 indicate that current post-training methods on reasoning models have limitations in handling the complex multi-step legal analysis.

6.3.
Computational Efficiency and Reasoning Quality

All models exhibit substantial increases in token usage as task complexity increases, with gpt-5 showing a 14.4-fold increase from Task 1 (487 tokens) to Task 3 (7,025 tokens). However, the correlation between computational effort and performance is not straightforward. qwen3-thinking uses the most tokens across all tasks (2,011 tokens for Task 1, 9,596 for Task 2, and 15,678 for Task 3) but does not achieve the highest accuracy in Task 2. In contrast, gpt-5 achieves the highest accuracy on Task 2 (92.09%) while using significantly fewer tokens (3,189) than qwen3-thinking (9,596).

The performance patterns reveal distinct characteristics of different model families. Gpt-5 demonstrates the most efficient reasoning in this study, achieving high accuracy with relatively moderate token usage. Gemini-pro shows interesting efficiency characteristics, using less tokens than gemini-flash on Task 1 but achieving higher accuracy with more tokens on more complex tasks (Task 2 and Task 3). Qwen3-thinking exhibits a different pattern, consistently using the most tokens across all tasks and achieving moderate performance in Task 2 and the best performance in Task 3.

7.
Case Study

To provide a qualitative understanding of the models’ reasoning processes, we analyze excerpts from the thinking traces of qwen3-thinking and gpt-oss-120b on one instance of Task 3. This analysis reveals stark differences in reasoning styles and efficiency.

7.1.
qwen3-thinking: A Verbose and Repetitive Process

The reasoning trace for qwen3-thinking is characterized by its verbosity and a tendency to repeatedly re-state definitions and the primary goal. The trace begins with a lengthy preamble where the model outlines the task and defines the core concepts multiple times before processing the input.

Excerpt 1: Repetitive Goal Setting

First, I need to identify all significant distinctions between the current case (C1) and the precedent case (C2). […] The key definitions:

•

A distinction is an unshared factor between C1 and C2. There are two types:

(1)

A factor favoring C2’s winner (defendant) is present in C2 but absent in C1.

(2)

A factor favoring C2’s loser (plaintiff) is present in C1 but absent in C2.

[…] So we have four distinctions: F11(d), F27(d), F15(p), F18(p). Now, for each of these, I need to check if it is significant.
A significant distinction: can be emphasized and cannot be downplayed. […] Let me read carefully.
“A distinction can be emphasized if…” “A distinction can be downplayed if…” […]

When analyzing specific distinctions, the model proceeds with a step-by-step application of the rules. However, this process is again marked by verbosity. The excerpt below shows the model correctly concluding that distinction F27(d) is not significant because it can be downplayed, but the reasoning is exhaustive.

Excerpt 2: Exhaustive Analysis of a Single Distinction

[…] Now, for downplay:
a distinction can be downplayed if there is a concern/issue where F27 has support for defendant and C1 has alternative effective support for defendant.
For C102: F27 has support for defendant, and C1 also has support for defendant (from F1 via C122), so for C102, C1 has alternative support for defendant. […] So for C102, downplay is possible. […] Therefore, for F27(d), it can be emphasized […] but it can be downplayed […], so not significant. […]

This trace illustrates an inefficient reasoning style while ultimately capable of reaching the correct conclusions for intermediate steps. The model’s tendency to “think out loud” about every detail, including redundant definitions, contributes to its high token consumption.

7.2.
gpt-oss-120b: A More Concise, but Flawed, Approach

In contrast, the reasoning trace for gpt-oss-120b is more concise. The model quickly identifies the distinctions and proceeds to evaluate them without the extensive preamble observed in qwen3-thinking.

Excerpt 1: Direct Identification of Distinctions

[…] C2 winner is defendant. So "favoring C2’s winner" = factors that favor defendant (d) present in C2 but not in C1. […] distinctions: F11(d), F27(d) (type 1). Next plaintiff factors present in C1 but not in C2. […] distinct plaintiff factors: F15(p), F18(p). Those are type 2 distinctions. Thus total distinctions: F11(d), F27(d), F15(p), F18(p). […]

However, the model’s haste appears to cause a factual error. It fails to identify a key support path in the hierarchy for one of the concerns, leading it to an incorrect conclusion.

Excerpt 2: Factual Error due to Missed Path

[…] Distinction D: F18(p) – effective support for I112 and I110.
Check C2 effective support for I112? C2 factors: none linking to I112. […] So C2 lacks support for I112. Also for I110 (downstream via I112). C2 lacks support.
Any alternative support for I112 or I110 from C2? No. So no downplay. Thus F18(p) seems emphasized […] and not downplayed. So it’s significant. […]

The model missed that in C2, factor F22(p) provides strong support for I110 via concern C111. This oversight led it to incorrectly identify F18(p) as significant. This comparison reveals a trade-off: qwen3-thinking’s verbose process, while inefficient, was more methodical and led to a more accurate application of the rules. In contrast, gpt-oss-120b’s faster, more direct approach was brittle and resulted in reasoning failures. This suggests that for complex, rule-based reasoning, a more deliberative process may be necessary to ensure accuracy.

8.
Discussion

Figure 5.
Token usage patterns reveal inefficient reasoning strategies. The figure shows the difference in thinking tokens between incorrect and correct responses across Tasks 2–3, highlighting how models often expend more computational effort on answers they ultimately get wrong.

Our findings reveal insights on the relationship between computational effort and reasoning quality in current reasoning LLMs.

One of the findings is the consistent pattern where models expend more computational resources on incorrect responses than correct ones. As shown in Figure
5
and detailed in Table
2
, this phenomenon is observed across multiple models and tasks. For example, gpt-5 used 4,456 tokens on average for incorrect Task 2 responses compared to 3,081 for correct ones, which was a 45% increase in computational effort for worse outcomes.

This pattern suggests that when models encounter difficult problems, they may fall into inefficient reasoning loops or engage in extensive but unfocused analysis. Rather than converging on the correct solution, they appear to “overthink” the problem, exploring multiple reasoning paths without successfully identifying the most effective approach.

The consistency of this pattern across different models suggests that it may represent a limitation in current LLM reasoning mechanisms and the corresponding post-training approaches. The models appear to lack effective strategies for recognizing when they are on the wrong track and redirecting their reasoning efforts accordingly.

A second important finding is the lack of strong correlation between performance and reasoning effort across models. Qwen3-thinking exemplifies this disconnect between performance and reasoning effort, using 9,596 tokens on average for Task 2 while achieving only 78.66% accuracy, compared to gpt-5’s 3,189 tokens and 92.09% accuracy. This represents a 3.0-fold increase in computational effort for worse performance.

Gpt-5 demonstrates the most efficient reasoning in this study, achieving high accuracy with moderate token usage. Gemini-pro shows an interesting pattern where it uses less tokens than gemini-flash on Task 1, it achieves higher accuracy with more tokens on Task 2 and Task 3.

This finding challenges the assumption that more reasoning efforts leads to better outcomes. Instead, it suggests that the quality of reasoning is more important than their quantity. Models that can identify and follow effective reasoning paths achieve better results with less computational overhead than those that engage in extensive but unfocused analysis. Simply increasing the reasoning length may not improve their performance on reasoning tasks.

9.
Conclusion

This work presents a systematic investigation into the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in hierarchical legal reasoning, specifically focusing on the task of identifying significant distinctions between legal cases.

Our primary contribution is the development of a formal, three-stage framework that decomposes the process of identifying significant distinctions into increasingly reasoning tasks. The formal representation in our framework exposes a core limitation in LLMs’ hierarchical reasoning. This same limitation may exist but be obfuscated when LLMs operate on contextual narrative legal work, especially in areas well-represented in their pre-training data or accessible via retrieval-augmented generation. In such cases, models might appear to reason effectively by retrieving and adapting existing solutions, masking the underlying deficit in hierarchical problem-solving.

This decomposition reveals that while LLMs excel at surface-level pattern recognition (Task 1: 100% accuracy across all models), their performance degrades when required to engage in hierarchical reasoning about argumentative roles (Task 2: 64.82%-92.09%) and collapses when synthesizing multiple reasoning steps (Task 3: 11.46%-33.99%). This performance gradient provides insights into the specific failure modes of current LLMs in legal reasoning.

As demonstrated by the comparison between thinking and non-thinking models, the introduction of reasoning capabilities improves the accuracy. The qwen3-thinking model achieved 78.66% accuracy on Task 2 compared to 30.04% for its non-thinking counterpart, and maintained 33.99% accuracy on Task 3 where the non-thinking model failed. This finding demonstrates that the reasoning capabilities enabled by the reinforcement-learning-based post-training improves the accuracy on hierarchical reasoning tasks.

Our analysis reveals a disconnect between the quantity and quality of reasoning in current LLMs. Models consistently expend more computational resources on incorrect responses than correct ones, with gpt-5 using 4,456 tokens on average for incorrect Task 2 responses compared to 3,081 for correct ones. This pattern suggests that models may fall into inefficient reasoning loops when encountering difficult problems. The relationship between reasoning effort and performance varies across models. Qwen3-thinking uses 3.0 times more tokens than gpt-5 while achieving lower accuracy on Task 2. These findings suggest that the next frontier in LLM development lies not only in longer reasoning chains, but also in elevating the caliber of training data and refining the models’ reasoning abilities in various domains.

The systematic failure of LLMs on integrated reasoning tasks (Task 3) has implications for the deployment of AI systems in legal contexts. The ability to identify significant distinctions is important to legal practice, and the current inability of models to perform this task reliably raises concerns about their readiness for real-world legal applications. Our framework provides a methodology for identifying and addressing these limitations before deployment. The hierarchical nature of legal reasoning, as captured by our CATO-based framework, reveals that effective legal AI must be capable of multi-step, multi-level abstraction and synthesis. The current generation of LLMs, while impressive in their surface-level capabilities, lacks the sophisticated reasoning mechanisms necessary for the nuanced analysis required in legal practice.

10.
Limitations and Future Work

Our evaluation is currently limited to the domain of trade secret law within the U.S. legal system, as represented by the CATO framework. This focus, while providing a controlled environment for systematic evaluation, may not capture the full complexity and diversity of legal reasoning across different jurisdictions and legal domains. The hierarchical structures, reasoning patterns, and argumentative strategies may vary across different areas of law.

The systematic failures we observe in integrated reasoning tasks (Task 3) suggest the need for innovative approaches to LLM post-training. Current reasoning LLMs are typically trained using reinforcement learning on mathematical and logical reasoning tasks, which may not adequately prepare them for the domain-specific reasoning patterns required in legal analysis. The domain shift from mathematical and logical reasoning to legal analysis may impact models’ performance in this study. Developing specialized training datasets and post-training methodologies designed for legal reasoning, with careful attention to the hierarchical and multi-step nature of legal argumentation, might improve their reasoning capabilities in legal context.

Our current framework provides a solid foundation, but several enhancements could improve its utility and scope. Future work should develop adaptive difficulty mechanisms that adjust task complexity based on model performance, providing more nuanced insights into capability boundaries. Incorporating more human legal experts in the evaluation loop would provide insights into the practical significance of model performance in real-world legal contexts.

Appendix B
An Illustrative Example of the Decomposed Framework

To illustrate our three-task framework, we use an example from U.S. trade secret law to walk through each task. For example, we can consider the following cases:

•

Current Case (C1)
: Company A (plaintiff) is suing a former employee (defendant) for trade secret theft, but they lacked basic security measures.

•

Precedent Case (C2)
: Company B (plaintiff) won a trade secret lawsuit against a former employee (defendant) because they took extensive security measures (locked facilities, employee confidentiality agreements, restricted access). However, the plaintiff also waived their employees’ confidentiality agreement.

Factor Representation

The natural language narratives can be translated into a set of pre-defined factors. A (d) marks a factor favoring the defendant, while (p) favors the plaintiff. For instance, “lacked basic security measures” corresponds to F19_No-Security-Measures(d), which hurts the plaintiff’s claim. The factor representations for our example cases are as follows:

•

Current Case (C1) Factors
: F19_No-Security-Measures(d)

•

Precedent Case (C2) Factors
: F6_Security-Measures(p), F23_Waiver-of-Confidentiality(d)

•

Precedent (C2) Winner
: Plaintiff

B.1.
Task 1: Identify Distinctions

With the cases represented as factors, the first task is to identify all unshared factors that could serve as distinctions.

Analysis of Distinctions

A distinction is an unshared factor that weakens the analogy between the precedent and the current case.

•

Pro-Plaintiff factor in C2, absent in C1
: F6_Security-Measures(p). This is a distinction because the precedent winner (plaintiff) was strengthened by a factor that the current plaintiff lacks. The defendant in C1 can argue that the precedent is not a good analogy because the plaintiff’s victory in C2 relied on F6, which is absent here.

•

Pro-Defendant factor in C1, absent in C2
: F19_No-Security-Measures(d). This is a distinction because the current case contains a weakness for the plaintiff (a strength for the defendant) that was not present in the precedent.

Conclusion for Task 1
: The identified distinctions are F6(p) and F19(d).

B.2.
Tasks 2 & 3: Analyze and Identify Significant Distinctions

The next steps involve using a legal knowledge hierarchy to determine which distinctions are significant.

The Factor Hierarchy

Factors provide evidence for more abstract legal concerns, which in turn inform high-level legal issues. In our example, the factors relate to the concern C102_Efforts-To-Maintain-Secrecy, which supports the legal issue I101_Trade-Secret. Figure
6
illustrates this structure.

Figure 6.
An example factor hierarchy for trade secret law, connecting base-level factors to the concern of Efforts-To-Maintain-Secrecy and the legal issue of Trade-Secret. Solid lines indicate strong support and dashed lines indicate weak support; this support can be for or against a concern, depending on which party the factor favors.

Effective Support and Blocking

A key step is determining if a factor’s support is “effective.” Weak evidence can be blocked by strong opposing evidence. For example, F6_Security-Measures(p) provides strong support for C102, while F23_Waver-of-Confidentiality(d) provides only weak support. In C2, the strong pro-plaintiff support from F6(p) blocks the weak pro-defendant support from F23(d). Therefore, only F6(p) provides effective support for the concern C102 in the precedent case.

Emphasis, Downplay, and Significance

The significance of a distinction depends on whether it can be emphasized or downplayed. A distinction is significant if it can be emphasized and cannot be downplayed.

•

Analysis of F6(p)
: It can be
emphasized
because it provides effective support for concern C102 in C2, and the current case C1 lacks any pro-plaintiff factor to support this concern. It
cannot be downplayed
because C1 has no alternative pro-plaintiff factor to make the same point.

•

Analysis of F19(d)
: It can be
emphasized
because it provides effective support for C102 in C1, and the precedent case C2 lacks any pro-defendant factor with effective support for this concern (as F23(d) was blocked). It
cannot be downplayed
because C2 has no alternative pro-defendant factor.

Legal Argument: Emphasizing a Distinction

Defendant’s Counsel in C1 argues:
“Your Honor, opposing counsel’s reliance on the precedent C2 is misplaced. The court in C2 found for the plaintiff largely because they had implemented extensive security measures (F6), which strongly supported the concern of efforts to maintain secrecy (C102). Our current plaintiff, Company A, has no such factor to its name. This is not a minor difference; it is a critical gap in their argument. The very basis for the precedent’s ruling is absent here, making the distinction significant.”

Legal Argument: Failed Attempt to Downplay

Plaintiff’s Counsel in C1 would need to counter-argue:
To downplay this distinction, the plaintiff would need to show that the underlying concern is still addressed. For instance: “While it is true we did not have the same extensive security measures (F6) as the plaintiff in the precedent, the concern for efforts to maintain secrecy (C102) is met by an alternative factor in our case.” However, since Company A has no such alternative factor, this downplaying argument fails, leaving the distinction successfully emphasized by the defendant.
