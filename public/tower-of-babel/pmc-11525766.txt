Angiographically occult spinal dural arteriovenous fistula diagnosed by exploratory surgery with intraoperative ultrasound: illustrative case
Authors: Kelly Jiang, Carly Weber-Levine, Max J Kerensky, A Daniel Davidar, Amir Manbachi, Carlos A Pardo, Philippe Gailloud, Nicholas Theodore, Christopher M Jackson, Daniel Lubelski, Elias S Sotirchos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDA 78-year-old male presented with progressive myelopathic symptoms. The clinical course and imaging findings raised a high suspicion for venous hypertensive myelopathy due to a spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (SDAVF).OBSERVATIONSMagnetic resonance angiography and four complete spinal angiograms did not reveal the presence of an SDAVF. Despite multiple negative angiograms, intraoperative ultrasound revealed abnormal cord edema and arterialized pulsatile vessels, confirming the presence of an SDAVF. The fistula was found and cauterized, which resulted in a decrease in the caliber of the dilated veins and an observed reduction of spinal cord stiffness posttreatment. The patient exhibited gradual improvement in neurological function. Retrospective analysis of the multiple complete spinal angiograms failed to reveal an anomaly at the treated level or any other level.LESSONSThis case underscores the diagnostic utility of intraoperative Doppler ultrasound and the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for SDAVF in cases with consistent clinical characteristics and a lack of alternative diagnoses, even with negative spinal angiography. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE24438

Low-flow spinal dural arteriovenous fistulas (SDAVFs) are characterized by an acquired arteriovenous shunt from a radiculomeningeal artery to a radiculomedullary vein.1 SDAVFs most commonly affect older men and generally present with slowly progressive myelopathy due to spinal venous hypertension. The thoracolumbar region is most frequently affected, but SDAVFs can occur at any spinal level, and the location of the SDAVF may not coincide with the topography of the spinal cord lesion.1, 2 SDAVFs are considered rare, but magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features are nonspecific, often resulting in misdiagnosis.2, 3 Spinal digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for the diagnosis of SDAVFs.

Herein, we present a case of venous congestive myelopathy due to an SDAVF that went undetected despite four spinal DSA studies and was diagnosed intraoperatively with the assistance of ultrasound.

Illustrative Case

A 78-year-old male with atrial fibrillation on anticoagulation, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia presented for evaluation of progressive myelopathy. Symptom onset had occurred 4 years prior, initially with the insidious onset of numbness and paresthesias involving the distal lower extremities. Over the subsequent years, the sensory symptoms ascended, and he developed progressive lower-extremity weakness and sphincter dysfunction. He denied any periods of significant improvement or rapid decline, with the exception of transient worsening that would consistently occur after engaging in physical therapy. Neurological examination demonstrated pyramidal pattern weakness in the lower extremities, increased tone and hyperreflexia in the lower extremities, bilateral extensor plantar responses, and a lower thoracic sensory level to pinprick. He was able to take only a few steps with bilateral support and was wheelchair dependent.

Prior to his evaluation at our center, MRI had revealed a thoracic spinal cord T2-hyperintense lesion spanning from the midthoracic to the lower thoracic cord, with a short segment area of postgadolinium enhancement (Fig. 1A–D). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis did not demonstrate pleocytosis or elevated protein. Spinal angiography at 1 year after symptom onset had been reported as unremarkable. Aquaporin-4 and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein IgG testing was negative. He had received several courses of high-dose corticosteroids, as well as plasma exchange and intravenous immune globulin, for empiric treatment of a possible inflammatory myelopathy, but he denied any symptomatic improvement. Given the clinicoradiological characteristics, there remained a high suspicion for venous congestive myelopathy due to an underlying SDAVF, despite the previously negative spinal angiogram, based on prior experience with false-negative results.4 Thus, complete spinal angiography was performed twice, 3 weeks apart, investigating all the intersegmental arteries of aortic origin (T2–L4), the subclavian artery and its branches, the carotid arteries, and the abdominal/pelvic vasculature, including selective injections of the lateral sacral arteries, without evidence of a spinal vascular malformation (Fig. 1E and F).5 Spinal MRI with contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) demonstrated stable findings, without evidence of spinal vascular malformation. Postgadolinium enhancement of the proximal nerve roots of the cauda equina led to a broadening of the differential diagnosis to include an inflammatory myeloradiculitis, but this finding has also been reported with SDAVF.6 Whole-body fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography scanning was performed to assess for evidence of malignancy or a systemic inflammatory process (i.e., sarcoidosis) and was unremarkable. Repeat CSF analysis was unrevealing (white blood cell count 1/mm3, protein 45 mg/dL, glucose 71 mg/dL, negative cytology, and microbiological workup).

In this context, concern persisted for SDAVF, and a new complete spinal angiogram (including arteriography and retrograde venography) was pursued. There was again no direct evidence of a spinal vascular malformation (Fig. 1G). The vertebral venogram failed to opacify the thoracic internal vertebral venous system, while normal opacification of the lumbar vertebral plexus without passage through the thoracic level was observed, suggesting flow impairment, possibly from partial thrombosis of the thoracic internal vertebral venous system. Given the continued neurological deterioration of the patient and the presence of disc herniations adjacent to the thoracic spinal cord lesion, consideration was given to the possibility of compression leading to impaired venous outflow, and he was referred for decompression with expansile duraplasty.7

A T7–11 laminectomy was performed with intraoperative ultrasound, including shear wave elastography (SWE). Ultrasound revealed cord edema and arterialized pulsatile vessels at the proximal end of the exposure (Video 1). Visualization of reversed blood flow direction by Doppler ultrasound confirmed the presence of an arteriovenous shunt, and a midline durotomy was then performed. A connection between the engorged veins and a fistulous connection traveling with the right T8 nerve root was identified at the T8 foramen (Fig. 2). A temporary clip was placed for 10 minutes without changes in somatosensory evoked potentials. Repeat ultrasound was performed, which no longer showed arterial pulsations dorsal to the cord (Video 2). The temporary clip was removed, and the fistulous point was cauterized and divided adjacent to the dura. On SWE, the spinal cord’s baseline shear wave velocity of 1.92 m/sec decreased to 1.68 m/sec following cauterization of the fistula (Fig. 3). At the 4-month follow-up, the patient reported some gradual improvement in symptoms but remained wheelchair dependent. MRI of the thoracic spine demonstrated decreased cord caliber and extent of the cord signal abnormality compared to prior imaging. Retrospective analysis of the 4 negative spinal angiograms revealed no anomalies, including at the level of the SDAVF (right T8), on the opposite side (left T8), and at immediately adjacent levels (T6–10), despite each injection being of good angiographic quality.

Discussion

Observations

This case demonstrates that, while extremely rare, true-negative spinal angiography (as opposed to inadequate or incomplete angiography) remains possible.4 One or more negative spinal angiograms may thus not adequately rule out the presence of SDAVF in patients with clinicoradiological characteristics that are highly suggestive. Spinal DSA is the gold standard for evaluating spinal cord arteriovenous malformations, including SDAVFs. Nondiagnostic spinal DSA is typically due to correctable, operator-dependent factors, including nonrecognition of angiographically documented lesions, lack of investigation of the level of interest, and technically inadequate injections.4 For these reasons, angiography should only be considered negative when it is performed by experienced angiographers, second opinion readings are obtained, and the quality is carefully assessed.5

This patient represents a unique case in which multiple high-quality angiograms visualizing each level were genuinely negative, even upon retrospective review. There are several potential explanations for this, including a possible aberrant feeding vessel (e.g., arising directly from the aorta or one of its visceral branches) that was only revealed upon intraoperative exploration. Though severe atherosclerosis or an aortic aneurysm was not present and all intersegmental branches were angiographically documented in our patient, these pathologies can occasionally prevent adequate visualization.8

This case also illustrates the utility of intraoperative ultrasound in diagnosing and evaluating appropriate treatment of the SDAVF.9 Notably, despite the lack of flow voids on MRI (a common circumstance with low-flow shunts) or the demonstration of dilated veins on contrast-enhanced spinal MRA, intraoperative ultrasound was able to identify the shunt.3 The lesion was identified as a spinal arteriovenous fistula through the visualization of reversed blood flow direction from the radicular dural sleeve to the perimedullary plexus on Doppler ultrasound. Micro-Doppler has been used to visualize and confirm intraoperatively the obliteration of SDAVFs previously identified on angiography.10 In addition to Doppler, intraoperative ultrasound can include SWE, which uses the velocity of shear waves to quantify mechanical properties of tissue, such as stiffness.11 Though uncommonly used in neurosurgery, SWE can detect reduced spinal cord tension or stiffness after decompressive surgery for pathologies such as spinal cord tumors, tethered cord syndrome, and spinal stenosis.12, 13 In this case, the shear wave velocity of the spinal cord decreased following SDAVF cauterization, consistent with a decrease in spinal cord stiffness.

Lessons

SDAVFs can present a diagnostic challenge due to their nonspecific clinical manifestations and the limitations of conventional imaging modalities. Intraoperative Doppler ultrasound can serve as an additional diagnostic tool. This case underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in cases with consistent clinicoradiological characteristics and lack of alternative diagnosis, even with multiple negative complete angiographic studies.
