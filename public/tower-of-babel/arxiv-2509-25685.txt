Hierarchical Diffusion Motion Planning with Task-Conditioned Uncertainty-Aware Priors
Amelie Minji Kim, Anqi Wu, Ye Zhao

Abstract
We propose a novel hierarchical diffusion planner that embeds task and motion structure directly in the noise model. Unlike standard diffusion-based planners that use zero-mean, isotropic Gaussian noise, we employ a family of task-conditioned structured Gaussians whose means and covariances are derived from Gaussian Process Motion Planning (GPMP): sparse, task-centric key states or their associated timings (or both) are treated as noisy observations to produce a prior instance. We first generalize the standard diffusion process to biased, non-isotropic corruption with closed-form forward and posterior expressions. Building on this, our hierarchy separates prior instantiation from trajectory denoising: the upper level instantiates a task-conditioned structured Gaussian (mean and covariance), and the lower level denoises the full trajectory under that fixed prior. Experiments on Maze2D goal-reaching and KUKA block stacking show improved success rates, smoother trajectories, and stronger task alignment compared to isotropic baselines. Ablation studies indicate that explicitly structuring the corruption process offers benefits beyond simply conditioning the neural network. Overall, our method concentrates probability mass of prior near feasible, smooth, and semantically meaningful trajectories while maintaining tractability. Our project page is available at https://hta-diffusion.github.io .

In this work, we propose to encode such structure in the form of a task-aware noise model. This design enables our diffusion planner to respect smoothness and constraint satisfaction by construction. We realize this idea with a two-level hierarchical diffusion framework. The upper level uses a denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM)
[
9
]
with isotropic Gaussian noise to produce sparse, task-centric key states.
For example, in navigation tasks, key states can be chosen as evenly spaced waypoints between the start and goal. In manipulation tasks, they naturally align with task phases (e.g., approach, grasp, lift), augmented by a binary contact-flag trajectory.

Hereafter, we use key states to denote both waypoints in navigation and keyframe states in manipulation.
We treat upper-level key states as soft observations with explicit uncertainty, so these imperfect predictions guide sampling without hard constraints or rejection. This yields a non-isotropic and temporally correlated noise model whose mean and covariance encourage smooth dynamics between key states, while the key states act as anchors that prevent global oversmoothing and allow the trajectory to adapt at sharp turns or task-phase changes. Accordingly, this framework produces temporally consistent and constraint-aware plans.

In contrast, our design directly biases the generative process toward trajectories that are both dynamically consistent and task-aware, rather than only improving multi-modality.

Hierarchical diffusion has been used to separate coarse-level decisions from finer-level motion, for example: subgoal generation followed by refinement
[

[

, and diffusion–flow hybrids that pair high-level diffusion with low-level rectified flow

A common pattern feeds high-level outputs to the low level as conditioning, while low-level corruption remains isotropic. This leaves the forward process and loss unchanged, so temporal coupling, boundary consistency, and waypoint fidelity must be relearned at every step. Our hierarchy instead reparameterizes the low-level prior by conditioning a GP on upper-level key states. This yields a trajectory posterior whose mean interpolates the key states and whose covariance couples time steps, so reverse updates follow a Mahalanobis geometry that narrows the search to trajectories consistent between key states and treats imperfect key states probabilistically. This improves feasibility without post hoc constraints or rejection.

We evaluate on Maze2D goal reaching and KUKA block stacking from the Diffuser benchmarks
[
2
]
. We compare (i) a single layer diffuser with isotropic noise, (ii) a hierarchical diffuser that only conditions on upper-level outputs while keeping isotropic noise, and (iii) our full hierarchical model that constructs the structured prior from upper-level key states and denoises under that prior. Across tasks, our model (iii) outperforms the conditioning-only baseline (ii), indicating that conditioning alone is insufficient. Embedding structure in the generative noise yields consistent gains in success and collision or constraint satisfaction.

Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

A two-level hierarchical diffusion planner where the upper-level DDPM infers sparse, task-centric key states and the lower level denoises under a GPMP-based motion prior conditioned on these states. Key states are treated as soft observations with explicit uncertainty, encoding smoothness, boundary and phase consistency, and handling non-stationarities without hard constraints.

•

A diffusion formulation with
task-conditioned
non-isotropic Gaussian corruption obtained by conditioning a GPMP-based prior on key states. We derive closed-form forward marginals and posteriors for variational training, yielding a Mahalanobis loss that generalizes MSE while preserving the standard DDPM pipeline.

•

A conceptual and empirical comparison to conditioning-only diffusion hierarchies. Reparameterizing the low-level prior alters the diffusion landscape and concentrates sampling on trajectories consistent with key states, improving feasibility without extra constraints or loss terms.

•

Experiments on Maze2D goal reaching and KUKA block stacking showing higher success and better constraint satisfaction than baselines with isotropic noise or conditioning alone, with ablations demonstrating the benefits of the structured prior.

II
Preliminary

II-A

DDPM for motion planning

Let a robot trajectory be
\tau=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{H}]\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d}
, where
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the robot state at time step
t
and
H
is the planning horizon. We denote by
\tau^{i}
the trajectory after
i
forward diffusion steps.

Diffusion models learn to denoise trajectories that are gradually corrupted by Gaussian noise. Under the standard DDPM corruption
[
9
]
, the forward process is

\tau^{i}=\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\,\tau^{i-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_{i}}\,\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I),

(1)

where
\alpha_{i}:=1-\beta_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,N
,
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset(0,1)
is a scalar variance schedule, and
N
is the total number of diffusion steps. Let
\bar{\alpha}_{i}:=\prod_{k=1}^{i}\alpha_{k}
. Then the closed-form marginal is

\tau^{i}\mid\tau^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{i}}\,\tau^{0},\ (1-\bar{\alpha}_{i})I\big).

The one-step forward posterior is also Gaussian,

q(\tau^{i-1}\!\mid\!\tau^{i},\tau^{0})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tau^{i-1};\,\hat{\mu}_{i}(\tau^{i},\tau^{0}),\,\hat{\beta}_{i}I\big),

(2)

where
\hat{\mu}_{i}
and
\hat{\beta}_{i}
are the posterior mean and variance coefficient at the
i^{\rm th}
diffusion step (closed forms omitted for simplicity).

A denoising network
\mu_{\theta}(\tau^{i},i,\texttt{cond})
is trained to predict the posterior mean with the objective

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\big\|\hat{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{\theta}(\tau^{i},i,\texttt{cond})\big\|_{2}^{2}\right],

(3)

where
cond
may include initial and goal states, maps, waypoints, or other task variables. At test time, sampling runs the reverse Markov chain starting from
\tau^{N}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
.

In Sec.
III
, we generalize (
1
) by biasing the mean and allowing a
non-isotropic
covariance in the corruption process. The standard DDPM is recovered as
\mu=0
and
\mathcal{K}=I
.

II-B

Gaussian Process Motion Planning

Gaussian Process Motion Planning (GPMP) models a continuous time trajectory as a Gaussian process (GP), i.e.,

\tau(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(\tilde{\mu}(t),\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(t,t^{\prime})),

(4)

whose mean and kernel are induced by a linear time varying (LTV) dynamics prior
[
10
]
. Consider the stochastic LTV model
\dot{x}(t)=A(t)x(t)+u(t)+w(t)
where
w(t)
is zero mean white noise with spectral density
Q_{C}\succeq 0
. Let the initial mean and covariance at time
t_{0}
be
\mu_{0}
and
\mathcal{K}_{0}
, and let
\Phi(t,s)
denote the state transition matrix of
A(\cdot)
. Then the GP has mean
\tilde{\mu}(t)=\Phi(t,t_{0})\mu_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\Phi(t,s)u(s)\,ds
and kernel
\tilde{\mathcal{K}}(t,t^{\prime})=\Phi(t,t_{0})\mathcal{K}_{0}\Phi(t^{\prime},t_{0})^{\top}+\int_{t_{0}}^{\min\{t,t^{\prime}\}}\Phi(t,s)Q_{C}\Phi(t^{\prime},s)^{\top}ds
, which admit sparse and exactly computable discrete counterparts.

Following
[
1
]
, our work uses a discrete-time version of (
4
) derived from a constant velocity or constant acceleration model with step
\Delta t
. Writing the state at timestep
t
as
x_{t}
, the one-step evolution is
x_{t+1}=\Phi_{t,t+1}x_{t}+q_{t}
with
q_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q_{t,t+1})
, where
\Phi_{t,t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}I&\Delta t\,I\\
0&I\end{bmatrix}
and
Q_{t,t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}\tfrac{1}{3}\Delta t^{3}Q_{C}&\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}Q_{C}\\
\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta t^{2}Q_{C}&\Delta t\,Q_{C}\end{bmatrix}
. The same structure applies, no matter if the state includes positions only or positions and velocities; velocities can be included explicitly or marginalized to obtain an equivalent GP prior over positions with a block tridiagonal precision matrix. In our experiments, Maze2D uses positions and velocities, whereas the manipulation arm uses joint positions only.

III
Method

III-A

Diffusion with a task conditioned Gaussian and hierarchical planner overview

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dataset of robot trajectories where each clean sample
\tau\!\in\!\mathcal{D}
is paired with task information that may admit multiple feasible solutions. In our settings, this task information includes
start and goal
or a
waypoint template
in
Maze2D
, and
grasp and place timing
with key joint configurations in
KUKA manipulation
block stacking.

It is desirable for the corruption (forward) process to retain task information so that reverse diffusion starts from a task-relevant noise prior. To this end, we modify the standard DDPM to use a non-isotropic Gaussian with a task conditioned mean and covariance. Reusing the schedule notation from Sec.
II-A
, with
\alpha_{i}=1-\beta_{i}
and
\bar{\alpha}_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{i}\alpha_{k}
, we define

\tau^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big(\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}\,\tau^{i-1}+(1-\sqrt{\alpha_{i}})\,\mu,\;(1-\alpha_{i})\,\mathcal{K}\Big),

(5)

where
\mu\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{H\times d}
and
\mathcal{K}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{Hd\times Hd}
with
\mathcal{K}\succeq 0
encode task-dependent trajectory structure.
\tau^{0}
is just
\tau
.

As in DDPM, (
5
) yields closed form conditionals with respect to
\tau^{0}
:

\tau^{i}\mid\tau^{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\!\Big(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{i}}\,\tau^{0}+(1-\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{i}})\,\mu,\;(1-\bar{\alpha}_{i})\,\mathcal{K}\Big).

(6)

The one step forward posterior is also Gaussian,

q(\tau^{i-1}\!\mid\!\tau^{i},\tau^{0})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tau^{i-1};\,\tilde{\mu}_{i}(\tau^{i},\tau^{0},\mu),\,\tilde{\beta}_{i}\,\mathcal{K}\big),

(7)

with coefficients
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{i}
that directly generalize the DDPM posterior mean
\hat{\mu}_{i}
and variance coefficient
\hat{\beta}_{i}
from Sec.
II-A
(explicit derivations are given in the Appendix).
As
N\to\infty
, the terminal distribution approaches
\tau^{N}\!\sim\!\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathcal{K})
, so at test time we initialize the reverse diffusion from this task-specific Gaussian.

At test time, we aim to generate a trajectory
\tau
that is both dynamically feasible and aligned with task constraints. Consequently, the mean
\mu
and covariance
\mathcal{K}
of the non-isotropic Gaussian should encode
both
dynamics and task information. We achieve this with a two-level hierarchy. The upper level extracts task information that is available without access to the full trajectory and uses it to instantiate
(\mu,\mathcal{K})
. Concretely, it generates sparse task-centric key states with associated uncertainties that we treat as soft observations. The lower level integrates these key states with the GPMP prior (
4
) from Sec.
II-B
. Conditioning the GP prior on the soft observations yields a posterior mean and covariance that define
\mu
and
\mathcal{K}
in (
5
). During test time, reverse diffusion then proceeds under this non-isotropic and time correlated Gaussian, starting from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathcal{K})
.

The overall planner therefore has two functions: the upper level produces key states and their associated timings, and the lower level generates the full trajectory by reverse diffusion given
(\mu,\mathcal{K})
. Inputs are a task specification and an initial condition.
An overview of the framework is shown in Fig.
1
. The next subsections detail each level.

III-B

Upper level: key states and timings

The upper level identifies task-relevant
key states
and their timings,
using a lightweight diffusion module parameterized by
\phi
, combined with simple rule-based heuristics as needed.
By key states, we mean boundary conditions, waypoints, or states at contact events.

Given a full trajectory
\tau
, let
Y=[y_{1};\ldots;y_{n}]\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
be the stack of
n
key states. Define a binary selection matrix
C\in\{0,1\}^{nd\times Hd}
that extracts the key states from the full trajectory, so that
Y=C\,\tau
.
Note that the selection matrix
C
is equivalent to the timings for the key states
Y
.
The upper level generates these values, and the construction of them
depend on the task, as described below.

Maze2D.
Key states are evenly spaced temporal waypoints between the start and the goal, so
C
is fixed by design.
In this example,
the upper-level diffusion is trained to generate the waypoint set
Y
.

KUKA manipulation.

Key states are defined as the joint configuration at the grasp and release timings.
The upper-level diffusion is trained to predict the grasp and release timings by generating a binary contact-flag trajectory (1 for contact, 0 for no contact).
From this trajectory, we can obtain
C
.
The corresponding joint-state set
Y
is constructed using simple rules (e.g., block-selection heuristics, detailed in Sec.
IV
) together with inverse kinematics (IK).

The lower level is agnostic to how the high level selects key states. Even if the high level produces imprecise or partially inconsistent
(Y,C)
with the full trajectories, the lower level treats them as soft observations with uncertainty and proceeds unchanged. Through GP conditioning, this uncertainty enters as observation covariance, so imperfect inputs are down-weighted rather than enforced as hard constraints, and the reverse diffusion remains stable and feasible. For more details, please refer to the subsection below.

III-C

Lower level: trajectory generation by denoising

Given the key states
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{nd}
and the corresponding selection matrix
C\in\{0,1\}^{nd\times Hd}
, we treat
Y
as
soft observations
of the stacked trajectory
\tau=[x_{1};\ldots;x_{H}]\in\mathbb{R}^{H\times d}
with observation covariance
\mathcal{K}_{y}\succeq 0
. The unconditioned GPMP prior (
4
) induces a GP prior over
\tau
. Conditioning this GP prior to the soft observations
(Y,C,\mathcal{K}_{y})
yields a
task conditioned
posterior over the full trajectory,

\tau(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\big(\mu(t),\,\mathcal{K}(t,t^{\prime})\big),

(8)

with a closed form mean and covariance
[
10
]

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mu}+\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\,C^{\top}\!\left(C\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\,C^{\top}+\mathcal{K}_{y}\right)^{-1}\!\big(Y-C\,\tilde{\mu}\big),

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}-\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\,C^{\top}\!\left(C\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}\,C^{\top}+\mathcal{K}_{y}\right)^{-1}\!C\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}.

(10)

Small variances in
\mathcal{K}_{y}
enforce tight adherence to key states (e.g., fixed initial or goal states), while larger variances allow flexibility (e.g., soft waypoints). Intuitively,
Y
anchors the trajectory near important states and
\mathcal{K}_{y}
encodes confidence in those anchors, without imposing hard constraints.

The conditioned GP (
8
) determines the non-isotropic and time-correlated Gaussian noise used in our diffusion model; at each step, the injected noise is drawn with a mean and covariance shaped by linear time varying dynamics and the key state and timing information. In particular, the lower level initializes reverse diffusion from
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\mathcal{K})
and denoises under the general forward and posterior relations introduced in Sec.
II-A
. Recall that the one-step forward posterior for our biased and non-isotropic setting has the closed form

q(\tau^{i-1}\!\mid\!\tau^{i},\tau^{0})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tau^{i-1};\,\tilde{\mu}_{i}(\tau^{i},\tau^{0},\mu),\,\tilde{\beta}_{i}\,\mathcal{K}\big),

(11)

where
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
and
\tilde{\beta}_{i}
generalize the DDPM posterior coefficients
\hat{\mu}_{i}
and
\hat{\beta}_{i}
. For learning we approximate the reverse kernel

p_{\theta}(\tau^{i-1}\!\mid\!\tau^{i})=\mathcal{N}\!\big(\tau^{i-1};\,\mu_{\theta}(\tau^{i},i,\texttt{cond}),\,\Sigma^{i}\big),

where
cond
may include
Y
,
C
, initial states, and other context, and we take
\Sigma^{i}
to match the scaled structure
\tilde{\beta}_{i}\,\mathcal{K}
. Following
[
9
]
and Sec.
II-A
, minimizing the KL divergence between
p_{\theta}
and the true forward posterior reduces to a weighted regression toward
\tilde{\mu}_{i}
. Using the Mahalanobis norm
\|v\|^{2}_{A}:=v^{\top}A\,v
, the training objective becomes

L(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\big\|\tilde{\mu}_{i}-\mu_{\theta}(\tau^{i},i,\texttt{cond})\big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{K}^{-1}}\right],

(12)

up to a scalar schedule dependent factor. Compared to the isotropic MSE, (
12
) naturally weights errors by the inverse of the task conditioned covariance, encouraging denoising steps that stay consistent with the GP induced temporal coupling and the confidence encoded in
\mathcal{K}_{y}
.

Remark 2

Motion Planning Diffusion (MPD)
[
1
]
applies a GP as a guidance cost only during reverse sampling, while the forward corruption remains isotropic. We instead parameterize the noise with a GP: conditioning on key states sets the mean
\mu
and covariance
\mathcal{K}
that govern both the forward and posterior distributions. This yields three benefits. First, structure is present at every step of training and sampling, so denoising follows a Mahalanobis geometry that narrows the search to trajectories consistent with temporal coupling and task constraints. Second, uncertainty in key states enters as an observation covariance
\mathcal{K}_{y}
, which downweights imperfect cues without hard constraints. Third, feasibility comes from the generative model rather than from external costs, reducing sensitivity to guidance weights and improving stability over long horizons.

IV
Experiments

We adopt Diffuser
[
2
]
as the baseline and evaluate the proposed structured-noise formulation on two domains:
(i) Two-dimensional maze navigation (Maze2D) with initial and goal conditioning, and
(ii) KUKA block stacking with grasp and release events represented with contact flag trajectory for each block.
We report goal success, dynamic feasibility, and constraint satisfaction.

IV-A

Experimental Setup

IV-A
1
Comparison models

We compare four planners that share the same network backbone and differ only in how they use key states and in the structure of the diffusion noise:

•

Isotropic (without key states).
A standard DDPM with isotropic Gaussian corruption and no high-level key states (the flat Diffuser baseline).

•

Isotropic (with key states as conditioning).
A standard DDPM at the lower level that receives the high-level key states as conditioning inputs, but still uses isotropic Gaussian corruption at the low level.

•

GPMP prior (without key states).
A single-level diffuser with non-isotropic Gaussian noise shaped by the GPMP prior, but without key states. This highlights that relying only on a GP prior for dynamic feasibility can harm trajectory generation by causing oversmoothing.

•

GPMP prior (with key states).
Our method: a two-level hierarchy in which the upper level produces task-centric key states and timings, and the lower level denoises under the non-isotropic Gaussian from GPMP.

Note that the choice of noise prior applies only to the lower-level diffusion.
Whenever a hierarchy is used, the upper-level diffusion remains a standard DDPM with isotropic Gaussian noise.

IV-A
2
Neural network architecture

Both levels use a UNet architecture as in
[
2
]
. For the lower level, the network is conditioned on the key states and their timings produced by the upper level. Apart from this conditioning and the choice of noise model (isotropic versus GP-conditioned), the architecture and training schedule are identical across all models so that any performance differences are induced by the effect of the noise structure.

IV-A
3
Maze2D goal reaching

Each task specifies a start and a goal state on a fixed maze map. The upper-level diffusion produces temporal waypoints
Y
at fixed times
C
, which are treated as soft observations with associated uncertainty. These waypoints instantiate the task-conditioned GP prior, from which the lower level generates full trajectories by reverse diffusion.

IV-A
4
KUKA arm block stacking

The task is grasp-and-place with a 7-DoF KUKA arm.
The dataset provides 7-DOF joint position trajectories and a binary contact-flag trajectory (one bit per time step) for each block,
while block poses are determined deterministically from kinematics and contact.

As explained in Sec.
III
, the key states are grasp and release joint positions.
Given a ground-truth trajectory
\tau^{0}
and its contact flags,
we define the grasp configuration as the joint state at the first
0\!\to\!1
transition
and the release configuration as the joint state at the first
1\!\to\!0
transition.
The stack
Y
consists of the initial joint state together with these grasp and release joint states,
while
C
is taken to be the binary contact-flag trajectory over the horizon.
The upper-level diffusion is trained to generate
C
conditioned on
Y
.

At test time, we input the grasp and stack targets with the following heuristics:
(1) if a block is currently grasped, use it as the grasp target;
(2) choose the release target as the block with the largest vertical position, excluding any grasped block;
(3) if no block is grasped, choose the block closest to the initial end-effector position;
(4) if the chosen grasp–release pair is already stacked (
|dx|<\mathrm{tol}
,
|dy|<\mathrm{tol}
), select a different grasp block.
After the block selection, we
compute the grasp and release joint states by IK to form
Y
.
Then, the upper-level diffusion predicts the contact-flag trajectory
C
; grasp and release timings are extracted from the first
0\!\to\!1
and
1\!\to\!0
transitions, respectively.

1\!\to\!0

2\times 2

versus

\mu

without

Tables

and
II
report the success rates. Across both tasks, the best performance is achieved by combining the upper-level key-state generator with the GPMP prior. Intuitively, upper-level key states and timings supply task semantics (e.g., guidance through turns in a maze or contact phases in manipulation), while the GPMP prior encodes smoothness and soft constraint satisfaction. Together, these two components concentrate probability mass of the reverse process near dynamically feasible and task-consistent manifolds, thereby reducing the burden on the denoiser.

Interestingly, the GPMP prior
without
informative key states underperforms even the isotropic baseline. This occurs because, although the GPMP prior captures temporal correlations and promotes smoothness, it can oversmooth when applied uniformly across the entire horizon. Realistic trajectories often involve non-stationarities: sharp turns in navigation and abrupt grasp/release events in manipulation. Without explicit key states to anchor the dynamics, the GP prior may bias trajectories away from the task-critical states, leading to degraded success. Effective planning, therefore, requires a balance:
the hierarchical key-state generator anchors task-critical events,
while the prior provides
tighter variance around the key states ensuring they are roughly respected,
while maintaining smoothness and temporal consistency.
The combination of both yields robust, dynamically feasible, and task-aligned plans.

V
Discussion and Conclusion

Embedding GPMP structure
in the noise
yields temporally consistent, constraint-aware trajectories, improving success under sparse or imperfect high-level guidance.
Our framework is
task-centric
: the upper level extracts task-relevant key states/timings, and the lower-level diffusion uses the GP-conditioned
(\mu,\mathcal{K})
to bias sampling toward feasible solutions.
We validate the approach through experiments on two robotic domains.
In Maze2D, the prior concentrates probability mass near start/goal and along smooth waypoint-guided paths; in KUKA stacking, predicted contact timing shapes the kernel so denoising honors grasp/release phases.
Although we proposed a lightweight diffusion model for the upper level, the lower level is agnostic to
how
the key states are obtained—be it rule-based, model-based, or learned. It always treats
(Y,C)
as soft observations with uncertainty; GP conditioning down-weights imperfect inputs rather than enforcing them as hard constraints, leaving room to pair this framework with alternative task specifiers or high-level motion planners.

Moreover, our method does not require handcrafted reward terms or auxiliary objectives, and no advanced neural architectures are used beyond conditioning on upper-level-generated features.
Thus improvements in sample quality and task success arise from
where the structure lives
—in the
noise
—rather than from heavier networks or guidance heuristics.
This shows our method’s efficacy even with simple mechanics.
