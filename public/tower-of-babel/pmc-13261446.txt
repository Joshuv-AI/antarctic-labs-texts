Modulation of the 1D/2D Dimensionality by Ligand Exchange in a Series of Cu- and Ag-Naphthalenethiolate Coordination Polymers: Effect on Conductivity and Photoemission
Authors: Alexis Giraudon, Buqin Xu, Chloé Andrade, Léonie Autran, Anne Bonhommé, Eric Puzenat, Miguel Monge, Gilles Ledoux, Nathalie Guillou, Florent Perret, Stéphane Pailhès, Aude Demessence
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal–organic chalcogenolates (MOCs) are an expanding family of coordination polymers that have attracted increasing interest from both chemists and physicists due to their intriguing physical properties, including intense and tunable photoemission, semiconducting behavior, and strong light–matter interactions. These materials are typically composed of a soft metal center, most often d10 coinage metals, coordinated to soft chalcogenolate ligands such as thiolates or selenolates, and they frequently form one- or two-dimensional (1D/2D) networks. However, the limited number of reported MOCs and the lack of systematic studies hinder a clear understanding of structure–property relationships. In this work, we investigate the influence of ligand functionalization and structural dimensionality on the physical properties of MOCs through the synthesis and the structural determination from powder X-ray diffraction data of four new coordination polymers based on Cu­(I) and Ag­(I) with 1- and 2-naphthalenethiolate ligands (1-NT and 2-NT). The 1-NT ligand leads to 1D structures, whereas 2-NT gives rise to 2D networks. Photophysical and electrical measurements reveal that [Cu­(1-NT)] n exhibits both the highest quantum yield and the highest electrical conductivity among the series. These results highlight the combined influence of the metal center (Cu vs Ag) and structural dimensionality (1D vs 2D) in designing efficient MOC-based materials.

Introduction

Metal organic chalcogenolates (MOCs), made of thiolate-, selenoate-, or even telluroate-based ligands and d10 coinage metals, have emerged as a promising class of coordination polymers (CPs) owing to their high chemical stability, anisotropic (1D or 2D) structures, and tunable physical properties. − In particular, these materials can exhibit intense and adjustable photoemission associated with the d10 metals, along with efficient charge transport arising from their anisotropic frameworks. −

MOCs derived from a monotopic thiolate ligand with the general formula [M­(SR)] n typically adopt either 1D or 2D networks, which strongly impact their properties. The dimensionality is governed by steric effects near the thiolate function: ortho- and meta-substituted thiophenolates generally form 1D networks, whereas para-substituted analogues favor 2D frameworks. Weak intermolecular interactions can further direct the structure. For example, [M­(m-SPhCO2H)] n (M = Cu, Ag) forms a 2D network through hydrogen bonding between the carboxylic acid groups, whereas substitution of an ester group yields a 1D structure in [M­(m-SPhCO2Me)] n (M = Cu, Ag). A consistent trend is that 1D MOCs display stronger photoemission than their 2D analogues, which are typically nonemissive at room temperature. In the series of [M­(x-SPhCO2R)] n (M = Cu, Ag; x: ortho, meta, para; R = H, Me), the 1D [M­(o-SPhCO2R)] n (M = Cu, Ag; R = H, Me) and [M­(m-SPhCO2Me)] n (M = Cu, Ag) MOCs exhibit bright yellow-to-red emission, with a maximum quantum yield (QY) of 52% for [Ag­(o-SPhCO2H)] n . In contrast, 2D [M­(m-SPhCO2H)] n (M = Cu, Ag), [M­(p-SPhCO2H)] n (M = Cu, Ag), and [Ag­(p-SPhCO2Me)] n are nonemissive at room temperature. A similar behavior is observed for methoxy-substituted systems, where weak emission is detected for 1D [Ag­(o-/m-SPhOMe)] n MOCs, while 2D [Ag­(p-SPhOMe)] n shows no emission.

About the electrical conductivity, studies with lead­(II) ions reveal that 2D structures can enhance charge transport: 2D [Pb­(p-SPhOMe)2] n exhibits higher photoconductivity than the corresponding 1D MOCs [Pb­(o-/m-SPhOMe)2] n . Although Cu- and Ag-based MOCs are often isostructural, some notable differences can exist. For instance, [Cu­(SPh)] n forms a 1D network with red emission and a conductivity of 3.7 × 10–4 S·cm–1, whereas [Ag­(SPh)] n adopts a nonemissive 2D structure. Furthermore, 2D [Cu­(p-SPhOH)] n exhibits higher electrical conductivity on pressed pellets (2 × 10–3 S·cm–1) than 1D [Cu­(SPh)] n , highlighting the combined influence of dimensionality and ligand functionalization. Investigations of substituent effects in 2D [M­(p-SPhR)] n (M = Cu, Ag; R = −CO2H, -F, -OMe, −OH, -NH2) show that only [Cu­(p-SPhOH)] n behaves as a semiconductor, whereas the other MOCs remain insulating (σ < 10–8 S·cm–1). These results emphasize the key role of the −OH group and indicate that the copper thiolates generally display higher conductivity than their silver analogues.

Despite these advances, systematic studies correlating the structure, dimensionality, and physical properties in MOCs remain limited. In particular, the influence of ligand functionalization on both the network dimensionality and resulting properties is not yet fully understood. Herein, we report the synthesis of a new family of MOCs based on two naphthalenethiolate isomers to probe the effect of aromatic substitution on the structure and properties. Thus, four MOCs, [M­(x – NT)] n (M = Cu and Ag and x = 1 and 2), were obtained with 1-naphthalenethiolate (1-NT) and 2-naphthalenethiolate (2-NT) ligands. The more sterically hindered 1-NT leads to 1D networks, whereas 2-NT yields 2D frameworks. Photophysical and electrical measurements show that 1D [Cu­(1-NT)] n exhibits both the highest QY and the highest electrical conductivity among the series. In addition, ligand exchange experiments involving copper- and silver thiolates demonstrate that the dimensionality of these MOCs is governed by naphthalenethiolate functionalization.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

All reagents and solvents were used as received. 1-Naphthalenethiol (1-HNT) and 2-naphthalenethiol (2-HNT) were purchased from TCI (>98% Purity). Copper­(II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O, ≥99%), silver­(I) nitrate (AgNO3, >99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%, ACS reagent), and sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 95–97%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol, acetone, and dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from VWR Chemicals.

Syntheses of the CPs

Synthesis of [Cu­(1-NT)] n (1)

In a 100 mL flask equipped with a reflux condenser, CuCl2·2H2O (68 mg, 0.4 mmol, 1 equiv) was added in 12 mL of water followed by the delicate addition of a solution of 1-naphthalenethiol (0.055 mL, 0.4 mmol, 1 equiv) in 12 mL of toluene at the surface of the aqueous solution. The reaction was let to proceed at 100 °C for 24 h without stirring. The obtained product was then filtered over Büchner and washed three times with water (3 × 30 mL), ethanol (3 × 30 mL), and acetone (3 × 30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained yellow powder is not soluble in any common organic solvent. Yield: 85% (76 mg). Elemental analysis wt %, found (calc.): C; 53.15 (53.90), H; 2.98 (3.17), S; 13.89 (14.40). Copper content from TGA (calc.) wt %, 36.2 (35.7). FT-IR wavenumbers (cm–1): 3046, 1587, 1560, 1502, 1375, 1249, 968, 782, 757, 660. UV–visible absorption (solid state; nm): 378, 320, and 252.

Synthesis of [Ag­(1-NT)] n (2)

In a vial of 20 mL, a solution of 1-naphthalenethiol (0.14 mL, 1 mmol, 1 equiv) in 5 mL of DMF was added to 10 mL of an aqueous solution of AgNO3 (170 mg, 1 mmol, 1 eq). The vial was sealed and the reaction was briefly stirred, then heated at 120 °C for 3 h. To ensure good homogeneity and to avoid any darkening of the precipitate formed, the mixture was manually stirred every 30 min. The obtained product was then filtered over Büchner and washed three times with water (3 × 30 mL), ethanol (3 × 30 mL), and acetone (3 × 30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained yellow powder was not soluble in any common organic solvent. Yield: 80% (213 mg). Elemental analysis wt %, found (calc.): C; 44.34 (44.97), H; 2.58 (2.64), S; 12.07 (12.0). Silver content from TGA (calc.) wt %, 39.2 (40.4). FT-IR wavenumbers (cm–1): 3048, 1587, 1560, 1502, 1375, 1249, 968, 782, 757, 660. UV–visible absorption (solid state; nm): 440, 330, and 253.

Synthesis of [Cu­(2-NT)] n (3)

In a vial of 20 mL, a solution of 2-naphthalenethiol (320.5 mg, 2.5 mmol, 2 equiv) in 5 mL of DMF was added to 5 mL of CuCl2·2H2O (136.4 mg, 0.8 mmol, 1 equiv) in DMF, followed by the addition of 1 mL of 1 M H2SO4. The vial was sealed and the reaction was briefly stirred, then heated at 120 °C for 24 h. The obtained product was then filtered over Büchner and washed three times with hot DMF (3 × 30 mL) and then with acetone (3 × 30 mL). The resulting shiny white-off flakes were then dried in air. The flakes were not soluble in any common organic solvent. Yield: 67% (119 mg). Elemental analysis wt %, found (calc.): C; 53.04 (53.90), H; 3.18 (3.17), S; 13.93 (14.40). Copper content from TGA (calc.) wt %, 35.4 (35.7). FT-IR wavenumbers (cm–1): 2986, 1574, 1266, 1066, 807, 735, 629. UV–visible absorption (solid state; nm): 356, 308, and 258.

Synthesis of [Ag­(2-NT)] n (4)

In a vial of 20 mL, a solution of 2-naphthalenethiol (160 mg, 1 mmol, 1 equiv) in 5 mL of DMF was added to 10 mL of an aqueous solution of AgNO3 (136 mg, 0.8 mmol, 0.8 equiv). The vial was sealed and the reaction was briefly stirred, then heated at 120 °C for 24 h. To ensure good homogeneity and to avoid any darkening of the precipitate formed, the mixture was manually stirred every 30 min. The obtained product was then washed by centrifugation twice with ethanol (3 × 30 mL) and twice with acetone (3 × 30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained white powder was not soluble in any common organic solvent. Yield: 60% (159 mg). Elemental analysis wt %, found (calc.): C; 43.91 (44.97), H; 2.56 (2.64), S; 11.88 (12.0). Silver content from TGA (calc.) wt %, 40.8 (40.4). FT-IR wavenumbers (cm–1): 3047, 1580, 1496, 1128, 1068, 812, 736, 627. UV–visible absorption (solid state; nm): 356, 310, and 254.

Ligand Exchange Reactions

The names, m (n) , of the compounds obtained from ligand exchange reactions are for m: the resulting structure obtained after ligand exchange and n: the starting CP used before the ligand exchange. m′ (n) , is for the obtention of an intermediate product from the incomplete ligand exchange reaction.

Synthesis of 3(1)

In a vial of 20 mL, [Cu­(1-NT)] n (1) (50 mg, 0.22 mmol, 1 equiv), 2-naphthalenethiol (1.24 mL, 9 mmol, 40 equiv) and 10 mL of acetone were added. After a vigorous stirring, the vial was sealed and heated at 120 °C for 24 h. The product was then washed by centrifugation with acetone three times and then dried in air. The obtained white powder is named 3 (1) . Yield: 14% (7 mg).

Synthesis of 1(3) via the Intermediate 1′(3)

In a vial of 20 mL [Cu­(2-NT)] n (3) (50 mg, 0.22 mmol, 1 equiv), 1-naphthalenethiol (1.24 mL, 9 mmol, 40.1 equiv) and 10 mL of MeOH were added. After a vigorous stirring, the vial was sealed and heated at 120 °C for 3 h. The product was then washed three times by centrifugation with acetone (30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained brown powder was not soluble in any solvent. The mass of this intermediate, named 1’ (3) , was 32 mg. Then, 1’ (3) was redispersed in a 20 mL vial with 1-naphthalenthiol (1.24 mL, 9 mmol, 40.1 equiv) and 10 mL of MeOH, sealed and stirred for 5 days at 120 °C. The product was then washed three times by centrifugation with acetone (30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained brown powder is named 1 (3) . Yield: 60% (30 mg).

Synthesis of 4(2)

In a vial of 20 mL [Ag­(1-NT)] n (2) (50 mg, 0.19 mmol, 1 equiv), 2-naphthalenethiol (1.2 g, 7.5 mmol, 40 equiv) and 10 mL of MeOH were added. After a vigorous stirring, the vial was sealed and heated at 120 °C for 3 h. The product was then washed three times by centrifugation with acetone (30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained white powder is named 4 (2) . Yield: 54% (27 mg).

Synthesis of 2(4) via the Intermediate 2′(4)

In a vial of 20 mL [Ag­(2-NT)] n (4) (50 mg, 0.19 mmol, 1 equiv), 1-naphthalenethiol (600 mg, 3.74 mmol, 0.52 mL, 19.7 equiv) and 10 mL of MeOH were added. After a vigorous stirring, the vial was sealed and heated at 120 °C for 3 h. The product was then washed by centrifugation with acetone (30 mL) three times and then dried by air. The 28 mg of the obtained brown/yellow powder is not soluble in any solvent. This intermediate was named 2′ (4) and then redispersed in a 20 mL vial with 1-naphthalenethiol (600 mg, 3.74 mmol, 0.52 mL) and 10 mL of MeOH, sealed and stirred for 5 days at 120 °C. The product was then washed three times by centrifugation with acetone (30 mL) and then dried in air. The obtained brown powder is named 2 (4) . Yield: 50% (25 mg).

Material Characterizations

Routine PXRD

Routine powder X-ray diffraction was carried out on a Bruker D8 Advance A25 diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation equipped with a 1-dimensional position-sensitive detector (Bruker LynxEye). XR scattering was recorded between 4 and 90° (2θ) with 0.02° steps and 0.5 s per step (28 min for the scan). Divergence slit was fixed to 0.2° and the detector aperture to 189 channels (2.9°)

Structural Determinations

High resolution powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data were collected on the CRISTAL beamline at Soleil Synchrotron (Gif-sur-Yvette, France) for 1, 2, and 3. A monochromatic beam was extracted from the U20 undulator beam by means of a Si(111) double monochromator. The wavelengths (0.67152 Å for 1 and 2 and 0.6714 Å for 3) were refined from LaB6 (NIST Standard Reference Material 660a) powder diagrams recorded prior to the experiments. Diffraction data were collected in continuous scanning mode with a MYTHEN2 × 9K detector (Dectris), allowing a measurement in less than 5 min. PXRD data of 4 were collected on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with the Debye–Scherrer geometry, equipped with a Ge(111) monochromator producing CuKa 1 radiation (λ = 1.540598 Å) and a LynxEye detector. Samples were loaded in capillaries (Borokapillaren, GLAS, Schönwalde, Germany) of 0.7 mm for synchrotron data and 0.3 mm for laboratory ones and mounted on a spinner to improve the particles’ statistics.

Calculations of structural investigations were performed with TOPAS (indexing, simulated annealing, and Rietveld refinement) and EXPO (direct methods for 3) programs. The LSI-indexing method converged to monoclinic unit cells with satisfactory figures of merit for 1, 2, and 3 (see Table S1). Indexing of 4 was trickier, and the only parameter that was nonambiguous was the large b parameter, which was similar to the a parameter of 3. Therefore, the LP-search method of TOPAS was used, assuming that its unit cell parameters are close to those of 3. Systematic extinctions were consistent with the P21/c space group for all of the compounds. Direct methods (for 3) and simulated annealing processes for the others led us to locate the metal and sulfur atoms. The organic ligands were then treated as rigid bodies and allowed to rotate around sulfur. The final Rietveld plots (Figures S3–S6) correspond to satisfactory model indicators and profile factors (Table S1).

The CCDC numbers for 1 to 4 are 2542617, 2542620, 2542621, and 2542622, respectively. These crystallographic data are provided free of charge by the Cambridge Crystallographic Data Centre through www.ccdc.cam.ac.uk/structures.

TGA

Thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) were performed with a TGA/DSC 1 STARe System from Mettler Toledo. Around 5 mg of sample was heated at a rate of 5 °C·min–1, in a 70 μL alumina crucible, under air atmosphere (30 mL.min–1).

Elemental Analysis

C, H, S, and O elemental analyses have been carried out with a FlashEA 1112 analyzer from Thermo Scientific.

FT-IR

Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) analyses were carried out in a transmittance mode using a Nicolet IS5 equipped with an ID7-ATR accessory with a diamond crystal from Thermo Scientific. The sample was directly placed over the diamond, and the IR signal was recorded with a resolution of 4 cm–1, 32 scans, and a spectral range of 4000 to 525 cm–1.

SEM

Scanning electron microscopy images were obtained with a ZEISS Merlin Compact VP scanning electron microscope and a JEOL IT800. Samples were mounted on stainless pads and sputtered with carbon to prevent charging during observation.

UV–vis

UV–vis absorption spectrum was carried out in diffuse reflection at RT with a LAMBDA 1050+ spectrophotometer with a 100 mm integrating sphere from PerkinElmer. The sample was mixed in a solid state with BaSO4 at 1 wt %.

Photoemission Spectra Measurements

Measurements of solid-state emission, with the temperature from 473 to 93 K, were performed on a homemade apparatus at Institut Lumière Matière, University of Lyon. The sample was illuminated by an eq 99X laser-driven light source filtered by a Jobin Yvon Gemini 180 monochromator. The exit slit from the monochromator was then reimaged on the sample by two 100 mm focal length, 2-in. diameter MgF2 lenses. The whole apparatus has been calibrated by means of a Newport 918D low-power calibrated photodiode sensor over the range 190–1000 mm. The emitted light from the sample is collected by an optical fiber connected to an ANDOR Kymera 328i monochromator equipped with a cooled CCD detector. At the entrance of the monochromator, different long-pass filters can be chosen in order to eliminate the excitation light. The resolution of the detection system is 2 nm in excitation and 0.5 nm in emission. Temperature control over the sample was regulated with a THMS-600 heating stage with T95- PE temperature controller from Linkam Scientific Instruments

Quantum Yields Measurements

The quantum yields were measured in the solid state using a Hamamatsu Quantaurus-QY C11347–11 integrating sphere with excitations in the 320–420 nm range (excitations every 10 nm). The integrating sphere encloses the sample, capturing all emitted fluorescence (regardless of the direction). A xenon lamp paired with a monochromator selected the excitation wavelengths. Solid samples were directly measured in a quartz-covered plate.

Formation of Pellets and Density Measurements

Between 20 and 30 mg of powder is pressed at 60 Pa for 15 min. The resulting pellets have a diameter of 4.0 mm and a thickness of around 1.2 mm (Table S3). The densities of the pressed pellets are measured by Archimedes’ method and are 1.69, 1.73, 1.99, and 2.01 g·cm–3, respectively. Knowing the theoretical density from the structure, the deduced compactness is above 90% (Table S3), meaning a small volume of interparticular pores, which is a key point for the measurement of the conductivity through the pellet. PXRD on the crushed pressed pellets has been checked and the patterns confirm that the four compounds retain their crystallinity after compression (Figures S16 and S17).

Electrical Properties Measurement

The electrical conductivity was measured using a home-built apparatus specifically designed for highly resistive materials, described in detail elsewhere. It includes a guarding system connected via a three-lug triaxial connector to the Keithley 6517A high-resistance electrometer, allowing accurate measurement of very small current down to about 10–13 A (leaking currents are in the 10–14 A range). The electrical conductivity is measured across the pellet thickness, i.e., along the compression axis used to compact the powders. The contact pressure between the electrodes and the pellet is precisely controlled and can be tuned up to 17 MPa with a resolution of 0.05 MPa. Notably, this maximum pressure is 50 times lower than the pressure applied during the cold-sintering process. Therefore, the effects observed under electrode loading can be primarily attributed to interfacial contact effects between the pellet and the electrodes. Electrical conductivity is measured as a function of contact pressure to identify a plateau region in which its variation remains weak over a voltage range from −10 to +10 V. The contact-pressure dependence of the conductivity for [Cu­(1-NT)] n , [Ag­(1-NT)] n , [Cu­(2-NT)] n , and [Ag­(2-NT)] n is presented in Figures S19, S24, S29 and S34, respectively. The intrinsic electrical conductivity and its standard deviation are obtained by averaging all values within the plateau region of the conductivity-contact pressure curve. Temperature-dependent measurements (20–80 °C) are performed using a Peltier system under ambient conditions, with the contact pressure fixed within the plateau region. The corresponding contact pressure values are reported in the ESI (Table S4).

Results and Discussion

Syntheses and Routine Characterizations of the MOCsThe syntheses of the CPs are carried out in solvothermal conditions, starting with copper­(II) dichloride for the copper thiolates, which induces an oxido-reduction reaction between the metal and the thiol molecules, and silver­(I) nitrate for the silver thiolates (Part 2.2 and Scheme ). The four CPs are obtained in relatively good yields, between 60 and 80% based on the metal content. The CPs with the 1-NT ligand are yellow, while those containing 2-NT are white (Figure S1). The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) patterns of 1 and 2 have three first intense reflections at d = 14.30, 12.17, and 8.84 Å for 1 and at d = 16.02, 11.98, and 10.23 Å for 2, showing that they exhibit a signature similar to the one already attributed to 1D [M­(SR)] n (Figure a). , In contrast, 3 and 4 show predominant 00l reflections characteristic of lamellar structures (d = 18.42, 9.21, and 6.14 Å for 3 and 18.84, 9.42, and 6.28 Å for 4) (Figure a). , The crystalline quality of 2, 3, and 4 compounds is good enough for structural determinations from powder diffraction, but that of 1 is low. Numerous synthetic conditions were therefore tested to improve it, but unfortunately without any success. The SEM images support these dimensionalities and crystallinity observations (Figure b). Indeed, 1 and 2 are made of thin needles of around 1 μm length in good accordance with a 1D structure whereas 3 and 4 form thin platelets of around 100 μm for 3 and 1 μm for 4, characteristic of 2D materials. The thermogravimetric analyses (TGA) confirm the neutral chemical composition of the [Cu­(SR)] n and [Ag­(SR)] n CPs and their purities with a remaining copper oxide and silver contents in accordance with the expected values: 36.2 and 35.4% for 1 and 3 (calculated CuO: 35.7%) and 39.2 and 40.8 for 2 and 4 (calculated Ag: 40.4%) (Figure S2). In addition, the TGA curves show a relatively good thermal stability up to 250 °C under air for the four CPs. The C, H, and S elemental analyses also support the Metal/Ligand ratio = 1 (see Part 2.1).

Structure Description

The PXRD data of the four CPs have been collected at the CRISTAL beamline of the SOLEIL synchrotron facility for compounds 1, 2, and 3 and on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer for 4, to allow ab initio structural determinations. The final Rietveld refinements lead to satisfactory model indicators and profile factors (Table S1 and Figures S3 to S6). Both [M­(1-NT)] n , 1 and 2, MOCs crystallize in the P21/c monoclinic space group and present a 1D structure (Figures a and S7). For 1, the chain is along the a axis and along the b axis for 2 (Figures b and S7). The chains of these two MOCs are made of two M3S3 hexagons that are in a half-chair form and are connected together to generate tubular 1D networks (Figures c and S7). These 1D structures are self-assembled through weak bonds with the presence of short metallophilic interactions: 2.87(3) Å for Cu–Cu in 1 and 3.006(2) Å for Ag–Ag in 2. , π–π interactions between naphthalene moieties along the chains are observed for both compounds with 3.24(1) and 3.56(1) Å for 1 and 3.659(1) and 3.721(1) Å for 2. The lamellar MOCs, [Cu­(2-NT)] n (3) and [Ag­(2-NT)] n (4), crystallize also in the P21/c monoclinic space group (Table S1). They are made of metal–sulfur inorganic layers and noninterpenetrated organic layers (Figures d,e and S18). In case of [Cu­(2-NT)] n , the inorganic layer is made of two Cu3S3 hexagons in a twisted boat form (Figure f) and in [Ag­(2-NT)] n , there is one Ag3S3 hexagon and two Ag2S2 quadrilaterals (Figure S8), meaning that one silver is coordinated to three sulfur atoms and one is coordinated to four sulfur atoms. Silver thiolate CPs have been known to exhibit either three or four coordinated silver atoms.

The structures of these four CPs highlight that the dimensionality of the metal thiolate CPs is governed by the steric hindrance around the thiolate function. Indeed, 1-NT, which has an aromatic ring in the ortho position of the thiophenolate, close to the sulfur atom, forms 1D structures. On the contrary, 2-NT with an aromatic ring in the meta position of the thiophenolate induces the formation of 2D networks. Such an effect of the steric hindrance in thiophenolate ligands on the dimensionality of the MOCs has already been observed for the phenyl analogues: [M­(o-SPhCO2R)] n (M = Cu and Ag; R = H and Me) CPs exhibit 1D structures, ,, whereas the [M­(p-SPhCO2R)] n analogues adopt 2D networks. ,−

Spectroscopic Studies

The FT-IR spectra of the four CPs show the absence of the peak of the -SH stretching frequency around 2600–2550 cm–1, which confirms the total deprotonation of the thiol groups and the coordination of the thiolates to the metals (Figure S9). An important feature in these spectra is the clear differences of the vibrations of the CC–C and CC–H in the 1-NT from 1 and 2 and in the 2-NT from 3 and 4 (Figure S9). Thus, the C–H out-of-plane vibrations of 1-NT are identified at 758 and 782 cm–1 and at 735 and 810 cm–1 for 2-NT. This will be used in the ligand exchange reactions to follow the signature of the ligand as 1-NT or 2-NT. The solid-state UV–visible spectra of the four MOCs exhibit three main bands (Figure S10). The first band is centered between 356 and 440 nm, the compound with the redder shifted band is 2 (at 440 nm), which is in good accordance with its bright yellow color, followed by 1 (at 378 nm), which is pale yellow, and 3 and 4 (both at 356 nm), which are white. The second band is centered between 308 and 330 nm and the third one is between 252 and 258 nm. The high energy absorption band can be assigned to π-π* transitions of the naphthalenyl groups, and the other bands to the metal-perturbed intraligand or ligand-to-metal charge-transfer transitions. The estimated optical band gaps from these data by applying the Tauc plot method are around 2.46, 2.91, 3.05, and 3.19 eV for 1 to 4, respectively. These values are in the range of those observed for the previously described copper- and silver-thiolate CPs and showed that the 1D MOCs have a slightly lower optical band gap than the 2D. , In addition, the 2D [Cu­(2-NT)] n and [Ag­(2-NT)] n have smaller optical band gaps than the [Cd­(2-NT)2] n analogues, which are between 3.25 and 3.34 eV.

Photoluminescence

Photophysical studies at solid state of the four MOCs have been conducted. Under a UV light at RT, only 1 exhibits a faint red emission (Figure S11). This is supported by the emission spectrum with the maximum of emission centered at 720 nm for λex = 380 nm (Figure S12). The quantum yield at RT is 2.5%. At 90 K, 2 and 3 also become emissive and 4 remains nonemissive (Figures S13 and S14). From the emission spectra at low temperature, with λex = 380 nm, the emission maximum of 1 is still centered at 720 nm, 2 has a main peak at 800 nm and two small bands at 572 and 608 nm, and 3 has a vibronic structure with peaks at 536, 551, 576, and 596 nm (Figure a). This vibronic structure observed in 3 is typical of the singlet electronic transitions of the naphthalene moiety.

Thus, it appears from these observations that the 1D copper CP (1) has the most efficient photoemission among the four MOCs. Indeed, it has already been demonstrated that 1D copper and silver MOCs often display bright photoemission originating from some MLCT (metal-to-ligand charge transfer) or LMCT (ligand-to-metal charge transfer) transitions and that 2D MOCs are nonemissive at RT and exhibit a vibronic structure at low temperature coming from the ligand. ,, Nevertheless, compared to other Cu- and Ag-based MOCs, the photophysical properties of these naphthalenethiolate-based CPs are modest. This has also been observed for a 2D silver-2-naphthaleneselenoate [Ag­(2-SeNapht)] n MOC, which exhibits very weak emission centered at 740 nm, among a series of eight 2D silver para-functionalized benzeneselenote MOC that display bright blue narrow-line width PL at RT. In addition, the 1D MOCs, 1 and 2, exhibit quite short metallophilic interactions (2.87(3) Å for 1 and 3.006(2) Å for 2) and very low photoemission, highlighting that the emission efficiency in these MOCs is more correlated to the external function of the ligands than the length of the metallophilic interactions. To compare, some 1D MOCs have longer metallophilic interactions and higher QY: [Ag­(o-SPhCO2H)] n has an Ag–Ag = 3.440(5) Å and a QY = 52%, [Cu­(o-SPhCO2Me)] n , Cu–Cu = 3.506(3) Å and QY = 5%, and [Ag­(o-SPhCO2Me)] n , Ag–Ag = 3.475(2) Å, QY = 18%. Still, this study highlights that the best photoluminescent MOC in this naphthalenethiolate-based series is the 1D copper-based MOC.

Electrical Conductivity

Linear or ohmic voltage–current (V–I) regimes are observed for the four pressed pellets of MOCs at RT (Figures S18–S19, S23–S24, S28–S29, and S33–S34 for 1 to 4 and Table S4). The highest electrical conductivity at RT is of 3.8 × 10–6 S·cm–1 for [Cu­(1-NT)] n , followed by 2.5 × 10–10 S·cm–1 for [Cu­(2-NT)] n and 8.8 × 10–13 S·cm–1 for [Ag­(2-NT)] n , whereas [Ag­(1-NT)] n exhibits the lowest conductivity, 1.4 × 10–15 S·cm–1. For the latter compound, the measured current reaches the sensitivity limit of the instrument, which explains the large standard deviation in the reported value. Thus, the electrical conductivity spans more than 7 orders of magnitude across the four compositions. Then, the electrical conductivity was investigated as a function of temperature up to 80 °C (Figures S20–S21, S25–S26, S30–S31, and S35–S36 for 1 to 4, respectively). The V–I curves of the four MOCs remain ohmic over the temperature range, and at 80 °C, the conductivity increases by a factor of 10 (Table S4), as typically observed for semiconducting materials. The temperature-dependent conductivities of the four CPs were fitted using an Arrhenius-type law: σ=σ∞exp(−EakT) with σ, the conductivity, and Ea, the activation energy (Figures S22, S27, S32, and S37 for 1 to 4). Their activation energies are 0.21 eV for [Cu­(1-NT)] n (1), 0.32 eV for [Cu­(2-NT)] n (3), 0.45 eV for [Ag­(1-NT)] n (2), and 0.69 eV for [Ag­(2-NT)] n (4) (Table S4). Thus, in this series of MOCs, the copper-based CPs have better conductivity than silver-based one, confirming the reports from literature. In addition, between the two anisotropic copper MOCs, the 1D CP exhibits better conductivity than the 2D CP, showing the effect of the dimensionality, independently of the functionality of the ligand, that is the same naphthalenethiolate moiety. Other copper phenylthiolate-based CPs show slightly better electrical conductivity like the 1D [Cu­(SPh)] n with 3.7 × 10–4 S·cm–1, or the 2D [Cu­(p-SPhOH)] n with 2.1 × 10–3 S·cm–1 on pressed pellet. The electrical conductivity in these materials likely arises from a combination of through-bond transport along the metal–sulfur backbone and limited through-space interactions between π-systems. The higher conductivity observed for copper-based compounds compared to their silver analogues is consistent with stronger Cu–S orbital overlap, which facilitates charge delocalization. In addition, the Cu–S connectivity appears to play a dominant role in electronic transport, which is influenced by the steric effects of the ligands and the presence of weak interactions that may disrupt optimal orbital overlap and reduce effective electronic coupling, resulting in tunable dimensionality and conductivity. Furthermore, sample shaping and experimental measurement conditions may also affect the observed conductivity. Therefore, further studies should be carefully conducted and reported to fully understand the relationships between structure and electronic transport in these anisotropic MOCs.

Ligand Exchange Reactions

Ligand exchange reactions on silver and gold thiolate MOCs have shown that nucleophilic displacement occurs and allow reversible 1D to 2D dissolution-recrystallization depending of the thiolate ligand functionality. ,, In the case of copper thiolate, only one reversible transformation has been observed between the 1D [Cu­(SPh)] n and 1D [Cu6I3(SPh)3(MeCN)2] n , without ligand exchange and change in dimensionality. Here, ligand exchange reactions have been carried out first on the copper thiolate MOCs and then on the silver-based ones. Thus, the 2D [Cu­(2-NT)] n (3) was dispersed in a large excess of 1-naphthalenethiol in methanol at 120 °C for 3 h. The PXRD pattern of the obtained washed powder, 1′ (3) , shows that the 2D phase still remains with the appearance of an additional peak at 5.9° (in 2θ), corresponding to the first reflection of the 1D phase (Figure a). To complete this transformation, 1’ (3) is redispersed in an excess of 1-HNT in methanol at 120 °C for 5 days. In these conditions, the exchange is complete, as confirmed by the disappearance of the 2D phase in the PXRD pattern of 1 (3) and with only the reflection at 5.9° remaining. This points out that 1 (3) has a very low crystallinity and follows the difficulty to get highly crystalline 1D [Cu­(1-NT)] n as it was observed before by direct synthesis. The presence of the 1-NT ligand and the absence of 2-NT are confirmed by the IR spectrum of 1 (3) with the C–H out-of-plane vibrations of 1-NT at 756 and 782 cm–1 (Figure S38). The ligand exchange reaction is also carried out from the as-synthesized 1 and mixed with an excess of 2-HNT in acetone at 120 °C for 24 h. The obtained product, 3 (1) , is highly crystalline and corresponds to the PXRD of the 2D [Cu­(2-NT)] n with the characteristic C–H out-of-plane vibrations of 2-NT at 735 and 808 cm–1 (Figures a and S38). The shorter reaction time and better crystallinity point out that the ligand exchange from 1D to 2D for Cu MOC is more favored and implies a complete dissolution-recrystallization of the initial network. The same study has been done with the silver analogues. Thus, when the synthesized 2D [Ag­(2-NT)] n , 4 is dispersed in excess of 1-HNT in methanol at 120 °C for 3 h, a mixed phase product, 2′ (4) , of 2D [Ag­(2-NT)] n and 1D [Ag­(1-NT)] n , is observed on the PXRD pattern and FT-IR spectrum (Figures b and S39). 2′ (4) is redispersed in an excess of 1-HNT in methanol at 120 °C for 5 days. In these conditions, a pure and highly crystalline 1D [Ag­(1-NT)] n , 2 (4) , is characterized (Figures b and S39). The total ligand exchange from 1D [Ag­(1-NT)] n in an excess of 2-HNT is reached in 3 h and the obtained product, 4 (2) , corresponds to the 2D [Ag­(2-NT)] n (Figures b and S39). This study shows that the kinetics of ligand exchange are faster from 1D to 2D MOCs and allows a better crystallinity, whatever the copper or silver metal. This tendency was already observed for silver thiolate MOCs and is reported for the first time for copper thiolate CPs.

Conclusions

A new series of Cu­(I)- and Ag­(I)-based MOCs made of the 1- and 2-naphthalenethiolate ligands has been synthesized. The results demonstrate that ligand steric hindrance controls the structural dimensionality, yielding 1D networks for 1-NT and 2D frameworks for 2-NT. Photophysical and electrical measurements reveal that the 1D copper-base MOC, [Cu­(1-NT)] n , exhibits the best performance in this series, with the highest emission efficiency and conductivity, highlighting the key role of structural anisotropy and of the metal center. Ligand exchange experiments further confirm the tunability of these systems, enabling reversible control over dimensionality. These findings provide important insights into structure–property relationships in MOCs and the efficiency of 1D copper thiolate CP, over 2D networks and silver analogs, and offer guidelines for the design of functional optoelectronic materials.
