Low‐Level Human Epididymis Protein 4 in Seminal Plasma Is an Independent Risk Factor for Poor Semen Liquefaction: A Case Control Study
Authors: Feifei Wang, Jiangnan Wu, Shijie Gao, Zhuohan Zheng, Zhiheng Wang, Jing Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between human epididymis protein 4 (HE4) levels in seminal plasma and semen liquefaction in males.MethodsA total of 314 healthy men from the andrology department were included. The correlations between HE4 levels and prostate‐specific antigen (PSA) levels as well as conventional semen parameters (sperm density, the proportion of non‐progressively motile spermatozoa, seminal liquefaction time) were analyzed, and logistic regression was further employed to examine the association between HE4 levels and the risk of poor semen liquefaction.ResultsThe level of HE4 in seminal plasma was negatively correlated with semen liquefaction time (r = −0.207, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with seminal plasma PSA levels (r = 0.280, p < 0.001), suggesting a potential link to the process of semen liquefaction. Continuous HE4 levels were standardized by dividing by 100, and participants were then categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4) based on the standardized HE4 values. After adjusting for confounding factors, the risk of poor semen liquefaction in the Q3, Q2, and Q1 groups of the lower HE4 level was 4.024 times (95% CI: 1.908–8.485; p < 0.001), 6.320 times (95% CI: 2.818–14.175; p < 0.001), and 5.190 times (95% CI: 2.217–12.15; p < 0.001) higher, respectively, compared to the highest HE4 quartile (Q4).ConclusionThis suggests that a low level of HE4 in seminal plasma is an independent risk factor for poor semen liquefaction.

Introduction

Epidemiological studies indicate that infertility affects about 15% of couples worldwide after 12 months of unprotected intercourse [1]. In China alone, up to 50 million people are affected by infertility, 30%–40% of which involve male factors, while 20% are purely male‐related [2]. Poor semen liquefaction is a significant cause of male infertility, affecting 12%–32% of men [3]. Following ejaculation, semen coagulates rapidly and then undergoes gradual liquefaction through proteolytic degradation of seminal proteins, primarily mediated by a cascade of enzymes with PSA playing the dominant role [4]. This liquefaction is crucial for sperm to acquire motility and successfully travel to the fertilization site in the fallopian tube [5]. Poor semen liquefaction has been demonstrated to adversely impact fertilization rates, pregnancy rates, and embryonic development [6]. While assisted reproductive technologies (ART) like in vitro fertilization (IVF) have mitigated the consequences of male infertility, growing evidence indicates that impaired semen liquefaction compromises the efficacy of IVF‐embryo transfer procedures [3]. Additionally, the IVF and test‐tube baby procedures involve ovarian stimulation, fetal reduction, and multiple embryo transfers, which pose certain health risks to women. These include ovarian damage, isthmocele, and other adverse effects [7]. The most severe cases of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome can be life‐threatening [8]. Therefore, treating impaired semen liquefaction may serve as an effective approach to overcome male infertility. Therefore, assessment of liquefaction time and influencing semen parameters is of significant clinical importance.

PSA is a proteolytic enzyme secreted by the prostate that participates in the liquefaction process. Several studies have demonstrated significantly reduced PSA expression in semen with impaired liquefaction compared to normal controls [9, 10]. Additionally, some studies suggest that increased semen viscosity is not associated with PSA levels but rather correlates with its proteolytic activity [4].

Studies have demonstrated that HE4, functioning as a physiological serine protease inhibitor [11], exhibits inhibitory effects against major proteolytic enzymes [12] in semen. First characterized in the distal epididymis, HE4 is constitutively expressed by mucosal epithelia of the respiratory, digestive, and reproductive systems, with only minimal detection in peripheral blood [13, 14, 15]. HE4 is currently primarily investigated as a tumor biomarker, demonstrating significant diagnostic value for ovarian cancer, endometrial cancer, and lung cancer [16, 17, 18, 19].

As one of the most abundant proteins in human semen [20], the functional role of HE4 in seminal biology remains poorly characterized. Some researchers have postulated multifaceted roles for HE4 in fertilization processes—particularly in sperm capacitation, motility, and maturation—based on its interaction networks with other seminal proteins [21]. Judith Hall et al. [22] demonstrated that PSA, as the established enzymatic mediator of semen liquefaction, functioned to enhance the antimicrobial activity of HE4 through proteolysis. This evidence suggests a potential regulatory relationship between HE4 and semen liquefaction. Therefore, we intend to employ a case–control study design to analyze the correlation between seminal plasma HE4 levels and conventional semen quality parameters, thereby investigating the role of HE4 in male reproductive function.

Methods

Design and Participants

Our study employed a case–control design involving 447 patients recruited from the andrology department at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University (Shanghai, China) between January and June 2023. Exclusion criteria encompassed: reproductive tract infections, clinically diagnosed varicocele, severe metabolic disorders (including diabetes mellitus and thyroid dysfunction), history of malignancy, prior hormonal therapy, suboptimal semen sample quality (specifically non‐compliance with abstinence period guidelines), and absence of complete medical records. Clinical data and laboratory results were retrieved from the Hospital Information System (HIS) and Laboratory Information Management System (LIS), respectively. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University (approval number: 2020–144), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Semen samples were collected by masturbation after 5 days of sexual abstinence. Following collection, samples were incubated at 37°C to assess liquefaction time. The sample was examined every 5 min by tilting the container and by gently aspirating the semen with a pipette. The time to complete liquefaction (formation of a non‐viscous fluid without any coagulum) was recorded. Normal liquefaction was defined as complete liquefaction within 60 min, while delayed or incomplete liquefaction beyond 60 min was classified as poor liquefaction [23].

We categorized all cases using two distinct grouping methods. First, based on semen liquefaction status, the samples were divided into Group A (Normal semen liquefaction, n = 158) and Group B (Poor semen liquefaction, n = 156). The age range of Group A was 23 to 45 years (mean 30.3 years), while that of Group B was 22 to 45 years (mean 30.7 years). Second, using the median HE4 level of 564.6 pmol/mL as the cutoff: the low HE4 group (n = 157) and the high HE4 group (n = 157).

Semen Parameter Analysis

For the assessment of sperm kinetic parameters and concentration, a Computer‐Assisted Sperm Analysis (CASA) system was employed [24]. In this study, we utilized the WLJY‐9000 Color Sperm Quality Analysis System (Weili Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) to quantify the following parameters: sperm concentration, count, and percentage of progressively motile (PR) sperm, rapidly progressively motile (PR (A)) sperm, slowly progressively motile (PR (B)) sperm, non‐progressively motile (NP) sperm, and immotile (IM) sperm. For the motility and concentration analysis, a precisely 5 μL aliquot of well‐mixed, liquefied semen was loaded into a dedicated disposable counting chamber (depth: 20 μm) provided by the manufacturer (Weili Co. Ltd., Beijing, China).

Measurement of HE4 and PSA Levels

All semen samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min within 2 h after collection to isolate seminal plasma, which was then stored at −80°C until analysis. HE4 and PSA levels were quantified using a Cobas e 601 automated electrochemiluminescence immunoassay system (Roche Diagnostics) with matched reagents. Due to the significantly higher concentrations of HE4 and PSA in seminal plasma compared to serum, samples were diluted 1000‐fold with phosphate‐buffered saline prior to measurement.

Data Analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed variables and median (interquartile range) for non‐normally distributed measures. Parameters with non‐normal distribution included: PSA, sperm density, PR (A), PR (B), PR (A + B), and NP. Two groups of independent samples of continuous variables with normal distribution were compared using the Student's t‐test, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between non‐normally distributed variables. The boxplot was drawn using GraphPad Prism for graphical display. For semen parameters that showed significant intergroup differences, Spearman's rank correlation analysis was further employed to evaluate the relationship between HE4 and these parameters. The continuous variable HE4 was scaled by dividing by 100 for standardization and then categorized into quartiles: Q1 (≤ 4.10, n = 79), Q2 (4.11–5.65, n = 78), Q3 (5.66–8.45, n = 79), and Q4 (≥ 8.46, n = 78). Logistic regression analysis was employed to assess the association between HE4 and semen liquefaction status, with results expressed as odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0), and a two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among 447 patients who visited the andrology department and provided informed consent, 314 met the inclusion criteria (Figure 1). The overall level of HE4 showed a non‐normal distribution, we first divided 314 patients into two groups based on the median HE4 level of 564.6 pmol/mL (range: 148.0–2722.0 pmol/mL): the low HE4 group (HE4 ≤ 564.6 pmol/mL, n = 157) and the high HE4 group (HE4 > 564.6 pmol/mL, n = 157). There was no difference in age between the two groups of patients. Patients in the high HE4 group exhibited significantly higher sperm density (94.66 (51.26–141.83) × 106/mL), NP sperm proportion (8.64 (5.22–14.23) %), and complete liquefaction rate (60.51%) compared to those in the low HE4 group, which had corresponding values of 51.22 (28.96–71.32) × 106/mL, 6.90 (3.45–11.19) %, and 40.13%. All these differences were statistically significant (p < 0.05). The Association Between HE4 and the Risk of Poor Semen Liquefaction

Binary logistic regression analysis revealed that, compared to the highest HE4 quartile (Q4, n = 78), each decrease in HE4 level was associated with a significant trend toward increased risk of poor semen liquefaction (p < 0.001). Specifically, the third HE4 quartile (Q3, n = 79) had a higher risk of poor semen liquefaction than the Q4 group (OR 2.611, 95% CI 1.347–5.059, p < 0.001). The second HE4 quartile (Q2, n = 78) exhibited the highest risk (OR 4.073, 95% CI 2.080–7.973, p < 0.001), while the first HE4 quartile (Q1, n = 79) had a 3.548‐fold increased risk compared to the Q4 group (95% CI 1.824–6.904, p < 0.001). After adjusting for confounding factors including patient age, sperm density, total sperm motility, the proportions of PR, PRA, PRB, NP, IM sperm, and PSA level of seminal plasma, the risk of poor semen liquefaction was significantly higher in the Q3, Q2, and Q1 groups compared to the Q4 group. Specifically, the adjusted odds ratios were 4.024 (95% CI: 1.908–8.485; p < 0.001), 6.320 (95% CI: 2.818–14.175; p < 0.001), and 5.190 (95% CI: 2.217–12.15; p < 0.001), respectively. This indicates that low levels of HE4 in seminal plasma are an independent risk factor for poor semen liquefaction, with the highest risk observed particularly when HE4 falls within the range of 411.0–565.0 pmol/mL (Table 3).

Discussion

Main Results

Poor semen liquefaction resulting in high viscosity is considered to negatively impact sperm motility, quantity, and progression within the female reproductive tract, ultimately leading to fertilization failure and male infertility [9]. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying impaired semen liquefaction remain unclear. Our study is the first to demonstrate that seminal plasma HE4 levels are negatively correlated with semen liquefaction time, and that reduced HE4 levels constitute an independent risk factor for poor semen liquefaction.

Comparison With Findings of Previous Studies

Sperm are produced in the seminiferous tubules of the testes. After leaving the testes, sperm have not yet acquired the capacity for progressive motility or the ability to fertilize an oocyte. To gain fertility, they must undergo maturation in the male epididymis and capacitation in the female reproductive tract [25]. During transit through the epididymis, sperm undergo morphological remodeling [26], and acquire both motility and fertilizing capacity [27, 28]. HE4 is secreted by the distal epididymal epithelial cells, suggesting its potential role in sperm maturation within the epididymis. A domestic study [29] has indicated that sperm density, total sperm motility, and the proportion of progressively motile sperm decrease with increasing levels of HE4 in seminal plasma. The expression of HE4 was significantly higher in patients with azoospermia, oligospermia, asthenospermia, and teratospermia compared to the normal fertility group. In contrast, Krishna Kant [21] reported opposing findings. Using ELISA to measure HE4 concentration in human seminal plasma, the study showed higher HE4 expression in normal fertility individuals compared to those with azoospermia or asthenospermia, while no significant difference was observed between azoospermia and asthenospermia groups.

In a somewhat different manner, our data indicate a positive correlation between sperm density and seminal plasma HE4 levels (r = 0.493, p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant differences in total sperm motility, the proportion of IM or PR sperm between the high and low HE4 level groups, suggesting that HE4 in seminal plasma may not play a significant role in sperm maturation. Surprisingly, we observed that the complete liquefaction rate was significantly higher in the high HE4 group compared to the low HE4 group (p < 0.001), and HE4 levels were negatively correlated with semen liquefaction time (r = −0.207, p < 0.001). Notably, after adjusting for various confounding factors using binary logistic regression, the risk of poor semen liquefaction in the Q3 (566.0–845.0 pmol/mL), Q2 (411.0–565.0 pmol/mL), and Q1 (≤ 410.0 pmol/mL) groups of HE4 level was 4.024 times (95% CI: 1.908–8.485; p < 0.001), 6.320 times (95% CI: 2.818–14.175; p < 0.001), and 5.190 times (95% CI: 2.217–12.15; p < 0.001) higher, respectively, compared to the Q4 (≥ 846.0 pmol/mL) group. This conclusively demonstrates that low levels of HE4 in seminal plasma are an independent risk factor for poor semen liquefaction. To our knowledge, this case–control study is the first to report this association.

Potential Mechanisms Underlying the Association Between HE4 and Semen Liquefaction

As is well known, PSA is widely recognized as a proteolytic enzyme involved in the liquefaction process [30]. After ejaculation, Semenogelin I, Semenogelin II, and fibronectin aggregate to form a gel‐like substance, which liquefies within 5–20 min. This process is primarily mediated by PSA through the degradation of Semenogelin I and Semenogelin II, thereby releasing motile sperm [31, 32]. Our data show that the PSA level in the high HE4 group is higher than that in the low HE4 group, and the HE4 level in seminal plasma is positively correlated with PSA content. This suggests that HE4 may participate in semen liquefaction by interacting with PSA.

The HE4 protein is encoded by the Whey Acidic Protein Four‐Disulfide Core 2 (WFDC2) gene and contains two WFDC domains with eight cysteine residues. Since the WFDC domain can bind to active sites to form loop structures that inhibit proteases, most members of the WFDC family function as protease inhibitors, including epididymal protease inhibitor (EPPIN) [33]. EPPIN can bind to EPPIN receptors on the sperm surface, serving as a bridge for the binding of Semenogelin to sperm, and is involved in semen liquefaction by regulating the digestion of Semenogelin by PSA [34] Based on the structural similarity between HE4 and EPPIN, HE4 may participate in semen liquefaction through a mechanism analogous to that of EPPIN.

Judith Hall et al. [22] demonstrated that PSA could hydrolyze HE4 to enhance its antibacterial activity. Furthermore, the team led by Krishna Kant [21] identified fibronectin 1 as an HE4‐binding protein through mass spectrometry. We speculate that HE4 may participate in semen liquefaction by interacting with both fibronectin 1 and PSA, which also provides a direction for our future research.

Strength and Limitations

The main strength of this study lies in its systematic evaluation, for the first time, of the relationship between seminal HE4 levels and semen liquefaction in males. Using a case–control design involving 314 participants, stratified regression analysis revealed a strong association between low HE4 levels and abnormal liquefaction (OR = 6.320, 95% CI 2.818–14.175; p < 0.001), suggesting that low seminal HE4 is an independent risk factor for impaired semen liquefaction. The use of stratified regression also enhanced the internal validity of the results by helping to control for known confounding factors, thereby improving the reliability of the association estimates. However, the study has several limitations. Most importantly, the case–control design itself limits causal inference. Furthermore, seminal HE4 showed only a weak but statistically significant correlation with PSA (r = 0.280, p < 0.001), and its precise origin and pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. Future studies with larger sample sizes and prospective designs, along with in‐depth mechanistic investigations, are needed to validate these findings and elucidate their biological basis. Although this study analyzed the relationship between HE4 and liquefaction time, sperm density, and motility, data on sperm morphology were lacking. Morphology is a core indicator of semen quality, and its absence limits a comprehensive assessment of the association between HE4 and overall semen quality. Furthermore, no correlation was observed between HE4 and sperm motility; however, this negative finding may be influenced by sample size or the detection method used and warrants confirmation in a larger cohort. Covariables such as BMI, smoking, alcohol, and comorbidities were not available, and that residual confounding by these factors cannot be excluded. Future studies should include these variables to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, there is a negative correlation between HE4 levels in the seminal plasma and semen liquefaction time (r = −0.207, p < 0.001). Stratified regression analysis revealed a strong association between low HE4 levels (411–565 pmol/mL) and abnormal liquefaction (OR = 6.320, 95% CI 2.818–14.175; p < 0.001), suggesting that low seminal HE4 is an independent risk factor for impaired semen liquefaction and may play a role in the process of semen liquefaction. In the next step, we will further unravel the mystery of HE4's function in seminal plasma by studying its mechanism of involvement in liquefaction.
