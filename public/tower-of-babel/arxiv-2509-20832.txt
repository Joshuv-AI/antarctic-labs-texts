Galactic Contrail in NGC 3627 caused by Dwarf Galaxy Candidate or Massive Black Hole Flyby
Mengke Zhao, Guang-Xing Li

Abstract
We report the discovery of a kiloparsec-scale molecular contrail in the spiral galaxy NGC 3627—a narrow structure spanning > 8 >8 kpc in length with a width of ∼ 200 \sim 200 pc and an extreme aspect ratio > 40 >40 , observed in both mid-infrared dust emission (PHANGS-JWST) and CO(2-1) gas (PHANGS-ALMA). This contrail size significantly exceeds the size of any known analogues in the Milky Way and exhibits supersonic turbulence ( σ v ≈ 10 ​ km ​ s − 1 \sigma_{v}\approx 10\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}} ). Its morphology and dynamics are consistent with gravitational focusing by a flyby compact object of mass ≳ 2 × 10 6 ​ M ⊙ \gtrsim 2\times 10^{6}\penalty\ M_{\odot} , likely a massive black hole or a dwarf galaxy nucleus, traversing the disk at ≳ 300 ​ km ​ s − 1 \gtrsim 300\penalty\ \mathrm{km\penalty\ s^{-1}} . The crossing time of such a contrail, estimated from its width and velocity dispersion, is only ∼ 20 \sim 20 Myr, implying a recent interaction. This contrail can be caused by a dwarf galaxy, or massive black hole nucleus. This discovery establishes galactic-scale contrails as probes of massive dark objects interacting with medium in and around galactic disks.

Introduction

Galaxies are basic ingredients of the universe
(
Shu 2016
;
Sellwood and Masters 2022
)
, consisting of complex systems with stars, gas, and dark matter.
They are considered the main sites for star formation in the universe.
In galactic discs, gas is organized into spiral arms with significant curvature,
primarily driven by stellar gravitational potentials arising from disc
instabilities
(
Lee and Shu 2012
)
, where stellar density waves generate
asymmetric gravitational potentials that torque and compress gas, forcing it
into coherent, curved trajectories

(
Roberts 1969
;
Kalnajs 1973
;
Shu 2016
)
.

Molecular contrails represent coherent linear structures in the interstellar medium
(ISM) with high aspect ratios

(
Li and Shi 2021
;
Scott et al. 2022
;
Kitajima and Inutsuka 2023
;
van Dokkum et al. 2023
;
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
)
.
They are unlikely to form through typical galactic processes nor turbulence, and
a promising formation mechanism is gravitational focusing caused by passages of compact objects (e.g., massive stars, black holes

(
Li and Shi 2021
;
Kitajima and Inutsuka 2023
;
van Dokkum et al. 2023
;
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
)
,
or star clusters
(
Li and Shi 2021
)
, even dwarf galaxies

(
Zaritsky et al. 2023
)
) through ambient gas.

The population of compact systems around galaxies is diverse, and our knowledge
about them remains limited.
Many dwarf galaxies exist as satellite systems around host galaxies
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
.
While large dwarf galaxies (e.g., the Large Magellanic Cloud around the Milky Way) are readily observable
(
Sacchi et al. 2021
;
Richstein et al. 2024
)
, ultra-faint dwarfs remain extremely challenging to detect directly and often require indirect methods
(
Mao et al. 2024
;
Cerny et al. 2025
)
.
Detection of Milky Way satellites proves particularly difficult

(
Weżgowiec et al. 2012
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, though their presence in
external galaxies like NGC 3627 may be inferred through gravitational
interactions. Recent JWST observations have unveiled the little red dots
(
Geraldi et al. 2024
;
Marconcini et al. 2025
;
Akins et al. 2024
;
Labbe et al. 2025
)
, which
are considered compact, red-tinted, early-universe galaxies, likely powered by
supermassive black holes or dense star-forming regions. The discovery of these
little red dots points to a gap in our understanding of the population of
compact systems around galaxies, and kpc-scale contrails serve as unique indirect
tracers for such systems.

2.2
Galactic-scale contrail in NGC 3627

An intriguing narrow, straight ISM structure is observed in the external galaxy NGC 3627.
As shown in Fig.
2
, this structure contains both gas, traced by CO, and dust, traced by JWST F770W emission.
In contrast to other regions of the galaxy, it exhibits lower star formation activity, as probed by F2100W, and weaker emission from young stellar objects, as indicated by F335M.
This structure extends at least 6 kpc in length (a lower limit due to
projection effects) while being only 200 pc wide (see Fig.
2
), whose
aspect ratio exceeds 30. It likely represents a galactic-scale contrail

(
Li and Shi 2021
;
Scott et al. 2022
;
Kitajima and Inutsuka 2023
;
van Dokkum et al. 2023
;
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
)
,
whose dimension far exceeds that of any known
small-scale contrails in the Milky Way.

The velocity structure of this galactic-scale contrail is unique and different from the spiral arms and even its own extended region.
As Fig.
3
shows, the gas within the main 6-kpc contrail has a very flat velocity gradient along its length, only about 10 km/s per kpc.
This is much lower than the gradient along the adjacent spiral arm, which is about 30 km/s per kpc.
However, the extended region of the structure, which lies closer to the spiral arm and the galactic bar, has a steeper velocity gradient similar to the arm’s.
The 30 km s
-1
kpc
-1
gradient matches the galaxy’s angular frequency
(Ω = Vc/R) at that distance from the center, as calculated from its circular
velocity
(
Chemin et al. 2003
;
Beuther et al. 2017
)
. This indicates that this extended region is more affect by the galaxy’s shear
compared against the main part of the contrail.

3
Flyby-induced contrail formation in NGC3627

We propose that this striking feature, with a width of 200 pc and a velocity dispersion of
\sim
10 km s
-1
, is the result of a
flyby event, where an object, possibly an massive black hole or the nucleus of a dwarf galaxy, traversed the galactic disk.

Formation Mechanism: From Warm Gas to Dense Molecular Contrail

The formation of this galactic-scale contrail is attributed to the momentum injected into the ISM as the massive object crossed the galactic disk through gravitational force
(
Li and Shi 2021
)
. This compressed the warm neutral medium (HI gas, T
\approx
8000 K;
Field et al. 1969
;
Heiles and Troland 2003
;
Wolfire et al. 2003
), which typically fills galactic disks
(
Walter et al. 2008
)
.
The compression triggered a rapid cooling process, transforming the warm neutral gas into dense molecular gas, thereby forming the collimated contrail structure
(
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
)
.
Evidence supporting this shock-driven cooling mechanism comes from the observed turbulent velocity of the contrail, measured at
\sim
10 km s
-1
from CO spectral lines (see P-V diagram in Fig.
2
).
This velocity exceeds the sonic speed of warm HI (
\sim
7.5 km s
-1
), confirming the presence of supersonic dynamics consistent with a shock-driven event.
Since the infall velocity is expected to exceed the sound speed of the ambient
medium, we expect the passage dense object to trigger the formation of the cold
molecular gas, and this is consistent with the detection of the CO molecules in
the contrail, where gas should also reach the self-shielding limit
Sternberg et al. 2014
.
Furthermore, magnetic fields, probed via synchrotron polarization, are found to align with the contrail
Soida et al. 2001
;
Liu et al. 2023
. This contrail-magnetic field alignment can be the result of contraction of the contrail.

Dynamical age and stability

Since differential rotation (shear) and turbulent motions can disrupt such intricate
structures, we expect them to be produced in a recent event, so short that
neither shear
(
Binney and Tremaine 2008
)
nor turbulence

(
Dobbs and Baba 2014
;
Li and Shi 2021
)
has had time to take effect.

From the width (200 pc) and velocity dispersion (
\sim
10 km s
-1
) of the
contrail, we estimate a crossing time of

t_{\rm cross}\approx\frac{d}{\sigma_{v}}\approx\frac{200\,{\rm pc}}{10\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}}\approx 20\,{\rm Myr}\;.

(1)

The crossing time is also the time for the contrail to disperse. For the
contrail to stay collimated, the flyby time must be comparable to or shorter than
the crossing time. Thus, we expect

t_{\rm flyby}\lesssim t_{\rm cross}\approx 20\,{\rm Myr}\;,

(2)

and a speed of

v_{\rm flyby}\gtrsim\frac{L}{t_{\rm cross}}\approx\frac{6\,{\rm kpc}}{20\,{\rm Myr}}\approx 300\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}}\;.

(3)

From Fig.
3
, the velocity gradient of the contrail is around 10
km s
-1
kpc
-1
, which implies a timescale of

t_{\rm shear}\approx\frac{1}{10\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,kpc^{-1}}}\approx 100\,{\rm Myr}\;.

(4)

Since
t_{\rm flyby}\approx t_{\rm cross}<t_{\rm shear}
, gravity from
the dark matter in the galaxy is not strong enough to bend the trajectory
significantly, thus the collimated structure is preserved.

Constraints on the Massive Object

Gravitational wake theory

(
Li and Shi 2021
)
, see also
Kitajima and Inutsuka 2023
;
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
predicts a
mass of

M\sim\frac{\sigma_{v}v_{\rm flyby}r}{G}

(5)

for the dense object, using
r=100
pc as the effective interaction radius and 10 km s
-1
as the velocity
dispersion of contrail, we derive

M\sim 1\times 10^{7}\,M_{\odot}\;,

(6)

which matches expectations for an massive black hole or a dwarf galaxy’s central region.
If a dwarf galaxy caused the contrail, it should lie along the contrail’s projected trajectory. However, at NGC 3627’s distance (11 Mpc), a faint dwarf (
\lesssim 10^{8}\,M_{\odot}
, V
>
22 mag) would require deep surveys for detection.

Dynamical differences and 3D structure

The first 6 kpc of the contrail has a flat velocity gradient of about 10
km s
-1
kpc
-1
, which is much lower than the velocity gradient of the
spiral arm (30 km s
-1
kpc
-1
). This difference indicates that the
first part of the
contrail, which we expect to stay outside of the Galactic disk, is not
significantly affected by the galaxy’s overall rotation, as compared to the
extended part, which may stay at the interface between the galactic disk and Circumgalactic medium (CGM).

Massive Dark Objects as the origin of Galactic Contrails

Such contrails serve as unique tracers of dark compact objects. The required
mass and velocity parameters overlap with predicted black hole populations

(
Ogiya and Nagai 2024
)
and dwarf galaxies
(
Pace et al. 2025
)
.
The contrail may also other origins: Recent JWST observations have unveiled the little red dots
(
Geraldi et al. 2024
;
Marconcini et al. 2025
;
Akins et al. 2024
;
Labbe et al. 2025
)
, which
are considered as compact, red-tinted, early-universe galaxies, likely powered
by supermassive black holes or dense star-forming regions, and these and their counterparts can also
be the cause of this contrail.
Future deep surveys (e.g., Rubin LSST
(
Fantin et al. 2020
)
) could search
for stellar counterparts along the contrail axis, while higher-resolution ALMA
observations may reveal kinematic signatures of the perturbing object.
