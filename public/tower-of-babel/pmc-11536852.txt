High-throughput synthesis and optimization of ionizable lipids through A3 coupling for efficient mRNA delivery
Authors: Jingjiao Li, Jie Hu, Danni Jin, Haonan Huo, Ning Chen, Jiaqi Lin, Xueguang Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe efficacy of mRNA-based vaccines and therapies relies on lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) as carriers to deliver mRNA into cells. The chemical structure of ionizable lipids (ILs) within LNPs is crucial in determining their delivery efficiency.ResultsIn this study, we synthesized 623 alkyne-bearing ionizable lipids using the A3 coupling reaction and assessed their effectiveness in mRNA delivery. ILs with specific structural features—18-carbon alkyl chains, a cis-double bond, and ethanolamine head groups—demonstrated superior mRNA delivery capabilities. Variations in saturation, double bond placement, and chain length correlated with decreased efficacy. Alkynes positioned adjacent to nitrogen atoms in ILs reduced the acid dissociation constant (pKa) of LNPs, thereby hindering mRNA delivery efficiency. Conversion of alkynes to alkanes significantly enhanced mRNA delivery of ILs both in vitro and in vivo. Moreover, combining optimized ILs with cKK-E12 yields synergistic LNPs that showed markedly augmented mRNA expression levels in vivo.ConclusionsOverall, our study provides insights into the structure–function relationships of ILs, providing a foundation for the rational design of ILs to enhance the efficacy of LNPs in mRNA delivery.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02919-1.Keywords: Ionizable lipids, Lipid nanoparticles, mRNA, A3 coupling, High-throughput screening

Introduction

Messenger RNA (mRNA)-based therapeutics and vaccines have revolutionized biomedicine by providing innovative solutions to longstanding challenges in vaccine development and disease treatment [1–5]. However, mRNA encounters significant obstacles in passing through the cell membrane due to its negative charge, hydrophilicity, and large size [6]. Additionally, unprotected mRNA is quickly degraded by enzymes in biological environments [7]. Therefore, effective delivery systems are essential to transport mRNA into cells and enhance its stability [8]. Among various delivery systems, including polymers [9–12], exosomes [13–15], inorganic nanoparticles [16, 17], and peptides [18], Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) stand out as the only carriers with regulatory approval for mRNA [19, 20]. The COVID-19 mRNA vaccines developed by Moderna and Pfizer/BioNTech, as well as the small interfering RNA therapy developed by Alnylam, utilize LNPs as carriers, demonstrating their effectiveness and safety in clinical settings [21, 22].

LNPs are assembled from specific ratios of ionizable lipids (ILs), helper lipids, cholesterol, polyethylene glycol (PEG)-lipids, and mRNA molecules through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions [23–25]. Among these components, the chemical structure of ILs plays a pivotal role in determining the efficiency of LNP delivery [26]. Clinically approved ILs typically comprise hydrophobic alkyl chains and a hydrophilic head group containing a tertiary amine [27–29]. During LNP fabrication, these lipids carry positive charges in acidic buffers, facilitating interaction with the negatively charged mRNA. Importantly, at physiological pH, ILs become uncharged, reducing the potential toxicity associated with positive charges. Upon cellular entry, ILs regain their positive charge within the acidic endosomal environment, facilitating endosomal escape and subsequent release of mRNA into the cytoplasm [30]. The continued development of new ILs is essential to enhance mRNA delivery efficiency while mitigating the toxicity risks associated with high doses of mRNA and LNPs.

A major hurdle in developing new ILs is the unclear relationship between their chemical structure and function, which hinders the rational design of ILs with high delivery efficiency. To tackle this challenge, researchers have employed combinatorial chemistry to synthesize a library of lipids for subsequent screening to identify candidates with superior delivery efficiency [31–34]. This high-throughput approach has led to the development of many ILs with high delivery efficiency and tissue- or cell-targeting capabilities [35–37]. The Michael addition reaction between amines and double bonds or epoxides is the most widely used method for synthesizing lipid libraries due to its simplicity and high yield [38–40]. Additionally, multicomponent reactions involving three or more reactants in a single step are a promising approach for synthesizing large libraries of lipids with diverse chemical structures [41]. Several recent studies have studied isocyanide-mediated three-component or Ugi four-component reactions to identify new ILs [42–44]. Unique ILs bearing imidazole or amidine were identified with high delivery efficiency [45]. New reactions suitable for synthesizing lipid libraries with unique chemical structures are still being sought to broaden the diversity of ionizable lipids.

Anderson et al. demonstrated that incorporating alkyne groups in the lipid tails of ILs significantly enhances the membrane fusion of LNPs and boosts mRNA delivery efficiency [46]. Despite this promising finding, very few ILs containing alkyne groups have been reported, and high-throughput synthesis of such ILs remains largely unexplored. The A3 coupling, a three-component reaction combining an aldehyde, an alkyne, and an amine to produce a propargylamine, offers a promising approach to address this gap [47, 48]. Herein, we used A3 coupling to synthesize a library of 623 new ILs bearing alkyne groups and screened their mRNA delivery efficiency in vitro. We found that the lipid tail with an 18-carbon double-substituted cis-alkene tail exhibited better transfection efficiency.

Methods

Materials and instruments

Amines, aldehydes, alkynes, and other chemical reagents were purchased from Beijing Innochem Science & Technology unless noted specifically. 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC) and 1,2-dimyristoyl-rac-glycero-3-methoxypolyethylene glycol-2000 (DMG-PEG2000) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids. Cholesterol was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. cKK-E12 was purchased from WuXi AppTec. 2-(p-toluidine)-6-naphthalene sulfonic acid (TNS) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. mRNA encoding firefly luciferase (mLuc) was purchased from ApexBio. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker Advance II 400 MHz NMR spectrometer with deuterated chloroform (CDCl3) as solvent with tetramethylsilane (TMS) as the internal standard. Electrospray ionization high-resolution mass spectrometry (ESI-HRMS) measurements were carried out using an Agilent Infinity Lab LC/MSD time-of-flight mass spectrometer.

Preparation of starting materials

Preparation of secondary amines

Amines A29–A30 are commercially available. Amines A1–A28 are synthesized by the following procedure:

Amine (10.0 eq.), bromoalkane(1.0 eq.), and K2CO3 (1.5 eq.) were dissolved in acetonitrile (CH3CN; 5 mL per 1 mmol). The reaction mixture was refluxed at 86 °C for 24 h. After filtration to remove K2CO3, the solvent was removed in vacuo. The residue was dissolved in CH2Cl2 and then washed with saturated brine. The organic layers were combined, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4,and then filtered. After removing the solvent in vacuo, the desired secondary amines were obtained as a white solid or colourless liquid. The crude solid was recrystallized from petroleum ether (PE) or purified through column chromatography via PE:EtOAc from 100/1 to 20/1 (v/v) to obtain the pure product.

Preparation of various terminal alkynes

Alkynes B1–B7 were commercially available. Other alkynes (B8–B11) are synthesized as the following procedures:

NaH (1.3 eq.) was dissolved in dimethylformamide (DMF) (5 mL per 1 mmol), and alkyl alcohol (1.0 eq.) was added. The mixture was stirred at 0 °C for 1 h. 5-Chloropentylene (1.5 eq.) was then added, and the reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h. EtOAc (5 mL per 1 mmol) was added for dilution, and the mixture was washed with saturated brine (3 × 5 mL per 1 mmol). The aqueous phase was extracted with EtOAc (2 × 5 mL per 1 mmol). The combined organic phase was dried with anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, and the solvent was removed in vacuum. The product was then purified by silica gel column chromatography (PE/EtOAc = 100/1, v/v) to obtain a colorless oily product.

General procedures for the synthesis of ester-bearing terminal alkynes B12–B16:

Hept-6-yonic acid (1.0 eq.) was dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (2 mL per 1 mmol), and dicyclohexyl carbodiimide (DCC) (1.0 eq.) was added to the reaction system with continuous stirring at room temperature. 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP) (0.3 eq.) and alkyl alcohol (1.0 eq.) were dissolved in dry CH2Cl2 (3 mL per 1 mmol) and added to the reaction system. The reaction was carried out at room temperature for 12 h. After filtering, water (2 mL per 1 mmol) was added and then the system was extracted with CH2Cl2 (30 mL × 3). The organic phase was collected and dried with anhydrous Na2SO4, and filtered. After removing the solvent in vacuo, the crude product was purified through silica gel column chromatography (PE/EtOAc = 30/1, v/v).

General procedures for the synthesis of amide-bearing terminal alkynes B17–B21:

Hept-6-ynoic acid or hex-5-ynoic acid (1.0 eq.), BOP (tri(dimethylamino)benzotriazol-1-yloxyphosphonium hexafluorophosphate)reagent (CAS: 56602-33-6; 1.2 eq.), and N-ethyl-N-isopropylpropan-2-amine (DIPEA; 1.2 eq.) were dissolved in DMF (3 mL per 1 mmol), and stirred at r. t. for 30 min, amine (1.0 eq.) was added and the system was stirred at 50 °C for 12 h. The reaction mixture was diluted with EtOAc (5 mL per 1 mmol) and extracted with saturated brine (50 mL × 3 per 1 mmol). The aqueous phase was extracted with EtOAc (5 mL per 1 mmol). The organic phase was combined and dried over anhydrous Na2SO4. After filtering, the crude product was further purified through silica gel column chromatography (PE/EtOAc = 10/1, v/v).

Synthesis of ionizable lipids A11B2, A11B4, A11B7, A11B9 and A28B10

Secondary amine (1.0 eq.), paraformaldehyde/formaldehyde solution (2.0 eq), and CuCl (0.3 eq) were added in a reaction tube. Ethanol (2 mL per 1 mmol) was then added to dissolve the mixture, followed by the addition of the alkyne (1.5 eq, 0.15 mmol). The reaction tube was stirred at 50 °C for 8 h. After filtering the solvent in vacuo, CH2Cl2 (5 mL per 1 mmol) was added and the reaction system was washed with saturated brine (2 mL × 2 per 1 mmol). The combined organic phases were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4. After filtering the Na2SO4, the solvent was removed in vacuo and the crude product was further purified by silica gel column chromatography (CH2Cl2/CH3OH = 50/1, v/v). The complete set of characterization data is provided in the supporting information.

High-throughput synthesis of ionizable lipids via A3 Coupling reaction

Ethanol solutions were prepared as follows: 30 types of secondary amines (0.4 mol/L), 21 types of alkynes (0.6 mol/L), formaldehyde (0.8 mol/L), and CuCl (0.12 mol/L). The solid components were dispersed into a suspension using ultrasonic vibration to ensure thorough mixing. 0.1 mL of each of the four solutions was transferred into a 4 mL reaction vial using a pipette. The reaction mixture was stirred at 50 °C for 8 h. Upon completion, the mixture was allowed to return to room temperature, and the vial was then uncapped to facilitate complete evaporation of the liquid. Subsequently, 400 μL of ethanol was added to each vial to redissolve the contents before proceeding with LNP synthesis. The reaction efficiency was assessed using TLC, as shown in Figure S45. For purification of ionizable lipid, the solids in the reaction mixture were filtered out. The filtrate was dried and redissolved in CH2Cl2, followed by extraction with water. The collected organic phase was then washed twice with saturated brine. The organic phase was collected, dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, and filtered. After removing the solvent in vacuo, the residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography with CH2Cl2 and CH3OH (50/1, v/v) as the eluent.

Reduction of ionizable lipids A11B2, A11B4, A11B7, A11B9 and A28B10

Lindlar catalyst (0.05 eq.) was added to a 25 mL round-bottom flask. The flask was sealed with a rubber stopper. The flask was charged with hydrogen gas and then the reaction system was replaced with hydrogen gas three times. The lipid (1.0 eq.) was dissolved in EtOAc (10 mL per 1 mmol) and then injected into the flask. The reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 12 h. After filtering the catalyst, the solvent was removed in vacuo and the crude product was purified by silica gel column chromatography with CH2Cl2 and CH3OH (50/1, v/v) as the eluent.

Preparation of LNPs

LNPs were synthesized by mixing the lipids and mLuc via pipette. Briefly, ionizable lipids, DSPC, cholesterol, and DMG-PEG2000 at a molar ratio of 50:10: 38.5:1.5, respectively, were dissolved in ethanol. mLuc was dissolved in 50 mM citrate buffer (pH = 4). The ratio of nitrogen of ionizable lipids to the phosphate of mRNA is 5.67:1. The ethanol phase and aqueous phase were rapidly mixed with a volume ratio of 1:3 using a pipette to formulate LNPs and then incubated at room temperature for 10 min. For purified lipid molecules, the resulting LNPs were dialyzed against 1 × PBS at 4 °C for 6 h using a dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 12-14 k Da (Biorigin). The mRNA content in the LNPs and mRNA encapsulation efficiency were quantified using the Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA assay (Thermo) in the presence or absence of 1% Triton-X 100. The size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential of the LNPs were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano ZSP instrument (Malvern Instruments) at 25 °C. The pKa of LNP was measured using the TNS assay following a previously published method [52].

Cell culture

Murine dendritic cells (DC2.4) were cultured in the Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in the presence of 5% CO2.

In vitro mRNA delivery efficiency of LNPs

To evaluate the mRNA delivery efficiency in vitro, DC2.4 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in a Corning® 96-well plate. The cells were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. LNPs containing 100 ng of mRNA were added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 17 h, the 96-well plate was equilibrated to room temperature for 30 min. Then, 100 μL of the culture medium was removed from each well. One-lite Luciferase Assay substrate in 100 μL of lysis buffer was added to each well. The plate was gently shaken for 5 min in the dark and the supernatant was transferred to a white 96-well plate. The luminescence was then read using a BioTek Synergy H1 multimode microplate reader (Agilent).

Animals

Female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks) were obtained from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology. All animal experiments reported here were performed according to a protocol approved by the Peking University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (No. LA2023163).

In vivo delivery efficiency of LNPs

LNPs containing 2 μg of mLuc mRNA were injected into mice through intravenous (i.v.) administration. After 3, 6, and 12 h, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane. 200 μL of potassium (S)-2-(6-hydroxybenzo[d]thiazol-2-yl)-4,5-dihydrothiazole-4-carboxylate (Bidepharm, 20 mg/mL) in PBS was intraperitoneally administered. After 10 min, the mice were imaged using an In vivo imaging system (IVIS Spectrum, Perkin Elmer). At 12 h post-injection of LNPs, mice were sacrificed, and the major organs of mice were excised for ex vivo imaging to determine the biodistribution of mRNA expression.

Statistical analysis

Data was expressed as mean ± SEM using GraphPad Prism. Statistical evaluation was analyzed using an unpaired two-tailed t-test, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. ns.: no significance, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

The A3 coupling reaction requires transition metal catalysts to activate the alkyne, forming a nucleophilic reagent that undergoes nucleophilic addition with the iminium cation formed by the reaction between aldehyde and amine. This process results in a one-step synthesis of alkyne propylamine (Scheme 1). Before synthesizing a large library of lipids using A3 coupling, we first evaluated the reaction's efficiency using CuCl as the catalyst, ethanol as the solvent, formaldehyde, dodec-1-yne, and 2-(dodecylamino) ethan-1-ol as substrates (Figure S1A, Table S1). The reaction was conducted at 55 °C with minimal formation of side products, as confirmed by TLC analysis (Figure S1B). A high yield of approximately 85% was achieved (Figure S2).

Schematic of the A3 coupling reaction and the chemical structures of substrates used for the synthesis of ionizable lipids

This clean and efficient reaction is well-suited for synthesizing large libraries of lipids for high-throughput screening.

To synthesize ILs with diverse structural properties, we prepared twenty-eight amine substrates (A1 to A28) featuring various head groups (ethanolamine, propanolamine, methylamine, diolamine), tail lengths, and degrees of saturation through nucleophilic addition between amino alcohols and bromo-alkanes (Scheme 1, Figure S3 to S30). Additionally, two amine substrates (A29 and A30) that possess methyl head groups were included in the substrates. We also obtained or synthesized twenty-one alkyne substrates (B1 to B21) with different functional groups (ether, ester, amide), chain lengths, and branched alkyl tails (Scheme 1, Figure S31 to S44). The A3 coupling reaction involving these amines, alkynes, and formaldehyde will yield a substantial lipid library (630 lipids) with diverse structural variations. These include alkyl chain lengths ranging from 12 to 22 carbons, degrees of unsaturation from 0 to 3, hydrophilicity of head groups, number of lipid tails (2 to 5), and functional groups on the tails. By introducing additional aldehydes, the number of lipids in our library can exponentially increase. Therefore, we first synthesized a library of 623 lipids by combining formaldehyde with amines (A1 to A30) and alkyne substrates (B1 to B21).

Thin layer chromatography (TLC) analysis revealed that the reactions proceeded with yields exceeding 70% (Figure S45). The reaction mixtures were subsequently used to formulate LNPs without further purification. The synthesized ILs, along with 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DSPC), cholesterol, and 1,2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycerol-methoxypolyethylene glycol 2000 (DMG-PEG2000) at molar ratios of 50:10:38.5:1.5, were dissolved in ethanol. These lipids were then mixed with firefly luciferase-encoded mRNA (mLuc) in citrate buffer to yield LNPs. The obtained LNPs were then incubated with DC2.4 cells for 17 h before measuring mLuc expression (Fig. 1A). Most ILs displayed poor mRNA delivery efficiency, as indicated in the heat map (Fig. 1B). However, a select few ILs demonstrated notably high mRNA expression levels, with A11 exhibiting the highest expression among all substrates (Fig. 1B).

A Workflow for high-throughput in vitro screening of LNPs for mRNA delivery. B The average relative luciferase expression after incubating with mLuc LNPs with DC2.4 cells is shown in a heat map. RLU represents relative light units. n = 3 technical replicates

The average values from each column of the heat map were plotted to illustrate the impact of different amine substrates on overall mRNA delivery efficiency (Fig. 2A). LNPs composed of A11, A22, and A28 showed higher in vitro transfection efficiency compared to those with other amine substrates (Fig. 2A). Notably, among the ILs synthesized with A28, only A28B10 exhibited high transfection efficiency, while the rest showed poor performance. Both A11 and A22 feature an alkyl chain with 18 carbons and a cis-double bond (Fig. 2B), indicating that this particular structure significantly influences mRNA delivery efficiency in this lipid library. The primary difference between A11 and A22 is their head groups, with A11 having ethanolamine and A22 having propanolamine. The higher mRNA expression levels observed with A11-based lipids suggest that ethanolamine head groups are superior to propanolamine.

C The mRNA delivery efficiency of LNPs that are made from ILs with different alkynes. D Chemical structures of the four top-performing alkynes. n = 3 technical replicates. Data are shown as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM)

Further comparisons show that A7, A9, A12, and A13 share the same ethanolamine head group and chain length as A11 but differ in the degree of unsaturation (A7), double bond position (A9), double bond conformation (A12), or number of double bonds (A13) (Fig. 2B). These variations result in significantly lower mRNA delivery efficiency compared to A11 (Fig. 2A). These findings suggest that cis-alkenes provide higher mRNA delivery efficiency than trans-alkenes and alkanes, and the efficiency decreases when double bonds are near the chain end or when their number increases. Additionally, the comparison between A22 and A20 indicates that lipid tails containing cis-alkenes are more effective than those containing alkanes (Fig. 2A, B).

We then plotted the average values from each row of the heat map to analyze the impact of alkyne substrates on overall mRNA delivery efficiency (Fig. 2C, D). As shown in Fig. 2C, the different alkyne substrates had minimal effect on the mRNA delivery efficiency of the ILs. These results suggest that the degradable linkers (alkane, ether, ester, amide) on the alkyl chain did not significantly influence the mRNA delivery efficiency of the LNPs. It is also possible that the effect of the alkyne substrates is overshadowed by the amine substrates, which showed a substantially higher impact on mRNA delivery efficiency.

We then investigated ILs with various aldehyde substrates. Aldehydes with longer alkyl chains can create higher steric hindrance, potentially affecting the efficiency of the A3 coupling reaction. To examine the reaction efficiency using long-chain aldehydes, we selected dodecanal, 2-(dodecylamino)ethan-1-ol and dodec-1-yne as substrates. However, the reaction was accompanied by side reactions and yielded low efficiency when using CuCl as the catalyst and EtOH as the solvent. We proceeded to optimize the catalyst, reaction temperature, and reaction time (Table S2). Ultimately, we identified the optimal reaction conditions: 120 °C for 36 h under a nitrogen atmosphere, with Zn (OTf)2 as the catalyst and toluene as the solvent. Under these conditions, the reaction yield was approximately 65% (Figure S46). Nitrogen protection was necessary to prevent side reactions, but setting up multiple reactions under nitrogen proved to be time-consuming. Consequently, we did not further explore the lipid library with long-chain aldehydes, as this approach contradicts the principle of high-throughput screening.

Among the 623 ILs screened, five ILs (A11B2, A11B4, A11B7, A11B9, and A28B10) with the highest mRNA delivery efficiency were synthesized and purified (Fig. 3A, Figure S47 to S51). These ILs were then used to formulate LNPs encapsulating mLuc, with a nitrogen-to-phosphate ratio (N/P ratio) of 5.67:1. A previously published ionizable lipid (cKK-E12) that has shown efficient mRNA delivery was used as control [49]. These LNPs exhibit hydrodynamic diameters between 140 to 180 nm and low polydispersity index (PDI < 0.2) (Fig. 3B, Table S3). The mRNA encapsulation efficiencies are between 51 to 81% (Figure S52). These data demonstrated the successful fabrication of LNPs. The in vitro mRNA delivery efficiency of these LNPs was evaluated using DC2.4 cells. As shown in Fig. 3C, A28B10 exhibited higher mRNA delivery efficacy compared with A11-based four lipids. Nonetheless, the mRNA delivery efficiencies of these new ILs were significantly lower than those of cKK-E12 (Fig. 3C).

We hypothesized that the low delivery efficiency might be due to the presence of the carbon–carbon triple bonds adjacent to the nitrogen of ILs. Compared to saturated chains, the electronegativity of triple bonds could affect the acid dissociation constant (pKa) of LNPs by reducing the basicity of the nearby nitrogen. The pKa value of LNPs greatly affects their ability of endosomal escape and the subsequent mRNA delivery [50]. To address this, we reduced the carbon–carbon triple bonds of the five ILs to alkanes using a Lindlar catalyst and hydrogen (Fig. 3D). A previous study showed that carbon–carbon triple bonds adjacent to amino or hydroxyl groups were more prone to over-reduction to alkanes [51]. 1H and 13C NMR analysis confirmed the complete reduction of carbon–carbon triple bonds while retaining the carbon–carbon double bonds in these ILs (Figure S53 to S57).

We then synthesized five new LNPs using these reduced ILs (R-A11B2, R-A11B4, R-A11B7, R-A11B9, and R-A28B10). The hydrodynamic diameters of these LNPs are between 150 to 180 nm, similar to those formulated using alkyne-based ILs (Fig. 3E, Table S3). We then tested the pKa of these LNPs using the TNS assay (Figure S58) [52]. Previous studies suggest that the ideal pKa for ILs is around 6.2 to 6.5, with efficacy decreasing as the pKa deviates from this range [53]. Figure 3F shows that the pKa values of LNPs formulated with alkyne-based ionizable lipids are between 4.1 and 4.6. After reducing the ILs, the pKa values of LNPs increased to 4.8 to 5.7. We then tested the mRNA delivery efficiency of these new LNPs in DC2.4 cell lines. As shown in Fig. 3G, except for A28B10, the mRNA delivery efficiency of the other four ILs significantly increased after reduction, with the highest increase exceeding 300 times (A11B9). These results, combined with previously published data, suggest that the position of alkyne groups is critical for the mRNA delivery efficiency of ILs, with the alkyne group needing to be placed away from nitrogen. R-A28B10 exhibits greatly reduced mRNA encapsulation efficiency compared to A28B10, which could be the potential reason for the reduced mRNA delivery efficiency after reduction.

To further validate the mRNA delivery efficiency of these ILs in vivo, we intravenously administered LNPs containing 2 μg of mLuc to C57BL/6 mice. The mice were then imaged using an in vivo imaging system to evaluate the bioluminescence intensity at 6 h post-injection. As shown in Fig. 3H, LNPs formulated with reduced ILs exhibited significantly higher mRNA expression compared to those with alkyne-based ILs, with all ILs showing mRNA expression primarily in the liver area. Among the reduced ILs, R-A11B9 showed the highest mRNA expression in vivo, surpassing R-A11B4 (Fig. 3H, I). These results further suggest that in vitro screening of ILs is a poor predictor of their in vivo efficacy. Overall, the data indicate that the presence of an alkyne group adjacent to the nitrogen negatively affects the mRNA delivery efficiency of formulated LNPs. Reducing the alkyne group of ILs synthesized by A3 coupling appears to be a promising strategy for screening more effective ILs in the future.

Despite the significant enhancement in mRNA delivery efficiency after reduction, the reduced ILs still exhibit slightly lower mRNA delivery efficiency compared to cKK- E12. The leading ILs contain long alkyl tail chains, which are typically associated with good membrane fusion capability but less ideal cellular uptake efficiency. Previous studies have shown that combining ILs bearing long alkyl chains with cKK-E12 exhibits synergistic effects to enhance mRNA delivery [21]. Therefore, we combined R-A11B2, R-A11B4, or R-A11B7 with cKK-E12 at a molar ratio of 3:7 as ILs to formulate three five-component LNPs. We then injected these LNPs containing 2 μg mLuc to C57BL/6 mice through intravenous injections. In vivo bioluminescence imaging of mice and ex vivo imaging of major organs showed that five-component LNPs exhibit significantly higher mRNA expression at all tested time points compared to cKK-E12 LNP (Fig. 4A, B). All LNPs exhibited the strongest mRNA expression in the liver, followed by the spleen (Fig. 4B, E). The area under the curve (AUC) analysis showed a 2.9-, 1.9-, and 2.9-fold increase in cumulative luciferase expression for cKK-E12/R-A11B2, cKK-E12/R-A11B4, and cKK-E12/R-A11B7, respectively, compared to cKK-E12 alone (Fig. 4C, D). These results suggest synergistic effects between the screened ILs and cKK-E12 to further improve mRNA delivery efficiency. To further explore if this synergy is applicable to other ionizable lipids, we combined R-A11B2 with MC3 at a ratio of MC3: R-A11B2 = 7:3 and evaluated the mRNA delivery efficiency in mice. We found that the MC3/R-A11B2 LNP exhibited higher mRNA delivery efficiency compared to MC3 alone (Figure S59), indicating that this synergistic effect can be applied to other ionizable lipids. Lastly, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of R-A11B2 in DC2.4 cells, both R-A11B2 LNP and cKK-E12/R-A11B2 LNPs showed negligible toxicity at all tested concentrations (Figure S60), suggesting its good safety profile.

Conclusion

We synthesized a library of alkyne-bearing ILs (623 lipids) using A3 coupling and evaluated their mRNA delivery efficiency in vitro. Initial screenings showed that ILs with specific alkyl chain structures, including 18 carbons, a cis-double bond, and ethanolamine head groups, exhibited superior mRNA delivery. Variations in the degree of unsaturation, double bond position, and chain length significantly reduced efficiency. The presence of an alkyne adjacent to the nitrogen was found to decrease the basicity and pKa of LNPs, thus negatively impacting their efficiency. By reducing these alkynes to alkanes, we achieved significantly improved mRNA delivery both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, combining the reduced ILs with cKK-E12 at a molar ratio of 3:7 led to synergistic effects, resulting in markedly enhanced mRNA expression levels in vivo. The enhanced delivery efficiency observed in the five-component LNPs underscores the potential of these optimized ILs for future mRNA therapeutic applications. Our findings pave the way for further high-throughput screening of new ILs using the A3 coupling reaction. Integrating additional aldehydes and the reduction of alkynes to alkanes into the A3 synthesis of the lipid library has great potential to exponentially increase the number of ILs and their efficiency.
