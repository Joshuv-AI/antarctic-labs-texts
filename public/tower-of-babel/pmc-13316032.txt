Cryopreservable dopaminergic progenitors derived from human iPSCs with accelerated loss of pluripotency and early functional restoration in Parkinsonian rats
Authors: Chia-Yu Chang, Cheng-Yun Chiang, Hsiao-Chien Ting, Yung-Jen Tsai, Po-Chiang Chan, Mei-Fang Chen, Yun-Ting Guo, Yuan-Hsuan Liu, Yu-Shuan Chen, Chi-Hsuan Chuang, Po-Cheng Lin, Lin-Hsiang Chuang, Mao-Hsuan Huang, Yi-Chung Tung, Po-Yi Chen, Horng-Jyh Harn, Hong-Lin Su, Shinn-Zong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMidbrain dopaminergic progenitors (mDAp) derived from human pluripotent stem cells have demonstrated promising safety and efficacy in Phase I clinical transplantation trials for Parkinson’s disease (PD). To further improve the translational potential of this approach, strategies that accelerate the loss of pluripotency, increase the yield of in vitro mDA progenitors, and promote post-transplantation neurite outgrowth may be beneficial. Here, we developed an optimized protocol building upon our previously established neural induction method, with further refinements, to efficiently convert induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) into mDA neurons. Additionally, we applied n-butylidenephthalide, which selectively reduced pluripotency-associated gene expression and enhanced neurite outgrowth during differentiation. With these improved techniques, 71% of iPSCs differentiated into mDA neurons, showing burst dopamine secretion and phasic electrophysiological activities with external stimuli. To evaluate the safety, cryopreserved mDAp were transplanted into immunodeficient mice. No teratoma or neural tumor was observed within 24 weeks post-implantation. In 6-OHDA PD rats, mDAp survived and differentiated into mDA neurons in the host striatum within eight weeks post-transplantation, leading to significant functional recovery. The current differentiation process, therefore, enables the generation of cryopreservable, off-the-shelf mDAp, with accelerated loss of the pluripotency marker OCT4 in vitro and early functional recovery following transplantation.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Stem-cell differentiation, Induced pluripotent stem cells, Stem-cell research, Translational research

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a major neurodegenerative disorder that progressively impairs motor functions and daily activities, affecting over 1% of individuals aged 65 and older1. Irreversible degeneration of A9 midbrain dopaminergic neurons (mDA neurons) in the substantia nigra is the primary cause of PD. This leads to disruption in the basal ganglia motor circuit and results in loss of motor function2. Current clinical treatments include levodopa, which replenishes dopamine levels, and deep brain stimulation, which compensates for circuit dysfunction resulting from mDA neuron loss3. Consequently, these treatments only offer partial relief, and approximately 80% of individuals may experience resistance4 or adverse effects5. Therefore, the replacement of mDA neurons continues to be a major focus in the development of therapeutic strategies for PD.

Recently, midbrain dopaminergic progenitors (mDAp) derived from human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) have been applied in animal models6–24 and human clinical trials25–28 for PD, demonstrating promising therapeutic effects. Therefore, exploring alternative strategies to minimize the risks associated with residual undifferentiated iPSCs while improving therapeutic efficacy may help facilitate the widespread adoption of this therapy. Currently, some critical targets may serve as potential entry points for further development. First, the efficiency of mDA neuron differentiation ranges from 20% to 40%12–15,17–19,23,27. To enhance the efficiency of mDA neuron production, several strategies, including fluorescence reporter and surface marker-based cell sorting12,13,25,29–32, incorporation of biomaterials16,33–36, and precise adjustment of morphogen combinations and concentrations6–10,13,16,20–22,26,36–43, have been integrated into differentiation protocols. These approaches resulted in a significant breakthrough in the production of mDA neurons. However, the complexity of cell manipulation procedures may pose challenges for clinical translation. Therefore, an alternative, simpler method to harvest high-yield mDA neurons would advance the clinical production of transplantable mDA neurons. Several studies have demonstrated that tuning the activation level of the Wnt signaling pathway and temporally controlled treatment with retinoic acid (RA) significantly enhances the efficiency of mDAp production6–10,13,16,20–22,36–43. These findings suggest that directly optimizing mDAp differentiation protocols represents the most straightforward approach to obtaining high yields of mDAp. Another challenge is the in vivo maturation of transplanted mDAp, which is closely associated with functional recovery after transplantation. Previous studies have shown that functional improvement following transplantation of human mDAp often requires several months in rodent xenotransplantation models6,8,10,12–18,20,21. The maturation timeline may differ across transplantation contexts, including nonhuman primate models19,22,23 as well as allogeneic25,26,28 and autologous27 transplantation in clinical settings. A prolonged latent period before functional recovery is therefore commonly observed. This delay represents a major limitation for clinical translation, as patients may continue to experience PD symptoms during this time. A recent study reported that physical and cognitive training accelerated graft integration and motor function improvement in a primate model44. Therefore, accelerating graft maturation and motor recovery remains an important goal for improving the therapeutic efficacy of mDAp transplantation. A third important consideration in iPSC-based PD transplantation therapy is the risk of tumorigenicity driven by cell proliferation, which could result from residual undifferentiated iPSCs or immature neural progenitors after transplantation11,45,46. Although residual pluripotent cells are rarely detected under standard differentiation conditions and tumor formation has not been a major issue, accelerated loss of pluripotency markers may nevertheless further reduce the potential risk.

In this study, we developed an efficient protocol for converting iPSCs into mDA neurons, building upon our previously established neural induction method47–50. Furthermore, we applied the chemical compound n-butylidenephthalide (n-BP) to selectively reduce pluripotency-associated gene expression and promote neurite formation during mDA differentiation. Consequently, we developed an integrated protocol to produce 71% mDA neurons. In immunodeficient mice, neither teratoma nor neural tumor formation was detected in any analyzed animals at 24 weeks post-transplantation. Within eight weeks, we observed mDA neuron maturation and neurite outgrowth from the transplanted mDAp, leading to significant motor function recovery in 6-OHDA PD rats. These findings may eventually be applied to clinical settings to address current challenges and improve the development of iPSC-based therapies for PD transplantation.

Results

Efficient generation of mDA neurons from CHSF-induced neural stem cells

Previously, we developed a differentiation method for inducing neural stem cells (NSCs) that involved the use of CHIR (a GSK-3β inhibitor), SB431542 (SB, a TGF-β1 inhibitor), and FGF-basic (FGFb), the CHSF neural induction method47–50. Based on this, we incorporated morphogens, including LDN193189 (LDN), SAG, and CHIR, to differentiate NSCs into mDA neurons6–10. As shown in Fig. 1A, various CHIR treatment conditions were tested to optimize the mDA differentiation protocol since studies have shown that different CHIR dosages significantly influence the efficiency of mDA neuron production6,7,9,16,20,22,36,37,40–43. We applied midbrain floor plate markers FOXA2 and LMX1A and mDA neuron marker tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) to evaluate the mDAp and mDA neuron purity on days 12 and 38 of differentiation. As this analysis was intended for optimization of differentiation conditions, stage-specific markers were assessed at distinct time points, whereas comprehensive molecular characterization of mDA differentiation is presented in Fig. 4. Immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining (Fig. 1B) indicated that the “high CHIR transient” condition was the only one that induced a high proportion of LMX1A and FOXA2 on day 12, leading to the highest TH expression on day 38. In contrast, the “medium CHIR transient” and “medium CHIR” conditions resulted in FOXA2 expression but almost no LMX1A expression, resulting in the lowest levels of TH expression. The “low CHIR” condition demonstrated unstable LMX1A, FOXA2, and TH expression between replicates. In addition, the “high CHIR” condition caused significant cell death during treatment (Fig. 1C). Therefore, our results suggest that transiently altering the CHIR concentration could effectively direct the patterning of NSCs toward the mDA fate, similar to previous findings reported by Kim et al.9.

n-BP selectively reduced pluripotency-associated gene expression during neural differentiation

Developing strategies to selectively reduce pluripotency-associated gene expression might reduce the tumorigenicity risk of iPSC-based cell therapy. In fact, a previous report identified quercetin, an inhibitor of the pluripotency-specific gene survivin, as an efficient agent for removing iPSCs from differentiated cells15. Based on this finding, compounds capable of targeting iPSC-specific genes may have differential effects on undifferentiated iPSCs and differentiated cells. One such compound that has come to our attention is n-BP, which has been shown to influence the expression of several iPSC-specific genes, including SOX2, DNMT1, and AXL51–55.

To assess the effect of n-BP on different cell states, undifferentiated iPSCs, mDAp, and mDA neurons at differentiation days 15 and 30 were treated with n-BP. In undifferentiated iPSCs, n-BP significantly reduced live cell number and cell viability (Fig. 2A, B), suggesting that cell death is a major contributing factor to the observed reduction in iPSC number. In contrast, mDAp and mDA neurons showed substantially lower sensitivity to n-BP treatment.

To further evaluate whether n-BP treatment influenced the ability of neurite formation and neurophysiological function, mDAp and mDA neurons were subjected to long-term (120 h) of n-BP treatment. Subsequently, they underwent terminal differentiation, followed by specific marker expression and Ca2+ flux assessments (Fig. 2C). After 120 h of 200 μM n-BP treatment, both mDAp and mDA neurons retained the ability to differentiate into functional mature neurons, as evidenced by the expression of mDA-specific protein TH (Fig. 2D), as well as Ca2+ flux responses to KCl stimulation (Fig. 2E). Thus, long-term treatment with 200 μM n-BP did not alter the properties and neurophysiological function of mDA cells.

Based on the differential sensitivity of iPSCs and mDA cells to n-BP, we hypothesized that this compound could selectively eliminate iPSCs or reduce pluripotency-associated gene expression from differentiated mDA cells. To test this hypothesis, we first examined the dynamics of the pluripotency marker OCT4 during the differentiation process. OCT4 mRNA expression rapidly decreased during mDA differentiation, reaching 16.21 ± 2.76% on day 3, 1.12 ± 0.21% on day 5, and 0.22 ± 0.05% on day 10, relative to undifferentiated iPSCs (Fig. 2F). This minimal expression level remained largely unchanged at day 20 of differentiation and was comparable to that observed in the non-pluripotent reference control, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) (Fig. 2G), suggesting that pluripotency-associated gene expression had stabilized at a background level by day 10. Because a small fraction of OCT4 RNA was still detectable on day 5, we next evaluated whether n-BP could further suppress residual pluripotency marker expression during differentiation. Cultures at day 3 and day 4 of differentiation were treated with n-BP, and OCT4 expression was analyzed on day 5. n-BP treatment significantly reduced OCT4 expression in a dose- and time-dependent manner, suggesting that n-BP preferentially suppresses pluripotent gene expression during mDA differentiation (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, live cell number and cell viability did not change after n-BP treatment (Fig. 2H), indicating that the reduction in OCT4 expression was not due to generalized cytotoxicity.

To further evaluate whether n-BP can reduce OCT4 expression during non-patterned neural differentiation, iPSC-derived neural cultures at day 5 were treated with n-BP for 24 and 48 h. After n-BP treatment, the number of OCT4+ cells significantly decreased compared to the control (Fig. S1A, S1B; quantification in Fig. S1C). The live cell number also did not change after n-BP treatment (Fig. S1D), consistent with the result of mDA experiments.

To explore the potential mechanism underlying this effect, we next examined whether n-BP affects iPSC-related gene expression. Following n-BP stimulation, AXL protein levels increased in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. S2A–C). Previous studies have reported that AXL is expressed at higher levels in NSCs than in undifferentiated iPSCs56–58, which is consistent with our results (Fig. S2D). Based on this expression pattern, we hypothesized that AXL signaling may be involved in the reduction of OCT4 expression. To test this, day 5 differentiated mDA cells were treated with the AXL ligand GAS6, which resulted in a reduction in OCT4 expression comparable to n-BP during mDA differentiation (Fig. 2G). This observation suggests that activation of AXL signaling may contribute to the suppression of pluripotency-associated gene expression in differentiating mDA cultures.

n-BP promoted mDA neurite formation

Certain phthalide analogs have demonstrated the ability to promote neurite formation in various animal and cell models of neurodegenerative diseases59–66. Interestingly, we observed that day 15 and day 30 differentiated mDA cells treated with 200 μM n-BP may have higher neurite density (Fig. 2D). To further confirm the influence of n-BP on neurite formation, we treated iPSC derived mDA cells with varying doses of n-BP for 120 h at both day 15 and day 30. Following this treatment, we conducted ICC staining for the mDA neuron-specific marker TH. Figure 3A, B show that treatment with 100 μM n-BP resulted in the highest neurite density and longest neurite length in mDA neurons at days 20 and 35, respectively. Quantitative analyses revealed a significant increase in these parameters (Fig. 3C, D for day 20 and in Fig. 3E, F for day 35).

To further evaluate the effect of n-BP on neurite growth, iPSC-derived motor neurons and non-patterned neurons were treated with n-BP. As shown in Fig. S3A and S3B, n-BP significantly promoted neurite outgrowth in iPSC-derived motor neurons. Fig. S3C illustrates neurite outgrowth in non-patterned neurons following n-BP treatment; however, the effect did not reach statistical significance (data not shown). These results suggest that n-BP may have the potential to promote neurite growth in multiple neuronal cell types.

Integrating core techniques into a differentiation process for generating functional mDA neurons

After identifying the suitable CHIR dosage and time course, we integrated the “high CHIR transient” mDA patterning condition and n-BP into our previously NSC induction protocol. Figure 4A outlines the mDA differentiation procedure. Figure 4B shows cell morphologies from days 4 to 23. The growth curve indicates that the cell number increased to approximately 328-fold by day 18 and 391-fold by day 22 of differentiation (Fig. 4C). By day 15, cells expressed early NSC markers such as SOX1 and N-cadherin (NCAD), midbrain floor plate markers including FOXA2, CORIN, and LMX1A67 (Fig. 4D). OTX2+EN1+ labeling indicated midbrain region specificity6,7,9. ICC demonstrated midbrain floor plate progenitor generation with 94.91% ± 3.22% SOX1, 94.78% ± 1.8% FOXA2, 93.73% ± 2.53% LMX1A and 83.57% ± 5.18% OTX2+EN1+ expression (Fig. 4F). TH expression became detectable by day 18 and reached its highest level at day 38, with 71.13 ± 1.96% of cells co-expressing TH and FOXA2, 71.4 ± 1.37% co-expressing TH and NURR1, and 64.61 ± 2.88% co-expressing TH and PITX3 (Fig. 4E–G). The relatively lower proportion of TH⁺PITX3⁺ cells may reflect that PITX3 is a later-stage marker and is enriched in a subset of A9-like mDA neurons. On day 55 of differentiation, TH⁺ mDA neurons co-expressed G protein–activated inwardly rectifying potassium channel 2 (GIRK2), suggesting the presence of A9-like mDA neuronal characteristics (Fig. 4E).

In whole-cell patch-clamp analysis, day 38 mDA neurons responded to voltage stimulation, showing Na+-associated inward and K+-associated outward currents (Fig. 4H). Neuronal membrane properties were analyzed via depolarizing current pulses and firing action potentials (Fig. 4I). The day 50 mDA neurons also exhibited burst-like firing patterns (> 15 spikes/s) in MEA recordings, highlighted by yellow regions (Fig. 4J). Furthermore, day 38 cells secreted dopamine after KCl stimulation, confirming classical mDA neuronal function (Fig. 4K).

To assess the efficiency of our differentiation protocol across pluripotent stem cell lines, we applied it to the human ESC line H9 and the iPSC line IMR-90. ICC analysis showed co-expression of FOXA2 and LMX1A on day 15 and TH expression by day 40 (Fig. S4A). Quantification revealed that 78.15% of H9-derived cells and 67.29% of IMR-90-derived cells co-expressed FOXA2 and TH (Fig. S4B).

Cryopreservation of mDAp

For the efficient translation of mDAp to clinical use, a reliable method for preparing off-the-shelf mDAp must be established. Cryopreservation of mDAp was utilized in this study to address this requirement. Previously, we had developed a method to cryopreserve day 32 motor neuron spheroids47,50. To determine whether this method can be applied to the preservation of mDAp, day 22 mDAp were dissociated and allowed to re-aggregate into approximately 300 μm spheroids. The spheroids were cultured overnight to allow stabilization and were cryopreserved the following day. After 3 months of cryopreservation, the spheroids were thawed and exhibited healthy morphology. Neurite outgrowth was observed as early as day 1 after thawing and was maintained through day 22, with representative morphology during the first 15 days shown in Fig. 5A. The average number of viable cells per spheroid before and after cryopreservation was 6.16 ± 1.03 × 10⁴ cells/spheroid and 5.24 ± 0.75 × 10⁴ cells/spheroid, respectively (Fig. 5B). The survival rate on the day of thawing was 85.48% ± 5.16%, with a recovery rate (defined as the ratio of post-thaw to pre-cryopreservation cell numbers) of 83.96% ± 9.96% (Fig. 5C, D). The thawed cells showed limited expansion during the early recovery phase, with cell numbers increasing approximately 1.75-fold by day 15, followed by stabilization without further proliferation from day 15 to day 22 (Fig. 5E).

On day 22 after thawing, the proportion of TH⁺FOXA2⁺ cells reached 71.04% ± 1.98% (Fig. 5F, G). No significant difference in TH⁺FOXA2⁺ expression was observed between cells cryopreserved for 3 months and 11 months (Fig. 5G). Interestingly, on day 22 after thawing (corresponding to day 45 of differentiation), mDA marker expression reached levels comparable to those observed in day 38 fresh mDA cells, suggesting that cryopreservation may slightly delay the maturation process of mDA cells.

To further evaluate the expression of NSC and proliferation markers during mDA differentiation and maturation, ICC for SOX1 and Ki67 was performed on day 14 and day 18 of differentiation, as well as on day 1 after thawing (corresponding to day 24 of differentiation) and day 15 after thawing (corresponding to day 38 of differentiation) (Fig. S5). On day 14, 79.93% of cells co-expressed SOX1 and Ki67, indicating proliferative early NSC. By day 18, SOX1 and Ki67 expression decreased to 39.82% and 15.51%, respectively. After cryopreservation and thawing, SOX1 and Ki67 expression further declined. On day 1 after thawing, 5.72% of cells were SOX1⁺ and 4.32% were Ki67⁺, with only 0.87% of cells co-expressing both markers. By day 15 after thawing, both markers were nearly undetectable. Notably, the SOX1⁺Ki67⁺ population declined rapidly during differentiation and was nearly absent 1 day after thawing, indicating a minimal proliferative NSC population at a time point corresponding to one day post-transplantation. These results are consistent with the cell growth data shown in Figs. 4C and 5E.

In vivo tumorigenicity and biodistribution assessment following transplantation

To assess the in vivo safety of mDAp derived from our method, we transplanted these cells into NPG immunodeficient mice. Eight thawed spheroids (approximately 4.2 × 105 cells) were grafted into the striatum. We first observed similar rates of sporadic death in medium control (control) and mDAp-transplanted mice during the terminal period of the experiments (Fig. 6A). Thus, mDAp transplantation did not decrease animal survival.

For tumorigenicity analysis, transplantation of undifferentiated iPSCs served as a positive control, and brain tissues were examined by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. No tumors were detected up to 24 weeks after transplantation in the mDAp group. In contrast, transplantation of undifferentiated iPSCs resulted in tumor formation in 50% of mice (4/8) (Fig. 6B), and the tumors rather exhibited teratoma-like structures (Fig. 6C). No evidence of tumor formation was observed in the mDAp group within the observation period.

Time-course analysis of Ki67 and EdU expression revealed a progressive decline in proliferating cells after transplantation. Figure 6D showed Ki67 and EdU expression at weeks 4, 8, and 24 post-transplantation, with Ki67⁺EdU⁺ cells indicated by arrowheads. The proportion of Ki67⁺ cells was 3.15% at week 4 and markedly decreased to 1.46% and 0.11% at weeks 8 and 24. Similarly, the proportion of EdU⁺ cells decreased from 1.96% at week 4 to 0.76% and 0.08% at weeks 8 and 24, respectively (Fig. 6E).

To assess the biodistribution of transplanted cells, PCR analysis was performed using human-specific primers. Human genomic DNA was not detected in the spinal cord, blood, lung, liver, spleen, or kidney of transplanted mice (4/4; representative result shown in Fig. 6F). These results indicate that transplanted cells remained confined to the brain.

mDAp survived and differentiated into mDA neurons, accelerating motor function recovery in Parkinsonian rats

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy and to characterize the identity of grafted cells, we performed rat transplantation experiments and analyzed behavioral outcomes together with co-labeling of human-specific and mDA markers (Fig. 7). Motor function was assessed using the rotameter test, and tissue analysis was conducted for up to 20 weeks following mDAp transplantation in Parkinsonian rats (Fig. 7A). Remarkably, apomorphine-induced rotation tests revealed significant functional recovery at 8 weeks post-transplantation, with a reduction from 12.34 ± 2.58 to 7.99 ± 2.05 rotations/min compared to the control group. The number of rotations continued to decrease over 24 weeks, from 11.72 ± 2.72 to 5.44 ± 1.16 rotations/min, ~54% recovery compared to the control group (Fig. 7B). To further assess motor function recovery independent of pharmacological stimulation, the cylinder test was performed. Significant improvement was observed at week 8 after mDAp transplantation (control: 63.29 ± 14.26% ipsilateral forepaw use; mDAp: 43.52 ± 7.73%), and the improvement persisted through week 16, indicating a marked improvement in forelimb asymmetry (Fig. 7C).

ICC was performed from week 2 to week 20 post-transplantation to monitor the survival of grafted cells. The graft was identified by co-labeling with the human-specific marker STEM121 and the mDA marker TH (dotted outline in Fig. 7D). Over time, the graft area appeared to increase, accompanied by a gradual decrease in cell density (Fig. S6), suggesting a more dispersed distribution of grafted cells.

By week 8, TH⁺ neurites showing overlap with STEM121 were observed extending from the graft into different regions of the striatum. These regions are highlighted by colored boxes in Fig. 7E, indicating the graft site (red), ventromedial striatum (yellow), and dorsolateral striatum (green). The blue, purple, and gray regions correspond to adjacent sections at matched anatomical positions. TH was expressed in 77.87 ± 1.44% of HuNu⁺ grafted cells (HuNu, human nuclear antigen) (Figs. 7E, F). In addition, TH⁺HuNu⁺ neurons co-expressed FOXA2 (91.21% ± 1.64%) and GIRK2 (81.59% ± 5.26%), indicating A9-like mDA neuronal characteristics in the graft after transplantation (Fig. 7E, G). Notably, 98.05 ± 2.37% of TH⁺ cells were HuNu⁺ (Fig. 7H), indicating that the majority of TH⁺ cells were derived from the graft. Medium-sized spiny neurons (MSNs) are the principal neuronal population forming dopaminergic circuits in the striatum68. Confocal imaging revealed TH-positive neurites in close apposition to DARPP32-positive MSNs (Fig. 7E, arrowheads).

By week 20 post-graft, TH⁺STEM121⁺ nerve fibers were observed at the graft site and in the striatum (Fig. 7I), with the blue box corresponding to an adjacent section at a matched anatomical position. TH+ fibers in close association with DARPP32-positive MSNs were also noted (Fig. 7I, indicated by arrowheads). IHC staining of the human-specific markers HuNu and STEM121 confirmed that human cells were retained at the graft site and extended nerve fibers into the striatum at 20 weeks post-transplantation (Fig. S7). Our in vivo findings demonstrate maturation and migration of grafted cells, along with motor functional recovery within eight weeks after mDAp transplantation.

To determine whether n-BP treatment during the differentiation process influences transplantation outcomes, mDAp generated without n-BP were transplanted and rotational behavior was evaluated. Although these grafts also led to functional recovery, the improvement occurred later than that observed in rats receiving n-BP-pretreated mDAp (Figs. S8A and 7A). ICC analysis at week 8 post-transplantation revealed limited TH+STEM121+ neurite extension into the ventromedial and dorsolateral striatum (Fig. S8B). The limited neurite projection may contribute to the slower behavioral recovery.

In the mDAp transplantation model, 4 of 13 rats exhibited marked cortical injury along the injection tract. To evaluate its potential impact, animals with minimal cortical injury (n = 4) were used for comparison (Fig. S9). No obvious difference in apomorphine-induced rotational behavior was observed between groups, suggesting that cortical injury was unlikely to substantially affect functional recovery in this study.

Discussion

We found that n-BP exhibits two distinct effects during neural differentiation, including reduction of pluripotency-associated gene expression and enhancement of neurite outgrowth in mDA differentiation. The anticancer and neurotrophic effects of n-BP and its analogs have been extensively investigated in previous studies51–55,59–66. Here, we found that n-BP increased AXL expression in human iPSCs. Both previous reports56–58 and our results showed that AXL is expressed at higher levels in early NSC than in undifferentiated iPSCs. Consistent with this observation, treatment with the AXL ligand GAS6 also reduced OCT4 expression during mDA differentiation, suggesting that activation of AXL signaling may contribute to the reduction of pluripotency-associated gene expression during differentiation. In addition, n-BP treatment promoted neurite outgrowth in mDA cells and other neuronal types. Although the mechanism underlying this effect was not investigated in the present study, previous studies have suggested that AXL signaling may participate in neuronal maturation and neurite development69, which may potentially contribute to this phenotype.

In our experiments, n-BP also exhibited cell-state–dependent effects on cell viability. In undifferentiated iPSC cultures maintained under pluripotency conditions, n-BP significantly reduced cell viability, indicating that pluripotent cells are particularly sensitive to n-BP treatment. In contrast, during neural differentiation, n-BP reduced OCT4 expression without significantly affecting overall cell viability. One possible explanation is that the reduction of pluripotency-associated gene expression may have different consequences depending on the cellular context. Under pluripotency-maintaining culture conditions, downregulation of OCT4 may conflict with the signaling environment that supports pluripotent state maintenance, potentially leading to cellular stress or reduced survival. In contrast, during neural differentiation, where loss of pluripotency is part of the developmental program, reduced OCT4 expression may not lead to widespread cytotoxicity. However, further studies would be required to determine whether AXL signaling directly mediates these effects.

Electrophysiological maturation of the differentiated neurons was assessed using MEA recordings, which revealed spontaneous firing activity and episodes of increased network activity, consistent with burst-like patterns. However, classical burst firing, a hallmark feature of midbrain dopaminergic neurons, is typically evaluated at the single-cell level using patch clamp recordings and has been reported only in a limited subset of human stem cell–derived dopaminergic neurons, often at advanced maturation stages9. In the present study, canonical burst firing was not observed, suggesting that although the cells exhibit functional neuronal activity, further maturation and electrophysiological characterization will be required to fully establish their intrinsic dopaminergic firing properties.

In the mDAp transplantation model, some rats exhibited cortical injury along the injection tract. Comparison with animals showing minimal injury revealed no obvious difference in rotational behavior, suggesting limited impact on functional recovery. Nevertheless, procedure-related brain injury remains a concern. Previous studies have indicated that needle insertion can induce mechanical damage and secondary inflammation14,70–72. In our study, the observed injury is likely associated with the use of a relatively large-bore needle (22G), which was required to deliver spheroid-like aggregates and prevent clogging during transplantation. For clinical translation, additional refinements such as reducing aggregate size or using smaller-gauge or modified cannulas would likely be required to minimize tissue damage while maintaining reliable cell delivery.

Developing a simple method to achieve high-yield mDA neurons would advance clinical production. Fundamentally, optimizing differentiation methods is the most straightforward approach. Current methods usually involve a two-step process starting with dual SMAD inhibition to induce NSC differentiation73, followed by the use of neural tube developmental patterning ligands to guide NSCs toward the ventral midbrain floor plate. This process involves morphogens, including sonic hedgehog (Shh), Wnt activators, BMP inhibitors, and FGF-8b6–10,74. FGF-8b and Wnt signaling are key regulators for generating midbrain-specific neuron types, but appropriate CHIR concentration6,7,9,16,20,22,36,37,40–43 and timing10,13,38,39 are important for positioning mDA progenitors within the midbrain fate. In several previously reported protocols, CHIR concentrations ranging from 0.4 to 1 μM have been used6,7,16,20,22,36,40,41,43, and variations in CHIR concentration or activity may influence the consistency of mDA differentiation. To address these issues, novel strategies involving biphasic Wnt activation9,21 and timed retinoic acid8 treatment have shown promise in producing high-purity mDA neurons without the need to fine-tune CHIR concentrations. In this study, we established an alternative, highly efficient mDA differentiation method based on our previously developed CHSF NSC induction method. By using a simple set of patterning factors, including transiently increasing CHIR9,37, we achieved 71% mDA neurons within 38 days of differentiation. This method is straightforward and easy to handle, requiring no gene editing, cell sorting, biomaterials, FGF-8b, or fine-tuning of CHIR concentration.

Previous studies have evaluated the expression of the NSC marker SOX1 and the cell-cycle marker Ki67 to assess the proliferative risk of mDAp intended for transplantation. In most reports, SOX1 positivity typically ranged from <2% to approximately 10%20,24, whereas Ki67 expression ranged from <1% to about 20%12,14,15 in cell populations used for transplantation. In some studies, the combined expression of SOX1, PAX6, and Ki67 was further analyzed to assess residual neural progenitor characteristics and proliferative potential12,15,17,24. In our differentiation protocol, mDAp exhibited robust proliferative activity before day 18, followed by a progressive decline. After thawing, the mDAp displayed limited proliferative activity and no evident expansion during the late maturation stage (days 15–22 after thawing), while expression of the mDA marker TH reached its maximal level. ICC results were consistent with the cell growth curve, showing that SOX1 and Ki67 expression peaked around day 14 and gradually declined thereafter. On day 1 after thawing, 5.72% and 4.32% of cells expressed SOX1 and Ki67, respectively, while only 0.87% of cells co-expressed both markers. During the late maturation stage after thawing, the expression of both markers became nearly undetectable. Notably, these expression levels fall within the range reported in previous studies for transplantation-competent mDAp. Following transplantation, a small proportion of Ki67⁺ cells was detected at week 4, which further declined to nearly undetectable levels by week 24 in NPG mice. In the PD rat model, graft volume increased over time, accompanied by a significant reduction in cell density, consistent with a previous report21. These findings suggest that the increase in graft volume is unlikely to result from continuous cell proliferation, but may instead reflect limited early proliferation followed by cellular rearrangement within the graft. In addition, a small number of rosette-like NSC structures were observed in early grafts in the PD rat model but were no longer detected at the endpoint (data not shown), indicating progressive maturation of the transplanted cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that the transplanted cells exhibit limited and self-restricted proliferative activity after transplantation, supporting the safety profile of the graft. Importantly, no abnormal overgrowth or tumor-like structures were observed throughout the observation period in either mice or rats.

Tumor formation can arise from residual undifferentiated iPSCs. In addition, graft overgrowth derived from proliferative neural progenitors is theoretically possible. In our previous work46, we used an earlier differentiation protocol to generate mDA progenitors for transplantation. In that study, graft overgrowth was observed at the injection site, although the precise cellular origin of this overgrowth was not determined. In the current study, we implemented a refined differentiation protocol that includes n-BP treatment together with several adjustments to optimize the differentiation conditions. Using this updated protocol, we did not observe tumor formation or abnormal graft overgrowth after transplantation, suggesting improved graft safety relative to our previously reported differentiation protocol.

The functional improvement observed at 8 weeks post-transplantation suggests that graft-derived mDA neurons may contribute to early functional effects. In this study, TH+ neurons were already present at this time point, accompanied by evident neurite extension into the host striatum. In addition, the expression of key mDA markers in vivo was comparable to that observed during in vitro differentiation, supporting the establishment of an mDA identity. Importantly, the control group underwent the same surgical procedure and received vehicle injection without cells, yet did not exhibit comparable functional improvement, suggesting that the observed effects are unlikely to be attributable to surgical intervention or injection alone. Control experiments using cells differentiated without n-BP treatment also did not exhibit comparable neurite extension or behavioral improvement, suggesting that the observed functional changes may be associated with the differentiation state and integration capacity of the grafted cells. These findings support a model in which early mDA characteristics, together with neurite outgrowth, may contribute to the observed functional improvement.

Our in vivo results showed early motor function recovery within eight weeks post-transplantation, as demonstrated by a significant reduction in apomorphine-induced rotational behavior. By 24 weeks post-transplantation, rotation decreased by approximately 54% compared to the control group. Rotational behavior was evaluated using apomorphine, a widely used assay that reflects postsynaptic dopamine receptor supersensitivity in the lesioned striatum. Because apomorphine directly stimulates postsynaptic dopamine receptors, the magnitude of rotation reduction is generally smaller than that observed in amphetamine-induced rotation tests12,75,76, which depend on presynaptic dopamine release. Therefore, results obtained using these two assays are not directly comparable. Notably, when compared with previous studies that also used apomorphine-induced rotation, the overall degree of behavioral recovery observed in our study was comparable77, while functional improvement was observed at an early time point in our model12,75,78–80. Notably, the functional improvement observed here was achieved using mDAp alone, whereas greater recovery in the same model and apomorphine-based behavioral assay has been reported when mDAp were co-transplanted with regulatory T-cell14. The cylinder test also showed significant improvement in forelimb use asymmetry by eight weeks post-transplantation. Furthermore, animals transplanted with n-BP–treated cells exhibited earlier behavioral recovery compared to those receiving cells generated without n-BP treatment, supporting the beneficial effect of this differentiation strategy.

The conclusion of this study was summarized as Fig. 8. Our results demonstrate that n-BP can reduce pluripotency-associated gene expression and promote neurite elongation. The CHSF-based mDA differentiation protocol consistently yields high numbers of mDA neurons without complex manipulations. By combining these core techniques, we established a method to achieve 71% FOXA2+TH+ mDA neurons after differentiation. In PD animal studies, we found accelerated motor function recovery within eight weeks, accompanied by extensive spreading of mDA nerve fibers. Furthermore, no tumors or abnormal tissue overgrowth was detected in NPG mice. Thus, our results suggest that optimizing the differentiation and preparation of mDAp can improve transplantation outcomes, which may contribute to the future development of iPSC-based PD therapy.

Methods

iPSC line establishment and culture

An iPSC line was generated from PBMCs of a healthy donor in our laboratory in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital (IRB105-131-A). The H9 human ESC and IMR-90 iPSC lines were purchased from WiCell (Madison, WI, USA). The PBMC-derived iPSC line was established as follows. The donor provided informed consent. PBMCs were cultured in StemPro 34 SFM (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with SCF, FLT-3, IL-3, and IL-6 (all from Peprotech, Cranbury, NJ, USA). Reprogramming was carried out using the CytoTune iPS 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) following the kit manual81. Emerging colonies were transferred to culture dishes coated with 1% Geltrex (ThermoFisher Scientific) and expanded in either TeSR-E8 medium (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) or Pluto human iPS/ES cell culture medium (DuoGenic Stem Cells, Taichung, Taiwan). The iPSCs could also be successfully cultured and expanded in Duo-ESy iPS/ES culture medium82 (DuoGenic Stem Cells) without requiring a pre-coating process. iPSCs were passaged every 3–5 days using Accutase (Merck-Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and reseeded at 1:5–1:10 ratios. In addition, 5 μM of Y27632 (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) was added during the first 24 h to avoid iPSC blebbing. Culture media were refreshed daily. The classical morphology of the iPSC line and its normal karyotype are shown in Fig. S10A and S10B, respectively. The cells expressed pluripotency markers (SSEA4, SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG; Fig. S10C) and were capable of differentiating into mesoderm (T/Brachyury⁺), endoderm (SOX17⁺), and ectoderm (SOX1⁺/NCAD⁺) lineages in vitro (Fig. S10D).

mDA neuron differentiation

When iPSCs reached 80% confluence in a culture dish, they were treated with Accutase and reseeded at ratio 1:10 in culture dishes coated with 1% Geltrex. The day of cell passage was considered day 0 of mDA differentiation. During cell seeding, the culture media were switched to neural induction medium supplemented with RevitaCell supplement (ThermoFisher Scientific). The neural induction medium consisted of DMEM-F12 basal medium supplemented with 1% N2 supplement, 1 mM of nonessential amino acids (NEAAs), and 2 mM of glutamate (all from ThermoFisher Scientific). NSC induction factors, including CHIR99021 (Cayman Chemical), SB431542 (Sigma-Aldrich), and recombinant human FGF-2 (rh-FGF2; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), were added from day 0 to day 12. On day 2 of differentiation, mDA patterning factors, including LDN193189 (Cayman Chemical) and Smoothened agonist (SAG; Cayman Chemical), were added from day 2 to 12. The concentrations of the used factors were as follows: SB431542, 10 μM; FGF-2, 10 ng/mL; and LDN193189, 0.2 μM. The CHIR99021 concentration was 0.6 μM from day 0 to 4, increased to 6 μM from day 4 to 8, and was reduced to 0.6 μM from day 8 to 12. The SAG concentration was 1 μM from day 2 to day 6 and reduced to 0.5 μM from day 6 to day 12. The reduction of SAG concentration after day 5 was empirically optimized during protocol development to improve culture stability while maintaining efficient mDA differentiation. On day 8, cells were treated with Accutase and reseeded at a ratio of 1:10 in 1% Geltrex-coated culture dishes, and the culture medium was switched to neurobasal medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) supplemented with 1% N2 supplement, 1 mM NEAAs, and 2 mM glutamate, without any induction factors. Cells were passaged again at a ratio of 1:5 on day 15. From day 15 to day 20, 100 μM n-BP was added to the medium. For subsequent experimental assays, cells were maintained in neurobasal medium and cultured up to differentiation day 40–50. Cells were passaged with Accutase upon reaching confluency. For mDA neuron maturation, cultured cells were supplemented with 0.2 μM compound E (Cayman Chemical), brain-derived neurotrophic factor, glial cell-derived neurotrophic factor, and dibutyryl cyclic adenosine monophosphate starting on day 15 of differentiation.

mDAp cryopreservation and thawing

On day 22, adherent cells were dissociated using Accutase and formed into spheroids for 1 day. On day 23, approximately 40 spheroids with a diameter of ~200 μm were suspended in 1 mL of STEM-CELLBANKER Stem Cell Freezing Media (Amsbio) supplemented with ROCK inhibitor (ROCKi) and 1% RevitaCell (ThermoFisher Scientific). The cell suspension was then transferred to 1-mL cryotubes. These cryotubes were placed in a Cryo 1 °C freezing container (Nalgene) pre-cooled to 4 °C and filled with fresh isopropanol. The freezing container was then transferred to a −80°C freezer for 24 h and subsequently moved into a liquid nitrogen tank for long-term storage. For thawing, a cryotube containing the spheroids was transferred into a 37 °C water bath until only a small amount of unmelted ice remained in the tube. Then, the spheroids were quickly transferred into a 15-mL centrifuge tube and immediately supplemented with 2 mL of DMEM-F12. Then, the tube was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 2 min and the supernatant was discarded. Next, the tube was gently tapped to loosen the pellet, and the spheroids were transferred to neurobasal media supplemented with 1% N2, 1 mM NEAAs, 2 mM glutamate, and 2% B27. For further experiments of thawed mDAp, the spheroids were transferred to a dish coated with 1% Geltrex for adhesion and cultured as freshly differentiated mDAp. During the first 24 h after thawing, 1% RevitaCell supplement was added to the culture medium. For transplantation, the thawed spheroids were directly subjected to injection without further manipulation.

Non-patterned neuron and motor neuron differentiation

The CHSF differentiation protocol was performed as previously described47,48,50. Briefly, iPSCs at ~80% confluence were treated with 1 mg/mL Dispase II, dissociated into ~200 μm clusters, and transferred to ultra-low attachment dishes in TeSR-E6 medium to form embryoid bodies (EBs). ROCK inhibitor Y-27632 (5 μM) was added during the first 4 h. Neural induction was performed in DMEM/F12 supplemented with 1% N2, 1 mM NEAAs, and 2 mM glutamate, with 3 μM CHIR99021, 10 μM SB431542, and 10 ng/mL FGF-2 from days 1–4. On day 4, the medium was changed to Neurobasal medium containing 1% N2, 1 mM NEAAs, and 2 mM glutamate. For motor neuron patterning, 3 μM CHIR99021, 2 μM SB431542, 200 nM LDN193189, 0.5 μM SAG, and 0.5 μM retinoic acid were added until day 25, whereas non-patterned neuronal cultures were maintained without these patterning factors. B27 (2%) was added from days 4–15. EBs were dissociated into 200–300 μm clusters every 2–3 days and cultured for an additional 4–8 weeks with medium changes every other day.

Live cell number and cell viability

Cultured cells were resuspended to ensure an even distribution. An equal volume of the cell suspension was mixed with 0.4% Trypan Blue and incubated for 1–2 minutes. A coverslip was placed on the hemocytometer, and 10 µL of the stained cell mixture was carefully pipetted into one of the counting chambers, allowing it to spread by capillary action. Under a microscope, both unstained (viable) and blue-stained (non-viable) cells were counted within the four large corner squares of the grid. The live cell number was calculated by multiplying the cell concentration by the suspension volume, and was determined by normalizing the viable cell count to the control group, which was set at 100%. Cell viability was determined as the percentage of trypan blue–negative cells.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from PBMCs, undifferentiated iPSCs, and iPSC-derived mDA cells at different stages of differentiation, with or without treatment with n-BP (Sigma-Aldrich) or Recombinant Human GAS6 Protein (R&D Systems), using TRIzo Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 5 μg RNA was reverse-transcribed using SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using PowerTrac SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The primer sequences used were as follows: hOCT4 forward, 5′-TGGGCTCGAGAAGGATGTG-3′; hOCT4 reverse, 5′-GCATAGTCGCTGCTTGATCG-3′; hGAPDH forward, 5′-AACAGCGACACCCACTCCTC-3′; and hGAPDH reverse, 5′-GTCCACCACCCTGTTGCTGT-3′. Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH.

ICC staining

First, iPSCs and differentiated cells were seeded in 24-well plates and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature. Subsequently, they were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-100 for 15 min at 4°C and then blocked with 5% horse serum. Primary antibodies were incubated with cells at 4°C for 16 h, washed twice, and conjugated with secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies used in this study included antibodies against OCT4 (1:200, sc-8628, RRID:AB_653551), Brachyury (1:50, sc-17743, RRID:AB_634980), N-cadherin (1:500, sc-53488, RRID:AB_628822), SOX1 (1:200, sc-17318, RRID:AB_2195365), FOXA2 (1:100, sc-6554, RRID:AB_2262810) (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology); NANOG (1:30, AF1997, RRID:AB_355097), CORIN (1:20, MAB2209, RRID:AB_2082224), OTX2 (1:30, AF1979, RRID:AB_2157172), SOX17 (1:20, AF1924, RRID:AB_355060) and NURR1 (1:100, PP-N1404-00, RRID:AB_2251476) (all from R&D systems); PAX6 (1:100, 901301, RRID:AB_2749901), Nestin (1:500, 841901, RRID:AB_2565468), βIII-tubulin (1:500, 801201, RRID:AB_2313773) (all from Covance, Princeton, NJ, USA); NF (1:500, N4142, RRID:AB_477272), TH (1:200, AB152, RRID:AB_390204), NeuN (1:100, MAB377, RRID:AB_2298772) and PITX3 (1:100, HPA044639, RRID:AB_10964194) (all from Sigma-Aldrich); SOX2 (1:200; GTX101507, RRID:AB_2038021, GeneTex, Irvine, CA, USA), SSEA4 (1:100; 560073, RRID:AB_1645601, BD Pharmingen, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA); EN1 (1:50; 4G11c, RRID:AB_528219, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, DSHB, Iowa City, IA, USA); as well as LMX1A (1:100; ab139726, RRID:AB_2827684) and Ki67 (1:250; ab16667, RRID: AB_302459) (all from Abcam, Cambridge Biomedical Campus, Cambridge, UK); TH (1:200, P60101-150, RRID:AB_3677426, Pel-Freez Biologicals, Rogers, AR, USA). Cell nuclei were stained with diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Fluorescence images were captured using an upright microscope (Imager Z1, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). SOX1, FOXA2, LMX1A, TH and other markers were counted using Image J, normalized, and presented as the ratio (%) of DAPI+ cells. Each identifiable DAPI+ single nucleus not overlapping with other nuclei was included for specific marker counting. Neurite density and nerve fiber length were calculated using Olympus CellSens Dimension Desktop 3.1 (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) in images from three independent experiments.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in a 200 μL lysis buffer consisting of Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (M-PER), 1% phosphatase protease inhibitor, and 50 mM EDTA. Subsequently, they were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm and 4°C for 15 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Purified proteins (15 mg) were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h, and probed with primary antibodies against GAPDH (1:1000, 2118, RRID:AB_561053), AXL (1:1000, 8661, RRID:AB_11217435) and DNMT1 (1:1000, 5032, RRID:AB_10548197) (All from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MD, USA), and SOX2 (1:1000; GTX101507, RRID:AB_2038021, GeneTex) at 4°C overnight. Membranes were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 before incubating with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. All proteins were detected using Immobilon Crescendo Western HRP substrate (Millipore) and recorded with FUSION Solo S (Vilber, Collégien, France). The band intensity in the Western blot was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health) and subjected to statistical analysis.

Calcium imaging

Cells were seeded on Geltrex-coated 10-mm cover slides and cultured in physiological buffer containing 1 μM of Fluo-4 at 37°C for 40 min. Subsequently, they were incubated in physiological buffer (comprising 30 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES) for 20 min. Then, cells were transferred to a Ca2+ imaging chamber, washed with physiological buffer for 30 s, and treated with 60 mM KCl for 60 s. After a 5-min wash in physiological buffer, cells were treated with 1 mM L-glutamate for 60 s. Finally, images were captured using an ECLIPSE Ti2-E microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and analyzed with NIS-Elements AR software (Nikon).

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings

Two solutions were utilized in this protocol: artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) and a pipette solution. The aCSF contained 127 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 26 mM NaHCO3, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, and 10 mM D-glucose (pH 7.45). The pipette solution consisted of 140 mM K gluconate, 10 mM NaCl, 0.5 mM EGTA, 10 mM HEPES, 3 mM ATP-Mg, and 0.4 mM GTP (pH 7.3, adjusted with KOH). Electrodes were pulled from 1.5-mm outer diameter and 1.0-mm inner diameter capillary glass (World Precision Instruments PG52151-4) using a Sutter P-97 puller (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA, USA), and the tips were slightly fire-polished using an MF-830 microforge (Narishige, Tokyo, Japan). After filling with the pipette solution, the electrode impedance of the aCSF solution was 6–15 MΩ. The series resistance was not compensated, and the junction potential zeroed. DA neurons on coverslips were transferred to a recording chamber continuously perfused with aCSF. MN responses were recorded using an Axoclamp 200B amplifier (Axon Instruments, Union City, CA, USA). The voltage clamp mode was used to examine ion channel or receptor-mediated currents and intrinsic membrane properties, while the current-clamp mode was used to investigate the firing properties of DA neurons. Cells were classified as neurons only if they exhibited fast-inactivating inward currents. Two recording protocols were employed to elicit cell responses: (1) The membrane potential was held at −60 mV, and increased from −80 to +40 mV in 10 mV increments for 400 ms to assess the presence of Na+ and K+ currents. (2) In current-clamp mode, current steps from −60 to +120 pA in 20 pA increments were applied to the cells to examine their ability to generate action potentials. Data were recorded and analyzed using Digidata 1322 A and pClamp 9.0 software (Axon Instruments, Inc.).

Microelectrode array (MEA) recording using the MED64 system

Neuronal activity of iPSC-derived mDA neurons was analyzed using a MED64 multi-electrode array system (Alpha MED Scientific, Osaka, Japan). Cells were dissociated by ACCUMAX cell detachment solution (StemCell Technologies) and plated onto polyethyleneimine/laminin-coated MED64 probe dishes (MED-P210A) containing 64 planar microelectrodes and maintained under standard culture conditions. Spontaneous extracellular field potentials were recorded at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂. Signals were amplified and digitized using the MED64 Mobius recording system and analyzed with MED64 Mobius software. Spikes were detected using a threshold above the background noise level for each electrode. The firing rate was calculated to evaluate the electrophysiological maturation of mDA neurons.

Dopamine release analysis

For dopamine measurement experiments, day 22 mDA neurons were dissociated, plated onto Geltrex-coated 24-well plates in 5 × 105 cells, and used by day 38. Prior to supernatant collection, cells were incubated in fresh NeuroBasal media with N2 for 30 min. After exposure to either Tyrode’s saline alone or supplemented with high KCl (40 mM, Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature, the supernatant was collected and mixed with perchloric acid (0.1 M final concentration). Samples were sonicated at room temperature for 10 min, centrifuged at 10,000 g for 5 min, stored at −80 °C, and analyzed by LC MS/MS.

Animals

Male Sprague–Dawley rats (8–10 weeks of age) and NPG mice (NOD.Cg-Prkdc^scid Il2rg^tm1Vst/Vst, 6–8 weeks of age, male) were procured from the National Laboratory Animal Center and BioLASCO company, Taiwan. Animals were housed in ventilated micro isolation cages under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Laboratory Animal Center of National Chung Hsing University. The animals were maintained on a 12-h light-dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water, and regulated ambient temperature and humidity. All animal-related procedures were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the US National Institutes of Health and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Chung Hsing University (approval number: 112-134 R), Taiwan.

PD animal model surgery

To generate the PD model, rats were placed in a stereotactic apparatus (David Kopf Instruments, CA, USA). Burr holes were drilled in the right side of the skull, and 6-OHDA (30 µg/6 µL; Sigma-Aldrich) or 0.2% ascorbic acid saline solution (sham) was injected into the ascending mesostriatal pathway (4.4 mm posterior to bregma, 1.2 mm lateral to the midline, 7.8 mm below the dura) near the medial forebrain bundle to remove dopaminergic innervation to the striatum.

Rotational and cylinder behavior test

Motor imbalance in 6-OHDA lesioned animals was assayed by apomorphine (1 mg/kg, ip; Sigma-Aldrich)-induced rotation. The rats were placed on a multichannel motor meter (Med Associates, Fairfax, VT, USA) connected to a computer to record clockwise or counterclockwise rotation. Apomorphine-induced rotation was evaluated at 4 weeks after lesioning with 6-OHDA. Rats rotating >300 turns in 60 min (5 turns/min) were recruited and randomly assigned for mDAp implantation.

Forelimb use asymmetry was assessed using the cylinder test. Rats were individually placed in a transparent acrylic cylinder and allowed to explore freely for 5 min while being video-recorded. During spontaneous rearing, wall contacts made with the left forelimb, right forelimb, or both forelimbs simultaneously were counted. Only weight-bearing contacts during rearing or landing were included, whereas contacts associated with grooming or non–weight-bearing movements were excluded.

mDAp transplantation

After the first rotational behavior test, the rats were assigned to control and mDAp groups based on their initial rotation counts to ensure that the starting rotation levels were comparable between the two groups. Under gas anesthesia, the head of the experimental animals was shaved and secured in a stereotaxic apparatus (David Kopf Instruments, CA, USA). After disinfecting the scalp with iodine and alcohol, an incision was made using a surgical blade to expose the bregma, serving as a reference point. A hole was drilled at the following coordinates: AP, 1 mm; L, 3 mm, targeting the striatum with an electric bone drill. A 50 µL Syringe (Model 705 RN, Hamilton Company, Reno, NV, USA) and a 22-gauge Small Hub RN Needle (Hamilton Company) were used to inject cells at two sites, at 4 and 5 mm depth (striatum coordinates from bregma: A, +1.0; L, −3.0; V, −5.0, and −4.0; and TB, 0). Utilizing a microinfusion pump (KDS 310, KD Scientific, MA, USA), a mDAp solution at a concentration of 105 cells/μL (mDAp transplantation) and solution only (control) were delivered (2 μL/site; total 4 μL, eight mDAp-spheroids, equivalent to 4 × 105 cells) at a rate of 1 μL/min. Following completion, the needle was left in place for 3 minutes before slow withdrawal. The skull defect was filled with bone wax, and the wound was sutured, disinfected with iodine, and coated with chloramphenicol. Subsequently, the experimental animals were transferred to clean cages and allowed to recover from anesthesia. Starting two days before mDAp transplantation, rats were treated with 15 mg/kg cyclosporine via intraperitoneal injections daily until 26 weeks post-transplantation.

For safety assessment, mDAp and undifferentiated iPSC were transplanted into NPG mice using a similar stereotaxic procedure. A burr hole was drilled at AP + 0.5 mm and L − 1.9 mm in the right hemisphere, and the needle was inserted to a depth of 3 mm. A total of 2 μL cell suspension (2 × 10⁵ cells/μL) was injected into the brain using a microsyringe.

Tissue collection, IHC, and ICC

At different time points of the mDAp transplantation study, animals were deeply anesthesized by intraperitoneal ketamine–xylazine injection, followed by intracardiac perfusion with ice-cold PBS for 10 min. After perfusion, the brain was collected and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Sequential dehydration was performed using 5–30% sucrose (Sigma-Aldrich), followed by embedding in optimal cutting temperature compound (O.C.T., Sakura Tissue-Tek, Torrance, CA, USA). Then, samples were sliced into 20 μm thick sections using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S, Nussloch, Germany) at −20 °C. Next, the tissue sections were mounted onto glass slides and air-dried at room temperature for ≥24 h before staining. Prior to staining, the slides with tissue sections were stored at −80 °C.

The Novolink™ DAB (Polymer) kit (Leica, cat. No. RE7230-K) was used for IHC. Tissue sections from the different experimental groups were retrieved from the −80 °C freezer and allowed to thaw at room temperature for 30 min. Then, sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min. To facilitate antigen retrieval, clean tissue sections were immersed in sodium citrate buffer (10 mM sodium citrate, 0.05% Tween-20, Sigma-Aldrich, pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 3 min. Subsequently, the sections were treated with Peroxidease Block buffer for 5 min, washed thrice with TBS buffer for 5 min followed by Protein Block buffer for 10 min. After washing thrice with TBS buffer for 5 min, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody human NCAM/CD56 (1:200, sc-106, RRID:AB_627128, Santa Cruz) at 4°C for 24 h to terminate the reaction. The sections were then washed thrice with TBS buffer for 5 min. Next, the Post Primary Block buffer was applied for 10 min, followed by washing twice with TBS buffer for 5 min. Then, a polymer buffer was applied for 10 min, followed by washing twice with TBS buffer for 5 min. DAB was applied for 2–5 min; to stop the reaction, the slides were quickly immersed in ddH2O. Hematoxylin buffer was applied for 2–10 min, followed by immersion of the slides in ddH2O for 5 min. Finally, the slides were coverslipped. Finally, images were captured with a microscope (BX61VS, Olympus Corporation) and processed using Olympus CellSens Dimension Desktop 3.1.

For ICC, tissue sections from different experimental groups underwent the following protocol. First, they were washed thrice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 10 min. Subsequently, they were washed thrice with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min at 4 °C. Sections were then treated with TrueBlack Lipofuscin Autofluorescence Quencher (Cell Signaling Technology) diluted 1:20 in 70% ethanol and applied to completely cover the tissue sections for 30 s at room temperature to reduce autofluorescence. After washing, sections were blocked with BlockPro Blocking Buffer (Visual Protein, New Taipei City, Taiwan). Cells were incubated with the primary antibodies at 4°C for 16 h, washed thrice, and conjugated with secondary antibodies. The primary antibodies used in this study included FOXA2 (1:100, sc-6554, RRID:AB_2262810) and SOX1 (1:200, sc-17318, RRID:AB_2195365) (Both from Santa Cruz Biotechnology); TH (1:200, AB152, RRID:AB_390204, Sigma-Aldrich), GIRK2 (1:100, ab65096, RRID:AB_1139732), DARPP32 (1:100, ab40801, RRID:AB_731843) and Human Nuclear Antigen (HuNu; 1:200, ab191181, RRID:AB_2885016) (All from Abcam); and STEM121 (1:500, Y40410, RRID:AB_2801314, Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BX61VS, Olympus Corporation) and a confocal microscope (ANDOR BC43, Oxford Instruments, Abingdon-on-Thames, Oxford, UK). Finally, images were processed using Olympus CellSens Dimension Desktop 3.1 and IMARIS (Oxford Instruments). SOX1, Ki67, FOXA2, GIRK2, STEM121, and TH expression was quantified using ImageJ.

EdU incorporation assay in vivo

Animals were intraperitoneally injected with EdU (50 mg/kg; MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) 24 h before sacrifice. After perfusion fixation, brain tissues were collected, sectioned, and processed for EdU staining using a Click-iT Plus EdU Alexa Fluor 594 Imaging Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. EdU-positive cells in the graft were analyzed by fluorescence microscopy.

Genomic DNA PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tissues using a PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A total of 100 ng genomic DNA was used for PCR amplification with amaR OnePCR (GeneDireX, Inc., Taoyuan, Taiwan). PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel. DNA bands were visualized using OneMARK 100 DNA ladder (GeneDireX, Inc.). The primer sequences used were as follows: human chromosome forward, 5′-ATTGCCCCAAAACTTTTTTG-3′, and reverse, 5′-TTGAAGACCAGTCTGGGAAG-3′; mouse DNA forward, 5′-CCACATCTCCCTCCAGAAAA-3′, and reverse, 5′-AGGGTCTGGGCCATAGAACT-3′.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected from ≥3 independent experimental replicates and were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). For ICC and IHC image analysis, at least two images were analyzed per independent experiment. Statistical analyses were conducted using Student's t-test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test or two-way ANOVA with post-test Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc). Statistical significance was represented in Figures by: *, p-value < 0.05; **, p-value < 0.01; ***p value < 0.001.
