Viral clinical profile and mortality outcome of adult HIV-positive patients presenting in the emergency department of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Ayenew Genet, Bitania Debalkew, Merahi Kefyalew, Aklilu Azazh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman Immunodeficiency Virus has been a global health problem since its identification. Despite a decline in new infections, many patients still present to emergency departments with advanced disease and critical systemic infections in Ethiopia.ObjectiveThe main aim of this study was to assess the clinical profile and outcomes of adult HIV-positive patients presenting to the emergency department of Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.MethodsThis study involved a retrospective cross-sectional electronic medical records review from January 1 to June 30, 2025, taking place in Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital adult emergency department. Data was extracted using a structured Kobo tool and then analyzed with SPSS v27. Multivariable logistic regression was applied to identify the factors associated with mortality, with a p-value of < 0.05 regarded as statistically significant.ResultsOut of a total of 104 HIV-positive patients included, the mean age was 45.78 ± 14.25 years, and 64(61.5%) were females. The leading clinical presentations were vomiting (14.1%), diarrhea (10.5%), and fatigue (10.1%). Outcomes included ICU admission at 4(3.8%), and emergency department mortality at 15(14.4%). Advanced WHO clinical stage (AOR = 6.068, 95%CI: 1.273–28.880, P = 0.024), high white blood cell count (AOR = 1.205, 95%CI: 1.015–1.429, P = 0.033), and hemoglobin (AOR = 0.704, 95% CI: 0.540–0.918, P = 0.009) were significant independent predictors of mortality. The majority (76.4%) stayed in the ED for ≥ 24 h, with a median stay of 2 days (IQR: 1–3).ConclusionDespite high ART coverage, many HIV-positive adult patients presenting to the TASH ED had advanced disease, high mortality, and prolonged ED length of stay. Advanced WHO clinical stage, elevated white blood cell count, and low hemoglobin were independently associated with increased mortality. Patients with these factors should be prioritized at triage for urgent evaluation and targeted resource allocation, and considered for early aggressive management, to improve patient outcomes and enhance emergency care system performance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13596-w.Keywords: HIV, Emergency department, Clinical profile, Outcomes, Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains a global health problem since its identification in the early 1980s [1, 2]. An estimated 39.9 million people worldwide live with HIV, among whom 38.6 million are adults aged 15 years and older as of 2023 [3]. An estimated 1.7 million new infections make Sub-Saharan Africa the highest HIV-burdened region as of 2021 [3]. In the same year, 630,000 deaths were reported globally, with Sub-Saharan Africa experiencing the greatest decline in HIV-related deaths in 2021 [4].

The first HIV-1 infection and clinically diagnosed AIDS case in Ethiopia were confirmed in Addis Ababa in 1984 and 1986, respectively [4]. The national prevalence of HIV was 0.91% among adults aged 15 years and older, with Gambella accounting for 3.7%, Addis Ababa 3.1%, Dire Dawa 2.69%, Harari 2.55%, Tigray 1.02%, Amhara 0.9%, Afar 0.81%, Sidama 0.77%, Benishangul Gumuz 0.68%, Oromia 0.55%, SNNP 0.38%, and Somali 0.17% [5]. With the expansion of ART programs, 98% of people living with HIV receive ART, and 98% of those on ART are virally suppressed (5). The majority were on a first-line HAART regimen (TDF + 3TC + EFV/NVP) [6, 7], with low CD4 cell counts (< 200) [6, 7]. Because of the introduction and scale-up of ART programs in Ethiopia, new AIDS-related deaths have declined to 13.6% in 2021 [5], although adult women died more frequently [8], with varying death rates across the country [9, 10].

Despite these achievements, HIV infection continues to be associated with significant clinical complexity. Survival was significantly associated with low clinical stage, high baseline haemoglobin levels, and the administration of cotrimoxazole prophylaxis therapy [9]. Conversely, comorbid conditions, suboptimal medication adherence, lower CD4 counts, and higher viral loads were identified as factors associated with increased early mortality in the emergency setting [10, 11]. Opportunistic infections remain a major concern, where bacterial pneumonia is the most common opportunistic infection in international and regional studies [6, 12], while pulmonary tuberculosis is the most common OI locally [13, 14].

Clinical presentations of HIV vary widely depending on disease stage and setting. In the emergency setting, chronic cough (40%) and night sweats (25%) are among the most frequently reported symptoms, making respiratory system pathology (43.5%) the most common, followed by genitourinary (20.3%), gastrointestinal (18.2%), and central nervous system pathologies (11.8%) in regional studies [9]. However, weight loss (87.5%), prolonged fever (84.3%), chronic diarrhea (59.2%), and persistent coughing (68.1%) were major clinical manifestations in the non-emergency setting locally [15]. Furthermore, comorbidities are frequent among people living with HIV. In England, 29% of people with HIV/AIDS had at least one comorbidity, with hepatitis being the most common, followed by mental health disorders and cardiovascular disease [16]. In Kenya, dyslipidemia and hypertension affected 21.2% and 20.0% of patients, respectively [17]. Locally, 14.1% had hypertension and 8.6% had diabetes, and nearly one in three individuals suffered from a common mental disorder, with depression being the most prevalent (25%) [18, 19].

Even though prevention programs, antiretroviral therapy (ART) expansion, and national health initiatives have been implemented to combat HIV/AIDS, significant challenges persist in emergency settings in Ethiopia. In the era of ART, knowledge about the clinical profile and outcomes of HIV-positive patients in Ethiopian emergency departments (EDs) is limited, and no publications have specifically focused on this topic despite the high burden of HIV/AIDS in the country. Most available evidence has been generated in non-emergency settings [20, 21].

As the final tertiary referral center, the Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) ED in Addis Ababa serves as a major referral and treatment hub for critically ill HIV-positive patients, managing a wide range of presentations and a high burden of severe cases.

Therefore, the main aim of this study was to assess the clinical profile and outcomes of adult HIV-positive patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) of TASH, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. The findings of this study will fill a knowledge gap, help optimize emergency care, support timely interventions, elevate the overall quality of care, strengthen ED physician training, reinforce clinical standards, and promote better integration of emergency HIV care into national treatment programs. Finally, the findings will also provide an evidence-based foundation for policies and procedures aimed at improving emergency care nationally.

Methods

Study design, study setting, and period

A hospital-based, cross-sectional, retrospective chart review using electronic medical records was conducted from January 1 to June 30, 2025, in the Adult ED of TASH, one of the largest and most comprehensive tertiary care centers in Ethiopia. Located in Addis Ababa, TASH serves a diverse patient population and functions as a national referral hospital. The Adult ED provides care to more than 12,000 patients annually [22]. Data extraction for this study was conducted from August 1 to September 30, 2025.

Population, eligibility criteria, sample size determination and sampling methods

Among all HIV-positive patients who presented to the Adult ED of TASH during the study period, those aged ≥ 15 years with confirmed HIV status and an emergency visit were included in the study. Patients whose records could not be retrieved, those presenting with trauma, and those with repeated visits during the study period were excluded.

The required Sample size was computed using a single population proportion formula based on the following assumptions: no previous estimates for ED visits, an absolute precision of 5%, Zα/2 = 1.96 at a 95% confidence interval (CI), a 10% contingency for charts excluded due to incomplete information, and an estimated total population of 120 patients visiting the Adult ED over six months [23]. The final sample size was calculated as 103. Based on the sampling approach, all 104 eligible patients were included in the final analysis.

Study variables, data measurement methods

The independent variables included socio-demographic characteristics, referral source, triage category, duration of illness, clinical presentation, ART and chemoprophylaxis information, ART-related side effects, CD4 count, and comorbidities. The dependent variables were patient outcomes.

Data collection tools, methods, and procedures

A structured data collection form included sections on demographic information, HIV history and immunological data, laboratory and imaging results, clinical presentation, comorbidities, management in the Emergency Department, and patient outcomes were developed by the principal investigator after reviewing previously published literature and considering the study area’s context, to extract information from patient medical records.

Before formal data collection, standardized data extraction procedures were practiced by principal investigator and assistants. A pilot test was conducted on 10 patients on non-study records at Zewditu Memorial Hospital to assess the clarity, completeness, and practicality of the tool. Each data collector reviewed eligible patient EMRs using the standardized abstraction form. The principal investigator reviewed each entry, handled missing or incomplete data by applying ID numbers and coding, and ensured accurate data entry. Data extraction was performed using the KOBO Toolbox.

Data quality control

The principal investigator conducted daily random double-checks of a subset of charts. Weekly meetings were held with data assistants to address challenges and ensure adherence to protocols. The data collection form was completed in English, and all responses were checked for completeness before being entered into SPSS.

Data processing and analysis

Data obtained with KOBO Toolbox was coded in the beginning and exported to Microsoft Excel before being exported into SPSS version 27 to be statistically analyzed.

Data exported was subject to a consistency check during and post to minimize data entry errors. Data of patients and their outcomes were recorded with descriptive statistics on clinical profile and results. Tables reported results in terms of frequency and percentages. For continuous variables normality was calculated by the Shapiro-Wilk test. The normally distributed variables were represented by means and standard deviations, and the skewed variables were represented by medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and represented in tables.

For categorical variables and binary outcomes chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were employed to examine associations of independent variables with clinical outcomes. For continuous variables, independent t-test was performed if the data were normally distributed, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Model assumption tests including an independent observation in the dataset, no multicollinearity among predictors (VIF for White blood cell count and hemoglobin were 1.162 and 1.138 respectively) and, no outliers, and linearity between continuous independent variables and the log-odds of the dependent variable were checked before running the analysis. Model fitness was also evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P value = 0.235). Binary logistic regression was then performed to find independent predictors of clinical outcomes such as death and ICU admission. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results were presented in clear summary tables to support right interpretation and effective communication of the study findings.

Missing data assessment

The completeness of all key study variables was evaluated, and the proportion of missing observations is reported in Table 1. Analyses were performed on available cases. As CD4 count (62.5%) and viral load (95.2%) were often missing, while other variables were 100% complete, a complete-case analysis was considered proper.

Result

Baseline patient characteristics

Out of a total of 7,346 patients presenting to the ED, 136 were HIV-positive. After excluding 3 trauma cases and 19 repeat visits, 104 patients were included finally. Of these, 64 (61.5%) were female and 40 (38.5%) were male, with a mean age of 45.78 ± 14.25 years. Most patients, 90 (86.5%), were from Addis Ababa, 43 (41.3%) were self-referred, and 103 (99%) had an earlier HIV diagnosis. Advanced disease was common, with 41 (39.4%) of patients classified as WHO clinical Stage IV. ART coverage was high, 102 (98.1%), on therapy, and the most common regimen was TDF + 3TC + DTG (34.6%). ART-related side effects were unknown. CD4 counts at presentation were available for 37 patients (35.6%), and viral load results were available for 5 patients (4.8%) (Look Table 2).

Clinical presentation of HIV positive patients

The most commonly presenting complaints were vomiting, 35 (14.1%), diarrhea 26 (10.5%), and easy fatigability 25 (10.1%). Most patients were triaged as orange, 40(38.5%), followed by green, 39 (37.5%), using the South African Triaging Score (SATS). Additionally, 58 (55.8%) of the 104 patients had comorbidities. Malignancies accounted for 36 (43.9%) of these, followed by hypertension at 14 (17.1%). Chief complaint and comorbidity were multi-response variables, where percentages reflect proportion of total responses, not total patients (look Table 3).

Laboratory and imaging

Among the study population, the median white blood cell count, hemoglobin, and platelet count are 6.85 × 10³/µL (IQR: 4.75–11.60),11.45(8.73–13.10) g/dL, and 240 × 10³/µL (IQR: 142–329) respectively.

About imaging, 65 (62.5%) patients underwent investigations, with ultrasound being most frequent (48.1%), followed by X-ray (37.7%) and CT scan (14.3%). The most common X-ray findings were pneumonia 11 (10.6%) and pulmonary tuberculosis 7 (6.7%). Eight patients (8, 22.2%) revealed unremarkable ultrasound scans, while acute deep vein thrombosis and acute kidney injury (likely) 3 (8.3% each) were the common abnormalities found. The most common CT scan finding was unremarkable. Some patients had more than one imaging modality at the same time (look Table 4).

Diagnoses of HIV-positive patients at ED

Among the ED diagnoses, hematologic disorders were the most common, affecting 50 (48.1%) patients, followed by respiratory disorders in 46 (44.2%) and gastrointestinal(GI) disorders in 30 (28.8%) patients. The leading specific diagnoses were anemia 42 (56.8%), acute gastroenteritis 21 (53.3%), pneumonia 19 (41.3%), and pulmonary tuberculosis 15 (36.2%). Sepsis and septic shock accounted for 28.8% of cases, with GI and respiratory systems being the most common sources of infection. Surgical disorders accounted for 16 (15.4%) of all diagnoses, with nephrolithiasis being the most frequent, reported in 3 (18.8%) patients. Outcomes of HIV-positive patients in the ED

The majority were discharged home, 51 (49%), followed by admission to the wards, 32 (30.8%), died at the ED, 15 (14.4%), and admitted to the ICU, 4 (3.8%). Most patients, 81 (76.4%), stayed in the ED for ≥ 24 h, with a median length of ED stay of 2 days (IQR: 1–3). Refractory shock accounted for the majority ED death, 6 (5.8%), followed by multi-organ failure in 3 cases (2.9%), and Pulmonary embolism 2 (1.9%), highlighting the high vulnerability of HIV-positive patients to severe infectious and multi-organ complications in the emergency setting (look Table 7).

Discussion

This study emphasizes the significant clinical burden met at the TASH ED and offers one of the first comprehensive accounts of emergency presentations and outcomes among adult HIV-positive patients in Ethiopia.

Most patients were middle-aged and female [5, 6, 8], showing higher HIV prevalence and mortality among women, and from Addis Ababa. Nearly all patients (99%) had a prior HIV diagnosis and were receiving ART (98.1%) [5], indicating that ED presentations at TASH predominantly reflect complications among previously diagnosed patients rather than new HIV cases. However, documentation of immunologic and virologic status at presentation was limited, with CD4 counts available in only 35.6% and viral load measurements in 4.8% of patients. This gap restricted the assessment of disease severity at ED presentation and limited the ability to evaluate associations between immunologic status and key ED outcomes such as mortality, ICU admission, and length of stay.

Vomiting (14.1%), diarrhea(10.5%), fatigue(10.1%), dyspnea (9.7%), and fever (9.3%) were the most common presentations, distinct from the South African study, which indicated chronic cough (40%) and night sweats (25%) were the most common [6]. The FMOH report also showed weight loss (87.5%), fever (84.3%), and chronic diarrhea (59.2%) were the commonest symptoms in the outpatient settings [15].

Hematologic disorders (48.1%), respiratory diseases (35.6%) and gastrointestinal illnesses (28.8%) were ranked among the highest prevalent ED diagnoses. This reflected TASH as the major hematologic care center in the country, unlike the South African study which reported most patients with respiratory system diseases (43.5%), followed by pathology involving the genitourinary system (20.3%), and gastrointestinal system (18.2%) [6]. Bacterial pneumonia (41.3%) then pulmonary TB(36.2%) were identified as the most common OIs like global and regional studies [9, 12], but differing from local studies where Pulmonary TB was the most common OI reported [13, 14].

Using the South African Triage Scale (SATS), most patients were triaged as orange, 40 (38.5%), followed by green, 39 (37.5%), and approximately 11.5% of patients were classified as red, often requiring immediate lifesaving interventions.

Anemia was the primary laboratory abnormality, with a median (IQR) hemoglobin of 11.45(8.73–13.10) g/dL [6]. Bacterial pneumonia and pulmonary TB stood for the most common imaging findings, consistent with reports from South Africa and China [6, 12].

Results revealed a heavy burden, with a death rate of 14.4% in the ED, comparable to results from South Africa [6], while exceeding the 10.3% death at Shashemene and Assela hospitals [9], and the 6.85% death at Dessie Comprehensive Hospital [10]. Advanced WHO clinical stage [9, 10], low hemoglobin [9], and white blood cell count all predicted mortality independently. Therefore, patients with these factors are more likely to have severe underlying infections, complications, and a higher risk of mortality, and should be identified promptly at triage, prioritized for urgent evaluation, and considered for aggressive management, including early monitoring, resuscitation, and possible ICU admission.

ICU admissions were still low (3.8%), below the 16.7% reported in Johannesburg [6] due to limited ICU bed availability or poor prognosis.

Compared to the South African study, which reported a median hospital stay of 4.9 days (IQR: 3.5–8.0) and approximately one-third (32.2%) of patients requiring admission for ≥ 7 days [6], the median length of stay in this study was shorter, with a median ED stay of 2 days (IQR: 1–3). However, prolonged ED stay was still common, as 76.4% of patients remained in the ED for ≥ 24 h, reflecting bed shortages, delayed diagnostic turnaround times, and the need for inpatient management.

Overall, this study provides insight into the thorough sociodemographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics as well as outcomes from HIV-positive adults presenting to the ED. Conducted at the country’s largest tertiary teaching hospital and using descriptive and multivariable analyses, the study allowed for a detailed characterization of HIV-related emergencies, provides important foundational evidence for further research, and has clear implications on triage, early intervention, and resource distribution.

Limitation of the study

Its retrospective design, single-center setting, small sample size, six-month data collection period, and missing CD4 and viral load reduced consistency of the variables, representativeness, generalizability, and potential for causal inferences.

Conclusion

Despite high ART coverage, many HIV-positive adult patients presenting to the TASH ED had advanced disease, high mortality, and prolonged ED length of stay. Advanced WHO clinical stage, elevated white blood cell count, and low hemoglobin were independently associated with increased mortality. Patients with these factors should be prioritized at triage for urgent evaluation and targeted resource allocation, and considered for early aggressive management, including close monitoring and timely resuscitative and therapeutic interventions, to improve patient outcomes and enhance emergency care system performance.

Recommendations

I recommend multicenter prospective studies to nationally characterize HIV emergency patterns. I also recommend increasing the availability of laboratories and imaging modalities within the hospital environment. Ultimately, clear ED to ward transfer protocols need to be set up to reduce avoidable morbidity and prolonged stays.
