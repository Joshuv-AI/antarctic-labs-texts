Bioavailable testosterone reduces the risk of lung squamous cell carcinoma: a comprehensive data study
Authors: Jihang Luo, Puyu Liu, Jian Song, Yin Li, Meng Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between testosterone and lung cancer remains unclear. This study investigates the relationship between testosterone levels and lung cancer risk, focusing on bioavailable testosterone levels (BTLs), total testosterone levels (TTLs), and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) in relation to lung cancer subtypes.MethodsWe utilized bidirectional and multivariable Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses based on genome-wide association studies (GWAS) to assess causal links. To validate the findings clinically, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for androgen receptor (AR) expression and survival analyses were conducted on a cohort of 90 patients with lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).ResultsMR analysis demonstrated that higher BTLs were significantly associated with a reduced risk of LUSC (OR = 0.365, P = 0.001), while no significant associations were observed for TTLs or SHBG. Reverse MR analysis found no causal effect of lung cancer on testosterone levels. Multivariable MR confirmed BTLs as an independent protective factor. In the clinical cohort, AR expression was significantly associated with better prognosis, showing improved median progression-free survival (12.3 vs. 9.0 months, P = 0.01) and median overall survival (35.3 vs. 29.4 months, P < 0.01). Cox regression identified AR expression as an independent protective factor for patient outcomes. However, study limitations include potential residual confounding, ethnic heterogeneity between European GWAS data and Asian clinical cohorts, and the lack of direct experimental validation.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that higher BTLs may play a protective role against LUSC. BTLs and AR expression show potential as valuable biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognostic assessment of LUSC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07639-0.Keywords: Bioavailable testosterone levels (BTLs), Androgen receptor (AR), Total testosterone levels (TTLs), Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), Mendelian randomization (MR)

Background

Testosterone is a primary sex hormone with an extremely important dual role. It not only controls primary sexual development and the appearance of male secondary sexual characteristics, but is also responsible for the metabolism of the three major nutrients, promoting muscle growth and the initiation of adipose tissue production [1]. Testosterone affects a range of target organs, including the prostate, skeletal structure, muscle tissue, central nervous and peripheral nervous system. A lack of testosterone can lead to conditions such as metabolic syndrome, obesity, sexual dysfunction, and anemia [2, 3]. In the body, testosterone is mainly bound to sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) and a small amount of albumin, and is separated within the microvasculature of the tissue, thereby producing free testosterone that exerts its androgenic potential. The interaction between testosterone and albumin shows a fragile bond and is prone to reversibility. Therefore, the combined fractions of free testosterone and albumin-bound testosterone are collectively referred to as bioavailable testosterone [4].

At the same time, people also found that testosterone may be related to the occurrence and development of many tumors. In a case control study comprising 397 lung cancer cases and a control group consisting of postmenopausal women who had never smoked, researchers observed that the odds ratio (OR) for the highest quartile compared to the lowest quartile of free testosterone was 0.55 (95%Confidence Interval (CI) = 0.34–0.90; P = 0.03). These findings suggest a potential association between elevated levels of circulating free testosterone and reduced lung cancer risk in postmenopausal women who have never smoked [5]. A study examining the relationship between free testosterone, total testosterone, and SHBG and prostate cancer, including aggressive subtypes, found that free testosterone increased the risk of associated prostate cancer (OR = 1.23, 95%CI = 1.08–1.40, P < 0.05). However, no clear connection was found between the other two [6]. In a prospective study, higher levels of free testosterone were found to be associated with an increased risk of melanoma and prostate cancer in men. Elevated total testosterone levels were linked to a higher risk of liver cancer (Hazard Ratio (HR) = 2.45, 95%CI = 1.56–3.84, P < 0.05), while higher SHBG levels were associated with an increased risk of liver cancer (HR = 1.56, 95%CI = 1.31–1.87, P < 0.05) and a decreased risk of prostate cancer (HR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.91–0.96, P < 0.05) in men. In postmenopausal women, elevated levels of free testosterone, total testosterone, and SHBG were correlated with a higher risk of endometrial cancer and breast cancer [7]. While there is a wealth of research investigating the correlation between testosterone and the risk of various cancers, studies elucidating its direct causal effects on lung cancer remain scarce. Lung cancer is a highly heterogeneous disease with lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and small cell lung cancer (SCLC) representing the major pathological subtypes each possessing unique etiological landscapes [8]. Biologically, prior studies have indicated that hormone sensitivity varies across these histological types with distinct androgen receptor (AR) expression profiles observed [9] thereby raising the hypothesis that androgen signaling could differentially influence tumor development depending on the subtype. In particular, the potential for bioavailable testosterone to attenuate the chronic inflammatory microenvironment driving squamous carcinogenesis alongside AR dependent suppression of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) provides a plausible biological basis for hypothesized subtype specific associations [10]. Consequently, a unified analysis might obscure these potential distinctions necessitating an investigation that evaluates these major subtypes individually.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological approach that utilizes genetic variations, such as nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causal relationships between exposure and outcomes. SNPs are randomly assigned during conception, independently of confounding factors, enabling MR to mimic randomized controlled trials and mitigate the limitations of previous observational studies [11, 12]. Within this framework, two-sample MR (TSMR) synthesizes genetic associations from separate exposure and outcome datasets to estimate causal effects [13] whereas multivariable MR (MVMR) extends this capability by permitting the simultaneous analysis of multiple correlated exposures to isolate the independent effect of specific risk factors [14]. With the extensive expansion of online Genome-wide Association Studies (GWAS) data, MR has emerged as a pivotal tool in oncology research as evidenced by its success in disentangling complex metabolic associations in lung cancer previously obscured in observational cohorts [15]. In this study, we employed a combinatorial framework of TSMR and MVMR to investigate the causal relationships between bioavailable testosterone levels (BTLs), total testosterone levels (TTLs), and SHBG levels with various lung cancer subtypes followed by clinical validation to corroborate the genetic findings.

Methods

Public data sources and research procedures

This study adheres to the key principles of the STROBE-MR guidelines, with the extension statement following the STREGA checklist to ensure methodological transparency and scientific credibility [16]. The research methodology is outlined in Fig. 1. MR analysis here utilizes publicly available summary statistics data from the Open GWAS (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) project [17]. Summary-level data for BTLs, TTLs and Any parental history of lung cancer are derived from the EBI project, encompassing 382,988, 194,453 and 407,521 participants, respectively [18]. The data on SHBG and daily smoking amount were sourced from the IEU project, involving 214,989 and 249,752 participants. TTLs and SHBG were measured using one-step competitive assays and two-step sandwich immunoassays, respectively, on the Beckman Coulter UniCel Dxl 800, with results expressed in nmol/L. BTLs(nmol/L) were calculated based on TTLs and albumin concentrations, the latter of which was measured using the BCG method on the Beckman Coulter AU5800 [19]. GWAS summary-level data for lung cancer were obtained from the FinnGen database [20]. This included 571 cases and 218,221 controls for LUAD, 365 cases and 218,427 controls for LUSC, and 179 cases and 218,613 controls for SCLC. Lung cancer cases were defined based on a set of criteria established by experts, incorporating information from hospital records, mortality records, primary care records, and self-reported data. Cases were identified using diagnostic codes from hospital records (ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes) or diagnostic codes from primary care medical records [21]. Detailed data for all GWAS are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Genetic instrument selection for exposures and outcomes

Utilizing the “TwoSampleMR” R package, we conducted the selection of genetic IVs. From the GWAS summary data of BTLs, TTLs, and SHBG, we identified independent SNPs that exhibited strong associations ( P < 5 × 10− 8) without any linkage disequilibrium (LD) [22]. Many of these selected variants map to established biological candidate gene loci involved in sex hormone biosynthesis and metabolism, serving as biologically validated proxies for testosterone levels [23, 24]. Additionally, we filtered these SNPs based on low genetic correlation within a 10,000 kb window (R2 < 0.001). During the SNP selection process, we also excluded SNPs with incompatible alleles, such as rs5889386, and removed palindromic SNPs with moderate allele frequencies, such as rs10822163, rs12121301, to avoid issues of ambiguous linkage. The remaining SNPs were chosen as IVs. Assessment of Weak Instrument Bias within the Selected IVs, indicating a weak association between the chosen genetic variants as IVs and the exposure factor, was carried out by calculating the F-statistic [F = R2(n − k−1)/k(1 − R2)]. Here, R2 represents the proportion of exposure variance explained by the selected IVs, with n as the sample size and k as the number of IVs. If the instrument-exposure association’s F-statistic exceeds 10, it indicates a lower likelihood of weak instrument bias in the instrumental variables [25].To comprehensively assess the relationship between exposure and different types of lung cancer, we utilized outcome data from the FinnGen database, including LUAD, LUSC, and SCLC. In these datasets, we extracted the same SNPs as used in the aforementioned analysis.

Mendelian randomization analysis

To evaluate the relationship between testosterone, SHBG, and lung cancer, we initially employed bidirectional TSMR analysis using three different methods: Inverse Variance Weighting (IVW), MR-Egger regression and Weighted Median. For these three methods, when IVs show no heterogeneity or pleiotropy, IVW method estimates are preferred. If heterogeneity is present without pleiotropy, the weighted median method is prioritized. In cases of pleiotropy, MR-Egger method results take precedence. Since weighted median and MR-Egger methods apply to a broader range of scenarios, they exhibit lower statistical efficiency and wider confidence intervals compared to IVW [26]. Subsequently, we included BTLs, TTLs, SHBG, smoking, and parental lung cancer history in a MVMR analysis [27] to explore their relationship with lung cancer. To rigorously address multicollinearity and identify robust causal risk factors, we employed Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression. This regularization method automatically performs feature selection by shrinking the coefficients of redundant or highly collinear variables to zero based on a tuning parameter [28]. Consequently, SHBG was excluded from the final multivariable model due to its redundancy identified by the LASSO algorithm. Causal relationships in the MVMR analysis were assessed using the IVW method, supplemented by MR-Egger and LASSO methods.

Sensitivity analysis

To address potential horizontal pleiotropy, we employed a heterogeneity test derived from the IVW method, utilizing a significance threshold of P < 0.05 based on Cochran’s Q statistic. Additionally, intercepts obtained from MR-Egger regression served as indicators of directional pleiotropy, with significance considered at P < 0.05. To assess and mitigate the impact of horizontal pleiotropy, we applied the MR-Multivariate Heterogeneity Residuals and Outlier (MR-PRESSO) method. Furthermore, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted to evaluate whether individual SNPs exerted undue influence or bias on the MR estimates. Finally, Steiger filtering was applied to verify the direction of causality. The above analyzes were performed using the TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.6) 、MendelianRandomization(version 0.7.0)and MVMR(version 0.4) package in R (version 4.1.3).

Immunohistochemical (IHC)

To validate the results of the Mendelian randomization analysis, this study included patients diagnosed with LUSC at the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University from January 1, 2014, to January 31, 2019. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients aged over 18 and under 75 who had undergone at least one surgical treatment, with all patients receiving management according to established treatment guidelines [29–31]. Prior to surgery, all patients provided written informed consent to allow the use of resected tumor tissues for scientific research. We collected paraffin-embedded tissue sections from these patients. The IHC staining procedure involved several key steps: deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, followed by serum blocking, then the addition of AR antibody (CAR-0210, Celnovte), secondary antibody (SE134, Solarbio), and streptavidin (SE068, Solarbio). Subsequently, diaminobenzidine (DAB, DA1010, Solarbio) was used for chromogenic development. After staining, the samples underwent dehydration and sealing, with images captured using an optical microscope. Quantitative analysis and scoring of the IHC digital images were performed using the IHC Profiler plugin in ImageJ software [32]. The software automatically scored the images based on a predefined formula, classifying the results into four levels: 4, 3, 2, and 1, where levels 3 to 4 indicate positivity and levels 1 to 2 indicates negativity.

Collection and processing of clinical data

Clinical characteristic data of patients were collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Follow-up was conducted through hospitalization, outpatient visits, or telephone contact, with the last follow up date set at December 31, 2022. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from surgical intervention to death from any cause for patients diagnosed with squamous cell lung carcinoma. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from the initiation of treatment until disease progression or until the last follow up when the disease had not progressed. Tumor progression was assessed according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 criteria [33], with imaging results confirmed by licensed diagnostic radiologists and clinical physicians.

All data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 27.0. Chi-square tests and rank-sum tests were employed to investigate the associations between clinical characteristics and AR expression. Survival analysis was performed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of AR and other clinically relevant risk factors on patient prognosis, with *P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

IV selection for exposures

Detailed information about SNPs related to BTLs, TTLs, and SHBG can be found in Supplementary Table S2. All SNPs used in the analysis were strong instrumental variables, with F-values exceeding the recommended threshold of 10, indicating the absence of bias due to weak instruments in the study.

Bidirectional TSMR analysis of testosterone, SHBG, and different types of lung cancer

In the absence of heterogeneity and pleiotropy, the results are primarily presented using the IVW method. We observed that the results supported a protective effect of BTLs against the occurrence of LUSC (OR = 0.365, P = 0.001), translating to a 63.5% reduction in risk per unit increase in genetically predicted BTLs. However, there was no apparent causal relationship between BTLs and LUAD (OR = 1.337, P = 0.223) or SCLC (OR = 1.664, P = 0.244). TTLs showed no significant association with LUSC (OR = 0.688, P = 0.058), LUAD (OR = 0.806, P = 0.176), or SCLC (OR = 0.622, P = 0.126). Similarly, SHBG exhibited no significant relationship with LUSC (OR = 0.717, P = 0.092), LUAD (OR = 0.774, P = 0.093), or SCLC (OR = 0.845, P = 0.532). These results are depicted in Fig. 2. Sensitivity analysis indicated no evidence of heterogeneity for any analysis association based on Cochran’s Q test (P > 0.05), and MR-Egger regression analysis suggested the absence of horizontal pleiotropy (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S3). Moreover, Steiger filtering analysis confirmed the directionality of BTLs on LUSC, ruling out potential reverse causation bias (Supplementary Table S6). Scatter plots in Supplementary Figure S1 illustrate the causal relationships between these three exposures and the risk of different types of lung cancer. Leave-one-out analysis indicated that no individual SNP had a significant impact on the estimates (Supplementary Figure S2). Funnel plots and forest plots depict the SNPs associated with exposure and their implications for the risk of various types of lung cancer, which can be found in Supplementary Figure S3 and Supplementary Figure S4, respectively. Reverse MR analysis results indicate no causal relationship between any type of lung cancer and testosterone or SHBG, as detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Two-sample mendelian randomization (TSMR) analysis of the associations between exposures and lung cancer subtypes

MVMR analysis of testosterone, SHBG, and common risk factors in relation to lung cancer

Considering that smoking and family history of lung cancer may influence the relationship between testosterone levels and lung cancer risk, we included all these factors in the MVMR analysis. Using the LASSO method to eliminate variables with high collinearity, SHBG was found to have high collinearity and was therefore excluded. Adjusted results revealed that BTLs still had a causal relationship with a reduced risk of LUSC (OR = 0.409, P = 0.008), corresponding to a 59.1% reduction in risk per unit increase in genetically predicted BTLs. In contrast, parental lung cancer history and smoking increase the risk of LUSC(OR = 3.358, P = 0.013 and OR = 3.319, P < 0.001). Similarly, the analysis results for the risk of LUAD and SCLC associated with testosterone showed no significant statistical association (P > 0.05). Other MVMR results indicated that parental history of lung cancer remains a high-risk factor for LUAD and SCLC(OR = 3.102, P = 0.013 and OR = 5.807, P = 0.038), and smoking also increases the risk of LUAD(OR = 2.157, P = 0.007).The results of other related methods such as LASSO and MR-Egger can be found in Fig. 3. At the same time, the MVMR sensitivity analysis did not show heterogeneity in the results(P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table S3).

Clinical characteristics and AR expression in patients with LUSC

Our previous MR analysis results suggest that BTLs may influence the risk of developing LUSC. Given that bioavailable testosterone typically exerts its effects through binding to AR, we have decided to include clinical cases of LUSC in our study. This study included a total of 90 eligible patients diagnosed with LUSC. Detailed clinical characteristics and AR expression data are presented in Table 1. Among these patients, 25 exhibited positive AR expression (Fig. 4A and B), while 65 showed negative AR expression(Fig. 4C and D), resulting in a positive expression rate of 27.8% (25/90). AR was primarily localized in the nuclei of tumor cells (Fig. 4B). Analysis of the relationship between AR expression and clinical characteristics of LUSC patients revealed a significant difference in AR expression concerning AJCC staging (P = 0.021). However, no significant differences were observed in other clinical factors. (P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Relationship between AR expression and prognosis in patients with LUSC

After classifying patients based on AR immunohistochemistry results into negative and positive groups, we found that patients in the positive group had a better prognosis compared to those in the negative group. Specifically, PFS was longer in the positive group (median PFS: 12.3 months vs. 9.0 months, P = 0.01), as was OS (median OS: 35.3 months vs. 29.4 months, P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A and B). To assess the impact of clinical factors and AR expression on OS, we conducted univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Univariate Cox analysis revealed that age, T staging, and AJCC staging are risk factors affecting OS, while AR expression served as a protective factor (Fig. 5C). Multivariate Cox analysis similarly indicated that AR expression and therapy had a protective effect on the prognosis of LUSC patients (HR = 0.342, HR = 0.520, P < 0.05), with age and AJCC staging continuing to be significant risk factors for prognosis (HR = 2.845, HR = 3.342, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Elucidating the biological determinants of tumor progression remains a fundamental pursuit in oncology [34, 35], as prominently illustrated by the well-established role of testosterone in driving the growth and progression of prostate cancer, which serves as a foundational paradigm in the study of hormone-dependent malignancies [36]. Subsequent investigations have extensively explored the impact of testosterone on other malignant tumors(Supplementary Table S5). For instance, utilizing the SEER medicare database, scholars observed a negative correlation between testosterone therapy and distant prostate cancer as well as colorectal cancer, while observing a positive correlation with distant melanoma [37]. Moreover, studies have indicated that elevated testosterone levels in female patients may have adverse effects on breast cancer and endometrial cancer. Glaser et al. [38] demonstrated that in female breast cancer patients exhibiting hormone deficiency, the implantation of testosterone-anastrozole pellets in peritumoral tissue significantly reduced tumor volume, providing therapeutic evidence supporting a direct anti-proliferative effect of testosterone on tumor cells.

Advancements in molecular profiling have increasingly elucidated the biological and pathophysiological roles of testosterone in peripheral non-reproductive organs like the lung [39]. Our bidirectional TSMR and MVMR analyses revealed that elevated BTLs are associated with a reduced risk of LUSC. However, TTLs and SHBG showed no significant association with the three types of lung cancer. MVMR results indicated that smoking and parental history of lung cancer remain common risk factors for all three types of lung cancer. A prospective study with a median follow-up duration of 22 years has suggested that elevated overall testosterone levels in the body are associated with a 30% to 80% increased risk of early post-cancer diagnosis mortality. However, elevated overall testosterone levels do not appear to affect cancer incidence [40]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that BTLs represent the biologically active fraction of testosterone, thereby exerting a more direct influence on cellular processes and oncogenic risk. Our previously mentioned study involving postmenopausal never-smoking women further supports our relevant conclusions. In this population, higher levels of circulating BTLs and estimated aromatase activity may be associated with reduced lung cancer risk [5]. A population-based cohort study of 3,635 older men with a mean age of 77 years measured testosterone and SHBG and recorded cancer cases over a mean follow-up period of 6.7 ± 1.8 years. During this time period, there were 82 cases of lung cancer. The study found that elevated testosterone levels are associated with an increased risk of lung cancer, and men with a level of 20 nmol/L are more likely to develop lung cancer (HR = 1.38, P < 0.05). However, no correlation between lung cancer and SHBG was observed [41]. These discrepancies may stem from age-related alterations in AR expression. Given that the study comprised significantly older men with a mean age of 77 years, potential changes in receptor sensitivity or downstream signaling associated with aging could influence the observed outcomes. Additionally, tumor heterogeneity may also contribute to these distinct findings. While previous studies often analyzed lung cancer as an aggregate outcome, our results indicate a subtype-dependent protective effect of BTLs in LUSC that might be less evident when assessing total testosterone levels alone.

BT typically bind to AR within the cell, subsequently modulating cellular processes associated with growth, metabolism, and immune function, thereby exerting their effects on tissue homeostasis and function [42]. Among the 90 patients with LUSC included in the study, we found that patients with positive AR expression had significantly better prognoses compared to those with negative AR expression, with notable differences in AJCC staging. Specifically, the PFS and OS were significantly longer in the AR positive group, indicating that AR expression may be a key factor influencing the role of BTLs in the development of lung squamous carcinoma. Cox regression analysis further corroborated the protective role of AR expression. Despite the observed baseline disparities in AJCC staging, the prognostic significance of AR expression remained robust in the adjusted model. By controlling for potential confounders, specifically disease stage and age, our results suggest that the survival benefit associated with AR positivity is not merely an artifact of early stage distribution, but likely reflects an independent biological contribution of AR signaling to the modulation of tumor progression. Beyond the canonical genomic actions of AR, we hypothesize that the protective association between BTLs and LUSC may be mediated through distinct inflammatory and hormonal interactions. First, testosterone is known to modulate immunity and suppress inflammation by attenuating inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6 and TNF-α [43]. Given that chronic inflammation constitutes an established driver of lung squamous carcinogenesis, particularly within the context of smoking [44], elevated BTLs may ameliorate the inflammatory microenvironment favoring tumorigenesis, thereby impeding tumor initiation or progression. Second, the metabolic conversion of testosterone to estradiol via aromatase represents another plausible mediator [45]. Since estrogen signaling plays a complex role in lung cancer biology, the protective effect of BTLs might rely on a homeostatic equilibrium between androgenic and estrogenic pathways [46] which likely exhibits significant sex-dimorphism given the differential baseline hormonal milieus and receptor distributions characterizing male and female physiology. Research has found that the AR plays a crucial role in suppressing cell invasion and enhancing cisplatin response in NSCLC by downregulating circ-SLCO1B7. AR binds to the SLCO1B7 promoter, leading to reduced circ-SLCO1B7 expression, which releases miR-139-5p to target and decrease TPD52, thereby inhibiting lung cancer progression [47]. At the same time, researchers found that miR-224-5p is upregulated in NSCLC tissues and exosomes, promoting tumor growth by enhancing cell proliferation and metastasis. The underlying mechanism involves miR-224-5p inhibiting the AR, thereby inducing EMT and driving tumor progression [10]. The presence of AR appears to confer a biological advantage, potentially improving patient prognosis by influencing the tumor microenvironment or regulating cellular proliferation and apoptosis. These findings support the importance of the interaction between BTLs and AR in lung squamous carcinoma, emphasizing the need for further research into their mechanisms. Additionally, assessing the role of testosterone levels in screening, prognosis, and personalized treatment should be a focus of future research.

Limitations

This study entails several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the clinical validation cohort was relatively small (n = 90) and derived exclusively from a single center which restricts statistical power and may impact the robustness of our conclusions thereby highlighting the imperative for future validation in larger multi-center populations. Second, generalizability constitutes a potential concern as the MR analysis primarily utilized GWAS data from European populations whereas our clinical validation involved Chinese patients meaning ethnic heterogeneity in genetic backgrounds and hormonal profiles may affect the applicability of these findings across diverse demographic groups; moreover, the reliance on aggregate GWAS data may obscure sex-specific genetic architectures and hormonal interactions which are crucial given the distinct physiological roles of testosterone in men and women. Third, although our multivariable framework rigorously adjusted for established risk factors including smoking and parental history, the possibility of residual confounding persists as unmeasured environmental exposures such as occupational hazards or socioeconomic determinants could conceivably influence both hormonal profiles and carcinogenic trajectories [48]. Furthermore, complex gene-environment interactions or horizontal pleiotropy mediated through unobserved metabolic pathways might persist despite statistical correction necessitating cautious interpretation of the causal inference as highlighted in contemporary methodological evaluations of instrumental variable analyses [49]. Finally, verifying the causal link necessitates rigorous experimental validation involving in vitro assays utilizing AR overexpressing or AR silenced LUSC cell lines to quantify biological responses to physiological testosterone concentrations alongside in vivo studies employing orthotopic xenograft mouse models to evaluate tumor growth kinetics under distinct androgenic backgrounds.

Conclusions

The stark disparity in the therapeutic landscape between LUAD and LUSC underscores an urgent need for novel intervention strategies. Our study substantiates the causal protective role of elevated BTLs against LUSC susceptibility. These findings position BTLs not merely as risk factors, but as viable prognostic biomarkers for refining clinical risk stratification. Furthermore, targeting BTLs associated pathways offers a compelling direction for drug development, potentially paving the way for breakthroughs in precision oncology for this challenging malignancy.
