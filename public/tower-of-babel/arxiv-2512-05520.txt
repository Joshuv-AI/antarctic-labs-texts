Stochastic Zeroth-Order Method for Computing Generalized Rayleigh Quotients
Jonas Bresch bresch@math.tu-berlin.de, Oleh Melnyk melnyk@math.tu-berlin.de, Martin Schoen, Gabriele Steidl steidl@math.tu-berlin.de

Abstract
The maximization of the (generalized) Rayleigh quotient is a central problem in numerical linear algebra. Conventional algorithms for its computation typically rely on matrix–adjoint products, making them sensitive to errors arising from adjoint mismatches. To address this issue, we introduce a stochastic zeroth-order Riemannian algorithm that maximizes the generalized Rayleigh quotient without requiring adjoint or matrix inverse computations. We provide theoretical convergence guarantees showing that the iterates converge to the set of global maximizers of the (generalized) Rayleigh quotient at a sublinear rate with probability one. Our theoretical results are supported by numerical experiments, which demonstrate the excellent performance of the proposed method compared to state-of-the-art algorithms.

Introduction

In this paper, we are interested in the maximization of the generalized Rayleigh quotient

\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle}

(1)

without explicitly using the inverse of the positive definite
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
or the transpose of
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
The maximization of (
1
) is a fundamental problem in various applications such as spectral equivalence of the matrices
A
and
B

[
1
]
, generalized singular value
[
2
]
, and tensor
[
3
]
decompositions.
For the identity matrix
B
, the Rayleigh quotient is also known as the numerical abscissa and is used for the stability analysis of nonsymmetric matrices in partial differential equations
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
.

The maximization of (
1
) can be performed by a number of methods. Classical iterative schemes for real eigenvalue problems use Rayleigh quotient iterations
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
and their block
[
11
]
, Krylov-style
[
12
]
or matrix-free
[
13
]
variants.
A more robust class of approaches is based on the min–max characterizations for generalized eigenvalue problems
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
.
Alternatively, maximization of (
1
) can be performed using Riemannian optimization techniques
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
Another class of algorithms relies on constructing rank-one perturbations of
A
leading to a stable approximation
of the largest eigenvalue

[
20
]
.
These can be prohibitively expensive for large-scale matrices, and a more scalable algorithmic approach based on subspace methods was proposed in
[
21
,
22
]
. Sketching methods
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
solve the problem using random dimensional reduction techniques. All the above methods rely on matrix–adjoint product and/or require access to the inverse of
B
.

The motivation for developing inverse- and adjoint-free methods stems from two main considerations. First, computing

B^{-1}
or performing matrix–vector products involving it,
is computationally expensive and susceptible to numerical inaccuracies. Second, in imaging applications such as computed tomography
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
, the transpose

A^{\mathrm{T}}

is often replaced by an approximate, but computationally tractable operator. This substitution introduces what is known as adjoint mismatch, which can lead to significant reconstruction errors.

When
A^{\mathrm{T}}
and
B^{-1}
are unavailable, zeroth-order optimization methods can be employed, as they rely solely on evaluation of products with
A
and
B
. These methods approximate the gradient using finite-difference schemes
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
. In the context of Rayleigh quotient optimization, the bundled gradient method
[
33
]
or Oja’s algorithm and its versions
[
34
]
were employed, which require very limited storage
and involve computationally simple updates. Another promising approach is consensus-based optimization
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
,
where the search space is explored by a system of interacting particles governed by coupled stochastic differential equations that balance random exploration with attraction toward the current best estimate of the optimum.

In this paper, we propose a new simple and efficient algorithm for solving (
1
) that combines ideas from stochastic zeroth-order Riemannian optimization
[
32
]
and slicing methods
[
39
,
40
]
. Namely, we iteratively solve the maximization problem in a randomly sampled one-dimensional subspace. This yields a provably convergent algorithms that neither involve the computation of
B^{-1}
nor
A^{\mathrm{T}}
, and each iteration requires only a limited number of matrix-vector products. Numerical experiments show the outstanding performance of our method compared to other techniques in the literature. Our algorithms can be extended for computing the maximal real generalized Rayleigh quotient for complex matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
.

Preliminaries

We start by recalling generalized Rayleigh quotients
and methods for its computation from the Riemannian optimization point of view.

2.1
Generalized Rayleigh Quotients

Throughout the paper, let

\|\cdot\|
be the Euclidean norm induced by the inner product
\left\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\right\rangle

and
I_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
the identity matrix.
For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we denote by

A^{\textup{H}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}(A+A^{\mathrm{T}})

its symmetric part. By the symmetry of the inner product, we have

\langle v,Av\rangle=\tfrac{1}{2}(\langle v,Av\rangle+\langle Av,v\rangle)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\langle v,Av\rangle+\langle v,A^{\mathrm{T}}v\rangle)=\langle v,A^{\textup{H}}v\rangle.

(2)

Let
\mathrm{Sym}_{\succ 0}^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be the set of symmetric, positive definite matrices.
Then, for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
B\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\succ 0}^{d}
,
we define the
generalized Rayleigh quotient
of
A
and
B
with respect to

v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
by

r(A,B,v)\coloneqq\frac{\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle}=\frac{\left\langle{v},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle}.

(3)

We are interested in computing its maximum

\mathcal{R}(A,B)\coloneqq\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}r(A,B,v)=\max_{v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}}\;\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle,

(4)

where
\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
denotes the unit sphere with respect to
B
given by

\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\coloneqq\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|v\|_{B}=1\}

(5)

and
\|v\|_{B}^{2}\coloneqq\left\langle{v},{v}\right\rangle_{B}\coloneqq\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle
.
For
B=I_{d}
, we just set
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\coloneqq\mathbb{S}_{I_{d}}^{d-1}
.
Figure
1
visualizes the
Rayleigh quotient and, in particular, the constrained part of interest on
\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
.

r(A,B,v)

for
v=(v_{1},v_{2})^{\mathrm{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}

and

A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}3&1\\
1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Its values on
\mathbb{S}_{B}^{1}
are highlighted by the solid line.

Left
:

,
Right
:

.

A value
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is called a

generalized eigenvalue
of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
, it there exists

v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
fulfilling

A^{\textup{H}}v=\lambda Bv.

(6)

Indeed, such
\lambda
is also an eigenvalue of
B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}}
, resp. of
B^{-\frac{1}{2}}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and we denote
the corresponding eigenspace by

G_{\lambda}\coloneqq\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}:A^{\textup{H}}v=\lambda Bv\}.

The value
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
is the largest generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)

and we set

G_{\max}\coloneqq G_{\mathcal{R}(A,B)}\quad\text{and}\quad G\coloneqq\bigcup_{\lambda\text{ gen. eigen-}\atop\text{value of }(A^{\textup{H}},B)}G_{\lambda}.

(Numerical abscissa)

The numerical range of

W(A)\coloneqq\Bigl\{\frac{\left\langle{z},{Az}\right\rangle}{\|z\|^{2}}:z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}\Bigr\}

of

.
This is equal to the largest eigenvalue
of

by
[

.

In this paper, we are interested in the computation of
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
without explicitly using the inverse of
B
or the transpose of
A
. We will propose a new zeroth-order method on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
, which we prepare in the next section.

2.2
Riemannian first- and zeroth-order optimization on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}

We review the main concepts of the Riemannian gradient ascent method on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
based on
[
43
, § 3]
and a stochastic zeroth-order method,
which we will use in the numerical part for comparisons.

The
(d-1)
-dimensional embedded submanifold
\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

has the tangential space
T_{v}
at
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
,

T_{v}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle{x},{Bv}\right\rangle=0\},

and its orthogonal complement reads as

T_{v}^{\perp}=\{\lambda Bv:\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\}.

In particular, we see by (
6
),
if
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}\cap G
,
then

A^{\textup{H}}v\in T_{v}^{\perp}\quad\text{and}\quad(\text{span}\,A^{\textup{H}}v)^{\perp}=T_{v}.

(7)

The orthogonal projection
P_{v}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T_{v}
is given, for
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by

P_{v}y=\Bigl(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv}{\|Bv\|}\tfrac{(Bv)^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv\|}\Bigr)y=y-\langle y,\tfrac{Bv}{\|Bv\|}\rangle\tfrac{Bv}{\|Bv\|}.

To map points from
T_{v}
back to
\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
, we will use the retraction
R_{v}:T_{v}\to\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
defined by

R_{v}(x)\coloneqq\frac{v+x}{\|v+x\|_{B}}.

(8)

For a smooth function
f:\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
, the Riemannian gradient is given by

\grad f(v)=P_{v}\nabla\bar{f}(v)\in T_{v}

(9)

where
\bar{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with
\bar{f}|_{\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}}=f
is a smooth extension of
f
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For optimizing (
4
), we consider

f(v)\coloneqq\left\langle{v},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle,\quad\text{and}\quad\bar{f}(v)=\left\langle{v},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle,\quad v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(10)

Its Riemannian gradient is

\grad f(v)=P_{v}\nabla\bar{f}(v)=2\bigl(A^{\textup{H}}v-\tfrac{(Bv)^{\mathrm{T}}(A^{\textup{H}}v)}{\|Bv\|^{2}}Bv\bigr)

(11)

The critical points
\{v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}:\grad f(v)=0\}

admit
A^{\textup{H}}v\in T_{v}^{\perp}
.

To solve (
10
),
we can apply the
Riemannian gradient ascent method
: starting in an arbitrary

v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
, we compute for step size
\tau_{k}>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
the updates

v^{k+1}=R_{v^{k}}(\tau_{k}\,\grad f(v^{k}))=\frac{v^{k}+\tau_{k}\,\grad f(v^{k})}{\|v^{k}+\tau_{k}\,\grad f(v^{k})\|_{B}}.

(12)

The convergence of the Riemannian gradient ascent method is ensured by the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f
be defined by (
10
) and

L\geq 2\|A^{\textup{H}}\|(1+\kappa(B))
, where
\kappa(B)\coloneq\|B\|/\|B^{-1}\|
is the condition number of
B
.
Then the sequence
(v^{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
generated by (
12
) with
\tau_{k}=1/L
fulfills

\grad f(v^{k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
at a sublinear rate, meaning that

\min_{k=0,\ldots,n}\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{2L}{n+1}\big(f(v^{0})-\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}}f(v)\big).

Proof.

By
[
43
, Cor. 4.8]
, the assertion holds true, if
f
is bounded from above and fulfills

|f(R_{v}(x))-f(v)-\left\langle{\grad f(v)},{x}\right\rangle|\leq\tfrac{L}{2}\|x\|^{2}

(13)

for all
x\in T_{v}
and
v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
.
Indeed, our functional
f
is bounded from above by the largest eigenvalue of
B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}}
.
Further,

\left\langle{\grad f(v)},{x}\right\rangle=2\left\langle{P_{v}A^{\textup{H}}v},{x}\right\rangle=2\left\langle{A^{\textup{H}}v},{P_{v}x}\right\rangle=2\left\langle{A^{\textup{H}}v},{x}\right\rangle

and

\displaystyle f(v)+\left\langle{\grad f(v)},{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{v},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle+2\left\langle{A^{\textup{H}}v},{x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{v+x},{A^{\textup{H}}(v+x)}\right\rangle-\left\langle{x},{A^{\textup{H}}x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\|v+x\|_{B}^{2}\left\langle{R_{v}(x)},{A^{\textup{H}}R_{v}(x)}\right\rangle-\left\langle{x},{A^{\textup{H}}x}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(1+\|x\|_{B}^{2})\left\langle{R_{v}(x)},{A^{\textup{H}}R_{v}(x)}\right\rangle-\left\langle{x},{A^{\textup{H}}x}\right\rangle

yields with

\|B^{-1}\|\|y\|^{2}\ \leq\|y\|^{2}_{B}\leq\|B\|\|y\|^{2}\quad\text{for all}\quad y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(14)

that

\displaystyle|f(R_{v}(x))-f(v)-\left\langle{\grad f(v)},{x}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle=|\left\langle{R_{v}(x)},{A^{\textup{H}}R_{v}(x)}\right\rangle\|x\|_{B}^{2}+\left\langle{x},{A^{\textup{H}}x}\right\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\textup{H}}\|(\|R_{v}(x)\|^{2}\|x\|_{B}^{2}+\|x\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\textup{H}}\|(\|R_{v}(x)\|^{2}\|B\|+1)\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\textup{H}}\|(\|B\|/\|B^{-1}\|+1)\|x\|^{2}.

∎

In the special case
B=I_{d}
and
A^{\textup{H}}\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\succ 0}^{d}
, the convergence result can be strengthened to convergence to the set of global maximizers based on
[
18
]
.

Computing
\grad f
requires evaluating products with
A^{\mathrm{T}}
, which we want to avoid. This can be done by using, e.g. the
stochastic zeroth-order optimization
proposed for general embedded manifolds in
[
32
]
:
starting in a arbitrary
v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
, the methods computes for step size
\tau_{k}>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
the updates

v^{k+1}=R_{v^{k}}(\tau_{k}\,\widehat{\grad}_{m}f(v^{k}))=\frac{v^{k}+\tau_{k}\,\widehat{\grad}_{m}f(v^{k})}{\|v^{k}+\tau_{k}\,\widehat{\grad}_{m}f(v^{k})\|_{B}},

(15)

where
\widehat{\grad}_{m}
is
m
-sample approximation of the Riemannian gradient

\widehat{\grad}_{m}f(v)\coloneq\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{f(R_{v}(\mu P_{v}x_{i})-f(v))}{\mu}x_{i},\quad x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

(16)

with scaling parameter
\mu>0
.
Note that sampling from the Gaussian distribution can be replaced by any rotation-invariant distribution. Further, the zeroth-order iteration (
15
) can be seen as an instance of a larger class of inexact gradient methods, see, e.g.,
[
44
]
. As stated in the next theorem, the general convergence result from
[
32
]
can be applied
to our special minimization problem (
4
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f
be defined by (
10
) and

L\geq 2\|A^{\textup{H}}\|(1+\kappa(B))
. Then the sequence
(v^{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
generated by (
15
) with
\tau_{k}=1/\big(2(d+4)L\big)
and scaling parameters
\mu_{k}
satisfying
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mu_{k}^{2}<\infty
fulfills
\grad f(v^{k})\to 0
a.s. as
k\to\infty
and there exists random variable
C
such that

\min_{k=0,\ldots,n}\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2}\leq\frac{C}{n+1}\quad\text{a.s.}

3
New Stochastic Zeroth-Order Algorithm

In this section, we propose a simpler and more powerful stochastic zeroth-order algorithms for maximizing the generalized Rayleigh quotient.
To make the convergence analysis better accessible, we start with
a one-sample method, i.e.,
m=1
. Then the algorithm is generalized
to more samples.

Here is our one-sample Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

One-sample zeroth-order method

1:

Initialize
v^{0}\coloneqq\tilde{v}/\|\tilde{v}\|_{B},\quad\tilde{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

3:

Sample

x^{k}:=P_{v^{k}}\tilde{x}/\|P_{v^{k}}\tilde{x}\|,\quad\tilde{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

4:

If
\langle x^{k},A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\rangle=0
then
stop.

5:

Otherwise, calculate step size

\tau_{k}\coloneqq\argmax\limits_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}r(A,B,v^{k}+\tau x^{k})

6:

Update

v^{k+1}\coloneqq\frac{v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}}{\|v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}\|_{B}}

Sampling from a Gaussian, we clearly have that
\tilde{v}=0
as well as
P_{v^{k}}\tilde{x}=0
occurs with probability zero.
By the following theorem, the step sizes
\tau_{k}
in Algorithm

1
can be computed analytically. To this end, we note that by construction

v^{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
and
x^{k}\in T_{v^{k}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
and
x\in T_{v}\setminus\{0\}
, let

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle,\quad b\coloneqq\left\langle{x},{Av}\right\rangle+\left\langle{v},{Ax}\right\rangle=2\left\langle{x},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle,

(17)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle{x},{Ax}\right\rangle,\quad d\coloneqq\left\langle{x},{Bx}\right\rangle.

(18)

If
b\not=0
, then

\tau^{*}\coloneqq\argmax_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}r(A,B,v+\tau x)

(19)

is given by

\tau^{*}=\sign(b)\left(\tfrac{c-ad}{|b|d}+\sqrt{\tfrac{(c-ad)^{2}}{(bd)^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{d}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\}.

(20)

We emphasize that the computation of
b
requires just access to
A
, but not to
A^{\mathrm{T}}
.

Proof.

By definition (
3
) and since
x\perp Bv
, we obtain

g(\tau)\coloneqq r(A,B,x+\tau v)=\frac{\langle x+\tau v,A(x+\tau v)\rangle}{\langle x+\tau v,B(x+\tau v)\rangle}=\frac{a+\tau b+\tau^{2}c}{1+\tau^{2}d}

(21)

so that

g^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{b+2\tau(c-ad)-\tau^{2}bd}{(1+\tau^{2}d)^{2}}=0.

(22)

Since
d>0
and
b\not=0
, this equation has the solutions

\tau_{\pm}=\tfrac{c-ad}{bd}\pm\sqrt{\tfrac{(c-ad)^{2}}{(bd)^{2}}+\tfrac{1}{d}}.

(23)

Straightforward computation shows that for
b>0
the maximum is attained
at
\tau_{+}
and for
b<0
at
\tau_{-}
. Combining both cases, we conclude that
\tau^{*}
reads as in (
20
).
∎

A multi-sample version of Algorithm
1
is provided by the following Algorithm
2
. For a motivation, see Remark
7.1
.

Algorithm 2

m
-sample zeroth-order method

1:

Initialize Initialize
v^{0}\coloneqq\tilde{v}/\|\tilde{v}\|_{B},\quad\tilde{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

2:

for

k=0,1,2,\dots

do

Sample

x^{k,i}:=P_{v^{k}}\tilde{x}^{i}/\|P_{v^{k}}\tilde{x}^{i}\|,\quad\tilde{x}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),\quad i=1,\ldots,m

4:

Construct

\displaystyle\bar{x}^{k}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{k,i}\,x^{k,i},\quad b_{k,i}\coloneqq 2\langle x^{k,i},A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\rangle

\displaystyle x^{k}

\displaystyle=\bar{x}^{k}/\|\bar{x}^{k}\|

5:

If
\langle x^{k},A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\rangle=0
then
stop.

6:

Otherwise, calculate step size

\tau_{k}\coloneqq\argmax\limits_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}r(A,B,v^{k}+\tau x^{k})

7:

Update

v^{k+1}\coloneqq\frac{v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}}{\|v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}\|_{B}}

In the following sections, we provide convergence results
for Algorithm
1
.
We give only an informal justification that all these results
can be extended to Algorithm
2
:
we show in the next section for Algorithm
1
that for
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
, the case
b_{k}=0
appears with probability zero with respect to the uniform measure on a sphere. Although the conditional distribution of

given
v^{k}
constructed in Algorithm

is no longer uniform, it is still absolutely continuous, for which the results can be obtained similarly.

4
Termination of the Algorithm

In this section, we deal with the termination behavior of Algorithm
1
.
Our main result in Theorem
4.6
states that
for
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
, the values
b_{k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
will “in general” not vanish, so that the algorithm does not terminate
until an adjusted stopping criterion is reached.

The following remark sets up our stochastic setting.

Remark 4.1
.

We consider a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})

and random variables
V:\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
with law

\mathbb{P}_{V}=V_{\sharp}\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}\circ V^{-1}
.
Further, given a fixed
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we deal with random variables

X:\Omega\to T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
which are uniformly distributed
\mathbb{P}_{X}=\mathcal{U}(T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

by the following Lemma
4.1
.
Therefore, we deal with surface measures
\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{d-2}}
.
In the realm of the algorithm, these random variables become conditional ones with laws
\mathbb{P}_{X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}=\mathcal{U}(T_{v^{k}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.
Then the updates
v^{k+1}
are samples from a conditional random variable with law

\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}
depending just on the previous step.
In other words,

\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}}

are Markov kernels, meaning that

\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}
is a measure
for any
v^{k}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
and
\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=\cdot}(A)
is a measurable function for any Borel set
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
.
Then our algorithm produces samples from a Markov chain

(V^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Finally, note that we use
x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, if
x
is sampled from the standard normal distribution and
X\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})

to say that the random variable
X
is standard normally distributed.

As announced in the remark, we will need the following lemma, the proof of which is given in the Appendix
A
. Note that the proof
specifies the isomorphism
T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For a fixed
v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
and
\tilde{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the random variable

X\coloneq P_{v}\tilde{X}/\|P_{v}\tilde{X}\|

is uniformly distributed
on
T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
.
Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}[xx^{\mathrm{T}}]=\frac{1}{d-1}P_{v}=\frac{1}{d-1}\Bigl(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv}{\|Bv\|}\tfrac{(Bv)^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv\|}\Bigr).

Further, the following fact is required, see Appendix
A
for the proof.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
M
be an affine subspace in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of dimension
r
,
and
\varphi:M\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1},x\mapsto x/\|x\|
.

i)

If
0\in M
and
r<d
, then
\varphi(M)
is of measure zero with respect to the surface measure
\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

ii)

If
0\not\in M
and
r<d-1
, then

\varphi(M)
is of measure zero
with respect to
\sigma_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.

We have to distinguish the three cases
\dim(G_{max})=d
or
d-1

and
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
. The first case is handled in the following remark.

Remark 4.2
.

i)

If
\dim(G_{max})=d
, then every vector of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a generalized eigenvector of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
belonging to the eigenvalue
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
and thus is a maximizer
of
r(A,B,\cdot)
. In particular, we have
v^{0}\in G_{max}
.
But then
A^{\textup{H}}v^{0}=\mathcal{R}(A,B)\,Bv^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}^{\perp}

and since
x^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}
, we get
\langle x^{0},A^{\textup{H}}v^{0}\rangle=0
. Thus, the algorithm terminates.

ii)

If
\dim(G_{max})<d
, then clearly
\dim(G_{\lambda})<d
for any generalized eigenspace
G_{\lambda}
of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
. Then,
v^{0}\in G_{\lambda}

if and only if
\tilde{v}\in G_{\lambda}
which is only possible on a zero set, since
\tilde{v}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
. Thus, by additivity of the measure,
v^{0}\not\in G
a.s.

The next lemma deals with the case
b=0
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}

and
x=P_{v}\tilde{x}/\|P_{v}\tilde{x}\|
,
\tilde{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
.
Then
v
is a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)

if and only if
b\coloneqq\langle x,A^{\textup{H}}v\rangle=0
a.s.

Proof.

If
v\in G
, then we have by (
7
) that
A^{\textup{H}}v\in T_{v}^{\perp}

and since by construction
x\in T_{v}
, we get
b=0
.

On the other hand, if
v\not\in G
, then

\dim((\text{span}\,A^{\textup{H}}v)^{\perp}\cap T_{v})<d-1
, and therefore

\mathbb{P}_{X}\big(x\in T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}:b=\langle x,A^{\textup{H}}v\rangle=0\big)=0.

∎

Further, we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\lambda
be a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
. Then the affine subspace

(v+T_{v})\cap G_{\lambda}

is either empty or of dimension
\dim(G_{\lambda})-1
.

Proof.

We show

(v+T_{v})\cap G_{\lambda}=\{u\in G_{\lambda}\mid\left\langle{u},{Bv}\right\rangle=1\}

(24)

which yields the assertion.
Assume that
u\in(v+T_{v})\cap G_{\lambda}
. Then
u=v+x
for some
x\in T_{v}
and

\left\langle{u},{Bv}\right\rangle=\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle+\left\langle{x},{Bv}\right\rangle=\|v\|_{B}=1.

(25)

Conversely, if
u\in G_{\lambda}
and
\left\langle{u},{Bv}\right\rangle=1
, we get

\left\langle{u-v},{Bv}\right\rangle=\left\langle{u},{Bv}\right\rangle-\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle=0.

Therefore,
u-v\in T_{v}
and
u=v+(u-v)\in v+T_{v}
.
∎

Now we can treat the case
\dim(G_{max})=d-1
.

Theorem 4.5
.

If
\dim(G_{max})=d-1
, then
v^{1}
generated by Algorithm
1
is a.s. in
G_{max}
.
In other words, Algorithm
1
terminates a.s. after one step.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
,
the intersection
S\coloneqq(v^{0}+T_{v^{0}})\cap G_{max}
is empty or
\dim(S)=\dim(G_{max})-1=d-2
.
By (
24
), we have that

S=\emptyset\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\left\langle{u},{Bv^{0}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{Bu},{v^{0}}\right\rangle=0\quad\text{for all }u\in G_{max}.

Since
B\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\succ 0}^{d}
, it holds

W\coloneqq BG_{max}=\dim(G_{max})=d-1\quad\text{and}\quad\dim\left(W^{\perp}\right)=1.

But by construction, the probability that
v^{0}\in W^{\perp}
is zero,
so that
S\neq\emptyset
a.s. Consequently, we have

\dim(S)=d-2
a.s.

We rewrite
S=u+T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}
with
u\in(T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max})^{\perp}
.
Let
\{u_{j}:j=1,\ldots,d-1\}
be an orthonormal basis of
T_{v^{0}}
with
u_{1}=u/\|u\|

and
u_{j}\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}
,
j=2,\ldots,d-1
.
For each direction
x\in T_{v^{0}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we have an expansion

x=\left\langle{u_{1}},{x}\right\rangle u_{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{d-1}\left\langle{x},{u_{j}}\right\rangle u_{j}=\frac{\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle}{\|u\|^{2}}u+\sum_{j=2}^{d-1}\left\langle{x},{u_{j}}\right\rangle u_{j}

If
\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle\neq 0
, multiplying both sides with
\alpha\coloneqq\|u\|^{2}/\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle
yields

\alpha x=u+\alpha\sum_{j=2}^{d-1}\left\langle{x},{u_{j}}\right\rangle u_{j}\in u+T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}.

If
\left\langle{u},{x}\right\rangle=0
, we get
x\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}
. By Lemma
4.1
we chose
x^{0}
given
v^{0}
uniformly at random on
T_{v^{0}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Since
\dim(T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max})=d-2
and
0\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}
, we have
by Lemma
4.2
for all
v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\backslash W^{\perp}
that

0=\mathbb{P}_{X^{0}|V^{0}=v^{0}}(x^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}).

Then, by the law of total probability,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X_{0}}(x^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max})=\int_{v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}}\mathbb{P}_{X^{0}|V^{0}=v^{0}}(x^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max})~\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{V^{0}}(v^{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{v^{0}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\backslash W^{\perp}}\mathbb{P}_{X^{0}|V^{0}=v^{0}}(x^{0}\in T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max})~\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{V^{0}}(v^{0})=0.

Thus,
x^{0}\notin T_{v^{0}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
a.s. and there exists
\alpha\neq 0
such that

\alpha x^{0}\in u+T_{v^{0}}\cap G_{max}=T_{v^{0}}\cap(-v^{0}+G_{max})

and
v^{0}+\alpha x^{0}\in G_{max}
a.s.
By Remark
4.2
ii), we know that
v^{0}
is not a generalized eigenvector of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
a.s. and by
Lemma
4.3
,
we have
b_{0}\neq 0
a.s. Thus,
\tau_{0}
is a unique maximizer of
r(A,B,v^{0}+\tau x^{0})
,
we get
\alpha=\tau_{0}
.
∎

Finally, we deal with the case
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
.
If
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
, then
v^{k}
is not a generalized eigenvector of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
a.s. In other words, the algorithm does not terminate a.s.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
and consider an associated eigenspace
G_{\lambda}
. We first show by induction that
v^{k}\notin G_{\lambda}
a.s. if
\dim(G_{\lambda})<d-1
.

By Remark
4.2
ii),
v^{0}\notin G_{\lambda}
a.s. For the induction step, assume that
v^{k}\notin G_{\lambda}
a.s. We show that
v^{k+1}\notin G_{\lambda}
holds
\mathbb{P}_{X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}
-a.s.

By construction,
v^{k+1}\in G_{\lambda}
if there exist some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in T_{v^{k}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
such that
v^{k}+\alpha x\in G_{\lambda}
.
We have that

\{v^{k}+\alpha x\in G_{\lambda}:\alpha\in\mathbb{R},x\in T_{v^{k}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\}=(v^{k}+T_{v^{k}})\cap G_{\lambda}=:S.

Hence, all search directions
x
that may yield
v^{k+1}\in G_{\lambda}
are given by
D\coloneqq\{u/\|u\|:u\in-v^{k}+S\}.
Note that even if
x^{k}\in D
, the choice of the step size
\tau_{k}
may not give
v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}\in S
.
By Lemma
4.4
, we know that
S
is either empty or
\dim(S)=\dim(G_{\lambda})-1
.

In the first case, we get immediately
v^{k+1}\notin G_{\lambda}
.
Otherwise, we have

\dim(-v^{k}+S)=\dim(S)=\dim(G_{\lambda})-1<d-2
.
Clearly, it also holds
0\notin-v^{k}+S
if
v^{k}\notin G_{\lambda}
. Recall that by Lemma
4.1
,
we chose
x^{k}
given
v^{k}
uniformly on

T_{v^{k}}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
. Thus, by Lemma
4.2
,

\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(v^{k+1}\in G_{\lambda})\leq\mathbb{P}_{X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(x^{k}\in D)=0\quad\text{if}\quad v^{k}\notin G_{\lambda}.

Since
v^{k}\notin G_{\lambda}
a.s., by the law of total probability we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}}(v^{k+1}\in G_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}\backslash G_{\lambda}}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(v^{k+1}\in G_{\lambda})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k}}(v^{k})=0.

This finishes the induction step.

Now, if
\dim(G_{\lambda})<d-1
for all generalized eigenvalues
\lambda
, then the assertion of the theorem follows by subadditivity of the measure.

Otherwise, there exists an eigenspace of dimension
d-1
. This is only possible if there are exactly two unique eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
with
\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
,
\dim(G_{max})=1
and
\dim(G_{\lambda_{2}})=d-1
. By Remark
4.2
ii), we know that

v^{0}\notin G
a.s. and we have
r(A,B,v^{0})>\lambda_{2}
a.s. By Lemma
4.3
, we get that
b_{0}\neq 0
a.s. and

r(A,B,v^{1})=\max_{\tau\in\mathbb{R}}r(A,B,v^{0}+\tau x^{0})\geq r(A,B,v^{0})>\lambda_{2}\quad\text{a.s.}

Therefore,
v^{1}\notin G_{\lambda_{2}}
a.s. Furthermore, since
\dim(G_{max})=1<d-1
, we have
v^{1}\notin G_{max}
. Summarizing, we get
v^{1}\notin G
. Continuing inductively, we get that
v^{k}\notin G
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
a.s.
∎

5
Convergence of the Algorithm

In this section, we investigate the convergence of
(r(A,B,v^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
in the case
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
In accordance with Theorem
3.1
,
we use the notation

\displaystyle a_{k}\coloneqq\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle,\quad b_{k}\coloneqq 2\left\langle{x^{k}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}}\right\rangle,\quad c_{k}\coloneqq\left\langle{x^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle,\quad d_{k}\coloneqq\left\langle{x^{k}},{Bx^{k}}\right\rangle.

(26)

First, we quantify the change of the objective in terms of
\tau_{k}
and
b_{k}
. In analogy to the sufficient decrease inequality
[
43
, Eq. 4.7]
for the Riemannian gradient ascent (
12
), here
|b_{k}|
plays the role of the norm gradient
\|\grad f(v^{k})\|
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
Then, we have
r(A,B,v^{k})=a_{k}
and

\displaystyle r(A,B,v^{k+1})-r(A,B,v^{k})=a_{k+1}-a_{k}=\tfrac{1}{2}\tau_{k}b_{k}>0\quad\text{a.s.}

Furthermore,
(r(A,B,v^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges a.s. and
\tau_{k}b_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
a.s.

Proof.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
1=\|v^{k}\|_{B}^{2}=\left\langle{v^{k}},{Bv^{k}}\right\rangle
. Hence,

r(A,B,v^{k})=\frac{\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle}{\left\langle{v^{k}},{Bv^{k}}\right\rangle}=\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle=a_{k},

yielding the first equality. On the other hand, by (
21
), we obtain

\displaystyle r(A,B,v^{k+1})-r(A,B,v^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{k}+\tau_{k}b_{k}+\tau_{k}^{2}c_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}-a_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{k}+\tau_{k}b_{k}+\tau_{k}^{2}c_{k}-(1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k})a_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}(b_{k}+\tau_{k}(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})).

Since
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
, by Theorem
4.6
for all
k\geq 0
we have
v^{k}\notin G
, and by Lemma
4.3
we get
b_{k}\neq 0
a.s. Then,
\tau_{k}\neq 0
holds by Theorem
3.1
and

\tau_{k}b_{k}=\tfrac{c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k}}{d_{k}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})^{2}}{d_{k}^{2}}+\tfrac{|b_{k}|^{2}}{d_{k}}}>0\quad\text{a.s.}

Also, in the proof of Theorem
3.1
we have shown that
\tau_{k}
satisfies (
22
).
Thus, it holds
\tau_{k}(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})=\tfrac{1}{2}((\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}-1)b_{k})
giving

\displaystyle r(A,B,v^{k+1})-r(A,B,v^{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{k}}{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}(1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k})\tfrac{b_{k}}{2}=\frac{\tau_{k}b_{k}}{2}>0\quad\text{a.s.}

Finally, we note that by the above inequality
(r(A,B,v^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a monotonically increasing sequence that is bounded from above by
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
. Hence, it converges a.s. and its increments admit
\tau_{k}b_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
a.s.
∎

Since we have by Lemma
4.3
that
b_{k}=0
a.s. if and only if
v^{k}
is the generalized eigenvector of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
, we want to ensure that
b_{k}
vanishes as
k\to\infty
. Then, we can use
b_{k}^{2}
as an adjusted stopping criterium.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
. Then it holds
b_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
a.s.

Proof.

Under the assumption,
b_{k}\neq 0
a.s. for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
by Theorem
4.6
. Then, the optimal step size
\tau_{k}
from Theorem
3.1
satisfies (
22
).
Multiplying it with
b_{k}\neq 0
and rearranging the terms yields

\displaystyle 0<b_{k}^{2}=\underbracket{\tau_{k}b_{k}}_{>0}(\tau_{k}b_{k}d_{k}-2(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})),\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}\quad\textrm{a.s.}

(27)

Since
\tau_{k}b_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
a.s. by Theorem
5.1
, it remains to show that
\tau_{k}b_{k}d_{k}-2(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})
is bounded a.s.
Starting with
d_{k}
,
\|x^{k}\|=1
implies that

d_{k}=\left\langle{x^{k}},{Bx^{k}}\right\rangle\leq\|B\|.

Therefore, using (
14
), we obtain

\displaystyle|a_{k}|

\displaystyle=|\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle|\leq\|A\|\|v^{k}\|^{2}\leq\|A\|\|B^{-1}\|\|v^{k}\|_{B}^{2}.

Thus, Theorem
5.1
yields

0<\tau_{k}b_{k}\leq 2(a_{k+1}-a_{k})\leq 2(|a_{k+1}|+|a_{k}|)\leq 4\|A\|\|B^{-1}\|\quad\text{a.s.}

(28)

Analogously, we can show

|c_{k}|=|\left\langle{x^{k}},{Ax^{k}}\right\rangle|\leq\|A\|\|x\|^{2}=\|A\|,

and

|c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k}|\leq\|A\|(1+\|B\|\|B^{-1}\|)=\|A\|(1+\kappa(B)).

Combining this, we arrive at

\displaystyle 0<\tau_{k}b_{k}d_{k}-2(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k})

\displaystyle\leq 4\|A\|\|B\|\|B^{-1}\|+2\|A\|(1+\kappa(B))

\displaystyle=\|A\|(2+6\kappa(B))\quad\text{a.s.}

(29)

for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, by (
27
) and Theorem
5.1
, it follows
b_{k}^{2}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
a.s.
∎

Before, we made an analogy between
|b_{k}|
and
\|\grad f(v^{k})\|
. We formalize this connection in the next lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

The following relation holds true:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]=\tfrac{4}{d-1}\|P_{v^{k}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2}.

Proof.

We rewrite the conditional expectation of
b_{k}^{2}
in terms of
x^{k}
and
v^{k}
as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[\left\langle{x^{k}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}}\right\rangle^{2}]

\displaystyle=4\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[(v^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\textup{H}}x^{k}(x^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}]

\displaystyle=4(v^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\textup{H}}\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[x^{k}(x^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}]A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}

\displaystyle\hskip-16.0pt\overset{\textrm{Lemma~\ref{l: direction main}}}{=}\tfrac{4}{d-1}(v^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\textup{H}}P_{v^{k}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|2P_{v^{k}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\|^{2}=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2},

where we have used
that the projection onto
T_{v^{k}}

is idempotent.
∎

Using Lemma
5.3
and Theorem
5.2
, we
show that
(r(A,B,v^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
and a subsequence of
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges a.s. to the respective eigenspace. Recall that the distance of a point
x
from a set
\mathcal{S}
is defined by

\dist(\mathcal{S},x)\coloneqq\inf_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\;\|x-y\|.

(30)

For finite-dimensional subspaces
\mathcal{S}
, the infimum is attained for some
y\in\mathcal{S}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
Then there exists a random variable
\lambda
, which is a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
a.s., such that the sequence
\lim_{k\to\infty}r(A,B,v_{k})=\lambda
a.s.
Moreover, there exits a subsequence
(v^{k_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying

\dist(G_{\lambda},v^{k_{j}})\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

By Theorem
5.2

we have
b_{k}^{2}\to 0
for
k\to\infty
a.s.
Since
|b_{k}|\leq 2\|A^{\textup{H}}\|\|B^{-1}\|^{1/2}
,
Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem
implies that
\mathbb{E}[b^{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{(x^{k},v^{k})\sim(X^{k},V^{k})}[b_{k}^{2}]\to 0
for
k\to\infty
.
Therefore,
Lemma
5.3
implies

\displaystyle\tfrac{4}{d-1}\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}[\|P_{v^{k}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}[\,\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]\,]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{(x^{k},v^{k})\sim(X^{k},V^{k})}[b_{k}^{2}]\to 0

for
k\to\infty
.
Hence, there exists a subsequence
(v^{k_{j}})_{j}
such that

\|P_{v^{k_{j}}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}\|\to 0
a.s.
This, in turn, shows
that

\dist(T_{v^{k_{j}}}^{\perp},A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}})\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

Thus, there exist a random sequence
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}-\lambda_{j}Bv^{k_{j}}\|\to 0\quad\text{as}\quad j\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

(31)

We observe that

\displaystyle a_{k_{j}}=\left\langle{v^{k_{j}}},{Av^{k_{j}}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{v^{k_{j}}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\underbracket{\left\langle{v^{k_{j}}},{\lambda_{j}Bv^{k_{j}}}\right\rangle}_{=\lambda_{j}}+\underbracket{\left\langle{v^{k_{j}}},{(A^{\textup{H}}-\lambda_{j}B)v^{k_{j}}}\right\rangle}_{\to 0,\ j\to\infty,\ \text{a.s.}}

and using the a.s. convergence of
(a_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
established in Theorem
5.1
,
we conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{k}=\lim_{j\to\infty}a_{k_{j}}(\omega)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\lambda_{j}=:\lambda.

Since
({v}^{k_{j}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
, there exists a convergent subsequence
(v^{k_{j_{\ell}}})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}
with limit
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
.
Combining it with (
31
) yields

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{\ell\to\infty}\|A^{\textup{H}}{v}^{k_{j_{\ell}}}-\lambda_{j_{\ell}}B{v}^{k_{j_{\ell}}}\|=\|A^{\textup{H}}v-\lambda Bv\|\quad\text{a.s.,}

showing that
\lambda
is a generalized eigenvalue of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
a.s.
Finally,

\displaystyle\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}-\lambda Bv^{k_{j}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}-\lambda_{j}Bv^{k_{j}}\|+|\lambda-\lambda_{j}|\|Bv^{k_{j}}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{j}}-\lambda_{j}Bv^{k_{j}}\|+|\lambda-\lambda_{j}|\|B\|^{1/2}\to 0,\quad j\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

gives the convergence of
\dist(G_{\lambda},{v}^{k_{j}})
to
0
.
∎

Next, we show that
\lambda
in Theorem
5.4
only takes the value
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
a.s. First, we state a technical result that with probability at least
p>0
for all
v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
, performing an iteration of Algorithm
1
yields a vector close to a maximizer.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0

and
v,v^{*}\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
.
Consider

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{v}\coloneqq\{x\in T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\mid\;\exists\tau\in\mathbb{R}:R_{v}(x)\in\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon,B}(\pm v^{*})\cap\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\},

where
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon,B}(\pm v^{*})\coloneqq\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon,B}(v^{*})\cup\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon,B}(-v^{*})

is the union of the
\varepsilon
-balls with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{B}

around
v^{*}
and
-v^{*}
.
Then, for the surface measure of the unit sphere
\sigma_{Bv}
in
T_{v}
,
there exists
p=p(\varepsilon,d,B)>0
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}}\;\sigma_{Bv}(\mathcal{D}_{v})\geq p.

Proof.

The proof is analogous to
[
41
, Lemma 2.16]
,
see Appendix
A
.
∎

Now, we are able to prove the strictly monotone convergence
of our sequence of objective values
(r(A,B,v^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

towards
\mathcal{R}(A,B)

almost surely.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
Then, it holds

\displaystyle r(A,B,v^{k})\to\mathcal{R}(A,B),\quad\text{and}\quad\dist(G_{max},v^{k})\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

Consider

\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\{v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}:\left\langle{v},{A^{\textup{H}}v}\right\rangle=r(A,B,v)>\lambda_{2}(B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}})\},

and events
E_{k}\coloneqq\{V^{k}\not\in\mathcal{L}\}.

By monotonicity of
(r(A,B,V^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
established in Theorem
5.1
we get
E_{k+1}\subseteq E_{k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,

\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(v^{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{L})=0\quad\text{for all}\quad v^{k}\in\mathcal{L},

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}}(v^{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{L})=\int_{\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}\backslash\mathcal{L}}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(v^{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{L})\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k}}(v^{k}).

Let
v^{*}\in G_{max}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
. By continuity of
r(A,B,v)
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathbb{B}_{\varepsilon,B}(\pm v^{*})\subseteq\mathcal{L}
. By Lemma
5.5
, we bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V^{k+1}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(v^{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(R_{v^{k}}(\tau_{k}x^{k})\not\in\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}(x^{k}\not\in\mathcal{D}_{v})\leq 1-p.

Consequently, we get

\mathbb{P}(E_{k+1})\leq(1-p)\mathbb{P}_{V^{k}}(v^{k}\not\in\mathcal{L})=(1-p)\mathbb{P}(E_{k}),

and, inductively,
\mathbb{P}(E_{k})\leq(1-p)^{k}\,\mathbb{P}(E_{0})
.
Hence,
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(E_{k})<\infty
and by the first Borel-Cantelli lemma
[
45
, Theorem 7.2.2]
, event
E_{k}
only occur finitely many times and there exists
k_{0}\geq 0
such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
we have
V^{k}\in\mathcal{L}
.
As result, together with Theorem
5.4
and monotonicity of
(r(A,B,V^{k}))_{k=0}^{\infty}
we get that
\lim_{k\to\infty}r(A,B,V^{k})=\mathcal{R}(A,B)
a.s.

Let
u_{1}
, …,
u_{d}
be the eigenvectors of
B^{-1/2}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-1/2}
forming the basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\lambda_{j}
be the corresponding eigenvalues. Since

B^{-1/2}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-1/2}u=\lambda u\quad\text{is equivalent to}\quad B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-1/2}u=\lambda B^{-1/2}u,

the eigenvectors of
B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}}
are
v_{j}=B^{-1/2}u_{j}
and the eigenvalues of
B^{-1}A
and
B^{-1/2}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-1/2}
coincide. Then, with
w^{k}\coloneqq B^{1/2}v^{k}
, we have

\displaystyle r(A,B,v^{k})=\left\langle{v^{k}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}}\right\rangle=\left\langle{w^{k}},{B^{-1/2}A^{\textup{H}}B^{-1/2}w^{k}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{i,\ell=1}^{d}\left\langle{w^{k}},{u_{i}}\right\rangle\left\langle{w^{k}},{u_{\ell}}\right\rangle\lambda_{\ell}\left\langle{u_{i}},{u_{\ell}}\right\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\lambda_{\ell}\left\langle{w^{k}},{u_{\ell}}\right\rangle^{2}\leq\mathcal{R}(A,B)\|w^{k}\|^{2}.

Since
\|w^{k}\|^{2}=\|B^{1/2}v^{k}\|^{2}=\|v^{k}\|_{B}^{2}=1
and
r(A,B,v^{k})\to\mathcal{R}(A,B)
as
k\to\infty
, we get an equality in the limit. This is only possible if
\left\langle{w^{k}},{u_{\ell}}\right\rangle^{2}\to 0
except for
u_{\ell}
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
.
∎

In general, we do not get a convergence of
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
to some
v\in G_{max}
. Yet, we show it in the special case
\dim(G_{max})=1
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})=1
. Then the sequence
(v^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
converges a.s. to a generalized eigenvector of
(A^{\textup{H}},B)
corresponding to
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
5.6
, we have

\dist(G_{max},v^{k})\to 0,\quad\text{as}\quad k\to\infty\quad\text{a.s.}

Since
\dim(G_{max})=1
, we can write
G_{max}=\{\alpha v\mid\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\}
, where
v
is an eigenvector with
\|v\|_{B}=1
corresponding to
\mathcal{R}(A,B)
. Since
\|v^{k}\|_{B}=1
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the only two possible accumulation points of
v^{k}
are
v
and
-v
. Hence, there exists a random sequence
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq\{-1,1\}
, such that
\|e_{k}v^{*}-v^{k}\|_{B}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Then, for
0<\varepsilon<\min\{1,\tfrac{1}{2}\kappa^{-1/2}(B)\}
,
there exists some random
k_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\|e_{k}v^{*}-v^{k}\|_{B}<\varepsilon,\quad\text{for all }k\geq k_{\varepsilon}.

(32)

We show that the sequence
(e_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
is constant for all
k\geq k_{\varepsilon}
by contradiction. Let us assume that
e_{k}=1
and
e_{k+1}=-1
, for some
k>k_{\varepsilon}
. Expanding (
32
)
gives

\tfrac{1}{2}<1-\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}<\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k}}\right\rangle,\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k+1}}\right\rangle<\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}-1<-\tfrac{1}{2}.

On the other hand, following update of Algorithm
1
and
x^{k}\in T_{v^{k}}
, we get

\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k+1}}\right\rangle=\frac{\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k}+\tau_{k}x^{k}}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}}=\frac{\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k}}\right\rangle+\tau_{k}\left\langle{B(v^{*}-v^{k})},{x^{k}}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}}.

We bound the second term by

|\left\langle{B(v^{*}-v^{k})},{x^{k}}\right\rangle|\leq\|(-1)^{e_{k}}v^{*}-v^{k}\|_{B}\|x^{k}\|_{B}\leq\varepsilon\|B\|^{1/2},

which with
d_{k}=\left\langle{x^{k}},{Bx^{k}}\right\rangle\geq\|B^{-1}\|^{-1}
yields the contradiction

\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k+1}}\right\rangle>\frac{\left\langle{Bv^{*}},{v^{k}}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}}-\frac{\varepsilon|\tau_{k}|\lambda_{1}^{1/2}(B)}{\sqrt{1+\tau_{k}^{2}d_{k}}}>-\frac{\varepsilon\lambda_{1}^{1/2}(B)}{\sqrt{d_{k}}}>-\frac{1}{2}.

Analogously, we show that
e_{k}=-1
and
e_{k+1}=1
is impossible, implying that the sequence
(e_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
takes the same value for
k\geq k_{\varepsilon}
.
∎

6
Convergence Rates

In this section, we derive a convergence rates for
(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
and the error in the eigenvector relation

\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}-\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle Bv^{k}\|.

We start with the sublinear convergence result for
(b_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq n}b_{k}^{2}\leq\tfrac{8}{n+1}\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))\quad\text{a.s.}

Proof.

In the proof of Theorem
5.2
, we showed (
27
) and (
29
). Combining these inequalities yields

\displaystyle 0<b_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle=\tau_{k}b_{k}(\tau_{k}b_{k}d_{k}-2(c_{k}-a_{k}d_{k}))

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\tau_{k}b_{k}\|A\|(2+6\kappa(B)),\quad\text{a.s.}

(34)

By Theorem
5.1
we have
\tau_{k}b_{k}=2(a_{k+1}-a_{k})
.
Therefore, using (
28
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0<\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|A\|(1+3\kappa(B))\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tau_{k}b_{k}=4\|A\|(1+3\kappa(B))\sum_{k=0}^{n}(a_{k+1}-a_{k})

(35)

\displaystyle=4\|A\|(1+3\kappa(B))(a_{n+1}-a_{0})\leq 8\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))\quad\text{a.s.}

(36)

On the other hand, it holds
\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}^{2}\geq(n+1)\min_{0\leq k\leq n}b_{k}^{2}
,
which yields the assertion.
∎

Further, we will apply the following uniform bound, whose proof is given in the Appendix
A
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
B\in\mathrm{Sym}_{\succ 0}^{d},v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then it holds

\|(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})y\|\leq\sqrt{\kappa(B)}\|(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv(Bv)^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv\|^{2}})y\|.

Finally, we establish the probabilistic bound for the error in the eigenvector equation.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
\dim(G_{max})<d-1
.
Then,
for all
\varepsilon>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, it holds

\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k}}\Bigl(\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}-\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle Bv^{k}\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\Bigr)\leq\tfrac{8(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))\kappa(B)}{(n+1)\varepsilon}.

Proof.

Taking the expectation in (
36
)
yields

\sum_{k=0}^{n}\mathbb{E}[b_{k}^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}^{2}\Bigr]\leq 8\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B)).

Hence, we have
\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}[b_{k}^{2}]\leq 8\tfrac{\|A\|^{2}}{(n+1)\lambda_{min}(B)}(1+3\kappa(B))
.
In Lemma
5.3
we showed that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\|(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv^{k}(Bv^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv^{k}\|^{2}})A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\|^{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Using
\mathbb{E}[b_{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{(x^{k},v^{k})\sim(X^{k},V^{k})}[b^{k}]
and the law of total expectation
we obtain

\displaystyle\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}\bigl[\big\|\bigl(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv^{k}(Bv^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv^{k}\|^{2}}\bigr)A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}\big\|^{2}\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad=(d-1)\cdot\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}[\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]]

\displaystyle\qquad=(d-1)\cdot\min_{0\leq k\leq n}\mathbb{E}_{(x^{k},v^{k})\sim(X^{k},V^{k})}[b_{k}^{2}]\leq\tfrac{8(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))}{(n+1)}.

Let
k_{*}=k_{*}(n)
be the index
which minimizes the left-hand side.
Then,
by Markov’s inequality,
we obtain for any
\varepsilon>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V^{k_{*}}}\bigl(\big\|(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv^{k_{*}}(Bv^{k_{*}})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv^{k_{*}}\|^{2}})A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}\big\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}\bigl[\big\|\bigl(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv^{k_{*}}(Bv^{k_{*}})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv^{k_{*}}\|^{2}}\bigr)A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}\big\|^{2}\bigr]}{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq 8\tfrac{(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))}{(n+1)\varepsilon}.

(37)

To finish the proof,
we observe that

\displaystyle A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}-\left\langle{v^{k_{*}}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}}\right\rangle Bv^{k_{*}}=(I_{d}-Bv^{k_{*}}(v^{k_{*}})^{\mathrm{T}})A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}

and, consequently, by Lemma
6.2
, we get

\displaystyle\min_{k=0,\ldots,n}\mathbb{P}_{V^{k}}(\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}-\left\langle{v^{k}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}}\right\rangle Bv^{k}\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}_{V^{k_{*}}}(\|A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}-\left\langle{v^{k_{*}}},{A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}}\right\rangle Bv^{k_{*}}\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{P}_{V^{k_{*}}}(\|(I_{d}-Bv^{k_{*}}(v^{k_{*}})^{\mathrm{T}})A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}_{V^{k_{*}}}(\kappa(B)\big\|(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv^{k_{*}}(Bv^{k_{*}})^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv^{k_{*}}\|^{2}})A^{\textup{H}}v^{k_{*}}\big\|^{2}\geq\varepsilon)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq 8\tfrac{(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))\kappa(B)}{(n+1)\varepsilon}.

∎

In particular, setting
\varepsilon\coloneqq 1/\sqrt{n+1}
in Theorem
6.3
, implies that the error is bounded by
1/\sqrt{n+1}
with probability at least
1-\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{n})
. Alternatively, for
0<p<1
, with probability at least
1-p
the error is
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
.

7
Connection to the zeroth-order methods

We can also reinterpret Algorithm
1
as a zeroth-order method.

Theorem 7.1
.

It holds

b_{k}x^{k}=2\widehat{\grad}_{1}f(v^{k})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[b_{k}x^{k}\mid v^{k}]=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\grad f(v^{k}),

where
\grad f(v^{k})
is the Riemannian gradients (
11
) of
f(v^{k})=\left\langle{v^{k}},{Av^{k}}\right\rangle
on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}_{B}
and
\widehat{\grad}_{1}f
is its zeroth-order one-sample approximation (
16
) with scaling parameter
\mu=\tau_{k}
. Consequently, Algorithm
1
can be viewed as a zero-order variant of Riemannian gradient ascent (
15
).
Furthermore, we have

\min_{k=0,\ldots,n}\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}[\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2}]\leq 8\frac{(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))}{(n+1)}.

Proof.

If
b_{k}\neq 0
, by Theorem
3.1
we get
\sign(b_{k})=\sign(\tau_{k})
and the vectors
b_{k}x^{k}
and
\tau^{k}x^{k}
are aligned. Furthermore, we have
r(A,B,v^{k})=f(v^{k})
and Theorem
5.1
yields

b_{k}x^{k}=\frac{2}{\tau_{k}}\frac{\tau_{k}b_{k}}{2}x^{k}=2\frac{f(R_{v^{k}}(\tau_{k}x^{k}))-f(v^{k})}{\tau_{k}}x^{k}=2\,\widehat{\grad}_{1}f(v^{k}).

If
b_{k}=0
, then by Theorem
3.1
, we set
\tau_{k}=0
and

b_{k}x^{k}=0=2\widehat{\grad}_{1}f(v^{k})
with
\mu=0=\tau_{k}
. Here
0/0
is interpreted as
0
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}x^{k}]=\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[x^{k}\cdot 2(x^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}]

\displaystyle\quad=2\,\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[x^{k}(x^{k})^{\mathrm{T}}]A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}=\tfrac{2}{d-1}P_{v^{k}}A^{\textup{H}}v^{k}=\tfrac{1}{d-1}\grad f(v^{k}).

Lemma.
5.3
yields that
(d-1)\mathbb{E}_{x^{k}\sim X^{k}|V^{k}=v^{k}}[b_{k}^{2}]=\|\grad f(v^{k})\|^{2}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

and following the same proof strategy as in Lemma
6.1

leads to

\min_{k=0,\ldots,n}\mathbb{E}_{v^{k}\sim V^{k}}[\|\grad f(v^{k})^{2}\|]\leq 8\tfrac{(d-1)\|A\|^{2}\|B^{-1}\|(1+3\kappa(B))}{(n+1)}.\qed

Remark 7.1

(Improved gradient estimate)
.

Theorem
7.1
shows that
b_{k}x^{k}
serves as an estimate of the Riemannian gradient. We can reduce the variance of this estimate by employing multiple samples. Namely, by sampling independently
x^{k,i}
,
i=1,\ldots,m
and computing the corresponding
b_{k,i}
, the vector
\bar{x}^{k}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}b_{k,i}x^{k,i}
is again an estimate of
\grad f(v^{k})
with (conditional) variance reduced by
1/m
. Furthermore, by Theorem
7.1

\bar{x}^{k}=2\widehat{\grad}_{m}f(v^{k})
.
This observation is the main motivation for Algorithm
2
. Unlike zeroth-order gradient ascent (
15
), we select step sizes
\tau_{k}
optimally, leading to a drastically better performance as shown in Section
8.4
.

Lemma
4.1

For a fixed
v\in\mathbb{S}_{B}^{d-1}
and
\tilde{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, the random variable

X\coloneq P_{v}\tilde{X}/\|P_{v}\tilde{X}\|

is uniformly distributed
on
T_{v}\cap\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\simeq\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
.
Moreover, it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}[xx^{\mathrm{T}}]=\frac{1}{d-1}P_{v}=\frac{1}{d-1}\Bigl(I_{d}-\tfrac{Bv}{\|Bv\|}\tfrac{(Bv)^{\mathrm{T}}}{\|Bv\|}\Bigr).

Proof.

For an orthonormal basis
\{u_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:i=1,\ldots,d-1\}
of
T_{v}
and
u_{d}\coloneqq Bv/\|Bv\|\in T_{v}^{\perp}
, set

U\coloneqq(u_{1}\,\ldots\,u_{d})=(U_{v}\,u_{d})
.
Then it holds

\tilde{X}=UU^{\mathrm{T}}\tilde{X}=U\tilde{Z},\quad\tilde{Z}\coloneqq U^{\mathrm{T}}\tilde{X}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

Then
P_{v}\tilde{X}=U_{v}Z
with
Z\coloneqq(\tilde{Z}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{Z}_{d-1})^{\mathrm{T}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d-1})
and we know by
[
47
, Ex. 3.3.7]
that
Y\coloneqq Z/\|Z\|
follows a uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
.
Finally, since

X=\frac{P_{v}\tilde{X}}{\|P_{v}\tilde{X}\|}=\frac{U_{v}Z}{\|U_{v}Z\|}=U_{v}\frac{Z}{\|Z\|}=U_{v}Y

(39)

and
U_{v}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to T_{v}
is an isometry, this yields the first assertion.

Next, we use the representation
X=U_{v}Y
to compute the covariance of
X
. Since
Y
is uniform distribution on
\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
and does not depend on
v
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}[xx^{\mathrm{T}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[U_{v}y(U_{v}y)^{\mathrm{T}}]=U_{v}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[yy^{\mathrm{T}}]\,U_{v}^{\mathrm{T}}.

The random vector
\sqrt{d-1}\,Y
is isotropic, see
[
47
, Def. 3.2.1, Ex. 3.3.1]
, which means that the covariance of
\mathbb{E}_{y\sim Y}[yy^{\mathrm{T}}]=\frac{1}{d-1}I_{d-1}
. Furthermore, by the properties of the orthogonal projection, we have

P_{v}w=\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}\left\langle{w},{u_{i}}\right\rangle u_{i}=U_{v}U_{v}^{\mathrm{T}}w,\quad\text{for all}\quad w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Consequently,

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim X}[xx^{\mathrm{T}}]=\tfrac{1}{d-1}U_{v}I_{d-1}U_{v}^{\mathrm{T}}=\tfrac{1}{d-1}P_{v}.\qed

\displaystyle(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})y

\displaystyle=\alpha(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})u+(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})P_{v}y

\displaystyle=\alpha(u-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}}u)+(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})P_{v}y=(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})P_{v}y,

and hence

\displaystyle\|(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})y\|\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})w\|\cdot\|P_{v}y\|.

(42)

Expanding the norm in the supremum gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})w\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\|w\|^{2}-2\left\langle{v},{w}\right\rangle\left\langle{Bv},{w}\right\rangle+\left\langle{v},{w}\right\rangle^{2}\|Bv\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}1+\left\langle{v},{w}\right\rangle^{2}\|Bv\|^{2}.

Since
\left\langle{v},{w}\right\rangle^{2}=\left\langle{P_{v}v},{w}\right\rangle^{2}
for all
w\in T_{v}
, the supremum is attained at
w=P_{v}v/\|P_{v}v\|
yielding

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\langle{v},{w}\right\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\|P_{v}v\|^{2}=\|v-\left\langle{v},{u}\right\rangle u\|^{2}=\|v\|^{2}-\left\langle{v},{u}\right\rangle^{2}=\|v\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{\|Bv\|^{2}}.

Hence,
we have

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in T_{v}\\
\|w\|=1\end{subarray}}\|(I_{d}-Bvv^{\mathrm{T}})w\|^{2}=\underbracket{1+\|Bv\|^{2}(\|v\|^{2}-\tfrac{1}{\|Bv\|^{2}})}_{=\|v\|^{2}\|Bv\|^{2}}\leq\kappa(B).

(43)

Combining (
42
) and (
43
) gives the assertion.
∎

Appendix B
Extension to the complex case

In this appendix, we extend our method to complex matrices.
That is, our goal is to find the maximum of the generalized real Rayleigh quotient

\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\max_{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\operatorname{Re}[r(A,B,v)]=\max_{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}[\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle]}{\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle}

(44)

for complex vectors and complex inner product
\left\langle{x},{y}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}y_{i}\bar{x}_{i}
. Here
A\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
and

B\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
is a
Hermitian
positive definite matrix.
Recall that a matrix is Hermitian if
B^{*}=B
with conjugate transpose
B^{*}\coloneqq\bar{B}^{\mathrm{T}}
.
Just as in the real case, maximizing (
44
) is equivalent to finding the leading real eigenvalue of
B^{-1}A^{\textup{H}}
with
A^{\textup{H}}\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}(A+A^{*})

[
5
]
.

To apply our algorithms for the complex problem (
44
),
we identify a complex vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
with real vector
\tilde{v}=(\operatorname{Re}v,\operatorname{Im}v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and a matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
with

\tilde{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
so that
\widetilde{Mv}=\tilde{M}\tilde{v}
for all
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Then, for all
x,v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
and
M\in\mathbb{C}^{d\times d}
we have

\langle\tilde{x},\tilde{M}\tilde{v}\rangle=\operatorname{Re}[\left\langle{x},{Mv}\right\rangle]

and

\mathcal{R}(A,B)=\max_{v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\operatorname{Re}[\left\langle{v},{Av}\right\rangle]}{\left\langle{v},{Bv}\right\rangle}=\max_{\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{\langle\tilde{v},\tilde{A}\tilde{v}\rangle}{\langle\tilde{v},\tilde{B}\tilde{v}\rangle}.

Consequently, we compute
\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{S}^{2d-1}_{B}
by applying Algorithms
1
or
2
for
(\tilde{A},\tilde{B})
and constructing the corresponding
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
. Moreover, the convergence guarantees extend to the complex case.
