Sudan and the erosion of global health ethics: reclaiming the forgotten mandate of sustainable development goals
Authors: Emmanuel Edwar Siddig, Ayman Azhary, Claude Mambo Muvunyi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Sudan struggles to meet Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) targets due to conflict and socio-economic issues.•Millions face hunger, water scarcity, and a failing healthcare system in Sudan.•Achieving SDGs requires justice, reform, and prioritizing human rights in Sudan.

Introduction

Sudan, a nation marked by decades of conflict, political upheaval, and humanitarian crises, faces urgent challenges in achieving equitable health outcomes. While the global community has committed to the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to promote health, peace, and prosperity, many of these objectives remain elusive in Sudan [1]. This situation underscores the urgent need to revisit the ethical foundations of global health initiatives and to recognize the vital role that adherence to SDG mandates plays in safeguarding health justice and equity in Sudan [2].

Sudan's complex history includes multiple civil wars, internal displacement, and chronic underdevelopment, which have severely compromised its healthcare infrastructure. The ongoing conflict in regions like Darfur, along with political transitions, has exacerbated health disparities, leaving vulnerable populations without access to essential services. These circumstances have often led to a disconnect between international aid efforts and local needs, risking the neglect of ethical considerations central to health equity [3].

Hunger and malnutrition have historically been manipulated as strategic tools of control and oppression in conflict-affected areas [4]. According to the most recent report issued by the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), Sudan is currently experiencing a deepening crisis, marked by widespread starvation and a significant increase in cases of acute malnutrition [5]. The IPC Famine Review Committee (FRC) has confirmed the presence of famine in at least five distinct areas within the country, and projections indicate that an additional five regions are anticipated to face famine conditions between December 2024 and May 2025. Moreover, there is a notable risk that 17 more areas could also experience famine during this period. Overall, approximately half of the Sudanese population—around 24.6 million people—are confronting extremely high levels of acute food insecurity, revealing an unprecedented worsening and broadening of the national food and nutrition emergency. This alarming situation is primarily driven by ongoing, devastating conflict, which has resulted in mass displacement on a large scale, a collapsing economy, the disintegration of vital social services, severe societal disruptions, and significant obstacles to humanitarian access.

Specifically, the FRC reports that famine conditions (classified as IPC Phase 5), first identified in August 2024 in Zamzam camp in North Darfur State, have persisted and worsened, expanding into other key locations such as Al Salam and Abu Shouk camps, as well as the Western Nuba Mountains, during October through November of 2024. Moreover, between December 2024 and May 2025, there is a projected expansion of famine in several North Darfur localities, including Um Kadadah, Melit, El Fasher, At Tawisha, and Al Lait. There is also an imminent risk of famine in the Central Nuba Mountains. Regions likely to experience high influxes of internally displaced persons in North and South Darfur are at particular risk, including Tawila, Nyala Janoub, Nyala Shimal, Beliel, Shattaya, As Sunta, Buram, and Kas in South Darfur, along with Medani Al Kubra and Sharg Al Jazirah in Al Jazirah State, Mayo and Alingaz in Jebel-Awilia, Khartoum State, and Al Firdous in East Darfur State [5].

Furthermore, the latest IPC analysis—integrating findings from the Technical Working Group and the FRC—offers an updated outlook that revises the initial projections published in June 2024 for the period from October 2024 to February 2025 [5]. This recent analysis highlights that the severity of food insecurity is now worse than previously anticipated. Between December 2024 and May 2025, an estimated 24.6 million individuals will face severe levels of acute food insecurity (classified as IPC Phase 3 or above), representing a substantial rise of 3.5 million people compared to earlier projections. This figure accounts for more than half of Sudan’s total population. Specifically, about 15.9 million people—equivalent to 33 percent—are expected to be in IPC Phase 3 (Crisis), while approximately 8.1 million people—or 17 percent—are projected to be in IPC Phase 4 (Emergency). Additionally, at least 638,000 individuals—around 1 percent—are likely to be in IPC Phase 5 (Catastrophe), facing conditions indicative of famine. This is not merely an unintended consequence of conflict; it is a deliberate and organized deprivation of food and nutritional resources. Such actions explicitly violate the principles outlined in Sustainable Development Goal 2.1, which commits to ending hunger and guaranteeing access to safe, nutritious food throughout the year. The siege and intentional destruction of food supply chains—including farms, bakeries, and humanitarian aid convoys—have played a direct role in creating conditions that resemble famine.

In addition to the widespread famine crisis exacerbated by the ongoing conflict in Sudan, the current war has precipitated a catastrophic collapse of the country's healthcare system [6]. The violence has led to widespread destruction of hospitals and medical infrastructure, with numerous healthcare facilities damaged or rendered inoperable. Attacks on healthcare workers and professionals by the militia have further worsened the situation, causing a significant decline in medical personnel available to provide essential services [6]. As of July 2025, approximately 75% of hospitals in Sudan are either non-functional or severely damaged, leaving a critical gap in healthcare delivery across the nation. Medical staff operate under dire conditions, often without access to electricity, anesthesia, sterilized equipment, or basic supplies. This ineffectiveness in infrastructure has led to grim outcomes, with many patients suffering from treatable injuries dying due to infections, uncontrolled bleeding, or sepsis. The management of chronic diseases has become challenging, and vaccination campaigns have come to a halt, increasing the risk of preventable disease outbreaks such as measles [7], cholera [8], diphtheria [9], polio [10], and rabies [11]. The health crisis is further compounded by the mental health toll on the population, particularly among children, who are suffering from widespread trauma, anxiety, and depression—conditions that remain largely unaddressed due to the ongoing lack of mental health services and resources [12]. The cumulative impact of deliberate deprivation, international blockades, and intensified violence has systematically dismantled Sudan’s health system, pushing it toward near-total collapse. This dire situation severely hampers progress toward SDG 3 and its “triple billion” targets, which aim to promote healthier populations, achieve universal health coverage, and ensure health emergency preparedness and response. The current crisis highlights the urgent need for sustained international assistance, peacebuilding efforts, and comprehensive health system revitalization to prevent further deterioration and to restore essential healthcare services for the populations in Sudan.

Meanwhile, the violation of SDG 6 remains equally alarming within Sudan. According to reports from humanitarian agencies, a significant portion of the population lacks access to safe drinking water, with many relying on contaminated sources [13]. The ongoing conflict has severely damaged water infrastructure—including wells, water treatment plants, and pumping stations—leading to widespread disruption of water supplies. Over 80% of sewage continues to be discharged untreated into the environment, escalating public health risks [13]. The resurgence of waterborne diseases such as cholera, with over 100,000 cases and 2500 deaths, hepatitis A, and dehydration-related fatalities is rapidly increasing [[14], [15], [16]], further straining the fragile health system. Women and girls are especially vulnerable; thousands of women of reproductive age face inadequate access to essential reproductive health services, safe hygiene facilities, and sanitation. The destruction of water infrastructure and the compromised sanitation systems constitute a clear breach of SDG 6.1, which aims to ensure universal access to safe, affordable drinking water and sanitation for all. This crisis not only violates international commitments and legal norms but also represents a profound betrayal of global efforts to promote health, well-being, and dignity for all populations affected by conflict and displacement in Sudan.

The silence of the global health community: a call to action in Sudan

The ongoing health crisis in Sudan, marked by the collapse of healthcare infrastructure, mass displacement, and widespread disease, demands urgent and sustained international attention. Despite the monumental scale of suffering—including hospitals rendered inoperable, shortages of essential medicines, and a surge in preventable illnesses—the global health community remains largely silent. This silence exacerbates the vulnerability of millions who lack access to life-saving services, particularly in conflict-affected regions where healthcare workers are targeted or unable to operate safely. The world cannot afford to turn a blind eye to Sudan's mounting health catastrophe; silence in the face of such suffering is a form of neglect that perpetuates the cycle of desperation and death. This moment calls for a decisive and collective response from the global health community. Immediate action is needed to restore health services, provide emergency medical supplies, and support health workers risking their lives in conflict zones. Advocacy for increased humanitarian aid, investment in rebuilding health infrastructure, and diplomatic efforts to cease hostilities are crucial steps toward halting this crisis. The international community has a moral obligation to prioritize Sudan, ensuring that no one is left behind in the pursuit of health, peace, and stability. The time to act is now—silent complacency only deepens the suffering and delays the path to recovery and resilience for the Sudanese people.

In conclusion, achieving SDGs 2, 3, and 6 in Sudan necessitates a transition from emergency response to systemic reform and inclusive governance. To protect these basic rights, the world needs to make a new commitment to peace and fairness. Sudan is a key test of the international community's determination not to leave anyone behind.
