Hepatokine FGL1 drives endothelial injury and atherogenesis via integrin β1-mediated endothelial–mesenchymal transition
Authors: Yixiu Zhao, Huan Chen, Liuqing Yin, Ruirui Meng, Jiangfei Zheng, Yue Zhang, Zhiqi Wang, Yi Chen, Rui Xu, Jing Liu, Yanxi Li, Na An, Houwei Li, Yan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtherosclerosis is driven by metabolic dysregulation and endothelial dysfunction; however, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying its pathogenesis remain incompletely elucidated. Fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1) is a well-established hepatokine with critical roles in regulating tumorigenesis and metabolic dysfunction, while its involvement in atherogenesis remains unclear. Our clinical and preclinical studies revealed elevated plasma FGL1 levels in atherosclerotic patients and mice, correlating with arterial stenosis severity. Prolonged high-fat diet exposure specifically upregulated hepatic FGL1 expression. Hepatic FGL1 overexpression accelerated atherosclerosis, while liver-specific knockdown reduced plaque burden. Exogenous administration of FGL1 accelerated endothelial injury and atherosclerosis progression. Mechanistically, liver-derived FGL1 accumulates in vascular endothelium through fibrinogen C-terminal domain Trp225-mediated binding to integrin β1's vWFA domain. This interaction promotes the conformational change of integrin β1 and the formation of integrin β1/ILK1 complex, co-activating FAK/ERK and Smad signaling to drive transcriptional reprogramming of endothelial cells. Therapeutic interventions targeting FGL1–integrin β1 axis by integrin β1 blocking antibody, FGL1-neutralizing antibody, or RGD peptide effectively attenuated endothelial injury and atherogenic progression in murine models. These findings establish hepatic FGL1 as a novel endocrine regulator of vascular pathophysiology, highlighting the FGL1–integrin β1 axis as a promising therapeutic target for endothelial protection and atherosclerosis management.Key words: Atherosclerosis, Hepatokine, EndoMT, FGL1, Integrin β1, ILK1, RGD peptide, vWFA domain

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a chronic, metabolic-associated disease and the pathological basis for life-threatening cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarction and stroke. Risk factors for atherosclerosis, such as hypercholesterolemia, inflammation, elicit the development of atherosclerotic plaques by promoting endothelial dysfunction and injury1, 2, 3. Among the cellular processes implicated in this pathology, endothelial–mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) has emerged as a key adaptive mechanism. EndoMT is defined as the transdifferentiation of endothelial cells into mesenchymal-like phenotypes. This process involves dynamic phenotypic and functional remodeling of endothelial cells, manifested by decreased expression of endothelial markers, such as platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1(CD31) and VE-cadherin, and concomitant with increased expression of mesenchymal markers, such as Vimentin or N-Cadherin. These pathological changes directly contribute to enhanced stress fiber assembly and destabilization of intercellular junctions, synergistically impairing vascular barrier integrity while heightening endothelial permeability4,5. Under proatherogenic conditions, EndoMT serves as a critical driver of both subendothelial lipid deposition and enhanced monocyte transmigration, thereby facilitating the formation and advancement of atherosclerotic lesions6,7.

Fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1), also designated as hepatocyte-derived fibrinogen-related protein-1 or hepatocyte proliferation-promoting factor, is a hepatokine originally identified in human hepatocellular carcinoma and has been established as a distinctive member of the fibrinogen-related protein family8, 9, 10. Emerging evidence has identified FGL1 as a critical prognostic biomarker across multiple malignancies. Elevated circulating FGL1 concentrations drive tumor proliferation through coordinated induction of immune evasion, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) reprogramming, and extracellular matrix remodeling, culminating in metastatic dissemination11, 12, 13. Beyond oncogenesis, FGL1 has emerged as a modulator in metabolic pathophysiology, with its hepatic secretion pattern directly influencing insulin signaling cascades and lipid homeostasis14, 15, 16, 17. Nevertheless, its regulatory functions in vascular EndoMT—a cellular transition analogous to EMT—and the ensuing loss of endothelial integrity and progression of atherogenesis remain poorly defined.

Structurally, FGL1 comprises 312 amino acids and harbors a fibrinogen-related domain (FReDs) at its C-terminal region, resembling other members of fibrinogen superfamily, including fibronectin, angiopoietins, and the β and γ chains of fibrinogen. This structural homology implies that FGL1 may execute its biological functions via interactions with fibrinogen-related receptors on target cell membranes10,18. Integrins, a family of transmembrane heterodimeric proteins composed of α (120–180 kDa) and β subunits (90–110 kDa), serve as critical mediators of cell-matrix adhesion. By binding extracellular matrix components, integrins undergo conformational changes upon activation and initiate downstream signaling cascades, thereby playing pivotal roles in cellular differentiation19, 20. Endothelial integrin β1 activation drives EndoMT through focal adhesion kinase (FAK), extracellular regulative kinase (ERK), and transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β)/SMAD pathway activation, positioning it as a viable therapeutic target for endothelial dysfunction and vascular remodeling21, 22, 23, 24, 25. Hemodynamic abnormalities and oxidized lipoproteins deposition in vascular endothelium promote integrin β1 high-affinity conformational changes, thereby enhancing its activation and endothelial phenotype transition26. However, the intrinsic mechanism of integrin β1 activation in endothelial injury and atherogenesis, and the responsiveness of endothelial integrin β1 to FGL1 remains unexplored.

In the present study, we demonstrate that plasma FGL1 upregulates in response to high-fat diet. Hepatic FGL1 play a critical role in high-fat-diet-disrupted endothelial integrity and atherosclerotic plaque formation. FGL1 exacerbates the vascular EndoMT and atherogenesis through endothelial integrin β1 activation and subsequent downstream FAK/ERK and SMAD signaling cascades. Therapeutic interventions through hepatic FGL1 expression, circulating FGL1 neutralization or integrin β1 inhibition substantially reduced aortic EndoMT progression and atherosclerotic lesion burden in preclinical models. Structural analysis identified the Trp225 residue of in the fibrinogen domain of FGL1 as the critical binding interface mediating interaction with integrin β1's extracellular von Willebrand factor A (vWFA) domain. Collectively, our findings establish FGL1 as both a diagnostic biomarker for early-stage atherosclerosis and a pathogenic driver of disease progression. Importantly, our work demonstrates the therapeutic potential of targeting the FGL1–integrin β1 axis for cardiovascular disease management.

Materials and methods

Human blood samples and ethics statement

Peripheral blood samples were collected from 52 healthy volunteers and 52 atherosclerosis patients (aged 35–65 years) at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Harbin, China), excluding individuals with hyperglycemia (fasting blood glucose ≥7.0 mmol/L) or elevated glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%). All procedures received ethics committee approval (No. IRB5029722) with waived individual consent. Plasma was isolated by centrifugation (3000×g, 15 min) and stored at −80 °C until analysis. The study strictly adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki principles and complied with national ethical guidelines for clinical research.

Mice, antibody, peptide, recombinant protein, virus and virus infection

Apoe−/− mice (Male, 20–22 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. (Beijing, China). All animals were provided with unrestricted access to food and water and were housed in a controlled environment maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C and a relative humidity ranging from 50% to 70%. The mice were randomly grouped according to their initial bodyweights. Firstly, Apoe−/− mice were subjected to either a 16-week normal diet (ND) or a high-fat-diet (HFD, consisting of 63.6% basal feed, 20% sucrose, 15% lard, 1.2% cholesterol, and 0.2% sodium cholate) to induce the development of atherosclerosis. In another series of experiments, AAV8-TBG vector was constructed by Cyagen Biosciences and injected to atherosclerotic Apoe−/− mice to increase or suppress hepatic FGL1 expression. An AAV8-TBG-NC vector was employed as a negative control. In the separate set of experiments, Apoe−/− mice were intravenously injected with FGL1 recombinant protein (15 μg/kg; R&D Systems; 10285-HE-050; once weekly) to increase circulating FGL1 levels. The other group of mice were injected with an equivalent volume of saline served as controls. Apoe−/− mice were intravenously injected with FGL1 antibody (15 μg/kg; Bio X Cell; 177R4; BE0332; once weekly) to reduce circulating FGL1, while mice received an equivalent volume of control immunoglobulin G (IgG) served as controls. The intravenous injection of integrin β1 antibody (15 μg/kg, R&D Systems, MAB17781, once weekly) and RGD peptide (Gly–Arg–Gly–Asp–Asn–Pro, GRGDNP, 5 mg/kg, MCE, HY-P1740A, twice weekly) were employed to antagonize the receptor–ligand interaction and the activation of integrin β1. The body weight of the mice was recorded once a week. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation at the end of the experiment. The blood was obtained from the inner canthus and the plasma was subsequently separated by centrifugation. The entire thoracic aorta was dissected and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for oil red O staining to investigate plaque deposition. Mouse hearts were isolated and embedded in OCT (#4683, Sakura) and stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

All animal procedures were performed in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 and the National Institutes of Health guide for the care and use of laboratory animals. The experimental protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the College of Pharmacy at Harbin Medical University (No. IRB3017619). Sample sizes were determined based on previous experimental results and aligned with those commonly used in the field, ensuring sufficient statistical power while adhering to animal welfare principles. Moreover, this study employed a randomized, double-blind experimental design. Animals were randomly grouped according to the serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels using a computer-generated random number table. The researcher who was not involved in subsequent experimental procedures or data analysis assigned animals to the respective groups accordingly. Researchers that conducted interventions were unaware of the intervention reagents, and individuals responsible for data collection and statistical analysis were also blinded to the group identities. The group codes were only revealed after all analyses were completed, ensuring an unbiased interpretation of the results.

ELISA assay

The plasma concentration of FGL1 and C-reactive protein (CRP) of human plasma samples was quantified using an ELISA assay kit (#CSB-EL008653HU, Cusbio; #KE00004, Proteintech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The plasma concentration of FGL1 of atherosclerotic mice was quantified using an ELISA assay kit (#CSB-EL008653MO, Cusbio) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Serum biochemical analysis

The blood sample was collected from the inner canthus and the serum was subsequently separated by centrifugation. Serum lipid profiles, including total cholesterol (TC), total triglyceride (TG), LDL-C, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), glutamic-pyruvic transaminase (ALT), glutamic oxaloacetic transaminase (AST) were determined using an Automatic Biochemical Analyzer (Hitachi 7180, Japan) with corresponding kits provided by Medical System (Hangzhou, China).

Doppler ultrasound

Doppler ultrasound imaging of the aorta was conducted on mice using the VINNO6 imaging system. The mice were anesthetized with continuous inhalation of low-dose isoflurane, and then fixed on the electrocardiogram (ECG) electronic board in a supine position. The inner diameters of the ascending aorta and aortic arch during diastole and systole, as well as the ratio of retrograde to antegrade blood flow at the aortic arch, were measured by ultrasound. Systolic aortic diameter (AS) was measured at the point of maximal anterior motion of the ascending aorta (systole), and diastolic aortic diameter (AD) was measured at the Q wave on ECG (end diastole) using M-mode echocardiography. Oil red O staining of the aorta and aortic root

Mice were deeply anesthetized before being euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the entire aortas were isolated and immersed in PBS. The surrounding adipose tissues and connective tissues were meticulously dissected under microscopic observation. Subsequently, the aortas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, followed by immersion in a 30% sucrose solution overnight. The sunken thoracic aortas were then incubated in an Oil Red O solution (0.5 g of Oil Red O powder dissolved in 100 mL of isopropyl alcohol) (#O0625, Sigma) on a shaker for 30 min. Any non-specific staining was effectively removed through washing with 60% isopropyl alcohol. The staining results were observed and documented using a microscope (Olympus SZX-SDO2, Japan).

The aortic root sections of 10 μm were prepared using a freezing microtome (#CM1950, Leica). Sections were immersed in 60% isopropyl alcohol for 20 s, transferred to an ice–water bath for 10 s, and stained with Oil Red O for 15 min. Excess dye was removed by brief immersion in 60% isopropyl alcohol (3 s) and ice water (10 s). Sections were then stained with Mayer's hematoxylin (#G1080, Solarbio) for 3 min and rinsed in water for 10 min. After blotting dry with filter paper, sections were mounted with glycerol jelly and examined under a microscope (Olympus SZX-SDO2, Japan).

Hematoxylin–eosin staining of aortic root

The hematoxylin–eosin staining procedure was performed according to the Hematoxylin–Eosin Stain Kit (#G1120, Solarbio) guidelines. Aortic root sections were fixed with Davidson's fixative (#G2340, Solarbio) for 1 min at room temperature and rinsed with running water. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 6 min, rinsed in running water for 10 min to restore blue color, differentiated with acid alcohol for 2 min, and stained with eosin for 2 min. Excess stain was removed with deionized water. Tissue was dehydrated sequentially in 75%, 85%, 95%, and 100% ethanol solutions, cleared with xylene, and mounted with glycerol jelly. Staining results were observed and recorded using a microscope (Olympus SZX-SDO2, Japan).

Masson staining of aortic root

The Masson staining procedure was performed according to the instructions in the Modified Masson's Trichrome Stain Kit (#G1346, Solarbio). Frozen aortic root sections were fixed with Davidson's fixative for 1 min at room temperature and rinsed under running water. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, differentiated with acidic ethanol for 5–10 s, and washed with deionized water. They were then stained sequentially with Masson blue for 1 min, ponceau solution for 2 min, phosphomolybdic acid for 1 min, and aniline blue for 1 min, with intermediate washes and differentiation steps as needed. Excess dye was removed using a weak acid solution. Finally, sections were mounted with glycerol jelly and examined under a microscope (Olympus SZX-SDO2, Japan).

Determination of cholesterol and triglyceride content

Liver tissue was accurately weighed, and anhydrous ethanol was added to liver tissue with a ratio of liver weight to anhydrous ethanol of 1:9. The following operations were all carried out in ice-cold conditions. The homogenate of liver tissue was prepared and centrifuged at 2500×g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected as the test sample. The cholesterol and triglyceride contents in the samples were determined using the corresponding assay kits (BC1985, BC0625; Solarbio Life Science).

Cell culture

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs) and mouse aortic endothelial cells (MAECs) were obtained from Otwo Biotech, Shenzhen, China (Catalog No. HTX1922). Cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#S711-001, Lonsera) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (S0198, Haigene) in a controlled environment maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% air. Cells were passaged when their confluence exceeded 90%, and for subsequent experiments, HUVECs from passages 3 to 8 were selected. HUVECs were uniformly seeded in six-well plates and cultured until they reached a confluence of 90%. HUVECs were pretreated with FGL1 recombinant protein (#10285-HE, R&D Systems) for 24 h for FGL1's efficacy evaluation. In the other series of experiments, HUVECs were pretreated with FAK inhibitor GSK2256098 (#T2281, Topscience), MEK inhibitor PD98059 (#T2623, Topscience), ALK inhibitor SB431542 (#T1726, Topscience) for 30 min, or pretreated with Integrin β1 antibody (#MAB17781, R&D Systems) for 6 h, followed by treatment with FGL1. Then the cells were harvested for the following experiments.

Construction and transfection of siRNA, pcDNA3.1 plasmid and recombinant protein

The ILK siRNA was designed and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China) based on the human ILK1 gene (NCBI Gene ID: 3611). A non-targeting scrambled siRNA was used as the negative control. The ITGB1 pcDNA3.1 plasmid was designed and synthesized by GeneChem (Shanghai, China) based on the human ITGB1 gene (NCBI Gene ID: 3688). Transfection was performed using the Lipofectamine 2000 system according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded in 6-well plates. When the cells reached 70%–80% confluence, the original culture medium was discarded and replaced with 1 mL of empty DMEM medium. The siRNA solution was thawed on ice. The Lipofectamine 2000 transfection mixture was prepared in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions with a final siRNA concentration of 50 nmol/L per well. Add the mixture to the corresponding cell wells and incubate for 6 h. Replace the medium with complete medium and culture cells for another 24 h before subsequent experiments. The transfection of the integrin β1 (ITGB1) pcDNA3.1 plasmids was performed using the same protocol. The transfection efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis.

The recombinant FGL1 mutagenesis protein was synthesized by GeneScript (Nanjing, China) based on the human FGL1 gene (NCBI Gene ID: 2267), with the tryptophan at position 225 was mutated to alanine (FGL1W225A). The plasmid was transfected into suspension-cultured HEK293 cells and cultured at 37 °C under 8% CO2 for 5–6 days. The cell lysate was collected, centrifuged, and filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to remove cell debris. Target proteins were first captured via nickel-affinity chromatography and eluted with an imidazole gradient to purify the His-tagged fusion protein. Subsequent purification was performed using Strep-Tactin XT affinity chromatography to obtain highly purified protein. Following buffer exchange and concentration, protein purity, molecular weight, and specificity were assessed by SDS-PAGE and SEC-HPLC analysis.

Determination of collagen content

The collagen content was determined following the instructions provided by the Sircol Soluble Collagen Assay Kit (#S1000, Biocolor). Fresh cell culture supernatant (600 μL) was combined with pre-cooled isolation concentration reagent (120 μL) for thorough homogenization. The resulting mixture was then incubated at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min. Then the supernatant was carefully discarded and Sircol staining reagent (1 mL) was added. The tube was gently inverted every 5 min for a total of 30 min to ensure thorough mixing, followed by centrifugation at 12,000×g for 10 min. After removing the supernatant, pre-cooled Acid-Salt Wash Reagent (750 μL) was added. The tube was centrifuged again at 12,000×g for 10 min, and the resulting supernatant was discarded. The precipitate was dissolved by adding Alkali Reagent (250 μL) and thoroughly mixing it using a vortex. The sample solution (10 μL) was subsequently diluted with Alkali Reagent (180 μL) and transferred to a 96-well plate. Following this, the absorbance of the sample was measured at a wavelength of 555 nm using microplate reader (ELX800, Bio-Tek, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

HUVECs were cultured in glass bottom dishes until they reached a confluence of 70%. The cells were gently washed with PBS and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. After rinsing with PBS, the cells were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (BS084, Biosharp) (1 mL) for 10 min. Following this, the cells were washed with PBS again and blocked with 2% BSA solution for 50 min. Then the cells were incubated with a diluted primary antibody at 4 °C overnight in darkness. On the following day, the cells were washed with PBS and incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. The nuclei were labeled with DAPI (#C1006, Beyotime) for 3 min. The images were captured using laser confocal microscopy (FV1000, Olympus, Japan).

The aortic root sections were treated by infiltration with pre-cooled acetone for 10 min. After washed with PBS, the sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min. Following another PBS rinse, the sections were sealed with a 1% BSA-PBS solution at room temperature for 1 h. After rinsing with PBS, the sections were treated with a diluted primary antibody and incubated at 4 °C overnight. On the subsequent day, the sections were washed with PBS incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The nuclei were labeled with DAPI, and images were captured using laser confocal microscopy (FV1000, Olympus, Japan).

Frozen sections of the aortic root were initially fixed with a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 10 min. Subsequently, they were rinsed with PBS and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. Following this, the permeable solution was discarded, and the sections were washed with PBS again. Thereafter, the sections were incubated with a blocking solution at 37 °C for 30 min. The sections were subsequently incubated with a primary antibody solution, prepared using Duolink antibody diluent, overnight at 4 °C. Following this incubation, the primary antibody solution was removed, and the sections were washed with washing buffer A at room temperature. The sections were then exposed to the PLA probe solution at 37 °C for 100 min. After incubation, the sections were washed with washing buffer A and then incubated with the connecting solution for 30 min at 37 °C. Following another washing with washing buffer A, the sections were incubated with the amplified solution at 37 °C for 100 min, and then washed by washing buffer B. Then the sections were sealed with Duolink® in situ sealer containing DAPI. The results were documented using Confocal microscopy, and the Image J software was utilized for the statistical analysis of the results.

Transwell permeability assay

HUVECs were seeded into the upper chamber of a 12-well Transwell insert (pore size: 0.4 μm) at a density of approximately 200,000 cells per well and cultured for 24 h until a confluent monolayer formed. The culture medium was then removed, and 500 μL of serum-free DMEM was added to the lower chamber. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP, 1 mg/mL, 5 μL, #A0308, Beyotime) solution was added to the upper chamber. Cells were treated with recombinant FGL1 protein at concentrations of 50 ng/mL and 100 ng/mL for 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 h, respectively. At each time point, 10 μL of medium from the lower chamber was transferred to a 96-well plate. Subsequently, 100 μL of TMB chromogenic substrate solution (#P0206, Beyotime) was added to each well to initiate the enzyme reaction, and the plate was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Then the reaction was terminated by adding 100 μL of stop solution for TMB substrate (#P0215, Beyotime), and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm to calculate the concentration of HRP based on a standard curve generated from the HRP standard.

Western blot analysis

Protein extraction was carried out under ice-cold conditions. For cell or tissue lysis, ice-cold protein lysis buffer (comprising RIPA: phosphatase inhibitor: protease inhibitor in a ratio of 100:10:1) was employed. Then the lysis mixture was centrifuged at 16,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration was measured using BCA kit (#P0012, Beyotime). The protein samples were then heated in a metal bath at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by cooled and subjected to gel electrophoresis for separation. After complete separation, the proteins were transferred to a NC membrane, which was then blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h. Subsequently, the NC membrane was washed with PBST on a shaker for 30 min and incubated with diluted primary antibodies for corresponding proteins at 4 °C overnight. Afterward, the NC membrane was washed with PBST on a shaker for 30 min and subsequently incubated with anti-HRP antibodies (1:10,000) for 50 min in the dark at room temperature. Then the NC membrane was washed with TBST on a shaker in darkness for 30 min. The protein abundance was detected using a fluorescence scanning system (Odyssey CLX, LI-COR, USA). The gray value of protein bands was analyzed using the Image J software.

Immunoprecipitation

Co-immunoprecipitation studies were performed using Immunoprecipitation Assay Kit (Protein A/G Magnetic Beads, #P2179S, Beyotime). The protein extraction was performed under cold conditions. Cells were incubated with lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (#P8340, Sigma) and the lysate was centrifuged at 14,000×g and 4 °C for 15 min. Subsequently, the supernatant (100 μL) was transferred into a tube and incubated overnight at 4 °C on a rotating mixer with primary antibody or control Immunoglobulin G working solution to prepare the antigen–antibody complex. The remaining supernatant served as the Input. The antigen–antibody complex (100 μL) was incubated with magnetic beads (100 μL) suspended in TBS for 1 h at room temperature. Then the sample tube was placed on a magnetic rack to facilitate the collection of the beads–antigen–antibody complex. After washing the beads–antigen–antibody complex with lysate, the complex was diluted with SDS-PAGE loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 5 min. The sample was placed on the magnetic rack to separate the magnetic beads (#FMS012, Beyotime). The resulting supernatant was collected for Western blot analysis.

Pull-down

His-Pull down assays were performed using the PierceTM Pull-Down PolyHis Protein Kit (#21277, Thermo Scientific). Polyhistidine-tagged FGL1 protein and its mutant (100 μg each, GenScript) were separately added to marked Pierce Spin Columns containing well-equilibrated HisPur™ Cobalt Resin. The columns were incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with gentle rocking on a rotating platform. Subsequently, HEK-293T cell lysates (transfected with Flag-tagged ITGB1 plasmid) were collected. After the initial incubation of recombinant proteins with the resin, the resin was centrifuged at 1300×g for 1 min and washed five times with wash buffer. Next, 1000 μg of cell lysate was diluted to 600 μL and added to the spin columns, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with gentle shaking on a rotating platform. The resin was again centrifuged at 1300×g for 1 min and washed five times. Finally, the bound proteins were eluted with 250 μL of 290 mmol/L Imidazole Elution Buffer by centrifuging at 1300×g for 1 min. The eluate was used for SDS-PAGE and immunoblotting analyses.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding experiments

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) binding experiments were conducted on the Biacore 8K system. The running buffer was freshly prepared 10 mmol/L HEPES (pH 7.4), 150 mmol/L NaCl, and 0.05% Tween-20, and was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter. First, the optimal pH condition for CD29 protein immobilization was determined by pH scouting experiments at 25 °C: CD29 protein was diluted to 10 μg/mL in sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.5, 5.0, and 5.5, respectively, and coupling tests were performed. Sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.5 achieved the best coupling efficiency and was selected as the subsequent immobilization condition. Subsequently, CD29 protein (10 μg/mL, dissolved in sodium acetate buffer at pH 4.5) was covalently coupled to the CM5 chip surface at a flow rate of 10 μL/min. The specific operation included EDC/NHS activation (420 s), protein coupling (1000 s), and ethanolamine blocking (420 s). A stable immobilization level of approximately 1500 RU was achieved in one detection channel. FGL1 was prepared in six gradient concentrations ranging from 15.625 to 1000 nmol/L using the running buffer. Binding analysis was performed using the multi-cycle kinetics mode, with each cycle consisting of a 120 s binding phase and a 300 s dissociation phase. All binding reactions were completed at 25 °C and in a constant buffer system. Experimental data were processed using Biacore™ Insight Evaluation Software 4.0, and the multi-cycle kinetics analysis process was selected. Five consecutive concentration data points were selected and fitted based on the 1:1 Langmuir binding model to accurately calculate the association rate constant (ka), dissociation rate constant (kd), and equilibrium dissociation constant (KD).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments using individual animals or cell cultures and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 software. The sample sizes are provided in the Figure legends, and the distribution of data was tested. For normally distributed variables, two groups of data were tested using an unpaired t-test; one-way ANOVA was applied for multiple groups of data for one factor analysis; two-way ANOVA was applied for multiple groups of data for two factor analysis. For non-normally distributed variables, a nonparametric Mann–Whitney test was applied for two groups of data, and Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's multiple comparisons test was applied for multiple groups of data. Pearson correlation analysis was used to calculate R and P values of data with normal distribution. Spearman correlation analysis was used to calculate the R and P values of data distributed abnormally. A P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hepatokine FGL1 shows elevated plasma levels, endothelial accumulation and associates with EndoMT in atherosclerosis models

To elucidate the relevance of circulating FGL1 in atherosclerosis progression, 52 angiography-confirmed coronary atherosclerosis patients (63.8 ± 10.9 years; 56% male) and 52 age-/sex-matched healthy controls were enrolled in the study. No significant differences in BMI, fasting blood glucose, or HbA1c were found between the two groups, and neither had a history of statin use (Table 1). Coronary artery disease severity was quantified using SYNTAX scoring. Normal subjects presented with angiographically normal coronary arteries, while atherosclerotic patients (AS) demonstrated obstructive lesions primarily localized to the left anterior-descending, circumflex, and right coronary arteries (Fig. 1A). High-sensitivity ELISA quantification identified 1.7-fold elevated plasma FGL1 levels in AS patients (88.34 ± 27.29 ng/mL) versus normal controls (51.91 ± 6.50 ng/mL) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Stratification by angiographic severity (Level I: 20%–49% stenosis; Level II: 50%–75%; Level III: >75%) demonstrated FGL1 level progression across disease stages (Fig. 1C). Moreover, FGL1 concentration in plasma showed a significant positive correlation with stenosis severity (Pearson's r = 0.68, 95%CI: 0.50–0.80, P < 0.01) and c-reactive protein concentration (r = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.27–0.69, P < 0.01), indicating a positive association with atherosclerosis progression (Fig. 1D and E). However, ASCVD patients had higher systolic blood pressure (P = 0.0437), fibrinogen (P = 0.0441), and ApoA1 (P = 0.0241) levels, suggesting endothelial dysfunction and abnormal lipid transport (Table 1). To validate the correlation between FGL1 and atherosclerosis, we established a murine model by feeding APOE−/− mice (male) HFD for 16 weeks (Fig. 1F). We observed a significant increase in body weight and serum lipids contents, including elevated TC, TG, LDL-C as well as reduced HDL-C, in HFD-fed mice (Supporting Information Fig. S1A and S1B). Histopathological analysis confirmed the formation of aortic plaque in HFD-fed mice (Fig. S1C–S1F). Temporal analysis of plasma FGL1 were determined during atherosclerosis induction. ELISA analysis demonstrated the progressive elevation of FGL1 during atherosclerosis induction and a substantial elevation in atherosclerotic mice (99.90 ± 38.70 ng/mL) compared to control mice (27.10 ± 14.51 ng/mL) at the endpoint of the experiments (Fig. 1G and H). Ultrasonographic hemodynamic assessment showed significant reductions in aortic diameter variability (end-diastole to end-systole) and forward/reverse flow velocity ratios, correlating inversely with plasma FGL1 levels (Aortic arch: r = −0.55; Outflow tract: r = −0.54; Flow velocity ratio: r = −0.54; P < 0.01) (Fig. 1I–K). To clarify the source of elevated FGL1 in plasma, we respectively detected the protein expression and mRNA levels of FGL1 in liver and aortic tissues. Immunofluorescence and Western blot analysis showed that FGL1 protein levels were significantly elevated in both aortic tissue and liver, but the mRNA expression patterns were different. In the aorta, there was no significant difference in FGL1 mRNA levels between Control and AS mice; however, in the liver, FGL1 mRNA levels were significantly upregulated in AS mice (Fig. 1L and M). This result suggests that FGL1 may release into the blood in response to metabolic stress from the liver and accumulate in the plaque area. Similar results were also obtained from the RNA sequencing data of human and mouse atherosclerotic tissues (GEO data set GSE28829; GSE43292; GSE163154; GSE117782) (Fig. 1N). To elucidate the pathophysiological link between FGL1 and endothelial dysfunction, we assessed EndoMT in the endothelium of atherosclerotic mice by CD31/Vimentin co-staining. Results showed the downregulation of endothelial marker and upregulation of mesenchymal marker in aortic roots (Fig. S1G). Immunofluorescence co-localization studies revealed endothelial abundance of FGL1 and concurrent with CD31 downregulation and Vimentin upregulation in atherosclerotic aortic endothelium (Fig. 1O and P). These findings establish circulating FGL1 as a biomarker positively associated with vascular endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis progression.

Hepatic FGL1 overexpression exacerbates aortic EndoMT and accelerates atherosclerotic plaque progression

To investigate the pathophysiological effects of hepatic FGL1 on vascular EndoMT and atherogenesis, an AAV8-TBG-EGFP/Fgl1 vector was constructed for enhancing hepatic FGL1 expression in atherosclerotic APOE−/− mice (male, 8 weeks old), with an empty vector-injected mice serving as negative controls (Fig. 2A). Quantitative RT-PCR and immunofluorescence analysis confirmed marked upregulations in both hepatic FGL1 mRNA and protein expression following AAV8-TBG-Fgl1 vector treatment for 4 weeks (Supporting Information Fig. S2A and S2B). ELISA quantification demonstrated a significant plasma FGL1 elevation after hepatic FGL1 overexpression (Δ = 36.68 ng/mL, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2B). Doppler ultrasound analysis revealed hepatic FGL1 overexpression induced hemodynamic impairment in atherosclerotic mice, characterized by reduced systolic (Δ = −17.95 cm/s, P = 0.002) and mean flow velocities (Δ = −5.60 cm/s, P = 0.010) without diastolic flow velocity alterations (Fig. 2C). M-mode echocardiography further showed compromised vascular compliance through increased elastic modulus index (aortic outflow tract: Δ = +193.8, P = 0.0036; aortic arch: Δ = +161.0, P = 0.0018) and stiffness index (aortic outflow tract: Δ = +2.087, P = 0.0005; aortic arch: Δ = +1.871, P = 0.0008), accompanied by reduced distensibility index (aortic outflow tract: Δ = −0.021, P = 0.0008; aortic arch: Δ = −0.011, P = 0.0086) and attenuated diameter variation (aortic outflow tract: Δ = −0.1283 mm, P = 0.0054; aortic arch: Δ = −0.1214 mm, P = 0.0081) (Fig. 2D). Immunofluorescence co-staining confirmed endothelial FGL1 accumulation (FGL1+ area: Δ = 2.0-fold, P = 0.0003) concurrent with CD31 downregulation (Δ = −50.03%, P = 0.0025) (Fig. 2E). EndoMT progression analysis in the plaque revealed a significant reduced VE-Cadherin expression, increased α-SMA expression, and a higher ratio of α-SMA+/VE-Cad+ in plaque endothelium (Fig. 2F). Consistent results were observed using an alternative set of EndoMT markers, CD31 and Vimentin, and the findings were further validated by immunoblotting (Fig. 2G and H). In vivo morphological examination showed that increased hepatic FGL1 enhanced plaque burden in aortic arch and brachiocephalic arteries. Oil Red O staining analysis revealed that hepatic FGL1 induced plaque expansion (Total aorta: Δ = +2.21-fold, P < 0.001; aortic root: Δ = +1.56-fold, P = 0.011) (Fig. 2I and J). H&E staining demonstrated that hepatic FGL1 significantly increased the percentage of necrotic core area within atherosclerotic plaques (Δ = +40.78%, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2K). Masson's trichrome staining revealed decreased collagen deposition (Δ = −49.78%, P = 0.0035) in the plaques of mice after FGL1 overexpression (Fig. 2L). Moreover, FGL1 overexpression induced elevated serum triglyceride levels (TG: Δ = +21.87%, P = 0.038) and hepatotoxicity (AST: Δ = +43.5%, P = 0.028) without affecting serum content of LDLC, the most prominent driver of atherogenesis, and body mass (Fig. S2C and S2D). However, FGL1 overexpression increased serum content of HDLC levels (HDLC: Δ = +16.29%, P = 0.020), suggesting that FGL1 seems enhance the reverse transport capacity of serum cholesterol to liver. Consistent with this observation, FGL1 overexpression resulted in an increase in liver volume accompanied by significant hepatic steatosis, as evidenced by a significant elevation in the liver index and intrahepatic lipid deposition (Fig. S2E–S2G). These findings suggest elevated cholesterol deposition and hepatic steatosis induced by FGL1, which is consistent with previous studies27. Taken together, these findings establish that FGL1 drives aortic EndoMT and atherogenesis independent of circulating LDLC alterations, while simultaneously increasing serum HDLC content and inducing hepatic steatosis.

Hepatic FGL1 suppression mitigates aortic EndoMT and atherosclerosis in mice

To further validate origin of elevated circulating FGL1 levels during atherosclerosis progression, an AAV8-TBG-EGFP/shFgl1 vector was constructed for suppressing hepatic FGL1 expression in atherosclerotic APOE−/− mice (male, 8 weeks old), with an empty vector-injected mice serving as negative controls. Exogenous injection of recombinant FGL1 protein (15 μg/kg, once a week, 12 weeks) was performed to evaluate its recovery effect after hepatic FGL1 knockdown. All the mice were subjected to high-fat diet for 12 weeks to induce atherosclerosis (Fig. 3A). Quantitative RT-PCR and immunofluorescence analysis confirmed significant reductions in both FGL1 mRNA (Δ = −58.82%, P < 0.001) and protein expression (Δ = −45.45%, P < 0.0001) following AAV8-TBG-shFGL1 vector treatment for 4 weeks (Fig. 3B and C; Supporting Information Fig. S3A). The liver-specific FGL1 knockdown significantly decreased circulating FGL1 levels (Δ = −40.06%, P = 0.0085) and vascular endothelial FGL1 deposition (Δ = −56.43%, P < 0.001) in atherosclerotic mice; however, the exogenous administration of recombinant FGL1 protein restored these alterations (Fig. 3B–D). Hepatic FGL1 knockdown significantly suppressed EndoMT, as evidenced by increased CD31, decreased Vimentin, and a lower Vimentin+/CD31+ ratio in aortic endothelium (Fig. 3E). Immunofluorescence analysis of another set of EndoMT markers, VE-Cadherin and α-SMA, showed consistent results (Fig. 3F). However, exogenous administration of recombinant FGL1 protein abrogated these effects (Fig. 3E and F). Oil red O analysis demonstrated 28.82% (P = 0.0385) and 45.00% (P < 0.001) reductions of lipid accumulation in total aorta and aortic root, respectively. In vivo morphological examination revealed substantial decreases in plaque burden across aortic arch, brachiocephalic, and carotid arteries after hepatic FGL1 knockdown (Fig. 3G and H). Histopathological evaluation showed 45.94% decrease in necrotic core area (P = 0.0004) (Fig. 3I) accompanied by 47.25% increased collagen content (P = 0.0054) (Fig. 3J), indicating enhanced plaque stability. Doppler ultrasound and M-mode echocardiography revealed that hepatic FGL1 knockdown improved aortic hemodynamic parameters, stiffness, and stenosis in atherosclerotic mice (Fig. S3B and S3C). Just as expected, exogenous administration of recombinant FGL1 protein abrogated the beneficial effects induced by hepatic FGL1 knockdown. Notably, hepatic FGL1 suppression exerted hepatoprotective effects against from high-fat diet through reduced ALT (Δ = −13.38 mmol/L, P = 0.0399) and AST levels (Δ = −58.13 mmol/L, P = 0.0241), without altering body weight, lipid profile, or glucose tolerance (Fig. S3D–S3F). Ultrasound quantification confirmed attenuated hepatic steatosis through reduced liver volume, improved hepatic morphology, as well as decreased in the liver index (Fig. S3G and S3H), suggesting hepatic FGL1 knockdown alleviates hepatic injury and hepatic steatosis independently of systemic lipid metabolism. Similarly, the exogenous administration of recombinant FGL1 protein abrogated the protective effect against hepatic steatosis induced by liver FGL1 knockdown. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that hepatic FGL1 knockdown reduces circulating FGL1 levels and endothelial FGL1 accumulation, suppresses aortic EndoMT and decelerates the progression of atherosclerosis.

FGL1 compromises endothelial barrier integrity by inducing EndoMT

To confirm the potential impact of FGL1 on endothelial injury, we systematically examined its effects on EndoMT in cultured endothelial cells. Primary HUVECs were treated with FGL1 at different concentrations (50, 100 and 200 ng/mL, corresponding to plasma levels in atherosclerotic patients) for 24 h. Parallel experiments with TGFβ1 (10 ng/mL) served as positive controls. Untreated endothelial cells maintained characteristic cobblestone morphology with intact intercellular junctions, whereas TGFβ1-treated cells exhibited spindle-shaped transformation with a significant reduction in intercellular junctions. Similarly, FGL1 induces morphological and connectivity alterations dose-dependently in endothelial cells, promoting a transition towards the mesenchymal phenotype (Fig. 4A). Similar results were also obtained in HAECs and MAECs, confirming FGL1's conserved role in vascular endothelial cells (Supporting Information Fig. S4A). EndoMT drives cytoskeletal reorganization through F-actin redistribution, characterized by stress fiber formation and membrane protrusion development (pseudopodia/lamellipodia)28. To quantify these morphological changes, we performed TRITC-phalloidin staining followed by quantitative morphometric analysis in HUVECs. Our results revealed that untreated cells exhibited characteristic peripheral F-actin organization with minimal cytoplasmic stress fibers or pseudopodia surrounding the cell membrane. FGL1 treatment induced significant cytoskeletal remodeling, characterized by a dose-dependent increase in cytoplasmic stress fiber compared to controls (Fig. 4B). Consistent results were also obtained in HAECs and MAECs (Fig. S4B). EndoMT induces cell cycle dysregulation that suppresses endothelial proliferative capacity. Quantitative analysis using CCK-8 assays revealed that FGL1 dose-dependently inhibited HUVEC proliferation (100 ng/mL: Δ = −16.44%, P < 0.001; Fig. 4C). EndoMT promotes the activation of the collagen synthesis signaling pathway, thereby enhancing extracellular matrix synthesis and secretion. In FGL1-treated HUVECs, we observed a significant elevated collagen I secretion (100 ng/mL: Δ = 237.8 μg/mL vs control, P < 0.001) compared to untreated cells (Fig. 4D). EndoMT compromises endothelial barrier function and enhances pathological vascular permeability. To quantify FGL1's effects on endothelial intercellular permeability, we performed Transwell Leakage Analysis using HUVEC monolayers. FGL1 exposure increased the permeability of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (P < 0.001), with a more pronounced effect observed at higher concentrations and longer treatment durations (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis revealed that FGL1 dose-dependently downregulated endothelial markers (FGL1 100 ng/mL, VE-Cadherin: Δ = −34.40%, P = 0.0073; CD31: Δ = −48.89%, P = 0.0041) and upregulated mesenchymal cell markers (FGL1 100 ng/mL, Vimentin: Δ = 1.900-fold, P = 0.0022; N-Cadherin: Δ = 1.682-fold, P = 0.0087) in HUVECs (Fig. 4F). Snail, Slug, and Twist function as crucial transcription factors that orchestrate EndoMT through epigenetic activation of mesenchymal genes and simultaneous repression of endothelial markers. FGL1 showed a dose-dependent upregulation in the expression of Slug, Twist, and Snail (Δ = 1.54-, 1.91- and 1.99- fold, respectively following 100 ng/mL FGL1 treatment; P < 0.05) (Fig. 4G and H). In summary, these findings demonstrate that FGL1 initiates EndoMT by activating the Snail/Slug/Twist transcriptional axis and orchestrating epigenetic reprogramming of EndoMT markers.

Next, we aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying FGL1-induced EndoMT. The C-terminal FReDs of FGL1 exhibits structural similarities and similar active site with the β/γ chains of fibrinogen (FGB/FGG) (Fig. 4I), suggesting FGL1 may mediate its biological functions through interaction with fibrinogen-related receptors expressed on endothelial cell membrane8,9. STRING database analysis indicates that FGL1 is predicted to interact with various fibrinogen and integrins (Fig. 4J). Integrin β1 plays key regulatory roles in cellular differentiation by modulating cytoskeletal dynamics and ECM interactions, thereby driving EndoMT through Snail/Slug transcriptional activation in vascular endothelial cells22,23,25. The above structural analysis and literature review suggest that integrin β1 may serve as a key receptor mediating FGL1-induced EndoMT. Immunofluorescence analysis of HUVECs showed membrane localization of His-tagged FGL1 following treatment, suggesting membrane receptor-mediated binding (Fig. 4K). Western blot analysis validated a significant upregulation of phospho-integrin β1 (Tyr788/789), expression was obtained in the aortic tissues of atherosclerotic mice compared to that in normal mice (Fig. 4L). Immunofluorescence staining analysis of the aortic root sections revealed progressive enhancement of phospho-integrin β1 (Tyr788/789) expression during atherosclerotic plaque development; moreover, the significant increased co-localization of phospho-integrin β1 with FGL1 within the vascular intima suggests the strong interaction between FGL1 and integrin β1 (Fig. 4M). The above findings indicate that integrin β1 may represent a direct target through which FGL1 induces endothelial phenotypic dysregulation.

FGL1 initiates EndoMT by promoting integrin β1 activation through the induction of integrin β1/ILK1 complex formation

Building upon these findings, we subsequently investigated the mechanistic relationship between FGL1-induced EndoMT and integrin β1 activation. Western blot analysis demonstrated FGL1-induced integrin β1 activation in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). Time-course experiments revealed rapid phosphorylation of integrin β1 in HUVECs, with detectable activation within 30 min post-FGL1 stimulation, peaking at 1 h. Although integrin β1 phosphorylation intensity declined after 6-h exposure, levels remained significantly elevated compared to baseline (Fig. 5B). Prolonged FGL1 stimulation (48/72 h) induced significant upregulation of total integrin β1 protein expression levels (Supporting Information Fig. S5). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the upregulation of phospho-integrin β1 (Tyr788/789) of FGL1-treated HUVECs, showing increased co-localization with administered FGL1 on the membrane of endothelial cells (Fig. 5C and D). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis in FGL1-stimulated HUVECs (100 ng/mL, 1 h) confirmed the interaction between FGL1 and integrin β1 (Fig. 5E). In vivo validation demonstrated FGL1-mediated enhancement of phosphorylated integrin β1 in atherosclerotic aortic intima (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these findings establish FGL1 as a direct activator of endothelial integrin β1.

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying FGL1-induced activation of integrin β1, we focused on integrin-linked kinase 1 (ILK1), a receptor–proximal protein kinase that interacts with the cytoplasmic domain of integrin β1 to modulate its activity. Ligand binding to the extracellular domain of integrin β1 induces conformational changes, exposing its cytoplasmic tail for ILK1 recruitment and subsequent serine/threonine phosphorylation (Fig. 5G)29. We systematically investigated whether FGL1-mediated phosphorylation of integrin β1 (Thr788/789) is regulated by ILK1. Western blot analysis revealed no significant alterations in ILK1 expression following FGL1 treatment in endothelial cells (Fig. 5H). Co-immunoprecipitation analysis confirmed the formation of integrin β1–ILK1 complex and the phosphorylation of integrin β1 in FGL1-treated cells (Fig. 5I). Duolink in situ Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA) demonstrated minimal interaction between phospho-integrin β1 (Thr788/789) and ILK1 under basal conditions but showed a marked enhancement upon FGL1 stimulation (1.94-fold increase, P < 0.001; Fig. 5J). To validate the critical role of ILK1 in FGL1-induced integrin β1 activation, we employed siRNA-mediated knockdown of ILK1 in endothelial cells. The most efficient siRNA achieved >50% reduction in ILK1 expression, as confirmed by Western blot and qPCR analyses (Supporting Information Fig. S6). Following ILK1 knockdown, FGL1-induced phosphorylation of integrin β1 (Thr788/789) was significantly attenuated (P < 0.001; Fig. 5K). Moreover, ILK1 depletion disrupted the interaction between FGL1 and phospho-integrin β1 (Thr788/789), indicating abrogation of FGL1-mediated integrin β1 activation (Fig. 5L). Collectively, these findings establish that FGL1 activates integrin β1 by inducing conformational changes in its extracellular domain and enhancing its interaction with ILK1, thereby promoting threonine 788/789 phosphorylation in the intracellular domain of integrin β1.

FGL1 orchestrates EndoMT by sequentially activating the FAK/ERK and Smad signaling cascades downstream of integrin β1 activation

Emerging evidence establishes integrin β1 activation as a critical driver of cell trans-differentiation through activating FAK and various downstream signaling molecules, such as ERK, Smad, AKT, mTOR, and STAT3, pathways that are involved in regulating the EndoMT process under different stimulation conditions21,22,30. To elucidate the intracellular signal transduction pathway underlying FGL1-induced EndoMT, we systematically mapped the activation dynamics of integrin β1/FAK and downstream molecules. Western blot quantification demonstrated FGL1-triggered activation of FAK (Tyr397; 1.58-fold, P < 0.01) at 0.5 h of FGL1 treatment which showed temporal consistency with integrin β1 activation kinetics (Fig. 6A). Moreover, FGL1 induced phosphorylation of ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204; 1.45-fold, P < 0.05) and Smad2/3 (Ser465/467; 1.67-fold, P < 0.05), and Smad4 nuclear translocation at 1 h, which was observed to occur later than the activation of integrin β1 (Fig. 6A; Supporting Information Fig. S7). In contrast, other downstream signaling molecules, such as AKT, STAT3, and mTOR, did not exhibit significant activation under the same conditions (Supporting Information Fig. S8). To elucidate the hierarchical signaling relationships underlying FGL1-induced EndoMT, we employed pharmacological perturbation using pathway-specific inhibitors. FAK inhibition with GSK2256098 (0.5 μmol/L; 1 h pretreatment) demonstrated 81.08% reduction in FAK (Tyr397) phosphorylation (P < 0.05), 81.37% decrease in ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) activation (P < 0.05), and almost complete rescue of endothelial marker expression (P < 0.05) (Fig. 6B). Notably, ERK1/2 inhibition using PD98059 (30 μmol/L; 1 h pretreatment) abolished ERK1/2 phosphorylation (81.28% reduction, P < 0.001) and significantly restored endothelial phenotype (P < 0.05), while showed no interference with upstream FAK activation (Fig. 6B). These data establish the hierarchical FAK–ERK signaling axis in FGL1-mediated EndoMT. Furthermore, Smad signaling inhibition with 1 μmol/L SB431542 completely inhibited Smad2/3/4 signaling and rescued endothelial integrity (P < 0.05; Fig. 6C; Fig. S7). Furthermore, FAK and Smad inhibition completely suppressed the FGL1-induced activation of transcription factors SNAIL and TWIST (Fig. 6D). To investigate the activation of the FAK/ERK and Smad signal molecules by FGL1 in vivo, we administered recombinant FGL1 protein to mice via intravenous injection and assessed both plasma FGL1 levels and the activation of FAK/ERK and Smad in the vascular endothelium (Fig. 6E). ELISA analysis demonstrated an obvious elevation of plasma FGL1 concentration (Fig. 6F). Immunofluorescence analysis of atherosclerotic aortic roots confirmed FGL1-mediated endothelial co-activation of phospho-FAK (Tyr397) (P = 0.0019), phospho-ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) (P < 0.0001) and phospho-Smad2/3 (Ser465/467) (P < 0.0001) in FGL1-challenged atherosclerotic aortas (Fig. 6G–I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that FGL1 orchestrates EndoMT through parallel FAK/ERK and Smad signaling cascades downstream of integrin β1 activation.

FGL1 induces EndoMT by interacting with the extracellular domain of integrin β1 and activating it

The extracellular domain of integrin β1 orchestrates intracellular signaling cascades through ligand-dependent conformational changes30,31. To mechanistically dissect the role of the extracellular domain of integrin β1 in FGL1-mediated activation of intracellular FAK/ERK and Smad signaling, as well as EndoMT induction, we employed a functional integrin β1 blocking antibody (P5D2) targeting its extracellular region. Pretreatment of HUVECs with 10 μg/mL P5D2 antibody (β1 Ab) for 1 h abolished FGL1-induced phosphorylation of integrin β1, FAK, ERK1/2 and Smad2/3 (Fig. 7A–D). Control IgG had no inhibitory effects on FGL1. Subsequent analysis revealed β1 Ab-mediated preservation of endothelial homeostasis, evidenced by CD31 upregulation and Vimentin downregulation via Western blot analysis (Fig. 7E and F). Treatment with the RGD peptide, an inhibitor of integrin β1 activity, yielded similar results (Fig. 7G–I). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that both β1-Ab and RGD peptide could significantly reverse the inhibitory effect of FGL1 on the tight junctions of endothelial cells, increase the protein abundance of VE-cadherin, and reduce the protein abundance of Vimentin (Fig. 7J and K). Cytoskeletal reorganization analysis demonstrated significant prevention of intracellular F-actin aggregation and collagen overproduction (Fig. 7L and M). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that FGL1 induces EndoMT through interaction with the extracellular domain of integrin β1 and activating it. Blocking integrin β1 with a β1 antibody or inhibiting its activity with RGD peptide abolishes FGL1-induced pro-EndoMT signaling, restores endothelial tight junctions, and prevents cytoskeletal reorganization and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Trp225 within the C-terminal FReDs of FGL1 interacts with the vWFA domain in the extracellular region of integrin β1 to induce EndoMT

Next, we further investigated the interaction site between FGL1 and integrin β1. The FGL1 protein has only one functional domain, namely C-terminal FReDs (FreDs: 74–306 Aa), and the functional sites associated with fibrinogen activity are predominantly located within Glu200–Asn265. Among this domain, Val223, Gln224, Trp225 and Trp226 (VQWW) form a highly conserved “WW motif” with strong hydrophobic characteristics, forming a stable hydrophobic core. This suggests it may serve as the primary functional interface to interact with integrin β1. Through systematic investigation of the potential binding sites on the extracellular region of integrin β1, the vWFA domain (Asp140–Leu378) has drawn our attention (Fig. 8A). The vWFA domain is a conserved 230-residue region that is critical for cell adhesion and interactions with the extracellular matrix and protein32. Molecular docking analysis using MOE Protein Patch Analyzer identified hydrophobic interaction surfaces between FGL1 and the vWFA domain (Supporting Information Fig. S9A and S9B). From 48 high-affinity interaction patterns (binding energy < −55 kcal/mol) (Supporting Information Table S2), protein–ligand interface fingerprint analysis revealed Trp225 as the most frequent (33.3% occurrence) and strongest-affinity (−76.91 kcal/mol) binding residue, forming critical contacts with integrin β1's Lys368 residue (Fig. 8B; Fig. S9C and S9D). The Trp225 residue of FGL1 and the Lys368 residue of integrin β1 form surface contacts with an affinity of −76.91 kcal/mol, representing the highest binding affinity among all calculated interaction modes (Fig. 8B). Evolutionary conservation analysis demonstrated complete Trp225 preservation across human, rat, and mouse homologs (Supporting Information Fig. S10A–S10C). To determine the functional significance of Trp225 in FGL1–integrin β1 interaction, we engineered a Trp225Ala substitution mutant (FGL1W225A) through site-directed mutagenesis (Fig. S10D–S10F). Coimmunoprecipitation assays showed that the interaction between FGL1 and integrin β1, as well as FGL1's activation of integrin β1, were significantly inhibited in FGL1W225A in comparison with FGL1WT (Fig. 8C and D). Direct interaction using pull-down assay confirmed that FGL1WT interacts directly with integrin β1, and this interaction was almost completely abolished following the Trp225 mutation of FGL1 and the Lys368 mutation of Integrin β1 (Fig. 8E). Importantly, the SPR results demonstrate that FGL1 exhibits stable binding to integrin β1 across the tested concentration range (31.25–500 nmol/L), with an equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 4.15 × 10−12 mol/L. The FGL1W225A mutant exhibits a severely impaired binding affinity for integrin β1 compared to the wild-type protein (Fig. 8F). Immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis revealed that FGL1W225A failed to induce activation of integrin β1 in HUVECs (Fig. 8G and H). Moreover, FGL1W225A completely fails to phosphorylate FAK, ERK and Smad, along with EndoMT-associated transcription factors and marker proteins (Fig. 8H–K). Cytoskeletal staining showed 93.23% reduction of F-actin polymerization and stress fiber formation in FGL1W225A-treated endothelial cells (Fig. 8L). Collectively, the Trp225 residue serves as a major contributor in the interaction between FGL1 and the vWFA domain of integrin β1, thereby promoting FGL1-induced activation of integrin β1 and the subsequent progression of EndoMT.

Targeting integrin β1 preserves endothelial integrity and attenuates the atherosclerotic plaque progression

We systematically investigated the effects of integrin β1 blockade or inhibition on FGL1-induced vascular EndoMT and the atherosclerotic progression in vivo. Atherosclerotic models were established through a 12-week high-fat diet feeding of APOE−/− mice (male, 8 weeks old). Intravenous administration of integrin β1-blocking antibody (β1 Ab, 15 μg/kg) once a week for 12 weeks, with IgG serving as the negative control (Fig. 9A). ELISA quantification showed comparable plasma FGL1 levels between β1 Ab and IgG-treated mice (Fig. 9B). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed β1 Ab diminished FGL1's endothelial accumulation (P < 0.001) and integrin β1 activation (P < 0.001) in aortic roots (Fig. 9C). Moreover, treatment with β1 antibody effectively preserved endothelial CD31, decreased Vimentin and Vimentin+/CD31+ ratio in aortic endothelium (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9D). Immunofluorescence analysis of another set of EndoMT markers, VE-Cadherin and α-SMA, showed consistent results (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9E). Aortic doppler ultrasound imaging examination showed that treatment with β1 Ab resulted in an increase in the forward/reverse flow ratio of the aortic arch (1.50-fold, P = 0.002), as well as an increase in both systolic (Δ = 18.85 cm/s, P < 0.001) and mean flow velocity (Δ = 5.71 cm/s, P = 0.005) of aortic arch (Supporting Information Fig. S11A). M-mode echocardiography of the aortic arch and outflow tract indicated that β1 Ab therapy significantly enhanced the aortic distensibility index and diameter variation, while simultaneously reducing the aortic elastic modulus and stiffness index, collectively indicating improved aortic mechanical function (Fig. S11B). Atherosclerotic plaque assessment revealed that treatment with β1 Ab significantly decreased aortic plaque formation, particularly within the inner arch of the aortas and at branches of the brachiocephalic artery. Aortic en face Oil Red O staining showed that β1 Ab decreased lipid deposition throughout the whole aorta (Δ = −54.46%, P = 0.0041) (Fig. 9F). Oil red O staining of aortic roots revealed that the administration of β1 Ab resulted in decreased lipid deposition and plaque formation within the aortic root (47.14%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 9G). Additionally, β1 Ab exhibited no significant impact on serum lipid profiles and hepatic transaminases (Fig. S11C). These data demonstrate integrin β1 blockade effectively attenuates FGL1-driven EndoMT and atherosclerotic progression.

To investigate the effect of suppressing integrin β1 activity in aortic pathophysiology and atherosclerosis, Apoe−/− mice (male, 8 weeks old) received twice-weekly intravenous RGD peptide (5 mg/kg) to inhibit the activation of integrin β1 for 12 weeks (Fig. 9H). ELISA quantification showed that RGD peptide did not affect plasma FGL1 levels (Fig. 9I). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that RGD peptide treatment suppressed integrin β1 activation (57.61% decrease, P < 0.001) in atherosclerotic endothelium without affecting endothelial FGL1 accumulation (Fig. 9J). RGD peptide effectively preserved endothelial CD31, decreased Vimentin and Vimentin+/CD31+ ratio in aortic endothelium (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9K). Immunofluorescence analysis of VE-Cadherin and α-SMA showed consistent results (P < 0.001) (Fig. 9L). Hemodynamic evaluation revealed RGD peptide significantly improved aortic compliance parameters. Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated a 1.60-fold increase in Forward/Reverse flow ratio (P = 0.005) with concurrent elevation of systolic flow velocity (Δ = 9.57 cm/s, P = 0.021) in the aortic arch, values comparable to those achieved with FGL1 antibody treatment (Supporting Information Fig. S12A). M-mode echocardiography showed enhanced vascular compliance through the reduced elastic modulus index and stiffness index, with the increased distensibility index and diameter variation (Fig. S12B). Therapeutic evaluation demonstrated that the RGD peptide significantly alleviated atherosclerotic burden. Morphometric analysis showed decreased plaque deposition in the aortic inner arch and brachiocephalic artery. En face Oil Red O quantification indicated 53.72% less lipid accumulation across the whole aorta (P = 0.0183) and 49.79% reduction in the aortic roots (P = 0.0217) (Fig. 9M and N). It is noteworthy that no significant effects of the RGD peptide on blood lipid profiles or hepatic enzyme levels were observed (Fig. S12C). These findings establish integrin β1 as an effective therapeutic target for combating FGL1, preserving endothelial integrity and slowing atherosclerosis progression.

Pharmacological targeting of circulating FGL1 is a promising therapeutic approach for alleviating endothelial injury and atherosclerosis

Given the demonstrated role of circulating FGL1 in promoting endothelial integrin β1 activation, vascular EndoMT, and atherosclerosis progression, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of plasma FGL1 neutralization in atherosclerotic mice. Apoe−/− mice (male, 8 weeks old) received high-fat diet and intravenous administrations of FGL1-neutralizing antibody once a week (Clone: 177R4; Cat: BE0332; 15 μg/kg; epitope: FGL1[1–312aa], GenBank BC007047, Fig. S10G) or anti-IgG for 12 weeks (Fig. 10A). Quantification of circulating FGL1 revealed a 50.9% reduction in plasma concentration in anti-FGL1-treated mice compared to IgG controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 10B), indicative of antibody-mediated neutralization rather than hepatic modulation, as hepatic FGL1 protein/mRNA levels remained unchanged (Supporting Information Fig. S13A and S13B). It is noteworthy that circulating FGL1 levels remained unchanged in integrin β1 antibody or RGD-treated mice compared to controls, whereas they were significantly reduced in FGL1 antibody-treated groups (Fig. 9B–I; Fig. 10B). Immunofluorescence analysis indicated that FGL1-neutralizing antibody treatment substantially reduced endothelial FGL1 accumulation (P = 0.0044), and integrin β1 activation (P < 0.001) in aortic endothelium (Fig. 10C). EndoMT marker analysis demonstrated increased VE-Cadherin and CD31, decreased α-SMA and Vimentin, and reduced α-SMA/VE-Cadherin and Vimentin/CD31 ratios (P < 0.001) in aortic endothelium following FGL1 antibody intervention (Fig. 10D and E). Furthermore, FGL1 antibody significantly reduced the phosphorylation of FAK (Tyr397), ERK1/2 (Thr202/Tyr204) and Smad2/3 (Ser465/467) in aortic endothelium (Supporting Information Fig. S14A–S14C), alongside integrin β1 suppression, VE-cadherin elevation and Vimentin reduction in aortic tissues confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. S14D). Aortic hemodynamic and mechanical assessments showed that FGL1 antibody significantly improved aortic compliance, with a 1.44-fold elevation in Forward/Reverse flow ratio (P = 0.028) and a 12.52 cm/s increase in systolic flow velocity (P = 0.005) (Fig. 10F). M-mode echocardiography further showed enhanced mechanical parameters through marked reductions in elastic modulus index (aortic outflow tract: 54.60%, P = 0.003; aortic arch: 73.24%, P = 0.008) and stiffness index (aortic outflow tract: 58.62%, P = 0.005; aortic arch: 75.95%, P = 0.002), accompanied by elevated distensibility index (aortic outflow tract: 2.38-fold, P = 0.013; aortic arch: 3.54-fold, P = 0.002) and increased diameter variation (aortic outflow tract: Δ = 0.098 mm, P < 0.001; aortic arch: Δ = 0.115 mm, P < 0.001) (Fig. 10G). These findings suggest that FGL1 antibody antagonizing circulating FGL1 via antigen–antibody complex formation, effectively counteracting its EndoMT-promotive activity. Moreover, therapeutic evaluation further revealed significant attenuation of atherosclerotic burden, with morphometric analysis showing reduced plaque deposition in aortic inner arch and brachiocephalic artery induced by FGL1 antibody. Oil Red O staining demonstrated 45.95% less lipid accumulation in the whole aorta (P = 0.075) and 67.75% reduction in aortic roots in anti-FGL1 treated mice (P = 0.0105) (Fig. 10H and I). Histological assessment revealed 50.39% smaller plaque area (P < 0.001) and 2.41-fold enhanced collagen deposition (P < 0.001) in aortic roots following treatment with anti-FGL1 (Fig. 10J and K). Notably, FGL1 antibody (at the concentration used in this study) did not significantly affect body weight or blood lipid profiles, nor did it improve hepatic steatosis induced by high-fat diet (Supporting Information Fig. S15). Collectively, these results establish circulating FGL1 neutralization as an effective intervention for ameliorating endothelial dysfunction and arresting atherosclerotic lesion progression.

Discussion

FGL1, a secreted hepatokine, regulates diverse biological processes and has well-established roles in liver pathology, cancer progression, and metabolic diseases12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18. This study provides the first evidence highlighting FGL1's critical role in vascular pathophysiology and atherosclerosis pathogenesis. We identified a positive correlation between circulating FGL1 levels and atherosclerotic progression in murine models and ASCVD patients. Functional studies disclosed FGL1's pathogenic role in impairing endothelial integrity and increasing plaque burden, confirming that hepatic FGL1 is a major contributor to the advancement of atherosclerosis. Mechanistically, secreted FGL1 binds to the vWFA domain in extracellular region of integrin β1 on endothelial cells, with Trp225 in the FReDs being a key contributor. This interaction promotes integrin β1 conformational change and formation of integrin β1/ILK1 complex, sequentially activating FAK/ERK and Smad pathways and transcriptional reprogramming to drive EndoMT. Therapeutic studies demonstrated that reducing circulating FGL1 with specific antibodies or suppressing integrin β1 activation significantly retards vascular EndoMT and atherosclerosis progression. These findings establish FGL1 as both a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for early-stage atherosclerosis. Reducing plasma FGL1 levels or suppressing FGL1-induced endothelial integrin β1 activation represents promising novel therapeutic strategies.

Endothelial cells act as “gatekeepers” of vascular homeostasis through their selective barrier function. Under stimulation by excessive oxidized lipoproteins, triglycerides, pro-inflammatory and pro-aging mediators, endothelial cells undergo damage, thereby promoting the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis33, 34. Compelling lineage tracing evidence confirms EndoMT in early atherosclerosis and its role in facilitating plaque progression. Although TGFβ/Smad, Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, and ERK/MAPK cascades are well-established drivers of EndoMT, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, reflecting the complexity of this process35, 36, 37. Intriguingly, while secreted FGL1 has established roles in promoting EMT in tumor invasion, its potential involvement in vascular EndoMT remains unexplored13,38. Our study unveils FGL1 as a novel and independent inducer of EndoMT. FGL1 elicits concentration-dependent morphological, cytoskeletal, functional, and molecular shifts toward mesenchymal phenotypes in endothelial cells, which coincides with activation of key pro-EndoMT transcription factors. Interestingly, plasma FGL1 levels in healthy volunteers (∼50 ng/mL) approximate this in vitro pro-EndoMT concentration. This apparent discrepancy may be due to that the physiological complexity of in vivo systems potentially requiring higher FGL1 thresholds for EndoMT induction. Furthermore, our in vivo findings substantiate the pathological role of hepatic FGL1 in promoting vascular EndoMT, establishing hepatokine FGL1 as a novel mediator of vascular EndoMT. Although several hepatokines (e.g., FGF21, ANGPTL3, Adropin, Fetuin-A/B, LECT2, miR-122) has been reported to influence cardiovascular diseases progression through multiple mechanisms, including lipid and glucose metabolism, calcification balance, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Notably, therapies targeting ANGPTL3 with antisense oligonucleotides and miR-122-based gene delivery vectors show promising clinical potential, highlighting the liver–cardiovascular axis in disease prevention and management39, 40, 41. However, the specific hepatokines responsible for inducing endothelial barrier dysfunction and promoting atherosclerosis remain to be elucidated. This study identifies FGL1 as a key hepatokine that induces endothelial injury and atherosclerosis. Like the aforementioned hepatokines, FGL1 is upregulated under metabolic stress. However, it induces endothelial injury through non-metabolic mechanisms, as hepatic FGL1 inhibition significantly ameliorates endothelial dysfunction and plaque progression without markedly altering atherogenic cholesterol levels. Taken together, this study reveals the specific interaction between FGL1 and its endothelial receptor integrin β1, uncovering a novel mechanism by which the hepatokine causes endothelial injury and broadening our understanding of liver–cardiovascular disease mechanisms.

Our study delineates a novel FGL1–integrin β1 signaling axis driving EndoMT pathogenesis. Systematic validation identified endothelial integrin β1 as the principal mediator of FGL1-induced EndoMT. Integrins are transmembrane receptors that mediates bidirectional communication between extracellular matrix and the cytoskeleton19,20. Ligand binding induces receptor clustering and focal adhesion complexes assembly, initiating outside-in signaling. Conversely, intracellular activators enhance ligand binding by priming integrin β1 through inside-out signaling30,31. The Thr788/Thr789 residues are critical for integrin β1 activation and function, regulating conformational changes, focal adhesion assembly, and actin cytoskeleton reorganization. Upon extracellular stimulation, Thr788/Thr789 residues are phosphorylated and recruit FAK, promoting its autophosphorylation at Tyr398. In contrast, phosphorylation of integrin β1 at Tyr783, Tyr795, and Ser785 promotes the dissociation of β1 integrin from focal adhesions, impairs FAK autophosphorylation at Tyr397, and subsequently inhibits actin cytoskeleton assembly42. Our results indicate that FGL1 is an extracellular factor that activates integrin β1 and phosphorylates it at its Thr788/Thr789 sites. Specifically, our analysis has primarily focused on the well-characterized Thr788/Thr789 sites without evaluating all potential phosphorylation sites since the phosphorylation of Thr788/Thr789 is crucial for activating integrin β1 and its downstream FAK signaling pathway. The phosphorylation of integrin β1 and FAK serves as the key signaling hub, regulating a series of downstream signaling networks, such as ERK, AKT, mTOR, STAT3, which are involved in regulating the EndoMT process under different stimulation conditions30,43. Integrin β1 activation also enhances Smad2/3 signaling by inducing TGF-β receptor I/II complex and repressing VE-cadherin and upregulating Vimentin22,44. Moreover, direct FGL1–integrin β1 interaction was confirmed in endothelial cells and atherosclerotic aortic intima, and FGL1 activate integrin β1 and the downstream FAK/ERK and Smad cascades to induce EndoMT. Under the current experimental conditions, FGL1 did not significantly activate the AKT, mTOR, or STAT3, suggesting that ERK and Smad are more sensitive mediators in FGL1-induced endothelial cell injury. Notably, the FAK/ERK cascade exhibits immediate responsiveness to integrin β1 signaling, whereas SMAD2/3 displays delayed activation kinetics but demonstrates persistent signaling capacity, which further demonstrates that FGL1 orchestrates the temporal activation of pro-EndoMT signaling sequentially. Future research should explore FGL1's role in promoting EndoMT under various pathological conditions to fully uncover its multidimensional regulatory mechanisms.

Our study identifies Trp225 in the FReDs of FGL1 and Lys368 in the vWFA domain of integrin β1 as the major contributors to the FGL1–integrin β1 interaction. The structural and functional analysis of FGL1 reveals that the Val223–Trp226 (VQWW) motif forms a highly conserved hydrophobic core, potentially mediating membrane binding or protein interactions. Molecular docking simulation revealed that Trp225 exhibited the highest interaction frequency and can form multiple stable interactions with vWFA domain, indicating it likely plays a key role in protein recognition. As we proposed, the FGL1-W225A mutation significantly reduced FGL1–integrin β1 binding, impaired integrin β1 and downstream signaling activation. Importantly, the ITGB1-K368A mutation further disrupted the formation of the FGL1–integrin β1 complex. Integrin β1 Lys368 is a focal point due to its essential location within the vWFA domain. Although multiple ligands interact with this domain, the specific residues and molecular mechanisms involved in their binding exhibit considerable variation45. Structural docking and modeling analysis indicate that FGL1 does not utilize the classical RGD motif for binding. Instead, Trp225 and Trp226 of FGL1 form a robust hydrophobic core that, together with the surrounding polar environment, engages the side chain of Lys368 in a distinct intermolecular interaction network. The resulting FGL1-Trp225:vWFA-Lys368 interaction constitutes a distinctive, non-canonical binding mechanism. This mechanism diverges from classical ligand recognition pathways, offering unique insights with therapeutic potential. Besides, although our study identified the critical residues involved in this interaction, the complete protein–protein interaction interface may involve other residues. Future studies, including high-resolution structural analysis, should help clarify the more accurate interaction interface. As for the activation mechanism, FGL1-mediated integrin β1 activation requires ILK1, a pivotal adaptor connecting integrins to cytoskeletal elements and signaling effectors. ILK1 enhances the affinity of integrin β1 for extracellular ligands by directly binding to its intracellular domain and inducing phosphorylation of integrin β1 at Thr788/Thr789 residues. This subsequently enables ILK1–integrin β1 complex to activate FAK, thereby driving cytoskeletal remodeling29. In our study, ILK1 knockdown abolished FGL1-induced integrin β1 activation, highlighting its critical scaffolding role in this process. Therefore, we propose a sequential activation model that FGL1 binding to integrin β1's extracellular domain induces conformational changes that enable ILK1 access to the cytoplasmic tail, facilitating phosphorylation-dependent signal propagation.

Furthermore, we confirmed that targeting the extracellular domain of integrin β1 reverses FGL1-mediated activation pro-EndoMT signaling pathways. Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the integrin β1 antibody attenuated FGL1 accumulation in endothelial cells, alleviated endothelial barrier disruption, and inhibited atherosclerosis progression during the disease process, without altering circulating FGL1 levels. Notably, this is the first report suggesting that the integrin β1 antibody may serve as a potential therapeutic strategy to delay atherosclerosis progression. Collectively, our findings confirmed the mechanism that FGL1 engages integrin β1's extracellular domain and induces ILK1-dependent activation, thereby driving EndoMT signaling and atherogenesis. This mechanistic understanding provides novel therapeutic targets for intercepting vascular endothelial phenotypic dysregulation in atherosclerosis. Although FGL1 exhibits similarities to fibronectin in the capacity to activate integrin β1, the pathological stimuli to which FGL1 responds are distinct from those associated with fibronectin. It is noteworthy that cell-derived fibronectin and plasma-derived fibronectin exert distinct effects on endothelial function, with only the former being implicated in the induction of vascular inflammatory responses46. In addition, our results demonstrate that endothelial integrins can be activated not only by ligands produced locally within vascular tissues but also by endocrine factors in the plasma.

In this study, the impact of the targeted FGL1–integrin β1 axis on the progression of atherosclerosis was evaluated systematically. Treatment with the integrin β1 antibody (P5D2) or RGD peptide significantly suppresses FGL1-mediated activation of integrin β1 and the downstream pro-EndoMT signaling in endothelial cells, and consistent results were also obtained in atherosclerotic mice. The two interventions similarly inhibit FGL1-induced pro-EndMT effects but act through distinct mechanisms: P5D2 blocks FGL1–integrin β1 binding, whereas RGD peptide does not disrupt binding but directly inhibits integrin β1 activation, as evidenced by the fact that FGL1 accumulation differs in endothelial regions. Nevertheless, our study did not employ ITGB1 knockout or knockdown to validate integrin β1's critical role in FGL1's pathological function, because ITGB1 knockdown alone suppresses EndoMT. Thus, the benefits of ITGB1 knockout cannot be definitively attributed to blocking FGL1, as they may result from ITGB1 knockdown itself31. We also confirmed that FGL1-neutralizing antibody effectively improve aortic compliance and hemodynamics, reduce plaque burden, and decelerate atherogenesis through multi-level inhibition of the FGL1–integrin β1 axis. FGL1 antibody reduces circulating FGL1 levels and effectively inhibits its accumulation in endothelial cells and downstream β1 activation, whereas P5D2 antibody or RGD peptide selectively blocks integrin β1 activation without altering systemic FGL1 levels. Importantly, the vascular protection conferred by these interventions is independent of metabolic regulation, though emerging evidence identifies FGL1 as a pivotal modulator of metabolic homeostasis14, 15, 16. Consistent with previous reports, our data confirm that hepatic FGL1 overexpression or exogenous FGL1 administration increases serum triglycerides, transaminase levels, liver volume, and weight; liver-specific FGL1 knockdown suppresses intrahepatic lipid synthesis. Although FGL1 is implicated in hepatic lipid metabolic disorders, its contribution to vascular injury and atherosclerosis appears to be predominantly mediated through non-metabolic pathways. This is supported by the observation that selective modulation of hepatic FGL1 influences atherosclerotic plaque progression with minimal effects on serum pro-atherogenic lipoproteins such as TC or LDL-C. Notably, 12-week FGL1 antibody treatment achieved circulating FGL1 reduction without modulating hepatic volume and mass abnormality or serum cholesterol profiles. This mechanistic decoupling suggests the antibody's vascular benefits arise predominantly from interrupting endocrine FGL1 signaling in distant vasculature rather than local hepatic actions. Moreover, our study does not include validation in female atherosclerotic mice. Evidence indicates that atherogenesis in women is closely associated with hormonal status. Endothelial cell lineage tracing studies indicate that female atherosclerotic mice display more pronounced upregulation of inflammation and apoptosis-related genes, while male mice predominantly exhibit EndoMT and intraplaque neovascularization47. Future studies should further investigate the effects of targeting the FGL1–integrin β1 axis in female atherosclerotic models, thereby offering deeper insights into the pathological role of FGL1 in atherosclerosis progression. Taken together, these findings collectively confirm that the FGL1–integrin β1 axis serves as a pivotal signaling hub regulating EndoMT progression, thereby underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target in endothelial dysfunction and fibrotic cardiovascular diseases.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study proposes the critical role of FGL1 in atherosclerosis and its mechanism of promoting disease progression. Plasma FGL1 levels are significantly elevated in patients with atherosclerosis, suggesting its potential as an early diagnostic biomarker. Mechanistically, the Trp225 residue of FGL1 serves as a key site to interact with the vWFA domain of integrin β1, thereby activating downstream pro-differentiation signaling pathways and inducing EndoMT. Furthermore, targeting FGL1–integrin β1 signaling markedly inhibited vascular EndoMT, reduced plaque burden, and attenuated disease progression of atherosclerotic mice. Collectively, these findings highlight FGL1 as a novel driver of atherosclerosis and provide new insights into its potential as both an early diagnostic marker and therapeutic target for the disease.
