Introduction

We use standard graph notation and terminology. Throughout the paper, let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a simple graph of
order

n(G)
,
size

m(G)
, and
adjacency matrix

A(G)
. We denote the eigenvalues of
A(G)
(also called the
spectrum
of
G
) by
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Suppose eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
appears with multiplicity
m_{i}
. Then often we will write the spectrum of
G
as
\lambda_{1}^{(m_{1})},\ldots,\lambda_{k}^{(m_{k})}
, where
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{k}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
A(G)
. Let
n^{+}(G),n^{0}(G)
, and
n^{-}(G)
denote the number of positive, zero, and negative eigenvalues of
A(G)
, counted with multiplicities, respectively. The
inertia
of
G
, denoted by
\In(G)
, is the ordered triple
(n^{+}(G),n^{0}(G),n^{-}(G))
. The
rank
of graph
G
is given by
\rank(G)=n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G)
. The
signature
of a graph
G
is given by
s(G)=n^{+}(G)-n^{-}(G)
. Two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
are said to be
twins
if they have the same neighbours, i.e.,
N(u)=N(v)
. A graph is called
reduced
if
G
has no twins (or
twin-free
) and has no isolated vertices. For
A\subseteq V(G)
,
G[A]
denotes the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices in
A
.

The following is a well-known result of Delsarte, Goethals, and Seidel
[
10
]
(as discussed in
[
8
, Section 10]
), proved using the theory of equiangular lines.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
10
]
cf.
[
31
,
8
]
)
.

Let
G
be a regular graph of order
n
with least eigenvalue
\lambda
. If
\lambda<-1
and multiplicity of
\lambda
is
n-k
for some
k\geq 1
, then
n\leq\frac{k(k+1)}{2}-1
.

Theorem
1.1
was later generalized by Bell and Rowlinson
[
6
]
using the technique of star complements.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of a graph
G
with multiplicity
n-k
for some positive integer
k
. Then either
\lambda\in\{0,-1\}
or
n\leq\frac{k(k+1)}{2}
. Furthermore, if
G
is regular, then
n\leq\frac{(k-1)(k+2)}{2}
.

An important corollary of Theorem
1.1
is the following well-known
absolute bounds
for (primitive) strongly regular graphs (SRGs). These bounds are effectively used to rule out otherwise feasible SRG parameters.

Theorem 1.3

(Absolute bound for SRGs, cf.
[
31
]
)
.

Let
G=\SRG(n,d,\lambda,\mu)
denote a strongly regular graph with spectrum
d^{(1)},r^{(f)},s^{(g)}
, where
d>r>0
and
s<-1
. Then

(i)

2n\leq f(f+3)
;

(ii)

2n\leq g(g+3).

Neumaier
[
30
]
(cf.
[
8
, Theorem 11.4.3]
) generalized the above result to association schemes.

Note that
n=n^{+}+n^{-}
for (primitive) SRGs, and thus Theorem
1.3
(ii)
is equivalent to
2n^{+}\leq n^{-}(n^{-}+1)
. We believe that this bound is valid for all graphs.

Conjecture 1.1
.

For any graph
G
, we have

2n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)(n^{-}(G)+1).

The above conjecture, if true, would generalize the absolute bound for SRGs to all graphs. The purpose of this note is to bring the above conjecture to the notice of researchers and provide evidence for its validity. The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide more reasons why Conjecture
1.1
is interesting. In Section
3
, we address the equality case for Conjecture
1.1
, and mention some weaker bounds. In Section
4
, we verify the conjecture for various classes of graphs, including planar graphs and line graphs. We conclude with some remarks in Section
5
.

Torgašev’s problem

The problem of upper-bounding the order of a graph in terms of some graph invariant involving inertia has received attention in the literature. Note that by adding isolated vertices or twins to a graph, one can increase its order without changing its (positive and negative) inertia (see Lemma
3.1
). So the problem is meaningful only for reduced graphs.

Motivated by their interest in the connection between chromatic number and rank of a graph, the problem of bounding the order of a graph in terms of the rank was first studied by Kotlov and Lovász
[
23
]
. They proved that there is a constant
c>0
such that any reduced graph with rank
r
has order at most
c2^{r/2}
. Akbari, Cameron, and Khosrovshahi
[
2
]
later proposed the following.

Conjecture 2.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

For
r\geq 2
, the order of any reduced graph of rank
r
is at most
m(r)
, where

m(r)=\begin{cases}2^{(r+2)/2}-2\quad\quad\,\text{ if }r\text{ is even},\\
5\cdot 2^{(r-3)/2}-2\quad\text{ if }r\text{ is odd}.\end{cases}

In
[
2
]
, the authors also provide constructions of graphs for which the bounds in the above conjecture are tight. Ghorbani, Mohammadian, and Tayfeh-Rezaie
[
19
]
proved that if Conjecture
2.1
is false, then a counterexample must exist with rank at most
46
. They also showed that every reduced graph of rank
r
has at most
8m(r)+14
vertices.

In the 1980s, Torgašev
[

For any fixed integer

Theorem 2.2

Let

… any estimate of the growth of the function

Let

can also be rephrased in terms of signature as follows.

Conjecture 2.4

For any graph

3
A coarse upper bound

We have verified Conjecture
1.1
for graphs of order at most 9, and graphs with order at most
100
in the Wolfram Mathematica database and the
House of Graphs
database.

We note that the quadratic upper bound for
n^{+}
in terms of
n^{-}
cannot be improved. The line graph of
K_{n}
is called
triangular graph

T(n)
. It is known that
T(n)
is an SRG and has inertia
(n,0,n(n-3)/2)
, see
[
8
, p. 10]
. The complement of
T(n)
, therefore, provides an infinite family of graphs for which
n^{+}=\Omega((n^{-})^{2})
.

It is also worth noting that there does exist a rather coarse upper bound for
n^{+}
in terms of
n^{-}
.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
2
]
)
.

For any graph
G
, we have

n^{+}(G)\leq R(n^{-}(G)+2,2^{n^{-}(G)})-n^{-}(G)-1,

where
R(m,n)
denotes the Ramsey number.

The upper bound given in the above result is exponential in
n^{-}
, whereas in Conjecture
1.1
the upper bound is quadratic in
n^{-}
. As the gap is huge, it is worth proving even a polynomial bound in
n^{-}
, see Problem
5.1
.

We have found many graphs for which equality holds in Conjecture
1.1
. These include
K_{2}
,
C_{5}
, the generalized quadrangle
GQ(2,4)
on 27 vertices with spectrum
(10^{1},1^{20},-5^{6})
, the McLaughlin strongly regular graph on 275 vertices with spectrum
(112^{1},2^{252},-28^{22})
, and certain circulant and matchstick graphs

In light of the following lemma, infinitely many tight examples can be obtained for Conjecture

Lemma 3.1

(

Let

n^{+}(G)=n^{+}(G-u)

Does there exist an infinite family of reduced graphs for which equality holds in Conjecture

4
Special graph families

In this section, we verify Conjecture
1.1
for some special graph families.

Clearly, the conjecture holds for strongly regular graphs. By the results of Torgašev
[
32
,
34
]
(see Table
1
), the conjecture is true for graphs with at most three negative eigenvalues.

4.1
Random graphs

Here, we consider the inertia of the random graph
G(n,1/2)
. Martin and Wong
[
26
]
proved that almost all integer matrices have no integer eigenvalues. It follows that
n^{0}(G(n,1/2))=0
with probability tending to 1.

Using Wigner’s semicircle law
[
36
]
in the form given by Arnold
[
3
]
(with appropriate normalization), we have (also see the discussion in
[
11
, Section 2]
)

n^{-}(G(n,1/2))=\frac{2n}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{0}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}dx=\frac{n}{2}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad n^{+}(G(n,1/2))-1=\frac{2n}{\pi}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{1-x^{2}}dx=\frac{n}{2}.

The
-1
term for
n^{+}
corresponds to the largest eigenvalue, which is excluded from Wigner’s symmetry about zero. Therefore,
n^{+}\approx n^{-}
and hence Conjecture
1.1
holds for almost all graphs.

4.2
Graphs with a cut vertex

If
G
is the disjoint union of two graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, and both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
satisfy Conjecture
2.4
(equivalently, Conjecture
1.1
), then

s(G)=s(G_{1})+s(G_{2})\leq\binom{n^{-}(G_{1})}{2}+\binom{n^{-}(G_{2})}{2}\leq\binom{n^{-}(G_{1})+n^{-}(G_{2})}{2}=\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}.

Hence, to prove Conjecture
2.4
(equivalently, Conjecture
1.1
), it suffices to work with connected graphs. We now prove that it suffices to work with 2-connected graphs. We first recall some known results. We call a vertex a
leaf
if its degree is
1
.

Lemma 4.1

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph containing a leaf
u
with neighbour
v
, and
H=G-\{u,v\}
. Then
n^{+}(G)=n^{+}(H)+1
and
n^{-}(G)=n^{-}(H)+1
.

Lemma 4.2

(
[
25
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph,
v\in V(G)
and
H=G-v
. Then

|s(G)-s(H)|\leq 1.

Moreover, if
\rank(G)=\rank(H)
or
\rank(G)=\rank(H)+2
, then
s(G)=s(H).

Theorem 4.1
.

For any graph
G
, we have

s(G)\leq\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2},

provided the inequality holds for all
2
-connected graphs.

Proof.

Assume that the inequality holds for 2-connected graphs. Using induction on the order
n
, we will prove that the inequality holds for all graphs. For
n=3
, the assertion is trivial. Suppose that
G
is a connected graph of order
n\geq 4
.

First, assume that
G
has a leaf
u
with neighbour
w
. By Lemma
4.1
, we have
s(G)=s(G-\{u,w\})
. By the induction hypothesis, we have

s(G)=s(G-\{u,w\})\leq\binom{n^{-}(G-\{u,w\})}{2}\leq\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}.

So, assume that the minimum degree
\delta(G)\geq 2
.

Let
v
be a cut-vertex in
G
and
H=G-v
. By Lemma
4.2
,
|s(G)-s(H)|\leq 1
. We consider the following cases:

Case 1:

s(G)=s(H)
. Then by the induction hypothesis, we have

s(G)=s(H)\leq\binom{n^{-}(H)}{2}\leq\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}.

Case 2:

s(G)=s(H)-1
. By Lemma
4.2
,
\rank(G)=\rank(H)+1
. It follows that

n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G)=n^{+}(H)+n^{-}(H)+1,\quad n^{+}(G)-n^{-}(G)=n^{+}(H)-n^{-}(H)-1.

Adding yields
n^{+}(G)=n^{+}(H)
and subtracting yields
n^{-}(G)=n^{-}(H)+1
. By the induction hypothesis, we have

s(G)=s(H)-1\leq\binom{n^{-}(H)}{2}-1\leq\binom{n^{-}(H)+1}{2}=\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}.

Case 3:

s(G)=s(H)+1
. Again, by Lemma
4.2
,
\rank(G)=\rank(H)+1
. It follows that

n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G)=n^{+}(H)+n^{-}(H)+1,\quad n^{+}(G)-n^{-}(G)=n^{+}(H)-n^{-}(H)+1.

Adding yields
n^{+}(G)=n^{+}(H)+1
, subtracting yields
n^{-}(G)=n^{-}(H)
. Since
v
is cut-vertex,
H
has components
C_{1},\dots,C_{t}
for some
t\geq 2
. We have

n^{+}(H)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n^{+}(C_{i}),\quad n^{-}(H)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}n^{-}(C_{i}),\quad s(H)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}s(C_{i}).

By induction, we have

s(C_{i})\leq\binom{n^{-}(C_{i})}{2},

for each
i
, which implies

s(H)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{t}\binom{n^{-}(C_{i})}{2}\leq\binom{n^{-}(H)}{2}-\sum_{i<j}n^{-}(C_{i})n^{-}(C_{j}).

Since
\delta(G)\geq 2
, each
C_{i}
has order at least 2, implying
n^{-}(C_{i})\geq 1
, for
i=1,\ldots,t
. Hence

\sum_{i<j}n^{-}(C_{i})n^{-}(C_{j})\geq 1.

It follows that

s(G)=s(H)+1\leq\binom{n^{-}(H)}{2}=\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

4.3
Graphs with chromatic number at most 4

Let
G
be a graph with chromatic number
\chi(G)\leq 4
. We can assume that
n^{-}(G)\geq 4
; otherwise Conjecture
1.1
holds by Table
1
.

Recently, the following result was obtained by Elphick, Tang and Zhang
[
12
]
.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
12
]
)
.

For any graph
G
, we have

1+\max{\left\{\frac{n^{+}(G)}{n^{-}(G)},\frac{n^{-}(G)}{n^{+}(G)}\right\}}\leq\chi_{f}(G),

where
\chi_{f}(G)
denotes the fractional chromatic number of
G
.

An immediate corollary is that

1+\max{\left\{\frac{n^{+}(G)}{n^{-}(G)},\frac{n^{-}(G)}{n^{+}(G)}\right\}}\leq\chi(G).

So if
G
is a graph such that
\chi(G)\leq 3
, then

n^{+}(G)\leq 2n^{-}(G),

which implies
2n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)(n^{-}(G)+1)
, whenever
n^{-}(G)\geq 3
. Hence, Conjecture
1.1
holds for
G
. In particular, we note that Conjecture
1.1
holds for subcubic graphs.

Now, assume that
\chi(G)=4
. Arguing as before, we have

n^{+}(G)\leq 3n^{-}(G).

So if
n^{-}(G)\geq 5
, then Conjecture
1.1
holds for
G
. The remaining case is
n^{-}(G)=4
. We leave this case for future work, but we prove Conjecture
1.1
for two families: graphs with maximum degree 4 and planar graphs. We require the following lemmas.

Lemma 4.3

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
A
and
B
be two Hermitian matrices of the same order. Then

(i)

n^{+}(A)-n^{-}(B)\leq n^{+}(A+B)\leq n^{+}(A)+n^{+}(B)
.

(ii)

n^{-}(A)-n^{+}(B)\leq n^{-}(A+B)\leq n^{-}(A)+n^{-}(B)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a connected graph of order
n\geq 2
,
u\in V(G)
and
H=G-N[u]
. Then
n^{+}(G)\geq n^{+}(H)+1
and
n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(H)+1
.

Proof.

Let
w\in N(u)
. Consider the induced subgraph
H^{\prime}=G-(N(u)\backslash\{w\})
of
G
. Clearly,
u
is a leaf with neighbour
w
in
H^{\prime}
and
H^{\prime}-\{u,w\}=H
. By Lemma
4.1
, we have

n^{+}(H^{\prime})\geq n^{+}(H)+1\quad\text{and}\quad n^{-}(H^{\prime})\geq n^{-}(H)+1.

By the Interlacing Theorem, we have
n^{+}(G)\geq n^{+}(H^{\prime})
and
n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(H^{\prime})
. The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 4.3
.

Conjecture
1.1
holds for graphs with maximum degree four.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph with maximum degree
\Delta(G)\leq 4
. By Brooks’ Theorem and the discussion above, we only need to consider the case
n^{-}(G)=4
. Let
u\in V(G)
, and
H=G-N[u]
. By Lemma
4.4
, we have
n^{-}(H)\leq 3
. By Table
1
, we get
n^{+}(H)\leq 6
. Define
F
to be the subgraph of
G
induced by the edges
E(G)\backslash E(H)
. Clearly,
V(F)=N[u]
. Observe that
E(F)
can be decomposed into four stars centred at the vertices in
N(u)
. By Lemma
4.3
, we see that

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{+}(H)+n^{+}(F)\leq 6+4=10.

Since,
n^{-}(G)=4
, we have
n^{-}(G)\left(n^{-}(G)+1\right)=20
, and Conjecture
1.1
holds for
G
.
∎

Proposition 4.1
.

If

Let

. We verified that all the graphs of order at least 10 in the table have

, and so their induced subgraphs also have

by the Interlacing Theorem. We verified by computer

Theorem 4.4
.

Conjecture
1.1

Proof.

By the Four Color Theorem and the discussion above, we only need to consider a planar graph
G

n^{-}(G)=4
. Since
G
is planar, there exists
u\in V(G)
such that
\deg(u)\leq 5
. Let
H=G-N[u]
. By Lemma
4.4
, we have
n^{-}(H)\leq 3
. By Proposition
4.1
,
n^{+}(H)\leq 5
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.3
, let
F
be the subgraph of
G
induced by the edges
E(G)\backslash E(H)
. Clearly,
V(F)=N[u]
and
E(F)
can be decomposed into five stars centred at the vertices in
N(u)
. Now, by Lemma
4.3
, we see that

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{+}(H)+n^{+}(F)\leq 5+5=10.

As
n^{-}(G)\left(n^{-}(G)+1\right)=20
, Conjecture
1.1
holds for
G
.
∎

4.4
Line graphs

Here, we verify Conjecture
1.1
for line graphs.

Let
G
be a graph of order
n
, size
m
and line graph
L(G)
. Let
\mathcal{L}(G)
and
\mathcal{Q}(G)
denote its Laplacian and signless Laplacian matrix. It is well-known that if
\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{n}
are the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{Q}(G)
, then
\theta_{i}-2
are the eigenvalues of
A(L(G))
with remaining eigenvalues equal to
-2
, refer
[
8
, Chapter 1]
. In particular, the least eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(L(G))\geq-2
and has multiplicity at least
m-n
.

We first prove the following lemma. Recall that for a graph
G
of order
n
, the
energy
of
G
is defined as
\mathcal{E}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(G)|
.

Lemma 4.5
.

For any graph
G
of order
n
, we have

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)\left(2|\lambda_{n}(G)|-1\right).

Moreover, if
\lambda_{1}(G)\geq 3.3
, then

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)\left(2|\lambda_{n}(G)|-1\right)-1.1.

Proof.

Observe that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have
x\geq\ln x+1
. Now,

\mathcal{E}(G)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(G)|\geq n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G)+\ln\left(\prod_{\lambda_{i}(G)\neq 0}|\lambda_{i}(G)|\right).

Note that
\prod_{\lambda_{i}(G)\neq 0}|\lambda_{i}(G)|
is the first non-zero coefficient of the characteristic polynomial of
G
. Hence
\prod_{\lambda_{i}(G)\neq 0}|\lambda_{i}(G)|\geq 1
and therefore

\mathcal{E}(G)\geq n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G).

Since the trace of the adjacency matrix
A(G)
is zero, we know

\mathcal{E}(G)=2\sum_{\lambda_{i}(G)<0}|\lambda_{i}(G)|\leq 2|\lambda_{n}(G)|n^{-}(G).

Combining the above inequalities for
\mathcal{E}(G)
gives the first inequality in the assertion.

Now, if
x\in\mathbb{R}
is such that
x\geq 3.3
, then
x\geq\ln x+2.1
. So if
\lambda_{1}(G)\geq 3.3
, then following the same steps as above gives the second inequality in the assertion.
∎

We note that the inequality
\mathcal{E}(G)\geq n^{+}(G)+n^{-}(G)
was first observed in
[
14
]
(cf.
[
1
]
).

Theorem 4.5
.

For any graph
G
with line graph
L(G)
, we have

n^{+}(L(G))\leq\min\left\{3n^{-}(L(G)),\,\frac{1}{2}n^{-}(L(G))\left(n^{-}(L(G))+1\right)\right\}.

Proof.

Let
G
be a connected graph of order
n
. If
n\leq 8
, then the assertion is verified using a computer. So we assume
n\geq 9
. Throughout this proof, we denote
n^{+}(L(G))
and
n^{-}(L(G))
by
n^{+}
and
n^{-}
, respectively.

Using the first inequality in Lemma
4.5
, we get
n^{+}\leq 3n^{-}
since
|\lambda_{\min}(L(G))|\leq 2
. If
n^{-}\geq 5
, then
3n^{-}\leq\frac{1}{2}n^{-}(n^{-}+1)
and the assertion follows. If
n^{-}\leq 3
, we are done by Table
1
.

So, assume that
n^{-}=4
. We need to show that
n^{+}\leq 10
. We consider the following cases:

Case 1:

\lambda_{1}(L(G))\geq 3.3
. Then using the second inequality in Lemma
4.5
and noting that
n^{+}
is an integer, we get
n^{+}\leq 3n^{-}-2=10.

Case 2:

\lambda_{1}(L(G))<3.3
. Since
K_{\Delta(G)}
is an induced subgraph of
L(G)
, we have
\lambda_{1}(L(G))\geq\Delta(G)-1
by the Interlacing Theorem. It follows that
\Delta(G)\leq 4
.

Since
n^{-}=4
and the least eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(L(G))
has multiplicity at least
m-n
, we have
m\leq n^{-}+n=4+n
. If
\deg(u)\geq 3
for all
u\in V(G)
, then

n+4\geq m=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u\in V(G)}\deg(u)\geq\frac{3n}{2},

implying
n\leq 8
, a contradiction. So let
u\in V(G)
be such that
\deg(u)\leq 2
and let
v\in N(u)
. Then
\deg(u)+\deg(v)\leq 2+4=6
. It follows that
\deg_{L(G)}(e)\leq 4
, where
e=uv
. Let
H=L(G)-N_{L(G)}[e]
. Using Lemma
4.4
, we get
n^{-}(H)\leq 3
. By Table
1
, we get
n^{+}(H)\leq 6
. As in the proof of Theorem
4.3
, using Lemma
4.3
, we get

n^{+}\leq n^{+}(H)+4=10.\qed

Computational investigation suggests that the upper bound for
n^{+}
of line graphs can be further improved.

Conjecture 4.1
.

For any connected graph
G
, with line graph
L(G)
,

n^{+}(L(G))\leq n^{-}(L(G))+1.

Equivalently,
s(L(G))\leq 1
.

We have tested this conjecture on numerous graphs with
m(G)=n(L(G))\leq 100
using the LineGraph function in Wolfram Mathematica and found no counterexample. In addition, no counterexample was found for any graph
G
with at most 9 vertices. Conjecture
4.1
is tight, for example, for odd cycle
C_{4k+1}
whenever
k\geq 1
,
C_{5}\cup C_{5}
joined by an edge, and various KayakPaddle, Tadpole and Prism graphs
3
3

3

These graph families are named according to the Wolfram Mathematica graph database:
KayakPaddle
,
Tadpole
, and
Prism
.
. We require that the line graph is connected; otherwise,
C_{5}\cup C_{5}
is a disconnected counterexample.

It is known that for bipartite graphs, the signless Laplacian and the Laplacian spectrum are the same; see, for instance,
[
8
, Chapter 1]
. The following was shown by Guo
[
21
]
(cf.
[
7
]
).

Theorem 4.6

(
[
21
]
, cf.
[
7
]
)
.

Any tree of order
n
has at least
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
Laplacian eigenvalues in the interval
[0,2)
.

Using the above facts, it is easy to see that for any tree
T
, we have
s(L(T))\leq-1
. It follows that Conjecture
4.1
holds for line graphs of trees.

Furthermore, we note that Conjecture
4.1
is true for dense line graphs. In other words, if
G
has size
m
and order
n
, with
m\geq 2n-1
, then

n^{+}(L(G))\leq n\leq m-n+1\leq n^{-}(L(G))+1.

So Conjecture
4.1
is open only for the line graph of
G
when
n\leq m\leq 2n-2
.

4.5
Self-complementary graphs

A graph
G
is called
self-complementary
if it is isomorphic to its complement
\overline{G}
.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
G
be a self-complementary graph. Then

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)+1.

Proof.

It is known that for any graph
G
of order
n
, the following hold (see
[
13
, Theorem 7]
):

n^{+}(G)+n^{+}(\overline{G})\leq n+1\quad\text{and}\quad n-1\leq n^{-}(G)+n^{-}(\overline{G}).

For a self-complementary graph
G
, these inequalities simplify to

2n^{+}(G)\leq n+1\quad\text{and}\quad 2n^{-}(G)\geq n-1.

Thus,

n^{+}(G)\leq\frac{n+1}{2}\leq n^{-}(G)+1.\qed

Any self-complementary graph of order
n\geq 6
contains a triangle since the Ramsey number
R(3,3)=6
. Hence by the Interlacing Theorem,
n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(K_{3})=2
. Moreover, there is no self-complementary graph of order at most 5 with
n^{-}=1
. Thus, by Theorem
4.7
, we have

2n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G)(n^{-}(G)+1),

i.e., any self-complementary graph
G
satisfies Conjecture
1.1
.

Remark
.

The inequality in Theorem
4.7
is tight for Paley graphs
4
4

4

See
https://mathworld.wolfram.com/PaleyGraph.html
for a description of Paley graphs.
.

4.6
Graphs with few odd cycles

In
[
24
, Theorem 5.2]
, the following inequality was established for a general graph
G
.

Theorem 4.8

(
[
24
]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph. Then

|n^{+}(G)-n^{-}(G)|\leq c_{1}(G),

where
c_{1}(G)
denotes the number of odd cycles in
G
.

Therefore, if
c_{1}(G)\leq\binom{n^{-}(G)}{2}
, then Conjecture
1.1
holds. By Table
1
, it suffices to consider the case
n^{-}(G)\geq 4
. So, if
c_{1}(G)\leq\binom{4}{2}=6
, Conjecture
1.1
holds. In particular, the conjecture holds for unicyclic, bicyclic, and tricyclic graphs.

4.7
Graph products

We verify Conjecture
1.1
for some graph products. For the definitions of the graph products mentioned here, refer
[
4
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
G
and
H
be two graphs for which Conjecture
1.1
holds. Then

2n^{+}(G\otimes H)\leq n^{-}(G\otimes H)(n^{-}(G\otimes H)+1),

where
G\otimes H
denotes the direct (tensor) product of
G
and
H
.

Proof.

It is known that if
\lambda_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq|V(G)|
) and
\mu_{j}
(
1\leq j\leq|V(H)|
) are the eigenvalues of
G
and
H
respectively, then the eigenvalues of
G\otimes H
are given by
\lambda_{i}\mu_{j}
(see
[
4
]
). It follows that

n^{+}(G\otimes H)=n^{+}(G)n^{+}(H)+n^{-}(G)n^{-}(H);\quad n^{-}(G\otimes H)=n^{+}(G)n^{-}(H)+n^{-}(G)n^{+}(H).

We have

\displaystyle n^{-}(G\otimes H)(n^{-}(G\otimes H)+1)

\displaystyle=(n^{+}(G)n^{-}(H)+n^{-}(G)n^{+}(H))^{2}+n^{-}(G\otimes H)

\displaystyle\geq 4n^{+}(G)n^{-}(G)n^{+}(H)n^{-}(H)+n^{-}(G\otimes H)

\displaystyle\geq 2n^{+}(G)n^{+}(H)+2n^{-}(G)n^{-}(H)+n^{-}(G\otimes H)

\displaystyle>2n^{+}(G\otimes H).\qed

Next, we consider the join of graphs. We require the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
G
be a graph and
u,v\in V(G)
be such that
N[u]=N[v]
. Then

n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(G-\{u,v\})+1.

Proof.

Let
n=|V(G)|
and
H=G-\{u,v\}
. Let
k=n^{-}(H)
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
denote the orthogonal unit eigenvectors corresponding to the negative eigenvalues of
A(H)
. Let
x_{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the unit eigenvector of the adjacency matrix of
K_{2}\cong G[\{u,v\}]
corresponding to the eigenvalue
-1
, given by

x_{k+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
-1\end{bmatrix}.

Extend
x_{i}
to
\tilde{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by padding with zeroes for
1\leq i\leq k+1
. Consider the
(k+1)
-dimensional subspace
W
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
spanned by the orthogonal unit vectors
\tilde{x}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{x}_{k+1}
and
z=\alpha_{1}\tilde{x}_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k+1}\tilde{x}_{k+1}\in W
with
\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}\alpha_{i}^{2}=1
. Using the Courant-Fischer-Weyl Min-Max Theorem (see
[
22
]
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{n-k-1}(G)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{z}z^{T}A(G)z

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{1}\tilde{x}_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k+1}\tilde{x}_{k+1})^{T}A(G)(\alpha_{1}\tilde{x}_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k+1}\tilde{x}_{k+1})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{k+1}^{2}x_{k+1}^{T}A(K_{2})x_{k+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}^{2}x_{i}^{T}A(H)x_{i}

\displaystyle<0.

We conclude that
n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(H)+1
.
∎

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
G
and
H
be two connected graphs, each of order at least
2
, for which Conjecture
1.1
holds. Then

2n^{+}(G\vee H)\leq n^{-}(G\vee H)(n^{-}(G\vee H)+1),

where
G\vee H
denotes the join of
G
and
H
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
n^{-}(G)\geq n^{-}(H)\geq 1
. By Lemma
4.3
, we have

n^{+}(G\vee H)\leq n^{+}(G)+n^{+}(H)+n^{+}(K_{|G|,|H|})=n^{+}(G)+n^{+}(H)+1,

and

n^{-}(G\vee H)\geq n^{-}(G)+n^{-}(H)-n^{-}(K_{|G|,|H|})=n^{-}(G)+n^{-}(H)-1.

We consider the following cases:

Case 1:

n^{-}(H)\geq 2
. If
n^{-}(G)\geq 3
, then

\displaystyle 2n^{+}(G\vee H)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{+}(G)+2n^{+}(H)+2

\displaystyle\leq n^{-}(G)\left(n^{-}(G)+1\right)+n^{-}(H)\left(n^{-}(H)+1\right)+2

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{-}(G)+n^{-}(H)-1\right)\left(n^{-}(G)+n^{-}(H)\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-}(G\vee H)\left(n^{-}(G\vee H)+1\right).

And if
n^{-}(G)=2
, then
n^{-}(H)=2
. By Table
1
, we have
\max\{n^{+}(G),n^{+}(H)\}\leq 3
. It follows that

n^{+}(G\vee H)\leq 3+3+1=7\ \text{ and }\ n^{-}(G\vee H)\geq 2+2-1=3.

If
n^{-}(G\vee H)=3
, then we are done by Table
1
. So assume
n^{-}(G\vee H)\geq 4
, which implies

2n^{+}(G\vee H)\leq 14<n^{-}(G\vee H)\left(n^{-}(G\vee H)+1\right).

Case 2:

n^{-}(H)=1
. Then
H
is a complete bipartite graph, say
K_{p,q}
with
p+q\geq 2
.

Again, by Lemma
4.3
, we have

\displaystyle 2n^{+}(G\vee H)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{+}(G)+2n^{+}(K_{p,q,|G|})

\displaystyle=2n^{+}(G)+2

\displaystyle\leq n^{-}(G)\left(n^{-}(G)+1\right)+2

\displaystyle\leq\left(n^{-}(G)+1\right)\left(n^{-}(G)+2\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-}(G\vee K_{2})\left(n^{-}(G\vee K_{2})+1\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-}(G\vee H)\left(n^{-}(G\vee H)+1\right).

The second last inequality holds by Lemma
4.6
and the last inequality holds by the Interlacing Theorem since
G\vee K_{2}
is an induced subgraph of
G\vee H
. This completes the proof.
∎

4.8
Cographs

A graph is called
cograph
if it does not contain
P_{4}
as an induced subgraph. Another characterization is that every nontrivial induced subgraph of a cograph has a pair of vertices with the same open or closed neighbourhoods. We verify Conjecture
1.1
for cographs. We first recall some results.

Theorem 4.10

(
[
27
,
18
]
)
.

Let
G
be a cograph,
u,v\in V(G)
be distinct vertices and
H=G-u
. Let
\mult(G,\lambda)
denote the eigenvalue multiplicity of
\lambda
in the spectrum of
G
. Then the following statements are true.

(i)

G
has no eigenvalues in the interval
(-1,0)
.

(ii)

If
N(u)=N(v)
, then
\mult(G,0)=\mult(H,0)+1
.

(iii)

If
N[u]=N[v]
, then
\mult(G,-1)=\mult(H,-1)+1
.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
G
be a cograph. Then

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{-}(G).

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the order
n
. If
n=3
, then the assertion holds. Let
G
be a cograph on
n\geq 4
vertices. Then
G
has a pair of vertices
u,v
such that one of the following occurs:

Case 1:

u
and
v
have the same open neighbourhood, i.e.,
N(u)=N(v)
.

Let
H=G-u
. By Lemma
3.1
,
n^{+}(H)=n^{+}(G)
and
n^{-}(G)=n^{-}(H)
. The assertion holds by the induction hypothesis.

Case 2:

u
and
v
have the same closed neighbourhood, i.e.,
N[u]=N[v]
.

By the Interlacing Theorem, if
H
has
t
-many eigenvalues less than
-1
, then
G
has at least
t
-many eigenvalues less than
-1
. Using Theorem
4.10

(i)
and
(iii)
, we conclude that
n^{-}(G)=n^{-}(H)+1
.

By the induction hypothesis, we have

n^{+}(G)\leq n^{+}(H)+1\leq n^{-}(H)+1=n^{-}(G).

The proof is complete.
∎

5
Concluding remarks

The purpose of this article is to introduce and motivate Conjectures
1.1
and
4.1
. We have made modest progress in proving these conjectures, but the evidence for them appears to be strong, and there are diverse graphs for which the conjectures are tight. We hope that this paper will encourage others to make further progress. Also, since these conjectures do not involve NP-hard parameters, they are well-suited to the use of AI tools to search for counterexamples.

Given the apparent difficulty of proving Conjecture
1.1
, we believe the following weaker conjecture may be more tractable.

Conjecture 5.1
.

For any graph
G
of order
n
, we have

2n\leq(n-n^{+}(G))(n-n^{+}(G)+3).

The above conjecture is equivalent to Conjecture
1.1
when
n^{0}=0
. It also refines a question of Mohar
[
29
]
on upper-bounding the order of a graph by a function of its non-positive eigenvalues. Even the following weaker problem is of interest.

Problem 5.1
.

Does there exist a polynomial
f(x)
such that for every graph
G
,

n^{+}(G)\leq f(n^{-}(G)).
