Dynamic Vascular Spatiotemporal Heterogeneity on Multiphase CT for Preoperative Prediction of Microvascular Invasion in Hepatocellular Carcinoma
Authors: Shucheng Yang, Meng Liu, Yongjie Zhou, Jinhong Zhao, Yongming Tan, Weijia Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMicrovascular invasion (MVI) is an important determinant of postoperative recurrence in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) but is usually confirmed only after surgery. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a multiphase CT framework integrating dynamic vascular trajectory, spatial intratumoral heterogeneity, and deep imaging features for preoperative MVI prediction and exploratory prognostic stratification.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective multicenter study included 405 patients with pathologically confirmed HCC: 198 in the development cohort and 207 in two independent external-validation cohorts. Clinical variables, static multiphase radiomics, Delta vascular trajectory features, three-dimensional intratumoral heterogeneity (3D-ITH)/habitat features, and Vision Transformer (ViT) features were extracted. All preprocessing, model fitting, and threshold selection were confined to the development cohort. A capacity-constrained multimodal Transformer was compared with clinical, single-modality, simple concatenation, and tree-based models.ResultsThe cohort included 148 MVI-positive and 257 MVI-negative patients according to the locked blinded multireviewer reference standard. In pooled external validation, Transformer fusion achieved an area under the curve of 0.941 (95% CI, 0.906–0.969), sensitivity of 0.951, specificity of 0.754, accuracy of 0.831, negative predictive value of 0.960, and Brier score of 0.113. Corresponding AUCs were 0.957 and 0.934 in the two external cohorts. Delta-only and clinical models achieved pooled external AUCs of 0.793 and 0.675, respectively. High fusion-risk patients had poorer composite RFS/PFS than low-risk patients (hazard ratio, 2.05; 95% CI, 1.36–3.08; P < 0.001).ConclusionThe proposed dynamic vascular heterogeneity-centered framework demonstrated high external discrimination for preoperative MVI prediction in HCC and provided exploratory stratification of the available composite RFS/PFS endpoint. Prospective validation and recalibration are required before clinical implementation.Keywords: delta radiomics, habitat imaging, vision transformer, multimodal fusion, external validation, recurrence-free survival

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related death, and recurrence after curative-intent resection is common.1 Microvascular invasion (MVI) is a central pathologic marker of invasive tumor biology and is associated with early recurrence, vascular dissemination, and worse survival.2–5 Yet MVI is usually confirmed only after surgery. A reliable preoperative imaging biomarker could influence surgical margin planning, transplantation or resection strategy, adjuvant therapy discussion, and surveillance intensity.6

Dynamic contrast-enhanced CT is already embedded in routine HCC diagnosis and staging.7 The noncontrast, arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases collectively depict arterialization, washout, delayed retention, necrosis, and peritumoral perfusion disturbance.8,9 These enhancement behaviors are not incidental image appearances; they are macroscopic consequences of tumor angiogenesis, sinusoidal remodeling, stromal pressure, hypoxia, and microscopic invasion.10–12 Static tumor size or single-phase enhancement descriptors therefore capture only part of the biology relevant to MVI.

Over the past five years, radiomics and deep learning studies have improved noninvasive MVI and recurrence prediction in HCC.13–19 CT radiomics has shown reproducible signal in multicenter studies and meta-analyses, peritumoral analysis has highlighted the invasive interface, habitat imaging has partitioned tumors into spatially heterogeneous subregions, and radiogenomic work has linked CT phenotypes to proliferative and immune-related tumor states.20–26 However, much of this literature still treats multiphase CT as a set of static channels. Features are often extracted from individual phases and concatenated, whereas the actual vascular trajectory from noncontrast to delayed imaging is not modeled as the primary biological signal.

This limitation is clinically important. MVI is not merely a static morphologic property; it reflects a spatial and temporal process involving vascular recruitment, perfusion redistribution, stromal remodeling, and tumor-parenchyma interaction. A biologically aligned imaging framework should therefore ask how the tumor changes across phases, how heterogeneous these changes are in three dimensions, and whether dynamic vascular behavior adds information beyond clinical and static imaging variables.27–31 Robust evaluation of such models requires careful handling of reference-standard uncertainty, strict separation of model development from external validation, control of model complexity, development-derived threshold selection, and assessment of both discrimination and calibration.

Beyond blood-vessel-centered invasion, remodeling of lymphatic vessels at the tumor boundary may represent another component of the invasive microenvironment. The HCC capsule is not simply a passive anatomical barrier; it may serve as an interface where lymphangiogenesis, stromal remodeling, and tumor-cell dissemination converge. In a recent cohort of patients with Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer stage 0–A HCC undergoing curative resection, Li et al32 quantified lymphatic vessel density (LVD) within the tumor capsule by D2-40 immunohistochemistry and found that high capsular LVD was associated with microvascular invasion, satellite nodules, and postoperative recurrence. These pathological observations complement imaging-based evaluation of capsular integrity and dynamic vascular heterogeneity, and suggest that the tumor–capsule interface contains prognostically relevant information that may not be fully captured by conventional morphology alone.

We therefore developed a dynamic vascular spatiotemporal heterogeneity framework for preoperative MVI prediction in HCC. Delta vascular trajectory features quantified phase-to-phase enhancement changes across noncontrast, arterial, portal venous, and delayed CT. Three-dimensional intratumoral heterogeneity (3D-ITH)/habitat features characterized spatial heterogeneity, while Vision Transformer (ViT) features provided a complementary deep imaging representation. A capacity-constrained multimodal Transformer was used to integrate these heterogeneous feature domains12,33–40 We hypothesized that integrating dynamic vascular trajectory with spatial heterogeneity and deep features would improve externally validated MVI prediction and provide exploratory stratification of the available composite recurrence-free survival/progression-free survival (RFS/PFS) endpoint. Secondary analyses evaluated the influence of temporal feature construction and reference-label definition and explored whether the resulting fusion score stratified the available composite RFS/PFS endpoint, including across tumor-capsule subgroups (Figure 1).

Material and Methods

This retrospective multicenter study was reviewed and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (Approval No. IIT-2025-806). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all individual participants. The study used the latest locked blinded multireviewer MVI label as the only primary gold standard. The complete analysis set comprised 405 patients, including 198 patients from hospital 0 for development, 124 from hospital 1 for external validation 1, and 83 from hospital 2 for external validation 2. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed HCC; (2) availability of preoperative contrast-enhanced CT within one month before surgery; and (3) complete clinical and follow-up data. Patients with poor image quality or incomplete clinical information were excluded.

CT Acquisition and Image Preprocessing

All patients underwent preoperative multiphase CT with noncontrast, arterial, portal venous, and delayed phases. Across scanners, acquisition used 120 kV, 210–350 mA, 0.4–0.5-second rotation time, 1–3-mm slice thickness, 512×512 matrix, and approximately 350-mm field of view. Contrast-enhanced imaging used 100 mL iopromide 370 mg/mL injected at 2.5 mL/s. Arterial and portal venous phases started approximately 10 and 45 seconds after the abdominal aorta reached 100 HU. The CT scanning protocol is provided in Supplementary Material 1. A detailed summary of the CT scanning protocols used by each institution is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Tumor Segmentation and Feature Extraction

Tumor lesions were initially delineated along the visible outer tumor margins on portal venous phase CT images using ITK-SNAP by experienced abdominal radiologists blinded to clinicopathological outcomes. The anatomical tumor boundary was defined according to the visible lesion extent. For quantitative feature extraction, the tumor region of interest (ROI) was adjusted approximately 1–2 mm inward from the visible tumor margin to reduce partial-volume effects and contamination from adjacent normal hepatic tissue. This adjustment represented a preprocessing strategy for quantitative analysis rather than exclusion of the anatomical tumor boundary. When a capsule-like peripheral rim was visually identifiable, it was considered during anatomical assessment but was excluded from the quantitative tumor ROI. Detailed standardized segmentation rules and capsule handling criteria are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

The feature system included clinical and semantic imaging variables, static radiomic features extracted from the noncontrast (N), arterial (A), portal venous (V), and delayed (D) phases, Delta vascular trajectory features, 3D-ITH/habitat descriptors, and ViT deep features. Delta features included arterial-minus-noncontrast, portal-minus-noncontrast, delayed-minus-noncontrast, arterial-minus-portal, portal-minus-delayed, wash-in slope, portal washout, delayed washout, delayed retention, curve AUC index, volatility, and peak phase index.

Temporal Delta-Feature Ablation Analysis

To evaluate whether predictive performance depended on the selected temporal calculation strategy, we performed a post hoc temporal ablation analysis in which the downstream modeling pipeline was held constant and only the composition of the Delta feature set was varied. Ten temporal feature groups were evaluated: the complete trajectory representation; all direct pairwise differences; noncontrast-referenced changes; adjacent-phase changes; individual A–N, V–N, D–N, A–V, and V–D intervals; and derived trajectory indices. The derived trajectory group included wash-in slope, portal washout, delayed washout, delayed retention, arterial dominance, curve-AUC index, volatility, and peak-minus-noncontrast descriptors. For each feature group, missing values were imputed using the development-cohort median, features were standardized, and PCA was capped at 32 components. All preprocessing transformations were fitted exclusively in the development cohort. A class-balanced, L2-regularized logistic regression model was then fitted, and the development-derived Youden threshold was locked before external evaluation. AUCs with 2000-bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated separately for the two external-validation cohorts and for pooled external validation. Each ablation model was compared with the complete trajectory model using a paired DeLong test, and the resulting P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure.

Hospitals 1 and 2 were held out as independent external validation cohorts. The main PCA setting retained at most 32 components per modality; a 95% cumulative-variance sensitivity analysis was also performed. Development of the Constrained Multimodal Transformer Fusion Model

Models included a clinical elastic-net model, a static radiomics model, Delta-only, 3D-ITH-only, and ViT-only models, a Delta+3D-ITH model, a Delta+3D-ITH+ViT model, a clinical+Delta+3D-ITH+ViT simple-concatenation model, a ranger tree-based comparator, and the capacity-constrained multimodal Transformer fusion model. The Transformer integrated four modality-specific feature sets: clinical variables, Delta vascular trajectory features, 3D-ITH/habitat features, and ViT deep features. Within each modality, missing values were imputed using the development-cohort median, features were standardized, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using parameters fitted exclusively in the development cohort. A maximum of 32 principal components was retained per modality to produce a uniform four-modality input.

Each scalar principal component was mapped by a modality-specific linear projection into a 32-dimensional token and combined with a learnable component embedding. Within each modality, the resulting 32-token sequence was processed by a one-layer, four-head self-attention encoder. Cross-modal fusion was then performed using modality-specific cross-attention blocks: the encoded tokens of each modality served as queries, whereas the concatenated tokens from the other three modalities served as keys and values. The cross-attention outputs were mean-pooled to obtain four 32-dimensional modality representations, which were concatenated into a 128-dimensional fused vector and passed through a 128–64–2 classification head to estimate the MVI probability. Model capacity was deliberately constrained through PCA dimensionality reduction, a shallow encoder, a 32-dimensional latent space, dropout, weight decay, gradient clipping, and development-only early stopping. Full architecture parameters, attention equations, training settings, and the step-by-step fusion workflow are provided in Supplementary Material 3 and Supplementary Table 2.

Performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals (CIs), sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Brier score, calibration intercept and slope, calibration curves, and decision-curve analysis (DCA). AUCs were compared using paired DeLong tests. Continuous net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were reported as secondary comparative metrics and interpreted together with their bootstrap 95% CIs. The available follow-up dataset contained only one Time/status pair and did not permit separate adjudication of recurrence and progression. Therefore, the survival analysis was treated as an exploratory composite RFS/PFS analysis rather than as separate RFS or PFS validation.

Survival and Capsule-Stratified Analyses

To assess whether prognostic stratification by the Transformer fusion score was consistent across tumor-capsule subgroups, an additional post hoc capsule-stratified survival analysis was performed in the pooled external-validation cohort. Capsule status was categorized as complete or non-complete according to the original semantic-imaging annotation; the non-complete category included absent and incomplete capsules. Patients were classified as high or low fusion risk using the median fusion probability of 0.340 derived exclusively from the development cohort. This cutoff was not re-estimated within either capsule subgroup.

Within each capsule subgroup, survival distributions were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the Log rank test. Cox proportional-hazards models estimated hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals for high versus low fusion risk. Effect heterogeneity was formally evaluated using a Cox model containing fusion-risk group, capsule status, and a fusion-risk-by-capsule-status interaction term. The original three-level capsule classification—absent, incomplete, and complete—was additionally evaluated as a sensitivity analysis. Because only one Time/status pair was available, these analyses used the same composite RFS/PFS endpoint as the primary prognostic analysis. The capsule-stratified survival analysis was conducted according to the procedures detailed in Supplementary Method 4, including the interaction model used to formally test whether capsule integrity modified the prognostic association of the fusion-risk score.

Results

Study Cohort and Reference-Standard Adjudication

The final main analysis included 405 patients, including 148 MVI-positive and 257 MVI-negative patients. Compared with the manifest MVI label, 155 of 405 labels changed after blinded multireviewer review; compared with the first audit label, 20 of 405 labels changed. The locked MVI-positive rate was 36.5% overall, 33.8% in the development cohort, 33.9% in external validation cohort 1, and 47.0% in external validation cohort 2 (Table 1).

Overall Model Performance in External Validation

In pooled external validation, Transformer fusion showed the highest discrimination and best overall threshold-dependent performance among evaluated models (Table 3 and Figure 2). The Transformer fusion model achieved an AUC of 0.941 (95% CI, 0.906–0.969), sensitivity of 0.951, specificity of 0.754, accuracy of 0.831, PPV of 0.713, NPV of 0.960, and Brier score of 0.113. By comparison, Delta-only features achieved an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI, 0.731–0.855), static radiomics achieved an AUC of 0.785 (95% CI, 0.722–0.848), deep features achieved an AUC of 0.803 (95% CI, 0.738–0.858), and the clinical model achieved an AUC of 0.675 (95% CI, 0.594–0.749).

Temporal Delta-Feature Ablation Analysis

Temporal ablation showed that isolated phase-pair calculations generally provided lower external discrimination than the complete trajectory representation (Supplementary Table 3; Supplementary Figure 1). In pooled external validation, the complete trajectory model achieved an AUC of 0.795 (95% CI, 0.730–0.855). The corresponding AUCs were 0.704 for A–N only, 0.767 for V–N only, 0.723 for D–N only, 0.754 for A–V only, and 0.689 for V–D only.

After Benjamini–Hochberg correction, the complete trajectory representation significantly outperformed A–N only (adjusted P=0.027), D–N only (adjusted P=0.037), V–D only (adjusted P=0.027), and all direct pairwise differences (AUC, 0.763; adjusted P=0.045). The adjacent-phase model achieved an AUC of 0.782 and did not differ significantly from the complete trajectory model (adjusted P=0.527). Derived trajectory indices alone achieved an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI, 0.726–0.858) and were statistically indistinguishable from the complete representation (DeLong P=0.957), indicating that integrated dynamic summaries accounted for a substantial proportion of the Delta vascular signal.

Prognostic Performance of the Transformer Fusion Score

RFS/PFS analysis used the development-derived median Transformer fusion cutoff of 0.340 in the pooled external cohort. High-risk patients had worse RFS/PFS than low-risk patients (log-rank P <0.001; Figure 5). In Cox analysis, high versus low fusion risk had HR 2.05 (95% CI, 1.36–3.08; P = <0.001; C-index, 0.577), and continuous fusion probability had HR 5.04 (95% CI, 2.53–10.02; P = <0.001; C-index, 0.637) (Table 7).

Capsule-Stratified Prognostic Analysis

Capsule-stratified survival analysis was performed in the pooled external-validation cohort using the development-derived fusion-risk cutoff of 0.340 (Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary Figure 2). The non-complete-capsule subgroup included 157 patients and 94 composite RFS/PFS events. Within this subgroup, high fusion risk was associated with poorer survival compared with low fusion risk (HR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.21–3.03; Cox P=0.005; log-rank P=0.005).

The complete-capsule subgroup included 50 patients and 23 events. High fusion risk showed a directionally consistent adverse association, although the estimate was less precise and did not reach conventional statistical significance (HR, 2.31; 95% CI, 0.94–5.68; Cox P=0.068; log-rank P=0.058). The fusion-risk-by-capsule-status interaction was not significant (interaction HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 0.44–3.28; P=0.722), providing no evidence that the prognostic association of the fusion-risk score differed materially according to capsule integrity.

Biological Plausibility and TCGA-LIHC Analyses

Biological interpretation emphasized Delta vascular trajectory and 3D-ITH/habitat contributions (Figures 6 and 7). TCGA-LIHC review did not identify a reliable MVI/vascular invasion field in the inspected cBioPortal clinical tables. Therefore, the TCGA-LIHC analysis was restricted to evaluating the associations of two previously validated hypoxia scores - BUFFA_HYPOXIA_SCORE and WINTER_HYPOXIA_SCORE - with OS, PFS, and DFS. Both hypoxia scores were significantly associated with OS, PFS, and DFS time (Table 8). Figure 8 summarizes the biological pathways potentially relevant to the proposed imaging framework. Discussion

This multicenter analysis shows that dynamic vascular spatiotemporal heterogeneity on routine multiphase CT can support preoperative prediction of MVI in HCC. Under a locked blinded multireviewer pathology standard and hospital-level external validation, the constrained Transformer fusion model achieved high discrimination in both external cohorts and in pooled external validation. The result is clinically relevant because the same fusion score also stratified RFS/PFS risk in the external validation cohort.

The central biological signal is Delta vascular trajectory. HCC vascular invasion is coupled to angiogenesis, perfusion redistribution, hypoxia, stromal remodeling, and tumor-parenchyma interface changes. Phase-to-phase CT enhancement captures this biology more directly than static single-phase summaries. The temporal ablation analysis further clarified the source of the Delta vascular signal. No isolated phase interval reproduced the discrimination of the complete trajectory representation, and the complete model significantly outperformed several single-interval and direct-pairwise alternatives. These findings support modeling contrast enhancement as a multiphase trajectory rather than relying on one arbitrarily selected phase subtraction. Such a trajectory-based representation may more comprehensively reflect the sequential processes of arterial enhancement, portal redistribution, washout, and delayed retention associated with tumor angiogenesis and microvascular invasion. However, the derived trajectory indices alone performed similarly to the complete feature set. This result suggests that integrated summaries of wash-in, washout, delayed retention, curve shape, and temporal volatility captured much of the relevant vascular information. Therefore, the ablation findings support the biological and computational rationale of trajectory-aware modeling but do not establish that every raw pairwise Delta term in the complete representation is indispensable. Future prospective studies should determine whether a more parsimonious trajectory-index panel can preserve predictive performance while improving model simplicity, stability, and interinstitutional transportability.

The 3D-ITH/habitat features add spatial organization of heterogeneity, while ViT features provide complementary high-dimensional texture and shape representations.12,41,42 The Transformer should therefore be interpreted as a constrained integration tool, not as an isolated black-box claim.

Several findings strengthen the analysis. First, the gold standard was updated to a locked multireviewer label rather than the original manifest label. Second, preprocessing, principal component analysis (PCA), feature selection, model tuning, and thresholding were fitted only in the development cohort. Third, the model was evaluated separately in two external hospitals and in a pooled external cohort. Fourth, DeLong, NRI/IDI, calibration, DCA, label sensitivity, and RFS/PFS analyses were reported rather than relying on AUC alone. Capsule-stratified analysis further suggested that the prognostic information conveyed by the Transformer fusion score was not confined to a single capsule phenotype. High fusion risk was significantly associated with poorer composite RFS/PFS in patients with a non-complete capsule and showed a directionally consistent adverse association in patients with a complete capsule. Although the latter comparison did not reach conventional statistical significance, its point estimate was not attenuated; rather, its confidence interval was wide because the complete-capsule subgroup contained only 50 patients and 23 events.

The nonsignificant fusion-risk-by-capsule-status interaction provided no evidence that the prognostic association differed materially between capsule strata. These findings suggest that the fusion score may provide prognostic information complementary to the conventional assessment of capsule integrity. Nevertheless, the subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because the analysis was post hoc and the complete-capsule subgroup had limited statistical power. Larger prospective cohorts are needed to confirm the stability of the prognostic association across capsule phenotypes.

Capsular lymphatic remodeling provides a further pathological context for this complementary value. Li et al32 reported that, among 212 patients with BCLC stage 0–A HCC, high D2-40-positive LVD in the tumor capsule was independently associated with shorter recurrence-free survival (adjusted HR, 2.506; 95% CI, 1.197–5.243) and was also independently associated with MVI and satellite nodules. This evidence supports a clinically plausible two-stage assessment pathway. Before surgery, the present multiphase CT fusion score could noninvasively identify patients with vascularly heterogeneous tumors and a higher probability of MVI or adverse RFS/PFS, thereby supporting surgical planning and early risk communication. After resection, standardized pathological assessment of the tumor capsule—including capsular integrity and D2-40-based LVD—could provide direct information on the invasive microenvironment and refine the postoperative risk estimate. Such integration may be especially useful when preoperative imaging and routine pathological indicators are discordant. However, the imaging score should be considered complementary to, rather than a replacement for, postoperative histopathology, and the incremental clinical value of adding capsular LVD requires prospective validation.

The high external AUC should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. Model performance varied substantially according to the reference-label definition, highlighting the importance of the locked blinded multireviewer standard. The old-probability-versus-new-label analysis was included only as a sensitivity audit and should not be regarded as independent confirmatory validation. Although discrimination remained high in the external cohorts, the calibration intercepts and slopes indicated residual miscalibration, particularly in external validation cohort 1. Accordingly, the predicted probabilities should not yet be interpreted as directly calibrated individual risks, and prospective validation with prespecified recalibration procedures may be required before clinical implementation.

For the TCGA-LIHC biological plausibility analysis, two previously validated hypoxia scores, BUFFA_HYPOXIA_SCORE and WINTER_HYPOXIA_SCORE, were evaluated for their associations with OS, PFS, and DFS. The mechanistic rationale linking these signatures to our imaging framework is as follows: high Delta vascular trajectory features (eg, steep wash-in, early washout, and enhancement volatility) may reflect aberrant angiogenesis and arteriovenous shunting, processes related to HIF-mediated VEGF upregulation. High 3D-ITH/habitat features capture spatial heterogeneity of enhancement, which may correspond to regional differences in hypoxia, necrosis, and stromal remodeling within the tumor.39 Both pathways are biologically relevant to MVI pathogenesis, as hypoxic conditions may promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition, extracellular matrix degradation, and intravasation of tumor cells into the microvasculature. Both hypoxia scores showed statistically significant associations with OS, PFS, and DFS time, providing contextual biological support for the prognostic relevance of hypoxia-related tumor states.43 However, the imaging cohort was not linked to TCGA-LIHC at the patient level, and no reliable MVI/vascular invasion field was identified in the inspected cBioPortal clinical tables. Therefore, these findings do not establish a direct association between the imaging features identified in the present cohort and tumor hypoxia or MVI.

Given that Delta vascular trajectory features are derived from phase-to-phase enhancement changes, their robustness against inter-site variations in contrast injection protocols and scan timing warrants specific consideration. However, several design features mitigate this concern. First, Delta features were computed as intra-patient relative changes rather than absolute attenuation values, which reduces inter-scanner and inter-phase calibration dependencies. Second, PCA was applied to the Delta feature set, which tends to capture consistent covariance patterns across patients rather than protocol-specific noise. Third, the strong and consistent external validation performance across two independent hospitals with different CT platforms and protocol nuances (external validation 1 AUC = 0.957; external validation 2 AUC = 0.934) provides empirical evidence that the Delta-driven signal generalizes despite residual scanner heterogeneity.44–46 Nevertheless, prospective harmonization efforts—such as standardized phantom-based calibration, contrast timing normalization, or the use of multi-center Delta feature reference distributions—would be valuable to further strengthen the clinical translatability of this framework in real-world settings with greater protocol diversity.

The study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective multicenter study, and prospective validation with predefined thresholds is required before clinical implementation. Second, although the constrained Transformer architecture was designed to improve multimodal feature integration and reduce overfitting risk, its decision-making process is not fully mechanistically transparent. Third, the absence of patient-level linkage between the imaging cohort and transcriptomic data precluded direct imaging–hypoxia and imaging–MVI association analyses. Fourth, the available follow-up data contained only one Time/status pair and did not provide separately adjudicated recurrence and progression dates. Accordingly, the endpoint was analyzed as a composite RFS/PFS outcome, and independent RFS and PFS analyses could not be performed. The capsule-stratified survival analysis was post hoc, and the complete-capsule subgroup contained only 50 patients and 23 events, resulting in limited statistical precision. Therefore, the subgroup findings require confirmation in larger prospective cohorts with separately defined recurrence and progression endpoints.

Fifth, standardized pathological quantification of capsular LVD was not available in the current multicenter dataset. Consequently, we could not determine whether the Transformer fusion score correlates with D2-40-positive capsular lymphangiogenesis, whether LVD explains part of the imaging-derived prognostic signal, or whether a combined imaging–pathology model provides incremental value beyond either component alone. The proposed integration should therefore be regarded as a biologically informed future direction rather than a validated application of the present model.

Future studies should focus on prospective multicenter validation, automated segmentation and scanner harmonization, assessment of interobserver robustness, and integration of Delta/ITH phenotypes with spatial pathology or molecular assays. In particular, incorporating automated or semi-automated tumor segmentation into a standardized multiphase CT pipeline could substantially reduce the time and interobserver variability associated with manual delineation and streamline the extraction of Delta vascular trajectory and 3D-ITH/habitat features in busy clinical workflows. A clinically deployable system could integrate multiphase image registration, automated lesion segmentation, segmentation-quality control, and feature calculation into a single workflow, while allowing radiologists to review and correct the generated contours when necessary. Before clinical implementation, such tools would require rigorous external validation of segmentation accuracy, cross-phase consistency, processing time, failure rates, and the stability of downstream Delta and 3D-ITH features across scanners and institutions. They should also evaluate whether postoperative capsular lymphatic vessel density provides incremental prognostic information beyond the locked preoperative imaging model. Such studies should use prespecified tumor-capsule sampling, standardized D2-40-based lymphatic vessel counting, blinded pathological assessment, and a locked imaging model and decision threshold. Comparisons of imaging-only, pathology-only, and integrated models would clarify whether combining preoperative imaging with postoperative pathological characterization improves prognostic stratification, calibration, and clinical net benefit.

Conclusion

Using the locked multireviewer MVI labels, a dynamic vascular heterogeneity-centered framework integrating Delta vascular trajectory, 3D-ITH/habitat, and ViT features demonstrated high discrimination across two hospital-level external validation cohorts for preoperative HCC-MVI prediction. In pooled external validation, Transformer fusion achieved an AUC of 0.941 and a high NPV, while the fusion score also showed exploratory stratification of the available composite RFS/PFS endpoint. Prospective validation using prespecified pathology criteria, independent model locking, and assessment of calibration and clinical utility is required before clinical implementation.
