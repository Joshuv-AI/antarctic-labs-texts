Microtubule posttranslational modifications provide unique recognition patterns for associated proteins
Authors: Arya Krishnan, Eva Desvigne-Hansch, Veronique Henriot, Alica Dodoková, Sinda Khanfir, Mariya Genova, Lenka Grycova, Maria M Magiera, Marcus Braun, Zdenek Lansky, Carsten Janke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrotubules are key components of the eukaryotic cytoskeleton involved in vital functions in virtually every cell. Among the emerging molecular mechanisms to adapt microtubules to their diverse functions is the biochemical diversification of tubulin molecules by posttranslational modifications (PTMs) and differential gene expression, a concept known as the ‘tubulin code’. A key question remains whether the tubulin code has the potential to selectively control microtubule interactions of different microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs) to act as a specific signalling system. To answer this question, we used a medium-throughput in vitro approach to screen 46 proteins for their binding preferences to microtubules with altered PTM or isotype composition. We demonstrate that subsets of these MAPs have unique sensitivities to PTMs, while other proteins are not affected. As a result, PTMs, or combinations of them, differentially attract or repulse individual MAPs to microtubules. Our findings offer mechanistic proof for a key hypothesis of the tubulin code—the capacity to selectively and differentially regulate MAP–microtubule interactions.Subject terms: Cell Adhesion, Polarity & Cytoskeleton; Post-translational Modifications & Proteolysis

Introduction

Microtubules play essential roles in every eukaryotic cell. How these functions are coordinated between different microtubule populations in the cell, or how they are fine-tuned to adapt to changing physiological requirements, are fascinating questions that have, until today, not been fully answered. The discovery of posttranslational modifications (PTMs) on tubulin led already in the 1970s to the hypothesis that biochemical diversification of tubulin might influence microtubule functions (Fulton and Simpson, 1976). However, solid proof of this concept, later baptised as the ‘tubulin code’ (Verhey and Gaertig, 2007), was difficult to obtain.

The tubulin code hypothesis stipulates that specific signatures on tubulin molecules, either encoded in different tubulin genes that give rise to α- and β-tubulin isotypes, or posttranslationally generated by tubulin PTMs (Janke and Magiera, 2020; McKenna et al, 2023), provide unique signals that can be sensed and interpreted in a specific manner by ‘readers’—i.e. microtubule-interacting proteins. Indeed, studies in cells found effects of tubulin PTMs or isotypes on the distribution of several microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs; e.g. Arru et al, 2021; Backer et al, 2012; Mohan et al, 2019; Peris et al, 2006), on cargo transport in neurons (Bodakuntla et al, 2020; Gilmore-Hall et al, 2019; Konietzny et al, 2024; Konishi and Setou, 2009; Magiera et al, 2018), or on ciliary and flagellar beating (Gadadhar et al, 2021; Ikegami et al, 2010; Pathak et al, 2011; Suryavanshi et al, 2010). This provided exciting insights into the potential of the tubulin code to regulate the function of the microtubule cytoskeleton within cells, which was further bolstered by observations of subsets of microtubules with unique PTMs in different cell types (Katrukha et al, 2021; Mohan et al, 2019; Orbach and Howard, 2019).

Direct in vitro measurements have demonstrated that tubulin PTMs can directly control microtubule-interacting proteins. For example, the activity of the microtubule-severing enzymes spastin and katanin is regulated by different types (Genova et al, 2023; Lacroix et al, 2010; Szczesna et al, 2022) or degrees (Valenstein and Roll-Mecak, 2016) of the PTM polyglutamylation. Molecular motors from the kinesin family display selective sensitivities to the type and degree of polyglutamylation on the microtubules they use as tracks (Genova et al, 2023; Lessard et al, 2019; Sirajuddin et al, 2014). Cytoplasmic dynein, on the other hand, requires tyrosinated α-tubulin for initial binding to microtubules, but can then walk with similar efficiency on microtubules with different PTM decoration (McKenney et al, 2016). Among the large family of MAPs, the Alzheimer-related MAP Tau, as well as centriole and spindle-associated protein (CSAP), were shown to bind more strongly to microtubules if they are polyglutamylated (Backer et al, 2012; Genova et al, 2023), while CLIP170 requires tyrosinated α-tubulin to track the plus-ends of microtubules (Bieling et al, 2008; Chen et al, 2021). Within the echinoderm microtubule-associated protein-like (EML) family, it was shown that only a short isoform of EML2 is sensitive to tubulin tyrosination, while other family members could not sense this PTM (Hotta et al, 2022).

While these striking examples have provided evidence that tubulin PTMs can control events at the cellular and molecular levels, the language of the tubulin code has so far not been deciphered. To do so requires demonstrating that individual tubulin PTMs, or combinations of them, are selectively and differentially read by effectors that interact with microtubules, while others are not affected.

Here, we put in place an approach to quantify how a representative panel of tubulin PTMs affects the interactions of a broad selection of MAPs with microtubules in vitro under directly comparable experimental conditions. We tested 46 arbitrarily chosen proteins described as structural MAPs - proteins that bind microtubules without enzymatic or motile function (Bodakuntla et al, 2019) and measured their interactions with microtubules purified from native sources that selectively lack tubulin PTMs (Genova et al, 2023). We found that about half of the tested proteins that showed robust enough binding in our assay are sensitive to tubulin composition. We then refined our analyses to single PTMs, which allowed us to determine a precise ‘PTM signature’ for every single MAP. Our work provides evidence for the existence of a mechanistic framework for the tubulin code: that different tubulin PTMs, alone or in combination, create signals that are differentially recognised, or ‘read’, by distinct microtubule interactors.

Results

Setting up a pipeline for medium-throughput analyses

Microtubules interact with a wide variety of proteins, and additional MAPs are discovered regularly. Despite this wealth of information, only a few MAPs have been characterised in detail (Bodakuntla et al, 2019). The restriction to a few selected MAPs is related to scientific interest, but all too often also to technical limitations. For instance, in vitro reconstitution assays are typically performed with purified proteins, and purification of proteins can require cumbersome and time-consuming optimisation, making it impossible to screen large numbers of proteins. To be able to broaden in vitro analyses to a wider collection of proteins, we used lysates of mammalian cells overexpressing the proteins of interest tagged with a fluorescent protein. We selected 46 proteins (Appendix Table S1) from the literature which are either bona fide microtubule binders with strong experimental evidence, or have been proposed to be MAPs (Bodakuntla et al, 2019). Most of the MAPs tested here have previously been confirmed as microtubule binders (Jijumon et al, 2022). We expressed GFP-labelled constructs of these MAPs (Appendix Table S1) in HEK-293 cells and prepared cytosolic lysates.

The second component of our assay are microtubules with well-defined tubulin PTMs. All tubulins used in this study are purified from mouse brain tissue or HeLa cells through repeated cycles of polymerisation and depolymerisation (Souphron et al, 2019), a method that has been a standard in the field for decades (Vallee, 1986). Tubulin from both native sources is a mixture of tubulin isotypes. However, a unique isotype that is present in brain tissue but not in wild-type HeLa cells is β3-tubulin (TubB3; Denoulet et al, 1986). The primary amino acid sequence of β3-tubulin shows some unique differences from other β-tubulin isotypes. Incorporation of β3-tubulin into microtubules can change their properties (Ti et al, 2018) as well as their interactions with other proteins (Sirajuddin et al, 2014). Another difference between brain tubulin and HeLa tubulin is the strong enrichment of the well-characterised PTMs in brain tubulin, whereas none of them is present on HeLa tubulin (Barisic et al, 2015). To determine the role of single tubulin variants, we used the following approaches: For polyglutamylation, we purified tubulin from mouse brains of double-knockout mice lacking Ttll1 and Ttll7, the two major polyglutamylases in the brain (Bodakuntla et al, 2021; Ikegami et al, 2006; Janke et al, 2005). To study the role of acetylation and β3-tubulin, we purified tubulin from Atat1-KO brains that does not contain acetylation (Kalebic et al, 2013), and from mice lacking the β3-tubulin (TubB3) isotype (Latremoliere et al, 2018), respectively. To study the role of detyrosination, brain tubulin, which is a mixture of tyrosinated, detyrosinated, and ∆2-tubulin (Paturle-Lafanechere et al, 1991), is rather cumbersome to separate into these different PTM variants (Giraudel et al, 1998; Lafanechere and Job, 2011). We thus used HeLa tubulin, which is naturally fully tyrosinated, and converted it to detyrosinated tubulin by treatment with carboxypeptidase A (Raybin and Flavin, 1977), and established a method known to entirely remove tyrosine from α-tubulin.

SDS-PAGE analysis showed high purity of the tubulin samples, and no visible differences between the different tubulin variants (Fig. 1A). Immunoblot analyses (Fig. 1B) confirmed that tubulin from Ttll1−/−Ttll7−/− brain lacked polyE signals, confirming the absence of α- and β-tubulin polyglutamylation. The absence of polyglutamylation was further confirmed by quantitative top-down proteomics analysis of the samples in another study, showing a drop of average polyglutamylation from ~1.5 glutamates in wild-type α-tubulin to ~0.2 in Ttll1−/−Ttll7−/− tubulin, and from ~1.7 on β-tubulin to ~0.01 in Ttll1−/−Ttll7−/− tubulin (Gant et al, 2026). Atat1−/− tubulin specifically lacked acetylation, and no β3-tubulin was detected in tubulin purified from Tubb3−/− mice. We further confirmed that tubulin purified from HeLa cells does not contain any of the above-tested PTMs, as well as no β3-tubulin. After treatment with carboxypeptidase A, tyrosination of HeLa tubulin is entirely replaced by detyrosination (Fig. 1B,C). The established specificity of the antibodies for tyrosinated and detyrosinated tubulin makes this result unambiguous.

This panel of tubulins enabled us to systematically test how MAPs interact with microtubules with distinct PTM or isotype patterns. Microtubule-binding assays were set up with microtubules either polymerised in the presence of GTP and then stabilised with Taxol (Schiff et al, 1979), or directly grown and stabilised with the GTP analogue GMPCPP (Hyman et al, 1992). For each experiment, microtubules of two different PTM or isotype compositions were sequentially flown into a microscopy chamber, where they were anchored by tubulin antibodies on passivised glass coverslips. To differentiate between the two microtubule species, interference reflection microscopy (IRM; Mahamdeh et al, 2018) snapshots were taken at each step (Fig. 2A,B). We then introduced the diluted cell lysates containing the overexpressed MAP-GFP and quantified the fluorescence intensities on the two different types of microtubules with total internal reflection (TIRF) microscopy (Fig. 2A,B). Given that several tested MAPs displayed the same binding preferences over a range of concentrations spanning an order of magnitude (Appendix Fig. S1), all MAP-GFP lysates were diluted such that the MAP-GFP binding to the microtubule was not saturated (Fig. 2C).

Determination of the binding preferences of MAPs for differentially modified microtubules

To determine which MAPs can sense differences in tubulin composition, we performed an initial screen in which we compared the binding of all GFP-MAPs with brain versus HeLa microtubules. Overall, of the 46 proteins we tested here (Appendix Table S1; Fig. 2A), 35 showed a clear binding to microtubules in our assays. Each protein was tested in three sets of experiments (Appendix Figs. S2–S24), each performed with independently purified tubulins and cell lysates prepared separately in two independent batches (representative examples of the assay are shown in Fig. 2B,C). The mean values determined in the three experiments were averaged, and fold changes were determined. Here we considered fold changes ≥1.2 or ≤0.8 as differential binding, thus 17 out of 35, i.e. about 50% of the tested proteins, showed clear differential binding (Fig. 2D; Appendix Figs. S2–4, S10, S17 and S18 —no differential binding; Appendix Figs. S5–S9, S11–S16 and S19–S24 differential binding). Of those, 10 preferentially bound to brain microtubules and 7 showed preferences to HeLa microtubules (Fig. 2D). This result suggested that the different PTM and isotype signatures of these two fundamentally different types of microtubules can selectively attract or repel certain MAPs, while other MAPs are insensitive to these signatures.

To determine which tubulin PTM or isotype is responsible for the differential binding between brain and HeLa microtubules of the 17 MAPs (Fig. 2D), we compared the binding of each of them between microtubule subtypes differing in only one PTM or tubulin isotype. We also included 3 MAPs that did not show differential binding in the initial screen (EML4, Appendix Fig. S10; MAP6, Appendix Fig. S17; MAP7, Appendix Fig. S18). Strikingly, we found that the mechanisms underlying the observed preferences to either brain or HeLa tubulin are highly divergent for different MAPs. The assays for each MAP were performed with independently purified tubulin batches and cell lysates prepared separately in three independent batches. The data for each set of experiments are shown separately, and the means of these subsets are then directly compared. For all MAPs tested here, we found a high degree of reproducibility among the single sets of experiments (Appendix Figs. S5–S24; MAP1B shown as a representative example in Fig. 3A–C).

Proteins such as EML1 (Appendix Fig. S7), SNPH_A (Appendix Fig. S21), and TRIM46 (Appendix Fig. S24) are predominantly, if not exclusively, affected by the state of tyrosination of microtubules, as they only show differences in binding between HeLa and detyr-HeLa microtubules (Fig. 3D), but for none of the other microtubule variants. This finding straight-forwardly explains their preferential association with HeLa over brain microtubules in the initial screen, given that brain tubulin contains less than 25% of tyrosinated tubulin (Barra et al, 1980; Paturle et al, 1989; Raybin and Flavin, 1977), while HeLa tubulin is almost fully tyrosinated (Fig. 1B). A selective sensitivity for a single PTM was also observed for MAP1A, which showed stronger binding to polyglutamylated versus non-glutamylated microtubules, but no discernible sensitivity to the tyrosination status (Fig. 3D; Appendix Fig. S12). All other MAPs showed sensitivity to two of the tested PTMs, predominantly tyrosination and polyglutamylation. By contrast, neither the absence of tubulin acetylation nor of β3-tubulin had pronounced effects on the microtubule interactions of most MAPs investigated here (Fig. 3D).

When tested for single PTM preferences, EML2 (Appendix Fig. S8) and EML3 (Appendix Fig. S9) bound preferentially to tyrosinated over detyrosinated HeLa, and to low-glutamylated over polyglutamylated brain microtubules, which makes their preference for HeLa over brain microtubules obvious: HeLa microtubules are tyrosinated and non-glutamylated, while brain microtubules have low tyrosination levels and are enriched in polyglutamylation. This, however, was rather unique, as other MAPs showed preferential binding to both tyrosinated and polyglutamylated microtubules (Fig. 3D: CSAP, MACF1(C1023), MAP1B, MAP2, MAP4 isoforms, MAP7D1, MTCL1, SNPH_C, Tau(0N-3R)). As polyglutamylation is present predominantly in brain tubulin, and tyrosination is more abundant in HeLa tubulin, the overall impact of a single PTM on the binding preferences in this group of MAPs was therefore not inferable from the initial screen. Similarly, knowing binding preferences of MAPs for a specific PTM (for example, CSAP, MACF1(C1023), MAP7D1 and Tau(0N-3R), each showing an about 2-fold reduced binding to detyrosinated HeLa microtubules and an about 2-fold increased binding to polyglutamylated brain microtubules), was not sufficient to predict the overall effect on the binding of these MAPs to native microtubules from different sources: While CSAP bound preferentially to HeLa over brain microtubules, MACF1(C1023), MAP7D1 and Tau(0N-3R) showed a strong preference for brain microtubules (Appendix Figs. S6, S11, S19 and S23). Thus, we find that combinatorial effects of concurrent PTMs are not entirely predictable and instead depend on each MAP individually, which underpins the importance of direct tests.

Our results thus provide experimental evidence for the basic assumption of the tubulin code—that combinations of tubulin PTMs create unique ‘codes’ on microtubules, which can be specifically and selectively understood by ‘readers’ (i.e. MAPs). That polyglutamylation and detyrosination/tyrosination dominate in the regulation of microtubule-MAP interactions is not surprising in light of their position on the microtubule lattice: both PTMs take place at the carboxy-terminal tails of tubulin, which protrude from the outer microtubule surface where most MAP interactions take place. Moreover, the use of stabilised microtubules in our assays might preclude the possibility that MAPs can reach into the microtubule lumen where acetylation is taking place, which, however, could happen in dynamic microtubules (Nihongaki et al, 2021). Our observations thus do not exclude the possibility that acetylation or the presence of different tubulin isotypes could also affect MAP–microtubule interactions.

Changing the readout of the tubulin code by alternative splicing

Several of the here-tested MAPs exist in different splice variants. The well-characterised MAPs Tau, MAP2 and MAP4 are spliced in their microtubule-binding regions (Chapin et al, 1995; Doll et al, 1993; Jakes et al, 1991). In the current work, Tau(0N-3R) showed similar binding preferences as Tau(2N-4R) in our previous study (Genova et al, 2023), and the two MAP4 isoforms X16 and X20 also showed similar binding preferences to the different PTM variants (Appendix Figs. S15 and S16).

Syntaphilin (SNPH), by contrast, is alternatively spliced in a so-far little-characterised domain. The two splice isoforms of syntaphilin we tested here differ in an amino-terminally located stretch of 69 amino acids present in SNPH_A (amino acids 17–85) and spliced out in SNPH_C (Fig. 4A). Comparing the two isoforms, we first observed opposite preferences for HeLa and brain tubulin: SNPH_A bound stronger to HeLa, while SNPH_C preferred brain microtubules (Fig. 4B; Appendix Figs. S21 and S22). When we verified the impact of single tubulin PTMs, we found that both isoforms bound preferentially to tyrosinated over detyrosinated tubulin (Fig. 4C; Appendix Figs. S21 and S22), but showed a striking difference in their sensitivity to polyglutamylation: SNPH_A was insensitive to this PTM, whereas SNPH_C bound much more efficiently to polyglutamylated microtubules (Fig. 4D; Appendix Figs. S21 and S22). It thus appears that with a single splicing event, the preference of SNPH_A for non-modified HeLa microtubules is switched to a preference for highly modified brain microtubules by generating a previously non-existent sensitivity for polyglutamylation in SNPH_C.

But how is this preference for polyglutamylation generated? As the splicing event removes a part of the amino acid sequence, the only perceivable possibility is that a new sequence motif is generated from the two sequences flanking the spliced-out exon. Indeed, the alternatively spliced region is flanked by two positively charged sequence motifs, which after splicing are joined in the SNPH_C isoform, generating a unique sequence motif which cannot be found in any other mammalian protein. To test whether the formation of this positively charged motif is sufficient to generate a sensitivity to polyglutamylation, we created the same sequence motif within the SNPH_A isoform by retaining the alternatively spliced exon (Fig. 4A). Strikingly, the chimeric SNPH_AC protein behaved like SNPH_C in our in vitro binding assays: it preferentially binds to brain over HeLa microtubules, and it shows a preference to polyglutamylated microtubules with a similar fold change as SNPH_C (Fig. 4B,C,D; Appendix Fig. S25). This demonstrates that the inclusion of the alternatively spliced exon, possibly arisen evolutionary in an event of exonisation (Keren et al, 2010; Schmitz and Brosius, 2011), disrupts a functional protein domain able to sense microtubule polyglutamylation. The example of syntaphilin demonstrates that the regulation of splicing events can serve as a switch that changes the behaviour of MAPs in cells, for instance, by guiding them to different types of microtubules that are differentially modified following physiological reprogramming of the cytoskeleton.

Differential PTM reading within the highly conserved EML protein family

Echinoderm microtubule-associated proteins (EMAPs) are a family of MAPs of which the first member was identified in sea urchin eggs as a protein associated with the mitotic spindle (Suprenant et al, 1993; Suprenant and Marsh, 1987). EMAP has six homologues in mammals, EMAP-like (EML)1-6, out of which four, EML1-4, are highly similar in terms of domain organisation and primary peptide sequences (Fig. 5A). EML1-4 bind microtubules in cells and in in vitro reconstitutions (Eichenmüller et al, 2002; Hotta et al, 2022; Jijumon et al, 2022; Pollmann et al, 2006; Richards et al, 2015).

Despite their high level of sequence conservation and similar domain structures (Figs. 5A and EV1), EML1-4 showed different binding preferences in our initial screen: EML1, EML2, and EML3, but not EML4 preferentially bound to HeLa over brain microtubules (Fig. 5B). In the subsequent analyses, we found even more differences: EML1 was sensitive to tyrosination level, but not to any other PTM (Fig. 5C,D; Appendix Fig. S7), while EML2 (Fig. 5C,D; Appendix Fig. S8) and EML3 (Fig. 5C,D; Appendix Fig. S9) preferentially bound to tyrosinated as well as non-glutamylated microtubules. EML4, by contrast, did not show preferential binding to any PTM or isotype variants tested here (Fig. 5C,D; Appendix Fig. S10). To confirm that those binding preferences are intrinsic features of the EML proteins and not related to other proteins present in the cell lysates, we repeated all experiments with proteins purified from insect cells, which confirmed the initial results (Fig. EV2; Appendix Figs. PTM specificity might be directly encoded in the microtubule-binding domains. Previous work had defined the amino-terminal part of EMLs as microtubule-binding domains (Adib et al, 2019; Hotta et al, 2022; Richards et al, 2015; Figs. 5A and EV1). Indeed, versions of EMLs lacking these domains did not bind microtubules in our experiments. We thus generated GFP-tagged constructs of the amino-terminal presumed microtubule-binding and trimerisation domains of EML1-4 following the domain assignment from previous work (Adib et al, 2019) and assessed their binding to HeLa and brain microtubules using cell lysates. Unexpectedly, these domains alone did not show preferences for the two microtubule variants, with the EML3 construct not showing any detectable microtubule binding (Fig. EV3). It thus appears that the amino-terminal domains alone are not responsible for the microtubule binding of EMLs; instead, they might be necessary but not sufficient parts of larger microtubule-binding modules. This would predict that the PTM preferences of EMLs are encoded by an entire module, composed of sequences localised in both, amino- and carboxy-terminal domains.

To test this hypothesis, we generated chimeric proteins swapping the amino- and carboxy-terminal domains of EML1 and EML4 (Figs. 5A, EV4 and EV5). Both chimeric proteins bound to microtubules, indicating that the amino- and carboxy-termini of EMLs are functionally redundant and exchangeable. Strikingly, both chimaeras showed a preference for tyrosinated microtubules; their binding was reduced by about 50% to detyrosinated as compared to tyrosinated microtubules (Figs. EV4 and EV5). This, however, was less pronounced to what was observed for wild-type EML1, which almost entirely loses its capacity to bind microtubules if they are detyrosinated (Fig. 5C; Appendix Fig. S7). Deletion of a 112-amino-acid sequence that is uniquely found at the carboxy-terminus of EML4, but not present on EML1, did not change this result (Fig. EV6). Together, these results suggest that both the amino- and carboxy-terminal domains participate not only in microtubule binding of EML proteins, but also in their reading of the microtubule PTM status, while the unique carboxy-terminal domain found on EML4 has little impact on PTM preferences. Our results reveal that more complex, multi-domain recognition motives than the previously identified CAP-Gly domain (Bieling et al, 2008; Peris et al, 2006) can be involved in the recognition of tubulin tyrosination.

Control of EML–microtubule interactions by tyrosination in cells

To confirm that the binding preferences we determined in our in vitro settings are relevant for MAP–microtubule interactions in living cells, we chose to investigate EML proteins because of the differential and strong impact of detyrosination on the binding of some but not all EMLs to microtubules (Fig. 5; Appendix Figs. S7–10). To abolish tyrosination, which is ubiquitously present on microtubules in the MDCK cell line, we overexpressed the tubulin detyrosinating enzyme Svbp-Vash2 (Aillaud et al, 2017; Nieuwenhuis et al, 2017), which, as previously shown (Kesarwani et al, 2020), is an effective tool to remove tyrosination from all microtubules in cells. Indeed, overexpression of Svbp-Vash2, but not of the enzymatically dead mutant, entirely detyrosinated all microtubules (Fig. EV7).

Next, we expressed EML(1-4)-GFP alone, co-expressed with Svbp-Vash2-mCherry, or with the enzymatically dead mutant of this enzyme as a control. While in the control condition and in the presence of an enzymatically dead enzyme, where microtubules are tyrosinated (Fig. EV7), all four EMLs showed strong co-localisation with microtubules. EML1 is entirely delocalised from microtubules when co-expressed with Svbp-Vash2-mCherry (Fig. 6A), and EML2 as well as EML3 binding is strongly reduced (Fig. 6B,C). By contrast, binding of EML4 is not affected by the expression of Svbp-Vash2-mCherry, confirming the insensitivity of this protein to detyrosination that was found in vitro (Fig. 5B,C) in cells (Fig. 6D). These experiments demonstrate that even under the condition of overexpression, when MAPs are present at saturating concentrations and tend to strongly bind and bundle microtubules (Jijumon et al, 2022), changes in tubulin PTM levels have the capacity to completely impede the binding of certain MAPs to microtubules in living cells (Fig. 6). The experiments in cells validate our in vitro observations and confirm the presence of selective regulatory mechanisms of EML proteins in response to different tubulin PTMs.

Discussion

The key question we addressed in our study is whether the signals generated by the tubulin code can be specifically and, most importantly, differentially ‘read’ by a wide variety of microtubule interactors. To answer this question, we screened binding preferences of a comprehensive selection of 46 mammalian proteins that are known as, or were suggested to be, MAPs. A key requirement for our work was the possibility to screen many proteins under similar conditions. Purification of proteins is a limiting step for many in vitro reconstitution experiments and would have strongly limited the number of MAPs to be screened due to time limitations and technical challenges. We thus chose a cell lysate-based approach adapted from our previous work (Jijumon et al, 2022). Importantly, in contrast to our previous and other studies (Alfaro-Aco and Petry, 2015; Field et al, 2017; Schimert et al, 2019; Soppina et al, 2014), we highly diluted the cell lysates in buffer (Fig. 2A,B), which minimises the effect of cytosolic factors on the interactions we studied. This observation validates the use of cell lysates as a robust and feasible approach for medium-throughput in vitro experiments. However, even diluted, a potential difference in using cell lysates versus purified proteins is that residual cytosolic components could affect MAP–microtubule interactions. Here we tested this possibility by repeating an entire set of experiments with four purified proteins: EML1-4, which demonstrated that the PTM preferences initially identified in cell lysates (Fig. 5) were fully reproduced with the purified proteins (Fig. EV2). Similarly, we reproduced data obtained with purified Tau previously (Genova et al, 2023) in cell lysates. Nonetheless, we cannot exclude that in some cases, cytosolic proteins or other components in the cell lysates may affect the binding of the overexpressed proteins.

By covering an unprecedentedly wide range of MAPs, our study provides direct experimental evidence for the tubulin code hypothesis. Our observations that only about half of the MAPs could sense tubulin PTMs, and that among those that could sense them, each MAP recognised different, and often unique combinations of PTMs, demonstrate that the tubulin code can differentially and selectively regulate microtubule interactions. We further show that PTM-guided MAP re-distribution functions in cells, underpinning how the tubulin code could control the functions of different microtubules side-by-side in cells, where normally not all microtubules are homogeneously modified.

An unexpected finding was that alternative splicing can switch the binding preference of a MAP. On the example of syntaphilin, we demonstrate how splicing out a single exon leads to the emergence of tubulin-PTM sensor domains that can drastically change how a protein reads the tubulin code. The preference for polyglutamylation that emerges after splicing out the exon was strong enough to overwrite the preference of this protein for tyrosinated tubulin, switching its binding preference for HeLa tubulin to brain tubulin in our experiments. It remains an intriguing question whether this splicing event originated in an evolutionary introduction of a new exon (Keren et al, 2010; Schmitz and Brosius, 2011) that led to a loss of sensitivity for polyglutamylation, or whether it represents a generic splicing event that allows the protein to adapt to changing PTM landscapes in cells. Our findings might have far-reaching implications for the function of syntaphilin as a mitochondria adaptor protein that controls the motility of these organelles (Canty et al, 2023; Chen and Sheng, 2013; Kang et al, 2008). Given the importance of mitochondria transport and re-distribution in neurons (Jenkins et al, 2024), where polyglutamylation is a prominent PTM, differential splicing of syntaphilin might play a key role in linking mitochondria homeostasis to tubulin code signalling. Indeed, it has been shown that changes in tubulin polyglutamylation levels strongly affect mitochondria motility in cultured neurons (Bodakuntla et al, 2021; Gilmore-Hall et al, 2019; Magiera et al, 2018).

Similarly unexpected was the discovery that EML proteins, despite their high degree of sequence conservation, show profound differences in their sensitivities to tubulin PTMs. These specificities could be a mechanism assuring that individual EMLs, while all being present at the same time in the same cytosol, are guided to distinct microtubule populations in cells. In the light of the emerging roles of EML proteins in cell division (Adib et al, 2019; Bizzotto et al, 2017; Chen et al, 2015; Luo et al, 2019), and of tubulin PTMs at mitotic spindles (Akera et al, 2017; Barisic et al, 2015; Zadra et al, 2022), it is tempting to hypothesise that tubulin PTMs regulate the distribution of EML proteins among distinct microtubule subpopulations during mitosis. Our observations do not entirely align with previous work that did not find differences in PTM sensitivity for EML1, 2, 3 and 4, while only a shorter isoform of EML2 preferentially interacted with tyrosinated over detyrosinated microtubules (Hotta et al, 2022). We believe that methodological differences, in particular the use of co-pelleting assays that quantify bulk binding of MAPs to microtubules, could mask PTM preferences, e.g. by strongly increasing the cooperativity of MAP–microtubule interactions in the bundles formed in these assays. This finding underpins that multiple and complementary experimental approaches are needed to draw a complete picture of a complex biological process. Given that many PTM-mediated effects we have observed in the current study had rather gradual effects on MAP–microtubule interactions, we expect that more sensitive experimental approaches will find novel tubulin code readers in the future.

While further studies are required to determine the physiological readouts of this regulation, current work at the organism level already highlights important roles in homeostasis and disease. For example, mutations in tubulin-modifying enzymes lead to a broad spectrum of pathologies, such as neurodegeneration (Magiera et al, 2018; Shashi et al, 2018), male fertility defects (Gadadhar et al, 2021; Lin et al, 2024; Vogel et al, 2010), retina degeneration (Astuti et al, 2016; Mercey et al, 2024), or heart disease (Chen et al, 2018; Le Dour et al, 2022; Robison et al, 2016). Understanding pathways that are controlled by a selective regulation of microtubule interactors via tubulin PTMs could thus provide a fresh view on disease mechanisms and might pinpoint novel molecular targets for the development of therapeutic approaches.

Mouse lines and genotyping

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the European Community (2010/63/UE) on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes. The use of these animals was approved by the ethics committee of the Institut Curie CEEA-IC #118 (authorisations no. 04395.03 and 37315-2022051117455434 v2 given by the National Authority) in compliance with the international guidelines. Both male and female mice aged between 2 and 8 months were used in this research.

Ttll1−/−/Ttll7−/− (Genova et al, 2023), Atat1−/− (Kalebic et al, 2013), and Tubb3−/− (Latremoliere et al, 2018) mice have been previously described.

For genotyping, ear fragments were collected from mice during weaning. DNA was isolated from these tissues using a lysis buffer composed of 100 mM Tris–HCl pH 8.0, 200 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, and 1% Triton X-100, supplemented with 0.1 mg/mL proteinase K. Samples were incubated at 56 °C for at least 4 h for digestion. Proteinase K was subsequently inactivated by heating the lysates at 95 °C for 10 min. The details of the PCR primers, amplification conditions, and expected product sizes for each mouse line are provided in Appendix Table S2.

Tubulin purification from murine brains

Tubulin with specific posttranslational modifications was purified from mouse brains using temperature-dependent microtubule polymerisation-depolymerisation cycles. The detailed protocol is described in (Souphron et al, 2019). After sacrificing the mouse, the skulls were opened immediately to extract the brain. Homogenised the brains using Ultra-Turrax® blender in ice-cold lysis buffer consisting of BRB80 (80 mM K-PIPES pH 6.0, 1 mM K-EGTA, 1 mM MgCl2), 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 1 mM PMSF, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail composed of 20 μg/ml leupeptin, 20 μg/ml aprotinin and 20 μg/ml 4-(2-aminoethyl)-benzene sulfonyl fluoride. For every gramme of brain tissue, 2 ml of ice-cold lysis buffer was added.

After homogenisation, lysates are centrifuged at 112,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to obtain the first supernatant (SN1). The volume of SN1 was measured, and 1/200 of its volume of 0.2 M GTP and 1/2 volume of pre-warmed 100% glycerol were added. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 30 °C for polymerisation and then centrifuged at 112,000 × g for 20 min at 30 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet (P2) was depolymerised by adding 1/10 of the SN1 volume of ice-cold BRB80. The pellet was left in this buffer for 5 min, followed by pipetting up and down for 20 min. The depolymerised microtubules were then centrifuged at 112,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. To the supernatant (SN3), pre-warmed 1 M PIPES and 100% glycerol were each added at a volume equal to that of SN3, along with 1/100 of the SN3 volume of 0.2 M GTP. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at 30 °C for polymerisation and then centrifuged at 112,000 × g for 20 min at 30 °C. For the second depolymerisation, the pellet was resuspended in 1/40 volume of the SN1 of ice-cold BRB80, and the same steps as the first depolymerisation were followed. The resulting supernatant (SN5) was collected, and 1/200 of its volume of 200 mM GTP and half its volume of pre-warmed 100% glycerol were added. This mixture was incubated for 30 min at 30 °C for polymerisation, followed by centrifugation at 112,000 × g for 20 min at 30 °C. For the final depolymerisation, 1/40 volume of the SN1 of ice-cold BRB80 was added to the pellet (P6) and incubated on ice for 5 min. The mixture was then pipetted up and down for 10 min, followed by centrifuging at 112,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The concentration of tubulin in supernatant was determined using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific; absorbance at 280 nm; MW = 110 kDa; ε = 115,000 M−¹cm−¹). The tubulin solution was diluted to 4 mg/mL, aliquoted, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Analyses of the PTMs and isotypes of brain tubulin showed the absence of the PTM or isotype for which the corresponding genes were knocked out. The levels of other PTMs were similar to their wild-type levels, except for acetylation, which was enriched in Ttll1−/−Ttll7−/− brain tubulin as shown before (Genova et al, 2023). We also detected some decrease in β3-tubulin levels in Ttll1−/−Ttll7−/− brain tubulin, as well as lower tyrosination signals in tubulin from Atat1−/− and Tubb3−/− brain, which might be related to slight variations in the loading of our gels, given that signals for two linked PTMs detyrosination and ∆2-tubulin were unchanged.

Tubulin purification from HeLa S3 cells in suspension culture

Tyrosinated and detyrosinated tubulin were purified from HeLa S3 cells in suspension culture. HeLa S3 cells were grown in spinner bottles containing 1 L of DMEM supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum, 200 mM L-glutamine, and 1× penicillin–streptomycin on a stirrer table in a cell culture incubator for 1 week. The detailed protocol for amplifying cells for growing them in suspension culture is described in (Souphron et al, 2019).

The cell suspension culture was collected in 1 L centrifuge bottles and centrifuged at 250 × g for 15 min at room temperature. The pellet was resuspended in a minimal volume of PBS and transferred into a 50-mL Falcon tube. The cells were then centrifuged again at 250 × g for 8 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the volume of cells was estimated, and an equal volume of ice-cold lysis buffer was added as described above. The cells were lysed using a French press at medium pressure. The lysate was then centrifuged at 112,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to obtain the first supernatant (SN1). The volume of SN1 was recorded, and it was transferred using a syringe and a long needle to another tube, avoiding the floating layer on the supernatant. To SN1, 1/200 of its volume of 200 mM GTP and 1/2 volume of pre-warmed 100% glycerol were added, and polymerisation was performed as previously described. The pellet (P2) was depolymerised by adding 1/60 of the SN1 volume of ice-cold BRB80. After centrifugation, the supernatant (SN3) was transferred to an Eppendorf tube for Carboxypeptidase A (CPA) treatment for detyrosination. SN3 was split into two equal samples ( + CPA and –CPA). To the portion for detyrosination, 1/300 of its volume of CPA (Sigma C9268; 5.56 U/mL) was added. Both samples were incubated at 30 °C for 5 min. The second polymerisation was conducted as previously described, followed by a second depolymerisation, to which 1/100 of the SN1 volume of ice-cold BRB80 was added, following the same steps as described above for the purification of tubulin from mouse brains. The third polymerisation was identical for both samples. For the final depolymerisation, 1/300 volume of the SN1 of ice-cold BRB80 was added to the pellet, and all subsequent steps, including dilution and storage, were performed similarly as described above.

Immunoblotting

Purified tubulin samples were mixed with Laemmli buffer (4% SDS, 90 mM DTT, 80 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.8, 10% glycerol, Bromophenol blue) and denatured for 5 min at 95 °C. In all, 60–80 ng of purified tubulin per sample was run on a special type of 10% SDS-PAGE gel that allows for the separation of α- and β-tubulin (Magiera and Janke, 2013). Proteins were transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane using the Bio-Rad Trans-Blot® Turbo system. Membranes were incubated in 5% fat-free dry milk in PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 (PBST) for 1 h at room temperature for blocking. Primary antibodies were diluted in 2.5% milk in PBST and incubated with the membrane at RT for 1.5 h. After three 5-min washes with PBST, membranes were incubated with secondary HRP-labelled antibodies diluted in PBST for 45 min at room temperature. Following three additional 5-min washes with PBST, membranes were developed by incubating with Clarity™ Western ECL Substrate for 1 min, and the chemiluminescence signal was detected using the Vilber Fusion FX imaging system. All antibodies and dilutions used are listed in Appendix Table S3.

Preparation of stabilised microtubules

For microtubule-MAP binding experiments, Taxol-stabilised microtubules were prepared following a previously described protocol (Braun et al, 2011; Nitzsche et al, 2010). The polymerisation mixture was prepared by combining 25% DMSO, 20 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM GTP in BRB80 on ice. 1.25 μl of this mixture was added to 5 μl of 4 mg/mL purified tubulin, and the sample was incubated on ice for 5 min. It was then incubated at 37 °C for 30 min for polymerisation. After polymerisation, 100 μL of BRB80T (10 μM Taxol in BRB80) was added to the microtubule mix and gently mixed. The reaction was then centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 30 min at room temperature, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 100 μL BRB80T using a cut-off pipette tip to prevent the breaking of long microtubules.

For Tau and MAP2 binding assays, microtubules were polymerised using the slowly hydrolysing GTP analogue GMPCPP (Guanosine-5′-[(α,β)-methyleno]triphosphate; Jena Bioscience NU-405S). Purified tubulin was mixed with 1 mM GMPCPP and 1 mM MgCl2 to a final concentration of 2 μM in BRB80, and incubated for 7-8 h at 37 °C. After polymerisation, the microtubules were centrifuged at 18,000 × g for 30 min at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 120 μL of BRB80T using a cut-off pipette tip.

Generation of expression vectors and preparation of lysates from mammalian cells

The preparation of cell lysates containing MAPs from HEK-293 (Human embryonic kidney; ATCC CRL-1573) cells has been described in detail in our previously published paper (Jijumon et al, 2024). We cloned the open reading frames of MAPs into the mammalian expression vector pTRIP (Gentili et al, 2015) in frame with the fluorescent protein of our choice using the Sequence and Ligation Independent Cloning (SLIC) method (Jeong et al, 2012). Most clones have been described in our previous work (Jijumon et al, 2022). For SNPH, the human splice isoforms characterised in a previous study (Caino et al, 2017) differ by the presence of an additional stretch of 44 amino acids that is absent from both murine isoforms identified here. For MAP1A, our clone amplified from murine brain cDNA differs in several codons from the available published sequence NM_032393 with 60 bp insertion at pos. 7614 and 141 bp lacking at pos. 8314.

In total, 106 HEK-293 cells were seeded on a 22-cm2 dish for transfection and grown in a 37 °C cell culture incubator. Expression plasmids encoding GFP-tagged MAPs were transfected using jetPEI (Polyplus #101000020) when the cells reached ~80% confluency. For each dish, 5 μg of DNA and 10 μl of jetPEI were mixed in serum-free DMEM containing 2 mM L-glutamine and 1× PenStrep.

After verifying GFP expression levels in HEK-293 cells using a fluorescence microscope, the cells were detached from the dish with trypsin, collected in DMEM, and centrifuged at 453 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and for each dish of cells, 20–30 µL of freshly prepared ice-cold lysis buffer (HEPES buffer (50 mM K-HEPES pH 7.3, 1 mM EGTA and 2 mM MgCl2) containing 0.05% Triton-X100 and protease inhibitors (10 μg/ml leupeptin, aprotinin and 4-(2-aminoethyl)- benzene sulfonyl fluoride)) was added. The cell pellets were resuspended on ice for 10 min, and the samples were sonicated with three short pulses using a 6.5-mm probe output control set at 1 and duty cycle set at 10% (Branson Sonifier 450, model no. B450-WP). The samples were centrifuged at 33,800 × g for 25 min at 4 °C, supernatants were collected, aliquoted into 5–10 μL samples and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C.

Purification of EML proteins

Full-length mouse EML proteins C-terminally tagged with mNeonGreen-3C-protease-cleavage-site followed by a histidine tag were expressed in SF9 insect cells using the open-source FlexiBAC baculovirus vector system (Lemaitre et al, 2019). Cells were lysed in 50 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.3, containing 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 0.1% Tween-20, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM ATP, 30 mM imidazole, Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (complete, EDTA-free, Roche), 25 units/ml benzonase. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 30,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and incubated with Ni-NTA beads at 4 °C for 1 h on a rotator. Then loaded the bead-extract mixture onto the column. The column was washed with lysis buffer first and then with 50 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.4, containing 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 0.1% Tween-20, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM ATP, and 60 mM imidazole. Then we added 3 ml of 50 mM HEPES buffer pH 7.4, containing 300 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 5% glycerol, 0.1% Tween-20, 2 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.1 mM ATP, 60 mM imidazole, and 3C protease to the column and incubated overnight for cleavage of the histidine tag. We then collected the flow through which contains the overexpressed protein of interest the next day and concentrated the protein in an Amicon centrifugal filter 50 K (Millipore) to a final volume of 500 μL. Protein was aliquoted to smaller aliquots, flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C.

Preparation of microscopy chambers and microtubule–MAP binding assay

To prepare the microscopy chambers, we need two types of glass coverslips (22 × 22 mm2 and 18 × 18 mm2, Marienfeld High Precision). We cleaned the coverslips in Piranha solution, which was prepared by mixing 30% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4) in a 1:2.5 ratio by volume. We silanised the coverslips by immersing them in a solution of 200 μL dichlorodimethylsilane (DDS) mixed in 350 mL trichloroethylene for 1 h at room temperature.

To prepare the flow chamber for microscopy, we attached two cleaned and silanised coverslips using parafilm strips, which had been heated for 20 s at 62 °C and lightly compressed, to form parallel flow chambers of about 3 mm width. Then we incubated each channel with 30 μg/ml monoclonal anti-β-tubulin antibody (Sigma-Aldrich T7816) for 10 min at room temperature to attach the microtubules to the coverslip surface. Then, we blocked the coverslip surface with 1% Pluronic-F127 in BRB80 for 45 min. We washed the channels with BRB80T (10 μM Taxol in BRB80) and introduced stabilised microtubules into the channel. For all assays, two different types of modified microtubules were added sequentially to each channel. We injected the first type of microtubules and incubated them in the channel for ~1 min, after which unbound microtubules were removed by flushing BRB80T. The microtubules were then imaged at several positions in the measurement chamber using interference reflection microscopy (IRM) using a 50:50 UVFS plate beamsplitter (ThorLabs BSW29R). The positions were saved using NIS-Elements imaging software (Nikon). We repeated the same steps for the second type of microtubules at the previously saved positions (Fig. 2B).

We equilibrated the channel with assay buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.3, 75 mM KCl, 20 mM glucose, 1 mM ATP, 10 mM DTT, 20 μg/mL casein, 220 μg/mL glucose oxidase, and 20 μg/mL catalase). We diluted MAP lysate in the assay buffer, flushed it into the channel with microtubules, and recorded MAP binding using Total Internal Reflection Fluorescence (TIRF) microscopy.

TIRF microscopy was performed using an inverted widefield microscope Nikon Eclipse Ti-E equipped with a motorised XY stage, Perfect Focus System (PFS), Nikon CFI SR Apo TIRF 100× Oil, NA 1.49, WD 0.12 mm objective and sCMOS Hamamatsu ORCA 4.0 V2 camera (Hamamatsu Photonics) or an inverted widefield Nikon Eclipse Ti-E equipped microscope equipped with motorised XY stage, PFS, Nikon Apo TIRF 60× Oil, NA 1.49, WD 0.12 mm objective and PRIME BSI sCMOS camera (Teledyne Photometrics). Movies were recorded with an exposure time of 100 ms or 50 ms for 5 min at a rate of 0.5 fps.

Immunostaining and imaging

Thirty thousand MDCK (ATTC CRL-2935) cells were seeded on coverslips in 24-well plates containing DMEM complete medium (supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 1× penicillin–streptomycin). Cells were co-transfected with two different plasmids: one encoding VASH2 and the other encoding either EML1, EML2, EML3, or EML4. Co-transfection was performed using JetPEI (Polyplus#101000020) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 1 µg of each plasmid and a total of 4 µl of JetPEI per well. After 17 h, cells were fixed using a protocol designed to preserve the cytoskeleton while permeabilising the membrane.

Cells shown in Fig. 6 were pre-permeabilised for 2 min at 37 °C in 0.3% glutaraldehyde and 0.25% Triton X-100 in pre-warmed Cytoskeleton Buffer (10 mM MES (Sigma #M8250) pH 6.1, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EGTA, 5 mM glucose, 5 mM MgCl2), followed by fixation for 10 min at room temperature in 4% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde in Cytoskeleton Buffer. Cells shown in Fig. EV7 were permeabilised and fixed at the same time with 0.3% glutaraldehyde, 0.2% Triton X100 and 4% PFA in pre-warmed Cytoskeleton Buffer for 10 min at room temperature. To quench background fluorescence, cells were incubated for 7 min at room temperature in 0.1% NaBH4 in PBS. The cells were then blocked for 30 min in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS, followed by primary and secondary antibody incubations for 1 h each in 5% BSA in PBS. After each antibody incubation, we washed the cells three times with PBST (PBS + 0.1% Tween-20) at 5-min intervals. Coverslips were then mounted using ProLong Gold anti-fade reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective, operated via Zen software, and the images were processed using Fiji and Adobe Photoshop.
