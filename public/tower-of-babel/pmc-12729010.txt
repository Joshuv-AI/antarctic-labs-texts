Hemodynamic subphenotypes in septic shock and their different responses to fluid balance in relation to ICU mortality
Authors: Yan Chen, Rong Liufu, Run Dong, Mao-mao Cao, Jin-min Peng, Chun-yao Wang, Wei Jiang, Xiao-yun Hu, Shan Li, Li Weng, Bin Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHemodynamically unstable septic patients often require aggressive fluid resuscitation, but limited evidence exists on which hemodynamic subphenotypes benefit most.MethodsThe retrospective cohort study conducted in a tertiary hospital of China. Patients with septic shock who underwent pulse index continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) monitoring within 24 h were included from November 2013 to January 2024. We applied latent profile modelling to identify subphenotypes using all hemodynamic parameters monitored via PiCCO. We then tested the association of 48-h fluid balance with ICU mortality in different subphenotypes using logistic regression.ResultA total of 691 patients were included. Latent profile models indicated that a four-profile model was the best fit. Hyperdynamic subphenotype (phenotype1) was characterized by highest cardiac output and lowest systemic vascular resistance index (SVRI); high preload subphenotype (phenotype 2) was characterized by highest preload; hypodynamic subphenotype (phenotype 3) was characterised by lowest cardiac output, highest SVRI, lung function, as well as inotropic score; preserved subphenotype (phenotype 4) was presented with relative normal hemodynamic parameters. No significant difference was observed in 24- and 48-hour fluid balance among subphenotypes, and overall, no significant correlation was found between 48-hour fluid balance and ICU mortality. However, a significant interaction existed between 48-hour fluid balance and phenotype 4; in this group, higher fluid balance at 48 h was associated with increased ICU mortality (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.46; p = 0.011).ConclusionFour hemodynamic subphenotypes in septic shock was identified. The impact of 48-hour fluid balance on ICU mortality varied by subphenotype, with increased mortality observed only in the preserved hemodynamic group.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03495-z.

Introduction

Septic shock results in millions of deaths every year worldwide, and the incidence is steadily rising. Timely, effective fluid resuscitation is crucial in sepsis management [1]. However, the accumulation of fluid can lead to adverse outcomes, such as respiratory failure [2, 3],tissue edema, and even a higher risk of death [4].Although several studies indicated the potential benefits of a restrictive fluid strategy over a liberal fluid strategy [4–6], recent studies showed no difference in outcomes when comparing restrictive approaches to unguided resuscitation [7, 8]. This inconsistency may be attributed to the heterogeneity of patients.

Hemodynamic assessments—such as transpulmonary thermodilution and pulmonary artery catheterization—are suggested in patients with severe shock to determine the type of shock and guide resuscitation [1].Classic hemodynamic phenotyping is mainly based on the evaluation of volemia, vasoplegia, and cardiac dysfunction. However, overlaps between these abnormalities complicate the clinical situation and make the interpretation of parameters difficult [9]. This highlights the need to identify hemodynamic phenotypes of septic shock to facilitate personalized care based on individual patient needs.

Although research has made substantial progress toward identifying phenotypes of sepsis [10], little work has focused on hemodynamics subphenotyping in septic shock. Geri et al. characterized distinct cardiovascular phenotypes in septic shock based on echocardiographic and clinical parameters from two published prospective databases [11]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear how these findings could inform the optimization of hemodynamic support in clinical practice.

According to a study by Zhang et al., different subclasses of sepsis identified by clinical and laboratory variables responded differently to the amount of fluid resuscitation [12]. In subclass 3 of sepsis in their study—characterized by circulatory shock, the lowest mean blood pressure, and a heightened requirement for vasopressors—a higher cumulative fluid intake during the initial 48 h was associated with a reduced risk of hospital mortality. These patients are often hemodynamically unstable and require individualized fluid resuscitation. However, their study lacked hemodynamic monitoring data, limiting further analysis in patients with septic shock. Which hemodynamic subphenotypes might benefit most from fluid therapy remains unknown.

Therefore, in this study, we sought to identify hemodynamic subphenotypes of septic shock and evaluate their association with clinical outcomes and responses to fluid balance.

Method

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study. Patients were included between November 2013 and January 2024 across three ICUs of Peking Union College Hospital in China. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of Peking Union Medical College Hospital; the approval included a waiver for the informed consent of the patients and physicians.

All consecutive patients with septic shock who underwent pulse index continuous cardiac output (PiCCO) monitoring within 24 h were included. The local management strategy for septic shock is detailed in appendix. The Septic Shock 3.0 definition was used to define septic shock in this study [13]. Patients younger than 18 years old, as well as those who were discharged or died within 48 h after ICU admission, were excluded. Patients were followed up until discharge from ICU.

Measurements

Demographic data and chronic comorbidities were collected on ICU admission. The acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II score, daily fluid intake and balance, hemodynamic measurements, renal replacement therapy, and vasopressor requirements were evaluated after enrollment. For patients who were receiving continuous renal replacement treatment (CRRT), replacement/dialysate fluids are included in total input and effluent in total output. These CRRT exchange volumes are approximately equal and therefore cancel out in the calculation of net fluid balance. Hemodynamic measurements were performed by transpulmonary thermodilution (PiCCO; Pulsion Medical Systems AG, Munich, Germany). Only the first set of hemodynamic variables including systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), heart rate (HR), cardiac output (CO), systemic vascular resistance index (SVRI), cardiac function index (CFI), extravascular lung water index (EVLWI), global end-diastolic volume index (GEDI), global ejection fraction (GEF), intrathoracic blood volume index (ITBI), pulmonary vascular permeability index (PVPI), and left ventricular stroke work index (LVSWI) were studied. The dose of vasoactive/vasopressor agents is expressed as the inotropic score, a dimensionless variable calculated as: (adrenaline dose×100) +(noradrenaline dose×100). Variables with more than 20% missing values were removed. As a result, dPmax and stroke volume variation were excluded from the study. Single imputation was performed for the variables with missing value less than 20%.

The primary outcome was ICU mortality rate. The interaction between subphenotype and 48-hour fluid balance for ICU mortality was tested.

Latent profile analysis

Hemodynamic variables including SBP, DBP, HR, CO, SVRI, CFI, EVLWI, GEDVI, GEF, ITBVI, PVPI, LVSWI, and inotropic agent requirements were considered as profile-defining variables in the latent profile analysis (LPA) model. In the study, the number of profiles was determined by Akaike information criterion (AIC) [14] and bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT). Lower AIC values suggest a better model fit. A P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in BLRT for the comparison of the k-class model with the (k-1)-class model [15]. The subphenotype characteristics were visualized using a profile plot, which displayed the standardized mean of subphenotype defining variables.

Statistical analysis

Values were presented as the mean (SD) or median (interquartile range (IQR)) for continuous variables as appropriate and as percent for categorical variables. Continuous data were compared with analysis of variance and non-continuous dichotomous data was compared by the chi-square test across the different subphenotypes. Clinical characteristics, hemodynamic parameters, and outcomes including ICU mortality rate and length of stay in ICU were compared between latent profiles. Post hoc analysis was used to identify which specific phenotype groups differ from each other.Tukey’s HSD was used for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed ones. And post hoc pairwise comparisons used Mann-Whitney U or Dunn’s test and Bonferroni correction.

Multivariable logistic regression modeling was performed to determine associations between 48-hour fluid balance and ICU mortality. Model diagnostics included assessment of multicollinearity (adjusted GVIF), linearity (Box-Tidwell test), and calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow test).

The model was adjusted for confounding variables selected based on a p value < 0.05 in univariate analysis, as well as potential confounders identified by the clinical expertise. A model including a 48-hour fluid balance × subphenotype interaction term was also created to evaluate the interaction between fluid balance and hemodynamic subphenotypes. Odds ratio of ICU mortality was reported for each 1000-mL increase in fluid balance at each level of subphenotypes.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0, R studio, Boston, MA). GraphPad Prism 10.3.1 was used to graph results.

Result

Latent profile analysis

A total of 691 patients were included for identification of hemodynamic subphenotypes in septic shock (Figure S1). Analysis of latent-profile models suggested that a four-class model provided the best fit (Table 1, Figure S2). The p value testing the number of profiles indicated that four-profile models had a significant improvement over three-profile model, but five-profile model did not have a significant improvement. The value of the AIC decreases as the number of profiles increased until profile four. This trend was also seen in both the Bayesian information criteria and sample-sized adjusted-Bayesian Information Criteria (data not shown). The entropy exhibited a significant decrease when transitioning from a 4- to a 5-profile model. Collectively, the 4-profile model was determined to be the most optimal choice.

Different hemodynamic parameters and clinical features between subphenotypess

Hemodynamic parameters are shown in Table 2; Fig. 1. Phenotype definitions were mainly based on features of the parameters based on detailed reference values. Eighty-nine patients (12.9%) were in hyperdynamic status (phenotype 1). They were characterized by highest cardiac output and lowest SVRI. Twenty-one patients (3.0%) had a subphenotype reflecting high preload (phenotype 2). They were characterized by the lowest CFI and GEF, as well as the highest preload indicators like GEDI and ITBVI. Twenty-three patients (3.3%) could be considered as hypodynamic subphenotype (phenotype 3). They were defined by lowest cardiac output, highest SVRI, PVPI and EVLWI, as well as inotropic score among all subphenotypes. Finally, 558 patients (80.8%) belonged to preserved subphenotype (phenotype 4). This was the largest group and was presented with relative normal hemodynamic parameters. Pairwise comparisons of the parameters across four phenotypes were shown in Table S1.

Association of fluid balance and ICU mortality

The median 48-hour cumulative fluid balance for phenotype 1, phenotype 2, phenotype 3, and phenotype 4 was 83mL,1153mL, 259mL and 597mL, respectively. No significant differences were found in the amount of 24-hour and 48-hour fluid balance among four subphenotypes (Table 3). As shown in Figure S3 and Table 3, the amount of fluid balance decreased from 24 h (median 896 mL, IQR − 163 to 2426 mL) to 48 h (median 531 mL, IQR − 1774 to 2841mL) after enrollment.

According to the univariate regression analyses, a more positive 48-hour fluid balance was associated with an increased ICU mortality (OR 1.22, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.28; p < 0.001). After controlling for variables including age, sex, APACHEII score, malignancy, and renal replacement therapy, no significant association was found between 48-hour fluid balance and ICU mortality (Table 4). However, there was a significant interaction between 48-hour fluid balance and phenotype 4. As shown in Table 4; Fig. 2, higher cumulative fluid balance within 48 h was associated with an increased risk of ICU mortality for phenotype 4(OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.46; p = 0.011).

Discussion

Our study identified four hemodynamic subphenotypes in septic shock by combing the latent profile analysis and hemodynamic parameters. Hyperdynamic subphenotype (phenotype 1) was characterized by highest cardiac output and lowest SVRI; high preload subphenotype (phenotype 2) was characterized by highest GEDI and ITBVI and lowest CFI; hypodynamic subphenotype (phenotype 3) was characterized by lowest cardiac output, highest SVRI, PVPI and EVLWI, as well as inotropic score; preserved subphenotype (phenotype 4) was presented with relative normal hemodynamic parameters. The study indicated a significant interaction between 48-hour fluid balance and phenotype 4, showing that a higher cumulative fluid balance within 48 h was associated with an increased risk of ICU mortality in this subphenotype. Perhaps most importantly, the different responses to fluid accumulation among various hemodynamic subphenotypes suggest that the identification of subphenotypes might be crucial for future clinical trials in septic shock.

Several studies have focused on the identification of hemodynamic subphenotypes. In a prospective observational study, a total of 127 septic patients admitted to the emergency department were included. All the patients were presented with non-invasive continuous hemodynamic monitoring including cardiac index and systemic vascular resistance index [16]. The study identified three clusters by K-means clustering: cluster 1 (56.7%) with high CI and normal SVRI, cluster 2 (39.4%) with low CI and increased SVRI and cluster 3 (3.9%) with very low CI and very high SVRI. However, the hemodynamic phenotypes are insufficiently characterized and rely on too simplistic and inadequate parameters. Another study characterized five cardiovascular phenotypes in septic shock using Hierarchical clustering in a principal components approach: cluster 1, patients well resuscitated (16.9%), cluster 2, left ventricular systolic dysfunction (17.7%), cluster 3, LV hyperkinesia (23.3%), cluster 4, right ventricular failure (22.5%) and cluster 5, sustained hypovolemia (19.4%)11. The latter study comprehensively distinguished between different macrocirculation phenotypes. Nevertheless, neither study determined the relationship between hemodynamic subphenotypes and fluid treatment.

Our study explored hemodynamic subphenotypes using a different method of latent profile analysis. LPA uses maximum likelihood estimation to identify or approximate possibly meaningful sub-grouping of subjects that may help to better understand sample heterogeneity [17]. In the study, besides classic hyperdynamic (phenotype 1) and hypodynamic (phenotype 3) status, two more patterns were recognized. For phenotype 2, they were featured by the highest preload indicators like GEDI and ITBVI indicating high preload. Although GEF and CFI appeared decreased relative to normal values, cardiac output remained within the normal range; thus, the low GEF and CFI reflect the effect of markedly elevated GEDVI rather than intrinsic cardiac dysfunction. For phenotype 4, they exhibited no significant hemodynamic abnormalities and was therefore classified as the preserved group.

It is often assumed that fluid accumulation in patients with excess preload parameters may lead to bad outcome. Meanwhile, fluid administration is unlikely to cause serious consequences in patients whose hemodynamic parameters are within the normal range. However, our study indicates that a greater cumulative fluid balance within 48 h is linked to a higher risk of ICU mortality in phenotype 4 (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.46; p = 0.011). Because normal hemodynamic parameters may sometimes lead to negligence regarding fluid accumulation, fluid may be administered as long as the patient shows improvement in hemodynamic factors, as described in the 2016 Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines [18]. And it is worth noting that phenotype 4 account for over three quarters of all patients.

This finding appears to be inconsistent with the conclusions of a related study conducted by Zhang et al.12. Their study suggested that a higher amount of fluid input within 48 h correlated with a decreased hospital mortality rate in the subclass exhibiting the highest demand for vasopressors (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.95; p = 0.046). However, only about half of the patients in this subclass were treated with vasopressors. This implies that our study population differed from theirs. Patients in our study were those who were hemodynamically unstable, potentially complicated by a combination of cardiac dysfunction, and in need of hemodynamic monitoring. As a result, a more personalized management of fluid is needed. Based on our findings, there was a 24% increase in ICU mortality rate for every 1000 ml increment of 48-hour fluid balance in phenotype 4. Whereas no significant correlation was found between fluid accumulation and ICU mortality among patients in other subphenotypes. However, this was likely due to small sample sizes and limited power. A trend toward higher mortality with increased fluid balance was observed in phenotype 2 and 3, suggesting that further studies with larger cohorts may be necessary to confirm these associations.

Overall, our findings may partly explain the conflicting result of recent trials of fluid strategy. A large randomized clinical trial involving patients with septic shock showed no difference in 90-day mortality when comparing a restrictive approach to standard intravenous fluid therapy (42.3% vs. 42.1%)8. However, hemodynamic factors were not collected in their study. According to our findings, patients with different hemodynamic subphenotypes respond differently to fluid balance on ICU mortality. Therefore, studies failed to support the correlation between fluid accumulation and poor outcomes, which may be attributed to the different hemodynamic characteristics of the patients.

Several limitations must be acknowledged in this study. First, this is an observational retrospective study. Our study can only identify associations between fluid and outcome among different hemodynamic subphenotypes, the causal relationship cannot be confirmed. Second, the study covered a large span of time, and clinical characteristics and treatment regimens may vary over years. However, the shock resuscitation protocol of our center was generally consistent in a decade. Third, there were missing values in the cohort study that may cause selection bias. Thus, the variables with a proportion of missing value greater than 20% were excluded from analysis. Moreover, single imputations were performed for the remaining variables with missing values. Fourth, patients with pulmonary artery catheter were not included in the study. Nevertheless, a previous study comparing hemodynamic management guided by transpulmonary thermodilution with that of pulmonary artery catheter showed no significant difference in ICU mortality among patients with septic shock [19]. Fifth, SVVwas not included in our analysis due to substantial missing data, which prevented its use for identifying patients who might most benefit from intravenous fluid administration. Sixth, the parameters used in this study do not distinguish between left and right ventricular failure, which is a notable limitation since patients with right ventricular failure may represent a high-risk subgroup for fluid administration. Seventh, CRRT‑associated volumes are included in gross totals—replacement/dialysate as input and effluent as output. This may inflate the apparent magnitude of total input and output among CRRT recipients; however, because these exchange volumes are approximately balanced, the net fluid balance is expected to be minimally affected. Finally, only the initial PiCCO measurements were used to define hemodynamic subphenotypes, which may not fully reflect the dynamic changes in hemodynamic status or patients’ responses to fluid therapy over time. Additional research is required to validate and expand upon these findings.

Conclusion

This study identified four hemodynamic subphenotypes of septic shock, which showed different responses to fluid in relation to ICU mortality. Notably, increased fluid balance was linked to higher mortality only in the preserved hemodynamic group. These findings support hemodynamic subphenotyping to guide fluid management and improve outcomes in septic shock.
