In situ and real-time monitoring of intracellular activities in single live cells using a nanopore probe
Authors: Xiaobin Huang, Ziyi Li, Yanling Huang, Ke Zhou, Lei Liu, Hai-Chen Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding molecular dynamics within single live cells is essential for elucidating how cellular heterogeneity drives physiology and disease. Yet, existing single-cell methods cannot continuously track multiple biomolecules in situ and in real time. We developed a nanopore probe that integrates a nanostraw extraction interface with a protein nanopore sensor, enabling label-free, quantitative, and multiplexed detection of intracellular molecules in single living neurons. Using this system, we simultaneously monitored glutamate, ascorbic acid, and adenosine triphosphatedynamics during ischemic stress, revealing molecular events underlying neuronal edema. This study represents a successful application of nanopore sensing inside live cells, offering a powerful platform for probing cellular processes and disease mechanisms at single-molecule resolution.

The cell is the fundamental unit of life, and dynamic changes in intracellular biomolecules directly regulate critical biological processes such as gene expression, metabolic activity, and signal transduction (1–3). Continuously monitoring cells in their native states is essential for tracking fundamental processes such as differentiation and senescence, understanding cellular heterogeneity, disease progression, and interactions within complex microenvironments (4–8). Conventional single-cell analysis typically involves micromanipulation or cell sorting using microfluidics or optical tweezers, followed by cell lysis (9–15). The extracted samples are then analyzed by techniques such as mass spectrometry (11, 16, 17), fluorescence (12–14, 18), electrochemistry (19–21), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (22, 23), PCR (24, 25), and other techniques (26, 27). While these methods provide quantitative insights into cellular components, they are incapable of capturing in situ, real-time dynamic transitions. Recent advances, including single-cell extraction using nanostraws (23, 28–30) and fluid force microscopy (31, 32) have enabled the analysis of subcellular contents or organelles while preserving cell viability. However, these methods still rely on offline quantification, offering only discrete temporal snapshots and lacking real-time monitoring capabilities. Fluorescent labeling (33, 34) and electrochemical techniques (35, 36) can provide temporal profiles of cellular activity, but are limited by the need for labeling, restricted substrate compatibility, and limited capacity for multiplexed detection. Therefore, there is a critical need for technologies that allow continuous, real-time monitoring of dynamic molecular changes within live single cells.

Nanopore sensing is a label-free single-molecule detection technique capable of identifying individual biomolecules. When an analyte interacts with a nanopore, it perturbs the ionic current, producing measurable fluctuations. By analyzing changes in current amplitude and the average dwell time, the analyte’s identity can be determined. Additionally, the event frequency correlates with the analyte concentration, providing quantitative information (37). To date, nanopores have been widely applied in stochastic sensing (38–40), single-molecule chemistry (41–44), DNA sequencing, and protein sequencing (45–50). Nanopore sensors offer several unique advantages such as single-molecule resolution, label-free detection, real-time analysis, quantitative output, high throughput, all of which are highly desirable for single-cell analysis. However, their deployment in single-cell studies has been limited due to technical challenges, particularly the fragility of lipid bilayers and difficulties in nanopore insertion into live cells.

In this work, we developed a nanopore probe capable of simultaneously monitoring multiple biomolecules and intracellular activities inside single live cells in real time. The probe comprises an outer capillary equipped with a nanostraw membrane for intracellular extraction, and an inner capillary incorporating a glass nanopore membrane for single-channel recordings (Fig. 1). The nanostraw membrane was fabricated by coating a track-etched polycarbonate (TEPC) membrane with alumina via atomic layer deposition (ALD), followed by reactive ion etching (RIE) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). These micron-long nanostraws gently penetrate the cell membrane, enabling minimally invasive sampling of intracellular contents. The glass nanopore membrane was prepared by sealing a nano-tapered platinum wire inside a glass capillary, followed by mechanical polishing and chemical etching (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). A lipid bilayer is formed over the membrane, into which a chemically modified mutant of Mycobacterium smegmatis porin A (MspA) is inserted. This engineered MspA can selectively recognize glutamate (Glu), ascorbic acid (AA), and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) via distinct ionic current signatures. We applied this nanopore probe to a hippocampal neuron model of ischemia-hypoxia, induced by oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD), to explore the real-time dynamics of intracellular small molecules during neuronal edema. The results demonstrate that our system enables real-time, in situ molecular monitoring at the single-cell level, revealing rich dynamic information that is inaccessible with conventional techniques. This platform provides a powerful tool for investigating molecular interactions within the cellular microenvironment and opens avenues for studying disease progression and developing therapeutic strategies.

Results and Discussion

Construction of the Nanopore Probe.

To monitor cellular activities using biological nanopores, an intuitive strategy involves embedding protein pores into the cell membrane and recording ionic currents under high-impedance sealing conditions—similar to patch-clamp techniques used for ion channels. Several studies have attempted to record the translocation of DNA molecules and neurotransmitters through protein nanopores inserted into the cell membrane (51, 52). However, these efforts yielded unsatisfactory results due to low insertion efficiency, poor sensing resolution, and interference from the cell’s resting membrane potential. In our own experiments, we attempted high-impedance sealing on the HeLa cell membrane using a micropipette, followed by insertion of MspA nanopores into the membrane. We observed current fluctuations between “open” and “closed” states after MspA insertion—likely a result of the dynamic nature of the live cell membrane and interactions between MspA and the membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (52). Additionally, frequent current bursts were observed, presumably caused by the cell’s resting potential, which led to noisy recordings. These challenges underscored the need for a new approach to effectively apply nanopore sensing in live-cell measurements.

To overcome the challenges of in situ nanopore insertion and minimize interference from membrane potential, we developed a preassembled nanopore probe capable of penetrating single cells while simultaneously detecting translocating molecules. Protein nanopores were embedded in a lipid bilayer membrane supported on a glass nanopore membrane (53). This bilayer was then protected by a larger concentric capillary tube with a conical tip (~500 nm in tip diameter, SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B), allowing controlled insertion into HeLa cells using a micromanipulator. However, early tests showed that ATP molecules were scarcely detectable through the modified MspA nanopore embedded in the bilayer (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). We attributed this limitation to the long diffusion path (~6.0 mm) within the conical region of the capillary, which posed a substantial diffusion barrier for ATP molecules to reach the nanopore. To resolve this issue, we incorporated an aluminum oxide (Al2O3) nanostraw membrane at the probe tip, effectively reducing the diffusion distance to ~20 µm (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D–F). The Al2O3 nanostraw membrane was fabricated following established protocols (Fig. 2A) (23, 30). Upon attaching it to the outer capillary tip, the nanopore probe assembly was complete (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 D–F).

Next, we validated the ability of the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane to extract intracellular biomolecules. Calcein (1.5 mM) was used as a fluorescent tracer, placed above the nanostraw membrane. The membrane was then used to puncture hippocampal neurons, followed by application of a −100 mV potential for 10 min. Driven by both concentration gradients and electric fields, calcein successfully entered the neurons through the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane (Fig. 2B). Fluorescence imaging showed bright green calcein signals in neurons located beneath the nanostraw membrane (Fig. 2 C and D, inside the dashed line), while neurons outside the membrane area exhibited no significant fluorescence—consistent with calcein’s hydrophilic nature and inability to passively cross the membrane. SEM analysis verified that the Al2O3 nanostraw membranes retained their structural integrity over the course of the experiments (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These findings demonstrate that the nanostraw membrane effectively enables transport of small molecules into and out of cells.

The biocompatibility of Al2O3 nanostraw membrane and its effect on membrane integrity were evaluated using live-dead staining. Hippocampal neurons were first penetrated by the nanostraw membrane and exposed to a +100 mV potential for 30 min. The neurons were then stained with calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM) and propidium iodide (PI). Fluorescence imaging showed strong green fluorescence in both penetrated and nonpenetrated regions (Fig. 2 E and F), while minimal red fluorescence. Calcein-AM, a membrane-permeable dye, enters live cells and fluoresces green upon esterase-mediated hydrolysis, whereas PI fluoresces red only in cells with compromised membranes. The absence of PI staining confirmed that nanostraw penetration caused minimal membrane disruption, thereby maintaining high cell viability. This assessment was further supported by complementary assays: negligible lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and a JC-1 fluorescence profile dominated by red (aggregated) over green (monomeric) signal in hippocampal neurons after Al2O3 nanostraw penetration (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Additionally, the SEM imaging confirmed that the nanostraws retained their structural integrity following cell penetration (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Together, these results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility and minimal invasiveness of the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane. The efficient molecular transport and membrane compatibility of this system provide a strong foundation for building an integrated extraction–detection platform combining the glass nanopore membrane and Al2O3 nanostraw—enabling dynamic single-cell biomolecule monitoring with high fidelity.

Simultaneous Sensing of Glu, AA, and ATP.

Under OGD conditions, the intracellular concentrations of biomolecules such as Glu, AA, and ATP undergo significant changes. These alterations disrupt intracellular ion homeostasis, leading to cytotoxic edema and subsequent neuronal dysfunction. To address this, we developed an integrated system combining an Al2O3 nanostraw membrane with modified MspA nanopores for molecular extraction and detection (Fig. 3A). To enable simultaneous recognition of Glu, AA and ATP, we chemically conjugated 1,10-phenanthroline (Phen) to a hetero-octameric MspA mutant, (N90C)1(M2)7 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), which comprises one monomeric subunit containing a single cysteine at position 90 and seven wild-type monomers with asparagine residues (54). The Phen moiety chelates a Cu2+ ion to form the MspA-Phen-Cu construct, which selectively binds small biomolecules via coordination between the Cu2+ center and N or O donor atoms (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). We individually added Glu, AA, and ATP to the cis solution, in which the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane was immersed. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) analysis further revealed negligible adsorption of the target molecules (Glu, AA, ATP) on the nanostraw surface (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Upon applying a +100 mV transmembrane potential, we observed distinct current signatures corresponding to each molecule, driven by a combination of diffusion and electric field forces through the nanostraw membrane (Fig. 3C). Statistical analyses revealed that the current blockades induced by Glu, AA, and ATP were well separated (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, we performed quantitative measurements at varying concentrations and established standard calibration curves for each molecule (Fig. 3 E–G and SI Appendix, Figs. S11–S13). These results provide a robust foundation for real-time monitoring of bioactive small molecules within live neurons using the nanopore probe.

Real-Time Monitoring OGD-Induced Neuronal Cell Edema.

Under normal physiological conditions, the concentration of sodium ions (Na+) is higher outside the neuron than inside, while the concentration of potassium ions (K+) is higher inside the neuron than outside. Driven by this concentration gradient, Na+ tends to flow inward, and K+ tends to flow outward. To maintain these gradients, the Na+/K+ pump actively transports Na+ out of the cell and K+ into the cell by consuming ATP. This process maintains ionic homeostasis and keeps the resting membrane potential below the activation threshold of voltage-gated calcium channels (VGCCs), ensuring they remain closed and preventing Ca2+ influx (55). Meanwhile, N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs) are moderately open, allowing limited Ca2+ entry for the regulation of synaptic transmission (Fig. 4). Additionally, excitatory amino acid transporters tightly regulate extracellular Glu levels to prevent excessive accumulation and overactivation of NMDARs, thereby avoiding excitotoxicity (56). Under OGD conditions, cellular energy metabolism is disrupted, resulting in reduced ATP production. Consequently, the Na+/K+ pump failed to maintain ion gradients (Fig. 4) (57). This leads to an intracellular buildup of Na+ and loss of K+, causing membrane depolarization. Once the membrane potential exceeds the threshold, VGCCs open, triggering a surge of Ca2+ influx. Elevated intracellular Ca2+ promotes synaptic vesicle fusion and Glu release via exocytosis. Increased extracellular Glu further activates NMDARs, amplifying Na+ and Ca2+ influx (58). The resulting NaCl accumulation drives water into the neuron, leading to cell swelling. OGD also induces oxidative stress, accelerating the consumption of AA and sharply reducing its intracellular concentration (56). Thus, impaired ATP production under OGD conditions disrupts ion, Glu, and AA homeostasis, ultimately contributing to excitotoxic neuronal edema.

Schematic illustration of the molecular mechanisms underlying excitotoxic neuronal edema under normal physiological and OGD conditions. Under normal physiological conditions, the Na+/K+ pump maintains ionic gradients across the neuronal membrane by actively transporting Na+ out of the cell and K+ into the cell, consuming ATP in the process. This activity sustains the resting membrane potential below the activation threshold of VGCCs, keeping them closed and preventing Ca2+ influx. NMDARs remain partially open, allowing limited Ca2+ entry to regulate synaptic transmission. Meanwhile, excitatory amino acid transporters tightly regulate extracellular Glu levels, preventing overstimulation of NMDARs and excitotoxicity. Under OGD conditions, cellular energy metabolism is impaired, leading to reduced ATP production and dysfunction of the Na+/K+ pump. This results in intracellular Na+ accumulation, K+ efflux, and membrane depolarization (process 1). Once the membrane potential exceeds the activation threshold, VGCCs open, allowing a substantial influx of Ca2+ (process 2). Elevated intracellular Ca2+ promotes synaptic vesicle fusion and Glu release via exocytosis (process 3). Excess extracellular Glu overstimulates NMDARs, further increasing intracellular Na+ and Ca2+ levels (process 4). Simultaneously, OGD-induced oxidative stress depletes intracellular AA, disrupting redox homeostasis (process 5). Collectively, these processes culminate in excitotoxic neuronal edema under OGD conditions (process 6).

With the successful construction of the nanopore probe, we were able to monitor intracellular changes of ATP, Glu and AA during OGD-induced neuronal cell edema (processes 1, 3, and 5 in Fig. 4). In this study, we established three experimental models—OGD, inhibitory OGD, and excitatory OGD—to investigate the dynamic changes in intracellular concentrations of Glu, AA and ATP in hippocampal neurons, as well as the effect of excitotoxic edema on soma size. To accurately determine the actual concentrations of Glu, AA, and ATP within individual hippocampal neurons, we first calculated the system’s extraction efficiency (η) to correlate the recorded signals with true intracellular levels and minimize experimental error (SI Appendix, Figs. S14 and S15). Given the potential for interference from the NMDARs antagonist 2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (AP5), the agonist N-methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA), and other biomolecules, we evaluated the selectivity of our detection system. The changes in I/I0 of Glu, AA, and ATP in the presence of AP5 and NMDA demonstrated that neither compound interfered with signal identification (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 A–F). Additionally, although intracellular ATP levels are typically orders of magnitude higher than those of ADP in hippocampal neurons, we examined whether ADP could interfere with ATP detection. The results confirmed that ADP had negligible influence (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 G–J). To assess the sensor’s specificity, we evaluated its response to representative endogenous interferents. While strong coordinating molecules (glutathione, aspartic acid, serine, glutamine, and N-acetyl-aspartic acid) produced current blockades that were clearly distinguishable from those of Glu, AA, and ATP, high-abundance metabolites (glucose and lactate) generated no detectable signal. These results confirm the high specificity of the MspA-Phen-Cu nanopore sensor for its target analytes (SI Appendix, Fig. S17). To further validate our detection strategy, we spiked standard solutions of Glu, AA, and ATP into the system following recordings from single hippocampal neurons. The characteristic current blockades of Glu, AA, and ATP remained consistent before and after spiking (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), indicating that other intracellular molecules do not interfere with their detection or identification.

Next, we monitored intracellular changes in Glu, AA, and ATP levels in live neurons under various OGD conditions. In normal hippocampal neurons, the event counts for Glu, AA, and ATP increased linearly over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S19, Left), indicating a constant capture rate and suggesting that the intracellular levels of these biomolecules remain stable. In the OGD model, sodium dithionite and iodoacetic acid were added to the culture medium to inhibit oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, respectively. Real-time current traces recorded from individual neurons (Fig. 5A) enabled quantification of Glu, AA, and ATP event counts over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S19 A, D, and G, Right). Compared with normal neurons, ATP and Glu levels declined rapidly under OGD conditions, while AA levels decreased more gradually (Fig. 5 B, Right). These molecular changes were accompanied by significant neuronal soma swelling within 15 min (Fig. 5C and SI Appendix, Figs. S20A and S21). This outcome is attributed to impaired ATP synthesis, which disrupts ionic homeostasis and membrane polarization, leading to Ca2+ influx. The resulting intracellular Ca2+ accumulation promotes Glu release and activates NMDARs within minutes (Fig. 5D). Concurrently, oxidative stress gradually depletes intracellular AA. Together, these effects trigger intracellular NaCl accumulation and water influx, ultimately resulting in neuronal edema (55).

In the inhibitory OGD model, the NMDARs antagonist AP5 (100.0 μM) was introduced to suppress receptor activity. Real-time current traces from individual neurons (Fig. 5E) showed a gradual decline in Glu, AA, and ATP event counts over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S19 B, E, and H, Right). Compared to the standard OGD conditions, AP5-treated neurons exhibited a markedly slower reduction in ATP and Glu concentrations during the early stages of OGD (Fig. 5 F, Right), and neuronal soma size remained nearly unchanged (Fig. 5G and SI Appendix, Figs. S20B and S21). These findings suggest that AP5 effectively alleviates excitotoxic edema by inhibiting NMDARs (Fig. 5H), thereby preserving energy metabolism, lowering energy consumption, and mitigating oxidative stress (55).

In the excitatory OGD model, the NMDARs agonist NMDA (100.0 μM) was added to further activate receptor activity. Real-time current traces from individual neurons (Fig. 5I) showed a rapid decrease in Glu, AA, and ATP capture events (SI Appendix, Fig. S19 C, F, and I, Right). Compared with the standard OGD model, NMDA-treated neurons exhibited an accelerated drop in ATP, Glu, and AA levels within minutes of NMDA administration (Fig. 5 J, Right). Neuronal soma swelled rapidly, reaching maximum size within 5 min (Fig. 5K and SI Appendix, Figs. S20C and S21), due to NMDA-induced NMDARs overactivation (Fig. 5L), which exacerbated excitotoxic edema, intensified oxidative stress, and accelerated energy depletion (55).

Intrinsic cellular heterogeneity in neuronal metabolism results in cell-to-cell variability in intracellular Glu, AA, and ATP concentrations. However, the temporal trends in Glu, AA, and ATP changes under identical stimulation conditions are highly consistent across cells, demonstrating the reproducibility of the proposed method (SI Appendix, Fig. S22).

Accurate and continuous measurements of Glu, AA, and ATP concentrations inside live neuronal cells under different OGD-conditions provided rich insights into the progression of OGD-induced neuronal edema. This nanopore-based approach offers a powerful tool to directly “see” dynamic intracellular processes in individual live cells—capabilities that are often beyond the reach of conventional methods such as fluorescence imaging, which typically focus only on the initial and final states of a given substrate.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have developed a nanopore probe that integrates an Al2O3 nanostraw membrane with a functionalized protein nanopore (MspA-Phen-Cu) for in situ and real-time molecular monitoring within individual cells. The nanostraw membrane enables nondestructive penetration into cells to extract intracellular small molecules. These molecules migrate under the influence of both diffusion and an applied electric field toward a lipid bilayer membrane embedded with the MspA-Phen-Cu nanopore. Their translocation generates distinct current signatures that can be used to identify individual molecules and quantify their concentrations in the cellular environment. Using an OGD-treated hippocampal neuron model, we successfully monitored the dynamic changes in intracellular Glu, AA, and ATP concentrations at the single-cell level, and confirmed the NMDARs as a key regulatory switch in excitotoxic neuronal edema.

Compared with existing single-cell analysis technologies, our platform offers several notable advantages: 1) Minimal cellular disruption, allowing for long-term, continuous observation; 2) In situ extraction and real-time monitoring of intracellular biomolecules; and 3) Simultaneous detection of multiple target molecules. Despite these advances, several technical challenges remain for further optimization. First, precise chemical modification of the nanopore could tune the recognition sites, thereby improving molecular specificity and signal resolution. Second, employing additional driving forces—such as electroosmotic or pressure-driven flow—could enhance molecular transport and extraction efficiency. Third, to reveal the true spatiotemporal dynamics of intracellular concentrations, methods for precise subcellular sampling, including from specific organelles, will be essential.

Overall, this work highlights the significant potential of nanopore sensing for advanced single-cell analysis. In the future, integration with high-throughput microfluidics and AI-assisted signal analysis may unlock new possibilities in cell biology, neuroscience, and personalized medicine. As nanopore-based probes continue to evolve, they may ultimately become powerful tools for dissecting complex intracellular dynamics with unprecedented precision and depth.

Buffer Preparation.

External buffer of the glass nanopore membrane (cis).

145.0 mM NaCl (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), 4.0 mM KCl (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich), 2.0 mM CaCl2 (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), 1.0 mM MgCl2 (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), 10.0 mM glucose (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), and 10.0 mM Tris (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.4.

300.0 mM KCl (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich), 20.0 μM CuCl2 (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), and 10.0 mM Tris (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), pH 7.4.

All buffers were prepared using deionized water (Millipore, MA) and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane prior to use.

Preparation of glass nanopore membrane.

The glass nanopore membrane was prepared according to a previously reported method (44). Briefly, a conical-tip Pt nanoelectrode was fabricated by electrochemically etching a Pt wire (25.0 µm in diameter) using an alternating voltage (5 V, 64 Hz). The nanoelectrode was encapsulated within the glass capillary using an H2-O2 flame. Then, the sealed glass capillary was polished with sandpaper. The exposure of the Pt tip triggered the self-assembled buzzer system, indicating the successful fabrication of the nano-Pt disk electrode sealed within the glass membrane. Finally, nano-Pt disk electrode sealed in the glass membrane was immersed in aqua regia and refluxed to dissolve the Pt disk. The glass nanopore membrane was then obtained by sequential washing with piranha solution, distilled water, and ethanol.

Fabrication of nanostraw membrane.

The nanostraw membrane was fabricated following a previously published protocol (30). In brief, a 20.0 nm-thick layer of Al2O3 was deposited onto the surface and inner walls of the TEPC nanochannels using ALD (TFS 200, Beneq). The outer Al2O3 layer was selectively removed via RIE (ICPRIE 500, SENTECH Instruments), followed by polycarbonate etching to form the nanostraw structure. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, SU 8020, Hitachi) image shows that the TEPC membrane contained numerous nanochannels, with nanostraws exhibiting an average diameter of ~100 nm and a length of ~1.5 μm. The resulting membrane was assembled at the bottom of a plexiglass tube to construct a cell content extractor. The successful penetration of the nanostraws into cells was verified by microscopic observation (TI2 E, Nikon).

Preparation of hetero-octameric protein (N90C)1(M2)7.

Hetero-octameric protein (N90C)1(M2)7 was prepared according to a previously reported protocol (45). It consists of one N90C MspA-H6 monomeric subunit and seven M2 MspA-D16H6 monomeric subunits, with a single cysteine residue available for site-specific chemical modification. To prepare this protein, the genes encoding M2 MspA-D16H6 and N90C MspA-H6 (Genscript) were coinserted into the pETDuet-1 plasmid (Genscript). The recombinant plasmid was then transformed into Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) competent cells (Sangon Biotech) for protein expression. The expressed protein was purified using nickel affinity chromatography, and various hetero-octameric MspA assemblies were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The gel band corresponding to (N90C)1(M2)7 was excised, and the protein was recovered and stored at −80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Structural stability and nonspecific adsorption of Al2O3 nanostraw membranes.

Structural stability.

To evaluate stability in aqueous media, Al2O3 nanostraw membranes were immersed in a buffer for 0, 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h. After each interval, membranes were rinsed with deionized water, dried, and prepared for imaging by SEM.

Nonspecific adsorption.

Adsorption of Glu, AA, and ATP was assessed using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS, IONTOF GmbH). Membranes were prepared under three conditions: i) Control: untreated membranes; ii) Loaded: membranes incubated in 10 mM standard solutions of the respective analytes and dried; iii) Eluted: membranes incubated in solution, rinsed with deionized water, and dried prior to analysis.

Extraction and culture of hippocampal neurons.

Newborn Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Beijing HFK Bioscience. Under sterile conditions, hippocampal neurons were isolated and cultured as follows: neonatal rats were immersed in 75% ethanol for surface disinfection. The heads were transferred to precooled HBSS, and the brains were exposed via longitudinal incision of the scalp and skull. The bilateral hippocampi were carefully dissected, minced, and digested with trypsin at 37 °C for 15 min. Digestion was terminated by adding inoculation medium (79% DMEM/F12, 20% FBS, 1.0% penicillin-streptomycin). The cell suspension was filtered through a 75 μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in inoculation medium. Cells were seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated culture dishes and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. On day 2, the inoculation medium was replaced with culture medium (97% Neurobasal, 2.0% B-27 supplement, 1.0% penicillin-streptomycin). Half-medium changes were performed on days 5 and 6 (ref. 59). Neurons cultured for 8 d were used in subsequent experiments.

LDH release assay and mitochondrial membrane potential analysis.

Membrane integrity (LDH release).

Cell membrane integrity was assessed using a LDH assay kit (Solarbio). Total intracellular LDH was determined from lysed hippocampal neurons. To quantify released LDH, neurons were penetrated with the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane, and the culture supernatant was collected. UV–vis absorption spectra of LDH from both lysates and supernatants were recorded. Relative LDH levels were calculated and normalized to the total intracellular LDH content.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 staining).

Mitochondrial function was evaluated by measuring membrane potential using a JC-1 fluorescent probe kit (Solarbio). Following nanostraw penetration, neurons were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C and gently washed. Bright-field and fluorescence images were acquired using confocal microscopy.

Detection of biomolecules in cell lysates using the nanopore probe.

Hippocampal neurons were cultured at varying cell densities. Mature neurons were harvested by trypsin digestion, followed by neutralization with culture medium, washing with PBS, and resuspension in a cold external buffer for cell counting. The cells were then lysed using a probe-type ultrasonic homogenizer to release intracellular contents, generating cell lysates. The nanopore probe was subsequently used to detect biomolecule concentrations within the lysates, enabling quantification of the average biomolecular content per cell.

Real-time biomolecules detection in single hippocampal neuron using the nanopore probe.

The hippocampal neurons were incubated in an external buffer to preserve their physiological state. A glass nanopore membrane containing the MspA-Phen-Cu nanopore was stably positioned above the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane, which was then slowly advanced to puncture the cell membrane. Biomolecules from the hippocampal neurons were transported across the Al2O3 nanostraw membrane, driven by a combination of concentration gradients and electric field forces, and subsequently entered the MspA-Phen-Cu nanopore. This process generated characteristic current blockade events, enabling the quantification of biomolecular contents at the single-cell level.

Single-channel current recordings.

Phospholipid bilayer on glass nanopore membrane was prepared according to a previously reported method (53). Glass nanopore membrane was rendered hydrophobic through overnight treatment with 3-cyanopropyldimethylchlorosilane (2%, in acetonitrile), then coated with DPhPc (10 mg mL−1 in decane). Bilayer formation was facilitated by gently brushing with a mink hairbrush under asymmetric buffer conditions, external buffer (cis): 145.0 mM NaCl, 4.0 mM KCl, 2.0 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgCl2, 10.0 mM glucose, and 10.0 mM Tris, pH 7.4; internal buffer (trans): 300.0 mM KCl, 20.0 μM CuCl2, and 10.0 mM Tris, pH 7.4. In vitro synthesis of MspA-Phen was achieved by incubating MspA-N90C with 100.0 μM maleimido-Phen for 1 h at room temperature, followed by purification through micro bio-spin P-6 gel column (Bio-Rad) to remove unreacted reagents. Precise regulation of the gas pressure inside the glass nanopore membrane is critical for the controlled insertion of protein nanopores into the suspended lipid bilayer. Purified MspA-Phen was added to the cis chamber, while a positive gas pressure was applied from the trans chamber toward the cis chamber, with a holding potential of +100 mV. Once the characteristic open-pore current of 36.0 pA was observed, the applied gas pressure was immediately released to prevent the insertion of multiple pores. The subsequent stabilization of the current at 36.0 pA confirmed the successful insertion of a single MspA-Phen nanopore into the lipid bilayer.

Data Analysis.

Current traces were analyzed with Clampfit 10.7 software (Axon Instruments). Events were detected using the Event Detection feature and used to construct amplitude and dwell time histograms. For statistical analysis, Glu and AA current blockade events were processed with a 1 kHz filter, while ATP events were not filtered. Current events were identified using the single-channel search function in Clampfit for statistical analysis. OriginPro and Clampfit were used for histogram construction, curve fitting, and graph presentation. Adobe Illustrator was used for making figures.
