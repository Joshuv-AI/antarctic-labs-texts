Efficient Simulation of High-Level Quantum Gates
Adam Husted Kjelstrøm, Andreas Pavlogiannis, Jaco van de Pol

Abstract
Quantum circuit simulation is paramount to the verification and optimization of quantum algorithms, and considerable research efforts have been made towards efficient simulators. While circuits often contain high-level gates such as oracles and multi-controlled X ( C k ​ X \text{C}^{k}\text{X} ) gates, existing simulation methods require compilation to a low-level gate-set before simulation. This, however, increases circuit size and incurs a considerable (typically exponential) overhead, even when the number of high-level gates is small. Here we present a gadget-based simulator which simulates high-level gates directly, thereby allowing to avoid or reduce the blowup of compilation. Our simulator uses a stabilizer decomposition of the magic state of non-stabilizer gates, with improvements in the rank of the magic state directly improving performance. We then proceed to establish a small stabilizer rank for a range of high-level gates that are common in various quantum algorithms. Using these bounds in our simulator, we improve both the theoretical complexity of simulating circuits containing such gates, and the practical running time compared to standard simulators found in IBM’s Qiskit Aer library. We also derive exponential lower-bounds for the stabilizer rank of some gates under common complexity-theoretic hypotheses. In certain cases, our lower-bounds are asymptotically tight on the exponent.

Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we use
a,b,\dots\in\{0,1\}
to denote single (classical) bits,
and
x,y,\dots\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to denote bit-strings of length
n
, where

is either explicit or clear from the context.
Given such a bit-string

, we denote by

the

’th bit of

.
For two bit-strings

Quantum states.

An
n
-qubit quantum state is a
2^{n}
-dimensional vector

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{x}\ket{x}

where the set of basis states
\{\ket{x}\}_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}
form an orthonormal basis for a
2^{n}
-dimensional vector space,
and
\psi_{x}\in\mathbb{C}
are complex numbers satisfying
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\psi_{x}|^{2}=1
.
For convenience, we often write states such as
\ket{0}+\ket{1}
, with the understanding that they are scaled by real, non-negative constants such that
\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}|\psi_{x}|^{2}=1
.
To be explicit about this, we write, e.g.,
\ket{\psi}\propto\ket{0}+\ket{1}
to mean that,
\ket{\psi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{0}+\ket{1})
.
For quantum states
\ket{\psi_{1}}
and
\ket{\psi_{2}}
, we write their (tensor) product state as
\ket{\psi_{1}}\ket{\psi_{2}}
.
One state that is used throughout this paper is the uniform superposition of
n

\ket{+^{n}}\propto\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\ket{x}\ .

Quantum gates and circuits.

A quantum state evolves by applications of quantum gates.
A quantum gate is a
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
-dimensional unitary matrix
U
.
We write
U^{\dagger}
for the conjugate transpose of

.
Applying

\ket{\psi}
means multiplying

\ket{\psi}
, obtaining the state

.
Since

U\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\psi_{x}U\ket{x},

a quantum gate can be described by how it acts on the basis states.
Given gates

,
U_{1}
,
U_{2}
, we write
U_{1}\otimes U_{2}
for the tensor product between
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
, and

for the

.
If a gate

A diagonal gate

\ket{x}\xrightarrow{D}D_{x}\ket{x}

A circuit is a sequence of gates,

U_{C}=U_{m}U_{m-1}\cdots U_{1}

For convenience, given a state
\ket{\psi}

We say that a circuit

Clifford gates.

The Clifford gate set consists of the Phase (

), and CNOT (

\displaystyle\ket{a}\xrightarrow{\text{S}}i^{a}\ket{a}\qquad\qquad\ket{a}\xrightarrow{\text{H}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{b=0}^{1}(-1)^{ab}\ket{b}\qquad\qquad\ket{a,b}\xrightarrow{\text{CX}}\ket{a,a\oplus b}

The Clifford gates suffice to construct other gates such as

Measurements.

While gates are invertible and affect the state deterministically, measurements are probabilistic and are not invertible.
Suppose we measure the

’th bit of a state
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{x}\psi_{x}\ket{x}

The probability of outcome

After measurement, the state is updated to
\ket{\psi^{\prime}}\propto\sum_{x\colon x_{j}=b}\psi_{x}\ket{x}
.
This notion naturally generalizes to simultaneous measurements on several qubits.

In simulation, as opposed to the real world, measurement outcomes can be post-selected, meaning the outcome is given instead of being sampled
1
1

1

Naturally, we cannot post-select on an outcome that has

.
As an example, given a state

Stabilizer circuits.

Together, Clifford gates and 0,1-measurements are called stabilizer gates
[
Gottesman1996_source_for_stabilizer_formalism
]

Circuits consisting only of stabilizer gates are called stabilizer circuits.
Any state of the form

Concrete examples include any basis vector
\ket{x}
, for
x\in\{0,1\}^{n}

\ket{+^{n}}

While stabilizer circuits can be efficiently simulated on a classical computer

Aaronson04sim_of_stabilizer_circuits
]

Such states are called non-stabilizer states, and can be reached using non-stabilizer gates.

Universal circuits.

A common choice of a non-stabilizer gate is the

\ket{a}\xrightarrow{\text{T}}(e^{i\pi/4})^{a}\ket{a}
.
Another choice is the
\text{R}_{Z}

\ket{a}\xrightarrow{\text{R}_{Z}(\theta)}(e^{i\theta})^{a}\ket{a}

Access to
T

gates makes stabilizer circuits universal for quantum computation, meaning that any quantum state can be approximated to arbitrary precision

Dawson2005solovay_kitaev_thrm
]

Both

\text{R}_{Z}
are diagonal gates, hence, in order to simulate arbitrary quantum computations, it suffices to simulate stabilizer+diagonal circuits.

Given a circuit

Zhou_2000_gadgetize_T_gates_magic
]
, defined as

\ket{D}:=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^{n}}D_{x}\ket{x}\ .

This is known as magic state injection, and is commonly simulated with post-selection
[
Bravyi2019simulating_q_circuits_by_low_rank_stabilizer_decomp
]
, which reduces the size of the state injection gadget.
Specifically, if
D
acts on
n
qubits, the magic state injection gadget uses
n

CX
gates and
n
measurement gates with post-selection, as well as
n
ancillary bits to store
\ket{D}
.
