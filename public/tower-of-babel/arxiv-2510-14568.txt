Decidability and Characterization of Expansivity for Group Cellular Automata Journal: Journal of Computer and System Sciences
Niccolò Castronuovo, Alberto Dennunzio, Luciano Margara

Abstract
Group cellular automata are continuous, shift-commuting endomorphisms of G ℤ G^{\mathbb{Z}} , where G G is a finite group. We provide an easy-to-check characterization of expansivity for group cellular automata on abelian groups and we prove that expansivity is a decidable property for general (non-abelian) groups. Moreover, we show that the class of expansive group cellular automata is strictly contained in that of topologically transitive injective group cellular automata.



1
Introduction

Cellular Automata (CAs) are discrete-time dynamical systems consisting of a regular grid of variables, each of them taking values from a finite set called alphabet. The
configuration of a CA is a snapshot of the values of all variables at a certain time
t
and it evolves in discrete time steps by means of a given local rule defining the CA. The local rule updates in a synchronous and homogeneous way each variable of the grid on the basis of the values at time
t-1
of its neighboring variables (for an introduction to CAs theory, see for example
[
1
,
2
]
).

CAs have been the subject of significant research and are successfully applied in various fields, including computer science, physics, mathematics, biology, and chemistry, for purposes such as simulating natural phenomena, generating pseudo-random numbers, processing images, analyzing universal computation models, and cryptography (see for example
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
).

One of the central challenges in CAs theory is describing the global behavior of a CA based on the analysis of its local rule. While the local rule has a finite representation (e.g., a finite table), the global behavior can encompass an arbitrarily large, potentially infinite, amount of information. In fact, the grid of variables whose values define a CA configuration may be infinite and the desired global behavior might only emerge after an arbitrarily large number of time steps.

Many properties related to the temporal evolution of general CAs have been proved to be undecidable (see Section
2
for an overview of the main ones).
Since in practical applications one needs to know if the CA used for modelling a certain system exhibits some specific property, this can be a severe issue.

Fortunately, the undecidability issue of dynamical properties of CAs can be tackled by placing specific constraints on the model. In many cases, like the one we are exploring in this paper, the alphabet and the local rule are restricted to being a finite group and a homomorphism, respectively, giving rise to group CAs (GCAs). We stress that these constraints do not at all prevent such CAs from being effective
in practical applications. In fact, GCAs can exhibit much of the complex behaviors of general CAs and they are often used in various applications (see for example
[
5
,
8
,
7
]
).

Over the past few decades, considerable effort has been devoted to study the decidability of the dynamical behavior of CAs defined on various algebraic structures, ranging from cyclic to general finite groups.
See Section
2
for an overview of the main results on this topic.

Expansivity is one of the most important and widely studied dynamical properties (see for example
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
).
It applies only to reversible systems. A reversible discrete-time dynamical system is said to be expansive if every pair of distinct configurations eventually separate under iterations of the system, either in the future or in the past. An expansive behavior of a system is a hallmark of chaotic dynamics, as it implies that even a small error in the description of the initial state of the system grows over time, making an accurate long-term prediction impossible. A classical example of an expansive system is the shift map in symbolic dynamics.

To our knowledge, there are no significant results on expansive GCAs.
This is due to the technical difficulties of studying reversible systems and the complexity that arises when dealing with non-abelian groups. Some preliminary findings exist for GCAs over
\mathbb{Z}_{m}

[
14
]
, along with more general—but less conclusive—results concerning automorphisms of compact groups
[
15
,
10
,
11
,
16
]
.

Results Overview

The main results of this paper can be summarized as follows.

-

A GCA
{\cal F}
on a group
G
is expansive if and only if
a finite number of simpler and computable GCAs obtained decomposing
{\cal F}
and
G
are expansive. Some of them are defined on abelian groups, while others, if any, are defined on products of non-abelian isomorphic simple groups (Theorem

-

A GCA
{\cal F}

is expansive if and only if the GCA
{\cal H}={\cal F}-{\cal F}^{-1}
is positively expansive (Theorem

).
Since positive expansivity is an easily checkable property on abelian groups and the inverse of a GCA is easily computable, we conclude that expansivity is an easily checkable property as well.

-

For GCAs defined on products of non-abelian isomorphic simple groups,
expansivity is equivalent to topological transitivity (Theorem
5
).

-

Expansivity for GCAs is a decidable property (Theorem
3
).

-

Expansive GCAs are topologically transitive (Theorem
6
), although the converse does not hold (Example
1
).

Decidability and Characterization of dynamical properties

Let
C
be a possibly infinite set of CA, such as the set of GCA.
Let
P
be a property that a CA may or may not satisfy, such as surjectivity or topological transitivity.

P
is decidable for
C
if and only if there exists an algorithm that, given a CA
{\cal F}\in C
, returns “Yes" if
F
satisfies
P
, and “No" otherwise.
If, instead of a property
P
, we consider a numerical function
N:C\to\mathbb{R}
, such as topological entropy or Lyapunov exponents,
N
is computable for
C
if and only if there exists an algorithm that, given a CA
{\cal F}\in C
, computes
N({\cal F})
.
Deciding a property (or computing a function) involves a computational cost that the notion of decidability (or computability) does not take into account. We will therefore say that a property is efficiently decidable (or a function is efficiently computable) if the algorithm that decides whether the property holds (or computes the function) is efficient, where efficient usually means polynomial-time.
As for CA, efficient algorithms typically analyze the structure of the local rule, which is a finite object, whereas inefficient algorithms usually operate on the space-time dynamics of the CA, which is potentially infinite in size.
The literature contains a number of results related to the decidability and computability of properties and functions, respectively, across various classes of CA.
Below, we list some of the most significant ones.
In what follows, we will denote by
D
-CA the class of
D
-dimensional CA. The same notation will also be used for GCA.

General CAs.

- Every non-trivial property of limit sets of 1-CA is undecidable
[
17
]
.

- Surjectivity and injectivity are decidable for 1-CA
[
18
]
and undecidable for 2-CA
[
19
]
.

- Topological entropy for 1-CA is uncomputable
[
20
]
.

- Topological entropy is efficiently computable for positively expansive CA
[
21
]
.

- Sensitivity to the initial conditions and topological transitivity are undecidable for 1-CA (even when restricting to the case of reversible 1-CA)
[
22
]
.

GCAs.

- Surjectivity and injectivity are decidable for
D
-GCA with
D\geq 1

[
23
]
.

- Strongly transitive and positively expansive GCA do not exist unless the underlying group is abelian
[
24
]
.

- Sensitivity to the initial conditions and equicontinuity
are decidable for
D
-GCA with
D\geq 1

[
23
]
.

- Non-transitivity is semi-decidable for
D
-GCA with
D\geq 1

[
23
]
.

GCAs on abelian groups.

- Sensitivity to the initial conditions, equicontinuity, topological transitivity, ergodicity, positive expansivity, and denseness of periodic orbits are efficiently decidable for 1-GCA on abelian groups

GCA on

Preliminaries

In this section, we review the fundamental definitions and key results related to CAs and GCAs. For additional definitions and results, we refer the reader to those introduced in
[
32
]
.

3.1
General CAs

Let
G
be a finite set. A CA
configuration
is any function from
\mathbb{Z}
to
G
, i.e., an element of
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Given a configuration
c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}
and any integer
i\in\mathbb{Z}
,
c_{i}
denotes the value of
c
at position
i
, while for any
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
with
i\leq j
,
c_{[i,j]}
denotes the word
c_{i}\cdots c_{j}\in G^{j-i+1}
and
c_{[i,+\infty)}
denotes the infinite word
c_{i}c_{i+1}\cdots
.
The set
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is also a topological space with the prodiscrete topology, i.e., the product topology when each factor
G
is given the discrete topology.
When equipped with that topology,
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
turns out to be a compact, Hausdorff, and totally disconnected topological space. Moreover,
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a Polish space, i.e., a separable completely metrizable topological space.
Indeed, the set
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
can be equipped with the standard Tychonoff distance
d

defined as

\forall c,c^{\prime}\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}:\ d(c,c^{\prime})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }c=c^{\prime}\\
2^{-\min\{|j|:j\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }c_{j}\neq c^{\prime}_{j}\}}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and the topology induced by the Tychonoff distance coincides with the prodiscrete topology. Since it has no isolated points, the set
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is also a Cantor space.

A
CA
on (the alphabet)
G
is any continuous function
{\cal F}:G^{\mathbb{Z}}\to G^{\mathbb{Z}}
which is also shift commuting, i.e.,
{\cal F}\circ\sigma=\sigma\circ{\cal F}
, where the shift map
\sigma:G^{\mathbb{Z}}\to G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is defined as follows

\forall c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}\;\;\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}:\ \sigma(c)_{i}=c_{i+1}.

Any CA can be equivalently defined by means of a local rule
f:G^{2\rho+1}\to G
, where
\rho\in\mathbb{N}

[
1
]
. Namely, a CA
{\cal F}
with local rule
f
is defined as follows:

\forall c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}\;\;\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}:\ {\cal F}(c)_{i}=f(c_{i-\rho},\dots,c_{i+\rho}).

The natural
{\rho}
, also denoted by
\rho({\cal F})
, will be said to be the
radius
of the CA
{\cal F}
.

A CA
{\cal F}
is said to be
injective
(respectively,
surjective
) if the map
{\cal F}
is injective (respectively, surjective).
We recall that injective CAs are also surjective, and, hence, bijective. Moreover, the inverse of any injective CA is a CA, too, and for this reason injective CAs are sometimes referred to as
reversible
.

We now recall the definitions of the dynamical properties that are investigated in this paper with particular emphasis on the first one.

Definition 1
.

An injective (reversible) CA
{\cal F}
is
expansive
if
there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that for any two configurations
c,c^{\prime}\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}
, there exists an integer
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

d\big({\cal F}^{n}(c),{\cal F}^{n}(c^{\prime})\big)>\epsilon.

Similarly, a (possibly non injective) CA
{\cal F}
is
positively expansive
if the previous condition holds but with the difference that the involved integer
n
is required to be positive.

Definition 2
.

A CA
{\cal F}
is
topologically transitive
if for any pair of nonempty open subsets
U,V\subseteq G^{\mathbb{Z}}
there exists a natural
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\cal F}^{n}(U)\cap V\neq\emptyset
.

3.2
Background on Groups and Group CAs

Let
G
be a group with identity element
e
. Unless otherwise stated, the operation will be the multiplication. As usual, if the operation is commutative then the group is said to be
abelian
.
A set
H\subseteq G

is a
subgroup
of
G
, denoted by
H\leq G
, if
H
forms a group with the same operation of
G
. The set
\{e\}
is called the
trivial
group and is a subgroup of any group. The
centralizer
of a subset
S\subseteq G
is the subgroup

C_{G}(S)=\{x\in G:xg=gx\;\text{ for all }g\in S\}
.

A subgroup
N
of
G
is said to be
normal
(denoted by
N\trianglelefteq G
) if for all
g\in G
and
n\in N
, it holds that
gng^{-1}\in N
, where
g^{-1}
is the inverse element of
g
. Let
N
be a normal subgroup of
G
. The
quotient group
is the set

G/N=\{gN:g\in G\}
, where each element
gN=\{gn:n\in N\}
is called
left coset
of
N
in
G
and it is also denoted by
[g]
.
The operation on
G/N
is the coset multiplication:

(gN)(hN)=(gh)N

for all
g,h\in G
.
The group
G
is called
simple group
if it is a nontrivial group and it has no proper nontrivial normal subgroups.

Given two groups
G
and
H
, their
direct product
is the group
G\times H
consisting of ordered pairs
(g,h)
, where
g\in G
and
h\in H
, with the group operation defined component-wise:

(g_{1},h_{1})\cdot(g_{2},h_{2})=(g_{1}\cdot g_{2},h_{1}\cdot h_{2}).

The identity element of
G\times H
is
(e_{G},e_{H})
, where
e_{G}
and
e_{H}
are the identity elements of
G
and
H
, respectively.

A
homomorphism
between two groups
G
and
H
is a map
\varphi:G\to H
such that for all
a,b\in G
,

\varphi(a\cdot b)=\varphi(a)\cdot\varphi(b).
The set of all homomorphisms from
G
to
H
is denoted
\operatorname{Hom}(G,H)
.
A bijective homomorphism
\varphi:G\to H
is called
isomorphism
and
G
and
H
are said to be
isomorphic
(denoted
G\cong H
).
An
endomorphism

is a homomorphism
from a group to itself and

\mathrm{End}(G)

stands for the set of all the endomorphisms
of a group
G
.

The
kernel
of a homomorphism
\varphi:G\to H
is the set

Ker(\varphi)=\{g\in G:\varphi(g)=e_{H}\},

where
e_{H}
is the identity element of
H
. The kernel is a normal subgroup of
G
. The homomorphism
\varphi
is said to be
trivial
iff
Ker(\varphi)=G
. As usual,
\mathrm{Im}(h)
denotes the image of
h
.

A
fully invariant
subgroup of a group
G
is a subgroup
H
of
G
such that, for every
\phi\in\mathrm{End}(G)
,
\phi(H)\leq H
.
In other terms, the restriction of an endomorphism of
G
to a fully invariant subgroup
H
induces an endomorphism on
H
.
We recall that a fully invariant subgroup is normal.
A group
G
is called invariantly simple if its only fully invariant
subgroups are the trivial subgroup and
G
itself.
It is known that every invariantly simple finite group is a direct product of isomorphic simple groups
[
24
]
.

Let
G
be now a
finite
group with identity element
e
. The set
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is also a group, with the component-wise operation defined by the group operation of
G
, and we denote by
e^{\mathbb{Z}}
the configuration taking the value
e
at every integer position, i.e.,
e^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the identity element of the group
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Clearly, when equipped with the prodiscrete topology,
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
turns out to be both a profinite and Polish group. A configuration
c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is said to be
finite
if the number of positions
i\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
c_{i}\neq e
is finite.

If
H\leq G
, then
H^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a closed subgroup of
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
. Moreover,
H\trianglelefteq G
if and only if
H^{\mathbb{Z}}\trianglelefteq G^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In this case, the prodiscrete topologies on
H^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
(G/H)^{\mathbb{Z}}
agree with the subspace topology on
H^{\mathbb{Z}}
and with the quotient topology on
(G/H)^{\mathbb{Z}}
, respectively.

A CA
{\cal F}:G^{\mathbb{Z}}\to G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is said to be a
group CA (GCA)
if
{\cal F}
is an endomorphism of
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
.
In that case, by
[
33
, Lemma 3]
, the local rule of
{\cal F}
is a homomorphism
f:G^{2\rho+1}\to G
. Furthermore, given any function
f:G^{2\rho+1}\to G
, by
[
34
, Lemma 8]
(see also the later
[
32
, Thm. 1]
), it holds that
f\in\mathrm{Hom}(G^{2\rho+1},G)
if and only if there exist
2\rho+1
endomorphisms
h_{-\rho},\ldots,h_{\rho}\in\mathrm{End}(G)
, such that
f(g_{-\rho},\ldots,g_{\rho})=h_{-\rho}(g_{-\rho})\cdots h_{\rho}(g_{\rho})
for all
g_{-\rho},\ldots,g_{\rho}\in G
and

\mathrm{Im}(h_{i})\subseteq C_{G}(\mathrm{Im}(h_{j}))
for every
i\neq j
.
In this case, we will write
f=(h_{-\rho},\ldots,h_{\rho})
. Notice that some of the
h_{i}
’s could be trivial but we will always assume, if not otherwise stated, that at least one between
h_{-\rho}
and
h_{\rho}
is non-trivial.

A GCA with local rule
f
is called shift-like if
\rho\geq 1
and only one between the
h_{i}
’s is non trivial, while it is called identity-like if
\rho=0
. Surjective shift-like GCA are topologically transitive.

4
Expansivity on abelian groups

We define the following two subsets of
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
for any finite group
G
.

\displaystyle L(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}:\ c_{k}\neq e\text{ and }c_{i}=e\text{ for }i>k\}

\displaystyle R(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{c\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}:\ c_{k}\neq e\text{ and }c_{i}=e\text{ for }i<k\}

Sometimes we will refer to elements of
L(k)
as left fronts and to elements of
R(k)
as right fronts.
The position of an element
c
in
L(k)
or
R(k)
, denoted by
pos(c)
, is
k
.
A potential ambiguity in the notation could arise when referring to the position of a configuration that belongs to both
L(k)
and
R(k^{\prime})
(i.e., a finite configuration). Since this situation never occurs, we will avoid overloading the notation by introducing separate left and right positions.

The following remark is an immediate consequence of the definition of expansivity and
the additivity of
{\cal F}
.

Remark 1
.

Let
{\cal F}
be any injective GCA on a finite group
G
and let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It holds that

{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if

\displaystyle\forall c\in L(k)\;\;\exists n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ pos\bigl({\cal F}^{n}(c)\bigr)>k\text{ and }

(1)

\displaystyle\forall c\in R(k)\;\;\exists n\in\mathbb{Z}:\ pos\bigl({\cal F}^{n}(c)\bigr)<k.

(2)

A counterpart of the equivalence stated in Remark
1
holds as far as positive expansivity and any (possibly non injective) GCA
{\cal F}
are concerned, with the difference that the integer
n
involved in both conditions (
1
) and (
2
) is required to be positive.

Since
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
is a compact space, we are able to prove the following Lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
{\cal F}
be any injective GCA on a finite group
G
and let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. It holds that

{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if there exists a natural
k_{{\cal F}}>0
such that:

\displaystyle\forall c\in L(k)\;\;\exists n\in\{-k_{{\cal F}},\dots,k_{{\cal F}}\}:\ pos\bigl({\cal F}^{n}(c)\bigr)>k\text{ and }

(3)

\displaystyle\forall c\in R(k)\;\;\exists n\in\{-k_{{\cal F}},\dots,k_{{\cal F}}\}:\ pos\bigl({\cal F}^{n}(c)\bigr)<k.

(4)

Proof.

The “only if” implication is trivial. We now prove the “if” implication.

As we will see, the existence of this value
k_{{\cal F}}
follows from the compactness of
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that for every
k_{{\cal F}}>0
condition (
3
) is false (the same reasoning applies assuming condition (
4
) to be false).
Let
c\in L(k)
and let

m(c)=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}:\ \max\{pos({\cal F}^{-i}(c)),\dots,pos({\cal F}^{i}(c))\}>k\}

or, equivalently,

m(c)=\min\{i\in\mathbb{N}:\ \max\{pos({\cal F}^{-i}(c)),pos({\cal F}^{i}(c))\}>k\}.

Since
{\cal F}
is expansive, for every
c\in L(k)
we have
m(c)<\infty
.
Since
(
3
)
is false for every
k_{{\cal F}}>0
, we can find a sequence
s=(c^{(i)})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of configurations in
L(k)
such that
for every

we have
m(c^{(i+1)})>m(c^{(i)})
.
Since

is a compact space, we can extract from

converging to some

. Since for every

,

as well.
We now prove that

cannot be finite and then
{\cal F}
is not expansive.
By the definition of

, either

pos({\cal F}^{m(c)}(c))>k
or

pos({\cal F}^{-m(c)}(c))>k
.
Without loss of generality assume that
pos({\cal F}^{m(c)}(c))>k
.

Since
c^{(i_{j})}
converges to

m(c^{(i_{j})})
is a strictly increasing sequence, for every

we can always pick
an element

in the sequence
(c^{(i_{j})})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
such that

m(\overline{c})>m(c)\text{ and }\overline{c}_{[k-h,k]}=c_{[k-h,k]}

that leads to
a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 2

Let
{\cal F}

Since

is an upper angular configuration,
l
must be negative.

Consider now the ordered set

A=\{{\cal F}^{-1}(c),\dots,{\cal F}^{-k_{\cal F}}(c)\}
.
Let
x\in A
be the first element such that

pos(x)>k
. Since
x
is the first element of
A
for which

pos(x)>k
, then
x
is an upper angular configuration as well.
Therefore, the theorem can be applied again starting from
c=x
.

All elements
y\in A
that precede
x
will satisfy
pos(y)\leq k<pos(x)
, and therefore the statement is also proved for them.
∎

In the following lemma,
we prove that for an injective
{\cal F}
which is also expansive, if the position of a left front
c
increases, after
n
iterations of
{\cal F}^{-1}
,
by more than a constant threshold (independent of
c
), then
{\cal F}^{-n}(c)
cannot be
a lower angular configuration.
Similarly, if the position of a left front
c
increases, after
n
iterations of
{\cal F}
,
by more than a constant threshold (independent of
c
), then
{\cal F}^{n}(c)
cannot be
an upper angular configuration.

Lemma 3
.

Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on
G
which is also expansive.
Let
c\in L(k)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
pos({\cal F}^{-n}(c))>k+k_{\cal F}\,\rho({\cal F}^{-1})
then

{\cal F}^{-n}(c)
cannot be a lower angular configuration.

Proof.

Assume, for contradiction, that
{\cal F}^{-n}(c)
is a lower angular configuration
with
pos({\cal F}^{-n}(c))>k+k_{\cal F}\,\rho({\cal F}^{-1})
.
Let
c_{max}
be a configuration in

\{{\cal F}^{-i}(c)\}_{0\leq i\leq n}
having the maximum position.
This ensures that

pos(c_{max})\geq pos({\cal F}^{-n}(c))
. By construction,
c_{max}
is a lower angular configuration as well.
In order to have
{\cal F}^{m}(c_{max})=c
we need that

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{pos(c_{max})-pos(c)}{\rho({\cal F}^{-1})}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{pos({\cal F}^{-n}(c))-pos(c)}{\rho({\cal F}^{-1})}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle k_{\cal F}

But
m
cannot be larger than
k_{\cal F}
, otherwise
c_{\max}
would violate
Lemma
1
.
∎

In the reminder of this section, we will deal with GCAs on abelian groups. For this reason, for a sake of simplicity, the “additive” notation will be employed: the group operation will be denoted by
+
, and so
-
will be used as far as an inverse element is involved.

We are now ready to prove the following theorem, which establishes the equivalence between the expansivity of
{\cal F}
and the positive expansivity of
{\cal F}-{\cal F}^{-1}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p>1
be a prime number and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
{\cal F}
be any injective GCA on
G=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
. It holds that

{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if the GCA
{\cal H}={\cal F}-{\cal F}^{-1}
on
G
is positively expansive.

Proof.

We are going to show the two implications separately.

We first prove that if
{\cal H}
is positively expansive, then
{\cal F}
is expansive.

Suppose instead that
{\cal F}
is not expansive.
Then, there exists a configuration
c\in L(k)

(the same reasoning applies to any
c\in R(k)
as well)
such that for every
i\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
pos({\cal F}^{i}(c))\leq k
.
Since for every
m\in\mathbb{N}

{\cal H}^{m}(c)=({\cal F}-{\cal F}^{-1})^{m}(c)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}\,{\cal F}^{\,m-2j}(c),

we get that
pos({\cal H}^{m}(c))\leq k
.
Hence,
{\cal H}
is not positively expansive.

We now prove that if

{\cal F}
is expansive, then
{\cal H}
is positively expansive.

Let
c
be a configuration in
L(k)
(the same reasoning applies to
R(k)
as well).
Let
U(c)
and
D(c)
be two sequences of integers defined by

\displaystyle\forall i\geq 0:\ D(c)_{i}=pos({\cal F}^{i}(c))\text{ and }

\displaystyle\forall i\leq 0:\ U(c)_{i}=pos({\cal F}^{i}(c)).

If
{\cal F}
is expansive, then at least one of the two sequences is unbounded.
Without loss of generality, suppose that
D(c)
is unbounded.
This implies that there is an infinite ordered set
I(c)
of positive integers
such that

\forall i\in I(c)\;\;\forall j\in\{0,\dots,i-1\}:\ D(c)_{i}>D(c)_{j}.

From the definition of
I(c)
, it immediately follows that

\forall l\in I(c)\text{ with }l\geq k_{\cal F}:\ {\cal F}^{l}(c)\text{ is a lower angular configuration.}

By applying Lemma
2
, we can state that

\forall l\in I(c)\text{ with }l\geq k_{\cal F}:\ I(c)_{l+1}-I(c)_{l}\leq k_{\cal F}.

In other words, two successive lower angular configurations cannot be more than
k_{\cal F}
iterations of
{\cal F}
apart.

For
{\cal H}
not to be positively expansive, it is necessary that there exists
c\in L(k)
such that for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have
pos({\cal H}^{i}(c))\leq k
and, in particular,

pos({\cal H}^{p^{\alpha}}(c))\leq k
for every
\alpha\geq 0
.
Since
{\cal H}^{p^{\alpha}}={\cal F}^{p^{\alpha}}-{\cal F}^{-p^{\alpha}}
, we conclude that

pos({\cal H}^{p^{\alpha}}(c))\leq k\implies{\cal F}^{p^{\alpha}}(c)_{[k+1,+\infty)}={\cal F}^{-p^{\alpha}}(c)_{[k+1,+\infty)}.

Unfortunately, it is not necessarily the case that
p^{\alpha}\in I(c)
or, equivalently, that
{\cal F}^{p^{\alpha}}(c)
is a lower angular configuration.
But, by Lemma
2
, we can always find an index
q_{\alpha}\in I(c)
such that
|p^{\alpha}-q_{\alpha}|\leq k_{\cal F}
. Since
q_{\alpha}\in I(c)
, then
{\cal F}^{q_{\alpha}}(c)
is a lower angular
configuration.

By taking
\alpha
sufficiently large, we can ensure that
pos({\cal F}^{q_{\alpha}}(c))
becomes arbitrarily large as well. Since

{\cal F}^{p^{\alpha}}(c)_{[k+1,+\infty)}={\cal F}^{-p^{\alpha}}(c)_{[k+1,+\infty)}\text{ and }|p^{\alpha}-q_{\alpha}|\leq k_{\cal F},

the configuration
{\cal F}^{-q_{\alpha}}(c)
is guaranteed to be a lower angular configuration.
However, by Lemma
3
,
{\cal F}^{-q_{\alpha}}(c)
cannot be an angular configuration.
∎

Theorem
1
can be generalized to abelian groups as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Let
{\cal F}
be any injective GCA on an abelian group
G
. It holds that

{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if the GCA
{\cal H}={\cal F}-{\cal F}^{-1}
on
G
is positively expansive.

Proof.

For
G=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
the thesis follows from Theorem
1
.
To extend the result from
G=(\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})^{n}
to general abelian groups,
it is enough to apply the technique introduced in
[
28
, Theorem 2, Corollary 1, and Theorem 3]
.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Expansivity is an easy-to-check property for injective GCAs on abelian groups.

Proof.

By the results from
[
28
]
positive expansivity turns out to be an easy-to-check property for GCAs on abelian groups. Moreover,
since the inverse of an injective GCA on an abelian group is easily computable,
by Theorem
2
we conclude that expansivity is an easy-to-check property for injective GCAs on abelian groups as well.
∎

Expansivity on general groups

Let

For every element

Definition 4

Let
G

.
Let

We define the maps

\displaystyle:H^{\mathbb{Z}}\to H^{\mathbb{Z}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\cal F}}_{H}

\displaystyle\forall c\in H^{\mathbb{Z}}:\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\cal F}}_{H}(c):={\cal F}(c),

(5)

\displaystyle\forall[c]\in(G/H)^{\mathbb{Z}}:\quad

(6)

Note that, by Equations (

It is straightforward to verify that if

The next theorem will enable us to reduce the study of the expansivity of any GCA to
the study of the expansivity of two GCAs defined on smaller groups.

Theorem 3
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
H\trianglelefteq G
. Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on
G
such that
{\cal F}(H^{\mathbb{Z}})\subseteq H^{\mathbb{Z}}
. It holds that
{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if both the (injective) GCAs
\widetilde{{\cal F}}
and
\overline{{\cal F}}
are expansive.

Proof.

First of all, note that, by
[
24
, Theorem 2]
, both the GCAs
\widetilde{{\cal F}}
and
\overline{{\cal F}}
are injective. The equivalence regarding expansivity follows directly from
[
35
, Theorem A]
.
We point out that in
[
35
]
the authors adopt a slightly different definition of expansivity:
an automorphism
\alpha
of
G
is called expansive if there exists a neighborhood
U
of

such that

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\alpha^{n}(U)=\{e\}.

Since the prodiscrete topology on

is induced by the bi-invariant

, this definition of expansivity is equivalent to that provided in Definition

from Section
3
(see also
[

]
).
∎

We now briefly recall (in a slightly modified form) the decomposition function for GCAs introduced in

Function

The function

takes as input a GCA

and produces
a finite collection

of GCAs, where each

is either abelian or non-abelian invariantly simple (see

for details). Moreover,

is injective iff each

Note that if

is abelian, then it is isomorphic to

for some prime

.
This follows from the fact that all simple abelian groups are isomorphic to

We are now ready to state the following result.

Theorem 4
.

Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on

The following equivalence holds:

({\cal F},G)\text{ is expansive }\iff\forall i\in\{1,\dots,k\}:\ ({\cal F}_{i},G_{i})\text{ is expansive.}

The thesis follows from the definition of the

We recall the following definition (see
[
24
]
).

Definition 5
.

A group
G
that is the product
S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{m}
of finite non-abelian isomorphic simple groups
S_{i}
is said to be

with respect to the action of a given GCA
{\cal F}
on

if there do not exist two non-empty, disjoint sets

with

such that

{\cal F}\left(\left(\prod_{i\in I}S_{i}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\subseteq\left(\prod_{i\in I}S_{i}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}\text{ and }{\cal F}\left(\left(\prod_{i\in J}S_{i}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}\right)\subseteq\left(\prod_{i\in J}S_{i}\right)^{\mathbb{Z}}.

Clearly, minimality is a decidable property.

The next theorem plays a crucial role in extending the
decidability of expansivity from abelian groups to general groups.

Theorem 5
.

Let
G
be a product of finite non-abelian isomorphic simple groups. Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on
G
.
It holds that
{\cal F}
is topologically transitive if and only if it is expansive. Furthermore, both these properties are decidable.

Proof.

If
G
is not minimal with respect to
{\cal F}
, then we factor
({\cal F},G)
into the direct product of a finite number of GCAs
\{({\cal F}_{1},G_{1}),\dots,({\cal F}_{k},G_{k})\}

such that every
G_{i}
is a product of non-abelian isomorphic simple groups that is also minimal with respect to
{\cal F}_{i}
.
Clearly,
{\cal F}
is expansive (respectively, topologically transitive) on
G
if and only if, for every
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
,
{\cal F}_{i}
is expansive (respectively, topologically transitive) on
G_{i}
.
Hence, without loss of generality, we will assume for the remainder of this proof that
G
is minimal with respect to
{\cal F}
.

Write
G=S_{1}\times\cdots\times S_{m},
where the
S_{i}
’s are finite non-abelian isomorphic simple groups. Denote by
f=(h_{-\rho},\ldots,h_{\rho})
the local rule of
{\cal F}
.
Assume that
{\cal F}
is surjective, otherwise,

{\cal F}
would be neither topologically transitive nor expansive and the thesis would be proved.
By
[
24
, Lemma 11]
we know that
for every
i\in\{-\rho,\dots,\rho\}
there exists
J_{i}\subseteq\{1,\dots,m\}
such that

\mathrm{Im}(h_{i})=\prod_{t\in J_{i}}S_{t}
. Moreover, there exist two positive integers
K
and
C
depending only on
G
and

such that
{\cal F}^{K}=\sigma^{-C\Delta}
where

\Delta=\sum_{i=-\rho}^{\rho}i|J_{i}|.

(7)

Hence, either
{\cal F}^{K}
is the identity map (when
\Delta=0
), or it is a non-negative power of the shift map (when
\Delta\neq 0
).
In the first case,
{\cal F}
is neither topologically transitive nor expansive, whereas in the second case,

is both topologically transitive and expansive.

For deciding
both these properties, it is sufficient to compute the value of

∎

In the next theorem, we show that for injective GCAs, expansivity implies topological transitivity.
For general CAs, to the best of our knowledge, this has not yet been established.

Theorem 6
.

All injective GCAs that are expansive are also topologically transitive.

Proof.

By
[
24
, Theorem 4]
, it is sufficient to prove the thesis for abelian groups and for products of simple non-abelian isomorphic groups.

Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on an abelian group
G
. Suppose by contradiction that

{\cal F}
is not topologically transitive. By
[
36
]
we know that either
{\cal F}

is not surjective or there exists
n>0
such that
{\cal F}^{n}-I
is not surjective, where
I
denotes the identity map.
By hypothesis, since
{\cal F}

is injective, it is also surjective. So, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
{\cal F}^{n}-I
is not surjective.
By the Garden of Eden Theorem
[
37
,
38
]
, this implies that there exist two finite configurations
x,y\in G^{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
({\cal F}^{n}-I)(x)=({\cal F}^{n}-I)(y)
and, hence,
there is a finite configuration
c=x-y
such that
({\cal F}^{n}-I)(c)=e^{\mathbb{Z}}
or, equivalently,
{\cal F}^{n}(c)=c
.
Since

is finite and
{\cal F}^{n}(c)=c
, for every

we can find a sufficiently large

such that

\forall i\in\mathbb{Z}:\ d({\cal F}^{i}(\sigma^{j_{\epsilon}}(x)),{\cal F}^{i}(\sigma^{j_{\epsilon}}(y)))<\epsilon.

This shows that
{\cal F}
is not expansive.

In the case of products of simple non-abelian isomorphic groups, the thesis has been already proved in Theorem
5
.
∎

An alternative and completely different approach to proving Theorem

is to show that the traces of expansive GCAs are transitive subshifts of finite type
[

.

Remark 2
.

We stress that, as a consequence of Theorem
6
and the results from
[
24
]
concerning the equivalence of topological transitivity to the main topological mixing and ergodic properties, it also holds that expansive (reversible) GCAs exhibit all those properties.

Furthermore, since for GCAs on simple non-abelian isomorphic groups, expansivity is equivalent to topologically transitivity (Theorem
5
), any GCA that is topologically transitive but not expansive must be defined on an abelian group. In the following example, we show that such a GCA indeed exists. Before illustrating it, recall that a Linear CA
{\cal F}
over
G=(\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^{n}
is a GCA on

defined by

matrices with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
and by a local rule which is a linear combination matrices-vectors. In this way, an

of Laurent polynomials in the variables
X
and
X^{-1}
and with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
is associated with

. Almost all the dynamical properties of
{\cal F}
have been characterized in terms of easy-to-check conditions on the characteristic polynomial of

.

Example 1
.

Let
G=(\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})^{2}
and let
{\cal F}

be the Linear CA over
G
having the following matrix associated with it:

M_{\cal F}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&X\end{bmatrix}.

Since
\det(M_{\cal F})=1
, it holds that

{\cal F}
is injective.
The characteristic polynomial of
M_{\cal F}
is
\chi(t)=1+t^{2}+tX
.
Since
\gcd(1+t^{2},t)=1
, by the results from
[
29
]
we get that

{\cal F}
is topologically transitive. Now, the matrix associated with

We are going to show that

is not expansive.

with

. Since neither

contains polynomials in which

appears with negative exponents, we get that

for every

Hence, we conclude that
{\cal F}
is not expansive.

Based on the results proved so far, we can now establish the most significant result of this work.

Theorem 7
.

Expansivity for GCAs is decidable.

Proof.

Let
{\cal F}
be an injective GCA on a group
G
.
Let

\{({\cal F}_{1},G_{1}),\dots,({\cal F}_{k},G_{k})\}={\tt Decomposition}({\cal F},G).

By Theorem
3
,
{\cal F}
is expansive if and only if
all the GCAs
({\cal F}_{i},G_{i})
are expansive.
By Theorem

, if

is abelian testing the expansivity of

is decidable.
By Theorem

, if

is not abelian expansivity is decidable as well.
∎

6
Conclusions and further work

The dynamical behavior of group automorphisms is a central topic in the theory of dynamical systems. In 1975, Nobuo Aoki
[
15
]
proved that ergodic automorphisms of compact metric groups are isomorphic to Bernoulli shifts. This result highlights the strong connection between ergodic automorphisms and Bernoulli shifts, which serve as a prototypical example of expansive dynamical systems.
In this paper, we have focused on a specific class of compact metric groups, namely
G^{\mathbb{Z}}
with
G
a finite group, and on a specific class of automorphisms, namely those that are continuous and commute with the shift map, i.e., the injective group cellular automata.
For this class of automorphisms, we proved that expansivity is a decidable property and implies topological transitivity (that is not known to be decidable), which in turn is equivalent to ergodicity. Moreover, we showed that there exist topologically transitive injective GCAs that are not expansive, demonstrating that the isomorphism proposed by Aoki does not preserve expansivity.

Future work includes incorporating into our results a study of the computational cost of the algorithms we propose for deciding the expansivity of GCAs, which was not addressed in the present work. It would also be interesting to extend our analysis to more general algebraic structures beyond groups, and to automorphisms that do not commute with the shift.
