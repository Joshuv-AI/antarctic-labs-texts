Acute Heart Failure Caused by a Ruptured Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysm: A Rare Presentation
Authors: Mucahit Tan, Sinan Karakus, Naci Babat, Sahin Sahinalp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sinus of valsalva aneurysm (SOV) is a rare aortic pathology that may be congenital or acquired and often remains clinically silent until rupture. When rupture occurs, it can lead to acute and life-threatening hemodynamic deterioration. We report the case of a 65-year-old male who presented with sudden-onset dyspnea and peripheral edema and was diagnosed with acute heart failure secondary to a ruptured SOV. Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, followed by computed tomography angiography, demonstrated a ruptured aneurysm originating from the noncoronary sinus with significant left-to-right shunting. Owing to extensive aortic root involvement, the patient underwent a Bentall procedure with successful clinical outcome. This case highlights the importance of considering ruptured SOV in the differential diagnosis of acute heart failure, particularly in elderly patients, and underscores the critical role of comprehensive imaging and timely surgical intervention in guiding optimal management.

INTRODUCTION

The sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOV) is a rare cardiovascular anomaly characterized by abnormal dilation of the aortic sinus. It may be congenital – often associated with other structural cardiac defects such as ventricular septal defects or bicuspid aortic valves – or acquired, typically resulting from trauma, infection, or connective tissue disorders. Although SOV often remains asymptomatic until rupture, its rupture can lead to life-threatening complications including heart failure, arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death. Timely diagnosis is of critical importance and management depends on the size, location, and rupture status of the aneurysm. While asymptomatic cases may be managed conservatively with close follow-up, symptomatic or ruptured aneurysms generally require surgical repair or transcatheter intervention to prevent the fatal outcomes.[1]

In this report, we present the case of a 65-year-old male patient with acute heart failure who was diagnosed with a ruptured SOV originating from the noncoronary cusp. The patient underwent a successful Bentall procedure. This case is notable due to the patient’s advanced age, the atypical rupture site into the right atrium from the noncoronary sinus, and the presentation with acute decompensated heart failure despite preserved left ventricular systolic function, ultimately necessitating a Bentall procedure rather than isolated repair.

CASE REPORT

A 65-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department with progressively worsening dyspnea over the past few days. On physical examination, he was conscious, oriented, and cooperative; blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, heart rate 80 bpm with a regular rhythm, and respiratory rate 24 breaths per min. His medical history included diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and chronic deep-vein thrombosis. The patient was on regular medication with apixaban 5 mg twice daily, gliclazide 60 mg twice daily, and atorvastatin 20 mg once daily. Fine crepitant rales were heard at the lung bases and bilateral pretibial edema (+/+) was noted. Electrocardiography showed sinus rhythm with normal QRS and ST segments. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 55%, left ventricular hypertrophy, moderate tricuspid regurgitation, and an estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of 60 mmHg. A structure adjacent to the right atrium and the interventricular septum was suggestive of a SOV. The patient subsequently underwent transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) [Figure 1], which confirmed the pathological appearance consistent with SOV. TEE demonstrated a saccular aneurysm originating from the noncoronary sinus of Valsalva, measuring approximately 20 mm × 18 mm, protruding into the right atrium through a narrow neck of about 6–8 mm. Color Doppler imaging revealed a continuous high-velocity left-to-right shunt through a rupture orifice estimated at 4–6 mm. To further evaluate the presence of additional anomalies and confirm the diagnosis, cardiac computed tomography angiography was performed [Figure 2a and b], revealing no critical coronary artery stenosis.

The patient was admitted to the coronary intensive care unit and urgent surgical intervention was planned. Given the involvement of the aortic root, the close proximity of the aneurysm to the aortic valve, and the risk of residual aortic insufficiency with isolated patch repair, a Bentall procedure was preferred by the cardiovascular surgery team. Congestive symptoms were managed with intravenous diuretics until the patient became euvolemic. Intraoperatively, the aneurysm was confirmed to originate from the noncoronary sinus and the aneurysmal sac was resected. A Bentall procedure was successfully performed [Figure 2c and d]. The postoperative course was stable and diuretic and anticoagulant therapy was continued.

DISCUSSION

SOV may develop due to either acquired or congenital causes. Aneurysms resulting from hereditary collagen disorders such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers–Danlos syndrome are considered congenital in origin, whereas infectious diseases such as bacterial endocarditis and syphilis, trauma, and elastic connective tissue degeneration leading to weakening of the sinus wall represent acquired causes.[12]

In the present case, the etiology of the SOV was carefully considered. Although SOVs are most commonly congenital and typically present in younger patients, several features in our patient favored an acquired or degenerative origin. These included advanced age at presentation, the absence of associated congenital cardiac anomalies such as ventricular septal defect or bicuspid aortic valve, and the lack of a known history of infective endocarditis. Degenerative changes related to long-standing hypertension and age-related weakening of the aortic wall may have contributed to aneurysm formation in this patient. Nevertheless, a congenital origin cannot be completely excluded, as some congenital SOVs may remain clinically silent until late adulthood.

Rupture of SOV has been reported in approximately 35% of cases and only about 25% of these present with acute symptoms. Rupture most commonly occurs into the right ventricle, followed by the right atrium. The complications of a ruptured SOV vary depending on its anatomical location and communication with the cardiac chambers. Advanced age at presentation is uncommon in ruptured SOV, which is usually diagnosed in younger adults due to its congenital origin.[3] Rupture from the noncoronary sinus into the right atrium, as observed in our patient, is less common and may present diagnostic challenges due to the nonspecific symptoms of right-sided heart failure.

The acute clinical presentation in this patient can be directly explained by the hemodynamic consequences of the continuous left-to-right shunt from the aorta to the right atrium. Rupture of the SOV resulted in abrupt volume overload of the right atrium and right ventricle, leading to elevated pulmonary artery pressures and acute right-sided heart failure. This hemodynamic burden accounts for the patient’s rapid onset of dyspnea, peripheral edema, and pulmonary congestion despite preserved left ventricular systolic function.

Marked elevations in liver transaminases were most likely secondary to acute hepatic congestion and hypoperfusion caused by right heart failure and increased central venous pressure. Similar laboratory abnormalities have been described in acute shunt-related heart failure and congestive hepatopathy. Although B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) levels were not available in this case, elevated BNP would be expected in the setting of acute cardiac volume overload and myocardial wall stress.

According to the 2024 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, surgery remains the cornerstone of treatment for adults with congenital heart disease, including SOV. However, there is no specific guideline dedicated exclusively to the surgical repair of SOV.[4] Depending on the size and location of the aneurysm and the involvement of the aortic valve, various surgical techniques may be employed. A common approach is aortic root replacement, in which the aneurysmal portion of the aortic root is replaced with a synthetic graft. This procedure may include valve-sparing techniques or valve replacement if the aortic valve is affected. Alternatively, in less extensive aneurysms, patch repair, involving reinforcement of the aneurysm with a patch, may be performed. Surgical mortality ranges between 1.9% and 3.6%, while the 15-year survival rate approaches 90%. Ohno et al. reported a successful Bentall procedure in a 76-year-old patient with unruptured multiple SOVs.[2] A limited number of cases in the literature have reported promising outcomes with transcatheter closure.[56] This approach offers a viable alternative to conventional open-heart surgery, particularly for patients who are considered high-risk surgical candidates due to age, comorbidities, or other factors. In the present case, the cardiovascular surgery team decided to proceed with urgent surgical intervention, and the patient underwent a Bentall procedure, in which the aortic root was replaced along with aortic valve replacement.

CONCLUSION

This case underscores the importance of recognizing ruptured SOV as a rare but reversible cause of acute heart failure. Comprehensive imaging and timely surgical decision-making are crucial, as the extent of aortic root involvement may necessitate complex procedures such as the Bentall operation, even in elderly patients.
