Comparing Hemostatic Efficacy of Four-factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate with Andexanet Alfa in an Apixaban-treated Polytrauma Male Porcine Model
Authors: Oliver Grottke, Till Braunschweig, Lars Heubner, Eva Herzog, Farahnaz Rayatdoost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This study directly compared the hemostatic efficacy of two first-line treatments for reversing the effects of direct oral anticoagulants in patients with massive bleeding, andexanet alfa and four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC), in an apixaban anticoagulated male porcine polytrauma model.Methods:Male pigs (N = 32) received 20 mg/day apixaban for 3 days. Trauma was simulated by inducing standard liver injuries and bilateral femur fractures. After resuscitation, the animals received control, 4F-PCC (25 or 50 U/kg), or andexanet alfa (1,000-mg bolus followed by 1,200-mg infusion over 2 h); n = 8 animals per group. Blood loss, survival, and coagulation parameters were evaluated.Results:Both 50 U/kg 4F-PCC and andexanet alfa treatment achieved 100% survival. Blood loss was significantly reduced compared with the control group (mean ± SD 3,843 ± 406 ml), with the largest effects observed for andexanet alfa (1,123 ± 197 ml) followed by 50 U/kg 4F-PCC (1,855 ± 372 ml) and then 25 U/kg 4F-PCC. Consistent with the mechanism of action, andexanet alfa led to significantly lower apixaban anti-factor Xa activity (15.6 ± 6.2 ng/ml) compared with control or 4F-PCC treatment until 180 min after trauma. However, 4F-PCC treatment was able to dose-dependently overcome the trauma-induced reduction in coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X in contrast to andexanet alfa. While andexanet alfa shortened lag time and increased peak thrombin generation, 4F-PCC dose-dependently improved peak height and endogenous thrombin potential. No thromboembolic complications were observed.Conclusions:In a male polytrauma porcine model of apixaban anticoagulation, both high-dose 4F-PCC and andexanet alfa were fully effective in achieving survival and hemostatic control. Andexanet alfa achieved hemostasis more rapidly, while 4F-PCC showed sustained restoration of factors II, VII, IX, and X levels, as well as thrombin generation.

The management of major bleeding events and the reversal of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), such as the factor Xa (FXa) inhibitor apixaban, remains a significant challenge for trauma surgery. In 2018, the recombinant coagulation FXa, andexanet alfa, was approved by the Food and Drug Administration for use in patients treated with the oral anticoagulants rivaroxaban and apixaban when the reversal of their anticoagulant effects are required in cases of life-threatening or uncontrolled bleeding.1 Andexanet alfa is a recombinant, enzymatically inactive, modified factor Xa molecule that acts as a decoy, which rapidly binds to FXa inhibitors with high affinity, thus decreasing their anticoagulant activity and restoring endogenous FXa activity.2

Clinical trials have demonstrated effective hemostasis with andexanet alfa in patients with FXa inhibitor–associated major bleeding. In the Andexanet Alfa, a Novel Antidote to the Anticoagulation Effects of FXa Inhibitors (ANNEXA-4) trial, which assessed the efficacy and safety of andexanet alfa in patients who had acute major bleeding within 18 h of FXa inhibitor administration, excellent or good hemostasis occurred in 80% of patients.3 Similarly, in the Andexanet for FXa inhibitor–associated intracerebral hemorrhage (ANNEXA-I) trial, which compared andexanet alfa with usual care in patients with FXa inhibitor–associated intracerebral hemorrhage, hemostatic efficacy was achieved in 67% of patients who received andexanet alfa compared with 53% of patients receiving usual care, consisting mostly of prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs; 85.5% of patients).4 However, thrombotic events occurred in approximately 10% of patients treated with andexanet alfa in both studies, with a higher incidence observed compared with usual care in ANNEXA-I (10.3% vs. 5.6%, respectively). Andexanet alfa is also associated with substantial acquisition costs, with reported per-treatment costs far exceeding those of PCCs, which has contributed to limited availability and adoption in clinical practice.5 In the absence of specific reversal agents, treatment guidelines recommend the off-label use of nonspecific agents such as PCCs.6–11 PCCs are plasma-derived products and include three-factor PCC, which contains coagulation factors II, IX, and X, and four-factor PCC (4F-PCC) containing all four vitamin K–dependent coagulation factors (II, VII, IX, and X), as well as proteins C and S. 4F-PCC has been shown in multiple preclinical and clinical studies to be an effective approach to managing DOAC-associated bleeding, including after treatment with rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban, and dabigatran.12–39

Currently, there are limited data evaluating the use of andexanet alfa in the surgical setting, and most studies assessing 4F-PCC in DOAC-treated patients focus on spontaneous major bleeding or intracerebral hemorrhage rather than trauma. Therefore, we conducted the current study to compare the efficacy of andexanet alfa and 4F-PCC in a male porcine polytrauma model of severe or life-threatening bleeding. We hypothesized a priori that treatment with 4F-PCC would result in hemorrhage control comparable to that achieved with andexanet alfa, with total blood loss over the predefined observation period defined as the primary outcome. Secondary endpoints included coagulation parameters (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time [aPTT], fibrinogen concentration, D-dimers, thrombin–antithrombin [TAT] complexes), viscoelastic measures, thrombin generation parameters, coagulation factor levels, hematologic variables, and hemodynamic measurements.

Materials and Methods

Study Site and Compliance

This study was conducted at the RWTH Aachen University Hospital (Aachen, Germany), adhering to the ethical standards of German legislation for animal research and conforming to the guidelines of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The Landesamt für Natur, Umwelt und Verbraucherschutz in Recklinghausen approved the research protocol (permit No. 84-02.04.2016.A091). Before surgical intervention, the pigs underwent a 7-day acclimatization period in an adequately ventilated environment. Fasting was enforced the night before surgery, but all animals had unrestricted access to water.

Experimental Methods

The study employed methods similar to those used in earlier research.16,20,23,25,40–42 Male German Landrace pigs (n = 32) with an average ± SD body weight of 42.3 ± 3.3 kg were included in the study. Only male pigs were used to reduce biological variability associated with sex-dependent differences in coagulation, hormonal status, and trauma response.

Each pig was administered a 20-mg daily dose of apixaban orally for 3 days. The apixaban dosing regimen was selected based on previous work from our group demonstrating that 20 mg orally once daily for 3 days produces reproducible and clinically relevant anticoagulation in a porcine polytrauma model, as evidenced by increased bleeding, elevated anti–factor Xa activity, and decreased thrombin generation.43,44 On the day of surgery, anesthesia was delivered via azaperone (4 mg/kg, intramuscular), atropine (0.1 mg/kg, intramuscular), and propofol (3 mg/kg, intravenously). The pigs were then subjected to midline laparotomy and cystostomy. Femur fractures were inflicted using a captive bolt gun (Karl Schermer and Co., Germany), while a specialized instrument, as referenced in other literature,16,42 was used to induce standardized liver injuries. The femur fracture plus solid-organ injury was intentionally included to create a clinically relevant polytrauma model, followed by uncontrolled hemorrhage. Hemorrhagic shock was managed by a predefined 5-min postinjury resuscitation using Ringer’s solution (1,000 ml over 5 min) followed by continuous infusion (40 ml · kg−1 · h−1 for 120 min and then 30 ml · kg−1 · h−1 for the next 180 min). The animals were continuously monitored using arterial, central venous and pulmonary arterial pressure; tail pulse oximetry; and electrocardiography. Hemodynamic compromise consistent with hemorrhagic shock was characterized by a marked reduction in arterial blood pressure requiring early fluid resuscitation. Detailed descriptions of monitoring, instrumentation, and physiologic variables are provided in the Supplemental Digital Content (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519).

After the procedural preparations, the pigs were randomly divided into four groups using a random number–based sequence generated in Microsoft Excel, each consisting of eight animals. The first group acted as a control, receiving only placebo, while the other three groups were administered their respective test agents 12 min after trauma induction. Specifically, one group received 4F-PCC at a dose of 25 U/kg (PCC25; Beriplex P/N; CSL Behring, Germany), another received 4F-PCC at 50 U/kg (PCC50), and the final group was treated with andexanet alfa with a 1,000-mg bolus followed by an infusion of 1,200 mg over a 2-h period (Ondexxya; AstraZeneca UK Limited, United Kingdom). The andexanet alfa dosing was selected based on pharmacodynamic considerations and previous preclinical evidence from our group demonstrating dose-related effectiveness for sustained reversal of apixaban anticoagulation in polytrauma models.43,44 The experiment was terminated either 300 min after trauma or upon early death of the animals (fig. 1). The surviving pigs were euthanized using pentobarbital. All animals that underwent trauma induction and received the assigned intervention were included in the analysis. No a priori exclusion criteria were defined for animals or data points. The animals were excluded from time point–specific analyses only if death occurred before the scheduled sampling time. Tissue samples from vital organs such as the heart, lungs, liver, and kidneys were collected and then processed in paraffin, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and subjected to further histopathologic analysis. All examinations were conducted by a pathologist who was blinded to the treatment groups.

Blood Sampling, Analytical Methods, and Blood Variables

Blood samples from the arterial line were taken at baseline, pretrauma, and at several points after injury: 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, and 300 min (fig. 1). Arterial blood gas analyses and electrolyte assessments were conducted using heparinized blood with the ABL700 Flex blood gas analyzer (Radiometer, Denmark). Hemoglobin levels and platelet counts were determined by analyzing blood drawn into potassium-EDTA anticoagulant tubes (final concentration of 1.9 mg/ml; Sarstedt, Germany) with the MEK-6108 standard hematology analyzer (Nihon Kohden, Germany; supplemental digital table 1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519). Specialized analyses required blood treated with a 3.2% sodium citrate solution (Sarstedt). Blood loss was measured at set intervals after trauma (10, 30, 60, 120, 180, and 300 min) by quantifying accumulated abdominal blood. Fracture-associated bleeding was not quantified, as hematoma formation occurs diffusely within the surrounding musculature and cannot be reliably measured in this experimental setting. If a subject died before the 300-min postinjury mark, the final blood sample was collected postmortem. Further details on the assays conducted on the blood specimens can be found in the Supplemental Digital Content (https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519).

Apixaban Calibrated Anti-FXa Activity

Anti–factor Xa activity was measured using a modified chromogenic assay based on the Coamatic heparin kit (Diapharma, USA), as previously described.45 Pooled pig plasma was spiked with defined concentrations of apixaban (1 mg/ml) to generate reference samples. The assay conditions were adjusted to minimize plasma dilution, and the same assay set-up was used for all samples with andexanet.43,44

Coagulation Tests and Thromboelastometry

From platelet poor plasma, several coagulation parameters were quantified, including aPTT, prothrombin time, D-dimer, fibrinogen concentration, and the activity of specific coagulation factors (II, VII, IX, and X). Factor activities are reported relative to assay reference plasma and may exceed 100% in this model. These measures were taken using the ACL TOP550 analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, Werfen Group, Germany). Whole-blood thromboelastometry was performed on the rotational thromboelastometry analyzer (Instrumentation Laboratory, Werfen Group), using 3.2% sodium citrate blood samples, per the manufacturer’s instructions. An extrinsically activated assay using recombinant tissue factor (EXTEM) assessed clotting time (s), clot formation time (s), and maximum clot firmness (mm), and an intrinsically activated assay using ellagic acid (INTEM) assessed clotting time.

Thrombin Generation Measurement

Thrombin generation was ascertained in plasma on a Fluoroskan Ascent plate reader (Thermo Labsystems OY, Finland) using final concentrations of 5 pM tissue factor and 4 μM phospholipids. Thrombinoscope software (version 4; Thrombinoscope BV, The Netherlands) analyzed thrombin generation curves to determine endogenous thrombin potential (ETP), lag time, time to peak, and peak thrombin formation. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, TAT complexes were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (Enzygnost TAT micro; Siemens, Germany).

Statistical Analysis

No formal power analysis was performed, as this was an exploratory study. The sample size of eight animals per group was selected based on our previous experience with similar porcine polytrauma models, which has proven sufficient to detect significant differences in bleeding and coagulation outcomes while maintaining adherence to ethical guidelines for animal use.22–25,43,44 For total blood loss, we employed a one-way analysis of variance, followed by post hoc Tukey–Kramer tests to compare the means among different groups. Secondary endpoints, such as coagulation parameters, blood cell counts, and hemodynamic variables, were evaluated using a repeated-measures analysis of variance with a mixed model utilizing restricted maximum likelihood. This model incorporated the type of intervention as a group factor and time as a repeated factor. To account for alpha error in multiple comparisons, the Sidak method was applied for correction. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data. Survival was analyzed using pairwise log-rank tests. All data are presented as means ± SD, with a significance level set at P < 0.05. No animals were excluded from study or from the primary analyses. Reduced sample sizes at later time points were solely due to death before completion of the 300-min observation period. In the control group, three animals were included 240 min after the trauma. Meanwhile, in the PCC25 group, six animals were included at 240 min after trauma, and five animals were included at 300 min after the trauma. A sham-operated group (n = 4) was included as a baseline reference; the data are shown only for descriptive references in thrombin generation curves but are excluded from statistical analysis. Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 28.0), and graphs were generated through GraphPad Prism (version 9.5.1; GraphPad Software, Inc., USA).

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Early Trauma Response

At baseline, all study groups exhibited comparable mean ± SD plasma concentrations of apixaban, averaging 218 ± 16 ng/ml. There was no statistically significant difference in the apixaban levels between the groups at the time of trauma induction (173 ± 12 ng/ml). Before trauma, all animal subjects exhibited similar values for plasmatic coagulation factors. Regarding hemodynamic values, all study groups started on similar physiologic conditions, with comparable heart rates, mean arterial pressures, and other vital indicators (Supplemental Digital Content fig. S1, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519).

A marked shift in physiologic state was observed across all groups 10 min after trauma induction, consistent with trauma-induced shock. Twenty minutes after trauma (i.e., 10 min after the initial andexanet alfa loading dose), anti-FXa activity was significantly lower in the andexanet alfa group compared with all other groups (15.6 ± 6.2 ng/ml; P < 0.0001 for all), with the greatest mean difference observed at 30 min after trauma. This difference diminished after 120 min (after cessation of the 2-h andexanet alfa infusion) but remained statistically significant until 180 min after trauma (P < 0.01 vs. control group and P < 0.001 vs. other groups). By 240 and 300 min after trauma, the mean apixaban anti-FXa activity among all groups was no longer significantly different (fig. 2).

Blood Loss

After trauma, blood loss in the immediate phase was comparable across all study groups, with no significant differences found at the 10-min time point (835 ± 135 ml; fig. 3A). Between 30 and 300 min after trauma, statistically significant differences in blood loss were evident, corroborated by physiologic markers such as increased heart rates and a drop in mean arterial pressure. Total postinjury blood loss was significantly higher in the control group (3,843 ± 406 ml) compared with the groups treated with PCC25 (2,865 ± 334 ml; P < 0.01), PCC50 (1,855 ± 372 ml; P < 0.001), and andexanet alfa (1,123 ± 197 ml; P < 0.001; fig. 3A), with differences in blood loss between treatment groups being significant (all P < 0.05).

Survival

The various interventions significantly influenced both survival duration and overall mortality. The control group experienced a 100% mortality rate with a mean survival time of 179 ± 40 min. Animals treated with 4F-PCC at 25 U/kg showed partial survival (37.5%; mean survival time, 238.6 ± 65.1 min). In contrast, all animals treated with 4F-PCC at 50 U/kg or andexanet alfa survived the entire 300-min observation period, resulting in 100% survival in both groups (fig. 3B).

Viscoelastometry

From 20 min after trauma, EXTEM clotting time was significantly shortened in the andexanet alfa group (44 ± 9 s) compared with the other groups (pooled mean, 95 ± 18 s; P < 0.05; Supplemental Digital Content fig. 2A, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519). This difference was not visible in INTEM clotting time; all three treatment groups showed significantly shortened INTEM clotting time compared with the control group (Supplemental Digital Content fig. 2B, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519). Compared with the 4F-PCC 25 group (113 ± 18 s), 4F-PCC 50 U/kg resulted in significantly shorter EXTEM clotting time at 180 min after trauma (91 ± 25 s; P < 0.05).

Prothrombin Time and aPTT

The control group displayed significantly elevated prothrombin time and aPTT compared with the other treatment groups (P < 0.001; Supplemental Digital Content fig. 3, https://links.lww.com/ALN/E519).

Markers of Thrombogenicity and Fibrinolysis

After reversal with andexanet alfa, D-dimer levels increased significantly compared with both PCC groups and the control group at 60 to 180 min; at 60 min, D-dimer concentrations were 2,110 ± 546 μg/l in the andexanet alfa group versus 686 ± 204 μg/l in the PCC25 group, 910 ± 167 μg/l in the PCC50 group, and 1,261 ± 368 μg/l in controls (P < 0.05). In contrast, D-dimer levels in both PCC groups showed only a marginal increase after substitution and remained lower in the PCC25 group than in control animals (fig. 4A).

Furthermore, the andexanet alfa group exhibited significantly higher fibrinogen concentrations at 60 min (0.87 ± 0.12 g/l) and persistently elevated levels up to 300 min (0.94 ± 0.1 g/l) after trauma compared with the other groups (P < 0.05; fig. 4B). Despite initially exhibiting similar TAT levels as the control group (11 ± 7 μg/l), the andexanet alfa group revealed a significant elevation in TAT levels at 20 min (101 ± 35 μg/l) until the end of the experiment (P < 0.05; fig. 4C).

Coagulation Factors

Subsequent to the inflicted trauma and ensuing hemorrhage (FII, 74 ± 15%; FVII, 121 ± 4%; FIX, 261 ± 36%; and FX, 63 ± 21%) but before the administration of study treatments, a uniform decrement in coagulation factors was observed across all animals (FII, 51 ± 10%; FVII, 82 ± 9%; FIX, 206 ± 20%; and FX, 39 ± 14%). Upon initial resuscitation and treatment administration, a dose-dependent augmentation was noted in factors II, VII, IX, and X within the PCC25 and PCC50 groups in line with the active ingredients and mechanism of action of 4F-PCC (fig. 5, A through D). In contrast, andexanet alfa administration resulted in no significant changes to the levels of factors II and IX (fig. 5, A and C), reflecting its role as a specific antidote for FXa inhibitors rather than a factor supplement; however, slight, nonsignificant elevations in FVII was observed after dosing, which did not persist at the magnitude seen with PCC treatments. At the higher 4F-PCC dose of 50 U/kg, elevations in FII, FIX, and FX were sustained throughout the observation period of 300 min after trauma (fig. 5, A, C, and D).

Thrombin Generation

Thrombin generation curves are shown in figure 6. In the context of thrombin generation preceding trauma, the administration of apixaban was linked to a prolongation of lag time (7.2 ± 2.6 min; P < 0.05). After treatment, the andexanet alfa group showed a considerable shortening in lag time (2.4 ± 0.4 min; P < 0.05) 8 min after administration, while only marginal reductions in lag time were evident in both 4F-PCC and control groups (PCC25, 4.6 ± 0.6 min; PCC50, 5.4 ± 1.9 min; control, 6.2 ± 1.7 min; P < 0.0001; fig. 7A). Moreover, andexanet alfa drastically curtailed the time to peak (3.8 ± 0.4 min; P < 0.05), while 4F-PCC exhibited no significant impact on this parameter (PCC25, 8.9 ± 1.3 min; PCC50, 10.9 ± 2.3 min), yielding values comparable to those of the control animals (9.7 ± 2.3 min; fig. 7B). Peak thrombin generation increased rapidly after andexanet alfa administration (96 ± 8.5 nM; P < 0.001), followed by a slight decline between 30 (91 ± 9.0 nM) and 120 min after trauma during the time of andexanet infusion (78.5 ± 12.4 nM; P < 0.05). Conversely, in the 4F-PCC groups, peak height exhibited a significant increase in overall magnitude (PCC25, 53 ± 8 nM; PCC50, 53 ± 22 nM at 30 min), which was sustained throughout the observation period (fig. 7C).

A dose-dependent enhancement in ETP was observed in the 4F-PCC groups after intervention (PCC25, 621 ± 102 nM × min; PCC50, 983 ± 407 nM × min; P < 0.0001), and both were significantly higher than control (226 ± 58 nM × min; P < 0.001 for each). In contrast, ETP in the andexanet alfa group (285 ± 21 nM × min) did not differ from control (P = 0.863) but was significantly lower than in both PCC-treated groups (P < 0.001; fig. 7D).

Histopathologic Assessment

The histopathologic examination did not unveil any distinctive differences among the study groups. Upon both macroscopic and microscopic inspection, employing hematoxylin and eosin along with elastica van Gieson stains, critical organs such as the heart, lungs, kidneys, and liver did not exhibit any signs of thrombus formation in any of the animal subjects.

Discussion

This preclinical study in a male porcine polytrauma model was performed to address the lack of a direct comparison between andexanet alfa and 4F-PCC for the reversal or control of hemostasis under the direct FXa inhibitor apixaban in a surgical setting. The results demonstrated a rapid reduction in anti-FXa activity after andexanet alfa infusion that was sustained for the first 180 min after induction of trauma, whereas apixaban anti-FXa activity remained comparable to that of control animals in those treated with either dose of 4F-PCC. These pharmacodynamic differences were accompanied by marked differences in postinjury blood loss. Andexanet alfa was associated with the lowest total blood loss and a more rapid reduction in bleeding compared with high-dose 4F-PCC; however, high-dose 4F-PCC was also effective in terminating trauma-induced bleeding and achieving hemostasis. The reduction in blood loss was associated with both high-dose 4F-PCC and andexanet alfa achieving 100% survival in this polytrauma model.

Our findings are comparable with ex vivo flow chamber experiments, which found that high-dose 4F-PCC exhibited similar efficacy in mitigating the anticoagulant effect of DOACs compared with andexanet alfa.46 In contrast, in vitro plasma-based anticoagulation experiments found that andexanet alfa corrected all thrombin generation parameters, while 4F-PCC dose-dependently increased peak thrombin and ETP.47 These experiments show conflicting results on the effect of 4F-PCC on thrombin generation, highlighting the importance of assay design in evaluating these agents.

The hemostatic effectiveness of low-dose (25 to 34 U/kg) versus high-dose (35 to 50 U/kg) 4F-PCC in apixaban or rivaroxaban anticoagulated patients has been evaluated in retrospective clinical studies. One study found no significant differences in hemostasis or in-hospital mortality between the two dosing groups, while a second study in patients with intracranial hemorrhage reported greater hemostatic control with high-dose 4F-PCC (greater than or equal to 35 U/kg) compared with low-dose (less than 35 U/kg) treatment (89.2% vs. 46.7%; P = 0.002), although in-hospital mortality remained similar. These clinical studies included heterogeneous patient populations and a wide range of PCC doses, which were arbitrarily categorized as “low” or “high,” potentially confounding the results. In contrast, our preclinical model employed precisely defined low (25 U/kg) and high (50 U/kg) 4F-PCC doses in a controlled experimental setting, allowing for a more accurate assessment of dose-dependent effects on hemostasis. This distinction may help explain differences between clinical observations and our findings regarding the impact of PCC dose on trauma-induced bleeding.32,48

In the current preclinical study, blood loss was decreased in animals receiving the 4F-PCC 50 U/kg dose as compared to animals treated with the lower dose (25 U/kg) of 4F-PCC. Furthermore, low-dose 4F-PCC–treated animals had a lower survival rate than animals treated with the high dose of 4F-PCC, the latter achieving 100% survival. These data provide evidence that there is a direct dose effect of 4F-PCC, with the higher dose providing greater hemostatic control and, as a result, an increased survival rate compared with the low dose. These findings are also consistent with another porcine polytrauma model study, in which dose-dependent effects of 4F-PCC on blood loss were observed. Survival (assessed in a 240-min observation period) was 100% in animals receiving 25 or 50 U/kg PCC compared with 88% in the 12.5 U/kg PCC dose group.16

Observational clinical data have suggested lower in-hospital mortality in patients treated with andexanet alfa compared with 4F-PCC for FXa inhibitor–associated major bleeding; however, these findings are largely derived from nonrandomized cohorts in which most patients received low-dose (25 U/kg) 4F-PCC, limiting comparisons with higher-dose PCC regimens.37,39 In contrast, randomized and meta-analytic data indicate that while andexanet alfa may provide greater hemostatic efficacy in certain clinical contexts, this effect is accompanied by a higher incidence of thromboembolic events, whereas mortality outcomes appear broadly comparable between both strategies.4,49–51

Although treatment with andexanet alfa was associated with increases in D-dimer and thrombin–antithrombin complex levels in this male porcine polytrauma model, these laboratory findings were not associated with histopathologic evidence of thrombosis or differences in survival. Interpretation of these results should account for the limited 300-min observation period, which constrains assessment of delayed thromboembolic events, as well as the use of young, healthy animals without comorbidities or concomitant therapies common in perioperative and critically ill patient populations. Accordingly, while the observed biochemical signals suggest enhanced coagulation activation after andexanet alfa administration and may contribute to the rapid reduction in bleeding, their clinical relevance cannot be fully determined within the temporal and biological constraints of this experimental model.

The polytrauma male porcine model used in this study has several important limitations. Injuries were induced in healthy, young, male animals under anesthesia, which limits generalizability to the heterogeneous clinical population, including patients with comorbidities or those receiving concomitant medications. The observation period of 300 min was relatively short; thromboembolic events did not occur, potentially due to the young age, the health of the animals, and the limited observation period. Consequently, the study was not powered to detect infrequent but clinically important outcomes such as thrombosis. In addition, interspecies differences in coagulation, platelet function, and the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of andexanet alfa and 4F-PCC may affect the results of this study.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that both high-dose 4F-PCC and andexanet alfa reduced posttrauma blood loss and improved hemostatic parameters in a male porcine model of apixaban anticoagulation. Andexanet alfa, however, produced a more rapid reduction in bleeding and anti-FXa activity. These effects likely reflect their distinct mechanisms of action: andexanet alfa acts as a specific FXa inhibitor antidote, whereas 4F-PCC enhances thrombin generation in a nonspecific manner at sites of injury. Overall, while both interventions provide hemostatic benefit, the kinetics and magnitude of response differ between agents.
