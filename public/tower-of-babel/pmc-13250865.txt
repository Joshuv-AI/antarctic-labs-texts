Thrombotic burden and longitudinal outcomes in Thai patients with polycythemia vera
Authors: Nonthakorn Hantrakun, Adisak Tantiworawit, Lalita Norasetthada, Ekarat Rattarittamrong, Chatree Chai-Adisaksopha, Thanawat Rattanathammethee, Sasinee Hantrakool, Pokpong Piriyakhuntorn, Teerachat Punnachet, Sirichai Srichairatanakool, Piangrawee Niprapan

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycythemia vera (PV) is a myeloproliferative neoplasm frequently complicated by thromboembolic events. However, data regarding thrombotic burden and long-term outcomes among Asian patients remain limited.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at a tertiary referral center to evaluate thromboembolic events, treatment-related complications, disease progression, and overall survival in patients with PV.ResultsA total of 133 patients were included, with a median age of 61 years (range 18–88). Most patients were male (60.2%). Fifty-one patients (38.3%) experienced at least one thromboembolic event, with 54 events in total, of which 87% occurred before or at PV diagnosis. Arterial thrombosis accounted for 90.7% of all events. Age ≥60 years was significantly associated with thromboembolic events, with an odds ratio of 2.34 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.07–5.11). After a median follow-up of 7.7 years, the 5-year overall survival was 82.7% (95% CI, 74.61–88.35). Prior thrombosis was associated with an increased risk of death (hazard ratio, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.15–3.42). Hydroxyurea resistance or intolerance occurred in 9.8% of patients. Bleeding complications were observed in 6.8%, including major bleeding in 4.5%. Fibrotic transformation, leukemic transformation, and secondary solid malignancies occurred in 4.5%, 3.8%, and 6.0% of patients, respectively.ConclusionThromboembolic events were common and predominantly arterial in this Asian PV cohort, occurring mainly before or at diagnosis. Prior thrombosis was associated with inferior survival. Long-term follow-up also revealed disease progression and secondary solid malignancies, underscoring the importance of continued surveillance.Keywords: Polycythemia vera, thrombosis, myeloproliferative neoplasm, Asian population, long-term outcomes

Introduction

Polycythemia vera (PV) is a BCR::ABL1–negative myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) characterized by clonal myeloid proliferation driven predominantly by JAK2 mutations. Among patients with PV, thromboembolic complications represent a major cause of morbidity and mortality, occurring in both arterial and venous circulations and frequently preceding or coinciding with disease diagnosis [1].

Large epidemiologic studies have demonstrated a substantial burden of thrombotic complications in PV. In the European Collaboration on Low-dose Aspirin in Polycythemia Vera (ECLAP) study, the cumulative incidence of nonfatal thrombosis was 3.8 events per 100 person-years [2]. While thrombotic risk differs across MPNs and is lower in primary myelofibrosis, PV consistently demonstrates one of the highest risks of arterial thrombosis. Such events are associated with increased mortality and healthcare utilization.

Most data informing thrombotic risk stratification in PV are derived from Western populations. Emerging evidence suggests that the incidence and pattern of thrombosis in Asian patients may differ, particularly with respect to venous thromboembolism [3,4]. In a Korean cohort, thromboembolic events were reported in 27.3% of patients with MPNs, with most events occurring before or within the first year of diagnosis [5]. However, PV-specific data in Asian populations remain limited, and long-term outcomes following diagnosis are insufficiently characterized.

Comprehensive regional data on thrombotic burden and disease outcomes are essential to refine risk assessment and optimize management strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the incidence, timing, and risk factors of thrombosis in Thai patients with PV. Long-term clinical outcomes, including treatment patterns, disease progression, and survival, were evaluated as secondary endpoints.

Materials and methods

Study designs and populations

This was a single-center retrospective observational study conducted at Chiang Mai University Hospital. All patients aged ≥18 years who were diagnosed with PV between June 2006 and February 2024 were included. Patients diagnosed prior to 2022 were evaluated according to World Health Organization (WHO) 2016 criteria [6], whereas more recent cases were classified according to WHO 2022 [7]. In JAK2 V617F-negative patients, diagnosis was supported by bone marrow morphology and subnormal serum erythropoietin levels. JAK2 V617F testing was performed using institutional standard methods. Testing for JAK2 exon 12 mutations was not routinely performed during the study period. Patients without bone marrow examination were excluded. We identified all patients through the hospital’s registry and the laboratory database. The cut-off date for the analysis follow-up was established as the end of December 2025. Clinical data were retrieved from medical records. These included demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters, treatment modalities (cytoreductive, antiplatelet, and anticoagulant therapies), thromboembolic events, bleeding complications defined according to International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) criteria [8,9], hydroxyurea resistance or intolerance defined by European LeukemiaNet criteria [10], and overall clinical course. Data on dates of death were retrieved from the national database. Clinical characteristics of patients were summarized using descriptive analysis. The primary objective was to characterize the burden and timing of thrombotic events in patients with PV. Secondary objectives included evaluation of factors associated with thrombosis, disease progression, and overall survival (OS). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Chiang Mai University [MED-2567-0677, Approval No. 474/2024]. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the ­ethics committee because the study involved no more than minimal risk to participants.

Definitions of thromboembolic events

Criteria for the diagnosis of index arterial thrombosis

Arterial thrombosis included acute coronary syndrome (ACS), ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), intra-abdominal arterial thrombosis, and peripheral arterial disease (PAD).

Criteria for the diagnosis of index venous thrombosis

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) was defined as follows:

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT): Presence of one or more filling defects on compression ultrasonography, Doppler ultrasonography, CT venography, or magnetic resonance (MR) venography involving the popliteal vein or more proximal veins.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT): Presence of one or more filling defects on compression ultrasonography, Doppler ultrasonography, CT venography, or magnetic resonance (MR) venography involving the popliteal vein or more proximal veins.

Pulmonary embolism (PE): One or more intraluminal filling defects detected by CT pulmonary angiography.

Pulmonary embolism (PE): One or more intraluminal filling defects detected by CT pulmonary angiography.

VTE at other sites (e.g., splanchnic vein thrombosis): Radiologic evidence of filling defects on Doppler ultrasonography, CT venography, MR venography, or contrast-enhanced CT at the involved sites.

VTE at other sites (e.g., splanchnic vein thrombosis): Radiologic evidence of filling defects on Doppler ultrasonography, CT venography, MR venography, or contrast-enhanced CT at the involved sites.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean with standard deviation (SD), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Variables with established clinical relevance and those with a p-value < 0.20 in univariable analysis were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to identify factors independently associated with thromboembolic events.

Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and Cox proportional hazards regression. OS was defined as the time from PV diagnosis to death from any cause. OS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Patients who were alive at the last follow-up were censored at that time point. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify factors associated with OS and to estimate hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 133 patients diagnosed with PV were included in the analysis. The median age at diagnosis was 61 years (range, 18–88), and 75 patients (56.4%) were aged 60 years or older. Most patients were male (60.2%). The mean (± SD) body mass index was 22.0 ± 3.3 kg/m2.

Hypertension was the most common comorbidity, present in 50.4% of patients, followed by dyslipidemia (24.1%) and diabetes mellitus (9.8%). Splenomegaly was observed in 12.8% of patients at diagnosis. Iron deficiency defined by serum ferritin < 30 ng/mL was documented in 30.0% of patients.

At diagnosis, the mean (± SD) hemoglobin level was 18.0 ± 2.6 g/dL, median white blood cell count was 16.9 (IQR, 12.9–23.5) × 109/L, and median platelet count was 667.0 (IQR, 476.0–948.0) × 109/L. Ninety patients (67.7%) were classified as high risk, while the remaining patients were classified as low risk. The JAK2 V617F mutation was detected in 88.0% of patients. Cytoreductive therapy was commonly administered, with phlebotomy and hydroxyurea used in 91.7% and 92.5% of patients, respectively. Most patients received antiplatelet therapy (94.7%) (Table 1).

Characteristics and timing of thromboembolic events

Overall, 54 thromboembolic events occurred in 51 patients, corresponding to a prevalence of 38.3%. Arterial thrombosis accounted for the majority of events (90.7%). Cerebrovascular disease was the most frequent arterial manifestation, representing 74.1% of all thrombotic events, followed by coronary artery disease (14.8%). VTE was uncommon and comprised 9.3% of events, most frequently involving the splanchnic veins (Table 2).

Factors associated with thrombosis

In univariable analysis, age 60 years or older was significantly associated with thromboembolic events (odds ratio 2.30; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.10–4.78; p = 0.026). Other clinical factors, including sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, iron deficiency, leukocytosis, and thrombocytosis, were not significantly associated with thrombosis.

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, age 60 years or older remained independently associated with thromboembolic events (odds ratio 2.34; 95% CI, 1.07–5.11; p = 0.033). No other variables demonstrated a statistically significant association (Table 3).

Survival analysis and long-term outcomes

After a median follow-up of 7.7 years (IQR, 3.9–11.4), the estimated 5-year overall survival was 82.7% (95% CI, 74.61–88.35). Patients with a history of thrombosis prior to or at PV diagnosis had significantly poorer OS compared with those without prior thrombosis (log-rank p = 0.012). In Cox proportional hazards analysis, a history of thrombosis was associated with an increased risk of death (hazard ratio, 1.98; 95% CI, 1.15–3.42; p = 0.014). The estimated 5-year OS was 88.5% (95% CI, 79.06–93.88) in patients ­without prior thrombosis and 72.1% (95% CI, 56.04–83.15) in those with prior thrombosis. Hydroxyurea resistance or intolerance occurred in 9.8% of patients receiving hydroxyurea. Cutaneous adverse events were the most frequently reported toxicities, with leg ulcers and oral ulcers observed in 4.1% and 1.6% of patients, respectively. Cytopenia developed in 3.3% of patients. Only two patients subsequently received ruxolitinib, and no patients received interferon-based therapy during the study period. Bleeding complications were observed in 6.8% of patients, including major bleeding in 4.5% and clinically relevant non-major bleeding in 2.3% (Table 4).

Discussion

In this cohort of Thai patients with PV, thromboembolic events were common and clustered around the time of diagnosis. More than one-third of patients experienced thrombosis prior to or at diagnosis, with arterial events accounting for the vast majority. Importantly, the history of thrombosis was independently associated with inferior OS, highlighting thrombosis not only as a frequent complication but also as a marker of adverse disease biology in this population.

The median age at diagnosis in our cohort was 61 years, consistent with Western registry data showing that PV is predominantly a disease of older adults [11,12]. Notably, approximately one-third of patients had iron deficiency at diagnosis, underscoring a clinically important challenge in PV management. While therapeutic phlebotomy remains a cornerstone of cytoreduction, it can further exacerbate iron depletion and contribute to symptoms such as fatigue, impaired concentration, and reduced quality of life. The increasing recognition of iron dysregulation in PV pathophysiology has led to the development of novel therapeutic approaches such as rusfertide, a hepcidin mimetic that limits iron availability for erythropoiesis. In the REVIVE phase II trial, rusfertide significantly reduced phlebotomy requirements while maintaining hematocrit control, suggesting a promising strategy to mitigate both erythrocytosis and iron deficiency in PV [13].

We observed that 38.3% of patients experienced thrombosis, with arterial events accounting for over 90% of all thrombotic episodes. Nearly half of these events occurred within three months before or at the time of PV diagnosis, supporting the concept that vascular complications often represent the first clinical manifestation of occult disease. In a Korean cohort [5], thrombosis occurred in 34.9% of patients with PV, predominantly arterial, with nearly one-quarter of events occurring before diagnosis. Similarly, a large Chinese cohort reported thrombosis in 36.6% of patients, again with arterial events predominating [14]. Importantly, a large Western cohort with a median follow-up of 6.9 years demonstrated a similarly high overall thrombotic burden, with arterial and venous thrombosis documented in 16.0% and 7.4% of patients before or at diagnosis, and in 12.0% and 9.0% of patients after diagnosis, respectively [15]. In addition, a meta-analysis reported an overall prevalence of thrombosis at diagnosis of 28.6% in patients with PV [16], which is slightly lower than that observed in our cohort but still supports a substantial thrombotic burden. The predominance of arterial thrombosis in our cohort is consistent with findings from other Asian studies. However, this pattern may be influenced not only by biological factors but also by healthcare-related factors, such as physician awareness, access to care, and the frequency of routine blood count testing. Disease-related mechanisms, including hyperviscosity, platelet activation, and leukocyte-mediated inflammation, may also contribute [17,18].

An important observation from our cohort was the low incidence of new thromboembolic events after PV diagnosis, in contrast to the high burden of events occurring before or at the time of diagnosis. This pattern suggests that the highest thrombotic risk in PV may be concentrated in the untreated phase of the disease. Once PV is recognized and appropriate therapy is initiated, including phlebotomy, antiplatelet agents, cytoreductive treatment, and effective control of hematocrit levels, the thrombotic risk may be lower in the treated phase of the disease. However, given the retrospective design of this study, alternative explanations such as under-detection of asymptomatic events, variability in treatment adherence, and the relatively small sample size cannot be excluded. Despite their low incidence after diagnosis, thrombotic events may still carry important clinical implications. They have been associated with poorer survival and may reflect a more aggressive disease phenotype. Emerging evidence also suggests a potential link between thrombosis and subsequent secondary malignancies [19,20], supporting the need for continued vigilance during long-term follow-up.

Although VTE was relatively uncommon in our cohort, splanchnic vein thrombosis represented a substantial proportion of venous events. This is clinically important because splanchnic thrombosis is a well-recognized manifestation of underlying MPNs [21]. Current recommendations, including those from Asian expert groups [22,23], advise routine evaluation for MPN in patients presenting with splanchnic vein thrombosis. Our findings reinforce the need for a high index of suspicion for PV in this clinical setting, particularly in regions where PV may remain undiagnosed until a vascular event occurs.

Age 60 years or older was independently associated with thromboembolic events, consistent with established Western risk models in which advanced age is a major predictor of vascular complications [2]. Recent studies revealed that leukocytosis was also associated with an increased risk of thrombosis [24,25]. In our cohort, a white blood cell count above 11 × 109/L was associated with a higher risk of thrombosis, although this association did not reach statistical significance. These findings suggest that, in addition to hematocrit control, attention to leukocyte burden may be relevant in optimizing thrombotic risk reduction in patients with PV.

After a median follow-up of 7.7 years, patients with thrombosis prior to or at diagnosis had significantly worse OS. This observation, consistent with large Western cohorts, supports the concept that thrombosis in PV may reflect a more aggressive disease phenotype characterized by heightened inflammatory and prothrombotic activity [15]. Beyond survival outcomes, long-term follow-up also revealed clinically relevant treatment-related complications. Compared with Western cohorts [26], the frequency of hydroxyurea resistance or intolerance in our cohort appeared lower. Nevertheless, nearly 10% of patients developed resistance or intolerance, most commonly due to cutaneous adverse events or cytopenia, highlighting the limitations of prolonged cytoreductive therapy. Despite this, subsequent use of alternative therapies, including interferon or ruxolitinib, was limited in our cohort. This treatment pattern likely reflects differences in drug accessibility and healthcare resource availability, which may have implications for long-term disease control and outcomes. However, these findings may also provide insight into real-world outcomes of PV in resource-constrained settings. In addition, bleeding complications were observed in a subset of patients, including major bleeding events, underscoring the need to balance thrombotic risk reduction with bleeding risk during long-term antithrombotic and cytoreductive management. Together with the observed rates of fibrotic transformation, leukemic evolution, and secondary solid malignancies, these findings emphasize that polycythemia vera is a chronic disease requiring ongoing surveillance not only for disease progression and survival, but also for treatment-related toxicity and late complications.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability and introduce selection bias. Thrombotic events were identified from medical records, so some events may have been missed, and data on the specific causes of death were not available from the national database, precluding further evaluation of thrombosis-related mortality. We did not perform a formal time-to-event analysis for post-diagnosis thrombosis because the low number of incident events after PV diagnosis limited statistical power and precluded meaningful modeling of thrombosis risk over time. Detailed data on post-treatment hematocrit control were not consistently available, limiting further evaluation of its role in thrombotic risk reduction. Data on JAK2 V617F allele burden, which has been associated with thrombotic risk and disease severity in MPNs [27], were not available in our study, limiting further risk stratification analyses. In addition, JAK2 exon 12 mutation testing was not routinely performed, which may have limited the diagnostic evaluation in JAK2-negative cases. Furthermore, the relatively small sample size limited the ability to detect modest associations and rare outcomes. Despite these limitations, this study provides important real-world data on thrombotic burden and long-term outcomes in Asian patients with PV.

Conclusions

Thromboembolic events were common in this Asian cohort with PV and occurred mainly around the time of diagnosis, with arterial thrombosis predominating. Age 60 years or older was significantly associated with thromboembolic events. Prior thrombosis was associated with worse OS, and clinically important late complications were observed during long-term follow-up. These findings support early evaluation for PV in patients presenting with arterial or splanchnic thrombosis and emphasize the importance of sustained long-term monitoring for both disease progression and treatment-related complications.
