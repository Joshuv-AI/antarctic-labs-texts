How physical activity influences motoric cognitive risk syndrome in older adults
Authors: Xin Chen, Zihao Zhang, Yijiang Zhang, Lijuan Deng, Haihua Guo, Wen-quan Zou, Dexin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMotoric cognitive risk (MCR) is a pre-dementia syndrome linked to higher risks of cognitive decline and dementia. This study aims to investigate the relationship between total physical activity (TPA), healthy physical activity (PA), and MCR in older adults.Study designPopulation-based cohort study with both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses.MethodsLogistic regression was used for cross-sectional analyses to examine the associations between TPA, healthy PA, and MCR at baseline. Using mediation analysis to explore the associations of gait speed and cognition in the link between physical activity and MCR. Cox regression was applied for longitudinal analyses to evaluate the relationships between TPA, healthy PA, and incident MCR risk over a 5-year follow-up. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were employed to investigate potential nonlinear associations between TPA and MCR risk. Sensitivity analyses were conducted across different age groups to confirm the robustness of the results.ResultsLogistic regression analysis revealed a significant negative association between TPA, healthy PA, and MCR. Participants engaging in healthy PA had a 36.9% lower risk of MCR (OR 0.631[0.502,0.793]). Mediation analysis showed that gait speed significantly mediated the association between PA and MCR. Cox regression further demonstrated that both TPA and healthy PA were significantly related to a lower risk of MCR. RCS analysis identified a nonlinear relationship between TPA and MCR risk, with the lowest risk observed at 86.1 METs. Sensitivity analyses across different age groups confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionHigher levels of TPA and healthy PA are negatively associated with MCR, underscoring the importance of maintaining adequate PA in older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06824-6.

Introduce

Motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome is characterized by the presence of subjective cognitive complaints (SCC) and slow gait speed in individuals who are not yet diagnosed with dementia [1]. As populations continue to age globally, MCR has emerged as a valuable early marker for identifying individuals at risk of developing dementia, particularly Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [2, 3]. Research shows that MCR significantly increases the risk of dementia, with individuals diagnosed with MCR being two to three times more likely to develop AD or other dementias compared to those without MCR [4].

The diagnosis of MCR relies not only on slow gait speed but is also closely linked to cognitive impairment, highlighting the interaction between motor and cognitive functions [5]. This overlap is believed to arise from shared neurodegenerative processes that simultaneously affect both domains [6]. Supporting this hypothesis, neuroimaging studies have demonstrated reductions in brain volume and white matter integrity in MCR patients, suggesting widespread neurodegenerative changes [7, 8]. Furthermore, vascular health plays a pivotal role in these processes, with diminished cerebral blood flow being implicated in both cognitive decline and motor abnormalities, including gait disturbances [9]. These findings underscore the need for early intervention in MCR, which could prevent further decline and reduce the overall burden of dementia.

Physical activity (PA) is widely recognized as a protective factor against cognitive decline and dementia [10]. Numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated that regular PA significantly reduces the risk of cognitive impairment. The beneficial effects of PA on brain health are thought to occur through mechanisms such as promoting neurogenesis, enhancing brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels, improving cerebrovascular function, and reducing systemic inflammation and insulin resistance—all of which are linked to both cognitive and motor decline [11, 12]. Importantly, for individuals with MCR, maintaining cognitive and motor function is crucial, and PA may play a critical role in achieving this by improving gait speed, executive function, and overall physical fitness.

However, despite the well-documented benefits of PA, few studies have specifically examined its impact on MCR, a pre-dementia syndrome. While aerobic exercise and strength training are known to enhance cognitive and motor functions in general populations, the relationship between PA and MCR remains underexplored. Given the dual impact of MCR on motor and cognitive abilities, further investigation into the role of PA as an early intervention for MCR is needed.

Methods

Study population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative, ongoing survey that began in 2011 [13, 14]. It has been conducted in waves, with updates every two to three years. To date, four follow-ups have been completed in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. However, as only the 2013 and 2015 follow-ups include data on walking speed, physical examinations, and blood tests, our study focuses exclusively on these two follow-ups. A total of 1,638 participants, with available data on gait speed, physical examinations, blood tests, demographic information, and other necessary variables, were included in the analysis. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria can be found in Fig. s1.

Physical activity

PA was measured using a modified short version of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [15, 16]. This questionnaire assesses the frequency and duration of vigorous, moderate, and low-intensity physical activities. Total physical activity (TPA), including exercise, recreation, work, and other purposes, was calculated. The daily duration of each activity type was encoded as follows: 1 (≤ 0.5 h), 2 (0.5 to 2 h), 3 (2 to 4 h), and 4 (≥ 4 h). TPA score = 8.0 × vigorous activity weekly duration score + 4.0 × moderate activity weekly duration score + 3.3 × walking weekly duration score. To ensure we captured structured exercise more effectively, we also introduced healthy physical activity, defined as engaging in moderate to vigorous activities for at least 30 min per session, three times per week [17].

MCR syndrome

MCR was characterized by the co-occurrence of both subjective cognitive complaints and slow gait speed in individuals who had no diagnosis of dementia or mobility limitations [1, 18]. Participants who reported a physician diagnosis of dementia or related memory disorders were excluded before defining MCR [19]. Participants were asked, “How would you rate your present memory?” and were given five response options: “excellent,” “very good,” “good,” “fair,” and “poor.” Those who responded with either “fair” or “poor” were considered to have subjective cognitive decline. Gait speed was calculated by having participants walk twice along a 2.5-meter straight path, and using the average time. Slow gait speed was defined as walking speed that was more than one standard deviation below the mean for specific age and sex groups [20]. The cutoff values for slow gait speed in participants younger than 65 years were 47.05 cm/s for men and 43.75 cm/s for women, while for those 65 years and older, the values were 40.03 cm/s for men and 35.64 cm/s for women.

Covariates

Covariates included age, gender, education level, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, alcohol consumption, and the presence of hypertension and diabetes. Blood biomarkers included glucose, total cholesterol, creatinine, and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Participants were divided into two groups based on healthy PA. Kruskal-Wallis test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to describe baseline characteristics, blood indicators, and body measurements for continuous variables, while the Chi-square test was used for categorical variables.

Logistic regression was conducted using baseline data to examine the associations of TPA and healthy PA (independent variables) with MCR (dependent variable) in both unadjusted and covariate-adjusted models. The Baron and Kenny approach was used to examine whether gait speed and subjective cognition mediated the associations between baseline PA, and MCR. Additionally, longitudinal changes in gait speed and cognition were analyzed to assess their mediating roles in the associations between baseline PA and incident MCR [21]. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for TPA and healthy PA using longitudinal CHARLS data from 2011, 2013, and 2015, with 2011 as baseline and a 5-year follow-up. Participants without MCR at baseline were included. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was used to assess differences in MCR incidence between the healthy and non-healthy PA groups. Interaction and stratified analyses were performed based on age, sex, smoking, drinking, hypertension, and diabetes status to examine whether the associations between PA and MCR differed across population subgroups. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) models with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles were applied to explore potential nonlinear relationships between baseline TPA and the 5-year risk of incident MCR. Nonlinearity was assessed using the Wald chi-square test. Since age affects cognitive decline and motor function, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess robustness across age groups: (1) < 75 years and ≥ 75 years, and (2) < 65, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥ 75 years.

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

Participants’ characteristics

There were 1,638 participants, with a mean age of 67.52 ± 6.26 years, and 50.4% were female. A total of 201 participants had MCR. Participants were divided into two groups based on whether they engaged in healthy PA. Those who engaged in healthy PA were younger on average, had a higher proportion of males, lower BMI, lower rates of hypertension and hyperglycemia, faster average walking speed, a lower proportion with MCR, and significantly higher MET values compared to those who did not engage in healthy PA. Results of the logistic regression between physical activity and MCR

TPA was significantly negatively associated with MCR (OR 0.977[0.962, 0.993], p = 0.004), and this relationship remained significant after adjusting for covariates (OR 0.976[0.960, 0.992], p = 0.004). Interaction analysis revealed a significant interaction with age (P < 0.001). Stratified analysis showed that TPA was negatively associated with MCR in participants aged 65 and older (OR 0.576[0.431, 0.746], p < 0.001). Mediation analyses

We examined whether baseline gait speed and subjective cognition mediated the associations between PA and MCR. The results showed that gait speed significantly mediated the associations of both total PA and healthy PA with MCR, with mediation proportions of 77.4% and 61.0%, respectively, whereas subjective cognition showed no significant mediating effect. (Fig. 1) Additional analyses incorporating longitudinal gait speed, cognition, and MCR yielded results consistent with those of the cross-sectional analysis. Association of physical activity with survival rates and risk of MCR

Each unit increase in TPA was associated with a 0.2% reduction in MCR risk (HR 0.998 [0.997, 1.000], p = 0.005). Stratified showed that MCR risk decreased with increasing TPA levels, with HR ranging from 0.996 to 0.998 among participants aged 65 and older, females, non-smokers, non-drinkers, and those without hypertension or diabetes. (Table 4) Although the HR values were close to 1, suggesting a minimal effect, RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear relationship between TPA and MCR risk (p = 0.005), with the lowest risk observed at 86.1 METs. (Fig. 2A) For participants younger than 65, the lowest risk occurred at 105.5 METs, while for those 65 and older, it was at 87.6 METs. (Fig. 2B-C) When stratified by sex, the RCS analysis showed no significant nonlinear association between TPA and MCR risk in either men or women. Sensitivity analysis

To assess the potential impact of different age groups on the relationship between TPA and MCR, two sets of sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, a division based on 75 years, a common age threshold, was used to explore effects in older populations. The sensitivity analysis showed a negative association between TPA and MCR (OR 0.999 [0.999, 1.00], p = 0.002), with a similar trend in those aged 75 and older (OR 0.987 [0.977, 0.996], p = 0.004). (Table. s1) Each unit increase in TPA was associated with a 0.1% reduction in MCR risk (HR 0.999 [0.998, 1.000], p = 0.007). (Table. s2) Although the nonlinearity test did not indicate a significant association between TPA and MCR risk, a reduction in MCR risk was observed when TPA was less than 103.3 MET. A similar trend was found in participants younger than 75 years. (Fig. s4)

The Logistic regression results showed a negative association between participation in healthy PA and MCR (OR 0.764 [0.691, 0.844], p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis revealed that, except for participants with hyperglycemia, other subgroups showed a significant association between healthy PA and MCR. (Table. s4) Additionally, the Cox regression analysis indicated that compared to participants without healthy PA, those engaging in healthy PA had a 35.54% lower risk of MCR (HR 0.645 [0.513, 0.813], p = 0.001). Healthy PA was significantly associated with reduced MCR risk in all subgroups except for those aged ≥ 75 and participants with hyperglycemia. (Table. s2)

Next, age was divided into four categories (< 65, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥ 75 years) to capture more detailed age-related differences in the relationship between physical activity and MCR risk. TPA was negatively associated with MCR (OR 1.000 [0.999, 1.000], P = 0.045), with each unit increase in TPA reducing MCR risk by 0.1% (HR 0.999 [0.997, 1.000], P = 0.017). (Table. s4-5) The RCS analysis indicated a significant nonlinear association between TPA and MCR risk (P = 0.012). When TPA was below 96.9 MET, MCR risk gradually decreased with increasing TPA, with a similar trend in participants aged 65–69 (P = 0.021). (Fig. s5)

Healthy PA was also negatively associated with MCR (OR 0.791 [0.715, 0.874], p < 0.001). (Table. s6) In age groups 65–69, 70–74, and ≥ 75 years, healthy PA remained negatively associated with MCR. Subgroup analyses by gender, smoking, drinking, and hypertension all showed significant negative associations between healthy PA and MCR. Cox regression showed that participants with healthy PA had a 30.3% (HR 0.697 [0.540, 0.900], p = 0.006) lower risk of MCR compared with those without healthy PA. In the age-stratified analysis, only participants aged 65–70 showed a significant reduction in MCR risk with healthy PA (p = 0.016). (Table. s5)

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that both TPA and healthy PA are negatively associated with MCR risk. There is a nonlinear relationship between TPA and MCR risk, with the lowest risk observed at 86.1 METs. Compared to participants who did not engage in healthy PA, those who did showed a significantly reduced risk of MCR. Additionally, sensitivity analyses across different age groups further confirmed the robustness of these findings. Maintaining a moderate frequency and duration of PA is crucial for older adults.

Gait slowing, as one of the core components of MCR, has been considered an early warning signal for dementia [22]. However, the definition of gait slowing varies across studies. Some research defines gait slowing as a walking speed of less than 0.8 m/s [23], but this standard is subject to debate due to differences in individuals’ age, sex, and physical condition. Additionally, with aging, significant degenerative changes occur in brain structures, particularly in key regions involved in motor and cognitive control [24]. For example, as age increases, the incidence and severity of white matter lesions also rise. White matter, responsible for connecting different regions of the brain and ensuring precise control over complex movements like gait, experiences fiber degeneration that reduces signal transmission efficiency, thus impacting walking speed [25]. The hippocampus, a critical region for memory and spatial navigation, also undergoes atrophy during aging, affecting not only cognitive function but indirectly influencing gait, as the hippocampus is closely linked to spatial awareness and gait planning [26]. Furthermore, the frontal lobe, particularly its anterior regions, which are responsible for executive function, attention, and motor planning, also experiences gray matter atrophy [27, 28]. This leads to diminished control over gait, manifesting as a reduction in walking speed. Based on the impact of aging on brain structural changes, this study further explored the relationship between physical activity and MCR risk across different age and sex subgroups. Among participants younger than 65, although no significant nonlinear association was observed between TPA and MCR risk, there was a decreasing trend in MCR risk as METs increased when METs were less than 105.5. Similarly, in participants aged 65 and older, MCR risk also showed a decreasing trend with increasing METs when METs were less than 86.1. Furthermore, analysis of healthy PA revealed that in the group aged 65 and older, those engaging in healthy PA had a 36.9% lower risk of MCR compared with those without healthy PA. When participants were stratified by age at 75, those younger than 75 who engaged in healthy PA had a 36.2% lower risk of MCR compared with those without healthy PA. In a more refined age stratification (60–64, 65–69, 70–74, ≥ 75 years), the greatest reduction in MCR risk was observed in the 70–74 age group, with a reduction of 52.5%. These results suggest that healthy PA is associated with lower MCR risk across different age groups of older adults, with particularly stronger associations observed in certain age ranges.

Aerobic exercise and moderate-intensity PA have been widely supported by numerous studies for their role in improving cognitive function and reducing the risk of dementia. Moderate-intensity exercise can reduce the risk of cognitive impairment in middle-aged and older adults and is associated with AD pathological markers [29]. Specifically, it shows a positive correlation with Aβ42 and a negative correlation with Tau/Aβ42 and pTau/Aβ42 [30]. Aerobic exercise has been shown to increase the expression of BDNF, particularly in the hippocampus, a region that experiences early atrophy in AD [31]. The increase in BDNF is associated with greater hippocampal volume, which is crucial for spatial memory and gait planning, further demonstrating the protective effect of moderate physical activity on cognition [32]. Additionally, aerobic exercise can improve the connectivity of the brain’s default mode network, enhancing the efficiency of resource allocation in the brain, which is critical for preventing further cognitive decline [33, 34].

Moreover, studies have suggested that PA can mitigate the negative impact of brain pathology, such as medial temporal lobe atrophy and amyloid-beta deposition, on dementia risk. This suggests that PA may help build cognitive reserve, delaying the progression of cognitive decline [35]. In a Japanese cohort of older adults, moderate to high-intensity physical activity was found to significantly reduce the risk of dementia [36]. Both total daily PA and high-intensity leisure-time activities were associated with a lower incidence of dementia, particularly among men, where higher levels of leisure-time exercise were linked to a marked reduction in dementia risk. However, some studies suggest that women tend to experience greater cognitive benefits from exercise interventions, particularly in executive function [37]. This may be due to the fact that certain brain regions, especially the neural circuits in the prefrontal cortex, remain more intact in women and are more responsive to the enhancements from aerobic exercise [38]. Our results showed that higher levels of PA were significantly associated with a lower risk of MCR among women, whereas no significant association was observed among men. This sex-specific pattern is consistent with previous studies suggesting that women may experience greater cognitive benefits from exercise. Several studies have reported that interventions involving a higher proportion of female participants tend to produce larger improvements in executive function [37]. Moreover, randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that aerobic exercise significantly enhances executive performance in women [39].

The relationship between PA and MCR may not be unidirectional. Longitudinal cohort studies have shown that reductions in gait speed often precede decreases in physical activity, rather than the reverse, suggesting that early motor decline may contribute to reduced engagement in physical or social activities [40]. Further evidence indicates that deterioration in balance and mobility performance is commonly accompanied by lower self-reported physical activity and reduced daily movement [41]. Objective measurements using wearable devices have also confirmed that impairments in motor ability—such as slower turning speed and reduced dexterity—can predict subsequent declines in daily activity levels [42]. These studies suggest that individuals with MCR or gait slowing may also have reduced levels of PA.

Although some studies have found no significant association between PA levels and the prevalence of MCR [43], research from the TILDA cohort has examined mobility and gait parameters—such as the Timed Up-and-Go (TUG), usual gait speed, and dual-task gait speed—in relation to cognitive changes [44]. However, longitudinal analyses showed that these mobility indicators did not significantly predict cognitive decline over a follow-up period of up to 5.9 years. Furthermore, bidirectional temporal analyses between TUG performance and cognitive domains revealed no strong unidirectional effects, suggesting that differences in sample characteristics, measurement tools, or follow-up duration may have influenced the results.

This study has some limitations. First, our findings cannot determine a causal relationship between PA and MCR. Second, the study did not account for other lifestyle factors (such as diet, sleep, and social engagement) that could potentially influence MCR risk [44–46]. Additionally, sedentary behavior, which may affect the relationship between PA and MCR, was not considered. Finally, self-reported measures of subjective cognition may be less accurate than standardized cognitive assessments.

Conclusion

TPA and healthy PA are negatively associated with MCR. The nonlinear relationship between TPA and MCR risk emphasizes the importance of maintaining moderate levels of PA. Engaging in moderate-intensity exercise three or more times a week, for an appropriate duration each session, is associated with a lower likelihood of MCR.
