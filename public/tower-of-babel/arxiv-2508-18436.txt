The infinite-dimensional dissipation inequality
Timo Reis

Abstract
We study the dissipativity of linear infinite-dimensional systems with respect to a prescribed quadratic supply rate functional. We characterize this property via an operator inequality that also yields the system’s dissipation rate. We also derive implications for the linear–quadratic optimal control problem on the nonnegative real half-line.

Notation and preliminaries

We denote by
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
the norm on the complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
, and by
I_{\mathcal{X}}
.
The symbol
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
stands for the

of
\mathcal{X}

\mathcal{X}

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{X}^{\ast},\mathcal{X}}

(

Operators and forms

We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
the space of all bounded linear operators from the Hilbert space
\mathcal{X}
to the Hilbert space
\mathcal{Y}
. As usual, we abbreviate
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
. The domain
\dom(A)
of a possibly unbounded linear operator
A\colon\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(A)\to\mathcal{Y}
is equipped with the graph norm

\lVert x\rVert_{\dom(A)}\coloneqq\big(\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}+\lVert Ax\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\big)^{1/2}.

A subspace of
\dom(A)
is called a
core of
A
, if it is dense in
\dom(A)
with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\dom(A)}
.

The adjoint of a densely defined linear operator
A\colon\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(A)\to\mathcal{Y}
is
A^{\ast}\colon\mathcal{Y}\supset\dom(A^{\ast})\to\mathcal{X}
with

\dom(A^{\ast})=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}\,|\,\mathopen{}\exists\,z\in\mathcal{X}\text{ s.t.\ }\forall\,x\in\dom(A):\langle y,Ax\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}}=\langle z,x\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\}.

The vector
z\in\mathcal{X}
in the above set is uniquely determined by
y\in\dom(A^{\ast})
, and we set
A^{\ast}y=z
.

Essential for the definition of storage functions are
real quadratic forms
. That is, for a subspace
\dom(\mathcal{S})
, a mapping
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(\mathcal{S})\to{\mathbb{R}}
, such that
h:\dom(h)\times\dom(h)\to{\mathbb{C}}
with

h(x,z)=\tfrac{1}{4}\big(\mathcal{S}(x+z)-\mathcal{S}(x-z)+\imath\mathcal{S}(x+\imath z)-\imath\mathcal{S}(x-\imath z)\big)

(10)

is a sesquilinear form. A real quadratic form is called
densely defined
, if
\dom(\mathcal{S})
is dense in
\mathcal{X}
, and

bounded
, if

\exists\,c>0:\quad|\mathcal{S}(x)|\leq\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}\;\forall x\in\mathcal{X},

and
semi-bounded

exists some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
such that one of

x\mapsto(\pm\mathcal{S}(x)+\lambda\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{2})^{1/2}

(11)

defines a norm on
\dom(\mathcal{S})
. If, in addition,
\dom(\mathcal{S})
is complete with respect to the aforementioned norm, then
\mathcal{S}
is called
closed
. A
core of
\mathcal{S}
is a subspace of
\dom(\mathcal{S})
, which is dense in
\dom(h)
with respect to the aforementioned norm in
\dom(\mathcal{S})
.
For a closed, densely defined and semi-bounded real quadratic form

, Kato’s First Representation Theorem
(
Kato80
, Chap. VI, Thm. 2.1)
yields that there exists some self-adjoint
P:\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(P)\to\mathcal{X}
, such that
\dom(P)
is a core of
\dom(\mathcal{S})
, and

\displaystyle h(x,z)

\displaystyle=\langle Px,z\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\forall\ x\in\dom(h),\,z\in\dom(P),

\displaystyle\dom(P)

\displaystyle=\{z\in\mathcal{X}\,|\,\mathopen{}\exists\,w\in\mathcal{X}\text{ s.t.\ }h(x,z)=\langle x,w\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\}.

The operator
P
is uniquely determined by

, and we call

the operator induced by

\dom(P)
is a core of
\dom(\mathcal{S})
implies that

extends uniquely to an operator
\widetilde{P}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\dom(\mathcal{S}),\dom(\mathcal{S})^{\ast})
via

h(x,z)=\langle x,\widetilde{P}z\rangle_{\dom(\mathcal{S}),\dom(\mathcal{S})^{\ast}}\quad\forall\ x,z\in\dom(\mathcal{S}).

(12)

3
The dissipation inequality

Now we are ready to introduce the concept of a dissipation inequality for input-state-output systems defined by system nodes. In our considerations, we restrict to semi-bounded, closed and densely defined storage functions.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
be a system node on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
, assume that
Q=Q^{\ast}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{Y})
,
S\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
,
R=R^{\ast}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{U})
, and let
s:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be as in (
8
). Further, let
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(\mathcal{S})\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a semi-bounded, closed and densely defined real quadratic form. Then the system (
6
) is dissipative with respect to the supply rate functional
s
and storage function
\mathcal{S}
, if for all
T\in{\mathbb{R}}_{>0}
, any classical solution
(x(\cdot),u(\cdot),y(\cdot))
of (
6

[0,T]
, it holds that
x(0),x(T)\in\dom(\mathcal{S})
, and

\mathcal{S}(x(T))-\mathcal{S}(x(0))+\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl(y(t),u(t)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}\geq 0.

(16)

Hereby, we call (

Remark 3.2
.

Assume the framework of Definition
3.1

, so we can assume that
\lambda\neq 0
in the following.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
and the definition of the value function, there exists a generalized trajectory
(x(\cdot),u(\cdot),y(\cdot))
with
x(0)=x_{0}
and

\mathcal{J}\bigl(y(\cdot),u(\cdot)\bigr)\leq\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda|^{2}}.

Using that
(\lambda x(\cdot),\lambda u(\cdot),\lambda y(\cdot))
is a generalized trajectory with
\lambda x(0)=\lambda x_{0}
, we obtain, by
quadraticity of
s
,

\mathrm{Val}(\lambda x_{0})\leq\mathcal{J}(\lambda x(\cdot),\lambda u(\cdot))=|\lambda|^{2}\mathcal{J}(y(\cdot),u(\cdot))=|\lambda|^{2}\,\mathcal{J}(y(\cdot),u(\cdot))\\
\leq|\lambda|^{2}\left(\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{|\lambda|^{2}}\right)=|\lambda|^{2}\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})+\varepsilon.

Since the latter holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we have

\mathrm{Val}(\lambda x_{0})\leq|\lambda|^{2}\mathcal{S}(x_{0})
.
Applying this with
x_{0}
replaced by
\lambda x_{0}
and with the factor
1/\lambda
gives the reverse inequality, hence
\mathrm{Val}(\lambda x_{0})=|\lambda|^{2}\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})
.

Step 2
: We show the parallelogram identity

\forall\,x_{0,1},x_{0,2}\in\mathcal{X}:\quad\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}+x_{0,2})+\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}-x_{0,2})=2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1})+2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,2}).

Let
x_{0,1},x_{0,2}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exist
generalized trajectories
(x_{i}(\cdot),u_{i}(\cdot),y_{i}(\cdot))
with
x_{i}(0)=x_{0,i}
such that

\mathcal{J}\bigl(y_{i}(\cdot),u_{i}(\cdot)\bigr)\leq\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,i})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4},\qquad i=1,2.

Clearly, the sums and differences

(x_{\pm}(\cdot),u_{\pm}(\cdot),y_{\pm}(\cdot)):=\bigl(x_{1}(\cdot)\pm x_{2}(\cdot),\;u_{1}(\cdot)\pm u_{2}(\cdot),\;y_{1}(\cdot)\pm y_{2}(\cdot)\bigr)

are generalized trajectories with initial states
x_{0,1}\pm x_{0,2}
. Since
s
is quadratic,
we have the integrand-level parallelogram identity, and hence

\mathcal{J}\bigl(y_{+},u_{+}\bigr)+\mathcal{J}\bigl(y_{-},u_{-}\bigr)=2\mathcal{J}\bigl(y_{1},u_{1}\bigr)+2\mathcal{J}\bigl(y_{2},u_{2}\bigr).

Therefore,

\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}+x_{0,2})+\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}-x_{0,2})\leq 2\,\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1})+2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,2})+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we obtain

\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}+x_{0,2})+\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}-x_{0,2})\leq 2\,\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1})+2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,2}).

For the reverse inequality, set
\tilde{x}_{0,1}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{0,1}+x_{0,2})
and

\tilde{x}_{0,2}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(x_{0,1}-x_{0,2})
. Applying the previous estimate to

\tilde{x}_{0,1},\tilde{x}_{0,2}
and using Step 1 with
\lambda=\tfrac{1}{2}
, we get

2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1})+2\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,2})=4\mathrm{Val}(\tilde{x}_{0,1})+4\,\mathrm{Val}(\tilde{x}_{0,2})\\
\leq\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}+x_{0,2})+\mathrm{Val}(x_{0,1}-x_{0,2}),

which proves the parallelogram identity.

Step 3:
We conclude that
\mathrm{Val}
is a quadratic form. Indeed, Steps 1-2 verify the hypotheses of
(
Kurepa1965QSF
, Thm. 1)
; hence, by that theorem, (
10
) is a sesquilinear form. Thus
\mathrm{Val}:\mathcal{X}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a quadratic form.

Step 4:
We show that
\mathrm{Val}
is a bounded quadratic form. It can be concluded from Lemma
5.2
that, for
M
as in that lemma,

\forall\,x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}\text{ with }\lVert x_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}\leq 1:\quad\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})\leq M\,\left\lVert\left[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&S\\
S^{\ast}&R\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right\rVert.

Hence, by using that
\mathrm{Val}
is quadratic, we have

\forall\,x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}:\quad\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})\leq M\,\left\lVert\left[\begin{smallmatrix}Q&S\\
S^{\ast}&R\end{smallmatrix}\right]\right\rVert\,\lVert x_{0}\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}^{2}.

(b)

Assume that
(x(\cdot),u(\cdot),y(\cdot))
is a classical trajectory on
[0,T]
,
T>0
.
We essentially follow the argument of the corresponding finite-dimensional proof in
Will71a
.
For
\varepsilon>0
, let
({x}_{1}(\cdot),{u}_{1}(\cdot),{y}_{1}(\cdot))

be a generalized solution with
{x}_{1}(0)=x(T)
and

\mathcal{J}\bigl({y}_{1}(\cdot),{u}_{1}(\cdot)\bigr)\leq\mathrm{Val}(x(T))+{\varepsilon}.

Now consider the
concatenation
of the two trajectories, defined by

(\tilde{x}(t),\tilde{u}(t),\tilde{y}(t))=\begin{cases}(x(t),u(t),y(t)),&0\leq t\leq T,\\
\bigl(x_{1}(t-T),u_{1}(t-T),y_{1}(t-T)\bigr),&t>T,\end{cases}

Then
(\tilde{x}(\cdot),\tilde{u}(\cdot),\tilde{y}(\cdot))
is a generalized trajectory with
\tilde{u}(\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{U})
and
\tilde{y}(\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})
, whence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Val}(x(0))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Val}(\tilde{x}(0))\leq\mathcal{J}(\tilde{y}(\cdot),\tilde{u}(\cdot))

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl(\tilde{y}(t),\tilde{u}(t)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}+\int_{0}^{\infty}s\bigl(\tilde{y}(t+T),\tilde{u}(t+T)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl({y}(t),{u}(t)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathcal{J}\bigl({y}_{1}(\cdot),{u}_{1}(\cdot)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl({y}(t),{u}(t)\bigr)\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathrm{Val}(x(T))+\varepsilon.

Since this holds for all
\varepsilon>0
, we can conclude that the dissipation inequality holds.

(c)

Let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
, with
\mathcal{V}
as in (
14
). For every classical trajectory

(x(\cdot),u(\cdot),y(\cdot))
on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}
with
x(0)=x_{0}
we have, for all
T>0
,

0\;\leq\;\mathcal{S}(x(T))-\mathcal{S}(x_{0})\;+\;\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl(y(t),u(t)\bigr)\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}.

Since
\mathcal{S}\geq 0
, letting
T\to\infty
, we obtain

\mathcal{S}(x_{0})\;\leq\;\int_{0}^{\infty}s\bigl(y(t),u(t)\bigr)\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}.

Taking the infimum over all trajectories with
x(0)=x_{0}
yields

\mathcal{S}(x_{0})\;\leq\;\mathrm{Val}(x_{0}).

Thus
\mathcal{S}
is bounded above by
\mathrm{Val}
on
\mathcal{V}
. By density of
\mathcal{V}
in
\mathcal{X}

(and nonnegativity of
\mathcal{S}
), the inequality in fact holds for all

x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, which completes the proof.

∎

Next we show that, along any generalized trajectory whose input and output are square-integrable,
\mathrm{Val}(x(t))
tends to zero for
t\to\infty
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Assume the standing hypotheses of Theorem
5.4
, and let

(x(\cdot),u(\cdot),y(\cdot))
be a generalized trajectory of (
6
) on
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

with
x(0)=x_{0}
,
u(\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{U})
, and
y(\cdot)\in\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})
. Then

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathrm{Val}\bigl(x(t)\bigr)=0.

Proof.

By a simple shift, we see that

\forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}:\quad 0\leq\mathrm{Val}(x(t))\leq\int_{t}^{\infty}s(y(\tau),u(\tau))\mathop{\mathrm{d}\tau}.

Then the result follows, since, by invoking that
s
is nonnegative, Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem gives

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{t}^{\infty}s(y(\tau),u(\tau))\mathop{\mathrm{d}\tau}=0.

∎

Note that, up to this point, we have not dealt with the optimal control itself (i.e., a control input that attains the infimum of the cost functional), but only with the optimal value of the cost. In general, an optimal control need not exist; one may have only a minimizing sequence of admissible trajectories. This phenomenon already occurs for finite-dimensional systems
(
IlchmannReis2017Outer
, Secs. 7&8)
. Nevertheless, whenever an optimal control does exist, it can be characterized via the dissipation inequality. We do not state this as a theorem; instead, we record it in the following remark.

Remark 5.6

(The optimal control)
.

We have seen that, under the standing assumptions of nonnegativity of the cost functional and state–feedback stabilizability, the value function is a bounded storage function with respect to the supply rate
s
.
Boundedness implies the existence of a self-adjoint
P\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathcal{X})
such that

\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})=\langle x_{0},Px_{0}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}\quad\text{for all }x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}.

Moreover, Theorem
4.1
yields a Hilbert space
\mathcal{Z}
and an operator
K\&L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\dom(A\&B);\mathcal{Z})
with dense range such that (
27
) holds for all
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\dom(A\&B)
.
Applying Proposition
4.3
, we obtain the following: for
K\&L\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\dom(A\&B))
as in Theorem
4.1

(iv’)
, and for all
T>0
, every generalized solution of (
6
) on
[0,T]
satisfies

\mathrm{Val}(x(T))-\mathrm{Val}(x(0))\;+\;\int_{0}^{T}s\bigl(y(t),u(t)\bigr)\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}\;=\;\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert K\&L\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x(t)\\
u(t)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Z}}^{2}\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}t}.

Invoking Proposition
5.5
, which states that
\mathrm{Val}(x(t))\to 0
as
t\to\infty
for any generalized trajectory with
u,y\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})
, we conclude that

\mathrm{Val}(x_{0})\;+\;\left\lVert K\&L\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x(\cdot)\\
u(\cdot)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Z})}^{2}\;=\;\mathcal{J}\bigl(y(\cdot),u(\cdot)\bigr).

Hence the quantity
\left\lVert K\&L\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x(\cdot)\\
u(\cdot)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\rVert_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Z})}^{2}
measures the suboptimality gap of the control
u(\cdot)
relative to the optimal cost. In particular, an optimal control exists if and only if the infinite-dimensional differential–algebraic equation

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\dot{x}(t)\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)\;=\;\begin{bmatrix}A\&B\\[-2.84526pt]
K\&L\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x(t)\\
u(t)\end{smallmatrix}\right),\qquad x(0)=x_{0},

admits a solution; any such solution then yields an optimal control.
In the finite-dimensional case, this closed-loop system coincides with the classical feedback system obtained by the solution of the algebraic Riccati equation
Will71a
.

Finally, we briefly comment on closely related results in the literature.

Remark 5.7
.

In
OpmeerStaffans2014
;
OpmeerStaffans2019
, the minimization of the cost functional

\lVert u(\cdot)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{U})}^{2}+\lVert y(\cdot)\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}^{2}

subject to a prescribed initial value is considered. In our notation, this corresponds to a supply rate
s(\cdot,\cdot)
as in (
8
) with
S=0
,
R=I_{\mathcal{U}}
, and
Q=I_{\mathcal{Y}}
. One essentially obtains the same equations as in (
19
) for this setting. On the one hand, that cost functional is more restrictive than ours; on the other hand, the class of systems is broader, as
operator nodes
rather than system nodes are treated. Roughly speaking, this framework does not require the state operator
A
to generate a strongly continuous semigroup; it suffices that
A
be densely defined with a nonempty resolvent set (e.g., this allows one to treat the backward heat equation).

In
OpmeerStaffans2014
;
OpmeerStaffans2019
, a frequency-domain viewpoint is adopted, and for a subdomain
\Omega
of the resolvent set of
A
intersected with the complex right half-plane, the output (when it belongs to
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})
) is defined via the inverse Laplace transform of the analytic continuation of

\displaystyle\widehat{y}:\quad\Omega

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{Y},

\displaystyle s

\displaystyle\mapsto G(s)\widehat{u}(s)+C\&D\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(sI_{\mathcal{X}}-A)^{-1}x_{0}\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right),

where
\widehat{u}(\cdot)
is the Laplace transform of
u(\cdot)
, and
G(\cdot)
is the transfer function of
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
, see
(
Staf05
, Sec. 4.6)
. Indeed, by
(
Staf05
, Lem. 4.7.12)
, generalized trajectories satisfy the above frequency-domain relation. We also note that the solution concept in
OpmeerStaffans2014
;
OpmeerStaffans2019
is considerably more general, not only because operator nodes (rather than system nodes) are treated, but also because the state trajectory need not be continuous as a map
{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}\to\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, no state-feedback stabilizability assumption is imposed; instead, the existence of
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-trajectories is required for a dense subspace of
\mathcal{X}
(which also leads to unbounded value functions).

It is important to observe that those results make essential use of the fact that the
\mathrm{L}^{2}
-norm of the input enters the cost functional. Without going into details, these results can be applied more or less directly to optimal problems (
30
) with full input weighting (that is,
R
in (
8
) is boundedly invertible). The case of more general cost functionals (even nonnegative ones), however, still requires additional work beyond the scope of this article.

At the outset, note that although the monograph

contains almost no PDE examples. Partial differential equations, especially with boundary control, are very naturally described within the system node framework. See, for instance,

In selecting the two examples below, we strike a balance between transparency and nontriviality.
The first example is deliberately simple: a transport equation with boundary control and observation. From the author’s perspective, this example is well suited to illustrate the spaces
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
from (
14

15

Thereafter we consider the heat equation on some Lipschitz domain in

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and trivial input (i.e.,
\mathcal{U}=\{0\}
). As output we take the Neumann trace and use a supply rate with

. We show that, while the system is dissipative with respect to a suitable storage function, no bounded storage function exists in this case.

6.1
Boundary-controlled transport equation

Consider the first-order transport equation on
[0,1]
with rightward flow and boundary control at the inflow point:

\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}x(t,\xi)=-\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x(t,\xi),\qquad x(0,\xi)=x_{0}(\xi),\quad t\geq 0,\ \xi\in[0,1],

together with the input and output

u(t)=x(t,0),\qquad y(t)=\alpha\,x(t,0)+x(t,1),

where
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a parameter.
A formal consideration yields that

\displaystyle\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}|x(t,\xi)|^{2}\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}\xi}

\displaystyle=2\Re\int_{0}^{1}\overline{x(t,\xi)}\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}x(t,\xi)\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}\xi}

\displaystyle=-2\Re\int_{0}^{1}\overline{x(t,\xi)}\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x(t,\xi)\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}\xi}

\displaystyle=-\Re\int_{0}^{1}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\bigl|x(t,\xi)\bigr|^{2}\,\mathop{\mathrm{d}\xi}

\displaystyle=|x(t,0)|^{2}-|x(t,1)|^{2}=|u(t)|^{2}-|y(t)-\alpha\,u(t)|^{2}.

This suggests that
\mathcal{S}
with

\mathcal{S}(x(t))=\int_{0}^{1}|x(t,\xi)|^{2}\mathop{\mathrm{d}\xi}=\left\lVert x(t,\cdot)\right\rVert_{\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])}^{2}

is a storage function with respect to any supply rate functional with

\forall\,u,y\in{\mathbb{C}}:\quad s(u,y)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y\\[-2.84526pt]
\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{\ast}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}q&s\\
\overline{s}&r\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y\\[-2.84526pt]
\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
\geq|y-\alpha\,u|^{2}-|u|^{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y\\[-2.84526pt]
\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{\ast}\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-\alpha\\
-\overline{\alpha}&\;|\alpha|^{2}-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\left(\begin{smallmatrix}y\\[-2.84526pt]
\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

(34)

for
q,r\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Note that (
34
) is equivalent to positive semidefiniteness of the matrix

M:=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}{q-1}&{s+\alpha}\\[2.84526pt]
{-\overline{s+\alpha}}\;&{\;\;r+1-|\alpha|^{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right].

(35)

In what follows, we show that the system node framework, together with the results obtained in this article, yields exactly the same conclusion as the formal considerations above. To this end, we first formulate the foregoing system in the system node setting.

In a certain sense, the formulation of the operator
A\&B
for this example is prototypical for system nodes: the action of the PDE (here, the negative spatial derivative) is mirrored by the action of
A\&B
, whereas the domain encodes the boundary condition (namely, that the left boundary value equals the input). Concretely,

\dom(A\&B)=\left\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in H^{1}([0,1])\times{\mathbb{C}}\,\middle|\,\mathopen{}x(0)=u\right\},\qquad A\&B\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)=-\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x.

Moreover, in accordance with the output definition, the output operator is

C\&D\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)=\alpha\,x(0)+x(1)=\alpha\,u+x(1)\quad\forall\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\dom(A\&B).

Note that these boundary evaluations are well defined for
x\in H^{1}([0,1])
by the Sobolev trace theorem
(
AdamFour03
, Thm. 4.12)
. The same theorem also implies that
C\&D\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\dom(A\&B);{\mathbb{C}})
. Closedness of
A\&B
follows from the definition of the weak derivative, whereas, for all
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the constant function
x(\cdot)\equiv u
fulfills
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\dom(A\&B)
. For the property of
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
being a system node on
(\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1]),{\mathbb{C}},{\mathbb{C}})
, it remains to show that main operator generates a strongly continuous semigroup on
\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])
. This however follows, since
A
is given by

Ax=-\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}x,\qquad\dom A=\{x\in H^{1}([0,1])\,|\,\mathopen{}x(0)=0\},

and it is shown in that it generated the right-shift semigroup on
\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])
(see e.g.
(
TucsnakWeiss2009
, Ex. 2.8.7)
.

In passing, we note that the spaces
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
from (
14
) and (
15
) are given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=\{x\in H^{1}([0,1])\,|\,\mathopen{}\exists\,u\in{\mathbb{C}}\text{ s.t.\ }x(0)=u\}=H^{1}([0,1]),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}

\displaystyle=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in H^{1}([0,1])\times{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,\mathopen{}x(0)=u\,\wedge\,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x\in H^{1}([0,1])\}

\displaystyle=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in H^{2}([0,1])\times{\mathbb{C}}\,|\,\mathopen{}x(0)=u\}.

We now show that the functional
\mathcal{S}:\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by

, is a storage function for the system with respect to the supply rate functional in (
34
). To this end we first observe that the identity operator on
\mathrm{L}^{2}([0,1])
is induced by
\mathcal{S}
. Further, by using that the definition of the weak derivative implies that

\forall\,x\in H^{1}([0,1]):\quad 2\Re\left\langle x,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x\right\rangle=|x(1)|^{2}-|x(0)|^{2}.

Using the latter, we obtain that, for
M
as in

\displaystyle\;2\Re\left\langle Px,A\&B\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}+s\!\left(C\&D\,\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right),\,u\right)

\displaystyle=-2\Re\left\langle x,\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\xi}x\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}}+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\
x(0)\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{\ast}\begin{bmatrix}q&s\\
\overline{s}&r\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\
x(0)\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle=|x(0)|^{2}-|x(1)|^{2}+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\
x(0)\end{smallmatrix}\right)^{\ast}\begin{bmatrix}q&s\\
\overline{s}&r\end{bmatrix}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\alpha x(0)+x(1)\\

where the latter holds due to positive semidefiniteness of

. Then Theorem

6.2
Heat equation with Neumann observation

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t}x(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\Delta x(t,\xi),

\displaystyle x(0,\xi)

\displaystyle=x_{0}(\xi),

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\ \xi\in\Omega,

\displaystyle x(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle y(t,\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial x(t,\cdot)}{\partial\nu}\Big|_{\partial\Omega},

\displaystyle t\geq 0,\ \xi\in\partial\Omega,

where
\nu

\mathcal{U}=\{0\}
), the state space is chosen as
\mathcal{X}=\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
, and the main operator is given by the Dirichlet Laplacian, i.e.,

\dom(A)=\{x\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,|\,\mathopen{}\nabla x\in H_{\operatorname{div}}(\Omega)\},\quad Ax=\Delta x,

(36)

where
H_{\operatorname{div}}(\Omega)
of all elements of
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{C}}^{d})
whose weak divergence is in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
.
The output is given by the Neumann trace of
x
, which, in the sense of
(
Tartar2007Sobolev
, Chap. 20)
, is the weak normal trace of
\nabla x
and takes values in the fractional Sobolev space

\mathcal{Y}:=H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega):=H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)^{\ast}.

Accordingly (and since the input space is trivial, we write
C
in place of
C\&D
), the output operator is

Cx=\gamma_{\mathrm{n}}(\nabla x),\qquad\gamma_{\mathrm{n}}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}\!\big(H_{\operatorname{div}}(\Omega),\,H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)\big),

where
\gamma_{\mathrm{n}}
denotes the normal trace operator. The system node properties were established in
(
PhilippReisSchaller2025
, Sec. 4.1)
for the more general class of advection–diffusion equations with Dirichlet control; hence they apply here as well.
Since the input space is trivial, the spaces
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{W}
from (
14
) and (
15
) are, for
A
the Dirichlet Laplacian as in (
36
), given by

\mathcal{V}=\dom(A),\qquad\mathcal{W}=\dom(A^{2}).

In what follows we consider the supply rate functional

s(y)=-\lVert y\rVert_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}.

For academic purposes, we seek for a nonpositive storage function. By Theorem
3.5
, the search for such a storage function reduces to the operator inequality

2\Re\left\langle\widetilde{P}x,Ax\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\ast},\mathcal{V}}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}\geq 0\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{V},

which is essentially a Lyapunov inequality in the sense of
(
TucsnakWeiss2009
, Thm. 5.1.1)
for
-\widetilde{P}
.
This implies that, if there were a bounded nonpositive solution
\widetilde{P}=P\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega))
, the system would be admissible in the sense of
(
TucsnakWeiss2009
, Def. 4.6.4)
.
However, as shown in
PreusslerSchwenninger2024LpAdmissibility
, the heat equation with Dirichlet boundary control is not admissible in the sense of
(
TucsnakWeiss2009
, Def. 4.6.1)
; and a duality argument then yields a contradiction for our setup.
Consequently, no bounded and nonpositive storage function exists for this problem. However, the following argumentation shows that an unbounded storage function exists: Let
\mathfrak{A}
be the semigroup generated by
A
. Then, by exponential stability of the Dirichlet Laplacian,
the mapping

\displaystyle\Psi:\quad\dom(A)

\displaystyle\to\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y}),

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto C\mathfrak{A}(\cdot)x

is well-defined. Then, by
(
ReisSchaller2025LQ
, Prop. 2.10)
,
\Psi
is closable as an operator from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})
. Denote this closure by
\overline{\Psi}:\mathcal{X}\supset\dom(\overline{\Psi})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:\quad\dom(\mathcal{S})=\dom(\overline{\Psi})

\displaystyle\to{\mathbb{R}},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto-\left\lVert\overline{\Psi}x\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}

is a closed and nonnegative form with

\mathcal{V}=\dom(A)\subset\dom(\overline{\Psi})=\dom(\mathcal{S}).

The operator

\widetilde{P}\in\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{b}}(\dom(\mathcal{S}),\dom(\mathcal{S})^{\ast})
such that (
12
) holds for
h
as in (
10
) then fulfills

\forall\,x_{1},x_{2}\in\dom(\mathcal{S}):\quad\langle\widetilde{P}x_{1},x_{2}\rangle_{\dom(\mathcal{S})^{\ast},\dom(\mathcal{S})}=-\langle\overline{\Psi}x_{1},\overline{\Psi}x_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}.

Further, by
\mathfrak{A}Ax=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\mathfrak{A}x
for all
x\in\dom(A)
, we obtain that

\forall\,x\in\dom(A^{2}):\,\Psi Ax=\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\Psi x.

Invoking the latter, we obtain, by integration by parts, that for all
x\in\mathcal{W}=\dom(A^{2})
,

\displaystyle 2\Re\left\langle\widetilde{P}x,Ax\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\ast},\mathcal{V}}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\Re\left\langle\overline{\Psi}x,\overline{\Psi}Ax\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\Re\left\langle{\Psi}x,{\Psi}Ax\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\Re\left\langle{\Psi}x,\tfrac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}{\Psi}x\right\rangle_{\mathrm{L}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0};\mathcal{Y})}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert(\Psi x)(0)\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}.

However, by
\mathfrak{A}(0)=I_{\mathcal{X}}
, we have

\forall\,x\in\dom(A^{2}):\quad\big({\Psi}x\big)(0)=Cx.

This gives

2\Re\left\langle\widetilde{P}x,Ax\right\rangle_{\mathcal{V}^{\ast},\mathcal{V}}-\lVert Cx\rVert_{\mathcal{Y}}^{2}=0\geq 0,

which shows that
\mathcal{S}
is a storage function.

Auxiliary results on system nodes

We collect here a few auxiliary results on system nodes. Thematically, placement at the end of Section

We start with a lemma which states that the inclusions
\mathcal{W}\subset\dom(A\&B)\subset\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{X}
with respect to the norm
\lVert x\rVert_{\mathcal{X}_{-1}}:=\lVert(\alpha I_{\mathcal{X}}-A)^{-1}x\rVert_{\mathcal{X}}
for some
\alpha
in the resolvent of

. The operator

extends to closed and densely defined operator

, with the same spectrum as

(
TucsnakWeiss2009

.
Further, there exists an operator

such that

is an extension of
A\&B
. The domain of
A\&B
(equally: the domain of
S
) satisfies

\dom(A\&B)=\{\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}\,|\,\mathopen{}A_{-1}x+Bu\in\mathcal{X}\},

(37)

see
(
Staf05
, Def. 4.7.2 & Lem. 4.7.3)
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\
C\&D\end{smallmatrix}\right]}
be a system node on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{Y})
. Then

\mathcal{W}
as in (
15
) is a core of
A\&B
, and
\dom(A\&B)
is dense in
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}
.

Proof.

Since
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup, it is densely defined
(
EngeNage00
, Chap. 2, Thm. 1.4)
. Hence, a combination of (
37
) with property
(c)
from Definition
2.1
implies that
A\&B
is densely defined.

It remains to prove that
\mathcal{W}
is a core of
A\&B
. Since
A
generates a strongly continuous semigroup, it follows from
(
EngeNage00
, Chap. 2, Thm. 1.10)
that
\lambda I_{\mathcal{X}}-A
is bijective for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Consequently, the operator

\displaystyle T:\quad\dom(A\&B)

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U},

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)-\lambda x\\[-2.84526pt]
\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

is bijective. It can be further seen that the inverse of

\displaystyle T^{-1}:\quad\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle\to\dom(A\&B),

\displaystyle\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\displaystyle\mapsto\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(A-\lambda I_{\mathcal{X}})^{-1}x-(A_{-1}-\lambda I_{\mathcal{X}})^{-1}Bu\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right).

Now assume that
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\dom(A\&B)
. To prove the desired result, we have to show that there exists a sequence
\big(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{n}\\
u_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big)
in
\mathcal{V}
which converges in
\dom(A\&B)
to
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. Since

, there exists a sequence
\big(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}z_{n}\\
w_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big)

w_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big).

Then it follows immediately that
\big(\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{n}\\
u_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\big)
converges in
\dom(A\&B)
to
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x\\
u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

\forall\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}:\quad A\&B\left(\begin{smallmatrix}x_{n}\\

The latter gives

u_{n}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathcal{W}

Next we prove two lemmas on smooth trajectories of systems governed by system nodes.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
{\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A\&B\\

u(\cdot)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in\mathrm{C}([0,T];\mathcal{W}).

Proof.

Closedness of

together with smoothness of

,

and
