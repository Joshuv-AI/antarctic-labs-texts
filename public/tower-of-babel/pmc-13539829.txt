Chlordecone (Kepone) exposure in the French West Indies: the urgent need to address a flawed management approach
Authors: Dabor Resiere, Jonathan Florentin, Astrid Monfort, Frédéric Lapostolle, Bruno Megarbane, Hatem Kallel, Remi Nevière
Published: 2026

Chlordecone is an organochlorine pesticide that has been used in banana plantations on the French Caribbean islands to combat the banana weevil, a pest that ravaged crops. While chlordecone poses major risks to human health, many countries, including the United States, cancelled all registrations for chlordecone use by the end of 1977.1 In the French Caribbean islands, chlordecone was used massively until 1993, leading to the contamination of 20 000–30 000 hectares of agricultural land. Due to its persistence, it has permanently contaminated the soil and water and even continues to affect crops and livestock production. For example, 11% of local food products exceed safety thresholds.2

Dietary exposure through the consumption of contaminated food products is the main route of human exposure to chlordecone with the highest bioavailability, owing to its high rate of intestinal absorption (approximately 90% of the ingested amount) and passage into the general circulation. Currently, dietary exposure remains the main route and source of exposure to chlordecone for Caribbean populations.1 The molecule is transported via the circulatory system (portal vein) to the liver, which allows its conjugation with glucuronic acid (glucuro-conjugation) or a sulfate group (sulfo-conjugation), thus facilitating its elimination via the biliary tract and then excretion in the faeces. While urinary excretion is negligible, most of the molecules eliminated from the body via the biliary tract are reabsorbed by the intestinal wall. This enterohepatic recirculation contributes to the persistence of the molecule in the body, with an average plasma half-life of 131 days in humans.1 In 2021, the French Sanitary Agency proposed a chronic internal toxicity reference value (TRV) of 0.4 µg of chlordecone per litre of plasma, which is the concentration of chlordecone in the blood below which the long-term risk of adverse effects is considered negligible.2 Consistent with this peculiar pharmacokinetics limiting elimination, chlordecone has been detected in >90% in the general population of Martinique and Guadeloupe in a survey performed in 2013–2014. Among those, 14% of people in Guadeloupe and 25% in Martinique present concentrations higher than the internal TRV.3 Moreover, 40% of breast milk samples contain measurable chlordecone levels.

Occupational, environmental and dietary exposure to this contaminant has been consistently associated with major health risks, such as chlordecone exposure and prostate cancer,4 transgenerational epigenetic effects,5 neurodevelopmental impairments6 7 and prematurity.8 Chlordecone is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans), based primarily on animal evidence and human data. Notably, Martinique records among the world’s highest prostate cancer incidence (227 per 100 000), more than double the rate of mainland France (approximately 90–103 per 100 000).4 9 Moreover, recent exploratory geographical studies have provided detailed information on the link between cancer incidence and organochlorine pesticide exposure in the French West Indies (REF: doi: 10.1186 /s12885-022-09886-6 and doi: 10.1186 /s12885-024-13221-6).9 In these studies, the risk of developing thyroid, stomach and rectal cancer in women and melanoma in men increased significantly with the level of pesticide contamination. In sharp contrast, no link between the incidence of prostate cancer and chlordecone soil contamination was documented in these studies. To address this health issue, France’s Plan Chlordecone IV (2021) mandated universal free chlordecone testing—a well-intentioned political gesture that unfortunately substitutes biomarker measurement for comprehensive clinical assessment. Since 2021, chlordecone blood measurements have been performed in >100 000 inhabitants between Martinique and Guadeloupe, with minimal therapeutic intervention beyond dietary recommendations to limit exposure.10 11 Strategies aimed at increasing chlordecone elimination were omitted in the Plan Chlordecone IV despite valuable efforts to promote clinical research in this area (ClinicalTrials.gov ID NCT06026228, University Hospital of Martinique).

We challenge the fundamentally flawed public health strategy in the French West Indies, where chlordecone blood levels serve as the primary criterion for clinical management and compensation, an approach lacking scientific validity that perpetuates health inequities. Martinique and Guadeloupe face an unprecedented environmental threat affecting approximately 800 000 people. The French National Assembly’s March 2024 recognition of state responsibility advances environmental justice, yet only 45 victims had received compensation by late 2023, and a March 2025 court ruling compensated merely 11 individuals (nine with prostate cancer, two with recurrent miscarriages).12

This chlordecone-centric approach is toxicologically unsound. Single biomarker measurements inadequately characterise chronic exposure patterns or predict disease severity. Similar to how blood alcohol levels produce vastly different clinical presentations at identical concentrations, chlordecone levels reflect body burden at one timepoint but cannot distinguish ongoing exposure from recent contamination or declining historical exposure without serial measurements. While biomonitoring serves essential functions for population surveillance and epidemiological research, relying predominantly on arbitrary blood thresholds for individual clinical management and compensation creates a dangerous paradox: patients with levels below thresholds may be excluded from care despite being symptomatic, whereas individuals with levels above thresholds receive unnecessary interventions despite being asymptomatic.

We propose chlordecone testing for vulnerable populations (preconception couples, pregnant women, newborns via cord blood and agricultural workers), epidemiological surveillance and research and voluntary annual screening for exposed populations with proper counselling. Clinical decisions must integrate exposure history, symptomatology, disease manifestation and longitudinal health monitoring rather than isolated biomarkers. Decades of chronic environmental exposure demand clinical expertise and rigorous longitudinal research—not reductionist reliance on single measurements that perpetuate patterns of medical surveillance while failing to deliver meaningful therapeutic interventions. In this context, the University Hospital of Martinique and Guadeloupe are equipped with clinical units, the Regional Institute for Occupational and Environmental Pathology (CRPPE), devoted to these specific issues. As part of its duty, CRPPE helps in the inclusion of prostate cancer among compensable occupational diseases in workers exposed to chlordecone.

Prostate cancer linked to excessive exposure to chlordecone is now officially recognised as an occupational disease following the official publication of a decree on 22 December 2021 of the French National Assembly. The CRPPE’s plan to establish a specific support system for individuals in the Caribbean to help them complete the necessary procedures to obtain compensation from pesticide victims’ compensation fund, which has a budget of €92 million.

In conclusion, the well-established health concerns related to chlordecone contamination in the French Caribbean Islands of Martinique and Guadeloupe require substantial investment in comprehensive epidemiological studies, clinical trials and multifaceted assessment integrating clinical presentation with scientific evidence to ensure appropriate care and just compensation for all victims.
