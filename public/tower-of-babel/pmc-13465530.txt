Biomechanical comparison of three-dimensional titanium and CFR-PEEK plates for mandibular subcondylar fracture fixation: a three-dimensional finite element analysis
Authors: Recep Ünal, Yeliz Kılınç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFixation of mandibular subcondylar fractures presents challenges due to limited surgical access and the small size of the proximal fragment; therefore, single-piece and easily applicable three-dimensional (3D) plates may be considered as an alternative. Although titanium is frequently used as a plate material, it has been reported to have certain clinical and imaging-related limitations. In comparison, Carbon Fiber–Reinforced Polyetheretherketone (CFR-PEEK) has emerged as a favorable alternative for mandibular fracture fixation due to its bone-like elastic properties, radiolucency, and biocompatibility. This study aimed to compare CFR-PEEK and titanium in terms of stress distribution and fixation stability under loading conditions using 3D finite element analysis (FEA).MethodsThree single-piece, 3D plate designs (lambda, rhombic, and trapezoid) were modeled with two materials (titanium alloy and 30% CFR-PEEK), yielding six fixation models of mandibular subcondylar fracture. Two static loading conditions were applied: contralateral molar loading and incisal loading. Von Mises stresses in plates and screws, principal stresses in cortical and cancellous bone, and interfragmentary displacement were calculated.ResultsFor both simulated loading conditions, CFR-PEEK exhibited lower von Mises stresses in plates and screws and lower principal stresses in cortical and cancellous bone than titanium, but showed greater interfragmentary displacement. Contralateral molar loading produced higher stresses and displacement than incisal loading. The lowest von Mises stresses in both plates and screws were observed with the CFR-PEEK lambda plate, whereas bone stresses were lowest and most uniformly distributed with the CFR-PEEK rhombic plate. Interfragmentary displacement was minimal in the titanium lambda model and maximal in the CFR-PEEK trapezoid model.ConclusionsCompared with titanium, CFR-PEEK 3D plate constructs reduced stress concentrations in the fixation hardware and surrounding bone but resulted in greater interfragmentary displacement under both loading conditions. Plate geometry and material choice should balance the clinical priority of rigidity versus stress mitigation.

Introduction

Mandibular fractures represent the most frequently encountered facial skeletal injuries requiring operative management, with subcondylar fractures constituting the most common fracture pattern [1]. Fractures of the mandibular condylar process are highly prevalent and have been reported to account for approximately 25–35% of all mandibular fractures [2]. Condylar fractures are typically produced by indirect mechanisms. After an impact to the symphysis or body, forces are transmitted along the mandible and concentrate at the contralateral subcondylar area, where a fracture may occur [3, 4]. Mandibular condyle is integral to temporomandibular joint (TMJ) biomechanics and occlusal function. Management prioritizes preservation of TMJ function, anatomical reduction, and the establishment of a stable occlusion [5, 6].

Closed reduction for subcondylar fractures is a noninvasive, conservative option, but it can be used only in selected cases and usually requires a long period of intermaxillary fixation [6, 7]. In contrast, open reduction can be used in more cases, provides better fracture stability, and often needs no intermaxillary fixation or only a short period. For these reasons, open reduction has been preferred more often in recent years [8].

Studies show that using only one straight miniplate often does not give enough stability, while two straight miniplates placed in different directions provide better support in biomechanical tests [9, 10]. However, access to the condylar region is often limited, and the small size of the fracture segments favors the use of minimal hardware. Applying two straight miniplates with four screws each may compromise the remaining bone stock and can be more difficult to position and secure intraoperatively [11]. These challenges have stimulated the development of compact single, 3D plate designs for subcondylar fixation. Such designs aim to occupy less space and may facilitate endoscopically assisted approaches while providing favorable biomechanical performance [1, 12].

Titanium has long been considered the gold-standard material. However, the literature reports several limitations, including infection and implants that may be felt or seen under the skin. In some cases, implant removal may be required. Additional concerns include the release of titanium particles and possible changes in radiation dose during radiotherapy [13–15].

In recent years, CFR-PEEK has emerged as a viable alternative to titanium owing to its “bone-like” elastic modulus and favorable biocompatibility profile. An elastic modulus approximating that of cortical bone (~ 18 GPa) promotes more physiological load sharing within the implant–bone complex, thereby limiting local stress concentrations [16, 17]. Its intrinsic radiolucency reduces Computerized Tomography and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (CT/MRI) artifacts, while chemical stability and biological inertness mitigate the risk of adverse tissue responses [18, 19]. Within this context, CFR-PEEK offers a rational advantage—particularly with respect to stress management in peri-screw regions and compatibility with diagnostic imaging [20, 21].

Evidence for the “bone-like” mechanical behavior of CFR-PEEK has also been reported at the plate–screw interface. Schliemann et al. observed that closer elastic-modulus matching between implant and bone was associated with a significant reduction in interfacial stresses [22]. Dessoky et al. noted that the greater flexibility (lower rigidity) of PEEK constructs has been reported to reduce stresses at the bone–screw interface and may decrease the risk of screw pullout/pushout [23]. Consistently, a systematic review by Li et al. synthesized findings indicating favorable performance of CFR-PEEK across multiple domains—including durability—supporting its consideration as a reliable alternative [24].

FEA is widely regarded as a noninvasive, repeatable, and highly accurate method, grounded in mathematical modeling, for characterizing complex anatomical structures and analyzing their mechanical properties [25]. In this context, 3D FEA enables a comparative assessment of subcondylar fracture biomechanics by evaluating different material–geometry combinations under identical boundary conditions and jointly analyzing clinically relevant outputs [26, 27]. While FEA studies do not yield definitive clinical conclusions, they can elucidate how forces and stress fields are distributed and interrelated within the bounds of biological variability [28].

CFR-PEEK plates have been directly compared with titanium plates in only a limited number of FEA studies, particularly with respect to load sharing in the condylar/subcondylar region and stress distribution around the screws. This study aimed to evaluate, by means of 3D FEA, the biomechanical behavior of three plate-osteosynthesis methods for mandibular subcondylar fractures—using titanium and CFR-PEEK plates—in terms of load transfer and stress distribution across the fixation hardware and bone.

Materials and methods

This FEA was conducted in collaboration with Gazi University Faculty of Dentistry and Tinus Technologies. Geometry preparation, meshing, model generation, and static structural analyses were performed on HP workstations. The modeling pipeline combined 3D medical image processing, reverse engineering/CAD, and solver-based analysis. A Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) scan of an adult mandible was acquired and reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.1 mm. DICOM data were imported into 3D Slicer for segmentation based on appropriate Hounsfield thresholds, yielding 3D polygonal models that were exported in STL format. STL models were imported into ANSYS SpaceClaim (Ansys Inc., Canonsburg, PA, USA) for reverse engineering and solid modeling.

Surface meshes were first generated for all components using triangular (tria) elements, followed by volumetric meshing with tetrahedral solid elements. A general mesh size of 0.1–0.2 mm was used to preserve geometric detail while improving numerical resolution, particularly in contact-related regions. Triangular surface meshing and tetrahedral solid elements were preferred because the investigated structures exhibited complex three-dimensional geometries, curved transitions, irregular boundaries, and multiple contact interfaces. In addition to the global mesh-size definition, finer meshing was applied in regions where contact, load transfer, and stress concentration were expected in order to improve local solution accuracy and maintain comparability across models [29, 30].

A 2 mm uniform offset was applied to generate the cortical shell [31]. The inner surface of the cortical shell defined the trabecular core, which was created accordingly. All parts were positioned and oriented in the anatomical coordinate system to complete the baseline mandible model.

A left subcondylar fracture configuration was created on the baseline mandible [32]. The same fracture pattern was used in all models to enable direct comparison across fixation designs and materials.

Three plate geometries (lambda, rhombic, and trapezoid) were modeled in SpaceClaim with a thickness of 1 mm. Screws were generated as 2 mm in diameter and 6 mm in length. The 3D models of plates and screws were developed based on dimensions from the KLS Martin catalog (KLS Martin, Tuttlingen, Germany). For each plate design, two different materials were analyzed: titanium alloy (Ti–6Al– 7Nb) and 30% CFR-PEEK, producing six distinct models (Fig. 1). The models were as follows:Model 1 included one seven-hole lambda plate in titanium, positioned along the posterior border of the mandible with the oblique extension under the sigmoid notch; two screws were inserted in the proximal (condylar) fragment, and the remaining five were distributed along the distal ramus (Fig. 1A).Model 2 included one four-hole rhombic plate in titanium, centered across the fracture line with two screws on each side of the fracture (superior and inferior to the line) (Fig. 1C).Model 3 included one four-hole trapezoidal plate in titanium, oriented with the wider base centered in the distal (ramus) fragment and the narrow end in the proximal condylar fragment; two screws were placed on each side of the fracture (Fig. 1E).Models 4–6 were identical to Models 1–3, respectively, except that the plates were manufactured from CFR-PEEK instead of titanium; the plate geometry, screw number, and positions were unchanged (Fig. 1B-D-F).

Loading and boundary conditions

Two static vertical loading conditions were simulated for each model:

Contralateral molar loading: A static vertical load of 250 N was applied perpendicular to the occlusal surface of the right first mandibular molar [12] (Fig. 2A).Incisal loading: A static vertical load of 30 N was applied to each mandibular incisor (total 120 N) [36] (Fig. 2B).

The mandibular condylar region was constrained by selecting the corresponding surface areas through node/area definitions, and all translational and rotational degrees of freedom (Ux, Uy, Uz, Uxy, Uxz, Uyz) were fixed to zero to represent condylar support. In total, 12 linear static analyses were performed.

A frictionless contact was assigned at the fracture interface, permitting separation under load and free tangential sliding [37, 38]. All other contacting components were modeled with bonded contact, which constrains relative motion and enforces displacement compatibility across the interface. Each plate–monocortical screw configuration was positioned to maximize conformity and contact with the underlying bone surface as well as with the associated screw hole in the plate.

Models prepared in ANSYS® Workbench were exported to and solved with LS-DYNA (LSTC, Livermore, CA, USA). Linear static solutions were obtained for each loading condition.

Results

Von Mises stresses were calculated for fixation plates and screws, while Pmax and Pmin values were evaluated for the cortical and cancellous bone. All stress values were given in MPa (N/mm²). The results were visualized using a color scale. Displacements along the X, Y, and Z axes were measured; these components were combined vectorially to calculate the resultant total displacement in millimeters.

Von mises stresses

The evaluation of von Mises stresses revealed that the stress values in titanium plates and screws were markedly higher than those in CFR-PEEK plates and screws under simulated loading conditions. Contralateral molar loading produced higher von Mises stresses than incisal loading (Fig. 3).

Fig. 3Von Mises stress distribution in plates under contralateral molar and incisal loading conditions

Von Mises stress distribution in plates under contralateral molar and incisal loading conditions

Von Mises stresses were determined to be higher in both the plates and the screws in the regions adjacent to the fracture line (Figs. 4 and 5).

The lowest von Mises stress values in the fixation plates and screws were observed in the CFR-PEEK lambda design, whereas the highest values were recorded in the titanium rhombic design under contralateral molar and incisal loading. The trapezoid plate exhibited lower von Mises stress values than the rhombic plate for CFR-PEEK and titanium material under both loading conditions.

Principal stress values in bone

For titanium plates, both Pmax and Pmin values in cortical bone were higher than those observed with CFR-PEEK plates under contralateral molar and incisal loading conditions. Contralateral molar loading produced substantially higher Pmax and Pmin values than incisal loading (Figs. 6 and 7).

Pmin stress values in cortical and cancellous bone under contralateral and incisal loading conditions

Pmax and Pmin values in cortical bone showed the same ranking across plate designs for CFR-PEEK and titanium material for both loading conditions. The rhombic plate exhibited lower Pmax and Pmin values with a more homogeneous stress distribution, followed by the lambda and trapezoid plates, respectively.

Regarding the localization of stress accumulation in cortical bone, Pmax values were predominantly observed around the screws superior to the fracture line and along the region extending from the sigmoid notch toward the condylar head, with additional concentrations around the remaining screws (Fig. 8). Pmin values were concentrated around the screws superior to the fracture line and along the posterior ramus, extending superiorly toward the condylar head at the level of these screws (Fig. 9).

Displacement

In the present study, CFR-PEEK demonstrated higher displacement values than titanium under all loading conditions. Contralateral molar loading produced markedly greater displacement than incisal loading (Fig. 12).

Discussion

The treatment approach for mandibular condylar fractures is determined by the fracture type, the extent of displacement, the patient’s overall condition, and occlusion [38]. Although closed reduction provides satisfactory outcomes in many cases, Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF) becomes necessary in situations such as displacement of the condyle into the middle cranial fossa, the presence of a foreign body, marked extracapsular displacement, or malocclusion that cannot be corrected with closed treatment [39].

Recent innovations in osteosynthesis systems and advances in rigid fixation techniques have made open reduction a more widely adopted approach in the management of mandibular condylar fractures [40]. Contemporary miniplate–screw systems have become a reliable and effective option for the treatment of mandibular fractures by offering advantages such as less invasive application, greater stability, and improved biomechanical compatibility [41].

In craniofacial surgery, various implant materials are used; current evidence does not demonstrate a clear superiority of any single material, and titanium is widely used especially for fracture fixation. In radiotherapy, metal implants can affect target delineation and dose distribution and should therefore be considered during treatment planning; moreover, after maxillary/mandibular plate–screw fixation, titanium particles have been detected in the periosteum and in the soft tissue surrounding titanium plates [42–45].

CFR-PEEK has increasingly been investigated as a promising alternative, offering advantages such as bone-like elasticity, reduced radiographic artifacts, and a favorable biocompatibility profile [46, 47]. CFR-PEEK are most commonly used outside the dental field in spine surgery, orthopedic trauma/osteotomy, and sports medicine. CFR-PEEK spinal implants are radiolucent and may reduce postoperative imaging artifacts, which can theoretically facilitate more precise radiotherapy planning [48]. In orthopedic trauma and osteotomy procedures, screw and plate options for long-bone fractures, as well as high tibial osteotomy plates, are available [20]. In sports medicine, suture anchors and interference screws used for anterior cruciate ligament fixation are widely utilized [49, 50].

In the present study, the authors aimed to determine the clinical implications of comparing the effectiveness of CFR-PEEK with titanium for the fixation of mandibular subcondylar fractures, thereby providing guidance for future clinical studies and contributing to the identification of an ideal plate material.

Fracture-interface contact assumptions are known to materially influence finite element predictions in mandibular fracture models. In a parasymphyseal mandibular fracture model, Caraveo et al. showed that, relative to a non-contact model, frictionless contact reduced interfragmentary displacement and plate stress, whereas the addition of friction reduced these values even further, indicating that a frictionless interface should be interpreted as an intermediate simplification rather than as a no-contact worst-case condition [37]. Available tribological evidence also suggests that a single universally accepted coefficient of friction cannot yet be assumed for mandibular fracture surfaces. In particular, Ben Achour et al. demonstrated that friction measurements involving vital mandibular bone vary with material pairing and emphasized that absolute values across studies are strongly affected by motion pattern, donor bone, harvesting region, specimen preparation, and surface condition [51]. This broader uncertainty is consistent with earlier bone-friction work in long bones, where both bone type and saw-induced surface roughness significantly altered the coefficient of static friction, underscoring that friction data are highly interface- and preparation-dependent rather than directly transferable across anatomical sites [52]. In addition, studies using a frictionless contact definition at the fracture surfaces are also present in the literature, indicating that this simplification continues to be employed in contemporary and validated mandibular fixation models [53–55]. Accordingly, the fracture interface was modeled as frictionless in the present study to avoid anchoring the analysis to an uncertain mandibular-specific friction value. This assumption may overestimate absolute displacement magnitudes and hardware stresses relative to frictional-contact models, while still yielding lower values than models that neglect fracture-surface contact altogether. As the same interface definition was applied consistently across all models, the ranking of the plate designs would likely remain the same under the frictionless contact assumption.

Various studies have shown that during the healing period, masticatory force measurements can reach approximately 44–128 N in the incisal region, whereas forces in the molar region may increase to 121–250 N [36, 56]. FEA studies on condylar fractures have reported that loading applied to the contralateral side results in greater loading of the subcondylar fracture region; therefore, contralateral loading has been adopted as the primary condition in such analyses [12, 28]. Based on the reviewed literature, loads of 120 N at the incisal region and 250 N at the contralateral first molar were applied in the present study, representing the upper range of postoperative bite-force values during the healing period and simulating potential worst-case static scenarios rather than routine functional loading. Under these conditions, the maximum von Mises stress recorded in the titanium plates was 435 MPa. Comparable mandibular finite element studies have reported nominal yield-strength values of approximately 825–880 MPa for titanium alloys used in fixation systems [34, 57]. With respect to CFR-PEEK, interpretation requires greater caution. Previous mandibular FEA has cited a value of 380 MPa for short CFR-PEEK [34]. However, representative manufacturer data for 30% carbon-fiber–reinforced PEEK show that failure-related strength values differ according to the selected mechanical criterion. In the KetaSpire® guide, 30% CF-PEEK grades are reported to have tensile strengths of 201–223 MPa, flexural strengths of 311–321 MPa, and compressive strengths of 173–188 MPa by ASTM methods [58].

In the present study, the maximum von Mises stress recorded in the CFR-PEEK plates was 201 MPa. Accordingly, the present plate results do not suggest immediate static fracture of either titanium or CFR-PEEK under the simulated loading scenario. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted primarily in a comparative biomechanical sense rather than as direct predictors of absolute clinical failure.

Some FEA studies have shown that, compared with incisal loading, contralateral molar loading produces higher von Mises stresses and greater interfragmentary micromotion in mandibular fracture fixation models [34, 35, 59]. Comparable findings were obtained in our study. Although incisal loading is anatomically farther from the plate region and therefore has a longer moment arm, it is applied at the midline within a symmetric region and may therefore generate a smaller asymmetric bending moment than contralateral molar loading. In addition, masticatory forces are greater in the molar region. The locations of peak stress concentration do not change between contralateral molar and incisal loading; however, stress magnitudes are lower under incisal loading than under contralateral molar loading [34, 60, 61].

In FEA studies on subcondylar mandibular fractures, the frequency of investigations focusing on the use of double miniplate fixation is noteworthy [1, 12, 62]. However, in cases where the condylar fragment is small, placement of multiple miniplates may be technically challenging. For this reason, 3D, one-piece plate designs were selected in our study given their easier adaptation, shorter placement and operative times, compatibility with endoscopic-assisted and minimally invasive approaches, and the potential to achieve outcomes comparable to or better than those of two straight miniplates [11, 12, 63, 64].

Diker et al. reported lower von Mises stress values in the fixation devices in models using CFR-PEEK plates [34]. Avcı et al. reported that CFR-PEEK plate–screw systems showed lower stress values within the fixation system compared with titanium plate–screw systems [35]. Some recent finite element studies on mandibular and long-bone fractures have reported lower von Mises stresses within CFR-PEEK–based fixation constructs than in comparable titanium systems; moreover, because their elastic modulus is closer to that of bone, CFR-PEEK implants may help mitigate stress shielding, which is considered a contributing factor to loosening [65, 66]. Consistent with previously reported findings, lower von Mises stress values were observed in all CFR-PEEK plates and screws than in their titanium counterparts in our study. In addition, all CFR-PEEK fixation systems showed lower principal stresses in the surrounding bone compared with titanium plates. Collectively, these findings indicate that CFR-PEEK may help reduce stress shielding and the associated bone resorption, and—by lowering stresses at the bone–screw interface—could potentially decrease the risk of screw loosening/pullout [23, 67].

Gupta et al. reported the highest values in the titanium lambda plate under molar loading [1]. Liokatis et al. reported that lambda plates are more advantageous under higher loads, whereas trapezoid plates may carry certain risks [68]. De Jesus et al. demonstrated that the lambda plate provides a more homogeneous stress distribution in both the osteosynthesis hardware and the surrounding bone. They suggested that it may be a more suitable option in cases where single-plate fixation is preferred [12]. Similarly, in our study, the lowest von Mises stresses in the plates and screws were observed in the CFR-PEEK lambda configuration, whereas the highest von Mises stresses were recorded in the titanium rhombic configuration. This finding was considered to be related to the lambda plate’s two-arm geometry, which runs parallel to the principal tensile and compressive stress trajectories in the bone [69, 70].

Finite element studies have shown that peak von Mises stresses commonly localize in the plate region and at the plate–screw junction, particularly in the screws (and adjacent plate holes) closest to the fracture/osteotomy line; in subcondylar configurations, additional stress concentrations may also be observed near the sigmoid notch [12, 59]. Similarly, in our study, higher von Mises stress concentrations were generally observed in the plates and screws superior to the fracture line and in the region adjacent to the sigmoid notch.

Regarding the localization of stress accumulation in cortical and cancellous bone, Pmax values were concentrated in regions adjacent to the sigmoid notch and around the screws, whereas Pmin values predominantly accumulated in areas adjacent to the posterior ramus. This pattern is consistent with studies indicating that functional loading generates tensile regions along the anterior aspect of the mandibular ramus and compressive regions along its posterior aspect [12, 71]. In the literature, a cortical–cancellous distinction in stress distribution is made only rarely; when reported, principal stress magnitudes are often described as lower in cancellous bone [12, 72]. Baş et al. and Ayali et al. reported that, under traumatic loading, stresses tend to concentrate predominantly within the cortical bone, whereas the presence of cancellous bone may promote a more homogeneous stress distribution and reduce localized stress accumulation [73, 74].

Several studies have also reported that, with titanium plates, displacement is lower than that observed with CFR-PEEK plates [22, 34, 75]. In parallel with these reports, CFR-PEEK demonstrated higher displacement values than titanium under all loading conditions in our study. In the FEA by De Jesus et al. evaluating condylar fractures, displacement at the fracture line was lowest with two straight plates, followed by slightly higher values with the lambda plate and the highest values with the trapezoid plate [12]. In Liokatis et al. FEA, all plate systems provided adequate stability under 135 N (displacement remained below the 0.15 mm critical threshold), whereas under 500 N the lambda plate showed the lowest displacement, and the trapezoidal and two straight plates exhibited higher displacement approaching the critical limit [68]. Some finite element studies have reported that fixation constructs providing a greater number of fixation points may reduce displacement at the fracture site [12, 76, 77]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated that, under both loading conditions, displacement decreased in a screw number–dependent manner; accordingly, displacement values were ordered from lowest to highest for the lambda, rhombic, and trapezoid plate configurations, respectively. Within this context, plate material and geometry can be considered to contribute substantially to the stabilization of fracture fragments.

Recent computational studies have broadened mandibular biomechanics research beyond conventional linear static stress comparisons. Wan et al. extended conventional stress-distribution analysis by combining FEM and extended FEM, demonstrating that residual stresses generated during plate prebending and screw tightening may increase fracture susceptibility in titanium mandibular reconstruction plates and underscoring the importance of considering both static and cyclic loading in the assessment of implant longevity [78]. Xin et al. proposed a preclinical workflow for polymer-based mandibular implants that integrates digital-twin finite element analysis with in vivo implantation and in vitro mechanical testing. More broadly, current finite element research is moving toward personalized in silico models for design optimization and translational decision-making, including patient-specific mandibular reconstruction systems developed with finite element analysis and topology optimization [79]. Future studies should extend beyond conventional linear static evaluation to include more detailed fracture and fatigue analyses and, where possible, incorporate integrated in silico, in vitro, and in vivo validation strategies to strengthen the translational relevance of the findings.

Nevertheless, the literature also highlights several limitations indicating that FEA may not fully capture the true nature of fractures. Because models can be highly sensitive to boundary conditions and loading assumptions, FEA may yield stress values that are higher than those observed clinically [80, 81]. Moreover, representing bone (and, where applicable, fixation components) with homogeneous and isotropic material properties may not adequately reflect the inherently heterogeneous and direction-dependent mechanical behavior of biological tissues; therefore, absolute stress and displacement values should be interpreted with caution [80]. Although CFR-PEEK may exhibit direction-dependent behavior depending on fiber architecture, the material considered in the present study was 30% short carbon-fiber–reinforced PEEK rather than a continuous-fiber system. Short CFR-PEEK has been described as a composite of randomly aligned short carbon fibers, most of them shorter than 0.4 mm, producing an isotropic homogeneous material [20]. Green likewise described short CFR-PEEK as a discontinuous short-fiber composite in which most fibers in injection-molded parts fall below approximately 0.4 mm, with lower reinforcing efficiency than continuous-fiber composites and a practically processable fiber content of around 30%; in contrast, continuous-fiber CFR-PEEK shows clearer orientation-dependent mechanical behavior [33]. Similar isotropic representations have also been adopted in previous finite element studies. Diker and Burak modeled short CFR-PEEK as a homogeneous, isotropic, linearly elastic material in mandibular fracture fixation analysis [34]. Yu et al. also modeled 30% CFR-PEEK within an isotropic, homogeneous framework, while Sağsöz et al. explicitly noted that CF-PEEK may exhibit orthotropic behavior due to fiber orientation but nevertheless adopted isotropic properties for comparability and consistency with previous implant finite element studies [82, 83]. Another recent study likewise treated CFR-PEEK as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic while acknowledging that this simplification may be limiting in the interpretation of polymer behavior [84]. Accordingly, the isotropic approximation used here should be interpreted as an accepted comparative finite element simplification for short 30% CFR-PEEK rather than as a claim that the material is universally orientation-independent under all conditions. Idealized contact definitions (e.g., bonded or frictional contact) at the bone–implant and screw–implant interfaces can alter load transfer and thereby affect local stress distributions. Mesh parameterization and geometric discretization may also lead to localized peak stress concentrations; therefore, absolute von Mises magnitudes should be interpreted with caution and are often more suitable for comparative analyses than for direct comparison with strength limits [80, 81]. In models based on static loading, neglecting mandibular kinematics and dynamic effects may misrepresent stress patterns [85]. Moreover, uncertainties in muscle forces, material constants, and fixation parameters limit the clinical accuracy of the outputs [53]. The present model used simplified static occlusal loading and idealized condylar support rather than a full physiologic representation of masticatory muscle forces and temporomandibular joint contact. Mandibular finite element models including muscle-force vectors, TMJ reaction analysis, and additional anatomic structures have been reported in the literature [1, 62]. However, simplified boundary conditions similar to those used here are also common in mandibular FEA studies, including models with condylar restriction under static molar loading, condylar-surface restriction under contralateral molar loading, and complete constraint of both condyles during incisal and molar clenching simulations [12, 34, 68]. Accordingly, this model should be regarded as a controlled structural simplification, not as a complete physiologic simulation of mandibular function. Such assumptions may influence absolute stress and displacement values by modifying mandibular kinematics, side-specific load transfer, and temporomandibular joint reaction behavior. Because the same loading and boundary conditions were applied consistently to all configurations, the comparative trends among plate designs and materials are likely to be more reliable than the absolute values.

Conclusion

CFR-PEEK fixation plates and screws generated lower von Mises and principal stresses than titanium under both loading conditions but showed greater displacement. Contralateral molar loading produced higher stresses and displacement than incisal loading; stresses were lowest in the CFR-PEEK lambda model and highest in the titanium rhombic model. Displacement was minimal in the lambda model and maximal in the trapezoid model. According to the results of the present study, titanium may be preferred when rigid stability is the primary goal, whereas CFR-PEEK may be considered when reducing stress concentration is prioritized. Future studies should improve material–geometry selection by focusing on more comprehensive approaches that are validated through biomechanical testing and prospective clinical outcomes, and that incorporate patient-specific variables (e.g., bone quality and fracture patterns), dynamic loading, and long-term fixation-related complications such as screw loosening.
