Efficient Identification of Permutation Symmetries in Many-Body Hamiltonians via Graph Theory
Saumya Shah, Patrick Rebentrost

Abstract
The computational cost of simulating quantum many-body systems can often be reduced by taking advantage of physical symmetries. While methods exist for specific symmetry classes, a general algorithm to find the full permutation symmetry group of an arbitrary Pauli Hamiltonian is notably lacking. This paper introduces a new method that identifies this symmetry group by establishing an isomorphism between the Hamiltonian’s permutation symmetry group and the automorphism group of a coloured bipartite graph constructed from the Hamiltonian. We formally prove this isomorphism and show that for physical Hamiltonians with bounded locality and interaction degree, the resulting graph has a bounded degree, reducing the computational problem of finding the automorphism group to polynomial time. The algorithm’s validity is empirically confirmed on various physical models with known symmetries. We further show that the problem of deciding whether two Hamiltonians are permutation-equivalent is polynomial-time reducible to the graph isomorphism problem using our graph representation. This work provides a general, structurally exact tool for algorithmic symmetry finding, enabling the scalable application of these symmetries to Hamiltonian simulation problems.



I
Introduction

The simulation of quantum many-body systems is a foundational problem in quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics. For classical computers, the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space in the number of qubits presents a computational challenge. While quantum computers offer a path forward, near-term algorithms are constrained by limited resources and noise. A primary strategy for mitigating these costs is to exploit the intrinsic physical symmetries of the target Hamiltonian.
The existence of a symmetry generally implies a conserved quantity, which partitions the system’s exponentially large Hilbert space into smaller, independent subspaces, or
symmetry sectors

[
34
,
19
]
. Confining the simulation to a relevant sector can thus reduce the dimensionality of the problem. To exploit this technique in a scalable manner, the symmetries of the Hamiltonian must be identified algorithmically.

Several common types of symmetries are often identified. Methods for identifying discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries, for example, are well-established and enable
tapering
, a technique that removes qubits from the simulation by replacing their operators with eigenvalues
[
8
,
41
]
. Similarly, continuous symmetries like
U(1)
and
SU(2)
are typically identified by checking if the Hamiltonian commutes with their respective generators
[
13
]
.
However, a general method for identifying the full permutation symmetry group
G_{\mathcal{H}}
of an arbitrary interacting Pauli Hamiltonian is less established. The lack of a general algorithm to find it represents a significant research gap that hinders the construction of scalable permutation-equivariant circuits.

In this work, we address this gap by establishing a formal graph-theoretic isomorphism. We demonstrate that any Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
, expressed as a weighted sum of Pauli strings, can be mapped to a faithful coloured bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}
such that the Hamiltonian’s permutation symmetry group is isomorphic to the graph’s automorphism group:

G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\text{aut}(\mathcal{G}).

(1)

This result reduces the problem of identifying permutation symmetries to the well-studied computational problem of finding a graph’s automorphism group. We further show that for physical Hamiltonians, the resulting graphs have bounded-degree properties that permit efficient, polynomial-time solutions. We provide numerical verification of the results on common physical Hamiltonians.
The algorithmic finding of permutation symmetries discussed in this work promises the scalable construction of permutation-equivariant circuits to find ground states of many Hamiltonians.

This paper is structured as follows: Section
\RN
2 surveys existing literature and presents deficiencies that motivate the method introduced in this paper. Section
\RN
3 discusses the mathematical preliminaries for the method, including permutation symmetries, their mathematical formalisms, and automorphism groups. It also discusses certain physical models of interest and their known symmetry groups. Section
\RN
4 introduces the explicit graph construction, proves the isomorphism between the permutation symmetry group and the automorphism group of this constructed graph, and presents a runtime analysis demonstrating polynomial-time complexity for physical systems. Section
\RN
5 provides empirical validation by applying this algorithm to several physical models and verifying that the computed automorphism group generators exactly match the known, analytically-derived permutation symmetries. Section
\RN
6 presents conclusions and directions for further research.

II
Related Work

2.1
Symmetries in Hamiltonians

An effective strategy to mitigate the exponential scaling of the Hilbert space in Hamiltonian simulation problems is to identify and exploit the intrinsic physical symmetries of the system’s Hamiltonian. The existence of a symmetry group implies a block-diagonal structure for the Hamiltonian, partitioning the Hilbert space into independent symmetry sectors. Subsequent confinement of the simulation to a single sector thus offers a significant reduction in the problem’s effective dimensionality.
The scalable application of this strategy depends on the algorithmic identification and exploitation of these symmetries. There are robust and efficient methods for several common symmetry classes. This section surveys methods in the algorithmic identification and exploitation of Hamiltonian symmetry groups.

Exploiting Symmetries in Hamiltonians.

The exploitation of symmetries is a primary strategy for mitigating the computational cost of finding properties of Hamiltonians. These strategies can be broadly partitioned into several distinct technical applications.

The most direct application has been the building of
symmetry-preserving ansätze
, which guarantee that the variational search remains in the correct subspace. Methods for this include symmetrising the generator pool using techniques like twirling
[
34
]
, building circuits that preserve particle number and other
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
symmetries
[
4
]
, and introducing specific symmetry-preserving gates for VQE
[
19
]
. This concept has been extended to compressing VQE ansätze by removing symmetry-breaking gates
[
23
]
, constructing minimal complete operator pools for adaptive methods
[
45
]
, and building permutation-symmetric quantum neural networks
[
40
]
. This approach is well-tested and has been shown to yield shallower circuits and reduce shot counts for adaptive methods
[
44
,
7
]
, and mitigate barren plateaus
[
40
]
in QNNs.

A complementary application of symmetry is
problem reduction
, which reduces the effective Hilbert space of the problem. The most prominent example is qubit tapering, first presented by Bravyi et al. to remove qubits based on
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries
[
8
]
. This was later extended to molecular point group symmetries
[
41
]
. Other reduction methods include mapping a local stoquastic Hamiltonian to one acting on a smaller symmetric subspace
[
9
]
, accelerating QAOA by reducing the cost Hamiltonian’s Hilbert space
[
42
,
43
,
37
]
, and compressing quantum protocols into perturbative gadgets
[
15
]
.

Finally, symmetries have been exploited in other contexts, such as developing symmetry-projected bases for
efficient measurement

[
47
]
and developing efficient
classical simulation
algorithms using tensor networks for permutation-invariant systems
[
3
]
.

Algorithmic Identification of Symmetries in Hamiltonians.

Several distinct families of methods have been developed for the algorithmic identification of Hamiltonian symmetries. The most established are
algebraic and constraint-based methods
. Bravyi et al.
[
8
]
present methods to algorithmically identify
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries from fermionic Hamiltonians by finding the kernel of a constructed check matrix. Setia et al.
[
41
]
review this method and introduce a new method to find molecular point group symmetries. Similarly, Varjas et al.
[
50
]
introduce the Qsymm framework to algorithmically find unitary symmetry generators from non-interacting Hamiltonians by solving linear commutation constraints.

A second, related approach involves
graph-theoretic techniques
. Bringewatt et al.
[
9
]
introduce techniques to map stoquastic Hamiltonians to vertex-coloured graphs to construct reduced Hamiltonians that act on a smaller symmetric subspace. Shaydulin et al.
[
43
]
propose mapping the automorphism group in graph problems to permutation symmetries in its corresponding Ising cost Hamiltonian for QAOA applications. Similarly, Prakash
[
37
]
also uses the automorphism group of the unweighted max-cut problem to partition edges into equivalence classes for cost Hamiltonian reduction.

A recent approach is the use of
machine learning and data-driven methods
. Dierkes et al.
[
14
]
introduce a Lie algebra framework that allows Hamiltonian neural networks to simultaneously learn both the symmetry group action and the total energy. Van der Ouderaa et al.
[
17
]
, for instance, present “Noether’s Razor”, a Hamiltonian neural network that learns parametrised conserved quantities directly from classical trajectory data. Hou et al.
[
24
]
also present a framework to learn observables from classical time evolution data.

Finally, various
numerical methods
were established. Moudgalya et al.
[
35
]
provide numerical techniques to find the commutant algebra of a Hamiltonian using simultaneous block diagonalisation and Liouvillian MPS methods. Ruzicka et al.
[
38
]
introduce spectral and algebraic methods to identify conserved observables for non-Hermitian, PT-symmetric Hamiltonians. Others have developed methods to reveal symmetries that are masked by common transformations like the Jordan-Wigner transform
[
16
]
.

2.2
The Graph Automorphism Problem

The Graph Automorphism Problem (GA) is in the same complexity class as the Graph Isomorphism Problem (GI), as each is polynomial-time reducible to the other
[
18
,
46
]
. GI is one of the few problems in the complexity class NP that is not known to be either P (solvable in polynomial time) or NP-complete
[
6
]
. While early methods had moderately exponential bounds
[
32
,
51
]
, the fastest known algorithm for the general case is Babai’s quasipolynomial-time algorithm
[
5
]
. This algorithm runs in time
O\left(\exp\left((\log n)^{O(1)}\right)\right)
and remains the best-known upper bound for arbitrary graphs, thus bounding the complexity of GA in the most general case.

For special classes of graphs, however, significantly faster algorithms are known. Of particular relevance to this work is the class of
bounded-degree graphs
, where the maximum vertex degree
d
is a constant. For these graphs, polynomial-time algorithms exist. Luks introduced a foundational algorithm based on recursive graph partitioning
[
32
]
with a runtime of
O\left(n^{O(d/\log{d})}\right)
. This bound has been subsequently improved by related techniques to
O(n^{\text{polylog}(d)})

[
36
,
20
]
.

In practice, GA is often solved by finding a
canonical labelling
of the graph, a problem to which GA is also reducible
[
28
]
. Modern solvers, such as Nauty
[
33
]
, Bliss
[
27
]
, and Saucy
[
28
]
, implement backtracking search trees based on individualisation-refinement algorithms
[
48
]
. While these practical solvers retain a worst-case exponential runtime
[
33
,
48
]
, they are known to be efficient for the majority of graphs encountered in practice.

2.3
Unaddressed Research Gaps

This work is motivated by primary deficiencies in existing methods.

First, the algorithmic identification of symmetries is effective for predefined, a priori known symmetry classes. Robust methods exist for discrete
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetries
[
8
]
, molecular point group symmetries
[
41
]
, and continuous groups like
U(1)
and
SU(2)
, which are typically found by checking commutation with known generators. Methods for non-interacting Hamiltonians are similarly limited by their reliance on linear commutation constraints
[
50
]
. A general-purpose, group-agnostic algorithm to find the full permutation group
G_{\mathcal{H}}
of an arbitrary, interacting Pauli Hamiltonian is notably lacking.

Second, existing graph-theoretic techniques are not general. These methods are specialised to specific problems such as stoquastic Hamiltonians
[
9
]
or specific Ising Hamiltonians used as cost functions for graph problems such as MaxCut
[
37
,
43
]
. A general isomorphism mapping an arbitrary Pauli Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
to a graph
\mathcal{G}
such that
G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
is absent from the literature.

Finally, recent numerical and machine learning methods are indirect or non-deterministic. Data-driven approaches, for example, rely on classical trajectory data to learn conserved quantities rather than deterministically identifying the symmetry group from the Hamiltonian’s structure
[
17
,
24
,
14
]
. Conversely, numerical methods that compute the full commutant algebra solve a far more general and computationally expensive problem than identifying the specific permutation subgroup
G_{\mathcal{H}}
.

In summary, the literature lacks a general, deterministic, and structurally exact algorithm to identify the full permutation symmetry group
G_{\mathcal{H}}
of an arbitrary Pauli Hamiltonian. This paper proposes to address this gap by introducing a novel graph-theoretic isomorphism for the algorithmic identification of the permutation symmetry group from Hamiltonians represented as weighted sums of Pauli strings.

III
Mathematical Preliminaries

3.1
Symmetries

Hamiltonian.

A Hamiltonian is an operator that represents the total energy of a physical system. In this paper, we consider a Hamiltonian represented as

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j}c_{j}p_{j},

(2)

where
c\in\mathbb{C}~\backslash~\{0\}
, and each
p
belongs to the Pauli group
P_{m}=\pm\{I,\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z}\}^{\otimes n}
for
n
qubits. Since it is known that Pauli strings provide a complete basis of operators on the Hilbert space of N qubits
[
29
]
, this construction generalises to any qubit Hamiltonian.
A Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
acting on an
n
-site system is called
k
-local if it can be expressed as a sum of terms

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{i}H_{i},

(3)

where each
H_{i}
acts non-trivially on at most
k
sites. The interaction graph
G=(V,E)
of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is defined by associating a vertex for each site in
V\in\{1,...,n\}
, and an edge
(u,v)\in E
whenever sites
u,v
jointly appear in the support of some term
H_{i}
. The Hamiltonian has bounded interaction degree
d
if the degree of every vertex in
G
satisfies

\deg(v)\leq d\quad\forall~v\in V,

(4)

i.e., each site interacts with at most
d
other sites.
Hamiltonians representing physical systems are known to be local
[
31
]
and have a bounded interaction degree
[
1
]
. This is observed in common physical systems like the Ising and Heisenberg models
[
25
,
30
,
22
]
.

Symmetries.

Let
X
be a mathematical object. A symmetry transformation is a mapping
g:X\to X
that preserves a relevant property of
X
, i.e., that property is invariant to
g
. The set of all transformations that preserve this property forms a
group

[
11
]
.
An operator
U
is a symmetry of Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
if it leaves the Hamiltonian invariant, i.e.,
U\mathcal{H}U^{\dagger}=\mathcal{H}
, which is equivalent to the commutation relation
[
13
]
:

[\mathcal{H},U]=0.

(5)

Permutation Symmetry.

Permutation invariance is a discrete symmetry where the system is invariant upon the exchange of particles. For example, if a two-qubit system with state
\ket{k}\ket{k'}
is invariant to
P_{12}
, where
P_{ij}
corresponds to an exchange of qubits
i
and
j
, then
\ket{k}\ket{k'}
,
\ket{k'}\ket{k}
, and, in general, any linear combination
c_{1}\ket{k}\ket{k'}+c_{2}\ket{k'}\ket{k}
lead to an identical set of eigenvalues upon measurement
[
39
]
. In this paper, we use the cyclic permutation notation, where successive elements are transposed in a cyclic manner. For example,

(\alpha\quad\beta\quad\gamma)

is a
3
-cycle that corresponds to the transpositions
\alpha\to\beta,\beta\to\gamma,\gamma\to\alpha
.
Using the Hamiltonian representation given in Eq. (
2
) with
n
qubits and
m
Pauli strings, we represent
\mathcal{H}
by the set of its (coefficient, Pauli string) pairs:

T=\{(c_{r},p_{r}):r\in[1,m]\}.

(6)

We assume that for each Pauli string
p
, there is at most one pair
(c,p)
, and that each
p_{r}
is chosen from
\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
(i.e., scalar phases are absorbed into the coefficient).
Define a permutation operator
\varsigma\in S_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
elements (representing qubits). The action of
\varsigma
on a Pauli string
p=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\alpha}^{i}
is defined as:

\varsigma(p)=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{\alpha}^{\varsigma^{-1}(i)}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n}\sigma_{\alpha}^{j}\text{ on qubit $\varsigma(j)$}.

(7)

For example, if
\varsigma=(1\quad 3)
, then
\varsigma(XIY)=YIX
. Define the symmetry group
G_{\mathcal{H}}
as the set of permutations that leave
\mathcal{H}
invariant, i.e.,

G_{\mathcal{H}}=\{\varsigma\in S_{n}:\varsigma(\mathcal{H})=\mathcal{H}\}.

(8)

This is equivalent to
\varsigma(T)=T
, as
\varsigma
is a permutation and
T
is finite.

3.2
Graph Automorphisms and Isomorphisms

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,C_{V},C_{E})
be a vertex- and edge-coloured graph as constructed in Sec.
4.1
. An automorphism of
\mathcal{G}
is a permutation
\phi
of the vertex set
\phi:V\to V
that preserves all structural properties and colouring of the graph
[
10
]
. Specifically, a permutation
\phi
is an automorphism iff it satisfies the following conditions for all t
v\in V
and all edges
(u,v)\in E
:

1.

Adjacency Preservation
:
(u,v)\in E\iff(\phi(u),\phi(v))\in E
.

2.

Vertex Colour Preservation
:
C_{V}(v)=C_{V}(\phi(v))
.

3.

Edge Colour Preservation
:
C_{E}((u,v))=C_{E}((\phi(u),\phi(v)))
.

The set of all such automorphisms for a graph
\mathcal{G}
forms a group under the operation of function composition, with the trivial identity permutation mapping all vertices to themselves. This group is known as the automorphism group of
\mathcal{G}
, and is denoted by
\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
in this paper.
\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
is a subgroup of the symmetric group
S_{|V|}
acting on vertices
V

[
10
]
. The computational problem of finding this group is the Graph Automorphism (GA) problem, which is known to be in the same complexity class as the Graph Isomorphism (GI) problem
[
18
,
46
]
. Some existing methods to solve this problem are discussed in Sec.
2.2
.

More generally, two coloured bipartite graphs
\mathcal{G}_{1}=(V_{1},E_{1},C_{V_{1}},C_{E_{1}}),\mathcal{G}_{2}=(V_{2},E_{2},C_{V_{2}},C_{E_{2}})
are isomorphic (i.e.,
\mathcal{G}_{1}\cong\mathcal{G}_{2}
) if there exists a bijection
\phi:V_{1}\to V_{2}
that preserves adjacency and colouring.

The Ising model was introduced by Lenz and Ising to theoretically characterise magnetic phase transitions in ferromagnetic lattice materials
[
30

25
]
. The transverse Ising model (TFIM) is a quantum analogue of the Ising model. It features a lattice with
Z_{i}Z_{j}
nearest-neighbour interactions as well as
X_{j}
terms representing interaction with an external transverse magnetic field
\Omega

[
49
]
. The TFIM is thus most generally characterised by the equation:

\mathcal{H}=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J_{ij}Z_{i}Z_{j}-\sum_{j}\Omega_{j}X_{j},

(9)

where
\langle ij\rangle
denotes nearest-neighbour interactions.

1D TFIM

Homogenuous TFIMs.

In the homogeneous case, all interaction strengths and fields are assumed uniform, i.e.,
J_{ij}=J
,
\Omega_{j}=\Omega
for all
i,j
. Therefore, the equation (
9
) reduces to the following equation:

\mathcal{H}=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}Z_{i}Z_{j}-\Omega\sum_{j}X_{j}.

(10)

It can be shown that the 1D homogeneous TFIM with
n

D_{n}
of order

n
-gon. Let the set of sites be
S=\{1,...,n\}

is a subgroup of the symmetry group

1.

Rotation
(

R_{1}:S\to S

R_{1}(k)=(k+s)\mod n

(1\quad 2\quad\dots\quad n)

(11)

\gcd(s,n)=1

2.

Reflection
(
P_{r}
): The operation of reflecting the system with the axis of symmetry passing through

. For odd

corresponds to a site. For even

,
r
corresponds to the midpoint of two sites.
P:S\to S
where
P(k)=(2r-k)\mod n
. This corresponds to the generator

(1\quad 2r-1)(2\quad 2r-2)\dots

(12)

It is also known that for open boundary conditions, the 1D homogeneous TFIM only exhibits the
C_{2}
symmetry group, which corresponds to the cyclic group of 2. The reflection operator
P_{(n-1)/2}
generates the symmetry group

Inhomogeneous TFIMs.

In the inhomogeneous case, interaction strengths between different pairs of nearest neighbours may vary, possibly due to impurities

. We consider the case where the coupling strength

2D TFIM

In the 2D homogeneous case, all interaction strengths and fields are assumed uniform. In addition, each site has up to four interaction terms (corresponding to the four nearest neighbours). We restrict our analysis to the simple case of the square lattice.

3.4
Heisenberg Model

The Heisenberg model
[
22
]
is used in the study of quantum phase transitions in spin systems
[
26
]
. Here, we consider a system composed of
n
sites, which is described by the Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}=-J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{j},

(16)

where
\langle ij\rangle
denotes neighbour interactions.
Furthermore, we consider the case where there is interaction between every pair of sites. This is also known as the mean-field Heisenberg XXX model
[
2
]
. Therefore, equation (
16
) reduces to:

\mathcal{H}=-J\sum_{i\neq j}X_{i}X_{j}+Y_{i}Y_{j}+Z_{i}Z_{j}.

(17)

Due to the uniform fully-connected nature of the system, it can be shown that Eq. (
17
) exhibits the symmetry group
S_{n}
, which corresponds to the set of all bijective functions from a set to itself, i.e.,
G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong S_{n}
. For a set of sites
S=\{1,...,n\}
, this group is generated by the set of adjacent transposition operators
\{T_{i}:i=1,...,n-1\}
, where each operator
T_{i}
swaps sites

, i.e.,

T_{i}(k)=\begin{cases}i+1&\text{if }k=i\\
i&\text{if }k=i+1\\
k&\text{Otherwise}.\end{cases}

(18)

These generators can be expressed explicitly as
(1\quad 2),(2\quad 3),...,(n-1\quad n)
.

Methodology

4.1
Graph Construction

From the permutation operator representation described in Sec.
3.1
, we construct a coloured bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E,C_{V},C_{E})
as follows:

•

V=V_{Q}~\cup~V_{T}
representing the
n
qubits, where
V_{Q}=\{q_{1},...,q_{n}\}
(the set of qubit nodes), and
V_{T}=\{t_{1},...,t_{m}\}
(the set of term nodes), with a bijection
f:T\to V_{T}
mapping
(c_{r},p_{r})\to t_{r}
.

•

E\subseteq V_{Q}\times V_{T}
. An edge
(q_{i},t_{r})
exists iff the Pauli operator of
p_{r}
at qubit
i
acts non-trivially (i.e., not an identity).

E=\{(q_{i},t_{r}):[p_{r}]_{i}\neq I\}

(19)

•

C_{V}:V\to\text{Colours}_{V}

–

For
q_{i}\in V_{Q}
,
C_{V}(q_{i})=\text{``qubit"}
.

–

For
t_{r}\in V_{E}
,
C_{V}(t_{r})=\text{(``term",$~c_{r}$) }
.

•

C_{E}:E\to\{X,Y,Z\}
. For an edge
e=(q_{i},t_{r})\in E,~C_{E}(e)=[p_{r}]_{i}

As an example, consider the Hamiltonian with real-valued coefficients:

\mathcal{H}=-1\cdot IXY+1\cdot YZZ-2\cdot YXI+2\cdot ZIZ-3\cdot XXI+3\cdot YZI.

(20)

Its corresponding graph is constructed as shown in Figure
2
.

We propose that finding the set of permutation operators is equivalent to finding the automorphism of this coloured bipartite graph, i.e.,

. Therefore, if we can compute the automorphism group of the graph

4.2
Proofs of Isomorphism & Faithful Representation

In this section, we prove that finding the set of permutation operators is equivalent to finding the automorphism of the coloured bipartite graph as constructed in Sec.
4.1
. We also prove that the constructed graph is a faithful representation of the permutation equivalence of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
.

Proposition 1
.

The permutation symmetry group of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to the automorphism group of the graph
\mathcal{G}
constructed from
\mathcal{H}
as Sec.
4.1
.

G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

Proof.

An automorphism of
\mathcal{G}
is a bijection
\phi:V\to V
that preserves all graph structures and colours as detailed in Sec.
3.2
. From vertex colour preservation, any
\phi\in\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
must map
V_{Q}\rightarrow V_{Q}
and
V_{T}\rightarrow V_{T}
, as their colours are distinct.

We define a map
\Psi:G_{\mathcal{H}}\to\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
and prove it is a group isomorphism. For any
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
, define
\Psi(\varsigma)=\phi_{\varsigma}
, where
\phi_{\varsigma}:V\to V
is a bijection defined as:

•

V_{Q}:\phi_{\varsigma}(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}

•

V_{T}
: Since
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
, for every term node
t_{r}=(c_{r},p_{r})\in T
, the permuted term
t_{s}\coloneq(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))
is also in
T
.

The map
\phi_{\varsigma}
is a bijection, as
\varsigma
is a bijection on
V_{Q}
and
\varsigma
action on
T
(and thus
V_{T}
) is also a bijection.

To prove that
G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
, it suffices to show that:

1.

\Psi
is well-defined, i.e.,
G_{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

2.

\Psi
is a homomorphism, i.e., for any
\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}\in G_{\mathcal{H}},\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}=\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}

3.

\Psi
is injective

4.

\Psi
is surjective, i.e.,
G_{\mathcal{H}}\supseteq\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

Part 1:
\Psi
is well-defined, i.e.,
G_{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

We show that
\phi_{\varsigma}
is a group automorphism for any
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Adjacency preservation.

An edge
(q_{i},t_{r})
exists iff the Pauli operator
[p_{r}]_{i}\neq I
. Similarly, the permuted edge
(\varsigma(q_{i}),\varsigma(t_{r}))=(q_{j},t_{s})
exists iff
[p_{s}]_{j}\neq I
. By definition of the permutation action on a Pauli string
\varsigma(p)
, we have
[p_{s}]_{j}=[\varsigma(p_{r})]_{\varsigma(i)}=[p_{r}]_{i}
. Thus, the condition
[p_{r}]_{i}\neq I
holds iff
[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(i)}\neq I
. This demonstrates that
(q_{i},t_{r})\in E\iff(\varsigma(q_{i}),\varsigma(t_{r}))
, satisfying the adjacency preservation condition.

Vertex colour preservation.

For any qubit node
q_{i}\in V_{Q}
, the colour
C_{V}(q_{i})=\text{``qubit"}=C_{V}(q_{\varsigma(i)})
. For any term node
t_{r}\in V_{T}
corresponding to
(c_{r},p_{r})
, the map
\phi_{\varsigma}
yields
t_{s}
corresponding to
(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))
. The colour
C_{V}(t_{r})=(\text{``term"},c_{r})
is identical to
C_{V}(t_{s})=(\text{``term"},c_{r})
because
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
preserves the coefficient
c_{r}
of the permuted term. Thus, vertex colours are preserved for all
v\in V
.

Edge colour preservation.

By construction, the edge colour of
(q_{i},t_{r})\in E
is
C_{E}((q_{i},t_{r}))=[p_{r}]_{i}
. The colour of the permuted edge is
C_{E}((\varsigma(q_{i}),\varsigma(t_{r})))=C_{E}((q_{j},t_{s}))=[p_{s}]_{j}
. As established in our proof of adjacency,
[p_{s}]_{j}=[p_{r}]_{i}
. Therefore, edge colours are preserved.

Since
\phi_{\varsigma}
preserves adjacency, vertex colours, and edge colours, it is a valid graph automorphism by definition. This confirms that
\Psi
is a well-defined map
\Psi:G_{\mathcal{H}}\to\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
.

Part 2:
\Psi
is a homomorphism, i.e., for any
\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}\in G_{\mathcal{H}},\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}=\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}

Let
\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
. We show that
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}=\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}
for both sets of vertices
V_{Q},V_{T}
.

Qubit nodes.

Consider the action on an arbitrary qubit node
q_{i}\in V_{Q}
. By definition, the composition
(\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}})(q_{i})
is evaluated as
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}(\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}(q_{i}))
, which simplifies to
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\left(q_{\varsigma_{1}(i)}\right)=q_{\varsigma_{1}(\varsigma_{2}(i))}=q_{(\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2})(i)}
, which is the definition of
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}(q_{i})
.

Term nodes.

Consider the action on an arbitrary qubit node
t_{r}\in V_{T}
that corresponds to the pair
(c_{r},p_{r})
. The composite action
(\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}})(t_{r})
is evaluated as
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}(\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}(t_{r}))
. By definition,
\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}(t_{r})
maps to the node
t_{s}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma_{2}(p_{r}))
. Applying
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}
gives
t_{u}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma_{1}(p_{s}))=(c_{r},\varsigma_{1}(\varsigma_{2}(p_{r})))=(c_{r},(\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2})(p_{r}))
. This final term, by definition, is the node
t_{u}
that
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}(t_{r})
maps to.

Since the actions of
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}\circ\varsigma_{2}}
and
\phi_{\varsigma_{1}}\circ\phi_{\varsigma_{2}}
are identical on
V=V_{Q}\cup V_{T}
, the automorphisms are equal, and
\Psi
is a group homomorphism.

Part 3:
\Psi
is injective

Suppose
\Psi(\varsigma)
is the identity automorphism, i.e.,
\phi_{\varsigma}=\text{id}
. Then, for any
q_{i}\in V_{Q},~\phi_{\varsigma}(q_{i})=q_{i}
. By definition,
\phi_{\varsigma}(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}\implies\varsigma(i)=i~\forall~i\in[1,n]
. This means that
\varsigma
is the identity permutation
e\in S_{n}
. The kernel of
\Psi
is trivial. Therefore,
\Psi
is injective by definition.

Part 4:
\Psi
is surjective, i.e.,
G_{\mathcal{H}}\supseteq\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

We show that for any arbitrary automorphism
\phi\in\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
, there exists a permutation symmetry
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
such that
\Psi(\varsigma)=\phi
.

Let
\phi\in\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
be an arbitrary automorphism. Because
\phi
must preserve vertex colours, and
V_{Q}
and
V_{T}
have distinct colours,
\phi
must map these partitions to themselves, i.e.,
\phi(V_{Q})=V_{Q}
and
\phi(V_{T})=V_{T}
. We can therefore define a permutation
\varsigma\in S_{n}
based on the action of
\phi
on the qubit vertices:
\phi(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}
for all
i\in[1,n]
.

We show that this
\varsigma
is a valid permutation symmetry of the Hamiltonian, i.e.,
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
. To this end, we show that for any term
(c_{r},p_{r})\in T
, the permuted term
(c_{r},\varsigma(t_{r}))\in E
.

Consider the node
t_{r}\in V_{T}
corresponding to
(c_{r},p_{r})
. By vertex colour preservation,
C_{V}(t_{r})=C_{V}(t_{s})
. This implies that their coefficients are identical, i.e.,
c_{r}=c_{s}
, so
t_{s}
corresponds to a term
(c_{r},p_{s})\in T
. To prove
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
, we show that
p_{s}=\varsigma(p_{r})
. This equality holds iff
[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}=[p_{r}]_{j}
for all
j\in[1,n]
. We verify this by inspecting the graph structure for an arbitrary qubit index
j
:

Case 1:
[p_{r}]_{j}\neq I
.

An edge
(q_{j},t_{r})
exists in the graph. By adjacency preservation, an edge
(\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r}))=(q_{\varsigma(j)},t_{s})
must also exist. It is known that the colour of the edge
C_{E}((q_{j},t_{r}))=[p_{r}]_{j}
and the colour of the permuted edge
C_{E}((\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r})))=[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}
. By edge colour preservation,
C_{E}((q_{j},t_{r}))=C_{E}((\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r})))=C_{E}((q_{\varsigma(j)},t_{s}))
. Therefore,
[p_{r}]_{j}=[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}
.

Case 2:
[p_{r}]_{j}=I
.

No edge
(q_{j},t_{r})
exists in the graph. Thus, by adjacency preservation, no edge
(\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r}))
can exist. The non-existence of these edges implies
[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}=I=[p_{r}]_{j}
.

In both cases, we show that
[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}=[p_{r}]_{j}
. Therefore, for any term
(c_{r},p_{r})\in T
, the automorphism
\phi
maps its corresponding node
t_{r}
to the node
t_{s}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))
. This proves that the set of terms
T
is invariant under the action of
\varsigma
, and thus
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Given a
\varsigma\in G_{\mathcal{H}}
, we confirm that
\Psi(\varsigma)=\phi
. By definition,
\Psi(\varsigma)=\phi_{\varsigma}
. The action of
\phi_{\varsigma}
on
V_{Q}
is
\phi_{\varsigma}(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}
, which is identical to
\phi(q_{i})
by definition of
\varsigma
. The action on
V_{T}
is
\phi_{\varsigma}(t_{r})=t_{s}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))
, which as proved, is identical to the action of
\phi(t_{r})
. since
\phi
and
\phi_{\varsigma}
agree on all vertices
V=V_{Q}\cup V_{T}
, they are the same automorphism. Therefore,
\Psi
is surjective.

From the four parts above, we prove that
G_{\mathcal{H}}
is isomorphic to
\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

\hfill\square

Next, we show that the graph
\mathcal{G}
constructed as Sec.
4.1
provides a faithful representation of the permutation equivalence of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
. Two
n
-qubit Hamiltonians
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
represented by their sets of (coefficient, Pauli string) pairs
T_{1},T_{2}
are permutation equivalent iff there exists a permutation
\varsigma\in S_{n}
, the symmetric group on
n
qubits, such that the action of
\varsigma
on
T_{1}
yields
T_{2}
. The mathematical statement is

\mathcal{H}_{1}\sim_{P}\mathcal{H}_{2}\iff\exists~\varsigma\in S_{n}\text{ such that }\varsigma(T_{1})=T_{2}.

(22)

Proposition 2
.

The graph
\mathcal{G}
constructed in Sec.
4.1
is a faithful representation of the permutation equivalence of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}_{1}\sim_{P}\mathcal{H}_{2}\iff\mathcal{G}_{1}\cong\mathcal{G}_{2}.

Proof.

We prove sufficiency (
\implies
) and necessity (
\impliedby
) separately.

Part 1:
\mathcal{H}_{1}\sim_{P}\mathcal{H}_{2}\implies\mathcal{G}_{1}\cong\mathcal{G}_{2}

Assume that
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2}
are permutation-equivalent given a permutation
\varsigma
. We construct a graph isomorphism
\phi:V_{1}\to V_{2}
from
\varsigma
. Define the bijection
\phi
on the vertex set
V=V_{Q}\cup V_{T}
as:

•

V_{Q}
:

\phi
applies the permutation
\varsigma
, i.e., for any
q_{i}\in V_{Q,1},~\phi(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}\in V_{Q,2}
.

•

V_{T}
:
Since
\varsigma(T_{1})=T_{2}
by definition, it is known that for every term node
t_{r}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},p_{r})\in T_{1}
, there exists
t_{s}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))\in T_{2}
. Thus,
\phi(t_{r})=t_{s}
, where
t_{s}\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))
.

We verify the conditions for graph isomorphism:

Adjacency preservation.

An edge
(q_{i},t_{r})\in E_{1}
exists iff
[p_{r}]_{i}\neq I
. The image of this edge is
(\phi(q_{i}),\phi(t_{r}))=(q_{\varsigma(i)},t_{s})
. An edge exists here if
[\varsigma(p_{r})]_{\varsigma(i)}\neq I
. By definition of the permutation operation on Pauli strings, we have
[p_{r}]_{i}=[\varsigma(p_{r})]_{\varsigma(i)}
. Thus,
(q_{i},t_{r})\in E_{1}\iff(\phi(q_{i}),\phi(t_{r}))\in E_{2}
.

Vertex colour preservation.

For qubit nodes, there is no change in colours, i.e., for
q_{i}\in V_{Q,1}
,
C_{V,1}(q_{i})=C_{V,2}(q_{\varsigma(i)})=\text{``qubit"}
. For term nodes
t_{r}
,
C_{V,1}(t_{r})=(\text{``term"},c_{r}).
Since
\varsigma
only permutes the qubits within the Pauli string, the coefficient is preserved, i.e.,
C_{V,2}(t_{s})=C_{V,1}(t_{r})=(\text{``term"},c_{r})
. Therefore, vertex colours are preserved.

Edge colour preservation.

The colour of the original edge is
C_{E,1}((q_{i},t_{r}))=[p_{r}]_{i}
, and the colour of the permuted edge is
C_{E,2}((q_{\varsigma(i)},t_{s}))=[\varsigma(p_{r})]_{\varsigma(i)}
. As established, the operators are identical under the permutation operation on the Pauli string, i.e.,
[p_{r}]_{i}=[\varsigma(p_{r})]_{\varsigma(i)}
. Therefore, edge colours are preserved.

Since
\phi:V_{1}\to V_{2}
preserves the required properties,
\phi
is an isomorphism, and thus,
\mathcal{G}_{1}\cong\mathcal{G}_{2}
.

Part 2:
\mathcal{H}_{1}\sim_{P}\mathcal{H}_{2}\impliedby\mathcal{G}_{1}\cong\mathcal{G}_{2}

Assume that
\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2}
are isomorphic given a mapping
\phi:V_{1}\to V_{2}
. We construct a permutation
\varsigma\in S_{n}
from the automorphism
\phi
.
Because the vertex colouring distinguishes qubit nodes
V_{Q}
from term nodes
V_{T}
, the automorphism
\phi
maps
V_{Q,1}\to V_{Q,2}
and
V_{T,1}\to V_{T,2}
. Define the permutation
\varsigma\in S_{n}
from the action of
\phi
on the qubit vertices:

\phi(q_{i})=q_{\varsigma(i)}~\forall~i\in[1,n].

(23)

We show that the action of
\varsigma
on
T_{1}
yields
T_{2}
, i.e.,
\varsigma(T_{1})=T_{2}
. Consider a term node
t_{r}\in V_{T,1}
corresponding to
(c_{r},p_{r})\in T_{1}
, and its image node
\phi(t_{r})=t_{s}\in V_{T,2}
corresponding to
(c_{s},p_{s})\in T_{2}
.

Coefficient preservation.

By vertex colour preservation,
C_{V,1}(t_{r})=C_{V,2}(t_{s})
. Since the colour of a term node includes it coefficient, this implies that
c_{r}=c_{s}
.

Pauli string equivalence.

To prove that
p_{s}=\varsigma(p_{r})
, we show that the two strings have identical operators on the qubits, i.e.,
[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}=[p_{r}]_{j}~\forall~j\in[1,n]
qubit indices.

•

Case
[p_{r}]_{j}\neq I
: An edge
(q_{j},t_{r})
exists. By adjacency preservation,
(\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r}))=(q_{\varsigma(j)},t_{s})
must also exist. By edge colour preservation,
C_{E,1}((q_{j},t_{r}))=C_{E,2}((q_{\varsigma(j)},t_{s}))
, which is true only if
[p_{r}]_{j}=[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}
.

•

Case
[p_{r}]_{j}=I
: No edge
(q_{j},t_{r})
exists. By adjacency preservation,
(\phi(q_{j}),\phi(t_{r}))=(q_{\varsigma(j)},t_{s})
cannot exist, implying
[p_{r}]_{j}=[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}=I
.

In both cases, we show
[p_{r}]_{j}=[p_{s}]_{\varsigma(j)}
, i.e.,
p_{s}=\varsigma(p_{r})
. Since the isomorphism
\phi
maps every term
(c_{r},p_{r})\in T_{1}
to a term
\phi(t_{r})\leftrightarrow(c_{r},\varsigma(p_{r}))\in T_{2}
, the set of terms
T_{2}
is equivalent to the set of terms
T_{1}
under the action of permutation
\varsigma
. Thus,
\varsigma(T_{1})=T_{2}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{1}\sim_{P}H_{2}
.

From the two parts above, we prove that the graph
\mathcal{G}
is a faithful representation of the permutation equivalence of the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
.

\hfill\square

4.3
Degree bound

Hamiltonians representing physical systems often have a constant locality
k
and a bounded interaction degree
d
. From these properties, it can be shown that their graphs have a bounded degree that does not scale with system size.

Proposition 3
.

The degree of the constructed graph
\mathcal{G}
for a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
with constant locality
k
and bounded interaction degree
d
is bounded, i.e.,

\deg(\mathcal{G})\in O(1).

(24)

Proof.

The graph
\mathcal{G}
has vertex set
V=V_{Q}\cup V_{T}
, where
V_{Q}
are qubit vertices and
V_{T}
are term vertices corresponding to Pauli terms of the Hamiltonian.
For each term vertex
v_{T}\in V_{T}
,

\deg(v_{T})\leq k,

(25)

since each term acts non-trivially on at most
k
qubits by the locality of the Hamiltonian.
Consider a qubit vertex
v_{Q}\in V_{Q}
. The degree
\deg(v_{Q})
is the number of terms in
\mathcal{H}
that act non-trivially on qubit
v_{Q}
.
Since the Hamiltonian has an interaction range bounded by
d
, any Pauli term acting non-trivially on qubit
v_{Q}
must have its support within a neighbourhood of size
d
around
v_{Q}
in the interaction graph of the Hamiltonian.

Within this neighbourhood of size at most
d
, we select subsets of
k-1
qubits (excluding itself) to form a support of size
k
including
v_{Q}
. The number of such subsets is bounded by the binomial coefficient

\binom{d}{k-1}.

For each such choice of support, there are
3^{k}
possible non-trivial Pauli strings, since each qubit operator can be
X
,
Y
, or
Z
.
Therefore, the total number of terms acting non-trivially on
v_{Q}
is bounded by

\deg(v_{Q})\leq\binom{d}{k-1}\times 3^{k}.

(26)

Therefore, the degree of the graph is

\displaystyle\deg(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle=\max_{v\in V}\left\{\max_{v_{Q}\in V_{Q}}\deg(v_{Q}),\max_{v_{T}\in V_{T}}\deg(v_{T})\right\}

\displaystyle\deg(v_{T})

\displaystyle\in O(k)

[From Eq. (
25
)]

\displaystyle\deg(v_{Q})

\displaystyle\in O(3^{k})

[From Eq. (
26
)]

\displaystyle\implies\max_{v_{T}\in V_{T}}\deg(v_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\max_{v_{Q}\in V_{Q}}\deg(v_{Q})

\displaystyle\implies\deg(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\in O(3^{k})

\displaystyle\therefore\deg(\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\in O(1)

\displaystyle\text{[Since $k,d\in O(1)$]}.

\hfill\square

This result enables the use of Luks’
[
32
]
and Neuen’s
[
36
]
methods for polynomial-time graph automorphism finding, and thus, permutation symmetry group finding on Hamiltonians representing physical systems.

G_{\mathcal{H}}

\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})

VI
Conclusion

This work addresses a notable lack of a general, deterministic, and structurally exact algorithm to identify the full permutation symmetry group (
G_{\mathcal{H}}
) of an arbitrary Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
expressed as a weighted sum of Pauli strings. We have introduced a novel method that establishes an isomorphism between the Hamiltonian’s permutation symmetry group and the automorphism group of a corresponding coloured bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}
as constructed in Sec.
4.1
. The isomorphism
G_{\mathcal{H}}\cong\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
was proven in Sec.
4.2
by demonstrating that the mapping
\Psi:G_{\mathcal{H}}\to\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
is a well-defined group isomorphism. Furthermore, the proof of faithful representation establishes that the problem of deciding whether two Hamiltonians are permutation-equivalent is polynomial-time reducible to the Graph Isomorphism (GI) problem.

This result has significant computational implications for the simulation of Hamiltonians of physical systems. We proved that for physical Hamiltonians, which are characterised by bounded locality
k
and a bounded interaction degree
d
, the constructed graph
\mathcal{G}
necessarily has a bounded vertex degree (
\deg(\mathcal{G})\in O(1)
). This result reduces the complexity of finding
\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
from the quasi-polynomial time required for general graphs to polynomial time using established algorithms for bounded-degree graphs. Our method thus provides a computationally efficient and scalable tool for a problem previously lacking a general solution. The validity of this graph-theoretic isomorphism was empirically confirmed by applying the algorithm to several physical models with analytically known symmetry groups, such as various 1D and 2D Transverse Field Ising Models and the mean-field Heisenberg XXX model, and verifying in all cases that the generators of the computed
\text{aut}(\mathcal{G})
correctly reproduced the known
G_{\mathcal{H}}
.

The algorithm’s performance must be validated through large-scale benchmarking against existing numerical and constraint-based methods. Theoretical extensions should be explored, such as adapting the graph construction to directly identify symmetries in fermionic Hamiltonians prior to transformations (like the Jordan-Wigner transform) that may obscure them. Finally, investigating how small, symmetry-breaking perturbations in
\mathcal{H}
are reflected in the structure of
\mathcal{G}
could provide a pathway to identifying approximate symmetries by relaxing the conditions of the exact graph automorphism method presented in this paper.
