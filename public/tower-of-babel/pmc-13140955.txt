Barriers to and facilitators of paediatric early hearing detection and intervention: protocol for a systematic review and meta-synthesis of qualitative evidence
Authors: Mehrdad Amir-Behghadami, Mehri Maleki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEarly hearing detection and intervention (EHDI) is a crucial public health initiative focused on the prompt identification and management of hearing impairments in children, significantly impacting their communication abilities, cognitive development and overall well-being. Although the global acceptance of EHDI programmes is gradually expanding, delay in diagnosis and enrolment in early intervention continues to challenge optimal outcomes. Qualitative studies examining the barriers and facilitators within EHDI remain limited, particularly regarding the experiences of caregivers, healthcare providers and policymakers across various contexts. This systematic review and meta-synthesis of qualitative evidence aims to comprehensively identify and synthesise these barriers and facilitators from multiple stakeholder perspectives aiming to inform policies and strategies that improve EHDI programme effectiveness.Methods and analysisA comprehensive search will be conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, Scientific Information Database and MagIran, from database inception to December 2025, with no language restrictions. Quantitative-only studies, non–peer-reviewed sources and studies without accessible full texts will be excluded. Two reviewers will independently screen titles/abstracts/full texts, resolving disagreements via discussion or a third reviewer. Quality appraisal will be done using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist. Data synthesis will employ thematic content analysis.Ethics and disseminationThis systematic review and meta-synthesis was approved by the Resarch Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.TBZMED.VCR.REC.1404.331). Introduction

Hearing is a crucial sense for the communicative, cognitive and social development of children. Untreated childhood hearing loss, even if mild, leads to poorer speech, language acquisition, educational attainment, social functioning, cognitive performance and quality of life.1 2 Early-onset unilateral hearing impairment similarly impairs sound localisation, speech perception in noise and preverbal vocalisations.3 Delayed diagnosis or intervention of hearing impairments can result in substantial speech and language delays, academic challenges and social isolation.2 Early hearing detection and intervention (EHDI) programmes have been established as essential strategies to enable early identification of hearing loss and the provision of timely interventions. Nevertheless, numerous studies have highlighted that the implementation of these programmes encounters hindrances from various challenges that may compromise their effectiveness.4

In light of increasing diversity in Canada, the need for culturally competent EHDI services has become paramount. A recent qualitative study explored the experiences of practitioners delivering paediatric hearing loss services to families from minority cultural backgrounds. The findings revealed that language barriers significantly obstruct service provision at multiple stages, and practitioners employed various communication strategies as facilitators to navigate these challenges.5

In South Africa, the implementation of EHDI services has been hindered by several barriers, as identified by a recent qualitative study involving audiologists and speech therapists in KwaZulu-Natal. The study revealed that the lack of resources within healthcare facilities is a significant barrier to effective EHDI, which affects both public and private sectors. Participants noted that existing guidelines for EHDI are more applicable to urban settings than to rural areas, highlighting a gap in contextual relevance. Additionally, inadequate knowledge and awareness of EHDI practices were identified as critical obstacles. However, facilitators such as the establishment of dedicated task teams for EHDI programmes, enhancement of communication networks and targeted training for healthcare professionals were recognised as potential avenues for improvement. The study concluded that addressing these barriers and leveraging facilitators through increased resources, education and the development of culturally and linguistically appropriate practices is essential for advancing EHDI services in South Africa. Further research and consideration of clinical implications were also suggested to enhance the efficacy of EHDI implementation.6

The systematic review examined 62 studies to identify barriers and facilitators affecting the EHDI for congenital hearing loss. The findings highlighted that both individual and family-level factors, such as economic stability and the infant’s medical condition, as well as system-level barriers, including service capacity and provider knowledge, significantly impede timely diagnosis and intervention. Additionally, specific social determinants of health were identified as obstacles to effective EHDI. However, the review also noted that system-level facilitators, such as coordinated care, co-location of services and family support programmes, can alleviate the adverse effects of these sociodemographic barriers. The conclusions emphasise that while numerous challenges exist for families seeking timely EHDI, implementing system-level changes could enhance the process and improve long-term outcomes.4

A systematic review of low-income and middle-income countries in Asia found that although screening tools and protocols were similar to those used in high-income countries, no uniform protocol was followed in each country. Furthermore, the long-term sustainability of EHDI programmes was uncertain owing to limited information on impact outcomes such as cost-benefit.7

A study conducted in the Appalachian region of Kentucky reveals significant barriers faced by rural families in accessing timely infant hearing healthcare after newborn hearing screening. Through interviews with 40 parents, the research identified challenges such as poor communication of results, difficulties in obtaining outpatient testing and inconsistencies in healthcare information. Despite these obstacles, parents demonstrated a strong desire for timely care and a willingness to utilise telemedicine. The findings emphasise the importance of reducing misinformation and improving access to care to prevent delays in diagnosing and treating hearing loss in infants. In contrast, factors such as parental awareness, social support and the presence of integrated national policies have been shown to serve as effective facilitators.8 9 Enablers include provision of resources, supportive professionals and family-centred knowledge sharing, which improve navigation of health and education services.10

Beyond structural challenges, the experiences of stakeholders—particularly parents—are pivotal to the success of hearing screening programmes. Qualitative research indicates that receiving a diagnosis of hearing loss often constitutes an emotional crisis for parents, and insufficient psychological support and inadequate information can delay their acceptance of the condition and engagement with treatment follow-up.11 12 Moreover, in certain communities, cultural beliefs and misconceptions surrounding hearing loss may result in denial and resistance to medical interventions.13 14

A comprehensive analysis of the lived experiences of stakeholders—including parents, service providers and policymakers—is essential given the complex and diverse factors influencing hearing screening programmes. Conducting a systematic review and meta-synthesis of existing qualitative evidence can identify shared patterns as well as culturally and socially specific contexts. Such an approach not only elucidates barriers and facilitators but also offers practical recommendations to enhance the effectiveness and timeliness of screening and intervention programmes. This synthesis of qualitative perspectives can guide the development of tailored strategies to improve programme implementation and outcomes across different populations and settings.

Despite the extensive number of studies on childhood hearing screening, there remains a distinct lack of a comprehensive meta-synthesis that thoroughly examines the diverse experiences of stakeholders from parents, providers and policymakers. This protocol addresses this qualitative evidence gap by systematically synthesising existing studies to integrate barriers, facilitators and stakeholder perspectives, thereby providing consolidated insights to guide EHDI policy and practice improvements.

The primary objective is to identify and synthesise barriers and facilitators to EHDI programmes from the perspectives of parents, healthcare providers and policymakers. The secondary objective is to explore variations in these barriers and facilitators across different geographic regions and countries with varying income levels, where sufficient data permit, to inform context-specific policy recommendations.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-synthesis of qualitative studies is to identify and integrate evidence on barriers to and facilitators of EHDI from stakeholder perspectives.

Methods and analysis

Reporting

This protocol is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines (online supplemental additional file 1).15 16 The findings of the review will be presented following the PRISMA and Enhancing transparency in reporting the systhesis of qualitative research (ENTREQ) guidelines for systematic reviews of qualitative evidence.17 The review is not registered.

Research question

This systematic review and meta-synthesis will address the following primary research question: What barriers and facilitators do stakeholders—such as parents, healthcare providers and policymakers—identify regarding early hearing detection and intervention for childhood hearing issues?

Eligibility criteria

This research will utilise the Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type (SPIDER) framework, an established and validated instrument for developing and structuring search strategies in qualitative and mixed-methods studies, to systematically guide the literature search and establish clear inclusion and exclusion criteria. There will be no limitations regarding publication date, geographic origin, setting or method of application. Analysis will be confined to the qualitative components of mixed-methods research.18

Search strategy and information sources

An initial exploratory search was conducted to identify relevant terms, keywords and synonyms related to childhood hearing screening, barriers, facilitators and outcomes. This preliminary step helped in refining the concepts and ensuring comprehensive coverage of the topic. Subsequently, a detailed search strategy was developed in collaboration with an expert librarian, incorporating both controlled vocabulary such as Medical Subject Headings and free-text keywords. The strategy was iteratively refined through team discussions and validated by subject-matter experts to maximise retrieval sensitivity and specificity.

A systematic and comprehensive literature search will be conducted in the international electronic databases PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science. To identify nationally relevant studies, the Iranian Databases Scientific Information Database (SID) and MagIran will also be searched. The search strategy will retrieve all relevant records from database inception to December 2025, with no date restrictions. The initial search design was created for MEDLINE and subsequently adapted for other databases to align with their unique syntax and indexing (online supplemental additional file 2). To reduce the potential impact of publication bias, we will also conduct searches of grey literature sources such as Google Scholar and ClinicalTrials.gov. The choice of databases, including Google Scholar, was informed by the optimal retrieval strategies recommended by Bramer et al.19 Hand-searching reference lists of included articles will supplement electronic searches to ensure no relevant studies are missed. To address Google Scholar’s retrieval constraints and ensure methodological reproducibility, searches will be confined to the first 100 records ranked by relevance.

Study selection process

Records retrieved from the systematic database searches will be imported into EndNote V.9.3 (Clarivate Analytics) for duplicate removal.

The study selection process will adhere to PRISMA guidelines and proceed through a structured, three-phase approach. Two independent reviewers will first assess the relevance of studies based on title and abstract against the eligibility criteria. In the second phase, the same reviewers will perform a detailed full-text evaluation of potentially eligible articles to finalise inclusion, with all exclusion rationales documented systematically. To ensure comprehensive coverage, a manual search of the reference lists and citations of all included studies will be conducted to identify any further pertinent publications.

In instances where study information is incomplete, such as when only abstracts are available, corresponding authors will be contacted via email to obtain missing data or full-text manuscripts. Any disagreements between reviewers during the screening stages will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer to reach consensus. The entire selection process will be documented and presented in a PRISMA flow diagram to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies will be independently evaluated by two reviewers (MAB and MM) using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist tailored for qualitative research.20 Differences in assessment will be resolved through discussion or, if required, consultation with a third reviewer. To measure inter-rater reliability, Cohen’s kappa (κ) will be utilised as the primary statistic to account for agreement beyond chance, whereas the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) will be reported as supplementary.21 22 Kappa values between 0.69 and 0.79 will be considered indicative of moderate agreement, and values of ≥0.80 will be interpreted as good agreement. An ICC exceeding 0.75 will be viewed as evidence of strong reliability.23

Data synthesis

Content analysis was selected for this review because it provides a systematic and flexible approach to identifying and quantifying patterns within qualitative data, which is particularly suitable for synthesising diverse qualitative evidence on barriers and facilitators. Unlike thematic synthesis or meta-ethnography, content analysis allows for a more structured categorisation of data while maintaining interpretative depth, facilitating clear summarisation of prevalent themes across studies.24 25 The initial coding and theme development will be conducted by the first author, who possesses specialised expertise in this area. This will be followed by a detailed review and finalisation of the synthesis by the second author to enhance reliability and validity. The process will begin with thorough immersion in the data to capture breadth and depth, followed by the generation of preliminary thematic categories.26 Thematic saturation will be gauged iteratively by monitoring the emergence of new themes and continuing coding until no additional themes arise.27 Relevant studies will be organised within these categories, with continuous refinement of their contents to strengthen the findings. To ensure consistency and robustness of the synthesis, coding outcomes will be critically reviewed by the second author, facilitating consensus on the final thematic framework. This structured, iterative process safeguards both comprehensive coverage of data and methodological rigour, enabling a trustworthy integration of qualitative evidence related to childhood hearing screening.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics

The protocol for this systematic review and meta-sysnthesis recieved ethical approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (approval number:IR.TBZMED.VCR.REC.1404.331). The committee confirmed that the study complies with ethical standards, as it involves only the analysis of published literature, with no direct contact with human participants, no collection of primary data, and no clinical procedures.

Dissemination

The findings of this systematic review and meta-synthesis on barriers, facilitators and outcomes in childhood hearing screening will be submitted for publication in a high-quality, peer-reviewed journal specialising in health policy, public health or health services research. Furthermore, the results will be shared at relevant national and international conferences to reach a diverse audience of researchers, clinicians and policymakers. Targeted summaries for policymakers and clinicians will be produced. Additional dissemination activities will include presentations through seminars and journal clubs within academic and clinical institutions engaged in hearing health and child development research. This comprehensive dissemination approach is designed to maximise accessibility, promote knowledge translation and inform evidence-based policy and practice across multiple stakeholder groups.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-synthesis will provide an in-depth understanding of the barriers, facilitators and outcomes associated with childhood hearing screening from the perspectives of key stakeholders, including parents, healthcare providers and policymakers. Although prior qualitative research in hearing science has predominantly explored the emotional, social and psychological effects of hearing loss,28 this synthesis will distinctively emphasise the processes and experiences associated with early hearing detection and intervention programmes.

By integrating findings from diverse contexts—ranging from urban to rural settings and across different cultural backgrounds—this study will illuminate the multifaceted challenges families face, such as navigating early diagnosis and accessing timely interventions, as highlighted in studies of parental experiences in Kuwait,8 rural regions29 and broader NHS narratives. The review will also identify facilitators that support effective engagement and intervention uptake, such as parental education, healthcare system support and culturally sensitive communication strategies.8

Furthermore, synthesising this qualitative evidence will deepen current knowledge about how hearing screening programmes are experienced in daily practice and reveal systemic gaps that might delay diagnosis or hinder intervention effectiveness.29 However, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. The comprehensive search strategy, while broad across databases and grey literature, may still miss relevant unpublished studies, potentially introducing publication bias. Dual independent processes for screening, appraisal and extraction enhance reliability but cannot fully eliminate subjective interpretation in thematic synthesis. Reliance on the SPIDER framework ensures the targeted inclusion of qualitative evidence, yet heterogeneity across cultural and geographic contexts could challenge the transferability of synthesised findings. Exclusion of non-peer-reviewed sources and inaccessible texts prioritises rigour but limits the scope of evidence. Finally, while CASP provides robust quality and confidence assessments, their application depends on the transparency of primary studies, which may vary. The insights gained will not only contribute to theoretical frameworks around listening and communication in the context of hearing loss but will also have practical implications for improving policy and service delivery.8 29 Ultimately, this review aims to inform evidence-based strategies to enhance early hearing detection outcomes, thereby promoting improved developmental and social trajectories for children with hearing loss worldwide, despite these methodological constraints.
