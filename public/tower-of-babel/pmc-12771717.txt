Targeted elimination of latent endophytes improves cryopreservation success in in vitro grapevine (Vitis vinifera) cultures
Authors: Jinjoo Bae, Ji-Won Han, Jae-Young Song, Sung-Hee Nam, Jung Sook Sung, Ho Cheol Ko, Se Hee Kim, Ye-Ji Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLatent endophytic bacteria are common in grapevine in vitro cultures and, while not always causing visible culture decline, can negatively affect downstream applications such as cryopreservation by reducing post-thaw recovery. While antibiotic treatments are widely used for microbial control, their efficacy varies with bacterial species, plant genotype, and application conditions. Few studies have directly linked targeted elimination of identified endophytes with improved post-cryopreservation recovery.ResultsIn this study, we identified Leifsonia poae in ‘Ruby Seedless’ and Curtobacterium oceanosedimentum in ‘Merlot’ using 16 S rRNA sequencing. Species-specific antibiotic susceptibility testing revealed rifampicin (minimum bactericidal concentration, MBC = 31 µg mL⁻¹) as effective against L. poae, and cefotaxime (MBC = 1000 µg mL⁻¹) as effective against C. oceanosedimentum. In vitro application of these antibiotics eliminated the respective endophytes without significant phytotoxic effects at optimal concentrations. Amplicon-based 16 S metagenomic profiling confirmed complete removal of the target bacteria and revealed substantial shifts in community composition, with reduced abundance of dominant taxa but maintenance of overall endophytic diversity. Cryopreservation experiments in ‘Ruby Seedless’ showed that removal of endophytes increased post-thaw survival from 31.8 to 70.9% and eliminated variability in regrowth across replicates.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that targeted antibiotic elimination of dominant endophytes can restructure the in vitro microbial community and substantially improve cryopreservation outcomes in grapevine. The approach offers a reproducible sanitation strategy for clonal crops vulnerable to endophyte-related culture failures and can be readily adapted for germplasm conservation programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07821-y.

Background

Grapevine (Vitis spp.) is a globally significant crop cultivated for wine, table grape, and raisin production. The increasing demand for clean, disease-free planting material has accompanied the expansion of high-value cultivars, most of which belong to Vitis vinifera L [1]. To meet this demand, micropropagation has been widely adopted as a reliable method for producing pathogen-free plants, while cryopreservation serves as a complementary approach for the long-term conservation of grapevine genetic resources [2]. However, a persistent challenge in in vitro culture is the presence of endophytic bacteria—microorganisms residing asymptomatically within plant tissues that survive conventional surface sterilization [3].

While many endophytes promote plant health in natural environments by enhancing nutrient uptake, growth, and stress resilience [4–6], the nutrient-rich conditions of tissue culture can trigger their uncontrolled proliferation. Latent endophytes may become problematic after repeated subculturing or under stress events such as shoot tip excision and cryopreservation, leading to contamination, reduced shoot proliferation, tissue necrosis, or even culture loss [7–10]. Advances in sequencing have revealed that grapevine endophyte communities are far more diverse than previously detected by culture-dependent methods [4, 11, 12]. These communities are often tissue-specific, with roots typically harboring greater diversity than shoots [13–15]. Although some endophytes can contribute to host performance [5] or secondary metabolite production [16], their proliferation under aseptic conditions remains a barrier to both micropropagation and germplasm preservation [9, 17, 18].

Antibiotics are commonly used to suppress or eliminate endophytes, and compounds such as chloramphenicol, kanamycin, and rifampicin have been applied to grapevine cultures with varying success [19]. However, antibiotic efficacy depends on host genotype, bacterial strain, and treatment conditions, and some antibiotics negatively impact plant growth [20, 21]. Despite these efforts, standardized sanitation protocols remain elusive because the responses of grapevine endophytes and hosts to antibiotics are highly variable.

Because endophytes are often asymptomatic and considered beneficial in conventional culture, their role in cryopreservation success has been largely overlooked. During cryopreservation, explants are exposed to severe physiological stress—including excision, osmotic imbalance, dehydration, freezing, and thawing—that can activate latent bacterial populations. Proliferation of these bacteria may result in browning, tissue necrosis, and poor recovery, as reported in several species [8, 9, 22]. Consequently, eliminating latent endophytes before cryopreservation is crucial for maintaining shoot viability and improving post-thaw regrowth.

Cryopreservation protocols for grapevine have been developed since the 1990 s [23]. Because the process involves multiple complex steps and factors, numerous studies have provided valuable insights for optimizing vitrification and regrowth, enabling reliable implementation in genebanks [2, 17, 23, 24]. While major factors such as cryoprotectant composition, preculture conditions, and recovery media have been extensively investigated, the potential influence of endophytic bacteria has received little attention. Furthermore, although various sanitation strategies have been tested, few studies have directly demonstrated that targeted elimination of identified endophytes can improve cryopreservation outcomes.

In this study, we identified the dominant endophytes in two V. vinifera cultivars, ‘Ruby Seedless’ and ‘Merlot’, determined their antibiotic susceptibility profiles, and selected species-specific treatments for elimination. We then assessed the impact of antibiotic-mediated sanitation on in vitro shoot viability and post-cryopreservation regrowth. Our findings establish a reproducible framework for microbial control in grapevine conservation and demonstrate that targeted endophyte removal can significantly enhance cryopreservation success.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Tissue-cultured plantlets of two Vitis vinifera L. cultivars, ‘Ruby Seedless’ (National Agrobiodiversity Center accession IT227777) and ‘Merlot’ (IT227638), were derived from field-grown mother plants and established in vitro at the Department of Horticultural Crop Research, National Institute of Horticultural and Herbal Science (NIHHS), Rural Development Administration (RDA), Wanju, Republic of Korea, in May 2023. In 2024, two culture vessels per variety (each containing four plantlets) were transferred to the National Agrobiodiversity Center (NAC), National Institute of Agricultural Sciences (NIAS), RDA, Suwon, Republic of Korea. These accessions are curated within the national agricultural genetic resources system coordinated by NAC, and the cultivars used in this study are not included in the appendices of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).

Formal confirmation of cultivar identity (‘Ruby Seedless’, ‘Merlot’) was verified against institutional passport records and morphological descriptors by NIHHS–RDA. Voucher material is preserved as living in vitro accessions at NAC (Suwon, Republic of Korea) under accession codes IT227777 and IT227638. No pressed herbarium vouchers were prepared, as only cultivated materials from institutional collections were used. No wild collection was conducted, and no specific collection permits were required. All work complied with institutional, national, and international guidelines and applicable local legislation.

For the present study, plantlets received from NIHHS were multiplied to generate experimental material on half-strength Driver and Kuniyuki Walnut (DKW) [25] basal medium (Duchefa, Haarlem, Netherlands) supplemented with 2.5 g L⁻¹ activated charcoal (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose (Duchefa), and 8 g L⁻¹ plant agar (Duchefa) without the addition of any phytohormones, as internode segments rather than shoot tips were used for multiplication. The medium pH was adjusted to 6.0 prior to autoclaving (121 ℃, 20 min), and cultures were maintained at 25 ℃ under a 16-h photoperiod with a light intensity of 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ provided by cool-white fluorescent lamps (standard growth conditions) and were subcultured every 6 weeks.

Isolation and identification of endophytic bacteria

To isolate endophytes, approximately 2–3 mm of the basal portion of in vitro-grown plants from each cultivar was placed onto lysogeny broth (LB; Difco, Sparks, MD, USA) agar plates and incubated at 30 ℃ for 3 days in the dark to allow emergence of bacterial growth. Subsequently, bacterial growth was streaked onto fresh LB agar plates using a sterile inoculating loop to obtain isolated single colonies, following commonly used plant tissue plating and streak-plate purification procedures [26–28].

Colony PCR was employed; therefore, genomic DNA extraction was not performed. The bacterial 16 S rRNA gene (~ 1500 bp) was amplified using universal primers [29, 30]: 27 F (5′–AGA GTT TGA TCM TGG CTC AG–3′) and 1492R (5′–GGT TAC CTT GTT ACG ACT T–3′). PCR reactions contained a sample of a bacterial colony, 1 µL of each primer (10 pmol), 10 µL of 2× Taq PCR Master Mix (Genetbio, Daejeon, Korea), and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µL. The thermal cycling program consisted of: pre-denaturation at 94 ℃ for 3 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 ℃ for 1 min, annealing at 58 ℃ for 30 s, extension at 72 ℃ for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 ℃ for 10 min.

Amplification products were confirmed by electrophoresis on a 1.2% agarose gel in 1× TAE buffer, stained with Redsafe™ Nucleic Acid Staining Solution (Intron biotechnology, Daejeon, Korea), and visualized under UV illumination (180 V, 30 min). PCR products were purified using HiAccuBead (AccuGene, Incheon, Korea) and subjected to Sanger sequencing on an ABI 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) at BioTo Inc. (Daejeon, Korea).

Sequences were analyzed using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) at the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI). A distance-based phylogenetic tree was generated with the “Tree View” option in NCBI BLAST, based on pairwise alignment scores, and species-level identification was assigned for sequences showing ≥ 99% identity. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using the Neighbor-Joining method in the NCBI BLAST Tree View tool.

Antibiotic susceptibility assay

Antibiotic susceptibility was tested using the disk diffusion method [31]. A single bacterial colony was inoculated into 5 mL LB broth (Difco) in a 50 mL culture tube (SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, ROK) and incubated at 30 ℃ with shaking at 100 rpm in a rotary shaking incubator (Daihan Scientific, Wonju, Korea) for 24 h. A 100 µL aliquot of this culture was transferred into 20 mL fresh LB broth and incubated for another 24 h to an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 0.3–0.4. LB agar plates were spread with 200 µL of the bacterial suspension using disposable sterile spreader (SPL Life Sciences, Pocheon, ROK). Six antibiotic-impregnated paper disks (6 mm diameter; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) were applied: ampicillin (10 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), kanamycin (30 µg), streptomycin (10 µg), and rifampicin (5 µg). Commercial antibiotic disks were used for practical comparison across agents with varying working concentrations. A blank disk served as control. Plates were incubated at 30 ℃ for 3 days, and inhibition zones were measured using a 1 × 1 cm reference grid on the plate bottom.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

MIC and MBC were determined by the broth microdilution method using 96-well microtiter plates. Antibiotics were two-fold serially diluted in LB broth in sterile, flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates (SPL, Life Sciences) to a highest concentration of 2000 µg mL⁻¹. Each well was inoculated with 150 uL of bacterial suspension adjusted to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.3 and mixed with 50 uL of the antibiotic dilution, resulting in a final volume of 200 uL per well, and incubated at 30 ℃ for 3 days. Because rifampicin and cefotaxime interfere with OD₆₀₀ readings due to their intrinsic pigmentation, turbidity-based determination of the MIC was not feasible. Following the general definition that the MIC is the lowest concentration that inhibits visible bacterial growth under defined test conditions [32], cultures from the 96-well microdilution assay were spotted onto LB agar plates containing the corresponding antibiotic concentrations. In this study, the MIC was operationally defined as the lowest concentration showing no visible bacterial growth on the agar after 24 h of incubation, and the MBC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration producing no colony growth after 3 days of incubation.

In vitro culture and evaluation of antibiotic effects

Nodal explants of ‘Ruby Seedless’ and ‘Merlot’ were cultured on solid Woody Plant Medium (WPM; Duchefa, Haarlem, Netherlands) [33] with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose, 8 g L⁻¹ plant agar, supplemented with rifampicin (0, 1× MBC [31 µg mL⁻¹], 2× MBC [62.5 µg mL⁻¹], 3× MBC [125 µg mL⁻¹], or 4× MBC [250 µg mL⁻¹]) or cefotaxime (0, MIC [500 µg mL⁻¹], 1× MBC [1000 µg mL⁻¹]). Cultures were maintained at 24 ± 1 ℃ under a 16-h photoperiod, with a fluorescent light intensity of 40 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹. Shoots (~ 1 cm length) were transferred to antibiotic-free WPM (‘Ruby Seedless’) or WPM with activated charcoal (2.0 g L⁻¹; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for ‘Merlot’. Rooted plants were tested for bacterial regrowth by plating 2–3 mm basal segments on LB agar and incubating at 30 ℃ for 3 days.

Metagenomic analysis of bacterial communities

Metagenomic profiling was performed for ‘Ruby Seedless’ only due to practical considerations of material availability and project resources at the time. Nearly the entire shoot (leaves removed) was sampled for DNA extraction. Three independently maintained culture vessels (biological units), each containing five plantlets, were prepared per treatment: untreated (Ruby_C) and antibiotic-treated (Ruby_T). All five plantlets from a given vessel were pooled to generate one metagenomic library, resulting in three libraries across treatments. After taxonomic profiling, one Ruby_T library exhibited a community composition indistinguishable from the corresponding untreated baseline, indicating a sampling/handling mix-up; this library was excluded a priori from downstream analyses. The remaining libraries were retained for descriptive interpretation, focusing on presence/absence patterns and directional changes of focal taxa. Because the libraries originated from different vessels (rather than subsamples of a single vessel), no vessel-level inferential statistics were performed.

Total DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerSoil Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and quantified with the QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). The V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified with Illumina adapter-linked primers (V3-F: 5′–TCGTCGGCAGCGTCAGGATGTAAGACAGCCTAGGGGNGGCWGCAG–3′; V4-R: 5′–GTCTCGTGGGCTCGGAGATGTGTATAAGAGACAGGACTACHVGGGTATCTAATCC–3′) [34] using Herculase II Fusion DNA Polymerase (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min; 25 cycles of 95 ℃ for 30 s, 55 ℃ for 30 s, and 72 ℃ for 30 s; and a final elongation step at 72 ℃ for 5 min.

Amplicons were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), quality assessed with a Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies), and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp). Raw reads were trimmed with Cutadapt v3.2, and amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated in DADA2 v1.18.0. Chimeric sequences and ASVs shorter than 350 bp were removed. Reads were rarefied to the smallest library size using QIIME v1.9.0. Taxonomic assignment was performed against the NCBI_16S database using a Bayesian classifier with a minimum confidence = 50%. Chloroplast and mitochondrial ASVs were removed prior to downstream analysis. The top 20 most abundant species in Ruby_C were visualized as stacked column charts in Microsoft Excel.

Cryopreservation

Cryopreservation of Vitis vinifera ‘Ruby Seedless’ followed the protocol of Bettoni et al. [2]. Unless otherwise stated, the pretreatment, preculture composition, PVS2 exposure, vitrification, and cooling/warming procedures were performed as described therein. Nodal sections from 5-week-old in vitro plantlets were cultured for 4 weeks under standard growth conditions and subsequently transferred to 4 ℃ under a 16-h photoperiod with reduced light intensity (20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) for 1 week prior to cryopreservation. Apical shoot tips (1 ~ 2 mm) were excised and placed on preculture medium. Post-LN recovery followed a three-stage scheme: R1 was identical to that of Bettoni et al. [2]. In R2 and R3, WPM replaced MS. After shoot development, the routine multiplication medium (half-strength DKW supplemented with 30 g L⁻¹ sucrose, 2.5 g L⁻¹ activated charcoal, and 8 g L⁻¹ agar; pH 5.8) was used for post-recovery regrowth.

Data presentation

All experiments comprised ≥ 3 independent replicates. For the antibiotic assay, 10 nodal explants per replicate were used, and for cryopreservation, ≥ 10 shoot tips per replicate were used. Survival and regrowth after cryopreservation data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Survival was evaluated based on the emergence of shoots 4 weeks after cryopreservation, whereas regrowth was assessed when shoots elongated and developed roots 8 weeks after cryopreservation. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.3.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). For multiple treatment comparisons, Duncan’s multiple range test was conducted at p < 0.05 to determine significant differences among treatments. For pairwise comparisons between two treatments, differences in survival proportions were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.05). All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Microbial community shifts after antibiotic treatment

16 S rRNA amplicon sequencing compared untreated (Ruby_C) and rifampicin-treated (Ruby_T) samples after removal of plant-derived ASVs (chloroplast and mitochondria). In Ruby_C, Mycobacterium spp. were the most abundant taxa, followed by L. poae. In Ruby_T, L. poae was completely absent, and several other dominant taxa were markedly reduced (Fig. 7). Figure 7 summarizes relative abundance changes of the top 20 endophytic taxa identified in Ruby_C and compares their profiles with Ruby_T. Newly detected, low-abundance taxa included Pediococcus pentosaceus, Levilactobacillus brevis, and Latilactobacillus sakei. While overall richness remained similar, taxonomic composition shifted substantially, indicating a restructuring of the endophytic community. Cryopreservation

Cryopreservation of endophyte-containing ‘Ruby Seedless’ shoot tips resulted in variable survival (31.8 ± 27.6%, Table 2), with some explants failing due to bacterial overgrowth by 8 weeks (Fig. 8A). In contrast, antibiotic-treated, endophyte-free (no culturable endophytes on LB) explants had significantly higher and consistent survival (70.9 ± 1.6%, Table 2), producing healthy shoots at 4 weeks and sustaining growth through 8 weeks (Fig. 8B). Discussion

This study provides the first direct evidence that targeted antibiotic elimination of identified endophytes can markedly improve cryopreservation success in grapevine. Previous optimization studies have focused primarily on physicochemical parameters—such as cryoprotectant composition, dehydration duration, and regrowth media—to enhance post-thaw viability [1, 2, 23, 24, 38]. Our results reveal that the microbial status of in vitro plant material represents an equally critical but previously underestimated factor. Among the parameters assessed, two stood out as particularly influential: (i) the presence or absence of latent endophytes, and (ii) the efficiency of sanitation prior to vitrification. Endophyte-free explants exhibited consistent survival and regrowth, whereas untreated ones showed high variability, suggesting that microbial contamination introduces an uncontrolled biological variable into cryopreservation systems. This highlights the need to integrate microbial quality control into standard cryopreservation workflows as a key determinant of reproducibility and long-term genetic stability.

We first identified culturable endophytes in two grapevine cultivars to guide antibiotic selection. Leifsonia poae was isolated from ‘Ruby Seedless’ and Curtobacterium oceanosedimentum from ‘Merlot’, as confirmed by 16S rRNA sequencing. L. poae is typically associated with grasses and soils/root-gall environments (e.g., Poa annua) [35] and has seldom been reported in grapevine. C. oceanosedimentum, originally isolated from marine sediments [36, 37] and only recently reported as a grapevine endophyte [39, 40], likewise represents a non-typical host context. Their presence in our material likely reflects environmental carry-over from field soil, irrigation water, or adjacent wild plants during stock-plant establishment or in vitro initiation.

The detection of C. oceanosedimentum in ‘Merlot’ implies a cultivar-dependent endophytic association, possibly maintained under specific in vitro culture conditions or influenced by genotype-specific physiological traits. This is consistent with previous observations that grapevine endophytic community structure varies according to cultivar genotype [41, 42]. Future work assessing co-occurrence networks with other grapevine-associated bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium, Kosakonia) would clarify its ecological role [40].

Because antibiotic susceptibility data for these species are limited, we empirically determined both MIC and MBC using microdilution assays. Rifampicin was most effective against L. poae, consistent with its inhibition of bacterial RNA polymerase [43], while cefotaxime—a third-generation cephalosporin targeting cell wall synthesis [44]—was active against C. oceanosedimentum. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating the efficacy of these antibiotics in grapevine tissue cultures against Agrobacterium and endogenous contaminants [19, 45]. The effective elimination of both species at non-phytotoxic concentrations suggests that short-term antibiotic exposure can serve as a practical sanitation step prior to cryopreservation.

Treatment of internodal explants on MBC-supplemented medium for 2–3 weeks successfully produced endophyte-free shoots in our study. Although definitive benchmarks for antibiotic sanitation of nodal or internodal grapevine cultures are lacking, comparable in vitro sanitation studies in woody crops typically used 10–30 day treatment windows, such as in citrus, grapes and cherry rootstocks [9, 19, 46]. Antibiotic effects on plant tissues are known to be species-, genotype- and tissue-dependent. Several studies have shown that plant sensitivity to antibiotics (e.g., β-lactams, carbenicillin, cefotaxime) varies among cultivars and explant types [47, 48].

In our experiments, ‘Merlot’ explants initially showed severe stress symptoms under the same MBC-based regimen that yielded stable results for ‘Ruby Seedless’. Although the overall regrowth rate of ‘Merlot’ was ultimately higher in the MBC treatment than in the control, new shoots were markedly stressed when transferred to fresh medium. When the shoots were placed on medium containing activated charcoal, they gradually recovered and resumed normal growth. These observations indicate that the antibiotic regimen effective for ‘Ruby Seedless’ requires adjustment for ‘Merlot’, underscoring the need for cultivar-specific optimization of sanitation conditions. This stepwise approach aims to achieve efficient endophyte removal while minimizing phytotoxic effects [46].

Amplicon sequencing provided a deeper view of microbial dynamics underlying antibiotic sanitation. Previous studies on the grapevine endosphere have reported a consistent core microbiome dominated by Proteobacteria (e.g., Pseudomonas, Novosphingobium, Rhizobium), Actinobacteria (Streptomyces), and Firmicutes (Bacillus) in roots and xylem tissues [49, 50].

In contrast, our in vitro shoot cultures showed dominance of Leifsonia poae and Mycobacterium in the untreated state, both of which disappeared after rifampicin treatment, whereas several lactic acid bacteria (Pediococcus pentosaceus, Levilactobacillus brevis, and Latilactobacillus sakei) became more abundant. These differences likely reflect the distinct habitat and selective pressures between field-grown grapevines (root/xylem endospheres) and axenic shoot cultures, where antibiotic exposure and nutrient conditions shape a simplified but more stable microbiome.

The enrichment of lactic acid bacteria after antibiotic treatment suggests a shift toward a more benign and potentially protective microbial community. Despite comparable alpha diversity, this compositional re-ordering indicates a transition toward a less pathogenic and less competitive endophytic assemblage, consistent with antibiotic- or stress-induced restructuring reported in banana, sweet cherry, and rice [51–53]. Such restructuring may underlie the improved regrowth and culture stability observed following cryopreservation.

Metagenomic profiling thus provided an integrated view of how antibiotic sanitation removes target taxa while stabilizing the residual microbiome. Future studies should examine whether these newly detected taxa persist through subculturing and whether they exert neutral, beneficial, or compensatory roles in vitro. Although the present analysis was limited to ‘Ruby Seedless’ due to resource constraints, expanding metagenomic profiling to ‘Merlot’ and other cultivars will clarify whether microbial restructuring and endophyte elimination are genotype dependent.

Only L. poae and C. oceanosedimentum were successfully cultured from explants, whereas metagenomic sequencing detected additional low-abundance taxa. This discrepancy likely reflects both tissue-specific microbial distribution and methodological constraints: isolation targeted basal regions in contact with medium, whereas metagenomic analysis encompassed entire shoots. Similar inconsistencies between culture-dependent and -independent methods are commonly observed in plant microbiome studies [54, 55].

In our cryopreservation assays, untreated ‘Ruby Seedless’ explants containing endophytes displayed inconsistent survival (31.8 ± 27.6%), with visible bacterial regrowth often causing post-thaw decline. In contrast, antibiotic-treated, endophyte-free explants achieved consistent and higher survival (70.9 ± 1.6%). A comparable improvement in cryopreservation performance after microbial sanitation has been reported in a previous study using PPM-supplemented pretreatment and preculture media [56], and a study on citrus [9] identified effective antibiotics against endophytic contaminants, supporting the rationale for targeted antibiotic sanitation. These findings emphasize that endophyte elimination enhances both viability and reproducibility in cryopreservation.

While our approach emphasizes microbial removal, other researchers have explored inoculating beneficial endophytes to enhance in vitro performance. For instance, Vergani et al. [40] applied two beneficial bacteria to grapevine micropropagated plantlets, while Vinskienė et al. [52] examined microbial community shifts in sweet cherry during field, in vitro, and post-cryopreservation stages with beneficial strain applications. Although early effects were limited, other studies show that certain endophytes promote rooting and vigor during acclimatization [57, 58]. These contrasting strategies—elimination versus inoculation—underscore the need for precise microbial management according to propagation or conservation objective.

Although our study supports targeted antibiotic sanitation, broader concerns regarding antibiotic resistance remain valid. Identifying the minimum effective concentration and shortest exposure necessary for complete bacterial removal is essential to minimize environmental and phytotoxic risks. Controlled and judicious application can achieve effective sanitation while reducing ecological impact.

Overall, this study establishes a reproducible framework for integrating microbial quality control into grapevine cryopreservation. The approach can be extended to other clonally propagated crops susceptible to latent endophyte interference, supporting the production of clean, high-quality germplasm for breeding and long-term conservation.

Conclusion

This study provides clear evidence that controlling the endophytic microbiome is a critical yet often overlooked determinant of cryopreservation success in grapevine. By integrating molecular identification with targeted, cultivar-specific antibiotic treatments, we achieved complete removal of dominant endophytes while maintaining plant vitality, resulting in markedly higher and more reproducible post-thaw recovery. The work establishes an experimental framework that links microbial management with cryobiological performance, expanding current perspectives beyond physicochemical optimization alone. This approach offers a practical sanitation strategy for genebanks and micropropagation systems, and can be adapted to other clonally propagated crops where latent endophytes compromise culture stability or long-term preservation. Future applications integrating microbial profiling with cryopreservation protocols may enable more standardized, microbiome-aware conservation of plant genetic resources.
