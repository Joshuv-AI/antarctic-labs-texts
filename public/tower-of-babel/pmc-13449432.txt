The evolution of pharmacovigilance system in Bangladesh: present landscape and strategic priorities
Authors: Bulbul Ahamed, Rabia Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAdverse drug reactions (ADRs) represent a significant public health issue, substantially contributing to preventable morbidity, mortality, and healthcare expenditures globally. Pharmacovigilance is important for protecting public health and ensuring safe and effective medicine use, especially in low- and middle-income countries where regulatory systems are still developing. Bangladesh has achieved significant advancements in developing a national pharmacovigilance framework under the Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA), which includes the establishment of an Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring (ADRM) cells, involvement in the World Health Organization Programme for International Drug Monitoring, and the introduction of national pharmacovigilance guidelines. Despite these efforts, Bangladesh's pharmacovigilance system is still in its infancy and faces a number of challenges, such as significant underreporting of ADRs, a lack of awareness and training among healthcare professionals (HCPs), insufficient enforcement of regulations, inadequate technology infrastructure, and weak stakeholder coordination. The commentary will narrate the current status of pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh, identify the key challenges and potential areas for strengthening the system.KEYWORDS: Bangladesh, Pharmacovigilance, Adverse drug reaction, Drug safety

Introduction

Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) represent a substantial yet preventable threat to patient safety and are among the leading causes of death globally. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines adverse drug reaction as ‘any noxious and unintended response to a medicine which might occur at doses utilized for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or treatment’ (WHO, 2002). Morbidity and mortality in hospital and community settings have increased worldwide due to ADRs, as they represent a prevalent and costly problem that affects millions of patients, and responsible for 5% to 20% of hospitalizations (Hussain, Hassali, and Babar, 2020; Sattar, 2024; WHO, 2002). Several factors contribute to ADRs, including the limited external validity of clinical trial populations, real-world patient complexity with comorbidities and polypharmacy, off-label prescribing, and pharmacogenetic variability among individuals (Kumar, 2017; Moore & Begaud, 2010).

Pharmacovigilance is a science concerned with ADRs and is regarded as a crucial tool in public health and medical practice to lower the risks associated with medicines (Hussain & Hassali, 2019; Sawarkar et al., 2019). It is defined as ‘the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other possible drug-related problems’ (WHO, 2006). Pharmacovigilance reduces the risk associated with medicines, and drug safety cannot be assured without it. It plays a crucial role in protecting patients, improving treatment outcomes, and directing choices about drug approval and removal from the market (Mansuri & Zaman, 2024). Since, pharmacovigilance continues to evolve, many countries worldwide are still striving to establish robust and advanced systems for monitoring medicines safety (Abraham et al., 2023).

Following the thalidomide tragedy in the 1960s, when thousands of babies were born with phocomelia, pharmacovigilance systems were established in the majority of nations (Hussain & Hassali, 2019; Kim & Scialli, 2011). Since then, the necessity of pharmacovigilance in the healthcare system has been acknowledged globally and has expanded significantly in recent years. The WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring (PIDM), initiated in 1968, constitutes a global pharmacovigilance network that gathers, analyzes, and disseminates individual case safety reports via VigiBase, with operational assistance from the Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) to enhance post-marketing drug safety globally (WHO, 2025). Currently, the WHO–UMC programme for international drug monitoring is comprised of 160 full member nations and 22 associate members globally (UMC, 2025). The goal and scope of pharmacovigilance are vast and involve numerous components, such as monitoring of medication errors, unauthorized and counterfeit drugs, drug interactions, lack of efficacy, and rational prescribing of medicines (Najafi, 2018).

Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) confront particular problems regarding pharmacovigilance (Kiguba et al., 2023). ADR reporting is still low in LMICs due to a number of factors, including low pharmacovigilance awareness among the healthcare professionals (HCPs), high patient-to-HCP ratio with extremely short consultation times, heavy workloads, lack of pharmacovigilance training, unclear reporting processes, limited regulatory feedback, inadequate collaboration between national medicines regulatory authorities and public health programs, and the widespread use of herbal and traditional medicines (Hussain et al., 2018; Islam et al., 2026; Kiguba et al., 2023; Olsson et al., 2010).

Bangladesh is a lower-middle-income country with a projected nominal gross domestic product (GDP) of around $475 billion in 2025 and has one of the highest population densities worldwide (IMF, 2025; Nazmunnahar et al., 2023). Bangladesh's population is expected to reach above 200 million by 2050, from 167 million in 2022 (Karim et al., 2025; Khan, Hamid, and Babar, 2022). The country has been exporting pharmaceuticals since the late 1980s, and has supplied pharmaceuticals to 55 nations, covering a broad range of products including all major therapeutic classes and dosage forms, as well as high-tech items such as intravenous (IV) fluids, injectables, inhalers, suppositories, and nasal sprays (Helali et al., 2014). According to the Bangladesh Association of Pharmaceutical Industries (BAPI), 98% of the nation's pharmaceutical needs are met locally. Currently, Bangladesh exports pharmaceuticals to over 160 countries, including Australia, the United States, and multiple European countries (Tajmim, 2025). Despite these achievements, substantial gaps remain in the development of Bangladesh's pharmacovigilance (PV) system (Sharma et al., 2025).

Overview of the healthcare and regulatory system in Bangladesh

Bangladesh is a South Asian country with an administrative structure consisting of eight divisions and 64 districts (Nazmunnahar et al., 2023; Sattar, 2021). Bangladesh's health system is well-structured and has three tiers of primary health care: Community Clinics at the village level, Union Health and Family Welfare Centers at the union level, and Upazila Health Complexes at the sub-district level (Islam & Biswas, 2014). The majority of an individual's health needs are met by primary health care during their lifetime. Further support to the primary healthcare system is extended by district hospitals that provide secondary health care. Teaching hospitals, public health and medical institutes, and other specialty hospitals at the national level provide tertiary-level healthcare with a wide range of specialized and improved laboratory facilities (Islam & Biswas, 2014).

The four key stakeholders that determine the structure and operation of Bangladesh's health system are the government, the private sector, nongovernmental organizations (NGOs), and donor agencies. District hospitals, Upazila Health Complexes, Union Health and Family Welfare Centers, and Community Clinics operate under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW) and provide general health and family planning services (GSMA, 2021; Sattar, 2021). The MoHFW implements its programs and delivers services through various executing and regulatory agencies. The Ministry's five directorates, as well as a few other organizations – Health Services, Nursing Services, Drug Administration, Family Planning, and the Health Engineering Department are the executing authorities (Sattar, 2021). Alongside these executing bodies, Bangladesh Medical and Dental Council (BMDC), Bangladesh Nursing Council (BNC), State Medical Faculty (SMF), Ayurvedic, Homeopathy, and Unani Board, and Pharmacy Council of Bangladesh (PCB) work as the regulatory agencies for the healthcare sector in Bangladesh (Sattar, 2021; WHO, 2015).

The Directorate General of Drug Administration (DGDA) is Bangladesh's national drug regulatory body, which operates under the MoHFW. All pharmaceutical products are subject to its regulation, which includes the import, manufacturing, distribution, pricing, licensing, and quality assurance of conventional and traditional medicines, including Herbal, Homeopathic, Ayurvedic, and Unani products (Mohiuddin, 2018). In addition to these core regulatory functions, the DGDA regulates the national pharmacovigilance system, which includes post-marketing surveillance and ADR reporting in addition to its primary regulatory duties (Khan, 2019). The regulatory role is grounded in the National Drug Policy and related laws, which specify requirements for drug registration, manufacturing, quality control, licensing, pharmacovigilance, and post-marketing surveillance. Through licensing agencies, inspection systems, and specialized departments for quality control and safety monitoring, the DGDA carries out these regulations (Mohiuddin, 2018). Furthermore, to strengthen the effectiveness of pharmacovigilance activities, DGDA conducts several types of training programs to enhance HCPs’ pharmacovigilance and ADR reporting skills (Hossain et al., 2018).

Evolution of pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh

The DGDA took over the central responsibility to build pharmacovigilance systems and regulate pharmaceutical products in Bangladesh since 1982 (Hossain et al., 2018). Despite this regulatory mandate, concerns over medicine safety grew more prominent, and Bangladesh experienced one of the worst incidents ever recorded in the early 90s, with 339 deaths linked to paracetamol syrup contaminated with diethylene glycol from propylene glycol (Mustansir et al., 2013). After that incident, on July 6, 1997, the MoHFW established a 10-member ADR advisory committee to assess, examine, and offer recommendations for resolving ADR issues. Following this, pharmacovigilance began functioning in 1999 in Bangladesh when the Directorate of Drug Administration established an Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring (ADRM) cell and issued an ADR reporting form to teaching hospitals (Mohiuddin, 2018). Unfortunately, the ADRM cell was halted due to a paucity of staff and essential funds, as well as inadequate knowledge of the HCPs, and there was little pharmacovigilance activity, with few ADR reports and minimal systematic regulatory framework processes (Jahan et al., 2017). Bangladesh experienced another tragic drug toxicity event in 2009, when a local pharmaceutical manufacturer Rid Pharmaceutical Co. produced paracetamol syrup with diethylene glycol, which caused the deaths of 26 children between the ages of 1 and 3 years (Helali et al., 2014). Following these instances, when public health recognized the relevance of ADRs, the DGDA began focusing on surveillance of drug safety, as well as registration and quality control of drugs (Hasan et al., 2019). Consequently, drug safety guidelines were revived in 2013 when the DGDA created the ADRM cell with technical support from the Systems for Improved Access to Pharmaceuticals and Services (SIAPS) program (DGDA, 2023; Mohiuddin, 2018). In the same year, an independent ADR advisory committee was formed to provide technical guidance to the ADRM cell (Khan, Hamid, Ur-Rehman, et al., 2022). At the global level, Bangladesh became the 120th member to join PIDM by the WHO in 2014 (Nasir et al., 2020). This integration further enabled the nation to improve its capacity to handle drug-related issues by using the WHO's pharmacovigilance guidelines (Hasan et al., 2019). Besides, the National Guideline on the Pharmacovigilance System in Bangladesh was introduced in August 2018, which offered a framework for the systematic reporting, evaluation, and regulatory action of ADRs for pharmaceutical goods, including medical devices and vaccines (WHO, 2018).

Current pharmacovigilance system in Bangladesh

The DGDA leads pharmacovigilance and drug safety monitoring nationwide as the only regulatory organization in Bangladesh and it is known as the National Pharmacovigilance Center (NPC). Within this framework, the National Guideline on the Pharmacovigilance System in Bangladesh guides the governance of the DGDA, which oversees all pharmacovigilance activities across the nation (DGDA, 2023). Supporting this structure, the ADR advisory committee of the DGDA oversees daily pharmacovigilance operations, gives technical guidance, assesses reports, and recommends on regulatory issues related to drug safety. The autonomous ADR advisory committee is headed by the director general of the DGDA. It consists of Bangladeshi pharmaceutical specialists, members of BAPI, medical professionals, academics from teaching hospitals, members of the Bangladesh Medical Association, and members of the Consumer Association of Bangladesh (DGDA, 2023).

Most of the pharmacovigilance activities in Bangladesh are carried out by passive surveillance, which produces alerts or signals that can further contribute to active surveillance for additional investigation. The focus of surveillance can be determined using the signals or alerts produced by passive surveillance, and a more appropriate and rigorous approach can be applied to enable the assessment of causality for the adverse event (DGDA, 2023). Operationally, the ADR reporting system in Bangladesh relies on HCPs (including physicians, pharmacists and nurses) to report suspected ADRs to the DGDA and ADRM centers on a volunteer basis. To facilitate reporting, the DGDA website provides specific forms for reporting adverse events following immunization (AEFI), adverse drug events (Yellow Cards), and COVID-19 vaccine adverse events (DGDA, 2022). Meanwhile, the Extended Program on Immunization (EPI) of the Directorate General of Health Services (DGHS) is monitoring adverse events associated with routine immunizations with technical assistance from WHO. Moreover, the DGDA has been releasing its pharmacovigilance newsletter since September 2016, and five leaflets of medicine information were updated in 2021 (Khan, Hamid, Ur-Rehman, et al., 2022).

In Bangladesh, patients can now report ADRs more easily using digital platforms and mobile applications (Sharma et al., 2025). Besides, Pharmaceutical Manufacturers and Marketing Authorization Holders (MAHs) have to provide the ADRM cell with company-funded post-registration study reports and periodic safety update reports (PSURs) on a quarterly basis, as well as respond immediately and completely to the ADRM cell's requests for risk/benefit information. Despite the participation of multiple stakeholders, including public health organizations, regulatory bodies, training facilities, and international partners, collaboration among these parties is currently limited in Bangladesh (Sharma et al., 2025).

In Bangladesh, underreporting of ADRs is highly prevalent (Islam et al., 2026). Despite the establishment of the national pharmacovigilance program, ADRM cells, and Bangladesh’s membership in the WHO-PIDM since 2014, only a small percentage (∼1%) of ADRs are reported (Amin et al., 2016; Rasel et al., 2023). Furthermore, previous studies have also shown that underreporting is a major issue because HCPs don't have a reporting culture (Hussain, Hassali, ur Rehman, et al., 2020; Nahar et al., 2014). In response to these challenges, the revised National Guideline on Pharmacovigilance System acts as a model for implementing the system and offers a fundamental framework for carrying out Bangladesh's national pharmacovigilance system in a way that is comprehensive, systems-oriented, and compliant with international standards (DGDA, 2023).

Causality assessment in pharmacovigilance

The process of determining whether a medicine was the cause of a reported adverse event is known as causality assessment (DGDA, 2023). Many methods and algorithms exist for causality assessment, including the Jones’ algorithm, the Yale algorithm, the Naranjo algorithm, the Begaud algorithm, the Karch algorithm, the WHO-UMC causality assessment system, and a recent quantitative approach algorithm. The fundamental idea underlying all these techniques or algorithms is to properly establish the association between ADR and drug (Acharya et al., 2020). The most popular and recognized techniques for determining the causality of ADR are the Naranjo algorithm, the Uppsala Monitoring Center (WHO-UMC) causality assessment system, and the causality assessment system proposed by the WHO-PIDM because of their ease of analysis (Acharya et al., 2020; Zaki, 2011).

In Bangladesh, the ADR advisory committee and its subcommittee are mainly in charge of conducting causality assessments of reports, which is kept for further use at the ADRM Cell. The WHO Causality Assessment Scale, a standardized instrument for assessing the probability of a causative association between drug exposure and adverse events, is used to evaluate causality in a report (DGDA, 2023).

Thus, the ADR advisory committee can evaluate different degrees of certainty regarding whether a suspected drug has actually caused a particular ADR using a causality assessment. Following the receipt of adverse event reports, the ADRM Cell collaborates closely with the ADR advisory committee to establish a causality between the reported occurrence and the medicines that the patient or consumer was exposed to (DGDA, 2023).

Inherent limitations and key challenges in the current pharmacovigilance system

Pharmacovigilance systems have a number of intrinsic drawbacks despite their vital function in ensuring the safety of medicines. Underreporting of ADRs, which is widely acknowledged as a global problem and is especially noticeable in LMICs, is one of the most significant obstacles (Hussain, Hassali, and Rana, 2019). Lack of knowledge, insufficient training, time restrictions, and ambiguity about reporting procedures are the contributing factors of underreporting of ADRs (Hussain et al., 2018; Hussain, Hassali, & Rana, 2019; Hussain, Hassali, ur Rehman, et al., 2020; Islam et al., 2026; Kiguba et al., 2023; Menang et al., 2023; Olsson et al., 2010). Additionally, the dependability of pharmacovigilance databases is constrained by data quality problems, such as incomplete or erroneous reports (Kiguba et al., 2023). Data interpretation and signal detection may become more difficult as a result of duplicate reporting. Furthermore, pharmacovigilance systems frequently rely on spontaneous reporting, which can lead to reporting bias and make it more difficult to identify rare or delayed adverse reactions (Bate & Evans, 2009; Pariente et al., 2007; Santosh & Tragulpiankit, 2011). In Bangladesh, these challenges are made worse by insufficient regulatory capability, inadequate infrastructure, and a lack of organized feedback mechanisms for ADR reports (Rasel et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2025). Strengthening reporting systems, boosting stakeholder training, and improving data management and legal frameworks are all necessary to address these limitations.

Pharmacovigilance practices face several challenges that prevent efficient monitoring and reporting of ADRs in Bangladesh. One of the most serious issues in pharmacovigilance is a lack of awareness and understanding of the significance of ADR reporting among the HCPs and the general population (Sharma et al., 2025). In addition, many HCPs are not well-versed in causality assessment techniques, ADR reporting procedures, and the function of pharmacovigilance in the monitoring of drug safety (Islam et al., 2026). Moreover, pharmacovigilance is not systematically incorporated into the curricula of medical, nursing, or pharmacy programs, which may contribute to a lacking in necessary pharmacovigilance training and capabilities (Sharma et al., 2025).

A key challenge for pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh is the weak implementation of existing regulatory laws and guidelines. Furthermore, pharmacovigilance policy enforcement is complicated by a lack of sufficient regulatory surveillance and weak collaboration among the stakeholders (Kiguba et al., 2023). Poor funding and insufficient technological support further limit Bangladesh's pharmacovigilance system to establish the proper infrastructure, digitalize systems to process ADR data effectively, and conduct public awareness programs (Sharma et al., 2025).

Opportunities for strengthening pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh

The current landscape of pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh presents important opportunities for improvement and development towards a more robust and responsive medicines safety system . One of the most significant ways to strengthen pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh is to adapt the WHO's guidelines, which provide standardized approaches to improve drug safety reporting and monitoring systems worldwide (Menang et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2025). In addition, it is potentially necessary to set localized protocols for reporting ADRs and to establish clear communication channels among the stakeholders to develop a stronger pharmacovigilance system (Ogar et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2025). Improvements of pharmacovigilance knowledge and education among medical students and HCPs are also equally important to improve pharmacovigilance reporting rates and system efficacy, as targeted training programs have been proven to significantly enhance knowledge, attitudes, and ADR reporting rates (Ata et al., 2025; Hussain, Hassali, Hashmi, et al., 2021; Shamsuddoha et al., 2024). To achieve this, structured educational programs should be incorporated into medical and pharmacy curricula, and regular professional development seminars should be conducted (Hussain, Hassali, Hashmi, et al., 2021; Nahar et al., 2014; Sattar, 2024).

The adoption of digital platforms can make reporting ADRs easier and faster for both patients and HCPs (Mweetwa et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2025). Complementing digital tools, launching awareness programs about the pharmacovigilance system among the patients can improve ADR reporting rates, and these initiatives can then be publicized through social media and community outreach initiatives, giving the public more knowledge about drug safety and encouraging them to take part in medicines safety monitoring (Kumar et al., 2023; Sharma et al., 2025). Finally, pharmacovigilance in Bangladesh can be improved by collaborative efforts among many stakeholders, including government bodies, pharmaceutical companies, and international organizations. Such partnerships can facilitate resource sharing, exchange of knowledge, and the development of comprehensive pharmacovigilance initiatives (Hodel et al., 2024; Hussain, Hassali, Hashmi, et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2025).

A comparative assessment of pharmacovigilance practices in developing countries provides significant insights that might be implemented to strengthen the pharmacovigilance system in Bangladesh. While developing countries such as Pakistan, Ghana, and Nigeria have established comprehensive pharmacovigilance systems, Bangladesh still has a long way to go. Pakistan, being a close neighbor of Bangladesh with a comparable healthcare landscape has made substantial progress in strengthening its pharmacovigilance infrastructure in recent years (Hussain & Hassali, 2019). Through the establishment of an NPC and active participation in the WHO-PIDM, Pakistan has enhanced its ADR reporting systems and demonstrated growing regulatory commitment toward medicines safety (DRAP, 2024). Furthermore, the introduction of a systematic pharmacovigilance framework under the drug regulatory authority of Pakistan has helped to gradually enhance spontaneous reporting practices and regulatory monitoring (Hussain & Hassali, 2019). This initiative has enhanced drug safety monitoring and increased reporting rates by emphasizing HCP training and public engagement through reporting ADRs. Similarly, Nigeria's National Pharmacovigilance Center has worked to boost the capacity of HCPs through awareness-raising and training, which has improved data quality and reporting rates (Sharma et al., 2025).

Looking at other developing African nations, there are differences in the level of development of pharmacovigilance programs and systems. Pharmacovigilance systems are well-established in nations like Egypt, Tunisia, and Morocco. In particular, the WHO collaborating center of Morocco is one such example that may indicate the highly efficient pharmacovigilance system that has been developed (Alshammari et al., 2019). The pharmacovigilance systems in these nations are influenced by public participation, technological advancements, and comprehensive training programs. Drawing lessons from these experiences, Bangladesh can improve its own pharmacovigilance systems by adopting strategies that have proven successful.

Future directions and strategic priorities

Strengthening the pharmacovigilance system in Bangladesh necessitates a strategic transition from merely raising the numbers of ADR reports to elevating their quality, comprehensiveness, and regulatory applicability (Islam et al., 2026; Sharma et al., 2025; WHO, 2002). The establishment of a national pharmacovigilance center under the DGDA, participation in the WHO-PIDM, and the implementation of digital reporting platforms are significant accomplishments; however, the subsequent phase should prioritize the integration of pharmacovigilance into standard clinical practice (DGDA, 2023). This can be accomplished by the establishment of systematic follow-up procedures, the implementation of organized training on high-quality individual case safety report documentation, and the institutionalization of pharmacovigilance units and designated focal persons in public and private healthcare settings (Rasel et al., 2023; Shamsuddoha et al., 2024). By improving causality evaluation, signal detection, and prompt regulatory decision-making, these actions will lead to reporting on significant patient safety outcomes.

Adopting a more proactive and risk-based surveillance strategy is a second strategic aim. The actual burden and trends of medication-related damage cannot be fully captured by passive reporting alone (WHO, 2002). Sentinel sites and focused active surveillance for biologics, antimicrobials, vaccines, oncology medicines, and pediatric treatments would produce context-specific safety information to inform national policy. Concurrently, a lifecycle approach to medicines safety can be ensured by enhancing the accountability of MAHs by enforcing good pharmacovigilance practice requirements, such as risk management plans, regular safety update reports, and routine pharmacovigilance inspections. By combining pharmacovigilance with measures for antimicrobial stewardship, medication error reporting, substandard and counterfeit product monitoring, pharmacovigilance would be even more in line with the broader goals of patient safety and health system strengthening (Sharma et al., 2025).

Moving forward, digital innovation, regulatory transparency, and capacity enhancement will be essential facilitators for a mature and sustainable pharmacovigilance system (Nimesh et al., 2019). Enhancing data interoperability, building real-time dashboards for signal prioritization, and expanding the national electronic reporting platform can all significantly improve responsiveness and efficiency. Besides, Bangladesh needs to organize training courses on pharmacovigilance concepts, ADR identification, and reporting processes for HCPs, and establish a network of pharmaco-epidemiologists and pharmacovigilance for the development of national pharmacovigilance system (Hussain & Hassali, 2019; Mustansir et al., 2013; Sharma et al., 2025). Targeted safety alerts for HCPs, patient-focused communication tactics, and the regular release of national drug safety bulletins will promote reporting culture and public trust. Combined, these proactive, data-driven, and impact-focused measures will help Bangladesh move closer to a pharmacovigilance system that promotes safer and rational use of medicines.

Conclusion

Pharmacovigilance is vital for ensuring medicines safety and strengthening the healthcare system. Despite the establishment of the National Pharmacovigilance Program, ADRM cells, and Bangladesh’s membership in the WHO-PIDM, the pharmacovigilance system of Bangladesh is facing challenges with recurrent underreporting, little awareness among HCPs, infrastructural weaknesses, restricted resources, and insufficient stakeholder collaboration. Therefore, a comprehensive and coordinated approach is needed to close these gaps, with a focus on strengthening regulations, incorporating pharmacovigilance education into health curricula, growing digital ADR reporting platforms, and improving collaboration among academic institutions, industry, regulatory agencies, and healthcare organizations.
