Pancreatic stent and transpapillary pseudocyst drainage via endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography for children with blunt pancreatic duct injury
Authors: Katsunori Kouchi, Takashi Fumita, Chikako Nakata, Keita Kagaya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNonoperative management (NOM) in blunt pancreatic injury (BPI) is frequently complicated by pseudocyst formation and pancreatic atrophy. To address these complications, we have utilized a pancreatic stent via endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) for children with PDI since 2019.AimWe present our experience with ERCP-based NOM for PDI and provide a literature review of stent therapy in pediatric PDI.Materials and methodsTen children with BPI treated between January 2007 and December 2025 were classified according to the 2024 American Association for the Surgery of Trauma pancreatic injury grades: IB (n = 3), IIA (n = 3), IIIA (n = 2), and IIIB (n = 2). Pancreatic stents were placed in two IIIA cases and one IIIB case. The IIIB case subsequently underwent transpapillary pseudocyst drainage (TPPD) due to a pseudocyst infection.ResultsNOM was successful in all low-grade injuries (< II) without complications. One IIIB patient treated without stenting developed a pseudocyst requiring cyst-gastrostomy; 1-year follow-up demonstrated severe pancreatic atrophy. In the two IIIA stent cases, the duct injury was successfully bridged: one patient had no pseudocyst formation, and the other developed a pseudocyst that resolved spontaneously; both showed preserved pancreatic parenchyma. The IIIB patient treated with TPPD experienced rapid cyst resolution; 1-year imaging revealed approximately 50% distal pancreatic atrophy.ConclusionTPPD should be considered for symptomatic pseudocysts, even after stent placement. ERCP-based NOM for pediatric PDI can be performed safely, may prevent pancreatic atrophy, and represents a valuable option in the management of PDI in children.Keywords: Pancreatic duct injury, Pancreatic injury, Endoscopic retrograde cholangiography, Endoscopic retrograde pancreatography, Children

Introduction

The treatment for blunt pancreatic injury (BPI) widely varies based on the grade of injury, from nonoperative management (NOM) to definitive surgical procedures, including pancreatectomy [1–9]. According to the 2024 American Association for the Surgery of Trauma (2024AAST) [10] (Fig. 1), NOM is generally the gold standard for grade I and II pancreatic injuries as these injuries do not involve pancreatic duct injury (PDI) [6]. However, in grade III and IV injuries, PDI results in the leakage of pancreatic juice into the retroperitoneum or abdominal cavity, requiring specific interventions. According to previous studies on pediatric PDI, operative management results in earlier initiation of oral intake and shorter hospital stays compared with NOM, with no significant difference in mortality [1, 2, 5]. Conversely, other studies advocate for NOM in pediatric PDI, citing comparable outcomes in length of stay and mortality when compared with operative management [3, 4, 6–9]. However, in children with PDI exhibiting stable vital signs, NOM is associated with a high incidence of pancreatic pseudocyst formation, infection, hemorrhage, and rupture [3, 8, 9]. If a pseudocyst fails to resolve spontaneously during NOM, external drainage or internal fistula formation becomes necessary. Furthermore, atrophy of the pancreas caudal to the injury site has been reported in children who underwent internal drainage [11].

Recently, early endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) and transpapillary stenting have increasingly been performed in pediatric patients to address pseudocysts and pancreatic atrophy [1, 2, 5]. Pancreatic duct stenting is expected to preserve the integrity of the pancreatic duct by bridging the injured site and suppress pseudocyst enlargement by reducing intraductal pressure. Nevertheless, the clinical outcomes of stenting reportedly vary, with some studies suggesting that it is ineffective in preventing pseudocysts [15, 18]. Since 2019, we have performed early ERCP and pancreatic duct stenting for PDI. In one case where a pseudocyst failed to regress and was subsequently associated with infection, transpapillary pseudocyst drainage (TPPD) was performed, resulting in the rapid resolution of the cyst. Reports of the use of TPPD in children remain scarce. Herein, we present our experience with ERCP-based NOM for PDI and provide a literature review regarding the efficacy, complications, and long-term pancreatic function associated with stent therapy in pediatric PDI.

Materials and methods

Ten pediatric patients with BPI were treated at our institution between January 2007 and December 2025 (Tables 1 and 2). The cohort comprised 9 boys and 1 girl, aged 5–12 years. Injuries were graded according to the 2024AAST pancreatic injury grade classification [10] (Fig. 1) based on abdominal ultrasound (US) and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT). The 2024AAST pancreatic injury grades were as follows: grade IB (n = 3), grade IIA (n = 3), grade IIIA (n = 2), and grade IIIB (n = 2). We utilized the following protocol as the standard treatment for pediatric BPI. Patients with low-grade injuries (< grade II) were managed with bowel rest and peripheral intravenous fluid infusion for several days, with oral intake resumed once serum pancreatic enzyme levels peaked and began to decline after peaking. For high-grade injuries (> grade III), management included bowel rest, a 3-day course of prophylactic antibiotics, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), and protease inhibitors. In the absence of signs of infection, antibiotics were discontinued after 3 days. In one with grade IIIB injury treated prior to 2019, percutaneous transgastric drainage of pancreatic pseudocysts was performed 4 weeks post injury to allow maturation and stabilization of the pseudocyst wall. Since 2019, ERCP has been indicated for cases diagnosed with or suspected to have high-grade injury (> III). ERCP was performed under general anesthesia using a JF-260 V duodenoscope ((Olympus, Tokyo, Japan)) in all cases. A leakage of contrast medium was identified through pancreatography. Then, a 0.018-in guidewire was inserted into the pancreatic duct through the major papilla, and a 4- or 5-Fr endoscopic naso-pancreatic drainage (ENPD) tube was placed to overlap the rupture site of the pancreatic duct. For TPPD, a 5-Fr pigtail ENPD tube was placed transpapillary into the pancreatic pseudocyst under ERCP. Patients who underwent stenting or pseudocyst drainage resumed oral intake after confirmation of the absence or resolution of the pseudocyst on imaging and stabilization of serum pancreatic enzyme levels; TPN was maintained during this period. In patients with grade II or higher injuries, US and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were performed 1 year after the injury to evaluate the degree of atrophy in the pancreas distal to the injury site. Pancreatic atrophy was defined as thinning of the pancreatic parenchyma caudal to the duct injury based on imaging findings.

Stent position definitions

Stent placement was categorized as follows:

Bridging stent: The stent tube passed the site of ductal injury, with the tip positioned in the distal pancreas.

Peripheral stent: The catheter tip could not pass the injury site but was placed near the injury or within a pancreatic pseudocyst.

Short stent: The stent bypassed the papilla, but the tip remained proximal to the injury site.

Literature review

The Medline (via PubMed) and Web of Science databases were searched for articles on pediatric PDI using combinations of MeSH terms, including children, pancreatic duct injury, pancreatic injury, endoscopic retrograde cholangiography, and endoscopic retrograde pancreatography. The search period was from January 2000 to December 2025 and was restricted to English-language publications. In addition, the reference lists of selected articles were manually screened for potential studies. The outcome parameters for the review included the success rate of bridging, pancreatic pseudocyst formation, post-ERCP complications, distal pancreatic atrophy, and pancreatic function. Results

The clinical characteristics of the 6 children with grade II or lower injury, treated at our institution, are summarized in Table 1. Case 2 underwent laparoscopic intestinal repair because of a small bowel perforation. In Case 3, contrast-enhanced CT revealed extravasation in the spleen, and the injured splenic artery was treated with transcatheter arterial embolization. Although these 2 cases required treatment for associated injuries, all cases of low-grade injury (< grade II) were successfully managed with NOM without complications. Follow-up US performed 1 year after the injury showed no pancreatic atrophy in these cases. Table 2 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the 4 children with grade III injury. In one case of grade IIIB injury (case 7), PDI was located in the body. A pancreatic pseudocyst gradually enlarged (up to 10 cm in diameter) 2 weeks after admission and showed no signs of spontaneous regression. The patient presented with nausea caused by the pseudocyst, and endoscopic cyst-gastrostomy was performed on hospital day 28. The pseudocyst resolved following the procedure. However, follow-up US 1 year later revealed severe atrophy of the pancreas from the body to the tail. This case served as the clinical impetus for the introduction of early ERCP-based management at our institution.

ERCP with stent placement was performed in 3 cases (2 cases of grade IIIA injury and one case of grade IIIB injury). One patient with grade IIIA injury (case 8) was admitted 3 days after the injury. Contrast-enhanced CT at admission showed a low-density area in the pancreatic body and peripancreatic fluid collection (Fig. 2a). Laboratory tests showed elevated inflammatory markers and pancreatic enzymes (p-amylase 177 IU/L, lipase 105 IU/L, and C-reactive protein [CRP] 18.6 mg/dL). ERCP was performed on the day after admission, and successful bridging was achieved using a 4-Fr ENPD tube (Fig. 2b). On hospital day 10, a pancreatic pseudocyst (6 × 3 cm) was detected by US. However, it gradually regressed and completely resolved by the third week of admission. The other case with grade IIIA injury (case 9) presented on the day of the injury. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a low-density area in the pancreatic body. On hospital day 2, ERCP was performed, which revealed contrast extravasation from the pancreatic body. The condition was diagnosed as partial pancreatic duct disruption because the pancreatic duct alignment remained intact. A guidewire was successfully passed to the tail of the pancreas, and bridging was achieved using a 4-Fr ENPD tube. The case was successfully managed using NOM without developing a pancreatic pseudocyst. One case with grade IIIB injury (case 10) was admitted 6 days after the injury, and contrast-enhanced CT showed a pseudocyst (Fig. 3a). ERCP was performed on the day of admission. Although the guidewire entered the cyst, it could not advance to the distal pancreas duct. Consequently, a 5-Fr stent was placed as the peripheral stent. On the next day, the patient exhibited elevated pancreatic enzymes and intraperitoneal fluid on US, leading to a diagnosis of pseudocyst rupture, which was managed with laparoscopic drainage. On hospital day 17, the patient developed severe abdominal pain. Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a high-intensity region within the cyst (Fig. 3b), suggesting intracystic hemorrhage. As the patient’s vital signs remained stable, NOM was continued. However, the patient developed fever and abdominal pain on hospital day 26. Laboratory results (p-amylase 676 IU/L, lipase 1,723 IU/L, and CRP 10.3 mg/dL) indicated an infected pseudocyst. A 5-Fr pigtail catheter was inserted transpapillary into the cyst as TPPD by ERCP (Fig. 4a). Subsequently, the patient’s symptoms immediately subsided, and the cyst rapidly disappeared (Fig. 4b). Two weeks after TPPD, the stent was removed, and bridging via ERCP was attempted. However, the guidewire could not be advanced to the caudal pancreas. Therefore, a peripheral stent was placed. A subsequent attempt at bridging via ERCP was also unsuccessful. The patient became stent-free 6 months after the injury. Two patients with grade IIIA injury (cases 8 and 9) who underwent successful stent bridging showed no distal pancreatic atrophy 1 year post injury. By contrast, one case with grade IIIB injury (case 10) showed approximately 50% atrophy of the distal pancreas on MRI 1 year later, and the recent HbA1c level was 5.7%. In all 10 cases, no recurrences of abdominal pain or pancreatitis were observed during the follow-up period, and no evidence of glucose intolerance or steatorrhea was observed.

Since the report by Canty in 2001 [12], a total of 39 pediatric patients with PDI treated with ERCP, including our 3 cases described here, have been reported up to December 2025 (Table 3). The ages of the reported patients ranged from 4 to 18 years (mean, 10 years), and 25 and 14 cases were classified as grade III and grade IV, respectively, according to the 1990 AAST classification [20]. Regarding ERCP treatment, stent placement and endoscopic sphincterotomy (EST) were performed in 28 and 2 cases, respectively, whereas concomitant stent placement and EST were performed in 9 cases. Stent position was documented in 33 cases, with bridging, peripheral, and short stenting in 9 (27%), 14 (42%), and 10 (30%) cases, respectively. Among the 9 cases with stent bridging, one previously reported [15] underwent the procedure for a pancreatic duct stricture. The remaining 8 cases were associated with pancreatic duct injury; however, 6 (75%) cases did not develop pseudocysts or experienced spontaneous resolution. Conversely, 11 (46%) of the 24 cases with short or peripheral stenting required aspiration, drainage, or distal pancreatectomy (DP) because of pseudocyst persistence. Complications associated with ERCP treatment in children included elevated pancreatic enzymes in 7 cases (18%), pseudocyst rupture in 2 cases (5%), pseudocyst infection in 2 cases, and stent migration in 1 case. Documentation regarding pancreatic atrophy after stent placement was available for only 6 cases [16]; pancreatic atrophy was observed in none of the 3 (0%) cases with bridging stenting and in all 3 (100%) cases with short or peripheral stenting.

Discussion

The optimal management of pediatric BPI—specifically whether to choose NOM or surgical intervention—has long been debated [1–9]. In particular, this controversy is relevant in PDI cases. Although some studies suggest that surgical management reduces hospital stays and complications [1, 2, 5], others report no significant difference in hospitalization duration between the two approaches [3, 4, 6–9]. According to studies on adult and pediatric BPI, concomitant PDI significantly increases the risk of NOM failure [21, 22]. By contrast, recent analyses of large-scale pediatric databases (n = 811) in the United States [7] and systematic reviews of BPI in children (n = 1147) [9] have supported NOM as an effective treatment strategy for PDI. However, the following primary challenges remain: pseudocyst formation in NOM, fistula formation in pancreatectomy, and long-term preservation of pancreatic function in both approaches. Pseudocyst formation is more frequent in NOM (19%–62% overall) [2, 3, 5] and occurs at an even higher rate in PDI (70%–78%) [2, 8]. Historically, we have opted for NOM [23], with a focus on resolving pseudocysts and maintaining pancreatic function.

Using NOM for treating pancreatic pseudocysts carries the risks of rupture, infection, and hemorrhage [14, 17, 24]. Conventional treatments have included aspiration, external drainage, cyst-gastrostomy, or cyst-enteric anastomosis. Instead of these treatments, pancreatic duct stenting by ERCP has been reported to suppress or resolve pseudocysts in adult [25–28]and pediatric patients [12–19]. Stenting via the papilla is expected to provide two therapeutic benefits: (1) bypassing the sphincter of Oddi (which maintains a resting pressure of < 40 mmHg) to facilitate pancreatic juice outflow into the duodenum and reduce intraductal pressure and (2) bridging the disruption to divert pancreatic juice away from the injury site, thereby preserving ductal integrity and facilitating pancreatic duct recanalization. In literature, the efficacy of stenting remains varied. According to Biffl et al. [28], prophylactic stenting did not show significant benefits for treating ductal injuries. However, they noted the need for further prospective trials. Importantly, their study evaluated overall outcomes without classifying the results based on the stent position. By contrast, Varadarajulu et al. [25] investigated the relationship between stent position (bridging, peripheral, and short stents) and outcomes in 92 cases of ductal disruption. Bridging stents were successful in 52 cases (55%), with 49 cases showing long-term resolution of the disruption. In the peripheral stent group (n = 8), pseudocysts resolved, and clinical symptoms disappeared despite persistent ductal disruption. Meanwhile, in the short stent group (n = 32), the disruption persisted, eventually requiring surgery in 21 cases and percutaneous drainage in 7 cases. In pediatric BPI, the application of ERCP stenting has gradually increased since it was first reported by Canty in 2001 [12]. In our review of 39 pediatric cases (Table 3), bridging stents were achieved in 9 patients (27%). Among the 8 cases with ductal disruption, 6 cases (75%) either did not develop pseudocysts or showed spontaneous resolution, highlighting bridging stents’ high efficacy. Conversely, among the 24 cases of short or peripheral stenting, 11 cases (45%) required aspiration, drainage, or DP as the pseudocyst did not resolve. According to Ni et al. [29], combining stent placement with endoscopic transmural drainage is more effective for fluid collections associated with ductal disruption than either modality alone, noting that 55.2% of complete disruptions can achieve recanalization. Therefore, combined drainage should be prepared during NOM when stent bridging is unsuccessful. Pseudocyst rupture is a known complication of stenting, occurring in 2 out of 39 pediatric cases (5%). In patients with preexisting pseudocysts, stent bridging is often difficult. Therefore, a peripheral stent should be carefully placed to avoid rupture. If the cyst enlarges or becomes infected/hemorrhagic, TPPD or other drainage methods should be utilized. The primary difference between TPPD and standard stenting lies in the catheter design. Standard stents are typically straight with a terminal hole and side holes slightly apart from the tip, which may encourage ductal recanalization but offer poor cyst drainage. For TPPD, a pigtail catheter with multiple side holes is preferable for optimal drainage. However, we recommend removing the TPPD tube and attempting a bridging stent once the pseudocyst has diminished because the pigtail shape may hinder ductal recanalization after the cyst shrinks. Ni et al. [29] reported a bridging success rate of 87.3% in cases with partial pancreatic duct disruption. As illustrated in Fig. 1, cases with grade ⅢA/ⅣA injuries are considered to preserve main pancreatic duct alignment. Consequently, patients with ⅢA/ⅣA demonstrate a high likelihood of successful stent bridging, making them strong candidates for stent therapy.

With regard to pancreatic function, insulin and glucagon concentrations are higher in the pancreatic tail [30]. This likely explains why glucose intolerance is more common after DP than after pancreatoduodenectomy [31, 32]. Studies in adults following pancreatic resection for trauma have shown varied results. According to Colney et al. [33], the incidence of dysglycemia (prediabetes/diabetes mellitus [DM]) was higher in the DP group (50%) than in the no-resection group (38%). However, the difference was not statistically significant. Morita [34] reported that adult patients with BPI undergoing resection had higher rates of new-onset DM (50% vs. 25%) and steatorrhea (50% vs. 12.5%) compared with nonresected patients. In pediatric PDI managed with NOM without stenting, one study found significant atrophy of the pancreatic body and tail in 75% of cases by CT at a mean of 47 months post injury. However, no functional abnormalities were noted [11]. Other pediatric studies have reported varying rates of exocrine insufficiency and chronic pancreatitis-like changes [3, 24, 35]. In NOM for children, it is important to preserve the distal pancreas. Pancreatic atrophy after NOM with short or peripheral stenting [16, 35] and without stenting [11] was reported in 61%–75% of cases, and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups. Thus, bridging stents are hypothesized to offer the best chance for tissue preservation. Indeed, although only 3 pediatric cases documenting pancreatic atrophy in the present cohort were managed with bridging stents, none exhibited post-treatment pancreatic atrophy. Therefore, we consider early ERCP with attempted stent bridging to be important for preserving pancreatic function in pediatric PDI.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, the total number of pediatric PDI cases treated with stents was small (39 cases since 2001). Second, few reports explicitly document the presence or absence of pancreatic atrophy after injury. Finally, many reports lack specific details regarding stent positioning. Therefore, future studies should accurately document the stent tip location relative to the injury site to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of stents in pediatric cases.

Conclusion

Although the success rates remain modest, bridging stents in pediatric PDI show promise for suppressing pseudocyst formation and preserving pancreatic tissue. In cases where only short or peripheral stenting is achieved, additional pseudocyst drainage should be performed. If a pseudocyst fails to regress or develops complications (e.g., hemorrhage or infection), TPPD using a pigtail catheter is an effective drainage-focused strategy. By combining with ERCP techniques, NOM can be performed more safely in pediatric PDI, offering a higher potential for ductal recanalization and pancreatic parenchyma preservation than previously possible.
