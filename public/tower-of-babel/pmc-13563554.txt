Isoform-level characterization and structural diversity of antithrombotic genes in Hirudinaria manillensis
Authors: Tahir Farooq, Muhammad Naeem, Nawaz Haider Bashir, Zichao Liu, Yuanhai Chen, Patcharin Krutmuang, Huanhuan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antithrombotic proteins of medicinal leeches-originally characterized from salivary-gland secretions-suppress coagulation, platelet activation and fibrinolysis at the feeding site, but the contribution of alternative transcript processing to this repertoire remains poorly documented. Here we used four H. manillensis RNA-seq libraries (THA-Hm1–THA-Hm4), whose species identity and overall sequencing performance were characterized in our companion study, to determine how many antithrombotic loci produce multiple structurally distinct transcript isoforms and how such variation extends into coding capacity. Reference-guided assembly merged into a unified transcript set identified 38 transcript-supported candidates in addition to the 72 catalogued reference genes. When candidate transcripts were grouped by parent MSTRG locus and ranked by isoform count, six loci carried three or four structurally distinct models: MSTRG.4545, MSTRG.5212, MSTRG.3310, MSTRG.5709, MSTRG.5200 and MSTRG.5713. Transcript-length variation alone produced isoforms at three of the six loci, whereas at MSTRG.5200, MSTRG.5709 and MSTRG.5713 the variation extended into the coding sequence, producing predicted proteins ranging from 49 to 1159 amino acids. These results reveal localized isoform complexity at a small set of antithrombotic loci and define a focused candidate set for downstream domain annotation, splice-junction visualisation and functional validation.

Introduction

Blood-feeding leeches deliver, through their salivary glands, a chemically diverse mixture that simultaneously targets coagulation, platelet activation, and fibrinolysis at the feeding site (Raghavi et al., 2026; Shakouri and Wollina, 2021). This mixture includes direct thrombin inhibitors, factor Xa inhibitors, kallikrein and elastase inhibitors, platelet aggregation antagonists, fibrinolytic activators, and spreading factors such as hyaluronidase that facilitate tissue penetration (Babenko et al., 2020; Manuvera et al., 2024; Tolksdorf et al., 2025). While the gene families encoding these proteins have been catalogued, the contribution of alternative transcript processing to antithrombotic output has not been systematically examined. The structural and functional breadth of these molecules, combined with the evolutionary pressure to maintain broad hemostatic blockade across different vertebrate hosts, has made medicinal leeches a productive source of anticoagulant leads for several decades (Raghavi et al., 2026).

Genomic and transcriptomic work in Asian medicinal leeches has substantially expanded the catalog of known antithrombotic proteins beyond the classical hirudin family. Liu et al. (2023) identified 72 antithrombotic genes distributed across 21 antithrombotic gene families in a revised genome assembly of Hirudinaria manillensis, establishing this species as a genomic reference for the group (Liu et al., 2023). Comparative analyses showed 86 and 83 antithrombotic genes in Hirudo nipponia and H. tianjinensis, respectively, with broadly similar family compositions (Zhao et al., 2024), while Haemadipsa yanyuanensis carried 193 putative antithrombotic genes across 15 families, demonstrating that antithrombotic gene systems can expand very differently across leech lineages (Lin et al., 2025). Alongside gene-level surveys, targeted biochemical work in H. manillensis has continued to reveal previously uncharacterized molecules, including monomeric and oligomeric decorsin-like peptides and a novel elastase inhibitor, confirming that the expressed antithrombotic proteome of this species is not fully described even after genome-level annotation (Tolksdorf et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2021).

One dimension of antithrombotic diversity that has received little systematic attention is transcript-level isoform variation. Alternative splicing is a principal mechanism for generating protein diversity in eukaryotes, with more than 90% of multi-exon genes in vertebrates producing multiple transcript isoforms and comparable rates documented across diverse invertebrate lineages, including annelids (de la Fuente et al., 2025; Du et al., 2024). For secreted inhibitor proteins specifically, differences in exon composition can alter signal peptide structure, reactive-site loop architecture, and disulfide-bonding patterns, producing structurally and functionally distinct peptides from a single genomic locus (de la Fuente et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2023). Tick salivary transcriptomes have provided clear examples of this phenomenon, with alternative transcripts from Kunitz-type and Kazal-type inhibitor loci showing measurable differences in protease-binding specificity relative to the canonical form (Jmel et al., 2021). Equivalent documentation in leech antithrombotic genes is absent.

In the present study, we used four Hirudinaria manillensis RNA-seq libraries, THA-Hm1–THA-Hm4, to examine transcript-level antithrombotic diversity in this species. While the 72-gene H. manillensis antithrombotic framework and the 38 transcript-supported additional candidates define the gene-level landscape, the structural diversity of the transcripts themselves-exon count, transcript length, open reading frame length-has not been quantified. We therefore aimed to (i) define the expressed antithrombotic repertoire in these samples, (ii) confirm species identity independently, and (iii) identify loci that produce multiple structurally distinct transcript models with antithrombotic similarity.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, tissue source and processing

Hirudinaria manillensis specimens were collected from four provinces in northeastern Thailand: Nong Khai (17.9986°N, 103.09978°E), Bueng Kan (18.03167°N, 103.70833°E), Udon Thani (17.36056°N, 103.10556°E), and Nakhon Phanom (17.54611°N, 104.09137°E). Following collection, live specimens were held for a 7-day starvation period to clear their digestive tracts of host blood. Whole-bodied specimens (intact animals including all organs and tissue compartments, with the gut emptied of ingested blood) were then flash-frozen and stored under conditions appropriate for downstream nucleic acid extraction. Genomic DNA and total RNA were isolated from the same whole-body tissue mass of each individual using standard phenol-chloroform extraction or column-based purification. Because antithrombotic transcripts are well documented in salivary-gland tissue, all recovered transcripts-regardless of their putative tissue of origin-were retained for the antithrombotic-similarity screen described below; this design provides a comprehensive, organism-wide view of the expressed antithrombotic-like repertoire rather than a salivary-gland-restricted catalog. For de novo genome assembly, paired-end libraries (150 bp reads) were prepared and sequenced on the Illumina platform, complemented with ultra-long reads generated on the Oxford Nanopore Technologies platform. In parallel, strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform to obtain transcriptomic profiles.

Read quality control and adapter trimming

Raw paired-end RNA-seq reads were assessed for quality using FastQC v0.11.9 (Andrews, 2010), and per-sample reports were consolidated with MultiQC v1.14 (Ewels et al., 2016). Adapter sequences were removed and low-quality bases trimmed with fastp v0.23.2 in paired-end mode, applying a minimum Phred base quality of 20, a maximum unqualified base ratio of 30%, a minimum read length of 50 bp, and a maximum of five ambiguous bases per read. Post-trimming quality was verified by a second round of FastQC and MultiQC (Chen et al., 2018).

Species confirmation by COI barcoding and phylogenetic analysis

Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) consensus sequences were reconstructed from whole-genome sequencing reads for each sample. Trimmed reads were mapped to the H. manillensis COI reference sequence (GenBank: MN882685.1) using minimap2 v2.24 in short-read mode (Li, 2021), and alignments were coordinate-sorted and indexed with SAMtools v1.17 (Danecek et al., 2021). Per-sample consensus sequences were generated by variant calling with BCFtools v1.17 mpileup followed by BCFtools call, applying minimum base and mapping quality thresholds of 20 and 30, respectively, and the consensus was applied to the reference with BCFtools consensus (Danecek et al., 2021). Each consensus was queried against the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTn from BLAST + v2.14, retaining a maximum of ten target sequences (Camacho et al., 2009).

Genome alignment and gene-level quantification

The reference H. manillensis genome, gene annotation, and curated coding sequence sets used throughout this study were obtained from Liu et al. via Figshare (article 24187377) (Liu et al., 2023). Liu et al. reported a de novo assembly of the H. manillensis genome using a hybrid strategy that combined Illumina paired-end short reads (150 bp) with Oxford Nanopore Technologies ultra-long reads, generating a chromosome-level assembly with contig N50 of approximately 1.7 Mb and a total span of roughly 217 Mb across 11 leech chromosomes. The assembled genome was annotated using a combination of ab initio gene prediction, homology-based evidence (proteins from Hirudo medicinalis and other leech relatives), and RNA-seq-transcript support, yielding an annotation of more than 19,000 protein-coding gene models. The 72 antithrombotic gene set curated by Liu et al. and used as the reference catalog in the present work was drawn from this annotation. In the present study, the GFF annotation was converted to GTF format using GffRead v0.12.7. Trimmed paired-end reads for each of the four RNA-seq libraries (THA-Hm1 through THA-Hm4) were aligned to the indexed genome using HISAT2 v2.2.1 with the --dta flag to produce alignments suitable for downstream transcript assembly. Output SAM streams were piped to SAMtools v1.17 for coordinate sorting and indexing (Danecek et al., 2021).

Gene-level fragment counts were derived from coordinate-sorted BAM files using featureCounts v2.0.3 from the Subread package (Liao et al., 2014) in paired-end, fragment-counting mode with read-pair consistency and chimeric-pair exclusion enforced. All four libraries were quantified simultaneously against the reference GTF to generate a single count matrix.

Principal component analysis

The raw count matrix was filtered in R v4.3 (R Core Team, 2024) to retain genes with a cumulative count exceeding 10 across all four libraries. Retained counts were log2-transformed after addition of a pseudocount of one. Principal component analysis was performed using the prcomp function and the resulting ordination was visualised with ggplot2 v3.4.4 (Ginestet, 2011).

Antithrombotic gene expression profiling

The 72-gene antithrombotic reference catalog and corresponding curated coding sequences were taken from Liu et al. (2023). For the present family-level summary, related entries from the published catalog were organized into 18 reporting families: haemadin/hirudin, hirustasin, antistasin, lefaxin, therostasin, eglin, bdellin, LDTI, LCI, saratin, apyrase, lumbrokinase, destabilase, GGT, hyaluronidase, progranulin, HMEI, and other (Liu et al., 2023). Fragment counts from the featureCounts output were cross-referenced against the 72 annotated gene identifiers. Expression values were log2-transformed and per-gene z-scored, and the resulting matrix was visualised as a heatmap using pheatmap v1.0.12 in R (Kolde, 2019).

Transcript assembly and antithrombotic candidate discovery

Transcript models were assembled independently for each RNA-seq BAM file using StringTie v2.2.1 guided by the reference annotation (Kovaka et al., 2019). The four per-sample assemblies were merged into a unified transcript set with StringTie --merge. The merged assembly was compared against the reference annotation with GffCompare v0.12.6, which assigns a structural class code to each assembled transcript based on its relationship to annotated features (Pertea and Pertea, 2020). Transcript sequences were extracted with GffRead v0.12.7.

The extracted transcripts were searched against the curated antithrombotic coding sequence database using BLASTn from BLAST + v2.14 with an E-value threshold of 1 × 10−5 and a minimum nucleotide identity of 40% (Camacho et al., 2009). Transcripts returning a qualifying hit were retained as antithrombotic-like candidates. The majority of retained candidates carried class-code j, indicating at least one shared splice junction with a reference locus. Class-code o denoted exonic overlap without shared junctions and class-code x indicated overlap on the opposite strand. No class-code u transcript retained antithrombotic homology after filtering, indicating that transcript complexity was concentrated near established antithrombotic loci rather than in wholly novel genomic regions.

The use of BLASTn as the primary assignment criterion for antithrombotic-like status is consistent with prior leech transcriptome studies (Babenko et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024) and is supported by the fact that each well-characterized antithrombotic gene family in H. manillensis carries a family-specific sequence signature: hirudin-variant peptides carry the conserved N-terminal IT/SQ/EE motif followed by a C-terminal acidic tail; the antistasin family is defined by five internal disulfide-bonded domains of ∼30–35 aa each; hirustasin is recognized as a Kazal-type inhibitor with a reactive-site P1 residue; bdellin family members carry six conserved cysteines arranged in a Kazal-like disulfide pattern; HMEI family members contain the metalloendopeptidase-inhibitor signature and conserved downstream cysteines; saratin carries the platelet-binding RGD-like motif; and apyrase family members contain the conserved apyrase-domain ACT motif. These signatures are documented across Hirudo medicinalis, Hirudinaria manillensis, and related leeches (Manuvera et al., 2024; Tolksdorf et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2023). Because the BLASTn filter applied in this study uses a relatively permissive E-value threshold (1 × 10−5) combined with a 40% minimum nucleotide identity, candidates passing the filter are expected to retain family-assignable motifs in their nucleotide sequence. We therefore treat BLASTn hits as functional-similarity annotations rather than functional proofs, and the 38 additional records are reported throughout as antithrombotic-like candidates.

Isoform prioritization and open reading frame prediction

Antithrombotic-like candidate transcripts were grouped by their parent MSTRG locus identifier, and loci supporting two or more structurally distinct transcript models were ranked by isoform count. The six highest-ranked loci were selected for structural comparison. Exon counts were extracted from the merged GTF and transcript lengths were measured from the FASTA sequences. The longest open reading frame in each transcript was identified by scanning all six reading frames with a minimum length of 90 nucleotides (30 amino acids) using a custom Python script. Predicted protein sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.520 and inspected in Jalview v2.11 (Katoh and Standley, 2013; Procter et al., 2021). Structural summary plots were generated with ggplot2.

Results

Sample identification

All four RNA-seq libraries (THA-Hm1–THA-Hm4) were derived from specimens confirmed as Hirudinaria manillensis by cytochrome c oxidase subunit I barcoding using the pipeline and accession matching reported in our companion study; only libraries from confirmed specimens were carried forward into the isoform-level analyses below.

Inter-individual transcriptome variation

The four RNA-seq libraries analysed here represent one whole-body transcriptome per individual leech, collected from four geographically distinct provinces in northeastern Thailand. Because each library was derived from an independent biological individual rather than from a controlled treatment-versus-control design with biological replicates, the libraries are best described as independent biological samples rather than as true technical or biological replicates in the differential-expression sense. PCA of log2-transformed gene counts showed that PC1 (43.6%) and PC2 (29.8%) together captured 73.4% of the total variance. The four libraries occupied entirely distinct positions in this two-dimensional space: THA-Hm2 was displaced strongly along the positive PC1 axis; THA-Hm3 was displaced strongly upward along PC2; THA-Hm1 occupied the negative PC1, negative PC2 quadrant; and THA-Hm4 fell at intermediate coordinates in the lower-left region (Fig. 1). The reference antithrombotic catalog covers 72 genes and 18 functional families

Cross-referencing the feature Counts output against the 72-gene reference catalog confirmed that all 72 known antithrombotic genes were detectable in at least one library. When isoform-matched and overlap-matched records from the transcript discovery branch were added, the total number of transcript entries across these families reached 110 (Table 1; Fig. 2). HMEI was the largest family with 34 transcript entries, of which 18 were direct reference matches and 16 were isoform- or overlap-matched records. Hirustasin was the second most transcript-rich family with 19 entries (13 reference, 6 additional). Destabilase contributed six entries; haemadin/hirudin, apyrase, and hyaluronidase each reached five. Families such as LDTI, LCI, and therostasin were each represented by a single reference gene with no additional records. Seven sequences could not be assigned to any of the established antithrombotic families with confidence and were therefore grouped as 'other'. Antithrombotic gene expression varies across individuals

Library-level diagnostics

All four libraries passed the FastQC quality-control thresholds described in Section 2.2 (Phred ≥ 20, ≤5 ambiguous bases per read, ≥50 bp post-trim read length). Per-library HISAT2 mapping rates against the H. manillensis reference genome were uniformly high (all four libraries retained mapping percentages consistent with the species-identity confirmation results), and library sizes (post-trim read counts) were comparable across the four libraries within the typical two-to four-fold range seen across independent leech RNA-seq libraries. No library required exclusion for low quality or mapping failure. Per-gene fragment counts were therefore derived from libraries of comparable quality, and the expression differences reported in Section 3.4 are not driven by QC failures in any one sample.

Gene-level fragment counts for the 72 reference antithrombotic genes showed clear variability both across genes and across the four libraries (Fig. 3). Among the highest-expressed loci, lefaxin_Hman2 and lefaxin_Hman1 showed the strongest z-scored counts, followed by HMEI_Hman15 and HMEI_Hman06. Several HMEI paralogs displayed library-biased expression: HMEI_Hman13 and HMEI_Hman11 were most abundant in THA-Hm4, and HMEI_Hman15 peaked in THA-Hm3. hirudin_Hman1 also reached its highest relative expression in THA-Hm4. Progranulin_Hman was markedly more abundant in THA-Hm1 than in other libraries, and GGT_Hman showed elevated counts in THA-Hm2 specifically. The expression differences observed across libraries cannot be attributed to a single biological source with the available metadata. Contributing factors documented in leech and other blood-feeding invertebrate systems include feeding history (Jmel et al., 2021), who showed that anticoagulant transcripts in ixodid ticks peak immediately before or during feeding and decline during digestion), individual genetic background, age, sex and reproductive status, and gut-clearance timing. The present libraries were derived from wild-caught specimens with feeding history recorded only at the level of 'starved for 7 days pre-sampling' and with no data on age, sex, or reproductive status. The inter-sample expression differences reported in Fig. 3 should therefore be interpreted as descriptive observations of biological heterogeneity among independently sampled individuals rather than as evidence for any specific causal factor. In the absence of controlled feeding, age, and sex metadata, formal differential expression analysis is not appropriate.

Transcript discovery adds 38 antithrombotic-like candidate records

StringTie transcript reconstruction followed by GffCompare classification and BLASTn filtering recovered 38 additional antithrombotic-like transcript records beyond the 72 reference genes (Fig. 4). Class-code j transcripts, those sharing at least one splice junction with a known reference locus, accounted for 30 of the 38 records and represent the dominant mode of transcript complexity near antithrombotic loci. Class code o (exonic overlap without shared junctions) contributed five records and class code x (opposite-strand overlap) contributed three. No class-code u transcript retained antithrombotic homology after filtering, confirming that the discovery branch captured splicing complexity around established loci rather than wholly novel antithrombotic regions unrelated to the reference gene set. HMEI-related loci dominated the additional records with 16 entries, followed by hirustasin-related loci with six entries, and three entries each for bdellin and destabilase. When candidates were grouped by parent MSTRG locus and ranked by isoform count, six loci emerged as the highest-priority targets: MSTRG.4545, MSTRG.5713, MSTRG.3310, MSTRG.5709, MSTRG.5212, and MSTRG.5200 each carried three or four structurally distinct transcript models. Six candidate loci carry structurally distinct transcript models

Given that all isoforms reported here were assembled from short-read Illumina data, the structural models described below should be interpreted as candidates; in particular, alternative transcript boundaries at the 5′ and 3′ ends could be refined by full-length long-read sequencing. Structural comparison of the six prioritized loci revealed two distinct patterns of isoform variation (Fig. 5; Table 2). At MSTRG.3310 (hirustasin), four transcript models ranged in length from 5491 to 5997 bp but all shared an identical predicted longest ORF of 331 amino acids. Similarly, MSTRG.4545 produced four transcripts spanning 3711 to 7183 bp, all with the same predicted protein length of 71 amino acids. MSTRG.5212 (bdellin) had three transcripts of nearly identical length (4510 to 4533 bp) with a constant predicted protein of 121 amino acids. MSTRG.5200 ranged from 482 to 3703 bp with predicted proteins of 49 to 106 amino acids across two to four exons. MSTRG.5709 spanned 967 to 12,801 bp with predicted proteins of 98 to 201 amino acids and two to four exons. MSTRG.5713 showed the widest structural range: four isoforms spanning 1132 to 23,635 bp, with predicted proteins from 100 to 1159 amino acids across three to four exons. A predicted protein of 1159 amino acids is substantially larger than the canonical secreted HMEI inhibitor peptide, raising the possibility that the longest MSTRG.5713 isoforms encode multi-domain precursor proteins whose architecture differs from the compact inhibitor sequences typical of this family.

Discussion

Multi-transcript antithrombotic loci were concentrated rather than evenly distributed. Of the 38 transcript-supported candidates, six parent MSTRG loci each carried three or four distinct models, with HMEI- and hirustasin-associated signals dominating the discovery set (16 and 6 of the 38, respectively). This concentration suggests that the antithrombotic transcriptome of H. manillensis expands coding diversity at a small number of structurally plastic loci rather than uniformly across all families-a pattern consistent with tick salivary transcriptomes, where alternative transcripts are commonly seen at Kunitz and Kazal inhibitor loci. The coding impact of these isoforms is uneven. A comparable concentration of molecular diversity has been reported in blood-feeding ticks, where Kunitz/BPTI and other protease-inhibitor families have undergone lineage-specific expansion and contain both single-domain and multi-domain proteins (Dai et al., 2012; Karim et al., 2011).At MSTRG.3310 (hirustasin), MSTRG.4545 and MSTRG.5212 (bdellin), transcript-length variation sits outside the coding sequence and predicted protein size is conserved. In contrast, MSTRG.5200, MSTRG.5709 and MSTRG.5713 carry isoform variants that extend into the coding sequence, producing proteins of radically different size (49–106 aa, 98–201 aa, 100–1159 aa respectively). The MSTRG.5713 case is striking because its longest predicted ORF (1159 aa) is far larger than canonical mature HMEI peptides (typically <200 aa), raising the possibility of a multi-domain precursor architecture that remains speculative until mass-spectrometric detection, signal-peptide prediction and domain modelling are performed. The present analysis was designed to be complementary to the companion whole-genome and RNA-seq integration study. That study prioritized candidate antithrombotic loci by combining genome-level mapping, RNA-seq abundance and DNA-level variant burden. Here we instead prioritized loci by structural transcript diversity. The two approaches converge on HMEI-related and hirustasin-related loci as priorities, but they diverge at finer resolution: e.g. MSTRG.5713 emerges from this analysis as a candidate because of isoform architecture, not because of expression rank or variant count. Functional validation of the isoforms identified here (signal-peptide prediction, recombinant expression of the longest ORF, recombinant expression of the compact ORF, targeted proteomics of salivary extracts) is therefore the logical next step.

COI identity and the significance of identical consensus sequences

The most notable result from the COI analysis is that all four sample consensus sequences were identical to one another across the full 1266 bp alignment. This level of COI sequence uniformity within a set of four individuals is not unusual for samples collected from a captive or semi-captive commercial source, where founder effects and limited genetic diversity can reduce within-population variability below the detection threshold of a single-locus barcode. The 99.842% identity of the shared consensus to the NCBI reference accession MN882684 is entirely consistent with intraspecific COI variation in hirudinid leeches, which typically falls well below 2% (Saglam et al., 2016), and the phylogenetic placement of the study sequences within the H. manillensis reference clade, clearly distinct from Hirudo medicinalis, H. nipponia, Whitmania pigra, and Haemadipsa zeylanica, is unambiguous. This result matters because several commercially important leech species share geographic range and similar gross morphology, and COI misidentification has been documented in market-sourced collections (Saglam et al., 2016). Confirming species identity before interpreting antithrombotic gene catalogs is a genuine analytical requirement, not a formality.

The substantial separation of all four libraries in the PCA, despite their identical COI sequences, illustrates a point that is sometimes overlooked in salivary transcriptomics: molecular species identity and transcriptional individuality operate at different levels. Identical COI haplotypes among animals do not predict uniform antithrombotic gene expression. Babenko et al. (2020) documented comparable individual-level expression heterogeneity across Hirudo medicinalis libraries, and Liu et al. (2023) noted that salivary gene activity in H. manillensis varies substantially with the physiological state of the animal (Babenko et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Individual salivary transcriptomes can also differ substantially within the same tick species, with some expression patterns shared across individuals and others showing individual-specific behavior (Perner et al., 2018). In hematophagous invertebrates, feeding history is among the strongest determinants of salivary transcriptome composition, with many anticoagulant loci showing peak expression immediately before or during a blood meal and declining substantially during digestion (Jmel et al., 2021). Feeding period is an important biological variable in leech and tick salivary transcriptomics, because the abundance of transcripts associated with blood feeding can change during and after host attachment (Perner et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2024). Without controlled feeding records for these four animals, the biological source of the PCA separation cannot be specified, but the pattern is well documented across species and reinforces the importance of multi-individual sampling in leech salivary transcriptome studies.

A consequence of the present sampling design is that factors known to influence gene expression in leeches-including individual feeding history, age, sex, reproductive status, and seasonal or geographic variation in microclimate-were not controlled across the four individuals, and population-of-origin differences (four distinct Thai provinces) may therefore contribute to the observed variance. Babenko et al. reported comparable individual-level expression heterogeneity in Hirudo medicinalis libraries, and similar patterns have been documented in tick salivary transcriptomes (Babenko et al., 2020). The substantial separation of all four libraries in the PCA, against their identical COI haplotypes, indicates that transcriptional individuality in antithrombotic gene expression operates well above the resolution of a single mitochondrial barcode locus and should be expected in any population-resolved leech transcriptome survey. Comparative analyses of medicinal-leech anticoagulants have reported broad conservation across anticoagulant families, together with localized evidence of positive selection at particular sites (Kvist et al., 2013).

Family composition and its comparison with published leech genomes

The recovery of all 72 reference genes is consistent with the catalog of Liu et al. (2023), confirming that the analysis pipeline captured the established antithrombotic backbone of this species (Liu et al., 2023). For clarity of presentation, related categories from the published catalog were consolidated into 18 reporting families in the present analysis, without altering the underlying 72-gene reference catalog described by Liu et al. (2023). The HMEI family produced the most transcript entries in the present dataset, with 18 reference genes extending to 34 total entries when additional records were included. Zhao et al. (2024) also reported extensive antithrombotic gene diversity in Asian medicinal leeches, while Lin et al. (2026) showed that Haemadipsa yanyuanensis has a larger antithrombotic gene set with major expansion in bdellin, LDTI, and LCI families (Zhao et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025). The fact that HMEI sequences dominate the transcript discovery branch in the present analysis, contributing 16 of 38 additional records, suggests that this family may generate more transcript variants per locus than other families in H. manillensis.

The hirustasin family, with 13 reference genes and six additional records, is the second most transcript-rich group in this dataset. Hirustasin was originally characterized from H. medicinalis as a Kazal-type inhibitor of plasma kallikrein and mast-cell tryptase (Sommerhoff et al., 1994), and detailed structural work has since established how it engages the kallikrein active site. The large number of hirustasin-like sequences in H. manillensis is consistent with lineage-specific gene family expansion, and the isoform-rich candidate locus MSTRG.3310 aligning best to hirustasin_like_Hman08 indicates that at least part of this expansion is accompanied by transcript-level diversification. The recent identification of decorsin-like molecules (Tolksdorf et al., 2025) and a novel elastase inhibitor (Xu et al., 2021) in H. manillensis further reinforces that transcriptome-level discovery continues to add molecules beyond what genome annotation captures.

A related consideration concerns the completeness of the reference antithrombotic gene set used to calibrate the BLASTn filter. The 72-gene reference catalog of Liu et al. was the most comprehensive published antithrombotic inventory for H. manillensis at the time of analysis design, but it predates several more recent reports. Most notably, Tolksdorf et al. reported five decorsin-encoding genes in H. manillensis (one monomeric and four oligomeric forms), and Xu et al. reported a novel elastase inhibitor from the same species (Tolksdorf et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2021). Decorsin is a platelet-aggregation inhibitor that inhibits the von Willebrand factor-GPIb interaction and therefore belongs squarely within the antithrombotic functional category. The extent to which any of the 38 antithrombotic-like candidates recovered here correspond to decorsin-family transcripts cannot be resolved without re-running the BLASTn pipeline against the Tolksdorf et al. decorsin reference. This reanalysis is deferred to follow-up work, but the omission should be acknowledged as a known scope limitation of the present candidate set. A complementary check, the nucleotide-level alignment of each of the 38 candidates to the published decorsin sequences, would determine whether any of the candidates is a decorsin-family member misidentified as a generic antithrombotic-like candidate. Pending this work, the 38 candidates should be considered a preliminary catalog bounded by the 72-gene reference used here.

Two patterns of isoform variation and their biological interpretation

The structural comparison of the six prioritized loci revealed two mechanistically distinct categories of isoform variation. At MSTRG.3310, MSTRG.4545, and MSTRG.5212, transcript length varied across isoforms while predicted protein length was constant. This pattern is most consistent with alternative splice-site selection or alternative polyadenylation events that modify UTR boundaries without entering the coding exons. Changes of this kind can influence transcript stability, mRNA localization, and translational efficiency independently of protein sequence (de la Fuente et al., 2025; Du et al., 2024). Although the protein product may be identical, isoforms with extended UTRs could be regulated differently at the post-transcriptional level and might therefore be produced in different quantities or at different times, with practical implications for which salivary proteins are available to the host tissue during a given feeding event (Spies et al., 2013).

At MSTRG.5200, MSTRG.5709, and MSTRG.5713, isoform variation extended into the coding sequence and produced transcript models with different predicted protein lengths (Kalsotra and Cooper, 2011). This category is functionally more significant because different coding isoforms could produce proteins with markedly different biochemical properties-the longest isoforms at MSTRG.5713 are predicted to encode proteins of 1159 amino acids, far larger than any canonical secreted HMEI peptide (<200 aa). If translated and secreted, these long isoforms could represent larger multi-domain precursor-like architectures, although this interpretation remains speculative until supported by mass-spectrometric detection, signal-peptide prediction, and domain-level modelling. Confirming this interpretation requires mass-spectrometric detection of the full-length protein in salivary extracts, signal peptide prediction for each isoform, and domain-level modeling of the long ORF.

The bdellin-family locus MSTRG.5212 is noteworthy for carrying the highest exon density in the dataset, with six to seven exons per transcript model, while maintaining nearly identical transcript length and identical predicted protein size across all three isoforms. Bdellin-related and Kazal-type leech inhibitors are associated with serine protease inhibition, and a Kazal-type trypsin inhibitor has been purified and characterized from H. manillensis (Lai et al., 2016). The conservation of protein length across isoforms with different exon counts implies that any alternatively included exons are length-equivalent, or that compensatory splicing preserves the reading frame and maintains a protein of 121 amino acids regardless of which exon combination is present. Such precision is consistent with strong selection pressure on Kazal inhibitors to preserve the reactive-site loop geometry, where reading-frame disruptions would eliminate inhibitory activity (Jmel et al., 2021). If MSTRG.5212 isoforms do produce functionally distinct variants, the differences would have to lie in sequences flanking the reactive site, potentially altering affinity for different serine protease sub-targets.

Strengths, limitations, and future work

This study has several practical strengths. COI barcoding with full phylogenetic placement was applied before interpreting any transcript data. Transcript reconstruction and isoform prioritization were carried out with standard tools and explicit command-line parameters, making the workflow fully reproducible. The isoform prioritization criterion, isoform count per MSTRG locus combined with BLASTn homology support, provides an objective basis for selecting candidates for downstream work. The use of all four libraries, despite their COI identity, captures a broader range of expressed antithrombotic diversity than any single library would.

A structural limitation of the present analysis is that transcript reconstruction relied entirely on short-read Illumina RNA-seq data. Short-read-guided assembly with HISAT2 and StringTie can produce incomplete or chimeric transcript models, particularly at loci with high isoform density or at incompletely assembled genomic regions (Kovaka et al., 2019; Pertea and Pertea, 2020). Several pipeline-level safeguards partially mitigate this concern: StringTie was run in reference-guided mode with the --dta flag, which biases assembly toward transcript models compatible with the known splice junction structure of the reference; the GffCompare step assigned each candidate to a structural class code (j, o, x, u), and only transcripts sharing splice junctions with or overlapping an annotated reference locus were retained as antithrombotic-like candidates; and the six prioritized loci all mapped to the same MSTRG identifier across multiple independent assemblies, reducing the likelihood that they represent single-sample assembly artefacts. Nevertheless, the conclusions of this study should be read as 'candidate loci for downstream validation' rather than as confirmed isoforms. Independent confirmation by full-length long-read cDNA sequencing, RT-PCR amplification across diagnostic splice junctions, and mass-spectrometric detection of isoform-specific peptides would convert the present candidate catalog into an experimentally validated inventory (Pardo-Palacios et al., 2024).

Another scope limitation is the reference gene set used to define antithrombotic-like status. The present pipeline uses the 72-gene antithrombotic catalog of Liu et al. and does not include antithrombotic gene families reported after that publication. Most relevant is the decorsin family, described by Tolksdorf et al. (2025), which encodes platelet-aggregation inhibitors and therefore belongs to the antithrombotic category. The elastase-inhibitor family reported by Xu et al. is a similar case. Our BLASTn filtering protocol could therefore have missed antithrombotic-like transcripts belonging to these post-2023 families. Re-running the pipeline with the Tolksdorf et al. and Xu et al. sequences added to the reference catalog is the logical corrective step; the present candidate set should be interpreted as a 72-gene-bounded inventory.

A further interpretive limitation is the possibility of intraspecific geographic variation. H. manillensis is a broadly distributed Asian medicinal leech with documented genetic variation across its range, and the 38 additional antithrombotic-like candidates identified here could in principle represent (i) genuine, previously unannotated transcripts that expand the antithrombotic repertoire, or (ii) allelic variants at known loci that differ from the Chinese reference individual. Three observations, however, argue in favor of the genuine-candidate interpretation for the six prioritized loci: (a) all six loci share splice-junction structures with reference antithrombotic loci (class code j), not generic allelic substitutions; (b) the isoform architectures involve differences in exon inclusion (MSTRG.5713: 3 vs 4 exons), which is more readily explained by alternative splicing than by population-level polymorphism; and (c) class code o and x records, which would be the expected signature of simple allelic differences, account for only 8 of 38 candidates. The geographic-variation caveat should nonetheless be retained when interpreting the candidates, and the same six loci should be re-examined in additional Thai populations and in the Chinese reference individual to determine whether the isoform patterns are population-invariant. We provide the nucleotide sequences of all 38 candidates as supplementary files to enable this re-examination.

The main limitation is sample size. Four libraries without experimental groups prevent formal differential splicing analysis, event-level percent-spliced-in (PSI) calculations, or inferential expression comparisons. However, this sample size is appropriate for the present descriptive aim of cataloguing expressed antithrombotic-like diversity and identifying candidate loci for downstream validation, and each library originates from a distinct Thai province, giving population-level geographic coverage of the species' range in northeastern Thailand.

Without proteomics data, the predicted ORFs remain candidates rather than confirmed proteins (Jeong et al., 2018; Ning and Nesvizhskii, 2010). Some MSTRG loci could represent collapsed tandem paralogs in under-assembled genomic regions rather than genuine alternative transcripts of a single locus; a chromosome-level assembly would resolve this. Future work should expand the library count to at least eight to twelve individuals to allow event-level splicing analysis with tools such as rMATS or SUPPA2 (Shen et al., 2014; Trincado et al., 2018). Combining that expanded dataset with tissue-resolved proteomics of dissected salivary glands, Pfam domain annotation of candidate proteins, and recombinant expression of selected isoform pairs would convert the prioritized candidate list into experimentally validated functional variants. Among the six loci characterized here, MSTRG.5713, MSTRG.5709, and MSTRG.3310 are the most informative starting points for those experiments, given their combination of large coding-length variation, high exon density, and strong homology to functionally characterized antithrombotic gene families.

A second design feature of this study is its tissue scope. The four RNA-seq libraries were prepared from whole-body tissue rather than from micro-dissected salivary glands. This choice has two consequences. First, it provides an unbiased, organism-wide survey of the expressed antithrombotic-like repertoire in H. manillensis, including any antithrombotic transcripts that may be produced outside the salivary-gland compartment. Second, it does not resolve the per-tissue distribution of isoform expression; for example, the relative contribution of salivary gland versus other tissues to the isoforms recovered here at MSTRG.5713, MSTRG.5709, and MSTRG.3310 cannot be determined from the present data. Future work that combines whole-body RNA-seq with parallel salivary-gland dissection (using histological or marker-gene verification of the dissected tissue) would directly address this question.

Conclusions

Four H. manillensis salivary gland RNA-seq libraries were confirmed as Hirudinaria manillensis by COI barcoding and phylogenetic placement. All four samples shared an identical COI consensus sequence that matched GenBank accession MN882684 at 99.842% identity, and phylogenetic placement separated them clearly from other leech species in the dataset. Reference-guided StringTie assemblies recovered all 72 known antithrombotic loci and identified 38 additional transcript-supported candidates carrying class codes j, o or x; isoform-level analysis below focuses on loci that produced ≥3 structurally distinct transcripts, bringing the total to 110 transcript entries. Expression profiling showed pronounced inter-individual variability across the reference catalog, with no gene displaying a uniform abundance profile across all four libraries. Six candidate loci each carried three or four structurally distinct transcript models: at three loci, isoforms differed in transcript length but shared identical predicted coding sequences, while at MSTRG.5200, MSTRG.5709, and MSTRG.5713, isoforms differed in both transcript length and predicted protein size. These assembled transcript models suggest coding-level diversification at selected antithrombotic loci in this species. These results establish a structural isoform catalog for H. manillensis antithrombotic genes and define a focused candidate set for downstream domain annotation, splice-junction visualisation and functional validation.
