Spatially harnessing oxygenase enables paired and ultraselective electrooxidative waste depolymerization
Authors: Huaqin Wang, Jie Li, Jason Chun-Ho Lam, Bing Song, Song Yang, Mingyue Ding, Hu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrooxidative C − C bond cleavage often encounters poor regioselectivity and low efficiency in conventional single-anode systems. Here, we deliberately customize a bipolar enzymic electrocatalyst HM@MM-MWCNT featuring medium-spin FeIII sites by covalently anchoring natural hemin (HM) to carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) cross-linked by melamine (MM) that further axially coordinates the single-atom Fe. HM@MM-MWCNT can trigger the complete oxidative upcycling of diverse lignin and plastic derivatives concurrently on two electrodes to exclusively afford organic acids with yields reaching >95% (cathode) and >92% (anode), double to quadruple that of state-of-the-art electrodes. The axial MM switches FeIII from a high-spin to medium-spin state, boosting directional Cβ − H activation activity of cathodic FeIII − O2•− and anodic FeIV = O species. Additionally, in-situ formed FeIII − OOH and FeIII − OH with weaker Fe−O bonding enabled by axial coordination can facilitate the dissociation of *OOH and *OH, respectively, for the subsequent coupling with the substrate Cβ• generated by dehydrogenation, eventually achieving paired and selective Cα − Cβ bond scission. Bipolar co-depolymerization of corn stover lignin furnishes aromatic monomers in a total yield, and its techno-economic analysis highlights low production costs. Spatially customizing enzymic electrodes with self-adaptive active species enables bipolar co-oxidation, doubling electrosynthesis efficiency for upgrading waste carbon sources.

Introduction

Carbon−carbon (C − C) bonds constitute the dominant inter-unit linkages in both natural and synthetic polymers, endowing materials with exceptional physicochemical robustness that underpins modern society yet fundamentally limits their decomposition and upcycling1,2. Achieving sustainable development goals thus requires transformative strategies for the valorization of the rapidly accumulating polymeric waste. Among them, lignin and plastics represent the two largest and most underutilized carbon resources, with annual global productions of approximately 50 and 430 million tonnes, respectively, of which only ~2% of lignin and ~20% of plastics are currently converted into value-added products1–3. Despite their distinct origins and structures, a unifying bottleneck in the efficient utilization and upcycling of both substrates is the selective cleavage of recalcitrant C − C bonds (with dissociation energy: 280 − 320 kJ mol−1) using economically viable and environmentally sustainable approaches4,5. Overcoming this challenge would unlock a common platform for the circular conversion of diverse polymeric waste into high-value chemicals.

State-of-the-art C − C bond cleavage strategies rely either on severe treatments such as thermochemical splitting and strong oxidation5,6, which often cause extensive decomposition and undesired side reactions, or on mild methods including photo-, electro-, and biocatalysis that generally suffer from low efficiency and remain far from practical application1,6–8. Among them, electrocatalytic oxidation powered by green electricity has emerged as an eco-friendly and promising approach for polymer decomposition and upcycling. To date, electrocatalytic C − C bond cleavage has mainly focused on indirect oxidative processes9,10, often occurring at the anode, in which a reactive species is first generated and then used for C − H activation to form corresponding radicals/cations for ultimately breaking C − C bonds (Fig. 1a)11–15. Such strategies are limited in oxidative capability due to the fixed redox nature of reactive species, such as tert-butyl radicals (tBuO•)11,12 and hydroxyl radicals (•OH)13–15, which exhibit indiscriminate attack toward multiple chemical bonds by lacking regioselectivity, typically resulting in low product selectivity (<85%). In another case, cathodic oxidation reactions are rarely fulfilled by electro-driven O2 reduction to form an oxidizing intermediate for relatively facile oxidation processes such as the conversion of alcohol to aldehyde16, or thioether to sulfoxide17. Nevertheless, for the oxidative cleavage of stubborn C − C bonds, highly oxidizing intermediates are inevitably demanded, thus engendering an inherent contradiction in thermodynamics and kinetics: i.e., selectively generating potent reactive oxygen species within the cathodic reduction reaction environment. Alternatively, in biological systems, redox enzymes have emerged as highly selective catalysts for C − H activation by generating highly versatile metal−oxo (M − O) species that facilitate the efficient extraction of targeted hydrogen atoms18–21. Their primary limitation, however, lies in critically low electron transfer rates, which severely restrict the enzyme-catalyzed reaction turnover/rate22–24. Under these circumstances, the design of an enzyme-electrochemical coupling system that integrates both regional selectivity of the enzyme and high electron transfer efficiency in the electrochemical process is envisaged to achieve efficient and fine-tuned cleavage of targeted C − C bonds within polymers via the electro-driven in situ formation of M − O species with site-specific C − H activation on the catalytic center of the enzyme.Fig. 1Electrooxidative C−C bond cleavage of lignin feedstocks using different protocols.a Conventional electrocatalysis: Anodic C−C bond scission of lignin model compounds. b This work: Bipolar C−C bond scission of lignin and various derivatives.

Inspired by the spatial structure and catalytic mechanism of the ferrous heme cofactor (the catalytically active site of lignin peroxidase)25, herein, a spatially customizing bipolar enzymic electrocatalyst HM@MM-MWCNT with medium-spin state FeIII sites is tailor-made by covalently anchoring hemin (HM) onto carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNT) via condensation with melamine (MM) axially coordinating the single-atom Fe center. Operando characterization and theoretical calculations reveal that HM@MM-MWCNT self-regulates the directional generation of active species FeIII − O2•− and FeIV = O by pairing with oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) at the cathode and anode, respectively. Moreover, the axial ligand MM introduced in HM@MM-MWCNT reduces the FeIII spin state, weakening its bonding interaction with oxygen species, which enhances the interaction between the substrate Cβ − H and the active species FeIII − O2•−/FeIV = O at both electrodes to generate Cβ• and FeIII − OOH/FeIII − OH intermediates, respectively, and further promotes the succedent coupling of Cβ• with dissociated *OOH/*OH, thereby notably targeting Cα − Cβ bond cleavage across a wide range of lignin/plastic derivatives with yields up to 95.3% and 92.5%, respectively (Fig. 1b), twice that of the state-of-the-art electrodes. Bipolar co-oxidation of native lignin achieves ultrahigh monomer yields of 18.9 wt.% at the anode and 19.6 wt.% at the cathode with a market-competitive production cost, highlighting its industrial potential for upcycling polymeric wastes. This bipolar co-oxidation strategy, enabled by spatially customizing an enzymic electrode with self-adaptive active species, doubles the electrolytic efficiency, which provides a viable reference for directional waste upgrading and relevant organic transformations involving C − C bond scission and functionalization.

Results

Electrocatalyst synthesis and structural characterization

The oxygenase electrocatalyst HM@MM-MWCNT was prepared by a layer-by-layer method using MM as a crosslinking agent to anchor HM onto MWCNT via amide covalent bonds formed by stepwise condensation (Fig. 2a). Driven by crystal field stabilization energy, further axial coordination between the N atom in MM and the central Fe atom in HM is achieved, precisely regulating the spin state of the catalytic Fe center. As a result, the hybridization strength between the metal frontier orbitals (dz2, dxy, and dyz) and the p-orbital of reactive oxygen species is optimized, thereby enhancing the ability of reactive oxygen species to activate the specific C − H bond of the substrate. For comparison, enzymic catalyst HM-MM-MWCNT without axial coordination of MM, and MM-MWCNT lacking enzyme active sites (HM) were synthesized under otherwise identical conditions. Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) determines the Fe content in HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT to be 1.22 and 1.15 wt.%, respectively, which are in conformity with the full spectrum analysis of X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), demonstrating that HM is successfully grafted and distributed uniformly onto the MWCNT support (Supplementary Table 1). The elemental mappings of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT disclose a uniform distribution of Fe, N and C, further confirming that HM molecules are evenly anchored onto the MWCNT surface (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 1). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) show that the functionalized HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT and MM-MWCNT retain the original tubular morphology of the MWCNT (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2), proving that the immobilization of HM onto MWCNT does not compromise the substrate configuration. Only the 0.338 nm lattice spacing matching the (002) plane of graphitic carbon from MWCNT is observed by high-resolution (HR)-TEM with no aggregated Fe species for both HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3), suggesting molecular dispersion of HM26. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT are similar to that of MWCNT, only showing characteristic diffraction peaks at 25.9° and 43.0°, associated with graphitic carbon (Fig. 2e)27. It is indicated that Fe species in both catalysts exist as single atoms, further verifying that HM is attached to the MWCNT as isolated molecules without aggregation, in line with the HR-TEM results.Fig. 2Electrocatalysts synthesis and characterization.a Schematic diagram of the synthesis process of HM@MM-MWCNT. b–d Elemental mapping (b), TEM (c), and HR-TEM (d) images of HM@MM-MWCNT. e XRD patterns of HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT, and MWCNT. f FT-IR spectra of HM, MM, HM@MM-MWCNT, and HM-MM-MWCNT. g N 1 s XPS spectra of HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT, and MM-MWCNT.

The interactions between MM and HM, as well as between MM and MWCNT, in HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT were examined by Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) and XPS. FT-IR spectroscopy clearly shows the intensity of the C = O stretching vibration peak at around 1701 cm−1, representing the carboxyl of MWCNT and HM, and N − H stretching vibration peak at 3350 − 3500 cm−1 linked with the amino group in MM is considerably diminished in HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT compared to HM and MM, respectively, suggesting that the carboxyl group in MWCNT or HM, and the amino group in MM are transformed (Fig. 2f)28. Concurrently, a distinct amide C = O stretching vibration signal at ca. 1653 cm−1 was detected from both HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT, signifying that the carboxyl group in MWCNT and HM, along with the amino group in MM, undergoes a condensation reaction to form an amide covalent bond. The N 1 s XPS spectra of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT display four typical energy level peaks at 398.5, 399.0, 399.6, and 400.8 eV, belonging to amino-N, Fe−N, graphitic-N, and amide-N species, respectively28, in which the existence of amide-N species further verifies the covalent amide bond formation via condensation between amino group of MM and the carboxyl in MWCNT or HM (Fig. 2g). The peak area proportion of amino-N to amide-N in HM@MM-MWCNT (1.9) is higher than that in HM-MM-MWCNT (0.6) (Supplementary Fig. 4), suggesting that the proportion of MM residues is higher in HM@MM-MWCNT and the excess MM is available for axial coordination with the central Fe of HM. The aforementioned results indicate that HM in HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT is successfully anchored to the MWCNT surface as isolated molecules via a condensation reaction using MM as a cross-linker, whose configuration is anticipated to enhance the exposure and electron transfer at the catalytic sites. Furthermore, the excess MM in HM@MM-MWCNT can form axial coordination with the central Fe atom, enabling fine-tuning of the Fe atom electron structure in comparison to HM-MM-MWCNT.

XPS and X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) were employed to probe the electronic configuration and coordination microenvironment of the electrocatalysts. The Fe 2p XPS spectra of HM, HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT unanimously illustrate the presence of Fe 2p1/2 (722.8‒723.2 eV), Fe 2p3/2 (709.6‒710.0 eV) and a pair of satellite peaks (731.0‒731.3 eV, and 717.9‒718.1 eV) without disclosing any characteristic band of zero-valent iron29, meaning that the valence state of Fe species in all electrocatalysts is a mixed valence of +2 and +3 (Fig. 3a). Furthermore, Fe 2p XPS discloses the binding energy for HM and the two catalysts as follows: HM < HM@MM-MWCNT < HM-MM-MWCNT, in which HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT show markedly higher binding energy than HM, implying electron transfer from HM to MWCNT due to physical π-π conjugation between HM and MWCNT. Notably, the Fe 2p binding energy of HM@MM-MWCNT shifts slightly towards lower energy compared to HM-MM-MWCNT, pointing to the presence of extra electron donors that elevate electron density at the Fe center. The survey and Cl 2p XPS spectra reveal the absence of Cl element in HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT, indicating that the Fe−Cl bonds of HM are prone to break during the anchoring of HM to the MWCNT surface via dichlorination reaction (Supplementary Fig. 5). Density functional theory (DFT) calculations show the N atom on the MM ring is more susceptible to axial coordination with the Fe center of HM than the amino-N, owing to its lower reaction energy (−2.67 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 6). Consequently, without excess MM, the prepared HM-MM-MWCNT retains the Fe−N4 conformation, whereas the Fe center of HM@MM-MWCNT tends to form a spatial structure with a coordination number exceeding four, arising from excess MM readily coordinating with the Fe center of HM during synthesis.Fig. 3Revealing the fine structure of electrons and atoms.a Fe 2p XPS spectra of HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT and HM. b–d Fe K-edge XANES (b), FT-EXAFS (c), and WT-EXAFS (d) spectra of HM@MM-MWCNT and standard samples. e–g EPR spectra (e), magnetic hysteresis loops (f), and temperature-dependent inverse susceptibility χ−1 (g) of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT. h Influence of axial coordination on Fe 3 d orbital electrons.

The absorption edge of HM@MM-MWCNT is situated between Fe3O4 and Fe2O3, and is nearer to Fe2O3, as shown by Fe K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) (Fig. 3b), indicating Fe oxidation state located between +2 and +3 and approaching +3, which is further confirmed by the Fe 2p XPS analysis showing a much larger integration area for FeIII compared to Fe2+ (Fig. 3a). The pre-edge peak of FePc at 7112 eV arises from the 1s-4pz electric dipole transition induced by ligand-to-metal charge transfer, which is recognized as the fingerprint of the square-planar Fe−N4 configuration (D4h). The fingerprint peak of the D4h configuration in HM@MM-MWCNT is notably weaker than that in FePc, suggesting a reduced local symmetry of the Fe center and a tendency towards a quasi-octahedral C4v structure (Fig. 3b)27. The Fourier transform k3-weighted extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) curve of HM@MM-MWCNT displays a singular band at 1.59 Å, near the Fe−N peak of FePc (Fig. 3c), which is associated with Fe−N coordination30, as also identified by N 1 s XPS (Fig. 2g). The absence of the characteristic peak of the Fe−Fe bond at around 2.18 Å in HM@MM-MWCNT rules out the existence of Fe metal clusters, suggesting that the Fe species is atomically dispersed and directly coordinated by N atoms (Fig. 3c). Compared with FePc, the increased height and rightward shift of the primary peak (Fe−N) in HM@MM-MWCNT can be ascribed to alterations in the coordination number of Fe−N bonds. EXAFS curve fitting discloses that the main and shoulder peaks at 1.59 and 2.52 Å originate from Fe−N and Fe−N − C scattering paths, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 7)31, with a Fe−N bond length of 2.05 Å and coordination number of 5, demonstrating that the Fe coordination conformation is Fe−N5 (Supplementary Table 2). In HM@MM-MWCNT, the Fe atom is coordinated by N atoms at atomic dispersion, as again evidenced by only the maximum peak owing to Fe−N coordination at 4.1 Å being detected from the wavelet transform (WT) contour plot (Fig. 3d). Overall, both HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT effectively immobilize HM molecules onto MWCNT via condensation reactions, forming molecular catalysts with Fe-centered coordination configurations of Fe−N4 and Fe−N5, respectively. Compared to HM-MM-MWCNT, the incorporation of axial coordination in HM@MM-MWCNT decreases the symmetry of the Fe center and furnishes electrons for fine-tuning its electronic structure.

A detailed analysis of the electronic structure and spin state of the Fe site modulated by axial MM coordination has been investigated by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM) and magnetic susceptibility. The peak intensity of the EPR spectrum for HM@MM-MWCNT is diminished compared to that of HM-MM-MWCNT, which indicates a relatively lower number of unpaired electrons (Fig. 3e)32, meaning that the spin state of the central Fe is lowered through the introduction of axial ligands. The magnetic hysteresis loops of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT show non-collinearity as measured by VSM, indicating that both materials possess ferromagnetic behavior, and further verifying a certain number of unpaired electrons within the Fe atomic orbitals of both materials (Fig. 3f)33. Additionally, HM@MM-MWCNT displays a lower saturation magnetization (Ms) compared to HM-MM-MWCNT, which again suggests that the axial coordination decreases the number of unpaired electrons, consequently pointing to a lower spin state, consistent with EPR results. Electrocatalytic performance

Initially, the anodic and cathodic cleavage of 2-phenoxy-1-phenylethanol (PPE), the most abundant lignin dimer via β-O-4 linkage (containing both Cα − Cβ and Cβ(aryl)−O bonds), was independently investigated under air and O2 atmosphere, respectively, in a two-chamber proton exchange membrane electrochemical cell (H-type cell) employing a three-electrode system, where the aqueous electrolytes were 1 mM PPE and 0.5 M Na2SO4. Anodic linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves for HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT, and MWCNT all exhibit higher current densities and lower onset potentials in electrolytes containing 1 mM PPE compared to those without PPE (Fig. 4a), suggesting that the electrochemical oxidation reaction is markedly enhanced after PPE addition12. Additionally, the current densities of the three electrocatalysts in the PPE electrolyte are as follows: MWCNT < HM-MM-MWCNT < HM@MM-MWCNT, suggesting that the kinetics of their catalytic oxidation to split PPE are progressively enhanced by anchoring HM molecules onto MWCNT and subsequently modifying the central Fe atoms of the immobilized HM molecules with axial ligands (MM). HM@MM-MWCNT was selected as a model to examine the impacts of electrolyte pH and catalyst loading on the oxidative cleavage of PPE, demonstrating that optimum reaction conditions are pH = 3 and a catalyst loading of 0.25 mg cm−2 (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10a, b). The performance of catalytic cleavage of PPE by HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT was determined at different applied voltages of 2.2‒2.6 VRHE (RHE: reversible hydrogen electrode) for 13 h, after which it was observed that the PPE conversion initially increases and then decreases with rising voltage, with both catalysts reaching optimum conversion at 2.4 VRHE (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 10c). Notably, no products of Cβ − O cleavage of PPE (e.g., acetophenone and α-hydroxyacetophenone) were found on HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT over the entire range of potentials investigated, indicating that PPE degrades to produce benzoic acid (BA) and benzaldehyde (BD) via Cα − Cβ bond cracking. HM@MM-MWCNT demonstrates optimal PPE conversion of 99.2% and BA selectivity/yield of 95.1%/94.3%, whereas HM-MM-MWCNT achieves only 46.6% PPE conversion with selectivity of 58.2% for BA and 36.7% for BD. The markedly higher PPE conversion and selectivity for the deeply oxidized product (BA) on HM@MM-MWCNT compared to HM-MM-MWCNT illustrate that the introduction of axial ligands substantially enhances both Cα − Cβ bond cleavage activity and selectivity. The electrochemical double layer capacitance (Cdl) of HM@MM-MWCNT is 0.815 mF cm−2 greater than the relevant values for HM-MM-MWCNT (0.507 mF cm−2) and MWCNT (0.032 mF cm−2), which is correlated with richer electrochemically active sites (Supplementary Fig. 11a–d)34. HM@MM-MWCNT presents the highest current density normalized (jECSA) by electrochemically active surface area (ECSA), suggesting a higher intrinsic activity to catalyze PPE cleavage (Supplementary Fig. 11e), further validating that the axial coordination improves the inherent activity of the Fe center rather than increases the quantity of catalytic sites. The Tafel slope of 455.33 mV dec−1 for HM@MM-MWCNT is significantly smaller than that of HM-MM-MWCNT (686.38 mV dec−1) and MWCNT (942.73 mV dec−1) (Supplementary Fig. 12), demonstrating its fastest catalytic rate of anodic Cα − Cβ bond cleavage from PPE35. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analysis displays that HM@MM-MWCNT has a smaller semicircle than HM-MM-MWCNT at the anode (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 3), corresponding to a lower charge transfer resistance (Rct) for this electrode, further revealing that the axially coordinated Fe center in HM@MM-MWCNT facilitates charge transfer, thereby demonstrating greater kinetic favor for PPEOR36. Further, HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT and MWCNT were used as cathode electrodes to assess their PPE cracking potential. Interestingly, cathodic LSV curves show that HM@MM-MWCNT, HM-MM-MWCNT and MWCNT in an O2-saturated electrolyte with 1 mM PPE all achieve greater current densities and more positive onset potentials than those without PPE (Supplementary Fig. 14), providing evidence for the reaction sites being favorable for O2 and PPE coexistence that preferentially occur over ORR17. The highest current density and lowest onset potential are exhibited by HM@MM-MWCNT, indicating the most expeditious electrochemical reaction rate between O2 and PPE on its surface. Cathodic cleavage of PPE over HM@MM-MWCNT is ideal at an electrolyte pH of 3 and a catalyst loading of 0.25 mg cm−2, which aligns well with the observations from the anode process (Supplementary Fig. 15a, b). The effectiveness of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT in cracking PPE was reviewed under applied voltages ranging from −0.2 to −0.7 VRHE, revealing that the PPE conversion rate displays a volcano-shaped curve with negative voltage variation, peaked at −0.4 VRHE (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 15c). PPE conversion of 99.7% and BA yield of 98.7% at −0.4 V for 10 h are achieved by HM@MM-MWCNT, significantly surpassing those of HM-MM-MWCNT (57.9% conversion and 40.7% yield), which ascertains that the promotion of the Cα − Cβ bond cleavage activity of HM@MM-MWCNT by axial coordination also holds truly for cathodic electrolysis. Compared to HM-MM-MWCNT and MWCNT, HM@MM-MWCNT as a cathode also displays higher Cdl and jECSA (Supplementary Fig. 16), smaller Tafel slope and Rct (Supplementary Fig. 17 and Supplementary Table 3), further corroborating that the Fe center with axial coordination tailored to the medium-spin state in HM@MM-MWCNT also exhibits superior intrinsic catalytic activity and better kinetic activity34–36.

Encouraged by the good dual-electrode oxidation performance and high stability of HM@MM-MWCNT (Supplementary Fig. 18), further exploration was conducted into the integrated (−)HM@MM-MWCNT||HM@MM-MWCNT(+) system for simultaneous bipolar PPEOR (Fig. 4d). Firstly, (−)HM@MM-MWCNT||HM@MM-MWCNT(+) displays faster reaction kinetics in the c-PPEOR//a-PPEOR system than in the OER//ORR system, as evidenced by the lower onset potential observed in its LSV curves (Fig. 4e)37. Subsequently, the catalytic efficiency of the c-PPEOR//a-PPEOR system were evaluated at various voltages (1.9‒2.4 VRHE), unveiling that PPE conversion at both electrodes is close to 100% at 2.1 VRHE, with BA selectivity of 96.5% and 94.9% at the cathode and anode, respectively (Fig. 4f). In the bipolar co-oxidation system, a higher BA yield at cathode was obtained than that at anode, in line with our finding that the cathode outperforms the anode in single-electrode oxidation systems (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 19). In addition, the current density of the c-PPEOR//a-PPEOR system constructed employing HM@MM-MWCNT remains stable across ten cycle tests (Supplementary Fig. 20), with no significant decrease observed in either the PPE conversion (cathode >96%, anode >94%) or the BA selectivity (cathode >94%, anode >93%) (Fig. 4g), confirming that both electrodes exhibit high stability within the coupled system. The evolution of structure and composition of HM@MM-MWCNT before and after the stability test of the two electrodes was analyzed by SEM, (HR)-TEM, XRD and XPS, respectively, and the results illustrate that the tubular structure and Fe 2p binding energy of the catalyst remain unchanged, with no agglomeration creating nanoparticles, demonstrating that both electrodes maintain high stability after electrolysis (Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22a−c). The UV-Vis spectra of the electrolyte after the reaction does not show the characteristic absorption peak attributed to HM at 390 nm28, indicating that the HM is not shed from the electrode surface during the electrolysis process (Supplementary Fig. 22d). Overall, HM@MM-MWCNT displays catalytic specificity towards the targeted scission of recalcitrant Cα − Cβ bond in PPE to yield BA at both cathode and anode. The bipolar co-oxidation strategy employs a single catalyst to upgrade the substrate into a unified product through the generation of adaptive species via an enzyme-electrochemical coupling system, with the advantages of doubled electrosynthetic efficiency and simplified purification procedures.

Unveiling the active species

To understand the reaction mechanisms of cathodic and anodic PPEOR, the key oxidative species of HM@MM-MWCNT catalyzed PPE cracking in two electrode chambers were first analyzed separately. After the poisoning of the single-atom Fe of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT with KSCN, both catalysts exhibit markedly reduced current densities in the cathodic and anodic LSV curves, accompanied by negligible activity for PPE cleavage (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 24), demonstrating that the Fe center of the HM molecule in the two catalysts functions as a pivotal reaction site during PPE cleavage at both electrodes38. The key active oxidizing species for cathodic and anodic reactions were identified by quenching experiments and EPR methods, respectively. At the cathode, the conversion of PPE and the selectivity of BA are little affected in the presence of furfuryl alcohol (FFA), isopropyl alcohol (IPA) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), which are able to quench 1O2, •OH and FeIV = O, respectively, indicating that 1O2, •OH and FeIV = O are not major oxidant species (Supplementary Fig. 25a)39. However, the oxidative conversion of PPE is significantly inhibited by the addition of p-benzoquinone (p-BQ), a scavenger of superoxide radicals (O2•−), indicating that O2•− generated from ORR at the cathodic HM@MM-MWCNT surface plays a central role in promoting PPE cleavage. Using 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrrolidine-N-oxide (DMPO) as a spin capture for monitoring active intermediate at the cathode by EPR15,40, the distinctive sixfold single peak should be identifiable as 5,5-dimethyl-2-pyrrolidone-N-oxyl (DMPOX) adducts produced by oxidation with O2•− (Fig. 6b). Upon introducing IPA and DMSO to the electrolyte for scavenging •OH and FeIV = O, respectively, the intensity of the characteristic sixfold peak relating to DMPOX in the EPR spectra remains unchanged, but vanishes with the addition of p-BQ, further confirming that •OH and FeIV = O are not involved in cathodic PPE cleavage, with O2•− being the primary contributor, which is consistent with quenching experiments. The aforementioned findings illustrate that FeIII − O2•− is the cathodic key active species driving the oxidative Cα − Cβ bond breaking of PPE. At the anode, quenching assays and EPR analysis were initially performed to identify the key active species responsible for promoting PPEOR. Quenching experiments reveal that severally introducing the •OH, O2•−, and 1O2 scavengers IPA, p-BQ, and FFA does not degrade the PPE conversion and product selectivity, thereby ruling out the involvement of these three species (Supplementary Fig. 25b)40. In contrast, FeIV = O is identified as the principal oxidizing active species by the strong inhibition of PPE cleavage in the presence of DMSO. DMPO is also employed as a spin trapping agent, and a strong seven-line EPR signal with relative intensities of 1:2:1:2:1:2:1 is traced to the adduct created when DMPO is oxidized by FeIV = O, •OH, or 1O2 species to produce DMPOX (Fig. 6c)40. The characteristic peak associated with DMPOX only disappears upon DMSO introduction, which scavenges FeIV = O, further confirming that the main active species responsible for DMPO oxidation is FeIV = O rather than 1O2 and •OH, in agreement with the quenching assay results. To analyze the selectivity of FeIV = O in the oxidative cleavage of PPE at the anode, methyl phenyl sulfoxide (PMSO) was taken as a probe molecule because FeIV = O can selectively oxidize PMSO to methyl phenyl sulfone (PMSO2) via oxygen atom transfer41. The highest FeIV = O selectivity of 99.6% is achieved by consuming 2.71 mM PMSO and forming 2.70 mM PMSO2 with HM@MM-MWCNT as the anode electrode in the absence of PPE, which is much superior to that of HM-MM-MWCNT, demonstrating that the axial ligand has an excellent improvement of the catalysts in terms of yield and selectivity of FeIV = O (Fig. 6d). This result suggests that the axial ligand MM of the single-atom Fe center in the HM@MM-MWCNT electrode tailors a medium-spin state FeIII site, accelerating the oxidation of adsorbed H2O molecules at the anode surface to generate FeIV = O, a necessary intermediate in the OER process. The role of FeIV = O as the main active species promoting the PPE cleavage at the anode can again be evidenced by the marked inhibition of both PMSO consumption and PMSO2 creation in the HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT catalytic systems after PPE addition.

In-situ Raman spectroscopy was utilized to achieve direct evidence of the dynamic evolution of HM@MM-MWCNT as the cathode and anode, respectively, during the degradation of PPE. In the cathodic chamber, without PPE, an absorption peak is detected at 1161 cm−1 at −0.1 to −0.7 V, corresponding to O − O stretching vibrations of the FeIII − O2•− species generated by the ORR process (Supplementary Fig. 26a)42. As the voltage rises, the intensity of the O − O stretching vibrations for the FeIII − O2•− species (1161 cm−1) becomes volcano-shaped and reaches a maximum at −0.4 VRHE, indicating that FeIII − O2•− species accumulate and are difficult to convert at lower potentials of −0.1 to −0.4 VRHE, which can be created and then subsequently consumed at relatively higher potentials (−0.5 ~ −0.7 VRHE). After adding PPE, the intensity of the O − O absorption band within the FeIII − O2•− at 1161 cm−1 markedly diminished (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 26a), meaning that PPE effectively consumes the FeIII − O2•− species generated on the cathode surface, reaffirming its role as a reactive species promoting cathodic PPE cleavage, which is in line with both quenching experiments and EPR results (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 25a). To analyze the source of the O atoms in the detected FeIII − O2•− species, in-situ Raman experiments were conducted utilizing an 18O2-saturated electrolyte with PPE. On dosing with 18O2, the peak at 1161 cm−1 is redshifted towards 1093 cm−1, in perfect agreement with the 18O − 18O stretching of the FeIII − 18O2•− species based on the theoretically estimated values (1095 cm−1) (Fig. 6e and Supplementary Fig. 26b)42, further emphasizing that FeIII − O2•− is yielded by the electroreduction of O2 through the ORR process.

When HM@MM-MWCNT was utilized as an anode without PPE in the electrolyte, a peak appears at 848 cm−1 above 2.1 VRHE, denoting the Fe−O stretching of FeIV = O41, and a maximum peak intensity is attained at 2.4 VRHE with rising the applied potential (2.1‒2.7 VRHE) (Supplementary Fig. 26c), demonstrating that FeIV = O species produced by the OER process at the electrode surface initially aggregate at low potentials and will be rapidly consumed at potentials exceeding 2.4 VRHE. With the presence of PPE, FeIV = O species evolve at a higher potential (2.2 VRHE) and the strength of the peak decreases considerably (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 26c), implying that FeIV = O accumulation at the electrode surface is vital to triggering PPE fragmentation. When the H216O in the electrolyte is replaced by H218O, the characteristic FeIV = 18O peak is at about 819 cm−1, which is 29 cm−1 red-shifted from the FeIV = 16O peak (Fig. 6f and Supplementary Fig. 26d)42, supporting the fact that FeIV = O is selectively generated by water oxidation on the electrode surface. To summarize, the FeIII − O2•− species generated at the cathode via the ORR and the FeIV = O species formed at the anode through the OER function as the respective oxidation species at each electrode, which are captured by the adsorbed PPE molecule, triggering the directed cleavage reaction to produce BA (Fig. 6g).

Investigation of PPE conversion paths

To gain a deeper insight into the specific transformation pathways of PPE at the HM@MM-MWCNT cathode and anode individually, control experiments were conducted. Since the PPE dimer can be cleaved by oxidative breaking of Cβ − O or Cα − Cβ bonds, the two bond cleavage modes were analyzed separately. A series of controlled experiments validate that PPE degradation at both cathode and anode preferentially occurs through Cα − Cβ bond rupture rather than Cβ − O bond cleavage, and such a Cα − Cβ bond cleavage does not follow the conventional route involving prior oxidation of Cα − OH to generate intermediate 4a (Supplementary Figs. 27−30)11,15. Given that C−H activation is an effective means of inducing Cα−Cβ bond cleavage, kinetic isotope effect (KIE) experiments were conducted with Cα−H-deuterated PPE and Cβ−H-deuterated PPE as electrolytic substrates, namely PPE-Cα−D and PPE-Cβ−D (Fig. 7a, Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). The results illustrate that the conversion of PPE-Cα−D at both electrodes is very close to that of PPE, revealing that the Cα−H hydrogen atom extraction is not a rate-limiting step in PPEOR11. Moreover, substitution of Cβ−H by D in PPE-Cβ−D results in significantly slower reaction kinetics, as evidenced by PPE-Cβ−D conversion at both the cathode and anode being only approximately 35% of the corresponding PPE conversion. In-situ FT-IR spectroscopy was implemented for the direct observation of potential intermediates during the PPEOR process catalyzed by HM@MM-MWCNT at each electrode (Figs. 7b and 7c). As reaction time rises, the peak intensities of the asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration peaks belonging to Ar−O−C in PPE at both the cathode and anode noticeably diminish, with the peaks located at 1278 and 1105 cm−1 on the cathode and the corresponding peaks situated at 1241 and 1062 cm−1 on the anode, denoting the rapid degradation of PPE at both electrodes15. Notably, compared to the anode, the absorption peak of the Ar−O−C in the cathodic PPE exhibits a degree of blue shift, indicating that adsorption of the PPE on the cathode enhances the Ar−O−C bond strength. At the cathode, PPE displays a stronger Ar−O−C bond and better cleavage performance, suggesting that PPE degradation is not through the Ar−O−C bond rupture but via Cα−Cβ bond scission, which is in line with the experimental findings (Supplementary Figs. 27−29). Additionally, no doublet peak for the C = O stretching vibration in intermediate 4a is observed at 1705–1675 cm−1 for either electrode46, which once again confirms that Cα−Cβ cleavage of PPE dimer is not accomplished by Cα−OH oxidation to form Cα = O, but rather through Cβ−H activation. Moreover, the broad absorption peak at the anode (3000‒3200 cm−1) (Fig. 7c), primarily arises from the O−H stretching vibration of H2O, implying a significant water adsorption on the anode surface, which coincides with the requirement for coupling of water oxidation process for anodic PPEOR (Fig. 6g). At the cathode, only a slight O−H stretching vibration peak of H2O appears after 40 min of reaction, which is explained by FeIII active sites being reduced to FeII, facilitating the adsorption of O2 molecules onto the electrode surface in place of H2O, thereby promoting ORR to produce the cathodic oxide species FeIII−O2•− (Figs. 7b and 6g). Theoretical insights into the electrocatalytic mechanism

To clarify the reasons for the remarkable oxidative Cα−Cβ bond cleavage activity of both the cathode and anode, and how axial modification of the Fe center in the catalyst further improves the oxidation performance of both electrodes, further assessments were conducted using DFT calculations. Two models, HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT, were constructed according to the characterization (Supplementary Fig. 33), and their electron density difference (EDD) curves and spin density distributions illustrate that the central Fe atom of HM@MM-MWCNT exhibits a higher charge density and a weaker spin polarization than that of HM-MM-MWCNT (Fig. 7d and Supplementary Fig. 34), indicating that the introduction of the axial ligand (MM) provides electrons to Fe atom prompting electron redistribution and a significant decrease in the spin state, which is consistent with the findings characterizing EPR and VSM (Fig. 3e–h). As a result, it is postulated that the medium-spin FeIII in HM@MM-MWCNT is crucial to its elevated PPE cleavage activity and selectivity in producing BA, surpassing HM-MM-MWCNT at both electrodes. To validate the hypothesis, the optimal configuration for PPE interactions with active species at the anode and cathode was first examined. In comparison to the α-H and hydroxyl-H in PPE, the β-H displays more negative adsorption energies on the FeIV = O and FeIII−O2•− active species produced in situ at the anode and cathode, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 35), showing that the Cβ−H bond in PPE is more readily cleaved at both electrodes to generate Cβ•, which is in line with the findings of deuterated substrate experiments (Fig. 7a). Subsequently, the Gibbs free energies for the OER and ORR, along with cathodic and anodic PPEOR, catalyzed by HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT, were calculated, respectively (Fig. 7e, f and Supplementary Figs. 36−38). At the anode, the RDS for OER on HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT is the identical process of FeIV = O → FeIII−OOH, with energy barriers of 0.70 and 0.88 eV, respectively, both of which exceed the corresponding energy barriers of 0.66 and 0.85 eV for FeIV = O as the oxidant species catalyzing PPEOR. This demonstrates that anodic PPEOR occurs preferentially over OER on HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT47. At the cathode, the RDS for the ORR of HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT is the acquisition of protons from water by FeIII−O2•− to form FeIII−OOH, with energy barriers of 0.84 and 1.26 eV, respectively, which are greater than the corresponding RDS energy barriers of 0.57 and 0.68 eV for Cβ−H activation with FeIII−O2•− to instigate the PPEOR. This signifies that at the cathode, both catalysts show a heightened propensity to catalyze the oxidation cracking of PPE using FeIII−O2•− as the oxidizing species, which is generated by the reduction of O2.

Further theoretical studies aim to elucidate how axial coordination-induced spin-state transitions at Fe sites influence the pivotal steps of the PPEOR process at both electrodes. The Gibbs free energy chart for the anodic PPEOR process on HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT indicates upward energy steps, with the hydrogen atom extraction from the Cβ−H bond in PPE by the FeIV = O species to form FeIII−OH and Cβ• having energy barriers of 0.85 and 0.66 eV, respectively (*PPE→INT1’), followed by the *OH rebound from FeIII−OH to couple with Cβ• with 0.59 and 0.34 eV energy barrier (INT1’→INT2’), respectively (Fig. 7f, and Supplementary Figs. 37 and 38). In short, the oxygen atom transfer process from FeIV = O to the PPE molecule is thermodynamically unfavorable, suggesting that the strength of the Fe−O bond in FeIV = O and FeIII−OH intermediates is crucial for enhancing the efficiency of oxygen transfer and thereby improving the kinetics of PPEOR. The partial density of states (PDOS) investigation of the FeIV = O intermediate formed on HM@MM-MWCNT and HM-MM-MWCNT reveals a smaller orbital overlap area between Fe 3 d and O 2p in the former, indicating a comparatively weaker Fe−O interaction (Supplementary Fig. 39). Meanwhile, more electron transfers have been documented between the in situ generated FeIV = O intermediate and PPE on HM@MM-MWCNT than that on HM-MM-MWCNT (Fig. 7g), confirming that the reduced Fe−O interaction within the FeIV = O species enhance its interaction with the Cβ−H bond of PPE molecule, thereby lowering the Cβ−H activation energy barrier, which is aligned with the Gibbs free energy results (Fig. 7f). Orbital hybridization analysis indicates that the bond order between *OH and medium-spin FeIII is 1.5, whereas that with high-spin FeIII is 2 (Supplementary Fig. 40a), which suggests that the Fe−O bond order of FeIII−OH intermediate formed by *OH on HM@MM-MWCNT is lower than that on HM-MM-MWCNT, reflecting weaker Fe−O interactions33. The PDOS of Fe 3 d and O 2p orbitals for the FeIII−OH intermediate on HM@MM-MWCNT exhibits a markedly diminished orbital overlap region compared to HM-MM-MWCNT (Supplementary Fig. 40b), which further demonstrates that *OH exhibits weaker adsorption at the medium-spin Fe sites of HM@MM-MWCNT, rendering it more readily desorbed and thus facilitating the *OH-rebound process, thereby driving the PPE oxidative cleavage. As a whole, axial coordination modulates the spin state of the Fe active site from high spin to medium spin, weakening Fe−O bond strength of FeIV = O and FeIII−OH intermediates, boosting oxygen atom transfer efficiency, thus significantly enhancing the anodic PPEOR kinetics.

At the cathode, the primary thermodynamic barrier for PPEOR on both catalysts is the oxygen transfer process, specifically involving FeIII−O2•− extracting Cβ−H from PPE to create FeIII−OOH and Cβ• (*PPE→INT1), followed by Fe−O bond cleavage and the *OOH-rebound process to generate hydroperoxide species (INT1→INT2), which involves the concurrent transfer of both oxygen atoms from FeIII−O2•− to the PPE molecule (Fig. 7f). The energy barriers for the two aforementioned steps over HM@MM-MWCNT are 0.57 and 0.12 eV, respectively, both lower than the corresponding values of 0.68 and 0.27 eV for HM-MM-MWCNT, revealing that the axial ligands introduced in HM@MM-MWCNT significantly enhance oxygen transfer efficiency, thereby helping to improve cathodic PPEOR activity. Compared to HM-MM-MWCNT, the HM@MM-MWCNT features a markedly lower orbital overlap zone between the Fe 3 d and O 2p orbitals of the active species FeIII−O2•− on its surface (Supplementary Fig. 41), while the electron transfer between FeIII−O2•− and the adsorbed PPE is more pronounced (Fig. 7h), which together points to the weaker Fe–O interaction of the FeIII−O2•− species evolved on HM@MM-MWCNT facilitating stronger interactions between terminal oxygen and Cβ−H bond, effectively reducing the activation energy barrier for cathodic Cβ−H activation. Moreover, the Fe−O bond order is reduced, and the orbital overlap area between Fe 3 d and O 2p is diminished for the FeIII−OOH species formed on HM@MM-MWCNT compared to HM-MM-MWCNT (Supplementary Fig. 42)33, showing that the Fe−O interactions are weaker for the FeIII−OOH intermediate on HM@MM-MWCNT, thus favoring the *OOH-rebound process. This finding is further corroborated by the conclusion that the *OOH-rebound step on HM@MM-MWCNT possesses a lower potential energy (0.12 eV) compared to that on HM-MM-MWCNT (0.27 eV) (Fig. 7f). In brief, the exceptional cathodic PPE oxidation cracking performance of HM@MM-MWCNT is primarily due to the highly efficient oxygen atom transfer of the medium-spin FeIII center. Ultimately, it is crucial to highlight that the electron transfer between PPE and the FeIII−O2•− species at the cathode is considerably higher than that between PPE and the FeIV = O species at the anode (Fig. 7g, h), suggesting that FeIII−O2•− exhibits a more powerful Cβ−H dehydrogenation capacity than FeIV = O, which elucidates the fundamental reason for the superior oxidative Cα−Cβ bond cleavage performance of the cathode over the anode.

Depolymerization of natural lignin

Further investigations were conducted to evaluate the potential of HM@MM-MWCNT for depolymerizing polymers, motivated by its highly effective Cα−Cβ bond scission in lignin model compounds and plastic derivatives. Bipolar co-oxidation decomposition of corn stover lignin was undertaken at 2.1 VRHE employing the (+)HM@MM-MWCNT||HM@MM-MWCNT(−) system (Supplementary Fig. 43), and the structural evolution of lignin before and after electrolysis was analyzed by two-dimensional heteronuclear single-quantum coherence nuclear magnetic resonance (2D HSQC NMR) spectroscopy. Compared to original lignin, the lignin after electrolysis at both electrodes displays significantly weakened or absent cross-peaks in the side-chain region associated with β-O-4 linkages, as well as in the aromatic region tied to syringyl (S), guaiacyl (G), p-hydroxyphenyl (H), p-hydroxybenzoate (PB), ferulate (FA), and p-coumarate (pCA) units (Fig. 8a, b)48. These findings illustrate that the breakup of β-O-4 linkages and dissociation of the individual units mentioned above mainly proceeds via cleaving Cα−Cβ bonds, as supported by the wide applicability of HM@MM-MWCNT in various lignin derivatives on both electrodes (Fig. 5a). Additionally, qualitative and quantitative analyses of the monomeric products obtained from lignin degradation were conducted employing gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and gas chromatography (GC), respectively (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). A techno-economic analysis (TEA) was conducted to assess the economic viability of lignin depolymerization, with a daily production target of 1 tonne of syringic acid (highest monomer yield) (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Tables 7 and 8)49. Notably, the total production cost of syringic acid is $1,217.29 tonne−1, yielding a substantial profit of $28,782.71 tonne−1, and other valuable by-products including vanillic acid (0.66 tonne day−1), p-hydroxybenzoic acid (0.38 tonne day−1), BA (0.17 tonne day−1) and 3,4,5-trimethoxybenzoic acid (0.15 tonne day−1), which can further elevate the daily profit to $46,836.58, demonstrating the excellent economic viability of lignin depolymerization for producing high-value products (Fig. 8e and Supplementary Figs. 46−48). Sensitivity analysis shows that, even with variations of ±20%, ±50%, and ±75% in key parameters, the production cost of syringic acid remains under $1,418 tonne−1, $1,720 tonne−1, and $2,727 tonne−1, respectively, and far below its average selling price of $30,000 tonne−1 (Supplementary Fig. 49 and Supplementary Table 9), further confirming that the developed lignin depolymerization strategy achieves a considerable and stable return for practical implications. Moreover, a breakdown of total costs reveals that the cost of lignin feedstock is the primary cost driver (82.23%) (Fig. 8e), and sensitivity analysis illustrates that total production costs are most sensitive to lignin price and syringic acid yield (Supplementary Fig. 49). These findings confirm that developing a more cost-effective lignin extraction process, whilst maintaining a high syringic acid yield, is the most effective strategy for further enhancing economic profitability.

Discussion

In summary, we have meticulously engineered the bipolar enzymic electrocatalyst HM@MM-MWCNT to possess a spatial and electronic conformation analogous to that of lignin peroxidase, attaining highly selective oxidative cleavage of C−C bonds in various lignin/plastic derivatives and even lignin at both the anode and cathode to produce corresponding organic acids. Key active species FeIII−O2•− and FeIV = O are generated in situ through paired ORR and OER processes on the cathodic and anodic HM@MM-MWCNT surface, respectively, both of which demonstrate the capacity for directional activation of Cβ−H bonds, thereby inducing specific Cα−Cβ bond cleavage. Axially ligand-modulated medium-spin FeIII site boosts the hydrogen extraction of Cβ−H at the cathode/anode to form Cβ• and FeIII−OOH/FeIII−OH, and further facilitates *OOH/*OH rebound from FeIII−OOH/FeIII−OH to couple with Cβ•, which significantly improves the oxidation performance of both electrodes. Bipolar co-depolymerization of corn stover lignin affords aromatic monomers with pronounced yields of 18.9 and 19.6 wt.% at anode and cathode, respectively, and a considerable economic return for syringic acid ($28,782.71 tonnesyringic acid−1), demonstrating that the dual-electrode can selectively cleave intractable Cα−Cβ bonds within polymeric wastes while offering economic viability. Notably, the dual-electrode co-oxidation of various model compounds in the coupled system can afford organic acids with ultrahigh yields of up to 95.3% (cathode) and 92.5% (anode), double that of the most advanced electrocatalysts. This study highlights the spatial customization of an enzymic electrode for bipolar co-oxidation enabled by self-adaptive active species, which lays the foundation for the sustainable synthesis of high-value chemicals from polymeric wastes, delivering considerable economic and ecological benefits while providing a reliable reference for establishing a low-carbon economy.

Methods

Catalyst preparation

Synthesis of HM@MM-MWCNT

MWCNT (40 mg) was dispersed in anhydrous ethanol (10 mL) under ultrasound for 1 h, followed by adding N-hydroxysulfosuccinimide sodium salt (Sulfo-NHS, 15 mg) and 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC, 15 mg), and stirring at 4 °C for 2 h to activate the carboxyl group of MWCNT. Then, 15 mg MM was added and continued stirring at 4 °C over 12 h for the MM and MWCNT condensation reaction. Subsequently, 10 mg of HM was dissolved in the aforementioned mixture and subjected to stirring at 4 °C for 12 h to facilitate the condensation reaction between MM and HM, whilst MM coordinated with the central Fe atom of HM. The dispersion was subjected to centrifugation and washed three times with DMSO and anhydrous ethanol, respectively. The solid collected was dried via vacuum at 50 °C and labeled HM@MM-MWCNT50.

Synthesis of HM-MM-MWCNT

The preparation of HM-MM-MWCNT is similar to that of HM@MM-MWCNT, except that the dispersion was centrifugally separated and washed with DMSO to remove excess MM before adding HM. The resulting solid was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (10 mL) and sonicated for 0.5 h, after which Sulfo-NHS (15 mg) and EDC (15 mg) were introduced, and the mixture was stirred at 4 °C for 2 h. The incorporation of 10 mg of HM into the reaction solution was followed by operations conforming to those employed for the synthesis of HM@MM-MWCNT.

Electrochemical measurements

Preparation of working electrodes

5 mg HM@MM-MWCNT or HM-MM-MWCNT was introduced into 1000 μL of a mixed solution of ethanol (950 μL) and Nafion (50 μL) to prepare a black homogeneous ink through sonication for 1 h. Then, 50 μL of liquid ink was deposited onto the carbon paper (1×1 cm2, TGP-H-090) to form a uniform film and employed as a working electrode after natural drying (catalyst loading: 0.25 mg cm−2).

Anodic oxidative C−C bond cleavage

All electrochemical tests were conducted in an H-type reactor at room temperature, utilizing a CHI 760 workstation, with the cathode and anode separated via 117 proton-exchange membranes. The electrolyte in both electrode chambers was a solution (30 mL) obtained by adjusting a 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution to pH = 3 using sulfuric acid (H2SO4). In the anodic chamber, catalyst-modified carbon paper and Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) were applied as the working and reference electrodes, respectively, with various lignin/plastics derivatives as the substrate. A platinum sheet (1 × 1 cm2) was inserted in the cathodic chamber as an auxiliary electrode. Constant-voltage electrolysis (2.2 to 2.6 VRHE) was conducted with magnetic stirring at 300 rpm and all potentials with no iR compensation. Cathodic oxidative C−C bond cleavage

Cathodic oxidation differs from anodic oxidation as follows: (i) In the cathodic chamber, catalyst-modified carbon paper and Ag/AgCl were respectively served as the cathode and reference electrodes, with various lignin/plastics derivatives as the substrate and 1 atm of O2 (O2 bubbles slowly escaping from the balloon). (ii) A Pt sheet was inserted in the anodic chamber as an auxiliary electrode; (iii) Constant-voltage electrolysis was executed under stirring at 300 rpm within a range of −0.2 to −0.7 VRHE.

Coupled electrocatalytic system for oxidative C−C bond cleavage

In the cathodic chamber, a catalyst-modified carbon paper as the cathode was inserted into the electrolyte containing PPE (1 mM) and 1 atm of O2 (O2 bubbles slowly escaping from the balloon). Catalyst-modified carbon paper and Ag/AgCl were deployed as anode and reference electrodes, respectively, with PPE serving as the substrate. Constant-potential electrolysis (1.9 to 2.4 VRHE) was performed with magnetic stirring at 300 rpm to trigger PPE bipolar co-oxidation.

Oxidative depolymerization of natural lignin by a coupled electrocatalytic system

Electrolytic depolymerization was carried out using corn stover lignin (4 mg mL−1) as the substrate, dissolved in a mixed solvent of 1,4-dioxane and H2O (pH = 3, adjusted with H2SO4) in a volume ratio of 3:7. The electrocatalytic depolymerization of lignin resembles the oxidative cleavage of varying fragment Cα−Cβ linkages using coupled electrocatalytic systems. Subsequent to the completion of the electrolytic reaction, the residue was extracted for three times with ethyl acetate, followed by drying with anhydrous magnesium sulfate, and then removing the solvent using a rotary evaporator at 35 °C.

LSV, EIS, and CV tests for each of the two electrodes

The LSV was recorded at 10 mV s−1, and EIS was evaluated across a frequency range between 100 kHz and 0.1 Hz. The CV was measured at various scan rates (40−100 mV s−1) within non-Faraday voltage intervals. The ECSA was computed from the CV based on the equation (ECSA = Cdl/Cs), where the Cs represented the specific capacitance of a smoother surface under identical conditions (the Cs for carbon was documented as 27.5 μF cm−2)51.

In-situ Raman measurements

The reactor, electrodes, and electrolyte employed in in-situ Raman spectroscopy are congruent with those utilized in electrochemical testing. The experiment was conducted with a confocal Raman microscope (inVia Qontor, Renishaw) fitted with a 50× objective. The laser wavelength of 532 nm was consistent throughout all experiments. The spectra were gathered within the 2000−200 cm−1 range, with the signals being enhanced by an Au film.

In-situ FT-IR measurements

Spectroelectrochemical studies were conducted in a three-electrode H-type reactor designed for in-situ FT-IR testing. The working electrode was fabricated by depositing an ink drop of HM@MM-MWCNT onto a gold film. Infrared signals were acquired using an INVENIO-R FT-IR (Bruker) spectrometer fitted with a mercury cadmium telluride detector at −0.4 VRHE (cathode) and 2.4 VRHE (anode), employing the same electrolyte and atmosphere as those in the corresponding electrochemical tests. All data are presented as absorbance (−log (R/R₀)).

1H−13C HSQC NMR of lignin

Lignin (40 mg) was dissolved in DMSO-d6 (600 μL) and analyzed by 2D HSQC NMR spectroscopy at 600 MHz on a Bruker instrument. Data were analyzed by MestReNova software. Chemical shifts (ppm) are provided relative to the solvent, with references for DMSO-d6 being 2.50 ppm and 39.52 ppm (1H NMR and 13C NMR).
