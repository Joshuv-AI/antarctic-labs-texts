The Impact of Aprepitant on Post‐Operative Nausea and Vomiting in Endoscopic Skull Base Surgery
Authors: Anuj U. Patel, Cynthia Sun, Hewitt Chang, Jared Clouse, Kiarash Shahlaie, E. Bradley Strong, Toby Steele
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivePost‐operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is common following endoscopic skull base surgery despite multimodal prophylaxis and carries a risk of post‐operative bleeding and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage. This study aims to investigate if pre‐operative administration of the neurokinin‐1 (NK‐1) antagonist, aprepitant, reduces PONV in patients undergoing endoscopic skull base surgery.MethodsA retrospective review of 58 patients undergoing endoscopic skull base surgery was performed. Demographic variables and clinical variables such as anesthesia type, procedural time, intraoperative CSF leak, and co‐administration of other antiemetics were collected. Primary outcome was PONV events, defined as the documentation of nausea and/or vomiting in the medical record or use of rescue antiemetic therapy. Post‐operative bleeding and CSF leaks were recorded. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted in R with the following covariates: co‐administered antiemetics, TIVA use, smoking status, ASA score, procedural duration, and intraoperative CSF leak.ResultsOf patients who received aprepitant, 42.9% developed PONV compared to 43.2% in the non‐aprepitant group. On univariate analysis, there was no difference in PONV (OR 0.98, p = 1.00), vomiting (OR 0.33, p = 0.30), or post‐operative bleeding (OR 0.87, p = 1.00) between groups. Multivariate regression showed no association between PONV and aprepitant (OR 0.52, p = 0.32). Subgroup analysis of patients undergoing transsphenoidal surgery showed no association of PONV and aprepitant (OR 0.45, p = 0.34).ConclusionPONV occurs in nearly half of patients undergoing endonasal skull base surgery. Pre‐operative administration of aprepitant was not associated with reduced risk of developing PONV.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

Despite widespread adoption of multimodal antiemetic prophylaxis, post‐operative nausea and vomiting (PONV) continues to affect a substantial proportion of patients undergoing endoscopic skull base surgery [1, 2, 3]. Apart from the distress PONV causes to patients, vomiting and retching have been demonstrated as significant risk factors for post‐operative bleeding in other surgical populations [4], and are known risk factors for cerebrospinal fluid leakage following neurosurgical procedures due to effects of Valsalva‐related straining [5]. As such, determining novel methods aimed at reducing PONV in the endoscopic skull base surgery population remains an active area of investigation.

Aprepitant is a neurokinin‐1 (NK‐1) antagonist commonly used for chemotherapy‐associated nausea and vomiting and has begun being used for antiemetic prophylaxis in patients undergoing general anesthesia with known risk factors for severe PONV [6, 7, 8]. Use of aprepitant has been shown to reduce PONV in patients of varying surgical populations including in plastic surgery [7, 9] and gynecologic surgery [8]. More recently, a retrospective cohort study analyzing patients undergoing transsphenoidal endoscopic pituitary surgery showed a reduction of PONV from 22% to 2% with use of aprepitant [2]. However, the effects of aprepitant administration on a broader skull base cohort have yet to be examined.

This study sought to evaluate the association between pre‐operative aprepitant administration and PONV in patients undergoing endoscopic skull base surgery across a range of surgical corridors. Based on prior studies, we hypothesized that the use of aprepitant would be associated with reduced PONV in this patient population.

Materials and Methods

This study received exempt status from the institutional review board. A retrospective review of 58 patients who underwent expanded endonasal approaches to the skull base from January 2024 to July 2025 was included.

Data Collection

Patient demographic factors, including age, sex, history of PONV, smoking status, and American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) score, were collected. Clinical variables collected were primary vs. revision surgery, anesthesia type, procedural time, corridor of skull base accessed, presence of intraoperative CSF leak, as well as administration of other antiemetic drugs such as ondansetron, scopolamine, and dexamethasone. All patients undergoing surgery had an orogastric tube placed at the conclusion of the procedure to remove any blood products from the esophagus and stomach. The primary outcome was the incidence of PONV, which was defined as having any one of the three variables: need for rescue antiemetic therapy in post‐anesthesia care unit (PACU) or during admission, documented nausea and/or vomiting in PACU or during admission, or documentation of PONV in post‐discharge telephone calls. Any episode of vomiting was also separately noted. Secondary outcomes examined were post‐operative CSF leak and post‐operative bleeding events, defined as bleeding requiring presentation to emergency department or return to operating room during admission to control.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R (Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient demographic factors, operative characteristics, anesthetic variables, and antiemetic use. Between‐group variability was examined using Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests for continuous variables and Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables.

Univariate analysis was performed with the composite PONV endpoint as the primary outcome and aprepitant use as the primary variable of interest. Contingency tables were constructed and statistical significance was evaluated using Fisher's exact test. Odds ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and p values were reported. Similar univariate analysis was performed for vomiting alone, post‐operative bleeding, and post‐operative CSF leak.

Multivariate regression was used to assess the association between aprepitant use and PONV. Adjusted covariates included co‐administered antiemetics (ondansetron, dexamethasone, and scopolamine), TIVA use, smoking status, ASA score, procedural duration, and presence of intraoperative CSF leak. Firth penalized logistic regression was used to examine the association of aprepitant and secondary outcome measures including vomiting and post‐operative bleeding due to low event rates. Due to the rarity of post‐operative CSF leak events, adjusted regression analysis for this outcome was not performed.

A subgroup analysis comparing those who underwent transsphenoidal surgery and received aprepitant to patients undergoing transsphenoidal surgery who did not receive aprepitant was performed. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed with the same adjusted covariates as above.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Fifty‐eight patients were included in this study. The majority were male (58.6%). The mean age of all patients was 55.6 and 21 total patients received aprepitant. There was no significant difference in age (p = 0.60), sex (p = 0.79), history of PONV (p = 0.55), ASA score (p = 0.18), procedural time (p = 0.70), TIVA use (p = 0.41), intraoperative CSF leaks (p = 1.00), surgical corridor (p > 0.05 for all), intraoperative ondansetron administration (p = 0.18), and pre‐operative scopolamine (p = 0.53) between aprepitant and non‐aprepitant groups. There was a significant difference between groups in the administration of pre‐operative dexamethasone where the non‐aprepitant group was more likely to receive dexamethasone (p = 0.04) (Table 1).

Univariate Analysis

Of the patients who received pre‐operative aprepitant, nine (42.9%) developed PONV compared to 16 (43.2%) in the non‐aprepitant group. There was no statistically significant difference between groups (OR 0.98, CI: 0.29–3.30, p = 1.00). Similarly, there was no difference in vomiting (OR 0.33, CI: 0.03–1.87, p = 0.30), post‐operative bleeding (OR 0.87, CI: 0.07–6.75, p = 1.00), and post‐operative CSF leaks (p = 0.53) between aprepitant and non‐aprepitant groups (Table 2).

Multivariate Analysis

Following adjustment for covariates in multivariate regression analysis, there was no difference in PONV between aprepitant and non‐aprepitant groups (Table 3). Pre‐operative administration of dexamethasone was associated with lower odds of PONV in this model. Transsphenoidal Subgroup Analysis

In analysis restricted to patients undergoing pituitary surgery, aprepitant use was not associated with a reduction in composite PONV on univariate analysis (47.1% vs. 56.0%, OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.17–2.85, p = 0.75). On multivariate analysis, aprepitant remained unassociated with reduced PONV (OR 0.45, CI 0.08–2.23, p = 0.34). In this model, dexamethasone again showed significant association with reduced PONV (OR 0.09, CI 0.01–0.80, p = 0.048).

Discussion

Post‐operative nausea and vomiting remains a common occurrence following general anesthesia, despite standard measures for multimodal anti‐emetic prophylaxis [10, 11]. In other surgical fields, prophylaxis against PONV is often for patient comfort; however, in the realm of endoscopic skull base surgery PONV can carry major consequences including post‐operative bleeding and CSF leakage due to Valsalva‐related forces associated with vomiting and retching. As such, effective measures to reduce PONV could have a significant impact on clinically meaningful outcomes including post‐operative bleeding and reconstruction failure rather than representing a purely quality‐of‐life intervention. Prior work on the use of aprepitant has shown significant benefit in reducing PONV in patients undergoing endoscopic transsphenoidal pituitary surgery [2]; however, being a single‐center study with a set anesthetic protocol, the generalizability of these results to other institutions and varying skull base corridors is unclear. Accordingly, we aimed to examine the effect of aprepitant on PONV in a broader skull base cohort.

In this study of patients undergoing endoscopic skull base surgeries of varying expanded endonasal approaches, pre‐operative aprepitant was not associated with a reduction in PONV. This finding was consistent across univariate and multivariate analyses, controlling for factors known to affect PONV as well as co‐administered antiemetics. Furthermore, post‐operative bleeding, vomiting alone, and post‐operative CSF leaks were similarly not associated with administration of preoperative aprepitant. Additionally, overall rates of PONV in this cohort were high, approaching 45%. Given that minimizing Valsalva is a fundamental perioperative objective following skull base surgery, these findings exemplify a clear need for more effective strategies for PONV prophylaxis.

Several important factors may explain the discrepancy in these results compared to prior work. At our institution, it is common for anesthesiologists to use multimodal PONV prophylaxis in endoscopic skull base surgery with use of pre‐operative dexamethasone and scopolamine, intra‐operative ondansetron, and avoidance of volatile anesthetic agents. While the goal of multivariate regression analysis is to adjust for the effect that these co‐administered agents have on PONV, any incremental benefit of aprepitant may be attenuated by co‐administration of other agents. As such, small changes in PONV may be difficult to detect in the setting of multimodal prophylaxis. Regarding our relatively high rate of overall PONV at 43%, several factors may explain this difference. Namely, our composite outcome definition was broad, which captured nausea, vomiting, rescue antiemetic use throughout the hospital stay, and post‐discharge documentation. The observed rate remains consistent with reported high‐risk surgical patients, in whom PONV can approach 80% depending on risk factors [12].

Notably, dexamethasone was consistently found to have a protective effect against PONV across multivariate analyses, which supports the validity of the regression models. Corticosteroids are known to have a protective effect against PONV and are widely used as part of multimodal prophylactic regimens for patients undergoing general anesthesia [13]. However, in the setting of endoscopic skull base surgery, peri‐operative corticosteroid administration was avoided in patients undergoing pituitary surgery to allow for postoperative evaluation of cortisol levels to screen for secondary adrenal insufficiency. This is a major variation to Lee et al.'s prior study where hydrocortisone was given to all patients in their cohort undergoing transsphenoidal pituitary surgery [2]. This difference in routine perioperative steroid exposure represents an important source of heterogeneity between cohorts and may partially explain differences in observed PONV outcomes across studies.

Furthermore, aprepitant is known to block brainstem mediated substance P signaling at NK‐1 receptors. Apart from addressing this centrally mediated mechanism of PONV, there are significant peripheral mechanisms of PONV in the context of endoscopic endonasal surgery, such as swallowed blood and pharyngeal irritation. As surgical technique, swallowed blood, and pharyngeal irritation could not be easily standardized or quantified across groups, peripheral mechanisms of PONV could have preferentially played a larger role in our patient population.

Notably, restriction of our analysis to transsphenoidal cases specifically also yielded results consistent with those of the overall cohort, whereby there was no association with PONV and aprepitant. This subgroup analysis strengthens the generalizability of our findings and suggests that the lack of observed benefit is not likely attributable to inclusion of other expanded endonasal approaches. Together, these results suggest that routine prophylactic use of aprepitant may not meaningfully reduce PONV in endoscopic skull base surgery when standard multimodal antiemetic strategies are employed.

Routine use of aprepitant may be limited by both its cost and clinically relevant drug interactions. Notably, aprepitant reduces the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives, necessitating preoperative counseling regarding backup contraception for up to 1 month following administration [14]. Together, these factors underscore the importance of carefully selecting patients who are most likely to benefit from aprepitant rather than its routine use for PONV prophylaxis in all skull base surgery patients.

There are several limitations to this study. Firstly, this is a retrospective study and, as such, is subject to the limitations of observational data and reliance on electronic medical record documentation for outcome assessment. Secondly, the sample size was modest, which limited the ability to perform matching analyses and may have limited the ability to detect smaller differences in PONV outcomes, particularly in subgroup analyses and for rare outcomes such as post‐operative CSF leak. Given the retrospective nature of this study and lack of established data defining a clinically meaningful reduction in PONV with aprepitant in endoscopic skull base surgery, an a priori power calculation could not be reliably performed. Lastly, aprepitant administration was largely provider dependent and not randomized. Nonetheless, our study is strengthened by several internally consistent univariate and multivariate analyses, including subgroup analysis for transsphenoidal surgery specifically to more directly compare to prior work. Future studies should consider a prospective design that can control for anesthetic and surgical protocol variations in order to isolate the effects of aprepitant on PONV and compare it to standard multimodal prophylaxis.

Conclusion

Post‐operative nausea and vomiting occur in nearly half of patients undergoing endoscopic endonasal skull base surgery. Pre‐operative administration of aprepitant was not associated with reduced risk of developing post‐operative nausea and vomiting in this broad endoscopic skull base surgery cohort. Similarly, use of aprepitant did not affect post‐operative bleeding or CSF leaks.
