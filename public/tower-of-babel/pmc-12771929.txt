Donor-dependent heterogeneity in therapeutic effects of adipose tissue extracellular vesicles

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtracellular vesicles (EVs) are promising therapeutics for diseases associated with inflammation and tissue remodeling. However, a major limitation for the clinical translation of EV therapeutics is heterogeneity, which is donor dependent. In this study we sought to assess the physiochemical characteristics and therapeutic efficacy of tissue-derived EVs from different donors. Donor-dependent therapeutic effects of cell culture and biofluid-derived EVs have previously been shown, but remains largely unknown for tissue-derived EVs. We obtained EV-enriched samples from various sources of adipose tissue and examined their effect in reducing inflammation in a highly translational model of myocarditis.ResultsWe demonstrate that the molecular composition of EVs varies depending on the donor and that therapeutic efficiency is donor-dependent even when controlling for age and sex.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that further research is needed to identify critical donor characteristics that predict therapeutic ability of individual or pooled adipose tissue-derived EVs to reduce inflammation and fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02563-8.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are cell-released biomolecular nanopackages critical for intercellular communication [1, 2]. EVs have promising potential to be leveraged as biocompatible therapeutics, as evidenced from preclinical and early clinical studies in a range of diseases [3–9]. However, a major limitation for the clinical translation of EV therapeutics is heterogeneity [10, 11]. EV heterogeneity in terms of physical and molecular characteristics arises at the organismal, cellular, and subcellular level. EVs derived from different cells have been widely assessed, demonstrating cell type and cell condition-dependent effects and characteristics [1, 12–19]. EV heterogeneity in terms of subcellular source (biogenesis pathways) is more difficult to assess, as exosomes (inward budding of intracellular vesicles) and microvesicles (outward budding of the cell membrane) overlap in characteristics, making it challenging to distinguish and separate these subpopulations [1, 20]. EV heterogeneity is further complicated by disparate isolation and storage procedures [20].

In addition to the abovementioned confounders, the first step in addressing EV heterogeneity is based on the organism (donor) level. Previous studies have addressed donor heterogeneity of therapeutic EVs derived from cell culture [21, 22]. Studies have also assessed donor-dependent physicochemical characteristic of EVs derived from biofluids [23–25], some revealing that donor age impacts therapeutic effects [24, 25]. However, donor-dependent effects remain unknown for therapeutic tissue-derived EVs. This article is the first to assess the impact of donors on the therapeutic effects of tissue-derived EVs. The therapeutic performance of EVs was assessed using samples from various sources of adipose tissue. Adipose tissue was selected as a source of therapeutic tissue-derived EVs due to demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects in several studies [26–29]. Adipose tissue is a major systemic source of EVs where they play an important role in inter-organ metabolic signaling and immune protection [29]. Adipose tissue is highly heterogenous depending on whether it is brown versus white adipose tissue, adipose tissue from lean versus obese individuals, and based on its location in the body, such as, subcutaneous versus visceral [30]. Thus, adipose-derived EV content is likely to vary depending on its tissue source as well as other factors such as the health of the individual and their sex and age. In this study we examined the effect of adipose-derived EVs from two primary sources, liposuction of abdominal fat and fat obtained during vascular surgery, on inflammation in the heart. An inflammatory myocarditis disease model was chosen because we previously found that EVs from pooled plasma samples from multiple donors had an anti-inflammatory effect on disease [9]. The molecular immune pathways that promote disease in myocarditis have been well characterized [9, 31–34], providing a framework for evaluating the anti-inflammatory efficacy of the EVs. This work demonstrates donor-dependent heterogeneity in the therapeutic effects of EVs, emphasizing the need for future research efforts on the selection of optimal donors.

Methods

Thorough methods are detailed in the Supplementary Materials and in previous work [9, 26, 35]. Briefly, residual adipose tissue was collected from patients who underwent a surgical procedure under approval from Mayo Clinic. Adipose tissue was either removed during liposuction as a soluble mixture, then termed ‘lipoaspirate’, or was removed during vasculature surgery as a solid piece of tissue. Permission for use of human samples from surgery was obtained from the Mayo Clinic institutional review board prior to all studies. The lipoaspirate tissue was collected from four non-obese women over the age of 50. The vascular tissue was collected from four women: one aged 77 with a BMI of 30.9, one aged 57 with a BMI of 25.7, one aged 42 with a BMI of 30.5 and one aged 47 with a BMI of 26.9. The adipose tissue from either source was micro processed and extracellular particles were isolated by tangential flow filtration (TFF), including diafiltration, to obtain adipose-derived EVs (AEV). TFF effectively isolates nanoparticles according to size in a gentle and efficient manner from other soluble components of different sizes. TFF process components and AEV products were assessed for EV enrichment using a multiplex panel for several EV markers and enzyme linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) for apoprotein (apo) co-isolating contaminants. A representative AEV sample was authenticated by transmission electron microscopy to assess morphology and Western blotting to confirm presence of ‘positive’ EV markers (CD9, CD63) and assess AEV product purity with ‘negative’ EV markers (calnexin, apoA-I). The sample was then visualized with direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (dSTORM), allowing super resolution detection and quantification at the single molecule level of three common tetraspanins, CD9, CD63, and CD81, which vary in positivity across individual EVs among the heterogenous mixture of AEVs from one donor. The biomolecular characteristics of AEVs from multiple donors were compared, including multi-marker analysis using the aforementioned multiplex panel and ELISAs. Additionally, physiochemical characteristics of these samples were compared in both size distribution by nanoparticle tracking analysis and tunable resistive pulse sensing, and charge by zeta potential analysis. AEVs from a lipoaspirate adipose source from four donors of a selected age and sex-controlled group were injected into mice during an early timepoint in a model of viral myocarditis. Mice were approved for use in this study by the institutional animal care and use committee of Mayo Clinic and were maintained according to best ethical animal use practices. Mouse hearts were assessed histologically after humane harvest during peak acute myocarditis, with hematoxylin and eosin staining for inflammatory infiltrate, as well as trichrome blue staining for collagen deposition/fibrosis. Gene expression of a robust panel of key markers of myocarditis was measured with quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and protein expression of the key innate immune receptor Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) was examined with immunohistochemistry staining to compare therapeutic efficacy between donors.

Results

Characterization of AEVs

AEVs from human adipose tissue were isolated by a two-step TFF process (Fig. 1a, b, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1), including diafiltration, which results in higher EV yields and purity compared to conventional isolation techniques, such as ultracentrifugation [36]. To best demonstrate relative enrichment of known EV markers throughout the TFF process, we analyzed a five-marker multiplex panel for each component of the TFF process for three batches from the same patient, including the final AEV product, as well as a reference control of EVs from the human colorectal cell line HCT116, acquired from ONI as part of an EV staining kit (Fig. 1c). The reference control was included to allow comparison of our product to others via relative comparison to this standardized control. We also performed ELISA for apoAI and apoB to assess the extent of lipoprotein contaminants, which overlap in size, shape, and density with EVs [37]. Most EV isolation methods are unable to completely remove lipoprotein and other contaminants [26, 38, 39], and the focus of the field has been in demonstrating enrichment of EV markers. It is also possible for lipoproteins to form complexes with EVs [40, 41]. Our final AEV products and the EV reference standard showed high enrichment for markers CD9, CD63, CD81, cytochrome C, and syntenin-1, relative to other isolates of the process, which consist of large nanoparticles >650 nanometers (nm) and soluble biomolecules including proteins, fatty acids, carbohydrates, and free nucleic acids (<750 kilodaltons/kDa) (Fig. 1c). TFF-based isolation of AEVs demonstrated consistent batch-to-batch enrichment of EVs. EVs were further authenticated by visualizing lipid membranes, which are a characteristic feature [42], and distinct electron dense contents (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 2). While spherical morphology could be visualized in some particles in the field, there was also significant presence of non-vesicular components, that could be lipoproteins, which have been confirmed in similar samples in prior work [26]. This visually confirmed the heterogenous composition inferred from Fig. 1c. Single-EV resolution was also performed by super resolution microscopy (i.e., dSTORM), which allowed visualization of both the membrane tetraspanin CD81 (yellow) and the intracellular protein heat shock chaperone 70 (HSC70, blue) (Fig. 1e).

A five-marker, multiplex panel and measurements of apolipoproteins showed high biomolecular heterogeneity of AEVs from different donors (includes data from lipoaspirate 1–4 and vascular donors 5–8), but not batch differences from the same donor; the same marker enrichment patterns were observed between batches but not between different donors (Fig. 2a). Western blotting visualized this heterogeneity in classical EV markers and contaminant markers, showing similar results (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 3). Zeta potential was consistently negative across different donor-derived AEVs (Fig. 2b), although some samples had high variance indicating some particles with possible positive charge. Size distributions did not significantly vary between batches (Fig. 2c) or when compared between donors, with mean particle sizes between 100 and 200 nm (Fig. 2d).

To reduce heterogeneity for further studies on the therapeutic effects of AEVs, a narrow subset of lipoaspirate donors was selected (females, similar age, lack of comorbidities) who had undergone the same type of adipose collection (subcutaneous from the flank or abdomen). The size profiles of these AEVs were very similar, with modal peaks between 100 and 150 nm, and some samples had secondary modal peaks around 200 nm (Fig. 2e). These samples were representative of the larger sample set with mean particle diameters between 100 and 200 nm, showing a large amount of the particles in each sample were small EVs (Fig. 2f).

Effect of lipoaspirate AEVs on myocarditis

Recently, it was shown that platelet EVs pooled from multiple donors administered early during infection in a highly clinically relevant mouse model of coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3)-induced myocarditis prevented myocardial inflammation and improved cardiac function [9]. Thus, for this study, the same treatment protocol was assessed with AEVs from healthy female donors of similar age and without comorbidities. Inflammation during acute myocarditis (day 10 post-viral injection) in male mice in this model ranges from around 40–60%, as previously shown [9, 43, 44]. In this study, a similar average myocarditis score was found across separate experiments (Fig. 3a-c). EVs from two lipoaspirate donors (AEV1 and AEV2) significantly reduced myocarditis (p = 0.0032 and p = 0.0013, respectively) compared to vehicle controls while EVs from two other lipoaspirate donors (AEV3 and AEV4) showed no significant anti-inflammatory effects (p = 0.92 and p = 0.11, respectively) (Fig. 3a). None of the AEV lipoaspirate treatments increased myocarditis or altered the pericarditis score relative to vehicle controls (p = 0.18, p = 0.094, p = 0.82, and p = 0.56, respectively) (Fig. 3d-f).

Effect of lipoaspirate AEVs on fibrosis

A portion of patients with acute myocarditis progress to dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) [45, 46], and our model uses a susceptible mouse strain that is prone to the development of DCM. Fibrosis starts during acute myocarditis and can be measured at the 10-day organ collection, although it is mainly perivascular rather than interstitial at this timepoint. Cardiac fibrosis using trichrome blue staining, once quantitated and analyzed, showed that samples had varying effects on fibrosis at this timepoint. Specifically, AEV1, the first anti-inflammatory sample that reduced acute myocarditis did not reduce fibrosis (p = 0.08) (Fig. 3g), although limited fibrosis could be visualized in this experiment at day 10 post-viral injection. AEV2, the second anti-inflammatory sample that reduced acute myocarditis, resulted in a significant reduction in fibrosis (p = 0.0025) (Fig. 3g, h). Although AEV3 did not demonstrate anti-inflammatory capacity (nor proinflammatory) in the acute myocarditis setting, it displayed a non-significant increase in fibrosis (p = 0.084) (Fig. 3g), while the AEV4 sample (neutral in the acute myocarditis setting) did not affect fibrosis (p = 0.48) (Fig. 3g, i). The above-mentioned heterogeneity in therapeutic effects highlights concerns for donor variability in developing effective EV therapeutics.

Effect of lipoaspirate AEVs on key gene pathways that promote myocarditis/DCM

Gene expression of major immune cells and dominant mechanistic immune pathways in myocarditis (for example, TLR4, inflammasome and complement) were analyzed by qRT-PCR to compare myocarditis after treatment with AEV versus vehicle controls. A broad global downregulation of all major pathways known to be important in mediating myocarditis was observed in response to effective AEV samples, while this was not the case for the ineffective/neutral AEV samples (Fig. 4a, b, Supplemental Figs. 4 and 5). To confirm that donor-dependent heterogenous effects were not due to differences in viral levels in the heart, viral protein 1, the primary antigen of CVB3, was measured as previously described [9, 43, 47]. There was no difference in viral levels in the hearts of mice treated with an effective AEV sample versus vehicle control (p = 0.13) (Fig. 4a, c, Supplemental Fig. 4) or a neutral AEV sample versus vehicle control (p = 0.66) (Fig. 4b, c, Supplemental Fig. 4). These results indicate that the inflammatory differences between AEV-treated mice and controls were not due to different amounts of virus and that the observed global immunoregulation by effective AEVs was not immunosuppressive, as viral levels were similar to control samples.

TLR4 plays an important role in myocarditis, as it is upregulated on innate immune cell macrophages and mast cells during innate infection and myocarditis in males, and activates the proinflammatory Nlrp3 inflammasome which also activates a profibrotic cascade [31, 32, 44, 48–50]. This results in more severe myocarditis in males in susceptible mouse strains, which also progress to DCM, a fibrosis-based functional disease [32–34, 44, 51]. TLR4 RNA levels were found to be significantly reduced in the hearts of mice treated with effective AEVs compared to vehicle controls (p ≤ 0.0001) (Fig. 4a, d, Supplemental Figs. 4 and 5). TLR4, which is directly responsible for production of IL-1β and IL-18 after engagement of the inflammasome is a potent proinflammatory signal in males with myocarditis [31, 32, 44, 49, 50]. TLR4 protein expression was also reduced (p = 0.03) by effective AEV treatments compared to controls (Fig. 4d).

Effect of vascular AEVs on myocarditis

We observed similar results when we examined EVs obtained from vascular fat. EVs from two vascular fat-derived EV samples (AEV5 and AEV6) significantly reduced myocardial inflammation (CD45, p = 0.007, p = 0.04, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b), while two other vascular fat-derived EV samples (AEV7 and AEV8) showed no significant anti-inflammatory effects compared to controls (CD45, p = 0.26, p = 0.22, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). Although overall we had similar findings for lipoaspirate and vascular fat AEVs suggesting consistency between AEV tissue sources, we also observed variability in AEV effects on specific immune markers (i.e., TLR4) by donor.

Discussion

Studies have assessed donor-dependent impacts of cell culture [21, 22] and biofluid-derived therapeutic EVs in disease models [24, 25]. However, in the case of tissue-derived EVs donor-dependent heterogeneity of EV therapeutics was previously unexplored. This work is the first to demonstrate heterogeneity in therapeutic tissue-derived EVs (AEVs) from different donors. Specifically, AEVs, which have previously shown anti-inflammatory [26–28] and anti-fibrotic [52] effects demonstrated donor-dependent variability in therapeutic effects in a mouse model of myocarditis when controlling for donor age and sex. Two of four donor EVs demonstrated anti-inflammatory effects, while one of four showed anti-fibrotic effects. Despite the healthy donors being the same sex (female) and of similar age (over 50), substantial differences in therapeutic effects were observed.

There are several possible explanations for the functional variance observed between the age and sex controlled AEV samples. Adipose tissue is highly endocrine in function [53], and while these donors were non-obese, they may have had significant differences in their hormone profile despite being at a post-menopausal age. As estrogen and testosterone are known to affect the pathogenesis of myocarditis in opposite directions [46, 51], even small differences in the ratio of these hormones by individual donors may alter their immunoregulatory profile. Another key concept for biogenic EV-based therapies is that EV content is significantly affected by the environment of the donor at the time of donation. For example, a low-grade, undetected infection or underlying condition in an apparently healthy donor could influence cellular messaging in EVs at the time of donation. This could decrease the anti-inflammatory capacity of AEV samples obtained under these environmental conditions and may even increase inflammation if a strong proinflammatory signal is present. Importantly, no samples tested in this study demonstrated an increase in myocarditis severity, but this is certainly a possibility.

This work emphasizes the importance of an essential first step in the generation of therapeutic EVs: selection of appropriate donors. While variability in therapeutic effects can be decreased by pooling EVs from multiple donors [9], this limits overall therapeutic capacity if a proportion of EVs lack therapeutic benefits, or possibly, counteracts the effects of beneficial EVs. Here, important evidence is provided for substantial donor heterogeneity in the therapeutic effects of EVs in vivo. Notably, screening therapeutic markers in different donor-derived EVs and evaluating therapeutic effects in cell culture models may fail to correlate with in vivo results, as other factors, such as differences in EV clearance and biodistribution remain unaccounted for. In the case of cell therapy and cell culture-derived EV therapeutics, various donor-screening approaches have been proposed to optimize therapeutic effects, some based on genetic biomarkers [54, 55], while others rely on biomolecular profiling and/or in vitro potency assays [56–59]. A limitation of this study is that the mechanistic hierarchy underlying the observed pathway changes remains unresolved, and the specific EV cargo or markers responsible for donor-dependent differences are not yet defined and represent important future directions. It is likely that such approaches could be expanded to predict optimal donors for tissue and biofluid-derived EV therapeutics. The development of screening tools for optimal donor selection of tissue-derived EVs is beyond the scope of this study. The heterogeneity in the reported results encourages future research on donor-screening strategies for therapeutic tissue-derived EVs.

Conclusion

Using a highly translational animal model of myocardial inflammation and remodeling, we demonstrate that donor characteristics critically mediate the therapeutic efficiency of the tissue-derived EVs. Our findings indicate that further research is needed to identify critical donor characteristics that predict therapeutic ability of individual or pooled tissue-derived EV products for inhibition of inflammation and fibrosis.
