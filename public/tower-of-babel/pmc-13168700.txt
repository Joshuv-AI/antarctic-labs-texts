Succinate-driven PKM2 succinylation and dimerization accelerates age-associated cardiac fibrosis
Authors: Ziwen Wang, Ziyuan Zhang, Zheng Ping, Shu Yang, Yaqi Li, Ting Jiang, Xin Zheng, Qi Zhang, Zhaochuan Liu, Xiaoli Zhang, Zhongyong Jiang, Deng Li, Haimei Sun, Bo Wu, Deshan Zhou, Xuebin Cao, Hanping Shi, Tingyi Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Age-related cardiac fibrosis is a key driver of heart failure and hallmark of aging whose mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Here we show elevated succinate levels in aged mice and humans drive cardiac fibrosis by enhancing fibroblast activation and collagen production. This process is mediated through succinate-dependent succinylation of PKM2 at lysine 125, promoting its transition from tetrameric to dimeric states. Using SUCNR1–/– mice, we establish that succinate signaling through SUCNR1/GPR91 promotes PKM2 succinylation and dimerization, creating a profibrotic network associated with aging-related diastolic dysfunction. Nuclear translocation of dimeric PKM2 enables fibroblast activation through HIF-1α binding, enhancing DNA-binding affinity and upregulating fibrogenic genes. Metformin treatment suppresses fibroblast activation by reducing succinate accumulation, revealing therapeutic potential for mitigating age-related cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction. Our findings identify metabolic dysregulation as a critical target and characterize a succinate-PKM2 signaling axis whose interruption may attenuate cardiac aging.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of the global elderly population is a direct consequence of the substantial increase in global life expectancy1. Projections indicate that by 2050, the average lifespan will reach an impressive 82 years, marking a significant milestone in human longevity2. Heart failure, a debilitating outcome of aging, emerges as a pivotal standalone factor driving disease progression3. Among the elderly, a prominent manifestation of cardiac insufficiency is heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), often attributed to the progression of cardiac fibrosis4. The incidence of age-related cardiac fibrosis is rising, yet there is a lack of targeted therapies specifically designed to address fibrosis-induced ventricular stiffening and diastolic dysfunction associated with aging5. Therefore, developing targeted therapeutic approaches represents a crucial need for managing aging-related cardiac fibrosis and concomitant diastolic dysfunction.

Maintaining optimal cardiac mechanical function depends critically on the precise composition of the extracellular matrix (ECM), particularly through tightly regulated collagen synthesis mediated by fibroblasts and myofibroblasts6. Age-related cardiac fibrosis is driven primarily by excessive fibroblast activation and collagen deposition in affected myocardial regions. These findings suggest that targeted inhibition of fibroblast activation represents a promising therapeutic strategy to combat age-related myocardial fibrosis and its associated diastolic dysfunction.

The heart, with its abundant blood supply and capillaries intricately woven around each cardiomyocyte, underscores the cardiomyocytes’ reliance on aerobic metabolism and heightened sensitivity to disruptions in blood flow, such as ischemia and hypoxia7. Ischemia and hypoxia, inevitable consequences of aging, pose direct threats to mitochondrial function, disrupting the delicate balance of the mitochondrial quality control system and ultimately impairing energetic metabolism8,9. Recognizing the intimate link between oxygen and blood supply with myocardial metabolic activity, it is plausible that metabolic intermediates significantly contribute to cardiac fibrosis and heart failure pathways. However, this critical perspective has been largely neglected in past research endeavors.

Under conditions of oxygen deprivation, the accumulation of tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) intermediates, notably succinate, occurs due to the suppressive effects of ROS, unoxidized flavin, and nicotinamide nucleotides on succinate dehydrogenase, disrupting normal metabolic processes10,11. Recent studies have illuminated succinate’s capacity to uniquely interact and signal through the G protein-coupled receptor SUCNR1/GPR91, suggesting a diverse array of biological roles for this metabolite beyond its established function in energy production12,13. Succinate plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological and pathophysiological contexts, including diabetes, chronic neuroinflammation, rheumatoid arthritis, intestinal type 2 immunity, osteoclastogenesis, and even systemic lupus erythematosus14. Furthermore, succinate accumulation in exercising muscles facilitates ECM production and muscle regeneration15. The present study uncovers a correlation between plasma succinate levels and diastolic dysfunction, as well as cardiac fibrosis, in aged mice and humans, proposing plasma succinate as a potential biomarker for cardiac fibrosis. However, the intricate mechanisms underlying succinate’s effects on cardiac fibrogenesis and diastolic dysfunction during aging remain largely elusive.

Cardiac fibrogenesis in aging is characterized by fibroblast activation and collagen deposition

To evaluate fibrosis and collagen accumulation, we utilized Sirius red staining as a quantitative method to assess collagen content, revealing pronounced fibrosis in aged mice (Fig. 1A). Notably, the hydroxyproline concentration in the cardiac tissues of aged mice was markedly elevated compared to that of 2-month-old control animals (Fig. 1B), indicating a substantial increase in collagen content associated with aging. Maintaining the integrity of the extracellular matrix (ECM) is crucial for preserving cardiac mechanics and diastolic function16. In the setting of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), collagen processing becomes impaired, resulting in its accumulation17. This pathological process mirrors the mechanisms underlying cardiac aging and predicts adverse clinical outcomes. Echocardiographic assessment demonstrated no significant differences in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS) between young and aged mice (Fig. 1E, F). However, aged mice exhibited key indicators of diastolic dysfunction, including a decreased E/A ratio, an increased E/Em ratio, and prolonged isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) (Fig. 1G–I), a characteristic of aging hearts. Notably, congestion strongly associated with elevated BNP levels and increased lung-to-body weight ratios18, consistent with our observations in aged mice (Fig. 1J, K). Consequently, cardiac remodeling, frequently accompanied by diastolic dysfunction or heart failure, emerges as a consequence of the aging process.

Myofibroblasts, the dominant mesenchymal cell type and critical drivers of extracellular matrix (ECM) turnover, play a pivotal role in maintaining ECM homeostasis, ensuring a delicate balance within the extracellular environment19. In the left ventricle of aged mice, we observed a robust upregulation of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) and periostin (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C), indicative of an intensified activation of fibroblasts in the aging heart20. Subsequently, cardiac fibroblasts were isolated and meticulously characterized by phenotypic alterations that suggested the transition from quiescent fibroblasts to activated myofibroblasts. In line with our expectations, fibroblasts from aged mice exhibited elevated expression of common myofibroblast markers, including α-SMA, fibronectin, periostin, and collagen I21, at both the mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 1D) and protein levels (Fig. 1L). This finding further corroborates the transition to an activated myofibroblast phenotype.

Elevated succinate levels correlate with diastolic dysfunction and cardiac fibrosis in aging mice and humans

Metabolic alterations are pivotal phenomena in the aging process22, and targeted interventions such as dietary modifications, physical activity, and caloric restriction have proven effective in mitigating aging and its associated conditions23. To decipher the metabolic transformations occurring in the hearts of aging individuals, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of the metabolic profile of hearts sourced from both young and aged mice. Among the 3,564 cardiac metabolites identified through untargeted metabolomics analysis, only a select 30 demonstrated statistically significant variations (absolute log2 fold change >1, two-sided t-test, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 1). Among these significantly altered metabolites, succinate emerged as one of the most upregulated compounds, exhibiting a fold change of 3.95. To independently validate this finding, we employed a Succinate Colorimetric Assay Kit to quantify succinate levels (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, we examined the potential association between succinate levels and cardiac fibrosis. Our analysis revealed a strong correlation between succinate concentrations in ventricular tissues from both young and aged mice and the expression of fibrillar collagen mRNAs, particularly COL1A and COL3A (Fig. 2C, D). Importantly, even when excluding data from young animals, the analysis robustly demonstrated a persistent correlation between succinate levels and the expression of COL1A and COL3A in cardiac tissues sourced exclusively from aged mice (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Based on these succinate-collagen correlational patterns, we formulate a hypothesis that elevated succinate levels may play a pivotal role in the progression of fibrotic hearts.

To assess the feasibility of succinate as a potential biomarker for heart failure or cardiac aging, we initiated an investigation into its release into circulation. Our initial analysis encompassed young individuals (age < 30 years, n = 23) and older adults (age > 60 years, segregated into those without HFpEF, n = 10, and with HFpEF, n = 20) (Supplementary Table 2). Notably, within the elderly cohort, serum succinate concentrations were elevated compared to those observed in the younger controls (Fig. 2E). Concurrently, serum succinate levels in both young and older individuals exhibited a significant correlation with the fibrosis biomarker TIMP-125 (Fig. 2F) and the cardiac remodeling marker ST-226 (Fig. 2G). This correlation remained robust among the elderly, as evidenced by the strong association between serum succinate and both TIMP-1 and ST-2 (Supplementary Fig. 2C, D). Furthermore, a distinct difference in serum succinate concentrations was discernible between individuals with HFpEF and older adults without heart failure, highlighting its potential diagnostic utility (Fig. 2H).

Subsequently, patients diagnosed with HFpEF were stratified into two distinct groups based on their median serum succinate levels. The first group comprised individuals with elevated succinate concentrations (exceeding the median; designated as Succinate high), while the second group encompassed those with lower succinate levels (falling below the median; labeled as Succinate low). Patients diagnosed with HFpEF and elevated serum succinate levels exhibited exacerbated diastolic dysfunction, as evidenced by echocardiographic assessments. Specifically, these patients displayed a decline in early diastolic tissue velocity, including reductions in septal and lateral Em velocities (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, the ratio of early mitral inflow velocity to average early mitral diastolic velocity (E/Em) was found to be 1.74 times higher in this subgroup compared to their counterparts with lower succinate levels (Fig. 2J), highlighting the association between high succinate levels and impaired diastolic function. As anticipated, elevated levels of TIMP-1 and ST-2 were discernible among HFpEF patients exhibiting high succinate concentrations (Fig. 2K). Given that ST-2 is a serum biomarker for heart failure, sanctioned by the FDA, which monitors cardiac cardiomyocyte and fibroblast activities and holds a pivotal role in murine models elucidating the pathogenesis of HF27,28, it is plausible to speculate that succinate may underlie a potential contribution to cardiac aging and diastolic dysfunction, stemming from its associative link with ST-2/TIMP-1 dynamics.

Our initial emphasis was on elucidating succinate’s impact on the activation of fibroblasts. To do so, we exposed primary cardiac fibroblasts to a range of succinate concentrations. Notably, upon exposure, these fibroblasts demonstrated increased expression of extracellular matrix (ECM) components and myofibroblast-associated mRNA transcripts (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, corresponding protein levels were also upregulated (Supplementary Fig. 3A), confirming the pro-fibrotic effect of succinate. Succinate drives cardiac fibrosis in both young and aged mice

Next, we investigated the crucial role succinate plays in the progression of cardiac fibrosis in both young and aged mice. Our initial efforts were centered on elucidating whether succinate possesses the ability to promote cardiac fibrosis and impair cardiac function in young mice. Over a 3-month period, we administered succinate to 2-month-old mice (Fig. 3C, top). Our results demonstrated a significant increase in succinate levels in both the serum and cardiac tissue of these young mice (Fig. 3D, E), directly attributable to the treatment. In the succinate-treated young mice, we observed an upregulation of periostin and αSMA expression within the cardiac tissues (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Subsequently, we evaluated the degree of fibrosis and alterations in cardiac function in the succinate-treated young mice. Our assessment revealed an elevation in collagen deposition, indicated by heightened positive staining for Sirius red and collagen I (Fig. 3F, and Supplementary Fig. 4B) and hydroxyproline levels (Fig. 3G). Intriguingly, despite a slight trend suggesting a decline in diastolic function, no statistically significant differences in diastolic function were observed between the control and succinate-treated young mice (Fig. 3H–J). Based on these findings, we formulate the hypothesis that while succinate may indeed induce cardiac fibrosis and potentially expedite cardiac aging, the relatively short duration of treatment in young mice was insufficient to impair cardiac function due to their robust self-regulatory mechanisms.

We subsequently examined the impact of succinate supplementation in drinking water on cardiac fibrosis and heart function in 16-month-old mice over an 8-month treatment period (Fig. 3C, bottom). Daily administration of succinate effectively elevated succinate concentrations in both the serum and cardiac tissues of the aged mice (Fig. 3D, E). Moreover, we observed a marked increase in fibroblast activation, evidenced by immunofluorescence staining of periostin and αSMA in the succinate-treated old mice (Supplementary Fig. 4A). Compared to the vehicle control group, succinate administration led to a notable augmentation in collagen deposition in the aged mice, as indicated by elevated Sirius red staining (Fig. 3F, and Supplementary Fig. 4B), collagen I staining (Fig. 3F, and Supplementary Fig. 4B), fibronectin staining (Supplementary Fig. 4C) and hydroxyproline levels (Fig. 3G). Additionally, echocardiographic evaluations demonstrated that succinate exacerbated diastolic alterations in these aged mice, manifesting as a decline in the E/A ratio, an elevation in the E/Em ratio, and an elongation of isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT) (Fig. 3H–J). Consequently, succinate intensified both fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction in the aged mice.

Succinate-mediated PKM2 dimerization enhances fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis

Subsequently, we explored the underlying mechanisms by which succinate stimulates fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis. Our previous work has highlighted glycolysis as a crucial process in fibrosis24,29, and studies have established PKM2’s essential role in facilitating aerobic glycolysis in tumor cells30. PKM2 exists in two forms: homotetrameric and homodimeric, with the latter predominantly being inactive (under standard reducing Western blot conditions, the dimeric form completely dissociates into ~58 kDa monomers, leaving only the monomeric band detectable in our analysis)31. This inactivity of the dimeric form slows ATP generation during glycolysis, leading to the accumulation of glycolytic intermediates, which are crucial for biosynthetic processes. In contrast, the tetrameric form of PKM2 is highly active. Notably, PKM2 dimers translocate to the nucleus and interact with HIF-1α to enhance the expression of key genes involved in fibrosis32.

Thus, we initiated a comprehensive investigation to determine whether the nuclear translocation of PKM2 is associated with alterations in succinate levels. The treatment of fibroblasts with succinate not only augmented PKM2 expression within the cytosol but also induced its localization to the nucleus (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, immunofluorescence analysis corroborated these observations, demonstrating a pronounced nuclear translocation of PKM2 following succinate treatment (Fig. 4B). Additionally, a significant upregulation in PKM2 translocation to the nucleus was evident in cardiac fibroblasts derived from aged mice compared to those from younger mice (Fig. 4C). Similarly, an analogous pattern of enhanced PKM2 translocation was discernible in cardiac fibroblasts isolated from young mice subjected to succinate treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Moreover, our glutaraldehyde crosslinking analysis of lysates derived from NIH 3T3 cells, a mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line, demonstrated that succinate predominantly increases the abundance of dimeric PKM2 (Fig. 4D). In line with this, a substantial increase in the proportion of dimeric PKM2 was also observed in cardiac fibroblasts isolated from aged hearts (Fig. 4E) and in those derived from young mice treated with succinate (Fig. 4F). Next, we evaluated the effects of two highly targeted small-molecule activators of PKM2, TEPP-46 and DASA-58, which are known to stimulate the formation of PKM2 tetramers33, on the functional characteristics of PKM2 in the presence of succinate. As illustrated in Fig. 4G, the induction of PKM2 tetramerization through TEPP-46 or DASA-58 significantly hindered its succinate-induced translocation to the nucleus. Furthermore, glutaraldehyde crosslinking experiments demonstrated the presence of a high molecular weight complex containing PKM2 upon treatment with TEPP-46 and DASA-58 (Fig. 4D), providing direct evidence for the formation of PKM2 tetramers. Subsequently, we assessed the impact of TEPP-46 and DASA-58 on succinate-mediated fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis. Notably, fibroblasts (including both primary cardiac fibroblasts and NIH 3T3 cells) treated with these activators exhibited reduced expression of α-SMA, fibronectin, collagen 1, and MMP2 following succinate stimulation (Fig. 4H, and Supplementary Fig. 5B). These findings underscore the pivotal role of PKM2 dimerization in promoting fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis in vitro.

Additionally, we aimed to elucidate the role of PKM2 dimerization in modulating fibrosis processes in mice. In our in vivo studies, we utilized TEPP-46, a potent inhibitor of PKM2 dimerization and activator of its dimeric form, due to its superior efficacy compared to other PKM2 modulators in a biological context34. To investigate its effects, we administered TEPP-46 or a vehicle control to 16-month-old mice for an extended period of 8 months, while the mice concurrently consumed water supplemented with 1.5% succinate. Intriguingly, cardiac fibroblasts isolated from the TEPP-46-treated aged mice exhibited a substantial reduction in the proportion of dimeric PKM2 (Supplementary Fig. 5C). This decrease was further supported by the downregulation of PKM2 levels in the nucleus (Supplementary Fig. 5D).

Consistent with our expectations, TEPP-46 effectively inhibited fibroblast activation in aged mice, as evidenced by a notable decrease in the expression of periostin and αSMA in both cardiac tissues (Supplementary Fig. 6A) and isolated fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 6B) from the TEPP-46-treated mice. The administration of TEPP-46 resulted in the activation of PKM2, which subsequently hindered the progression of cardiac fibrosis, as evident through Sirius red staining (Fig. 4I), collagen I immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 6C), fibronectin immunostaining (Supplementary Fig. 6D) and hydroxyproline abundance analysis (Fig. 4J). Furthermore, TEPP-46 administration in aged mice led to an improvement in diastolic function, marked by an elevation in the E/A ratio, a reduction in the E/Em ratio, and a decrease in IVRT, all of which were observed in comparison to the control group (Fig. 4K–M). These favorable outcomes suggest that TEPP-46 not only reverses fibroblast activation and collagen deposition in aged mice but also underscores the immense potential of targeting PKM2 dimerization as a therapeutic strategy for age-related cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction.

Lys125 succinylation of PKM2 is the key to promote its tetramer-to-dimer transition

We further investigated the potential mechanism that contributes to conformational changes of PKM2. Modifications at the posttranslational level can modulate the functions and conformation of proteins in addition to expression regulation. To determine if PKM2 was post-translationally modified, we immunoprecipitated PKM2 from succinate-treated 3T3 fibroblasts. Analyses of mass spectrometry revealed that K125 (Lys125) and K135 (Lys135) (Fig. 5A), which are evolutionarily conserved from Xenopus to humans, were succinylated (Fig. 5B). Succinate-induced succinylation of PKM2 was further validated by immunoprecipitation (Fig. 5C). Next, Arg (R, conserves the positive charge and inhibits lysine succinylation) and Glu (E, mimics lysine succinylation) substitution mutants of PKM2, including K125R, K125E, K135R, and K135E, were generated to identify the exact succinylation sites involved in PKM2 dimerization. We then transfected endogenous PKM2-depleted 3T3 fibroblasts with WT PKM2 and the created mutants. Afterwards, immunoprecipitation and Western blotting were performed. Compared with WT PKM2, K125R retained 72.65% of pyruvate kinase activity, whereas K125E retained only 31.67% (Fig. 5G). K135R and K135E mutants did not significantly differ from WT PKM2 in terms of pyruvate kinase activity (Supplementary Fig. 7C). The results indicate that K125 succinylation inhibits PKM2 activity. We also explored whether K125 succinylation regulates the conversion of PKM2 from tetramer to dimer by examining PKM2 dimerization in 3T3 fibroblasts with depleted endogenous PKM2 and reconstituted FLAG-WT PKM2, FLAG-PKM2 K125R, or FLAG-PKM2 K125E expression. We observed that the K125E mutation substantially increased PKM2 dimeric formation, and succinate treatment increased the formation of WT PKM2 dimer, but not the FLAG-PKM2 K125R mutation (Fig. 5H). In addition, the K125E mutation promoted the nuclear translocation of PKM2 even in the absence of succinate, while the K125R mutation inhibited PKM2 translocation (Fig. 5I). Hence, K125 succinylation of PKM2 contributed to PKM2 dimerization and nuclear translocation.

To determine whether PKM2 K125 succinylation could promote fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis, we examined myofibroblast phenotypes in succinate-treated 3T3 cells with depleted endogenous PKM2 and reconstituted FLAG-WT PKM2, FLAG-PKM2 K125R, or FLAG-PKM2 K125E expression, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5J, the expression of αSMA, fibronectin, collagen 1, and MMP2 were increased in fibroblasts transfected with WT PKM2 in response to succinate treatment, but not in those transfected with the FLAG-PKM2 K125R mutation, and the K125E mutation significantly promoted fibroblast activation without succinate stimulation. These results suggest that K125 succinylation of PKM2 promotes fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis.

Binding of succinate to SUCNR1 activates multiple signaling pathways36, including those involving calcium signaling and the production of intracellular prostaglandin E2 (PGE2). To determine whether succinate activates SUCNR1 in fibroblasts, we analyzed these canonical downstream targets in NIH 3T3 cells. Following treatment of fibroblasts with succinate for 6 h, we observed an increase in intracellular Ca2+ levels (Fig. 6B); however, this effect was absent in fibroblasts transfected with SUCNR1-specific siRNA (Fig. 6B). We then evaluated the impact of SUCNR1 on PKM2 succinylation and dimerization. We found the PKM2 succinylation was inhibited by SUCNR1 knockdown in vitro (Supplementary Fig. 8C), and similarly, cardiac fibroblasts isolated from aged SUCNR1–/– mice showed reduced PKM2 succinylation in comparison to WT control (Supplementary Fig. 8D), indicating that succinylation of PKM2 promoted by succinate is SUCNR1-dependent. Moreover, Succinate treatment was found to elevate the levels of dimeric PKM2 (Fig. 6D). However, this effect was abrogated in fibroblasts transfected with SUCNR1-specific siRNA, as illustrated in Fig. 6D. To ascertain whether SUCNR1 activation plays a role in PKM2 dimerization in vivo, we isolated cardiac fibroblasts from both 20-month-old wild-type (WT) and SUCNR1 knockout (SUCNR1−/−) mice. The expression of SUCNR1 was absent in cardiac fibroblasts isolated from aged SUCNR1−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 8E). Notably, the translocation of PKM2 to the nucleus was significantly decreased in cardiac fibroblasts from aged SUCNR1−/− mice compared to those from WT control mice (Fig. 6E). Crosslinking studies employing glutaraldehyde revealed that the tetramer fraction of PKM2 was notably increased in cardiac fibroblasts isolated from aged SUCNR1−/− mice (Fig. 6F).

Given that SUCNR1 activation promotes PKM2 succinylation and dimerization, we investigated whether succinate promotes fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis through SUCNR1. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated a significant downregulation of α-SMA in fibroblasts when SUCNR1 was inhibited using SUCNR1-specific siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 8F). Furthermore, the succinate-induced upregulation of α-SMA, fibronectin, collagen 1, and MMP2 in fibroblasts was abrogated by SUCNR1 siRNA but not by control siRNA (Fig. 6G). Collectively, our in vitro results suggest that succinate activates fibroblasts and enhances collagen synthesis through the SUCNR1 signaling pathway.

We also examined the progression of fibrosis in aged (20-month-old) SUCNR1-knockout mice. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a pronounced downregulation of α-SMA and periostin in aged SUCNR1−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 9A). Western blot analysis further confirmed the reduced activation of fibroblasts derived from aged SUCNR1-knockout mice (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Immunohistochemical staining for Sirius red staining, collagen I staining and fibronectin staining demonstrated reduced collagen deposition in SUCNR1-knockout mice (Fig. 6H and Supplementary Fig. 9C). Additionally, we compared cardiac function between 20-month-old wild-type mice and SUCNR1-knockout mice using echocardiography. The results showed no significant differences in LVEF and LVFS between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 9D, E). However, notable alterations in diastolic function were observed in aged SUCNR1-knockout mice, including an elevated E/A ratio, a decreased E/Em ratio, and a shortened IVRT (Fig. 6I–K). In summary, these findings suggest that succinate, acting through the activation of SUCNR1, triggers PKM2 dimerization, ultimately contributing to cardiac fibrosis and significantly impairing diastolic function.

Nuclear translocation of dimeric PKM2 activates fibroblasts through enhanced HIF-1α activity

Subsequently, we delved into the intricate mechanisms underlying the activation of fibroblasts and the enhancement of collagen synthesis mediated by the dimeric form of PKM2. Our previous research highlighted succinate’s pivotal role in modulating cellular metabolism, specifically by augmenting glycolysis and concurrently suppressing fatty acid oxidation (FAO) through the stabilization of HIF-1α24. This metabolic reprogramming, in turn, functioned as a catalyst for the initiation of lung fibrosis, a process driven by the activation of lung fibroblasts. Given that PKM2 dimers relocate to the nucleus and interact with HIF-1α38, thereby enhancing the expression of key genes, we sought to determine whether succinate exerts its effects on fibroblast activation and collagen synthesis via the HIF-1α pathway.

Consistent with our expectations, cardiac fibroblasts subjected to succinate treatment exhibited a significant increase in HIF-1α expression (Fig. 7A) and transcriptional activity (Fig. 7B). The adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (AMPK) functions as a pivotal cellular energy sensor and metabolic regulator. A decline in AMPK activity has been implicated in the aging process, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction and the progression of organ fibrosis. Western blot analysis (Fig. 8D) and immunofluorescent staining (Supplementary Fig. 10C) demonstrated a significant reduction in Thr172 AMPK phosphorylation in the aging heart, indicative of a notable decrease in AMPK activity. This decrease resulted in the downregulation of mitochondrial biogenesis, as evidenced by decreased expression of the key regulators mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) (Fig. 8D, and Supplementary Fig. 10C).

To explore the role of mitochondrial biogenesis in succinate accumulation and cardiac fibrosis, we conducted an experiment involving the administration of metformin (an AMPK activator) or a vehicle control to 16-month-old mice for a duration of 8 months. Our findings indicated that AMPK activation enhanced the expression levels of PGC-1α and TFAM, as demonstrated by Western blot analysis (Fig. 8D) and immunofluorescence staining (Supplementary Fig. 10C). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed a decrease in swollen and damaged mitochondria within the cardiac tissues of the metformin-treated group (Fig. 8A). Additionally, metformin-administered mice exhibited elevated baseline mitochondrial oxygen consumption rates (OCR) and maximal respiratory capacity compared to aged control animals (Fig. 8B, C). Similarly, metformin treatment was found to ameliorate complex II-dependent respiration deficiencies (Supplementary Fig. 10A, B). These results suggest that metformin-induced AMPK activation may serve as a therapeutic strategy to mitigate the adverse effects of aging on mitochondrial function and cardiac fibrosis.

Crucially, we observed a reduction in succinate accumulation within the cardiac tissues of mice treated with metformin (Fig. 8E). Furthermore, metformin effectively inhibited fibroblast activation, as evidenced by decreased expression levels of periostin and αSMA in the cardiac tissues (Supplementary Fig. 10D). The identification of collagen deposition, through collagen I staining (Fig. 8F, G), Sirius red staining (Fig. 8F, G), fibronectin staining (Supplementary Fig. 10E), along with the measurement of hydroxyproline levels (Fig. 8H), further indicated that metformin reduced collagen accumulation in aged mice. Moreover, echocardiographic parameters demonstrated that metformin administration enhanced diastolic function in older mice. This improvement was evident from an elevated E/A ratio, a decreased E/Em ratio, and a shortened isovolumic relaxation time, as shown in Fig. 8I–K. Collectively, these findings suggest the potential of activating AMPK through metformin or alternative pharmacological approaches as a promising therapeutic strategy to mitigate mitochondrial dysfunction and succinate accumulation.

Discussion

Given the growing challenge of population aging, it is crucial to explore therapeutic approaches aimed at rejuvenating impaired physiological processes and mitigating the symptoms associated with advancing age. Projections suggest that the financial burden of cardiovascular diseases will double by 2030, with individuals aged 65 and older expected to bear a staggering two-thirds of these costs39. While the elderly generally maintain overall health, they naturally experience a decline in heart function, often accompanied by the development of cardiac fibrosis. A hallmark of cardiac aging is the progression of fibrosis, which not only predisposes individuals to heart failure with preserved ejection fraction but also exacerbates diastolic dysfunction. This complex process involves intricate interactions among various intracellular and extracellular molecules, subtly modulating the behavior of cardiac fibroblasts40. However, fibrosis has not been directly targeted as a therapeutic strategy for heart failure, and our understanding of the intricate relationship between aging and cardiac fibrosis remains limited.

Ischemia and hypoxia, inherent to the aging process, serve as direct triggers of alterations in mitochondrial energy metabolism41. Under chronic hypoxic conditions, oxidative phosphorylation and succinate dehydrogenase activity decrease, impeding the conversion of succinate to fumarate42. Consequently, succinate accumulates within both the cytoplasm and mitochondria. Consistent with our previous observations24, we noted a significant increase in succinate levels in hypoxic tissues. Despite decades of extensive research on succinate’s role in energy production, recent advancements highlight its ability to initiate cellular signaling cascades through SUCNR1/GPR9143. In this study, we utilized an untargeted metabolomic approach combined with quantitative analysis to identify elevated succinate concentrations in the cardiac tissues of aged mice. In both older mice and humans, succinate levels correlated with diastolic dysfunction and cardiac fibrosis, indicating that plasma succinate concentration could serve as a potential biomarker for cardiac fibrosis. Furthermore, succinate exacerbated cardiac fibrosis in mice by activating fibroblasts. Therefore, our findings imply that succinate plays a pivotal role in regulating cardiac aging processes, and modulating metabolic imbalances offers promise for treating cardiac fibrosis and heart failure in the older people. Succinate emerges as a compelling therapeutic target in this regard. However, our study provided a preliminary examination of the mitochondrial dysfunction and succinate accumulation phenomena induced by ischemia and hypoxia, necessitating further investigation to elucidate the complex mechanisms underlying the excessive accumulation of succinate.

While previous studies have established a correlation between elevated succinate levels and fibrosis24,44, our work delineates the underlying mechanism through which succinate promotes cardiac fibrosis via PKM2 succinylation and dimerization. We uniquely demonstrate that succinate acts as a signaling molecule to succinylate PKM2 at the K125 residue. This specific post-translational modification promotes PKM2 dimerization, inhibits its metabolic activity, and drives a pro-fibrotic shift in the aging heart. Thus, our study moves beyond correlation to identify PKM2 K125 succinylation as a precise molecular missing link between succinate accumulation and the pathogenesis of cardiac fibrosis in aging. Moreover, cardiac fibroblasts are perpetually subjected to dynamic mechanical stress and rhythmic electrical activity. In aging, the cumulative burden of metabolic disturbances (e.g., elevated succinate) and mechanical strain may preferentially drive a compensatory, hyperactive profibrotic response aimed at tissue reinforcement, albeit pathologically45,46. In contrast, skin fibroblasts primarily provide structural support and repair acute injuries. The chronic, lower-intensity stressors of skin aging (e.g., UV exposure) may push these cells toward a terminal senescence state, effectively halting their proliferative capacity47,48. Our finding that succinate drives PKM2 K125 succinylation to promote a pro-fibrotic shift reveals a potential metabolic checkpoint specific to the high-energy-demand cardiac environment. This mechanism may be less critical in skin fibroblasts, where other pathways (e.g., p53 activation) might dominate the aging response49. Thus, the tissue-specific metabolic milieu and functional demands likely dictate distinct fibroblast fates during aging.

We also comprehensively delineated the function of succinate and its corresponding receptor, SUCNR1/GPR91, in the context of aging-induced cardiac fibrosis and diastolic impairment. SUCNR1 emerges as a pivotal early indicator of metabolic distress across diverse physiological and pathological scenarios, encompassing ischemia/reperfusion damage, platelet cohesion, inflammatory responses, retinal neovascularization, and renin-mediated hypertension43,50. Intriguingly, our findings revealed that succinate elicits fibrogenic actions both in vivo and in vitro, contingent upon the expression of SUCNR1. To elucidate the profibrotic mechanisms mediated by succinate via SUCNR1, we developed SUCNR1 knockout (KO) mice devoid of SUCNR1 expression. Notably, aged SUCNR1 KO mice exhibited a mitigation of fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, suggesting that an age-associated physiological surge in succinate concentrations is sufficient to provoke cardiac fibrosis and diastolic impairment. Thus, SUCNR1 stands out as a promising therapeutic target for the management of age-related cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction. While our study utilizing a systemic SUCNR1 knockout mouse model demonstrates a clear role for SUCNR1 in cardiac fibroblast activation and fibrosis, we acknowledge a limitation of this approach. The systemic knockout precludes the definitive assignment of the observed effects solely to fibroblasts, as the deletion of SUCNR1 in other cell types (e.g., cardiomyocytes, immune cells) may also contribute to the overall phenotypic outcome. Therefore, the use of a fibroblast-specific SUCNR1 knockout model in future studies would be invaluable to more directly interrogate the cell-autonomous mechanisms by which succinate signaling promotes fibroblast activation, and to rule out potential contributions from other cell types.

In the glycolytic pathway, pyruvate kinase (PK) acts as the terminal enzyme, catalyzing the conversion of phosphoenolpyruvate to pyruvate while simultaneously producing ATP32. PKM1 maintains a constitutively active state, characterized by rapid substrate turnover and ATP generation. In contrast, PKM2 exhibits a dynamic range of activity, shifting between low and high states based on cellular demands, and is abundant in cells requiring enhanced anabolic processes, particularly those undergoing proliferation. Notably, PKM2 is prevalent in tumors and myofibroblasts51. Unlike PKM1, PKM2 has the flexibility to exist as both a dimer and a tetramer, allowing for allosteric regulation and a variety of post-translational modifications that critically determine its functional roles, including pyruvate kinase activity, transcription factor function, and even protein kinase activity30. In our investigation, we found that succinate promotes the conversion of PKM2 from its tetrameric to its dimeric form, which then migrates to the nucleus. In the nucleus, PKM2 functions as a coactivator by interacting with HIF-1α, enhancing the transactivation of target genes in fibroblasts. In summary, our findings reveal that PKM2 undergoes conformational changes, and these structural alterations play multifaceted roles in the aging heart.

It is worth noting that succinate administration increases collagen deposition in both young and old mice but induces diastolic dysfunction specifically in the old—highlights the concept of age-dependent metabolic resilience. The age-specific diastolic dysfunction observed despite similar collagen deposition can be explained by a threshold model of metabolic resilience. We propose that the aged heart, with its pre-existing metabolic stress, operates near a functional tipping point. Our finding that succinate inhibits PKM2 via K125 succinylation provides the mechanism: in young mice, compensatory pathways maintain sufficient PKM2 activity to uphold diastolic energy demands. In old mice, however, additional succinate-driven PKM2 succinylation pushes activity beyond a critical threshold, triggering the energetic crisis that directly impairs relaxation, unmasking an age-specific vulnerability that transcends structural changes.

The in vivo components of this study were conducted exclusively in male mice, a design choice that initially controlled for the potential confounding effects of the estrous cycle on metabolic and fibrotic pathways. However, we acknowledge that this approach limits the translational scope of our findings, given the well-established sexual dimorphism in both aging and fibrotic heart disease52. It is plausible that the succinate-PKM2 signaling axis we identified could be modulated by sex hormones; for instance, estrogen’s known cardioprotective effects, potentially mediated through mechanisms like the regulation of mitochondrial function, might attenuate this pathway in pre-menopausal females53,54. Therefore, establishing the relevance of our findings in female models and elucidating the role of estrogen in regulating PKM2 succinylation remain critical and necessary directions for future research.

Post-translational modification is vital for the structure and functioning of proteins, and it is one of the keys to fine cell regulation of physiological activities55. Lysine, one of the most easily modified amino acid groups, plays an important role in functional regulation56. Recently, lysine succinylation (Ksucc), a widespread and evolutionarily conserved post-translational modification like lysine acetylation (Kac), which regulates important cellular activities and major metabolic pathways, has been identified and investigated57. Compared with methylation and acetylation, succinylation gives lysine a larger structure and can induce more significant changes in the properties of the protein58. An abnormal level of succinylation modification is tightly associated with the development of many illnesses, including cardiovascular system diseases, metabolic diseases, nervous system diseases, tumors and immune inflammation58–60. In this study, a previously undescribed mechanism of PKM2 regulation was discovered to show PKM2 is succinylated at K125 by succinate. To further solidify our findings that PKM2 K125 succinylation promotes its dimerization and inhibits activity, we propose two specific future directions. First, generation of a highly specific anti-PKM2 K125succ antibody is crucial for directly detecting this modification in physiological and pathological contexts. More importantly, to definitively establish the functional role of this modification in vivo, we will perform AAV-mediated gene rescue experiments. Specifically, we will deliver AAV9 vectors (cardiotropic serotype) expressing either wild-type PKM2 or the succinylation-deficient PKM2 K125R mutant into aged mouse models of cardiac fibrosis. Subsequent assessment of cardiac function, fibrosis levels, and PKM2 enzymatic activity will unequivocally determine the pathological contribution of PKM2 K125 succinylation in cardiac ageing.

While our study demonstrates PKM2 succinylation’s role in cardiac fibroblasts, previous work suggests PKM2 may also influence cardiomyocyte metabolism and inflammation in diseased hearts61. The potential involvement of PKM2 succinylation in aged cardiomyocytes, and its relationship to fibroblast activation patterns observed here, presents an interesting avenue for future investigation into cardiac aging mechanisms. Meanwhile, the immunomodulatory role of succinate in chronic inflammation is well established62, particularly through its activation of immune cells and subsequent release of profibrotic cytokines. While this indirect pathway likely contributes to age-related cardiac fibrosis, our data highlights a more direct mechanism whereby succinate activates cardiac fibroblasts through SUCNR1-mediated PKM2 succinylation. Several lines of evidence support this fibroblast-autonomous pathway: (1) complete abrogation of fibrotic responses upon SUCNR1 knockout, (2) cell-autonomous PKM2 dimerization and nuclear translocation in isolated fibroblasts, and (3) preserved succinate responses in fibroblast cultures devoid of immune cells. Nevertheless, in vivo aging hearts likely exhibit a complex interplay between immune-derived and fibroblast-intrinsic succinate signaling. The relative contribution of these pathways may depend on microenvironmental succinate concentrations—with paracrine immune effects dominating in acute inflammation versus direct fibroblast activation prevailing in chronic low-grade aging contexts. Future studies comparing young/aged immune fibroblast cocultures could help dissect these interactions, particularly regarding how aging alters succinate metabolism in both cell types.

Besides, the potent induction of fibronectin expression emerges as a notable feature of succinate-driven fibroblast activation, pointing to a direct and coordinated regulatory mechanism. Our data position the succinate-PKM2 dimer-HIF-1α axis as a central signaling hub responsible for this pro-fibrotic gene expression profile. This model is strongly supported by established literature confirming HIF-1α as a direct transcriptional activator of the fibronectin gene63, and is further corroborated by our experimental evidence showing that HIF-1α inhibition effectively attenuates succinate-induced fibronectin upregulation (Fig. 7G). Therefore, we propose that this metabolic signaling pathway coordinately regulates the synthesis of a core fibrotic matrisome, encompassing both structural collagens and key matricellular proteins like fibronectin. This unified mechanism underscores how succinate-driven metabolic dysregulation can directly orchestrate the development of a pro-fibrotic microenvironment.

While our data firmly establish that extracellular succinate signaling through the SUCNR1 receptor drives the conformational shift of PKM2 from tetramers to dimers in fibroblasts, the precise molecular cascade linking this GPCR (G Protein-Coupled Receptor) activation to intracellular PKM2 succinylation remains to be fully elucidated. Based on established GPCR signaling principles, it is plausible that SUCNR1 engagement initiates downstream pathways (e.g., PI3K/Akt, MAPK) which may rewire cellular metabolism or modulate the activity of enzymatic machinery responsible for protein succinylation. This hypothesis is supported by precedent findings that other metabolic enzymes, such as GLS1, can be regulated by succinylation via specific signaling cascades64. Therefore, delineating the exact signaling intermediates between SUCNR1 and PKM2 succinylation represents a critical and exciting direction for future research, promising to unveil a deeper layer of regulation at the intersection of GPCR signaling and metabolic control.

It is also important to acknowledge the limitations of this study. The primary cardiac fibroblasts were isolated using a combination of enzymatic digestion and differential adhesion, a well-established method for fibroblast enrichment6. However, these isolates were not subjected to extensive purification or validation with specific lineage markers post-isolation. Furthermore, while the NIH 3T3 cell line was invaluable for mechanistic dissection, as a murine embryonic fibroblast line, it may not fully capture the intricate biology of adult human cardiac fibroblasts. Addressing these model-specific constraints will be a crucial focus of our subsequent work to fully elucidate the therapeutic potential of targeting this pathway.

In conclusion, succinate emerges as a key player in the progression of age-related cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction, presenting itself as a promising biomarker for heart aging. Studies in murine models have demonstrated that inhibiting PKM2 dimerization can mitigate both cardiac fibrosis and diastolic dysfunction. Thus, strategies targeting pathways influenced by succinate may offer promising avenues for preventing age-related cardiac diseases.

Methods

Animal studies

The Capital Medical University’s Laboratory Animal Center supplied C57BL/6J male mice. These mice were organized into three separate groups, each consisting of ten mice, categorized by their ages: 2, 16, and 24 months. This segmentation aimed to mirror the human life stages corresponding to ~20, 50, and 70 years of age65. As outlined in the text, mice were chosen at random from their cages for inclusion in both the control and experimental subgroups. The personnel responsible for conducting echocardiography and analyzing heart tissue were unaware of which mice belonged to the experimental group and which belonged to the control group. All animal experiments were carried out in strict adherence to the Capital Medical University’s Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Furthermore, all procedures were approved by the Capital Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee.

Echocardiography

All mice underwent trans-thoracic echocardiography utilizing the Vinno 6 Lab, a preclinical small animal ultrasound imaging system. In brief, once anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, the mice were maintained at a comfortable temperature of 37 °C on a heated platform. Thoracic hair was removed using depilatory cream, followed by the application of an acoustic coupling gel layer. We acquired indices of systolic function through a short-axis M-mode scan and indices of diastolic function via an apical four-chamber view. The parameters gathered encompassed ejection fraction (EF), fractional shortening (FS), prolonged isovolumic relaxation time (IVRT), peak Doppler velocity of early diastolic mitral inflow (E), peak Doppler velocity of late diastolic mitral inflow (A), early diastolic mitral annular velocity (Em), as well as septal and lateral Em (representing early diastolic tissue velocity). For each parameter, we calculated the average of at least three measurements. These measurements were conducted by two seasoned technicians who were unaware of the experimental group assignments.

Study population

The research project received clearance from the Institutional Review Board at the 82nd Group Army Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army, also known as the 252 Hospital of the PLA. We adhered strictly to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the International Conference on Harmonization’s Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Twenty patients from the 82nd Group Army Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army were enrolled in the study. These patients exhibited heart failure symptoms and/or signs despite having a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF ≥ 50%), an NT-proBNP level exceeding 125 pg/mL, and evidence of cardiac functional and/or structural alterations, which is known as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Based on echocardiographic analysis and recommendations from the European Society of Cardiology, these patients also demonstrated diastolic dysfunction. On the first day of admission, blood samples were collected from patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). During their hospital stay, an echocardiography was performed. Additionally, healthy control subjects were recruited from the communities of Baoding and the 82nd Group Army Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army. These controls included 23 young individuals and 10 older individuals, all without any significant systemic diseases such as ischemic heart disease, pulmonary disease, infectious diseases, or cancer. The baseline characteristics of both the patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and the healthy individuals are presented in Supplementary Table 2. Following the collection of blood samples, plasma was promptly separated through centrifugation and then stored at −80 °C for future use.

Reagents and antibodies

The Sirius red staining kit (ab150681) was acquired from Abcam. Sigma supplied the succinate (#S9512), Selleck provided DASA-58 (S7928) and TEPP-46 (S7302), and MCE offered metformin (HY-B0627).

Antibodies targeting Fibronectin (15613-1-AP), Lamin B1 (12987-1-AP), α-SMA (14395-1-AP), PGC-1α (66369-1-Ig), TFAM (22586-1-AP), MMP2 (10373-2-AP), PKM2 (15822-1-AP), and Periostin (66491-1-Ig) were obtained from Proteintech. CST (Cell Signaling Technology) furnished antibodies for HIF-1α (#48085), pT172-AMPK (#2535), Flag (#14793), HA (#3724), and IgG (#3900). Beyotime provided Actin (AA128) and GAPDH (AF0006). Invitrogen sold us Collagen I (PA1-26204), Collagen III (PA1-28870) and also offered secondary antibodies for various species IgG labeled with Alexa Fluor® 594 (red) or 488 (green). Lastly, Beyotime supplied the DAPI (C1005).

Cell isolation and culture

Mouse primary cardiac fibroblasts were obtained through an enzymatic digestion process, as previously described6. In summary, after euthanasia, hearts from C57BL/6 mice were collected and finely chopped into 1 mm pieces in cold PBS. These pieces were then placed in a buffer containing 0.04% collagenase II from Sigma-Aldrich and 0.07% trypsin from Solarbio Life Sciences, Beijing. The mixture was continuously stirred at 37 °C for 60 min. The supernatants were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min to harvest the cells. These cells were then resuspended in DMEM media containing 4.5 grams per liter of glucose, 10% fetal bovine serum, and 1% streptomycin/penicillin. After allowing the cells to pre-plate for 1–2 h, cardiac fibroblasts were isolated from the digested cell suspension. This was followed by further enzymatic digestion and re-plating steps—a well-established method for enriching adherent fibroblast populations.

The NIH 3T3 cells, a mouse embryonic fibroblast cell line utilized in this study, were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). They were cultivated in DMEM enriched with 10% newborn bovine serum and 1% streptomycin/penicillin, and incubated in a 5% CO2 environment at 37 °C.

Analysis of biomarkers in serum samples collected from patients

We utilized ELISA kits sourced from Proteintech Group, Inc. to quantify the plasma levels of ST-2 and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-1 (TIMP-1). Additionally, a Novus Biologicals ELISA kit was employed to measure NT-proBNP levels, following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Succinate measurement

We determined the concentrations of succinate in plasma and cardiac tissues using the Succinate Assay Kit (Colorimetric) from Abcam, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transfection

Twelve hours prior to transfection, the cells were plated in a six-well plate at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, as previously detailed66. For transfection, we utilized Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen), following the vendor’s instructions, to introduce various plasmids and siRNA into the cells.

Western blot analysis

To extract total proteins, we employed ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with a Roche protease inhibitor cocktail. For separating nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins, we used an extracting kit from Beyotime. The concentrations of these proteins were determined using a BCA kit from Beyotime.

We conducted Western blot analysis as previously described. In brief, we subjected equal amounts of protein from each sample to SDS-PAGE, and then transferred the proteins to PVDF membranes (Millipore) using a Tris-glycine buffer with a concentration ranging from 8% to 16%. We then blocked the membranes in QuickblockTM Blocking buffer (Beyotime) for 30 min, and then incubated them with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following this, we incubated the membranes with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-IgG (Beyotime) for 1 h at 37 °C. We utilized the Fusion Fx VILBER LOURMAT system for enhanced chemiluminescence detection to measure the band intensities. Representative images/figures were selected based on their clarity and ability to accurately depict the group mean/average across all available data.

Co-immunoprecipitation assays

To extract total proteins, we employed ice-cold RIPA buffer supplemented with a Roche protease inhibitor cocktail. We then incubated an antibody coupled to Protein A/G PLUS agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) with whole lysates for 12 h at 4 °C. For western blotting, we used corresponding antibodies to visualize the immunoprecipitated proteins, along with rabbit IgG antibodies as controls. We blotted whole cell lysates with corresponding antibodies to serve as input controls. Representative images/figures were selected based on their clarity and ability to accurately depict the group mean/average across all available data.

Protein cross-linking assay

Prior to adding 50 mM glycine to terminate the reaction, we included 0.02% glutaraldehyde to the cell lysates and incubated them for 30 min at room temperature. Following separation of the samples using 8% SDS-PAGE, a western blot was performed. To ensure accuracy, we selected representative images/figures based on their high quality and ability to best reflect the group mean/average across all available data.

Collagen content measurements

We measured the hydroxyproline content of collagen as previously outlined in reference. Utilizing Biovision’s assay kits, we assayed the hydroxyproline levels in cardiac tissue after hydrolyzing it with hydrochloric acid (HCl). To determine the total collagen content, we used the hydroxyproline content of a collagen standard as a benchmark.

Real-time PCR analyses

Cells were collected using the RNeasy Micro Kit from QIAGEN, and cDNA synthesis was carried out following the manufacturer’s guidelines provided by Invitrogen. Real-time PCR was executed utilizing the SYBR Green qPCR master mix from Takara, adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. For normalization, we employed GAPDH or Actin as internal references. The primers utilized for qRT-PCR are detailed in Supplementary Table 3.

Mitochondrial oxygen consumption

We conducted Seahorse experiments using an XFe96 analyzer from Agilent, adhering to the manufacturer’s instructions. Mitochondria isolated from the specified cardiac tissues were plated onto an XF96 culture plate. To measure basal mitochondrial oxygen consumption, we utilized a final concentration of 1.5 µM Oligomycin, 1.0 µM FCCP, and 0.5 µM of both Antimycin A and Rotenone. For assessing complex II-dependent respiration, we used 0.5 µM Rotenone and 10 mM Succinate.

Histological analysis

Tissues were fixed with 4% PFA for 48 h, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 µm slices for various staining procedures including histological staining, immunofluorescence analyses, and immunohistochemical staining. Alongside hematoxylin and eosin staining, we also performed Sirius red staining. After mounting and sealing the slices, we conducted morphometric analyses. Representative images/figures were selected based on their clarity and accuracy in reflecting the group mean/average across all the data available.

To quantify the level of fibrosis via Picrosirius red staining, we analyzed the ventricular myocardium. For each heart sample (n = 6), 10 non-overlapping fields were randomly selected from the mid-ventricular cross-section. Collagen content was then quantified using Fiji (ImageJ2) software by measuring the percentage of Sirius red-positive area per field.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The 5 µm slices underwent standard rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking procedures before being incubated with the appropriate specific antibody. Subsequently, they were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and DAB substrate, counterstained with hematoxylin, and sealed for morphometric analysis. Representative images/figures were selected based on their clarity and precision in reflecting the group mean/average across all available data.

To distinguish primary antibodies from nonspecific background and validate antibody specificity, we utilized a rabbit isotype antibody (ab172730, Abcam) or a mouse isotype antibody (#5415, Cell Signaling Technology) under identical experimental conditions. Furthermore, to ascertain background staining, both secondary and primary antibodies were used alone as controls under the same experimental settings.

Immunofluorescence analyses

The 5 µm slices underwent standard rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking steps before being incubated with the corresponding specific antibody. Afterward, secondary antibodies were conjugated with fluorescence dyes, and DAPI was added following established protocols. Microscopic images were captured and viewed using a confocal microscope (Leica, Germany). Representative images/figures were selected based on their clarity and accuracy in reflecting the group mean/average across the entire dataset. A negative control was conducted using an equivalent amount of secondary antibody alone to verify that the fluorescent signals represented genuine target staining rather than nonspecific background noise.

To quantify periostin- and α-SMA-positive cells, we first determined the ratio of immunopositive cells to total DAPI+ nuclei in each microscopic field. These ratios were then normalized to the mean value of control samples (set as 1.0).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

The cardiac tissues were fixed in glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 48 h and subsequently in 2% osmium tetroxide at 37 °C for another 6 h. After this, the tissues underwent embedding and staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and TEM images were captured using a JEM-1400PLUS microscope sourced from Japan.

HIF-1α transcriptional activity assay

The transcriptional activity was assessed using the HIF-1α Transcription Factor Assay Kit from Abcam. In brief, nuclear protein was isolated utilizing the Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit by Beyotime, and the isolated nuclear HIF-1 specifically bound to its corresponding response element. To identify the complex, we introduced specific primary antibodies targeting HIF-1α, followed by the addition of an HRP-linked secondary antibody, which facilitated a colorimetric measurement at 450 nm.

Statistics and reproducibility

All data are presented as Min to Max (show all points). To address reproducibility, each figure legend indicates the sample sizes (biological independents) and number of replicates. The detailed P values, compared to their corresponding controls, are displayed in the associated figures. The data analysis was performed using the SPSS 13.0 software program, developed by SPSS Inc., located in Chicago, IL, USA. The specific statistical methodologies used are outlined in the respective figure legends. The normality of all data was rigorously assessed prior to statistical analysis. Statistical significance was determined using various tests, including the two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test and non-parametric unpaired Mann-Whitney test. Correlation between parameters was analyzed using Pearson’s coefficients. The analysis did not adjust for confounders (e.g., sex, BMI, comorbidities) in Fig. 2, and the baseline characteristics were well-balanced between the older patients with and without HFpEF, data from both sexes were included in the analysis.
