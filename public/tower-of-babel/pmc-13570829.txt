Mitochondrial Function-Related Genes in Sleep Disorders: A Multi-Omics Mendelian Randomization Study
Authors: Junjun Xu, Ziyan Zhang, Liuyang Yu, Yi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mitochondrial dysfunction is linked to sleep disorders in previous report, but the potential roles of specific genes remain unclear. This study aimed to dissect different subtype-specific genetic associations and their underlying mechanisms. A multi-omics Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) approach was performed to identify potential causal links between mitochondrial function-related genes and sleep disorders. We integrated GWAS data from FinnGen database (the discovery set), independent GWAS datasets (covering different sleep-disorder subtypes and used for validation), and cis-QTLs (including mQTLs, eQTLs, and pQTLs) to perform systematic exploration. Specially, we performed targeted validation of tissue-specific effects, leveraging gene expression data from disease-relevant brain regions within the GTEx database. Our SMR analysis identified mitochondrial function-related genes potentially modulating sleep disorders across biological layers, initially identifying 102 genes at the methylation level, 48 at the gene expression level, and 6 at the protein abundance level. Integrative analysis subsequently prioritized DCXR and ACADVL and revealed their distinct, subtype-specific associations. DCXR exhibited a protective role in sleep apnea while ACADVL showed a paradoxical risk conferring role in daytime sleepiness. In addition, the analysis identified an epigenetic regulatory mechanism for DCXR in which its expression and protein levels are modulated by DNA methylation. Finally, validation in brain-hypothalamus tissue confirmed DCXR as a significant potential protective factor (OR = 0.929, 95% CI: 0.887–0.973, P_HEIDI = 0.999, FDR = 0.2449). Our findings implicate key mitochondrial genes, particularly DCXR and ACADVL, in the pathophysiology of specific sleep disorder subtypes, highlighting potential avenues for precision medicine. Introduction

Sleep disorders, including insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), present a range of symptoms such as difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, daytime fatigue, and cognitive impairment (Fell 2024). These conditions are particularly prevalent among older adults and individuals with chronic illnesses, representing a significant public health challenge (Grotto et al. 2024). They significantly reduce patient quality of life and increase the healthcare burden, especially among patients with comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease and immunodeficiencies (Esquivel-Mendoza et al. 2024; Hsueh et al. 2024). Current treatment strategies include non-pharmaceutical interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), as well as the use of positive airway pressure devices for OSA (Javaheri et al. 2024). Despite these approaches, treatment outcomes often remain suboptimal, particularly in terms of improving long-term quality of life and reducing healthcare costs (Iannella et al. 2024). This study aims to identify the risk factors associated for onset and progression, including genetic predispositions that may contribute to disease development (Jerčić et al. 2024).

Mitochondria are critical organelles responsible for energy production through oxidative phosphorylation and are integral to cellular metabolism and homeostasis (Rakhe and Bhatt 2024). In recent years, mitochondrial dysfunction has been increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of sleep disorders (Brunetti et al. 2021), with specific genetic mutations contributing to these conditions Mitochondrial function, particularly the accumulation of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS), regulates sleep stability by modulating the excitability of specific sleep-promoting neurons, influencing sleep homeostasis (Hartmann and Kempf 2023). In another study, gene expression patterns associated with mitochondrial function in isolated rapid eye movement (iRBD), a sleep disorder, were among the most significant contributors to cortical thinning (Rahayel et al. 2023). Reflecting this systemic impact, a growing body of evidence suggests that mitochondrial proteins released during cellular stress or actively secreted may serve as valuable biomarkers for mitochondrial health, making them suitable for large-scale genetic analyses (Lo et al. 2021). Furthermore, current studies indicate that mitochondria may play a crucial role in sleep disorders such as sleep deprivation and insomnia through alternations in gene expression and oxidative phosphorylation enzyme activity (Melhuish Beaupre et al. 2022). Therefore, exploring the intricate relationship between sleep disturbances and mitochondrial-related genes may provide new insights into disease mechanisms and identify potential therapeutic targets (Liu et al. 2024).

Mendelian Randomization (MR) is a robust epidemiological technique that uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to investigate causal relationships between genetic variants and complex diseases (Bowden and Holmes 2019). Traditional MR analyses typically depend on single omics datasets, like gene expression, which might not fully capture the intricate mechanisms underlying diseases. To address this limitation, multi-omics MR approaches have been developed, incorporating data from genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics. This integrative method offers a more comprehensive way to infer causal relationships by analyzing multiple layers of biological information (Chen et al. 2024a, b; Yazdani et al. 2022). In our study, we utilized Summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) to combine data on gene expression, DNA methylation, and protein abundance. This approach enhances the statistical power for identifying causal associations and provides deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms connecting mitochondrial function-related genes with sleep disorders across various biological levels.

Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the potential role of mitochondrial function-related genes in the pathogenesis of sleep disorders through a comprehensive multi-omics Mendelian randomization framework. Distinct from previous single-omics approaches, we integrate data across DNA methylation, gene expression, and protein abundance for the first time, aiming to clarify their intrinsic causal pathways, explore potential central mechanisms of sleep-wake regulation, and reveal novel, subtype-dependent pathogenic roles for specific genes. These findings may inform future precision medicine interventions.

Methods

Study Design

In this study, instrumental variables for mitochondrial function-related genes were extracted at the levels of methylation, gene expression, and protein abundance (mQTLs, eQTLs, and pQTLs). SMR and colocalization analyses were conducted for sleep disorders at each biological level. The primary discovery set was the FinnGen dataset for a broad sleep disorder phenotype. To dissect subtype-specific associations, three independent validation datasets were used for validation: insomnia, daytime sleepiness, and sleep apnea. To strengthen causal inference, colocalization analysis was applied to identify shared genetic variants across different omics levels. The integrated results from different MR analyses were used to identify potential causal candidate genes involved in sleep disorders. This study followed the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines, and the analysis process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Data Resources

Mitochondrial function-related gene data were obtained from the MitoCarta3.0 database (https://www.broadinstitute.org), comprising 1,136 genes. To minimize population stratification bias, all GWAS summary statistics and QTL reference panels used in this study were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry. GWAS summary statistics for a broad “sleep disorders” phenotype were retrieved from the FinnGen dataset (49,880 cases and 358,194 controls), which aligns with the sleep-wake disorders chapter in ICD-11 (e.g., 7A00-7A0Z). For validation purposes, we used three independent cohorts focusing on specific sleep disorder subtypes: the Lane cohort for insomnia (a condition defined under ICD-11 codes 7A00 Chronic insomnia and 7A01 Short-term insomnia; 345,022 cases and 108,357 controls) (Lane et al. 2019), ⁷, the DaytimeSleepiness dataset (104,786 cases and 34,452 controls) which focuses on self-reported excessive daytime sleepiness, a key symptom of disorders such as 7A21 Idiopathic hypersomnia in ICD-11 (Wang et al. 2019), and the Campos cohort (25,008 cases and 337,630 controls) directing sleep apnea syndrome, corresponding to 7A41 Obstructive sleep apnea in ICD-11 (Campos et al. 2023). Detailed GWAS information can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Blood mQTLs data were derived from two large European cohorts: the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study (n = 614) and the Lothian Birth Cohorts (n = 1,366) (Wu et al. 2018). Blood eQTLs data were obtained from the eQTLGen consortium, which included data from 31,684 individuals (Võsa et al. 2021), while blood pQTLs data were sourced from Pietzner et al. covering 10,708 individuals of European ancestry (Pietzner et al. 2021).

Tissue-specific expression data were accessed from the GTEx v8 database (https://gtexportal.org/home/), which contains data from 838 donors and 17,382 samples across 52 tissues. The summary data for blood eQTLs were derived from Brain_Hypothalamus specifically. All summary statistics used in this SMR analyses were generated by previous publicly available studies, all of which had obtained ethical approval.

Summary-Data-Based MR Analysis

We applied the SMR approach to interrogate potential causal associations between mitochondrial function-related genes and sleep disorders across methylation, expression, and protein abundance. This method providing enhanced statistical power over traditional MR approaches (Zhu et al. 2016). Concretely, for each probe or gene, the instrumental variable was the single top associated cis QTL, defined as the SNP with the smallest P value within a ± 1000 kb window of the corresponding gene and applying a stringent genome-wide significance threshold (P < 5.0 × 10⁻⁸) (Chen et al. 2024a, b). The LD reference panel used for both SMR and HEIDI analyses was the European population from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3. To assess instrument strength, we calculated the variance explained (R²) and corresponding F statistic for each top instrumental SNP. R² was computed as 2 × EAF × (1 − EAF) × β² using effect estimates and allele frequencies from the respective QTL summary data. The F statistic was computed as R² × (N − 2) / (1 − R²), where N represents the sample size of the QTL study. To ensure the robustness of our findings against common biases, we integrated two critical validation steps. First, to rigorously distinguish true causal pleiotropy from linkage disequilibrium (LD) artifacts, we applied the Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments (HEIDI) test. This test assesses whether the functional association is driven by a single shared causal variant; we required a non-significant HEIDI test (P_HEIDI > 0.01) to exclude associations likely driven by linkage to adjacent variants rather than true vertical pleiotropy (Chauquet et al. 2021). Second, to rigorously control the false positive rate inherent in high-dimensional multi-omics testing, we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method to adjust P-values. Subsequently, we performed a multi-SNP based SMR analysis using the smr multi flag in SMR software. This approach considers multiple SNPs at the QTL locus to evaluate whether the observed association is driven by a single causal variant or is confounded by linkage disequilibrium. Consequently, significant and robust causal associations were strictly defined by satisfying concurrent criteria: SMR significance after FDR correction (P_SMR < 0.05), validation in multi-SNP analysis (P_SMR-multi < 0.05) (Chen et al. 2024a, b), and passing the HEIDI test for heterogeneity (P_HEIDI > 0.01). All analyses were conducted using SMR software (v1.3.1) (Wu et al. 2018).

Colocalization Analysis

To strengthen the causal inference, colocalization analysis was performed using the coloc R package to identify whether the observed signals shared the same genetic variants across different omics levels (mQTLs, eQTLs, and pQTLs). The analysis was conducted within a ± 1000 kb window centered around the top cis-QTLs. Colocalization results were interpreted based on the posterior probability (PP) for five hypotheses: (1) no genetic association with SNPs for either trait (H0); (2) genetic association with SNPs only exists for gene expression (H1); (3) genetic association with SNPs only exists for disease risk (H2); (4) both traits associated but with different causal variants (H3); (5) both traits share the same causal variant (H4). A posterior probability greater than 0.5 for H4 (PPH4 > 0.5) was considered evidence for colocalization, indicating a shared genetic variant influencing both the QTL and the disease trait. This threshold serves as an exploratory screening criterion to avoid prematurely excluding potentially meaningful biological signals in high dimensional multi omics analyses, consistent with established coloc methodology and prior studies (Giambartolomei et al. 2014; Kousathanas et al. 2022). This analysis helped to prioritize the loci most likely to harbor causal variants underlying sleep disorder risk (Arvanitis et al. 2022; Huang et al. 2023).

All statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.0). SMR and HEIDI analyses were conducted using SMR software (v1.3.1). Colocalization analysis was performed using the R package coloc. The R package “ggplot2” was used for Manhattan plot generation, and “forestplot” was used for forest plot generation. Code for SMRLocusPlot and SMREffectPlot was sourced from Zhu et al. (2016).

Results

Multi-Omics SMR Identifies Candidate Genes

In the FinnGen discovery cohort, our multi-omics SMR analysis identified numerous genes associated with a broad sleep disorder phenotype based on predefined criteria (P_SMR-multi < 0.05 & P_SMR < 0.05 and P-HEIDI > 0.01). At the methylation level, we identified 187 significant sites across 102 genes, with associations indicating both increased risk (e.g., ABAT, C5orf63, COX6B2) and protection (e.g., ACADVL, CAT, CPT1C) (Fig. 2; see Supplementary Table S2 for full results). At the expression level, 48 genes were found to be significant. Risk factors included NAGS, BCL2L11, and FDPS, while protective factors included ACADVL, DCXR, and ISCU (Fig. 3; see Figure S1 for the DCXR locus plot and Supplementary Table S3 for full results). At the protein level, six proteins were found to be associated with sleep disorder risk. Proteins encoded by BOLA3 and PC were risk factors, whereas those from ACADVL, CAT, DCXR, and NFU1 were protective factors (Fig. 4; see Supplementary Table S4 for full results).

To explore the underlying regulatory pathways, we integrated these findings and observed a potential mechanism for DCXR. Higher methylation at the cg07073120 site was causally linked to decreased gene expression (Figure S2; see Supplementary Table S5 for full results), and decreased expression was linked to lower protein abundance (see Supplementary Table S6 for full results). This suggests a cascade in which methylation of DCXR reduces its expression and protein levels, thereby increasing sleep disorder risk. In contrast, for ACADVL, higher methylation was associated with increased gene expression, which was in turn linked to higher protein abundance, highlighting a distinct regulatory mechanism. The overall results of the SMR analysis for mQTLs, eQTLs, and pQTLs in the FinnGen discovery cohort are visualized in the Manhattan plot (Figure S3).

Validation of Key Findings in Hypothalamus Tissue

To further explore the causal relationships between these identified genes and sleep disorders, we conducted tissue-specific validation using data from the Brain-Hypothalamus tissue. In this analysis, key genes related to sleep disorders, including DCXR, MRPL43, MTRF1L, and SETD9, were validated as having significant eQTL effects. Among these findings, DCXR may be a crucial protective factor (OR = 0.929, 95% CI: 0.887–0.973, P-HEIDI = 0.999, FDR = 0.2449). Overall, these findings suggest that several key genes play crucial roles in sleep disorder pathology (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between mitochondrial function-related genes and sleep disorders by using SMR. Leveraging a discovery dataset from the FinnGen database, we identified a stringent set of 46 mQTLs, 10 eQTLs, and 4 pQTLs that passed both SMR significance and the HEIDI test for a general sleep disorder phenotype. To evaluate these findings and evaluate their relevance in more precise clinical contexts, we utilized three independent validation cohorts for insomnia, daytime sleepiness, and sleep apnea. This validation step was crucial as it revealed that the effects of key genes are highly subtype-specific. Notably, while DCXR and ACADVL both exhibited robust associations across multiple omics levels, our validation analyses confirmed a consistent protective role for DCXR specifically in sleep apnea. In contrast, a complex and reversed risk association was uncovered for ACADVL in the context of daytime sleepiness.

DCXR was particularly prominent in our study, validated across multiple levels of analysis. It showed positive associations with sleep disorder risk at the methylation level, while negative associations were observed at the expression and protein abundance levels. Our integrated analysis revealed that higher methylation at cg07073120 was associated with decreased DCXR gene expression. This decreased expression was linked to DCXR protein levels, potentially contributing to an increased risk of sleep disorders. Current studies indicate that DCXR, a multifunctional protein involved in carbohydrate metabolism and sperm maturation, may interact with other proteins to perform diverse roles, but its involvement in sleep disorders remains largely unexplored. Meanwhile, studies have shown that sleep disorders are often associated with primary mitochondrial disease, and protecting mitochondrial function also plays a positive role in improving sleep disorders (Brunetti et al. 2021; Megha et al. 2024). Although direct evidence linking DCXR to mitochondrial function and sleep disorders is currently lacking, its role in these basic processes suggests it may affect mitochondrial function and metabolic pathways, thereby impacting normal sleep function. Such involvement could have far-reaching implications for understanding the metabolic underpinnings of sleep disorders (Chen et al. 2024a, b). Crucially, our validation analysis in the sleep apnea cohort strongly reinforced this proposed mechanism. The protective effects of higher DCXR expression and protein levels were consistently replicated, suggesting that the regulatory cascade linking DCXR methylation to protein abundance is particularly relevant to the pathophysiology of sleep apnea. This subtype-specific validation provides a more focused direction for future functional studies and therapeutic targeting (Perco et al. 2019). Future research should aim to elucidate the mechanisms by which DCXR modulates mitochondrial function in sleep disorders, focusing on its interaction with metabolic pathways. Additionally, exploring how interventions targeting DCXR methylation could mitigate disease progression represents a promising avenue for therapeutic development.

ACADVL was a prominent gene identified in our integrated analysis, showing a consistent association with sleep disorders risk across methylation, expression, and protein abundance levels. It plays an essential role in mitochondrial beta-oxidation by catalyzing the initial step in the breakdown of very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), which is crucial for mitochondrial energy metabolism (Leslie and Saenz-Ayala 2009; Ahmed et al. 2022). Meanwhile, mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to sleep disorders. Impaired bioenergetics and oxidative stress disrupt cellular function, leading to neuroinflammation and metabolic imbalances. Studies indicate that sleep deprivation worsens mitochondrial damage, reduces energy production, and promotes the onset of neurodegenerative diseases (Zhang et al. 2024). Therefore, ACADVL may help maintain mitochondrial function and prevent sleep disorders by ensuring efficient ATP transport and energy production. However, this seemingly protective role was strikingly reversed in our validation analysis for daytime sleepiness. Across all three omic levels, higher levels of ACADVL were consistently associated with an increased risk of this specific phenotype. This apparent contradiction may be explained by the conceptual distinction between a broad ‘sleep disorder’ phenotype, which often reflects nocturnal sleep disruption, and its specific consequence, ‘daytime sleepiness’. It is well-established that poor nocturnal sleep is a primary causal factor for excessive daytime sleepiness (Shi et al. 2018). Therefore, a genetic variant that is protective against the root cause (i.e., the underlying pathology of sleep disruption at night) could paradoxically present as a risk factor for its most prominent symptom (daytime sleepiness). Our findings for ACADVL are consistent with this model, suggesting its role in mitochondrial energy metabolism may be protective against the underlying nocturnal pathology, but the clinical manifestation of that same pathology is the daytime sleepiness we observed.

Therefore, our findings not only support a significant role for ACADVL in sleep-wake regulation but also highlight the critical importance of phenotype specificity in genetic studies of sleep. Understanding the precise interplay between its methylation and expression in the context of different sleep disorder subtypes requires further functional studies (Shi et al. 2018). This will be essential to clarify ACADVL’s specific, and potentially dual, role in the pathogenesis of sleep disorders. Ultimately, these subtype-specific findings could pave the way for more precise diagnostic approaches and the development of targeted therapies for patients suffering from distinct sleep disorder phenotypes.

Beyond DCXR and ACADVL, our study implicated other core components of mitochondrial function in specific sleep disorder subtypes, with findings robustly supported by validation analyses. Notably, we identified consistent protective roles for NDUFB8 and ISCU against sleep apnea. NDUFB8 is a subunit of Complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain, and its dysfunction is a known contributor to oxidative stress, a key pathological feature of sleep apnea (Okoye et al. 2023). Similarly, ISCU is essential for the biogenesis of iron-sulfur clusters, which are critical prosthetic groups for the function of respiratory chain Complexes I, II, and III (Tsvetkov et al. 2022). The validation of these genes underscores the importance of maintaining respiratory chain integrity for mitigating sleep apnea risk. Furthermore, our analysis linked COX19, a key assembly factor for Complex IV, specifically to insomnia, where its genetically predicted protective effect was validated. This finding suggests that different sleep disorder subtypes may be associated with vulnerabilities in distinct complexes of the mitochondrial respiratory chain (Melhuish Beaupre et al. 2022).

Our comprehensive multi-omics analysis differs from traditional gene expression profiling. By incorporating epigenetic and protein expression levels, this study provides further insight into the complex mechanisms underlying sleep disorders. However, certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, our discovery analysis relied on a broad “sleep disorders” phenotype from FinnGen. The inherent heterogeneity of this phenotype may mask or dilute subtype-specific effects and likely contributes to the reversed associations we observed for ACADVL in the more granular daytime sleepiness cohort. Furthermore, some of the underlying GWAS data for our validation cohorts were derived from self-reported measures, which are susceptible to recall bias and potential phenotype misclassification. Second, our analysis primarily utilized QTL data derived from blood. While this is a powerful and widely-used approach, blood-based molecular traits may not perfectly proxy the relevant biological processes occurring within the central nervous system, the primary site of sleep regulation. Although our tissue-specific analysis in hypothalamus tissue provided some targeted support, the majority of our findings rely on this assumption. Additionally, all GWAS and QTL datasets used in this study were derived from individuals of European ancestry. While this restriction minimizes bias from population stratification, it limits the generalizability of our findings to other ethnic groups. Finally, our study is based on summary-level data, which precludes more granular analyses such as stratification by age, sex, or clinical severity. Despite these limitations, our findings remain significant as a reference. Future studies using individual-level data and direct functional experiments are necessary to validate our findings and fully elucidate the complex, context-dependent roles of these mitochondrial genes in sleep disorder pathophysiology.

In summary, our study underscores the significant role of mitochondrial-associated genes in the development of sleep disorders, but crucially reveals that their contributions are highly subtype-specific. We provide robust genetic evidence for a protective role of DCXR specifically in sleep apnea, while uncovering a complex, context-dependent role for ACADVL, which acts as a risk factor for daytime sleepiness despite being protective against a general sleep disorder phenotype. Building upon our findings, future research should move beyond a generalized view of these conditions. A deeper understanding of the intricate, subtype-specific biology of mitochondrial dysfunction is essential to pave the way for novel, tailored preventive and therapeutic approaches.
