Cassandra’s Curse
Authors: Michael S. Avidan, Jon Cohen, Jessica L. Saleska
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The poem Cassandra’s Curse, accompanied by a brief commentary, explores the psychological and existential terrain of routine medical surveillance through the lens of a patient who is also a physician and has stage IV leiomyosarcoma. Using the medium of poetry, the author hopes to offer clinicians, as well as a broader audience, deeper insight into the dread patients with life-limiting illness repeatedly face, even in relation to apparently mundane procedures, such as computed axial tomography scans.

On every sixth Monday

I visit Cassandra, and

lie submissively in her.

Tongue-less, eyeless soothsayer—

she binds and embraces;

she’s not dumb or blind,

only lie-less and guileless,

neither biased nor unfair.

Cassandra, Catscandra:

with your Curie-like stare,

you uncover what’s there.

The Friday before

is always a Fearday.

I’m dreading her gaze,

her secret-prying ways.

She lacks MRI’s flair;

she’s cold and severe,

and with AI software,

cannot actually care.

Cassandra, Catscandra:

with your radiant glare,

how much of you can I bear?

It’s Saturnine Saturday;

I’m in the doldrums.

Cassandra’s no demon,

but haunts with her hum.

Her sound surrounds me:

from my crown to my feet,

my exposure’s complete,

as I drown, she fillets me.

Cassandra, Catscandra:

many bare you their souls,

but none is special or rare.

Sunday, Bloody Sunday:

it can’t stave off Monday.

Will my scan be alarming?

Is my cancer advancing?

In her mood-lit round chamber,

she might make me glow,

but she’ll not create calm;

I’ll be frantic with panic.

Cassandra, Catscandra:

your gift is my curse;

I await you telling me the worst.

Monday is my Doomsday;

for her it’s humdrum.

She clasps and I gasp.

Warmth suffuses my body;

my insides inflame,

my throat constricts in pain.

Cassandra constrains me.

Cassandra contains me.

Cassandra, Catscandra:

I’m a cat of nine lives—how

many remain? Tell me no lies.

And then it’s Tuesday—

another Phews-day.

The augury’s over;

I gain a six-week delay.

Cassandra, don’t pander:

my malignant masses

rest placid, you say.

Or perhaps it is Tuesday—

an almighty Blues-day.

The augury’s over;

there’s no more delay.

Cassandra, don’t pander:

my malignant masses

spread plague-like, you say.

Cassandra, Catscandra —

I’m Schrödinger’s cat;

how will you prognosticate?

Cassandra, Catscandra —

I’m Schrödinger’s cat;

must you really seal my fate?

Without forewarning, Michael Avidan was diagnosed with stage IV leiomyosarcoma in 2022.1 The poem Cassandra’s Curse focuses on what could be viewed as a mundane aspect of his care. Unlike chemotherapy or an invasive procedure, a computed axial tomography or CAT scan is not painful and is physically well tolerated. But for Avidan, with his inexorably advancing stage IV cancer, the scan is profoundly consequential, as it shows disease progression, determines treatment, and forecasts trajectory. What might seem routine and unremarkable triggers in him an intense cycle of dread that builds in the days before his scan every six weeks. Cassandra’s Curse attempts to capture this experience of mounting dread.

Anxiety prior to CAT scans is well described and is probably underestimated by medical professionals.2 The structure of the poem conveys the relentlessness of medical surveillance; the best-case scenario is that the scan shows no or minimal disease progression, and the dread repeatedly resets every six weeks, on the Tuesday post scan. Most scenarios, however, are grim, with the scan showing degrees of disease progression ranging from slow to rampant.

We believe that Anesthesiology readers will sympathize with Avidan’s mounting fear in the poem and might consider that some patients who are confronting the prospect of major surgeries might experience a similar buildup of dread in the days before their procedures.3,4 Regarding dread, Avidan also wanted to convey the asymmetry between the indifferent machine and the frightened patient. This juxtaposition is at the core of the poem.

Although superficially Avidan has anthropomorphized Cassandra, the poem clearly establishes that she is fundamentally un-human-like, lacking capacity for empathy or tenderness. The poem speaks to a deficiency of modern medicine: mistaking surveillance for care and prognostication for compassion. As we move more into an era of artificial intelligence and information overload, it becomes more important than ever for empathetic clinicians to be engaged and to provide patients with meaningful interpretation of results and emotional support.5 Otherwise, dispassionate Cassandra and her AI Bot siblings will take over entirely, and patients will feel even more alienated and emotionally abandoned.

Like Cassandra in Greek mythology, Cassandra in the poem—or Catscandra—also always reveals the truth. Cassandra of Troy was cursed by Apollo never to be heeded or believed. Interestingly, when Avidan’s CAT scans have findings suggestive of disease progression, in discussions with his oncology team, and in hopeful desperation, he tries to have the most favorable interpretation possible. In medicine, facts are seldom unambiguous, and even Catscandra’s revelations can be equivocal, leaving Avidan in a Schrödinger’s cat–like suspension between reprieve and catastrophe.

Cassandra is not malevolent. In the case of cancer progression, a CAT scan is almost always helpful. But there are also instances when information overload can be distressing and is not in patients’ best interests. There is a danger that we focus entirely on medical surveillance and neglect the life activities from which we derive meaning and joy. Perhaps Avidan’s final plea should be, “Cassanda, release me.”
