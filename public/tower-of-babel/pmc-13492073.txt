Zero-premium Medicare Advantage plans and access to generic abiraterone and imatinib
Authors: Changchuan Jiang, Yixin Tang, Chuan Angel Lu, Ya-Chen Tina Shih, K Robin Yabroff, Xuesong Han, Xin Hu
Published: 2026

Introduction

Medicare Advantage (MA) is a privately managed alternative to Traditional Medicare. Enrollment has grown rapidly and now exceeds 50% of eligible beneficiaries, largely driven by plans charging no additional monthly premium. These “zero-premium” plans are disproportionately marketed to lower-income beneficiaries and those residing in high social vulnerability areas.1 While financially attractive, zero-premium plans may impede cancer care through utilization management (UM) and downstream cost-sharing.2

To examine this, we selected abiraterone and imatinib, 2 commonly used, high-cost generic oral oncolytics. In the 2023 Medicare Part D Drug Spending Dashboard, total annual spending for abiraterone and imatinib reached approximately $911 million and $284 million, with mean annual spending per beneficiary of $19 703 and $18 372, respectively. We compared formulary coverage, UM requirements, and cost-sharing structures between zero-premium and premium-charging MA plans for these therapies.

Methods

We conducted a cross-sectional analysis using MA prescription drug (MAPD) plan data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Q3 2025 public use files. Eligible plans included MA health maintenance organization (HMO), HMO–point of service (HMO/POS), and preferred provider organization plans, excluding employer-sponsored plans, Special Needs Plans, and plans with fewer than 10 enrollees. Plans were categorized by monthly premium as $0, $0.01 to $50, or >$50. Generic abiraterone 250-mg and imatinib 100-mg tablets were identified using National Drug Codes. For each oncolytic, we compared study outcomes across premium tiers: formulary coverage, UM requirements of prior authorization (PA) and quantity limits (QL), annual ingredient drug cost, and estimated annual beneficiary out-of-pocket (OOP) spending. Annual drug costs were estimated from plan-reported ingredient pricing and standardized to 365 days of daily therapy according to official FDA label dosing (abiraterone, 1000 mg; imatinib, 400 mg). OOP spending was simulated using plan-specific deductibles, copayment, and coinsurance amounts reported in CMS files, assuming continuous use of a single oncolytic for one year without low-income subsidy or other concurrent medication use. We also examined how OOP liability may have differed with and without the 2025 $2000 Medicare Part D annual cap, implemented under the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) on January 1, 2025. For capped estimates, beneficiary cost sharing was set to $0 after cumulative true OOP spending reached $2000. We additionally calculated realized OOP as a share of annual total drug cost to contextualize beneficiary cost-sharing relative to total drug spending.

This cross-sectional study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline and was exempt from review by the University of Texas Southwestern Human Research Protection Program.

Statistical analysis

All estimates were weighted by plan enrollment size to reflect the distribution of MAPD beneficiaries. Differences across premium categories were assessed using chi-squared tests for categorical variables and Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables because of skewed distributions. Two-sided P values less than .05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS and Python. Additional methodological details were provided in the Supplementary eMethods.

Results

Across 3266 MAPD plans, 2298 (70.4%) were zero-premium plans, covering 78.7% (16.3/20.7 millions) of total beneficiaries in MAPD. While generic abiraterone and imatinib were universally covered in the formularies, UM requirements were consistently more prevalent in zero-premium plans than in premium-charging plans. PA was required by 92.2% and 91.8% of zero-premium plans for abiraterone and imatinib, respectively, vs 88.3% and 87.3% in $0.01-$50 plans and 88.9% and 86.6% in >$50 plans (P = .005 and .007). QL followed the same gradient by 3 premium levels (abiraterone, 76.4% vs 73.2% vs 70.9%, P < .001; imatinib, 88.5% vs 85.3% vs 83.0%, P = .026; Table 1).

Over 1 year of therapy, zero-premium plans showed the highest annual drug cost for both drugs (abiraterone, $20 786; imatinib, $21 261), vs $0.01-50 plans ($16 937 and $17 639) and >$50 plans ($15 049 and $16 084; both P < .001). Without the IRA cap, zero-premium plans carried substantially higher projected OOP liability, roughly $1500 greater for both drugs than premium-charging plans (both P < .001). Disparities narrowed markedly under the cap, though zero-premium plans retained the highest mean annual OOP (abiraterone: $1344; imatinib: $1326) vs $0.01-$50 ($1174 and $1112) and >$50 plans ($1078 and $1119; both P < 0.001). Beneficiaries in these plans also most often reached the cap (abiraterone, 49.8%; imatinib, 53.6%) vs $0.01-50 (42.9% and 39.7%) and >$50 plans (35.6% and 34.3%; both P < .001). Correspondingly, the OOP share of annual total drug cost post-IRA was comparable across tiers for abiraterone (18.1% vs 19.4% vs 20.5%; P = .08) and lowest in zero-premium plans for imatinib (23.2% vs 25.8% vs 28.6%; P = .03), plausibly due to these plan's higher underlying drug costs and greater cap-related truncation of OOP (Table 1).

Discussion

In this national analysis of 2025 MAPD plans, zero-premium plans were widely adopted but consistently exhibited more restrictive UM and higher OOP spending for generic abiraterone and imatinib than premium-charging plans, despite partial attenuation of disparities under the IRA cap.

These findings highlight a trade-off in MA benefit design: zero-premium plans appear to offer forward-looking beneficiaries better value through premium savings and a modest annual OOP difference under the IRA cap. Two barriers, however, persist beneath this appeal. First, because the cap applies to cumulative rather than point-of-sale spending, beneficiaries in zero-premium plans, who face higher pre-cap costs, bear larger front-loaded OOP costs earlier in the year, when treatment abandonment is most likely.3 Second, the UM requirements are more concentrated in these plans, imposing administrative barriers the cap does not address and that can delay time-to-treatment.2 This trade-off is particularly concerning given the rapid expansion of zero-premium plans among beneficiaries in socioeconomically vulnerable, higher-cancer-mortality areas,1,4 where such barriers may compound existing disparities.

Our study has limitations such as cross-sectional design precluding causal inference, and reliance on plan-reported cost-sharing structures to estimate OOP costs rather than adjudicated claims. However, the results suggest that for cancer care, “low premium” does not necessarily equate to “better access” or lower total OOP expenses. Further research should examine whether the popularity of zero-premium MA plans inadvertently increases structural barriers to high-quality cancer care and differences in beneficiary outcomes, compared with other MA plans with premiums and Traditional Medicare.5
