Modulation of Maillard reaction pathways and aroma profiles in peanut oil using enzymatically tailored peanut meal hydrolysates
Authors: Ming Yang, Jing Wang, Haiming Shi, Yanlan Bi, Xuebing Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study establishes a mechanistic framework for directing Maillard-driven aroma formation in peanut oil through targeted enzymatic precursor engineering, exploring the potential of precursor engineering for flavor modulation in peanut oil. Defatted peanut meal was subjected to selective hydrolysis using distinct glycosidases (cellulase, hemicellulase, pectinase, α-amylase, and amyloglucosidase) in combination with proteases to systematically reconstruct the precursor microenvironment (reducing sugars and free amino acids). The resulting hydrolysates were thermally reacted with cold-pressed peanut oil at 120 °C, and the evolution of volatile profiles and sensory attributes was evaluated using an integrated sensory-directed flavoromics approach. Two enzymatic strategies, pectinase-protease and α-amylase-protease, emerged as highly effective in reprogramming Maillard pathways. These treatments generated a substantial expansion of key precursors, particularly glucose (up to >60-fold increase) and branched-chain amino acids, which were associated with selective formation of pyrazines and furan derivatives associated with roasted, savory, and caramel notes. Concurrently, lipid-derived off-flavor compounds such as hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol were significantly suppressed, indicating a possible shift in reaction dominance from lipid oxidation to Maillard chemistry. Correlation and network analyses revealed the precursor-volatile-sensory linkages, suggesting that specific precursor compositions (e.g., glucose-leucine-lysine systems) are associated with distinct aroma profiles. Notably, pentose-rich systems were associated with furan pathway activation, whereas hexose-dominant systems were associated with pyrazine formation. These findings provide evidence that enzyme-tailored precursor engineering can influence Maillard reaction pathways in edible oils in a potentially tunable manner, offering a promising strategy for producing peanut oil with tailored flavor profiles and suggesting potential improvements in oxidative stability.

Introduction

Peanut oil is widely valued for its characteristic roasted and nutty aroma, which arises primarily from complex thermal reactions during processing, including the Maillard reaction, Strecker degradation, and lipid oxidation (Ma et al., 2026). Comprehensive analyses have identified hundreds of volatile compounds in roasted peanut oil, yet only a limited subset, particularly alkylpyrazines, aldehydes, and selected heterocyclic compounds, dominates sensory perception (Ma et al., 2024). These key odorants originate from interactions between amino compounds and carbonyl precursors, highlighting the central role of precursor composition in defining final flavor quality.

However, conventional high-temperature roasting presents an inherent trade-off. While it enhances desirable roasted aromas, it simultaneously accelerates lipid oxidation, leading to the accumulation of off-flavor compounds such as hexanal and nonanal that impart grassy or rancid notes (Choe and Min, 2006, Yang et al., 2025), and reduce the quality of meal due to the increased denaturalization of the proteins in the meal. Moreover, variability in raw materials and processing conditions introduces significant inconsistencies in flavor quality, largely due to uncontrolled differences in precursor availability and reaction pathways. These limitations underscore a fundamental challenge in edible oil processing: the lack of precise control over the precursor systems that govern flavor formation.

Recent advances suggest that enzymatic pretreatment offers a promising route to address this challenge by selectively modulating the precursor pool prior to thermal processing (Sun et al., 2025). For example, Xu, Guo, et al. (2025) demonstrated that enzyme treatment of sunflower meal hydrolysates combined with Maillard reaction modification could prepare flavor enhancers for sunflower seed oil, illustrating the general feasibility of precursor engineering in oilseed systems. Proteases and glycosidases can hydrolyze the peanut matrix, releasing free amino acids, peptides, and reducing sugars, which serve as essential substrates for Maillard chemistry. Because Maillard reactions are highly sensitive to the type, concentration, and ratio of these precursors, manipulating their composition provides an opportunity to steer reaction pathways toward desired aroma profiles at relatively mild temperatures (Xu, Liu, et al., 2025). Despite this potential, existing studies have largely focused on empirical observations of flavor enhancement, with limited mechanistic understanding of how specific enzymatic activities reshape precursor systems and subsequently influence volatile formation.

In parallel, advances in analytical methodologies have enabled more precise characterization of flavor systems. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) remains the primary tool for volatile profiling (Ma et al., 2024), while multivariate approaches, including Pearson correlation and network analysis, have emerged as powerful tools for linking precursors, volatiles, and sensory attributes within complex food matrices (Kou et al., 2023). However, these approaches have rarely been integrated into a unified framework to elucidate how enzymatic pretreatment governs Maillard-driven flavor formation.

Identifying the key aroma-active compounds among the hundreds of volatiles detected is essential for understanding flavor formation mechanisms. Odor activity value (OAV), calculated as the ratio of concentration to odor threshold, is the standard metric for this purpose; however, its accurate determination requires absolute quantification, which is analytically challenging for the highly complex and dynamically changing volatile system generated by enzymatic pretreatment and subsequent thermal reaction. Semi-quantitative HS-SPME-GC–MS, while widely adopted for volatile profiling owing to its simplicity, does not yield true concentrations because different compounds exhibit different partitioning behaviors among the oil matrix, headspace, and SPME fiber (Jeleń and Wieczorek, 2023a, Jeleń and Wieczorek, 2023b). To address this limitation, a relative OAV (ROAV) approach has been developed in which each compound's modified OAV is expressed as a percentage of the maximum within the same sample, thereby enabling comparative ranking of odorant contributions across treatments. This approach has been widely applied in flavor chemistry studies and shown to reliably identify key aroma-active compounds (Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026).

Therefore, the central hypothesis of this study is that targeted enzymatic hydrolysis can act as a controllable “precursor engineering” strategy to direct Maillard reaction pathways and resulting sensory outcomes in peanut oil. To test this hypothesis, five glycosidase-protease combinations were systematically evaluated in parallel to generate distinct precursor microenvironments. The specific objectives were to: (i) quantify how different enzymatic treatments reshape key precursors, including free amino acids and monosaccharides; (ii) identify the resulting changes in volatile profiles and key odor-active compounds; and (iii) establish mechanistic precursor-volatile-sensory relationships through correlation and network modeling.

By integrating enzymatic engineering with sensory-directed flavoromics, this study aims to move beyond descriptive analysis toward an integrated framework for understanding how enzymatic precursor modulation influences flavor outcomes in edible oils, thereby providing both theoretical insight and practical guidance for industrial production of high-quality peanut oil with tailored sensory attributes.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Peanuts (Arachis hypogaea L., cultivar Yuhua 23) were kindly provided by Yihai Kerry Fragrant Oils and Fats Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). The following commercial enzymes were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), including cellulase (50 U/mg), hemicellulose (20 U/mg), pectinase (50 U/mg), α-amylase (100 U/mg), amyloglucosidase (100 U/mg), alkaline proteinase (AP, 20 U/mg)), and Flavorzyme® (20 U/mg). All other chemicals and solvents used were of analytical or chromatographic grade.

Preparation of enzyme hydrolysates and flavor-enhanced oils

Pre-treatment of peanut material

Whole peanuts were pulverized into a fine powder using a high-speed centrifugal grinder (FW100; Tianjin Taisite Instrument Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China). The powder was repeatedly extracted with n-hexane to obtain defatted peanut meal (DPM). The DPM was passed through a 40-mesh sieve and stored at 4 °C until use. Separately, a portion of whole peanuts was cold-pressed at 60 MPa for 20 min to obtain cold-pressed peanut oil for subsequent experiments.

Production of glycosidase hydrolysates

Five distinct glycosidase hydrolysates were prepared. For each, 15 g of DPM was suspended in 90 mL of 0.1 M citric acid buffer (pH 4.8). A specific glycosidase (cellulase, hemicellulase, pectinase, α-amylase, or amyloglucosidase) was then added at a dosage of 16 U per mg of dry DPM. The pH was precisely adjusted to 4.8 using 1 M HCl, and the slurry was incubated at 50 °C (based on recommended conditions from the supplier) for 3 h with continuous agitation. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by heating the mixture at 90 °C for 10 min. The supernatant, obtained by centrifugation (8000 ×g, 15 min), was collected as the polysaccharide hydrolysate fraction. Preliminary single-factor experiments were conducted to optimize enzyme dosages, with results provided in Tables S1–S4.

Production of protease hydrolysates

A separate protease hydrolysate was prepared. Briefly, 15 g of DPM was mixed with 90 mL of 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5). AP was added at a concentration of 14 U per mg of dry DPM. The pH was maintained at 8.5 using 1 M NaOH, and the mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 3 h. Subsequently, Flavorzyme® was added at 1 U/mg and hydrolysis continued for an additional 1 h to enhance the release of free amino acids (FAAs). The supernatant, collected after centrifugation (8000 ×g, 15 min), served as the protease hydrolysate.

Thermal treatment for flavor formation

The final reaction substrates for the Maillard reaction were prepared by blending each specific polysaccharide hydrolysate with the protease hydrolysate at a volume ratio of 2:1. This yielded six composite treatments: a control (DPM-BA, blank with no glycosidase) and five enzyme-combination treatments designated as DPM-CA (Cellulase+AP), DPM-HA (Hemicellulase+AP), DPM-PA (Pectinase+AP), DPM-AA (α-amylase+AP), and DPM-GA (Amyloglucosidase+AP).

To generate the flavor-enhanced oils, each mixed hydrolysate was combined with cold-pressed peanut oil at a ratio of 1:2 (v/v). The mixtures were transferred to a sealed reaction vessel and subjected to thermal treatment at 120 °C for 2 h. This temperature was selected based on the intention to reduce 30–50 °C from common roasting temperature (150–180 °C) for the purpose of reducing lipid oxidation and protein denaturalization. The time selection came from the preliminary experiments (see Supplementary Material Section S1) showing effective Maillard reaction promotion while avoiding excessive thermal degradation and undesirable compound formation at higher temperatures (> 150 °C) and is consistent with industrial practices for mild flavor development in specialty peanut oils. After the reaction, samples were centrifuged (5000 ×g, 10 min) for phase separation. The upper oil phases, enriched with volatiles, were collected and labeled as PO-RBA, PO-RCA, PO-RHA, PO-RPA, PO-RAA, and PO-RGA, and were used for all subsequent volatile and sensory analyses. The underlying aqueous phases (post-heating hydrolysates) were labeled correspondingly (e.g., DPM-RBA) for precursor analysis. A summary of all sample abbreviations, enzyme combinations, and thermal treatment conditions is provided in Abbreviation List and in Table S5.

Determination of browning intensity and color characteristics

The progression of the Maillard reaction was monitored by measuring the absorbance of diluted hydrolysates at 294 nm (intermediate products) and 420 nm (melanoidins) using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-1800; Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Color parameters of the hydrolysates were measured in the CIE Lab* color space using a non-contact colorimeter (HunterLab, Reston, USA) with D65 illumination and a 10° observer angle.

Monosaccharide composition analysis

Monosaccharide composition was determined by the method used before, with slight modification (Zhou et al., 2025). High-performance anion-exchange chromatography with pulsed amperometric detection (HPAEC-PAD) using an ICS5000+ system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used, equipped with a Dionex™ CarboPac™ PA100 analytical column (4 × 250 mm) and a corresponding guard column (4 × 50 mm). Samples were diluted, filtered (0.22 μm), and a 10 μL aliquot was injected. Separation was performed at 30 °C with a flow rate of 0.45 mL/min using a multi-step gradient of eluents: (A) 1000 mmol/L NaOAc, (B) 200 mmol/L NaOH, (C) 25 mmol/L NaOH, and (D) deionized water. The gradient program was: 0–10.0 min, 20% C / 80% D; 10.1–28.0 min, 15% B / 85% D; 28.1–40.0 min, 100% B; 40.1–55.0 min, 15% A / 50% B / 35% D; 55.1–75.0 min, re-equilibration. Quantification was achieved via external calibration with standard monosaccharides (0.1–10 mg/L, r2 > 0.995).

Free amino acid analysis

FAAs were analyzed according to a published method (Ma et al., 2026) with slight modification. Briefly, 1 mL of hydrolysate was mixed with 5 mL of 10% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid, made up to 25 mL with 0.02 M HCl, vortexed, and centrifuged. The supernatant was filtered (0.22 μm) and analyzed using an automated amino acid analyzer (LA8080; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

HS-SPME/GC–MS analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile compounds in peanut oil were analyzed by headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME/GC–MS) following the method of Xu et al. (2021) with modifications. Peanut oil (5 g) was placed in a 20 mL headspace vial. Volatiles were extracted using a 120 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Supelco, Bellefonte, PA, USA) exposed to the headspace at 55 °C for 50 min. The fiber was thermally desorbed in the GC injector at 250 °C for 3 min in splitless mode. Separation was performed on a DB-FFAP column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) with helium as carrier gas at 1 mL/min. The oven temperature program was: 40 °C (1.5 min), 10 °C/min to 100 °C (5 min), 2 °C/min to 150 °C (8 min), 10 °C/min to 185 °C, and 20 °C/min to 240 °C (16 min). The MS was operated in electron ionization mode (70 eV) with a scan range of m/z 35–350. Compounds were identified by matching mass spectra with the NIST 17 library and comparing calculated retention indices (determined using C7-C40 n-alkanes) with literature values. Semi-quantification was performed using 1,3-dichlorobenzene as an internal standard (Zhang et al., 2026). Abbreviations of amino acids are given in the Abbreviation List.

Relative odor activity value (ROAV) calculation

The contribution of individual volatiles to the overall aroma was assessed using ROAV (Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). The modified odor activity value (mOAV) was first calculated as mOAVi = Ci / OTi, where Ci is the concentration of the compound in the semi-quantitative analysis, and OTi is its odor threshold value in the oil. The ROAV for each compound was then calculated as: ROAV = (mOAVi / mOAVmax) × 100, where mOAVmax is the highest OAV in the sample, assigned a ROAV of 100. Compounds with ROAV ≥1 were considered key odorants. It is important to acknowledge that because HS-SPME extraction efficiencies vary among compounds due to differences in partition coefficients between the oil matrix, headspace, and fiber coating, the calculated concentrations are approximate rather than absolute. Correction factors for individual compound recoveries were not applied in this study. Future studies employing GC-olfactometry (GC-O) or omission/reconstitution experiments would provide more definitive identification of key odorants. Nevertheless, the ROAV approach remains widely accepted for comparative evaluation within a consistent analytical framework (Huang et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2026).

Sensory analysis (quantitative descriptive analysis, QDA)

The sensory profile of peanut oils was evaluated by a trained panel of ten assessors (5 male, 5 female, aged 25–35) using QDA as previously described (Ma et al., 2025). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments. The operation has been approved by the Ethical Evaluation Committee of Henan University of Technology. Reference standards for each attribute are provided in Table S6. For evaluation, 10 g of oil (equilibrated to 30 ± 1 °C) was presented in amber-coded glasses. Assessors rated the intensity of each attribute on a 9-point scale (0, not perceivable; 9, extremely strong). Samples were presented in a randomized order with three-digit codes. Evaluations were performed in duplicate, with breaks to prevent fatigue. The mean scores were used for data analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple range test (p < 0.05) were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 to identify significant differences. Data visualization, including correlation heatmaps and histograms, was performed using Origin 2026. Network graphs were constructed with Gephi 0.10.1, and clustered heatmaps and UpSet plots were generated using the ChiPlot online platform (https://www.chiplot.online/). Pearson correlation analysis was used to elucidate relationships between precursors, volatiles, and sensory attributes.

Results and discussion

Browning intensity and color development

UV–Vis absorbance was used to monitor Maillard reaction progression. After thermal treatment, the DPM-BA control showed negligible changes at both 294 and 420 nm, indicating limited precursor availability (Fig. 1). In contrast, all enzymatic pretreatments significantly enhanced intermediate product formation (A294) and final melanoidin accumulation (A420). Among them, DPM-GA exhibited the highest A420 value (0.988), followed by DPM-AA (0.787), suggesting that amylolytic enzymes more effectively promoted browning by releasing reducing sugars from starch (Dhital et al., 2017). In particular, amyloglucosidase likely generated a larger pool of glucose monomers than α-amylase, thereby accelerating advanced-stage Maillard polymer formation. Cell-wall-degrading enzymes (DPM-CA, DPM—HA, and DPM-PA) produced moderate increases in A420 (0.480–0.615), indicating that although cell wall disruption improved substrate accessibility, it was less effective than amylolytic hydrolysis in driving melanoidin formation. Consistently, colorimetric analysis showed substantial total color differences in all enzyme-treated samples after heating (ΔE > 8.0), whereas the control changed only slightly (ΔE, 0.99). DPM-AA and DPM-GA fundamentally reoriented the carbohydrate profile by mobilizing starch reserves. DPM-AA, acting as an endo-enzyme, hydrolyzed internal α-1,4 bonds to elevate Glc to 15,209.55 mg/kg while simultaneously generating a diagnostic maltose fraction (993.41 mg/kg). In contrast, the exo-acting amyloglucosidase in DPM-GA released Glc with higher efficiency (18,410.60 mg/kg) by cleaving from non-reducing ends and processing α-1,6 branch points, achieving more complete saccharification (Dhital et al., 2017). During thermal reaction, Glc consumption in the GA group exceeded 16,000 mg/kg. This massive carbonyl supply, paired with amino acids liberated by the coupled proteases, was associated with extensive pyrazine generation, consistent with the established role of Glc as the primary carbon skeleton for methylpyrazine and 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (Van Lancker et al., 2010).

While cellulase, hemicellulase, and pectinase all target the peanut cell wall matrix, they produced strikingly different sugar profiles reflecting their distinct substrate specificities. DPM-CA, acting on cellulose, released substantial Glc (10,467.64 mg/kg), confirming that the cellulosic framework itself represents a significant glucosyl reservoir. The additional detection of glucuronic acid (20.20 mg/kg) indicated deeper degradation of complex cell wall polysaccharides. DPM-HA, by contrast, showed preference for hemicellulosic side chains, significantly enriching arabinose (Ara, 24.76 mg/kg) and galacturonic acid (78.92 mg/kg). Ara is a pentose with a higher open-chain proportion than hexoses, conferring markedly greater Maillard reactivity (Martins et al., 2000). Its complete disappearance after heating (DPM-RHA, 0.00 mg/kg) validates its near-quantitative consumption during thermal reaction. The most remarkable performance was observed with DPM-PA, which triggered a non-linear “monosaccharide burst”. Glc reached 33,082.73 mg/kg (>60-fold over the control), accompanied by the highest levels of Ara (195.47 mg/kg) and xylose (154.10 mg/kg). This exceeds what pectin hydrolysis alone can explain, as pectin is not primarily a Glc polymer. We propose a multi-factor mechanism: (i) pectinase-mediated degradation of the homogalacturonan backbone destabilizes the middle lamella, facilitating protease and endogenous enzyme access to starch granules and cellulose; (ii) ancillary hemicellulase and cellulase activities in the commercial pectinase preparation contribute to xyloglucan and cellulose breakdown (Benoit et al., 2012); and (iii) the resulting precursor microenvironment uniquely combines a massive Glc pool for pyrazine synthesis with high pentose ratios for furan pathway activation (Perez Locas & Yaylayan, 2004).

The extent and selectivity of sugar consumption during roasting provide a direct readout of Maillard reaction flux. DPM-PA exhibited the highest absolute Glc consumption (∼29,203 mg/kg), consistent with its superior capacity for generating pyrazines. More importantly, pentoses (Ara and xylose) were virtually exhausted in all groups where they were present, despite far lower initial concentrations than Glc. This near-complete utilization reflects the faster reaction of pentoses, which preferentially generate furfural and related caramel-type furan derivatives via the 3-deoxyosone pathway (Martins et al., 2000; Perez Locas & Yaylayan, 2004). This explains why the PA and HA treatments, which liberate both hexoses and pentoses, produce oils with simultaneous roasted, nutty, and sweet caramel dimensions that are difficult to achieve through traditional roasting alone.

The structural integrity of peanut cotyledon cells, encapsulated by a robust network of cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, serves as the primary barrier to the release of intracellular proteins. Conventional thermal or mechanical processes often fail to fully exploit this protein reservoir (Liu et al., 2020). Our results suggest that the synergistic action of cell wall-degrading enzymes and proteases effectively bypasses this limitation. DPM-HA and DPM-CA catalyze the hydrolysis of β-1,4-glycosidic bonds in cellulose and xylan backbones, leading to the disintegration of the primary cell wall. In the CA group, the substantial release of hydrophobic amino acids like phenylalanine (Phe, 1627.05 mg/kg) and leucine (Leu, 776.77 mg/kg) indicates a high degree of protein mobilization from the lipid-protein colloid system (Alzagtat & Alli, 2002). Pectin is predominantly located in the middle lamella of the peanut cell wall, where it functions as a “cementing agent” that adheres adjacent cells together. Upon degradation of the homogalacturonan chains by PA, the peanut tissue underwent extensive disintegration. This not only facilitated the release of physically entrapped proteins but also likely enhanced the diffusion efficiency of hydrolyzed products by reducing the viscosity of the aqueous matrix (Khatri et al., 2015). The lysine (Lys) content in the PA group reached 618.86 mg/kg, representing a 12-fold increase over the control. This elevated concentration of basic amino acids serves as a primary driving force in the initial stages of the Maillard reaction. α-Amylase and glucoamylase primarily target the carbohydrate components of peanuts. Although peanuts have low starch content, the DPM-AA and DPM-GA yielded 8593.03 and 8357.39 mg/kg of total FAAs, respectively. This suggests that the degradation of structural polysaccharides and oligosaccharides by α-amylase and amyloglucosidase further disrupts the cellular matrix, releasing proteins otherwise trapped in the carbohydrate-protein network.

The Maillard reaction does not consume all amino acids uniformly; its selectivity toward amino acids with different structures influences the final aroma profile of peanut oil. Phe serves as the primary precursor for the sweet aroma in fragrant peanut oil. Through Strecker degradation, it generates phenylacetaldehyde, a key active aroma component in peanut oil (Ma et al., 2025). In DPM-PA, the initial Phe content was 1953.44 mg/kg, which sharply decreased to 1185.35 mg/kg after the reaction. This significant depletion predicts that PA treatment can enhance the sweet aroma of the oil. In contrast, Tyr also exhibited a high consumption rate across various enzyme treatment groups. For example, in the GA group, it decreased from 848.99 mg/kg to 660.58 mg/kg. Its degradation products play a complementary role in constructing the floral aroma. Arginine and Lys are pivotal for the synthesis of alkyl-pyrazines, which are the hallmark odorants of “roasted” and “nutty” flavor in hot-pressed peanut oil (Sun et al., 2024). Arginine consumption was particularly high in the DPM-AA (682.2 mg/kg, 59.5% reduction), while Lys showed a dramatic 46.8% reduction in the DPM-PA. Given that pyrazines have low odor thresholds, this indicates a robust generation of the characteristic peanutty aroma. Glutamic acid consistently accounts for a high proportion across all treatment groups. In DPM-PA, the Glu content decreased from 1318.76 mg/kg to 991.50 mg/kg. Glu not only participates in the Maillard reaction to produce pyrazines and pyrroles, but the remaining portion can also impart a subtle umami background aroma to the oil, enhancing the depth of flavor (Zhang et al., 2022). Notably, an increase in Asp content was observed in some reaction groups (e.g., from 150.38 mg/kg to 224.33 mg/kg in DPM-BA), likely due to the deamidation of asparagine and/or the thermal degradation of residual peptides under hydrothermal conditions.

Volatile flavor compounds

The volatile profile of roasted peanut oil comprises a complex array of chemical constituents shaped predominantly by thermal processing and enzymatic pretreatments applied prior to roasting. As shown in Fig. 2A and Table S7, 184 volatile compounds were identified across the control and enzyme-treated samples, encompassing alcohols (18), aldehydes (20), alkenes (9), esters and acids (8), furanones and lactones (19), furans (22), sulfur- and nitrogen-containing heterocycles (10), ketones (36), phenols (3), pyrazines (22), pyrroles (10), sulfides (5), and others (2). Total volatile concentrations varied substantially among treatments, with the PO-RPA and PO-RAA exhibiting the highest overall abundance, particularly of Maillard-derived heterocyclic species. This enhancement suggests that enzymatic degradation of cell wall pectin and the protein matrix effectively increased the accessibility and pool size of flavor precursors, namely FAAs and reducing sugars, which subsequently participated in Strecker degradation and browning reactions during roasting. Impact of enzyme pretreatments on Maillard reaction products

A primary objective of enzymatic pretreatment was to amplify the desirable “roasted peanut” and “nutty” sensory aromas. The results demonstrate that PO-RPA and PO-RAA markedly promoted the accumulation of pyrazines and furans. The PO-RAA achieved the peak concentrations for core pyrazines, with 2,5-dimethylpyrazine reaching 22,102.67 μg/kg and methylpyrazine at 15,605.78 μg/kg. Moreover, the total pyrazine concentration in the PO-RPA was approximately 13.8-fold higher than that in the PO-RBA. Notably, 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, a quintessential marker of roasted aroma, increased sharply from 1260.38 μg/kg in PO-RBA to 17,399.53 μg/kg in PO-RPA. Likewise, levels of methylpyrazine and 2,6-dimethylpyrazine were substantially elevated in the PO-RPA and PO-RAA, contributing to robust nutty and roasted aromas.

The PO-RGA exerted a distinctive influence on ketone and furanone formation. Although pyrazine concentrations were also elevated, this group was particularly characterized by a high level of 2,3-pentanedione (1690.48 μg/kg), a compound known to impart buttery and caramel-like aromas. PO-RHA exhibited a significant accumulation of 2,3-butanedione (1567.45 μg/kg), which reached an ROAV of 100. Furans such as furfural and 5-methyl-2-furancarboxaldehyde followed a similar trend, with the PO-RPA and PO-RGA showing the highest concentrations, thereby supplying almond-like and caramel aromas that balanced the intense roasted aromas. These findings underscore the synergistic roles of pectinase in loosening the peanut cell wall architecture and of amylase in augmenting the pool of carbonyl reactants, both of which constitute rate-limiting steps for pyrazine and furan synthesis.

ROAV analysis and contribution to sensory attributes

Relative odor activity values (ROAV) were employed to identify compounds exerting the greatest impact on sensory perception. As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 2B, compounds with ROAV ≥1 were designated as key odorants. In the PO-RBA, hexanal (ROAV, 100) and 1-octen-3-ol (ROAV, 25.19) were the predominant contributors, accounting for the prevalence of “green, grassy, and beany” off-flavors in the untreated sample. The ROAV of hexanal, however, decreased markedly to 2.48 in the PO-RPA and 3.32 in the PO-RAA, whereas that of 1-octen-3-ol was reduced to 0.81 in the PO-RPA and 0.45 in the PO-RGA. These reductions indicate that enzymatic pretreatment not only contributes to the formation of desirable aromas but also effectively suppresses off-flavors. The “roasted peanut” and “nutty” aromas in the PO-RPA and PO-RAA were primarily driven by 3-methylbutanal (ROAV, 100 in both groups) and pyrazines such as 2,5-dimethylpyrazine. 3-Methylbutanal, formed via Strecker degradation of Leu, contributes malty and fruity aromas that are essential for a complex roasted aroma profile. Interestingly, the PO-RHA was characterized by a dominant “butter-like” aroma, with 2,3-butanedione reaching an ROAV of 100. With respect to savory and sulfurous aromas, methanethiol (ROAV, 100 in PO-RCA; 37.30 in PO-RPA) and dimethyl disulfide were identified as key contributors, presumably liberated through proteolytic action on sulfur-containing proteins. In the PO-RGA group, the sweet and buttery profile was dominated by 2,3-pentanedione (ROAV, 100), highlighting the capacity of glucoamylase treatment to steer the aroma profile toward caramel attributes.

Regulatory mechanism of multi-enzyme modulation

The observed shifts in the volatile landscape illustrate the precision with which enzymatic modulation can tailor peanut oil flavor. The combined action of pectinase and protease (PO-RPA) and amylase and protease (PO-RAA and PO-RGA) proved most effective for developing a traditional roasted aroma, as reflected in the maximization of pyrazine concentrations. Pectinase-mediated disruption of the middle lamella facilitated more efficient hydrolysis of storage proteins by proteases into low-molecular-weight peptides and FAAs. These nitrogenous precursors subsequently condensed with sugars, derived either from amylolytic activity or from native carbohydrate degradation, to generate the core roasted flavor fingerprint (Xu et al., 2023).

To further delineate the relationships among oil samples based on their key aroma-active compounds, an unsupervised hierarchical cluster analysis (Z-score by row) was performed on the 28 key odorants (ROAV ≥1). As shown in Fig. 2C, the dendrogram revealed that PO-RCA and PO-RGA clustered closely together, both characterized by elevated levels of furfural, benzeneacetaldehyde, and several pyrazines including trimethylpyrazine and 2-ethyl-5-methylpyrazine. PO-RPA and PO-RHA formed a second distinct cluster, distinguished by particularly high abundances of 2,3-butanedione and 2,3-pentanedione, along with shared enrichment in 1-octen-3-ol and nonanal. Notably, PO-RBA segregated completely from all enzyme-treated samples, driven by the exclusive presence or markedly higher relative abundances of lipid-derived off-odorants such as 1-butanol, 1-heptanol, and dimethyl trisulfide, as well as a conspicuous absence of the Strecker aldehyde 3-methylbutanal and the sulfur compound methanethiol. PO-RAA occupied an intermediate position, sharing some characteristics with the control (e.g., elevated hexanal and pentanal) yet also displaying enhanced pyrazine levels characteristic of enzyme-treated oils.

UpSet analysis further revealed that while a core set of 10 compounds was shared among all samples, enzyme-treated groups uniquely acquired 2,3-pentanedione, 3-methylbutanal, methanethiol, and 2,3-butanedione, compounds contributing sweet, malty, and savory nuances absent in the control (Fig. 2C). Concurrently, the reduction of green-associated volatiles such as hexanal and 1-octen-3-ol implies that enzymatic treatments may inhibit lipoxygenase-mediated pathways (Xu et al., 2023).

Sensory attributes

Fig. 2D revealed that targeted enzymatic pretreatments markedly reshaped the flavor profile of peanut oil. The PO-RBA exhibited a relatively high “nutty” aroma (7.45), but showed limited intensity in “savory” and “caramel” attributes (6.35 and 5.45, respectively), accompanied by noticeable “green” (2.55) and “fermented” (2.45) off-flavors. This suggests that the absence of enzymatic pretreatment in PO-RBA left the rigid plant cell matrix largely intact, thereby restricting the liberation of essential Maillard precursors and retaining undesirable native volatiles. In contrast, the incorporation of polysaccharide-degrading enzymes led to a clear differentiation of sensory attributes, indicating that modulation of carbohydrate-derived precursors plays a decisive role in directing Maillard-derived flavor formation.

Among the treatments, PO-RCA most effectively enhanced the “roasted peanut” attribute (7.45 vs. 6.65 in PO-RBA). This improvement can be attributed to the disruption of the cellulose-based cell wall framework, which increases matrix permeability and facilitates the exposure of oil body-associated proteins to proteolysis (Gonzalez-Gonzalez & Miranda-Lopez, 2022). The resulting increase in FAAs, together with Glc released from cellulose hydrolysis, likely contributes to Strecker degradation and subsequent formation of alkylpyrazines, key contributors to roasted aroma. Therefore, cellulase pretreatment appears to optimize the balance of Maillard precursors, leading to a more intense and characteristic roasted flavor.

PO-RPA exhibited the highest savory intensity (5.00), accompanied by elevated caramel and burnt aromas. Pectin hydrolysis liberates neutral sugar moieties (particularly Ara and galactose) from side chains, which serve as reactive reducing sugars in Maillard reactions (Yu et al., 2023). These sugars, in combination with amino acids like cysteine, promote formation of sulfur-containing heterocycles (e.g., thiophenes, furanthiols) responsible for meaty aromas, while nitrogen-containing compounds (e.g., alkylpyrazines) contribute roasted/caramel aromas. The concurrent intensification of burnt aroma indicates progression into advanced Maillard stages, likely due to elevated reducing sugar concentration from pectin degradation. This underscores the necessity for precise time-temperature control following enzymatic pretreatment to optimize desirable aroma development while minimizing undesirable pyrolytic off-flavors (Hu, Li, Yu, et al., 2023).

In contrast, hemicellulase and amylolytic treatments mainly contributed to “sweet” and “caramel” attributes. The PO-RHA sample showed the highest “sweet” score (3.95), likely due to the release of pentoses such as xylose and Ara, which are known to exhibit higher reactivity than hexoses and preferentially generate furfural and related caramel-like compounds. Between the amylase treatments, PO-RGA outperformed PO-RAA, reflecting its ability to produce Glc efficiently, thereby providing a more efficient reducing sugar source for caramelization and Maillard reactions.

Interestingly, a general decrease in “nutty” perception was observed in all enzyme-treated samples compared to PO-RBA. This trend likely reflects a shift in flavor dominance rather than a quality loss. As more reactive precursors are released, the formation of potent Maillard-derived volatiles (e.g., pyrazines and furans) becomes dominant, masking the simpler nutty background. This indicates a transition from intrinsic raw-like aroma to a more complex, thermally developed flavor profile.

In addition to flavor enhancement, enzymatic pretreatments markedly suppressed off-flavors. The “green” score dropped to 1.00 in PO-RPA, which can be attributed to both the antioxidant activity of pectinase-released phenolics that inhibit hexanal formation (Stanek-Wandzel et al., 2024), and the masking effect of strong savory/caramel notes. The “fermented” attribute reached as low as 0.85 in PO-RGA and PO-RCA, suggesting that controlled carbon source release favors the Maillard pathway over off-flavor-generating microbial fermentation or non-enzymatic degradation, thereby ensuring aroma purity.

Correlation analysis between aroma-active compounds and sensory attributes

As shown in Fig. 3A, Pearson correlation analysis was performed to quantitatively delineate the molecular relationships between enzymatically liberated flavor precursors (reducing sugars and FAAs) and the generation of key aroma-active volatile compounds in roasted peanut oil. Among carbohydrate precursors, Glc exhibited strong positive correlations with the formation of roasted/nutty pyrazines, specifically methylpyrazine (r, 0.7795) and 2,5-dimethylpyrazine (r, 0.7563), consistent with a role as the primary carbon skeleton donor for the α-aminocarbonyl intermediates involved in pyrazine synthesis (Van Lancker et al., 2010). In stark contrast, Suc displayed significant negative correlations (r < −0.8) with major pyrazines, substantiating that native Suc functions merely as a non-participating reservoir that requires prior enzymatic hydrolysis to release reactive reducing sugars (Shi et al., 2024). Notably, Ara, a pentose liberated predominantly by pectinase and hemicellulase treatments, demonstrated a uniquely high positive correlation with methanethiol (r, 0.8999, p < 0.05), underscoring the role of pentose-derived dicarbonyl intermediates in promoting the Strecker degradation of sulfur-containing amino acids and thereby contributing to savory, meat-like aroma attributes. Regarding amino acid precursors, Leu correlated positively with 3-methylbutanal (r, 0.6586), imparting malty aromas, while Phe showed a significant positive association with benzaldehyde (r, 0.7082), accounting for the pronounced sweet and floral aroma characteristics observed in pectinase-treated oils. Negative correlations further illuminated the competitive interplay between Maillard-driven aroma generation and lipid oxidation pathways. The inverse association between hexanal (a well-established rancidity marker) and certain amino acids (e.g., Thr, r, −0.1490) supports the hypothesis that advanced Maillard reaction products, particularly melanoidins, exert antioxidant effects that suppress the formation of off-flavor aldehydes. Concurrently, Glc showed a strong negative correlation with dimethyl trisulfide (r, −0.8137), which may reflect competition between sulfur volatile formation and other Maillard pathways that produce pyrazines and brown pigments. At the same time, Glc was positively correlated with (E)-2-heptenal (r, 0.5571), suggesting that some unsaturated aldehydes can form directly from sugar breakdown during high-temperature heating, not just from fat oxidation.

A correlation network was constructed to visualize relationships between key aroma-active volatiles and sensory attributes (Fig. 3B). The network comprised 29 volatile compounds (ROAV ≥1) and 8 sensory descriptors, revealing three distinct modules. A “roasted/nutty” module was densely populated by pyrazines (e.g., 2,5-dimethylpyrazine, methylpyrazine) and strongly connected to “roasted peanut” and “nutty” aromas, supporting pyrazines as key contributors of the characteristic aroma in enzyme-treated oils (PO-RPA, PO-RAA). A “green/fermented” module, dominated by lipid-derived aldehydes (hexanal, nonanal) and alcohols (1-octen-3-ol), was prominent in the control (PO-RBA) but diminished upon enzymatic pretreatment, consistent with the suppression of off-flavors observed in ROAV analysis. A third “savory” module, anchored by sulfur compounds (methanethiol, dimethyl disulfide) and diketones (2,3-butanedione, acetoin), was particularly enhanced in PO-RHA and PO-RGA, reflecting the contribution of sulfur amino acid degradation and alternative Maillard pathways.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that targeted enzymatic pretreatment of defatted peanut meal, particularly with pectinase-protease or α-amylase-protease combinations, is an effective strategy for directing Maillard-driven flavor formation in peanut oil. Pectinase-protease pretreatment triggered a synergistic “monosaccharide burst,” simultaneously expanding the pools of glucose, arabinose, and xylose, while α-amylase-protease treatment efficiently mobilized starch reserves to generate a glucose-dominant precursor environment. These distinct precursor signatures were associated with divergent volatile profiles: the hexose-rich environment was associated with extensive pyrazine formation (roasted, nutty aromas), whereas pentose availability was associated with furan pathway activation (caramel, sweet notes). Critically, both strategies concurrently suppressed lipid-derived off-flavors (hexanal, 1-octen-3-ol), indicating that the enhanced Maillard reaction may competitively consume reactive carbonyl and amine precursors that would otherwise be channeled into lipid oxidation pathways. The decrease of roasting temperature could also play a big role in the suppressed lipid oxidation. Pearson correlation analysis and network visualization further supported that the type and ratio of reducing sugars and free amino acids are key factors of the final sensory profile. In contrast to conventional roasting, which relies on empirical process control, this approach of tailoring the precursor composition provides a rational basis for modulating peanut oil aroma, although further optimization studies are needed to establish predictive design rules. These findings contribute to the mechanistic understanding relevant to the industrial application of enzyme-assisted flavor modulation in edible oil processing, with practical implications for producing high-quality oils with customized sensory attributes.
