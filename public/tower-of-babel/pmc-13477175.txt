Implementing Pharmacogenomics in Contemporary Hospital Care: Insights from Multidisciplinary Knowledge Exchange
Authors: Safeera Y Hussainy, Rachel Conyers, Paul A James, Marliese Alexander, Claire Moore, Susan Fisher, Michelle Gerstman, Aaron K Wong, Stephanie Chau, Amy Clarke, Jennifer Philip, Carl M J Kirkpatrick, Senthil Lingaratnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPharmacogenomics (PGx) is increasingly recognised as a cornerstone of personalised medicine, with established recommendations for many commonly prescribed medicines. Despite this, translation into real-world clinical practice remains inconsistent, reflecting persistent implementation and health-system gaps.ObjectiveTo provide a multidisciplinary perspective on the system-level conditions required to normalise PGx within contemporary hospital care, drawing on structured knowledge exchange and priority-setting across clinical, digital and governance domains.MethodsA multidisciplinary knowledge exchange program convened clinicians, pharmacists, genetic services, informatics teams, researchers and health-service leaders across oncology, transplantation, peri-operative care and palliative care. Knowledge was exchanged through presentations, informing the generation and ranking of shared priorities in groups and individually using the Nominal Group Technique. Aggregate scores were calculated, and priorities further refined. Presentation insights were synthesised using a narrative, interpretive approach, to identify cross-cutting translational themes.ResultsEleven shared priorities were identified across four broad critical levers for sustainable integration: (1) computable, reusable PGx data embedded within electronic medical records (EMR); (2) coordinated and equitable testing and prescribing pathways; (3) workforce capability and stewardship; and (4) governance. Equity, access and data sovereignty were highlighted as foundational to responsible scale-up, particularly for First Nations peoples and under-represented communities.ConclusionInsights from this knowledge-exchange program highlight the feasibility and clinical relevance of PGx, and identify workforce capability, EMR integration, and system-level governance as critical implementation levers of adoption. Coordinated national investment and policy alignment will be central to realising the full value of PGx.Keywords: pharmacogenomics, clinical implementation, health-systems integration, electronic medical records

Introduction

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) has progressed from niche application to a core enabler of safer, more personalised medicines use across oncology and complex care.1,2 Increasingly, hospital inpatients are exposed to medications for which a PGx recommendation is available, with heightened risk of medication-related harm likely to occur in paediatric and geriatric patients, those with polypharmacy and those undergoing intensive systemic therapy. Concurrently, escalating health-system pressures amplify the importance of avoiding preventable toxicity and treatment failure.3,4 However, despite the availability of established PGx recommendations for many commonly prescribed medicines, implementation gaps persist across health services, limiting the consistent availability, interpretation and application of PGx information in clinical practice.

Translation of PGx into routine care requires more than evidence of drug-gene associations; it demands coordinated implementation, workforce readiness, digital enablement, equitable access, and policy and funding alignment. To support this shift, a multidisciplinary knowledge-exchange (KE) program brought together clinicians, researchers and consumer representatives across oncology and haematology, solid organ transplantation, palliative care, peri-operative medicine and health-system transformation from adult and paediatric metropolitan hospitals and academic centres from the Parkville Precinct, in Victoria. KE is a bidirectional process in which social scientists and individuals or organisations share learning, ideas and experiences.5 Encouraging dialogue and collaboration between “communities” ie, researchers and the private, public and civil society sectors, KE helps research to influence policy and practice.6

This Perspective synthesises translational insights arising from a multidisciplinary PGx KE initiative. Drawing on clinical exemplars, implementation experience and structured priority-setting discussions, it explores the conditions required to support sustainable integration of PGx into contemporary hospital care and highlights implications for policy, practice and future implementation efforts.

Approach and Context

A structured KE event was convened at the Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre (Melbourne) on 12 June 2025. The program was designed to support bidirectional learning, translation of practice-based experience, and identification of shared priorities for PGx implementation across oncology and complex care contexts.

Design and Purpose

KE was adopted as a translational methodology, recognising that PGx implementation requires integration of research evidence, clinical experience, digital capability, and organisational change processes. A modified version of the National Collaborating Centre for Methods and Tools five-step knowledge exchange checklist7 was followed.

The program was co-designed and co-chaired by pharmacy and medical leads, with explicit aims to share practice-based insights from clinical PGx programs across multiple specialties; surface contextual enablers and barriers to implementation; integrate perspectives from workforce, governance and digital health domains; and co-produce system-oriented priorities and next-step actions through facilitated dialogue. The event followed a multisession format incorporating structured presentations, panel discussion, facilitated roundtables, and deliberative prioritisation activities.

Participants

Participants were invited from across the Parkville Precinct, a designated central biomedical and research hub in Melbourne that brings together leading clinical services, academic institutions and research organisations within a world-class health and innovation district. Participating health services included Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre, Royal Melbourne Hospital, Royal Children’s Hospital, Royal Women’s Hospital, and collaborative groups included the Collaborative Centre for Genomic Cancer Medicine and Melbourne Genomics Health Alliance, as well as academic partners from the University of Melbourne and Monash University. Participants comprised physicians, pharmacists, clinical geneticists, informaticians, pathologists, researchers and health-service leaders working across paediatric and adult oncology, solid organ transplantation medicine, perioperative care, palliative care, digital health and electronic medical record (EMR) teams and implementation research. The multidisciplinary composition was designed to support cross-boundary learning, integration of diverse expertise, and translation of PGx experience across clinical and organisational settings.

Session Structure and KE Activities

The morning program focused on clinical implementation case exemplars and emerging research insights (Sessions 1–2). Presentations covered PGx implementation in paediatric and adult oncology, solid organ transplantation medicine, peri-operative care, palliative care, pharmacist-led education and capability building, EMR-enabled PGx workflows and governance considerations in genomic medicine. Each block incorporated moderated Q&A to enable bidirectional exchange between presenters and participants, encouraging articulation of real-world implementation challenges.

The afternoon program (Session 3) involved facilitated round-table group work, using the Nominal Group Technique,8 a quantitative methodology, to translate shared learning into collective strategic priorities and actions. Activities included: (1) independent identification of opportunities for PGx implementation by participants within facilitated round-table groups; (2) collation and thematic consolidation of ideas generated across groups into a shared list of candidate priorities; (3) individual silent ranking of priorities using the Nominal Group Technique to minimise anchoring bias and ensure equitable contribution; (4) aggregation of individual rankings to generate overall priority scores; and (5) facilitated discussion to refine the wording, scope and feasibility considerations of highly ranked priorities.

The session concluded with prioritisation of implementation opportunities and identification of strategic areas for future precinct-wide planning and collaboration.

Data Sources and Synthesis Approach

Data comprised: presenter slide decks and session materials; the program agenda and session design; and outputs from KE discussions as shared and documented during the event. Insights were synthesised using a narrative, interpretive approach, consistent with the purpose of KE. Key cross-cutting themes were identified through post-event iterative review of materials and triangulation across sessions and disciplines.

Scope and Nature of Evidence

Reported insights reflect a combination of findings from peer-reviewed or ongoing clinical studies presented at the event, and practice-based implementation learning, and experiential knowledge contributed by participants. Where applicable, interim or implementation-stage findings are presented as KE insights rather than definitive evaluative outcomes, consistent with the translational intent of the program.

Outcomes of Round-Table Priority Ranking for PGx Adoption

Highest-Priority Themes

1. Improving accessibility and visibility of PGx information across the health service (highest overall score).

The top-ranked priority focused on ready access to PGx results and prescribing guidance within routine workflows, including standardised reports, consistent terminology, improved discoverability of prior test results, and flagging of PGx-tested patients across care settings. Participants identified this as foundational for safety, continuity of care, and reduction of duplicate testing, aligning closely with the broader digital-enablement themes raised during the event.

2. Evaluating models for centralised and scalable testing and prescribing pathways.

The second-ranked priority related to centralisation of PGx testing and prescribing support, including definition of referral pathways, development of shared protocols, and identification of patient cohorts who may benefit from pre-emptive testing including generation of evidence for priority populations such as First Nations Peoples and CALD communities where there is limited ancestry-specific pharmacogenomic evidence. This reflects a desire to move away from fragmented, project-specific approaches toward coordinated, equitable service models.

3. Mapping capability and expertise across the precinct.

The third-ranked priority emphasised identification of existing PGx skillsets, experts and active projects, together with creation of a shared repository of research and implementation activity. Participants viewed this as necessary infrastructure to avoid duplication, enable collaboration, and support efficient mobilisation of expertise for emerging initiatives.

Priorities relating to national and international collaboration and establishing a PGx Centre of Research Excellence received lower relative scores. Discussion

This Perspective is the first published account of a pharmacogenomics-focused knowledge exchange initiative within an Australian health-service context. It highlights how multidisciplinary dialogue can function as a translational mechanism for identifying the clinical, digital, workforce and governance conditions required to normalise PGx within hospital systems.

Across the various exemplars, participants described high clinical actionability of PGx-guided prescribing and growing feasibility of pharmacist-led implementation pathways, accompanied by evidence of reduced treatment-related toxicity, improved symptom control and more individualised prescribing. These observations are consistent with international implementation studies demonstrating the feasibility and clinical utility of PGx in routine care.1,9 Wider adoption has been supported by recommendations from the Royal College of Pathologists of Australasia (RCPA),2,10 alongside targeted Commonwealth investment through the Medical Research Future Fund as articulated by the Australian Government Department of Health’s Genomic Health Futures Mission11 and recent Medicare reimbursement for select PGx tests (eg, DPYD and HLA).

A consistent message was that the value of PGx depends not only on the availability of genomic information, but also on the systems and processes that enable its effective application. Genotype alone is often insufficient to predict drug response, as phenoconversion arising from drug-drug interactions, comorbidities, inflammation and other clinical factors can alter functional metaboliser status and modify the relevance of genotype-informed recommendations.12–14 Consequently, PGx interpretation requires integration of genomic, pharmacological and clinical information and cannot be viewed as a purely laboratory-based activity.

Participants also highlighted broader implementation barriers that limit the longitudinal utility of PGx. Fragmented service models, often developed within individual specialties, research programs or institutions, can result in duplication of effort, inconsistent access to testing, variable interpretation practices and poor continuity of genomic information across episodes of care.1,15 These challenges are compounded by reliance on paper-based reports and static PDF laboratory results, which, while suitable for communicating individual test findings, restrict discoverability, reuse and integration with prescribing workflows. The inability to store PGx information as structured, computable data within electronic medical records limits opportunities for automated clinical decision support, future prescribing alerts and health-system learning.16 Successful implementation therefore requires genomic information to be embedded within clinical workflows so that it is visible, readily interpretable and actionable at the point of prescribing and throughout the patient journey.

Across these domains - clinical interpretation, service integration and digital enablement - pharmacists emerged as key stewards of PGx implementation. Their ability to integrate pharmacology, genomics, health informatics and implementation science positions them to support clinical decision-making, optimise models of care, contribute to governance and quality assurance, and translate PGx recommendations into usable clinical workflows.17,18 As PGx moves from pilot programs toward routine care, pharmacist leadership will be central to ensuring genomic information is effectively embedded within contemporary healthcare systems.

Realising the full value of PGx will require coordinated investment in digital infrastructure, stewardship, workforce capability and equitable service models. Pharmacists are well positioned to lead this translation, working alongside clinicians, genetic services, informatics teams and policymakers to embed PGx into routine care and support safe, sustainable implementation at scale.
