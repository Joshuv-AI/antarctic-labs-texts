Beyond childlessness: a qualitative exploration of the psychosocial impact of infertility among women in Southern Ghana
Authors: Deborah Armah, Josephine Kyei, Samuel Kwabena Ansong-Aggrey, Florence Naab
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivePregnancy and childbearing immediately after marriage are often viewed as an indication of womanhood and parenthood within the African context. This study explored the psychosocial impacts of infertility among women in Southern Ghana, seeking to answer the research question: ‘How do women in Southern Ghana experience the psychosocial impacts of infertility?’DesignA qualitative exploratory descriptive design was employed. Data were collected at a designated hospital using a semi-structured interview guide.Settings and participantsThe study was conducted in the obstetrics and gynaecology outpatient department of one of the public district hospitals in the Weija-Gbawe subdistrict in Ghana, where 20 women diagnosed with either primary or secondary infertility were interviewed for 45 min to 1 hour each. With all participants completing the study, interviews were audiotaped with consent, transcribed verbatim and analysed using content analysis.ResultsThe findings indicate that women experiencing infertility faced multiple psychosocial challenges, including depression, anxiety, reduced sexual desire, stigma, marital instability and deliberate social isolation.ConclusionInfertility significantly compromises women’s psychosocial well-being, leading to pervasive negative experiences across social and personal contexts. These challenges adversely affect quality of life and overall well-being, with potential implications for their reproductive outcomes.

Background

Infertility is commonly defined as a disorder of the male or female reproductive system, characterised by the inability to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular, unprotected and well-timed sexual intercourse without contraceptive use.1–3

Infertility impacts millions of individuals and couples worldwide, with an estimated rate and global prevalence of 186 million and 9–12.5%, respectively, with the highest prevalence in countries in Southeast Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.1 4–6 Individuals and couples within low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) record the highest rates of infertility, often with devastating consequences.1 4–6 In these settings, infertility carries devastating consequences, underscoring its relevance to achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3, which emphasises health and well-being for all.1

In many societies, particularly within LMICs, there is a strong expectation for women to conceive shortly after marriage or the initiation of cohabitation. Failure to meet this expectation often exposes women to intense psychosocial pressures that threaten marital stability, generate family and community tension and invite scrutiny from friends, in-laws and society at large.7–9

Issues surrounding infertility are linked with numerous negative psychological and social consequences for individuals and couples, especially women, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. These women encounter stigma, marital instability, abuse and financial hardship.5 8–10

In the African setting, high value is placed on pregnancy and childbearing, of which it is generally believed that having biological children is considered key to parenthood and an avenue to gaining respect, recognition and community acceptance in most homes, society and the community at large.5 8–10 Consequently, women who are unable to conceive are often perceived as socially incomplete, leading to exclusion and diminished social belonging.

Regarding the accepted norm and belief that having biological children is an important component and an indication of a lifetime achievement, and the fact that children are recognised as a means to stabilise one’s status in the family and within the community they live, failure to fulfil societal expectations regarding childbearing implies that one must not feel belonged and accepted within the society/community they find themselves.11

Evidence consistently demonstrates that infertility is associated with profound psychosocial consequences for women, including stigma, marital instability, abuse, financial hardship, depression, stress, divorce and intimate partner violence.8–10 12–15 These experiences significantly alter women’s thoughts, beliefs, emotions and life trajectories, often resulting in feelings of worthlessness, disappointment, shame, despair, maladaptive coping and poor self-care.16–18

Under the influence of these intense negative emotions that occur in the process of grief, a woman may see herself as worthless and unable to control her life in terms of her physical and emotional capabilities.6 17 Considering the magnitude and complexity of these psychosocial challenges, infertility management must extend beyond biomedical treatment to incorporate holistic, psychosocial interventions.16 17 19 Against this backdrop, the present study explored the psychosocial experiences of women living with infertility in Southern Ghana.

Methods

Study design

The study employed a qualitative design using an exploratory approach to generate ideas from women diagnosed with infertility regarding their psychosocial experiences, using focused group discussions (FGDs).

Study setting

The study was conducted at the obstetrics and gynaecology outpatient department of a public district hospital in the Weija-Gbawe subdistrict, Ghana. The site was selected because it serves as a major healthcare and referral centre for the Ga South Municipality and surrounding communities with diverse ethnic backgrounds.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Women diagnosed with infertility, between 26 and 44 years old, who understood and spoke English, Ga or the Twi language were purposively sampled for the study. Women who appeared visibly distressed at the time of recruitment were excluded based on the research team’s assessment, even though this might have limited generalisation, further limiting important information.

Data collection

In line with the study objectives and informed by relevant literature, face-to-face FGDs were conducted with women diagnosed with infertility to explore their psychosocial experiences. A moderator experienced in qualitative research facilitated the discussions after receiving a clear orientation on the study topic. To enhance reflexivity, the researchers continuously reflected on their assumptions, experiences and potential biases throughout the research process. Additionally, regular discussions were held among the research team during data collection to ensure that the findings remained grounded in participants’ accounts rather than researchers’ preconceptions. Data collection commenced in January 2019 and ended in March 2019. Written informed consent was obtained from all eligible participants before data collection, following a detailed explanation of the study aims and procedures.

A semi-structured interview guide (online supplemental file), developed specifically for this study, was used to direct the discussions, with probing questions employed to elicit rich and in-depth data after a pilot study on a small group of women with infertility had been conducted to ensure the interview guide’s feasibility and appropriateness. The FGDs were conducted in the local dialect (Twi) across five sessions, each lasting approximately 40–45 min. Data saturation was achieved after the fourth FGD, as no new themes emerged.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using thematic content analysis in line with established guidelines.20 The researchers first identified recurring patterns across the interviews, while consciously bracketing preconceived assumptions about women’s psychosocial experiences of infertility. Transcripts were read repeatedly to ensure immersion in the data and accurate interpretation of participants’ meanings. Themes were generated manually using an inductive, semantic approach by identifying and labelling relevant data segments aligned with the study objectives.

The interview transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve familiarity with the data. Meaningful text segments relevant to the study objective were identified and assigned initial codes. Similar themes were then grouped into subthemes based on shared meanings and patterns. Through an iterative process of comparison and refinement, related themes were organised into subthemes, which were subsequently synthesised into overarching themes that captured participants’ experiences of infertility. The research team ensured that each theme authentically reflected participants’ lived experiences, with themes being mutually exclusive and clearly defined. Finally, representative verbatim quotations were selected to illustrate and substantiate each major theme. To maintain confidentiality, participants were identified using pseudonyms (PA, PB, PC and so forth).

The researchers ensured they frequently supervised and evaluated the analysis process to produce accurate and reliable results. Again, to enhance the credibility of the findings, the coding framework and emerging themes were reviewed by experienced experts to ensure consistency, coherence and alignment with the data.

Results

A total of 20 women participated in the focus group discussions. Participants’ ages ranged from 26 to 44 years, with a mean age of 34.2 years (SD=4.7). Most participants were aged between 30 and 39 years, with fewer participants in the younger and older age categories. With respect to employment, most participants were traders (55%), followed by caterers (10%) and beauticians (10%). Other occupations, including nursing, teaching, business, pharmacy assistance, pastoral work, seamstress and cosmetology, each accounted for 5% of the sample. The majority of participants identified as Christian (85%), while a smaller proportion were Muslim (15%). Regarding infertility type, more than half of the women had primary infertility (60%), whereas 40% experienced secondary infertility. In relation to parity, most participants had no children (60%), while 40% had one child. The duration of infertility diagnosis varied widely, ranging from 2 to 18 years, indicating prolonged exposure to infertility-related stressors among several participants. Theme 1: psychological experiences

The findings indicated that women diagnosed with infertility were psychologically burdened. Psychological experiences generated three main themes, and these include depression, anxiety and diminished desire for sex.

Feeling depressed

Feelings of depression were paramount in the lives of these women. Almost every participant had something to share as far as depression is concerned. Some women ascribed their depressive feelings to factors such as having been married for a couple of years and ageing, yet there was no sign of pregnancy. Others attributed theirs to the pressures and threats they received from their in-laws. Humiliation from colleagues, friends and husbands gave them much more reason to feel depressed.

Feelings of depression were associated with remarks from in-laws, as shown in the following quotations:

My in-laws! My in-laws will also never let me be … they always want to give me pressure, and they keep asking the very same question that makes me feel unhappy … “When are we going to see our grandchildren” …. In fact, the actions of my in-laws at times make me feel so sad, I have even regretted ever marrying. (sounding very emotional) (Group 1; PA)

Because of the maltreatment and the constant reminders from my mother-in-law about my inability to have children, I am always worried and depressed. She keeps bringing up the issue whenever she gets the chance, and it makes me feel inadequate and emotionally drained. I am always worried and depressed. (Group 4; PN)

Other participants associated their depressive state with how friends and colleagues treated them at home and at the workplace. Two participants shared their experiences in this regard:

At the workplace, some friends would not let me be, especially when they see me putting on something new. Some are like, “Do what is needed in life first … give your husband children…” Honestly, I always feel depressed anytime I am within my work premises. (crying) (Group 1; PB)

A few participants shared how the negative attitudes of their husbands affected their mood. One woman bitterly shared her experience in a flood of tears:

I am no longer happy … my husband now leaves home and comes back when he likes… He is no longer the loving husband I once got married to … hm… just recently, he almost went in for another woman to bear him children…. It only took the intervention of God to have his mind changed… (Group 2; PF)

My husband has gone out there to impregnate another woman …. He no longer provides for my needs… what even depresses me most is when I expect my husband to stay by me in times like this, but he has just refused to do so …. Unfortunately, my husband rather makes me feel terrible…. (Group 3; PJ)

Anxiety

Some women diagnosed with infertility felt they were ageing and feared they would become old without having their children. These fears were also coupled with issues like their marriages being on the verge of collapse or their husbands already dating other women. Participants who became worried and anxious because of advancing age shared their experiences as follows:

There is no reason for me not to be worried every day. I am concerned because I am advancing in age, and I fear that my reproductive years will soon come to an end. This thought makes me anxious about my chances of having a child. (Group 2; PE)

Madam …! If I were to be in my menopause, then I know I have no chance of bearing a child, and in that case, the story would have been different … but that is not the case now, and yet, I still cannot conceive. (Group 5; PP)

Other participants also shared how the sudden change in their husbands’ attitudes made them scared and anxious. Although they did not all use the words ‘scared’ or ‘anxious’ specifically, they appeared worried and concerned when they expressed themselves in this regard:

I am always worried and anxious …. I am scared my husband will remarry because he is so desperate to have his own children …. He could leave me for another woman at any point in time. (Group 2; PF)

Diminished desire for sex

Most participants expressed how they have a diminished desire for sex. Some participants said that if all these years of lovemaking had not made them pregnant, they did not see the need to engage in further sexual intercourse. A few participants also confessed that the mere fact that they could not get pregnant made their husbands refuse to make love with them, and this has contributed to a diminished desire for sex. Due to this and many other thoughts that run through their minds, the desire for sex became less important for them. Two participants shared their experiences:

I am always sad and worried … this problem I am fighting has taken away all my joy and happiness in life …. There is no longer quality sexual intimacy between us …. The fact is, all these years of lovemaking, nothing positive has come out of it … what again can I do? (Group 1; PB)

I do not even remember the last time my husband touched me. I have stopped calling my husband for sex as well, even when he attempts himself, I do not give in. I feel very unhappy now, I also do not see the need to be engaging in sexual affairs all these years, and yet still nothing has come out. (Group 5; PQ)

Theme 2: social experiences

Most of the respondents were embittered when they shared their social experiences. This was due to how colleagues at work, friends in the same neighbourhood, church members and leaders in the church treated them. The social experiences of these women were grouped under three subcategories: stigmatisation, marital instability and intentional isolation.

Stigmatisation

Stigmatisation of people with infertility is very common in many parts of Ghana. The findings revealed that women diagnosed with infertility were stigmatised at church by friends and others, at social gatherings, and anywhere they went where they were known. Most of the participants shared their experiences as evidenced in the following quotations:

At church, some of the women in my fellowship group constantly remind me that my time is passing by. Their comments make me worried and distressed. It is not easy at all. …. (Group 3; PJ)

One participant shared how she eventually stopped going to church because she could not take the pressure any longer:

Some church members will intentionally come to me as if they are sympathizing with me and ask sensitive questions about my delay in giving birth …. I have finally stopped the church for them … I need peace …. (Group 1; PB)

…………almost 12 years now, madam, I have never been happy …. I feel so ashamed and stigmatized. This is my fourth time being wedded, is it not shameful for a woman to wear a wedding gown for four consecutive times just because she could not fulfil her womanhood responsibility? (Group 4; PO)

Marital instability

Another disturbing finding under social experiences is the issue of marital instability. Almost all the participants revealed how their infertility affected their marriages and caused tension in their homes and relationships. A few participants shared that the presence of a child would have gone a long way to minimise such tensions at home. This is what some participants shared about marital instability:

My relationship with my husband has never been the same ever since this issue of childlessness popped up. My husband one day threatened me that he was going to try having children outside. He only listens to what his mother says. My mother-in-law is the one who dictates and instructs my husband now … she is my trouble now…. (Group 2; PB)

My husband now finds fault with me unnecessarily, especially when his friends pressure him to look elsewhere for a child. This often creates tension between us and leaves me feeling hurt and distressed. …. (Group 5; PP)

This participant shared with the group how bad her situation is compared as compared with everybody’s:

My bond with my husband is gradually dying off …. (crying) My husband has gone to impregnate another woman. He no longer cares for me. To talk about communication between us, that is also out …. (Group 4; PK)

Intentional isolation

Participants described engaging in intentional isolation by deliberately limiting their social interactions and participation in social gatherings to avoid unnecessary humiliation and embarrassment. This behaviour appeared to serve as a coping strategy aimed at avoiding stigma, intrusive questions and negative comments related to their infertility. For some women, intentional isolation also reflected the internalisation of societal expectations and feelings of inadequacy associated with childlessness. This is evidenced in the following quotations:

I have no option but to intentionally isolate myself and stop going to social events… I have now decided to stay back and ponder over issues rather than go to social events for friends to humiliate me at the end of the day and come back home worrying. (Group 1; PA)

I do not like to mingle with people, especially when it has to do with honouring programs or invitations. I am always worried about what friends might say about my challenge of childlessness. (Group 4; PL)

One participant shared how she has vowed to herself never to visit her family’s house again:

For the past eight years, I have stopped going to my family’s house and my hometown as well… the humiliations and the sensitive questions they kept asking each time I visited have put me off … due to that, I have just vowed not to visit my hometown again. (shouting) (Group 5; PT)

Discussion

Childbearing remains a highly valued social expectation. In many Ghanaian communities, motherhood is closely linked to feminine identity, social status, marital stability, lineage continuity and family acceptance. Consequently, women who experience infertility often face a lot of social atrocities, pressure from both family and community members and concerns regarding their marital relationships. These sociocultural expectations may further intensify feelings of inadequacy, social isolation and emotional distress among affected women.

Recent qualitative studies in Ghana similarly found that infertility contributed to loss of self-esteem, social isolation, marital instability and various forms of social suffering among women seeking fertility care.21

Although the psychosocial consequences of infertility have been widely documented, this study extends existing infertility literature by providing context-specific insights into how sociocultural and religious expectations in Southern Ghana influence the psychosocial experiences of women living with infertility.

Although anxiety, stigma and depression are well-established consequences of infertility, this study provides deeper insight into how these experiences are influenced by local expectations surrounding motherhood, lineage preservation and women’s social roles in Southern Ghana.

Among women with infertility in Southern Ghana, depressive symptoms emerged prominently, manifesting as persistent sadness, emotional emptiness, loss of interest and unhappiness. These experiences may be influenced by sociocultural expectations that position motherhood as a key marker of feminine identity, social status and marital fulfilment. Consequently, infertility can threaten women’s sense of self-worth and belonging within their families and communities, while pressures to bear children and preserve family lineage may intensify psychological distress. These findings suggest that the psychological burden of infertility is shaped not only by the inability to conceive but also by prevailing cultural norms and social expectations. The current findings corroborate three extant studies where the researchers revealed that infertility has an undesirable impact on the mental health of women. The researchers further attested that depression scores among women suffering from infertility were significantly higher than those of their counterparts who did not have issues with childbearing.9 22–24 Previous studies further attribute depressive symptoms to advancing age, prolonged duration of infertility, repeated treatment failures and delayed diagnosis.8 17 25 26

Women in this study expressed anxiety related to ageing without children, marital instability and the possibility of their husbands engaging in relationships with other women. These concerns may be understood within the sociocultural context of Southern Ghana, where childbearing is often viewed as a fundamental expectation of marriage and a key indicator of womanhood. Consequently, women experiencing infertility may perceive advancing age as diminishing their chances of fulfilling these expectations, thereby increasing fears of perpetual childlessness. These findings suggest that infertility-related anxiety is shaped not only by concerns about conception but also by the cultural and social expectations surrounding marriage, motherhood and family continuity. These findings are consistent with findings from other African studies.9 24 27 28

Again, women expressed fear related to ageing without children, marital instability and the possibility of their husbands engaging in relationships with other women. Anxiety manifested as persistent worry, emotional restlessness and fear of losing marital security.9 24 27 28

Participants additionally reported reduced sexual desire and frustration with sexual relationships, often questioning the purpose of continued sexual activity after prolonged unsuccessful attempts to conceive. Again, due to the strong sociocultural emphasis on childbearing in Southern Ghana, where sexual activity within marriage is often viewed as a means of achieving parenthood, repeated unsuccessful attempts to conceive may alter sexual intercourse into a stressful and goal-oriented activity, reducing intimacy and satisfaction.

An existing body of literature on issues of sexuality among women diagnosed with infertility has revealed that women diagnosed with infertility experienced sexual dysfunction, which was associated with factors like having been married for three or more years and not being able to conceive, coupled with a history of previous infertility treatment.29–31

It is worth noting again that the sociocultural expectations in Southern Ghana closely associate womanhood, social status and marital fulfilment with childbearing, making the experiences of stigma, humiliation and social isolation pervasive. Again, due to the cultural importance of family lineage and motherhood, the infertility experience fostered feelings of shame and prompted social withdrawal, thereby exacerbating the psychosocial burden of infertility. These women reported being stigmatised in churches, social gatherings and community settings, particularly when their infertility status was publicly known.8 10 15 These experiences were closely associated with feelings of shame, secrecy, diminished self-esteem and threats to psychosocial well-being, as documented in earlier studies.32 33

Another important finding was the effect of infertility on marital relationships among women in Southern Ghana. Marital instability further compounded participants’ psychosocial distress, with many reporting increased tension and strained relationships. Although husbands were often supportive following the initial diagnosis, this support frequently diminished over time when conception did not occur. Participants attributed these challenges to the strong cultural expectation that marriage should result in childbearing and family continuity. As infertility persisted, pressure from family and community members often led to frustration, disappointment and blame, thereby reducing spousal support and increasing marital conflict. Some participants believed that having a child would have alleviated much of the tension in their marriages. These findings suggest that marital strain is influenced not only by infertility itself but also by societal expectations surrounding parenthood and lineage preservation. Few studies have revealed that children are considered a social value and a necessary condition for married women. An inability to fulfil these societal pressures leads to deep pain and bitterness in most homes, leading to marital instability. Failure to meet societal expectations of childbearing often generates profound emotional pain and resentment within households, contributing to marital instability. As children are widely regarded as a central social and marital value, childlessness intensifies emotional distress and heightens the risk of marital discord, a finding consistent with previous studies.15 34 35

Finally, many women adopted deliberate social isolation as a coping response, avoiding social events such as parties and child-naming ceremonies to escape humiliation and hurtful remarks. This intentional withdrawal mirrors findings from previous Ghanaian studies, which similarly reported social avoidance as a response to infertility-related stigma.10 The findings reinforce the need to address infertility as both a biomedical and sociocultural issue with significant implications for women’s identity, relationships and psychosocial well-being. Additionally, integrating mental health professionals into fertility services may also be beneficial, as infertility-related distress frequently coexists with diverse psychosocial stressors.

This study used a focus group discussion to elicit rich, interactive data that enhanced understanding of participants’ shared experiences and perspectives. Despite the study’s numerous strengths, it had some limitations that may have reduced participants’ willingness to openly share sensitive or personal experiences in the presence of other group members. Additionally, the findings are specific to women with infertility in Southern Ghana and may not reflect experiences across other cultural and geographical contexts.

Implications for practice

Practice

At the service level, healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, midwives, reproductive health providers and primary healthcare workers, should receive training in infertility counselling, stigma-sensitive communication and psychosocial assessment to better address the emotional burden experienced by women with infertility.

Policy

Infertility should be recognised as a public health and reproductive health priority. In contrast, more emphasis should be placed on public education campaigns through community outreach, media platforms and primary healthcare services to help address misconceptions and reduce infertility-related stigma.

Research

Future research should evaluate the effectiveness of peer-support programmes, couple-based interventions, stigma-reduction strategies and integrated mental health services for individuals and couples experiencing infertility.

Conclusion

The psychological and social experiences of infertility among women negatively affect their general well-being, further worsening their chances of conceiving. These women encountered several forms of psychosocial problems that ranged from depression, anxiety, diminished desire for sex, stigmatisation, marital instability and intentional social isolation. A stress-free mindset, as well as an environment free from psychosocial stressors, aids in improving the quality of life of women diagnosed with infertility, further improving their chances of getting pregnant.
