Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction in Ischemia With Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries and Associations With Sublingual Microvascular Abnormalities
Authors: Nathaniel R Smilowitz, Abel Salas, Amanda Joa, Lillian Na, Manuela Plazas Montana, Claudia Serrano-Gomez, Ayman Farid, Anaïs Hausvater, Jeffrey S Berger, Harmony R Reynolds
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is a mechanism of ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) diagnosed by invasive coronary function testing (CFT). It is unknown whether CMD reflects a systemic microcirculatory disorder. Sidestream darkfield (SDF) microscopy permits noninvasive imaging of red blood cells (RBCs) in the sublingual microcirculation.ObjectivesWe sought to evaluate whether SDF microscopy identifies sublingual microvascular abnormalities in patients with INOCA due to CMD.MethodsAdults with INOCA underwent CFT for CMD, defined by abnormal coronary flow reserve <2.5 or index of microcirculatory resistance ≥25. Sublingual SDF microscopy was performed to determine the endothelial glycocalyx perfused boundary region (PBR), RBC filling percentage (%RBC), and perfused microvascular density (PMVD).ResultsA total of 135 participants with INOCA (mean age 59.3 ± 12.1 years, 80% female) underwent CFT and sublingual SDF microscopy. CMD was present in 63 participants (46.7%). The median PBR was 1.96 (IQR: 0.4), median %RBC was 72.6 (IQR: 8.0), and median PMVD was 406 (IQR: 141). No differences in sublingual PBR, %RBC, and PMVD were observed by CMD status. No correlations between sublingual parameters and continuous measures of coronary flow reserve or index of microcirculatory resistance were observed.ConclusionsNoninvasive SDF microscopy did not identify sublingual microvascular abnormalities in INOCA patients with CMD.Key words: capillaries, coronary circulation, coronary microvascular dysfunction, microcirculation, myocardial ischemia, sublingual region

Background

Ischemia with nonobstructive coronary arteries (INOCA) affects millions of patients in the United States and is associated with chest pain, dyspnea, reduced quality of life, and increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events. The identification of underlying causes of INOCA is critical to guide appropriate management and improve patient symptoms and quality of life.1, 2, 3 Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is a key mechanism of INOCA that is commonly diagnosed by invasive coronary function testing (CFT).4 Unfortunately, CFT is invasive, expensive, involves radiation exposure, and is performed only at specialized centers. Simple, noninvasive approaches to the diagnosis of CMD in patients with INOCA are needed. Sidestream darkfield (SDF) microscopy is a safe, noninvasive modality that permits imaging of red blood cells (RBCs) in the sublingual microcirculation. SDF imaging can assess microvascular endothelial dysfunction in systemic disease states such as sepsis and pre-eclampsia, and has been associated with macrovascular brachial artery endothelial dysfunction.5, 6, 7 As it is unknown whether CMD reflects a systemic disorder of the microcirculation, we sought to evaluate whether sublingual SDF imaging could be used to identify extracardiac microvascular abnormalities in patients with INOCA due to CMD.

Methods

Participants

Adults age ≥18 years with chronic coronary syndromes were enrolled into a prospective diagnostic study at NYU Langone Health and Bellevue Hospital Center in New York between 2018 and 2025 (NCT03537586). Patients were eligible if they had ischemic symptoms and/or a stress test suggestive of myocardial ischemia and were referred for clinically indicated invasive coronary angiography. Patients were excluded if they had obstructive coronary artery disease defined as any ≥50% stenosis in a major epicardial coronary territory, severe valvular heart disease, left ventricular ejection fraction <40%, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or severe left ventricular hypertrophy. All eligible participants underwent invasive assessment of coronary microvascular function. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the participants were abstracted from the electronic medical record. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the NYU Grossman School of Medicine, and all enrolled participants provided written informed consent.

Invasive coronary function testing

Invasive CFT was performed using the bolus coronary thermodilution method with the PressureWire X (Abbott Vascular). CFT was preferentially performed in the left anterior descending coronary artery unless a different vessel was clinically indicated. After positioning the coronary pressure-temperature guidewire >60 mm into the target vessel, 3 mL boluses of room temperature saline were injected in triplicate to determine coronary transit times as a surrogate for coronary flow at rest. To assess hyperemic flow and resistance, intravenous adenosine was administered at 140 μg/kg/min for 2 minutes, followed by repeated measurements of coronary transit times by bolus coronary thermodilution. Coronary flow reserve (CFR) was calculated as the ratio of resting to hyperemic mean transit times; CFR <2.5 was considered abnormal. The index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) was defined as the product of the distal coronary pressure and the mean transit time during maximal hyperemia; IMR ≥25 was considered abnormal. Invasively determined CMD was defined as either an abnormal CFR <2.5, abnormal IMR ≥25, or both, in accordance with consensus definitions proposed by the Coronary Vasomotion Disorders International Study Group (COVADIS) and others.4,8 Microvascular resistance reserve (MRR) was calculated as the product of CFR/fractional flow reserve and the ratio of resting to hyperemic aortic pressures. The resistive reserve ratio (RRR) was calculated as the ratio of basal microvascular resistance to hyperemic microvascular resistance. Resting coronary blood flow was represented as baseline 1/Tmn, whereas hyperemic blood flow was estimated as 1/Tmn during intravenous adenosine infusion. In a subset of participants, coronary reactivity testing with intracoronary acetylcholine was performed to identify epicardial or microvascular coronary spasm. Escalating doses of 20 μg, 50 μg, 100 μg, and 200 μg were administered through the guide catheter into the left main coronary artery. Coronary spasm was defined as an epicardial narrowing >90% with associated ischemic symptoms. Microvascular spasm was diagnosed based on acetylcholine provocation of ischemic electrocardiographic changes and ischemic symptoms in the absence of epicardial coronary spasm.

Sublingual sidestream darkfield microscopy

Sublingual SDF microscopy was performed in all participants on the day of CFT to assess the sublingual microcirculation and endothelial glycocalyx. SDF microscopy was performed with a CapiScope HVCS Handheld Video Capillaroscopy System (KK Research Technology Ltd), a high-resolution video microscope equipped with stroboscopic diodes emitting green light at 525 nm, a wavelength at which hemoglobin absorption is independent of oxygenation state. This permits imaging of the columns of RBCs in the microcirculation in real time with resolution of 1 μm.9 Measures of endothelial glycocalyx function and microvascular density were determined using Glycocheck software (Microvascular Health Solutions, BioRegenx). After identifying vessels 5 to 25 μm in diameter, the software divides the microcirculation into >3,000 individual segments (10 μm in length). The perfused boundary region (PBR), a measure of RBC penetration depth into the endothelial glycocalyx, is calculated for each vascular segment as the one-half the difference between the maximal perfused diameter and the median RBC column width. Higher PBR values indicate a thin, damaged glycocalyx, whereas lower PBR values indicate a glycocalyx of higher integrity and adequate protective barrier function.10, 11, 12 The mean longitudinal volume fraction of vessels containing RBCs was calculated as the RBC filling percentage (%RBC); prior studies have reported an inverse correlation between %RBC and PBR.13,14 Perfused microvascular density (PMVD) was determined as the number of perfused microvascular segments per square millimeter of imaging. The PBR at a fixed high flow level was estimated based on flow-PBR curves for vessel diameters 5 to 25 μm. A summary microvascular health score was also reported, calculated as: (PMVD · %RBC/PBR)/100. Key sublingual SDF microscopy parameters of interest are graphically depicted in Figure 1. The primary study analyses included only the first measured value for each SDF imaging parameter for each participant, to avoid any potential impact of repeated imaging on the sublingual microcirculation. However, to address the potential for variability in SDF measurement, we also performed exploratory analyses using mean values for each SDF imaging parameter when duplicate or triplicate values were available for a given participant.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics and parameters of sublingual microvascular abnormalities were stratified by the presence or absence of CMD and compared by chi-square tests for categorical variables, t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables. Spearman correlations were performed to assess relationships between sublingual microvascular parameters (PBR, %RBC, and PMVD) and continuous measures of CMD (CFR, IMR, and MRR). Linear regression models adjusted for age and sex were generated to estimate the relationship between SDF imaging parameters and CMD. In exploratory analyses, participants were stratified by subtype of CMD (concordantly abnormal CFR and IMR, abnormal CFR with normal IMR, and abnormal IMR with normal CFR). To determine whether the presence of coronary vasomotor dysfunction might impact relationships between SDF imaging and microvascular parameters, we performed an exploratory analysis of patients who underwent coronary reactivity testing and compared individuals with and without coronary spasm. To evaluate the impact of demographics on SDF imaging parameters, an exploratory analysis was performed stratified by participant age, sex, and body mass index (BMI). Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis in which CMD was defined by CFR <2.0 or IMR ≥25. The study sample size was determined based on the assumption that with a 40% prevalence of coronary microvascular disease, 100 participants would provide 80% power to detect a minimum Pearson Correlation of 0.28 with an alpha of 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 135 participants underwent CFT and sublingual SDF microscopy. The median age was 61.0 (52.0, 69.0) years, 80% were females, and 65.9% were of White race. The median BMI was 28.2 (23.8, 32.5) kg/m2. A total of 65.2% of participants presented with chest pain, 32.6% presented with dyspnea as an anginal equivalent, and 2.2% had asymptomatic ischemia by stress testing. Invasive coronary function testing

Invasive CFT was performed in the LAD in 132 cases (97.8%), in a ramus branch in 2 cases (1.5%), and in the right coronary artery in 1 case (0.7%). The median IMR was 20.0 (13.0, 28.0) and the median CFR was 3.5 (2.6, 4.7). The median MRR was 4.24 (3.06, 6.10) and RRR was 3.97 (2.82, 5.73) (Table 1). CMD was present in 63 participants (46.7%), with an abnormal IMR ≥25 identified in 45 participants (33.3%) and abnormal CFR in 30 participants (22.2%).

Sublingual SDF microscopy

The mean PBR was 1.97 ± 0.3 μm (median 1.96 μm; IQR: 0.4), the mean %RBC was 72.53% ± 5.71% (median 72.6%; IQR: 8.0%), and the mean PMVD was 423.2 ± 110.4 μm/mm2 (median 406 μm/mm2; IQR: 141) in participants with INOCA (Table 2).

Sublingual SDF microscopy by CMD status

Sublingual microvascular parameter frequency distributions for PBR, PBR at high flow, % RBC, and PMVD stratified among participants with and without CMD are shown in Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 1. When participants with CMD were compared with those without CMD, no differences in mean PBR (1.95 ± 0.30 μm vs 1.98 ± 0.30 μm; P = 0.506), %RBC (73.03% ± 5.64% vs 72.09% ± 5.78%; P = 0.34), and mean PMVD (430.7 ± 104.7 μm/mm2 vs 416.7 ± 115.5 μm/mm2; P = 0.47) were identified (Table 2). PBR at high flow and a microvascular health summary score also did not differ between groups (Table 2). In age- and sex-adjusted models, CMD was not significantly associated with any of the SDF imaging parameters (data not shown). In exploratory analyses, no differences were observed by CMD status when mean values incorporating repeated SDF imaging measurements were evaluated (Supplemental Figure 2). With repeated measurements, the mean coefficient of variation was 9% for PBR, 5% for %RBC, and 18% for PMVD.

No correlations between parameters of sublingual microvascular abnormalities and measures of CMD, including CFR, IMR were observed as shown in Figure 3 and Supplemental Figures 3 and 4. Similarly, sublingual SDF imaging parameters did not correlate with measurements of MRR, RRR, or measures of resting and hyperemic coronary flow, as shown in Figures 4 and 5. Sublingual SDF microscopy and coronary spasm

In an exploratory analysis of a subset of 61 participants who had diagnostic coronary reactivity testing with acetylcholine (mean age 57 [IQR: 48-67], 88.5% female), epicardial spasm was present in 33 participants (54.1%) and microvascular spasm was diagnosed in 4 participants (6.6%). Vasoreactivity in response to acetylcholine (epicardial or microvascular spasm) was diagnosed in 60.7% of participants who underwent testing. We observed no differences in PMVD (460 [395-498] vs 418 [343-515]; P = 0.56), %RBC (72.5 [68.5-76.8] vs 72.2 [68.2-77.5]; P = 0.69), PBR (2.01 [1.83-2.17] vs 1.99 [1.79-2.20]; P = 0.99), PBR at a fixed high flow level (1.5 [1.19-1.91 vs 1.58 [1.37-1.88]; P = 0.62) or MVHS [3.11 [2.36-4.61] vs 3.19 [2.42-4.71]; P = 0.69) among participants with vs without epicardial or microvascular spasm in response to intracoronary acetylcholine.

Discussion

CMD is a key mechanism of INOCA that leads to reduced quality of life and increased risk of adverse cardiovascular events. Unfortunately, the diagnosis of CMD continues to be a challenge in clinical practice. Assessment of the coronary microcirculation requires advanced cardiac imaging or invasive physiology studies that are expensive, require technical expertise, and are not available at many centers. New, minimally invasive, inexpensive approaches to CMD screening and diagnosis are urgently needed. In this study of 135 adults with INOCA who underwent invasive CFT to define INOCA endotypes, we prospectively assessed the sublingual microcirculation through comprehensive noninvasive SDF imaging to determine its suitability as a surrogate modality to identify CMD. Unfortunately, sublingual microvascular density and measures of the endothelial glycocalyx derived from SDF imaging did not differ by CMD status, nor were these parameters correlated with coronary physiology measurements acquired during invasive CFT. Moreover, the sublingual microcirculatory SDF imaging parameters observed in this study of patients with INOCA are similar to values derived from healthy populations without angina.15 These results suggest that sublingual microvascular abnormalities are insufficient to identify individuals with INOCA due to CMD in clinical practice (Central Illustration). In an exploratory subset of participants who underwent coronary reactivity testing, we also observed no difference in sublingual measures derived from SDF imaging in participants with and without endothelial dependent coronary spasm.

There are several potential explanations for the lack of an association between sublingual microvascular assessments and CMD. First, sublingual SDF imaging is largely focused on endothelial glycocalyx properties that may not reflect the microcirculatory abnormalities most relevant to CMD. Despite our inability to identify differences in the sublingual microvascular endothelial glycocalyx by SDF imaging in participants with and without CMD, there is some evidence that glycocalyx shedding is abnormal in individuals with CMD. In a prior study of 528 adults with suspected coronary artery disease who underwent invasive coronary angiography, serum concentrations of syndecan-1, a major component of the endothelial glycocalyx, were independently associated with an elevated angiography-derived IMR.16 Alternatively, our findings may indicate that CMD is not a systemic process, and abnormalities may be limited to the coronary circulation without extracardiac vascular manifestations. Sublingual SDF imaging was also not independently associated with obstructive coronary artery disease in a large analysis of 413 individuals with and without cardiovascular risk factors and disease.15 In contrast, sublingual microvascular imaging appears to provide insights into other systemic disorders such as pre-eclampsia and sepsis.5,6 As sublingual SDF imaging was unable to reliably identify participants with CMD, our study reinforces the need for direct coronary vascular assessments at this time.

This is among the largest studies to explore noninvasive imaging to assess the peripheral microcirculation in INOCA, and the largest study we are aware of to evaluate sublingual microvascular assessments as they relate to CMD. Other noninvasive peripheral imaging modalities have been tested to assess the microcirculation in patients with CMD. In a recent study that included 43 adults with INOCA who underwent invasive CFT, cutaneous microvascular function was assessed using forearm laser speckle contrast analysis at baseline and with transdermal drug delivery of acetylcholine, sodium nitroprusside, and insulin.17 Baseline microvascular skin blood flow, microvascular blood flow in response to insulin, and blood flow in response to acetylcholine, were all similar in participants with and without coronary microvascular disorders, but individuals with coronary vasomotor dysfunction had reduced peripheral flow responses to sodium nitroprusside.17 In this same study, the reactive hyperemia index from endothelial peripheral artery tonometry was not associated with coronary vasomotor dysfunction.17 However, in a separate small study of 47 participants, individuals with an elevated IMR had a significantly lower reactive hyperemia index than those with normal microvascular resistance.18 These studies highlight past challenges to identify a reliable surrogate vascular bed for CMD, and reinforce the need for future investigation to identify and validate new noninvasive peripheral markers of microvascular dysfunction.

Study limitations

There are some important limitations to the present analysis. First, although the sublingual space is easily accessible, vascular density can be heterogenous, and the precise location of imaging may have impacted the measured sublingual SDF imaging parameters. Still, in a prior study in which 4 healthy participants underwent serial sublingual microvascular imaging, reproducibility of sublingual SDF imaging was good with an 8.1% and 4.1% intra-assay coefficient of variation for PBR and %RBC, respectively.7 Our primary analysis included only the first set of SDF measurements, given the potential for prolonged imaging to impact the intrinsic characteristics of the sublingual microcirculation through physical compression or vascular spasm. To address the potential for measurement-to-measurement variability, we performed acquisitions of SDF imaging parameters in triplicate in most, but not all, participants. However, inclusion of mean rather than first acquisition values for SDF imaging parameters did not significantly impact the study results. Second, although all participants were nil per os for invasive CFT and sublingual SDF imaging, we cannot exclude the impact of diet, exercise, or physical activity on sublingual microvascular parameters. Similarly, comorbid conditions and medication are additional potential confounders. Third, a mismatch in the vascular segments of the microcirculation assessed could also account for discordances between coronary and sublingual measures; whereas coronary microvascular resistance measured during invasive testing is largely mediated by arterioles ∼100 μm in diameter, sublingual SDF imaging assesses capillaries only 5-25 μm in caliber. Fourth, the SDF imaging system used in the current study was limited to mucosal vascular beds. We did not assess the microcirculation in alternate sites, such as nailbed or dermal capillaries. Other accessible vascular beds, such as retinal microcirculation, remain intriguing possibilities for assessment of the systemic microcirculation. Fifth, the exploratory subanalysis of patients who underwent coronary reactivity testing for coronary spasm was small, limiting study power to detect differences in sublingual parameters stratified by the presence or absence of coronary spasm. Finally, although the current study evaluated the sublingual and coronary microcirculation on the same day, we did not perform serial sampling over time. We are unable to explore changes in the sublingual microcirculation over time and associations with CMD.

Conclusions

Noninvasive SDF microscopy did not identify sublingual microvascular abnormalities in patients with INOCA due to CMD. Sublingual SDF microscopy does not appear to be a suitable surrogate marker of CMD. Future studies are needed to assess the clinical feasibility of other noninvasive, inexpensive screening or diagnostic tools to identify patients with CMD.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: INOCA is commonly caused by CMD. In the largest prospective evaluation of sublingual microvascular abnormalities in patients with and without CMD assessed by invasive coronary function testing, sublingual microcirculatory abnormalities were not observed in patients with CMD.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies are needed to identify other noninvasive approaches, including biomarkers or diagnostic imaging modalities, that more closely reflect coronary microvascular pathology and can be used to diagnose CMD.
