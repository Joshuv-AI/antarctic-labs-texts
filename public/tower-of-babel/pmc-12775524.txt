Searching for extraterrestrial life advances terrestrial sustainability
Authors: Alta E. G. Howells, Catherine G. Fontana, Sabrina Elkassas, Tristan A. Caro, Srishti Kashyap
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Astrobiology, while traditionally focused on understanding the origin of life on Earth and the potential for life elsewhere, offers powerful tools and insights for addressing urgent challenges on our planet. We call for a deliberate integration of astrobiological research with applied science. Knowledge gained from biological systems and study sites on Earth that guide investigation of planetary bodies in our solar system can inform strategies for carbon capture, low-carbon energy production, waste remediation, and biotechnology. Therefore, we argue that astrobiology must evolve to become a bidirectional science that not only explores the cosmos but also supports sustainable life on Earth.

Introduction

As astrobiologists, we recognize an increasing tension between the pursuit of fundamental scientific questions, such as “Where did life come from?” and “Does it exist elsewhere in our solar system or beyond?”, and the pressing environmental and societal challenges currently confronting life on Earth. These pursuits are often viewed as distinct or even competing priorities: one reaching outward to understand the universe, the other focused inward on sustaining our planet. We contend, however, that these efforts are complementary. The study of astrobiology relies on understanding the physical and chemical limits of habitability, the cycling of energy and matter, and the capacity of biological systems to adapt to extreme environments, all of which have direct relevance to Earth’s changing ecosystems and to developing sustainable technologies. Indeed, we and others1–3 increasingly observe that the tools, technologies, and field sites used in astrobiological research routinely intersect with and can be productively translated to Earth-facing applied sciences. In this perspective, we argue that astrobiology’s integrative framework not only informs the search for life beyond Earth but also provides a powerful foundation for addressing some of humanity’s most urgent environmental and technological challenges.

Astrobiology is the interdisciplinary scientific study of life in the universe, its origins, evolution, distribution, future, and potential beyond Earth, through the integration of fields such as biology, chemistry, geology, and planetary science. While astrobiology is grounded in hard sciences, the questions astrobiologists seek to answer carry profound philosophical implications for humanity’s understanding of its place in the universe and, by extension, for society as a whole. Leaders in astrobiology and astrophysics (Carl Sagan among them4) have expounded on the societal implications of the study of astrobiology. Crawford (2018)5 notes:“…although the cosmic perspective might be expected to intrude most forcibly into the public consciousness in the event of an actual discovery of extraterrestrial life, the search itself can also contribute. Indeed, it is only by sending spacecraft out to explore the Solar System, in part for astrobiological purposes, that we can obtain images of our own planet that show it in its true cosmic setting.” 5

“…although the cosmic perspective might be expected to intrude most forcibly into the public consciousness in the event of an actual discovery of extraterrestrial life, the search itself can also contribute. Indeed, it is only by sending spacecraft out to explore the Solar System, in part for astrobiological purposes, that we can obtain images of our own planet that show it in its true cosmic setting.” 5

Crawford and others5,6 argue that the search for life beyond Earth garners a ‘cosmic perspective’ and encourages us to look beyond a purely Earth-centered view, uniting humanity not by states, provinces, countries, or continents, but as inhabitants of a single planet. Moreover, the confirmation of life not being detected elsewhere in our solar system may create an even greater sense of urgency to protect and sustain the only known example of life through resource-conscious and sustainable practices. Given astrobiology’s potentially profound implications for the philosophical understanding of our place in the universe, we must also ask a practical question: how has, and how will, astrobiology help us address the challenges we face on Earth—both now and in the future?

The connection between human space exploration and astrobiology

Astrobiology stands to benefit from science, technology, and engineering fields focused on human space exploration7, and work in this sector provides clear examples of how investment in space exploration can translate into Earth applications8. Space bioprocess engineering (SBE) aims to deliver solutions for managing finite resources in space in anticipation of human space exploration9. Highly relevant to NASA’s Space Technology Mission Directorate’s (STMD) “Grand Challenges” that include in-space resource management; innovations in SBE, like wastewater treatment, habitat air revitalization, and biofabrication using microorganisms, map naturally onto closed-loop resource utilization on Earth, which likewise operates under finite constraints8,9. Closed Ecological (Bioregenerative) Life Support Systems (CELSS/BLSS), artificial, self-sustaining biospheres, have driven advances in controlled-environment agriculture, microbial biofertilizers, and nutrient capture, all of which could improve sustainable agricultural practices on Earth10,11.

A second, equally important bridge between astrobiology and Earth-focused fields is planetary protection. The same frameworks that minimize forward contamination (protecting other worlds from Earth life) and backward contamination (protecting Earth from potential extraterrestrial material) have direct terrestrial value12. Contamination-control protocols directly translate to environmental biosurveillance, public-health diagnostics, and protection of Earth’s extreme and pristine environments. In practice, these methods reduce false positives in life-detection and set operational standards for working in sensitive ecosystems (e.g., deep-sea vents, subglacial lakes, polar deserts), where inadvertent biological introduction or cross-site transfer would undermine both science and conservation.

Building connections between astrobiology and sustainability on Earth

Although the terrestrial value of space-derived biotechnologies for human space exploration is well established, the pathways by which astrobiology analog research can advance Earth’s sustainability are less clearly defined. Analog research uses terrestrial field sites whose conditions, processes, or features approximate those of extraterrestrial environments like Mars to study habitability, biosignature formation, and mission operations under controlled but natural conditions. These Earth-based analogs not only inform life-detection strategies and mission design but also generate insights into energy flow, biogeochemical resilience, and resource utilization in extreme environments, all of which are important as we face climate change. In this perspective, we highlight three illustrative examples demonstrating how astrobiological science and technology can be translated to address sustainability challenges on Earth:

1. Astrobiology for environmental science and engineering: Investigating the interactions between biological and geological processes in analog environments to develop innovative solutions for energy production and carbon capture.

2. Astrobiology for biotechnology: Harnessing the unique capabilities of extremophiles and other environmentally relevant microorganisms to develop biotechnological applications that can address issues such as waste management, bioenergy production, and bioremediation.

3. Astrobiology for resource management: Applying knowledge gained from studying microorganisms in analog environments to improve resource management practices on Earth, ensuring sustainable acquisition and use of natural resources.

An early career perspective

Within NASA missions and related programs, early-career scientists work closely alongside senior investigators, contributing to mission design and developing plans for data analysis and interpretation. This is done intentionally, acknowledging that missions such as the Curiosity rover, still in operation more than a decade after landing on Mars, can extend beyond the careers of the senior scientists that first envisioned them. Therefore, the future of the mission falls to early-career scientists. We believe the same is true for the field of astrobiology. As emerging contributors to the field, we bring integrative and forward-looking viewpoints that reflect contemporary challenges in sustainability, technology, and interdisciplinary collaboration. While not distinct in merit from those of senior researchers, our perspective can complement established voices by highlighting emerging research frontiers and training needs that will shape the field’s future. We aim to ensure a balanced and forward-oriented discourse within the astrobiology community.

Therefore, as early-career researchers we urge the astrobiology community to advocate for and engage in translating our research in the aid of our own planet. We assert that there is significant motivation to reexamine and harness the extensive capabilities of microorganisms, and the analog environments they inhabit, to address the growing energy and resource demands of human life on Earth and potentially beyond. Here, by presenting a broad variety of examples across scientific fields we demonstrate that there is a path forward that fosters synergy between Earth analog research and the application of biology and geochemistry of these natural systems to applied science. Given that astrobiology intersects heavily with multiple Earth-facing fields of study, this early career perspective argues that an Earth-centric approach to astrobiology can benefit both the field of astrobiology and the only planet known to harbor life: Earth.

Astrobiology for sustainability

1. Astrobiology for environmental science and engineering

Many of the environments studied for decades within astrobiological frameworks are gaining interest in realms of environmental science and engineering. For example, astrobiologists have long recognized that water-rock interactions are crucial targets for life detection, outlining the fundamental “follow the water” and “follow the energy” life detection strategies13,14. Serpentinization, the hydration of ultramafic planetary crust, is of special interest due to its production of molecular H2 that fuels microbial ecosystems in the subsurface15, thus motivating the recent “follow the serpentine” strategy (see Fig. 1)16. Serpentinizing/serpentinized systems are widespread on the Martian surface, underscored by the discovery of aqueously altered igneous rocks in Jezero crater17 and the existence of liquid water deep into the Martian crust18. The moons Europa and Enceladus (moons of Jupiter and Saturn, respectively) are thought to host active serpentinization processes beneath their icy exteriors19. Because of its H2 generation and its potential to form steep redox gradients that can support microbial life in the absence of sunlight, active or previously active serpentinizing environments are considered to be of great interest for life detection missions on these other planetary bodies. In recent years, investigation of serpentinization has expanded beyond astrobiology into the industrial sector, which recognizes the capacity of geological systems to produce significant quantities of H2, a clean source of energy naturally. In addition to energy generation, serpentinizing as well as other analog environments are being recognized as promising sites for carbon capture and sequestration (CCS). In some strategies, CO2 directly captured from the atmosphere is concentrated and injected into alkaline subsurface aquifers to stimulate the precipitation of CaCO325. However, serpentinizing systems are just one system hosting “extreme” geochemistry that is becoming increasingly relevant to CCS, as injections of CO2 into hyperalkaline groundwaters have a demonstrated capacity to sequester large (>80%) of injected carbon22. Deep-sea sediments, often hosting life under extreme pressures and nutrient limitation, are being leveraged for the enhancement of oceanic biomass burial. For example, enhancement of oceanic biomass burial is utilized to stimulate the natural marine “biological pump” where organic carbon is transported to sediments and as particulate organic matter26,27. Hypersaline brines, often investigated in the context of extremophilic/halotolerant organisms that may persist in subsurface brines on Mars, are also being considered for carbon sequestration. Current proposals for these systems include landfilling harvested agricultural biomass in hypersaline enclosures below the water activity limits of life to sequester organic carbon underground, a process that may be economically tractable at a cost of $60/tonne of sequestered CO2 if water activity is held below critical thresholds24.

Although “extreme” and “analog” systems are increasingly applied to environmental engineering challenges, many of these efforts still lack thorough evaluation of the potential impacts of subsurface life. For example, microbial communities that rely on inorganic carbon in active CCS and GeoH2 sites may confound efforts to sequester carbon and generate H2 stably. Injections of CO2 may stimulate subsurface microbial activity, destabilizing sequestered CO2 and releasing CH4–a more potent greenhouse gas–through a microbial process called methanogenesis (4H2 + CO2 → CH4 + 2H2O)21. As H2 is a potent microbial electron donor, H2 stimulation may subsequently promote subsurface autotrophy, which may reduce industrial capture and lower economic viability. Similarly, sequestering carbon as buried biomass in hypersaline environments may be complicated by organisms’ ability to persist even at high salt concentrations28.

Many of the geochemical and microbiological advances in our understanding of serpentinization, hypersaline systems, the Earth’s deep marine subsurface, among other analog environments have been the direct result of fundamental astrobiology and geomicrobiology research supported by NASA and other funding agencies. This kind of knowledge is proving essential for the successful assessment and deployment of these applied technologies. However, many questions still remain: How will industrial perturbation affect local geochemistry? How will microorganisms interact with engineering efforts? These questions are directly relevant to environmental engineering challenges and are addressed by cutting-edge geochemical and microbiological tools that astrobiologists have honed for decades. Long-considered “extreme” or “astrobio-focused” microbiology and geochemistry are proving to be rapidly and crucially relevant to environmental engineering problems–a prime example of how fundamental science translates to unanticipated applied questions. Future work should intentionally acknowledge the dual purpose of these types of research - to elucidate the relevance of these systems for astrobiology and the search for life, and to guide the development of technologies that incorporate the influences of subsurface life.

2. Astrobiology for biotechnology

Astrobiology research, including studies of organisms in analog environments, in situ resource utilization (ISRU), and biosignature discovery, has led to significant biological discoveries that can be applied to technological innovation on Earth. Here, we highlight select exemplary organisms that have been examined through an astrobiological lens, including cyanobacteria, alkaliphilic microorganisms, and methanogenic archaea.

Ancient cyanobacteria are credited with oxygenating Earth’s atmosphere, which is considered to be a key step in the evolution of multicellularity29,30. Because of this, oxygen is also considered a potential biosignature in exoplanet discovery31. Over the last 3.5 billion years, cyanobacteria have colonized a diverse range of habitats and continue to play an important role in the global carbon cycle by sequestering roughly 30% of carbon dioxide each year32. Because of their prominence as drivers of planetary scale processes, their legacy as organisms that have co-evolved with our planet, and their establishment as well-understood genetic model systems, cyanobacteria hold immense promise for future studies at the intersection of astrobiology and carbon capture technologies (see Fig. 2). Further research on cyanobacteria’s carbon concentrating mechanism, the carboxysome, can provide both insights into organismal evolution in response to environmental pressures and critical information that can be used to bioengineer increased carbon fixation. Cyanobacteria are also master controllers of carbon sequestration via induced mineral precipitation of carbonates. In astrobiology, these induced carbonate precipitates contain indirect but telling biosignatures of microbial activity33. Studies of Enceladus and Europa’s oceans drive research into organisms adapted to predicted chemical conditions on these ocean worlds. Enceladus’s subglacial ocean, for instance, may undergo ongoing serpentinization, resulting in alkaline pH ~9–1135 and reducing conditions. Alkaliphilic microorganisms, uniquely suited to high-pH environments, are promising candidates for study. They produce organic acids to maintain intracellular homeostasis, secrete siderophores for metal acquisition, and generate exopolysaccharides, biosurfactants, alkaline-stable enzymes, and novel antibiotics36,37. Given ongoing astrobiological research on the molecular and genetic bases of alkaliphily38–41, these organisms hold significant biotechnological potential, particularly in industries reliant on inefficient or environmentally harmful processes. For example, the conventional alkali treatment of wood pulp in paper production requires harsh chlorine bleaching. However, thermostable, alkaline-active xylanases from microorganisms offer a more sustainable alternative, requiring fewer chemicals and causing less environmental damage37.

Methanogens, among the earliest known life forms on Earth, thrive on hydrogen (H2) and carbon dioxide (CO2) and produce methane (CH4). Geochemical models of dissolved H2 and carbonate species in Enceladus’s plumes originating from its subsurface ocean suggest conditions are energetically favorable for microbial methanogenesis35,42. Especially relevant in serpentinizing systems is the hydrogenation of CO2 by methanogenic microorganisms and the subsequent conversion of biological CH4 into solid functional carbon materials, including carbon nanotubes, fibers, and graphite, through an industrial process called CO2 valorization. In addition to sequestering large quantities of CO2, these materials could be applied to large-scale industrial processes to replace metal utilization43.

Astrobiology-inspired spin-off technologies are potentially of great value. The list of NASA “spin-off” technologies, tools, or ideas translated to commercial practice is well documented (https://spinoff.nasa.gov/)44. Notably, taq polymerase, which made possible the polymerase chain reaction and subsequently enabled modern biology, biotechnology, and bioinformatics, was isolated from Thermus aquaticus, a thermophilic bacterium first isolated from Mushroom Spring in Yellowstone National Park45,46. Recently, NASA-funded geomicrobiology research describing Fusarium fungi in Yellowstone hot springs led to the development of “Fy protein,” a nutritionally complete, sustainable protein source47 that is now marketed for human consumption. At the macroeconomic scale, it is estimated that space science R&D leads to substantial return-on-investment through technological development and technology transfer. For example, NASA spin-off technologies added upwards of $1.5 billion in value to 15 life science R&D companies alone48. As emphasized by others9, NASEM’s Origins, Worlds, and Life: A Decadal Strategy for Planetary Science and Astrobiology 2023–2032 emphasizes the development of mission-relevant technologies with significant potential for terrestrial application. Evidence of this potential is also starting to appear in machine learning and artificial intelligence sectors49. For example, the program Horizyn-150,51 employs machine learning to predict enzyme functions, including those for “orphan” biochemical reactions—metabolic transformations for which no enzyme catalysts are yet characterized. By exploring potential enzymatic solutions in a manner analogous to how early metabolism may have evolved, Horizyn-1 demonstrates how an astrobiological, systems-level approach to understanding life’s emergence can inform modern enzyme discovery and metabolic optimization.

These recent successes demonstrate the immense potential for translational applications of astrobiology research to new biotechnologies. Several steps should be taken to fuel the next wave of translational technologies. First, these discoveries hinge on the systematic exploration of microbial ecosystems, spanning the common to the extreme. Because metabolic response is tightly coupled to geochemical context, interdisciplinary teams must be assembled to comprehensively interrogate local biogeochemical processes and reveal the novel strategies that microbes use to survive in and adapt to their surroundings. Second, the identification of these novel microbial capabilities relies on functional characterization of undefined, hypothetical proteins. Leveraging new tools to decode these cryptic molecules, such as machine learning, could accelerate this challenging process. Similarly, the continued growth of accessible genome databases, such as those maintained by the National Institute of Health’s National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), is critical to this mission. Finally, dedicated communication pathways should be built across disciplinary silos, enabling researchers to coordinate around common organisms or functional capabilities and unify fragmented efforts.

3. Astrobiology for resource management

In recent years, research in analog microbiology and astrobiology has gained relevance for global change and resource management. Microorganisms examined within these research areas could hold significant potential in waste management, rare-metal mining, and bioremediation.

Río Tinto in southwestern Spain is a well-studied Mars analog site52, where geochemical processes and microbial activity in the subsurface and weathering due to mining at the surface in the Iberian Pyrite Belt produce highly acidic, iron- and sulfate-rich waters53,54 that mimic strictly anthropogenic acid-mine drainage55 and mirror the mineralogy and predicted redox chemistry of some early Mars environments52,54. Decades of astrobiological research, such as the Mars Astrobiology Research and Technology Experiment56 and the Iberian Pyrite Belt Subsurface Life Detection52 project for developing subsurface drilling missions to Mars, have revealed thriving communities of iron- and sulfur-oxidizing microorganisms that survive in low-pH, oxygen-limited settings, providing models for potential Martian metabolisms and guiding life-detection strategies for potential extant life on Mars52,56. Yet Río Tinto’s value extends well beyond planetary science: its microbes are now central to innovations in biomining that are far less detrimental to the environment. Acidophilic species detected and isolated from Rio Tinto like Acidithiobacillus, Leptospirillum, and Ferroplasma are being applied to enhance iron and copper recovery while reducing the need for harsh chemicals57,58 demonstrating how studying life in extreme, Mars-like environments can yield practical Earth applications. At the same time, the site’s centuries-long legacy of iron and copper extraction illustrates the importance of coupling astrobiological discovery with sustainable resource management on our own planet.

The development of microbial technologies for metal extraction on the Moon and Mars to support human space exploration simultaneously advances sustainable resource management on Earth and deepens astrobiological understanding of the habitability of these extraterrestrial environments. In 2016, 44.7 million metric tons of e-waste were produced, a figure that continues to rise with the increasing consumption of electronics59. Given the reliance on rare Earth elements in electronic products, recycling and reusing these metals are crucial for future electronics production. Researchers have leveraged the microorganism Gluconobacter oxydans, which produces gluconic acid to bind rare earth elements, as well as species such as Cupriavidus metallidurans, Delftia acidovorans, and Chromobacterium violaceum to biomine gold. C. metallidurans, a heavy metal-tolerant chemoautotroph and one of the organisms used to mine e-waste, has been studied on the ISS for bioleaching rare earth elements from basalt under microgravity and simulated Mars and Earth conditions60. Mars has a high concentration of perchlorate (ClO₄⁻) in the Martian regolith (0.5–1%) as detected by the Phoenix lander and Curiosity rover missions61. Perchlorates are highly oxidative and are known to be toxic to humans62. However, from an astrobiology perspective, these highly oxidizing compounds could have served as an electron acceptor for potential extant life on Mars, particularly in the case that the early Martian atmosphere was limited by other electron acceptors such as O2 as it is today63. To assess this, researchers have investigated the response of Earth halophilic microorganisms to perchlorates and find that not only can they tolerate perchlorates but may use them as an electron acceptor63. Research on perchlorates through an astrobiological lens has increasingly facilitated in situ resource utilization to support potential human activities on the Moon and Mars. Given that perchlorates pose a major challenge to human presence on Mars, particularly through thyroid disruption62,64, multiple studies64–68 have investigated the use of rhizosphere-derived and extremophilic microorganisms for perchlorate bioremediation and resource extraction on Mars. Lynch et al. (2019)66 demonstrated that microorganisms, such as Azospira, Haloarcula, Acinetobacter, Sporomusa, and Marinobacter, in perchlorate-rich sediments in the Pilot Valley Basin (a hyperarid environment with high perchlorate loadings and an established Mars analog site66) could reduce perchlorate under naturally enriched conditions, providing a promising avenue for Martian soil detoxification. In parallel, Shewanella oneidensis, a perchlorate-tolerant microorganism, has been shown to efficiently extract iron from synthetic Martian regolith at perchlorate concentrations comparable to those found in situ65. While perchlorate bioremediation and phytodegradation have been widely studied on Earth, they have yet to be implemented at scale. These studies not only inform future planetary engineering efforts but also hold untapped potential for advancing large-scale terrestrial bioremediation as perchlorate contamination continues to rise69.

Taken together, research across Mars-analog environments illustrates that these systems are more than conceptual stand-ins for other worlds: they are leading to discovery of microbial metabolic strategies with real technological relevance. Despite demonstrated potential in biomining, perchlorate detoxification, and rare-earth element recovery, most microorganisms from these settings remain both uncharacterized and under-leveraged. Expanding microbial isolate collections and studies of their physiologies, linking in situ metabolism to applied function, and designing experiments that bridge the planetary-relevant conditions with scalable bioprocesses will aid in filling in these knowledge gaps. Implementation will require intentional collaboration between scientists conducting fundamental research and engineers experienced in scaling biological systems beyond the bench, ensuring that discoveries made for astrobiology are translated into deployable biotechnologies. In doing so, microbiology in analog environments can refine life-detection strategies while accelerating the development of sustainable technologies for resource recovery, recycling, and environmental remediation.

Conclusions

To meet the ever-increasing energy and resource needs of human life on Earth, and potentially beyond, it is imperative to reconsider the remarkable capabilities of microorganisms and the diverse environments they inhabit through a new lens. While staying true to its mission of understanding the origin, evolution, distribution, and future of life in the universe, astrobiology should more intentionally frame its discoveries in an Earth-relevant context, contributing to progress in areas such as bioenergy production, natural resource recycling, and sustainable energy development.

As we strive to answer some of humanity’s most fundamental questions, such as “How did life on Earth originate?” and “Are we alone?”, we have a unique opportunity to translate our work into practical solutions to mounting environmental challenges. The call to action is clear: for every research project, methodology, or instrument developed for astrobiology, researchers should consider whether their efforts can be conceived with dual purpose: not only to study the origins and evolution of life in the universe, but also to safeguard life here on Earth.

In light of the translational research examples presented in this perspective, we urge the astrobiology community to adopt the following guiding principles:Embrace a dual-purpose mindset: From the outset, where possible, design missions, instruments, and experiments with both extraterrestrial discovery and terrestrial application in mind.Engage in cross-disciplinary partnerships: Forge stronger links between astrobiologists, synthetic biologists, environmental engineers, AI, and computational science groups to accelerate translation.Champion demonstration projects: Prioritize pilot studies that explicitly test astrobiology-inspired technologies in Earth settings (e.g., enzyme-based remediation, biosensors, waste recycling).Consider sustainability impacts: Build into astrobiological projects considerations for environmental impact and resource efficiency so that success includes both scientific and environmental return.

In short, we contend that astrobiology is not only essential for answering fundamental questions about life in the cosmos but also is uniquely positioned to help us take steps toward a more resilient, sustainable future for humanity. If we embrace this dual mission, the search for life elsewhere in the solar system becomes the practice of caring for the only planet yet known to harbor life.
