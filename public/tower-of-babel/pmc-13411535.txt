Assessing the nutritional status and micronutrient gaps in Sierra Leone: a systematic rapid review of women, children, and adolescents
Authors: Oluchi Ezekannagha, Edikan Uwatt, Busie Maziya-Dixon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSierra Leone continues to experience a substantial burden of malnutrition across all age groups. In response, the government has implemented several nutrition strategies, including the 2017–2021 RMNCAH&N Strategy and routine vitamin A supplementation, among others. While national surveys and programme evaluations have provided valuable insights, evidence on micronutrient status and dietary intake among women, children, and adolescents remains fragmented, limited, and in some cases outdated. This systematic rapid review aimed to synthesise existing data, map nutritional outcomes, identify priority evidence gaps, and provide insights to guide nutrition policy and the design of future national surveys and research.MethodsA systematic rapid review of 39 peer-reviewed quantitative studies published between 2001 and 2024 was conducted in PubMed. The review followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines and the methodological frameworks of Arksey & O’Malley and Levac et al.ResultsChild wasting ranged from 4.8% in a 2010 northern Sierra Leone survey to 9.3% nationally in the 2013 DHS, with higher estimates reported in studies focused on already malnourished populations. Stunting remained high at 37.9% in 2013 and 29.1% in 2019, with rural estimates exceeding 41%, highlighting regional disparities. Anaemia prevalence exceeded 60% among children under five, reaching 76.3% in some settings. Micronutrient evidence largely focused on haemoglobin, ferritin, vitamin A, and iodine, while zinc, riboflavin, and vitamin D were rarely assessed. Vitamin A deficiency was 17.4% among children in the 2013 Micronutrient Survey, and supplementation coverage reached 86.9% in 2019. Adolescent data were scarce, though available findings indicated poor birth outcomes among pregnant adolescents. Among women, overweight and obesity ranged from 27% to 30%. Exclusive breastfeeding was approximately 49% in 2017, approaching but not meeting 2025 and 2030 global targets, and dietary diversity remained low.ConclusionsMalnutrition remains pervasive in Sierra Leone, characterised by persistent undernutrition, widespread anaemia, and suboptimal feeding practices alongside overweight and obesity among women. Routine surveys (DHS and MICS) capture anthropometry and anaemia but lack comprehensive data on micronutrient biomarkers and dietary intake. Updated, nationally representative, age-disaggregated food consumption and micronutrient assessments are urgently needed to guide effective nutrition policies and programs.

Introduction

Sierra Leone is a small, densely populated country on the North Atlantic coast of West Africa, with a population of approximately 7.5 million. It is classified as a low-income country and ranked 185th out of 193 countries in the 2024 Human Development Index [1]. Poverty remains widespread; as of 2024, with more than half of the population living below the international poverty line of US$2 per day [2]. The country is highly vulnerable to external shocks. The lingering socioeconomic impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with the global consequences of the Ukraine crisis, and, more recently, substantial reductions in international development assistance, including the dismantling of USAID and cuts to UK aid programmes supporting maternal and child health in Sierra Leone, have deepened macroeconomic instability and aggravated food and nutrition insecurity [1, 3]. Findings from the September 2024 biannual Food Security Monitoring System (FSMS) assessment indicated that 77% of the population was food insecure, a marginal improvement from 80% during the same period in 2023. Notably, the proportion of households facing severe food insecurity declined from 28% in September 2023 to 17% in September 2024 [1].

Recent evidence highlights the persistent and evolving burden of malnutrition in Sierra Leone across all age groups. Among women of reproductive age (15–49 years), between the 2008 and 2013 DHS rounds, the country made no statistically significant progress toward the global target of reducing anaemia among women of reproductive age, with prevalence declining only marginally from 45.2% to 44.7% [4]. As of the most recent estimates, 48.4% of women are affected [5]. Furthermore, analysis of the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey indicates a double burden of malnutrition among this group: 6.7% are underweight, 19.7% are overweight, and 7.4% are obese, totaling 27.1% overweight or obese overall [6]. For comparison, the combined prevalence of overweight and obesity was 17.3% in 2008 and 13.2% in 2013 [7], suggesting a non-linear pattern over the preceding decade.

Mixed progress has been observed in infant and young child nutrition. According to the Global Nutrition Report, Sierra Leone was considered on track to achieve the World Health Assembly (WHA) 2025 exclusive breastfeeding target, with exclusive breastfeeding rates increasing from 31.2% in 2010 to 54.0% among infants aged 0–5 months in 2019 [5, 8]. Also, progress has been made in reducing the prevalence of low birth weight, which declined from 17.1% in 2001 to 14.4% in 2015 [5]. In contrast, stunting remains a major public health concern: 26.3% of children under five are stunted, a figure slightly lower than the Africa regional average of 30.7%, yet still indicative of significant chronic undernutrition [5]. The prevalence of wasting in Sierra Leone remains a challenge. Although the rate fluctuates over time, 4.6% in 2014, increasing to 6.8% in 2017, declining to 5.4% in 2019, and rising again to 6.3% in 2021, it remains slightly higher than the regional average of 6.0% [5]. While the prevalence of overweight (2.8%) is lower than that of undernutrition indicators, Sierra Leone is classified as off track to prevent further increase [5]. Emerging evidence suggests that early-life dietary exposures and suboptimal feeding practices may be contributing to this trend. Excessive sugar intake from commercially available complementary food has been shown to promote excess weight gain [9]. Furthermore, childhood overweight is more likely to occur when adherence to optimal breastfeeding practices is low [10].

Diet-related non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are also emerging as an important public health concern in Sierra Leone. According to the Global Nutrition Report, 15.6% of adult women and 4.7% of adult men were living with obesity in 2019 [5]. Although these figures remain lower than the African regional prevalence for the same period (20.8% for women and 9.2% for men), they indicate a growing public health challenge.

Trends over time further highlight this increase. Among women, the prevalence of obesity rose from 7.1% in 2000 to 13.3% in 2016, and reaching 15.6% by 2019. A similar upward trend is observed among men, where obesity prevalence increased from 1.6% in 2000 to 3.8% in 2016, and reaching 4.7% in 2019 [5].

In response to these challenges, Sierra Leone has implemented several nutrition-related strategies and programs. The country has been a member of the Scaling Up Nutrition (SUN) movement and has adopted the Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, Child, Adolescent Health and Nutrition (RMNCAH&N Strategy 2017–2021), which provides a life-stage approach to health and nutrition across women, newborns, children, and adolescents [11]. The World Food Programme’s Country Strategic Plan 2020–2025 targets nutritionally vulnerable populations, including children aged 6–23 months, pregnant and lactating women, and adolescent girls, and has expanded its Home-Grown School Feeding Programme [1]. Routine vitamin A supplementation has transitioned from mass campaign delivery to integration within routine health services [12], and community health workers screen over 100,000 children annually for acute malnutrition [13].

The most recent comprehensive national micronutrient biomarker survey in Sierra Leone is the 2013 Sierra Leone Micronutrient Survey [14], meaning that nationally representative micronutrient data are now more than a decade old. Consequently, despite the availability of some national surveys and situation analyses, evidence on micronutrient status and dietary intake among women, children, and adolescents in Sierra Leone remains fragmented and outdated. This challenge reflects broader evidence gaps commonly observed in many low- and middle-income countries [15], where accurate figures are limited by the absence of recent national and subnational surveys, insufficient use of biomarkers, and reduced attention to indicators such as vitamin A deficiency [15]. As a result, critical data gaps hinder the ability to track progress and evaluate the impact of interventions. There is therefore an urgent need for survey and research methodologies to generate disaggregated data on micronutrient deficiencies and intervention coverage, including food fortification, dietary diversification, and supplementation [15]. In response to this gap, this systematic rapid review aimed to synthesise existing evidence, map nutritional outcomes, identify priority evidence gaps, and generate insights to inform nutrition policy and guide the design of future national surveys and research in Sierra Leone.

Methodology

We conducted a systematic rapid review of published, peer-reviewed studies [16, 17] to map and analyse the nutritional status of women of reproductive age, children under five, and adolescents in Sierra Leone, and to identify key knowledge gaps and summarising the scope of recent quantitative studies on nutrition. This approach was necessary to address the urgent need for evidence to inform policy and program planning, and to identify gaps that could be addressed in future national surveys and research. This review was conducted in line with the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) checklist [18]. We adopted Arksey and O’Malley’s methodological framework for scoping studies [19]. To strengthen rigour and ensure practical relevance, we incorporated the refinements proposed by Levac, Colquhoun, and O’Brien [20]. This review was not registered in a protocol registry. Consistent with the rapid review methodology [16, 17], formal critical appraisal of individual study quality was not conducted; the aim was to map available evidence rather than assess methodological rigour.

Identifying the research questions

We used the Joanna Briggs Institute Population–Concept–Context framework [21] to guide our research questions, focusing on Population (women of reproductive age, children 6–59 months, adolescents), Concept (nutritional status, micronutrient deficiencies, dietary patterns, indicators), and Context (Sierra Leone). Identifying relevant studies—search strategy

The literature search was conducted by UE systematically using PubMed. Given the rapid nature of this review [22, 23]. The search was limited to PubMed, a comprehensive source for biomedical and public health literature. PubMed indexes a broad range of peer-reviewed journals covering nutrition, maternal and child health, and epidemiology [24], making it relevant for our study objective. Keywords, as outlined in Table 1, were combined to construct search queries. The advanced search feature in PubMed was used to combine keywords related to outcome measures, population groups, and geographic regions using the Boolean operator “AND.” This process generated individual search queries for each concept to be screened for the review.

Selecting relevant articles

Eligible studies included those that assessed micronutrient levels, nutritional status, food consumption patterns, dietary diversity, and dietary intake among women of reproductive age, children aged 6–59 months, and adolescents. The review considered surveys, cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, randomized controlled trials, and quasi-experimental designs that provided quantitative data on nutrition-related outcomes. Only peer-reviewed publications indexed in PubMed and published from the year 2000 onward were searched for. To ensure broad coverage, articles published in English or French were considered. The geographical focus was Sierra Leone, although multi-country studies were eligible if they provided extractable data specific to Sierra Leone.

The study selection process followed the PRISMA-ScR framework. Searches captured studies across four main domains: anthropometric, biochemical, clinical, and dietary assessments. Retrieved articles underwent a multi-step screening process. After duplicates were removed, titles and abstracts were independently screened by three reviewers to exclude studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria, such as those unrelated to nutrition, animal studies, or studies outside the country of interest. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were then assessed for eligibility by the same reviewers. At this stage, studies were excluded if they lacked extractable quantitative data, were review articles, or were qualitative in nature.

Discrepancies during screening and eligibility assessment were resolved through discussion and consensus among the three reviewers. Following this rigorous process, 39 articles were included in the final review. The detailed steps of the selection process are presented in Fig. 1.

Charting the data

Data extraction was carried out using a Microsoft Excel template developed for this review. The following information was systematically extracted from each included study: study identification details, study characteristics, population characteristics, nutritional indicators assessed, and key findings relevant to the review objectives. The extraction process was conducted independently by EO, UE, and MB to ensure consistency and reduce bias. Each reviewer extracted data from a subset of studies, after which the extracted information was cross-checked by another reviewer for accuracy and completeness. Discrepancies or uncertainties were discussed collectively, and consensus was reached through team deliberation.

Results

Literature search and study characteristics

A total of 39 studies (Table 2) were included in this scoping review, with publication dates ranging from 2001 to 2024. These studies captured diverse aspects of nutritional status and micronutrient deficiencies among women of reproductive age, children under five years, and, to a lesser extent, adolescents. They were retrieved from peer-reviewed journals and comprised both primary research (e.g., clinical trials, cross-sectional surveys) and secondary analyses (e.g., re-analyses of Demographic and Health Surveys [DHS], prior randomized controlled trials [RCTs], or multiple indicator cluster surveys [MICS]).

Study designs

Across the 39 studies included in this review, cross-sectional designs were the most common, accounting for 25 studies. These were primarily used to assess nutritional status, dietary practices, and micronutrient deficiencies, and included both primary data collection as well as secondary analyses of nationally representative datasets such as the DHS and MICS [6, 7, 12, 26–28, 30–32, 34–40, 42, 48, 52–55, 57–59].

After cross-sectional studies, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were the next most frequently used design, with seven studies assessing the effectiveness of interventions targeting maternal nutrition, therapeutic feeding, and the treatment of moderate and severe acute malnutrition [27, 33, 41, 44, 45, 47, 49]. Three studies conducted secondary analyses of existing RCTs to investigate outcomes such as infant growth trajectories, breastfeeding practices, and maternal health [51, 56, 60].

Two studies adopted retrospective dual-cohort designs to assess longitudinal outcomes in children recovering from malnutrition [46, 52]. Other study designs were less common, including a quasi-experimental trial assessing the impact of grandmother-inclusive feeding interventions [43], one longitudinal study tracking haemoglobin changes across trimesters in pregnant women [25], and a case-control study examining determinants of low birth weight in hospital settings [50].

Geographic coverage and populations

Although 9 studies were multi-country analyses that included Sierra Leone among other sub-Saharan African nations [7, 26, 29–32, 36, 53, 58]. The remaining 30 were country-specific investigations that included selected districts of Sierra Leone. These districts included rural locales such as Port Loko, Pujehun, Bombali, Bonthe, Tonkolili, and the Western Rural Area, as well as urban or peri-urban centres around Freetown [25, 39, 50, 51]. A few studies explicitly compared rural and urban nutritional indicators [38, 40, 42, 48].

Study participants ranged from infants and young children to adolescent girls and adult women. Specifically, 24 out of the 39 studies focused on children aged 6–59 months [27–29, 31–37, 40–42, 44–49, 52, 55, 59, 60], primarily to assess acute malnutrition, prevalence of anaemia, and growth outcomes.

Another seven studies included pregnant or lactating women, with most investigating maternal nutritional status, including undernutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, and their implications for pregnancy outcomes and early child health. Some studies also evaluated nutrition interventions, antenatal care screening, and community-based maternal nutrition programs [25, 35, 39, 43, 44, 51, 54]. Several analyses also covered the broader group of women of reproductive age (WRA, 15–49 years) for investigations into body mass index (BMI), underweight, overweight/obesity, and micronutrient deficiencies [6, 26, 30, 34, 35, 42, 54, 57, 58]. Only a few articles [56, 60] explicitly disaggregated data for pregnant adolescents or older adolescents (18–19 years).

Sample sizes ranged from fewer than 200 participants in some hospital-based or controlled clinical trial settings [25, 27, 50] to thousands of participants in studies using DHS or other national surveys [6, 7, 36, 42, 48, 54, 55, 57]. Many samples included both male and female children, although a subset of articles focused solely on women or girls.

Nutritional status of children - wasting

Eleven studies [27, 28, 33, 36, 37, 41, 45, 46, 49, 51, 52] reported findings on acute malnutrition (wasting) among children aged 6–59 months (Table 2). Three of these provided population-based prevalence estimates, whereas the remaining eight enrolled children were already wasted or at high risk of wasting.

Using nationally representative data, the 2013 Sierra Leone DHS estimated a wasting prevalence of 9.3% (95% CI: 8.5–10.2%) among children under five [36]. More recently, the 2019 DHS reported wasting at 5.9%, and the 2021 SMART survey at 5.2% [61, 62]. A cross-sectional baseline survey in December 2010 among eight rural communities in Bombali and Tonkolili districts in northern Sierra Leone showed that 4.8% of children under five were wasted [28]. Retrospective program data from Tonkolili District showed an increase in wasting prevalence from 3.6 to 5.1% in the pre-Ebola period to 7.9–8.2% post-Ebola [37]. These community or district-level data suggest that 4–10% of children are wasted in broader populations, although localised crises can raise these figures significantly [37].

The remaining eight studies [27, 33, 41, 45, 46, 49, 51, 52] conducted between 2009 and 2019 in Pujehun, Port Loko and Bombali districts reported recovery rates ranging from 39% to 83% depending on the protocol and product used (Table 3). Notably, oat-based RUTF achieved higher SAM graduation (56%) than standard peanut-based RUTF (45%; P = 0.001) [41] Integrated GAM management achieved 83% recovery with 71% community coverage [33]. Body composition analysis indicated that approximately 82% of the weight gained during MAM treatment was fat-free mass [45].

Stunting

Five studies [28, 36, 42, 48, 59] reported on stunting using data collected between 2005 and 2019 (Table 3). National survey data show a declining trajectory: stunting decreased from 36.4% (MICS 2005) to 37.9% (DHS 2013), 31.3% (SMART 2017), 29.1% (95% CI: 27.7–30.4%; DHS 2019) [48], and 26.2% (SMART 2021) [61]. However, this rate remains above the WHA 2030 target of 20% and is classified as a serious public health concern.

Sub-nationally, Winkler et al. [28] found 41.8% stunting (95% CI: 37.7–45.9%) in the rural districts of Bombali and Tonkolili in December 2010, substantially higher than contemporaneous national estimates. A rural–urban differential persists: the 2019 DHS showed 31.6% (95% CI: 29.8–33.2%) in rural areas versus 24.0% (95% CI: 21.6–26.1%) in urban settings [48]. Shibre et al. [42], analysing four survey rounds (MICS 2005, DHS 2008, MICS 2010, DHS 2013), documented persistent inequalities: stunting was consistently higher among children from the poorest quintile, those with uneducated mothers, and those in rural areas. Although stunting among the poorest declined by approximately 7% points between 2005 and 2013, overlapping confidence intervals limited conclusions about statistical significance [42].

Under-five underweight

Three studies reported on underweight [28, 36, 37], using data from 2008 to 2016. Akombi et al. [36] reported a national prevalence of 16.4% in the 2013 DHS, while Winkler et al. [28] found 23.2% in rural northern communities in December 2010. Kamara et al. [37] reported 10.6–11.3% MAM by weight-for-age in screenings at the Tonkolili District facility, with no significant change before versus during the Ebola outbreak.

Under-five overweight and obesity

Only one study [29] reported on child overweight/obesity using DHS data collected approximately 2010–2013. Sierra Leone had a combined prevalence of 16.9% (9.5% obesity, 7.4% overweight), the highest among the 26 sub-Saharan African countries, amounting to an estimated 178,126 affected preschoolers at the time of analysis.

Nutritional status of adolescents

Only two studies [56, 60] disaggregated results for adolescents, both drawing from the same RCT of undernourished pregnant women (MUAC < = 23 cm) in Pujehun and Western rural districts, 2017–2019. Koroma et al. [56] found that younger adolescent mothers (< 18 years; n = 236) had significantly smaller newborns than adults (≥ 20 years; n = 741): birth weight difference − 0.3 kg (95% CI: −0.3, − 0.2; P < 0.001) and birth length difference − 1.1 cm (95% CI: −1.5, − 0.7; P < 0.001). The nutritional intervention benefited adults more than younger adolescents (interaction P = 0.02 for birth weight). Koroma et al. [60] reported that EBF declined from 88% at 6 weeks to 17% at 24 weeks among infants of these mothers, with EBF associated with lower infant mortality (HR = 0.39; 95% CI: 0.18–0.84; P = 0.017).

Nutritional status of women of reproductive age

Four studies [6, 7, 30, 57] documented underweight and/or overweight/obesity among women of reproductive age (WRA) using DHS data from 2008 to 2019 (Table 3). For underweight: Razak et al. [30] using Sierra Leone’s 2008 DHS, 2.17% (95% CI: 1.33–3.01%) of women aged 20–49 years had severe thinness (BMI < 16.0), with rural prevalence (2.75%) more than twice the urban rate (1.10%). Two studies using the 2019 DHS [6, 57] reported a 6.7% prevalence of underweight (BMI < 18.5) among women aged 15–49 years, with the highest prevalence in the youngest age group (15–24 years, 3.8%), and no significant association with household wealth.

For overweight/obesity: Irache et al. [7] documented a U-shaped pattern across three DHS rounds among women 20–49 years: OWOB was 17.3% (95% CI: 15.0–19.8%) in 2008, 13.2% (95% CI: 11.7–14.8%) in 2013, and 19.6% in 2019. Ikoona et al. [6], using the 2019 DHS for women 15–49 years, found overweight 19.7% (95% CI: 17.7–21.7%), obesity 7.4% (95% CI: 5.2–9.6%), and combined overnutrition 27.1% (95% CI: 25.2–29.0%). The co-existence of 6.7% underweight and 27.1% overnutrition in the same 2019 survey population confirms an active double burden of malnutrition.

Micronutrient deficiencies

Anaemia

Anaemia emerged as the most frequently assessed micronutrient-related concern, with eight studies [25, 28, 34, 39, 54, 59] providing prevalence data across a timeline from 1995 to 2019 (Table 3).

For childhood anaemia, Wirth et al. [34], in the 2013 SLMS, found that 76.3% (95% CI: 71.8, 80.4%) were anaemic and classified the condition as a severe public health problem. Notably, iron deficiency was only 5.2% (95% CI: 3.3, 8.1%), and iron-deficiency anaemia (IDA) was 3.8% (95% CI: 2.5, 5.8%), indicating that non-iron factors (malaria at 52.6%, inflammation at 72.6%, and haemoglobinopathies) might be key drivers. Mutisya et al. [59], reported an overall anaemia prevalence of 68.9% in 3,459 children (2019 DHS); mild 35.8%, moderate 30.3%, severe 2.8%. Sub-nationally, Winkler et al. [28] documented 86.1% anaemia (95% CI: 83.1–88.9%) in 571 children in Bombali/Tonkolili (December 2010), with moderate anaemia (Hb 7.0–9.9 g/dL) affecting 56.2%.

Among non-pregnant women 15–49 years, Wirth et al. [34] reported 44.8% (95% CI: 40.1, 49.5%) in the 2013 SLMS; Salifu et al. [58] found comparable estimates across the 2008 and 2013 DHS rounds. Among lactating women, Arinda et al. [54] found 52.9% (95% CI: 50.9–55.8%) in the 2019 DHS. Sub-nationally, Bussink-Voorend et al. [39] reported 65.9% among 611 pregnant women at their first ANC visit in Tonkolili District (2017–2018), and Winkler et al. [28] reported 73.7% among women of reproductive age in Bombali/Tonkolili (December 2010). A longitudinal study by Torlesse and Hodges [25] (1995–1996, peri-urban Freetown) demonstrated that combined albendazole and iron-folate supplementation maintained haemoglobin levels throughout pregnancy (P > 0.05), whereas either supplement alone was associated with significant increases in anaemia (P < 0.05).

Iodine deficiency

Rohner et al. [35] reported 80.7% of households had adequately iodised salt (≥ 15 mg/kg). Median urinary iodine concentration was 175.8 µg/L in pregnant women and 190.8 µg/L in non-pregnant women, both above the WHO adequacy threshold. Women in households without adequately iodised salt had significantly lower UIC (P < 0.05 for pregnant women, P < 0.001 for non-pregnant women).

Vitamin A

Four studies provided data on vitamin A status or supplementation. Wirth et al. [34] (2013 SLMS) reported vitamin A deficiency (adjusted RBP < 0.7 µmol/L) in 17.4% (95% CI: 13.9–21.6%) of children, a moderate public health problem, and 2.1% (95% CI: 1.1–3.1%) of non-pregnant women. However, vitamin A insufficiency (RBP < 1.05 µmol/L) affected 79.9% of women. VAS coverage was 86.9% among children aged 6–59 months in 2018 [40], though a 2019 follow-up evaluation found a decline in caregiver awareness after the transition from mass campaigns to routine delivery [12].

Folate and other micronutrient deficiencies

Wirth et al. [34] reported 79.2% folate deficiency (plasma folate < 10 nmol/L) among women, an alarmingly high rate and a likely contributor to the high anaemia burden. Vitamin B12 deficiency was 0.5% among non-pregnant women.

Dietary intake and feeding practices

Exclusive breastfeeding practices

According to Koroma et al. [38], in data collected in approximately 2016–2017, approximately 49% of mothers reported practising EBF for the first six months, with notable regional disparities. EBF rates were particularly lower in the North compared to other regions [38]. Similarly, Aidam et al. [43] reported that when communities implemented a “grandmother-inclusive” approach, over 90% of mothers practised EBF during the first week of life, compared to 79% in control areas. However, the study did not extend follow-up to six months, limiting insights into sustained EBF.

Complementary feeding knowledge and practices

Koroma et al. [40] found that 77.2% of caregivers were aware that complementary feeding should begin at six months, but only 44.9% had offered their children aged 6–39 months foods from three or more food groups in the preceding 24 h. Similarly, Koroma et al. in another study [38] reported that among children aged 6–23 months, 52.6% of mothers met the minimum meal frequency appropriate for age, including 42.7% of mothers of children aged 6–11 months who fed their child at least twice daily and 61.1% of mothers of children aged 12–23 months who fed their child at least three times daily. The study also found that 49.2% of mothers achieved the minimum dietary diversity (≥ 3 of the 6 food groups) in the previous 24 h.

Regional variation was also evident, with the North performing poorly in exclusive breastfeeding but somewhat better in complementary feeding compared to the Western Area [38].

Importantly, household dynamics appeared to influence complementary feeding outcomes. Aidam et al. [43] demonstrated that active involvement of grandmothers significantly improved child feeding practices. In communities where grandmothers were engaged, children aged 6–23 months achieved higher minimum dietary diversity (77.2% vs. 51.8% in control) and were more likely to meet the minimum acceptable diet.

Issaka et al. [32], who compared Anglophone and Francophone West African countries, showed that only 34.3% of Sierra Leonean children aged 6–23 months met minimum dietary diversity, only 42.0% met minimum meal frequency, and only 18.5% achieved the minimum acceptable diet. Furthermore, 64.7% of infants aged 6–8 months had been introduced to solid or semi-solid foods.

Geographic and regional disparities in child undernutrition and maternal

Nutrition outcomes

Studies reviewed consistently pointed to a marked rural–urban divide in child nutrition outcomes in Sierra Leone. For instance, Sserwanja et al. [48] reported a stunting prevalence of 31.6% in rural areas compared to 24.0% in urban settings. Similarly, Winkler et al. [28] documented an even higher prevalence of 41.8% stunting among children under five in eight rural communities in the Northern Province near Makeni. These figures exceed national estimates, such as the 37.9% overall prevalence reported by Akombi et al. [36]. In addition to stunting, rural populations faced a high burden of anaemia [59]. Mutisya et al. [59] found that children living in rural areas were 1.55 times more likely to be anaemic than their urban counterparts, a finding consistent with earlier evidence from disadvantaged rural settings [28].

Regional differences are also apparent in nutritional outcomes among women. A secondary analysis of the 2019 Sierra Leone DHS reported that underweight prevalence among women aged 15–49 years was lower in the Northern region compared with the Eastern region [57]. In contrast, other evidence highlights different nutritional challenges within the same region. For example, high levels of child undernutrition have been reported in northern Sierra Leone, including findings from a study conducted in Bombali and Tonkolili districts that documented a high prevalence of stunting among children under five [28]. At the same time, another analysis of the 2019 SLDHS reported a higher prevalence of overweight and obesity among women in the Northern region, suggesting emerging overnutrition concerns among adult women [6]. Together, these findings highlight the coexistence of multiple forms of malnutrition across regions in Sierra Leone.

Although Winkler et al. [28] primarily examined the influence of rural poverty and access to health services in Bombali and Tonkolili districts, without disaggregating stunting by wealth quintiles, their findings indicate that children living in villages without health facilities had significantly lower haemoglobin levels than those in villages with access to health services [28]. Similarly, a secondary analysis of the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey reported higher stunting prevalence among children in rural areas compared with urban areas [48]. Together, these findings suggest that undernutrition in rural Sierra Leone is closely linked to structural constraints such as poverty and limited access to healthcare services.

Discussion

The reviewed studies collectively underscore multiple coexisting nutrition challenges in Sierra Leone, spanning undernutrition (stunting and wasting), micronutrient deficiencies, and an emerging double burden of malnutrition among adult women. Although the country has made significant strides in collecting national data on malnutrition and interventions, our findings underscore enduring challenges and uneven progress that demand strategic action and urgent, multifaceted efforts.

Prevalence of child stunting and wasting

Wasting and stunting emerged as key nutritional concerns across the included studies (Table 3). National estimates indicate that wasting declined from 9.3% in the 2013 Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) to 5.2% in the 2021 SMART survey. These levels are broadly comparable to recent global and regional estimates reported in the 2025 Joint Child Malnutrition Estimates, which indicate a wasting prevalence of 6.6% globally, 6.5% in West Africa, and 5.5% in sub-Saharan Africa in 2024 [63]. However, the Ebola-period doubling of community-level MAM in Tonkolili (3.6% to 8.2%) [37] demonstrates that crisis-driven reversals can occur rapidly.

National stunting prevalence declined from 40% in the 2005 MICS [64] to 26.2% in the 2021 SMART survey [61], representing a 14-percentage-point reduction over 16 years (Fig. 2, Panel A). Despite this progress, the prevalence remains above the global estimate of 23.2% in 2024 and is broadly comparable to regional levels in sub-Saharan Africa (32.3%) and West Africa (29.7%) in 2024 [63]. Stunting burdens are particularly pronounced in rural communities, where prevalence remains higher than national averages. Achieving the 2030 World Health Assembly (WHA) target of reducing stunting to 20% [8]will therefore require a significant acceleration of current progress.

Double burden of malnutrition in women

The co-existence of 6.7% underweight and 27.1% overnutrition among women in the 2019 DHS [6, 57] confirms an active double burden. Irache et al. [7] documented a U-shaped pattern in OWOB across three DHS rounds (2008: 17.3%, 2013: 13.2%, 2019: 19.6%), indicating fluctuation rather than a simple linear increase. The annual average relative change of + 0.24% per year across the period reflects a modest net increase. Meanwhile, severe thinness (BMI < 16) persisted at 2.17% among rural women in the 2008 DHS [30]. These findings align with broader global nutrition transitions [65–67]. It also highlights the necessity for context-specific interventions addressing both undernutrition, overweight/obesity and diet-related non-communicable disease (NCD) risks within the same population.

Micronutrient deficiencies: anaemia and beyond

The anaemia prevalence levels are consistent with broader African patterns: a pooled analysis estimated anaemia prevalence at 59% among children under five across Africa [68], while a multicountry study of low- and middle-income countries reported 39.5% among non-pregnant women and 45.2% among pregnant women [69].

Critically, Wirth et al. [34] demonstrated that iron deficiency accounted for only a small fraction of the anaemia burden in children (iron deficiency 5.2%, IDA 3.8%), underscoring the multifactorial aetiology involving malaria, inflammation, and haemoglobinopathies. This finding has direct implications for intervention design: iron supplementation alone will be insufficient without concurrent malaria control and infection management.

Our findings reveal that other micronutrient deficiencies remain insufficiently addressed. From our review, folate deficiency has been reported at alarmingly high levels, affecting 79% or more of the population [34], potentially higher than was reported in Morogoro, Tanzania (40% red blood cell folate insufficiency among non-pregnant women) [70], vitamin A deficiency was moderate in children (17.4%), but widespread insufficiency (79.9%) affected women [34]. Iodised salt coverage reached 80.7% of households in the 2013 SLMS [14, 35], consistent with progress towards universal salt iodisation. However, zinc, riboflavin, and vitamin D have never been assessed nationally (Fig. 3), and all micronutrient biomarker data beyond haemoglobin are now over 12 years old. This gap is the single most critical finding of this review.

Adolescents: understudied but vulnerable

Across sub-Saharan Africa, adolescent nutrition remains poorly captured in national surveys, with limited or no disaggregation by age, sex, pregnancy status, locality, comprehensive dietary information, and broader geographic coverage [71, 72]. This gap hides important vulnerabilities, as shown in Sierra Leone, where pregnant adolescents experienced poorer birth outcomes and lower exclusive breastfeeding rates [56, 60]. Comparable evidence from Ghana and Nigeria demonstrates high micronutrient inadequacies, elevated risks of adverse birth outcomes, and low breastfeeding intentions among adolescents [73, 74].

Collectively, these findings show that the absence of disaggregated data obscures important nutritional risks for adolescents. This gap reinforces the need for national surveys and research that disaggregate adolescent data by age, sex, pregnancy status, and other relevant factors.

Exclusive breastfeeding practices

EBF prevalence improved from 11% (DHS 2008) [75] to 53% (SMART 2021) [61], a nearly fivefold increase and one of Sierra Leone’s clearest nutrition successes (Fig. 3). However, this progress has not been linear. EBF prevalence declined from 61.6% in the 2017 SMART survey to 53% in 2021 [61, 62], indicating some recent reversal in gains. Despite this fluctuation, the current rate exceeds the West African average of 33.9% and the global average of 43.8% as of 2021 [5], and has surpassed the WHA 2025 target of 50%. However, it remains below the WHA 2030 target of 70% [8, 76]. The steep within-cohort attrition from 88% at 6 weeks to 17% at 24 weeks [60] indicates that sustaining EBF through six months remains a major challenge, even within supported intervention settings. Exclusive breastfeeding rates declined from 61.6% in the 2017 SMART survey to 53% in the 2021 SMART survey.

Findings in the context of national survey data

Although this review was limited to peer-reviewed studies indexed in PubMed, comparing findings with nationally representative survey data provides important context. The DHS (conducted in 2008, 2013, and 2019) [75, 77, 78] and MICS (2005, 2010) [64, 79] collect data on anthropometry, haemoglobin-based anaemia, breastfeeding, and infant and young child feeding practices. In addition, the 2017 and 2021 National Nutrition Surveys [61, 62], conducted using SMART methodology, provide mid-point estimates.

For stunting, the trajectory across these surveys indicates an overall declining trend over time, despite a slight increase between earlier survey rounds. Prevalence increased from 36% in the 2008 DHS to 37.9% in the 2013 DHS [75, 77], before declining to 31.3% in the 2017 SMART survey, 30% in the 2019 DHS, and 26.2% in the 2021 SMART survey [61, 62, 78]. These figures indicate meaningful progress, although stunting still remains classified as a serious public health concern. For wasting, national estimates show a declining trend, decreasing from 9% in the 2013 DHS to 5.1% in the 2017 SMART survey and 5.2% in the 2021 SMART survey [61, 62, 77], indicating an overall improvement with relative stability in recent years.

Regarding anaemia in children, the 2013 SLMS reported 76.3%, and the 2019 DHS reported 68.9% [34, 59], suggesting some decline. Among women, anaemia prevalence showed minimal change between the 2008 and 2013 DHS rounds [58].

Critically, however, the DHS and MICS do not routinely measure micronutrient biomarkers beyond haemoglobin, nor do they collect individual dietary intake data. The only comprehensive micronutrient biomarker assessment, the 2013 SLMS, measured retinol-binding protein, ferritin, folate, vitamin B12, urinary iodine, and C-reactive protein [14, 34, 35]. No equivalent survey has been conducted since. This means that for indicators such as vitamin A status, folate deficiency, zinc deficiency, and dietary intake patterns, the evidence base is over a decade old. This gap underscores the central conclusion of this review: that a nationally representative food consumption and micronutrient survey that incorporates a comprehensive biomarker panel and individual dietary assessment is urgently needed.

Data gaps

Despite identifying many articles, the fragmented nature of the available data posed a significant challenge. Many of the included studies were cross-sectional (e.g [28, 36, 37, 59]), limiting the ability to track long-term or seasonal changes in nutritional status. Intervention trials frequently enrolled only already-wasted children [27, 33, 46, 49, 51, 52], thus reducing the generalizability of their findings to broader child populations. Adolescent nutrition emerged as especially under-documented, with only [56, 60] disaggregating results for pregnant adolescents. Furthermore, comprehensive micronutrient panels were rarely assessed. While [34, 35] offered in-depth biochemical data (haemoglobin, ferritin, folate, and urinary iodine), information on other critical nutrients such as zinc, riboflavin, and vitamin D remained scarce.

Despite 39 included studies spanning 2001–2024, significant evidence gaps persist (Fig. 3). Anthropometric indicators (stunting, wasting) and exclusive breastfeeding have been tracked across multiple survey rounds (7 data points each), enabling trend assessment. In contrast, Micronutrient biomarkers beyond haemoglobin were comprehensively assessed only once, in the 2013 SLMS [35, 80]. Zinc, riboflavin, and vitamin D have never been measured nationally. Vitamin A, folate, B12, ferritin, and urinary iodine data are now over 12 years old. Individual dietary intake data (24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire) were not collected by any included study. Complementary feeding indicators are available from DHS rounds but lack the depth needed for dietary pattern analysis. Adolescent-specific data are limited to two studies from a single RCT in two districts. Non-pregnant adolescents and adolescent males have no nutritional data. Sub-national coverage is concentrated in Pujehun (9 studies), Tonkolili (4 studies), Bombali (2 studies), and Port Loko (1 study). Many districts have no study-level data beyond DHS reports. Seasonal variation in nutritional status has not been assessed by any of the included studies, despite its known importance in agrarian settings. These gaps collectively reinforce the central conclusion of this review: a nationally representative food consumption and micronutrient survey that incorporates a comprehensive biomarker panel, individual dietary assessment, and systematic disaggregation by age, sex, pregnancy status, and subnational geography is urgently needed.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this rapid review is its exclusive reliance on PubMed for peer-reviewed articles. While comprehensive, PubMed does not capture the most key nationally representative data (MICS, DHS,FSMS) nor other studies relevant to Sierra Leone, particularly those in local journals or grey literature such as reports, theses, and institutional documents. By focusing only on peer-reviewed, quantitative studies, the review may underestimate available evidence on nutrient intake, intervention effectiveness and efficacy, and population nutrition trends.

The exclusion of qualitative research is another limitation, as it omits important insights into cultural and behavioural drivers of malnutrition that require different search strategies. In addition, heterogeneity across study objectives, populations, and outcomes prevented meta-analysis or direct comparison of findings. Finally, no formal quality appraisal was undertaken, consistent with the rapid review approach, which aimed to map evidence rather than assessing methodological rigour.

Implications and future directions

Findings from this rapid review highlight the urgent need for a nationally representative, age-disaggregated food consumption and micronutrient survey in Sierra Leone, with explicit inclusion of adolescents aged 10–19 years. Adolescent nutrition emerged as a particularly under-documented area, with very limited evidence identified in the studies included in this review. Overall, the available data from the included studies are insufficient to comprehensively assess nutritional status across all age groups. Most studies focused primarily on haemoglobin and ferritin, while information on other important micronutrients, such as zinc, riboflavin, and vitamin D, remains largely undocumented.

While large national surveys such as the DHS and MICS provide some disaggregation for adolescents aged 15–19 years, this is largely restricted to reproductive health and demographic indicators, including fertility, early marriage, and contraceptive [81]. In contrast, nutrition-specific indicators for adolescents are rarely captured in these surveys [82]. This evidence gap constrains the ability to generate context-specific insights and limits the development of effective policies and strategies aimed at improving adolescent nutrition in low- and middle-income countries [82].

Conclusion

This systematic rapid review consolidates fragmented yet crucial data on nutritional status across women, children, and adolescents in Sierra Leone, drawing on 39 peer-reviewed studies published between 2001 and 2024. Several key findings emerge.

First, malnutrition remains a serious public health concern throughout the review period.

Second, micronutrient data beyond haemoglobin are critically outdated. The 2013 SLMS [14] remains the only nationally representative survey to have measured a comprehensive panel of biomarkers, including retinol-binding protein, ferritin, folate, vitamin B12, and urinary iodine. That survey revealed widespread folate deficiency in women, moderate vitamin A deficiency among children, and low iron deficiency relative to the high burden of anaemia, pointing to non-iron causes such as malaria and haemoglobinopathies. Third, adolescent nutrition is severely under-documented, with only two studies out of the 39 included studies disaggregating results for pregnant adolescents, both of which highlighted poorer birth outcomes and lower breastfeeding rates among younger mothers.

Fourth, among women of reproductive age, the coexistence of underweight and overweight/obesity underscores an emerging double burden of malnutrition.

A nationally representative, age-disaggregated food consumption and micronutrient survey, incorporating biomarkers for vitamin A, folate, zinc, and other key micronutrients alongside individual dietary intake assessment, is urgently needed. Efforts toward this goal are already underway, with the Sierra Leone Agricultural Research Institute recently awarded a grant to develop a proposal for such a survey [83]. Until comprehensive data become available, nutrition programming and policy must be guided by the best available evidence while remaining sensitive to the significant gaps identified in this review.
