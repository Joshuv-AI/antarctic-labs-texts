Generation of hTERT-immortalized human mesenchymal stromal cells with optical and magnetic labels for in vivo transplantation and tracking
Authors: Anna N. Gabashvili, Daria D. Namestnikova, Mikhail V. Gulyaev, Irina B. Pevzner, Alexey D. Bocharnikov, Natalya A. Alexandrushkina, Anastasiya V. Ryabova, Maria V. Efremova, Viktoria A. Sarkisova, Tatiana V. Tarasova, Stepan S. Vodopyanov, Pavel I. Makarevich, Petr I. Nikitin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs) are the focus of increasing research as a potential therapeutic agent for a range of nervous system diseases, due to their unique capacity for self-renewal and differentiation. The subsequent tracking of cells post-transplantation into the organism is of pivotal significance, as it elucidates their fate, distribution, and enables the timely monitoring of any adverse effects. In the context of cell monitoring, the utilization of a non-toxic label that exhibits long-term stability is of paramount importance.MethodsA human immortalized MSCs cell line was engineered to express a green fluorescent protein (GFP) and bacterial nanocompartments (high-molecular-weight icosahedral capsid-like protein complexes) via lentiviral transduction. The obtained cells were characterized by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and Perls staining as well as using the nonlinear magnetization method, confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. An animal study was conducted in Sprague-Dawley rats.ResultsIn this study, an immortalized human MSCs cell line with stable expression of a novel magnetic resonance (MR) reporter label was established for the first time. GFP was genetically produced for utilization as an optical tag. A nanocompartment of the bacterium Quasibacillus thermotolerans (Qt) was used as a carrier of the magnetic label. The Qt nanocompartment consists of a shell and a ferroxidase cargo protein. Ferroxidase provides the biomineralization of iron ions within the nanocompartment shell. As a result, ferric oxide nanoparticles are formed inside the encapsulin nanocompartments, which have T2-contrast properties and serve as genetically encoded labels for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and for quantification by the nonlinear magnetization method.ConclusionsThe experimental results indicate that the use of two complementary labels allows for the multimodal visualization of the derived cells post-transplantation into the rat brain striatum, which is promising for monitoring MSCs-based therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04748-x.

Background

Given the nervous system’s limited capacity for regeneration, the majority of neurological disorders are generally irreversible. A particularly salient development in the domain of regenerative medicine is the utilization of mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) for the purpose of cell-based therapy. The simplicity of isolating and culturing of MSCs from various biological niches, the low immunogenicity of these cells, and their immunomodulatory properties [1, 2] enable autologous and allogeneic transplantation of these cells. The features previously enumerated facilitate the application of MSCs in translational and clinical trials for the treatment of numerous neurological diseases, among which stroke is the most common.

According to Clinicaltrials.gov [3], a multitude of clinical trials have investigated the use of autologous and allogeneic MSCs of various origins, including adipose tissue, bone marrow, and umbilical cord, for the treatment of hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke. MSCs have found even more widespread application in various in vivo models of stroke. In the majority of studies, Sprague Dawley or Wistar rats have been utilized to model cerebral ischemia induced by middle cerebral artery occlusion. The transplantation of MSCs following ischemic stroke has been demonstrated to enhance cerebral function [4], exert a protective effect on ischemic neurons, facilitate the repair of brain damage [5], and reduce neurological deficits in animal models [6].

The selection of an optimal cell administration route and targeted delivery strategy is of paramount importance for the therapeutic efficacy of a cell-based drug. This applies to brain pathologies and other diseases including corneal failure [7], multiple sclerosis [8], and myocardial infarction Furthermore, post-injection monitoring of the cells is imperative to avert potential adverse effects. For instance, it has been established that, under certain circumstances, MSCs are capable of undergoing a malignant transformation, which can result in the development of malignant neoplasms [9, 10]. Consequently, post-injection cell tracking is of utmost importance.

In the context of cell monitoring, the utilization of a non-toxic label exhibiting long-term stability is highly desirable. A wide variety of labels can be used for this purpose, depending on the research objectives. Fluorescent dyes are typically lipophilic, enabling rapid and effective labeling of all cell types. These dyes are characterized by a simple labeling protocol and bright fluorescence, which renders them suitable for studying the distribution of transplanted cells postmortem [11–13]. Alternatively, magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) have been employed for cell tracking via magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [14] and by magnetic particle quantification (MPQ) technique [15–17]. The composition of the nanoparticles and the manner in which their surfaces are functionalized enable the utilization of MNPs for both MRI and multiscale imaging [18]. However, a common disadvantage of both magnetic and fluorescent labels is the decrease in signal intensity over time as the cells proliferate, making long-term tracking of the cells challenging. Another issue associated with the use of MNPs is that not all cells possess the capacity for phagocytosis, and thus, they may not be able to capture a sufficient number of nanoparticles to be detected by MRI. In the domain of optical imaging, an alternative approach to tracking cells involves the utilization of various stable or transient genetic modifications, such as fluorescent proteins encoding genes [19, 20].

A plethora of MR reporter genes are available for utilization in genetic modification of cells and subsequent visualization through MR imaging. The most prevalent of these are genes encoding the transferrin receptor (TfR), which facilitates the uptake of transferrin-bound iron atoms into cells by receptor-mediated endocytosis [21, 22]. However, although TfR1 expression is generally well-tolerated by cells, achieving sufficient iron accumulation for a significant MR signal often requires the coexpression of ferritin. In certain studies, ferritin heavy chain-1 (Fth1) has been utilized as an MR reporter, for instance, to detect neuronal differentiation in stem cells [23]. It has been established that the expression of the gene encoding Fth1 can impact the cell’s iron homeostasis, resulting in phenotypic abnormalities in the absence of iron supplementation and an upregulation of transcript and protein levels of the cell’s endogenous TfR [24].

An alternative approach involves the utilization of human tyrosinase, the pivotal enzyme in melanin synthesis, as an MR reporter. In a particular study, tyrosinase was utilized as a multifunctional reporter gene to track stem cells that had been transplanted into an area of myocardial infarction. Following the transduction of the tyrosinase gene into MSCs, melanin was synthesized and subsequently utilized light energy for photoacoustic imaging, bound with 18 F-5-fluoro-N-(2-[diethylamino]ethyl) picolinamide for positron emission tomography (PET) imaging, and combined with iron for visibility on MRI [25]. Despite the aforementioned advantages, tyrosinase-based reporters exhibit several disadvantages, including a suboptimal signal-to-noise ratio, the generation of reactive oxygen species, cellular toxicity, and a reliance on iron availability, which can result in signal instability and a delay in signal change [26]. Another enzyme employed as an MR reporter is β-galactosidase, an indirect imaging reporter gene encoded by the bacterial LacZ gene. The enzyme has been shown to cleave specific substrates, such as S-Gal, in the presence of ferric ions or a gadolinium (Gd)-based compound. This process has been observed to result in a change in T1 or T2* relaxation times [27].

The utilization of bacterial genes as MR reporters is also a subject of considerable interest. For instance, the bacterial iron transporter gene MagA has been employed as a reporter gene in stem cells MR imaging. It has been demonstrated that when expressed in stem cells and induced with a doxycycline (Dox)-controlled system, MagA facilitates the formation of iron oxide nanocrystals, creating an MRI contrast [28, 29]. However, while the expression of the MagA reporter is well tolerated by cells such as 293 T, findings suggest that stem cell viability might be compromised when magA is constitutively expressed. This poses significant limitations toward the effectiveness of MagA as a reporter gene for cell-based therapies [30].

The divalent metal transporter (DMT1), which is naturally present in mammalian cells, can be utilized for Manganese (Mn)-enhanced MRI (MEMRI) [31]. For instance, one study revealed that the in vitro uptake of Mn-based PET and MRI contrast agents (52Mn2+ and Mn2+, respectively), was augmented in DMT1-overexpressing human neural progenitor cells (hNPC-DMT1) in comparison to wild-type control cells. Following transplantation of cells into the rat striatum, an increased uptake of Mn-based contrast agents in transplanted hNPC-DMT1 was detected both in vivo using MEMRI and ex vivo using PET and autoradiography [32]. Another study demonstrated that DMT1 overexpression induces MRI contrast in diverse cell types, including mouse B16 melanoma and mouse GL261 glioma, both in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that ectopic DMT1 expression induces MRI contrast in the developing mouse brain. However, a notable disadvantage of the aforementioned system is that MEMRI can only be performed subsequent to systemic administration of MnCl₂ [33].

Another system that relies on an external source of iron or Mn is the T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing protein 2 (Timd2), which has been shown to mediate ferritin endocytosis. The utilization of Fe-loaded or Mn-loaded ferritin as a contrast agent enables the acquisition of T1- and T2-weighted MRI. The study demonstrated that Timd2-expressing cells successfully internalize ferritin, and that Timd2-mediated Mn-loaded ferritin uptake enhances T1 and T2 relaxation in cell pellet samples. Moreover, Timd2 facilitates the identification of Timd2-expressing cells within the living organism by means of T2-weighted imaging [34].

Additionally, there are MR reporter genes that do not require contrast, including aquaporins (Aqp) [35] and urea transporters (UT-B) [36]. These proteins facilitate the visualization of cells by increasing transmembrane water exchange [37]. Aquaporins offer a distinctive approach for imaging genetic activity in deep tissues by accelerating the rate of cellular water diffusion. However, distinguishing aquaporin signals from the tissue background is challenging because water diffusion is influenced by structural factors, such as cell size and packing density [38]. In order to overcome this limitation and improve sensitivity, one study proposed an all-genetic approach that combines heightened cellular paramagnetism with enhanced transmembrane water diffusion. The study utilized a dual-gene construct comprising an organic anion transporting polypeptide (Oatp1b3) and an Aqp1. It was demonstrated that the expression of Aqp1 potentiates MRI contrast in cultured cells engineered to express Oatp1b3. The contrast amplification was attributed to an Aqp1-driven increase in water exchange, which facilitated the internalization of Gd ions by Oatp1b3-expressing cells. The methodology employed enabled the detection of cells using concentrations of Gd that were approximately 10-fold lower than those utilized in the Aqp1-free scenario [39]. It is important to note that the use of such reporters in the context of stem cell tracking should be done with caution, as Aqp are known to be involved in cell proliferation and signaling pathways associated with cell stemness [40].

An alternative approach involves the utilization of reporters based on bacterial biotin acceptor peptide (BAP); a small, genetically encoded sequence employed in conjunction with the bacterial biotin ligase (BirA) for site-specific protein biotinylation. For MRI, these peptides are engineered into or fused with target molecules, allowing for their biotinylation and subsequent detection or targeting using avidin or streptavidin probes conjugated to MRI contrast agents [41]. Using this labeling strategy allows for the detection of both prokaryotic [42], and eukaryotic cells [43, 44].

Another molecular imaging technique that enhances MRI contrast by indirectly detecting specific metabolites with exchangeable protons is Chemical Exchange Saturation Transfer (CEST) MRI. This is an MRI contrast approach in which exogenous or endogenous compounds containing either exchangeable protons or exchangeable molecules are selectively saturated and, after transfer of this saturation, detected indirectly through the water signal with enhanced sensitivity. The merits of this approach are manifold. Foremost among them is the potential elimination of the need for extra injected agents. This is due to the fact that reporter gene proteins containing protons undergo rapid exchange with the water proton. CEST MRI employs frequency-selective radio-frequency pulses to saturate the magnetization of exchangeable protons on molecules such as proteins and metabolites. Proton exchange with bulk water has been demonstrated to result in a decreased water MRI signal [45]. Amide proton-based reporter genes, engineered into cells can be used as tags for CEST MRI to successfully detect gene expression. These include the lysine-rich protein (LRP) reporter [46], a human protamine-1 variant with an enhanced CEST signature [47], and green fluorescent protein enriched with lysine and arginine residues (supercharged green fluorescent proteins) [48]. However, despite the high CEST contrast, a significant in vivo background must be disentangled from the desired reporter protein signals. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that these background CEST signals may undergo alterations in conjunction with disease progression or in response to therapeutic interventions, particularly in cases where pH or protein synthesis is subject to modification [49]. This phenomenon contributes to the complexity of interpreting CEST signals, necessitating careful consideration of these variables in clinical and research settings.

To address these limitations, various new variants of CEST contrast are being developed. For instance, proteins and peptides containing tyrosine, tryptophan, and lysine residues exhibit CEST contrast that is shifted far downfield from water [50]. Furthermore, the frequently utilized CEST MRI LRP reporter gene exhibits instability due to the presence of a highly repetitive DNA sequence encoding the reporter, which leads to DNA recombination events and, consequently, the expression of a range of truncated protein fragments with reduced lysine amide protons. This results in diminished and constrained CEST sensitivity. In one of the works, this reporter was improved. The redesign employed an RHGP (arginine, histidine, glycine, proline) amino acid motif, separated by varying numbers of lysines. The reporter’s stability and contrast enhancement ability were evaluated in cell lysates from two distinct cell lines: 293 T and murine GL261 glioma cells. Additionally, the evaluation was conducted in vivo on brain tumors in mice that expressed LRP. Significant improvements in stability and contrast enhancement ability were demonstrated [51].

Summarizing, genetically encoded MR tags have emerged as valuable tools for MR imaging of cells. However, it is imperative to meticulously evaluate the potential limitations of these proteins in the contest of stem cell research. In addition to the aforementioned drawbacks, the potential immunogenicity of xenogeneic proteins and the impact of transgenic sequences on cell proliferation rates should be considered. Furthermore, the genetic modification of all cells does not occur with equal efficiency. In view of the aforementioned observations, the primary objective is to identify a label that enables non-invasive long-term monitoring of stem cells in vivo while exhibiting no limitations described above.

In 1994, protein complexes that possessed structural and functional characteristics analogous to those of ferritin were identified in the bacterial strain Brevibacterium linens [52]. Later, analogous systems were identified in bacteria of other strains [53, 54]. Subsequent studies demonstrated that these protein complexes, characterized by their high molecular mass, revealed structural homologies with viral capsids. Consequently, these structures were designated as encapsulins or bacterial nanocompartments. A substantial number of these genes have now been identified in bacteria and archaea [55]. As time has passed, the focus of nanocompartment research has undergone a shift from their study in a microbiological context to their potential use in the field of biotechnology. In this domain, encapsulins have demonstrated their efficacy as a substitute for conventional nanoplatforms. For instance, nanocompartments have been utilized as nanobioreactors [56–58], delivery systems [59, 60], as well as labels for TEM (transmission electron microscopy) [61, 62], and MRI [63, 64].with notable success.

The encapsulin system from Quasibacillus thermotolerans (Q. thermotolerans, Qt) described in detail in 2019 particularly stands out due to its large shell size (42 nm) formed by self-assembly from 240 monomeric proteins (32.2 kDa each). In Qt encapsulins, the process of iron biomineralization is executed by the cargo protein IMEF (Iron-Mineralizing Encapsulin-Associated Firmicute, comprising 42 dimers with a molecular weight of 22.6 kDa each). The cargo protein facilitates the oxidation of divalent iron to trivalent iron. Therefore, these nanocompartments coalesce to form an icosahedron, exhibiting an exceptionally high mass of 9.6 MDa, with a capacity to accumulate up to 30,000 iron atoms inside the shells [65, 66].

In this study, we present a novel genetically encoded MR reporter for noninvasive in vivo imaging of human MSCs transplanted into rat brain. The label based on Qt encapsulin that is stably expressed in human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) immortalized MSCs from adipose tissue (ASC52telo-Qt cell line). The genes responsible for encoding the shell and cargo encapsulin proteins (QtEncFLAG-QtIMEF), in addition to the genes encoding the divalent iron transporter mZip14, which is necessary for the transportation and loading of iron inside the encapsulin shells, were introduced into the MSCs genome by lentiviral transduction. Following the addition of ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS) to ASC52telo-Qt cells, the process of iron biomineralization in encapsulins is initiated, leading to the formation of iron-containing MNPs within the encapsulin shells. The presence of MNPs in ASC52telo-Qt cells enables their non-invasive in vivo monitoring via MRI after stereotactic injection into the rat brain striatum. Concomitantly, the presence of green fluorescent protein (GFP) allows the validation of results acquired through the utilization of optical methods. Therefore, the combination of two complementary tags facilitates multimodal cell imaging and provides versatile data on both cells’ localization and viability. For the first time in the known literature, a Qt nanocompartment-based label was successfully implemented in an in vivo study for non-invasive long-term tracking of human MSCs. The results are encouraging for the development of novel diagnostic and therapeutic cell-based agents for regenerative medicine.

Methods

Cell culture

hTERT immortalized adipose derived mesenchymal stem cells (ASC52telo cell line, ATCC) were cultured under standard conditions (37 °C and 5% CO2) in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) growth media supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) and antibiotics (100 U/mL penicillin, 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) in 100 mm cell culture dishes (Corning, New York, NY, USA). 293 T cells were cultured in full DMEM growth medium (5% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine and antibiotics, Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Adherent cells were harvested by trypsinization and subcultured at a 1:3–1:6 ratio.

Genetic constructs

Genes encoding the nanocompartment of the bacterium Quasibacillus thermotolerans were optimized for expression in a mammalian codon and synthesized de novo. The resulting sequence was cloned into the pUC57 vector (Fig. S1a). The final clone was verified by Sanger sequencing (Evrogen, Moscow, Russia). Lentiviral transfer plasmid was generated using pLCMV-PL4-Puro plasmid as a backbone; the primer pair QtEnc- QtIMEF_XbaI_for and QtIMEF_Sall_rev was used for recloning into a lentiviral plasmid (Fig. S1b).

Construction of lentiviruses and lentiviral transduction of ASC52telo cells

Construction of lentiviral vectors carrying QtEncFLAG-QtIMEF and mZip14 encoding genes (the maps of the lentiviral transfer plasmids are shown in Fig. S2a and b) was carried out using the 293 T packaging cell line and Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to manufacturer’s protocol. Тo assemble lentiviral vectors, packaging plasmids (pRSV-Rev, pMDLg/pRRE, and pCMV-VSV-G, Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA) were used. To allow ASC52telo cells visualization by laser scanning confocal microscopy, lentiviral vectors carrying GFP and RFP (the maps of the transfer plasmids are shown in Ref [67]. and Fig.S2c, respectively) encoding genes were also obtained. ASC52telo cells were transduced with the resulting lentiviral vectors in DMEM growth medium (10% heat-inactivated FBS, 8 µg/mL polybrene (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany)). Two and one rounds of lentiviral transduction were performed using QtEncFLAG-QtIMEF and mZip14 encoding vectors, respectively, with multiplicity of infection (moi) equal to 4 and 2 for each virus. ASC52telo-RFP and ASC52telo-GFP cells were generated by transducing ASC52telo cells using RFP or GFP encoding lentiviral vectors with moi equal to 2. Puromycin (1 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to start the selection after transduction. The medium with puromycin was changed to a new one every other day.

ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo cells differentiation

ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo cells differentiation into chondroblasts, adipocytes, and osteoblasts was carried out using a commercial StemPro Differentiation kit (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions [68–70]. After the differentiation was completed, the resulting chondroblasts, adipocytes, and osteoblasts, and were stained using Toluidine Alcian Blue, OilRed O, and Alizarin Red (all from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA), respectively. A CX41 FLLED light microscope (Ningbo Sunny Instruments, Yuyao City, China) was used to obtain images of the stained cells. All assessments of cell differentiation were conducted in three replicates.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To compare IL-6 secretion by ASC52telo-Qt cells and unmodified cells, ELISA was performed using the Human IL-6 Quantikine ELISA Kit D6050 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) according to manufacturer’s recommendations [71].

Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

The transduced ASC52telo-Qt cells were cultured in T75 flasks until 90% monolayer confluency was observed. In accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. A commercial extract RNA reagent (Evrogen, Moscow, Russia) was used to isolate the total RNA. Invitrogen SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and oligo-DT and random primers were employed to synthesize the cDNA. For PCR, Taq polymerase (Fermentas, Waltham, MA, USA) was utilized to amplify cDNA samples and a negative control (an RNA sample to which no reverse transcriptase was added during cDNA synthesis). Electrophoresis was used to separate the PCR products in a 1% agarose gel, and the fragments of interest were identified based on their length.

Western blot analysis

Prior the analysis ASC52telo-Qt cells were lysed with RIPA (radioimmunoprecipitation assay) buffer. Western blot analysis was performed with 10 µL of cell lysate according to the standard procedure using mouse AlexaFluor488-labeled monoclonal anti FLAG-tag antibodies (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA, dilution 1:1000) and goat anti-mouse IgG alkaline horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA, 1:1000). The results were captured using the ChemidocMP imaging system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Immunofluorescence staining

ASC52telo-Qt cells/brain sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary mouse anti-FLAG-tag M2 monoclonal antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA, 1:500). Cells were then washed three times with PBS and exposed in the dark with goat anti-mouse AlexaFluor647 labeled secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 1:500) for 3 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Lumiprobe, Moscow, Russia). Images were captured using the Zeiss LSM 710 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany).

Flow cytometry

The expression of RFP and GFP (λEx = 555 nm/λEm = 584 nm and λEx = 488 nm/λEm = 510 nm, respectively) in ASC52telo-RFP and ASC52telo-Qt cells, respectively, as well as the presence of double positive (GFP and AlexaFluor647) ASC52telo-Qt cells was assessed with an LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Unmodified cells were used as the negative control for the autofluorescence. Dead cells were excluded from the analysis by staining with SytoxBlue Dead Cell stain (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA); cell debris, and duplexes were excluded based on the forward and side light scattering parameters. Data analysis was performed employing FACSDiva Software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) and web-based Floreada flow cytometry analysis tool (floreada.io).

Cytotoxicity study of ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS)

To assess the potential cytotoxic effect of FAS (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) on ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells, resazurin assay was provided according to manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were seeded on opaque-walled 96-well plates (Corning, New York, NY, USA) in an amount of approximately 10,000 cells/well in full DMEM/F12 growth media. Then FAS was added to the cells at a concentration range of 0.13–8 mM for 24 h. After 24 h of incubation a fresh medium (100 µL/well) with 44 µM resazurin sodium salt solution (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MI, USA) was added to each well. After 4 h of incubation fluorescence of resorufin (λEx: 560 nm to 575 nm; λEm: 580 nm to 590 nm) was recorded using CLARIOstar Plus plate analyzer (BMG LABTECH, Ortenberg, Germany, λEx = 555 nm and λEm = 600).

Prussian blue staining of cell and tissue samples

To qualitatively assess the accumulation of iron in ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells, Prussian blue staining was performed. For this purpose, the cells were seeded in 24-well cell culture plates (30,000 cells/well) and left overnight to attach to the plastic. FAS was diluted in deionized water and then in a cell culture medium to obtain desired concentration (2 mM) and added to the cells. After 24 h of incubation, ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells were washed and fixed (4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS). Subsequently, the cells were washed with deionized water and stained using Iron Staining Kit (Biovitrum, Moscow, Russia). Afterward, the cells were washed again with deionized water, and the images were taken using a CX41 FLLED light microscope (Ningbo Sunny Instruments, Yuyao City, China). Brain sections were also stained by Prussian blue for detecting iron deposits in cells according to the protocol described above.

Measurement of intracellular iron content by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS)

To investigate the iron accumulation in ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells, the growth medium was supplemented with FAS (0.5 mM, 1 mM and 2 mM) for 24 h. Then, the cells were thoroughly washed with PBS, detached with 0.25% trypsin solution, precipitated by centrifugation, and counted. For each cell line, 1 × 106 cells were dissolved in 100 µL of concentrated (68%) nitric acid (Chimmed, Moscow, Russia) for 2 h at 60 °C. Iron concentration was determined using an NexION 2000 ICP (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) mass spectrometer.

Magnetic particle quantification (MPQ)

For monitoring of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles in the samples the magnetic particle quantification (MPQ) method was used [72, 73]. The updated MPQ readers are based on nonlinear magnetization of such particles by a magnetic field at two frequencies ƒ1 = 80 Hz and ƒ2 = 80 kHz and registration of particles at a combinatorial frequency ƒ2+2f1 [74, 75].

Animals

The animal studies were performed on healthy, sexually mature female Sprague Dawley rats (150–200 g weight). The animals were obtained from Pushchino Animal Facility (Pushchino, Russia). Rats were kept in cages (4 to 5 animals per cage) and standard conditions (12 h/12 h light/dark cycle, room temperature 22 ± 2 °C, humidity 45–65%). The surgical procedures were conducted using inhalation anesthesia supplied by SomnoSuite animal anesthesia system. (Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT, USA): 3.5–4% isoflurane (114318, laboratorios Karizoo, Barcelona, Spain) with 96.5 ± 97% oxygen for the induction of anesthesia and 1.8% isoflurane/oxygen mix for its maintenance. Throughout all surgical interventions, a heating pad was used to keep the animal’s body temperature at 37 °C. All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals in the experiment and exclude painful manipulations. At the end of the experiment, rats were euthanized with a lethal dose of isoflurane.

Hematological analysis

Prior to the surgery hematological analysis was performed for all rats. Samples of blood were collected from the sublingual vein, transferred to tubes containing trisodium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid as anticoagulant. Whole blood samples were analyzed using BC-6800 Auto Hematology Analyzer (Mindray, Shenzhen, China). The following hematology parameters were determined: white blood cell (WBC), platelet count (PLT), red blood cell (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), differential white blood cell (lymphocytes, monocytes, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) count was also provided (Table S1).

Cell transplantation into the rat brain

For stereotactic transplantation, animals were randomly divided into several groups: stereotactic injection of 0.3 × 106 ASC52telo-Qt cells in 15 µL of serum-free medium (n = 7); stereotactic injection of 0.3 × 106 ASC52telo-RFP cells in 15 µL of serum-free medium (n = 7); stereotactic injection of 15 µL of SFM (serum free medium, n = 3). For the transplantation, cells were cultured in a standard growth medium supplemented with 2 mM FAS for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were washed thoroughly with PBS, detached with 0.25% trypsin solution, centrifuged, and counted. Finally, the aliquots containing 0.3 × 106 cells in 15 µl of serum-free DMEM (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) growth medium were prepared. For stereotactic transplantation an RWD 71,000 stereotaxic system (RWD Life Science, Sugar Land, TX, USA) was used. After the skin dissection and removal of the subcutaneous tissues, a hole was drilled in the skull, and 15 µL of ASC52telo-Qt/ASC52telo-RFP cell suspension in DMEM basal medium or 15 µL of serum free medium was slowly injected using a 500 µL Hamilton microsyringe and Stoelting tabletop QSI 53,314 stereotaxic syringe pump system (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL, USA), at an injection rate of 1 µL/min. The injection was performed into the striatum of the right hemisphere using a stereotactic frame to precisely target + 0.6 mm anterior, + 3.5 mm lateral to Bregma, −4.5 mm ventral-dorsal. After the transplantation, the needle was left for 5 min to allow the cell suspension to distribute in the brain tissue and minimize the backflow of the transplanted cells. The needle was then slowly removed, and the incision was sutured with simple interrupted stitches.

Phantoms for the in vitro MRI studies

After 24 h of incubation with FAS (0.5 mM, 1 mM, and 2 mM) ASC52telo-Qt cells were prepared in 1.5 mL tubes, with three independent samples for each iron concentration. Cells were suspended in a 2% agarose solution in PBS. Intracellular iron content in each sample was quantified using ICP-MS. For MRI measurements the prepared tubes were arranged side by side within a 72 mm inner-diameter birdcage coil (Bruker, model T10720) at room temperature. T2 relaxation times were measured using a multi-slice multi-echo (MSME) T2−mapping pulse sequence with the following scan parameters: field of view: 6 × 6 cm², matrix:100 × 100, number of slices: 1, slice thickness: 5 mm, number of echo times (TE): 64, first TE: 17 ms, repetition time (TR): 2000 ms, bandwidth: 100 kHz, scan time: 3 min 20 s. Three series of measurements were performed for each concentration. ΔR2 values were calculated relative to a 2% agarose control, and the transverse relaxivity r2 = 4.009 ± 0.141 mM−1s−1 was determined by linear regression. Graph was plotted in Python v.3.10 using custom script.

Magnetic resonance imaging

MRI studies were performed on a 7 T small-animal MRI system (Bruker BioSpec 70/30 USR, Bruker, Germany) equipped with a BGA20S2 gradient system (maximum gradient strength: 100 mT/m) powered by a Xantrex XPR 150 − 40 Mod power supply. The system also included an AQS Chassis Multi-Rx processor, TOSI BS30 shim coils, and a BSPS12802 power module. Scanner operation and image acquisition were controlled using TopSpin 2.0 and ParaVision 5.1 software.

For radiofrequency (RF) transmission, a 72 mm inner-diameter birdcage coil (Bruker, model T10720) was used, and signal reception was performed using a dedicated receive-only RF coil (Bruker, model T11205) optimized for rat brain imaging. Anesthesia was induced with a mixture of isoflurane and oxygen. Oxygen was supplied by a Jay-10 oxygen concentrator (Longfian Scitech Co., Ltd., Baoding, China) at a constant flow rate of 1 L/min. Isoflurane was delivered at 4.5% for induction and maintained at 1.5% during scanning, using a precision vaporizer (Ugo Basile S.R.L., Gemonio, Italy). Animals were placed on a heated MRI cradle (maintained at 37 ± 0.5°C) to ensure normothermic body temperature, and their respiratory rate was continuously monitored using a pneumatic sensor positioned under the abdomen. In vivo MR imaging of rat brains was performed in the axial plane on days 3 and 10 after injection of either ASC52telo-Qt or ASC52telo-RFP cells. Images were acquired using a single-echo 2D gradient echo (GE) sequence (Bruker ‘MGE’ protocol) with the following scan parameters: field of view (FOV) = 2.58 × 2.58 cm2, matrix = 258 × 258, in-plane spatial resolution = 0.1 mm2, slice thickness = 0.5 mm, number of slices = 30, repetition time (TR) = 1000 ms, echo time (TE) = 9.5 ms, flip angle = 30°, number of averages = 2, acquisition bandwidth = 25 kHz, total scan time = 8 min 36 s. Sagittal brain projections were reconstructed using the Waxholm Space atlas of the Sprague Dawley rat brain [76].

Histology

Animals were euthanized as described above. Transcardiac perfusion was performed using PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS. Isolated brains were postfixed for 24 h in 4% paraformaldehyde solution in PBS. Transverse brain slices were obtained using a 7000smz-2 vibratome (Campden Instruments, Loughborough, UK), with a slice thickness of 40–60 μm.

Laser scanning confocal microscopy

The fluorescence of cells/histological sections were imaged using a laser scanning confocal microscope LSM-710-NLO (Carl Zeiss Microscopy, Jena, Germany), Plan-Apochromat 20x/0.8, 63x/1.4 oil and 40x/0.95 water immersion objectives, and 488, 561 and 633 nm lasers. The DAPI fluorescence was exited upon 730 nm (two-photon excitation) using femtosecond laser (Chameleon Ultra II, Coherent, Santa Clara, USA) and was detected between 400 and 500 nm. Images are acquired and analyzed using ZEN v.1.1.2.0 software.

Results

Generation and characterization of ASC52telo-Qt cells: from gene expression to non-invasive magnetic tracking

In the present study, we have engineered hTERT-immortalized adipose tissue-derived human MSCs co-expressing GFP together with sequences encoding the iron transport and sequestration system. We have also genetically engineered two control cell lines that express fluorescent protein (RFP) and GFP exclusively.

The lentiviral vectors were utilized to achieve stable expression of the Qt encapsulin shell and cargo proteins separated by an P2A sequence (Fig. 1a) and mZip14 metal transporter in MSCs. The expression of encapsulin encoding genes was confirmed by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR, Fig. 1b). QtEncFLAG expression was additionally verified by Western blot analysis against FLAG-tag sequence on the encapsulin shell protomer proteins. Western blot analysis (Fig. 1c) revealed the presence of an immunoreactive band with a molecular weight of approximately 35 kDa, corresponding to QtEncFLAG monomers. The expression of RFP and GFP in ASC52telo-RFP and ASC52telo-Qt cells, respectively, was determined via flow cytometry and laser scanning confocal microscopy (Fig. 1d, Fig. S3). Undirect immunofluorescence analysis of ASC52telo-Qt cells was also performed, employing primary monoclonal anti-FLAG tag antibodies and secondary AlexaFluor647-labeled antibodies. It can be observed that Qt encapsulins in ASC52telo-Qt cell are distributed throughout the cell cytoplasm as individual fluorescent spots. It is noteworthy that the fluorescent signal also has a propensity to accumulate on cell membrane (Fig. 1e, Fig. S4). However, we do not associate this signal distribution with the distribution of nanocompartments within the cell, since this contradicts the transmission electron microscopy data, we obtained earlier [77].

The quantification of double positive (GFP and AlexaFluor647) cells, was performed using flow cytometry. The Q2 region displays the double-positive ASC52telo-Qt cells that express GFP, along the x-axis, and Alexa Fluor 647 along the y-axis. Thus, the number of double-positive cells was 95% (Fig. 1f). Subsequently, the resazurin-based viability assay was employed to evaluate the toxicity of FAS for ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells (Fig. 1g). The study demonstrated that the supplementation of the growth medium with 0.13–2 mM FAS did not lead to pronounced cytotoxicity after 24 h of culturing. At the same time the supplementation of the culture medium with 4 mM FAS significantly decreased the viability of both ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cell lines.

The total iron content in ASC52telo-RFP and ASC52telo-Qt cells after 24 h of incubation in the growth medium supplemented with 0–2 mM FAS was further determined by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). As demonstrated in Fig. 1h, iron accumulation in ASC52telo-Qt cells occurs in a dose-dependent manner. The mean iron concentration in the 100 µL of acid extraction from 1 × 106 ASC52telo-Qt cells cultured in the growth medium supplemented with 2 mM FAS was 106.5 ± 6 µg/mL, while the same value for 1 × 106 ASC52telo-RFP cells cultured in the identical conditions was found to be only 2.3 ± 0.2 µg/mL. A qualitative assessment of iron accumulation in ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells was also carried out with the Prussian blue reaction. Ferric ferrocyanide deposits were found to be localized in ASC52telo-Qt cells both in the entire cell cytoplasm as well as in the form of individual cytoplasmic inclusions (Fig. S5). In contrast, no analogous staining was observed in ASC52telo-RFP cells (Fig. S6).

As previously demonstrated, iron-containing nanoparticles formed inside the encapsulin shells are detectable by the magnetic particle quantification (MPQ) method [78]. In the present study, the magnetic signal was recorded in a sample of 3 × 105 ASC52telo-Qt cells preincubated with 2 mM FAS. Concurrently, no pronounced magnetic signal was observed in the control sample (3 × 105 ASC52telo-Qt cells with no FAS added) (Fig. 1i).

To evaluate the impact of dual-label expression on MSCs differentiation process, osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation with subsequent staining of the obtained osteoblasts, adipocytes, and chondroblasts was performed. No discrepancy was observed in the rates of differentiation of ASC52telo-Qt cells and control cells. Notably, the most rapid differentiation occurred in the adipogenic direction, a characteristic that is typically noticed in MSCs derived from adipose tissue (Fig. S9a and b). We also verified that optical label expression remains constant during multilineage differentiation of ASC52telo-Qt cells (Fig S9c). Additionally, the secretion levels of IL-6 were assessed in ASC52telo-Qt and control cells (Fig.S9d) using ELISA. Subsequent analysis revealed statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001) between IL-6 concentrations in cell culture media obtained from ASC52telo-Qt cells and control cells.

Bivalent iron uptake in ASC52telo-Qt cells revealed via N-oxide-based fluorescent probe

In order to assess divalent iron transport into ASC52telo-Qt cells, we employed HMRhoNox-M (LysoRhoNox) – a Fe2+-selective fluorescent probe based on hydroxymethylrhodamine spirocyclization [79, 80]. In the absence of Fe2+ LysoRhoNox exists in the non-fluorescent form exhibiting only a negligible fluorescent signal. The addition of Fe2+ leads to a substantial enhancement of the fluorescent signal at 575 nm in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2a). The fluorescence response of HMRhoNox-M is highly selective for Fe2+ over other transition metal ions, including Fe3+. The intracellular distribution of the probe was validated by utilizing LysoTracker Deep Red fluorescent dye as a marker of cell lysosomes.

The analysis of the micrographs demonstrated that Fe2+ was rapidly internalized into the cells and accumulated in the lysosomes within 30 min of incubation of ASC52telo-Qt cells with FAS (Fig. 2b, Fig. S10a). Fluorescence colocalization analysis revealed, that the distribution pattern of the HMRhoNox-M fluorescent signal is consistent with the distribution pattern obtained using LysoTracker Deep Red. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (R) between HMRhoNox-M and LysoTracker Deep Red signals (Fig. 2b, scatterplot) was found to be 0.68 (Table S2a). The ASC52telo-GFP cells that were subjected to identical experimental conditions were used as a control (Fig. 2c, Fig. S10b). In this case, the Pearson correlation coefficient between lysosomes and HMRhoNox-M in colocalization studies slightly decreased to 0.59 (Fig. 2b, scatterplot and Table S2b).

A comparative analysis of the fluorescence intensity of the HMRhoNox-M sensor in non-iron-supplemented ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-GFP cells executed through the use of confocal microscopy and flow cytometry revealed that the medium intensity of the HMRhoNox-M fluorescent signal in ASC52telo-GFP cells (Fig. 3a) is slightly lower comparing to the intensity of the HMRhoNox-M signal in ASC52telo-Qt cells, even in the absence of additional iron added in the form of FAS (Fig. 3b). This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that iron is normally present in the composition of the DMEM F12 growth medium, as well as in the FBS added to the medium. It is known that mZip14 overexpression enhances the uptake of both labile and transferrin-bounded iron in different types of cells [81–84]. Therefore, it can be asserted that the observed outcomes are attributable to the expression of mZip14 in ASC52telo-Qt cells.

As previously stated, the IMEF cargo protein facilitates the oxidation of divalent iron to trivalent iron. It was hypothesized that as Fe2+ enters the nanocompartments and is converted to Fe3+ by the IMEF cargo protein, the amount of Fe2+ in ASC52telo-Qt cells will decrease over 24 h during MNPs formation. Consequently, the intensity of the HMRhoNox-M fluorescent signal in ASC52telo-Qt cells will also decrease. The results demonstrated that following a 24-h incubation of ASC52telo-GFP cells with FAS, the medium intensity of the HMRhoNox-M fluorescent signal, as measured by flow cytometry, was from 104 to 105 relative fluorescence units (RFU, Fig. 3c), while for ASC52telo-Qt cells, this value was from 103 to 104 RFU. The results obtained are in good agreement with the confocal microscopy data (Fig. 3d).

Consequently, the employment of Fe2+ sensitive probe facilitated the expansion of our understanding of the mechanisms of iron transport and sequestration in ASC52telo-Qt cells. A notable finding is the substantial discrepancy in iron accumulation observed between ASC52telo-Qt cells and control cells following 24 h of culturing with FAS reported by ICP-MS, underscores the importance of further investigation into the dynamics of iron accumulation in these cell lines. The utilization of a secondary, autonomous methodology reliant upon the fluorescent detection of divalent iron serves to augment these findings, thereby substantiating the assertion that the iron accumulated within ASC52telo-Qt cells is indeed present in the form of Fe3+.

The final step prior to in vivo experiments was MRI of ASC52telo-Qt cells after 24 h of incubation with FAS (0.5 mM, 1 mM, and 2 mM) in agarose phantoms. The utilization of phantoms in this context enables the acquisition of preliminary data regarding MR signal intensity, the determination of relaxation time, and the assessment of relaxivity for the MR contrast agent employed. This approach serves to eliminate ambiguity in the interpretation of MRI data when transitioning to experiments on small laboratory animals. Fig. S11a shows a representative MR image of the samples alongside the graph of ΔR2 versus iron concentration used to determine the transverse relaxivity r2. The results demonstrate a clear concentration-dependent decrease in transverse relaxation T2, confirming that the MNPs formed in the shells of Qt encapsulins can serve as MRI-detectable labels (Fig S11b).

To assess the suitability of the obtained cells for in vivo studies, we next performed cell transplantation into the rat brain, with subsequent visualization by MRI, confocal and conventional microscopy of brain sections.

MRI and histological visualization of ASC52telo-Qt cells implanted into the rat brain

In this study the distribution of ASC52telo-Qt and ASC52telo-RFP cells was investigated at two time points: 3 days and 10 days after the implantation into the rat brain striatum (Fig. S12). Representative histological and MR images obtained 3 days after the injection of ASC52telo-Qt cells into the right striatum of rat brain are showed in Fig. 4. Since brain tissue is highly vascularized, the needle may damage small capillaries during the transplantation process. In this scenario, microhemorrhages may occur. To differentiate microhemorrhages from transplanted cells and to visualize ferric iron deposits in ASC52telo-Qt cells, light microscopy of Perls-stained brain sections was utilized. Perls staining allows the staining of “non-heme” iron and is insensitive to iron in hemoglobin. Similar to the results obtained after Perls staining of ASC52telo-Qt cells in vitro, iron deposits are visualized as characteristic blue inclusions on histological sections. It can be seen that the cells are distributed in the corpus callosum and corpus striatum (Fig. 4a). Confocal microscopy of the obtained histological sections revealed a bright GFP fluorescent signal localized to the same anatomical regions of the brain (Fig. 4b). As mentioned above, the MNPs formed in the shells of nanocompartments allow the detection of the obtained cells by MRI. In MR images (Fig. 4c) the cells are visualized as hypointense areas that correspond to the regions with fluorescent signal in the cells as well as to the sites of iron accumulation detected by Perls staining.

Discussion

As a rule, iron is biomineralized in a soluble and bioavailable form within ferritins. Alternative iron storage strategies are used by certain organisms, such as Q. thermotolerans, which do not have ferritin encoding genes. This fact might explain the relatively large size of Qt encapsulins compared to other bacteria species. As noted above, due to the IMEF cargo protein, up to 30,000 iron atoms can be stored inside the encapsulin nanoshell, which is an order of magnitude larger than in ferritins. Due to this, there might be a possibility for the MRI and MPQ detection of the mammalian cells heterologously expressing encapsulin genes.

In the present study we choose an immortalized MSCs cell line to overcome the limitation associated with the number of passages inherent in primary cultures of MSCs. The advantage of the immortalized line can be used to obtain large numbers of cells for magnetic cell sorting. This, in turn, will make it possible to isolate a subpopulation of cells with the highest amount of MNPs per cell. This will improve the MR signal in cells and expand the range of tasks for which the obtained cell line can be applied. For example, to track single cells after systemic administration. In an effort to enhance the safety of bacterial proteins utilized in mammalian cells, we also opted to leverage the immune evasion properties of MSCs.

The development of multimodal imaging has accelerated significantly in recent years. Multimodal imaging combines the advantages of different imaging techniques to overcome the limitations of individual methods and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the sample. Here, we merge the advantages of two complementary labels and develop a cell line that can be studied using both molecular (fluorescent microscopy) and structural (MRI) imaging approaches. The undoubted advantage of MR imaging is its non-invasiveness; however, MRI does not provide information on cell viability. The presence of fluorescent protein in cells allows, in its turn, to indirectly assess their viability based on morphology. Not only a fluorescent protein, but also a bioluminescent reporter such as luciferase can be used as a complimentary label.

Since both labels used in the study have a genetic nature, they will exhibit a prolonged persistence within cells when compared with exogenous labels. As MSCs are not professional phagocytic cells, the use of exogenous MNPs for MRI tracking is often inefficient. This is particularly true when using such labels to study MSCs distribution in the brain, where nanoparticles can be ejected by MSCs and taken up by microglia. The use of a genetically encoded iron-storing nanocompartment has overcome this obstacle.

It should also be noted that the Qt nanocompartment is a relatively new nanomaterial. There are only a few papers addressing the application of these structures in the context of in vivo cell tracking. The immunogenicity and safety of these structures when used in small animal studies have been primary concerns since the inception of this research. Given the bacterial nature of the structure used, we were naturally concerned about the potential for an immune response. Since the Qt nanocompartment was first described in 2019, no previous studies on immunogenicity have been identified in the extant literature. Consequently, the decision was made to initiate such a study. The animal study was conducted utilizing a 4T1 carcinoma cell line with stable expression of the Qt encapsulins, and subcutaneous tumors were modeled using 4T1-Qt cells in an isogenic model in immunocompetent mice. Tumor rejection was not observed in any of the animals included in the study. Furthermore, no statistically significant differences in tumor growth dynamics were observed between tumors that developed after transplantation of 4T1-Qt cells and control 4T1 cells. A histological examination of tumor sections was conducted, revealing no indications of immunological rejection [64]. In addition, encouraging results were obtained in a recent study [85]. Nanocompartments of the Thermotoga maritima bacterium, expressed in E. coli BL21 cells, were isolated from the E. coli using size-exclusion and anion-exchange chromatography. The isolated nanocompartments were administered intravenously to immunocompetent mice and demonstrated an excellent safety profile.

It is important to acknowledge that the nanocompartment-based MR reporter label exhibits notable advantages over the conventional MR reporters frequently employed for cell tracking. The introduction of the target sequence into MSCs did not result in a decrease in cell proliferation rate or affect the dynamics of adipogenic, chondrogenic, or osteogenic differentiation. In addition, in contrast to tyrosinase-based contrast agents referenced in the text, the utilization of Qt encapsulin-based labels does not result in the generation of reactive oxygen species. Instead, it has been observed to enhance cell survival under conditions of elevated ferrous iron concentrations. Furthermore, the storage capacity of Qt nanocompartments is significantly higher than that of other known iron-storing proteins. The expression of Qt encapsulins in MSCs is well tolerated by cells; the protein itself is not toxic, and its presence in MSCs is not associated with the occurrence of immunological rejection or any other adverse effects. Given that both labels utilized in the present study possess a genetic basis for synthesis, their functionality is confined to living cells. This characteristic enables the assessment of both the localization and viability of MSCs.

Conclusions

In this study, we established an immortalized human MSCs cell line with stable expression of two genetically encoded labels – optical and magnetic. The cells were characterized by both intracellular iron accumulation assessment methods (ICP-MS, Prussian blue reaction) and optical methods (flow cytometry and confocal microscopy). Quantitative assessment of iron accumulation in cells showed that after FAS addition, iron accumulation in cells has a dose-dependent character. The amount of intracellular iron in ASC52telo-Qt cells is significantly higher compared to control cells (106.5 ± 6 µg/mL for 1 × 106 ASC52telo-Qt cells vs. 2.3 ± 0.2 µg/mL for 1 × 106 ASC52telo-RFP cells). This feature enables the visualization of ASC52telo-Qt cells in the Prussian blue reaction both in culture and on histological sections. It should be noted that additional transgenic sequences in the cell genome did not affect the proliferation rate as well as differentiation and cytokine secretion. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirm the presence of the MRI and MPQ signal changes produced by MNPs inside the encapsulins. It was found that the both magnetic signals remained discernible 10 days after transplantation of ASC52telo-Qt cells into the striatum of the rat brain. No abnormal gross pathology, such as inflammation or necrosis, was detected in the brain of any treated animals. The generation of an immortalized cell line of human mesenchymal stromal cells with the iron-storing nanocompartment and fluorescent proteins holds great promise for the further engineering of new agents for the diagnosis and treatment of currently indistinguishable diseases.
