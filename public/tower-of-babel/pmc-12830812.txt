Breath-based stratification of asthma severity using the MISTRAL platform with integrated H2S sensor and clinical validation
Authors: R Germinario, E Andriani, P Tondo, P Soccio, A la Grasta, I Cafagna, D Lacedonia, F Dell’Olio, G Scioscia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExhaled breath analysis offers a painless window into airway inflammation, yet its clinical adoption is limited by a shortage of robust biomarkers. Hydrogen sulfide () has been detected in biological fluids, but its diagnostic value across asthma severities is not well established. In this study, we integrated an electrochemical sensor with a detection limit of 1 ppb into the MISTRAL platform. We evaluated exhaled in a prospective cohort of 28 adult asthmatic patients. Mean exhaled concentrations were significantly higher in individuals with mild-to-moderate asthma () than in those with severe asthma (; one‑tailed t‑test , ). In early-stage disease, levels negatively correlated with maximal expiratory flow MEF, with a similar trend for MEF, whereas in severe asthma, positive but not fully statistically significant correlations emerged with FEV/FVC ratio, and vital capacity VC. These findings reveal a biphasic pattern: elevated may signify a compensatory antioxidant response that collapses as damage in the medium-to-peripheral airways progresses. The plug-and-play MISTRAL platform thus enables real-time, point-of-care assessment of exhaled as a promising, and complementary biomarker to FeNO and blood eosinophil count, offering a rapid, non-invasive indicator of disease control, treatment responsiveness, and exacerbation risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33084-6.Keywords: Breath analysis, Electrochemical sensor, Airway inflammation, Asthma, PoCT.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

The era of precision medicine has driven the development of non-invasive diagnostic tools capable of identifying disease-specific biomarker profiles in different biological fluids, such as saliva1, blood2, breath3 and urine4. In this context, breathomics has emerged as a promising technology for improving diagnostic methodologies and detecting alterations in patients’ health at an early stage5. Accordingly, breath analysis has increasingly been adopted in Point-of-Care (PoC) diagnostic systems, offering rapid and user-friendly solutions for both physicians and healthcare professionals. Combining breath analysis with PoC technology enables timely medical decision-making and enhances patient management, including follow-up care and personalized treatment6–9. Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterized by eosinophilic infiltration and airway remodeling, placing a substantial burden on health systems10–14. According to the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) guidelines, low-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are generally sufficient for patients with well-controlled asthma. In contrast, patients with more severe disease—particularly those at GINA Steps 4 and 5—often require medium- to high-dose ICS in combination with a long-acting -agonist (LABA). Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMA), short-acting -agonist (SABA) or biologic therapies are recommended in cases of persistent symptoms15–17.

Increasing attention has been directed toward mediators involved in airway inflammation. Among these, hydrogen sulfide (HS) has been found to play a crucial role in inflammatory airway diseases. Originally known for its toxicological properties18, HS is recognized as an endogenous signaling molecule produced by the activity of specific enzymes19,20. This molecule regulates airway tone and exhibits bronchodilatory, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties21–24. In an experimental rat model of ovalbumin (OVA)-induced asthma, the deficiency of the enzyme cystathionine -lyase (CSE) leads to increased airway hyperresponsiveness, suggesting that potential imbalances in HS may contribute to the pathophysiology of asthma25. This complements the findings provided by current asthma biomarkers, such as Nitric Oxide (NO) and Carbon Monoxide (CO)26,27. The detection of HS in the breath requires suitable PoC systems that must prevent condensation and ensure the sensitivity necessary to identify biomarkers at concentrations in the parts-per-billion (ppb)28. The present work focuses on the design of an upgraded version of the MISTRAL sampler, a patented technology (European Patent No. 3873347) developed by Predict S.p.A. and the University of Bari Aldo Moro, by integrating a commercial ppb-level amperometric electrochemical gas sensor29. The upgraded device aims to detect HS levels in the exhaled breath of two distinct patient cohorts — those with mild-to-moderate and severe asthma, respectively — to evaluate its potential anti-inflammatory role and usefulness as a complementary biomarker.

Results

HS concentration levels in patients

Patients with severe asthma exhibited the lowest endogenous levels of exhaled HS (149.3 ± 70.7 ppb), compared to those with mild-to-moderate asthma (192.4 ± 57.1 ppb). The difference in mean HS concentrations between the two groups was statistically significant (, ), suggesting that patients with mild-to-moderate asthma have higher mean HS levels than those with severe asthma. Discussion

Several studies have focused on the detection of HS in plasma and sputum, but a clear correlation with asthma severity or disease progression has yet to be firmly established22,30,31. Recent investigations have been focused on the detection of HS in exhaled breath, warranting further studies in clinical trials to assess its potential as a more specific and non-invasive biomarker of airway inflammation. Studies have shown that HS concentrations in human breath are typically below 1000 ppb32. Zhang et al. demonstrated that exhaled HS levels varied according to asthma phenotype33. In this paper, lower levels of exhaled HS were found in patients with severe asthma compared to those with mild-to-moderate asthma (Fig. 1). This significant difference in the mean values may indicate that, in milder stages of the disease, elevated endogenous HS levels may reflect a compensatory mechanism aimed at counteracting airway inflammation or oxidative stress. In contrast, in more advanced stages, this mechanism may be impaired, leading to reduced bioavailability of HS and subsequent exacerbation of symptoms. Table 2 provides a comparative analysis of clinical parameters between the two groups, including details on the therapeutic dosages and the corresponding treatment frequencies. It also specifies, for each parameter, the number of patients with available measurements, as some of them were experiencing an exacerbation episode during the tests or were attending their first access to the hospital. The analysis revealed that patients with severe asthma showed lower mean values of several key spirometric indices - FEV, FVC, VC, PEF, MEF and MEF - compared to patients with mild-to-moderate asthma, confirming a more pronounced impairment of respiratory performance in patients with severe asthma. To enhance the robustness of these findings, both Pearson’s and Spearman’s coefficients were reported (Table 1), with Spearman’s rank used as the primary correlation index. Spearman’s rank correlation showed a statistically significant negative association between exhaled HS and MEF in the earlier stages of asthma, with a similar but not fully statistically significant negative trend for MEF (Fig. 2). These indices are each associated with airflow measured in distinct airway regions. Overall, this result suggests that, although elevated exhaled HS levels may act as a potentially protective response to reduced lung functions in mild-to-moderate asthma, this mechanism may ultimately be not adequate to fully prevent airway dysfunction, especially within the medium-to-peripheral airways captured by MEF. This effect may be further supported by the observed negative, although not fully statistically significant, trend between exhaled HS and MEF, suggesting that the modulatory action may be insufficient to also preserve small-airway integrity. In line with these findings, it was observed that rats with OVA-induced asthma exhibited reduced PEF values. Conversely, the administration of exogenous HS leads to improved lung function and higher PEF values25. Tian et al. found a positive correlation between pulmonary function decline, reduced expression of CSE, and decreased serum HS levels in children with bronchial asthma30. Zhang et al. observed a positive correlation between ACT scores and exhaled HS in patients with eosinophilic inflammation33. In this study, Spearman’s coefficients showed positive but not fully significant trends between exhaled HS levels and lung function parameters—FEV/FVC, VC—in patients with severe asthma (Fig. 3). These results may suggest that lower exhaled HS levels are associated with reduced lung volume, and increased airway obstruction. Although these associations did not reach full statistical significance, the direction of the observed trends is biologically plausible and appears in line with the clinical profile of patients with severe asthma, who are treated with high doses of ICS and, in most cases, biologics therapies that inhibit eosinophil production and downregulate the expression of Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS). This therapeutic approach is reflected in their lower mean blood eosinophil counts and FeNO levels compared to patients with mild-to-moderate asthma (Table 2). Furthermore, the intensive treatment regimen ensures good disease control, resulting in similar ACT scores between patients with severe asthma and those with mild-to-moderate asthma (Table 2). Taken together with these clinical considerations, and although further studies are needed, the findings appear to be compatible with the complex and heterogeneous pathophysiology of asthma, suggesting a possible link between reduced exhaled HS levels, greater airway limitation and diminished pulmonary function. However, the results highlight the complex role of HS in asthma, which appears to be dependent on the stage of disease progression. In earlier disease, higher levels of exhaled HS may reflect an incomplete compensatory response to emerging airflow limitation, especially within the medium-to-peripheral airways. In cases of more advanced disease, although the observed trends were not statistically significant, their direction suggests that lower HS levels may point towards a possible association with reduced lung volumes and increased airway obstruction, in line with their need for more intensive treatment regimens. These trends show an overall consistency with asthma severity parameters that is at most compatible with a possible role of HS beyond that of a passive bystander and tentatively supports further exploration of exhaled HS as a promising, complementary biomarker in asthma. Traditional biomarkers such as FeNO and blood eosinophil count have certain limitations: FeNO levels are influenced by ICS use, smoking status, and atopic conditions, while blood eosinophil counts can be affected by systemic factors34. In contrast, exhaled HS may provide clinicians with complementary, rapid and minimally invasive information to support the monitoring of asthmatic patients at first access or during follow-up. In line with the stage-dependent findings, HS might also help in the evaluation of treatment response by informing therapies targeting airflow limitation in the medium-to-peripheral airways in earlier disease and, potentially, reduced lung volumes and increased airway obstruction in more advanced stages, offering added value over existing biomarkers. Peripheral neutrophil counts were not available in the dataset, preventing the assessment of their potential relationship with exhaled HS. This limitation should be acknowledged, as neutrophilic inflammation has been previously linked to HS in asthma and COPD22,35. In addition, baseline comparisons (Supplementary Information, Table S1) confirmed that age and FEV in liters were significantly different between groups, highlighting both demographic and functional disparities. Other parameters, such as blood eosinophils percentage, PEF and VC in liters, showed only borderline differences. These trends are clinically plausible but should be interpreted with caution, as they may be influenced by small effective sample sizes across parameters. To further address potential confounding factors, we examined whether the significant group differences in age and FEV might account for the variation observed in exhaled HS. Spearman’s correlation analyses revealed no significant associations between HS levels and either age (n = 28) or FEV (n = 25). These findings suggest that exhaled HS is not merely a reflection of demographic or spirometric differences but may instead capture a distinct biological signal related to asthma pathophysiology. Although the limited sample size did not allow performing a full multivariate regression analysis with adequate statistical power, the absence of significant univariate correlations may reduce the likelihood that these variables confound the observed association. This strengthens the interpretation of exhaled HS as a potential independent marker of asthma severity. Furthermore, the non-invasive collection of exhaled breath -achieved by a plug-and-play system based on an electrochemical cell- facilitates its seamless integration into the MISTRAL platform, making it suitable both for bedside use and large-scale screening applications. Requiring only minimal preparation (standard fasting), the novel sampling procedure implemented in the MISTRAL device significantly reduces waiting times for both patients and healthcare providers. These findings suggest that exhaled HS measurements may have practical implications for real-world asthma management. As a non-invasive biomarker, HS may help in stratifying asthma severity, particularly in patients where traditional metrics, such as spirometry or symptom scores, provide limited resolution. In clinical practice, lower HS levels could potentially reflect more reduced lung volumes and marked airway obstruction in more advanced stages, suggesting a hypothesis that they may help step-up therapy decisions, such as the initiation or intensification of inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting bronchodilators, or biologic treatments; however, this requires confirmation in prospective clinical studies. Conversely, rising HS levels during follow-up may suggest a therapeutic approach targeting airflow limitation in the medium-to-peripheral airways, but their role in medical decision-making remains to be established in future trials. Importantly, its rapid, point-of-care acquisition using the MISTRAL platform may enable timely treatment adjustments without reliance on blood tests or laboratory-based biomarkers. By integrating HS into routine clinical workflows, clinicians may improve early identification of patients at risk of exacerbations and enhance personalized treatment planning.

Nevertheless, the main limitations of exhaled breath sampling lie in the low concentrations of HS and the high relative humidity (95–100%) of the samples, which exceeds the H2S-B4’s operational range (15–90% RH). These factors, combined with the volatility of HS and its tendency to form polysulfides, pose significant analytical challenges for its reliable detection. Another limitation of the H2S-B4 cell lies in its cross-sensitivities to interfering gases such as nitrogen dioxide (NO), chlorine (Cl), and sulfur dioxide (SO), with interference levels of less than −10%, −12%, and 20% at 5 ppm exposure36. This constitutes a significant challenge in breath analysis, where trace-level HS must be distinguished from a background of chemically reactive species, reducing measurement precision. Another challenge is the small dataset size and lack of longitudinal follow-up, which may limit result robustness. Future studies should involve larger populations and multicenter validation to enhance generalizability of the findings.

Methods

Instrumental setup

The volume of human exhaled breath typically includes of anatomical dead-space air originating from the upper airways, and of alveolar air derived from the lungs, characterized by gas exchanges with blood37. The innovative sampler MISTRAL enables the collection of the alveolar respiratory portion by employing a circuit of actuators and breath sampling lines. Additionally, the sampling system includes a disposable buffer designed to contain the alveolar fraction typically targeted. Patients are trained to take a deep inspiration and then exhale into the device until the buffer is completely filled. The HS reading is performed by the action of a suction pump, which operates to empty the buffer at a controlled flow rate -regulated by a flow meter- and transfer the patient’s sample to the electrochemical cell. The system architecture comprises a microcontroller, a microcomputer and a main board. These units coordinate control tasks, data acquisition and inter-module communication. All components are managed by firmware accessible through an electrical interface, which also handles the activation of a heating foil, enabling the sampler to reach up to 40 °C, monitored by a temperature sensor. Additionally, a software layer supervises system operation and data management, enabling comprehensive control and monitoring capabilities.

HS sensing module

The electrochemical cell integrated into the MISTRAL platform is the four-electrode H2S-B4 sensor manufactured by Alphasense Ltd. (Great Notley, UK). The redox reaction occurs at the Working Electrode (WE) and is balanced by the Counter Electrode (CE), while the WE potential is controlled by the Reference Electrode (RE) and its zero current is corrected by the Auxiliary Electrode (AE). The H2S-B4 has a diameter of approximately 32 millimeters and is characterized by an operational range up to 100 parts-per-million (ppm), low Limit-of-Detection (LoD) of 1 ppb and a response time less than 60 seconds. The H2S-B4 exhibits a linear response, with gas concentration scaling proportionally to the electrical output and an error below ±4 ppb at full scale. It maintains reliable performance during transient exposures up to 200 ppm. The sensor generates a current between 1450 and 2600 nA/ppm and exhibits a zero drift below ppb per year in laboratory air. The cell operates reliably at temperatures ranging from −30 to 50 °C, making it suitable for breath analysis applications36,38. The manufacturer provides an algorithm to correct the zero background current for temperature effects. It incorporates a temperature-dependent compensation factor, , scaled by the ratio of the sensor-specific baseline signals from WE and AE (, ). Prior to correction, the raw signals from WE and AE (, ) are adjusted by subtracting their respective electronic offsets (, ). The resulting values are then thermally compensated using a value of 0.9, as specified by the manufacturer for an operating temperature of 40 °C. This approach mitigates the risk of overcompensation, especially when is small or has the opposite sign to . Clinical study design and participants

This was a prospective, single-centre, observational, non-interventional, no-profit clinical study. 28 adult patients with a physician-confirmed diagnosis of asthma in a stable-phase (20 females and 8 males) were enrolled at the Outpatient Unit for Asthma and Severe Asthma, Institute of Respiratory Diseases, University-Hospital Polyclinic of Foggia, from February to May 2025. No randomization, allocation, experimental intervention, or blinding was performed. All treatments were standard-of-care and were not assigned or modified by the investigators. The observational clinical study followed the guidelines on human experimentation of Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee (Ethics Approval Document No. 895; date of approval: 18 November 2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients were in a fasting state for at least 8 hours overnight before sample collection. Exclusion criteria included patients with a smoking history exceeding 10 pack-years (P/Y), due to their increased risk of structural lung damage and pathophysiological alterations. Additional exclusion criteria comprised patients with clinically active comorbidities (e.g. rheumatologic disorders, autoimmune diseases, malignant neoplasms, ongoing chemoradiotherapy or immunotherapy, pulmonary fibrosis) to avoid confounding effects on the measured outcomes. The enrolled patients were grouped according to the Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2024 guidelines15. According to these criteria, patients in this study were categorized into two distinct groups: 12 mild-to-moderate asthmatic patients (GINA steps 1–3; 25–73 years old) and 16 severe asthmatic patients (GINA steps 4–5; 37–77 years old). The mild-to-moderate asthma group received low- to medium-dose ICS-LABA treatments, with variations in dosing frequency and occasional use of as-needed inhalations. The severe asthma group was treated with high-dose ICS-LABA regimens, often combined with biologics, Leukotriene Receptor Antagonists (LTRAs), Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonists (LAMAs), or bronchodilators. Conventional tests

Clinical data were collected as part of the medical history of each asthmatic patient at the time of enrollment. All patients were administered the Asthma Control Test (ACT) to quantify the level of asthma control42. Spirometry test was conducted in both groups using a spirometer (JAEGER, MasterScreen, CareFusion, Germany) to assess pulmonary function. The spirometric parameters included Forced Expiratory Volume in the 1st second (), Forced Vital Capacity (FVC), , Vital Capacity (VC), Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) and Maximal Expiratory Flow (MEF) at 25 %, 50 % and 75 % of exhaled lung volume. , FVC and VC were recorded both in liters and as a percentage of the predicted normal values, based on the patient’s actual age, gender, weight and height. Following this, Fractional exhaled Nitric Oxide (FeNO) levels were assessed using an electrochemical analyzer (FeNO+, Medisoft S.A., Belgium), previously calibrated according to the producer’s specifications and interfaced with the medical laboratory computer43. Blood eosinophils were assessed via Complete Blood Count (CBC) and reported as both absolute values and as percentages of the total white blood cell count44.

Data processing and statistical analysis

Normality of the data was confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk and Anderson–Darling tests, supported by visual inspection of Q–Q plots in both groups (Supplementary Information, Figs. S1 and S2). For the mild-to-moderate group, the Shapiro–Wilk test yielded , () and the Anderson–Darling statistic was 0.18 (critical value 0.67 at = 0.05). For the severe group, , and 0.51 (critical value 0.68 at = 0.05). Therefore, the null hypothesis of normality was not rejected by either test. Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). The F-test was used to compare the variance between the two groups (, , indicates that there are no statistically significant differences in variance between the two groups). Subsequently, the one-tailed parametric independent samples Student’s t-test was performed to compare the levels of exhaled HS between the groups, with the significance level set at an alpha of 0.05. In accordance with this threshold, statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05. Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated in each group to assess the associations between exhaled HS levels and clinical test parameters. Spearman’s rank coefficients were used as the primary, more robust and conservative measure of association, as they are less sensitive to outliers and does not rely on strict linearity assumptions, whereas Pearson’s coefficients were also reported in Table 1 to allow comparison with the corresponding parametric estimates. Table 2 provides the number of patients for whom the correlations were calculated. Differences in each clinical parameter between the two groups were assessed using both the two-tailed Student’s t-test and the Mann–Whitney U test, with the results reported in Supplementary Information, Table S1.

Conclusions

The MISTRAL platform was upgraded by integrating an electrochemical HS sensor to investigate the role of hydrogen sulfide in modulating asthma-induced airway inflammation. Exhaled HS levels were measured in a total of 12 patients with mild-to-moderate asthma and 16 patients with severe asthma. Lower exhaled HS levels were observed in patients with severe asthma compared to those with mild-to-moderate asthma, suggesting its anti-inflammatory and protective role against lung tissue injury and exacerbation risk. Reduced exhaled HS in advanced stages may reflect the failure of an endogenous compensatory mechanism mediated by this molecule, resulting in increased airway obstruction, decreased lung volume, and heightened need for intensive pharmacological therapies. In contrast, in the earlier stages, this mechanism may still be partially active, but not sufficient to protect the medium-to-peripheral airways. The findings may indicate a potentially biphasic pattern of HS in asthma pathogenesis, with exhaled levels varying according to the stage of disease progression. Taking this into account, the detection of exhaled HS via a portable, low-cost, plug-and-play sensor may provide enhanced predictive insight into treatment response to biologics, potentially enriching the current panel of asthma biomarkers. This approach may open new perspectives in personalized medicine by enabling a multidimensional characterization of asthma and potentially facilitating more rapid outpatient follow-up.
