Ultrasound‑responsive nano‑contrast agents co‑delivering CIITA‑siRNA and rapamycin for targeted therapy against Hashimoto thyroiditis
Authors: Xiaoying Zhou, Lu Guo, Dandan Shi, Mengmeng Shang, Xiao Sun, Yading Zhao, Xiaoxuan Wang, Dong Meng, Shan Xiao, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Hashimoto thyroiditis (HT) is a common autoimmune thyroid disease for which effective treatments capable of suppressing local autoimmune injury remain limited. This study aimed to develop an ultrasound-responsive anti-inflammatory nano-contrast agent (AINA) for the targeted delivery of MHC class II transactivator (CIITA)-small interfering ribonucleic acid (CIITA-siRNA) and rapamycin to thyroid tissue.Methods:Thyroid tissue samples were obtained from patients undergoing total thyroidectomy at Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from March to April 2024. After pathological evaluation, samples from three patients with HT and three patients without HT were included. CIITA and human leukocyte antigen DR isotype (HLA-DR) expression was assessed using transcriptomic sequencing and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. AINAs were constructed with an erythrocyte-membrane shell carrying rapamycin and a core containing CIITA-siRNA-loaded, trans-activator of transcription (TAT)-modified mesoporous silica nanoparticles and liquid perfluorohexane. Their physicochemical properties, ultrasound imaging performance, cellular uptake, therapeutic effects, and preliminary safety were evaluated in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells, Jurkat cells, and NOD.H-2h4 mice.Results:AINAs had a mean diameter of 255 nm and a polydispersity index of 0.23. More than 90% of CIITA-siRNA was loaded onto the mesoporous silica nanoparticles, and the optimized preparation achieved a rapamycin encapsulation efficiency of approximately 75%. In vitro, ultrasound-assisted AINAs reduced Interleukin-23-induced reactive oxygen species accumulation and apoptosis in thyroid cells and suppressed CIITA and HLA-DR expression, antigen presentation, and Jurkat T-cell activation. In vivo, ultrasound enhanced the accumulation of AINAs in thyroid tissue. Treatment with AINAs plus ultrasound alleviated thyroid inflammation, reduced local CD3+ and CD4+ T-cell infiltration and serum thyroid autoantibody levels, and caused no apparent pathological injury to major organs.Conclusions:Ultrasound-responsive AINAs enabled the targeted delivery of CIITA-siRNA and rapamycin to thyroid tissue and showed anti-inflammatory and thyroid-protective effects in cellular and animal models of HT.

Introduction

Hashimoto thyroiditis (HT), also known as autoimmune thyroiditis, is an autoimmune thyroid disease (AITD) with a prevalence of 7.5% (95% confidence interval, 5.7–9.6%).[1] Severe hypothyroidism resulting from HT can lead to critical conditions such as hypothermia, respiratory failure, circulatory failure, and central nervous system dysfunction.[2,3] HT is also an independent risk factor for thyroid cancer (TC),[4,5] and recent studies have confirmed that 90.9% of primary thyroid lymphomas are associated with microscopic HT.[6]

Although lifelong thyroid hormone replacement therapy is available, effective treatments for HT are still limited. Thyroxine replacement therapy cannot halt the progression of histopathological changes in the thyroid gland or mitigate the risk of TC. Furthermore, levothyroxine, as a lifelong medication, requires continuous adjustment to maintain serum thyrotropin hormone levels within the optimal range and prevent adverse effects such as cardiac arrhythmias and progressive bone loss due to insufficient or excessive thyroxine levels.[7,8] Many patients continue to experience clinical symptoms even after thyroid hormone levels are normalized.[9]

As an organ-specific autoimmune disease, the primary pathological mechanism of HT involves helper T lymphocytes interacting with specific antigens on thyroid cells, resulting in lymphocytic infiltration and subsequent attack on and self-damage of the thyroid cells. In humans, the class II genes of the human major histocompatibility complex (MHC-II) is referred to as the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) system. They could encode 3 isotypes of alpha/beta heterodimeric proteins—namely human leukocyte antigen DR isotype (HLA-DR), human leukocyte antigen DQ isotype (HLA-DQ), and human leukocyte antigen DP isotype (HLA-DP)—which are crucial for antigen presentation and T-cell activation. Studies have confirmed an association between HT and elevated or abnormal HLA-DR levels.[10] The MHC class II transactivator (CIITA) acts as a “master regulator” of MHC-II expression.[11] A study found that suppression of CIITA using CIITA-siRNA nanoparticles (NPs) on donor endothelial cells (ECs) could protect allografts from acute rejection post-transplant.[12] This suggests that CIITA-targeting small interfering RNA (CIITA-siRNA) may inhibit autoimmune responses by suppressing HLA-DR expression. Given that siRNA has been used to treat inflammation in other diseases,[13,14] this study will explore the therapeutic effects of NPs carrying CIITA-siRNA in HT.

Rapamycin has multiple anti-inflammatory effects,[15] although the clinical risks of systemic rapamycin use primarily include metabolic defects, peripheral edema, cytopenia, and proteinuria.[16,17] In present study, we investigated thyroid-targeted delivery of rapamycin to enhance its local anti-inflammatory effects while minimizing systemic adverse effects.

Given the importance of CIITA-mediated MHC-II expression in thyroid antigen presentation and the anti-inflammatory properties of rapamycin, a combined strategy may offer a more effective approach for HT treatment. However, the therapeutic potential of these agents remains constrained by the lack of efficient thyroid-specific delivery and the risk of systemic side effects. To address this limitation, we designed an anti-inflammatory nano-contrast agent (AINAs) as a thyroid-targeted theranostic platform integrating mesoporous silica nanoparticles (MSNPs) loaded with CIITA-siRNA, trans-activator of transcription (TAT) transmembrane peptides, and perfluorohexane for ultrasound (US)-responsive delivery. Under US exposure, the system is intended to enhance thyroid imaging and promote localized drug delivery through US-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) effect, thereby improving the therapeutic index of both rapamycin and CIITA-siRNA.

Methods

Materials

MSNPs with a diameter of 50 nm were purchased from Ruixi (Xi’an, Shaanxi, China). CIITA-siRNA was synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Rapamycin was purchased from Sigma Germany (Munich, Germany). TAT protein was supplied by Nanjing Peptide Biotech Ltd. (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). Interleukin-23 (IL-23) was sourced from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Recombinant human interferon gamma (IFN-γ) was obtained from PrimeGene (Shanghai, China). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was procured from Bestbio (Shanghai, China). Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) anti-human clusters of differentiation 25 and 69 (CD25 and CD69) were obtained from BioLegend (San Diego, CA, USA).

Cells and animals

Nthy-ori 3-1 cells (normal human thyroid cells) were purchased from Shanghai Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Cat. No. ZQ0874, Shanghai, China) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) under 5% CO2 at 37°C. Jurkat cells (an immortalized human T-lymphocyte cell line) were grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium with 10% fetal calf serum and 2 mmol/L l-glutamine.

Following previous studies, we used iodine-induced non-obese diabetic mice congenic for the H-2h4 major histocompatibility complex haplotype ([NOD].H-2h4) mice to establish the HT mouse model.[18,19] (NOD).H-2h4 mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou) Inc. (Taicang, Jiangsu, China) and were used for experiments. The animals were housed in groups of 5 each at 25°C on a 12-h light–dark cycle with ad libitum access to food. All animal care and experimental procedures adhered to the Animal Management Rules of the Ministry of Health of the People’s Republic of China (No. 55, 2001) and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (No. SDU-IACUC-23097).

Human thyroid tissue RNA extraction and transcriptomic sequencing

At QHSU, we recruited 10 patients with unilateral TC requiring total thyroidectomy due to suspected contralateral nodules or other reasons. Five of these patients also had HT. Based on postoperative pathological findings, 4 cases with contralateral TC or lymph node metastasis were excluded. The remaining 6 thyroid tissue samples were divided into an HT group (n = 3) and a control group (n = 3) for specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and high-throughput transcriptomic sequencing. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of QHSU (approval No. KYLL-202401-012-1). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study.

To explore potential biological functions and signaling pathways of the identified genes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using the Database for Annotation, Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID, https://david.ncifcrf.gov/).

Preparation of TAT-siRNA-MSNPs

First, MSNPs with a diameter of 50 nm and a pore size of 4 nm were mixed with an excess amount of (3-aminopropyl) triethoxysilane (APTES) and a suitable amount of glacial acetic acid, and the mixture was stirred at 78°C for 5 h. This process functionalized the MSNP surfaces with amino groups via APTES treatment. We then centrifuged the suspension at 3000 × g for 5 min and redispersed it in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Afterward, an appropriate dose of CIITA-siRNA (50 µL, 70 µL, or 90 µL) was added to the solution, and the mixture was incubated overnight at 4°C. Simultaneously, we dissolved a precisely weighed TAT peptide in 2-(N-morpholino)ethanesulfonic acid (MES) (pH 6.5), followed by the addition of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS). The prepared TAT peptide solution was then added to the MSNP suspension and stirred for 6 h at 37°C to load the TAT peptide onto the MSNPs [Figure 1A].

AINA preparation methods

We tested four different methods to produce NPs with an erythrocyte membrane shell and rapamycin loading. The four preparation methods include obtaining erythrocyte ghosts through repeated freeze–thaw cycles or ultrasonic disruption, and then either adding rapamycin before preparing the nanodroplets or incorporating rapamycin after the nanodroplets have been formed. To preserve the integrity of the erythrocyte membrane’s phospholipid bilayer and ensure efficient rapamycin loading, we ultimately selected the following method [Figure 1B]. Red blood cells obtained from mice were subjected to three repeated freeze–thaw cycles between −80°C and 37°C to obtain erythrocyte ghosts. The solution was then centrifuged at 18,400 × g at 4°C for 20 min. The upper liquid was discarded, and the lower red blood cell membrane fragments were resuspended in PBS after being filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. Then, perfluorohexane and TAT-siRNA-MSNPs were added to this suspension. An ultrasonic sonicator (650 W, 20–25 kHz) was used to stir the suspension at high speed for 5 min in an ice bath. Rapamycin was pre-dissolved in N,N-dimethylformamide and conjugated with 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-([polyethylene glycol]-2000) (DSPE-PEG2000). Then, the resulting rapamycin solution was added to the previously prepared suspension. Finally, AINAs were obtained through ultrafiltration.

In vitro CIITA-siRNA transport assay

We mixed the MSNPs with fluorescent CIITA-siRNA (20 µmol/L) at volume ratios of 50 µL:1 mL, 70 µL:1 mL, and 90 µL:1 mL, respectively. Then, we measured the fluorescence intensity of the supernatant after precipitating the fluorescent siRNA-MSNPs using a microtiter plate spectrophotometer, and calculated the loading amount and efficiency of the siRNA.

To assess the ability of TAT-MSNPs to transport siRNA into cells, we added TAT-fluorescent CIITA siRNA-MSNPs to the culture medium of Nthy-ori 3-1 cells and incubated them for a specified period. After changing the cell culture medium, we observed fluorescent images of the cells under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Rapamycin-loading assay

We first dilute all the samples with acetonitrile. The rapamycin concentration in the AINAs suspension was measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC; Agilent 1260 Infinity II; Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The suspension was injected into an Agilent ZORBAX SB-C18 column (5 μm particle size; 4.6 mm I.D. × 250 mm length; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and detected by refractive index (RI) and ultraviolet (UV) absorbance at 280 nm.

In vivo and in vitro contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)

For in vitro CEUS imaging, we diluted AINAs with PBS and transferred them into a 15-mL centrifuge tube.[20] Then, the AINAs suspension was heated to 37°C. CEUS evaluations were performed using a real-time GEUS instrument (GE LOGIQ E9 Ultrasound System; GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA).

For in vivo imaging experiments, we anesthetized a rat with pentobarbital sodium on a heated stage. The AINAs suspension were injected into the tail vein, followed by a flush with 0.3 mL PBS. CEUS imaging was conducted using the GEUS instrument, with all imaging parameters (center frequency, 14 MHz; mechanical index [MI], 0.22; depth, 1.5 cm) kept constant.

Detection of apoptosis, autophagy, and oxidative stress (OS) in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells

This experiment is designed to observe the inhibitory effect of AINAs on OS and apoptosis in thyroid cells. We divided Nthy-ori 3-1 cells into 4 groups: (1) control; (2) IL-23; (3) AINAs; and (4) IL-23+ AINAs. In the AINAs group and the IL-23+ AINAs group, the cell culture media were pretreated with US (40 s; VCX400; Sonics & Materials, Inc., Newtown, CT, USA—power density, 1.0 W/cm2; frequency, 20 kHz) after the addition of the AINAs suspension. After treatment in each experimental group, we used Western blotting to measure the levels of B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2), Bcl-2-associated X protein (BAX), P62, light chain 3 II (LC3-II), and LC3-I in the cells. OS levels, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were assessed with detection kits. Apoptotic Nthy-ori 3-1 cells were detected using an Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis kit (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA).

Detection of CIITA and HLA-DR expression on Nthy-ori 3-1 cells

IFN-γ was used to induce the expression of HLA-DR on Nthy-ori 3-1 cells. To assess the inhibitory effect of AINAs on CIITA and HLA-DR expression, we divided Nthy-ori 3-1 cells into 4 groups: (1) control group; (2) IFN-γ group; (3) AINAs group; and (4) IFN-γ + AINAs group. CIITA and HLA-DR messenger RNA (mRNA) levels were determined by reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR). Additionally, HLA-DR protein expression was quantified using flow cytometry (FCM).

T-cell activation assay

Jurkat cells (targets) were co-cultured with monolayers of viable Nthy-ori 3-1 cells in 12-well plates for 48 h in RPMI 1640 with 5% serum. Some Nthy-ori 3-1 cells were pretreated with IFN-γ for 24 h, with or without AINAs, before co-culturing. Jurkat cells alone, without AINAs or IFN-γ, served as the controls. After an appropriate co-culture period, the expression of CD25 and CD69 on the surface of Jurkat cells was analyzed. Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated monoclonal antibodies targeting CD25 or CD69 and subjected to FCM on the CytoFlexS Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA). Data were acquired and analyzed using FlowJo-V10 software (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The concentration of IL-17 in the cell culture media was measured using an IL-17 ELISA Kit (Abcam) with a sensitivity of 0.5 pg/mL and a range of 1.6–100 pg/mL.

Testing targeting and sustained-release ability of AINAs in vivo

Twenty-four adult mice were randomly assigned to 2 groups: AINAs (n = 12) and siRNA-MSNPs (n = 12). Each group received an injection of AINAs (containing CY-3-siRNA) or an equal amount of fluorescent siRNA-MSNPs (containing CY-3-siRNA) into the tail vein. Mice in the AINAs group received neck US treatment for 2 min (1.25 W/cm2, 1 MHz) post-injection. Three mice from each group were sacrificed at different time points (0.5 h, 4 h, 12 h, and 24 h). Thyroid tissues were prepared as frozen sections and analyzed for fluorescence intensity using a fluorescence microscope.

Treatment of HT mice

Following the commonly used protocols for establishing HT mouse models, we utilized NOD.H-2h4 mice to create our HT mouse model. To establish the HT mouse model, mice were given 0.15% sodium iodide (NaI) in their drinking water, subcutaneously injected with 200 µg murine thyroglobulin (Tg) in complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma Germany), and then re-challenged with the same dose of Tg in incomplete Freund’s adjuvant after 2 weeks. The normal group received physiological saline injections at the same time points. Eight weeks after NaI administration, mice in the HT (AINAs) and HT (AINAs + US) groups were injected with AINAs suspension every other day via the tail vein for a total of 3 injections. In in vivo studies, AINAs were synthesized using mouse-specific Ciita-siRNA. Mice in the HT (AINAs + US) group received US treatment at the thyroid site for 2 min (1.25 W/cm2, 1 MHz) following each injection. Mice in the L-T4 group were intraperitoneally injected with levothyroxine (L-T4; 1.6 µg·kg−1·day−1).

Observation of weight changes and detection of serum antibodies

We monitored the body weight of all mice from day 1 of treatment until day 7 after completing all treatments, for a total of 14 days. On day 14, we measured serum levels of thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAbs), thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAbs), and T4 using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). TPOAb, TgAb, and T4 levels in the supernatant were quantified using a quantitative enzyme immunoassay technique with a Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. A microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to measure the color intensity developed in each well. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Hematoxylin eosin (HE) staining and immunohistochemistry

After 14 days from start of treatment, mice from each group were anesthetized and then euthanized. We fixed heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, and thyroid tissues in formalin and embedded them in paraffin for HE staining. After deparaffinization and rehydration, tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin solution for 3 min, then differentiated in acid ethanol for 15 s. Following rinsing in distilled water, the sections were stained with eosin solution (Boster Biological Tech, Hebei, China) for 3 min, dehydrated in graded alcohol, and cleared in xylene. Slides were examined under an Olympus CX41 microscope (magnification, ×200; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). CD3 and CD4 expression in thyroid tissue was evaluated using immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 18.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between 2 groups were made using Student t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Upregulated expression of CIITA and HLA-DR gene in HT

To confirm whether there are differences in the expression of CIITA and HLA-DR in thyroid tissues between HT patients and the general population, we measured the expression levels of these genes in thyroid tissues from HT patients and control subjects using quantitative RT-PCR and transcriptome sequencing. As shown in Figure 2A, PCR data indicated a significant increase in CIITA and HLA-DR expression in HT patients (P <0.01). GO molecular function (MF) analysis indicated that the upregulated genes in the thyroid tissues of HT patients are primarily involved in peptide antigen binding, MHC class II receptor activity, immune receptor activity, and transmembrane signaling receptor activity [Figure 2B]. KEGG term enrichment analysis showed that these genes play a crucial role in antigen processing and presentation signaling pathways, as well as in pathways related to influenza A, asthma, and Type I diabetes mellitus [Figure 2C]. These results indicated that CIITA and HLA-DR may be promising targets for treating HT.

Synthesis and characterization of AINAs

To enhance drug targeted delivery for HT treatment, we synthesized AINAs. AINAs are spherical nanodroplets that encapsulate CIITA-siRNA and simultaneously carry rapamycin, with US-guided tissue targeting capabilities. AINAs were uniformly distributed as observed under the microscope [Figure 3A]. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-1011; JEOL, Akishima, Tokyo, Japan) revealed that AINAs were roughly spherical and encased with CIITA-siRNA-MSNPs [Figure 3B]. Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of AINAs at 25°C were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a DelsaNano C particle size and zeta potential analyzer (Beckman Coulter, Fullerton, CA, USA). The average diameter of AINAs was 255 nm, with an average PDI of 0.23 [Figure 3C]. CIITA-siRNA-MSNPs are surface-loaded with TAT transmembrane proteins to facilitate their entry into thyroid cells. Figure 3D shows AINAs encapsulating MSNPs with surfaces coated in green fluorescent FITC-TAT. This indicates that fluorescent TAT transmembrane proteins have been successfully coated onto the surface of CIITA-siRNA-MSNPs, while AINAs have successfully encapsulated TAT-siRNA-MSNPs.

Loading efficiency and translocation of siRNA by AINAs

Since CIITA is highly expressed in HT patients, we encapsulated CIITA-siRNA-MSNPs in AINAs to reduce its expression for HT treatment. To assess the CIITA-siRNA loading capacity of the MSNPs, we mixed equal amounts of MSNPs with different doses of fluorescent CIITA-siRNA. AINAs containing siRNA-loaded MSNPs appeared as red fluorescent particles under a light microscope [Figure 3E]. After incubating various doses of CIITA-siRNA (50 µL, 70 µL, and 90 µL) with MSNPs for 6 h, over 90% of the siRNA was successfully loaded onto MSNPs across all doses, as determined by microtiter plate spectrophotometry [Figure 3F]. For subsequent experiments, we used 90 µL of CIITA-siRNA as the dose for MSNPs. We added fluorescence CIITA-siRNA-MSNPs to the culture medium of Nthy-ori 3-1 cells for a period of time and found that MSNPs coated with TAT peptide were able to deliver CIITA-siRNA into the cells [Figure 3G].

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) of rapamycin by AINAs

Rapamycin has been shown to possess anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties. Therefore, we loaded rapamycin onto the surface of AINAs for the treatment of HT. To maximize the attachment of rapamycin to the outer membrane of AINAs using DSPE-PEG2000-COOH, it is essential to maintain the integrity of the phospholipid bilayer structure of the red blood cell membrane fragments as much as possible during the preparation of AINAs. So AINAs were prepared using four different methods as described in this paper and their EE of rapamycin was tested [Figure 3H–L]. The results indicated that the highest rapamycin EE, nearly 75%, was achieved by first obtaining erythrocyte ghosts through repeated freeze–thaw cycles, then adding the required materials and preparing the complete nanodroplets through high-speed stirring, and finally loading rapamycin onto their surface [Figure 3L].

In vivo and in vitro US imaging of AINAs

During the process of CEUS of the thyroid gland, the effect of US-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) could induce the rupture of AINAs and the localized release of siRNA-MSNPs and rapamycin in the thyroid. CEUS was performed using the GE LOGIQ E9 Ultrasound System. For the in vitro experiment, the AINAs suspension was placed in a 15-mL centrifuge tube and heated to 37°C. Figure 4A, B shows that AINAs provided satisfactory US enhancement. For the in vivo experiment, a rat was sedated and positioned supine on a heated pad at 37°C. AINAs were dissolved in 1 mL saline and quickly injected (1 mL/kg) via the tail vein, followed by a 0.5-mL saline flush. A high-frequency transducer was used to image the thyroid. The rat thyroid gland was visible on US grayscale images [Figure 4C]. Post-injection, the enhancement imaging of the thyroid gradually becomes clearer and then gradually fades away [Figure 4D–H].

Effect of AINAs on cellular OS

Excessive accumulation of ROS is harmful in HT. IL-23, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, was used to stimulate OS and apoptosis in cells.[21,22] To assess the impact of rapamycin-loaded AINAs on OS in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells, the cells were treated with IL-23, AINAs suspension, or both for 12 h. Immunofluorescence (IF) analysis showed a significant increase in ROS levels in the IL-23 group compared with the control group, indicating IL-23-induced ROS accumulation. ROS levels were reduced when AINAs suspension was used as a pretreatment [Figure 4I]. Intracellular ROS and MDA fluorescence intensities were measured using a microplate spectrophotometer. The fluorescence analysis revealed that AINAs pretreatment significantly decreased IL-23-induced ROS and MDA accumulation in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells (P <0.01, Figure 4J, K).

Effect of AINAs on cell autophagy and apoptosis

Given rapamycin’s role in inhibiting cell apoptosis, we explored AINA effects on autophagy and apoptosis in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells. Western blotting analysis revealed that IL-23 stimulation markedly increased the expression of BAX, LC3-II, and P62 while decreasing Bcl-2 levels compared with the control group. AINAs effectively suppressed the elevated expression of BAX, LC3-II, and P62 induced by IL-23 and restored Bcl-2 expression [Figure 5A–G]. The suppression of BAX expression and the increase in Bcl-2 expression indicate that AINAs can inhibit IL-23-induced apoptosis in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells. To further assess the anti-apoptotic effect of AINAs, FCM was employed. Annexin V–FITC/PI staining of Nthy-ori 3-1 cells showed an increase in apoptotic cells in the IL-23 group, which was partially reversed by AINAs pretreatment [Figure 5H–L].

Effect of AINAs on CIITA and HLA-DR expression in cells

IFN-γ induces the production of MHC class II molecules and plays a role in the pathogenesis of HT.[12,23] To evaluate AINA impact on CIITA and HLA-DR expression, Nthy-ori 3-1 cells in the AINAs and IFN-γ + AINAs groups were treated with AINAs suspension and subjected to US treatment as described earlier. After 6 h, IFN-γ was added to the cell cultures in the IFN-γ and IFN-γ + AINAs groups for an additional 24 h. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis showed that CIITA, a key transcriptional regulator of HLA genes, was significantly elevated in the IFN-γ group compared with the control group, with a similar trend observed for HLA-DR expression. AINAs pretreatment prevented the increase in CIITA and HLA-DR expression (P <0.001, Figure 6A). This trend was consistent with the CIITA and HLA-DR protein expression observed in the FCM assay [Figure 6B, C]. These findings suggest that AINAs inhibit CIITA and HLA-DR expression in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells.

Effect of AINAs on antigen presentation and T-cell activation

To assess the inhibitory effect of AINAs on T cell activation during autoimmune processes, Jurkat cells, a T cell line commonly used in immunological research, were employed in this experiment. Jurkat cells were divided into 4 groups for this experiment: Group 1 (control), Group 2 (Jurkat cells co-cultured with IFN-γ-stimulated Nthy-ori 3-1 cells), Group 3 (Jurkat cells co-cultured with Nthy-ori 3-1 cells) , and Group 4 (Jurkat cells co-cultured with Nthy-ori 3-1 cells that had been pretreated with AINAs and stimulated with IFN-γ). FCM analysis showed that co-culture with IFN-γ-stimulated Nthy-ori 3-1 cells significantly increased CD25 and CD69 expression in Jurkat T cells [Figure 6D–G]. AINAs pretreatment effectively reversed this increase, indicating that AINAs inhibit Jurkat cell activation induced by IFN-γ–stimulated Nthy-ori 3-1 cells (P <0.01).

Jurkat cells typically secrete minimal IL-17. Co-culturing with IFN-γ-stimulated Nthy-ori 3-1 cells led to a significant increase in IL-17 secretion. Interestingly, AINA pretreatment significantly reduced Jurkat T cell activation and IL-17 secretion induced by antigen presentation from Nthy-ori 3-1 cells [Figure 6H]. These results suggest that AINAs reduced antigen presentation by thyroid cells in response to IFN-γ stimulation and subsequent activation of Jurkat T cells.

AINAs targeted delivery and sustained release in vivo

In in vitro experiments, our results indicated that AINAs inhibit OS and apoptosis in thyroid cells, as well as suppress T cell activation. We next assessed the therapeutic effects of AINAs on HT in vivo, starting with an evaluation of their in vivo targeting capabilities. Following the injection of fluorescent AINAs or an equivalent dose of siRNA-MSNPs suspension into the tail vein of rats, with concurrent US irradiation of the thyroid region for 2 min, we collected thyroid tissue at various time points. Frozen sections of the thyroid tissue were prepared and examined under a fluorescence microscope [Figure 7A, B]. Fluorescence intensity analysis revealed that, compared to the injection of an equivalent dose of siRNA-MSNPs, the injection of AINAs followed by US irradiation resulted in a significant increase in the average fluorescence intensity of rat thyroid tissue [Figure 7C]. This demonstrates that the combination of AINAs and US enhances the targeted accumulation of CIITA-siRNA in thyroid tissue; 24 h after injection, the fluorescence intensity in the AINAs group remained at a mean of 219.87 ± 18.01 integrated optical density (IOD). Additionally, since the degradation rate of siRNA on MSNPs within cells was nearly identical, the rate of fluorescence decline in thyroid tissue was similar between the AINAs and siRNA-MSNPs groups [Figure 7D].

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of AINAs (histopathologic changes in thyroid)

(NOD).H-2h4 mice are commonly used for HT modeling in current research.[18,19] To assess the effectiveness of various treatment regimens for HT, 25 NOD.H-2h4 mice were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 5 each): (1) normal, (2) HT, (3) HT (AINAs), (4) HT (AINAs + US), and (5) HT(levothyroxine [L-T4]). After the completion of treatment, we performed H&E staining on paraffin sections of mouse thyroid tissue. Microscopic observations [Figure 8A] revealed that thyroid follicles in the normal group were intact, uniformly distributed, and minimally infiltrated by lymphocytes. In contrast, the HT group exhibited significant inflammatory cell infiltration and severe inflammatory responses, with pronounced follicular atrophy, collapse, and uneven distribution. Severe inflammation led to the disappearance or fibrosis of thyroid follicles. Similar findings were observed in the thyroid tissue of the L-T4 treated HT group.

In the AINAs group, inflammatory cell infiltration and inflammatory responses were notably reduced compared with the HT group. Thyroid follicle disorder, destruction, and lymphocytic infiltration were diminished. No significant inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the AINAs + US treated group [Figure 8A].

To further assess AINA effects on inflammatory cell aggregation in thyroid tissue, IHC was used to detect CD3 and CD4 expression. Paraffin-embedded sections of thyroid tissues were stained with antibodies against CD3 and CD4. As shown in Figure 8B, CD3+ T cells were widely present in the thyroid tissue of the HT group. The number of CD4+ T cells was also significantly higher in the HT group compared with the normal group [Figure 8C].

There was no significant reduction in CD3+ or CD4+ T cell numbers in the thyroid tissue of the L-T4 treated HT group. Conversely, the number of these cells was markedly lower in the thyroid tissue of the AINAs + US treated group compared with the HT group, while the decrease in the AINAs-only treated group was modest [Figure 8C]. Statistical results show that AINAs combined with US therapy significantly reduced localized lymphocytic infiltration in thyroid tissue of HT mice (P <0.001), unlike L-T4 treatment [Figure 8D, E]. This indicates that the combination of AINAs and US can reduce autoimmune responses in HT by inhibiting T cell infiltration in the thyroid tissue.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy of AINAs (Serum differences in TPOAb, TgAb, and T4 levels)

To further assess the therapeutic effect of AINAs on HT, we also measured serum concentrations of TgAb, TPOAb, and T4 using an ELISA kit after various treatments. As shown in Figure 8F, G, TPOAb and TgAb levels were significantly elevated in HT mice compared with normal mice (P <0.05). AINAs therapy reduced TPOAb levels, with a more pronounced decrease in the AINAs + US group compared with the HT group (P <0.05). Serum TgAb levels also decreased in AINAs-treated mice, with a greater decline observed in the AINAs + US group. In contrast, L-T4 treatment did not reduce serum TPOAb and TgAb levels in HT mice and led to a slight increase in serum T4 levels. These results indicate that AINAs treatment for HT significantly reduces the levels of thyroid-related autoantibodies in the blood and alleviates thyroid autoimmune damage, a therapeutic effect that L-T4 treatment does not achieve. Except for the L-T4 group, no significant differences in serum T4 levels were observed among the other 4 groups [Figure 8H].

In vivo and in vitro safety of AINAs

To evaluate the safety of AINAs, we performed CCK-8 ·assays on Nthyori 3-1 cells at various concentrations of AINAs. After 24 h of treatment, cell viability remained over 90% even at the highest AINAs concentration of 600 µg/mL [Figure 9A]. In vivo, there were no significant differences in the average body weight among the groups of mice after treatment [Figure 9B]. H&E staining of heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues revealed no distinct lesions in the AINAs, AINAs + US, or L-T4 groups, indicating high biocompatibility of AINAs in animals [Figure 9C].

Discussion

HT is influenced by genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, and epigenetic influences. Its pathogenesis primarily involves the activation of CD4+ T cells against thyroid autoantigens, resulting in both cellular and humoral immunity. Noel Rose highlighted the role of MHC in HT susceptibility and the impact of environmental factors, as well as the presence of autoreactive T cells in healthy individuals.[24] Thyroid cells expressing MHC-II molecules can present antigens to T cells, leading to T cell activation. The connection between the human MHC-II protein HLA-DR and AITD has been established, with certain HLA-DR alleles showing significant associations with HT.[25,26] Our genomic and RT-PCR analyses indicated that HLA-DR and CIITA, an upstream regulator of HLA, were significantly elevated in thyroid tissue from HT patients compared with controls. These results are consistent with the findings of Kokaraki et al[25].

In previous studies, NPs have been shown to be effective as nanocarriers for targeted treatment of inflammatory diseases.[27–29] In this research, AINAs combined with US treatment were used to target the delivery of siRNA and rapamycin to thyroid tissues. This in vivo targeted therapy significantly reduces the risk of adverse effects associated with systemic use of rapamycin, such as thrombocytopenia, increased serum triglycerides and cholesterol, impaired wound healing, and higher incidence of cataracts.[30] It also reduces the dosage of siRNA required for treatment and lowers the safety risks associated with the therapy.

IFN-γ can induce HT in susceptible mice by upregulating MHC-II molecules on thyroid cells.[31] We used IFN-γ to induce HLA-DR expression in thyroid cells and assessed the inhibitory effect of AINAs on this expression. Our results demonstrated that AINAs loaded with Ciita-siRNA effectively inhibited HLA-DR expression in thyroid cells, thereby preventing these cells from activating T cells in vitro. In vivo experimental results showed that US-assisted AINAs treatment significantly reduced CD3+ and CD4+ T cells in the thyroid tissue of HT mice. This finding suggests that the in vivo targeted delivery of Ciita-siRNA to the thyroid reduced T cell accumulation and activation in the local tissue due to thyroid cell antigen presentation.

In this process, the role of MSNPs in AINAs is to protect free siRNA from degradation by the high levels of ribonucleases present in blood and tissue fluids after the US-induced rupture of the AINAs shell in thyroid tissue. TAT transmembrane protein coated on MSNPs helps facilitate the successful entry of more siRNA-carrying MSNPs into thyroid cells. In this study, in vitro fluorescence experiments demonstrated that TAT-coated MSNPs could deliver CIITA-siRNA into thyroid cells. In vivo results further confirmed that CIITA-siRNA within TAT coated MSNPs effectively exerted therapeutic effects in thyroid cells.

The death of thyroid cells in HT is attributed not only to cytotoxic effects from autoimmune reactions but also to apoptotic processes and elevated OS levels in the cells. Apoptosis plays a significant role in HT, with patients experiencing increased thyroid cell apoptosis and decreased autophagy.[32] Rapamycin has been shown to effectively prevent cardiomyocyte apoptosis, promote cardiomyocyte autophagy, and improve cardiac function by regulating the interaction between the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) complex 1 and endoplasmic reticulum stress pathways in chronic post-infarction heart failure.[33] Consequently, we used rapamycin-loaded AINAs to attempt to inhibit apoptosis in thyroid cells affected by HT. In vitro experiments demonstrated that rapamycin-loaded AINAs treatment reduced the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax while increasing the expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 in Nthy-ori 3-1 cells. This indicates that AINAs effectively inhibits apoptosis in thyroid cells.

Several studies have reported that elevated IL-23 levels in HT inhibit autophagy, whereas rapamycin can partially promote it.[32,34] Our in vitro experiments revealed that IL-23 simultaneously increased the expression of LC3-II/LC3-I in thyroid cells, while also raising P62 expression. The binding of autophagosomes to lysosomes to form autophagic lysosomes requires P62 consumption, and an increase in P62 suggests a blockage in the autophagic lysosome synthesis process. Accumulation of LC3-II may be related to incomplete autophagy.[35] IL-23 may inhibit autophagy by affecting downstream pathways, leading to impaired fusion between phagosomes and lysosomes and elevated levels of LC3-II and P62. Rapamycin-loaded AINAs reduced the elevated levels of LC3-II and P62 induced by IL-23 stimulation, likely by restoring downstream autophagic pathways and promoting cellular autophagy. Further research is needed to confirm these findings due to the limited sample size in our study.

OS represents an imbalance between the production of ROS and their removal by protective mechanisms, potentially leading to chronic inflammation.[36] Under normal physiological conditions, ROS are essential for thyroid hormone synthesis. However, elevated ROS levels can harm thyroid cells, triggering thyroid gland inflammation and contributing to HT development.[37,38] Surveys of HT patients have indicated increased oxidant levels and reduced antioxidant levels.[39] Rapamycin has been shown to inhibit OS and suppress the differentiation of pathogenic T helper 17 cells, known for their roles in autoimmune diseases, in favor of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs).[15,40] Localized rapamycin delivery via a biomimetic drug delivery system has proven effective in reducing vascular inflammation and thereby the progression of vascular disease.[41] The findings of this study demonstrated that rapamycin-loaded AINAs could lower ROS and MDA levels in thyroid cells, consequently reducing cell damage induced by excessively high OS levels in HT.

In the in vivo targeted delivery experiments, we aimed to compare the proportion and stability of siRNA in thyroid tissue after intravenous injection of AINAs or an equivalent amount of siRNA-MSNPs. Considering the fluorescence lifetime of the siRNA and its instability in the body, we conducted only one thyroid US scan for the AINAs group after injection to shorten the experiment time, then extracted the thyroid tissue for fluorescence analysis. This experiment demonstrated the in vivo targeting capability of US-assisted AINAs, leading to a significantly higher proportion of siRNA entering the thyroid tissue compared with the siRNA-MSNPs group. Due to the lack of multiple thyroid US treatments over several days following injection in the mice, the experiment could not verify the long-term circulation of AINAs in vivo. Nevertheless, to protect AINAs from immune attack in the bloodstream and reduce the risk of immune reactions from intravenous injection, we used erythrocyte ghost as the shell for the AINAs. NPs encapsulated in erythrocyte membranes, used as biomimetic nanocarriers, have been shown to offer advantages such as high biocompatibility, biodegradability, and a prolonged circulation half-life.[42,43] By encapsulating NPs within erythrocyte ghost, a platform can be created that combines the advantages of native red blood cell membranes with nanomaterials. This approach has been shown to provide high levels of biocompatibility and to extend circulation time in the body.[44] In both in vivo and in vitro toxicology studies, we did not observe any significant side effects from AINAs. Examination of key tissues such as the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys revealed no apparent pathological damage, indicating that AINAs have biosafety.

In conclusion, we designed smart AINAs for targeted treatment of HT. These AINAs are US-responsive and can targeted deliver CIITA-siRNA and rapamycin to thyroid tissue. US-assisted AINAs nanomaterial-targeted therapy enhances precision while significantly improving therapeutic outcomes. Erythrocyte ghost shells can enhance the safety and biocompatibility of AINAs treatment. Rapamycin on AINAs enters thyroid cells, effectively reducing OS levels and inhibiting cell apoptosis. Besides, both in vitro and in vivo studies demonstrate that AINAs effectively deliver CIITA-siRNA into thyroid cells, suppressing antigen presentation and T-cell activation in local tissues, resulting in outstanding anti-inflammatory effects. Our study represents an application of siRNA for the treatment of HT. As NPs, AINAs targeted combination therapy provides scientific support for the effective treatment of HT.
