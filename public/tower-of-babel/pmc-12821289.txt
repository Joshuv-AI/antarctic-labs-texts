Phosphorylated NCOA6 promotes aggressiveness and contributes patient outcomes in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer
Authors: Hongmei Dong, Ruijun Zhao, Weiheng Cui, Xijiao Pang, Hongzheng Ren, Yunlong Pan, Yexi Chen, Shegan Gao, Hao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer heterogeneity is not only involved by genetic and epigenetic alterations, but may also associated with the complexities at levels of protein modifications. Estrogen receptor (ER) positive breast cancer constitutes approximately 70% of all breast cancer cases, significantly impacting global female morbidity and mortality. Nuclear receptor co-activator 6 (NCOA6, also referred as AIB3) is a coregulator of sex hormone receptors as well as other transcription factors. Although the expression and functions of NCOA6 have been studied in breast, colon, lung, pancreatic cancers, and hepatocellular carcinoma, post-tranlational modification of NCOA6 is barely known. This study aims to study the NCOA6 phosphorylation, in particular at Serine 884, and its relevance to breast tumor aggressiveness and the prognostic significance.MethodsWe used bioinformatics and immunohistochemistry to detect NCOA6 expression and Ser884 phosphorylation in ER-positive breast cancer tissues. Cell proliferation, migration, and invasion were measured using CCK-8, transwell, and wound healing assays. Immunoblotting was used to detect protein levels and phosphorylation. Mouse xenograft models were established to evaluate tumor growth in vivo. Transcriptome and proteome analyses were performed to identify related signaling pathways.ResultsTotal NCOA6 expression was not linked to cancer stage, subtype, or prognosis. However, phosphorylation at Ser884 was significantly increased in ER-positive breast cancer and associated with poorer outcomes, indicating its potential as an independent prognostic marker. Functional studies demonstrated that Ser884 phosphorylation promoted tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro and enhanced tumor growth in vivo. Mechanistically, ERK2 activation induced NCOA6 phosphorylation at Ser884, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastatic behavior. Only the phosphomimetic S884E mutant, not the non-phosphorylatable S884F mutant, restored migration and invasion in NCOA6-deficient cells. Transcriptomic and proteomic analyses further suggested that NCOA6 influences cell cycle, PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling, and protein synthesis.ConclusionPhosphorylation of NCOA6 at Ser884 is markedly increased in ER-positive breast cancer, associates with aggressive clinicopathologic features, and portends poor prognosis. Functionally, enhanced NCOA6 phosphorylation promotes invasion and metastasis of ER-positive breast cancer cells. Collectively, these findings identify phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) as a promising prognostic biomarker and potential therapeutic target, supporting more personalized management of ER-positive disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04069-2.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major global health concern, consistently ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths in women [1, 2]. Witnessing an incremental rise of 0.6% per annum since the mid-2000s, the incidence of breast cancer has been notably propelled by an upsurge in the detection of localized-stage and hormone receptor-positive cases [1]. Breast cancer is a highly complex and heterogeneous disease that can be classified into four main molecular subtypes: Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, and the basal-like subtype, which is commonly referred to as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [3]. This inherent diversity necessitates personalized management strategies for the diagnosis and treatment of each patient subgroup. Estrogen receptor (ER) activity plays a crucial role in promoting the proliferation of luminal A and luminal B breast cancer subtypes, which together comprise approximately 70% of all breast cancer cases. The modulation of ER function is influenced by numerous proteins, most notably a range of coactivators and corepressors, which have a significant impact on its regulatory capacity [4, 5]. Therefore, endocrine therapies are widely regarded as the primary treatment strategy for ER-positive breast cancer. Notably, aromatase inhibitors such as anastrozole, letrozole, and exemestane impede estrogen synthesis by inhibiting the enzymatic conversion of androgens into estrogens [6]. Conversely, agents such as tamoxifen, which are classified as selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs), interfere with the binding of estrogen to its receptor. In addition, compounds like elacestrant and fulvestrant, known as selective estrogen receptor degraders (SERDs), prevent the translocation of estrogen receptors to the cell nucleus and promote their degradation [6]. Nonetheless, among patients with ER-positive breast cancer, a subset exhibits either innate or acquired resistance to endocrine therapies [7]. Although extensive genetic studies on breast tumor heterogeneity have been conducted and translated into clinical practice, a significant gap persists in research focusing on the protein modifications underlying this heterogeneity and their clinical implications. Prognostic outcomes for ER-positive breast cancer demonstrate considerable variability compared to those of other breast cancer subtypes [8]. Addressing this gap is crucial, as a deeper understanding of protein-level changes could lead to the identification of novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets, ultimately improving the prognosis for patients with ER-positive breast cancer.

NCOA6 (also known as TRBP, ASC-2, PRIP, RAP250, NRC, and AIB3) is amplified in breast cancer and is located on chromosome 20q11 [9–11]. Initially identified as an amplified gene in BT-474 breast cancer cells, NCOA6 is a multifunctional co-activator protein that plays essential roles in embryonic development, cell survival, wound healing, and the regulation of energy metabolism [12, 13]. NCOA6 is overexpressed in steroid hormone-dependent tumors, such as breast cancer, and may function as a co-regulator to enhance ER transcriptional activity, thereby promoting carcinogenesis [9, 14]. Previous studies have reported that NCOA6 significantly enhances the migration, invasion, and proliferation of human placental cells as well as hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and pancreatic cancer cells [15–17]. NCOA6 can be characterized as a strong transcriptional coactivator for many nuclear receptors (ERs, GR, RXR, RAR, PPARγ, TRs and VDR) and certain other transcription factors (AP-1, SRF, CBP, E2F-1 and NF-κB), which contribute to the regulation of many kinds of physiologic and pathological processes [12, 18]. Furthermore, accumulating evidence suggests that NCOA6 promotes gene transcription by interacting with or recruiting a variety of coactivator complexes, including the SRC-1/CBP complex, the ASC-2 complex, and the TRP or VDR-interacting protein complex. Through these interactions, NCOA6 facilitates chromatin remodeling and the assembly of general transcription factors, thereby enhancing the efficiency of transcriptional activation [19]. Studies demonstrate that NCOA6 gene alterations in mice lead to faster mammary tumor development through enhanced cellular proliferation and also impede healing processes by affecting cell migration [19, 20]. These findings indicate that NCOA6 may play distinct roles in both the initiation and progression of breast cancer. Therefore, elucidating the complex and multifaceted functions and mechanisms of NCOA6 in breast cancer is crucial for advancing our understanding of the disease.

Protein phosphorylation is a fundamental post-translational modification that plays a pivotal role in regulating diverse cellular processes, including cell growth, metabolism, apoptosis, and signal transduction pathways [21]. Phosphorylation primarily occurs on serine, threonine, and tyrosine residues, with phosphorylated serine (pSer) representing the most prevalent form [22]. This modification is particularly significant in tumor cells, which can modulate phosphorylation to adapt to unfavorable microenvironments, evade apoptosis, and facilitate metastasis. Notably, dysregulation of phosphorylation is closely associated with the onset and progression of various cancers [23]. Consequently, targeted therapies directed at specific phosphorylation sites may represent novel treatment strategies for breast cancer, particularly in overcoming therapeutic resistance and improving patient prognosis. Specifically, phosphorylation plays a critical role in modulating the ability of transcriptional coactivators to recognize and interact with specific transcription factors, thereby influencing cellular functions and signaling pathways [24]. AIB1 (Amplified in Breast Cancer 1) has been identified as a coactivator whose activity is regulated by phosphorylation, with its specificity for different transcription factors determined by distinct phosphorylation patterns on the AIB1 molecule [25]. Understanding the precise mechanisms and signaling pathways regulated by phosphorylation of NCOA6 in breast cancer is crucial for the development of targeted therapies. Notably, NCOA6 contains an LXXLL motif, which interacts with a diverse array of nuclear receptors, including TRs, RXRs, RARs, GR, ERs, VDR and the PPARs [26–29]. Previous studies have shown that there is a serine-proline (SP) motif within the S884 region adjacent to the LXXLL motif of NCOA6, which can serve as a phosphorylation site for mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK). Phosphorylation of NCOA6 at S884 has been demonstrated to modulate its interactions with ERα and ERβ differently in vitro [5]. Since the differential recruitment of ERα and ERβ by NCOA6 may depend on phosphorylation at S884, these findings provide novel insights into estrogen signaling and may lead to the development of therapeutic receptor-selective peptide antagonists.

To investigate the functional implications of NCOA6 phosphorylation in breast cancer progression, we utilized public databases, analyzed clinical tissue samples, and conducted both in vitro cell culture experiments and in vivo animal models to assess the impact of phosphorylated NCOA6 on patients with ER-positive breast cancer. Additionally, we generated expression plasmids encoding the NCOA6-S884 wild-type (WT), phosphorylation-deficient (S884F), and phosphorylation-mimetic (S884E) mutants to elucidate the specific role of phosphorylation at the S884 site in ER-positive breast cancer cells. Our results demonstrate that phosphorylation of NCOA6 at S884 significantly enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of ER-positive breast cancer cells. Furthermore, we analyzed the pivotal role of ERK2 in mediating NCOA6 phosphorylation and evaluated its association with the metastatic potential of ER-positive breast cancer cells.

Materials and methods

Patients and tissue samples

This study included 195 female breast cancer patients who underwent surgical treatment and were pathologically diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma. The clinicopathological characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Breast cancer tissue specimens were collected during surgery at the Department of Surgery, Cancer Hospital of Shantou University Medical College, between 2010 and 2014. All patients first received surgical treatment, followed by adjuvant therapies such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or hormone therapy as appropriate. The mean age of the patients was 50 years, ranging from 23 to 89 years. The histological grading of the primary tumors was determined using a modified World Health Organization (WHO) classification, and tumor staging was defined according to the 7th Edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM Staging Manual. Tissue specimens were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and embedded in paraffin for routine histological analysis. The clinical research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical College (IRB No. #04–070). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed as described previously [30, 31]. Briefly, 4-µm sections were prepared from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) breast cancer tissues. Sections underwent standard procedures including deparaffinization, rehydration, blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity, and antigen retrieval. The primary antibodies used included anti-phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) (kindly provided by Dr. Lan Ko), anti-Ki67 (Cat# ab21700; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-ERα (Cat# ab259427; Abcam), anti-PR (Cat# ab268102; Abcam), and anti-HER2 (Cat# ab214275; Abcam). Tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen, and hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining.

The expression of phospho-NCOA6 in tumor cells was assessed based on the proportion of positive cells: 0 (negative), 1 (≤ 10%), 2 (11–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (>75%). Staining intensity was scored as 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). An immunoreactive score (IRS) was calculated by multiplying the proportion score (P) by the intensity score (I), resulting in a total score (S = P × I). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal cutoff value for phospho-NCOA6 overexpression in breast cancer. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated at various IHC scores, and the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1) was used to objectively identify the threshold with the best discriminatory power. An IHC score of 3.5 yielded the highest Youden index and was thus selected as the appropriate cutoff value. Samples with an IHC score >3.5 were considered as having high phospho-NCOA6 expression, while those with scores ≤ 3.5 were classified as low expression for statistical analyses. The Ki67 index was assessed based on the percentage of tumor cells exhibiting nuclear staining. High Ki67 expression was defined as an index greater than 20%, while low expression was defined as 20% or less [32]. The specimens were evaluated by two independent observers with no clinical information. When two reviewers disagree, compare and discuss agreement.

Bioinformatics analysis

The relationship between NCOA6 and overall survival (OS) and relapse-free survival (RFS) of breast cancer patients was performed by Kaplan-Meier Plotter.

(http://kmplot.com/analysis/index.php? p=background) (Nagy et al. 2021). In addition, the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html; Chandrashekar et al., 2017) was utilized to compare NCOA6 protein expression levels between breast cancer tissues and normal breast tissues.

Cell culture, plasmids, and transfection

The human breast cancer cell lines SKBR-3, MDA-MB-453, MDA-MB-231, ZR75-1, and MCF-7 were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA). These cancer cells were cultured in F12/DMEM medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated in 5% CO2 at 37℃. Plasmids encoding wild-type NCOA6 (WT), the S884E mutant (mimicking constitutive phosphorylation), and the S884F mutant (mimicking constitutive non-phosphorylation) were generated using the pcDNA3.1 vector. These constructs were transiently transfected into MCF-7 and ZR75-1 cells using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

qRT -PCR

Total RNA extraction, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) were performed as previously described [30]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and was reverse-transcribed using M-MLV reverse transcriptase cDNA synthesis Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, equal quantities of cDNA were amplified using SYBR Green PCR amplification kit (Invitrogen) with the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). β-actin served as the internal control, with every reaction conducted in triplicate for consistency.

The following primers were used for PCR amplification:

NCOA6, forward: 5’-TGGGTGGTCAGTCTCGT-3’, reverse: 5’-CCGGCAGTAGGTCTCAT-3’;

β-actin, forward: 5’-GAACCCCAAGGCCAACCGCGAGA-3’, reverse: 5’-TGACCCCGTCACCGGAGTCCATC-3’.

Cell proliferation assays

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, approximately 1,000 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate. At the indicated time points, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂ for 2 h. After incubation, the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Wound-healing migration assay

The wound-healing assay was conducted to evaluate the migratory capacity of cells. Briefly, cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured until a confluent monolayer was formed. A straight scratch was then created across the cell monolayer using a sterile 10 µL pipette tip. After scratching, the wells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove detached cells, and the cells were subsequently cultured in medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Images of the wound area were captured using a 20× objective lens immediately after scratching (0 h) and at 48–72 h post-scratch to monitor wound closure. The wound healing rate was calculated as follows: wound healing rate = [(scratch width at 0 h – scratch width at 48/72 h)/scratch width at 0 h] × 100%. Each experiment was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Cell invasion assays

Cell invasion assays were performed using 24-well plates fitted with 8-µm pore size Transwell inserts (Corning Life Sciences, Tewksbury, MA, USA). Prior to cell seeding, the upper surface of each insert membrane was coated with 50 µL of Matrigel (BD Biosciences, CA, USA) to simulate the extracellular matrix. Subsequently, 2 × 10⁴ cells suspended in 200 µL of serum-free medium were added to the upper chambers, while the lower chambers were filled with 600 µL of medium supplemented with 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere, non-invading cells on the upper side of the membrane were carefully removed with a cotton swab. The invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. The number of invading cells was quantified by counting five randomly selected fields per well under a light microscope. All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

In Vivo xenotransplant in nude mice

Female nude mice (5 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). A total of 1 × 10⁶ MCF-7 cells, transfected with either wild-type NCOA6, the S884E mutant, the S884F mutant, or the control vector, were suspended in 100 µL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the flanks of the mice. Tumor size was measured every 3 days using a caliper after palpable tumors appeared. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula L × (S)² × 0.52, where L and S represent the long and short diameters of the tumor, respectively. Mice were euthanized 21 days after tumor formation was observed. Tumors were excised, weighed, fixed in formalin, and embedded in paraffin for further analysis.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting was performed as described previously [33, 34]. Briefly, total proteins were extracted from breast cancer cells on ice using RIPA lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay. Equal amounts of proteins were separated by 6–12% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following antibodies were used: anti-NCOA6 (Cat# 25241-1-AP; Proteintech Group Inc, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) (a kind gift from Dr. Lan Ko), anti-E-cadherin (Cat. No. 610182, BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), anti-N-cadherin (Cat. No. 610920; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), anti-Snail (Cat# 13099-1-AP; Proteintech Group Inc, Chicago, IL, USA), anti-phospho-ERK(Cat# 4370; Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA, USA) and β-actin (Cat# 81115-1-RR; Proteintech Group Inc, Chicago, IL, USA). After washing twice with TBST (10 min each), membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system (Millipore, MA, USA).

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/downloads.jsp) was performed using GSEA software (version 4.1.0) to identify pathways significantly associated with NCOA6 expression in ER-positive breast cancer. Samples were divided into “high” and “low” NCOA6 expression groups based on the median expression value. The analysis was conducted using the 50 hallmark gene sets from MSigDB (h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt). Pathways with a nominal P-value < 0.05 and FDR q-value < 0.25 were considered significantly enriched.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For comparisons between two groups with normally distributed data, Student’s t-test was used. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way ANOVA followed by appropriate post hoc tests was applied. The association between phospho-NCOA6 expression and clinicopathological factors was evaluated by the χ² test. Pearson’s correlation analysis was employed to assess correlations between two continuous variables. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test were used for survival analysis. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were utilized to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for OS. A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The level of phosphorylated NCOA6 is upregulated in breast cancer

To investigate the expression profile of the NCOA6 gene across different tissue types, both in normal and tumor samples, a pan-cancer analysis was performed using the TNMplot database (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/) (Fig. S1A). The results indicated that NCOA6 expression was significantly upregulated in various tumor tissues compared to their corresponding normal tissues, with a particularly notable increase observed in breast cancer samples (Fig. S1A). Further, the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database analysis reveals that the expression level of NCOA6 is significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues compared to the normal breast tissues (Fig. S1B). Additionally, we examined the expression of NCOA6 across different clinical stages and molecular subtypes of breast cancer utilizing the Cancer Proteome Atlas (CPTAC) dataset available on the UALCAN website (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html). However, compared to normal tissues, NCOA6 levels did not show a statistically significant increase in any of the clinical stages (stage I, II, and III) or molecular subtypes (Luminal, HER2-positive, and TNBC) (Fig. S1C and D). To further elucidate the relationship between NCOA6 expression and the prognosis of breast cancer patients, we utilized the Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (http://www.kmplot.com/) to evaluate the impact of NCOA6 expression levels on OS and RFS in breast cancer patients. However, no significant correlation was observed between NCOA6 expression levels and the prognosis of breast cancer patients (Fig. S1E and F). Dysregulation of phosphorylation is closely associated with cancer development and progression, as aberrant phosphorylation patterns can activate oncogenic signaling pathways or inhibit tumor suppressor functions. Such disruptions result in uncontrolled cell proliferation, increased cell survival, and enhanced metastatic potential, thereby promoting tumor growth and dissemination [23]. Lan et al. identified that NCOA6-S884 is a proline-directed serine residue, and demonstrated that the S884 site of NCOA6 can be phosphorylated by ERK2, a key kinase within the MAPK pathway [5]. Based on our analysis of the CPTAC dataset, we assessed the protein phosphorylation levels of NCOA6 in both normal breast tissues and primary breast tumor tissues. Our results revealed a significant elevation in the phosphorylation levels of NCOA6 at threonine 1749, threonine 1880, serine 1750, and serine 1751 in breast tumor tissues compared to normal breast tissue (Fig. 1A–D, P < 0.05). Conversely, phosphorylation at the serine 1892 site of NCOA6 was significantly reduced in tumor tissues relative to normal tissues (Fig. 1E, P < 0.001).Subsequently, we examined the expression of phospho-NCOA6 across various clinical stages and molecular subtypes of breast cancer. We observed a remarkable increase in phosphorylation at threonine 1749, serine 1750, and serine 1751 in tumor tissues from both early (stages I and II) and advanced (stage III) breast cancer patients compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1A–C, P < 0.001). Notably, phosphorylation levels at these sites were significantly elevated across all breast cancer subtypes, with the most pronounced increases seen in hormone receptor-positive subtypes (Fig. 1A–C, P < 0.05). In contrast, a significant elevation in phosphorylation at threonine 1880 was observed only in stage II tumors and in TNBC subtypes, compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1D, P < 0.05). Additionally, we observed a substantial reduction in the phosphorylation levels of NCOA6 at the serine 1892 site in stage II and III breast cancer, as well as across all subtypes, when compared to normal tissues (Fig. 1E, P < 0.01). Collectively, these findings indicate that NCOA6 phosphorylation is generally elevated in breast cancer tissues compared to normal tissues, with phosphorylation patterns closely associated with the molecular subtype and stage of the disease.

Upregulation of phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) expression is associated with poor prognosis in ER-positive breast cancer

To further elucidate the role of phospho-NCOA6 in breast cancer, we performed IHC staining for phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) in 195 paired breast cancer and adjacent normal tissue samples. The results demonstrated that phospho-NCOA6 is predominantly localized in the nucleus, with its expression levels significantly increased in breast cancer tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 2A-B, P < 0.001). We subsequently analyzed the association between phospho-NCOA6 expression and various clinicopathological parameters. Elevated phospho-NCOA6 levels were found to correlate with larger tumor size (P = 0.038), presence of lymph node metastasis (P = 0.002), advanced clinical stage (P = 0.018), ERα status (P = 0.007), PR status (P = 0.010), and higher Ki67 expression (P = 0.030, Fig. 2C) (Supplementary Table 1). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients with high phospho-NCOA6 levels had shorter overall survival than patients with low phospho-NCOA6 expression (P = 0.006, Fig. 2D).

Bioinformatics analyses have revealed that phosphorylated NCOA6 is significantly upregulated in hormone receptor-positive breast cancer. Previous studies have demonstrated that mutation of the S884 site within the LXXLL motif of NCOA6 influences its selectivity for ERα and ERβ, underscoring the critical role of this post-translational modification in ER-positive breast cance [5]. Based on these findings, we performed IHC staining to evaluate phospho-NCOA6 expression in 110 cases of ER-positive breast cancer, including both luminal A and luminal B subtypes. The results indicated that patients with high levels of phospho-NCOA6 expression had significantly shorter OS compared with those exhibiting low phospho-NCOA6 expression (log-rank test, P = 0.003; Fig. 3A–B). Furthermore, univariate Cox regression analysis identified histologic differentiation (P = 0.046), tumor stage (P = 0.005), and phospho-NCOA6 expression (P = 0.005) as significant factors associated with OS in ER-positive breast cancer patients (Table 1). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further demonstrated that tumor size (P = 0.035), stage (P = 0.043), and phospho-NCOA6 expression (P = 0.040) served as independent prognostic indicators in this cohort (Table 1). Collectively, these findings suggest that elevated phospho-NCOA6 expression is associated with a poor prognosis in patients with ER-positive breast cancer.

S884 phosphorylation of NCOA6 promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion in ER-positive breast cancer cells

To further elucidate the functional significance of NCOA6 phosphorylation at the S884 site in the progression of ER-positive breast cancer, we constructed expression plasmids encoding the wild-type NCOA6 (WT), a phosphorylation-inactive mutant (S884F), and a phosphorylation-mimicking mutant (S884E). These plasmids were subsequently transfected into two ER-positive breast cancer cell lines, MCF-7 and ZR75-1. Successful establishment of the stable cell lines was confirmed by both immunoblotting and qRT-PCR (Fig. 4A–C). The effects of NCOA6 S884 phosphorylation on cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion were assessed using the CCK-8 assay, wound healing assay, and transwell invasion assay, respectively. The results demonstrated that overexpression of NCOA6 significantly promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of MCF-7 and ZR75-1 cells in comparison to the control group (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D–F). Furthermore, relative to the NCOA6-WT group, expression of the phosphorylation-mimicking mutant NCOA6-S884E further enhanced cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, whereas the phosphorylation-inactive mutant NCOA6-S884F did not exert such effects (P < 0.05, Fig. 4D–F).

To further validate the role of NCOA6 in breast cancer in vivo, we conducted mouse tumorigenesis assays using MCF-7 cells transfected with wild-type NCOA6, the S884E mutant, the S884F mutant, or a control vector. Overexpression of NCOA6 significantly enhanced tumor growth compared to the control group (P = 0.005, Fig. 5A). Notably, the S884E mutant promoted tumor growth to an even greater extent than wild-type NCOA6, whereas the S884F mutant did not exhibit this effect (P = 0.002, Fig. 5A). Tumors derived from the NCOA6-WT group were significantly larger (Fig. 5B, left panel) and heavier (P < 0.001, Fig. 5B; right panel) than those from the control group. Moreover, both tumor size and weight were further increased in the NCOA6-S884E group compared to the NCOA6-WT group (P < 0.001, Fig. 5B), while no significant difference was observed in the NCOA6-S884F group (Fig. 5B). Taken together, our results suggest that phosphorylation of NCOA6 at S884 promotes malignant progression of ER-positive breast cancer.

ERK2-mediated phosphorylation of NCOA6 enhances the metastatic potential of ER-positive breast cancer cells

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying NCOA6 phosphorylation, we investigated the potential involvement of the MAPK/ERK pathway. Sequence analysis revealed that S884 of NCOA6 is a proline-directed serine, suggesting it could serve as a target for MAPK-mediated phosphorylation. Consistent with previous in vitro findings that S884 can be phosphorylated by ERK2 [5], we hypothesized that MAPK/ERK signaling regulates NCOA6 phosphorylation at this site in ER-positive breast cancer cells. To test this, MCF-7 and ZR75-1 cells were treated with the ERK2 inhibitor U0126 (1 µM) for 30 and 60 min. The results demonstrated a time-dependent reduction in phosphorylation levels of both ERK2 and NCOA6 following inhibitor treatment (Fig. 6A). Moreover, GSEA analysis indicated a positive correlation between elevated NCOA6 expression and the activation of both the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metastasis signaling pathways (Fig. 6B). To further explore the relationship between NCOA6 phosphorylation and EMT, we performed immunoblotting to assess the expression of key EMT markers, including the epithelial marker E-cadherin, the mesenchymal marker N-cadherin, and the EMT-associated transcription factor Snail. Compared to the NCOA6-WT group, the NCOA6-S884E group displayed a marked upregulation of N-cadherin and Snail protein levels, along with a significant downregulation of E-cadherin. In contrast, the NCOA6-S884F group showed no significant differences in the expression of these proteins relative to the NCOA6-WT group (Fig. 6C).

To further investigate the role of NCOA6 phosphorylation in ERK-mediated cell motility, we treated MCF-7 and ZR75-1 breast cancer cells with the ERK-pathway inhibitor U0126 and performed rescue experiments using the S884E and S884F mutants. Functional analyses were conducted with wound-healing and transwell invasion assays. In the wound-healing assay, U0126 markedly suppressed cell migration compared with the control (P < 0.001; Fig. 6D-E). Re-expression of S884E efficiently restored migratory capacity in U0126-treated cells, whereas S884F did not, indicating that phosphorylation at S884 is required for ERK-mediated cell migration (P < 0.001; Fig. 6D-E). Similarly, transwell invasion assays showed that U0126 significantly reduced invasion, an effect rescued by S884E but not S884F (P < 0.001; Fig. 6D-E). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ERK2-mediated phosphorylation of NCOA6 at Ser884 is crucial for promoting EMT, migration, and invasion, thereby enhancing the metastatic potential of ER-positive breast cancer cells under ERK activation.

Analyzing the potential downstream mechanisms of NCOA6 in ER-positive breast cancer

To elucidate potential downstream pathways through which NCOA6 drives ER-positive breast cancer progression, we first conducted GSEA analysis. The results revealed that high NCOA6 expression was significantly associated with several key oncogenic pathways. Specifically, samples with elevated NCOA6 levels exhibited marked enrichment in the HALLMARK_MITOTIC_SPINDLE, HALLMARK_G2M_CHECKPOINT, and HALLMARK_E2F_TARGETS gene sets, suggesting a potential role for NCOA6 in promoting cell cycle progression and mitotic activity (Fig. 7A–C). Additionally, the HALLMARK_PI3K_AKT_MTOR_SIGNALING pathway was significantly enriched in the high NCOA6 expression group, indicating that NCOA6 may contribute to the activation of crucial proliferative and survival signaling cascades (Fig. 7D).

To further validate these results, we analyzed proteomic data from the CPTAC ER-positive breast cancer cohort to investigate the downstream mechanisms of NCOA6. Weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed on the protein expression profiles, resulting in the construction of a scale-free coexpression network. Nine protein modules were identified using a soft-thresholding power of 3 and a merge cut height of 0.75 (Fig. 7E, F). Among these, the MEred module exhibited the highest correlation with NCOA6 expression and was therefore designated as the “NCOA6-related protein module,” consisting of 100 proteins (r = 0.39, P < 0.001; Fig. 7G). Further pathway enrichment analysis of this module using Metascape corroborated the transcriptomic findings. The proteins in this module were primarily enriched in biological processes related to protein synthesis, folding, modification, localization, and transport, as well as organelle organization, cellular structural components such as focal adhesions, secretion, and processes associated with viral activity and metabolic regulation (Fig. 7H). In conclusion, our integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses demonstrate that NCOA6 may promote the progression of ER-positive breast cancer by activating cell cycle processes and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, as well as by regulating protein synthesis and cellular organization. These findings provide novel insights into the downstream mechanisms of NCOA6 and highlight its potential as a therapeutic target in ER-positive breast cancer.

Discussion

Our study revealed elevated levels of phosphorylated NCOA6 in breast cancer specimens, which correlated with reduced survival among patients with ER-positive tumors. Phosphorylation at the Ser884 site on NCOA6 appears to promote aggressive cellular behavior, including enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion. These findings suggest that monitoring phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) levels may serve as a valuable prognostic biomarker for ER-positive breast cancer. Furthermore, this insight could inform the development of individualized therapeutic strategies tailored to patients with elevated NCOA6 phosphorylation, ultimately improving clinical outcomes in this population.

NCOA6 is recognized as a multifunctional co-activator for sex hormone receptors, as well as various transcription factors and nuclear receptors, implicating it in the regulation of numerous key cellular processes [12]. NCOA6 was first identified in breast cancer, is amplified and overexpressed in many cancers [16, 17, 35–37]. Previous studies have confirmed that the expression of NCOA6 in liver cancer tissues is significantly higher than that in adjacent liver tissues, and high expression of NCOA6 may be a risk factor for poor prognosis in patients with liver cancer [17]. Similarly, recent research has reported that NCOA6 expression is markedly increased in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) tissues, with high NCOA6 levels associated with poorer survival outcomes [16]. Furthermore, fluorescence in situ hybridization analyses have detected NCOA6 amplification in breast cancer specimens, and high NCOA6 mRNA expression has been confirmed in 11 distinct breast cancer cell lines, with BT-474 cells exhibiting the highest expression levels [9]. Although these studies highlight the potential role of NCOA6 in tumorigenesis, the precise relationship between NCOA6 expression and clinical prognosis in breast cancer patients remains unclear. In the present study, pan-cancer and TCGA database analyses revealed that NCOA6 expression is significantly upregulated in breast cancer tissues compared to normal breast tissues. However, our findings indicate that increased NCOA6 expression is not significantly associated with breast cancer patient prognosis.

Phosphorylation is a versatile post-translational modification that is critical for tumor initiation and progression. By modulating protein activity and mediating protein–protein interactions, it regulates key cellular processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, and migration. In cancer cells, dysregulated phosphorylation often leads to oncogene hyperactivation or functional inactivation of tumor suppressors, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis [23]. In addition, protein phosphorylation serves as an essential molecular mechanism for modulating the interactions between transcriptional coactivators and transcription factors, ultimately shaping their capacity to regulate gene expression effectively. AIB1 is an overexpressed transcriptional coactivator in breast cancer that plays a crucial role in the malignant progression of the disease [38, 39]. Additionally, AIB1 is a key contributor to the development of resistance against endocrine therapy, making it a central molecule both in disease advancement and in the challenge of achieving effective treatment [25, 40, 41]. The phosphorylation of AIB1 is essential for its robust transcriptional activation, with multiple phosphorylation sites involved in this regulatory process [25, 42]. Upon phosphorylation by kinases such as MAPK, the coactivator function of AIB1 is enhanced, leading to increased ER activity and promoting cellular proliferation. This process involves the recruitment of additional coactivators, such as p300, thereby amplifying gene expression associated with cell proliferation [43]. Moreover, phosphorylation of AIB1 at serine 543 has been shown to significantly impact its genomic interactions, particularly through the selective recruitment to promoters of ERα-responsive genes at a genome-wide level. This specific phosphorylation event not only serves as a prognostic marker but also as a predictive marker for response to endocrine therapy [44]. In the present study, we found that the phosphorylation levels of NCOA6 at threonine 1749, serine 1750, and serine 1751 were significantly elevated in breast cancer tissues compared to normal breast tissues. This increase in phosphorylation was observed consistently across early (stages I and II) and advanced (stage III) breast cancer tumors and was evident across all breast cancer subtypes, including Luminal, HER2-positive, and TNBC, with a particularly pronounced elevation in ER-positive breast cancers. Previous studies have identified six phosphorylation sites within the NCOA6 protein and have demonstrated a distinct phosphorylation pattern between liver cancer and normal liver tissues. Notably, the phosphorylation status of four out of these six sites was significantly correlated with the prognosis of liver cancer patients, suggesting that phosphorylation may play a crucial role in modulating the function of NCOA6 [17]. Our findings further support the importance of NCOA6 phosphorylation in breast cancer and highlight its potential as a biomarker for disease progression and therapeutic response. Overall, the significance of molecular markers and post-translational modifications, such as phosphorylation, is increasingly recognized in breast cancer research. Bibliometric analyses indicate that current research hotspots include TNBC, along with its immune, metabolic, and prognostic aspects, with particular focus on ubiquitination-related pathways as promising directions for future research and therapeutic development [45]. Moreover, a serum-based panel of five miRNAs has demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity as a non-invasive biomarker for early breast cancer detection [46], while elevated expression of CIAPIN1 and DBNDD1 has been associated with worse prognosis and increased immune infiltration in invasive breast cancer, further underscoring their potential as diagnostic and prognostic markers, as well as therapeutic targets [47, 48]. A recent study using single-cell RNA sequencing of metastatic HR+/HER2- breast cancer identified distinct molecular and immune microenvironment profiles in response to CDK4/6 inhibitors, highlighting tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T and NK cells as potential predictive biomarkers and the importance of considering microenvironmental changes in treatment strategies [49]. These studies collectively reinforce the current research focus on molecular markers, post-translational modifications such as phosphorylation, and immune-related pathways in breast cancer.

Phosphorylation sites are critical components of various signal transduction pathways, serving as key regulatory nodes that modulate cellular responses to external stimuli [50]. The addition of phosphate groups to specific amino acids can induce conformational changes in proteins and alter their interactions, thereby influencing essential cellular processes such as growth, differentiation, and apoptosis. In this context, we focus on a specific phosphorylation event involving serine 884 (S884) on the coactivator NCOA6. NCOA6 is known to interact with a wide array of nuclear receptors in a ligand-dependent manner, enhancing the transcriptional activation mediated by these receptors. Notably, a single LXXLL motif is essential for the interaction of NCOA6 with receptors such as TR, RXR, ER. Ko et al. identified a mutation at the S884 site within the LXXLL motif of NCOA6, which alters the selectivity of NCOA6 for ERα versus ERβ both in vitro and in vivo. This finding suggests that phosphorylation at S884 may enable NCOA6 to selectively modulate the binding affinity between different transcription factors and receptors, thus influencing the specificity of gene transcription through altered coactivator-receptor interactions [5]. However, the impact of NCOA6 phosphorylation at S884 on breast cancer progression has not been thoroughly investigated. In the present study, we assessed the expression of phospho-NCOA6 (Ser884) in breast cancer tissues using IHC. Our results revealed that phospho-NCOA6 is predominantly localized in the nucleus, and its expression is significantly higher in breast cancer tissues compared to adjacent normal breast tissues. Furthermore, we observed a correlation between phospho-NCOA6 expression and key clinicopathological parameters, including tumor size, lymph node metastasis, clinical stage, Ki67 proliferation index, and ERα and PR status. Notably, patients with high levels of phospho-NCOA6 expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival than those with lower expression, indicating its potential prognostic value in breast cancer. Bioinformatics analyses further indicated that three phosphorylation sites on NCOA6 are markedly elevated in ER-positive breast cancer samples. Importantly, high phospho-NCOA6 expression in ER-positive breast cancer was associated with worse overall survival, supporting its candidacy as an independent prognostic biomarker in this subgroup. This association underscores the potential of phospho-NCOA6 as a marker of poor prognosis in ER-positive disease. Recent studies on HCC have shown that increased phosphorylation at NCOA6 sites S1325, S1481, S2018, and S1809 correlates with reduced survival, highlighting a possible regulatory role of these modifications in NCOA6 function and their potential as therapeutic targets in HCC [17].

Our findings highlight the pivotal role of NCOA6 phosphorylation at the S884 site in promoting the malignant phenotype of ER-positive breast cancer. Specifically, we observed that NCOA6 phosphorylation at this residue markedly enhanced the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of ER-positive breast cancer cells, with the phospho-mimetic S884E mutant exhibiting even greater effects than the wild-type protein. In contrast, the phosphorylation-deficient S884F mutant failed to produce similar oncogenic effects. These in vitro observations were further substantiated by in vivo experiments, which demonstrated that S884 phosphorylation significantly accelerates tumor growth. Collectively, these results indicate that the S884 phosphorylation of NCOA6 serves as a crucial regulatory event driving the aggressiveness of ER-positive breast cancer. This mechanism is further supported by evidence from other tumor types. For instance, NCOA6 has been shown to promote the migration and invasion of human placental cells via activation of the NF-κB pathway and increased MMP9 transcription [15]. Likewise, NCOA6 has been implicated in the proliferation, migration, and invasion of pancreatic and HCC cells [16, 17]. Together, these findings suggest that phosphorylation-dependent activation of NCOA6 may represent a common mechanism underpinning tumor progression, and highlight its potential as a prognostic marker and therapeutic target in ER-positive breast cancer.

Previous studies have identified a distinct serine-proline sequence adjacent to the LXXLL motif of NCOA6, specifically at the S884 region, suggesting its potential as a phosphorylation target for MAPK pathways [5]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrates that NCOA6 can indeed be phosphorylated by ERK2 in ER-positive breast cancer cell lines. This modification appears to play a critical role in cancer progression by promoting the EMT, a fundamental mechanism underpinning metastasis. EMT allows epithelial cells, typically characterized by strong cell-cell adhesion and limited mobility, to acquire mesenchymal properties, thereby enhancing their migratory and invasive capacities. GSEA analysis further revealed that high NCOA6 expression is positively correlated with EMT and metastasis-related pathways. Consistently, only the phospho-mimetic NCOA6-S884E mutant increased mesenchymal marker expression and reduced epithelial markers, while the phosphorylation-deficient S884F mutant had no effect. Functional rescue studies confirmed that phosphorylation at S884 is necessary for NCOA6 to promote cell migration and invasion. Overall, our findings suggest that ERK2-mediated phosphorylation of NCOA6 at serine 884 is a key event that drives EMT and enhances metastatic potential in ER-positive breast cancer. Targeting this phosphorylation event could be a promising strategy for limiting metastasis in this cancer subtype.

In this study, our integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses indicate that NCOA6 may play a key role in the progression of ER-positive breast cancer by activating multiple oncogenic signaling pathways. Specifically, GSEA revealed that tumors with high NCOA6 expression are enriched in cell cycle regulation and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, suggesting that NCOA6 facilitates tumor cell proliferation and survival. Our proteomic network analysis further demonstrated that proteins co-expressed with NCOA6 are predominantly involved in protein synthesis, folding, transport, and cellular organization, highlighting that NCOA6 may also enhance the biosynthetic and adaptive capabilities required for tumor growth. Notably, these observations are consistent with previous research in HCC, where high NCOA6 expression was also associated with the activation of malignant signaling pathways, including NOTCH, TGF-β, WNT, PI3K, MYC, and the cell cycle [17]. Collectively, these results suggest that NCOA6 functions as a crucial regulator of oncogenic signaling across different cancer types and may represent a promising therapeutic target in ER-positive breast cancer.

In conclusion, our findings highlight the critical role of phosphorylated NCOA6 in breast cancer, particularly its association with poor survival rates in ER-positive tumors. The specific phosphorylation at Ser884 appears to drive cancer aggressiveness, suggesting that targeting this modification could be key to improving patient outcomes. The observed efficacy of ERK2 inhibitors in reducing phosphorylated NCOA6 levels presents a promising avenue for targeted therapy in ER-positive breast cancer, paving the way for more personalized treatment options and better prognoses for patients.
