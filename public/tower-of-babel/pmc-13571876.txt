Natural language processing-based model to predict radiation pneumonitis in patients with locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer undergoing chemoradiotherapy: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Julie R Bloom, Nicole Tignor, Tielman Van Vleck, Dexter P Mendoza, Sooyun Caroline Tavolacci, Olivia Fankuchen, Glen Chun, Stephanie Chang, Michael Chung, Stephanie Tuminello, Fred R Hirsch, Abraham Chachoua, Joshua K Sabari, Juan Wisnivesky, Ion Cotarla, Daniel Simmons, Kristin Hsieh, Chris Tackaberry, Kenneth E Rosenzweig, Robert M Samstein, Pei Wang, Rajwanth R Veluswamy

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundRadiation pneumonitis (RP) remains a significant treatment-related toxicity in patients with unresectable, locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) undergoing chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Most existing predictive models rely on static baseline demographic or dosimetry variables and lack real-time clinical applicability. We developed a novel predictive framework that integrates longitudinal symptom data extracted from clinical notes using natural language processing (NLP) with clinical and dosimetry features to improve early RP prediction.MethodsWe retrospectively identified 227 patients with locally advanced NSCLC treated with definitive CRT at a high-volume cancer center in the United States. We included all patients older than 18 years who were diagnosed between Jan 1, 2006, and Dec 31, 2022 with histologically or cytologically confirmed unresectable Stage 2 or 3 NSCLC and treated with conformal radiotherapy to a minimum dose of ≥45 Gy with or without chemotherapy. Of these, 31 RP events were identified through manual adjudication using radiologic criteria and chart review. NLP was used to extract the temporal relationship of 16 pre-specified symptoms with treatment from over 100,000 clinical notes spanning pre- and during-treatment intervals. We trained and validated machine learning models on combinations of baseline clinical data, radiation dosimetry, and NLP-derived symptom features. Model performance was evaluated using a nested cross-validation framework, with an outer cross-validation loop reserved for performance assessment and an inner cross-validation loop used for model training and integration, and summarized using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and partial AUC (pAUC) at high specificity thresholds. Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA).FindingsThe best-performing model incorporated longitudinal NLP features and achieved a median AUC of 0.759 (90% confidence interval 0.753–0.766), significantly outperforming baseline models using only dosimetry (AUC 0.613) or clinical variables (AUC 0.635). NLP-based features such as cough trajectory, shortness of breath, and wheezing were among the most important predictors. Inclusion of NLP-derived symptom data improved early identification of high-risk patients, particularly in the clinically relevant high-specificity range (pAUC 0.021 vs. 0.010 for dosimetry alone). DCA showed that the calibrated MLP model provided greater net benefit than default strategies of treating all or no patients across clinically relevant threshold possibilities.InterpretationIn this early work, NLP-based extraction of longitudinal symptoms from routine clinical documentation meaningfully enhances RP prediction in patients undergoing CRT for NSCLC. This approach leverages existing electronic health record infrastructure to deliver real-time, scalable, and interpretable risk estimates, offering a pathway toward potential early intervention and personalized toxicity management. The model and DCA requires external and prospective validation before clinical deployment; as such, future work should focus on this validation and integration into clinical decision support systems.FundingAstraZeneca.Keywords: Radiation pneumonitis, Natural language processing, Non-small cell lung cancer, Machine learning, Chemoradiotherapy

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Radiation pneumonitis is a clinically important toxicity following chemoradiotherapy for non-small cell lung cancer, with prior prediction models largely based on static clinical and dosimetry factors and demonstrating limited and inconsistent performance. We searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Google Scholar from database inception to May 2025 using terms including ‘radiation pneumonitis’, ‘non-small cell lung cancer’, ‘chemoradiotherapy’, ‘natural language processing’, and ‘machine learning. Existing studies rarely incorporated longitudinal symptom information and, to our knowledge, none used natural language processing of routine clinical documentation to extract treatment-aligned symptom patterns for early risk prediction.

Added value of this study

This study provides the first demonstration that natural language processing of routine clinical documentation can be used to extract treatment-aligned symptom information and meaningfully enhance early prediction of radiation pneumonitis beyond traditional clinical and dosimetric factors. By capturing the temporal relationship of symptoms with treatment initiation, this approach enables risk stratification at a clinically relevant early stage, using data already embedded in the electronic health record. Although further study and validation is needed, our findings demonstrate feasibility and potential value of incorporating NLP-derived, treatment-aligned symptom information into risk prediction. The value lies in demonstrating that this approach is scalable, requires no changes to clinical workflow, and represents a novel application of NLP for symptom-based risk stratification in oncology toxicity management.

Implications of all the available evidence

Collectively, existing evidence and our findings suggest that early symptom information represents an underutilized but clinically informative signal for predicting radiation pneumonitis. Integrating NLP-derived symptom features into risk models may enable earlier identification of high-risk patients, supporting more personalized surveillance, supportive care, and treatment decision-making. While external validation and prospective evaluation are required, this work provides a foundation for symptom-informed prediction models that could be incorporated into learning health systems and extended to contemporary chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy populations.

Introduction

Approximately 20–35% of patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) are diagnosed with locally advanced disease that is not amenable to surgical resection. The current standard treatment for these patients includes concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) with consolidation immunotherapy (IO) after demonstrating improved survival in phase 3 trials.1,2 However, the effectiveness of this approach is frequently limited by treatment-related toxicities, most notably radiation pneumonitis (RP) which affects 15–40% of patients following CRT.3,4 RP can further delay or preclude IO, thereby complicating treatment decisions and diminishing potential therapeutic benefit. Accurate early identification of patients at high risk for RP prior to initiating IO could enable proactive interventions, closer monitoring and improve clinical outcomes.

Existing predictive tools for RP remain limited in accuracy and clinical utility. Prior studies have primarily focused on clinical and dosimetric features. Among clinical factors, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), smoking history, poor baseline pulmonary function, and advanced age have been variably associated with increased RP risk.5,6 However, these factors lack sufficient discriminatory power for individual risk stratification. Radiation dosimetry parameters remain the most established predictors, particularly the volume of lung receiving ≥20 Gy (V20) and the mean lung dose (MLD), which show a dose-dependent correlation with RP risk.7 Accordingly, dose constraints such as limiting lung V20 to ≤35% and MLD to ≤20 Gy are commonly employed. Nevertheless, even with stringent adherence to dosimetric constraints, RP rates remain relatively common, indicating that radiation dose alone does not fully account for individual susceptibility to RP. Taken together, these gaps highlight the need for novel, dynamic clinical markers of RP risk that go beyond static baseline characteristics and radiation parameters.

One promising but underexplored approach is using the timing of symptom occurrence in relation to treatment to predict RP. Emerging data suggest that patient-reported symptom monitoring while on lung cancer treatment significantly improves survival outcomes by enabling earlier detection of disease progression, allowing for earlier intervention or changes to effective treatment.8, 9, 10 Other studies have demonstrated this approach to be feasible for toxicity management in lung cancer patients receiving chemotherapy, even among patients with baseline high symptom burden from their disease.11,12 While patient-reported symptoms are now routinely collected in prospective studies, analysis in retrospective studies is challenging as these data are often recorded as unstructured data in clinical notes. Natural language processing (NLP) offers a solution by extracting symptom data from narrative clinical documentation across multiple time points. To date, no study has integrated NLP-derived longitudinal patient reported symptoms to develop predictive models for clinical outcomes in NSCLC patients.

In this study, we aimed to apply NLP to extract the temporal relationship of clinical symptoms with treatment from clinical notes and integrate these data with clinical and radiation variables to develop machine learning models to predict RP in patients with unresectable locally advanced NSCLC treated with definitive CRT.

Methods

Study design and ethics

In this cohort study, we retrospectively identified patients with locally advanced NSCLC treated with definitive CRT at a high-volume cancer center in the United States with the aim of developing a natural language processing-based model to predict radiation pneumonitis in patients with locally advanced NSCLC undergoing chemoradiotherapy. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with TRIPOD guidelines for prediction model development.13

Regarding formal ethics review, the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board considered the study exempt due to the retrospective nature of this study. The requirement for written informed consent from participants was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Participants

Using a single institution database (Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, USA), we included all patients older than 18 years who were diagnosed between Jan 1, 2006, and Dec 31, 2022 with histologically or cytologically confirmed unresectable Stage 2 or 3 NSCLC (American Joint Committee on Cancer [AJCC] version 8) and treated with conformal radiotherapy to a minimum dose of ≥45 Gy with or without chemotherapy. All patients had radiologic assessment with computer tomography (CT) at least three months prior to radiotherapy and up to one year post radiotherapy for assessment of pneumonitis. Patients were excluded if they received consolidation immunotherapy, as our study is specifically intended to analyze pneumonitis risk from radiation. Patients were also excluded if they had a concomitant diagnosis of immunodeficiency or any conditions requiring systemic steroids or any other form of immunosuppressive therapy (i.e., autoimmune disease, organ transplant); were receiving any other cancer treatment other than radiation and chemotherapy; or had a history of interstitial lung disease or active, noninfectious pneumonitis.

For each patient, we then collected clinical data, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) using NLP, and dosimetry data to create risk classifiers for RP (see Fig. 1). Notably, all predictors of RP entered into the risk classifier prediction model discretely ended when treatment was completed—all PROs recorded after treatment completion and associated with RP were excluded.

Clinical data

For each patient, we obtained baseline sociodemographic data (i.e., age, sex, race and ethnicity), smoking status, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities and ECOG performance status within 3 months of treatment initiation. Baseline vital signs (temperature, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, and respiratory rate) were obtained within the Pre-treatment period. We also collected information about each patient’s tumor characteristics, including histology, stage (tumor size, lymph node stations), location and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) status.

Natural language processing for longitudinal symptom monitoring

In order to collect longitudinal clinical symptoms experienced by study patients starting 3 months before and up to the 6 weeks during CRT, we used NLP analysis to abstract clinical narrative free text written in a number of clinical interactions and documentations such as clinic progress notes, hospitalization notes, radiology reports, etc. We utilized the CLiX NLP engine (Clinithink, Alpharetta GA) to review all clinical notes found in the EMR for each study subject.14 The NLP software matches free text to fully post-coordinated Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine (SNOMED CT), a comprehensive healthcare terminology resource which exploit the graph-like structure of the SNOMED ontology (SNOMED CT compositional grammar—Specification and guide [2022]).15

CLiX NLP has been shown in previous studies to identify common abbreviations (i.e., N/V for nausea and/or vomiting), typographical errors and sentence context.16 For negation, the algorithm identifies terms such as “no”, “denies”, not” as negative and applies it to the SNOMED CT thereby marking the query as “present” or “absent”. Furthermore, CLiX NLP was able to assess changes in symptoms by identifying terms such as “new”, “worsening”, “resolving” and assigning them to create a longitudinal timeline of SNOMED CT terminology during treatment. In this way, all symptoms were categorized as present: 1) pre-treatment only; 2) after treatment initiation only; 3) absent altogether; or 4) always present. In total, 47 NLP-based clinical features from 16 features were included in the model.

Symptoms were chosen by expert consensus of medical oncologists (RV, AC, JS, FH), radiation oncologists (JB, RS, KH), pulmonologists (JW, GC) and radiologists (DM, GC) who routinely manage NSCLC patients undergoing CRT. CLiX enables traceability of extracted SNOMED CT concepts to the original source text within clinical notes. Using this functionality, an expert consensus team assessed NLP extraction performance in this cohort through manual review of a sample of clinical notes for key pulmonary symptoms (cough, dyspnea, wheeze, fever), with explicit attention to negation and temporality. NLP queries were iteratively refined through expert-in-the-loop review until extracted concepts consistently matched clinician interpretation of the source documentation across more than 10 consecutive evaluated note instances for each symptom.

Treatment exposure and dosimetry

We determined if study subjects underwent CRT from treatment plan archives and clinical records. We then extracted radiation treatment planning data with retrievable dosimetric parameters, including prescription dose (in centigray [cGy]), fraction size, mean lung dose (MLD), volume of normal lung tissue receiving more than x Gy (Vx values), and minimum dose to the hottest x% or xcc of lung volume (Dx values). In all, 53 radiation parameters were collected from each patient’s radiation treatment plan (see Supplementary Fig. S1).

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint of the study was RP following CRT completion as defined using clinical assessments by the treating oncologist and/or pulmonologist according to clinical documentation in the electronic medical record (EMR). Patients must have been clearly described as having “radiation pneumonitis”, have supporting imaging findings, and a management plan implemented (i.e., steroids, oxygen supplementation) for the designation. Common terminology criteria for adverse events (CTCAE) v6.0 was used to grade RP.17 All charts were manually reviewed independently by two trained clinical physicians (JB and ST) and imaging assessed by a board-certified radiologist (DM); disagreements were resolved based on input from a third trained physician (RV). All reviewers were blinded to the NLP and dosimetry data.

Statistical analysis

Feature evaluation and engineering

For univariable analyses assessing associations between RP occurrence and baseline clinical and dosimetric variables, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for continuous variables, and the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s Exact test was used for categorical variables. To develop predictive models for RP, we integrated features derived from NLP, clinical, and dosimetric data collected across the Pre-treatment and Treatment periods (Fig. 1), with RP status defined during the Post treatment period. Categorical variables were one-hot encoded (by creating a binary indicator for each category).

For each feature space defined by combinations of data modalities (e.g., NLP or NLP + demographic) considered for prediction model building, multicollinearity was reduced by aggregating highly correlated features. Pairwise Pearson correlations were computed and hierarchical clustering was applied; features within clusters exceeding a correlation threshold (r > 0.90) were aggregated using their mean. Dosimetric features exhibited substantial collinearity, reducing dimensionality from 53 to 15, whereas NLP features showed minimal correlation (no pairwise r > 0.90 among 47 features). For visualization, a single representative feature per cluster was selected; aggregated features used in modeling are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Ensemble machine learning models for predicting RP

We trained and evaluated ensemble machine learning models using multi-modality data with the ensembleIntegration package in Python. This package supports multimodal ensemble learning through nested cross-validation, using an inner 5-fold cross-validation for determining tuning parameters and a repeated random outer 5-fold cross-validation scheme to evaluate model performance (Fig. 2). Model building was reproducible via a fixed random seed (random_state = 38), and performance was evaluated using predefined metrics (AUC and pAUC).

Schematic of the repeated nested cross-validation workflow used for multimodal ensemble learning.

Model development (training)

The following classifiers were employed via the ensembleIntegration package in Python, which facilitates multimodal ensemble learning through nested cross-validation: 1) Tree-based methods: Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), AdaBoost (ADAB), and XGBoost (XGB); 2) Linear models: Logistic Regression (LR); 3) Distance-based: K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN); 4) Probabilistic: Gaussian Naive Bayes (GN); 5) Neural networks: Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP); and 6) Margin-based: Support Vector Machine (SVM). We implemented several ensemble learning strategies to combine data modalities, including: 1) Averaging predictions across models trained on individual modalities; 2) Model stacking, wherein a meta-classifier (MLP) was trained on base model predictions; and 3) Nested cross-validation integration (Ensemble Integration, EI) (Fig. 2) to minimize overfitting.18 A 5-fold inner cross-validation scheme was applied within the training data to determine the ensemble weights.

Model validation (testing)

Performance was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and partial AUC (pAUC) on the holdout testing data. While the traditional AUC provides a global summary of classifier performance, the pAUC offers a more granular evaluation by focusing on a specific range of specificities. By restricting the integration of the ROC curve to a low false positive rate (high specificity) range, pAUC aligns the performance metric with the constraints of clinical application. Specifically, we evaluated pAUC by focusing on the range of specificity ≥0.9. This targeted approach facilitated a performance evaluation customized to the clinical requirements of pneumonitis prediction. Ninety percent confidence intervals (90% CI) of AUC and pAUC were estimated via bootstrapping. To address class imbalance, undersampling of the majority class was applied within each training fold.

To evaluate the top features’ contribution to the performance of our best-performing NLP models, we applied permutation importance.19 Within the MLP framework, we permuted each feature N times by sampling replacement values from its marginal distribution and measured the resulting change in performance (here, the maximum F1 score on the minority class). We then defined a feature’s local importance as the mean performance change across those N permutations, and its uncertainty as the corresponding standard deviation. To aid interpretation of the MLP model and assess the direction of associations, we evaluated univariable relationships between individual features and pneumonitis using logistic regression. As a conservative strategy, we identified candidate risk factors only when features demonstrated both high predictive importance in the multivariable model and statistically significant associations in the logistic regression.

Prediction calibration and decision curve analysis

Predicted probabilities from the multilayer perceptron (MLP) model were calibrated to account for differences between the prevalence observed in the study cohort and potential target clinical populations. Calibration was performed using a logit-shift approach, in which predicted probabilities were transformed to the log-odds scale and adjusted by a constant offset to align the mean predicted probability with a target prevalence of 30%. This target was selected to reflect potential application in higher-risk clinical settings.

Clinical utility was evaluated using decision curve analysis (DCA) implemented with the rmda R package.20,21 Net benefit was calculated across threshold probabilities from 0 to 0.6 using the calibrated predicted probabilities. Ninety-five percent confidence intervals were estimated using bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations). Decision curves were generated to compare the net benefit of the model with default strategies of treating all or treating no patients.

Results

After applying the eligibility criteria to the institutional database of unresectable locally advanced lung cancer patients (n = 774 patients), the final study cohort consisted of 227 patients who received definitive CRT and had complete clinical and dosimetry data available (See Supplementary Fig. S2). Among them, 31 (13.7%) patients experienced RP. The median time from the end of radiation therapy to RP diagnosis with imaging findings was 80 days (IQR 59.5, 124.5). Of these patients, 25.8% (n = 8) experienced grade 1 RP, 58.1% (n = 18) were grade 2, and 16.1% (n = 5) were grade 3. No patients experienced grade 4 or 5 RP. Patients who experienced RP were older (p = 0.0027), but otherwise had similar clinical characteristics and dosimetry parameters than those that did not have RP (p > 0.05 for all comparisons; see Table 1).

Of all the machine learning models trained and evaluated (see Methods section), the best-performing model involved stacking NLP and demographic data using MLP, a type of neural network (AUC: 0.759, 90% CI: 0.753–0.766; pAUC: 0.021; 90%CI: 0.021–0.023). This model consistently outperformed all other configurations with superior AUC performance (see Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S3). Given the limited number of RP events, we performed additional analyses using more parsimonious models (e.g., ridge-penalized logistic regression [LR]) and found consistent results combining NLP and demographic data (AUC: 0.752, 90% CI: 0.746–0.759; pAUC: 0.0232; 90%CI: 0.022–0.024; See Supplementary Table S2 for all LR models). When comparing feature importance scores from our best-performing model (i.e., MLP stacking NLP and demographic data) to the odds ratios derived from univariable logistic regression (see Fig. 5), we found that the emergence of cough, fever, shortness of breath and wheezing after treatment initiation were strong predictors in the MLP model and significant univariable risk factors for pneumonitis. Several other features did demonstrate high local importance scores, signifying their contribution to the model prediction; however, they did not show a statistically significant univariable association. This finding likely reflected complex interaction effects or contextual information among NLP predictors captured by the MLP model, which utilizes a modern multi-layer neural network structure with nonlinear functions.22

Discussion

In this study, we developed and evaluated novel machine learning models to predict RP in patients with unresectable, locally advanced NSCLC treated with definitive CRT. Our approach uniquely integrated longitudinal patient symptoms extracted through NLP, along with baseline clinical characteristics and radiation dosimetry data. We found that NLP-derived symptom significantly improved model performance over models using clinical or dosimetric features alone. These results suggest that dynamic symptom monitoring offers valuable and previously underutilized signals for early identification of patients at elevated risk for RP.

Prior efforts to predict RP have largely focused on baseline clinical factors and radiation dosimetry. Dose-volume histogram (DVH) metrics, particularly lung V20 and mean lung dose, have been the most established predictors.5,7 Reported models using dosimetry alone have achieved AUCs in the range of 0.60–0.70, reflecting modest discrimination.23 Clinical risk factors such as older age, female sex, poor baseline pulmonary function, COPD, and interstitial lung disease have been variably associated with RP but show inconsistent predictive value across studies and generally low standalone AUCs (∼0.55–0.70).24, 25, 26, 27 The baseline dosimetry-only and demographic-only model performance we observed (AUC: 0.613 and AUC: 0.635, respectively) aligns with these prior reports, confirming that our baseline models are realistic and representative. Importantly, these prior approaches rely on static pre-treatment variables and do not capture dynamic clinical changes that may signal evolving pulmonary toxicity. Our study addresses this critical gap by integrating the temporal relationship of clinical symptoms with treatment to provide a dynamic approach for real-time risk stratification, achieving a superior performance (AUC: 0.759) compared to existing tools. Additionally, the NLP-enhanced model demonstrated a meaningful improvement in partial AUC (pAUC) within the high-specificity range compared to the dosimetry-only baseline model (0.021 vs. 0.010). This reflects the model’s enhanced ability to correctly identify high-risk patients while minimizing false positives—an essential characteristic for clinical tools aimed at guiding early intervention and avoiding overtreatment.

We also found the models incorporating NLP-derived symptoms during treatment outperformed its baseline counterpart, which suggests that the evolution of symptoms during treatment (rather than their presence at baseline alone) provides critical signals of impending radiation pneumonitis. Our models identified several symptoms, including cough, shortness of breath, fever, and wheezing, as key risk factors of RP. The emergence and/or worsening of these symptoms after treatment initiation aligned with the known clinical course of RP, where symptoms preceded radiographic changes. Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the model provides clinical net benefit across a range of plausible decision thresholds, supporting its potential utility for risk-guided surveillance or early intervention. Automated symptom monitoring using NLP could enable proactive risk stratification for RP during and after CRT, allowing clinicians to tailor surveillance and management strategies. For instance, patients identified as high-risk could undergo more frequent imaging or pulmonary evaluations, or receive early interventions such as steroids or fibrinolytics, if shown to be effective in this setting. Furthermore, if validated, this approach could be seamlessly implemented into EHR workflows, providing real-time decision support without additional burden on clinicians or patients.

Our study has several key strengths. First, we address a highly relevant clinical problem using a novel methodology–NLP-based extraction of longitudinal symptom patterns from routine clinical documentation–a scalable and practical approach that has the potential for integration into existing workflows. While direct symptom data collection practices tailored to RP prediction may improve signal quality and potentially enhance model performance, the NLP-based framework enables real-time deployment without imposing additional documentation burden on clinicians. Future implementations may benefit from hybrid approaches integrating both. Second, our machine learning framework was rigorously developed, employing multiple model architectures, robust cross-validation, and ensemble learning to enhance predictive performance. Notably, RP outcomes were manually adjudicated through comprehensive chart review and radiologic confirmation, addressing a major limitation of many prior retrospective studies. Finally, our methodology may have broader applicability to predicting other treatment-related toxicities in oncology, where dynamic symptom patterns remain underutilized.

This study also has limitations. It was conducted at a single institution, which may limit generalizability; external validation in multi-institutional datasets is needed. The modest number of RP events relative to the number of candidate predictors may increase the risk of overfitting. To mitigate this, we employed multicollinearity reduction, regularization, and fully nested cross-validation; however, these methods do not completely eliminate this limitation and external validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts will be essential to establish robustness across diverse patient populations. Our study also excluded patients receiving consolidation immunotherapy to isolate RP attributable to radiation; while prediction of RP remains clinically important to current CRT-IO treatment paradigms, extension of this framework will require retraining and revalidation, as immune-related pneumonitis has distinct underlying mechanisms, timing of onset, and clinical presentation. This study did not perform formal testing for the NLP performance, but rather iterative refinements via expert-in-the-loop approach—future work should include systematic annotation and quantitative evaluation of NLP performance. Additionally, symptom extraction relied on documentation within clinical notes, which may introduce reporting bias. Practice changes have evolved over time so that, more recently, clinical symptoms may be more heavily documented; given the long time period over which this study took place, this is a potential limitation. Prospective incorporation of structured patient-reported outcomes (PROs) could further enhance predictive performance.

In conclusion, NLP-derived longitudinal symptom data could substantially improve prediction of RP in NSCLC patients receiving CRT. This approach offers a practical and scalable means of enhancing clinical decision-making using data already captured in routine care. Competing exploratory approaches evaluating biomarkers (e.g., cytokines, genetic polymorphisms, radiomics) to predict RP have shown promise, but they are often limited by cost, data availability, and integration challenges.28 In contrast, our framework is directly implementable within EHR systems and could inform real-time risk stratification and personalized mitigation strategies. Future work will focus on external validation, extension to CRT-IO cohorts, and possible incorporation into future multimodal models (i.e., combined with imaging biomarkers) to evaluate whether early interventions informed by NLP-based risk models can improve patient outcomes.
