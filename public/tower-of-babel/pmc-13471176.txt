Anemia among children (6 To 59 months) in Qoloji internally displaced people camp, Somali Region, eastern Ethiopia
Authors: Umar Sead Hussein, Barwako Ibrahim Aden, Zerihun Ataro, Eydrus Mohammed Sherif, Habtamu Mitiku
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnemia remains a significant public health issue, especially in emergency areas and refugee camps. This study was conducted to determine the prevalence of anemia and its associated factors among children aged 6 to 59 months in Qoloji internally displaced people camp site, Somali Region, eastern Ethiopia.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 401 children aged 6 to 59 months. Capillary blood was collected and hemoglobin level was determined using HemoCue Hb 301. Data was analyzed using SPSS version 26. Bivariable and multivariable logistic regression were used to identify determinants of anemia.ResultsThe overall prevalence of anemia was found to be 51.4% (95% CI: 46.6%, 56.4%). Of the anemic children 29.4% (95% CI: 25.2%, 33.9%), 16.7% (95% CI: 13.2%, 20.7%) and 5.2% (95%CI: 3.2%, 7.5%) had mild, moderate, and severe anemia respectively. Initiation of complementary feeding below 6 months (AOR = 2.6;95% CI:1.5,4.6), household food insecurity (AOR = 5.4;95% CI:2.8,10.2), mothers of child having no formal education (AOR = 2.8; 95% CI: 1.3, 5.7), inadequate dietary diversity (AOR = 7.9; 95%CI:4.4, 13.9) and lack of antenatal care follow up during pregnancy (AOR = 3.5;95% CI:1.8, 6.7) were found associated with of anemia.ConclusionThe prevalence of anemia was high among children. Household food insecurity, dietary diversity, initiation of complementary feeding, maternal educational status and antenatal care during pregnancy were factors found associated with anemia. Therefore, adequate interventions should be designed and implemented considering improvement of household food security, access to maternal education and dissemination of nutrition and health related information.

Introduction

Anemia is one of the most common nutritional deficiencies in low and middle-income nations, carrying major health consequences [1, 2]. About 25% of people worldwide suffer from anemia, a serious public health issue characterized by low hemoglobin levels in red blood cells (RBCs). Anemia affects an estimated 273 million children under the age of five worldwide, with a startling prevalence of over 60.2% among children in Africa between the ages of 6 and 59 months [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa, in particular, sees a significant number of childhood anemia cases, with Ethiopia reporting a prevalence of 44.83% among children under five, especially affecting those who are two years old or younger [4]. According to the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), a striking 57% of children aged 6 to 59 months in Ethiopia were found to be anemic, highlighting the high rates of this condition in the population [5].

Children with anemia may experience severe consequences, including recurrent infections, increased rates of disease and mortality, and long-term developmental difficulties [3, 6]. Anemia can result from a number of conditions, including intestinal parasites, malaria, inadequate nutrition, and long-term medical conditions such sickle cell disease [5]. Additionally, socioeconomic factors, including low income and maternal health problems, significantly contribute to the prevalence of anemia in children. Certain groups, especially young children, adolescent girls, pregnant women, and those who are malnourished, are particularly at risk for developing anemia [7].

Internally displaced populations face some of the toughest challenges, often struggling with disrupted livelihoods, poor living conditions, and inadequate sanitation, limited access to diverse diets, and restricted health and nutrition services. Children between 6 and 59 months are particularly vulnerable due to their rapid growth, higher micronutrient needs, and greater risk of infections [8, 9]. A nutrition survey carried out between 2013 and 2016, looked at children aged 6 to 59 months across 121 refugee settings in 24 countries (n = 196 surveys). The findings revealed a median anemia prevalence of 44% among the children surveyed [9]. In a similar vein, cross-sectional studies focusing on children aged 6 to 59 months living in internally displaced People (IDP) camps in Somalia and Mozambique reported anemia rates of 17.6% and a staggering 83%, respectively [10, 11]. In Ethiopia, research on internally displaced children showed anemia prevalence of 33.62% in Northwest Ethiopia and 52.4% in eastern Ethiopia [12, 13].

Our knowledge of the prevalence and causes of anemia in children under five in the Qoloji IDP camp in the Somali Region of eastern Ethiopia is still lacking, despite the fact that we acknowledge that displaced groups face serious hazards. Policymakers, humanitarian groups, and health planners find it challenging to create focused, evidence-based solutions in the absence of locally collected data. The purpose of this study was to investigate the prevalence of anemia and the factors that contribute to it in children in the Qoloji IDP camp who were between the ages of 6 and 59 months. In order to reduce anemia-related health problems and deaths in this very vulnerable population, the results will offer vital epidemiological insights that can guide resource allocation, educate public health policies, and help define nutritional interventions.

Method and materials

Study design, setting and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among children aged 6 to 59 months at the Qoloji IDP Camp in the Somali Region, eastern Ethiopia, from February 1 to March 30, 2024. The camp is one of the largest sites in the country hosting internally displaced persons. It is located approximately 60 km west of Jijiga. Most of the internally displaced persons were displaced from the west Hararghe Zone of the Oromia Region, particularly from the districts (woredas) of Hawi Gudina, Daro Lebu, and Burqa Dhintu. According to the International Organization for Migration (IOM) and their Displacement Tracking Matrix (DTM), the Qoloji IDP settlement has seen significant growth from 2016 to 2020, expanding at an estimated rate of about 0.19 km² each year. Data from DTM Round 21 shows that the settlement is home to roughly 12,834 households, which together house around 79,148 individuals. Notably, over 6,000 of these households have children aged between 6 and 59 months, pointing to a considerable number of nutritionally vulnerable children under five living in the camp. Qoloji I, which was established in 2016, has a shelter density of 5,230 shelters per square kilometer and is administratively divided into 18 kebeles. Qoloji II, opened in 2017, has a higher shelter density of 7,742 shelters per square kilometer and is divided into 19 kebeles (IOM DTM data).

Sampling size determination and sampling procedure

A sample size of 421 was obtained by applying a single population proportion formula, accounting for 52.4% from an earlier study [13], assuming a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error (d), and adding 10% for non-response.

In this study, a simple random sampling technique was used to select households with children aged 6 to 59 months. At the study site, Qoloji IDP Camp, approximately 6,000 households were identified as having at least one child within this age range. After identifying the source population, a sampling frame of eligible households was prepared using a registry obtained from the Somali Disaster Risk Management Office. Each household was assigned a unique identification number from 1 to 6,000. A computer-generated random number method was then used to select the required sample of 421 households. If a selected household had more than one child aged 6 to 59 months, one child was chosen using a lottery method to participate in the study.

Children with serious medical or surgical disorders, significant bleeding, taking iron and vitamin supplements over the previous three months, and getting blood during the previous three months prior to data collection were not allowed to participate in the study.

Data collection

Questionnaires survey

Pre-prepared and pretested questionnaires written in the Somali language were distributed to the data collectors by the supervisors and principal investigators. The data collectors first explained the purpose and significance of the study to the parents or guardians of the children and obtained informed consent for both their own participation and that of their children before proceeding with data collection. Parents or guardians who were willing and able to read and write completed the questionnaire independently. Those who were unable to read, understand, or write were assisted by the data collectors in completing the questionnaire through an interview. The questionnaire included items on household socio-demographic and economic characteristics, household food security status (HHFSS), and child feeding practices.

Household food insecurity

Household food insecurity was determined by using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) developed by Food and Nutritional Technical Assistance (FANTA) [14]. The HFIAS tool has nine occurrence questions that represents an increasing level of severity of food insecurity (access) and nine ‘repetitiveness of occurrence’ questions that were asked as a follow-up for each occurrence question to determine how often the condition occurred during the last 4 weeks. The frequency of occurrence of an event was ranked as ‘ rarely’ [1], ‘ sometimes’ [2] and ‘ often’ [3]. Scores to the answer of each question were summed to create a household food security score, with a minimum score of ‘0’ and a maximum score of ‘27’. The higher the score, the more food insecurity the household experience, and the lower the score, the less food insecurity the household experiences. Households with a HFIAS score of 0–1 were categorized as food secure while those with score ≥ 2 were considered as food insecure [15].

Child meal frequency and dietary diversity score

Child meal frequency and dietary diversity scores were assessed using the World Health Organization (WHO)

Infant and Young Child Feeding (IYCF) indicators, with minor modifications to suit the local context. The assessment was based on a 24-hour dietary recall, in which parents or caregivers reported all foods consumed by the child, as well as the number of times the child ate solid, semi-solid, or soft foods during the previous 24 h [16].

For children aged 6–23 months, the Minimum Meal Frequency (MMF) indicator was assessed according to WHO IYCF guidelines. Accordingly, breastfed children aged 6–8 months were considered to have met the minimum meal frequency if they received solid, semi-solid, or soft foods at least two times in the previous 24 h. Breastfed children aged 9–23 months were considered to have met the minimum meal frequency if they received solid, semi-solid, or soft foods at least three times in the previous 24 h. Non-breastfed children aged 6–23 months were considered to have met the minimum meal frequency if they received solid, semi-solid, or soft foods and milk feeds at least four times in the previous 24 h. For children aged 24–59 months, meal frequency was operationalized as receiving at least three main meals per day, with or without additional snacks, during the 24 h preceding the survey. The variable was coded as: MMF = 1 meets minimum meal frequency and 0 does not meet minimum meal frequency [16].

Dietary Diversity Score (DDS) was defined as the number of food groups consumed by a child aged 6–23 months during the 24 h preceding the survey interview, assessed using a caregiver-reported 24-hour dietary recall method. According to the WHO and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) IYCF guidelines [17], dietary diversity was evaluated based on eight food groups: breast milk; grains, roots, tubers, and plantains; legumes and nuts; dairy products; flesh foods (meat, fish, poultry, and organ meats); eggs; vitamin A–rich fruits and vegetables; and other fruits and vegetables. Each food group consumed was assigned a score of “1,” while food groups not consumed were scored as “0,” resulting in a total DDS ranging from 0 to 8. A child was considered to have adequate dietary diversity if he or she consumed foods from at least five of the eight food groups during the previous 24 h, and inadequate dietary diversity if foods were consumed from fewer than five of the eight food groups during the same period.

For children aged 24–59 months, the DDS was calculated as the total number of food groups consumed from seven standard food groups during the 24 h preceding the survey. The seven food groups included grains, roots, and tubers; legumes and nuts; dairy products (milk, yogurt, and cheese); flesh foods (meat, fish, poultry, and liver/organ meats); eggs; vitamin A rich fruits and vegetables; and other fruits and vegetables. Each food group consumed was assigned one point, resulting in a total score ranging from 0 to 7. Dietary diversity was considered adequate if the child consumed four or more of the seven food groups during the reference period, and inadequate if the child consumed fewer than four food groups [17].

Anthropometry

Children aged 6–23 months had their length measured in a recumbent (lying) position, whereas children aged 24–59 months had their height measured in a standing position, ensuring that the head, shoulders, buttocks, knees, and heels touched the vertical board. Body weight was measured twice using a Seca electronic platform scale with a capacity of 150 kg and a precision of 50 g. Height was measured using a portable, locally manufactured wooden stadiometer with a sliding headpiece, accurate to the nearest 0.1 cm. For children whose length was measured, the same stadiometer was placed horizontally. For children under two years of age, both weight and length were measured in a recumbent position, while for those older than two years, weight and height were measured in a standing position. Height or length was measured twice by the same assessor, allowing a maximum difference of 0.5 cm between the two measurements. The average of the two readings was calculated and used for analysis.

A child was classified as stunted if their length-for-age Z-score was less than − 2 standard deviations, and as not stunted if the Z-score was greater than or equal to − 2 standard deviations. A child was considered wasted if their weight-for-length Z-score was less than − 2 standard deviations, and not wasted if the Z-score was greater than or equal to − 2 standard deviations. Similarly, a child was classified as underweight if their weight-for-age Z-score was less than − 2 standard deviations, and not underweight if the Z-score was greater than or equal to − 2 standard deviations [18].

Determination of anemia: Anemia was assessed by measuring hemoglobin levels in blood samples using a portable, battery-operated HemoCue Hb 301 analyzer, manufactured by Danaher Corporation. Capillary blood samples were collected via finger prick for older children and heel prick for children aged 6–11 months. The selected site was first disinfected with 70% ethanol, then pricked with a disposable lancet. A drop of blood was collected on a microcuvette, which was subsequently inserted into the HemoCue Hb 301 for measurement and recording by trained nurses. After adjusting hemoglobin values for relevant factors, anemia severity was classified according to World Health Organization guidelines: children with hemoglobin ≥ 11 g/dL were considered non-anemic, those with 10.0–10.9 g/dL had mild anemia, 7.0–9.9 g/dL indicated moderate anemia, and < 7.0 g/dL indicated severe anemia [1].

Parasitological examination

Fresh stool samples were collected from each study participant following standard operating procedures (SOPs) in clean, labeled, leak-proof stool cups. The samples were initially examined on-site using direct saline wet mount preparation to microscopically detect trophozoites of protozoan parasites. The remaining stool specimens were preserved in 10% formalin and transported in screw-capped containers to the laboratory at Jigjiga University, Department of Medical Laboratory Science. Within 4–8 h of collection, these samples were processed using the formal-ether concentration method to identify intestinal helminths and cysts of protozoan parasites through microscopic examination. Children presenting with fever were also screened for malaria parasites. Thick and thin blood smears were prepared, stained with 10% Giemsa, and examined microscopically for the presence of malaria parasites. Malaria was considered negative when no plasmodium parasites were detected in thick blood films after examination of 100 high-power fields using the ×100 oil immersion objective. Then it is considered positive when plasmodium parasite was detected in the examined fields.

Data analysis procedure

All collected data were coded, cleaned, and entered into EpiData version 3.1, then analyzed using SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were performed to generate summary measures, which were presented in tables and text. Normality testing was carried out using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables that were normally distributed were summarized using the mean, whereas variables that were not normally distributed were summarized using the median. Variables with p < 0.25 in the bivariable binary logistic regression analysis were selected as candidates for multivariable logistic regression analysis. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to estimate adjusted odds ratios (AOR). Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test, and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF). In the multivariable analysis, variables with a p-value < 0.05 and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were considered to be significantly associated with anemia. The strength of these associations was expressed using the AOR.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 421 children aged 6–59 months were initially recruited for the study. Of these, 401 children (95.2%) fully participated and provided complete data for the analysis. Among the participants, 237 (59.1%) were female. All parents or caregivers of the children were residing in the Qoloji IDP camp. The mean (± SD) age of the children was 28.6 (± 0.7) months, while the mean (± SD) age of their mothers or caregivers was 30.20 (± 0.3) years. Regarding formal education, 318 (79.4%) of the mothers or caregivers had not received any formal schooling (Table 1).

Nutrition status, dietary characteristics and intestinal parasite infection

The mean (± SD) height and weight of the children were 84.73 ± 14.0 cm and 11.1 ± 2.9 kg, respectively. Regarding the nutritional status of children aged 6–59 months at the study site, 81 (20.2%) were underweight, 39 (9.7%) were stunted, and 90 (22.4%) were wasted. In addition, 241 (60.1%) of the children had been introduced to complementary feeding before six months of age, whereas 160 (39.9%) started complementary feeding at six months or later. Thirty-seven (9.2%) of the children tested positive for intestinal parasitic infection. Among children who had a fever in the previous 24 h, none tested positive for malaria. Regarding household food security status, 284 (70.8%) households were food insecure, while 117 (29.2%) were food secure. Furthermore, 267 (66.6%) of the children met the minimum meal frequency, whereas 210 (52.4%) had an inadequate dietary diversity score (Table 2).

Prevalence of anemia

The overall prevalence of anemia among children aged 6–59 months in this study was 51.4% (95% CI: 46.6%–56.4%). Among the anemic children, 57.3% had mild anemia, 32.5% had moderate anemia, and 10.2% had severe anemia. The mean ± standard deviation hemoglobin concentration of the children in the study area was 11.3 ± 2.1 g/dL.

Factors associated with anemia

Bivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variable (anemia status). Independent variables with a p-value < 0.25 were considered as candidate variables for multivariable analysis. Based on this criterion, the following variables were selected: maternal/caregiver occupation, maternal/caregiver education, initiation of complementary feeding, household food insecurity, dietary diversity, antenatal care attendance during pregnancy, access to healthcare services, and place of delivery. These variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to determine their independent association with anemia while controlling for potential confounding factors (Table 4).

In the multivariable logistic regression model, the following factors were discovered to be associated with anemia among children aged 6 to 59 months. Children who have been introduced complementary feeding below 6 months were 2.6 times (AOR = 2.6; 95% CI: 1.5, 4.6) more likely to be anemic compared to children who have been introduced to complementary feeding ≥ 6 months old. Children from food-insecure households were 5.4 times (AOR = 5.4; 95% CI: 2.8, 10.2) more likely to be anemic compared to children from food-secured households. Children whose mothers/caregivers did not receive formal education were 2.8 times (AOR = 2.8; 95% CI: 1.3, 5.7) more likely to be anemic compared to children whose mothers/caregivers reached primary school. Children whose dietary diversity was less than 4 were 7.9 times (AOR = 7.9; 95% CI: 4.4, 13.9) more likely to be anemic compared to children whose dietary diversity was greater than or equal to 4 food groups. Children whose mother did not follow up antenatal care (ANC) during pregnancy were 3.5 times (AOR = 3.5; 95% CI: 1.8, 6.7) more likely to be anemic compared to children whose mothers followed ANC during their pregnancy (Table 5).

Discussion

Rewrite the following paragraph in good, appropriate English: “This study found that the prevalence of anemia among children aged 6 to 59 months in the Qoloji IDP camp was 51.4% (95% CI: 46.6%, 56.4%), which is a serious public health issue that calls for especial concern according to the WHO classification [1]. However, such a high prevalence of anemia shows that children aged 6 to 59 months in the study area suffer greatly from anemia, which makes them more susceptible to the long-term effects of anemia on their growth, development, and health [2]. Moreover, the study explored that maternal/caregiver having no formal education, household food insecurity, dietary diversity below 4 food groups, initiation of complementary feeding below 6 months, and mothers who did not follow up on antenatal care during pregnancy were factors identified as potential contributors to the high prevalence of anemia observed among children aged 6 to 59 months in the study area.

The prevalence of anemia observed in our study was comparable to that reported among children aged 6–59 months in the Kakuma Refugee Camp (55.5%), Kenya [19] and the Kebribeyah refugee camp Somali Region, Ethiopia (52.4%) [13]. It was also similar to findings from a study conducted among Rohingya children aged 6–59 months in the Kutupalong Refugee Camp, Bangladesh (47.9%) [20].

In contrast, the prevalence of anemia in this study was lower than that reported among children aged 6–59 months residing in the Ntele internally displaced persons camp, Mozambique (83%) [11]. However, the prevalence observed in our study was considerably higher than the global anemia prevalence (44%) reported among children aged 6–59 months across 121 refugee settings in 24 countries ( 9 ). It was also higher than findings from studies conducted in the Qoshtapa Camp and Darashakran Camps, Iraq (27%) [21]; in Somalia (17.6%) [10]; in Northwest Ethiopia (33.62%) [12]; and among Syrian refugees in Beirut (21.7%) [22]. The observed discrepancies between our findings and those of the aforementioned studies may be attributed to differences in sample size, nutritional status, socio-demographic characteristics, sampling techniques, wealth index, and geographic location.

Regarding factors associated with anemia, this study identified a significant relationship between childhood anemia and maternal/caregiver education. Children whose mothers or caregivers had no formal education were more likely to be anemic. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies [23, 24]. Lower maternal educational attainment may limit awareness and knowledge of appropriate health and nutrition practices, which can negatively affect the quality of food provided to children and increase their risk of anemia [25].

This study also found a significant association between anemia and household food insecurity, indicating that children from food-insecure households were at a higher risk of developing anemia. Similar findings have been reported by Tegegne et al. 2022 [26] and Belachew and Tewabe 2020 [4]. The increased risk among these children may be due to insufficient food intake, reliance on less diversified diets with low iron and vitamin content, and coping strategies adopted during periods of food shortage [15].

Additionally, dietary diversity of fewer than four food groups was significantly associated with anemia. Children consuming diets with less than four food groups were more likely to be anemic. Comparable results were reported in studies conducted in Kombolcha [27] and Debre Markos [28]. This association may be attributed to the reliance on monotonous, predominantly cereal-based diets lacking essential nutrients such as iron, vitamin A, vitamin B12, folate, and other minerals, which can increase the risk of childhood anemia [29].

Furthermore, this study found that the timing of complementary feeding was significantly associated with anemia. Children who were introduced to complementary foods before six months of age were at a higher risk of being anemic. This finding is consistent with previous studies by Yoseph et al. 2021 [28] and Haile et al. 2015 [30]. Early introduction of complementary foods may reduce breast milk intake, increase susceptibility to infections, and contribute to respiratory illnesses, all of which can elevate the risk of anemia in young children [31].

In this study, both maternal ANC follow-up during pregnancy and parental access to healthcare services were found to be associated with childhood anemia. Children whose mothers did not attend ANC during pregnancy were at a higher risk of developing anemia. A similar finding was reported in a study conducted in the Dodota district, Southeast Ethiopia [32]. ANC visits provide pregnant women with iron and folic acid supplementation, which has been shown to protect children from developing anemia, as demonstrated in research from India [33]. Additionally, parental access to healthcare services was significantly associated with anemia. Children whose parents lacked access to healthcare were at greater risk. This may be due to factors such as low income, limited wealth, insufficient public health awareness, and the poor quality of available healthcare services, all of which can contribute to an increased risk of childhood anemia.

This study reveals that anemia is alarmingly common among children aged 6 to 59 months in the Qoloji IDP camp, with a prevalence rate of 51.4%. This statistic categorizes it as a serious public health issue according to the World Health Organization’s standards [1]. Essentially, this means that over half of the young children in the camp are affected, putting them at a high risk for problems like stunted physical growth, hindered cognitive development, weakened immune systems, and long-term health complications [2]. The research also points out that anemia in this context is closely associated with changeable socio-demographic and feeding-related factors. These include a lack of maternal education, food insecurity at home, limited dietary diversity (with fewer than four food groups), starting complementary feeding too early (before six months), and insufficient antenatal care during pregnancy. Overall, the study highlights an urgent need for comprehensive public health initiatives that focus on educating mothers, enhancing food security at home, promoting proper feeding practices for infants and young children, and improving antenatal care services to help alleviate the anemia burden in this vulnerable group.

In this study, we didn’t find any significant link between anemia and the age of children (whether they were under 12 months or 12 months and older), their breastfeeding status if they were under 24 months, or any signs of undernutrition like being underweight, stunted, or wasted. This suggests that anemia can occur in children even if they don’t show typical signs of undernutrition. Essentially, kids who aren’t classified as underweight or stunted can still suffer from anemia. This points to a limitation in using growth indicators alone to spot children who are nutritionally at risk, as deficiencies in micronutrients, especially iron, can happen even when there are no clear signs of undernutrition. Moreover, the lack of a connection with age and breastfeeding status suggests that anemia in this context might not just be about age-related vulnerabilities or current breastfeeding habits. It’s likely influenced by a range of broader factors. These results highlight the importance of looking into other possible causes, such as deficiencies in micronutrients like iron, folate, and vitamin B12, as well as environmental health issues. A thorough biochemical and clinical evaluation could help shed light on the root causes of anemia in these children.

This study has a few limitations. First, due to its cross-sectional design, it cannot establish a causal relationship between anemia and the associated factors. Second, the study did not differentiate between types of anemia, such as hemolytic anemia, sickle cell anemia, or thalassemia. Third, information on household food insecurity, meal frequency, and dietary diversity was collected through parental or caregiver recall, which may have introduced recall bias.

Conclusion

This study revealed that anemia was highly prevalent among children aged 6 to 59 months in Qoloji IDP camp. The findings indicated that lack of formal education among mothers or caregivers, household food insecurity, poor dietary diversity, early initiation of complementary feeding before six months of age, and absence of ANC follow-up during pregnancy were significantly associated with anemia among the children studied. Therefore, reducing the high burden of anemia and its associated factors in this population requires comprehensive and integrated interventions. These should include improving parental access to formal and nutritional education, enhancing maternal awareness and utilization of ANC services during pregnancy, and addressing underlying socio-economic challenges such as food insecurity through income-generating activities and food assistance programs.
