Origin, Strength, and Speed of the Nonlinear Optical Response of Transparent Conducting Oxides to Single‐Cycle Optical Pulses
Authors: Ieng‐Wai Un, Subhajit Sarkar, Yonatan Sivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe present a first‐principles study of the nonlinear optical response of transparent conducting oxides at the nanoscale due to excitation by intense, extremely short pulses based on a density matrix framework. We identify a strong (O(1)) thermal nonlinearity, which is complemented with stimulated emission and excited‐state absorption; it yields a cumulative permittivity change decorated by quantum coherent oscillations. Further, rigorous calculations under far‐from‐equilibrium conditions show that electron–electron thermalization occurs within a few femtoseconds, supporting interpretations of high‐harmonic generation measurements and in agreement with a generalization of Fermi liquid theory.Keywords: optical nonlinearity, quantum coherence, single‐cycle pulses, thermalization, transparent conducting oxides

Introduction

The interaction of intense single‐ or sub‐cycle optical pulses with solids is of importance to our understanding of many‐body interactions, coherence, and dynamics in (electron) systems far from equilibrium. It also has practical importance, for example, as a potential source for single‐electron emission from metal tips [1], and as a source for high‐harmonic generation.

So far, most studies involved coherent extremely short pulse interactions with dielectrics [2, 3], (intrinsic) semiconductors [4, 5, 6], and less so with noble metals [7, 8, 9]. The accompanying theory usually relied on time‐dependent density functional theory (TD‐DFT) [9, 10, 11, 12] or the Maxwell–Bloch equations [13, 14] (or equivalently, the density matrix (DM) formulation [15, 16, 17]). More recently, advanced (and computationally much heavier) approaches such as finite‐temperature DFT [18] and many‐body perturbation theory [19, 20] were applied to extremely short pulse interactions with dielectrics or gases [21]. In these works, most attention was given to the early stages of the dynamics, to the coherence between electrons and photons and the associated nonlinearities, and to quantum and transport phenomena in nanometric structures. This involved a meticulous calculation of the various electron wavefunctions, self‐energies, and then the transition matrix elements in real space. Overall, the predictions from these approaches are in good agreement with measurements.

In contrast, the incoherent nonlinear optical response, that is, intensity‐dependent permittivity changes, received little attention; some exceptions are refs. [19, 22, 23]. Moreover, effects such as excited‐state absorption and stimulated emission were rarely studied, largely because the nonequilibrium electron population dynamics are difficult to measure experimentally or to monitor using the theoretical techniques mentioned above. Instead, population dynamics is usually treated with incoherent rate equations. Moreover, the later stages of the dynamics, namely the thermalization via electron–electron (e–e) interactions and relaxation via electron–phonon (e–ph) interactions, are usually treated by the simplified relaxation time approximation [15, 24, 25, 26] or assuming thermal populations [19, 20]; as a rare exception, the authors in ref. [27] studied the effect of e–e interactions on the harmonic generation, but not the effect of e–ph and electron–impurity (e–imp) interactions, nor the effect of the latter on the permittivity dynamics; similarly, electron collision dynamics were studied in insulators under the conditions of thermal equilibrium [19, 20]. As a result, there is not yet a detailed understanding of thermalization and relaxation dynamics in the far‐from‐equilibrium conditions created by the extremely short pulses. A prime example is in the field of high harmonic generation [12, 26, 28] where there are disagreements about the proper value that has to be assigned to the dephasing rates (typically termed as T2 in the context of atomic physics). Specifically, although originally values of (at least) a few 10s of femtosecond were used, some examples showed that good match with experimental data requires values as short as 1 fs [17, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. On the other hand, more recent works demonstrated that experimental data can be matched with longer T2 values (i.e., 10s–100s femtoseconds) if full k‐space modeling [12] and propagation effects are taken into account, for example, in diamond [12] or in GaAs [28].

To address these open questions, we combine the DM formulation with a rigorous treatment of the (many‐body) thermalization and relaxation [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. We employ the formulation to study the nonlinear optical response of transparent conducting oxides (TCOs; aka low electron density Drude materials [38, 39] or p‐doped metals [40]). TCOs have recently attracted the attention of the nonlinear optics community due to their extremely high nonlinearities. Indeed, permittivity changes of several 100s of percent were observed from thin films with pulses of a few 10–100s femtosecond long [41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. This has made TCOs promising candidates for photonic time crystals [49, 50]. Originally, the strong nonlinearity was associated with the zero permittivity crossing point [44, 51, 52], and then, with the conduction band nonparabolicity [53]; more recently, we showed that a significant contribution also comes from the increased collision rates induced by the heating [54] and that the heating can be far more significant than expected originally [38], even exceeding the Fermi temperature (∼10,000 K) [55].

However, to date, there is no comprehensive understanding of the interactions of TCOs with extremely short pulses. As a follow‐up of earlier work [39, 56], this work aims to close this knowledge gap by providing a nonphenomenological study of this problem and to match the rigorous model results to those from simpler ones. To do that, we first employ the DM formulation to study the dynamics of electron population and coherence, as well as the macroscopic polarization and local field. We elucidate the relative importance of the various electron interactions for the nonlinear response. We also find that the response to extremely short pulses has many similarities to the response to longer pulses, for example, the overall strength, the cumulative nature of the response, and the unique large changes to the real part of the permittivity. Nevertheless, the response to extremely short pulses has a more complex electric field dependence because of stimulated emission and excited state absorption and the effect of coherence between the various electron states on the permittivity dynamics. Nevertheless, we also show that, surprisingly, one can reproduce the permittivity dynamics quite accurately with a far simpler thermal model, which simplifies the modeling and understanding of this problem drastically.

Moreover, our treatment provides a rigorous insight into the accelerated collision rates and the associated nonlinearity decay in the context of extremely short pulses that goes beyond the most advanced treatments for noble metals [34] and insulators [19, 20]. Specifically, we find that the thermalization/collision time can be shortened to ∼10 femtoseconds for TW/cm2 intensities and that its scaling with |E|2 is linear (for the range of intensities studied here). This provides a first‐of‐its‐kind calculation that is inline with the debated few femtosecond thermalization times used previously to explain high harmonic generation measurements [12, 17, 26, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33]. We also provide a unique analytic generalization of Fermi's liquid theory (FLT) for the e–e collision (thermalization) rate for high temperatures and far‐from‐equilibrium conditions, which reproduces the rigorous numeric calculation very well. This is far from being a trivial result, since FLT is derived assuming the electron distribution is thermal, whereas the distribution becomes strongly nonthermal under the investigated conditions. This result would be useful in studying thermalization dynamics in high‐harmonic generation and attosecond science.

Finally, we compare our theoretical predictions to the measurements of the nonlinear optical response of TCOs to extremely short pulses [57], as well as to alternative (yet more phenomenological) modeling.

Formulation

We solve the DM equations [13, 14, 16] for a popular TCO, namely indium tin oxide (ITO) in momentum space (see details in ref. [58]). Electron transition moments are described within the dipole approximation assuming an infinitely deep potential is formed by the particle geometry [59, 60, 61]; we account for the conduction band nonparabolicity (Kane formula [62]) by writing the transition dipole moment in terms of the momentum operator using the p‐r relationship [63] and replacing the electron energy E by E(1+CE), namely −e〈ψk|rˆ|ψk′〉=ieℏ〈ψk|pˆ|ψk′〉/Ek1+CEk−Ek′1+CEk′, where C is the nonparabolicity parameter; see Section II B in ref. [58]. The collision rates, ηe‐e and ηe‐ph are treated rigorously in k ‐space under the Thomas–Fermi and deformation potential approximations, respectively, as in refs. [34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39].

Since the pulse duration is shorter than the material response time (i.e., the collision rates), we a priori refrain from defining a permittivity, and instead, compute the polarization density via tracing the dipole matrix element and the DM off‐diagonal terms [16].

Configuration

In order to focus on the features unique to the dynamics of conduction electrons, we chose a configuration of a few nm ITO sphere (Figure 1a), using the material parameters extracted from earlier experiments, as described in refs. [38, 39]. This approach has multiple advantages. First, as seen in Figure 1b, the dipolar resonance associated with the dipole mode of the nanosphere appears at ℏω≈0.7 eV, that is, at wavelengths far from the threshold for interband transitions (thus, making our “intraband‐only” treatment sufficient), as well as longer than the epsilon‐near‐zero point (thus, disentangling the dynamics from the associated pure electrodynamic effects associated with such points). Second, the electric field is uniform for such a system, thus entailing significant computational simplification and ensuring that dephasing due to spatial variations of the electromagnetic fields [12, 28] does not play a role.

Results

Nonlinearity Turn On

We initially look at the electron dynamics due to illumination. Specifically, Figure 2 and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2 show, respectively, the dynamics of the electron distributions and the excited electron density (Supporting Information S1: Equation (S27)) for E0=0.6 V/nm, 1.2 V/nm (comparable to the field levels used in refs. Although a strong deviation from thermal equilibrium is clearly visible in the electron distribution, fixed‐time snapshots (Figure 2b,e,h and Supporting Information S1: Figure S2b,e,h) show that the deviation does not resemble the nonthermal shoulders above the Fermi energy which are characteristic of metals under continuous wave and long pulse illumination (see, e.g., refs. Moreover, those snapshots also reveal transient strong and oscillatory population inversion near the Fermi energy for sufficiently strong pump pulses (Figure 2d–i). The oscillation (seen more clearly in fixed energy cross sections, Figure 2c,f,i and Supporting Information S1: Figures S2c,f,i) originates from the quantum coherence associated with the off‐diagonal elements of the DM, which also manifests as oscillations in the e‐photon interaction term [58]. Notably, after the passage of the pump pulse, the population changes persist for the duration of the simulation (∼20 femtoseconds; see Figure 2c,f,i) with no apparent rapid decay.

Now, to investigate the macroscopic optical response of the material arising from the microscopic dynamics, we perform a calculation that mimics the standard experimental procedure of a pump–probe measurement. Specifically, once we have determined the DM element dynamics for the single‐cycle pump pulse, we compute the polarization induced by a weaker short probe (at variable central probe frequencies ωpr, duration and pump–probe delay τd) in the time domain. We then Fourier transform the induced polarization and divide it by the transform of the local electric field. The resulting coherent (nonadiabatic and) nonthermal response can be viewed as a type of “instantaneous permittivity”, εωpr,τd. The results, shown in Figure 4a,b, reveal a rapid growth of the real and imaginary parts of εωpr,τd, reaching even ∼250% relative changes; such strong nonlinearity is comparable to that observed in experiments and simulations for longer pulses of comparable fluence (e.g., that employed in the examples of ref. [39], ∼160 GW/cm2. 30 femtoseconds ∼5 mJ/cm2). Importantly, the response is not instantaneous, but rather it accumulates in time, and like the population, persists thereafter, indicating its absorptive nature. In that sense, the Drude nonlinearity investigated here should not be approximated by a Kerr nonlinearity, which scales with the instantaneous intensity |E(t)|2 and follows the temporal envelope of the optical pulse, see, for example, ref. [22]. Nevertheless, for ultrashort excitation pulses, a Kerr‐type description can provide a reasonable approximation of the initial rise of the nonlinear response, whereas it cannot describe the subsequent persistence of the induced permittivity change. 1 A closer look shows that the rise time of the extracted imaginary part of the ITO permittivity decreases with the probe pulse duration (see Figure 4a,b), whereas the dynamics of the change of the real part is less sensitive to the probe duration.

Remarkably, unlike the incoherent dynamics employed in refs. [38, 39], the permittivity growth is decorated by the 1/2ω0∼3 femtoseconds oscillation seen in the electron dynamics; thus, it is the clear signature of the coherence between the different electron states (i.e., the off‐diagonal elements of the DM), as seen previously in TD‐DFT simulations [22].

[39]. The “instantaneous permittivity” is a time‐dependent quantity obtained by evaluating the permittivity for the instantaneous electron distribution. In this sense, it corresponds to the zero probe duration limit of the pump–probe curves; since ANTHEM is computed here from the coherent dynamics of the electron population obtained from the DM equations, it is oscillatory as well. Now, in order to understand the strength of the nonlinear response (i.e., the permittivity changes), in Figure 4c, we plot the maximal value of the permittivity change, 3 Δετd≡εωpr,τd−εωpr,−∞, as a function of the time‐integrated absorbed energy density, that is, Uabs≡∫−∞∞P˙NP,abs(t)⋅ENP(t)dt, where P˙NP,abs is the time derivative of the absorption component of the polarization density (see Supporting Information S1: Equation (S14)).

The result shows a clear sublinear scaling of Δε′ with the actual absorption. For comparison, we also consider the case in which the density‐matrix equations are solved without including stimulated emission. 4 In this case, the permittivity change scales linearly with Uabs. This indicates that the permittivity change has its roots in the change of the total energy of the electron system [39], whereas stimulated emission reduces the net nonlinear response.

This aspect of the nonlinear response can be further elucidated by decomposing the change in permittivity into distinct physical contributions, including modifications due to stimulated emission and absorption rates, as well as spectral shift and resonance broadening. In particular, the absorbed energy density associated with the contribution arising solely from spectral shift and broadening is obtained from integrating ωεlin′′(ω)|E˜NP(ω)|2/2 over the angular frequency, where εlin′′(ω) is the imaginary part of the permittivity in the weak‐field (linear) limit (Supporting Information S1: Equation (S11)), and E˜NP(ω) is the Fourier transform of the local field. As shown in Figure 4d and Supporting Information S1: Figure S3, the increased imaginary part (due to the so‐called quantum size effect) makes broadening dominate over the spectral shift and leads to a sublinear scaling of the permittivity change with |E0|2 (in similarity to the case of longer pulses [39]). Including the excited‐state absorption significantly enhances the permittivity change, resulting in a superlinear scaling with |E0|2. However, this enhancement is largely compensated by the stimulated emission, whose contribution scales sublinearly with |E0|4 (see Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). Consequently, the overall permittivity change exhibits a sublinear dependence on |E0|2 (hence, a sub‐cubic nonlinearity), only slightly exceeding that obtained from spectral effects alone. In that sense, the response of the few nm ITO particles differs from that of thicker ITO layers (for which resonance shifts are responsible for the sublinear growth of the temperature and the corresponding sub‐cubic nonlinear response) and shows a similar response to that of Au NPs under CW illumination [71, 72].

Specifically, one can observe that the e–e collision rate grows with the amount of energy injected into the electron system via photon absorption. As in standard FLT [73], electrons far from the Fermi energy undergo collisions more frequently than electrons close to the Fermi energy; yet the new linear correction we derived (see Supporting Information S1: Section S2) partially suppresses this effect. We also find, in similarity to standard FLT, that the dip of the e–e collision rate for electrons near the Fermi energy shifts from EF to μT˜e and is gradually washed out with increasing (effective) electron temperature (which reaches ∼4500 K for the incident illumination intensity of 1 TW/cm2 used in this example), such that the thermalization time becomes nearly energy‐independent, with a value of ∼12 fs. The success of the generalized FLT expression (3) to reproduce the rigorous thermalization dynamics also means that the deviation from thermal equilibrium per se has a small effect on the e–e collision rate, see Figure 5a. This is remarkable, since the derivation of FLT is performed under the condition that the electron system is nearly thermalized, a condition which clearly does not hold here (Figure 2).

Figure 5b further shows that the analytic approximation for ηe‐e scales linearly with the illumination intensity, with electrons of different energies exhibiting somewhat different slopes. Assuming this scaling persists for higher intensities, it is likely that e–e collision rates on the scale of a single femtosecond would be reached for intensities of 10 TW/cm2, which are in use for many high‐harmonic generation experiments. In that sense, our results a posteriori support the choices of fixed T2 values of single femtoseconds [12, 17, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33] shown to yield a good match with high‐harmonic generation experiments, at least after the initial excitation stage.

In that respect, it is also worth mentioning that the relaxation behavior we reveal may be different when exciting from the valence band [2, 3] or because of tunneling under a voltage bias [74, 75]. A comprehensive study of the relaxation dynamics in all these systems will be the topic of future investigations.

Although e–e collisions cause thermalization, only the Umklapp fraction contributes to the permittivity rise [58], whereas the permittivity relaxation is determined by the e–ph collision time, ηe‐ph. This is a natural consequence of the dependence of the permittivity change Δε on the increase of the electron energy (i.e., in proportion to the (net) absorption). Figure 5c shows that the e–ph collision rate is essentially independent of the dynamics/electron energy/distribution. This happens because the phonon energy is relatively small (below the Debye energy of 0.06 eV [76]), making ηe‐ph primarily governed by the average phonon number (see also Equation (13) of ref. [39]). Furthermore, Figure 5c shows that ηe‐ph is much slower than ηe‐e; hence, e–ph interactions do not explain the rapid decay observed in ref. [57]. This slow relaxation also has important implications for the realization of optical‐frequency photonic time crystals (PTCs) in TCOs [50, 77, 78]. Since optical/NIR PTCs require modulation periods on the order of a few femtoseconds, energy deposition occurs on timescales much shorter than those associated with thermal diffusion or heat extraction to the substrate. 5 The cumulative thermal nonlinearity identified in this work consequently imposes a strong constraint on TCO‐based optical PTCs, as repeated modulation cycles would progressively increase the energy of the electron system and could rapidly drive the material toward its damage threshold. This argument does not preclude PTC operation at lower frequencies, where the modulation timescale is sufficiently long to allow heat dissipation, as demonstrated experimentally in the THz regime [80].

This behavior contrasts the instantaneous SiO2 nonlinearity studied in ref. [22], which involved mostly virtual transitions from the valence to the conduction band [22, 81] (so that relaxation is inherent) rather than real transitions within the conduction band (for which the relaxation occurs via e–ph interactions); this reflects, again, the absorptive (or, in the language of ref. [22], irreversible) nature of the intraband nonlinearity of TCOs studied here.

Conclusion and Outlook

Our calculations yield a nonlinear response which is at least an order of magnitude stronger than the prediction in ref. [81] for the Kerr (interband) nonlinearity and much larger than the experimentally measured transmission and reflection changes in ref. [57]. Our prediction for the decay dynamics is also at odds with the observed 40‐fs decay time of the transmission. Our theory could nevertheless be reconciled with that measurement if, in the experiment, the electron system is brought closer to transparency than in our simulations, an effect which may then expose the weaker and instantaneous Kerr nonlinearity considered in ref. [81] and measured in ref. [22] in silica. This could happen for a different set of material parameters and/or much higher intensities than used here; It may also originate from multi‐photon absorption (which is more likely in the configuration of ref. [57] than in our configuration), which was shown to cause a partial cancellation of the intraband nonlinearity we studied [82]. Yet, such mechanisms do not explain the far slower decay of the reflection observed in ref. [57]. It is also still unknown how the Kerr nonlinearity would be affected by the drastic population changes and extremely high electron temperatures; a proper evaluation of these effects requires a comprehensive model that accounts for both interband and intraband transitions. Hints about the contributions of these effects, and many additional nonlinear mechanisms, can be extracted from the comprehensive hydrodynamic model used in ref. [56] to reproduce the experimental data of ref. [57]. Extending that approach to include the temperature and effective‐mass dynamics unique to ITO may indeed yield a better match to the experimental data.

In addition, the 4‐nm sphere considered in the present work supports nearly uniform electric fields and electron temperatures. This differs from the 300‐nm ITO film studied in ref. [57], where substantial field and temperature gradients may develop, contributing to the measured response through transport and spatiotemporal effects (some of which are discussed in ref. [56] as well). These additional effects may therefore play a role in the experimental response and could contribute to the discrepancy between our predictions and the measured dynamics.

Furthermore, the effect of nonlocality [83] which arises in few nanometer structures such as those we study may give rise to a size‐dependence plasma frequency and modify the resonance position and local‐field enhancement [84, 85, 86, 87, 88]. These effects are orthogonal to the main findings in the paper and are expected to affect the results in this work, at most, in a modest quantitative manner.

Finally, fieldoscopy measurements have resolved the transient electric‐field in ITO nanocrystals driven by two‐cycle optical pulses [23]. That work focused on the reversibility of the macroscopic ultrafast response, its intensity scaling and recovery time, thus, providing the previously missing experimental route for directly testing the microscopic electron dynamics and nonlinear response predicted in this work.
