An alternative explanation to the unusual neon pink color in a radiotherapy tattoo
Authors: Nicolas Kluger
Published: 2026

To the Editor: I read with interest the article by Boroumand et al, describing a 66-year-old woman presenting with autofluorescent neon pink pigmentation at the site of radiotherapy tattoos placed 3 years earlier.1 The authors proposed that “chemical modification and interactions between ink components may have occurred in unexpected ways, altering the tattoo's appearance.”1 However, black tattoo inks (CI 77266, Indian ink) consist solely of carbon black plus additives2; they are not, as the authors suggest, a blend of multiple-colored pigments. No known chemical process can convert carbon black into a pink, UV-reactive pigment. Since tattoos persist for a lifetime or fade only very gradually, it is striking that after just 3 years no black pigment whatsoever remained visible in the skin. The hypothesis of a dark pigment that would have progressively and completely faded away within 3 years without the patient’s knowledge is highly unlikely. Furthermore, one would expect to find some remaining pigments in the dermis. The use of luminescent ink for radiotherapy marking is a documented practice.3,4 The simplest and most plausible explanation is that the neon pink pigment was introduced into the skin through tattooing itself. Notably, InkAlign markets in North America a single-use medical tattooing device specifically using pink UV-reactive ink for radiotherapy marking.5 Rather than an unexplained chemical transformation of standard black ink, the authors should contact the radiotherapy center where the patient was treated to clarify their marking protocol and confirm which ink product was used.
