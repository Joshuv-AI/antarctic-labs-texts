Bidirectional Mendelian randomization between oral microbiota and allergic rhinitis in East Asian populations
Authors: Chenguang Zhang, Panlei Li, Yicong Wang, Chunlong Zhao, Wanru Chen, Yanan Ma, Bin Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo investigate the bidirectional causal associations between genetically predicted oral microbial taxa and allergic rhinitis (AR) in East Asian populations.MethodsWe used summary-level genome-wide association study (GWAS) data from East Asian populations to conduct a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis. The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was the primary method. Bayesian weighted Mendelian randomization (BWMR) was used to assess the robustness of taxa that remained significant after IVW-based FDR correction. Cochran’s Q test, the MR-Egger intercept test, and MR-PRESSO were used to assess heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy.ResultsIn the forward MR analysis, 16 tongue dorsum and 22 salivary microbial taxa were associated with AR risk. BWMR supported associations for 15 tongue dorsum and 17 salivary taxa, with effect directions generally consistent with those obtained using IVW. Representative associations included a positive association between genetically predicted salivary Campylobacter_A_mgs_1095 abundance and AR risk (OR = 1.381), and an inverse association between tongue dorsum Streptococcus_mgs_1057 abundance and AR risk (OR = 0.767).Sensitivity analyses indicated no substantial heterogeneity or horizontal pleiotropy for most taxa.ConclusionsGenetically predicted abundances of several tongue dorsum and salivary microbial taxa were associated with AR risk in East Asian GWAS datasets.These findings require validation in larger independent cohorts and experimental studies.Keywords: Oral microbiota, allergic rhinitis, Mendelian randomization, genome-wide association study, causal inference

Introduction

Allergic rhinitis (AR) is one of the most common allergic diseases worldwide, and its prevalence has increased over recent decades. Current estimates suggest that AR affects approximately 10%–30% of the global population [1,2]. Its burden extends beyond nasal itching, sneezing, rhinorrhea, and nasal obstruction. AR can impair sleep, concentration, academic performance, work productivity, and quality of life. It also increases the need for long-term medication and medical care [2–4]. The development of AR involves interactions between environmental exposure, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and immune dysregulation. Air pollutants and other environmental factors may damage the airway epithelium and promote type 2 inflammation [1,3]. The classical immune response involves allergen-specific immunoglobulin E and T helper 2 cells. Interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, and IL-13 promote IgE production and eosinophilic inflammation [3]. Microorganisms at the upper airway mucosal surface may also affect this response. Miao et al. found that Streptococcus salivarius was more abundant in the nasal microbiota of patients with AR than in healthy controls. Experimental analyses showed that this species could adhere to MUC5AC, impair epithelial integrity, and increase the production of IL-6 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin [5]. These findings show that the effect of a commensal bacterium may depend on its abundance, location, and host environment.

The oral cavity contains a diverse microbial community that occupies several distinct ecological niches [6,7]. Resident oral microorganisms help maintain ecological stability through competition for nutrients and adhesion sites. They also produce metabolites and small molecules that influence microbial interactions and the local environment [7,8]. The composition of these communities varies across the tongue dorsum, saliva, dental surfaces, gingival sites, and oral mucosa [7,9,10]. This spatial organisation is important because the function of an oral microorganism may differ according to its species, strain, abundance, and anatomical site. Host genetics, diet, oral hygiene, medication use, and immune status can also alter oral microbial composition. Therefore, an entire genus should not be classified uniformly as beneficial or pro-inflammatory. A case-control study by Pérez-Losada et al. found differences in oral bacterial communities between patients with AR or asthma and healthy controls [11]. However, an observational association cannot establish whether oral microbial changes contribute to AR or result from the disease. Medication use, oral hygiene, dietary habits, smoking, and altered breathing patterns may affect both the oral microbiota and AR. These factors can introduce confounding and make the causal direction difficult to determine.

Mendelian randomisation (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to assess the potential causal effect of an exposure on an outcome [12–14]. A valid MR analysis requires that the genetic variants are associated with the exposure, are independent of major confounders, and affect the outcome only through the exposure [14]. Because genetic variants are assigned at conception, MR is less susceptible to conventional environmental confounding and reverse causation than observational studies. A two-sample MR design can combine summary statistics from separate genome-wide association studies to examine the relationship between microbial traits and disease risk [12–14]. However, MR does not directly measure microbial abundance in patients and cannot identify the biological mechanisms of individual taxa. Experimental and clinical studies are still required to confirm its findings. In this study, we conducted a bidirectional two-sample MR analysis using genome-wide association study data from East Asian populations. We aimed to assess whether genetically predicted tongue dorsum and salivary microbial taxa affect the risk of AR and whether genetic liability to AR affects these oral microbial traits.

Methods

Data sources

All data utilised in this study were obtained from publicly available databases and do not involve any individual-level privacy information. According to institutional guidelines, ethical approval was waived for the use of these secondary datasets. The oral microbiome GWAS data used in this analysis included 309 tongue dorsum microbial traits (N = 2,017) and 285 salivary microbial traits (N = 1,915). Detailed information on sample collection, sequencing protocols, microbial phenotype definition, and genotyping procedures is provided in a previously published study [15]. This work represents a significant step forward in characterising the genetic architecture of oral microbiota in East Asian populations, offering a comprehensive overview of host genetic associations with a wide range of microbial taxa down to the species level. The summary-level statistics used to support our MR analysis—including host genetic associations with tongue and salivary microbiota—are publicly accessible via CNGBdb at: https://db.cngb.org/search/project/CNP0001664.The release of these datasets was approved by the Ministry of Science and Technology of China. GWAS summary data for AR (ID: ebi-a-GCST90018572) were obtained from the GWAS Catalogue maintained by the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI): https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/. Instrument selection

We applied rigorous criteria for the selection and evaluation of genetic instruments. Because few variants reached the conventional genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8, a relaxed threshold of P < 1 × 10−5 was used to select candidate instruments for the oral microbial traits. To ensure instrument independence, linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping was performed with a window size of 10,000 kb and an r2 threshold of <0.001. Only SNPs with F-statistics > 10 were retained to minimise the impact of weak instruments.

Mendelian randomisation analysis

To investigate the potential causal effects of oral microbiota on AR, we conducted two-sample MR analyses using multiple complementary methods. The primary analytical approach was the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method [16]. Sensitivity analyses were performed using the weighted median [17], MR-Egger regression [18], simple mode, weighted mode, and Bayesian Weighted Mendelian Randomisation (BWMR) models. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. To control for false positives due to multiple testing, we applied false discovery rate (FDR) correction, considering associations with PFDR < 0.05 as statistically significant. The IVW method was used as the primary analysis. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied separately to all valid IVW P values for the tongue dorsum and salivary microbial datasets. After the raw P values were arranged in ascending order, the adjusted P value for the ith test was calculated as PBH(i) = min[1, minj ≥ i{mP(j)/j}], where m denotes the total number of valid IVW tests within each dataset. An FDR-adjusted P value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. BWMR was used as a complementary analysis for taxa that survived the IVW-based FDR correction, and no additional FDR correction was applied to the BWMR results. Associations with P < 0.05 but PFDR ≥ 0.05 were interpreted as suggestive evidence of potential relationships between oral microbial taxa and AR. A schematic overview of the MR workflow is provided in Figure 1. To further explore potential reverse causality, we performed reverse MR analyses using AR as the exposure and microbial traits as outcomes. Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the primary IVW estimates, we performed several sensitivity analyses. Cochran’s Q test was used to evaluate heterogeneity among the instrumental variables. Directional horizontal pleiotropy was assessed with the MR-Egger intercept test, and the MR-PRESSO global test was used to identify evidence of horizontal pleiotropy and potential outlying variants. Leave-one-out analysis was conducted to determine whether any association was driven by a single SNP [19,20]. Steiger directionality tests were performed at both the overall trait level and the individual-SNP level to assess whether the instruments explained more variance in the microbial exposure than in AR. Radial IVW analysis and SNP contribution plots were also used to identify influential or outlying instruments. BWMR was applied as a complementary analysis to microbial traits that remained significant after IVW-based FDR correction. Nominal BWMR P values were reported without additional FDR correction, and consistency in the direction of effect between IVW and BWMR was assessed [21]. Reverse MR analyses were conducted separately, with genetic liability to AR as the exposure and the selected oral microbial traits as outcomes. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2 with the TwoSampleMR, MR-PRESSO, and BWMR packages.

Results

Mendelian randomisation results

Using IVW as the primary MR method, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across all tested microbial traits. The IVW analysis identified 16 tongue-dorsum microbial traits associated with AR (PFDR < 0.05). Associations with an OR > 1 indicated that a higher genetically predicted abundance of the corresponding microbial trait was associated with an increased risk of AR, whereas associations with an OR < 1 indicated a potentially protective effect; the direction and exact effect estimates for each trait are presented in Table 2. Of these 16 tongue-dorsum traits, 15 showed concordant evidence in the BWMR analysis, whereas g__Mogibacterium_mgs_1148 was not supported by BWMR. For salivary microbial traits, IVW analysis identified 22 associations after FDR correction, of which 17 were supported by BWMR (Table 3). The associations for g__Haemophilus_mgs_2283, g__Prevotella_mgs_2511, s__Centipeda_flueggei_mgs_419, g__Lachnoanaerobaculum_mgs_2166, and g__TM7x_mgs_2181 were not confirmed by BWMR. Overall, 32 site-specific oral microbial traits, including 15 tongue-dorsum traits and 17 salivary traits, showed concordant evidence from both IVW and BWMR analyses. Based on the P values derived from the BWMR method, a volcano plot was constructed to visualise the distribution of effect sizes and statistical significance of oral microbiota associated with allergic rhinitis (Figure 2). Complete results from the MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode analyses are provided in the supplementary material S1. In addition, the four diagnostic plots generated for each BWMR analysis and the complete harmonised datasets are provided as supplementary material S1.

Sensitivity analysis results

Sensitivity analyses were performed for the 32 oral microbial traits supported by both IVW and BWMR, with complete results provided in the Table 4. Cochran’s Q tests showed no evidence of significant heterogeneity among the instrumental variables for any of the evaluated traits (all P > 0.05). The MR-Egger intercept test indicated nominal evidence of directional horizontal pleiotropy for Neisseria sp000186165_mgs_3225 (P = 0.040) and Streptococcus sp000235485_mgs_302 (P = 0.042), whereas no evidence of directional pleiotropy was detected for the remaining traits (all P > 0.05). The MR-PRESSO global tests provided no evidence of significant horizontal pleiotropy (all P > 0.05), and leave-one-out analyses indicated that none of the causal estimates was materially driven by a single SNP. Steiger directionality tests were additionally conducted at both the overall trait level and the individual-SNP level to assess the hypothesised direction from oral microbial traits to AR. Radial IVW analyses and SNP-specific contribution plots were used to evaluate potential outlying instruments and quantify the contribution of individual SNPs to overall heterogeneity. The complete Steiger, Radial IVW, SNP contribution, and leave-one-out results are presented in the supplementary material S1. Steiger directionality tests supported the hypothesised direction from oral microbial traits to AR for all 32 associations, with consistent findings at the individual-SNP level. Radial IVW estimates were concordant with the primary IVW results, and no influential outlying instruments were detected after correction for multiple SNP testing.

Reverse Mendelian randomisation analysis

Reverse MR analysis was conducted using the 32 microbial taxa that showed significant or suggestive causal associations with AR in the forward MR analysis. No evidence of reverse causality was found between AR and these microbial taxa (supplementary materials S1).

Discussion

In this bidirectional MR study of East Asian populations, we identified 32 site-specific oral microbial taxa—15 from the tongue dorsum and 17 from saliva—with concordant evidence from IVW and BWMR analyses for associations with AR risk. Reverse MR provided no evidence that genetic liability to AR affected these microbial taxa. These findings support potential directional effects of specific oral microbial traits on AR and extend previous observational evidence by reducing susceptibility to confounding and reverse causation [22]. To our knowledge, this is the first bidirectional MR investigation of the oral microbiome and AR.

Previous 16S rRNA sequencing studies have reported differences in oral microbial composition between patients with AR and healthy controls, including higher relative abundances of Porphyromonas and Prevotella in AR and higher abundances of Streptococcus and Veillonella in healthy controls. These findings should be interpreted as context-, species-, and site-specific changes in microbial abundance rather than as evidence that entire genera are intrinsically pro-inflammatory or beneficial. For example, some Prevotella taxa have been associated with airway Th17-mediated inflammation and increased local pro-inflammatory cytokine production following microaspiration, whereas other Prevotella species may behave as commensals under different ecological conditions [11,22]. Similarly, Neisseria has been reported to be enriched in some allergic populations [23], but its effects on mucosal immunity may vary according to species, abundance, anatomical niche, and host condition. Streptococcus also appears to have heterogeneous roles: depletion of certain taxa may accompany oral dysbiosis, whereas S. salivarius can adhere to the nasal epithelium and promote allergic inflammation during sensitisation [5]. This taxon-specific interpretation is consistent with our MR findings, in which different Streptococcus-related traits showed associations in opposite directions. Moreover, the association observed for Prevotella in the IVW analysis was not supported by BWMR, and therefore should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a harmful causal effect. Collectively, these findings suggest that enrichment or depletion of individual oral microbial taxa, rather than classification of an entire genus as beneficial or harmful, may be relevant to AR pathophysiology. Further functional studies are needed to clarify how specific taxa, their abundance, and their anatomical niches influence mucosal immune responses in AR.

An important feature of our findings was the taxon- and site-specific heterogeneity of the identified associations. Even within the same genus, genetically predicted microbial traits showed different directions of association with AR. For example, among tongue-dorsum microbiota, Streptococcus_mgs_1057 was inversely associated with AR risk, whereas Streptococcus_mgs_1603 showed a positive association. Similar heterogeneity was observed in saliva, where S. oralis, S. mitis_F, and several other Streptococcus-related traits were inversely associated with AR, while Streptococcus_mgs_2111 was positively associated with disease risk. Site-dependent differences were also observed for Eikenella-related traits, with a positive association for a tongue-dorsum trait but an inverse association for a salivary species. These contrasting associations may reflect differences in species identity, strain-level functional capacity, ecological niche, microbial interactions, and host mucosal conditions. Therefore, the biological relevance of oral microbiota to AR is unlikely to be adequately captured by genus-level classifications alone [24,25]. Future studies combining metagenomic sequencing, strain-level characterisation, microbial functional profiling, and site-specific sampling are needed to determine the biological mechanisms underlying these associations.

Emerging findings in microbiota–immune interaction also highlight novel therapeutic targets. One example is the immunoregulatory cytokine IL-27, which appears to be downregulated in AR and negatively correlates with disease severity [26]. IL-27 deficiency may impair Treg function and enhance Th2/IgE responses. Animal studies support its therapeutic role: intranasal recombinant IL-27 reduced AR symptoms, eosinophilic infiltration, and upregulated Foxp3+Tregs and anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. IL-10, IL-35) in nasal-associated lymphoid tissues [27]. This suggests a potential role for IL-27 in correcting oral–nasal microbiota-induced immune dysregulation. Future AR therapies may require integrated microbiota modulation—targeting oral, nasal, and gut communities to restore systemic immune tolerance.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the oral microbiome GWAS included relatively modest sample sizes, and the instrument-selection threshold was relaxed to P < 1 × 10−5 because few variants reached conventional genome-wide significance. Although all retained instruments had F-statistics >10, weak-instrument bias and winner’s-curse effects cannot be completely excluded [28]. Second, microbial traits are dynamic, compositional, and strongly influenced by diet, oral hygiene, medication use, and other environmental factors. Genetically predicted abundance therefore reflects lifelong host-related predisposition rather than short-term changes in microbial abundance or the effects of microbiome-targeted interventions [29]. Third, horizontal pleiotropy remains possible even when multiple sensitivity analyses are applied. In particular, nominal evidence of directional pleiotropy was observed for two taxa, so these associations should be interpreted cautiously [30]. Fourth, the exposure GWAS was restricted to East Asian participants, which improves ancestry consistency but may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations because microbiome composition and host genetic associations can vary across cohorts [31]. Finally, summary-level data prevented evaluation of nonlinear effects, interactions among microbial taxa, and modification by age, sex, medication, or environmental exposures. Independent replication in larger multi-ancestry cohorts, together with longitudinal, metagenomic, and experimental studies, is required before these taxa can be considered causal biomarkers or therapeutic targets.
