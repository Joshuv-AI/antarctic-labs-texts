Machine learning clustering of carotid body tumor patients by clinical features and surgical approaches
Authors: Zhan Gao, Lvfan Feng, Huaxiang Lu, Chaobing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo classify CBT patients using machine learning-based clustering analysis to identify subgroups with different risks of complications and outcomes.Methods: This retrospective, dual-center study included 127 patients who underwent CBT resection between 2003 and 2019. Clinical data were collected. Clustering analysis was performed using the Gower distance and silhouette coefficient to determine the optimal number of clusters. Clinical characteristics and outcomes were compared between phenotypes.ResultsPhenotype 1 (n = 29) consisted of patients who received preoperative embolization therapy, with a Shamblin III classification in 55.2% of cases and the shortest operative time (110 min). Phenotype 2 (n = 46) included patients with high-grade Shamblin classification without embolization therapy, characterized by longer operative times (240 min) and higher rates of vascular reconstruction (58.7%). Phenotype 3 (n = 52) comprised mainly female patients (80.8%) with Shamblin I tumors (51.9%) and the smallest tumor diameter (3 mm). Phenotype 2 had significantly higher estimated blood loss, postoperative stay, and cranial nerve injury rates compared to other phenotypes.ConclusionsPatients in Phenotype 2, with large tumors and high Shamblin classifications, had higher complication rates. Preoperative embolization combined with surgical resection was associated with lower surgical risks in these high-risk patients. This approach may provide a more precise method for identifying high-risk patients and could guide clinical decision-making.Keywords: Carotid body tumor, Machine learning, Clustering analysis, Surgical complications, Shamblin classification

Introduction

Carotid body tumor (CBT) is a neuroendocrine tumor that originates from the paraganglionic tissue surrounding the carotid bifurcation of the carotid artery and is the most common type of head and neck paraganglioma, accounting for approximately 65%. However, CBT is still a rare tumor, with an incidence rate of approximately 1–2/100,000 [1]. The Shamblin classification, which was first reported in 1971 and is still widely used today, is the most commonly used clinical classification for CBT. However, the Shamblin classification has many limitations for assessing treatment prognosis of CBT [2, 3]. Common treatments for CBT include conservative treatment with regular follow-up, radiotherapy, surgical intervention, and preoperative embolization [4]. Among these, surgical intervention is the only treatment that can definitively cure CBT [5]. Due to the close relationship between CBT and the carotid and cranial nerves, surgical complications after CBT treatment can occur at a high rate of 20–27% [6]. Surgical complications are a major challenge in the clinical treatment of CBT, and therefore, the prognosis of surgical treatment for CBT needs to be analyzed in more detail. Selecting the subgroup of CBT and analyzing the characteristics of the population treated with different surgical approaches may provide a new perspective for the diagnosis and treatment of CBT.

Cluster analysis is a statistical method that can provide better clinical phenotype classification and risk classification. It is a machine learning method that can identify differences in large amounts of clinical data in patients, classify them homogeneously, and group patients into different subtypes. This method can help clinicians identify patients with special subtypes and take precise treatment measures for different disease characteristics. This method has been used in diseases such as atrial fibrillation, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, diabetes, and deep vein thrombosis [7–10]. As far as we know, there has been no previous study on applying cluster analysis to classify CBT, this rare disease.

Therefore, in order to better classify patients with CBT, we performed machine learning-based clustering analysis on clinical data, including patient baseline data and surgical information, from CBT cases that were followed up for 16 years, and compared the surgical complications and outcomes of patients in different categories. This approach provides a more precise method for identifying high-risk patients and could potentially provide guidance for clinical practice.

Method

Study design

This is a retrospective, dual-center study conducted at Anhui Public Health Clinical Center (the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University North District) and Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital. The study included all patients who underwent CBT resection surgery between January 2003 and December 2019 in the two centers. Clinical data, including demographic data, family history, comorbidities, course of disease, location and size of CBT, preoperative embolization, Shamblin classification, operation details, hospitalization time, postoperative complications, and follow-up information, were retrospectively collected for all patients. The Shamblin classification data were obtained from imaging and/or surgical records, and were used to assess the difficulty of surgical resection [2]. To ensure consistency across the two centers, Shamblin classifications were determined using a standardized protocol based on established criteria [2], integrating preoperative imaging (magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT)) and intraoperative findings. Classifications were independently assigned by two reviewers (experienced radiologists or surgeons from each center), with any discrepancies resolved by consensus. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using Cohen's kappa statistic on a random subset of 50 cases, resulting in a kappa of 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.95), indicating strong agreement. No significant center-specific differences in classification were observed (P = 0.41). Preoperative CBT volume was estimated using MRI or CT imaging, assuming the shape of the tumor to be ellipsoidal. CBT's maximum diameter was measured using MRI, CT, or ultrasound imaging. All radiological assessments were performed by two independent reviewers. Patients were followed up every six months after surgery and underwent cervical artery duplex ultrasound scanning. Patients with missing preoperative imaging or follow-up data were excluded from the study. A total of 152 patients who underwent CBT resection during the study period were initially identified. Of these, 25 were excluded due to missing preoperative imaging (n = 18) or incomplete follow-up data (n = 7), resulting in the final cohort of 127 patients. No data imputation was performed; only patients with complete records for all clustering variables were included to ensure analysis integrity. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval for this retrospective study was granted by the Local Ethics Committee under approval number [SH9H-2021-T343-1]. The requirement for individual patient informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the data analysis.

Treatment process

Prior to CBT resection surgery, surgeons make a comprehensive decision based on the patient's condition as to whether preoperative embolization is necessary, for which there is currently no consistent indication. The timing for preoperative embolization is typically within 24 to 72 h before the surgery. A 5F catheter is inserted via the femoral artery to assess cerebral arterial supply via Willis' circle. The important feeding arteries from the external carotid artery (ECA) to the CBT are identified and occluded as much as possible with spring coils. Blood vessel imaging is performed to confirm patency of the internal carotid artery (ICA) and evaluate the effect of embolization. The surgeon judges that the embolization is successful if more than half of the tumor blood flow is blocked [11]. This success criterion is uniformly applied across both centers using post-embolization angiography for objective assessment, with documentation required in patient records. Only successful embolizations (per this threshold) are considered in the study; no failed cases are included in the final cohort, as they will typically not proceed to standard resection or be captured in our dataset. Under general anesthesia, a surgical team consisting of two or more experienced surgeons performs the CBT resection surgery. The skin is incised along the anterior margin of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, taking care to protect the vagus nerve and hypoglossal nerve during dissection of the carotid artery sheath. The common carotid artery (CCA), ECA, and ICA are dissected and controlled by slings, and the CBT is completely resected from the tail end. Heparin is administered intravenously if necessary to maintain an activated clotting time of more than 250 s if the ICA needs to be clamped during the operation [12], with systolic blood pressure maintained at 120–130 mmHg. Carotid artery shunt surgery is performed for patients with incomplete Willis circles. Carotid artery reconstruction can be achieved by using artificial grafts, great saphenous vein grafts, patch angioplasty, or end-to-end anastomosis. Staged CBT resection surgery is performed for patients with bilateral CBT.

Statistical analysis

The database is composed of mixed data types, including binary, nominal and continuous variables, such as gender, maximum diameter, and Shamblin class. Variable selection for clustering was guided by clinical relevance to CBT prognosis and surgical outcomes, as informed by existing literature (e.g., demographic factors like age and gender [13], tumor characteristics such as diameter and Shamblin classification [2, 13–15], and treatment variables like preoperative embolization and operative details [10, 16–18]). We included 12 variables (Table 1) that were consistently available across all patients in our retrospective dual-center cohort to ensure data completeness and minimize bias from missing values. Potentially relevant variables like detailed preoperative imaging metrics (e.g., vascularity scores or advanced MRI parameters) were considered but excluded due to inconsistent documentation in historical records, which could compromise clustering reliability. Therefore, the Gower distance is used to obtain the dissimilarity matrix for the 12 variables (Table 1) in this study for unsupervised hierarchical clustering analysis [19]. Use the silhouette coefficient to determine the optimal number of clusters [20]. The silhouette coefficient is an internal indicator used to measure cluster dispersion, with a value range of -1 to 1. To assess clustering stability, we performed fivefold cross-validation: the dataset was divided into 5 folds, with hierarchical clustering (Gower distance, Ward's linkage) repeated on 80% training data per fold. Cluster assignments were compared to the full model using the adjusted rand index (ARI) for reproducibility. Hierarchical clustering was performed using Ward’s linkage method, which is deterministic and not subject to random initialization, ensuring stability and reproducibility across runs.

Clinical characteristics were described as the percentage for categorical variables and medians with IQR for continuous variables. For continuous variables, we use analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis test, and for binary or nominal variables, we use chi square test or Fisher's exact test to determine differences between phenotypes. The Welch’s t test and Mann–Whitney U test were used to compare continuous data and Fisher’s exact test was used to compare categorical data from different phenotypes. For each phenotype, characteristics were labeled “predominant” when higher/lower than at least one other phenotype, and labeled “discriminant” when higher/lower than all other phenotypes [9]. Key characteristics were visually summarized by radar plots. For radar plots, continuous variables (e.g., maximum diameter and operative time) were scaled using min–max normalization: scaled_value = 100 * (value—min)/(max—min), where min and max are the dataset's minimum and maximum values for that variable. This ensures reproducibility based on the cohort data. Categorical variables were represented as percentages or binary indicators.

Associations of phenotypes with clinical outcomes were determined by comparing the differences in clinical outcomes between different phenotypes. Using the same method of comparing clinical characteristics between different phenotypes as mentioned above. To explore potential interactions involving gender (a clustering variable), post hoc logistic regression models were fitted for key outcomes (e.g., cranial nerve injury, estimated blood loss > median), including interaction terms (e.g., gender × Shamblin class, gender × embolization). Models adjusted for age and tumor diameter; non-significant interactions (P > 0.05) were not retained. To assess potential outlier influence, we examined boxplots and z-scores for continuous variables; no outliers with z-score >|3| were found, confirming that clustering was not unduly affected by extremes. In all statistical tests, a two-tailed P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. P values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Bonferroni method. Statistical analyses were conducted in R statistical software version 4.2.3.

Results

The cohort comprised 127 patients with CBT, with a predominantly female gender (69.3% female), had a median age of 47 years. All patient characteristics were complete following exclusions for missing data, as detailed in the Methods. The median follow-up duration was 48 months (IQR: 24–72 months), with no significant differences across phenotypes (Phenotype 1: 52 months [IQR: 30–78]; Phenotype 2: 45 months [IQR: 24–66]; Phenotype 3: 50 months [IQR: 28–72]; P = 0.32). Clustering identified 3 distinct phenotypes as the optimal number of clusters to fit the data structure, determined by the highest silhouette coefficient among k = 2 to k = 5 (k = 2: 0.28; k = 3: 0.37; k = 4: 0.31; k = 5: 0.26). Cross-validation confirmed good stability (mean adjusted Rand index = 0.82 across folds), supporting phenotype reproducibility. The hierarchical interrelation of these phenotypes is depicted in Fig. 1. Clinical characteristics are presented and compared between phenotypes in Table 1 and summarized in Fig. 2. Identified phenotypes

Phenotype 1 (n = 29) consisted exclusively of patients who have received embolization therapy, with Shamblin III (55.2%) being the main type. In addition, the operative time is the least compared to other phenotypes (110 min). This phenotype will further be referred to as “patients using embolization therapy”.

Phenotype 2 (n = 46) was composed of Shamblin II and III (45.7% and 54.3%), with a lower embolization treatment rate (6.5%). Its operative time is significantly longer than other phenotypes (240 min). At the same time, its proportion of undergoing vascular reconstruction was significantly higher than other phenotypes (58.7%). This phenotype will further be referred to as “patients with high-grade Shamblin class without embolization therapy”.

Phenotype 3 (n = 52) was mainly female patients (80.8%), and mainly Shamblin Ⅰpatients (51.9%). Its tumor diameter is significantly lower than other phenotypes (3 mm). This phenotype will further be referred to as “women with Shamblin Ⅰ”. Post hoc analysis confirmed no significant gender interactions with other variables on outcomes (e.g., gender × Shamblin class: P = 0.21 for cranial nerve injury; gender × embolization: P = 0.34 for blood loss), suggesting clustering captured gender’s prognostic role [13] through phenotype delineation.

Association of phenotypes with clinical outcomes

The median estimated blood loss in phenotype 2 was 215 mL (range, 50–650 mL), a statistically significant increase compared with phenotype 3. The median length of postoperative stay in phenotype 2 was 5 days (range, 3–7 days), which was longer than all other phenotypes and was statistically significant. The incidence of cranial nerve injury in phenotype 2 was 67.4%, which was statistically higher than all other phenotypes. The incidence of hematoma in phenotype 2 was 15.2%, which was statistically higher than that in phenotype 3. Wound infection, stroke, relapse and death did not differ among the three phenotypes (Table 2). These differences suggest an association between the lack of embolization in high-risk patients (Phenotype 2) and elevated complication rates, though causation cannot be inferred due to the observational design. Relapse (tumor recurrence confirmed by ultrasound or imaging) rates were low overall (3.9%) and similar across phenotypes (Phenotype 1: 3.4% [1/29]; Phenotype 2: 6.5% [3/46]; Phenotype 3: 1.9% [1/52]; P = 0.41). Death rates were also low (overall 1.6%) with no differences (Phenotype 1: 0% [0/29]; Phenotype 2: 2.2% [1/46]; Phenotype 3: 1.9% [1/52]; P = 0.72). These long-term outcomes suggest clustering predicts perioperative rather than late complications.

Discussion

Fueled by the rapid expansion of artificial intelligence, machine learning has emerged as a pivotal subdomain that automatically and with high precision recognizes, extracts, classifies, and predicts complex, high-dimensional data. These capabilities now permeate basic biomedical science, drug discovery, and clinical research. Algorithms trained on nanomechanical signatures from atomic-force microscopy distinguish cells from extracellular-matrix components such as collagen with 100% accuracy and dynamically resolve their interactions [21]. Integration of multiparametric cardiac-microtissue data enables machine-learning models to categorize the myocardial mechanisms of unknown compounds at 86.2% accuracy, markedly enhancing pre-clinical cardiotoxicity screening [22]. Predictive algorithms identify patients with a history of drug allergy who are at high risk of progressing to drug-induced liver injury and subsequent liver failure [23]. By synthesizing multi-omics datasets, machine learning pinpoints and validates immune-related genes underlying epilepsy–depression comorbidity, elucidating pathogenesis and revealing therapeutic targets [24]. Likewise, fusion of imaging, histopathology, and multi-omics data accurately forecasts both response to cancer immunotherapy and impending immune-related adverse events, providing efficient, reproducible decision support for individualized treatment [25]. Collectively, machine learning functions as the core engine linking multi-omics big data to network pharmacology, propelling drug discovery from empirical trial-and-error toward data-driven intelligence and enabling rapid, personalized prediction and validation of targets, pathways, and therapeutics [26, 27]. Despite its widespread use for dissecting clinical phenotypes, cluster analysis, as a common machine learning method, has not yet been applied to CBT research.

We used cluster analysis to categorize the baseline data and surgical treatment strategies of patients with CBT to identify special baseline data and surgical treatment strategies associated with complications such as bleeding, hospitalization time, neurological complications, and hematoma. In this study, we identified three phenotypes and found that each different phenotype had a different correlation with complications. Notably, variables like operative time showed marked differences across phenotypes, but our sensitivity checks confirmed no significant outlier influence on clustering, as the Gower distance metric accommodates such variability in mixed datasets. While phenotypes differed in perioperative risks, long-term outcomes like relapse and death showed no significant differences, indicating that clustering is particularly useful for predicting short-term surgical complications in this curative procedure.

In our analysis results, Phenotype 3 had the lowest risk. In this clinical phenotype, the majority of patients were female, and Shamblin I type was the highest proportion while Shamblin III type was the least. This gender imbalance aligns with CBT epidemiology and ref [13], which suggests gender impacts prognosis (e.g., potentially better outcomes in females); our clustering implicitly captured such interactions by grouping females with favorable tumor features. The size of CBT has a significant impact on surgical resection, and Shamblin I and II types can be removed by careful dissection, while Shamblin III type often requires excision of ECA or ICA. The lower the Shamblin classification level, the lower the risk of vascular damage and bleeding [1, 2, 13–15]. Although patients with this phenotype did not undergo preoperative embolization, because of the smallest CBT diameter and low Shamblin classification level, the surgical difficulty was reduced, and there was minimal bleeding during the surgery, and there were no hematomas after the surgery.

Patients with large diameter CBT and high shamblin classification levels were divided into two phenotypes. Phenotype 1 patients were all treated with preoperative embolization combined with surgical resection. In the other phenotype, Phenotype 2, which included patients with large diameter tumors and high shamblin grading, the majority of patients received simple surgical resection. These patients had a significantly higher incidence of surgical complications, including bleeding, postoperative hospital stay, cranial nerve injury, and hematoma, compared to other subgroups. In 1980, Schick et al. reported the first case of preoperative embolization combined with surgical resection in a CBT patient [28]. Since then, the discussion about preoperative embolization has continued, but consensus has not been reached. Due to the rich blood supply and peculiar location of CBT, surgeons hope to reduce tumor blood supply and improve surgical safety by preoperative embolization, which has been confirmed in some studies [11, 16–18]. In Power's study, preoperative embolization significantly simplified the surgical procedure (simple excision rate 97% vs. 82%, ICA clipping rate 15% vs. 37%, p = 0.04) and reduced blood loss (263 ml vs. 599 ml, p = 0.002) compared to simple surgical resection [11]. In Zhang's study, preoperative embolization significantly reduced blood loss (80 ml vs. 200 ml, P = 0.001) and shortened the operation time (120 min vs. 160 min, P = 0.006) compared to simple surgical resection [16]. However, some studies have shown that preoperative embolization did not show advantages in intraoperative bleeding, operation time, and perioperative complications [29–32], and even prolonged the hospital stay of CBT patients [29, 30]. There are three meta-analyses on the safety effects of preoperative embolization on CBT surgery [17, 18, 33]. Among them, two studies have shown that preoperative embolization can significantly reduce intraoperative bleeding and surgery time [17, 18]. Another study showed that preoperative embolization had no advantage in CBT resection both intraoperatively and postoperatively [33]. These conflicting findings in meta-analyses may arise from the aggregation of heterogeneous patient populations, where benefits in specific subgroups (e.g., high-risk cases with large tumors or advanced Shamblin grades) are diluted by inclusion of low-risk patients who may not require embolization. Baseline imbalances, selection biases, and variability in embolization techniques across studies further contribute to inconsistencies. However, there is still a lack of high-level evidence on the value of preoperative embolization. Due to the different diameters of CBT, different Shamblin classifications, and the selection of embolization and resection surgery plans based on the unique situations of patients, it is difficult to avoid bias caused by differences in baseline data between groups using retrospective controlled studies alone. We have used cluster analysis to demonstrate that, from another perspective, for CBT patients with large diameters and high Shamblin classifications, the combined use of preoperative embolization and surgery resection was associated with lower surgical risks and may offer benefits in reducing complications. For clinical implementation, our phenotypes could inform a simple scoring system: e.g., assign points for high-risk features (Shamblin class ≥ II: + 2 points; tumor diameter > 3 cm: + 1 point; no embolization: + 1 point; male gender: + 1 point), with scores ≥ 3 indicating high risk (similar to Phenotype 2) warranting embolization consideration. This could be developed into a decision tree or nomogram, integrable into electronic health records for preoperative stratification, facilitating personalized decision-making. Machine learning-based clustering reveals these subgroup-specific associations by identifying latent phenotypes from multidimensional data, capturing interactions (e.g., between embolization, tumor size, and Shamblin class) that traditional analyses often overlook due to their reliance on predefined groupings or univariate comparisons. This explains why our approach highlights benefits in high-risk phenotypes (e.g., Phenotype 1 vs. 2) not consistently evident in aggregated meta-analytic data. To build on this, future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) could use our identified phenotypes for patient stratification or inclusion criteria, such as randomizing embolization versus no embolization specifically in Phenotype 2-like patients (large tumors, high Shamblin grades without prior embolization). This targeted design would reduce heterogeneity, enhance statistical power, and provide high-level evidence on embolization's causal effects in subgroups where it may be most beneficial.

This study has several limitations. As a retrospective analysis, our findings are observational and demonstrate associations rather than causal relationships, introducing potential biases such as recall bias (from historical records) and selection bias (e.g., in embolization decisions based on clinical judgment influenced by tumor size, Shamblin classification, and patient factors) without randomization. Potential confounding variables, such as surgeon experience, unmeasured comorbidities, or institutional protocols, could also affect outcomes. The dual-center design, while enhancing generalizability, may introduce variability in surgical practices between sites and limits broader applicability, with center-specific biases possible (e.g., differences in expertise between otorhinolaryngology and vascular surgery focuses, or regional patient demographics). Center was not included as a clustering variable to avoid overfitting, but post hoc stratification showed no significant outcome differences (e.g., estimated blood loss, P = 0.12; cranial nerve injury, P = 0.08). Although our sample size (n = 127) is reasonable for a rare tumor like CBT, the exploratory nature of unsupervised machine learning clustering—used here to identify patterns in mixed data rather than build predictive models—necessitates caution in interpretation, as it does not establish causality or prognostic accuracy. Internal cross-validation indicated stability (mean ARI = 0.82), but external validation on independent cohorts is essential to confirm generalizability and precision. The embolization success criterion (> 50% blood flow reduction) adds subjectivity, potentially biasing phenotype assignment (Phenotype 1 included only successful cases); standardized metrics could mitigate this in future work. While follow-up was consistent (every 6 months with ultrasound, median 48 months), our focus on perioperative outcomes leaves room for larger studies to assess long-term predictions (e.g., late recurrence). Overall, prospective, randomized, multi-center studies are needed to validate these exploratory findings, evaluate causal effects (e.g., of embolization), and test clinical utility.
