A Giant Free-Floating Left Atrial Thrombus With a Flipper Effect
Authors: Lorenzo Niro, Gianluca Sclafani, Teresa Cammelli, Anna Maria Traini, Riccardo Codecasa, Pierluigi Stefano, Anna Toso

ABSTRACT

History of Presentation

A 77-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with worsening dyspnea. She had recently been scheduled for permanent pacemaker implantation because of bradycardic atrial fibrillation (AF) associated with progressive symptoms. After admission to the Cardiology Department, she underwent single-chamber pacemaker implantation. Vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy was not interrupted for pacemaker implantation. To limit the risk of device-pocket bleeding, the international normalized ratio (INR) was maintained at 2.2 periprocedurally and was promptly restored to >2.5 thereafter. Before discharge, transthoracic echocardiography unexpectedly demonstrated a large mass within the left atrium (LA), and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was performed for further characterization. The clinical timeline is summarized in the Visual Summary.

Past Medical History

The patient presented systemic and pulmonary hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and hypothyroidism. She had undergone previous bileaflet mechanical mitral valve replacement and tricuspid annuloplasty and had permanent AF on long-term VKA therapy. Her prescribed INR range was 2.5 to 3.5 (target 3.0), consistent with contemporary guideline recommendations for a mechanical mitral prosthesis in a patient with thromboembolic risk factors.1 Anticoagulation had otherwise remained within the therapeutic range; however, 2 home INR measurements obtained during the month before admission were less than 2.0. She also had a history of systemic thromboembolism.

Differential Diagnosis

The principal differential diagnosis of the LA mass was between thrombus and myxoma. The large size, marked mobility, heterogeneous appearance, and initial impression of limited wall attachment raised suspicion for an atrial myxoma. However, the clinical substrate, including permanent AF, severe LA enlargement, dense spontaneous echo contrast, reduced left atrial appendage (LAA) emptying velocities, previous mechanical mitral valve replacement, and reported recent subtherapeutic anticoagulation strongly favored a diagnosis of thrombus.

Investigations

Initial TEE showed a markedly enlarged LA with dense spontaneous echo contrast. The LAA showed spontaneous echo contrast and low emptying velocities, without definite appendage thrombosis. A large heterogeneous, vacuolated, mobile mass measuring approximately 45 × 35 mm was identified along the anterolateral LA wall (Figure 1A, Video 1). No clear pedicle was demonstrated. The mechanical mitral prosthesis was functioning normally. Because thrombus was considered the most likely diagnosis, VKA therapy was suspended and intravenous unfractionated heparin was started. Repeat TEE was performed to reassess the mass after 72 hours. The mass was slightly larger (49 × 35 mm), no longer attached to the lateral LA wall, and appeared freely mobile within the LA cavity (Figure 1B, Video 2). Three-dimensional (3D) TEE was instrumental in visualizing the dynamic interaction between the mass and the prosthetic mitral discs (Video 3). The mass produced a “flipper effect,” defined as repeated oscillation of the free-floating thrombus with intermittent impingement on the mechanical mitral prosthetic discs, without an increase in transprosthetic gradients. Preoperative contrast-enhanced thoracoabdominal computed tomography showed marked left atrial dilatation and a 45 × 35 mm oval LA mass. The mass was heterogeneously hyperattenuating on unenhanced images, with peripheral microcalcifications and no postcontrast enhancement (Figure 2). In the context of the clinical substrate and serial echocardiographic findings, these features favored thrombus over myxoma.

Computed Tomography Characterization of the Left Atrial Mass

Unenhanced and postcontrast thoracoabdominal computed tomography scans showed marked left atrial enlargement and a 45 × 35-mm left atrial mass without postcontrast enhancement, supporting thrombus in the clinical and echocardiographic context. Axial views (A, B, D) and a coronal reconstruction (C) showed the spatial relationship between the mass, the left atrium, and the mechanical mitral prosthesis.

Management

Given the large size of the mass, its free-floating behavior, the potential risk of systemic embolization, and intermittent interaction with the mechanical mitral prosthesis, the cardiac surgery team was urgently consulted. The patient was transferred to a tertiary cardiac surgery center and underwent urgent redo surgery. Intraoperative inspection revealed a large LA mass with macroscopic features consistent with thrombus (Figure 3). The mechanical mitral prosthesis was carefully inspected and showed preserved disc excursion, without macroscopic thrombotic involvement of the hinge regions or evidence of mechanical dysfunction. The mass was removed, and the prosthesis was preserved rather than replaced.

Operative Specimen of the Giant Left Atrial Thrombus

Intraoperative specimen obtained during urgent redo cardiac surgery showing a large left atrial mass removed from the left atrial cavity. Its macroscopic appearance was consistent with thrombus, in agreement with the clinical context and findings on serial 2-dimensional and 3-dimensional transesophageal echocardiography and contrast-enhanced computed tomography. The large size and irregular morphology of the thrombus correlated with the dynamic free-floating behavior observed on repeat imaging.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was favorable, without neurological events. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function, no significant pericardial effusion, a normally functioning mechanical mitral prosthesis, and no residual images suggestive of LA thrombosis. The patient was transferred to a cardiac rehabilitation facility a few days after surgery.

Discussion

This case highlights the diagnostic implications of serial imaging in patients with LA masses and previous mechanical mitral valve replacement. First, the differential diagnosis between a giant LA thrombus and myxoma can be challenging.2 The distinction may be difficult when the mass is large, heterogeneous, highly mobile, and apparently attached to the atrial wall.3 In this patient, the pretest probability of thrombosis was high because of permanent AF, severe LA enlargement, dense spontaneous echo contrast, low LAA velocities, mechanical mitral prosthesis, and possible recent subtherapeutic anticoagulation.4

Second, serial TEE changed the perceived risk. The transition from an apparently wall-adherent mass to a free-floating LA mass suggested an unstable thrombus with immediate embolic potential.5 3D-TEE further clarified the spatial relationship between the thrombus and the mechanical mitral prosthesis.

Third, preserved transprosthetic gradients are not necessarily reassuring.6 Although no hemodynamically significant prosthetic obstruction was present at the time of imaging, the thrombus interacted intermittently with the mechanical prosthetic discs. This finding prompted urgent surgery because of the potential risk of sudden obstruction or systemic embolization.

Finally, multimodality imaging reinforced the diagnosis. TEE provided real-time assessment of mobility, attachment, and valve interaction, whereas computed tomography demonstrated lack of contrast enhancement, supporting thrombus rather than tumor. Surgical inspection showed a large LA mass with macroscopic features consistent with thrombus, further supporting the diagnosis suggested by the clinical context and multimodality imaging. From a therapeutic perspective, mechanical aspiration could be considered in highly selected patients with left-sided intracardiac thrombi when surgical risk is prohibitive or surgery is not immediately feasible. However, experience with this approach remains limited, and relevant concerns include systemic embolization, incomplete thrombus removal, and interference with the mechanical mitral prosthesis.7 In our patient, the large and highly mobile thrombus, its intermittent impingement on the prosthetic discs, and the feasibility of redo surgery favored surgical removal under direct visualization.

The decision to preserve the mechanical prosthesis was based on its normal intraoperative appearance and preserved disc motion, without macroscopic thrombus involving the hinge regions. Given the exceptional thrombotic burden, replacement with a bioprosthesis was subsequently discussed by the multidisciplinary heart team. However, in the absence of prosthetic dysfunction, the anticipated additional risk of valve replacement in this urgent redo setting was considered to outweigh its potential benefit. Conversion to a bioprosthesis was therefore reserved for a possible recurrent thrombotic event.

Conclusions

In patients with mechanical mitral prostheses and severe LA remodeling, thrombus may mimic myxoma and may rapidly change its mobility pattern. Serial 2-dimensional and 3D TEE can identify transformation into a free-floating thrombus and guide timely surgical management.

Take-Home Message

•Serial TEE can transform the evaluation of a left atrial mass from diagnostic uncertainty to urgent risk stratification by demonstrating dynamic changes in attachment and mobility.
