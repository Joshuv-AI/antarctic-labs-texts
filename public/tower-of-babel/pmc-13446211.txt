A curious instance of an immediate abandoned “delayed” immediate DIEP
Authors: Siri B. Gowda, Vikram Sinha, Pennylouise Hever, Charlotte L. Bendon, Martin E. Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEP) flap is the gold standard for breast reconstruction. While vascular delay has been described in the literature as a means of salvaging DIEP flaps that show evidence of compromise on the abdomen, or to recruit additional abdominal tissue, this is not routine clinical practice.We describe a case where immediate bilateral breast reconstruction was abandoned following complete raise of both hemi-DIEP flaps to the level of the venous confluence, due to unforeseen circumstances. Flaps were re-raised and successfully transferred 10 weeks after the primary surgery.The authors report that scarring surrounding the vascular pedicle following such significant length of delay, together with seroma, can facilitate repeat dissection, however, greater care is required when insetting to avoid twisting or kinking of the more rigid pedicle.

Introduction

The deep inferior epigastric artery perforator (DIEP) flap is widely considered as the gold standard for autologous breast reconstruction, providing pliable tissue with limited donor site morbidity.1 The vascular delay phenomenon is the practice of rendering a flap partially ischaemic prior to transfer. This can improve vascularity by stimulating dilatation of “choke” vessels that connect adjacent vascular territories and has been employed in flaps that show evidence of vascular insufficiency. When surgical delay is used, the second stage is usually performed within 2 weeks, as inflammation incited by the initial operation may increase the difficulty of subsequent dissection.2

We describe a case where immediate reconstruction was abandoned after both hemi-DIEP flaps were fully raised. The flaps were re-elevated and successfully transferred 10 weeks later. To our knowledge this is the first case of its kind to be described in the literature.

Case report

A 72-year-old woman with invasive ductal carcinoma and BRCA1 mutation presented for bilateral mastectomies and immediate reconstruction. During surgery she developed multiple arrythmias, resulting in need for early discontinuation. By the time both mastectomies were completed, both hemi-DIEP flaps had been raised to the level of the venous confluence. The muscle and sheath were closed, and the flaps sutured back to the abdomen. Following discharge, the patient was unable to return for a second operation until 10 weeks later. During this interval she attended outpatient clinic five times for abdominal seroma drainage.

At second operation there was a doppler signal from the previously isolated abdominal wall perforators. Significant seroma was found in both the abdomen and mastectomy pockets. The flap pedicles were encased in rubbery scar tissue throughout their length, facilitating repeat dissection (Fig. 1). Microvascular anastomosis was performed without complication in an anterograde fashion to the internal mammary artery and vein bilaterally with 8/0 nylon, and 2.5 mm couplers were used for venous anastomoses. The right-sided flap became congested following inset due to kinking of the pedicle within the scar tissue - akin to kinking of a garden hosepipe. As a result, a second retrograde venous anastomosis was performed, and the pedicle repositioned within a pectoralis groove to straighten out the pedicle.3Fig. 1Photo demonstrating thickened sheath around vascular pedicles at second dissection.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Discussion

“Delayed immediate” is a term given to performing a DIEP 2–3 weeks after a mastectomy and temporary expander, once the histology is known.4 The novelty of this case is that delay was unplanned, without evidence of flap compromise. Full flap raise was completed at the initial surgery, and the length of time between operations spanned 10 weeks.

Paradigms for microsurgical reconstruction developed in extremity trauma dictate that optimal timings for second surgery are within 10 days or after 12 weeks, in order to avoid peak inflammation.5 Although our second operation was performed during this period, we did not notice increased difficulty in dissection or increased rate of complications. This may be because a surgical insult, unlike trauma, tends to respect anatomical planes. While neither of our flaps were congested prior to detachment, the right hemi-DIEP was congested following inset due to kinking of the pedicle within the circumferential scar tissue, and compression from the weight of the flap resulting in a twisted hosepipe-like blockage – emphasising the need for meticulous inset.

Shakir et al. reported a series of 215 DIEP flaps that were intentionally delayed with suprafascial isolation of a single dominant perforator and expander placement. Subfascial dissection was performed 2 weeks later at the time of transfer.2 Similarly, Beugels et al. described a series of 82 cases that utilised delay to recruit volume for extended DIEP flap reconstruction, or in patients with significant scarring or previous liposuction. They also describe suprafascial perforator isolation, with intramuscular dissection only performed at second operation. Nevertheless, they also found that cases with significant time delay demonstrated thickening of the rectus sheath associated with chronic seroma.1 In our case, as complete flap raise was performed at primary surgery, this thickening extended along the full length of the pedicle, which facilitated repeat dissection.

Conclusion

We present a case of immediate abandoned “delayed” immediate DIEP reconstruction, demonstrating that in instances where operations are stopped due to unanticipated circumstances following full flap raise, DIEP flaps can safely be re-raised and transferred to the chest at a second operation. It is our recommendation that if similar cases are encountered, greater care is taken in insetting to avoid kinking and compression of the more rigid pedicle.
