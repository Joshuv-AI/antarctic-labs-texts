Rethinking the concept of disease modification in an evolving Parkinson's disease treatment landscape
Authors: Angelo Antonini, Robert E Gross, Maria De Leon, Kevin Kwok, Tom Foltynie, Günter Höglinger, Thomas Gasser, Jean-Christophe Corvol, David J. Pedrosa, Jens Volkmann, Claire Henchcliffe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We are in an era in which increasing numbers of potentially disease-modifying treatments for Parkinson's disease are being tested in clinical development. Current definitions of disease modification have inherent limitations; hence there is a need to establish a clear, unified definition. This unified definition of disease modification should be applicable to both idiopathic and genetic forms of Parkinson's disease. In addition to motor function, it must address important milestones (e.g., cognition, autonomic dysfunction) across all disease stages. This review examines existing definitions and highlights the need for a more holistic approach that integrates the perspectives of patients, healthcare professionals, and researchers.

Introduction

Currently, the standard of care for Parkinson's disease is based on oral dopamine replacement therapy and other measures, including non-dopaminergic oral medications, non-oral dopamine replacement, and surgical interventions to symptomatically treat typical motor and non-motor features. 1 While levodopa and other dopaminergic medications provide effective initial control of motor manifestations, their benefit significantly lessens over time. As Parkinson's disease progresses, patients frequently experience inadequate control of cardinal motor features (tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia) and develop axial features, such as disturbances of speech, gait, and balance. Furthermore, treatment-related motor complications, such as wearing-off phenomena with fluctuations in motor function and levodopa-induced dyskinesias, commonly emerge and cause additional functional impairment and reduce quality of life.2–4 Concurrently, non-motor features, such as fatigue, sleep disturbances, pain, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and cognitive deficits, become increasingly severe and are often refractory to current therapeutic interventions. These escalating challenges profoundly worsen quality of life, increase caregiver burden, and contribute significantly to the morbidity and mortality associated with advanced Parkinson's disease.4–7

With the inevitable progression of disease, including difficult-to-treat motor and non-motor features, the ability to modify the progression of Parkinson's disease remains a key unmet need and a priority for patients, clinicians, and scientists. Novel disease-modifying therapies may target the underlying pathology of Parkinson's disease with the aim of addressing all clinically important disease manifestations, or may aim to preserve or restore specific functions, such as dopaminergic control of movement. Interventions aimed at disease modification may therefore include approaches that either directly address the core biological mechanisms of the disease, indirectly activate compensatory pathways, or restore lost cells with varying impact on the underlying molecular basis of the disease.8–12

To advance this field, it would be of great value to establish a holistic definition of disease modification to meaningfully assess the potential of new disease-modifying therapies. This review explores existing definitions, assessment methods, and therapies in clinical development, while also considering patient and physician perspectives on what constitutes meaningful disease modification.

Current definitions of disease modification

Recent expert opinions have considered a clinically applicable definition of disease-modifying therapy focused on patients’ own pathophysiological processes, symptoms, and signs. This includes treatments that alter disease progression by either addressing pathophysiological processes directly, improving compensatory responses, or reducing the severity and/or propagation of pathology in other ways (Figure 1).8,13 Therefore, a treatment that delays, halts, reverses, or prevents progression of symptoms, and that can be reasonably ascribed to biological or structural changes in the brain, may be considered to have modified the disease, regardless of whether it has targeted its primary root causes. 13 We propose a reconsideration of interventions, encompassing a return to a holistic and inclusive consideration of what is considered disease modification. This should include not only a rigorous research perspective, but also broader viewpoints from healthcare professionals and, importantly, people with lived experience of Parkinson's disease. We believe that, in this way, a meaningful definition that can be used in clinical trials will advance our progress and support strategic pathways of engagement with regulatory agencies.

Varying definitions of disease modification and their relationships.

It is also important to incorporate into a new definition that the specific aims of disease modification in Parkinson's disease will vary with disease stages. In prodromal Parkinson's disease, preventing or delaying the first manifestation of motor and non-motor features should be considered disease modifying. Similarly, once motor symptoms manifest, a valuable aim may be reversing or delaying progression with a focus on increased functionality, preserved autonomy, and improved quality of life. In later stages, disease modification might be the prevention of motor complications of levodopa therapy or further decline, including dementia, severe disability, and premature mortality.

The importance of constructing and operationalizing a definition of a treatment that is disease modifying versus symptomatic is complicated by the knowledge that effective “symptomatic” approaches may achieve similar aims in at least some of these areas. Therefore, when evaluating disease modification in clinical trials, perceived improvements may be caused by direct or indirect effects on underlying disease mechanisms and symptoms. For instance, deep brain stimulation and dopamine infusion therapies may provide control of dopamine-responsive features but do not constitute direct disease modification, as the core neuronal loss continues.3,14,15 While long-term observational studies suggest that deep brain stimulation delays the onset of late-stage disability milestones and improves quality of life, it does not modify the progressive pathobiology of Parkinson's disease. 3

Challenges in assessing disease modification in Parkinson's disease

Although the goal of preventing or curing Parkinson's disease may not be within immediate reach,1,16,17 varying degrees of disease modification may still be attainable. Consideration of how to measure disease modification based upon a newly evolving definition will therefore be critical, and a lack of appropriate disease progression endpoints may have played a role in the negative results in previous clinical trials.

Importantly, the need for a flexible approach is mandated due to the heterogeneity of Parkinson's disease, both with respect to clinical features and etiology. Stratifying study participants by clinical phenotype, symptomatology, and/or staging by biological frameworks could benefit the ability of clinical trials to confirm or exclude the potential of new disease-modifying therapies. This means that clinical and biomarker measures therefore may not be a “one size fits all” approach.

The first key challenge in trial design is to distinguish between symptomatic and disease-modifying effects of the investigational treatment. In addition to established trial designs aimed at detecting disease-modifying treatment effects, such as washout, delayed-start, randomized-withdrawal and long-term, parallel group designs, more recent adaptive approaches such as multi-arm, multi-stage/platform trials may improve efficiency by enabling multiple candidate therapies or doses to be evaluated within a shared infrastructure.13,18 However, in addition to trial design, defining and selecting the right endpoints may play an equally important role in a trial's ability to detect disease modification. Commonly reported clinical outcome measures used as primary endpoints have focused upon motor symptoms and include the assessment of disease severity with the Movement Disorder Society-Sponsored Revision of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) or one of its subscales, and these can be influenced longitudinally by symptomatic treatment and/or disease modification. A well-known example is the double-blind, delayed-start trial of high and low doses of rasagiline, which demonstrated that the low dose met primary efficacy endpoints for disease modification, whereas the high dose did not. 19 Investigators speculated that the symptomatic benefit of the higher dose masked the disease modification signal, but this was impossible to verify given the clinical trial data.

Moreover, and unfortunately, demonstrating disease modification using slowly progressing changes in clinical outcomes as assessed using the MDS-UPDRS, as in the rasagiline ADAGIO study, necessitates longer clinical observations than traditional trials testing symptomatic efficacy, which raises multiple challenges, including patient retention and accounting for inevitable concomitant medication changes. 19 Ideally, clinical observations will be supported using biomarker-based outcomes that will reliably reflect disease modification. The richest experience in incorporating biomarkers into clinical trials testing disease modification has been with neuroimaging, in particular nuclear medicine techniques (including positron emission tomography and single-photon emission computed tomography), which offer minimally invasive evaluation of molecular targets, neurotransmitters, dopaminergic nerve loss, and network activity, providing the promise to possibly support evaluation of potential disease-modifying therapies. 20 These have been reviewed recently. 21

Although not yet in use as primary endpoints in Parkinson's disease clinical trials evaluating disease-modifying therapies, integrated patient staging systems have been proposed to incorporate validated biomarkers, such as characterized α-synuclein seed amplification assays, of defined sensitivity and specificity classifications, for detecting patients in the prodromal phase of the disease, and incorporating imaging via a dopamine transporter scan.13,17,22–24 However, significant shortcomings in applying staging systems based on α-synuclein aggregation persist, including the need to meet or exceed current sensitivity and specificity standards, while simultaneously considering the wide array of clinical presentations and the heterogeneity of Parkinson's disease progression. 25 It requires either a cerebrospinal fluid sample or a skin biopsy, and this testing is not yet validated as a suitable endpoint for clinical trials. Furthermore, it may be premature for the staging systems to solely emphasize α-synuclein aggregation, given that it has been increasingly recognized that patients with Parkinson's disease exhibit multiple co-pathologies, including amyloid-β, hyperphosphorylated tau, and transactive response DNA-binding protein 43.

Rather, an adaptable and responsive framework is needed to harmonize future clinical and scientific efforts in Parkinson's disease. 25 Once validated, and this may be done in various stages and various cohorts, such classification and staging of patients may offer a biologic construct of an operational system to treat patients who are more likely to benefit from specific treatments targeted to a defined biological process, as early as possible, and to accelerate the development of disease-modifying therapies. Another approach, such as quantified cell loss may not be easily evaluated during life or easily correlated to symptom development in cohorts of those with Parkinson's disease, which could instead reinforce the need for research using medical imaging.10,11,26

To ensure that biological shifts translate into tangible improvements for the individual, biomarkers must be calibrated against patient-reported outcomes and clinical scales using anchor-based methods. Under our proposed holistic definition, these biomarkers should be integrated with significant disease milestones—such as the onset of falls, emergence of dementia, or admission to long-term care—alongside objective digital health assessments. By shifting the focus toward these high-impact events and real-world functional data, the evaluation of disease-modifying therapies can move beyond generic clinical scales. This allows for a more personalized assessment framework that accounts for disease subtype, stage, and person-specific priorities, ensuring that “modification” is measured by its meaningful impact on a patient's life trajectory. 10

Finally, individuals with genetic forms of Parkinson's disease, or specific biomarker profiles denoting a particular dominant pathophysiology, may benefit from distinct disease modification assessments (for example, clinical trials of leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 [LRRK2] inhibitors, β-glucocerebrosidase enhancers, or PINK1 or Parkin gene therapy), as the biological mechanisms in these cases may be addressed directly using, for example, fluid-based biomarkers. In addition, accumulating evidence suggests that in subsets of patients without known genetic risk variants, certain cellular phenotypes or biomarkers may indicate the involvement of these pathways in disease pathogenesis and thus may help to stratify patients for pathway-specific treatments.27,28 For example, centrosomal cohesion deficit in blood cells of a subset of patients with Parkinson's disease supports the use of this biomarker to identify patients who may benefit from LRRK2-related therapeutics. 29 Regardless of the original disease trigger, individually tailored treatment strategies can be developed to target specific and known aberrant pathways in genetically caused Parkinson's disease.

In summary, while a treatment may not be considered disease-modifying based solely on the basis of biomarkers, we consider that appropriate biomarkers will add considerable value to developing disease modifying therapies that will have meaningful impact on patients. Their role will be key for distinguishing whether the impact of some therapies is symptomatic or disease-modifying.

The therapeutic pipeline for Parkinson's disease is currently undergoing a paradigm shift, moving away from late-stage symptom management toward early biological intervention targeting multiple points of the neurodegenerative cascade. Targeting causal mechanisms and immunomodulation

Current trials, such as those for the monoclonal antibody prasinezumab, utilize immunotherapy to bind and clear toxic aggregates.33,52 Other “upstream” approaches include small molecules targeting the LRRK2 pathway (e.g., BIIB122) and antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) designed to degrade LRRK2 messenger ribonucleic acid.36,53 These therapies aim to stop the initial triggers of cell death before significant neuronal loss occurs.

Anti-inflammatory and inflammation-related mechanisms

Recognizing that neuroinflammation accelerates Parkinson's disease progression, researchers are investigating GLP-1 receptor agonists (originally for diabetes) like lixisenatide and exenatide for their neuroprotective properties.39,40,54 Additionally, novel inhibitors of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 inflammasome are being tested to reduce the chronic immune activation in the brain that contributes to ongoing neurotoxicity.41–43

Restorative and regenerative interventions

For more advanced stages, the focus shifts to restoration. Cell therapies, such as bemdaneprocel (stem-cell-derived dopaminergic progenitors), aim to replace lost neurons in the putamen.55,56 Parallel to this, gene therapies use viral vectors (adeno-associated virus [AAV]) to either deliver growth factors like glial cell line–derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) to support surviving neurons or to deliver enzymes such as glucosylceramidase to restore lysosomal function and attenuate α-synuclein aggregation.50,51

The need for a new definition for disease modification

Traditional clinical trials in Parkinson's disease have relied almost exclusively on motor scales, like the MDS-UPDRS, to determine success. However, our review highlights that these measures often fail to distinguish between a temporary “symptomatic boost” and a fundamental change in the disease course. A new, holistic definition is helpful because it incorporates biological target engagement, sustained divergence of clinical paths, and patient-centered milestones.

Below are three specific examples of how this redefined concept changes the way we evaluate modern therapies, in our opinion.

Under traditional definitions, monoclonal antibody trials have often been labeled as failures because they did not significantly lower motor scores within one year. However, a new definition focused on pathological slowing may be proposed. If an antibody successfully reduces the accumulation of toxic α-synuclein or slows the rate of dopamine transporter loss on imaging, it is “modifying the disease” even if symptoms remain stable in some patients. Longer observation periods may also be needed to show benefit in more aggressive disease, in which slowing may only occur after the 2- or 3-year mark.

Cell therapies like bemdaneprocel pose a unique challenge because they add new dopamine-producing cells to the brain and create a symptomatic improvement that resembles that achieved by higher doses of levodopa. A traditional definition might fail to distinguish this from a drug effect. A new, holistic definition is helpful here because it shifts the focus to long-term integration and durability. Success is redefined as the survival of the graft (confirmed by F-DOPA positron emission tomography) and the postponement of late-stage milestones, such as the onset of “OFF” periods or dyskinesia, which would typically emerge as the native disease progresses. As such, cell therapies may align primarily with the group of disease-modifying treatments that halt, delay, or reverse progression (see Figure 1).

Gene therapies such as ametefgene parvec (AAV-GDNF) aim to reprogram neural brain circuits. Because these changes are structural and permanent, they may not show the “washout” effect seen with other treatments. A new definition of disease modification is helpful if it captures real-world functional shifts. Gene therapies may align most closely with the group of disease-modifying treatments that provide a durable delay of progression (see Figure 1).

By moving toward a definition that combines biological proof (biomarkers), clinical duration (sustained divergence), and functional relevance (patient-reported outcomes), the Parkinson's disease research community can better evaluate these advanced therapies. This approach ensures that we do not overlook treatments that are successfully protecting the brain simply because they do not act as “instant” symptom relievers.

Patient, physician, and regulatory perspectives of disease modification

In developing disease-modifying therapies, it is essential to consider both the acceptable time frame and clinically meaningful magnitude of effect. This necessitates addressing a complex array of issues requiring input from diverse stakeholders, including movement disorder specialists, health economists, and individuals with lived experience. Incorporating perspectives from patients, clinicians, and regulatory authorities is critical for guiding the future development of disease-modifying therapies.

Patient perspective

The involvement of patients in the clinical development of disease-modifying therapies in Parkinson's disease is important as they understand the limitations of existing treatments, can speak with authority on their perception of what is important to address and their expectations of a disease-modifying therapy, and are engaged in clinical trial participation through campaigns such as the Fox Trial Finder. Patient perceptions of disease-modifying therapies may differ at various stages of the disease. For example, a patient with young-onset Parkinson's disease may place higher value in the ability to remain employed and raise a family, whereas an advanced patient may be more concerned with maintaining balance, preventing falls, and the loss of independent living. Patient engagement efforts to help inform clinical practice have been recognized as a significant aspect of all stages of therapeutic development. This engagement is well documented by their willingness to undergo significant endeavors, including traveling long distances and participation in placebo-controlled trials with complex designs. 57

Recent regulatory guidance increasingly advocates for the transition toward clinical outcome assessments and endpoints that prioritize patient-centered meaningfulness. 46 However, the implementation of such measures is complicated by the inherent phenotypic and psychosocial heterogeneity of PD. Subjective symptom descriptions often diverge significantly between individuals, reflecting not only variable underlying pathophysiology but also distinct life situations and personal health goals. Consequently, a multi-perspective approach is essential; care providers frequently observe subtle but clinically relevant functional shifts, such as improved social engagement or cognitive fluency, that patients may fail to self-report due to gradual adaptation or reduced self-awareness.47,48 Furthermore, a truly holistic evaluation must integrate demographic variables into the assessment framework. This includes accounting for sex-specific differences in symptom profiles, age-related priorities in quality-of-life expectations, and cultural nuances that influence the perception and reporting of disability, ensuring that “disease modification” is measured against a contextually relevant baseline. Physician perspective

In clinical trials, the current forms of disease modification assessment, such as the MDS-UPDRS, were developed to support clinicians in evaluating motor features.19,72,73 However, these measures were not designed to be sensitive and specific enough to evaluate potential disease-modifying therapies and marginally consider important non-motor manifestations. 74 The assessment strategies listed in Table 2 may require further validation but offer a path toward more precise clinical trials. To confirm a disease-modifying effect, significant clinical improvements must be documented via validated scales and supported by objective biological evidence, such as biomarkers or imaging. Furthermore, the integration of digital health technologies and artificial intelligence will play a pivotal role in evolving how we monitor disease progression in real-world settings.63–67

Regulatory perspective

Our understanding of the regulatory requirements needed to support the claim of an intervention as a disease-modifying therapy in Parkinson's disease is also summarized in Table 2. 11 Over the last decade, the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency's therapeutic guidelines on developing new treatments for neurodegenerative disorders have focused mostly on the general approach to clinical development; additional guidance on expectations of adequate data and evidence for accepting a disease modification claim may benefit from the further evaluation of potential disease-modifying therapies.

Given the absence of any marketed disease-modifying therapy for Parkinson's disease to date, it remains uncertain whether regulatory agencies will adopt a specific terminology or formal designation for “disease modification”. Alternatively, regulatory agencies may prioritize an assessment of therapeutic effects objectively inferred from a comprehensive evaluation of the mechanism of action, robust clinical efficacy data, trial design, and the defined target patient population. In the latest European Medicines Agency Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use's Guideline on Clinical Investigation of Medicinal Products in the Treatment of Parkinson's disease from 2012, recommendations are given for therapies expected to improve symptomatic relief, modify or delay disease progression, postpone late motor fluctuations, or reverse neuronal loss. 75 As a general principle, studies should be randomized, placebo-controlled, and long-term (several years), and a disease-modification claim should include the demonstration of an altered disease progression supported by an effect on underlying pathophysiological processes, which correlates with a meaningful and persistent change in clinical function.

The US Food and Drug Administration has not explicitly addressed expectations pertaining to disease modification in their current regulatory guidelines. Recently, both the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency proposed changes to the primary clinical outcome measures, demonstrating a shift in expectations away from clinical outcome assessments and endpoints to ones that are meaningful to and reported by patients. 71 In addition, the European Medicines Agency suggests pairing disease modification outcome assessments with an enrichment biomarker of dopamine transporter density level analyses using single-photon emission computed tomography neuroimaging to evaluate the integrity and function of dopamine nerve terminals in the brain. 23 Dopamine transporter imaging is the first imaging biomarker qualified for use in clinical trials as a drug development tool for advancing treatments for patients with early-stage Parkinson's disease. While single-photon emission computed tomography scans with ioflupane are readily available, positron emission tomography ligands that allow quantification of active neuroinflammation require the use of an on-site cyclotron. However, implementation of new imaging tracers may be limited by the short half-life of the required isotopes.76,77

From a regulatory perspective, looking at the experience in the Alzheimer's disease field may be relevant to Parkinson's disease not only because amyloid-targeting therapies provide a direct template for α-synuclein therapies, but because recent Alzheimer's disease trials combined biological eligibility, target-engagement biomarkers, early-stage populations, clinical outcome measures, and explicit risk–benefit evaluation. For Parkinson's disease, analogous progress will likely require biologically stratified cohorts, mechanism-specific biomarkers, and clinical endpoints that demonstrate durable, patient-meaningful change. Moreover, with regards to Parkinson's disease, it may be prudent not to rely solely on the equivalent anti-synuclein approach (based on the assumption that a pathological protein transformation is the only disease mechanism), but instead to take a comprehensive look at all the disease mechanisms and consider how targeting each of these could positively impact on disease progression.78–80

Proposed evolution of disease modification in Parkinson's disease

Synthesizing the perspectives presented herein from disease experts and the patient community, we propose a holistic approach to address disease modification in Parkinson's disease. First, we consider a disease-modifying therapy to be a treatment with a demonstrated ability to prevent, delay, halt, and potentially reverse clinical progression on measures and endpoints that are meaningful and relevant to patients, physicians, and regulatory agencies. This would include endpoints that can be appreciated in daily life by patients, monitored by physicians in follow-up clinical visits, and that are supported by scientific rationales linked to the pathological mechanisms and brain circuits. It should, in its entirety, not be limited to motor features but must also include a broad spectrum of non-motor features that have been properly validated in the Parkinson's disease population. It should ideally consider quality-of-life parameters, as well as the ability to perform tasks of daily living that factor in age and stage of disease. For now, defining disease modification should not be restricted to only accepting interventions addressing the core biological mechanisms of the disease, but also those working indirectly through compensatory biological pathways, given that the precise etiology often remains elusive in individual patients. However, for Parkinson's disease with a well-defined etiology, such as genetic subtypes, it could be possible to provide direct evidence of impact on underlying pathological processes, such as neuroinflammatory, mitochondrial, or lysosomal function.

Regardless of whether direct pathophysiological modulation is a primary endpoint, clinical trials for potential disease-modifying therapies must be rigorously designed to differentiate and quantify their symptomatic versus disease-modifying effects, particularly when novel investigational treatments are evaluated in conjunction with the current standard of care. Our emphasis should be on the extent and longevity of improvement in disease progression and overall symptom severity across a broad spectrum of Parkinsonian manifestations, with an added consideration of the underlying therapeutic mechanism of action and its influence on the disease trajectory.

In summary, we propose a holistic evaluation of the potential impact of disease-modifying therapies in Parkinson's disease, primarily focusing on the magnitude and duration of changes in disease progression and symptom severity over a broad range of Parkinson's disease effects, supplemented by a potential consideration of how the underlying mechanisms of action may be affecting the disease course, as previously advocated. 11

Conclusions

The current standard of care for Parkinson's disease remains primarily focused on symptomatic relief, leaving a profound unmet need for interventions that can fundamentally alter the disease course. Addressing this gap requires a holistic framework that integrates biological markers, objective clinical features, and patient-reported outcomes to establish a unified and universally accepted definition of disease modification.

Central to this new definition is the concept of durability: for an investigational therapy to be truly classified as disease-modifying, it must demonstrate a persistent ability to prevent, delay, halt, or reverse clinical progression. Crucially, this benefit must be maintained independently of the compound's immediate pharmacological presence, distinguishing it from temporary symptomatic masking. Such outcomes must be observed through endpoints that carry clinical significance for both physicians and the patient community, moving beyond standard motor scales to include life-altering milestones like the preservation of autonomy or, e.g., the postponement of dementia.

While the lack of validated, primary biological biomarkers remains a hurdle, the strategic application of neuroimaging—including magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, and single-photon emission computed tomography—provides essential objective data to support a therapy's impact on underlying pathophysiology. The transition of innovative strategies, such as cell and gene therapies, from Phase 2 to Phase 3 trials offers tangible hope for the future. However, realizing this potential requires the research community to overcome historical limitations in trial design, such as insufficient target engagement data and poor characterization of the heterogeneous pathological substrates within the patient population. The emergence of these transformative therapies serves as a call to action to strengthen our collective efforts in defining and evaluating the science of disease-modifying interventions.
