Facet joint dilatation as a radiological indicator of vertebral segmental microinstability
Authors: Maka Khachidze, Nino Kraveishvili, Nini Nikabadze, Mikheil Okujava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Chronic low back pain (LBP) is a major healthcare burden that reduces quality of life and productivity. Vertebral segmental microinstability is considered a contributing factor in LBP, while radiological signs reflecting biomechanical dysfunction, including disc degeneration, Modic changes, muscle fatty infiltration, and facet joint (FJ) dilatation, remain insufficiently defined as clinical markers.Methods: This was a retrospective cohort study involving 168 adults with chronic LBP. Patients underwent clinical examination including passive lumbar extension (PLE), supine–sit, iliopsoas and quadratus muscle tests, along with lumbar spine magnetic resonance imaging. Imaging findings assessment included rating of FJ dilatation (grades 0–2), Modic changes (grades 0–2), disc degeneration (grades 1–5), and muscle fatty infiltration (grades 0–3). Pain intensity was measured using a 0–10 Visual Analog Scale.Results: Among 168 patients, 70.5% (n = 120) demonstrated some degree of FJ dilatation, while 16.7% (n = 28) had high-grade dilatation. Dilatation was significantly associated with Modic type 1 changes, grade 4/5 disc degeneration, muscle fatty infiltration grade 2/3, positive PLE test, and FJ asymmetry. Three factors that did not significantly impact FJ dilatation included disc protrusion, supine-sit test, and muscle dysfunction test.Conclusion: For patients with chronic LBP, FJ dilatation is significantly associated with vertebral degeneration and clinical indicators of lumbar microinstability.

INTRODUCTION

Spinal segmental microinstability is one of the major contributors to chronic low back pain (LBP).[7] Different radiological findings likely indicate the presence of biomechanical instability (i.e., Modic type 1 changes, intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD), facet joint (FJ) dilatation, and muscle fatty infiltration).[6] Here, we examined FJ dilatation’s association with radiological and clinical signs of vertebral segmental microinstability in chronic LBP patients.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical data

This was a retrospective cohort study involving 168 patients with chronic LBP, who met inclusion and exclusion criteria [Table 1]. More than half were female, and patients averaged 45.5 years of age. Notably, 70.5% (n = 120) demonstrated some degree of FJ dilatation, while 16.7% (n = 28) had high-grade dilatation. Pain intensity was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale.[5] Clinical instability was evaluated using the Passive Lumbar Extension (PLE) (i.e., the PLE test assessed lumbar instability in the prone position)[2] and supine-sit tests and recorded as positive or negative. Iliopsoas and quadratus tests scored dysfunction by 0–2. Supine-sit test evaluated muscle tone changes. Descriptive statistics summarized age, sex, and clinical characteristics.

Radiological assessment of microinstability

Radiological and clinical markers of microinstability were assessed. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was evaluated for FJ dilatation graded 0–2 [Figure 1]. MRI used 3T and 1.5T scanners and utilized the following sequences: T2(tse), T1(se), turbo inversion recovery magnitude (TIRM), and T1 (opposed-phased).

Representative axial T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging sections demonstrating FJ dilatation grades. (a) Grade 0 (no dilatation). (b) Grade 1 (mild dilatation with minimal effusion). (c) Grade 2 (marked dilatation and effusion). FJ: facet joint.

Statistical analysis

Chi-square and t-tests assessed associations between variables. Multivariable logistic regression identified predictors of segmental instability. Linear regression analyzed pain scores. Analyses used the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Chicago, IL) and Python with statsmodels version 0.14.2 (Statsmodels Developers, 2024).

RESULTS

Bivariate analysis

Participants with FJ dilatation had a higher prevalence of Modic type 1 changes (38.9% vs. 10.7%), Grade 4- and 5-disc degeneration (28.1% and 31.0%, respectively), and higher grades of muscle fatty infiltration (e.g., Grade 3: 10.7% vs. 5.7%). FJ asymmetry was strongly associated with FJ dilatation (96.4% vs. 58.3%). A positive PLE test was more common among those with FJ dilatation (71.4% vs. 45.7%) as well [Table 2].

Multivariable analysis

FJ dilatation was significantly associated with higher odds of Modic 1 changes (Adjusted Odds Ratio [AOR] = 5.21), Grade 4/5 disc degeneration (AOR = 3.92), muscle infiltration Grade 2/3 (AOR = 2.89), and a positive PLE test (AOR = 2.98). No significant associations were found for disc protrusion (AOR = 1.67), supine-sit test (AOR = 0.97), muscle dysfunction (Beta = −0.25), or pain score (Beta = 0.12) [Table 3].

DISCUSSION

FJ dilatation likely plays a major role in lumbar degenerative disease and appears to accurately reflect biomechanical instability. We found that FJ dilatation has a strong association with positive clinical test (PLE), Modic type 1 changes, advanced disc degeneration, muscle fatty infiltration, and FJ asymmetry.[4,6,7]

Shinto, et al reported that FJ effusion, observed on MRI, is a reliable indicator of lumbar instability.[9] Dudli, et al linked Modic type 1 changes to inflammatory processes and stress at the vertebral endplate, correlating dynamic instability with endplate changes and FJ opening.[1] Gao, et al. demonstrated that FJ asymmetry contributed to abnormal segmental motion and disc degeneration.[3] Seyedhoseinpoor, et al. validated that the PLE test was an effective tool for detecting functional instability as well.[8] In addition, Shinto, et al found that FJ osteoarthritis, often accompanied by FJ dilatation, was associated with degenerative changes at adjacent spinal levels.[9] Mechanistically, FJ dilatation likely contributes to microinstability through joint effusion or altered load distribution, exacerbating IDD.

These results further support the intervertebral disc-centered model of chronic LBP and degenerative findings positively correlating with FJ pathology.

CONCLUSION

In this retrospective cohort study, FJ dilatation emerged as a reliable independent radiological marker of vertebral segmental microinstability in patients with chronic LBP.
