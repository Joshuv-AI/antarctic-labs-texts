Hydrogen-Alpha as a Tracer of Star Formation in the SPHINX Cosmological Simulations
I. G. Kramarenko ,, J. Rosdahl, J. Blaizot, J. Matthee, H. Katz ,, C. Di Cesare

Abstract
The Hydrogen-alpha ( H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha ) emission line in galaxies is a powerful tracer of their recent star formation activity. With the advent of JWST , we are now able to routinely observe H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha in galaxies at high redshifts ( z ≳ 3 z\gtrsim 3 ) and thus measure their star-formation rates (SFRs). However, using “classical” SFR( H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha ) calibrations to derive the SFRs leads to biased results because high-redshift galaxies are commonly characterized by low metallicities and bursty star-formation histories, affecting the conversion factor between the H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha luminosity ( L H ​ α L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha} ) and the SFR. In this work, we develop a set of new SFR( H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha ) calibrations that allow us to predict the SFRs of H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha -emitters at z ≳ 3 z\gtrsim 3 with minimal error. We use the SPHINX cosmological simulations to select a sample of star-forming galaxies representative of the H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha -emitter population observed with JWST . We then derive linear corrections to the classical SFR( H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha ) calibrations, taking into account variations in the physical properties (e.g., stellar metallicities) among individual galaxies. We obtain two new SFR( H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha ) calibrations that, compared to the classical calibrations, reduce the root mean squared error (RMSE) in the predicted SFRs by Δ ​ RMSE ≈ 0.04 \Delta\mathrm{RMSE}\approx 0.04 dex and Δ ​ RMSE ≈ 0.06 \Delta\mathrm{RMSE}\approx 0.06 dex, respectively. Using the recent JWST NIRCam/grism observations of H ​ α \mathrm{H}\alpha -emitters at z ∼ 6 z\sim 6 , we show that the new calibrations affect the high-redshift galaxy population statistics: (i) the estimated cosmic star-formation density decreases by Δ ​ ρ SFR ≈ 12 \Delta\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}\approx 12 %, and (ii) the observed slope of the star-formation main sequence increases by Δ ​ ∂ log ⁡ SFR / ∂ log ⁡ M ⋆ = 0.08 ± 0.02 \Delta\partial\log\mathrm{SFR}/\partial\log\mathrm{M_{\star}}=0.08\pm 0.02 .

Introduction

One of the most fundamental and basic properties of galaxies is the rate at which new stars form, the star formation rate (SFR). The SFR is sensitive to the interplay between the gas inflow and outflow rates of galaxies, and the efficiency at which this gas is converted into stars
(
Bouché et al. 2010
;
Lilly et al. 2013
;
Behroozi et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
. The SFR can fluctuate on different timescales
(
Sparre et al. 2015
;
Iyer et al. 2020
;
Flores Velázquez et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
, which range from dynamical timescales of molecular clouds (
\sim 10
s of Myrs), through galaxy merger timescales (
\sim 100
Myr), to long (Gyr-scale) coherences in accretion rates
(
Matthee & Schaye 2019
;
Wang & Lilly 2020
;
Tacchella et al. 2020
;
Wan et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. Observationally, SFRs can be measured using various diagnostics
(
Kennicutt & Evans 2012
;
De Looze et al. 2014
;
Davies et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
that are either based on the light from massive, short-lived stars (e.g., the rest-frame UV), or processed radiation (e.g., nebular lines in HII regions or dust emission). Each of these diagnostics probes different timescales of star formation and is affected by dust attenuation differently.

At high redshifts (
z>3
), the rest-frame UV emission used to be the most sensitive SFR indicator that could be measured for large galaxy samples, tracing star formation on the
t\sim 100
Myr timescales
(
Bouwens et al. 2015
, e.g.,)
. However, dust corrections in the rest-frame UV are significant, with potentially up to 50% of the star formation rate density at
z\sim 5
being obscured by dust
(
Zavala et al. 2021
;
Sun et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
. In contrast, the Hydrogen-alpha (
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) emission – an alternative SFR indicator – is emitted in the rest-frame optical (
\lambda=6564
Å) and thus has a much lower sensitivity to dust attenuation. The luminosity of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line is directly proportional to the luminosity of ionizing photons that are predominantly emitted by very young stellar populations, making
\mathrm{H}\alpha
sensitive to star formation on shorter timescales
(
Leitherer et al. 1999
,
t\sim 10
Myr; e.g.,)
. As the
JWST
can now measure the distribution of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosities out to
z\sim 7
based on spectroscopy and narrow-band imaging
(
Clarke et al. 2024
;
Pirie et al. 2024
;
Covelo-Paz et al. 2025
;
Fu et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, it is timely to reassess the role of the H
\alpha
emission as a tracer of the SFR.

The SFH and the IMF are relevant because the ionizing photon emissivity from a single stellar population drops sharply after a few Myr when massive, short-lived O stars leave the main sequence. Binary interactions can extend these ionizing lifetimes

The classical SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration proposed by
Kennicutt 1998
(see also
Kennicutt & Evans 2012
) assumes a constant SFH over
t=100

(
Asada et al. 2024
;
Endsley et al. 2024
;
Cole et al. 2025
;
Carvajal-Bohorquez et al. 2025

. Given that the gas-phase oxygen abundances are typically measured to be
\sim 10

(

;
Scholte et al. 2025

, and early galaxies are expected to be

(

, e.g.)
, stellar metallicities are likely to be very low (
Z_{\star}\ll Z_{\odot}
), implying hotter stars and a more efficient
\mathrm{H}\alpha

(
Theios et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
, compared to low redshift.

The aim of this paper is to revisit the classical SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations to predict the SFRs of galaxies at high redshifts (
z\gtrsim 3
). Rather than using idealistic models with constant SFHs and fixed stellar metallicities, we use simulated galaxies and their modeled
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosities from the SPHINX cosmological radiation-hydrodynamical simulation
(
Rosdahl et al. 2018
;
Katz et al. 2023
)
. These cosmological simulations self-consistently model SFHs and metal enrichment processes, and are calibrated to match various observables at high redshifts (
z\sim 6
). The simulated volume is well matched to the typical volumes of deep JWST surveys, which enables a high resolution that can capture SFR variations on short timescales (
t\sim 1
Myr) and approximate the distribution of gas in the interstellar medium relevant for radiative transfer effects.

2
Methods

In this work, we use the simulated galaxy data from SPHINX, a suite of cosmological radiation-hydrodynamic simulations
(
Rosdahl et al. 2018
)
. SPHINX leverages the adaptive mesh refinement code
RAMSES-RT

(
Rosdahl et al. 2013
;
Teyssier 2002
)
to simulate tens of thousands of galaxies in the epoch of reionization. The ISM is resolved down to
76
co-moving pc, corresponding to the physical resolution of
11
pc at
z=6
. The dark matter halos are resolved down to the atomic cooling threshold (
M_{\mathrm{vir}}\approx 3\times 10^{7}
M
⊙
). Hydrodynamics, non-equilibrium thermochemistry, and radiative transfer of Lyman continuum radiation (LyC;
\lambda<912
Å) are performed self-consistently on-the-fly, with subgrid implementations for gas cooling, star formation, metal enrichment, and feedback. The on-the-fly radiative emission and propagation of LyC photons make SPHINX an excellent model to accurately predict how
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission traces star formation in the simulated galaxies.

Our analysis is based on the data from the SPHINX
20
Public Data Release, Version 1
(
Katz et al. 2023
, SPDRv1;)
. SPHINX
20
is the largest simulation of the SPHINX suite, with a periodic box with volume of (
20
cMpc)
3
simulated down to a redshift of
z=4.64

(
Rosdahl et al. 2022
)
. SPHINX
20
uses the
BPASSv
2.2
stellar population synthesis (SPS) model
(
Stanway & Eldridge 2018
)
that adopts a
Kroupa 2001a

⊙
and a slope of
-1.3
from
0.1
to
0.5
M
⊙

from
0.5
to
100

⊙

(

)
for gas cells that host stellar particles with unresolved Stromgren spheres, or by using the non-equilibrium ionizing fractions from the simulation otherwise. The equivalent widths are calculated by taking into account both the stellar and nebular continuum emission, generated as detailed in
Katz et al. 2023
. Post-processing of line and continuum radiation, subject to absorption and scattering by dust, is carried out using the radiative transfer code

(
Michel-Dansac et al. 2020

assuming the Small Magellanic Cloud (SMC) dust model from

only, which means that
\sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
is neglected even when using the updated calibration. To further improve on this, we add a correction to
eq.

2
that depends on the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
equivalent width (
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
). This choice is motivated by the fact that the
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
traces stellar metallicity and age that both drive
\sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}
(
fig.

2
). In addition, the
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
is only weakly sensitive to dust attenuation, which implies that dust corrections have a smaller impact on the results compared to when other observables are used (e.g.,
M_{\mathrm{UV}}
). We therefore run an OLS model that includes
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
, using the same training sample as in
eq.

2
. The resulting SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration can be written as follows:

\begin{split}\log_{10}\left[\frac{\mathrm{SFR}}{M_{\odot}\,\mathrm{yr}^{-1}}\right]{}=&\log_{10}\left[\frac{L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\right]{}-41.45\\
&-0.01\left(\log_{10}\left[\frac{L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}}{\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}}\right]{}-41.90\right)\\
&-0.26\left(\log_{10}\left[\frac{\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}}{\AA}\right]{}-2.67\right),\end{split}

(3)

where the last two terms are the effective corrections to
eq.

1
. The new calibration yields
\mathrm{RMSE}=0.11
dex on both the training and test datasets,
\approx 0.02
dex lower than the RMSE calculated using
eq.

2
, or
\approx 0.06
dex lower than the RMSE calculated using the T19 calibration (
Z_{*}=Z_{\odot}
).
Figure

3
compares these calibrations as a function of
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}
. In particular, this plot shows that the median
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}
in the
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}
bins is within
\pm 0.05
dex across the entire range of luminosities (
41.25<\log_{10}\left(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}/\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\right){}<43.25
) when using
eq.

2
or
eq.

3
. This is an improvement over the T19 calibration, which overpredicts the SFRs by
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}\gtrsim 0.1
dex in galaxies with low-to-medium luminosities (
41.25<\log_{10}\left(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}/\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\right){}\lesssim 42.5
). The standard deviation of
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}
in the
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}
bins (hereafter
\sigma_{\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}}
) is nearly identical between the T19 calibration and the SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration given in
eq.

2
(
\sigma_{\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{}\approx 0.11
dex), but it decreases by
\approx 0.03
dex (
\sigma_{\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}}{}\approx 0.08
dex) when using
eq.

3
, especially at medium luminosities (
41.8\lesssim\log_{10}\left(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}/\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\right){}\lesssim 42.5
).

4
Discussion

4.1
Implications

The
\mathrm{H}\alpha
line has long been known as a reliable indicator of the SFR as it traces the ionizing photon luminosity from massive (
M>10\mathrm{M}_{\odot}
), short-lived (
t\lesssim 10
Myr) stars
(
Kennicutt 1998
, e.g.,)
. Over the past decades,
\mathrm{H}\alpha
has been widely employed in numerous spectroscopic
(
Shim et al. 2009
;
Gunawardhana et al. 2013
;
Nagaraj et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
and narrow-band imaging
(
Ly et al. 2007
;
Hayes et al. 2010
;
Sobral et al. 2013
, e.g.,)
surveys to measure the cosmic star formation history (SFH) up to
z\sim 3
. With the recent advent of sensitive near-IR spectroscopy at
\lambda\sim 3
–
5

\mu
m, these measurements have been extended to
z\approx 3
–
6
, in particular thanks to the
JWST
/NIRCam slitless spectroscopic observations of large samples of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitters at the same redshifts
(
Covelo-Paz et al. 2025
;
Fu et al. 2025
;
Lin et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
.

As
\mathrm{H}\alpha
observations become more accessible at high redshifts, it is crucial to revisit the classical SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations
(
Kennicutt 1998
, e.g.,)
commonly used at
z\lesssim 3
. Based on a realistic galaxy simulation, we have shown that such calibrations bias the predicted SFRs under the physical conditions that are characteristic of high-redshift galaxies observed with
JWST
(i.e., low metallicity and bursty star formation), potentially also affecting population statistics. To quantify this effect, we assess the impact of the metallicity- and age-dependent SFR bias (see
fig.

2
) on the cosmic star formation rate density (
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
) measurements. Specifically, we convert the dust-corrected
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity function at
z\sim 6.3
presented by
Fu et al. 2025
to the star-formation rate function (SFRF) using the T19 calibration (
Z_{*}=Z_{\odot}
) and our new SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration given in
eq.

2
which, by design, takes into account the evolution of the SFR—
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation with metallicity. Following previous studies, we integrate both SFRFs down to
\mathrm{SFR}=0.24

M_{\odot}
yr
-1
which corresponds to
M_{\mathrm{UV}}=-17

(
Bouwens et al. 2015
)
. We find that the resulting
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
decreases from
\approx 0.017

M_{\odot}

\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

\mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}
to
\approx 0.015

M_{\odot}

\mathrm{yr}^{-1}

\mathrm{Mpc}^{-3}
, or by
\approx 12
%, when using the new calibration. This suggests that the influence of the evolving SFR—
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation on
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
is moderate, likely because galaxies where the SFR bias is highest contribute little to
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
. For example, galaxies with
\mathrm{SFR}<10

M_{\odot}

\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
contribute less than
30
% to the total
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
. If we integrate the SFRF only up to
\mathrm{SFR}=10

M_{\odot}

\mathrm{yr}^{-1}
, then the change in
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
would be more pronounced:
\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}
would decrease by
\approx 29
%.

The SFR bias also impacts the correlations between the SFR and other galaxy properties, such as stellar mass. In
fig.

4
, we show the SFR—
M_{*}
relation, also known as the galaxy main sequence
(
Noeske et al. 2007
)
, in the
JWST
All the Little Things (ALT) survey (
Naidu et al. 2024
, Di Cesare et al. in prep.). We find that the slope of the SFMS increases by
\Delta\partial\log\mathrm{SFR}/\partial\log\mathrm{M_{\star}}=0.08\pm 0.02
(
20\pm 6
%) when we use
eq.

3
instead of the T19 calibration to calculate the SFRs.
Equation

2
yields similar results, although with a smaller significance (
\Delta\partial\log\mathrm{SFR}/\partial\log\mathrm{M_{\star}}=0.02\pm 0.03
). In addition, we find that the scatter in the SFMS decreases by
\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}\approx 0.04
dex (
\approx 14
%) when using
eq.

3
, most noticeably at the low-mass end (
\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}\approx 0.06
dex at
\log_{10}\left(M_{\star}/M_{\odot}\right)<7
). In contrast,
eq.

2
produces almost the same scatter as the T19 calibration (
\Delta\sigma_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}\lesssim 0.01
dex). We note that the SMFS in
fig.

4
is impacted by selection effects: some low-mass galaxies are not detected in observations due to their faintness, resulting in a shallow SMFS slope. However, proper modeling of these selection biases is out of scope of this paper and will be discussed elsewhere (see Di Cesare et al. in prep.).

, Di Cesare et al. in prep.) with SFRs calculated using the T19 calibration (gray), and the new SFR(

eq.

) confidence interval. The dashed lines show the linear regression fits to the SFMS, individually for each calibration.

4.2
Dust attenuation

In
section

3
, we ignored the effects of dust attenuation, assuming that the H
\alpha
line measurements (i.e.,
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
and
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
) can be corrected for such effects without errors. This assumption is also usually made for the SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations presented in the literature. However, dust attenuation is known to be an important source of systematic error in the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-derived SFRs
(
Hopkins et al. 2001
, e.g.,)
. This effect likely persists even at
z\gtrsim 4
as the amount of dust in
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitters at these redshifts is found to be non-negligible (e.g.,
Covelo-Paz et al. 2025
reported the median
A_{\mathrm{H}\alpha{}}\approx 0.5
mag for a sample of
\approx 1000

\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitters at
4\lesssim z\lesssim 6
).

Here, we test the possibility of deriving SFRs directly from the observed
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission without prior dust corrections. We use the dust-attenuated
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity (
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
) and equivalent width (
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
) from SPHINX, calculated as described in
section

2
, and fit the same linear relations between the SFR,
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
, and
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
as in
section

3
. We present the resulting SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations in
appendix

B
. The calibration that depends on
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
only (
eq.

4
;
fig.

5
, cyan) yields
\mathrm{RMSE}=0.39
dex, while the calibration that additionally depends on
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
(
eq.

5
;
fig.

5
, magenta) yields
\mathrm{RMSE}=0.22
dex. In both cases, the RMSE is significantly higher compared to the RMSEs calculated using the calibrations that are based on the intrinsic
\mathrm{H}\alpha
properties (see
section

3
). We also find that the SFRs are significantly underestimated at high luminosities (
\log_{10}\left(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}/\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\right){}\gtrsim 42.5
), with
eq.

4
and
eq.

5
producing the median
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}=-0.52
and
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}=-0.27
, respectively. This can be explained by the fact that high-
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}
galaxies in SPHINX have, on average, very high attenuation values (
A_{\mathrm{H}\alpha{}}\gtrsim 2
) which we are unable to fully correct for while simultaneously trying to model the SFRs of low-
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}
galaxies. However, we caution that such
A_{\mathrm{H}\alpha{}}
are not reported in high-redshift observations (see
section

2
) and thus making accurate SFR estimates based on the dust-attenuated
\mathrm{H}\alpha
might still be possible in practice.

We also test whether the Balmer decrement (
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}{}/L_{\mathrm{H}\beta}^{\mathrm{obs}}{}
; hereafter
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
), whose measurements are commonly used to calculate the dust corrections for
\mathrm{H}\alpha
, can be a useful parameter to include in the SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration. Interestingly, we find that the
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
-based calibration (
eq.

6
;
fig.

5
, orange) yields
\mathrm{RMSE}=0.29
dex, which is
\approx 0.07
dex higher than the RMSE calculated using the
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
-based calibration. This indicates that
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
is not as powerful as
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
in constraining the SFRs of SPHINX galaxies. Including both
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
and
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
in the calibration (
eq.

7
; see also
fig.

5
, light blue) yields the best results (
\mathrm{RMSE}=0.16
dex). Observationally, however, the
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
measurements are hampered by slitlosses and the requirement of a broader wavelength coverage. This implies that the benefits of including
\mathrm{H}\alpha{}/\mathrm{H}\beta{}
in the SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration are likely limited, with
\mathrm{EW}_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}
remaining a key addition to the calibration.

, but for the observed (i.e., dust-attenuated)

.

4.3
Caveats

When using the SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations given in
eq.

2
and
eq.

3
, it is important to take proper account of the scope of applicability of these calibrations and the assumptions made to derive them. In particular, the selection criteria described in
section

2
imply that the new calibrations can be applied to a flux-limited sample when the following conditions are met:

•

4\lesssim z\lesssim 10
;

•

L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}{}\gtrsim 10^{41}
erg s
-1
.

Extending the calibrations to
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{obs}}{}<10^{41}
erg s
-1
is already possible with SPHINX but a larger halo catalog compared to the currently available SPDRv1 catalog is needed to create a complete
\mathrm{H}\alpha
sample down to very low luminosities. At low redshifts (
z\lesssim 4
), the SFHs become less bursty and metallicities increase, rendering the classical calibrations more suitable. Finally, at high redshifts (
z\gtrsim 10
), it remains to be established whether the new calibrations can be used, as the physical conditions of star formation at such redshifts are poorly understood.

We also stress the fact that the new calibrations assume the
BPASSv
2.2
SPS model with a Kroupa-type IMF (see
section

2
).
Figure

1
shows that the choice of the IMF significantly impacts the predicted SFRs, resulting in a systematic error of up to
\approx 0.2
dex. The choice of the SPS model, in particular whether the model includes binary stars or not, results in a smaller systematic error (
\approx 0.05
dex), possibly because binary stars have little effect on the LyC luminosity in the first 3 Myrs of a stellar population
(
Rosdahl et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
, however such errors are still non-negligible. In the future, these systematic errors can be reduced by testing the SPS models and constraining the IMF at
z>4
, e.g., by means of deep rest-frame UV spectroscopy
(
Steidel et al. 2016
;
Chisholm et al. 2019
, e.g.,)
.

Finally, we caution that SPHINX does not include black hole formation due to its small volume. This implies that the AGN contribution to the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission is neglected in the new SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations. Hence, it is necessary to remove the AGN from the sample before applying these calibrations.

5
Summary

In this work, we study the relationship between
\mathrm{H}\alpha
emission and the SFR in the SPHINX cosmological simulations at
4.64\leq z\leq 10
. Using the simulated galaxy data from the SPHINX Public Data Release, Version 1
(
Katz et al. 2023
)
, we select a sample of star-forming galaxies that are representative of the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitter population routinely observed with
JWST
at
z>3
. We find that the median SFR—
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation in SPHINX exhibits a significant evolution (
fig.

1
), with a downturn at low luminosities (
\log_{10}\left(L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}^{\mathrm{int}}/\mathrm{erg}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\right){}\sim 42.0
–
42.5
) that is consistent with fainter galaxies having low stellar metallicites (
Z_{\star}\sim 0.1Z_{\odot}
) and thus being more efficient producers of ionizing photons than brighter, relatively metal-enriched (
Z_{\star}\gtrsim 0.3Z_{\odot}
) galaxies.

Due to the metallicity dependence of the SFR—
L_{\mathrm{H}\alpha}
relation, the classical SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations (e.g.,
Kennicutt 1998
;
eq.

1
), on average, overestimate the SFRs of faint galaxies in SPHINX by
\Delta_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}\equiv\mathrm{SFR}(\mathrm{H}\alpha)/\mathrm{SFR}_{10}\gtrsim 0.1
dex (
fig.

3
, black). In
eq.

2
, we propose a new SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration that depends on the intrinsic
\mathrm{H}\alpha
luminosity only and reduces the error in the predicted SFRs by
\Delta\mathrm{RMSE}\approx 0.04
dex (
fig.

3
, cyan). In
eq.

3
, we propose another SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibration that additionally depends on the
\mathrm{H}\alpha
equivalent width, a parameter that is sensitive to the stellar metallicity and age (
fig.

2
). This calibration further improves the SFR estimate, reducing the error by a total of
\Delta\mathrm{RMSE}\approx 0.06
dex (
fig.

3
, magenta). The new SFR(
\mathrm{H}\alpha
) calibrations affect the galaxy population statistics at high redshifts (
z\sim 6
): the inferred cosmic star-formation density descreases by
\Delta\rho_{\mathrm{SFR}}\approx 12
%, and the slope of the star-formation main sequence increases by
\Delta\partial\log\mathrm{SFR}/\partial\log\mathrm{M_{\star}}=0.08\pm 0.02
(
fig.

4
).

\mathrm{H}\alpha

\mathrm{H}\alpha

;
fig.

6
, dashed line). Similarly, Kendall’s
\tau
peaks at
t_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}=5
Myr (
\tau=0.84
;
fig.

6
, solid line). This suggests that a slightly shorter timescale (
t_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}=5
–
6
Myr) might be more appropriate for
\mathrm{H}\alpha
. Nevertheless, for consistency with previous works, we use the SFRs averaged over
t_{\mathrm{SFR}}{}=10
