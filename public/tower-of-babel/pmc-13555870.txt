Evolutionary dynamics of the chloroplast genome in Abutilon (Malvoideae, Malvaceae)
Authors: Abdullah, Rushan Yan, Abdul Sammad, Atinga Vallada, Xiaoxuan Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe genus Abutilon Mill. (Malvaceae) comprises approximately 178 species distributed across tropical and subtropical regions, many of which hold significant ornamental, economic, and medicinal value; yet its taxonomic classification remains challenging. In this study, six species were sequenced from herbarium specimens, and the chloroplast (cp.) genomes of ten additional species were assembled de novo from publicly available raw data. Three previously reported cp. genomes were also incorporated to characterise cp. genome structure, identify polymorphic loci, and perform phylogenetic analyses. The cp. genomes ranged from 159,458 to 160,454 bp and exhibited the typical quadripartite structure, with each genome containing 112 unique genes (78 protein-coding, 30 tRNA, and 4 rRNA) that showed conserved content and organisation. These genomes exhibited high similarity in GC content, inverted repeat boundaries, relative synonymous codon usage, amino acid frequencies, and substitution patterns. However, notable variation was observed in the total number of simple sequence repeats, ranging from 70 to 97 per genome. Selection analyses indicated predominant purifying selection, with evidence of episodic positive selection detected in rpoC2, rbcL, and ycf1. Two codons in rbcL were clade-specific and provided phylogenetic signal distinguishing Australian and Old World pantropical species. Nucleotide diversity analysis identified six highly polymorphic intergenic spacers (trnH-psbA, rps19-rpl2, psbT-pbf1, psaC-ndhD, trnR-atpA, and ndhJ-ndhK) that may be suitable for taxonomic studies. The phylogeny from maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) resolved two major clades: one comprising an exclusively Australian lineage occurring predominantly in arid and semi-arid environments, and the other a pantropical lineage spanning multiple continents. Abutilon grandifolium was recovered as sister to the remaining sampled Abutilon taxa in both ML and BI analyses, although no biogeographic origin inference can be drawn from this placement pending broader taxon sampling and integration of nuclear genomic data. Introduction

The genus Abutilon Mill. (Malvoideae, Malvaceae) comprises approximately 178 recognised species distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, with major centres of diversity in the Americas, Australia, Africa, and Asia [1, 2]. These species display considerable morphological diversity and possess significant ornamental, economic, and medicinal value [1, 2]. Several species serve as important sources of fibre or as components in traditional remedies, with pharmacological studies confirming their ethnobotanical importance [2–4]. Despite their economic and medicinal significance, phylogenetic studies of Abutilon within the tribe Malveae of subfamily Malvoideae remain limited, resulting in persistent taxonomic uncertainties. Molecular analyses using nuclear ITS and chloroplast (cp.) markers (e.g., matK, ndhF) consistently place Abutilon within Malveae but suggest paraphyly, with some species appearing more closely related to genera such as Bakeridesia, Gaya, or Pseudabutilon. These findings have prompted researchers to propose both integration and segregation of species within Abutilon, based on morphological and molecular evidence [5–7].

The cp. genome is typically uniparentally inherited, lacks meiotic recombination, and evolves at a moderate rate, making it valuable for studies of population genetics, DNA barcoding, and phylogenetics [8–11]. This genome displays a conserved quadripartite structure comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeats (IRs) [8, 12]. Additionally, cp. genomes experience diverse mutational events, including substitutions, insertions–deletions, inversions, and IR contraction and expansion [8, 13, 14]. Previous studies in Malvaceae have provided insights into cp. genome organisation by analysing multiple genera and documenting correlations among mutational events [14–22]. However, complete chloroplast (cp.) genome studies of Abutilon remain scarce, with only three species described to date: Abutilon theophrasti Medik., Abutilon fruticosum Guill. & Perr., and Abutilon indicum (L.) Sweet. This scarcity restricts robust comparative and evolutionary analyses [23, 24].

Here, we sequenced six new species from herbarium specimens, de novo assembled the cp. genomes of 10 additional species from raw data available in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA), and downloaded the cp. genomes of three additional species directly from NCBI. A total of 19 species were included in the comparative analysis. Analysis of biogeographical distributions across these 19 Abutilon species revealed their remarkable ecological breadth and geographical range (Table 1). Species distributions span tropical and subtropical regions worldwide, including the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia. A significant proportion of sampled species (eight) are endemic to Australia, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions. Growth forms are predominantly shrubs or subshrubs, with few species classified as annuals or trees. Primary biomes range from seasonally dry tropical forests to desert and dry shrublands, reflecting the genus’s adaptability to diverse environmental conditions.

Our aims and objectives were to (i) characterise cp. genome structure, gene content, and sequence divergence; (ii) reconstruct a phylogeny of Abutilon using whole-plastome data; and (iii) identify variable loci with potential for DNA barcoding and species delimitation. These results provide a genomic foundation for refining taxonomy, informing conservation strategies, and supporting the sustainable utilisation of this economically and medicinally important genus.

Materials and methods

Plant material, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Leaf material from six Abutilon species was obtained from herbarium specimens housed at the Finnish Museum of Natural History (LUOMUS), University of Helsinki, Finland (Table 2). The specimens were taxonomically identified and curated by the staff of the LUOMUS Herbarium, where voucher specimens are permanently deposited under the following accession numbers: A. abutiloides (C.191641), A. angulatum (C.191645), A. endlichii (syn. A. longicuspe) (C.191664), A. geranioides (C.191674), A. grandifolium (C.191677), and A. hirtum (C.191692). Detailed specimen information, including collector data and images, is publicly accessible through the LUOMUS online database at https://laji.fi/en/view?uri=http:%2 F%2Fid.luomus.fi%2FC.191641. The last accession number in the link (e.g., “C.191641”) can be replaced by the corresponding specimen number listed above or in Table 2 to access data for each species.

As all materials were obtained from existing herbarium specimens, no field collection, permits, or ethical approvals were required. The study complies with institutional, national, and international guidelines, including the Convention on the Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES).

Taxon sampling was designed to maximise geographic, ecological, and morphological diversity within Abutilon rather than to provide exhaustive coverage of the genus, which comprises approximately 178 described species. The infrageneric classification of Abutilon remains poorly resolved, and no widely accepted subgeneric or sectional framework is currently available, with previous classifications largely based on geographically restricted or morphology-focused treatments [2, 25]. Consequently, the selected taxa span major regions of the genus’ distribution, including the Americas, Africa, Asia, and Australia, and encompass a wide range of growth forms and habitat types following the inclusion of newly sequenced species (Table 1). This sampling strategy provides an appropriate framework for investigating broad patterns of chloroplast genome evolution, large-scale phylogenetic and biogeographic relationships within Abutilon, and for identifying suitable polymorphic loci.

Genomic DNA was extracted from 5 mg of dried leaf tissue using the Plant Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing, China) with several modifications to optimise DNA recovery from herbarium materials. First, extraction buffer volume was increased to 2 mL (from the recommended 0.7 mL), divided equally between two 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes, and processed separately according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Second, the incubation at 65 °C was extended to 90 min (from the standard 15 min). Third, the supernatants from both tubes were combined and loaded onto a single DNA-binding column (CD3). Finally, DNA was eluted in 65 µL of elution buffer following a 40-minute incubation at room temperature, instead of the recommended 5-minute incubation.

DNA quality and concentration were assessed using the Agilent 5400 System (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was conducted by Novogene (Tianjin, China) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 350-bp insert libraries, which generated paired-end reads of 150 bp (2 × 150 bp).

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation

Raw sequencing data for 10 additional Abutilon species were retrieved from NCBI for de novo assembly. The SRA accession numbers are listed in Table 2. All cp. genomes were assembled using GetOrganelle v1.7.5.1 with default parameters [26] and annotated with GeSeq v2.03 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) [27] and PGA v1 [28].

Gene annotations were cross-validated against published cp. genomes of other Malvaceae species—Abutilon theophrasti Medik. (MT991007), Hibiscus rosa-sinensis L. (MK382984), and Malva parviflora L. (MK860036)—using Geneious Prime 2025 [29]. Predicted tRNAs were further verified with tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 [30] and ARAGORN v1.2.38 [31].

Assembly quality was evaluated by mapping raw reads back to the assembled genome using Geneious with default parameters to assess coverage depth and implement minor corrections where necessary. All assembled cp. genomes were submitted to GenBank (NCBI). The six newly sequenced species were assigned accession numbers PX334442–PX334447, whilst genomes assembled from NCBI raw data received accession numbers BK071759–BK071768. All genomes are also available as Data file 1 to facilitate future research and ensure data reproducibility.

Comparative analysis and evaluation of polymorphic loci

A total of 19 Abutilon species were analysed, including 16 de novo assembled genomes and three genomes retrieved directly from NCBI: Abutilon indicum (PP887421), Abutilon fruticosum (MT772391), and Abutilon theophrasti (NC_053702). Genomic features were examined using Geneious Prime 2025, and circular genome maps were generated with Chloroplot v1 [32]. Codon usage and amino acid frequencies were analysed using custom Python scripts (Scripts 1 and 2) as reported previously [33].

Gene order rearrangements were identified with Mauve progressive alignment [34], while contraction and expansion of IRs were visualised using CPJSdraw v1 [35]. Transition and transversion substitution rates were estimated for complete cp. genomes as well as LSC, IR, and SSC regions using pairwise alignments between each Abutilon genome and the reference genome of A. grandifolium generated with MAFFT v7 [36]. Substitution rates were then calculated using a custom Python script (Script 3).

Simple sequence repeat (SSR) analysis was conducted across all species using CGAS v1, CPStools, and MISA-web [37–39]. The CGAS and CPStools were used in a Linux environment, using thresholds of ≥ 10 repeats for mononucleotides, ≥ 5 for dinucleotides, ≥ 4 for trinucleotides, and ≥ 3 for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. There were negligible differences among all tools, which were manually checked by visualising the relevant part in Geneious [29].

Genetic divergence among Abutilon species was assessed by calculating nucleotide diversity (π) for intergenic spacers (IGS), introns, and coding regions using CPStool and CGAS; both provided the same results.

Positive selection analyses were conducted using HyPhy v2.5.7 [40]. The branch-site unrestricted statistical test for episodic diversification (BUSTED) [41] was applied to detect gene-wide signatures of episodic positive selection, whilst the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME) [42] was used to detect site-specific episodic diversifying selection across lineages after applying false discovery rate correction. Codon alignments for each gene were generated using the MACSE v2 aligner [43]. The tree for each gene was reconstructed using FastTree v2 [44] with GTR + R model. All relevant files are available as Data file 2.

Phylogenetic reconstruction

To reconstruct phylogenetic relationships, we included 19 Abutilon species and 14 additional species from the subfamily Malvoideae, representing its three main tribes: Malveae, Gossypieae, and Hibisceae. Bombax ceiba (Bombacoideae) was used as the outgroup. Three accessions of A. theophrasti and two accessions of Callianthe megapotamica were included, resulting in 37 taxa in the alignment. Accession numbers are provided in Fig. 1. The shared 78 protein-coding genes were extracted from all species, aligned using the codon-aware MACE aligner [43] with default parameters, and then the whole matrix was concatenated. The alignment was of high quality but still curated for possible alignment mismatch. ModelFinder identified GTR + F+R3 as the best-fitting substitution model [45]. The final alignment comprised 67,575 nucleotide sites, including 2,779 parsimony-informative sites and 1,696 distinct site patterns. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was reconstructed with this alignment using IQ-TREE v.3 [46] with 10,000 standard bootstrap replicates and 10,000 SH-aLRT tests. The alignment and all files are available as Data file 3.

Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree of 19 Abutilon species and related genera. The Bombax ceiba was included as an outgroup. The phylogeny was reconstructed using 78 shared protein-coding genes. Bootstrap support values are shown at nodes. Values <70% are shown in red to indicate weakly supported nodes. Clade A represents Australian endemic species, primarily distributed in arid and semi-arid regions, whereas Clade B comprises pantropical and subtropical taxa, predominantly from Asia, Africa, and the Americas

To evaluate the robustness of the ML topology, Bayesian inference (BI) was performed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [47]. The same concatenated alignment of 78 protein-coding genes was used as input. The substitution model was set to GTR + I+G (nst = 6, rates = invgamma). Two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs, each consisting of four chains (one cold, three heated), were executed for 20 million generations with sampling every 2,000 generations. Convergence between runs was confirmed by an average standard deviation of split frequencies of 0.001654, with per-split posterior probability standard deviations near zero for 30 of 35 internal nodes (maximum = 0.0065). The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded. The alignment and all files are available as Data file 4.

All nodes, including those with low bootstrap support, were retained in the consensus topologies of both trees but are interpreted with caution in the text. For interpretation of phylogenetic relationships, nodes with bootstrap support values < 70% and/or posterior probabilities < 0.90 were considered weakly resolved and were interpreted with caution.

In addition, all chloroplast simple sequence repeat (cpSSR) data were merged into a matrix with species as rows and loci as columns. Loci absent in a given species were coded as zero repeat counts, resulting in a quantitative repeat-number matrix rather than binary presence–absence data. Pairwise genetic distances were calculated using Euclidean distance, and an unrooted neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was reconstructed using the ape package in R, with branch lengths excluded to emphasise topological relationships. Clade support was assessed using bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates by resampling cpSSR loci with replacement; for each replicate, a distance matrix and NJ tree were reconstructed, and bootstrap values were calculated as the proportion of trees supporting each clade. Bootstrap values were computed but not displayed on the final tree, which was visualised as a topology-only phylogram and exported in PDF and Newick formats, while an additional Newick file containing bootstrap values was generated for reference. All analyses were conducted in R using readxl, dplyr, tidyr, and ape.

Results

Genome sequencing and de novo assembly

We generated high-quality raw sequencing data for six Abutilon species: A. abutiloides, A. longicuspe, A. hirtum, A. grandifolium, A. geranioides, and A. angulatum. Dataset sizes ranged from 5.24 GB (A. grandifolium) to 17.40 GB (A. longicuspe), with sequencing depths ranging from 99× to 519×. The number of whole-genome sequencing (WGS) reads varied from 15.08 million to 51.0 million, whilst cp-specific reads ranged from 0.11 million (A. grandifolium) to 0.56 million (A. hirtum) (Table 2). This high sequencing depth and cp. read recovery enabled accurate de novo assembly of complete cp. genomes for all six species. Additionally, sequencing data for ten additional Abutilon species were retrieved from the NCBI SRA. These datasets ranged from 1.15 to 1.99 GB and provided coverage depths of 38× to 143× (Table 2). Whole-genome sequencing read counts varied between 1.59 and 2.71 million, whilst cp. reads ranged from 0.05 to 0.18 million (Table 2). Despite their lower sequencing depth, these datasets contained sufficient cp. reads for reliable de novo assembly and comparative genomic analyses.

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genome features

The complete cp. genomes of 19 Abutilon species ranged from 159,458 bp (A. geranioides) to 160,454 bp (A. hirtum), demonstrating a high degree of size conservation, with less than 1,000 bp variation across species (Table 3; Fig. 2). All genomes exhibited the typical angiosperm quadripartite structure, consisting of a large single-copy (LSC) region that varied from 88,035 bp (A. geranioides) to 89,125 bp (A. hirtum), a small single-copy (SSC) region ranging from 19,906 bp (Abutilon sp. Pilbara) to 20,204 bp (A. cryptopetalum), and two inverted repeat (IR) regions varying in size from 25,588 bp (A. grandifolium) to 25,663 bp (A. longicuspe). These findings highlight the remarkable structural uniformity of Abutilon cp. genomes (Table 3).

The cp. genomes showed highly consistent overall GC content, ranging from 36.89% to 37.08%. Distinct regional variation was evident, with the IR regions exhibiting the highest GC content (42.85–42.96%), followed by the LSC (34.64–34.99%) and SSC regions (31.38–31.57%). Among functional gene categories, ribosomal RNA genes displayed the highest GC content (~ 55.4%), followed by transfer RNA genes (52.90–53.07%) and protein-coding genes (~ 38.09–38.14%) (Table 3).

Gene and intron content were highly conserved across the 19 Abutilon species. Each cp. genome contained 112 unique genes, comprising 78 protein-coding sequences (CDS), 30 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes (Fig. 2; Table 4). The IR regions duplicated a total of 16 genes, including 5 protein-coding genes (rpl2, rpl23, rps7, ycf2, ndhB), 7 tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnL-UAA, trnV-GAC, trnR-ACG), and 4 rRNA genes (rrn16, rrn23, rrn4.5, rrn5). Among the 17 intron-containing genes, 11 were protein-coding (rpoC1, rpl16, rps12, rpl2, clpP1, atpF, petB, ndhA, rps16, ndhB, petD) and 6 were tRNA genes (trnS-CGA, trnK-UUU, trnV-UAC, trnL-UAA, trnA-UGC, trnI-GAU), with intron positions conserved across all species (Table 4). Collinear block analysis (Mauve progressive alignment) confirmed consistent gene arrangement across cp. genomes and revealed no major inversions affecting gene order or transcription (Figure S1).

Inverted repeat contraction and expansion

Analysis of inverted repeat (IR) boundary positions across all Abutilon species revealed highly conserved junction architecture with minimal structural variation (Fig. 3). The four junction sites—JLB (LSC/IRb), JSB (IRb/SSC), JSA (SSC/IRa), and JLA (IRa/LSC)—displayed remarkable stability in gene organisation and positioning across the genus.

Codon usage, amino acid frequency, repeats, and substitutions

Relative synonymous codon usage

Analysis of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) in cp. protein-coding genes revealed a high degree of conservation across the 19 Abutilon species (Table S1). A clear codon bias was observed, favouring codons with A/T at the third position over those ending in G/C. For example, alanine showed a strong preference for GCT (1.77–1.78) over GCG (0.47–0.48), whilst leucine demonstrated a clear bias towards TTA (1.80–1.90) and CTT (1.25–1.27) compared with codons containing G/C at the 3′ end, such as CTG (0.38–0.39). Stop codon usage was also conserved, with TAA being the most frequent (1.75–1.82), followed by TAG (0.64–0.71) and TGA (0.54–0.57). The minimal variation in codon frequencies (typically < 0.05 difference between species) is consistent with selective constraints, though mutational bias cannot be excluded.

Amino acid frequency

Analysis of amino acid composition in cp. protein-coding genes revealed remarkable consistency across the 19 Abutilon species (Table S2). Leucine was the most abundant amino acid (10.58–10.62%), followed by isoleucine (8.61–8.65%) and serine (7.68–7.73%), whilst cysteine was the least represented (1.12–1.14%). The minimal variation in amino acid frequencies (typically < 0.1% difference between species) underscores strong selective constraints maintaining protein structure and function.

Hydrophobic amino acids collectively constituted approximately 30% of all residues, reflecting the membrane-associated nature of cp. proteins involved in photosynthesis and electron transport. Charged amino acids showed consistent proportions, with arginine (6.05–6.08%) and lysine (5.33–5.37%) uniformly represented across species. This conservation pattern suggests that stabilising selection has preserved critical biochemical functions throughout Abutilon evolution.

Simple sequence repeats

Comprehensive analysis of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) across 19 Abutilon species revealed significant variation in repeat abundance and distribution. Mononucleotide repeats were abundant, ranging from 51 (A. cryptopetalum) to 70 (A. theophrasti) per species. Dinucleotide and tetranucleotide repeats occurred at moderate frequencies, whilst pentanucleotide and hexanucleotide repeats were relatively rare across all species. Details are provided in Table S3.

Spatial distribution analysis showed that SSRs were predominantly located in intergenic spacers (46–67 per species), followed by introns (11–17) and genic regions (13–15) (Table S3). Regional partitioning revealed that the LSC region contained the majority of SSRs (50–77), whilst IR regions harboured the fewest (4–10), and SSC regions showed intermediate values (10–16). This distribution pattern suggests that SSRs accumulate preferentially in non-coding regions, potentially due to relaxed selective constraints in these genomic compartments (Table S3).

SSR length variation was substantial, with mononucleotide repeats extending up to 37 nucleotides (A. amplum, A. lepidum, and A. macrum) and compound repeats such as AAATT and TTTA occurring across multiple species (Table S3). The precise mapping of these repetitive elements provides a valuable resource for developing molecular markers for population genetic studies and understanding mutational dynamics in cp. genomes.

Nucleotide substitutions

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis across 19 Abutilon species revealed characteristic substitution patterns and evolutionary dynamics (Table S4). Transition-to-transversion (Ts/Tv) ratios ranged from 0.84 (A. longicuspe, A. abutiloides) to 1.03 (A. geranioides), with most species approaching or slightly exceeding 1.0. A-to-G and C-to-T transitions were the most prevalent substitution types (Table S4).

Total SNP counts varied considerably among species, with A. longicuspe exhibiting the highest polymorphism (1,063 SNPs) and A. macrum the lowest (848 SNPs) (Table S4). Purine transitions (A↔G) and pyrimidine transitions (C↔T) dominated the substitution spectrum, whilst transversions involving purine–pyrimidine changes occurred less frequently (Table S4).

Selection analysis of protein-coding genes

Across the Abutilon chloroplast genomes, most protein-coding genes evolved under strong purifying selection (Figure S2). BUSTED analysis detected no significant evidence of gene-wide episodic positive selection in 76 of 78 genes (97.5%; p > 0.05). Although a few genes showed elevated estimated ω values, BUSTED did not provide statistically significant support, suggesting these values likely reflect limited signal due to short gene length or few informative sites (Figure S2). Most genes had dN/dS values well below 0.5, and many were near 0, consistent with strong functional constraints on chloroplast protein-coding genes. Full results are provided in Table S5.

Despite strong overall conservation of chloroplast protein-coding genes, BUSTED detected gene-level evidence of episodic positive selection in two genes: rpoC2 (p = 0.0000) and rbcL (p = 0.0137), indicating that at least a subset of sites on a subset of branches evolved with ω > 1. In rpoC2, the majority of sites (98.03%) were under strong purifying selection, while a small proportion (1.97%) experienced diversifying selection (dN/dS = 62.5). Similarly, rbcL showed a tiny fraction of sites (0.355%) under intense diversifying selection (dN/dS = 3856.7), while most codons evolved under neutral or negative selection. These results indicate that adaptive evolution is localised to a few codons rather than across the entire gene, indicating localized adaptive evolution (Table S5).

MEME also revealed high purifying selection (Table S6). However, at the codon level, MEME identified episodic diversifying selection at specific sites in rbcL and ycf1. In rbcL, codon 13 showed significant evidence of positive selection (LRT = 5.682; p = 0.0265) with an inferred substitution from GGA to TTT, resulting in an amino acid change. Hence, species of Australian clades encoded glycine (G) while species of pantropical clade encoded phenylalanine (F) (Table 5). This codon state was associated with the major phylogenetic groups recovered in this study. The inferred rate distribution was 0.00/38518.01 with weights 0.79/0.21. In ycf1, two codons showed suggestive evidence of episodic selection: codon 874 (LRT = 3.222; p = 0.095) with substitution ACa→CTa (Thr→Leu) and codon 1013 (LRT = 3.435; p = 0.085) with substitution TTG→TTT (Leu→Phe). Both sites had a beta distribution of 0.00/475.37 (weights 0.68/0.32) and 0.00/7498.68 (weights 0.75/0.25), respectively. Each of these codons was inferred to be affected on one branch. Detailed results are provided in Table S6, which also show that purifying selection acts mostly on protein-coding genes.

Intergenic spacer regions, intronic regions, and protein-coding sequences were analysed to identify highly variable regions across all sampled species. The average nucleotide diversity (π) across the cp. genome was 0.0047 when all species were included in the analysis. Intergenic spacer regions exhibited the highest average diversity (π = 0.0067), followed by intronic regions (π = 0.0044) and protein-coding sequences (π = 0.0015) (Fig. 4).

Phylogenetic analysis

Maximum likelihood and Bayesian inference analyses of 19 Abutilon species and 14 other Malvoideae species (representing tribes Malveae, Gossypieae, and Hibisceae) resolved the three tribes as distinct lineages (Fig. 1, Figure S4). The Malveae clade included Malva parviflora, Althaea officinalis, Alcea rosea, and Malvastrum coromandelianum, all grouped with strong support (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00). The Gossypieae clade comprised Gossypium barbadense and G. hirsutum, which formed a strongly supported sister pair (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00). However, the inter-tribal backbone differed between the two analyses: in the ML tree, Gossypieae was placed sister to Malveae (bootstrap = 46%, below the 70% threshold for reliable support), whereas in the BI tree, Gossypieae was sister to Hibisceae (PP = 0.58, below the 0.90 threshold). Both arrangements received weak support, indicating that the tribal backbone remains unresolved with the current dataset; accordingly, no strong evolutionary or biogeographic conclusions should be drawn from this inter-tribal topology. The Hibisceae clade included Hibiscus rosa-sinensis, H. syriacus, Abelmoschus esculentus, Malvaviscus penduliflorus, and Urena procumbens, forming a monophyletic group (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00) sister to the combined Malveae + Gossypieae lineages. This study showed Abutilon forms a well-defined lineage within Malveae, providing a framework for future studies of diversification and biogeographic history (Fig. 1; Figure S4).

In Abutilon, Clade A (bootstrap support = 100%; PP = 1.00) comprised exclusively Australian taxa distributed across arid and semi-arid regions, including Western Australia, the Northern Territory, and other desert or dry shrubland biomes. Within this clade, A. amplum and A. cunninghamii formed a strongly supported sister pair (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00), whilst A. lepidum, Abutilon sp. Pilbara, and Abutilon sp. Pritzelianum clustered together with high support (bootstrap = 99–100%; PP = 1.00), forming a regional lineage within Western Australia. Other relationships within Clade A were weakly resolved (bootstrap = 36–52%, below the 70% threshold; PP = 0.67, below the 0.90 threshold), which may reflect limited phylogenetic signal, incomplete lineage sorting, or other unresolved evolutionary processes; this pattern was consistently recovered by both ML and BI analyses, indicating that the uncertainty reflects genuine signal limitations rather than a methodological artefact.

Clade B (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00) encompassed pantropical and subtropical taxa with geographically disparate distributions spanning Africa, Asia, the Pacific, and the Americas. Within this clade, A. theophrasti (three accessions) occupied the earliest-diverging position. A. fruticosum was sister to A. hirtum (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00), with A. abutiloides sister to both (bootstrap = 69%, just below the 70% reliability threshold; PP = 0.96), and A. indicum sister to this entire subclade (bootstrap = 99%; PP = 1.00), collectively consistent with trans-continental distributions across Asia, Africa, and the Americas. A. angulatum was sister to A. longicuspe (bootstrap = 78%; PP = 0.86, the latter below the 0.90 PP threshold), consistent with a geographic association between African and Asian taxa, though this requires further sampling to confirm.

Abutilon grandifolium, native to South America (Peru to Brazil and northern Argentina), was recovered as sister to the remaining sampled Abutilon taxa in both ML and BI analyses (bootstrap = 100%; PP = 1.00), indicating an early-diverging position within the sampled dataset. However, because only a single South American representative was included, broader taxon sampling across Abutilon, coupled with expanded genomic resources, particularly biparentally inherited nuclear genomic or transcriptomic data, will be necessary to further evaluate the relationships of early-diverging lineages within the genus.

In addition, a phylogenetic analysis based on chloroplast simple sequence repeat (cpSSR) repeat number variation was performed using a neighbor-joining approach. The resulting cpSSR-based tree showed limited topological congruence with the maximum-likelihood phylogeny inferred from chloroplast protein-coding genes. In particular, the cpSSR topology failed to consistently recover the biologically well-defined Clade A and Clade B, indicating that although cpSSRs are highly polymorphic, their high mutation rates and susceptibility to homoplasy limit their utility for resolving interspecific relationships at the genus level in Abutilon. The results of the cpSSR-based analysis are shown in Figure S3.

Discussion

Our comparative analysis of 19 Abutilon cp. genomes, including 16 newly assembled and three previously reported sequences, provides new insights into the evolutionary dynamics and biogeographic patterns within this economically important genus. Chloroplast genomes can be either conserved in terms of gene content and arrangement or polymorphic due to gene deletions and/or rearrangements [8, 9]. Conserved cp. genomes have been well-documented in Malvaceae, with no inversion events detected [14–18]. Consistent with this expectation, the Abutilon plastomes analysed here are highly conserved in gene content, gene length, and overall nucleotide composition. No large structural rearrangements or differences in GC content were detected between pantropical and Australian arid clades, indicating that plastome architecture has remained evolutionarily stable despite their distinct geographic and ecological associations. The same is in agreement with the previous comparative analysis of whole Malvaceae [15, 18]. However, an inversion was recently reported in the LSC region of the Triumfetta that inverts six genes [48]. Accordingly, chloroplast genomes are generally subject to strong purifying selection that preserves gene content and structure over evolutionary timescales because cp. genes are organised into operons that resist structural rearrangement, uniparental inheritance limits recombination, efficient DNA repair systems curb mutation accumulation, and rare plastid fusion events preserve lineage-specific integrity. Nevertheless, future studies may further provide insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the cp. genome in the family Malvaceae, and the recent report of inversion of Triumfetta supports further elucidation of Malvaceae.

The IR regions play an essential role in maintaining genome stability [49]. However, their contraction and expansion affect the total gene count [13, 15]. Expansion duplicates genes, whilst contraction converts duplicated genes into single copies, as reported in Malvaceae, Araceae, and Apocynaceae [13, 15, 50–52]. In this study, the high conservation at the junctions of the LSC, IR, and SSC regions indicates strong structural stability across Abutilon genomes, as reported in closely related taxa of Malvaceae and other plant lineages [15, 17, 47, 53–55]. In some cases, IR contraction and expansion are clade-specific and provide significant phylogenetic signals for population genetic studies [10, 13, 56].

Chloroplast genes are essential for plant survival, as they regulate core processes such as photosynthesis and protein synthesis [8]. Consequently, strong purifying selection is expected to preserve functional integrity across evolutionary timescales. Consistent with this expectation, the present results demonstrate that chloroplast protein-coding genes in Abutilon are highly conserved, with 97.5% of genes (76 out of 78) showing no evidence of gene-wide episodic positive selection. This pattern agrees with previous studies across Malvaceae and other angiosperms, which consistently identify functional constraint as the dominant evolutionary force shaping chloroplast genomes [11, 15, 48, 50, 57, 58]. Although positive selection has been reported in a limited number of chloroplast genes in Malvaceae including clpP, rps4, accD, psbK, psbD, rpl20, rpl23, rps7, rps16, and ycf2 [15, 17, 48] such cases are typically gene-specific and lineage-dependent rather than genome-wide. Collectively, these observations support the view that adaptive evolution in chloroplast genomes occurs sporadically and is restricted to particular genes or even individual codons, superimposed on a background of strong purifying selection.

In Abutilon, significant gene-wide episodic positive selection was detected in rpoC2 (p < 0.0001) and rbcL (p = 0.0137) using BUSTED analysis [41], while MEME [42] further identified codon-specific signals of episodic selection in rbcL and suggestive sites in ycf1. Importantly, evidence for positive selection was highly localized: only 1.97% of sites in rpoC2 and 0.355% of sites in rbcL showed signatures of diversifying selection. Although these sites were associated with extremely high ω values, such estimates are a known feature of episodic selection models when positive selection acts on a small subset of branches and sites. These ω values should therefore be interpreted as indicators of strong but highly localized adaptive events rather than as evidence for pervasive positive selection across entire genes or lineages. Overall, the results are consistent with episodic, site-specific adaptive evolution occurring against a genome-wide background of purifying selection.

The rpoC2 gene encodes the β′ subunit of plastid-encoded RNA polymerase (PEP), which regulates transcription of photosynthesis-related genes [59]. In contrast, rbcL encodes the large subunit of RuBisCO, the key enzyme mediating carbon fixation [60]. other genes have been repeatedly implicated in adaptive evolution across plant lineages exposed to contrasting environmental conditions. Adaptive changes in rpoC2 have been reported in Colobanthus species occupying diverse ecological niches [61]. Similarly, rbcL has been documented under positive selection in numerous plant lineages [60, 62, 63], with adaptive evolution linked to atmospheric CO₂ variation in gymnosperms [64], drought and temperature stress [65], and ongoing selection for improved catalytic efficiency [66–68].

In the present dataset, rbcL exhibits clade-specific codon variation at positions 11 and 13. Codon 11 displays a pronounced geographic pattern: it is present exclusively in the pantropical clade, where it encodes serine in five species and alanine in one species, but is absent from both the Australian arid clade and the American clade. This absence reflects an in-frame deletion relative to the pantropical lineage, with no disruption of the downstream reading frame, indicating a genuine structural difference rather than an alignment artefact. The Ser/Ala polymorphism within the pantropical clade suggests additional lineage-specific fine-scale variation. Although serine and alanine are both small amino acids, serine is polar, whereas alanine is nonpolar, potentially influencing local hydrogen bonding or protein water interactions. The in-frame deletion of this codon in the Australian and American clades may therefore reflect alternative structural configurations of RuBisCO that are compatible with, or favored under, distinct ecological conditions.

MEME analysis further detected significant episodic positive selection at codon 13 (p = 0.0265), corresponding to a glycine (G) to phenylalanine (F) substitution. This substitution represents a substantial biochemical change: glycine is small and highly flexible, whereas phenylalanine is bulky, hydrophobic, and aromatic. Such a change could have multiple structural and functional consequences. The reduction in conformational flexibility may rigidify the local protein structure, potentially altering the dynamics of the RuBisCO large subunit. In addition, the introduction of an aromatic hydrophobic residue could modify local folding, subunit interactions, or protein stability, particularly under temperature or water stress. If these positions occur near catalytic or regulatory regions, even subtle structural changes could influence substrate affinity, CO₂/O₂ specificity, or catalytic efficiency key parameters determining photosynthetic performance under environmental stress [66, 68]. Direct functional validation would be required to confirm the precise physiological effects of these substitutions.

The extremely high ω value associated with codon 13 on a subset of branches reflects intense but localized episodic positive selection during a specific evolutionary interval. This pattern suggests that the G→F substitution was strongly favoured in particular lineages, potentially during adaptation to novel or more extreme environments. In the ecological context of Abutilon, this selective episode is temporally consistent with divergence between pantropical and Australian arid lineages, where differences in water availability, temperature extremes, and light intensity may have imposed strong selective pressures on photosynthetic efficiency and enzyme stability. The confinement of this signal to a limited proportion of branches indicates that this adaptive change was targeted rather than genome-wide, reflecting a clade-specific response to distinct ecological challenges. These functional interpretations remain hypothetical because no experimental or structural analyses were conducted.

Finally, suggestive MEME signals were detected in ycf1 at codons 874 and 1013 (p < 0.1). The ycf1 gene, a large and functionally conserved chloroplast gene implicated in protein translocation and plastid stability, has been repeatedly reported to experience positive selection in other plant lineages [60, 63, 69]. Although the ycf1 sites identified here did not reach conventional significance thresholds, they are consistent with broader patterns of localised adaptive change in genes associated with chloroplast maintenance and stress response. The amino acid substitutions at codons 874 (Thr→Leu) and 1013 (Leu→Phe) involve shifts in hydrophobicity that could influence protein structure or membrane association, indicating localized adaptive evolution that warrants further investigation with broader taxon sampling.

Not all genes or loci provide high-quality results in plant taxonomy and barcoding [70]. Therefore, species-specific or lineage-specific markers enhance resolution for barcoding and taxonomy [10, 70, 71]. In the current study, non-coding regions (intergenic spacers and introns) exhibited higher polymorphism than coding regions, which is in agreement with previous reports from Malvaceae [15–17, 48]. The most highly polymorphic loci were identified in non-coding regions, including trnH-psbA, rps19-rpl2, psbT-pbf1, psaC-ndhD, ndhJ-ndhK, and trnR-atpA. Among the coding sequences, matK, ycf1, ndhF, rbcL, and ccsA showed the highest level of polymorphism. Several of these loci have also been reported as polymorphic hotspots in other Malvaceae genera, such as Hibiscus (e.g. trnH–psbA, rpl33–rps18) [15], Triumfetta (trnR–atpA, ccsA–ndhD, ycf1) [48], Firmiana (trnR–atpA, ndhD–ccsA, rpl33–rps18) [17], and Theobroma (trnH–psbA, rpl33–rps18, rpl32–trnL) [16], indicating partial conservation of mutation-prone regions across the family.

In contrast, protein-coding genes were generally less polymorphic; however, matK, ycf1, ndhF, ccsA, and rbcL showed relatively elevated variation compared with other coding regions. These genes have been repeatedly applied in phylogenetic reconstruction and DNA barcoding of angiosperms, including Malvaceae [69, 71]. Collectively, the identified non-coding spacers, together with moderately variable coding loci, represent promising candidates for species delimitation and barcoding in Abutilon, while also aligning with broader plastome-level diversity patterns observed within Malvaceae [70, 72].

Our cp. genome-based phylogenetic analysis of Malvoideae confirms the distinctiveness of the three main tribes Malveae, Gossypieae, and Hibisceae and is largely congruent with previous cp. genome-based phylogenies of the subfamily [14, 15, 18]. Within this framework, Abutilon forms a well-supported lineage within Malveae, providing context for genus-level diversification. The analysis is consistent with biogeographic structuring within Abutilon, with two major clades reflecting contrasting evolutionary histories and geographic distributions. Both ML and BI analyses recovered similar topologies for all major Abutilon clades, providing confidence in the genus-level phylogenetic framework, although the inter-tribal backbone of Malvoideae differed between methods, with the placement of Gossypieae unresolved (ML: sister to Malveae, BS = 46%; BI: sister to Hibisceae, PP = 0.58), consistent with previous reports of uncertain tribal relationships in the subfamily [6, 7, 14, 15, 73]. The monophyly of Australian Abutilon species (Clade A) supports the distinctiveness of the Australian Malvaceae flora, where 381 described and 70 undescribed species are recognised, highlighting the evolutionary significance of this region [74]. The occurrence of Clade A species in desert and dry shrubland habitats is consistent with patterns reported for other Australian arid-adapted plant lineages [75]. The sampled members of this lineage are predominantly associated with arid environments. However, the present dataset does not permit formal testing of whether adaptation to aridity influenced diversification patterns within Abutilon. The strong phylogenetic grouping of Western Australian taxa, particularly A. amplum, A. cunninghamii and the A. lepidum–Abutilon sp. Pilbara–Abutilon sp. Pritzelianum complex is consistent with the occurrence of closely related taxa within Western Australia. However, weak support values (bootstrap = 36–52%; PP = 0.67) for some relationships may reflect limited phylogenetic signal, incomplete lineage sorting, or other unresolved evolutionary processes [76], as documented in other rapidly diversifying plant lineages [9, 77]. The concordance between ML and BI in recovering these weak support values confirms that this uncertainty reflects genuine phylogenetic signal limitations rather than methodological artefacts. Accordingly, evolutionary and biogeographic inferences drawn from these weakly supported nodes, including the precise branching order within the Australian radiation and the inter-tribal relationships of Malvoideae, require further evaluation using broader taxon sampling and biparentally inherited nuclear genomic or transcriptomic data. Such approaches have been found suitable in other angiosperms [78–80].

In contrast, Clade B comprises pantropical and subtropical species that span multiple continents, consistent with complex biogeographic histories among taxa distributed across multiple continents. Within this clade, A. theophrasti occupied the earliest-diverging position, while A. indicum was sister to the (A. abutiloides + A. fruticosum + A. hirtum) subclade, consistent with trans-continental distributions across Asia, Africa, and the Americas. Comparable trans-continental links have been reported in pantropical genera, where both natural long-distance dispersal and human-mediated spread contributed to present distributions [76, 81, 82].

Abutilon grandifolium was consistently recovered as sister to the remaining sampled Abutilon taxa in both ML (bootstrap = 100%) and BI analyses (PP = 1.00). However, because only a single South American representative was included and no formal ancestral-area reconstruction was performed, the present dataset does not permit inference of the geographic origin of the genus. Broader taxon sampling across Abutilon will be required to evaluate this question. The inclusion of provisional taxa (Abutilon sp. Pilbara and Abutilon sp. Pritzelianum) within the Australian clade with strong support provides molecular evidence for their distinctiveness and underscores ongoing cryptic diversity within Western Australia’s Pilbara region, a region renowned for high levels of short-range endemism [83].

Whilst our cp-based phylogeny resolves deeper relationships within Abutilon, weak support values within Australian subclades highlight the limitations of single-genome approaches for resolving recent radiations. Moreover, the cpSSR-based phylogeny showed limited topological congruence with the protein-coding gene phylogeny, likely reflecting the high mutation rates and homoplasy inherent to microsatellite loci, which further underscores the need for multi-locus approaches. Future studies that integrate nuclear genomic data from hundreds of loci based on the Angiosperms 353 target capture approach, partitioned analyses, or transcriptomic approaches could provide insights into cp–nuclear discordance, assess the role of hybridisation in Abutilon evolution, and further clarify species-level phylogenetic positions, as employed widely in other plant lineages [79, 80].

Conclusion and future perspectives

Analysis of 19 Abutilon cp. genomes revealed a conserved structure with minimal variation despite wide geographic distribution. Polymorphic loci are identified to provide valuable molecular markers for population genetics and barcoding. Both ML and BI phylogenetic analyses based on cp. genome data recovered two major clades within the sampled Abutilon taxa: an Australian lineage occurring predominantly in arid and semi-arid environments and a pantropical lineage. However, several internal relationships within these clades received only weak support, indicating limitations of cp. genome data alone for resolving recent evolutionary divergences. Although A. grandifolium was recovered as sister to the remaining sampled Abutilon taxa, broader taxon sampling and nuclear genomic data will be required to clarify the geographic origin and diversification history of Abutilon. Future integration of biparentally inherited nuclear genomic or transcriptomic data with broader taxon sampling is needed to resolve evolutionary relationships and diversification patterns in this genus.
