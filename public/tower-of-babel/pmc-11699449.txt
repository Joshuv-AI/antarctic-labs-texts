Hepatitis B prevalence in an endemic area of hepatitis C virus: A population-based study implicated in hepatitis elimination in Thailand
Authors: Nawarat Posuwan, Rujipat Wasitthankasem, Napaporn Pimsing, Wijittra Phaengkha, Saranya Ngamnimit, Preeyaporn Vichaiwattana, Sirapa Klinfueng, Maneerat Raksayod, Yong Poovorawan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Chronic hepatitis B (HBV) and C (HCV) are major health challenges in Thailand, with Phetchabun province, a known HCV-endemic area, being a key target for elimination efforts. This study aimed to assess HBV prevalence and identify associated risk factors in this province. Data was collected from three cross-sectional population studies: (1) adults in 2015 (n = 1,667, age 30–64 years), (2) young adults in 2017 (n = 1,453, age 18–30 years), both from high HCV-endemic districts, and (3) a province-wide study in 2018 (n = 4,769, age 35–64 years). Plasma samples were tested for HBsAg using the ARCHITECT assay. Results showed HBsAg seropositivity in 3.1 % of young adults in high-endemic districts, with significant associations with age, education, injecting drug use, and MSM behavior. Among adults, HBsAg prevalence was 5.9 %, linked to age and family liver disease history. Province-wide, 6.3 % of adults tested positive, with factors like gender and history of blood donation playing significant roles. Notably, age and blood donation were protective factors against HBV in adults. Analysis revealed a moderate HBV prevalence in those born before Thailand Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) program, while those born after had rates below 1 %. The findings emphasize distinct HBV transmission patterns in different age groups, influenced by social and behavioral shifts. This knowledge is crucial for effective hepatitis elimination strategies in the Phetchabun province and nationwide.

Introduction

Viral hepatitis continues to pose a significant challenge to global health, being one of the leading causes of death worldwide after COVID-19 in 2022.1 In 2019, chronic hepatitis B (CHB) affected around 296 million people, leading to approximately 820,000 deaths, with chronic hepatitis C (CHC) in 58 million individuals, causing 290,000 deaths.2 The progression of CHB tends to result in more severe outcomes compared to CHC over the long term, with higher associated mortality rates.3,4 Many people remain unaware of these infections due to mild or asymptomatic presentations, allowing these diseases to progress over time into severe liver conditions like cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).3,5

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) spreads primarily through blood-borne transmission, including transfusions, needle-sharing, unsafe medical procedures, and sexual contact with infected body fluids. Additionally, mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) is a significant route of infection in Southeast Asia, with a high chronicity rate of 90 % for infections acquired at birth, compared to just 5 % for adult-acquired infections.5,6 The Southeast Asian region has the third-highest prevalence of CHB, with about 60 million affected, contributing to a global prevalence of 3.8 % or 296 million cases.7

Prior to the widespread hepatitis B vaccination program, Thailand was classified as an endemic region with an HBV prevalence of 8 %.8 The introduction of universal vaccination in 1992 markedly reduced HBV rates, bringing the prevalence down from about 4 % to 2 % over ten years.9,10 However, the older population—those born before 1992—still experiences a higher HBV burden, with HBsAg positivity rates ranging from 3.26 % to 7.14 %, affecting 0.32 to 1 million people.10,11 Between 2008 and 2012, Thailand reported some of the world's highest HCC incidence rates, comparable to South Korea. Unlike the declining trend in Korea, cases in Thailand increased, and Thai individuals often developed HCC at a younger age, around 45 years, despite HCC contributing to only 27 % of liver cancer cases.12

While Thailand has successfully implemented HBV screening in blood donations,10,13 gaps remain in the general population screening. Public awareness of HBV remains limited, leading to low diagnosis rates and restricted access to care.2

The Phetchabun province, a region in Thailand with a high prevalence of hepatitis C (HCV) and a significant burden of liver cirrhosis and HCC, has an HCV seroprevalence of 7 %.14 Given this burden, a targeted hepatitis elimination initiative is needed in the area. This study explores HBV epidemiology, including HBsAg prevalence in younger and older cohorts within the Phetchabun HCV-endemic areas, and projects the HBV burden across the province using a 1:100 population sampling approach. This study aims to investigate the incidence and risk factors for HBV in areas with a high incidence of HCV, in order to inform planning for control, prevention, and treatment, aligned with the World Health Organization's goal of hepatitis elimination by 2030.

Methods

Study population

This study assessed HBV infection in the Phetchabun province, Thailand, an area with a high prevalence of HCV. Data was collected from three previous studies conducted at different time points: 2015,15 2017,16 and 2018.14 The 2015 study included 1667 adult participants (ages 30–64) from Lom Kao and Lom Sak districts, the regions with the highest HCV prevalence. In 2017, a follow-up study focused on 1453 young adults (ages 18–30) in the same districts. In 2018, a randomized sample of 4769 adults (ages 35–64) with a 1:100 ratio was gathered across all 11 districts of the province. Ethical considerations

Participants were informed about the study objectives, and written consent was obtained before participation. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine at Chulalongkorn University (IRB No. 258/58 and 266/61).

Laboratory methods

Serological HBsAg levels were measured using a chemiluminescent microparticle immunoassay (CMIA) on the automated ARCHITECT platform (Abbott, Wiesbaden, Germany). A positive HBsAg result was defined as a sample-to-cutoff (S/CO) ratio >1, following the manufacturer's instructions. Specimens from all three previous studies were analyzed for HBsAg status, with values ≤ 1 S/CO indicating a lack of HBV infection.

Statistical analysis

Data was summarized using percentages and mean values with standard deviations (SD). The prevalence of HBsAg among the three studies was compared using Chi-square tests in 2x2 contingency tables. Questionnaire data from the earlier studies (2015 and 2017) in high HCV-endemic areas was used to explore correlations between HBsAg seroprevalence and various risk factors, such as age, gender, education, occupation, and behavioral exposures (e.g., HCV-infected partner, family history of liver disease, male-to-male sexual contact (MSM), oral drug use, blood transfusions, and other percutaneous exposures). Univariate analysis was performed for factors with a p-value <0.02, followed by multivariate logistic regression to determine independent associations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics version 22 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Results

HBV prevalence and associated risk factors among young adults in high HCV-endemic areas of the Phetchabun province

Among 1453 young adults aged 18–30 years living in the Lom Kao and Lom Sak districts, which have the highest HCV prevalence in the Phetchabun province, the prevalence of HBV infection was found to be 3.1 % as indicated by HBsAg positivity. HBV prevalence and risk factors among adults in high HCV-endemic areas of the Phetchabun province

In the 2015 study, which included 1667 adults aged 30–64 years, the prevalence of HBV infection was 5.9 % based on HBsAg positivity. A comparison of HBV and HCV infection rates between adults and young adults revealed that adults were nearly twice as likely to have either infection (OR = 1.98, 95 % CI = 1.38–2.83, p = 0.0002), as shown in Table 1.

The analysis of risk factors in adults from high HCV-endemic areas identified age, surgical history, and family history of liver disease as relevant factors. Age (OR = 0.7, 95 % CI = 0.5–0.9, p = 0.002) and history of surgeries (OR = 0.6, 95 % CI = 0.4–0.9, p = 0.028) were found to be protective, while a family history of liver disease was a significant risk factor (OR = 1.8, 95 % CI = 1.1–3.0, p = 0.015). Multivariate analysis confirmed that age (OR = 0.6, 95 % CI = 0.5–0.8, p = 0.001) and a family history of liver disease (OR = 2.0, 95 % CI = 1.2–3.3, p = 0.009) remained significant predictors of HBV infection (Table 2, Supplementary Table S2).

HBV prevalence among adults in the Phetchabun province with a 1:100 random sampling ratio

In 2018, a randomized study of 4769 adults aged 35–64 years across the Phetchabun province (1:100 ratio) showed an HBV prevalence of 6.3 % as determined by HBsAg seropositivity. This was higher compared to young adults with an HBV prevalence of 3.1 % (OR = 2.10, 95 % CI = 1.53–2.89, p = 0.0001) (Table 1). Notably, the HBV prevalence varied significantly among districts, with the highest rates observed in Nam Nao (18 %) and Lom Kao (9.2 %). Risk factor analysis among adults in the Phetchabun province showed that oral drug use was significantly associated with a higher risk of HBV infection (OR = 2.2, 95 % CI = 1.2–3.9, p = 0.007). Conversely, gender (OR = 0.69, 95 % CI = 0.54–0.87, p = 0.002), history of blood donation (OR = 0.70, 95 % CI = 0.53–0.92, p = 0.010), and having an HCV-infected partner (OR = 0.54, 95 % CI = 0.31–0.93, p = 0.026) were identified as protective factors. Multivariate analysis confirmed that gender (OR = 0.70, 95 % CI = 0.54–0.90, p = 0.005) and blood donation history (OR = 0.61, 95 % CI = 0.46–0.82, p = 0.001) remained significantly associated with lower HBV infection risk (Table 2, Supplementary Table S3).

Risk factors for HBV infection across different districts in the Phetchabun province

For each district with an HBV prevalence greater than 8 % and a minimum of 30 participants, further analysis was conducted to determine relevant risk factors. Nam Nao (18 %) and Lom Kao (9.2 %) had the highest HBV infection rates in the province, with all questionnaire-identified factors being associated with HBV infection (Fig. 1). In Wang Pong, where HBV prevalence was 8.2 %, key risk factors were consistent with those in the previous districts, though occupation and razor blade use were not significant. Other districts exhibited a moderate HBV prevalence (2–8%) with varying associated risk factors, as detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

The variations in HBV and HCV prevalence in the Phetchabun province, with particularly highly HCV-endemic districts areas, can be attributed to differences in their primary transmission routes. Both viruses are transmitted through blood and body fluids, such as saliva, vaginal fluids, and semen.17 However, HCV is predominantly spread through percutaneous exposure, including unsafe medical practices, use of unsterilized medical equipment, injection drug use (IDU) among people who inject drugs (PWID), and other high-risk factors such as low socioeconomic status and limited access to healthcare.18 Similar to previous studies, research in the Phetchabun province has highlighted that adults, particularly males of working age who engage in risky behaviors−such as IDU and tattoos−and those with lower socioeconomic status; education level and occupation, were at higher risk for HCV infection.15,16 On the other hand, this study reveals that key risk factors for HBV infection among younger adults include age, lower educational attainment, IDU, and MSM sexual behaviour, similar to the transmission route of HCV infection. In contrast, a family history of liver disease was identified as a risk factor for HBV infection among adult residents in the most HCV-endemic districts. The HBV infection rate gradually declined with increasing age; for example, adults aged 50–54 years had a lower HBsAg seropositivity rate than those under 50. Moreover, across the province, results showed that female and blood donation history served as protective factors that significantly influenced HBV infection.

Before the HBV vaccine was introduced into the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in 1992, Thailand had a high HBV prevalence, exceeding 8 %,19 with a substantial burden of HBV-related complications; 30 % of cases progressed to cirrhosis and over 50 % to HCC.8 Following the integration of the HBV vaccine into the EPI program, a meta-analysis highlighted that children under 15 had a significantly lower HBV prevalence compared to adults.11 Similarly, among EPI-vaccinated individuals, only 1 % remained HBsAg−positive, demonstrating the vaccine high efficacy in Thailand.10 Most younger adults in this study were born after 1992, and the HBV prevalence dropped to 3.1 %, suggesting that they likely had immunity due to HBV vaccination. However, lower educational attainment and risk behaviors−such as IDU and tattoos−were significant risk factors associated with both HBV and HCV infections in HCV-endemic areas. Lower educational levels were further linked to behaviors like IDU and MSM, increasing the risk of bloodborne HBV transmission.20 For instance, a study in the United States reported that 11.8 % of PWID and 19 % of MSM had been infected with HBV, highlighting these groups as high-risk populations.21 The risk of HBV transmission also varies with the infectivity of the source; for example, a needlestick injury from an HBsAg-positive, HBeAg-negative source carries a 1–6% transmission risk, which increases to 22–40 % if the source is HBeAg-positive.22 In highly HCV-endemic areas, younger adults are changing their behaviors in ways similar to the major routes of HCV transmission, leading to an increased risk of HBV infection compared to the national average.

In 1979, previous studies had shown that the primary route of HBV transmission in Thailand was through mother-to-child, with approximately 30.9 % of infants born to HBV carrier mothers becoming infected.23 Then in 2014, a national survey among Thais born before 1992 showed an increasing trend of past HBV infection (evidenced by both HBsAg and anti-HBc positivity) with age and significantly higher levels of these serological markers compared to individuals born after 1992 or those who had received HBV vaccination through the EPI program.10 Recent studies in PWID adults reported no correlation between IDU and recent HBV infection (indicated solely by HBsAg positivity).24,25 However, a significant association was found between recent or past HBV infections and individuals in both the unsure and unvaccinated groups. Conversely, these two groups had lower anti-HBs seropositivity (indicative of immunity from natural infection or vaccination) compared to the vaccinated group.25 Similarly, in this study conducted in the areas with high HCV-endemicity due to percutaneous exposure, a family history of liver disease was the only risk factor associated with HBV infection among adults. The prevalence of HBV infection ranged from 5.9 to 6.3 %, slightly above the national rate of approximately 4.5 % among individuals born before 1992.10 This suggests that HBV infection among adults was not directly influenced by IDU but likely resulted from prior infection, such as vertical transmission (mother-to-child) or exposure to infected family members.26 This likely contributes to the high seropositivity for both HBsAg and anti-HBc, along with low levels of anti-HBs positivity.10,27 Moreover, a history of blood donation significantly reduced HBV risk, with blood donation serving as a protective factor, as Thailand's blood donation policy has effectively reduced HBV transmission through systematic viral screening, including nucleic acid amplification testing since 2006, and individual testing since 2016.28

Thailand's comprehensive approach to eliminating hepatitis includes strategies such as universal infant vaccination, prevention of perinatal transmission, stringent blood screening, and regulation of injection practices in healthcare settings.26 Nowadays, polices are in place to screen the Thai population born before 1992 which have at least one risk factor for HBV infection, in line with national guidelines. However, the criteria for receiving HBV treatment are more complex than those for HCV. Consequently, HBV screening rates remain low in Thailand.

This study highlights that individual born after 1992, who were included in the EPI program, exhibited a reduced prevalence of HBV compared to those born earlier. Nevertheless, they remained susceptible to HBV due to some high-risk behaviors. To mitigate these risks, enhancing public awareness of the dangers associated with bloodborne infections and implementing targeted screening strategies for individuals born before 1992 are crucial. Moreover, people with high-risk behaviors, such as PWID, MSM or HIV posirive individuals, should be priority targets for screening for viral hepatitis and evaluation of their immunity. If they have no evidence of infection or immunity, they should receive immunization, regardless of whether they were born before or after 1992. Such measures could help lower the incidence of severe hepatitis-related conditions like liver cirrhosis and HCC, as well as reduce HBV-related mortality. Although the study focus was limited to a single province in Thailand, this region is recognized for having the highest prevalence of bloodborne infections in the country.

In summary, this study provides crucial information, particularly regarding the epidemiology and risk factors for HBV infection in areas with a high HCV incidence in Thailand. Both HBV and HCV viruses are bloodborne, but their modes of transmission are different. Hepatitis C was more commonly transmitted through blood transfusions, needle sharing, or sharp objects. In a high endemic area of HCV, HBV was transmitted through vertical transmission (mother to child) in about half of the cases, with the remainder typically occurring in childhood through early adulthood. This often resulted in asymptomatic carriers or individuals with both anti-HBc and anti-HBs antibodies. Thus, adult transmission, which is similar to that of HCV, is less common. Therefore, the epidemiological data and risk factors for HBV in regions with a high HCV incidence are valuable for guiding prevention and control measures, aligning with the policy to eliminate hepatitis by 2030.
