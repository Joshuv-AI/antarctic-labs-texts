Diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia with ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone syndrome in Asia
Authors: Wei-Lin Huang, Min-Shu Hsieh, Yuan-Yu Tsai, Mong-Wei Lin, Ching-Kai Lin, Chia-Hung Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia (DIPNECH) is a rare, preinvasive lung disorder characterized by multifocal neuroendocrine cell proliferation. Because it presents with chronic cough and wheezing, it is frequently misdiagnosed as asthma. Ectopic adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) syndrome (EAS) arising from DIPNECH is exceptionally rare. We present a rare case of a 62-year-old woman with a long-standing history of presumed asthma who developed ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. Bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling confirmed ectopic ACTH secretion. Subsequently, comprehensive thoracic imaging revealed a potential ectopic source in a dominant right middle lobe nodule, accompanied by subtle micronodules and mosaic attenuation, which are characteristic of DIPNECH. Targeted lobectomy achieved complete remission, yielding a histopathological diagnosis of a neuroendocrine tumor within background DIPNECH that demonstrated marked immunohistochemical ACTH heterogeneity among hyperplastic cells and tumorlets. Consistent with previously reported cases, our findings highlight that even subtle pulmonary nodules can secrete ACTH and must be evaluated in unexplained EAS. Furthermore, because these subcentimeter nodules possess secretory potential and morphological progression risks, lifelong multidisciplinary surveillance is imperative.

Introduction

Diffuse idiopathic pulmonary neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia (DIPNECH) is a rare pulmonary disorder characterized by multifocal proliferation of pulmonary neuroendocrine cells (PNECs), which may progress into tumorlets or frank neuroendocrine tumors (NETs) [1, 2]. Predominantly affecting middle-aged, nonsmoking women, DIPNECH typically exhibits an indolent clinical course [3]. Because it frequently presents with chronic cough and obstructive ventilatory defects, it is widely misdiagnosed as asthma [3]. Hallmark computed tomography (CT) features of DIPNECH include multifocal pulmonary nodules and mosaic attenuation [1, 4]. As a preinvasive lesion with an estimated 27.3% risk of morphological progression into NETs, DIPNECH necessitates extended clinical follow-up [1, 4]. Furthermore, while exceedingly rare, these PNECs are capable of secreting adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), resulting in ectopic ACTH syndrome (EAS) [5].

Although increasingly recognized, the epidemiological prevalence of DIPNECH-associated EAS remains unknown due to its rarity [2]. While DIPNECH accounts for approximately 20% of well-differentiated pulmonary NET [6], its presentation with EAS is limited to only 9 documented cases in the literature [5, 7-13]. A review of previous cases reveals an insidious clinical progression, with latency periods extending up to 15 years, underscoring the necessity of lifelong follow-up [5]. Furthermore, even subtle pulmonary nodules can secrete ACTH and therefore must be evaluated in unexplained EAS. Unfortunately, the multifocal nature of DIPNECH often precludes definitive localization and complete surgical resection, necessitating bilateral adrenalectomy (BLA) in nearly 40% of cases [5, 7-13].

EAS secondary to DIPNECH is an exceptionally rare clinical entity in Asian populations. While 9 Western cases have been documented [5, 7-13], it remains sparsely described in Asian populations. Herein, we present a 62-year-old Asian woman with presumed asthma who developed classic cushingoid features, new-onset hyperglycemia, and hypertension. Following a comprehensive diagnostic workup that localized the ACTH source to a dominant right middle lobe nodule, she underwent a targeted lobectomy. Pathology confirmed a NET with background DIPNECH. Postoperatively, she has maintained complete biochemical and clinical remission for 2 years.

Case presentation

A 62-year-old woman with a medical history of asthma presented with progressive proximal lower extremity weakness over the preceding 4 months, manifesting as difficulty rising from a squatting position without assistance and resulting in multiple falls. She also reported hair thinning and hair loss, facial puffiness, bilateral lower extremity edema, easy bruising, and unintentional weight gain of 15 kg over the past 6 months. Her medications included amlodipine-valsartan 5-160 mg daily. She was only on inhaled fluticasone furoate/vilanterol (92/22 mcg once daily) and fexofenadine 60 mg twice daily for asthma.

On physical examination, her blood pressure was elevated at 171/83 mmHg. She exhibited cushingoid features, including round face, dorsocervical fat pad, central obesity, wide violaceous striae over the lower abdomen and bilateral lateral thighs. Hyperpigmentation of both areolae and pitting edema of the lower extremities were also noted. The skin was thin and fragile, with multiple ecchymoses. Neurological examination revealed proximal muscle weakness, with bilateral hip flexor strength graded 4/5.

Diagnostic assessment

As shown in Table 1, initial testing revealed hypokalemia at 3.0 mEq/L (SI: 3.0 mmol/L) (reference range, 3.5-5.1 mEq/L [SI: 3.5-5.1 mmol/L]) and an elevated hemoglobin A1c of 7.6% (SI: 60 mmol/mol) (reference value, < 5.7% [SI: <39 mmol/mol]). Consequently, oral glucose-lowering medications with metformin and linagliptin were initiated. Upon hospital admission, inhaled fluticasone was withheld prior to and throughout the endocrinological investigations. Hormonal evaluation showed an abnormal circadian rhythm of cortisol, significantly elevated 24-hour urinary free cortisol, and nonsuppressible cortisol levels following a low-dose 2-day dexamethasone suppression test (0.5 mg every 6 hours for 2d). Concurrently, elevated ACTH levels indicated the diagnosis of ACTH-dependent Cushing syndrome. To evaluate the skeletal complications of hypercortisolism, a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scan was performed, which revealed a T-score of −2.6 at the right femoral neck and a T-score of −1.9 at the lumbar spine (L1-L4), consistent with osteoporosis.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed a 3 × 3-mm, hypoenhancing lesion at the posterior aspect of pituitary gland, suggesting a pituitary microadenoma. A comprehensive anterior pituitary panel revealed suppression of gonadotropins, including follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) at 1.0 mIU/mL (SI: 1.0 IU/L) (postmenopausal reference range, 25.8-134.8 mIU/mL [SI: 25.8-134.8 IU/L]) and luteinizing hormone (LH) at 0.2 mIU/mL (SI: 0.2 IU/L) (postmenopausal reference range, 7.7-58.5 mIU/mL [SI: 7.7-58.5 IU/L]). Serum prolactin was marginally elevated at 30.7 ng/mL (SI: 1333.0 pmol/L) (reference range, 5.18-26.53 ng/mL [SI: 225.2-1153.5 pmol/L]). Collectively, above findings indicated suppression of the noncorticotroph anterior pituitary axes secondary to hypercortisolism (Table 1).

To determine whether the microadenoma was an ACTH-secreting adenoma (Cushing disease) or an incidentaloma, high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (2 mg every 6 hours for 2d) was performed, which failed to suppress cortisol by more than 50%. Bilateral inferior petrosal sinus sampling (BIPSS) with desmopressin stimulation showed no elevated central-to-peripheral ACTH ratio at baseline or after stimulation, which excluded pituitary-origin of ACTH secretion, confirming EAS (Table 2) and establishing the pituitary microadenoma as a nonfunctioning incidentaloma.

CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis disclosed a lobulated nodular lesion in the right middle lobe at the right infra-hilar region, scattered small and tiny nodules, and mosaic attenuation of bilateral lungs (Fig. 1). The abdominal sections demonstrated mild thickening of bilateral adrenal glands without space-occupying lesions, consistent with bilateral adrenal hyperplasia secondary to chronic ACTH stimulation. 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography (PET)/CT identified an FDG-avid nodular lesion in the medial aspect of the right middle lobe (Fig. 2). A conventional transbronchial needle aspiration (c-TBNA) guided by augmented fluoroscopy was performed to sample a right middle lobe pulmonary nodule, as a pulsatile vessel was identified within the lesion. Histopathologic examination confirmed a carcinoid tumor. Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was positive for thyroid transcription factor-1 (TTF-1), synaptophysin and chromogranin A and focally positive for ACTH.

Treatment

Based on the histopathologic findings, the patient underwent uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery with right middle lobe lobectomy and systematic lymph node dissection. Preoperative pulmonary function testing demonstrated a moderate mixed ventilatory defect, with a forced vital capacity (FVC) of 55.9% predicted, forced expiratory volume in 1s (FEV1) of 46.2% predicted, and an FEV1/FVC ratio of 66.9%.

Histopathology confirmed a grade 1 pulmonary NET with a mitotic count of <2 per 2 mm2 and no necrosis [14]. IHC staining was positive for TTF-1, synaptophysin and chromogranin A, while somatostatin receptor type 2A was negative. The background lung tissue showed multiple tumorlets and foci of neuroendocrine cell hyperplasia (NECH), compatible with DIPNECH. ACTH staining was positive in both the primary tumor and NECH, whereas the surrounding tumorlets were negative for ACTH (Fig. 3).

Outcome and follow-up

Plasma ACTH and cortisol levels declined rapidly after surgery. On postoperative day 9, cortisol and ACTH concentrations decreased to 2.50 μg/dL (SI: 69.0 nmol/L) (reference range, 3.7-19.4 μg/dL [SI: 102.1-535.2 nmol/L]) and 9.94 pg/mL (SI: 2.19 pmol/L) (reference range, 7.2-63.3 pg/mL [SI: 1.6-13.9 pmol/L]), respectively. Notably, the patient remained clinically stable without symptoms and signs of adrenal insufficiency, precluding the need for glucocorticoid replacement therapy during the postoperative recovery. Concurrently, the potassium level normalized to 4.4 mEq/L (SI: 4.4 mmol/L), blood pressure stabilized at 111/83 mmHg by day 18, and hemoglobin A1c decreased to 5.5% (SI: 37 mmol/mol) 1 month postoperatively, allowing for the discontinuation of potassium supplements, antihypertensives, and glucose-lowering medications.

At 2 years postoperatively, her morning serum cortisol had recovered to 10.4 μg/dL (SI: 286.9 nmol/L), alongside ACTH level of 18.5 pg/mL (SI: 4.1 pmol/L). Her sustained clinical eucortisolism supports hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal axis recovery and long-term remission. Given the diagnosis of DIPNECH, the patient continues regular follow-up in pulmonology and endocrinology clinics. Follow-up pulmonary function testing demonstrated a persistent moderate obstructive ventilatory defect. Active surveillance with annual chest CT revealed multiple small solid nodules in both lungs that remained grossly stable in size.

Discussion

We report a rare case of EAS secondary to DIPNECH. The underlying DIPNECH masqueraded as asthma, its presence suggested by subtle hallmark CT features of scattered small nodules and mosaic attenuation [1]. Adding to this deceptive presentation, a confounding pituitary microadenoma created a clinical picture indistinguishable from Cushing disease. Consequently, BIPSS was essential to confirm the pulmonary source of ACTH secretion. Following targeted resection, IHC revealed marked heterogeneity in ACTH expression across the DIPNECH spectrum. This discordance indicates unpredictable secretory capacity among residual lesions, warranting close longitudinal tracking even after complete biochemical resolution.

DIPNECH is a rare, under-recognized pulmonary disorder characterized by abnormal proliferation of PNECs that can extend into the bronchial lumen or form subepithelial tumorlets [1, 2]. These hyperplastic PNECs narrow the airways and secrete bioactive substances; collectively, these mechanisms may induce bronchoconstriction, bronchiolar fibrosis and constrictive bronchiolitis [15]. Consequently, patients present with chronic cough, dyspnea, and wheezing [2]. Pulmonary function tests predominantly reveal obstructive or mixed ventilatory defects, a finding that closely mimics asthma physiology. Thus, up to 40% of patients are initially misdiagnosed as asthma [15], earning DIPNECH the designation of an “asthma mimicker” [3]. This disorder exhibits a striking predilection for middle-aged, nonsmoking women [3]. Our patient's clinical profile aligns perfectly with these classic features, exhibiting evolving obstructive ventilatory defects and hallmark high-resolution CT findings of multiple small nodules with mosaic attenuation.

Reports of DIPNECH complicated by EAS remain exceedingly rare, and this clinical entity is sparsely described in Asian populations. A comprehensive review of the English literature identified only 9 previously documented cases of EAS secondary to DIPNECH [5, 7-13]. Table 3 summarizes these 9 cases alongside our patient. Consistent with the literature, a strong female predominance (8/9) was observed [3]. The diagnostic sequencing of EAS and DIPNECH varied widely. In DIPNECH-preceding cases, EAS developed 4 to 15 years after diagnosis, reinforcing the importance of sustained vigilance. In EAS-preceding cases, cyclical ACTH secretion frequently complicates the clinical picture, resulting in variable delays in diagnosing DIPNECH (2 months to 10 years). On chest CT, most cases presented with pulmonary nodules, with or without a dominant lesion. Patients failing to achieve postoperative biochemical remission or complete resection (Cases 4, 7) experienced disease recurrence after pulmonary resection, ultimately requiring BLA. By contrast, complete resection of a dominant nodule achieved remission (Case 6 and the present case).

Functional imaging plays distinct roles in evaluating DIPNECH-associated EAS. SSTR-PET detects approximately 65% of DIPNECH, capturing dominant pulmonary NETs but often missing microscopic tumorlets or NECH due to spatial resolution limits [4]. This aligns with the cases summarized in Table 3, where SSTR-PET positivity was primarily observed in cases harboring a typical carcinoid [10, 12, 13]. The false-negative result of Case 2 likely reflects the inferior sensitivity of 111In-pentetreotide SPECT [8, 16]. Meanwhile, 18F-FDG PET primarily serves to exclude coexisting dedifferentiated or high-grade malignancies, as low-grade neoplasms exhibit variable and frequently lower FDG avidity [17, 18]. Despite these limitations with lesions <5 mm, SSTR-PET remains valuable for systemic staging, detecting occult metastases, and guiding SSTR-targeted therapies (eg, somatostatin analogs [SSAs] or peptide receptor radionuclide therapy [PRRT]) [19].

Histopathologically, ACTH expression exhibited marked heterogeneity across the DIPNECH spectrum, varying unpredictably among NECH, tumorlets, and NETs. These clinicopathological features dictate surgical efficacy and overall outcomes, with approximately 50% of patients reaching remission and 40% of cases eventually requiring BLA. Given our patient's residual multifocal nodules (similar to Case 4), ongoing surveillance is crucial. Furthermore, because characteristic CT features are hallmarks of DIPNECH, subtle pulmonary nodules with mosaic attenuation in cases of unexplained EAS must be meticulously evaluated rather than dismissed as incidental.

The histopathological heterogeneity of DIPNECH poses a dual threat of morphological progression and delayed functional emergence. Following targeted lobectomy, our patient's cortisol and ACTH levels rapidly declined to 2.50 μg/dL (SI: 69.0 nmol/L) and 9.94 pg/mL (SI: 2.19 pmol/L), respectively. While these hormones remained detectable, these levels fulfilled established criteria for biochemical remission, typically defined as postoperative morning serum cortisol < 5 μg/dL (SI: 137.9 nmol/L) [20]. However, adrenal insufficiency requiring routine glucocorticoid replacement is often anticipated following the complete resection of all ACTH-secreting tumor [21]. Our patient presented a unique clinical trajectory. She achieved biochemical remission and maintained detectable cortisol without the need for glucocorticoid supplement, which might raise the possibilities of the residual low-grade ACTH secretion inherent to the multifocal nature of DIPNECH. Because 87.5% of DIPNECH patients harbor more than 1 disease component along the DIPNECH spectrum [4], targeted resection inevitably leaves behind residual lesions. These residual lesions pose a long-term hazard. In our case, the residual foci of NECH were ACTH-positive. This latent secretory potential might carry the risk of delayed progression into overt Cushing syndrome. This aligns with reports of initially nonfunctioning DIPNECH nodules developing functional EAS up to 15 years postdiagnosis [10]. Conversely, the residual tumorlets were ACTH-negative; however, such functionally silent lesions can progressively enlarge [22] and carry a 27.3% risk of progressing into frank NETs [4]. Consequently, this dual threat requires diligent long-term clinical observation even after successful surgical resection.

This case highlights the critical role of BIPSS in preventing misdirected pituitary surgery when a microadenoma confounds unexplained EAS. While targeted resection achieved biochemical remission, limitations included a brief 2-year follow-up, unavailable NET biomarkers (eg, chromogranin A, 24-hour urinary 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid [5-HIAA], neuron-specific enolase [NSE], and pro-gastrin-releasing peptide [proGRP]), the absence of concurrent ACTH measurements during dexamethasone suppression tests and a lack of SSTR-based imaging to map residual micronodules. Crucially, this case reiterates that when evaluating unexplained EAS, subtle pulmonary nodules must not be overlooked. Given the persistent dual threats of morphological progression and delayed hormonal recurrence, this report underscores the necessity of lifelong clinical surveillance even after successful targeted resection.
