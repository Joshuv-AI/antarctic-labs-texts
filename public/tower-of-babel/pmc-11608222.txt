Identifying Parkinson’s disease and its stages using static standing balance
Authors: Dawoon Jung, Dallah Yoo, Jinwook Kim, Tae-Beom Ahn, Kyung-Ryoul Mun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe current assessment of Parkinson’s disease (PD) relies on dynamic motor tasks, limiting accessibility. This study aimed to propose an innovative approach to identifying PD and its stages using static standing balance and machine learning. A total of 210 participants were recruited, including a control group and five PD groups categorized by stage. Each participant completed a 10-s static standing balance task in which center of pressure trajectory data in the medial-lateral and anterior-posterior directions were collected. Features were extracted from these trajectory data and the data derived from them using both representation learning and handcrafting methods. A Transformer encoder-based classifier was trained on these features and achieved an F1-score of 0.963 in classifying the six study groups. This approach enhances the accessibility of PD assessment, enabling earlier detection and timely intervention. The novel data mining framework introduced in this study heralds a new era of time-series data-driven digital healthcare.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Biomedical engineering

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the fastest-growing neurological disease in the world1. Individuals with PD face significant challenges in controlling their body movements due to symptoms such as tremors, rigidity, bradykinesia, and postural instability. As these symptoms progress, maintaining balance, walking, and performing daily activities, such as eating and dressing, becomes increasingly difficult. In addition to these motor symptoms, non-motor symptoms including cognitive impairment, mood and behavioral problems, sleep disorders, and visual hallucinations profoundly impact the quality of life of PD patients2.

Regular assessment of PD is crucial for early detection and timely intervention, which improves prognosis and helps prevent unnecessary disease progression. The Hoehn and Yahr (HY) stages are widely used to identify PD and its progression3. Recently, the modified HY stages, which incorporate seven clinical stages to provide a more detailed and sophisticated evaluation of PD progression, were introduced4. However, using these HY stages for assessment requires face-to-face interviews and motor examinations by skilled professionals, which challenges efficient and continuous monitoring of PD.

Attempts to identify PD and its HY stages leveraging advancements in sensor and machine learning technologies have yielded remarkable achievements. A study trained a support vector machine model with resting tremor data collected using a 3-axis accelerometer and classified healthy controls and PD patients at HY stages 1, 2, 3, and 4 with an average accuracy of 89%5. Studies using deep learning models and gait data collected with smart insoles classified healthy controls and PD patients at HY stages 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0, achieving accuracies ranging from 88.9% to 98.9%6–10.

Despite these approaches opening new avenues for monitoring PD without the need for skilled professionals, some challenges remain unaddressed. The resting tremor data-based approach has limited applicability because resting tremors are not present in all patients and, moreover, they appear at random, meaning the data may not be available all the time and reflect the progression of the disease properly. Furthermore, the gait data-based approach requires a 2-min walking period, which may pose limitations in cases where such dynamic movements are not feasible. Additionally, this approach cannot identify patients at HY stages 1.0 and 1.5 which correspond to early-stage PD. Detecting early-stage PD is crucial for preventing the worsening of pathological conditions and achieving a good prognosis.

Hence, this study aims to propose an innovative approach to identifying PD and its stages using static standing balance, without necessitating tasks that require dynamic movements or the involvement of skilled professionals. Center of pressure (CoP) trajectory data will be collected during a 10-s static standing balance task. Features will be extracted from these data using both representation learning and handcrafting methods. A Transformer encoder-based classifier will be trained on these features and its performance in classifying individuals without PD and PD patients at HY stages 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, and 3.0 will be evaluated. This study will explore whether static standing balance function can provide new insights for PD assessment. The findings could reduce the burden of PD assessment, make self-monitoring in everyday life feasible, and facilitate earlier detection and timely intervention.

Discussion

This study pioneered an innovative approach to identifying PD and its stages using static standing balance, without necessitating tasks that require dynamic movements or the involvement of skilled professionals. Participants included individuals without PD as well as PD patients at HY stages ranging from 1.0 to 3.0. They were instructed to perform a 10-s static standing balance task. During the task, CoP trajectory data in the ML and AP directions were collected, and features were engineered from these trajectory data along with speed, velocity, acceleration, and jerk data derived from them. Both representation learning and handcrafting methods were employed in the feature extraction process. A Transformer encoder-based classifier trained with these features achieved an F1-score of 0.963 in classifying six study groups of control, HYS 1.0, HYS 1.5, HYS 2.0, HYS 2.5, and HYS 3.0. No misclassification of a PD patient as a control or vice versa was observed. By eliminating the need for dynamic motor tasks, which can be challenging in certain situations, this study contributes to easing the burden of PD assessment and making self-monitoring in everyday life feasible.

Studies have attempted to use kinematic or kinetic data as inputs for machine learning models to identify PD and its progression. However, in most of these studies, the kinematic or kinetic data have been primarily limited to dynamic motor functions which are often impaired in PD patients6–10. Inspiringly, there was a study that used CoP data collected during a static standing task and achieved an accuracy of 81% in classifying PD patients and individuals without PD14. Yet, this binary classification limits its implications in clinical practice, as it lacks information regarding PD stages. Requiring a somewhat long experiment time of approximately 25 min may also hinder its broader application. Our study can be considered an advancement, as not only was the achieved accuracy fairly high in the classification of multiple PD stages, but the duration required for the experiment was also reduced to 10 s. The 10-s static standing balance task is relatively less burdensome compared to dynamic motor tasks such as walking and requires minimal professional setup and assistance. This approach not only significantly increases the accessibility of PD assessment and monitoring but also eliminates many of the associated constraints. Consequently, it contributes to the earlier detection of PD and timely intervention, improving prognosis and the quality of life for both patients and caregivers. Notably, this study devised a method to identify PD in its early stages, specifically HY stages 1.0 and 1.5, which have been somewhat overlooked in previous studies. Early assessment of the disease allows for a broader range of treatment options and offers greater potential for slowing or even halting disease progression. By demonstrating that early-stage PD can be accompanied by subtle changes in static standing balance function, this study provides new insights into PD pathology and paves the way for future research.

The favorable results of this approach may have stemmed from the novel framework that utilized a multifaceted analysis of CoP data. Unlike studies that rely solely on ellipse parameters, this study incorporated parameters from various domains such as time, frequency, and nonlinear domains. The speed, velocity, acceleration, and jerk data derived from the trajectory data were rich with unique information, each encapsulating distinct intrinsic characteristics. Acceleration data provided crucial insights into movement intensity, while jerk data highlighted the impact of sudden movements or changes in motion, which are not as evident in speed or velocity data alone. This multifaceted analysis might have provided comprehensive information on static standing balance characteristics.

Extracting parameters sequentially over time and employing Transformer-based sequence learning may have also contributed to the fair performance of the proposed framework. Maintaining balance requires the integration of sensory inputs from vision, the vestibular system, and proprioception, as well as postural reflexes essential for quickly regaining balance when disturbed15. As PD progresses, the increasing impairment of sensory integration functions and postural reflexes delays postural responses, leading to temporal changes in CoP data while standing still15,16. Therefore, implementing time-sequential analysis to learn the temporal patterns and dependencies within the CoP data would have uncovered differences in static standing balance characteristics across the stages of disease progression. This framework can further be applied to understanding movement dynamics and identifying adaptive and compensatory mechanisms that preserve postural stability.

The study found that as HY stages advanced, handcrafted features such as MLAP_CoPT_RMI99 and AP_CoPT_HP increased, while ML_CoPT_RR decreased. These changes suggest that the CoP patterns involved in maintaining balance become increasingly variable, complex, irregular, nonlinear, and unpredictable as PD progresses. This may be driven by the gradual loss of dopamine, which plays a significant role in regulating motor functions, and result in worsening postural instability17.

When the performance of the Base classifier was compared to that of the Base-HC classifier, it was found that handcrafted features contributed to preventing underestimation. This may be due to handcrafted features capturing subtle yet critical aspects of the data that automated learning might overlook. The high sensitivity of these features in detecting critical aspects relevant to each HY stage might help prevent underestimation. On the other hand, when the performance of the Base classifier was compared to that of the Base-RL classifier, it was found that representation learning features contributed to preventing overestimation. This may result from the ability of representation learning to de-emphasize or ignore noisy data, which is more likely to be observed at higher HY stages. When the performance of the Base-HC and Base-RL classifiers was compared, the Base-RL classifier was found to be less accurate. This suggests that the representation learning features played a more significant role in the classification. Representation learning is known for its ability to automatically discover complex patterns and hierarchical representations within the data. It can also fine-tune the feature extraction process during training, capturing the underlying structure of the data more effectively.

When the performance of the Base classifier was compared to that of the Base-AP classifier, it was found that the Base-AP classifier produced four more misclassifications between the HYS 2.0 and HYS 2.5. This suggests that CoP characteristics in the AP direction are particularly effective in distinguishing between HY stages 2.0 and 2.5. HY stages 2.0 and 2.5 are both characterized by bilateral symptoms without severe balance impairment, but HY stage 2.5 marks the onset of mild postural instability. Remarkably, the insights typically gained from the pull test—where patients are pulled backward to test their ability to recover balance—can also be derived from a simple static standing balance assessment. When the performance of the Base classifier was compared to that of the Base-ML classifier, the Base-ML classifier produced two more misclassifications between the HYS 1.0 and HYS 1.5. This indicates that CoP characteristics in the ML direction are particularly effective in differentiating between HY stages 1.0 and 1.5. HY stages 1.0 and 1.5 are both characterized by unilateral symptoms, with HY stage 1.5 including progression to axial symptoms such as reduced facial expression, slight trunk bending, and decreased arm swing. These characteristics could be inferred from a simple static standing balance assessment without directly assessing axial symptoms. When the Base-AP and Base-ML classifiers were compared, the Base-ML classifier was found to be less accurate, suggesting that CoP characteristics in the ML direction contributed more to the identification of PD and its stages. A possible explanation can be found in that the degeneration of the basal ganglia accompanying PD progression affects the deterioration of lateral postural control mechanisms18–20.

As for the Base-DD classifier, which produced five additional underestimations and four additional overestimations compared to the Base classifier, it can be speculated that the speed, velocity, acceleration, and jerk data may have provided distinct insights not available from trajectory data alone.

Despite the achievements, this study has several limitations. First, the study was not able to include participants with a diverse ethnic or geographical background and all participants were exclusively Korean. To ensure generalizability, future validation in different ethnicities and larger populations should follow. Second, the study did not account for the progressive nature of PD over time. We are planning a follow-up longitudinal study incorporating the progression of PD into the proposed approach. Third, the equipment used for this study is rather specialized and given the circumstances, it may not be readily accessible. Future work with a more available alternative such as smart insoles is on its way. Last, the study did not include a comparison of the proposed approach with other assessment tools for PD. This study aimed to gauge the potential of static standing balance in the assessment of PD and future work that compares the proposed approach with traditional assessment tools for PD will follow.

Methods

Participants

A total of 210 participants were enrolled in the study. Figure 3 illustrates the participant recruitment process along with the data collection procedure. All participants were able to stand and walk unassisted and were without apparent cognitive impairment. None of them had paraparesis, hemiparesis, dyskinetic cerebral palsy, or structural abnormalities in both legs at the time of the experiment. A total of 188 participants were PD patients diagnosed by a neurologist at Kyung Hee University Hospital using the UK PD Society Brain Bank criteria21: 25 patients at HY stage 1.0 (HYS 1.0 group), 20 patients at HY stage 1.5 (HYS 1.5 group), 70 patients at HY stage 2.0 (HYS 2.0 group), 45 patients at HY stage 2.5 (HYS 2.5 group), and 28 patients at HY stage 3.0 (HYS 3.0 group). The remaining 22 individuals were age- and sex-matched controls who exhibited no Parkinsonian features on the neurological examination and had no history of neurological or psychiatric illness (control group).

Data collection and preprocessing

All participants were required to stand still for 10 s on the force distribution measurement system (Zebris FDM-T, Zebris Medical GmbH, Isny, Germany) with their eyes open, arms at their sides, and feet at shoulder-width apart. The system was placed on a flat surface and collected CoP trajectory data in the medial-lateral direction (ML_CoPT) and those in the anterior-posterior direction (AP_CoPT) with a sampling frequency of 60 Hz while the participants stood upright. Following the convention of previous literature, the 10-s duration was chosen to collect robust data without burdening or discomforting participants22–26. All PD patients were in an off-medication state during the experiment.

To minimize noise in the data, the initial and final seconds of the ML_CoPT and AP_CoPT were discarded. Data points with absolute robust Z-scores exceeding 3 were identified as outliers and replaced with the averages of the four nearest neighboring data points27. A 5th-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz was applied to the remaining data. The mean value of the data was subtracted from each data point to correct for variations in the foot positions of the participants. Classifier training and evaluation

This study employed stratified hold-out cross-validation to assess the generalizability of the classifiers. First, 75% of participants from each study group (16, 18, 15, 52, 33, and 21 participants from the control, HYS 1.0, HYS 1.5, HYS 2.0, HYS 2.5, and HYS 3.0 groups, respectively) were allocated to the training and validation datasets. Three-fold cross-validation was applied to these datasets to tune the hyperparameters of the classifiers with minimal risk of overfitting. The remaining 25% of participants (6, 7, 5, 18, 12, and 7 participants from the control, HYS 1.0, HYS 1.5, HYS 2.0, HYS 2.5, and HYS 3.0 groups, respectively) were assigned to the held-out test dataset, which was used to evaluate the performance of the optimized classifiers.

Three methods were employed to address class imbalance in the training dataset and enhance its diversity. The first method involved jittering, where random Gaussian noise with standard deviations of 0.5 and 1.0 was added to the original data. Since jittering simulates additional sensor noise, it can enhance the robustness of predictive models against noisy inputs28. Figure 4b shows data synthesized from the original data in Fig. 4a through the application of jittering. The second method involved decimation followed by interpolation, which can improve the robustness of predictive models against sparsely sampled inputs. This study used two decimation factors, 3 and 4, and applied linear and cubic spline interpolation methods. Figure 4c illustrates data synthesized from Fig. 4a through the application of decimation and interpolation. The third method combined decimation, interpolation, and jittering. Figure 4d shows the synthesis of data from Fig. 4a through the application of decimation followed by interpolation and jittering. The training datasets from the control, HYS 1.0, HYS 1.5, HYS 2.0, HYS 2.5, and HYS 3.0 groups were augmented approximately 14, 13, 15, 4, 7, and 11 times, respectively.

The second step involved extracting two types of features: representation learning features and handcrafted features. The representation learning features were automatically learned by a neural network designed to capture complex patterns within the data. Initially, Min-Max scaling was applied to the ML_CoPT, AP_CoPT, and the data derived from them, except for the MLAP_CoPT, to normalize the data within a consistent range. Subsequently, sequence merging was performed to combine multiple sequences into a single sequence by inserting delimiters between them. Segment embedding and positional encoding were then applied to capture diverse patterns and relationships within the sequence. Finally, a Transformer encoder with a multi-head attention mechanism was employed to generate informative and nuanced representation learning features29. In contrast, the handcrafted features were manually designed based on domain knowledge. The extraction of these features began with applying the time-sequential parameter extraction method to the ML_CoPT, AP_CoPT, and the data derived from them except for the MLAP_CoPT. A sliding window with a length of 2 s and an overlap of 1 s was used to construct parameter sequences for seven time-domain, six frequency-domain, and seventeen nonlinear parameters listed in Supplementary Table 3. Supplementary Figure 1 illustrates the construction of a parameter sequence for the standard deviation (SD) of the ML_CoPT. The parameter sequence designated as ML_CoPT_SD consists of seven SD values calculated from the ML_CoPT recorded between 0 and 2 s (ML_CoPT_SD1), 1 and 3 s (ML_CoPT_SD2), 2 and 4 s (ML_CoPT_SD3), 3 and 5 s (ML_CoPT_SD4), 4 and 6 s (ML_CoPT_SD5), 5 and 7 s (ML_CoPT_SD6), and 6 and 8 s (ML_CoPT_SD7). Twelve ellipse parameters listed in Supplementary Table 3 were extracted from the MLAP_CoPT. Min-Max scaling was applied to each parameter, followed by feature selection using the ReliefF algorithm30. The importance of selected features in identifying PD and its HY stages was evaluated using the ReliefF score. Statistical analyses for mean comparison were conducted to determine the statistical significance of differences in selected features among the six study groups.

The handcrafted features were transformed into high-dimensional vectors using single linear layers and subsequently merged with the representation learning features. Layer normalization and positional encoding were applied to the features and the processed features were fed into a Transformer encoder. The output of the Transformer encoder was passed through a multilayer perceptron head consisting of two fully connected layers and a softmax layer.

Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search within the range of values presented in Supplementary Table 1, and the best set of hyperparameters was selected based on classification performance on the validation datasets. Classification performance was evaluated using macro-averaged recall, precision, and F1-score to ensure an equitable evaluation across all classes. These metrics were calculated using Eqs. (1–3).

In Eqs. (1) and (2), TP refers to True Positives, which are cases correctly predicted as positive; FN refers to False Negatives, which are positive cases incorrectly predicted as negative; and FP refers to False Positives, which are negative cases incorrectly predicted as positive.

Ablation study

An ablation study was conducted on both the features and input data. Feature ablation was performed to analyze the respective impacts of the representation learning features and handcrafted features on classification performance. Input data ablation was carried out to evaluate the respective contributions of the ML_CoPT and AP_CoPT to classification performance.

The classifier using both the representation learning features and handcrafted features extracted from the ML_CoPT, AP_CoPT, and the data derived from them was designated as a Base classifier. When the handcrafted features were excluded from the Base classifier, it was designated as a Base-HC classifier. When the representation learning features were excluded, the classifier was designated as a Base-RL classifier. The impact of the handcrafted features was assessed by comparing the classification performance of the Base-HC classifier to that of the Base classifier, while the impact of the representation learning features was determined by comparing the classification performance of the Base-RL classifier to that of the Base classifier.

When the AP_CoPT and the data derived from it were excluded from the Base classifier, it was designated as a Base-AP classifier. When the ML_CoPT and the data derived from it were excluded, the classifier was designated as a Base-ML classifier. The contribution of the AP_CoPT to classification accuracy was assessed by comparing the classification performance of the Base-AP classifier to that of the Base classifier, while the contribution of the ML_CoPT was determined by comparing the classification performance of the Base-ML classifier to that of the Base classifier.

The classifier using only the ML_CoPT and AP_CoPT, without incorporating the speed, velocity, acceleration, and jerk data derived from them, was designated as a Base-DD classifier. Its classification performance was evaluated to determine the impact of the speed, velocity, acceleration, and jerk data on classification accuracy.
