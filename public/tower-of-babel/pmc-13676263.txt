Association of the sarcopenia index based on the serum creatinine to cystatin C ratio and genetic risk with venous thromboembolism: a population-based cohort study
Authors: Yuyang Sun, Tingting Liu, Jun Deng, Xi Cao, Liang Tang, Xingjie Hao, Yu Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) results from the interplay between environmental and genetic factors. The relationship between sarcopenia index (SI) and VTE risk remains unclear. The combined effects of SI and genetic susceptibility on VTE risk have not been fully elucidated. We therefore investigated these associations using data from the UK Biobank.MethodsCox proportional hazards models were used to assess the relationship between SI and genetic susceptibility with incident VTE. Restricted cubic spline models were applied to examine the exposure-response relationships.ResultsAmong 447,681 participants, 12,008 developed VTE during a median follow-up of 13.4 years. Higher SI was associated with a lower risk of VTE. The fully adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for Q2 to Q4 vs Q1 were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83-0.92), 0.82 (95% CI, 0.78-0.87), and 0.77 (95% CI, 0.72-0.82), respectively. Restricted cubic spline analysis showed an inverse nonlinear association between SI and VTE (P for nonlinear <.001). Participants with high genetic risk had a higher risk of VTE (HR = 2.56; 95% CI, 2.42-2.72). Furthermore, participants with both low SI levels and high genetic susceptibility exhibited a significantly increased risk of VTE compared with those with high SI and low genetic risk (HR = 3.60; 95% CI, 3.14-4.12).ConclusionParticipants with lower SI had a significantly higher risk of VTE. Consistent with previous UK Biobank findings, higher polygenic risk score levels were associated with an increased risk of VTE. Moreover, participants with both low SI levels and high genetic risk exhibited the greatest risk of VTE. These findings suggest that SI may serve as a simple and accessible marker for identifying individuals at elevated risk of VTE.

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) encompasses deep-vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism [1]. VTE is a prevalent thrombotic vascular disease with an annual incidence of 1 to 2 cases per 1000 individuals, posing a significant global public health burden [2].

VTE is a disease of multifactorial origin, driven by the intricate interaction between genetic susceptibility and environmental risk factors [3]. Sarcopenia refers to progressive and accelerated reduction of skeletal muscle function and mass, often leading to impaired physical performance, reduced mobility, and a higher risk of negative outcomes including falls, fractures, and premature mortality [4]. Mechanistically, loss of muscle mass may promote insulin resistance, leading to oxidative stress. This triggers inflammation and endothelial damage [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Chronic low-grade inflammation and endothelial damage may facilitate thrombus formation, thereby contributing to the development of VTE. Numerous assessment methods are currently available to characterize sarcopenia in both clinical practice and research settings. The conventional diagnosis of sarcopenia primarily depends on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, computed tomography, or bioelectrical impedance analysis [9]. However, such methodologies are resource-intensive, requiring dedicated equipment and technical expertise, thereby rendering the diagnostic process laborious and costly. Accordingly, there was a need for accessible, clinically validated, and cost-effective biomarkers to facilitate the early identification and diagnosis of sarcopenia in routine practice. Kashani et al. proposed the sarcopenia index (SI), which was calculated as the serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio × 100, and reported that SI could serve as an estimate of muscle mass [10]. Recently, a growing body of research has examined the association between SI and cardiovascular disease [11]. Evidence from a Chinese study involving middle-aged and older individuals indicated that lower SI was significantly associated with an elevated risk of incident cardiovascular disease, heart disease, and stroke [12]. Another study among individuals in middle to older age in China showed that low SI was closely related to elevated mortality [13]. Although previous studies have explored the associations between SI and diverse health outcomes, evidence regarding the association between this novel index and VTE risk remains scarce and inconclusive.

Genetic factors have emerged as significant contributors to VTE risk. Comprehensive genome-wide association studies have identified numerous genetic loci associated with VTE risk [14]. Polygenic risk score (PRS) has been developed to quantify an individual’s genetic susceptibility to VTE by integrating the cumulative effects of multiple genetic variants [15]. It remains unclear whether genetic susceptibility modifies the association between SI and the risk of VTE. Therefore, we aimed to investigate the association between SI and VTE risk in the UK Biobank population and to examine the joint effect of SI and genetic susceptibility on VTE risk.

Methods

Study design and population

This cohort study was performed using data derived from the UK Biobank, a large prospective population-based cohort including approximately 500,000 participants recruited across the United Kingdom from 2006 to 2010. Written informed consent covering questionnaire information and biological samples was provided by all participants. The UK Biobank received ethical approval from the North West Multi-Centre Research Ethics Committee. Participants were excluded if they had missing values for serum creatinine or cystatin C (N = 32,391), a diagnosis of VTE at baseline (N = 15,150), an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (N = 7174), nonEuropean ancestry (N = 75,655), or missing genetic information (N = 1417) (Figure 1). Baseline serum creatinine and cystatin C measurements

The UK Biobank measured a wide range of biochemical markers in biological samples collected at baseline in all participants. Blood samples were obtained at baseline and stored at –80 °C. Serum creatinine was assessed enzymatically using a Beckman Coulter AU5800. Cystatin C was measured by an immune-turbidimetric method on a Siemens Advia 1800. The calculation of SI was based on the following equation: SI = serum creatinine (mg/dL)/cystatin C (mg/L) × 100 [16,17]. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine formula [18].

Calculation of PRS

PRSs offer a personalized assessment of genetic susceptibility to disease by integrating genetic risk information from across the entire genome [19]. For our study, genetic risk was assessed using the standard VTE PRS set precomputed by the UK Biobank. Standard PRS values for VTE were provided for each participant and can be accessed via UK Biobank data field 26289 [20]. The VTE PRS used in this study was the UK Biobank standard PRS developed by Genomics plc (“plc” stands for “public limited company”) and derived from the external meta-analysis of multiple genome-wide association studies. Bayesian shrinkage and linkage disequilibrium–aware variant selection were used to construct the PRS. Individual PRS values were derived by summing allele dosages weighted according to their effect sizes, thereby representing cumulative genetic predisposition [21]. This PRS has been used in multiple studies based on UK Biobank data [22]. Detailed procedures about genotyping, imputation, and quality control have been described previously [23]. Based on PRS tertiles, participants were grouped into low, intermediate, and high genetic risk groups.

Ascertainment of VTE and follow-up

Participants with prevalent VTE at baseline were excluded, and the study outcome was incident VTE occurring during follow-up. VTE was identified according to the International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) and was defined as having at least one of the following ICD-10 diagnosis codes: I82 (DVT), I26 (pulmonary embolism), and I80 (thrombophlebitis) [24]. Diagnostic information in the UK Biobank was ascertained via record linkage to primary care data, death register records, hospital inpatient data, and self-reported medical conditions. Follow-up time was defined from the baseline attendance to the earliest occurrence of outcome diagnosis, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of follow-up (July 9, 2022).

Covariates

Baseline assessments encompassed a range of relevant covariates, which included sociodemographic indicators, lifestyle behaviors, and other additional confounders. Specifically, the covariates assessed comprised age, ethnicity, sex (female or male), smoking and drinking status, Townsend deprivation index, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, systolic blood pressure, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), white blood cell (WBC), urine creatinine (Ucr), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin, and glucose. BMI was derived from measured height and weight and classified using World Health Organization criteria: <18.5, 18.5–24.9, 25.0–29.9, and ≥30 kg/m2. Physical activity was evaluated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire, quantified in metabolic equivalent task–minutes per week, and subsequently classified into low, moderate and high levels. Smoking status was classified into nonsmokers, ex-smokers, and current smokers. Drinking status was classified into nondrinkers, ex-drinkers, and current drinkers. Townsend deprivation index was an area-based measure of socioeconomic deprivation. LDL and glucose were measured using enzymatic selective protection and enzymatic methods, respectively, on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer (Beckman Coulter). CRP was assessed with an immunoturbidimetric assay on the same analyzer. Ucr was assessed enzymatically with Beckman Coulter system. Blood pressure was determined as the mean of 2 consecutive measurements. WBC measurements were performed on the Beckman Coulter LH750 analyzer. All of the above information was obtained at baseline using electronic questionnaires, in-person interviews, physical examinations, and biological samples.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of the study population were described by SI quartiles. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with IQRs, whereas categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Missing data in all covariates were less than 20%, with details on the proportion of missingness provided in Supplementary Table 1. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations.

Cox proportional hazards models were applied to assess the associations of SI and genetic susceptibility with incident VTE by estimating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. Schoenfeld residuals were used to assess the proportional hazard assumption. For SI, participants were categorized into 4 groups using the quartiles of baseline SI levels, with the lowest quartile as the reference. In addition, SI was included as a continuous variable (per 1 SD increase) in the Cox proportional hazard model. Moreover, 3 models were developed to minimize the potential influence of confounding factors (model 1: adjusted for age and sex; model 2: adjusted for sex, race, age, smoking status, Townsend deprivation index, drinking status, and physical activity; and model 3: adjusted for sex, race, age, smoking status, Townsend deprivation index, drinking status, CRP, physical activity, BMI, LDL, WBC, systolic blood pressure, and glucose). In the association analysis of PRS with VTE, adjustments were additionally made for the first 5 genetic principal components and the genotyping batch. E values were calculated from the fully adjusted HRs and their 95% CI limits closest to the null to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounding. For HRs below 1, reciprocal estimates and upper confidence limits were used.

The cumulative incidence functions of incident VTE according to SI quartiles and PRS categories were estimated using the Aalen-Johansen estimator to account for the competing risk of death. Differences across groups were assessed using Gray’s test. The restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were performed to investigate the exposure-response relationship between SI and PRS with the risk of VTE in the multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models.

Subgroup analyses along with several sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of our findings. Subgroup analyses were performed according to age (<60 or ≥60 years), BMI (<18.5 kg/m2, 18.5-24.9 kg/m2, ≥25 kg/m2), sex (female or male), smoking status (never or other), and drinking status (never or other). Several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, to account for potential competing risk of death, cause-specific Cox proportional hazards models were conducted to evaluate the association between SI and incident VTE. Death before incident VTE was treated as a competing event, and participants were removed from the risk set at the time of death. Second, to reduce the potential for reverse causality, we conducted a 1-year landmark analysis. The landmark date was defined as 1 year after baseline, and the analysis was restricted to participants who were alive, remained under follow-up, and had not developed VTE before landmark date. Follow-up was restarted at the landmark date. Stabilized inverse probability weights were estimated to account for potential selection into the landmark cohort. Third, to address potential bias due to baseline frailty and comorbid conditions, we excluded participants with major pre-existing comorbidities, including atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, stroke, myocardial infraction, cancer, and type 2 diabetes, as well as those using aspirin at baseline. Fourth, we excluded participants with cystatin C based on eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2. The eGFRcys was calculated from the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equations [25]. Fifth, to account for substantial sex differences in SI distribution, sex-specific SI quartiles were generated separately for men and women, and the primary analyses were repeated to assess the robustness of the findings. Sixth, we further investigated the association between SI and risk of VTE-related mortality. Finally, to reduce the potential for outcome misclassification, we excluded participants with self-reported VTE during follow-up.

To examine the combined effects of SI and genetic factors on VTE risk, SI was divided into quartiles (Q1-Q4) and PRS into 3 categories (low, medium, and high), resulting in 12 combined groups; participants in the highest SI quartile and the low genetic risk group served as the reference.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value of <.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics according to SI quartiles are summarized in Table 1. Among the 447,681 participants included in the analysis, 12,008 incident VTE events occurred during a median follow-up period of 13.4 years. The study population had a mean age of 56.35 years (±8.09 years), of whom 205,701 (45.9%) were men. Individuals in higher SI quartiles were characterized by younger age, male predominance, a higher proportion of never smokers and alcohol drinkers, more favorable socioeconomic status, and greater engagement in physical activity compared with the lowest quartile. Participants with higher SI levels also had lower levels of BMI, WBC, LDL, CRP, glycated hemoglobin, total cholesterol, eGFR, and cystatin C. Participants with higher SI levels had higher levels of Ucr and serum creatinine. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Additional exploratory subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted. We examined the relationship between SI and VTE risk within subgroups defined by sex, age, BMI, smoking, and drinking status. After adjustment for potential confounders, the relationship between SI and VTE risk was maintained largely stable across most subgroups. Moreover, no significant interaction effects were observed in these subgroups (P for interaction >.05, Supplementary Table 3). In order to evaluate the robustness of our findings, several sensitivity analyses were performed. In the fully adjusted cause-specific Cox analysis treating death before VTE as a competing event, higher SI was associated with a lower cause-specific hazard of incident VTE. Compared with participants in Q1, the HRs (95% CIs) were 0.88 (0.83-0.92), 0.82 (0.78-0.87), and 0.77 (0.72-0.82) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively (Supplementary Table 4). In the inverse probability-weight 1-year landmark analysis, the association between SI and incident VTE remained consistent with that observed in the primary analysis (Supplementary Table 5). Excluding participants with atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, stroke, myocardial infraction, cancer, type 2 diabetes, and aspirin use at baseline, we found that participants in the highest SI quartile (Q4) had a 20% lower risk of VTE than those in the lowest SI quartile (Q1) (HR = 0.80; 95% CI, 0.74-0.86) (Supplementary Table 6). After excluding participants with eGFRcys <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, individuals in the highest quartile of SI had a significantly lower risk of VTE than those in the lowest quartile (HR = 0.82; 95% CI, 0.77-0.87) (Supplementary Table 7). The inverse association between SI and VTE risk remained largely unchanged in the sensitivity analysis using sex-specific SI quartiles (Supplementary Table 8). Compared with participants in the lowest sex-specific quartile, the adjusted HR (95% CI) for VTE in the highest sex-specific quartile was 0.82 (0.77-0.87). Participants in the highest quartile of SI showed a lower risk of VTE-related mortality after full adjustment for potential confounders (Supplementary Table 9). The association between SI and incident VTE remained essentially unchanged after excluding participants with self-reported VTE (Supplementary Table 10).

Discussion

In this large population-based cohort study of 447,681 participants, we observed a relationship between SI and VTE risk. Our findings indicated that a lower SI was associated with an increased risk of VTE. This association persisted after controlling for potential confounders. Moreover, consistent with previous UK Biobank findings, individuals with greater genetic risk had an elevated risk of VTE. Participants with both low SI levels and high genetic risk exhibited a significantly higher risk of VTE than those with high SI and low genetic risk.

Cystatin C and serum creatinine are widely used laboratory markers for assessing renal function. Serum creatinine is a breakdown product of creatine phosphate and is influenced by skeletal muscle mass [26]. Cystatin C, a small endogenous protein generated by nucleated cells, is reabsorbed and fully metabolized by the proximal tubules; its production shows minimal dependence on muscle or sex [27]. The SI is derived by multiplying the serum creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio by 100 [28]. Because cystatin C and serum creatinine are commonly measured in clinical practice, the SI provides a practical means of estimating skeletal muscle mass without relying on costly imaging techniques [29]. Tetsuka et al. [30] initially demonstrated that the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio was related to muscle function, reporting that patients with amyotrophic lateral sclerosis had significantly lower ratios than healthy controls. In 2016, Kashani et al. [10] confirmed that in intensive care unit patients, a positive association was observed between SI and muscle mass derived from computed tomography–based measurements of the paraspinal muscle surface area at the L4 vertebral level. In a large real-world cohort from the general population, Wilkinson et al. [31] found that the SI demonstrated adequate diagnostic performance in detecting individuals with sarcopenia. As the calculation of SI using cystatin C and serum creatinine is an easy-to-implement and low-cost method, an increasing number of studies have investigated its associations with various health outcomes. SI has been applied to predict clinical outcomes, such as individuals with Alzheimer’s disease and mild cognitive impairment [17], second hip fracture in older adults [32], malnutrition in patients with liver cirrhosis [33], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [34], frailty in the old people [35], metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease [36], and mortality in patients with cancer [37]. Several studies have also explored the association between SI and the prognosis of cardiovascular disease. Among them, Lu et al. [38] reported that lower SI was independently associated with major adverse cardiovascular events in obstructive coronary artery disease. Consistently, Wang et al. [39], analyzing data from 12,914 US adults, reported an inverse relationship between SI levels and the risk of both cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. In contrast to these studies focusing on cardiovascular prognosis, a recent cohort study of older Chinese adults conducted by Chen et al. [12] examined the association of SI with the development of cardiovascular disease, finding that lower SI levels were markedly associated with a higher risk of incident cardiovascular disease and stroke. Collectively, these studies indicate that SI, as a serologic marker of sarcopenia, has potential clinical relevance for prediction and management for cardiovascular disease. The association between SI and VTE risk has not been investigated in prior studies. Therefore, we explored the relationship between SI index and VTE based on the large prospective UK Biobank cohort.

Our findings indicated that lower SI levels were significantly associated with a higher risk of VTE. In comparison with individuals with higher SI levels, those with lower SI levels tended to be older and more often female. Because advancing age is associated with a progressive reduction in muscle mass, and women characteristically possess less muscle tissue compared with men [40], this may partly explain the observed differences. SI serves as a useful surrogate marker for skeletal muscle mass and function, with lower SI levels reflecting greater muscle loss and reduced muscle function. Therefore, lower SI levels are commonly observed in older adults and female individuals. After adjustment for potential confounding factors, an inverse association between SI levels and VTE risk was observed. This finding is broadly consistent with previous research demonstrating that decreased muscle mass or function is associated with a higher risk of VTE. Consistently, Leknessund et al. [41] reported that weak hand grip strength, a widely used indicator of overall muscular strength, was associated with a 27% increased risk of VTE. As for genetic risk, consistent with a previous study, our findings indicate that the VTE PRS effectively identifies individuals at both high and low genetic risk [14]. Individuals at high genetic risk exhibited the greatest risk of VTE (HR = 2.56; 95% CI, 2.42-2.72). In addition, we observed that those with both lower SI levels and high genetic susceptibility exhibited the greatest VTE risk (HR = 3.60; 95% CI, 3.14-4.12).

Although the precise biological mechanisms linking SI to VTE risk remain unclear, several potential mechanisms may be involved. First, epidemiologic findings demonstrating an association between sarcopenia and VTE further support the potential link between SI and VTE risk. In a study evaluating the 30-day incidence of VTE after radical cystectomy in bladder cancer patients with and without sarcopenia, the overall incidence was 4.76%, whereas patients with sarcopenia exhibited a markedly higher rate of approximately 10% [42]. Meanwhile, Alwani et al. [43] reported that patients with sarcopenia undergoing autologous head and neck free-flap reconstruction exhibited a significantly higher incidence of VTE. As SI serves as a serologic biomarker of sarcopenia, these findings suggest that lower SI levels may be associated with VTE risk. Secondly, the Virchow’s triad describes the 3 fundamental pathophysiological factors contributing to the development of VTE, including blood stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury [44]. Reduced lower leg muscle pumping action is likely to cause DVT formation due to blood stasis [45]. This could partly explain the thrombus formation observed in patients with sarcopenia, as a low SI reflects decreased muscle mass and strength. Finally, sarcopenia is associated with a chronic inflammatory state [46], which is an established risk factor for VTE.

By addressing a previously unexplored association between SI and VTE risk, our study offers multiple key advantages. The large sample size and comprehensive dataset allowed for adequate control of potential confounding factors and conduct thorough sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Moreover, we investigated the joint association of SI and genetic susceptibility with VTE risk, providing valuable insight into the prevention of VTE among individuals with high genetic risk.

Our research has several limitations that need to be noted. First, the SI, derived from the ratio of serum creatinine to cystatin C, was assessed only at baseline in the UK Biobank. Given the long follow-up period, changes in serum creatinine, cystatin C, and overall health status during the follow-up could not be captured, which precluded the capture of temporal variations and limited the ability to assess VTE risk dynamically. Future studies incorporating repeated SI measurements are needed to assess whether dynamic SI trajectories improve risk stratification. Second, as the study population consisted primarily of European ancestry to minimize population structure bias and spurious associations resulting from differences in genetic architecture across ethnicities, the applicability of our results to other ethnic populations may be limited. Future research in more diverse populations is necessary to substantiate these observations. Third, VTE outcome was ascertained using the ICD codes, which may be subject to misclassification. Although we excluded participants with self-reported VTE during the follow-up, the potential for outcome misclassification cannot be completely ruled out. Another limitation is that VTE events were identified using ICD codes, precluding reliable differentiation between provoked and unprovoked VTE. Future studies with more detailed clinical information are needed to investigate whether the association observed in this study differs according to provoked and unprovoked VTE. Finally, as with all observational studies, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded, and causal inferences cannot be established. Although we made extensive efforts to adjust for multiple confounding factors and conducted the E-value analysis, the possibility of residual bias cannot be entirely excluded given the observational design.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large cohort study showed that a decreased SI was associated with a higher risk of VTE. Consistent with previous UK Biobank findings, genetic susceptibility was also associated with VTE risk. Moreover, participants with both low SI and high genetic susceptibility exhibited the greatest risk of VTE. These results suggest that SI may serve as a simple and accessible marker for identifying individuals at elevated risk of VTE.
