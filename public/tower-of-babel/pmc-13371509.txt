Hepatitis C care cascade using direct-acting antivirals in rural new York State from 2013 to 2023: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Zoi Papalamprakopoulou, Melissa B Scribani, Nicole L Krupa, Paul L Jenkins, Jennifer M Victory, Arpan Dharia, Wendy M Brunner, Daniel A Freilich, Andrew H Talal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) infection rates are higher in rural areas than in urban areas, yet rural HCV care remains understudied. We evaluated the HCV care cascade using direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) in rural New York State.MethodsElectronic health record data from 2013 to 2023 were retrospectively collected from active Bassett Health Network primary care patients aged 18–79 years, consistent with the most recent US screening recommendations. We constructed 11 annual testing cohorts based upon the date of HCV serological testing. Annual care cascades were summarized using frequencies and percentages. We used chi-square to compare outcomes pre- and post-implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing and universal screening and to assess differences by opioid use disorder status.ResultsThe average annualized patient population was 72,615, White (96.45%) and male (44.58%). HCV seropositivity was highest (4.17%) among those aged 20–39 years. Annual HCV RNA positivity in HCV seropositive patients ranged from 18.27 to 76.60%. DAA initiation ranged from 26.32 to 56.52% in HCV RNA-positive patients. HCV antibody testing increased significantly after implementation of universal screening (p < 0.0001). Patients with opioid use disorder had higher RNA positivity (51.32% vs. 32.07%, p < 0.0001) and lower DAA initiation (35.65% vs. 57.71%, p < 0.0001) than those without opioid use disorder.ConclusionsWhile HCV diagnosis improved after universal screening implementation, DAA treatment remains suboptimal in rural New York State, especially among people with OUD. Background

In the United States (US), an estimated 67,400 acute hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections occurred in 2022, which had doubled from 2015, along with approximately 12,700 HCV-related deaths [1]. In 2013, direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) changed the HCV treatment paradigm: all oral medications with minimal side effects and excellent efficacy when taken for 2–3 months. As a result, the US Department of Health and Human Services and the World Health Organization established national goals to eliminate HCV, aiming for at least 80% of HCV-infected individuals to be cured by 2030 [2, 3]. However, nearly one-third of HCV-infected individuals remain unaware of their diagnosis [4], and only 34% of DAA-eligible individuals received treatment from 2013 to 2022 [5]. Improving HCV treatment initiation remains a challenge to achieving HCV elimination.

People with opioid use disorder (OUD) have the highest rate of HCV infection and lowest rate of HCV treatment, especially in rural areas [1, 6]. A cross-sectional study by Estadt et al. conducted in 10 rural US communities among 2,705 people with OUD found that only 12% of participants who reported an HCV diagnosis had ever taken HCV medication [6]. While urban areas report higher absolute numbers of HCV infections, the rates are higher in rural settings (11.8% vs. 1.4% for acute and 58.5 vs. 37.1 per 100,000 for chronic HCV cases) [1]. Additionally, recent data show a rapid rise in deaths due to hepatocellular carcinoma in rural areas compared to urban areas [7]. Improvements in diagnosis and treatment access are key to expanding treatment provision. In 2015, the Bassett Healthcare Network laboratory implemented HCV RNA reflex testing on all HCV seropositive samples [8]. In 2020, the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) and the CDC introduced recommendations for universal, once in a lifetime, HCV screening for all adults [9, 10]. However, it remains understudied how effective these national policies have been in rural healthcare settings. Given the continuing HCV epidemic in rural areas, research on HCV care in rural areas is needed.

Although rural areas report an overall lower rate of illicit drug use compared to urban areas, its impact is more severe in rural areas. People with OUD in rural areas face greater risk of overdose deaths than those in urban areas [11]. They also experience greater challenges accessing prevention and harm-reduction resources. Interviews with people with OUD residing in rural locales revealed inconsistent access to sterile syringes, widespread syringe sharing, and reliance on informal HCV education, leading to misconceptions about HCV status, transmission, and treatment [12]. Additionally, a recent cross-sectional study by Ballard et al., conducted among 3,000 people with OUD from rural areas in 10 states, found that 53.7% were experiencing homelessness, highlighting the severity of social instability in this population [13]. Barriers to HCV care in rural settings include a lack of healthcare infrastructure, limited access to specialized HCV care, geographic barriers, transportation challenges, and financial hardship [14]. People with OUD often face additional obstacles, such as stigma, competing medical and social priorities, and high-risk behaviors [15–17]. These compounded challenges explain why rural areas experience an ongoing HCV-OUD syndemic.

Given the paucity of data on current HCV management in rural areas, we sought to evaluate the HCV care cascade during the first decade of DAA use (2013–2023) among primary care patients in rural New York State. Given the high prevalence of HCV among people with OUD, we also aimed to evaluate the HCV care cascade within this subpopulation. The HCV care cascade describes the steps from HCV diagnosis to cure. It provides a framework that we used to retrospectively evaluate the outcomes of HCV management at the health system level since the introduction of HCV DAA medications. Additionally, we aimed to assess the effectiveness of national HCV screening and diagnostic recommendations in a rural setting. We hypothesized that the adherence to the HCV care cascade would improve over time, particularly after the CDC’s recommendation, and that outcomes would be worse in OUD vs. non-OUD patients. Our findings aim to provide insight into HCV care delivery in rural populations, identify existing care gaps, and potential strategies to improve HCV care in rural areas.

Methods

Study objectives

HCV care cascade

Our primary objective was to describe the HCV care cascade across 11 annual testing periods (2013–2023), among active primary care patients within the Bassett Healthcare Network. The HCV care cascade is useful for delineating steps in the diagnosis and treatment of HCV. The HCV care cascade steps included: (1) HCV serological assessment, (2) HCV seropositivity, (3) HCV RNA testing, (4) HCV RNA positivity, (5) DAA treatment initiation, (6) DAA treatment completion, and (7) confirmed durable viral clearance at least 6 weeks post-treatment completion.

Time to treatment initiation

As a secondary objective, we measured the time from a positive HCV RNA result to DAA initiation to assess the interval from diagnosis to treatment. We prespecified study outcomes prior to data acquisition/analysis.

Pre/post event frequency analyses

We also compared the numbers of individuals before and after implementation of (1) HCV RNA reflex testing and (2) universal HCV RNA screening.

Justification for cutoffs

HCV RNA reflex testing: To simplify the HCV diagnostic process, reflex HCV RNA testing was implemented so that a formerly two-step procedure is accomplished in a single step [8]. Specifically, HCV RNA testing is reflexively performed on a seropositive sample instead of requiring collection of an additional sample. We evaluated the proportion of patients receiving HCV RNA testing before/after the implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing (March 10, 2015). Expanded Insurance coverage in New York State for HCV: In 2016, the New York State Attorney General signed an agreement with major insurers in the state to expand HCV treatment access [18]. Universal HCV screening: In 2020, the US Preventive Services Task Force and the CDC substituted the previously proposed risk-based screening with universal screening [10]. Therefore, we evaluated the proportion of individuals receiving HCV serological testing before/after implementation of the universal HCV screening recommendation (2020). Similarly, in alignment with the age distribution for the most recent HCV screening guidelines from the US Preventive Services Task Force and CDC, we chose to stipulate eligibility criteria only for individuals aged 18 to 79 years.

Study setting

Bassett Healthcare Network is an integrated health system in central New York State, comprising an academic medical center, four hospitals, and over two dozen primary care clinics. It also provides OUD treatment and serves an 8-county region that is more than 90% rural by US Census definitions [19]. Within the Bassett Network, HCV RNA testing is performed at Bassett Medical Center with sample collected at other sites being sent to the main laboratory. Further details on study setting are provided in Supplementary Material.

Study design and data collection

We collected retrospective, cross-sectional data through the electronic health record (EHR; Epic® Systems, Verona, Wisconsin, USA).

Inclusion criteria

Eligible patients were active primary care patients (aged 18–79 years) of the Bassett Healthcare Network [9, 10], defined by at least one primary care office or telehealth visit in the prior 12 months.

Exclusion criteria

We excluded individuals seen only in the emergency department or who were hospitalized. Further details on study patients are provided in Supplementary Material.

Dataset construction

The electronic health record was used to construct annual datasets of eligible patient observations. We organized the datasets into 12-month cross-sections (January 1-December 31) based on the date of HCV serologic testing.

Participant enumeration

We analyzed 11 testing periods (2013–2023), including one observation per patient per period. For subjects with multiple serologic tests in the same period, the earliest seropositive result was used for that period. If all serologic tests in the same period were negative, the first test was used.

Excluded individuals due to missing value

There were 7 patients for whom it was unknown whether treatment was initiated, completed, or if viral clearance was achieved; these patients were excluded from the analysis. Further details on data collection and handling of missing data are provided in Supplementary Material.

Data security and approvals

All data were de-identified and stored in a secure database. The study complies with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Mary Imogene Bassett Hospital Institutional Review Board, which granted a full waiver of informed consent under 45 CFR 46.116(d). The study was designed in accordance with STROBE guidelines.

Statistical analysis

HCV care cascade

We summarized the distribution of patients reaching each step of the HCV care cascade by initial HCV antibody testing year using frequencies and percentages. Percentages represent the total number of patients reaching each step of the cascade divided by the total number of patients at each prior step of the cascade. Demographic characteristics were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

We conducted subgroup analyses comparing people with OUD and those without OUD across the care cascade using chi-square tests (Rao-Scott) with adjustment for clustering due to multiple observations per patient. The Rao-Scott test adjusts the chi-square statistic and error estimates to account for the dependency of observations, in this case, where sets of observations from the same individuals may be correlated [20].

HCV care cascade distributions were analyzed through graphical plots; plots were created using GraphPad Prism version 10. Annualized average numbers of patients were used to demonstrate the HCV care cascade from HCV seropositivity through durable viral clearance steps pooled over the study period (see Fig. 1). All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (Cary, NC). Probabilities of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Time to event analyses

Among patients with a positive HCV RNA test, the median time in days from HCV RNA positivity to DAA treatment initiation each year was compared over the study period using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Kaplan-Meier analysis was used to compare time to DAA treatment initiation before (2013–2016) and after (2017–2023) New York State’s widespread implementation of insurance coverage for DAAs.

Pre/post event frequency analyses

We assessed changes in the percentages of HCV RNA testing, DAA treatment and durable viral clearance pre- and post-implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing (2015) using the chi-square test (Rao-Scott) [20]. We also analyzed changes in the percentages of HCV serological testing pre- and post-implementation of the CDC’s universal screening recommendation (2020) via the Rao-Scott chi-square test. Universal screening was non-risk based and medical decision prompts were not implemented.

HCV care cascade: overall HCV sero- and RNA-positivity

The annualized average HCV care cascade among primary care patients is illustrated by the numbers of patient records (Fig. 1). Males had a slightly lower percentage of HCV serologic testing compared to females (4.95% vs. 5.17%) but had higher HCV seropositivity (3.13% vs. 2.22%) and HCV RNA positivity (42.58% vs. 38.85%). Individuals aged 20–39 years had the highest HCV seropositivity (4.17%), while HCV RNA testing in this age group (94.42%) was comparable to those aged 40–59 years (96.69%). A summary of demographic characteristics for unique patients across the study period 2013–2023 (one record per patient selected at random) is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

HCV care cascade (antibody and RNA results) for the total primary care population

Among the total primary care population, we found increasing percentages of HCV serologic testing (2.23% in 2013 to 8.30% in 2021) with HCV seropositivity (1.84% to 4.72%) consistently approximate with or above the national average among patient records [21]. Among HCV seropositive individuals, 95.83% received an HCV RNA test of whom 40.22% (range from 76.60% in 2016 to 18.27% in 2023) were HCV RNA positive (Fig. 2) when evaluating patient records.

HCV care cascade (antibody and RNA results) for the OUD patient population

HCV seropositivity among people with OUD varied from 8.33% (in 2014) to 21.72% (in 2018) (Fig. 3) among patient records. The highest confirmed HCV RNA percentage was recorded in 2016 at 76.67%, mirroring trends in the total population (Fig. 3). Since then, a downward trend has been observed, reaching the lowest HCV RNA positivity percentage of 30.61% in 2023.

HCV care cascade (HCV treatment initiation, completion, and cure)

A total of 45.94% (17/37) initiated DAAs, with 33.33% (7/21) of people with OUD initiating DAAs and 56.25% (9/16) of those without OUD initiating DAAs. Overall, after 2015, the percentage of HCV RNA positive cases who initiated DAAs followed an upward trend, rising from 36.21% in 2018 to 56.52% in 2020 (Fig. 4). However, in 2023, it declined to 26.32%. After 2018, the DAA treatment initiation percentages among people with OUD initially followed an upward trajectory, rising from 25.71% in 2018 and peaking at 51.85% in 2020. However, since 2020, DAA treatment initiation percentages have steadily declined, reaching a low of 13.33% in 2023 (Fig. 5).

DAA treatment completion was above 95% for all years. Viral clearance ranged from 40.00% (2023) to 95.00% (2015). However, DAA initiation was significantly lower among people with OUD (35.65% vs. 57.71%) (p < 0.0001). However, HCV RNA testing, treatment completion, and achieving durable viral clearance did not differ between people with and without OUD.

Time to initiate DAA treatment

The median elapsed time between occurrence of a positive RNA test result and DAA treatment initiation decreased significantly from a maximum median 824.5 days (interquartile range: 273–1167) in 2013 to a minimum of 102 (interquartile range: 36–167) days in 2022 (Supplementary Fig. 1) (p < 0.0001 by the Kruskal-Wallis test).

Changes pre- and post-implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing in 2015

HCV RNA testing increased, albeit not significantly, before/after implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing protocols (March 10, 2015) among HCV seropositive patients (p = 0.3552) (Table 2A). A corresponding statistically significant decrease in the percentage of positive HCV RNA tests was seen post-reflex testing implementation compared to pre-implementation (p = 0.0210). No significant changes in treatment completion or viral clearance occurred post-implementation of HCV RNA reflex testing.

HCV RNA testing among HCV seropositive individuals ranged from 86.36% (2017) to 99.19% (Fig. 2). From 2013 to 2016, the percentage of HCV RNA testing remained above 90%, and after 2019, it approached 100% (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Over the study period (2013–2023), HCV diagnosis improved, with declines in both HCV seropositivity and HCV RNA positivity. The time from diagnosis to treatment also decreased, likely reflecting enhanced HCV diagnostic policies and greater DAA availability. However, DAA treatment remained low (26.32–56.52%), underscoring the need to expand DAA treatment initiation. People with OUD had significantly lower DAA treatment initiation than those without OUD, despite higher HCV RNA positivity. HCV treatment initiation remains the largest obstacle to achieving an HCV cure. This observation is substantiated by a recently published randomized controlled trial [22]. This observation is further substantiated in recently published follow-on data documenting that the same predictors correctly identified HCV treatment initiation in 75% and an HCV cure in 94% of participants, respectively [23]. This finding suggests persistent barriers to HCV care for people with OUD, such as stigma and substance use.

The decrease in HCV RNA positivity over time is likely due to the successful therapeutic clearance of the virus and the result of ongoing treatment effectively clearing the virus at the health network level. A 20-year observational study found an increase in spontaneous clearance from 32.6% in 1998 to 68.7% in 2017 and that females and younger aged individuals had a higher clearance prevalence [24]. Due to the opioid epidemic, incident HCV infections occur most frequently in 20 to 39 year olds [1]. We also assessed two major policy changes for their effect on the HCV care cascade. The first was the introduction of HCV RNA reflex testing in 2015, triggering automatic HCV RNA testing following a seropositive result. The HCV RNA positivity decrease may result from inclusion of individuals with undocumented spontaneous resolution or undocumented prior treatment outside the Bassett Healthcare Network. Additionally, broader DAA availability, greater HCV awareness, education on safe injection practices, and enhanced harm reduction efforts among high-risk populations may have further contributed to reductions in HCV RNA positivity [25, 26]. Harm reduction approaches, such as syringe exchange, can prevent viral transmission. Correspondingly, new HCV cases in Delaware County, within the Bassett catchment area, rose from 30 in 2013 to 48 in 2017, then declined to 33 in 2019, suggesting an initial rise in injection drug use followed by the impact of harm reduction interventions [27].

After HCV RNA reflex testing was introduced within the healthcre system in 2015, we observed a four-year delay in a detectable effect, with HCV RNA testing percentages at approximately 85% in 2017–2018 after an HCV seropositive sample. From 2019 onward, HCV RNA testing percentages approached 100% following an HCV seropositive sample. From 2017 to 2019, chronic HCV rates in Chenango County, also within the Bassett Network, declined from 84.4 to 79.1 per 100,000 but remained higher than the New York State average [28]. Delays in implementing early HCV RNA reflex testing may have contributed to diagnostic gaps, underscoring the need for timely institutional policy implementation. Following the introduction of universal HCV screening in 2020, HCV serological testing nearly doubled, from 3.89% (2013–2019) to 7.25% (2021–2023). Despite improvements in HCV screening and diagnosis, we did not observe corresponding increases in DAA treatment or durable viral clearance. Small sample sizes may have limited our ability to detect such changes. Further research is needed to assess the full real-life impact of policy implementation across the HCV care cascade.

Among people with OUD, DAA treatment initiation remains significantly lower despite higher HCV prevalence. The decline in DAA treatment initiation after 2020 and especially after 2023, despite enhanced HCV screening and diagnostic efforts, highlights a growing disconnect between diagnosis and treatment. These decreases may be due to random fluctuation, although other systemic factors may have contributed at the level of the health system. Linkage-to-care continues to be a major barrier for high-risk populations seeking HCV treatment. Research has also identified urban-rural disparities in DAA utilization, emphasizing the need to improve linkage-to-care for rural patients [29]. Telemedicine, widely adopted during the COVID-19 pandemic, has proven effective in rural settings [22, 30, 31]. In rural Oregon, Seaman et al. reported higher DAA treatment uptake and viral clearance among people with OUD through peer-assisted telemedicine compared to those referred to local providers [32]. Similarly, Hoffman et al. reported high patient satisfaction with peer-assisted telemedicine HCV treatment in rural areas [33].

Co-locating HCV-OUD treatment has proven highly effective for people with OUD. Previous research demonstrated that integrating facilitated telemedicine for HCV treatment within opioid treatment programs resulted in 90% HCV cure [22]. Strengthening provider education on HCV care for people with OUD in rural areas, along with enhancing patient education and harm reduction practices, is critical. Policy initiatives to incentivize rapid DAA treatment initiation, particularly in high-risk settings such as methadone clinics and syringe service programs, could further enhance treatment uptake for people with OUD. Additionally, removing the remaining Medicaid restrictions on DAA access is essential for expanding HCV treatment in rural populations [34]. As part of the Community Health Needs Assessments for Herkimer, Schoharie, and Otsego counties (part of Bassett's catchment area) and in alignment with the New York State Prevention Agenda, local efforts aim to expand HCV treatment and reduce new infections, particularly among people with OUD [35–37].

This study has limitations, primarily related to its retrospective observational design, which may have introduced selection bias, limited data availability, and potential data misclassification [38]. We urge caution in the interpretation of the results based upon strata with small number of individuals, especially evaluating changes in DAA treatment and/or viral clearance. Another objective, however, is to highlight the opportunity for interventions to increase patient engagement at specific HCV care cascade steps and among population subsets. Additionally, spontaneous resolution was not documented because we did not conduct a longitudinal profile analysis. Also, we did not adjust for potential confounders, nor did we assess effect modifiers other than OUD vs. non-OUD subgroups. The largely homogeneous patient population, consisting predominantly of White individuals, may not capture the additional barriers faced by racial and ethnic minorities in accessing HCV care in rural areas [39]. Lastly, in some cases, it was not possible to determine whether patients received DAA treatment, and we excluded these individuals from the analysis. However, the minimal (n = 7) extent of missing data is not expected to have significantly affected the overall findings.

Conclusions

We observed significant improvements in HCV testing during the first decade of DAA utilization in rural New York State (2013–2023). Implementation of universal screening policies in 2020 significantly increased testing. DAA treatment initiation remains low, particularly among people with OUD. Expanding DAA utilization among high-risk rural populations is needed. Key efforts include incentivizing rapid DAA treatment initiation, enhancing provider and patient HCV education, strengthening linkage-to-care pathways, leveraging telehealth, and expanding harm reduction practices. HCV elimination procedures should prioritize immediate DAA initiation once HCV-infected individuals are identified. These initiatives should be supported by strong institutional commitment to achieve equitable access to curative HCV treatment.
