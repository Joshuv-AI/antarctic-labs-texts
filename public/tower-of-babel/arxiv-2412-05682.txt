On the Rank of a bicomplex matrix
amita, Mamta Amol Wagh, Suman Kumar, Akhil Prakash

Abstract
. The paper explores the concept of the rank of a bicomplex matrix, delving into four distinct types of ranks and investigating conditions under which these ranks are equivalent. It also defines and analyzes the concept of idempotent row space and idempotent column space of a bicomplex matrix. Some examples and counter examples have been presented to substantiate the study.

In this section, we provide a summary of several properties associated with bicomplex numbers.

Bicomplex numbers:
A bicomplex number can be represented as

\displaystyle{u}_{1}+{i}_{1}{u}_{2}+{i}_{2}{u}_{3}+{i}_{1}{i}_{2}{u}_{4}
, where
\displaystyle{u_{k}}
are the real numbers for
\displaystyle k=1
to
\displaystyle 4
and
\displaystyle i_{1}
,
\displaystyle i_{2}
are unit vectors satisfying the properties
\displaystyle i_{1}i_{2}=i_{2}i_{1},i_{1}^{2}=i_{2}^{2}=-1
. For the sake of convenience, we employed certain symbols
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{0},\mathbb{C}_{1}
, and
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, for the set of real numbers, the set of complex numbers, and the set of bicomplex numbers respectively. The set of bicomplex numbers can be represented in three different ways

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{{u}_{1}+{i}_{1}{u}_{2}+{i}_{2}{u}_{3}+{i}_{1}{i}_{2}{u}_{4}\;:\;{u}_{1},{u}_{2},{u}_{3},{u}_{4}\in\mathbb{C}_{0}\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{{z}_{1}+{i}_{2}{z}_{2}\;:\;{z}_{1},{z}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{1_{\xi}e_{1}+2_{\xi}e_{2}\;:\;{1_{\xi}=({z}_{1}-i_{1}{z}_{2}),2_{\xi}=({z}_{1}+i_{1}{z}_{2})\in\mathbb{C}_{1}}\}.

The introduction of two numbers,
\displaystyle e_{1}=\frac{(1+i_{1}i_{2})}{2}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}=\frac{(1-i_{1}i_{2})}{2}
, significantly simplifies the system of bicomplex numbers
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
. These numbers,
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
serve as zero divisors in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
. Furthermore, the numbers
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
form a basis for
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, which is called the idempotent basis. Thus, every bicomplex number
\displaystyle\xi
can be written in a unique way as:

\displaystyle\xi=1_{\xi}e_{1}+2_{\xi}e_{2}
. This representation is called the idempotent representation of a bicomplex number
\displaystyle\xi
.
The idempotent representation gives algebraic structure to the bicomplex space, for instance the product of two bicomplex numbers can be seen component-wise in the above idempotent representation, i.e. if
\displaystyle\xi=1_{\xi}e_{1}+2_{\xi}e_{2}
and
\displaystyle\eta=1_{\eta}e_{1}+2_{\eta}e_{2}
in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\xi\cdot\eta=(1_{\xi}\cdot 1_{\eta})e_{1}+(2_{\xi}\cdot 2_{\eta})e_{2}.

The set of singular elements in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
is denoted by
\displaystyle O_{2}
and defined as the collection of all non-invertible elements in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
.

Definition 1.1
.

(
Principal ideals

[
7
]
)
The principal ideals in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
determined by
\displaystyle e_{1}
and
\displaystyle e_{2}
, denoted by
\displaystyle I_{1}
and
\displaystyle I_{2}
, respectively are defined as:

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{1}=:\{\xi e_{1}:\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}\}=\{1_{\xi}e_{1}:1_{\xi}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\displaystyle I_{2}=:\{\xi e_{2}:\xi\in\mathbb{C}_{2}\}=\{2_{\xi}e_{2}:2_{\xi}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}\}.

Theorem 1.2
.

[
7
]
A bicomplex number
\displaystyle\xi=(Z_{1}+i_{2}Z_{2})
is singular if and only if
\displaystyle\xi\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

[
7
]
A bicomplex number
\displaystyle\xi=(Z_{1}+i_{2}Z_{2})
is non-singular if and only if
\displaystyle\xi\notin I_{1}\cup I_{2}
.

Definition 1.4
.

(
Cartesian product

[
2
]
) The
\displaystyle n
-times Cartesian product of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
is denoted by
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
and defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}=\{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}):\xi_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{2};i=1,2,\ldots,n\}.

Moreover, analogous to the idempotent representation of an element in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, every element of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
can be expressed uniquely as

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=(1_{\xi_{1}},1_{\xi_{2}},\ldots,1_{\xi_{n}})e_{1}+(2_{\xi_{1}},2_{\xi_{2}},\ldots,2_{\xi_{n}})e_{2},

where
\displaystyle(1_{\xi_{1}},1_{\xi_{2}},\ldots,1_{\xi_{n}}),(2_{\xi_{1}},2_{\xi_{2}},\ldots,2_{\xi_{n}})
are
\displaystyle n
-tuples of complex numbers.

Remark 1.5
.

[
2
]

If
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}),(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n})\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
and
\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathbb{C}_{2}
, then

•

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\cdot(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n})=(\xi_{1}\eta_{1},\xi_{2}\eta_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n}\eta_{n}).

•

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\ldots,\eta_{n})\iff 1\xi_{i}=1\eta_{i}\ and\ 2\xi_{i}=2\eta_{i}\ \forall\ i=1,2,\ldots n
.

•

\displaystyle\kappa(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})\kappa=(\kappa\xi_{1},\kappa\xi_{2},\ldots,\kappa\xi_{n})
.

Consequently, the product

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{n})e_{i}=(\xi_{1}e_{i},\xi_{2}e_{i},\ldots,\xi_{n}e_{i})=(i_{\xi_{1}}e_{i},i_{\xi_{2}}e_{i},\ldots,i_{\xi_{n}}e_{i});i=1,2.

This conclusion arises from the fact that
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
is not only a
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
module but also exhibits the structure of a
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}
-algebra.

Definition 1.6
.

(Bicomplex matrix)

The set
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
of bicomplex matrices of order
\displaystyle{n\times m}
is defined as

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}=:\{[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}:\xi_{ij}\in\mathbb{C}_{2};1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m\}.

The set
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times n}
with respect to ordinary multiplication, addition and scalar multiplication forms an algebra over
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}
. Similar to the concept of idempotent representation for elements in
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}
, every bicomplex matrix can be uniquely expressed as

[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}=[1_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}e_{1}+[2_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}e_{2};1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m,

where
\displaystyle[1_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}
and
\displaystyle[2_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}
are complex matrices of order
\displaystyle n\times m
, i.e. if

\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
then
\displaystyle A=1_{A}e_{1}+2_{A}e_{2}
, where
\displaystyle 1_{A}=[1_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n\times m}
,
\displaystyle 2_{A}=[2_{\xi_{ij}}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n\times m}
and
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n\times m}
denotes the set of all complex matrices of order
\displaystyle{n\times m}
.

Definition 1.7
.

(Bicomplex singular and non-singular matrix)
If
\displaystyle A\in C_{2}^{n\times n}
, then
\displaystyle A
is said to be non-singular if determinant of
\displaystyle A
is non-singular element, i.e.
\displaystyle\det(A)\notin O_{2}
and if determinant of
\displaystyle A
is singular element, i.e.
\displaystyle\det(A)\in O_{2}
, then it is called singular.

Theorem 1.8
.

[
7
]

If
\displaystyle A
is a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times n
, then

\displaystyle\det(A)=\det(1_{A})e_{1}+\det(2_{A})e_{2}
.

Corollary 1.9
.

[
7
]

Let
\displaystyle A
be a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times n
, then
\displaystyle A
is non-singular if and only if
\displaystyle\det(1_{A})\neq 0
and
\displaystyle\det(2_{A})\neq 0
.

Corollary 1.10
.

[
7
]

Let
\displaystyle A
be a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times n
, then
\displaystyle A
is singular if and only if either
\displaystyle\det(1_{A})=0
or
\displaystyle\det(2_{A})=0
.

2.
rank of Bicomplex matrix

This section deals with the rank, row rank, and column rank of a bicomplex matrix. Here we explore some results on these type of ranks. We define the idempotent row space and idempotent column space of a bicomplex matrix, also investigate some results.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle V
be a subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
, then we define the set
\displaystyle Ve_{i}
as follows

\displaystyle\displaystyle Ve_{i}=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})e_{i};(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in V\},\mbox{for}\ i\in\{1,2\}.

Furthermore, the set
\displaystyle Ve_{i}
is a subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
.

Definition 2.2
.

(
\displaystyle e_{1}
-matrix
)
If
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
and
\displaystyle\xi_{ij}={1_{\xi}}_{ij}e_{1}
for all
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
, then
\displaystyle A
is called
\displaystyle e_{1}
-matrix.

Definition 2.3
.

(
\displaystyle e_{2}
-matrix
)
If
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
and
\displaystyle\xi_{ij}={2_{\xi}}_{ij}e_{2}
for all
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
, then
\displaystyle A
is called
\displaystyle e_{2}
-matrix.

Definition 2.4
.

(
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
-matrix
)
If
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
and
\displaystyle\xi_{ij}\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}
for all
\displaystyle i
and
\displaystyle j
, then
\displaystyle A
is called
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
- matrix.

Remark 2.5
.

Every
\displaystyle e_{1}
-matrix is an
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
-matrix, also every
\displaystyle e_{2}
-matrix is an
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
-matrix, but converse need not be true. For example

A=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&e_{2}\\
e_{2}&e_{1}\end{bmatrix}

is
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
-matrix, but neither
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle e_{1}
-matrix nor
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle e_{2}
-matrix.

Theorem 2.6
.

If
\displaystyle B
is a sub-matrix of a bicomplex matrix
\displaystyle A
, then
\displaystyle 1_{B}
and
\displaystyle 2_{B}
are sub-matrices of
\displaystyle 1_{A}
and
\displaystyle 2_{A}
respectively.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle B
be a sub-matrix of a bicomplex matrix
\displaystyle A
. Therefore,
\displaystyle B
is obtained from
\displaystyle A
by omitting some rows and columns of
\displaystyle A
. Thus, we have sub-matrices
\displaystyle 1_{B}
and
\displaystyle 2_{B}
, which are obtained from matrix
\displaystyle B
by omitting some rows and some columns of
\displaystyle 1_{A}
and
\displaystyle 2_{A}
respectively. Hence,
\displaystyle 1_{B}
and
\displaystyle 2_{B}
are sub-matrices of
\displaystyle 1_{A}
and
\displaystyle 2_{A}
respectively.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The converse of Theorem
2.6
is not true in general. Consider a bicomplex matrix

\displaystyle\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}1&e_{1}&0\\
e_{1}&e_{2}&e_{1}\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&0&1\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}e_{1}+\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}e_{2}.

We see that

\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\quad and\quad\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}

are sub-matrices of
\displaystyle 1_{A}
and
\displaystyle 2_{A}
, respectively. But

\displaystyle\displaystyle B=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}e_{1}+\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}e_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}1&e_{1}\\
0&e_{2}\end{bmatrix}

is not a sub-matrix of
\displaystyle A
.

Definition 2.8
.

(
Rank of a bicomplex matrix
)
A number
\displaystyle r
is said to be the rank of a matrix
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
, if it possesses the following properties:

•

There exists a non-singular sub-matrix
\displaystyle B_{r}
of
\displaystyle A
and a series of non-singular sub-matrices of the matrix
\displaystyle B_{r}
such that

B_{1}\preccurlyeq B_{2}\preccurlyeq\ldots\preccurlyeq B_{r-1}\preccurlyeq B_{r},

(1)

where
\displaystyle B_{i}\preccurlyeq B_{i+1}
represents that
\displaystyle B_{i}
is a sub-matrix of
\displaystyle B_{i+1}
, and
\displaystyle i
represents the order of the matrix
\displaystyle B_{i}
.

•

If
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
is any sub-matrix of
\displaystyle A
with order
\displaystyle t;t>r
, then no series of the type (
1
) exists for the matrix
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
.

The rank of a matrix A is denoted by
\displaystyle\rho(A)
. Furthermore,
If no such
\displaystyle r
exists, then we define
\displaystyle\rho(A)=0
.

Remark 2.9
.

The definition
2.8
yields that

(1)

If
\displaystyle r
is the rank of a bicomplex matrix
\displaystyle A
, then there exists a non-singular matrix
\displaystyle B
of order
\displaystyle r
such that
\displaystyle B\preccurlyeq A
and
\displaystyle\rho(B)=r
, and any matrix
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
of order
\displaystyle t;t>r
such that
\displaystyle B^{\prime}\preccurlyeq A
, then
\displaystyle\rho(B^{\prime})\leq r
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
, then
\displaystyle\rho(A)\leq
min
\displaystyle(m,n)
.

(3)

If
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times n}
and
\displaystyle\rho(A)=n
, then
\displaystyle\det(A)\notin O_{2}
. But converse need not be true. For example, if

\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}e_{1}&e_{2}\\
e_{2}&e_{1}\end{bmatrix}
,

\displaystyle\rho(A)=0

\displaystyle\det(A)\notin O_{2}

(4)

If
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times m
. Then,
\displaystyle\rho(A)=0
if and only if
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
- matrix.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times m}
, and
\displaystyle\rho(A)=0
.
Let us assume that there exists an entry
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}
of
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}\notin O_{2}
for some
\displaystyle p\in\{1,2,...,n\}
and
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,...,m\}
.
Therefore the matrix
\displaystyle B=[\xi_{ps}]_{1\times 1}
is a non-singular sub-matrix of
\displaystyle A
and has a series of the type (
1
). There are two cases here:

Case (i):
If no series of the type (
1
) exists for all sub-matrix
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle A
with order
\displaystyle t;t>1
, then
\displaystyle\rho(A)=1
.

Case (ii):
If there is a non-singular sub-matrix
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
of
\displaystyle A
with order
\displaystyle t;t>1
such that a series of the type (
1
) exists for the matrix
\displaystyle B^{\prime}
, then
\displaystyle\rho(A)\geq t
.

Clearly, in each case
\displaystyle\rho(A)\geq 1
, which contradicts as
\displaystyle\rho(A)=0
. So, our assumption,
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}\notin O_{2}
for some
\displaystyle p\in\{1,2,...,n\}
and
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,...,m\}
, is not true. Thus,
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}\in O_{2}
for all
\displaystyle p\in\{1,2,...,n\}
and
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,...,m\}
. Hence
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
- matrix.

Conversely
Suppose
\displaystyle A=[\xi_{ij}]_{n\times m}
is an
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
- matrix. Let us assume that
\displaystyle\rho(A)=r,r\geq 1
.
Then there exists a non-singular sub-matrix
\displaystyle B_{r}
of
\displaystyle A
with order
\displaystyle r;r\geq 1
such that a series
\displaystyle B_{1}\preccurlyeq B_{2}\preccurlyeq\ldots\preccurlyeq B_{r-1}\preccurlyeq B_{r},
of the type (
1
) exists for the matrix
\displaystyle B_{r}
.
Clearly,
\displaystyle B_{1}
is a non-singular sub-matrix of
\displaystyle A
with order 1. This implies that there exists an entry
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}
of
\displaystyle A
such that
\displaystyle\xi_{ps}\notin O_{2}
for some
\displaystyle p\in\{1,2,...,n\}
and
\displaystyle s\in\{1,2,...,m\}
, which contradicts as
\displaystyle A
is
\displaystyle e_{1}e_{2}
- matrix. Therefore our assumption
\displaystyle\rho(A)=r,r\geq 1
is not true.
Hence
\displaystyle\rho(A)=0
.
∎

Theorem 2.13
.

Let
\displaystyle A
be a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times m
. Then,

\displaystyle\rho(A)\leq min\ (\rho(1_{A}),\rho(2_{A}))
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A
be a bicomplex matrix of order
\displaystyle n\times m
and
\displaystyle\rho(1_{A})=r_{1}
,
\displaystyle\rho(2_{A})=r_{2}
. Let us consider
\displaystyle\rho(A)=l
and
\displaystyle l>min(r_{1},r_{2})
. Then, there exists a sub-matrix
\displaystyle B
of
\displaystyle A
of order
\displaystyle l\times l
such that
\displaystyle\rho(B)=l
. This implies that
\displaystyle\det(B)\notin O_{2}
.

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\det(1_{B})\neq 0\ \mbox{and}\ \det(2_{B})\neq 0\ \{\mbox{by Corollary\ref{th9}}\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(1_{B})=l\ \mbox{and}\ \rho(2_{B})=l

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(1_{B})>r_{1}\ \mbox{or}\ \rho(2_{B})>r_{2}\quad\{\mbox{as}\ l>min(r_{1},r_{2})\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\rho(1_{B})>\rho(1_{A})\ \mbox{or}\ \rho(2_{B})>\rho(2_{A}),

which is a contradiction, because
\displaystyle(1_{B})
and
\displaystyle(2_{B})
are the sub-matrices of
\displaystyle(1_{A})
and
\displaystyle(2_{A})
respectively. Therefore, our assumption
\displaystyle\rho(A)=l
, and
\displaystyle l>min(r_{1},r_{2})
is not true.
Thus,
\displaystyle\rho(A)\leq min(r_{1},r_{2})
. Hence,
\displaystyle\rho(A)\leq min(\rho(1_{A}),\rho(2_{A}))
.
∎

Theorem 2.15
.

A matrix
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times n}
is non-singular if and only if
\displaystyle\rho(1_{A})=n

\displaystyle\rho(2_{A})=n

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle A\in\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n\times n}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\det(1_{A})\neq 0\ \mbox{and}\ \det(2_{A})\neq 0\Rightarrow\rho(1_{A})=n\ \mbox{and}\ \rho(2_{A})=n.

Conversely
Let
\displaystyle\rho(1_{A})=n
and
\displaystyle\rho(2_{A})=n

\displaystyle 1_{A},\ 2_{A}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n\times n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\det(1_{A})\neq 0\ \mbox{and}\ \det(2_{A})\neq 0.

Using Corollary

\displaystyle A
is non singular matrix. The proof of theorem is completed.
∎

\displaystyle\displaystyle((x_{1}+y_{1})e_{1},(x_{2}+y_{2})e_{1},\ldots,(x_{n}+y_{n})e_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(x_{1}+y_{1},x_{2}+y_{2},\ldots,x_{n}+y_{n})e_{1}\in Ve_{1}.

and

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha X

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha\{(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})e_{1}\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\alpha(x_{1}e_{1},x_{2}e_{1},\ldots,x_{n}e_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\alpha(x_{1}e_{1}),\alpha(x_{2}e_{1}),\ldots,\alpha(x_{n}e_{1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle((\alpha x_{1})e_{1},(\alpha x_{2})e_{1},\ldots,(\alpha x_{n})e_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\alpha x_{1},\alpha x_{2},\ldots,\alpha x_{n})e_{1}\in Ve_{1}.

Since
\displaystyle Ve_{1}
is a subset of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}
. Thus,
\displaystyle Ve_{1}
forms a subspace of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{2}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
.

Proposition 2.45
.

If
\displaystyle 1_{V}
and
\displaystyle 2_{V}
be two sub-spaces of

. Then
\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle 1_{V}
and
\displaystyle 2_{V}
be two sub-spaces of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}
and
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})\in 1_{V}e_{1}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}
. Then,

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})\in 1_{V}e_{1}
and
\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})\in 2_{V}e_{2}
.

This implies that there exist
\displaystyle(z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{n})\in 1_{V}
and
\displaystyle(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{n})\in 2_{V}
such that

(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})=(z_{1},z_{2},\cdots,z_{n})e_{1}\mbox{and}(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})=(w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{n})e_{2}.

Then, by using Remark
1.5
, we have

\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})=(z_{1}e_{1},z_{2}e_{2},\cdots,z_{n}e_{1})\ and\ (\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})=(w_{1}e_{2},w_{2}e_{2},\cdots,w_{n}e_{2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\xi_{i}=z_{i}e_{1}\ \ and\ \ \xi_{i}=w_{i}e_{2}\ \forall\ i;\ 1\leq i\leq n

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ z_{i}e_{1}=w_{i}e_{2}\ \forall\ i;\ 1\leq i\leq n

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle z_{i}=0\quad and\quad w_{i}=0\ \forall\ i;\ 1\leq i\leq n

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle(\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\cdots,\xi_{n})=(0,0,\cdots,0).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}\subseteq\{0\}.

(2)

Since
\displaystyle(0,0,\cdots,0)\in 1_{V}e_{1}
and
\displaystyle(0,0,\cdots,0)\in 2_{V}e_{2}
. Clearly
\displaystyle(0,0,\cdots,0)\in 1_{V}e_{2}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}
. So, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\{0\}\subseteq 1_{V}e_{2}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}.

(3)

Using (
2
) and (
3
), we get

1_{V}e_{2}\cap 2_{V}e_{2}=\{0\}.

The proof of proposition is completed.

Theorem 2.47
.

Let
\displaystyle 1_{V}
be a subspace of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
and
\displaystyle\dim(1_{V})=k
. Then,
\displaystyle\dim(1_{V}e_{1})=k
.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle 1_{V}
be a subspace of
\displaystyle\mathbb{C}_{1}^{n}(\mathbb{C}_{1})
and
\displaystyle\dim(1_{V})=k
.
So we take

\displaystyle S=\{(\beta_{11},\beta_{12},\ldots,\beta_{1n}),(\beta_{21},\beta_{22},\ldots,\beta_{2n}),\ldots,(\beta_{k1},\beta_{k2},\ldots,\beta_{kn})\}

as a basis for
\displaystyle 1_{V}
. Now, We claim that the set

\displaystyle S^{\prime}=\{(\beta_{11},\beta_{12},\ldots,\beta_{1n})e_{1},(\beta_{21},\beta_{22},\ldots,\beta_{2n})e_{1},\ldots,(\beta_{k1},\beta_{k2},\ldots,\beta_{kn})e_{1}\}

forms a basis for
\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}
. Suppose
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\{(\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\ldots,\beta_{in})e_{1}\}=0,\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
. Using Remark
1.5
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1}e_{1},\beta_{i2}e_{1},\ldots,\beta_{in}e_{1})=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\ \sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1}e_{1}),\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i2}e_{1}),\ldots,\alpha_{i}(\beta_{in}e_{1}))=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}((\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1})e_{1},(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2})e_{1},\ldots,(\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})e_{1})=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1},\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})e_{1}=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1},\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})\right\}e_{1}=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1},\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})=0

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\ldots,\beta_{in})=0.

Since
\displaystyle S
is a basis of
\displaystyle 1_{V}
, implies that
\displaystyle\alpha_{i}=0\ \forall i
. Therefore
\displaystyle S^{\prime}
is a linearly independent set.

Let
\displaystyle X\in 1_{V}e_{1}
. Then, there exists
\displaystyle 1_{X}\in 1_{V}
such that
\displaystyle X=1_{X}e_{1}
. Since
\displaystyle 1_{X}\in 1_{V}
, therefore

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1_{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\ldots,\beta_{in})

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle X=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\ldots,\beta_{in})\right\}e_{1}.

Using Remark
1.5
, it follows that

\displaystyle\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1},\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})\right\}e_{1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1},\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2},\ldots,\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})e_{1}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}((\alpha_{i}\beta_{i1})e_{1},(\alpha_{i}\beta_{i2})e_{1},\ldots,(\alpha_{i}\beta_{in})e_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1}e_{1}),\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i2}e_{1}),\ldots,\alpha_{i}(\beta_{in}e_{1}))

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}(\beta_{i1}e_{1},\beta_{i2}e_{1},\ldots,\beta_{in}e_{1})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\{(\beta_{i1},\beta_{i2},\ldots,\beta_{in})e_{1}\}.

This implies that

\displaystyle\displaystyle X\in\langle S^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}\subseteq\langle S^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}=\langle S^{\prime}\rangle.

Therefore,
\displaystyle S^{\prime}
is a basis for
\displaystyle 1_{V}e_{1}
. This implies that
\displaystyle\dim(1_{V}e_{1})=k
.

The proof of theorem is completed.
∎
