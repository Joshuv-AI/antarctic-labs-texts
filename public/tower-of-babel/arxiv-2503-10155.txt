Asymmetric Long-Step Primal-Dual Interior-Point Methods with Dual Centering
Yurii Nesterov

Abstract
In this paper, we develop a new asymmetric framework for solving primal-dual problems of Conic Optimization by Interior-Point Methods (IPMs). It allows development of efficient methods for problems, where the dual formulation is simpler than the primal one. The problems of this type arise, in particular, in Semidefinite Optimization (SDO), for which we propose a new method with very attractive computational cost. Our long-step predictor-corrector scheme is based on centering in the dual space. It computes the affine-scaling predicting direction by the use of the dual barrier function, controlling the tangent step size by a functional proximity measure. We show that for symmetric cones, the search procedure at the predictor step is very cheap. In general, we do not need sophisticated Linear Algebra, restricting ourselves only by Cholesky factorization. However, our complexity bounds correspond to the best known polynomial-time results. Moreover, for symmetric cones the bounds automatically depend on the minimal barrier parameter between the primal or the dual feasible sets. We show by SDO-examples that the corresponding gain can be very big. We argue that the dual framework is more suitable for adjustment to the actual complexity of the problem. As an example, we discuss some classes of SDO-problems, where the number of iterations is proportional to the square root of the number of linear equality constraints. Moreover, the computational cost of one iteration there is similar to that one for Linear Optimization. We support our theoretical developments by preliminary but encouraging numerical results with randomly generated SDO-problems of different size.



Keywords:
Conic optimization, self-concordant barriers, polynomial-time interior-point methods, primal-dual methods, predictor-corrector methods, Semidefinite Optimization.

Today, the primal-dual formulation of conic problems

Indeed, these formulations help each other in constructing powerful primal-dual methods by getting an additional information from a conjugate pair of self-concordant barriers for the primal and the dual cones. The most efficient algorithms are predictor-corrector methods

, which follow approximately the primal-dual central path by alternating the predictor step with several correcting iterations.

However, the superiority of these methods is not for free. For practical applications, we need to assume computability both for the primal and the dual barrier functions. For general cones, this ability is quite rare. Indeed, in order to compute the value of the dual barrier function, we need to find in a closed form a minimum of the sum of the primal barrier and a linear function with arbitrary coefficients. For nontrivial barrier functions, this is usually impossible. Of course, we could try to evaluate the dual barrier numerically. However, any inaccuracy in the computation of its value and/or its derivatives is damaging for the optimization schemes.

Thus, at this moment the classical primal-dual methods work well only for the self-dual barriers, which correspond mainly to symmetric cones.

For practical applications, the most important cones are the positive orthant, the cone of positive semidefinite matrices and the Lorentz cone.

The above asymmetry in the computational cost of the primal and dual barrier functions served as the main motivation for the paper

However, it appears that the computational difficulties with the dual barrier are not the only source of asymmetry in the primal-dual formulation. Even for symmetric cones, the complexity of primal and dual problems can be very different. In Semidefinite Programming this can be seen even at the structural level. Indeed, the primal variables there are symmetric
n\times n
-matrices, and the dual variables are vectors from

Moreover, it appears that in some cases the complexity of the dual feasible set, measured by the value of barrier parameter, is much smaller than the complexity of the primal feasible set. In Section
8

m

The main goal of this paper is the development of a primal-dual framework for solving problems of Conic Optimization, based mainly on the
dual

Hence, we present a dual counterpart of the primal technique developed in
[

Let
\mathbb{E}
be a finite-dimensional space and
\mathbb{E}^{*}
be the space of linear functions on

. For

, denote by

the value of function

at

for different spaces. Thus, its actual meaning is defined by the context.
For a linear operator
A:\mathbb{E}_{1}\to\mathbb{E}^{*}_{2}
, we denote by
A^{*}
its
adjoint operator
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle Ax,y\rangle&=&\langle A^{*}y,x\rangle,\quad x\in\mathbb{E}_{1},\;y\in\mathbb{E}_{2}.\end{array}

(2.1)

Thus,
A^{*}:\mathbb{E}_{2}\to\mathbb{E}_{1}^{*}
. The operator
A:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{E}^{*}
is positive semidefinite (notation
A\succeq 0
) if
\langle Ax,x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{E}
. We write
A\succeq B
if
A-B\succeq 0
.

In case of
\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
\mathbb{E}^{*}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle s,x\rangle&=&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}s^{(i)}x^{(i)},\quad\|x\|\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\langle x,x\rangle^{1/2},\quad s,x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}

Vector
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector of all ones, and
e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, are coordinate vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

For vectors
x,s\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, all arithmetic operations like
x\pm s
or

are understood in the coordinate-wise sense. The same is true for the partial orderings.

Denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
the space of
n\times m
-matrices with the Frobenius scalar product

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle S,X\rangle&=&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}S^{(i,j)}X^{(i,j)},\quad S,X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}.\end{array}

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, notation
X\equiv D(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is used for the diagonal matrix with
x
at the main diagonal. Thus, the identity matrix is
I_{n}\equiv D(e)
.

Notation
{\cal C}^{k}(\mathbb{E})
,

is used for the class of closed functions on
\mathbb{E}
with open domain
{\rm dom\,}f
, which admit
k
th directional derivative
D^{k}f(x)[h]^{k}
along any direction
h\in\mathbb{E}
at any point
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
. Function
f\in{\cal C}(\mathbb{E})
is differentiable on
{\rm dom\,}f
(notation
f\in\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{E})
) if

\begin{array}[]{rcl}DF(x)[h]&=&\langle\nabla f(x),h\rangle,\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\;h\in\mathbb{E}.\end{array}

Thus, the gradient
\nabla f(x)
belongs to
\mathbb{E}^{*}
,
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
.
Function
f\in{\cal C}^{2}(\mathbb{E})
is twice differentiable on
{\rm dom\,}f
(notation
f\in\mathbb{C}^{2}(\mathbb{E})
) if

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{2}F(x)[h]^{2}&=&\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)h,h\rangle,\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\;h\in\mathbb{E}.\end{array}

Thus, for any

, the linear operator (Hessian)

maps

to

Let us recall some facts from the theory of self-concordant functions (e.g.

Definition 1

Function
f\in{\cal C}^{3}(\mathbb{E})
is called self-concordant (SCF) if its third directional derivative is bounded by an appropriate power of the second derivative:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}D^{3}f(x)[h]^{3}&\leq&2\Big(D^{2}f(x)[h]^{2}\Big)^{3/2},\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\;h\in\mathbb{E}.\end{array}

(2.2)

If
{\rm dom\,}f
contains no straight line, then its Hessian is positive definite at any
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
. In this paper, we mainly deal with such functions.

For any SCF, its dual function
f_{*}(s)=\max\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}f}[-\langle s,x\rangle-f(x)]
is also self-concordant. Note that
-\nabla f(x)\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
for all
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla f_{*}(s)&=&-x(s)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\arg\min\limits_{x\in{\rm dom\,}f}[-\langle s,x\rangle-f(x)],\quad s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}\end{array}

For any
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
and
s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\nabla f(-\nabla f_{*}(s))&=&s,&\nabla^{2}f(-\nabla f_{*}(s))&=&[\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)]^{-1},\\
\\
\nabla f_{*}(-\nabla f(x))&=&x&\nabla^{2}f_{*}(-\nabla f(x))&=&[\nabla^{2}f(x)]^{-1}.\end{array}

(2.3)

One of the main properties of SCF is that the
Dikin Ellipsoid
defined as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}W^{f}_{r}(x)&=&\{u\in\mathbb{E}:\;\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x),y-x\rangle\leq r^{2}\},\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\end{array}

belongs to
{\rm dom\,}f
for any
r\in[0,1)
. Thus, for
{\rm dom\,}f
containing no straight line, the Hessian is positive definite at any feasible point.

In this paper, we often use the local norms defined by the Hessians of SCF. For
x\in{\rm dom\,}f
,
s\in{\rm dom\,}f_{*}
,

, and

, we adopt the following notation:

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}f(x)}&=&\langle\nabla^{2}f(x)u,u\rangle^{1/2},&\quad\|g\|_{\nabla^{2}f(x)}&=&\langle g,[\nabla^{2}f(x)]^{-1}g\rangle^{1/2},\\
\\
\|g\|_{\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)}&=&\langle g,\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)g\rangle^{1/2},&\quad\|u\|_{\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)}&=&\langle[\nabla^{2}f_{*}(s)]^{-1}u,u\rangle^{1/2}.\end{array}

If no ambiguity arise, we use the corresponding shortcuts

,
\|g\|_{x}
,
\|g\|_{s}
, and

For SCF, we often use the following relations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}(1-r)^{2}\nabla^{2}f(x)&\preceq&\nabla^{2}f(u)\;\preceq\;{1\over(1-r)^{2}}\nabla^{2}f(x),\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}f,\end{array}

(2.5)

where

,

,

, and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(\tau)&=&\tau-\ln(1+\tau),\quad\omega_{*}(\tau)\;=\;-\tau-\ln(1-\tau),\quad\tau\in[0,1).\end{array}

Sometimes the following variant of inequalities (

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega(\delta)\;\leq\;f(x)-f(y)-\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\rangle&\leq&\omega_{*}(\delta),\end{array}

(2.6)

with

(for (

Lemma 1

Let function

be self-concordant. Then, for any

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|\nabla f(y)-\nabla f(x)-\nabla^{2}f(x)(y-x)\|_{x}&\leq&{r^{2}\over 1-r}.\end{array}

Note that

Let us recall now the properties of
self-concordant barriers
(SCB) for convex cones.

Definition 2

A self-concordant function
F(\cdot)
is called a self-concordant barrier, if there exists a constant

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu&\geq&1\end{array}

(2.8)

such that for all
x\in{\rm dom\,}F
and
h\in\mathbb{E}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(x),h\rangle^{2}&\leq&\nu\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)h,h\rangle.\end{array}

(2.9)

The constant
\nu
is called the parameter of the barrier.
If
\nabla^{2}F(x)\succ 0
, then the condition (
2.9
) is equivalent to the following:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{F}^{2}(x)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;\|\nabla F(x)\|_{x}^{2}&\leq&\nu,\quad x\in{\rm dom\,}F.\end{array}

(2.10)

This value is responsible for complexity of the set
{\rm dom\,}F
for corresponding IPMs. A
lower bounds
for
\nu
can be obtained by the following statement (see Theorem 5.4.1 in
[
11
]
).

Theorem 1

Let
p_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,k
, be recession directions of set
Q
and
\bar{x}\in{\rm int\,}Q
. Define coefficients
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
such that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{x}-\beta_{i}p_{i}&\not\in&{\rm int\,}Q,\quad i=1,\dots,k.\end{array}

And let
\bar{x}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}p_{i}\in Q
for some positive
\alpha_{i}
,
i=1\dots,k
. Then
\nu\geq\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}{\alpha_{i}\over\beta_{i}}
.

In the theory of IPMs, the most important feasible sets are convex cones. A cone
K
is called
proper
if it is closed convex and pointed (contains no straight lines). For such cones, SCBs possess a natural property of
logarithmic homogeneity
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(\tau x)&\equiv&F(x)-\nu\ln\tau,\quad x\in{\rm int\,}K,\;\tau>0.\end{array}

(2.11)

This identity has several important consequences: for all
x\in{\rm int\,}K
and
\tau>0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\nabla F(\tau x)&=&{1\over\tau}\nabla F(x),&\nabla^{2}F(\tau x)&=&{1\over\tau^{2}}\nabla^{2}F(x),\end{array}

(2.12)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(x),x\rangle&=&-\nu,\quad\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)x,x\rangle\;=\;\nu,\end{array}

(2.13)

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\nabla^{2}F(x)x&=&\nabla F(x),&\quad\|\nabla F(x)\|^{2}_{x}&=&\nu.\end{array}

(2.14)

Thus, the parameter of a logarithmically homogeneous SCB for a proper cone (which we call
regular barrier
) is equal to the degree of logarithmic homogeneity.

It is important that the dual barrier
F_{*}(\cdot)
for the regular barrier
F(\cdot)
is a regular barrier for the dual cone

\begin{array}[]{rcl}K^{*}&=&\Big\{s\in\mathbb{E}^{*}:\;\langle s,x\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall x\in K\Big\},\end{array}

with the same value of barrier parameter. Note that for logarithmically homogeneous barriers, we can use a stronger version of Fenchel inequality:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x)+F_{*}(s)&\geq&\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle\over\nu}-\nu,\quad x\in{\rm int\,}K,\;s\in{\rm int\,}K^{*},\end{array}

(2.15)

where the equality is achieved only for
s=-\mu\nabla F(x)
with some
\mu>0
.

Primal and dual central paths

) consists in unifying them in a single
primal-dual problem
with zero optimal value:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\min\limits_{z=(x,y,s)}\Big\{\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle:\;x\in{\cal F}_{p},\;(y,s)\in{\cal F}_{d}\Big\}\;=\;0.\end{array}

(3.4)

This problem can be solved by a path-following scheme, based on the primal-dual barrier function
\Phi(z)=F(x)+F_{*}(s)
,
z\in{\cal F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}{\cal F}_{p}\times{\cal F}_{d}
. The majority of the efficient interior-point methods follow somehow the primal-dual central path defined as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}z(t)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&\arg\min\limits_{z\in{\cal F}}\Big\{F(x)+F_{*}(s):\;\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle={\nu\over t}\Big\},\quad t>0.\end{array}

(3.5)

This process is usually divided on two stages.

•

Corrector Stage
. At this stage, we fix some value of penalty parameter
t>0
and try to find a point
z\approx z(t)
by solving approximately the minimization problem (
3.5
). The closeness of point
z
to the points at the central path can be estimated by different proximity measures. For our purposes, the most convenient is the following
Functional Proximity Measure

[
9
]
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega(z)&=&\nu\ln\langle s,x\rangle+F(x)+F_{*}(s)+\nu-\nu\ln\nu\\
\\
&=&\nu\ln[\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle]+F(x)+F_{*}(s)+\nu-\nu\ln\nu\;\geq\;0,\quad z\in{\cal F}.\end{array}

(3.6)

•

Predictor Stage
. If
z
is close enough to the central path, we compute direction
d\approx z^{\prime}(t)
and move along it, keeping
z+\alpha d\approx z(t+\alpha)
,
\alpha>0
. It is convenient to estimate this closeness by checking the values
\Omega(z+\alpha d)
.

The bigger the value of
\alpha
is, the higher is efficiency of the scheme. It mainly depends on the quality of approximation
d
to the exact tangent direction to the central path. For an important family of
symmetric cones
, covering the problems of Linear, Quadratic, and Semidefinite Optimization, a good direction
d
can be defined by a
Scaling Point
.

For these cones, two components of a strictly feasible primal-dual pair
(x,s)\in{\rm int\,}(K\times K^{*})
can be linked by a uniquely defined scaling point
w\in{\rm int\,}K
, ensuring the following relation:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s&=&\nabla^{2}F(w)x.\end{array}

(3.7)

It appears that the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F(w)
can be used for defining a very good approximation
d=(\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta s)
to derivative of the central path, which is called
Affine-Scaling Directon
(ASD). This direction is a solution of the following system of linear equations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Delta s+\nabla^{2}F(w)\Delta x&=&p,\quad A\Delta x\;=\;0,\quad\Delta s+A^{*}\Delta y\;=\;0,\end{array}

(3.8)

where the right-hand side
p\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
depends on our particular goals. For example, for defining the
centering direction
, we choose
p=-s-\mu\nabla F(x)
with
\mu={1\over\nu}\langle s,x\rangle
, and for ASD, we take
p=-s
.

Thus, for symmetric cones, the sequence of our actions is as follows.

1.

Find
z=(x,y,s)\approx z(t)
ensuring the relation (
3.7
).

2.

Compute the scaling point
w\in{\rm int\,}K
.

3.

Compute the Affine-Scaling Direction by (
3.8
).

However, for one of the most important applications of this theory, the Semidefinite Optimization (SDO), the second action is quite expensive. It needs computation of square roots of two symmetric positive-definite matrices, while all other computational tasks are handled by the standard Cholesky factorization.

In this paper, we suggest to use an alternative sequence of actions.

1.

Compute the scaling point
w\in{\rm int\,}K
using an auxiliary minimization process either in the primal or in the dual spaces.

2.

Using the Hessian
\nabla^{2}F(w)
, form the strictly feasible point
z=(x,y,s)\approx z(t)
, which satisfies the scaling equation (
3.7
).

3.

Compute the Affine-Scaling Direction by (
3.8
) with
p=-s
.

As we will see later, with this strategy, all necessary computations be accomplished by the standard Cholesky factorization. A primal variant of this approach was already discussed in
[
10
]
. In this paper, we justify its dual variant, which is more appropriate for many practical applications.

For our purposes, it is important to define separately the central paths for the primal and for the dual problem:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}x_{t}&=&\arg\min\limits_{Ax=b}[\;f_{t}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}t\langle c,x\rangle+F(x)\;],\\
\\
(y_{t},s_{t})&=&\arg\max\limits_{s+A^{*}y=c}[\;\varphi_{t}(y,s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}t\langle b,y\rangle-F_{*}(s)\;].\end{array}

(3.9)

It appears, that this definition provides us with the same trajectories as (
3.5
). Denote

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}_{t}&=&f_{t}(x_{t}),\quad\varphi^{*}_{t}\;=\;\varphi_{t}(y_{t},s_{t}),\end{array}

and define the dual barrier function

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&F_{*}(c-A^{*}y),\quad y\in{\cal F}_{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\;c-A^{*}y\in K^{*}\},\\
\\
\psi_{t}(y)&=&\zeta(y)-t\langle b,y\rangle,\quad t>0.\end{array}

(3.10)

Note that
\zeta(\cdot)
is a self-concordant barrier for the set
{\cal F}_{y}
with parameter
\nu_{\zeta}\leq\nu
. Our approach is very efficient for the problems with small values of
\nu_{\zeta}
. In Section
8
, we discuss examples of SDO-problems with
\nu_{\zeta}=O(m)
.

Lemma 2

For all
t>0
, we have
z(t)\equiv(x_{t},y_{t},s_{t})
.
Moreover,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}s_{t}&=&-{1\over t}\nabla F(x_{t}),\quad\langle c,x_{t}\rangle-\langle b,y_{t}\rangle\;=\;{\nu\over t},\end{array}

(3.11)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x_{t})+F_{*}(s_{t})&=&-\nu\ln t-\nu,\end{array}

(3.12)

and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}_{t}&=&\varphi^{*}_{t}+\nu\ln t,\end{array}

(3.13)

Proof:

The first-order optimality condition for point
x_{t}
looks as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}tc+\nabla F(x_{t})&=&tA^{*}u_{t},\end{array}

(3.14)

where
u_{t}
is a dual multiplier for the linear equality constraints, which for convenience is multiplied by a constant factor
t
. Then,
c-A^{*}u_{t}=-{1\over t}\nabla F(x_{t})\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
, and we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla\psi_{t}(u_{t})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-LUOpt})}}{{=}}&-A\nabla F_{*}(-{1\over t}\nabla F(x_{t}))-tb\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom12})}}{{=}}-tA\nabla F_{*}(-\nabla F(x_{t}))-tb\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual})}}{{=}}&t(Ax_{t}-b)=0.\end{array}

Therefore,
u_{t}=y_{t}
, and we get the first equality in (
3.11
). It implies

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle c,x_{t}\rangle-\langle b,y_{t}\rangle&=&\langle c,x_{t}\rangle-\langle Ax_{t},y_{t}\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SX}_{1})}}{{=}}\;\langle-{1\over t}\nabla F(x_{t}),x_{t}\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomHX})}}{{=}}\;{\nu\over t},\end{array}

and the second relation in (
3.11
) is proved. Now, we see that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x_{t})+F_{*}(s_{t})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HFenchel}),(\ref{eq-SX}_{1})}}{{=}}&\nu\ln{\langle s_{t},x_{t}\rangle\over\nu}-\nu\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SX})}}{{=}}\;-\nu\ln t-\nu,\end{array}

and this is (
3.12
).

Thus,
(x_{t},y_{t},s_{t})
is feasible for problem (
3.5
), and for all
z\in{\cal F}
with
\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle={\nu\over t}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x)+F_{*}(s)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HFenchel})}}{{\geq}}&\nu\ln{\langle s,x\rangle\over\nu}-\nu\;=\;\nu\ln{\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle\over\nu}-\nu\\
\\
&=&-\nu\ln t-\nu\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SXF})}}{{=}}\;\;F(x_{t})+F_{*}(s_{t}).\end{array}

In view of uniqueness of the solution of problem (
3.5
), this means that
z(t)\equiv z_{t}
.
Finally,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}_{t}-\varphi^{*}_{t}&=&F(x_{t})+F_{*}(s_{t})+t[\langle c,x_{t}\rangle-\langle b,y_{t}\rangle]\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SX})}}{{=}}\;F(x_{t})+F_{*}(-{1\over t}\nabla F(x_{t}))+\nu\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom12})}}{{=}}&F(x_{t})+F_{*}(-\nabla F(x_{t}))+\nu+\nu\ln t\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HFenchel}),(\ref{eq-GX})}}{{=}}\;\nu\ln t.\hfill\Box\end{array}

For
z=(x,y,s)\in{\cal F}
and
t>0
, let us define now a
partial
proximity measure:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega_{t}(z)&=&[f_{t}(x)-f^{*}_{t}]+[\varphi^{*}_{t}-\varphi_{t}(y,s)]\\
\\
&=&t[\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle]+F(x)+F_{*}(s)-f^{*}_{t}+\varphi^{*}_{t}.\end{array}

(3.15)

Thus,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Omega_{t}(z)&=&t[\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle]+F(x)+F_{*}(s)-\nu\ln t.\end{array}

(3.16)

Lemma 3

For any
z\in{\rm rint\,}{\cal F}
, we have
\Omega(z)=\min\limits_{t>0}\Omega_{t}(z)
. The minimum is attained at

\begin{array}[]{rcl}t(z)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&{\nu\over\langle c,x\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle}={\nu\over\langle s,x\rangle}.\hskip 21.52771pt\Box\end{array}

(3.17)

Corollary 1

For
t=t(z)
, we have
\Omega_{t}(z)=\Omega(z)
.
\Box

For proper interpretation of the subsequent results, it is important to know that the primal and the dual problems in (
3.1
) have
complementary complexities
. In order to show this, let us look at the barrier function for the primal feasible set

\begin{array}[]{rcl}g(x)&=&F(x),\quad x\in{\rm rint\,}{\cal F}_{p}.\end{array}

This is a self-concordant barrier with parameter
\nu_{g}\leq\nu
.
In order to highlight its relation with the parameter
\nu_{\zeta}
of the dual barrier
\zeta(\cdot)
, let us prove the following identity.

Lemma 4

For any
t>0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{g}^{2}(x_{t})+\lambda_{\zeta}^{2}(y_{t})&=&\nu.\end{array}

(3.18)

Proof:

Indeed, for any
x\in{\rm rint\,}{\cal F}_{p}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\lambda^{2}_{g}(x)&=&\min\limits_{h\in\mathbb{E}}\Big\{\langle\nabla F(x),h\rangle+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|h\|_{x}^{2}\;:Ah=0\Big\}.\end{array}

Denote by
h^{*}
the optimal solution of this problem and by
u^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the vector of optimal dual multipliers. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla F(x)+\nabla^{2}F(x)h^{*}&=&A^{T}u^{*},\quad Ah^{*}\;=\;0.\end{array}

Hence,
h^{*}=[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}\Big[A^{T}u^{*}-\nabla F(x)\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-HomHX}_{1})}}{{=}}[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}u^{*}+x
. Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}u^{*}&=&-\Big[A[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}\Big]^{-1}Ax\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{prob-PD}_{1})}}{{=}}\;-\Big[A[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}\Big]^{-1}b,\end{array}

and we conclude that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(x),h^{*}\rangle&=&\langle\nabla F(x),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}u^{*}+x\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GX}_{1}),(\ref{eq-HomHX}_{1})}}{{=}}\;-\langle x,A^{T}u^{*}\rangle-\nu,\\
\\
\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)h^{*},h^{*}\rangle&=&\langle\nabla^{2}F(x)([\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}u^{*}+x),[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}u^{*}+x\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-GX}_{2})}}{{=}}&\langle\Big[A[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}\Big]^{-1}b,b\rangle+2\langle x,A^{T}u^{*}\rangle+\nu.\end{array}

Hence,
-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\lambda^{2}_{g}(x)=-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\nu+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\langle\Big[A[\nabla^{2}F(x)]^{-1}A^{T}\Big]^{-1}b,b\rangle
. For
x_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SX}_{1})}}{{=}}-{1\over t}\nabla F_{*}(s_{t})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla^{2}F(x_{t})&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Hom12}_{2})}}{{=}}&t^{2}\nabla^{2}F(-\nabla F_{*}(s_{t}))\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual}_{4})}}{{=}}\;t^{2}[\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s_{t})]^{-1}.\end{array}

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu&=&\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})+\langle\Big[A[\nabla^{2}F(x_{t})]^{-1}A^{T}\Big]^{-1}b,b\rangle\;=\;\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})+t^{2}\langle\Big[A\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s_{t})A^{T}\Big]^{-1}b,b\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-psi}_{1})}}{{=}}&\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})+t^{2}\langle[\nabla^{2}\zeta(y_{t})]^{-1}b,b\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-xys}_{2})}}{{=}}\;\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})+\langle[\nabla^{2}\zeta(y_{t})]^{-1}\nabla\zeta(y_{t}),\nabla\zeta(y_{t})\rangle\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-SCB1})}}{{=}}&\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})+\lambda^{2}_{\zeta}(y_{t}).\hfill\Box\end{array}

To the best of our knowledge, equality (
3.18
) is new. It has several important consequences. First of all, it provides us with strong arguments
against
the short-step primal-dual methods. In these methods, the steps are confined by the corresponding Dikin ellipsoids, and the penalty parameters for the primal and the dual central paths are the same. From the theory of self-concordant functions (see Section 5.3.1 in
[
13
]
), we know that the increase of the current value of penalty parameter
t
must be in inverse proportion to the local norm of the gradient of corresponding self-concordant barrier. Hence, by (
3.18
), either the primal or the dual norm is big. This fact cancels any hope for simultaneous large steps in the primal and the dual spaces.

Further, from inequality (
3.18
), we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-SCB1})}}{{\leq}}&\nu_{g}+\nu_{\zeta}.\end{array}

(3.19)

This relation is another motivation for a proper treating of the
Primal-Dual Asymmetry
. Indeed, if the dual feasible set
{\cal F}_{y}
is simple (and the barrier parameter
\nu_{\zeta}
is small), then the primal feasible set
{\cal F}_{p}

cannot
be simple since

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{g}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Ass})}}{{\geq}}&\nu-\nu_{\zeta}.\end{array}

In this case, it is better to apply strategies related to the dual central path. We will discuss several examples in Section
8
. Below, we consider a simple SDO-problem with this type of asymmetry.

Example 3

Let us choose some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\langle a,b\rangle>0
. Consider the following SDO-problem:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f^{*}&=&\min\limits_{X\succeq 0}\Big\{\langle I_{n},X\rangle:\;Xa=b\Big\}.\end{array}

(3.20)

In this problem, we have
{n(n+1)\over 2}
variables and
n
linear equality constraints. It is easy to see that its optimal solution is
X^{*}={bb^{T}\over\langle a,b\rangle}
with
f^{*}={\|b\|^{2}\over\langle a,b\rangle}
.

For rewriting the problem (
3.20
) in the standard form (
3.2
), denote
A_{i}=\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(ae_{i}^{T}+e_{i}a^{T})
,
i=1,\dots,n
. Then the equality constraints in (
3.20
) becomes as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle A_{i},X\rangle&=&\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\langle ae_{i}^{T}+e_{i}a^{T},X\rangle\;=\;\langle e_{i},Xa\rangle\;=\;b^{(i)},\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

Thus, in accordance to (
3.3
), the problem dual to (
3.20
) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\max\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\langle b,y\rangle:\;I_{n}\succeq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(ya^{T}+ay^{T})\Big\},\end{array}

(3.21)

with the barrier function
\zeta(y)=-\ln\det\left(I_{n}-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(ya^{T}+ay^{T})\right)
. Let us compute this determinant in the closed firm.

It is easy to see that the matrix
S(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}I_{n}-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}(ya^{T}+ay^{T})
has only two eigenvalues different from one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{1,2}&=&1-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\langle a,y\rangle\pm\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\|a\|\cdot\|y\|.\end{array}

Hence,
\det S(y)=(1-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\langle a,y\rangle)^{2}-{1\over 4}\|a\|^{2}\|y\|^{2}
.

Let us compute the barrier parameter
\nu_{\zeta}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
a=\alpha e_{1}
with
\alpha=\|a\|>0
. Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\det S(y)&=&\Big(1-\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\alpha y^{(1)}\Big)^{2}-{1\over 4}\alpha^{2}\|y\|^{2}\;=\;1-\alpha y^{(1)}-{1\over 4}\alpha^{2}\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}(y^{(i)})^{2}.\end{array}

This is a concave quadratic function and therefore
\nu_{\zeta}=1
(see Example 5.3.1(4) in
[
11
]
). Hence, the restriction
g(\cdot)
of the barrier
F(X)=-\ln\det X
onto the feasible set of the primal problem (
3.20
) has the parameter

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{g}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Ass})}}{{\geq}}&n-1.\end{array}

(3.22)

Inequality (
3.22
) is valid only for the barrier parameter of our particular barrier function. Let us show that the primal feasible set of problem (
3.20
) indeed requires a self-concordant barrier with large value of
\nu_{g}
.

Without loss of generality, we can think that
a=\alpha e_{1}
and
b^{(1)}>0
. Then the matrix
\bar{X}=I_{n}-e_{1}e_{1}^{T}+X^{*}
belongs to the relative interior of the feasible set
{\cal F}_{p}
of problem (
3.20
). Note that the directions
p_{i}=e_{i}e_{i}^{T}
,
i=2,\dots,n
are recession directions for
{\cal F}_{p}
. On the other hand,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{X}-p_{i}&\not\in&{\rm rint\,}{\cal F}_{p},\quad i=2,\dots,n,\quad\bar{X}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}p_{i}\;=\;X^{*}\;\in{\cal F}_{p}.\end{array}

Hence, by Theorem
1
, parameter of any barrier for
{\cal F}_{p}
satisfies inequality (
3.22
).

\Box

This example demonstrates the maximal asymmetry in the complexities of the primal and the dual barrier functions. In such a case, we should work with problem with the minimal value of the barrier parameter. However, in the real-life applications, it is usually impossible to get a priori good bounds for their values. Fortunately, in our situation we discover a small value of barrier parameter for the
standard
barrier function. We do not need to change it. As we will see later, an appropriate predictor-corrector strategy indeed can have global complexity bounds automatically dependent on
\min\{\nu_{g},\nu_{\zeta}\}
.

To conclude this section, let us give a simple sufficient condition for relation
\nu_{g}\geq\nu_{\zeta}
.

Lemma 5

Let set
{\cal F}_{y}
be bounded. Then
\nu_{g}=\nu\geq\nu_{\zeta}
.

Proof:

Indeed, if the set
{\cal F}_{y}
is bounded, then there exists its analytic center
y^{*}_{\zeta}
with
\nabla\zeta(y^{*}_{\zeta})=0
. Since
\lim\limits_{t\to 0}y_{t}=y^{*}_{\zeta}
, we get

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu\;\geq\;\nu_{g}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{def-SCB1})}}{{\geq}}&\lim\limits_{t\to 0}\lambda^{2}_{g}(x_{t})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-LNorms})}}{{=}}\;\lim\limits_{t\to 0}\,[\nu-\lambda^{2}_{\zeta}(y_{t})]\;=\;\nu.\end{array}

It remains to note that parameter
\nu_{\zeta}
does not exceed
\nu
too.

\Box

Of course, the symmetric statement is also valid: boundedness of
{\cal F}_{p}
implies
\nu_{\zeta}=\nu
. Sometimes, this distinction can be seen from the analytical structure of the problem. However, it is not always the case: in Example
3
both
{\cal F}_{p}
and
{\cal F}_{y}
are unbounded.

4
Dual Gambit Rule

Let us discuss now a simple way for computing the scaling point. For that, we consider a
pure dual
centering process, which finds an approximate solution of the dual problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{y\in{\cal F}_{y}}\Big\{\psi_{t}(y)=\zeta(y)-t\langle b,y\rangle\Big\}.\end{array}

(4.1)

This can be done either by the rudimentary Damped Newton Method:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lambda_{k}&=&\langle\nabla\psi_{t}(y_{k}),[\nabla^{2}\zeta(y_{k})]^{-1}\nabla\psi_{t}(y_{k})\rangle^{1/2},\\
\\
y_{k+1}&=&y_{k}-{1\over 1+\lambda_{k}}[\nabla^{2}\zeta_{d}(y_{k})]^{-1}\nabla\psi_{t}(y_{k}),\quad k\geq 0,\end{array}

(4.2)

or by an auxiliary path-following scheme (e.g. Section 5.3.5 in
[
11
]
). Recall that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nabla\zeta(y)&=&-A\nabla F_{*}(c-A^{*}y),\quad\nabla^{2}\zeta(y)\;=\;A\nabla^{2}F_{*}(c-A^{*}y)A^{*}.\end{array}

(4.3)

In any case, we can assume that after a finite number of iterations, we get a point
\bar{y}
, satisfying the following condition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\langle\nabla\psi_{t}(\bar{y}),[\nabla^{2}\zeta(\bar{y})]^{-1}\nabla\psi_{t}(\bar{y})\rangle^{1/2}&\leq&\beta\;<\;1.\end{array}

(4.4)

In our computations, we use the Newton direction

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{d}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&[\nabla^{2}\zeta(\bar{y})]^{-1}\nabla\psi_{t}(\bar{y}),\end{array}

(4.5)

which is also required for checking the condition (
4.4
). Note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\ \|\bar{d}\|^{2}_{\bar{y}}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-DTerm})}}{{=}}&\langle\nabla^{2}\zeta(\bar{y})\bar{d},\bar{d}\rangle\;=\;\bar{\lambda}^{2}.\end{array}

(4.6)

Denote
\bar{s}=c-A^{*}\bar{y}\in{\rm int\,}K^{*}
and
\bar{x}=-{1\over t}\nabla F_{*}(\bar{s})\in{\rm int\,}K
. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{s}&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-FDual}),(\ref{eq-Hom12})}}{{=}}&-{1\over t}\nabla F(\bar{x}).\end{array}

(4.7)

Consider now a special rule for generating a feasible point in a close neighbourhood of the primal-dual central path.

\begin{array}[]{|c|}\hline\cr\\
\mbox{\sc Dual Gambit Rule}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}\hat{y}&=&\bar{y}+\bar{d},&\hat{s}&=&c-A^{*}\hat{y}\;\equiv\;\bar{s}-A^{*}\bar{d}\\
\\
\bar{w}_{*}&=&\sqrt{t}\,\bar{s},&\hat{x}&=&\nabla^{2}F_{*}(\bar{w}_{*})\hat{s}.\end{array}\\
\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(4.8)

Example 4

Let us show how it works for the dual SDO-problem (

in problem (
3.1
) be symmetric and
parameters of method (
6.3
) satisfy the following conditions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\beta&\in&(0,1),\quad\delta\;>\;0,\quad\hat{\varkappa}(\beta,\delta)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;{\delta\over 1+\delta}-{2\beta(3+\beta)\over 1-\beta}\;>\;0.\end{array}

(7.11)

If the starting point
y_{0}\in{\cal F}_{y}
satisfies condition (
6.10
), then any predictor iteration
k\geq 0
of
method (
6.3
) ensures the following growth of penalty coefficient:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}t_{0}\;\geq\;{\beta\over 2\|b\|_{y_{0}}},\quad t_{k+1}&\geq&t_{k}\cdot\exp\Big\{{k\over\hat{\sigma}(\beta,\delta)}\Big\},\end{array}

(7.12)

where
\hat{\sigma}(\beta,\delta)={2\hat{c}_{\beta}+1\over\hat{\varkappa}(\beta,\delta)}\left(\delta+\omega_{*}\left({2\beta\over(1-\beta)^{2}}\right)\right)
and
\hat{c}_{\beta}={\beta+\sqrt{\nu_{\zeta}}\over 1-\beta}
.

Remark 3

Alternatively, we can estimate the growth of function
\xi_{\hat{z}_{k}}(\cdot)
using the second representation in (
7.9
).
For that, we need to prove that
\|\Delta x_{k}\|_{\hat{x}_{k}}\leq O(\sqrt{\nu_{g}})
and repeat the reasoning before Theorem
4
. We move these justifications in Appendix in Section
11
since they are more technical due to a non-trivial way of generating the point
\hat{x}_{k}
. This primal justification gives better results if
\nu_{g}\leq\nu_{\zeta}
. However, in both cases the method (
6.3
) is not changing. We just use different formulas for computing values of the same primal-dual proximity measure.
Therefore, in the sequel we claim that the complexity of method (
6.3
) for symmetric cones depends on
\min\{\nu_{\zeta},\nu_{g}\}
.
\Box

Thus, method (
6.3
), being applied to a conic problem (
3.1
) with symmetric cone
K
, solves it in
O\left(\min\{\nu_{\zeta},\nu_{g}\}^{1/2}\ln{1\over\epsilon}\right)
iterations of the dual Newton method. In Section
8
, we will discuss some classes of problems with
\nu_{\zeta}<<\nu
.

To conclude this section, let us present a variant of method (
6.3
), where we eliminate all unnecessary appearances of the primal variables.

\begin{array}[]{|l|}\hline\cr\\
\hskip 21.52771pt\mbox{\sc Dual Predictor-Corrector Method for Symmetric Cones}\\
\\
\hline\cr\\
\mbox{{\bf Initialization.} Let $\beta$ and $\delta$ satisfy (\ref{eq-condDBS1}). Define $A=\delta+\omega_{*}\left({2\beta\over(1-\beta)^{2}}\right)$. }\\
\\
\mbox{Compute $y_{0}\in{\cal F}_{y}$ with $\|\nabla\zeta(y_{0})\|_{y_{0}}\leq\mbox{${1\over 2}$}\beta$ and set $t_{0}={\beta-\|\nabla\zeta(y_{0})\|_{y_{0}}\over\|b\|_{y_{0}}}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{\bf$k$-th iteration ($k\geq 0$)}\\
\\
\begin{array}[]{rl}\mbox{a)}&\mbox{For $y_{k}\in{\rm int\,}{\cal F}_{y}$, compute $g_{k}=\nabla\psi_{t_{k}}(y_{k})$ and $B_{k}=[\nabla^{2}\zeta(y_{k})]^{-1}$.}\\
\\
&\mbox{Define $d_{k}=B_{k}g_{k}$, and $\lambda_{k}=\langle g_{k},d_{k}\rangle^{1/2}$.}\\
\\
\mbox{b)}&\mbox{{\bf If} $\lambda_{k}>\beta$, {\bf then} $y_{k+1}=y_{k}-{d_{k}\over 1+\lambda_{k}}$, $t_{k+1}=t_{k}$ ({Damped Newton Step})}\\
\\
\mbox{c)}&\mbox{{\bf else} ({Predictor Step})}\\
\\
&\mbox{1. Set $s_{k}=c-A^{*}y_{k}$, $\hat{y}_{k}=y_{k}+d_{k}$, $\hat{s}_{k}=c-A^{*}\hat{y}_{k}$, and $\Delta y_{k}=t_{k}B_{k}b$.}\\
\\
&\mbox{2. Define function \framebox{$\xi_{k}(\alpha)=\zeta(\hat{y}_{k}+\alpha\Delta y_{k})+\zeta\left(\hat{y}_{k}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}\Delta y_{k}\right)-2\zeta(\hat{y}_{k})$}}\\
\\
&\mbox{3. Compute the step $\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)$ satisfying the equation $\xi_{k}(\alpha_{k})=A$.}\\
\\
&\mbox{4. Define $y_{k+1}=\hat{y}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\Delta y_{k}$ and $t_{k+1}={\nu\,\cdot\,t_{k}\over(1-\alpha_{k})\|\hat{s}_{k}\|^{2}_{s_{k}}}$.}\end{array}\\
\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

(7.13)

In this scheme, as compared with method (
6.3
), the only comments are needed for the Step c
4
). Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\hat{s}_{k},\hat{x}_{k}\rangle&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-Out})}}{{=}}&\langle\hat{s}_{k},\nabla^{2}F_{*}(w^{*}_{k})\hat{s}_{k}\rangle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{met-DBS})}}{{=}}\;{1\over t_{k}}\langle\hat{s}_{k},\nabla^{2}F_{*}(s_{k})\hat{s}_{k}\rangle\;=\;{1\over t_{k}}\|\hat{s}_{k}\|^{2}_{s_{k}}.\end{array}

The last equality in this chain can be used at Step c4) for computing
t_{k+1}
.
For termination criterion, we can verify the condition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\nu\over t_{k+1}}&\leq&\epsilon\end{array}

(7.14)

in the end of the predictor step.
If it is fulfilled, we generate the output
\hat{z}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\Delta z_{k}\in{\cal F}
by the rules of algorithm (
6.3
).

8
Easy Linear Matrix Inequalities

Let us demonstrate the main advantages of method (
7.13
) as applied to problems of Semidefinite Optimization. We start from the following simple result.

Lemma 15

Let matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be positive definite and matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
m<n
, has full row rank.
Then the self-concordant barrier

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}&-\ln\det(C-A^{T}D(y)A),\quad D(y)={\rm Diag\,}(y),\end{array}

(8.1)

has the following short representation

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&=&-\ln\det(G^{-1}-D(y))-\ln(\sigma\gamma),\end{array}

(8.2)

where
G=AC^{-1}A^{T}
,

\sigma=\det C
, and
\gamma=\det G
. For its domain

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\rm dom\,}\zeta(y)&=&\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\;D(y)\prec G^{-1}\},\end{array}

the parameter

\nu_{\zeta}
has the best possible value:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{\zeta}&=&m.\end{array}

(8.3)

Proof:

Indeed, for
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}G^{-1/2}AC^{-1/2}
, we have
UU^{T}=I_{m}
. Therefore

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\det(C-A^{T}D(y)A)&=&\sigma\det(I_{n}-C^{-1/2}A^{T}D(y)AC^{-1/2})\\
\\
&=&\sigma\det(I_{n}-U^{T}G^{1/2}D(y)G^{1/2}U)\;=\;\sigma\det(I_{m}-G^{1/2}D(y)G^{1/2})\\
\\
&=&\sigma\gamma\det[G^{-1}-D(y)].\end{array}

Since the barrier parameter of function
-\ln\det Y
,
Y=Y^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
, is equal to
m
, we get
\nu_{\zeta}\leq m
. Let us prove now the inverse inequality.

Denote
{\cal F}_{y}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\;G^{-1}-D(y)\succeq 0\}
.
For
\tau>0
, let us choose
\bar{y}=-\tau e
. Note that
p_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}-e_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, are recession directions of the feasible set
{\cal F}
. For each
i
, define the value
\beta_{i}
from the condition

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{y}-\beta_{i}p_{i}\;=\;-\tau e+\beta_{i}e_{i}&\not\in&{\rm int\,}{\cal F}.\end{array}

That is
G^{-1}+\tau I_{m}\not\succ\beta_{i}e_{i}e_{i}^{T}
, which gives us
\beta^{-1}_{i}=\langle(G^{-1}+\tau I_{m})^{-1}e_{i},e_{i}\rangle
.
On the other hand, for
\alpha_{i}=\alpha\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\lambda_{\min}(G^{-1}+\tau I_{m})
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}G^{-1}+\tau I_{m}&\succeq&\alpha I_{m}\;=\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}e_{i}e_{i}^{T}.\end{array}

This means that
\bar{y}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}p_{i}=-\tau e+\alpha e\in{\cal F}_{y}
. Therefore, in view of Theorem
1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{\zeta}&\geq&\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}{\alpha_{i}\over\beta_{i}}\;=\;\lambda_{\min}(G^{-1}+\tau I_{m})\langle(G^{-1}+\tau I_{m})^{-1},I_{m}\rangle\\
\\
&=&\lambda_{\min}(\tau^{-1}G^{-1}+I_{m})\langle(\tau^{-1}G^{-1}+I_{m})^{-1},I_{m}\rangle.\end{array}

Taking the limit in the right-hand side of this inequality as
\tau\to+\infty
, we get
\nu_{\zeta}\geq m
.

\Box

Note that the barrier parameter of function
\zeta(\cdot)
does not depend on its representation. Thus, any of representations (
8.1
) or (
8.2
) can be used for numerical computations. The short form (
8.2
) is computationally more preferable, but it needs some pre-processing.

The result of Lemma
15
can be easily extended in the following way.

Corollary 4

Let matrix
C\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be positive definite and the matrices
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, be symmetric. Then the barrier parameter of self-concordant function

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&=&-\ln\det\Big(C-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}y^{(i)}A_{i}\Big)\end{array}

satisfies the following bound:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{\zeta}&\leq&\min\Big\{n,\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\mbox{\rm rank}(A_{i})\Big\}.\end{array}

(8.4)

Clearly, the right-hand side of inequality (
8.4
) is minimal when the rank of all matrices
A_{i}
is equal to one. In any case, this
result is useful for treating problems of Semidefinite Optimization (
3.2
), (
3.3
)
with
low-rank
equality constraints, especially when
n\to\infty
. It opens an interesting possibility for constructing polynomial-time methods for some problems of
Semi-Infinite Optimization
.

It appears that for Semidefinite Optimization, the dual representation of the growth of functional proximity measure (
7.8
) admits further simplification. Indeed, in the case

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F_{*}(S)&=&-\ln\det S-\nu,\end{array}

we can represent
S=L_{n}L_{n}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
L
is a lower-triangular matrix. Denote

\begin{array}[]{rcl}B&=&L_{n}^{-1}\Delta SL_{n}^{-T}.\end{array}

Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\det(S+\alpha\Delta S)-\ln\det\left(S-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}\Delta S\right)+2\ln\det S\\
\\
&=&-\ln\det(I_{n}+\alpha B)-\ln\det\left(I_{n}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}B\right)\\
\\
&=&-\ln\det\left(I_{n}-{\alpha^{2}\over 1-\alpha}(B+B^{2})\right).\end{array}

(8.5)

Further, in
O(n^{3})
we can compute representation
B=U_{n}T_{n}U_{n}^{T}
, where
U_{n}
is a unitary matrix and matrix
T_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is three-diagonal. Then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\det(I_{n}+\alpha T_{n})-\ln\det\left(I_{n}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}T_{n}\right)\\
\\
&=&-\ln\det\left(I_{n}-{\alpha^{2}\over 1-\alpha}(T_{n}+T_{n}^{2})\right).\end{array}

(8.6)

Note that matrix
T_{n}+T_{n}^{2}
has five nonzero diagonals. Therefore, one computation of the value of function
\xi_{z}(\cdot)
by any representation in (
8.6
) needs
O(n)
arithmetic operations. Hence, the complexity of solving equation at Step c3) of method (
7.13
) by a bisection scheme is
O(n\ln\epsilon_{a}^{-1})
arithmetic operations, where
\epsilon_{a}
is the requried accuracy for satisfying the target equation.

A similar trick works for the low-rank constraints. Assume for simplicity that all equality constraints in the problem (
3.2
1
) have rank one:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}A_{i}&=&a_{i}a_{i}^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\quad i=1,\dots,m.\end{array}

(8.7)

Then, in accordance to Lemma
15
, we have the following representation of function
\xi_{z}(\cdot)
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\det(G^{-1}-\alpha\Delta Y)-\ln\det\left(G^{-1}+{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}\Delta Y\right)+2\ln\det G^{-1},\end{array}

where
\Delta Y={\rm Diag\,}(\Delta y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. Denoting
B_{m}=L^{T}_{m}\Delta YL_{m}
, where
L_{m}L_{m}^{T}=G
, we can rewrite it as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\det(I_{m}+\alpha B_{m})-\ln\det\left(I_{m}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}B_{m}\right).\end{array}

It remains to compute factorization
B_{m}=U_{m}T_{m}U_{m}^{T}
with a unitary matrix
U_{m}
and three-diagonal matrix
T_{m}
. Then, we get the following representations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\det(I_{m}+\alpha T_{m})-\ln\det\left(I_{m}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}T_{m}\right)\\
\\
&=&-\ln\det\left(I_{m}+{\alpha^{2}\over 1-\alpha}(T_{m}-T_{m}^{2})\right).\end{array}

(8.8)

Computation of the value of the right-hand side in the representations (
8.8
) needs
O(m)
operations.

Let us show that for SDO-problem (
3.2
) with low-rank constraints, the dual problem is indeed much simpler.

Example 6

Consider the following primal-dual SDO-problem:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\min\limits_{X\succeq 0}\{\langle I_{n},X\rangle:\;\langle Xa,a\rangle=1\}&=&\max\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\{y:\;I_{n}\succeq y\cdot aa^{T}\},\end{array}

(8.9)

where
X=X^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
\|a\|=1
. Note that the feasible set of the dual problem is unbounded. Hence, we cannot rely on Lemma
5
. However, in accordance to Lemma
15
, for solving the dual problem we can use the following barrier function:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&=&-\ln\det(I_{n}-yaa^{T})\;=\;-\ln(1-y)\end{array}

with
\nu_{\zeta}=1
. At the same time, we can prove that the value of parameter
\nu_{g}
of any self-concordant barrier
g(\cdot)
for the primal feasible set

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal F}_{p}&=&\{X\succeq 0:\;\langle Xa,a\rangle=1\}\end{array}

cannot be less than
n-1
. Indeed, without loss of generality, we can assume that
a=e_{1}
. Let us choose
\bar{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}I_{n}\in{\cal F}_{p}
.
It is clear that
p_{i}=e_{i}e_{i}^{T}
,
i=2,\dots,n
, are recession directions of the set
{\cal F}_{p}
. At the same time, for
\beta_{i}=1
,
i=2,\dots,n
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{X}-\beta_{i}p_{i}&=&I_{n}-e_{i}e_{i}^{T}\;\not\in\;{\rm int\,}{\cal F}_{p}.\end{array}

On the other hand, for
a_{i}=1
,
i=2,\dots,n
, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{X}-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}p_{i}&=&e_{1}e_{1}^{T}\;\in{\cal F}_{p}.\end{array}

Therefore, in view of Theorem
1
, we get

\nu_{g}\geq\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}{a_{i}\over b_{i}}\;=\;n-1
.
\Box

A similar effect can also occur in some problems of Linear Optimization.

Example 7

Let us choose dimension
n\geq 1
and vector
b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with coefficients

\begin{array}[]{rcl}b^{(1)}&=&1,\quad b^{(i)}\;\in\;(0,1),\quad i=2,\dots,n.\end{array}

Denote

a\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(b^{T},1,-b^{T})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
. Consider the following pair of primal-dual problems:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\min\limits_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}_{+}}\{\langle e,x\rangle:\;\langle a,x\rangle=1\}&=&\max\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\{y:\;e\geq y\cdot a\}.\end{array}

(8.10)

For the primal feasible set
{\cal F}_{p}=\{x\geq 0:\langle a,x\rangle=1\}
, we have the following recession directions:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}p_{i}&=&(e_{i}^{T},0,e_{i}^{T})^{T},\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

At the point
\bar{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}e\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
, we can see that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\bar{x}-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}&=&e_{n+1}\;\in\;{\cal F}_{p},\quad\bar{x}-p_{i}\;\not\in\;{\rm int\,}{\cal F}_{p},\quad i=1,\dots,n.\end{array}

Hence, by Theorem
1
, any self-concordant barrier
g(\cdot)
for set
{\cal F}_{p}
has the barrier parameter
\nu_{g}\geq n
. This bound does not depend on the coefficients of vector
b
.

At the same time, the standard logarithmic barrier of the primal cone
K=\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}_{+}
is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}F(x)&=&-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2n+1}\ln x^{(i)},\quad\nu_{F}\;=\;2n+1,\end{array}

with the dual barrier
F_{*}(s)=-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2n+1}\ln s^{(i)}-\nu_{F}
. Hence, the barrier function for the dual feasible set
{\cal F}_{d}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(-1,1)
is

\begin{array}[]{c}\zeta(y)\;=\;F_{*}(e-y\cdot a)\;=\;\zeta_{1}(y)+\zeta_{2}(y),\\
\\
\zeta_{1}(y)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;-\ln(1-y)-\ln(1-y^{2}),\quad\zeta_{2}(y)\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;-\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}\ln\left(1-(b^{(i)}y)^{2}\right).\end{array}

Note that
\zeta_{1}(\cdot)
is a self-concordant barrier for
{\cal F}_{d}
with parameter
\nu_{1}=2
and

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)&=&{2\over(1-y)^{2}}+{1\over(1+y)^{2}}\;\geq\;2,\quad y\in(-1,1).\end{array}

For the second barrier function, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|\zeta_{2}^{\prime}(y)|&=&2\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}{(b^{(i)})^{2}|y|\over 1-(b^{(i)}y)^{2}}\;\leq\;B\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\;2\sum\limits_{i=2}^{n}{(b^{(i)})^{2}\over 1-(b^{(i)})^{2}},\quad y\in{\cal F}_{d}.\end{array}

Hence,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\nu_{\zeta}&=&\sup\limits_{|y|<1}{(\zeta^{\prime}(y))^{2}\over\zeta^{\prime\prime}(y)}\;\leq\;2\sup\limits_{|y|<1}{(\zeta_{1}^{\prime}(y))^{2}+(\zeta_{2}^{\prime}(y))^{2}\over\zeta_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)+\zeta^{\prime\prime}_{2}(y)}\;\leq\;2\left(2+\sup\limits_{|y|<1}{(\zeta_{2}^{\prime}(y))^{2}\over\zeta_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)}\right)\;\leq\;2\left(2+\mbox{${1\over 2}$}B^{2}\right).\end{array}

Thus, choosing the coefficients of vector
b
small enough, we can have
B\to 0
. Consequently, the barrier parameter
\nu_{\zeta}
will be as close to four as we wish. Again, this is a situation when the parameters of self-concordant barriers for the primal and the dual feasible sets are fundamentally different.

\Box

1.
For

Therefore,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-OGrow}_{2})}}{{=}}&-\sum\limits_{i=1}^{m}\Big[\ln(s^{(i)}+\alpha\Delta s^{(i)})+\ln\left(s^{(i)}-{\alpha\over 1-\alpha}\Delta s^{(i)}\right)-2\ln s^{(i)}\Big]\\

Thus, for
a_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}\delta_{1}+2\delta_{1}^{2}-\delta_{2}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\xi_{z}(\alpha)&=&-\ln\left(1-{2\alpha^{2}\over 1-\alpha}a_{1}+\left({\alpha^{2}\over 1-\alpha}\right)^{2}a_{2}\right).\end{array}

(8.13)

As we have seen in our examples (

Numerical experiments

For numerical experiments, we select a nonsmooth optimization problem in the space of symmetric matrices, which we call the
Low-Rank Quadratic Interpolation
:

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{X=X^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\Big\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|\lambda_{i}(X)|:\;\langle Xa_{i},a_{i}\rangle=b_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,m\Big\}.\end{array}

(9.1)

In this formulation, the right-hand sides of the equality constraints can be of any sign. We assume that the matrix
A^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}(a_{1},\dots,a_{m})
has full column rank. Thus,
G\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}AA^{T}\succ 0
.

This problem admits the following SDO-formulation:

\begin{array}[]{c}\min\limits_{X_{1},X_{2}\succeq 0}\Big\{\langle I_{n},X_{1}+X_{2}\rangle:\;\langle(X_{1}-X_{2})a_{i},a_{i}\rangle=b_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,m\Big\}.\end{array}

(9.2)

The dual problem to (
9.2
) is as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}f_{*}&=&\max\limits_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\Big\{\langle b,y\rangle:\;-I_{n}\preceq A^{T}D(y)A\preceq I_{n}\Big\},\end{array}

(9.3)

where
D(y)={\rm Diag\,}(y)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
. Thus, we can choose the following dual barrier function

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(y)&=&-\ln\det(I_{n}-A^{T}D(y)A)-\ln\det(I_{n}+A^{T}D(y)A)\\
\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-RepZ})}}{{=}}&-\ln\det(G^{-1}-D(y))-\ln\det(G^{-1}+D(y))-2\ln\sigma,\end{array}

(9.4)

where
\sigma=\det G
. Since the feasible set of problem (
9.3
) is bounded, the Hessian
\nabla^{2}\zeta(\cdot)
is positive definite at any feasible point.
Note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\zeta(0)&=&0,\quad\nabla\zeta(0)\;=\;0.\end{array}

Therefore, for method (
7.13
) we choose
y_{0}=0
.

In accordance to Lemma
5
,
the parameter of barrier function for the primal feasible set is big:
\nu_{g}=2n
.
Let us show that
\nu_{\zeta}=m
. This is a consequence of the following result.

Lemma 16

The barrier
F(Y)=-\ln\det(I_{m}-Y)-\ln\det(I_{m}+Y)
for the matrix box

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\cal B}_{m}&=&\{Y=Y^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}:\;-I_{m}\preceq Y\preceq I_{m}\}\end{array}

(9.5)

has parameter
\nu_{F}=m
.

Proof:

Let us compute the first derivative of
F(\cdot)
at some feasible
Y
along direction
H
, which is a symmetric
m\times m
-matrix:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla F(Y),H\rangle&=&\langle(I_{m}-Y)^{-1}-(I_{m}+Y)^{-1},H\rangle\\
\\
&=&2\langle(I_{m}-Y)^{-1}Y(I_{m}+Y)^{-1},H\rangle\;=\;\langle D\left({2\lambda\over 1-\lambda^{2}}\right),\hat{H}\rangle,\end{array}

where we represent
Y=UD(\lambda)U^{T}
by eigenvalue decomposition and denote
\hat{H}=U^{T}HU
. Similarly, for the second derivative we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla^{2}F(Y)H,H\rangle&=&\langle(I_{m}-Y)^{-1}H(I_{m}-Y)^{-1}+(I_{m}+Y)^{-1}H(I_{m}+Y)^{-1},H\rangle\\
\\
&=&\langle D\left({1\over 1-\lambda}\right)\hat{H}D\left({1\over 1-\lambda}\right),\hat{H}\rangle+\langle D\left({1\over 1+\lambda}\right)\hat{H}D\left({1\over 1+\lambda}\right),\hat{H}\rangle\\
\\
&=&\langle\tilde{H}{1\over 1-\lambda},{1\over 1-\lambda}\rangle+\langle\tilde{H}{1\over 1+\lambda},{1\over 1+\lambda}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle d^{2},{2(1+\lambda^{2})\over(1-\lambda^{2})^{2}}\rangle\;\geq\;\langle d^{2},\left({2\lambda\over 1-\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}\rangle,\end{array}

where the matrix
\tilde{H}
has elements
\tilde{H}_{i,j}=\hat{H}_{i,j}^{2}\geq 0
and
d={\rm diag\,}(\hat{H})
. Hence, by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\langle\nabla^{2}F(Y)H,H\rangle&\geq&\langle d^{2},\left({2\lambda\over 1-\lambda^{2}}\right)^{2}\rangle\;\geq{1\over m}\langle|d|,{2\lambda\over 1-\lambda^{2}}\rangle^{2}\;\geq\;{1\over m}\langle\nabla F(Y),H\rangle^{2}.\end{array}

This means that
\nu_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eq-SCB})}}{{=}}m

10
Conclusion

Let us summarize the main results of the paper.

1.
We proposed a new methodology for solving primal-dual problems of Conic Optimization by predictor-corrector schemes. Instead of finding at the Corrector Stage a primal-dual pair in a close neighborhood of the primal-dual central path, we run the corrector process only in the dual space. We use the obtained approximation of the dual path as a scaling point for a new feasible primal-dual pair, which can be easily generated by the Dual Gambit Rule (
4.8
).
After that, we perform a predictor step using the standard affine-scaling direction with the step size defined by
Functional Proximity Measure
. The computational complexity of this technique is very attractive even for SDO-problems, where we need to apply only the standard Cholesky factorizations.

2.
One of the main motivations for our approach is the observation that in many situations the complexity of primal and dual problems are different. For symmetric cones, our method is the first one with the complexity bounds dependent on the minimal value between the barrier parameters for the primal and the dual feasible sets. We prove that the standard assumption on boundedness of the dual feasible set necessarily results in the maximal value of barrier parameter for the primal feasible set. At the same time, as we show by examples, the barrier parameter for the dual feasible set can be much smaller. The automatic switching to the best value of the barrier parameter is a very desirable property. In our case, it is ensured by the use of functional proximity measure.

3.
We discuss applications of this technique to several problem of Semidefinite Optimization. It appears that computational complexity of some of them is similar to that of the problems of Linear Optimization with the comparable dimension.

4.
For our approach, we present the results of preliminary testing on randomly generated problems of Low-Rank Quadratic Interpolation. They are quite promising since the number of predictor steps is small and we observe in the end a local quadratic convergence.

It will be very interesting to check the performance of the proposed approach on other structural problems of Conic Optimization. One of the most important open questions is the possibility of having asymmetric complexity bounds for other cones and other primal-dual proximity measures. The second part of this question is open even for LO.
