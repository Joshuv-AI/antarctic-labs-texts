The dual benefits of professional values on income and health among healthcare workers in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yang Lu, Xiumei Tang, Meiru Chen, Shengxin Pei, Ke Ju, Tao Gu, Weimin Li, Ma Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChina’s healthcare system faces critical challenges with medical staff experiencing dual pressures of insufficient income and poor health status. Despite extensive research on professional values guiding ethical practice in healthcare, their economic benefits and impact on healthcare workers’ self-rated health remain significantly underexplored.ObjectivesTo examine how professional values influence income and health outcomes among Chinese healthcare workers, addressing potential endogeneity through instrumental variable approaches and exploring heterogeneity across different demographic and professional subgroups.MethodsA multi-center survey was conducted from October 2022 to March 2023 across 25 regional secondary and tertiary healthcare hospitals in China, involving 4,267 healthcare workers. An ordinal logit model was used for baseline analysis, with two-stage least-squares instrumental variable regression addressing potential endogeneity.Results.Professional values significantly increased both income (β=1.349, p<0.01) and health status (β=4.939, p<0.01). Each unit increase in professional values increased the probability of high income by 19.0% and healthy status by 77.5%, while decreasing the probability of lower income and health categories. Instrumental variable analysis confirmed these relationships were causal rather than reverse caused. Heterogeneity analysis revealed stronger effects for men versus women, highly educated versus less educated, and permanent versus contract employees. Among professional value dimensions, altruistic values showed the strongest association with both income (β=0.075) and health (β=0.248).ConclusionsProfessional values significantly influence both economic wellbeing and health status of healthcare workers in China, with differential effects across value dimensions, demographic characteristics, and professional roles.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13658-w.Keywords: Professional value, Healthcare workers, Health, Income, China

Introduction

China’s healthcare system is at a critical stage of deepening reform, and improving the motivation and well-being of medical staff is a core task in promoting the high-quality development of healthcare services [1]. However, real-world data show that the medical staff group faces the dual challenges of income and health: their average salary in 2022 (135,000 RMB) [2] is significantly lower than the average level of health industry (data from the National Bureau of Statistics), and only 50.99% of medical staff self-assess their health, which is lower than the national self-assessment rate of adult health (57.6%) [3]. A national survey found that 16.6% of Chinese healthcare workers reported symptoms of burnout, while 29.27% reported poor self-rated health [4, 5]. This contradiction highlights the urgency of optimizing medical human resources management and improving the professional environment for medical staff. As healthcare organizations face mounting challenges related to workforce retention, burnout, and service quality, understanding the factors that influence healthcare professionals’ well-being and economic status has become increasingly important [6].

Values are one of the essential components of ethical systems, which assist medical staff in adhering to ethical standards, sustaining moral obligation, and handling ethical dilemmas [7]. Professional values are work norms acceptable to professionals and practitioners, referring to the judgments about the value and quality of concepts and behaviors in a particular discipline [8]. Medicine is a unique profession that combines science, art, and humanities, involving many important values, such as self-motivation, commitment to teaching, responsibility, accountability, transparency, honesty, and discipline [9]. The conceptualization of professional values has evolved from unidimensional constructs to more nuanced, multifaceted frameworks. An interprofessional analysis of seven Australian health professions’ competency frameworks identified two core values: the rights of the client and the capacity of a particular profession to serve healthcare needs [10]. This suggests that these professions share a common value base despite differences in the scope of practice. A review of healthcare practitioners’ personal and professional values integrated the identified values into a single framework using Schwartz’s values model (from 50 included articles on doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals) and identified 11 values, including altruism, equality, and capability, as being the most prominent [11]. Professional values in healthcare have been extensively studied across various cultural and organizational contexts. The Professional Values Model has been described in nursing, emphasizing the importance of knowledge accumulation for professionalism [12]. This model has the potential to guarantee ethical professional practice, and its concepts are believed to enhance patient and nurse satisfaction, as well as the quality of care [13]. Another work established that professional values are standards for action accepted by practitioners and professional groups [14]. These values shape individual behavior and influence collective professional identity and institutional culture. The cross-cultural evidence presented by Rodríguez-Gázquez et al. [15] comparing Spanish and Colombian nursing students demonstrates that while professional values may have cultural nuances, core principles such as human dignity, integrity, and altruism transcend geographical boundaries. This universality underscores the fundamental role these values play in establishing global standards for healthcare delivery.

Professional values are defined as “enduring beliefs and standards regarding the worth and importance of specific behaviors, goals, and outcomes within a particular professional domain that guide practitioners’ decisions, actions, and interactions in their work” [12, 16, 17]. This definition encompasses three essential components: (i) Cognitive component: Beliefs about what is important, worthwhile, and desirable in professional practice; (ii) Evaluative component: Judgments about the relative priority and significance of different professional goals and behaviors; and (iii) Behavioral component: Guiding principles that shape actual conduct in clinical and organizational contexts [18–20]. Professional values can be the cornerstone of healthcare practice, guiding healthcare workers’ behaviors, decisions, and interactions with patients and colleagues [21]. Based on extensive research across multiple healthcare contexts, the significance of professional values cannot be overstated, particularly in nursing and medical practice, where they directly impact patient care quality and outcomes [12, 16]. Professional values shape nurses’ clinical practice and significantly influence their job satisfaction, retention, and overall professional identity [12, 22, 23]. These values function as internal compasses that guide healthcare professionals through complex ethical dilemmas and challenging clinical scenarios. As healthcare systems face increasing pressures from technological advancements, demographic shifts, and resource constraints, the importance of well-established professional values becomes even more pronounced [24]. Cao et al. [25] found that professional values can help shape development during formative education years among medical students, particularly in vocational settings. These values often determine future practice patterns and ethical frameworks. Moreover, Tuna et al. [16] established a direct correlation between nurses’ professional values and attitudes toward caregiving roles, with stronger professional values associated with more positive caregiving approaches. This connection between values and practice has profound implications for patient experiences and outcomes. A survey of doctors’ perception of professional values revealed that these values influence everything from communication styles to treatment decisions and interprofessional collaboration [22, 26]. When healthcare professionals operate from a strong foundation of professional values, they demonstrate greater resilience in the face of workplace challenges and moral distress examinations [8]. Beyond individual practice, professional values contribute significantly to organizational culture and system-level outcomes. Institutions with healthcare professionals who strongly adhere to professional values typically demonstrate higher quality metrics, better patient satisfaction scores, and improved staff retention rates [22, 27].

Despite the research exploring the multifaceted effects of professional value and its benefits, few have considered the economic benefits. The relationship between professional values and income among healthcare workers has not been explored in detail [28, 29]. Economic theory suggests aligning personal and organizational values could enhance productivity and earnings potential [30, 31]. However, the mechanisms linking professional values to income are complex and potentially bidirectional. On one hand, strong professional values may enhance performance, leading to greater recognition and financial rewards; on the other hand, higher income may facilitate the expression of professional values by reducing financial stress and creating opportunities for professional development [32]. This potential endogeneity presents methodological challenges that must be addressed through appropriate analytical strategies. Moreover, the relationship between professional values and health outcomes represents another critical area of inquiry.

Theoretical frameworks such as the Job Demands-Resources model suggest that alignment between personal values and work experiences can serve as a psychological resource, buffering against occupational stressors and promoting well-being [33]. Empirical evidence found that healthcare workers reporting strong professional values demonstrated better professional quality of life [34], illustrating its potential in enhancing the health of healthcare workers. However, no previous study has measured self-rated health as the outcome of professional values. The present study addresses these gaps by examining how professional values influence income and health outcomes among Chinese healthcare workers through a theoretically grounded, methodologically rigorous approach. To address these gaps and provide theoretical grounding for our investigation, we draw on two complementary frameworks: Person-Environment (P-E) fit theory [35] and the Job Demands-Resources (JDR) model [36].

We propose that professional values serve as both motivational drivers of performance (leading to income benefits) and psychological resources that buffer against occupational stressors (leading to health benefits). We test these propositions using a large, multi-center sample and employ instrumental variable approaches to address potential endogeneity. Furthermore, we examine heterogeneity across value dimensions, demographic characteristics, and professional roles to identify contexts where professional values exert the strongest effects. This comprehensive approach enables us to move beyond descriptive associations toward causal inference and nuanced understanding of when and how professional values matter for healthcare worker wellbeing.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection procedures

The survey was conducted from October 2022 to March 2023 and a multi-center purposive sampling across 25 regional secondary and tertiary healthcare hospitals representing diverse geographic regions and hospital characteristics. A nationally representative cohort of 4,267 Chinese health workers was selected. First, institutional approval obtained from each hospital’s administration; then, Email invitations were sent to the health workers’ email addresses; and invitation included study information sheet, ethical approval documentation, and survey link. Ethics approval was obtained by West China Hospital Ethics Committee (No. 2,023,822). Health workers included: (i) physicians, nurses, medical technology professionals, administrators, clinical pharmacists, and personal support workers. Before accessing the survey questionnaires, written informed consent was provided, and they were assured of their anonymity, informed that participation was voluntary, and had the option to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence. After consent was given, participants were given access to the questionnaire, which was designed to take approximately 15 minutes to complete, based on a pilot trial with healthcare workers who were not involved in the main study. A total of 4,267 valid responses obtained from 5,120 invited participants (response rate: 83.3%). The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows: inclusion criteria are (i) currently employed healthcare workers (physicians, nurses, medical technicians, administrators, clinical pharmacists, personal support workers); (ii) minimum 6 months tenure at current institution; and (iii) voluntary consent to participate. Exclusions are (i) incomplete questionnaires ( < 95% completion); (ii) failed attention checks; and (iii) logical inconsistencies in responses.

Theoretical framework and hypotheses

We have expanded our theoretical foundation by explicitly integrating P-E Fit Theory and the JDR Model to explain the mechanisms linking professional values to income and health outcomes. P-E Fit Theory posits that congruence between individual values and organizational/occupational characteristics serves as a critical determinant of work-related outcomes [35]. In our context, healthcare workers with stronger professional values experience greater value-congruence with their occupational roles, which enhances psychological resilience, reduces emotional exhaustion, and promotes sustained job performance. This alignment mechanism explains why professional values translate into both better health outcomes and higher income through improved performance recognition. The JDR Model suggests that personal resources, including value alignment, buffer against occupational stressors and promote well-being [36]. In the demanding Chinese healthcare environment characterized by high workloads and unstable extrinsic rewards, intrinsic value alignment serves as a critical protective factor against burnout and health deterioration, while simultaneously motivating behaviors that lead to career advancement and income growth.

Mechanism 1: Professional Values → Income: P-E fit theory suggests that healthcare workers with stronger professional values experience greater congruence between personal values and occupational demands, leading to: Enhanced intrinsic motivation and work engagement; Superior clinical performance and patient outcomes; Greater recognition from supervisors and patients; Increased opportunities for promotion and financial rewards.

Mechanism 2: Professional Values → Health: The JDR model posits that personal resources (including value alignment) buffer against job demands and prevent burnout through: Psychological resilience against occupational stressors; Enhanced sense of meaning and purpose in work; Reduced emotional exhaustion and depersonalization; Better work-life integration and self-care behaviors.

Mechanism 3: Dimensional Heterogeneity: Different dimensions of professional values may operate through distinct pathways: Altruistic values directly enhance patient care quality and professional reputation; Intrinsic values sustain long-term engagement despite external challenges; Extrinsic values align with organizational reward structures; Social values facilitate interprofessional collaboration and institutional support; Leisure values promote work-life balance and prevent burnout.

Based on this theoretical framework, we test the following hypotheses:

H1

Professional values are positively associated with income levels among Chinese healthcare workers, after controlling for demographic and professional characteristics.

H2

Professional values are positively associated with self-rated health status among Chinese healthcare workers, after controlling for demographic and professional characteristics.

H3

The relationships between professional values and both income (H1) and health (H2) remain significant when addressing potential endogeneity through instrumental variable approaches, supporting causal rather than spurious associations.

H4a

Altruistic values demonstrate the strongest associations with both income and health outcomes, given their centrality to healthcare professional identity.

H4b

Different value dimensions exhibit differential effects on income versus health outcomes, reflecting distinct underlying mechanisms.

H5a

The effect of professional values on income is stronger for men than women, reflecting potential gender disparities in how professional values are recognized and rewarded.

H5b

The effect of professional values on income is stronger for highly educated healthcare workers, as education facilitates the translation of values into performance and advancement.

H5c

The effect of professional values on both income and health is stronger for permanent versus contract employees, given greater institutional integration and job security.

H5d

The effect of professional values varies across professional roles (physicians, nurses, paramedics, administrators), reflecting different occupational demands and reward structures.

Measures and instruments

Dependent variables (income and health)

Self-Rated Health Status (SRH): The explanatory variables in this paper are health status and income of health workers, respectively. (i) Health status was measured using the Self-Rated Health Status (SRH) scale [3, 28]. The SRH is a widely used health assessment method due to its simplicity, affordability, and self-reported nature. The question “How would you assess your current health status in general?” was used; this method is particularly valuable in large-scale population surveys and can serve as a valid tool for individuals’ health perceptions [37]. The SRH strongly correlates with various biomarkers, providing a comprehensive view of an individual’s well-being, reflecting physical and mental health status, and is a reliable predictor of mortality [38, 39]. SRH can discern subtle physical changes that may not be identified by conventional symptom-based methods, underscoring its significance in comprehending and anticipating health outcomes through the interaction of social and biological mechanisms [40, 41]. In this study, a 5-point scale was utilized: The scale ranges from 1 to 5, with one representing “very unhealthy”, 2 “unhealthy”, 3 “average”, 4 “healthy”, and 5 “very healthy”. This measure has been extensively validated in Chinese populations, including the national health survey, supporting its appropriateness for our study context.

Income measurement: monthly income was assessed as an ordinal variable with five categories: (1) low income, (2) low-to-middle income, (3) middle income, (4) middle-to-high income, and (5) high income. Categories were defined based on regional healthcare sector salary distributions to ensure meaningful differentiation across income levels.

Independent variable (professional values)

Professional Values Scale: The core explanatory variable of this study is the professional values and were measured by using the reliability-tested Professional Values Scale for Medical Staff, an instrument consisting of a 34-item, five-point Richter scale, with a scale from 1 to 5 representing the respondent’s level of agreement (1 for “strongly disagree” to 5 for “strongly agree”) [42]. The 5 main dimensions included: (i) Intrinsic Values (items measuring internal motivation and professional fulfillment); (ii) Extrinsic Values (items assessing external rewards and recognition); (iii) Social Values (items evaluating community contribution and social impact); (iv) Altruistic Values (items measuring patient-centered care and helping orientation); (v) Leisure Values (items assessing work-life balance preferences). The Cronbach’s α coefficients ranged from 0.72 to 0.89 across subscales, the confirmatory factor analysis supported the five-factor structure (CFI = 0.92, RMSEA = 0.06), and the convergent validity established through correlations with job satisfaction (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) and organizational commitment (r = 0.52, p < 0.01). In our sample of 4,267 healthcare workers, the scale demonstrated excellent psychometric properties: the overall Cronbach’s α = 0.91; the Subscale reliability coefficients: Intrinsic (α = 0.85), Extrinsic (α = 0.82), Social (α = 0.79), Altruistic (α = 0.88), Leisure (α = 0.76); and Multicollinearity assessment: Mean VIF = 1.41 (all values < 5.0), confirming no problematic collinearity.

Entropy weight method for composite scoring

We adopt the entropy value method for the measurement (detailed process can be seen in supplementary files). Entropy is a measure of the degree of disorder in a system. In the entropy method, if the data of an indicator is highly discrete (significant differences), its entropy value is small, indicating that the indicator provides a large amount of information and should be given a higher weight; on the contrary, if the data is low discrete (convergence), the entropy value is significant, and the weight should be lower [43]. The entropy method determines the weights and importance by quantifying the information entropy of the indicators, which effectively overcomes the supervisor bias of expert assignment. It has been widely used in many disciplines (including environmental assessment [44], financial analysis [45], and supply chain management [46]. Data processing went through three stages: standardization, calculation of indicator weights, and synthesis of composite indices. First, to eliminate the shielding effect and the difference in scale between the data, the secondary indicators were standardized; second, the weights of the indicators were calculated; and finally, the weights of the indicators were summed to obtain a quantitatively comparable index of professional values of medical personnel. After using the entropy method, the composite score ranges from 0 to 1, with a mean value of 0.617, indicating a moderately high overall performance of the sample and relatively small between-individual differences. Intrinsic and Extrinsic Values results are relatively stable (Fig. 1). Social Values fluctuate wildly, but the degree of dispersion is only 19.1% (relatively low), with a mean value of 35.70, close to the median value of 30. The results of Altruistic Values are close to the median value of 9, with a mean value of 11.54 and a SD of 2.15, but account for only 17.9% of the range (2.15/12), which suggests that the scores have a strong convergent neutral characteristic. Leisure values have a strong tendency towards neutral traits, with a mean value of 24.90 and an SD of 6.11; their distribution shows right-skewed characteristics, reflecting that most respondents recognized the convenience and relaxation of medical work (Fig. 2).

Income model

The coefficient of the principal regression on income is 1.349, which is significant at the 1% level. This indicates that the higher the professional values, the greater the likelihood healthcare workers will earn higher incomes. Further, the paper estimates marginal effects. Columns (2) to (4) of Table 2 show that the marginal effects of professional values on ‘low income’, ‘low to middle income’ and ‘middle income’, which suggests that the probability of being in these lower income categories decreases by 3.4%, 11.0%, and 8.1%, respectively, for each 1-unit increase in professional value. Meanwhile, columns (5) to (6) of Table 2 show that the probability of being in the “middle to high” and “high” income increased by 3.5% and 19.0%, respectively, for each 1-unit increase in professional value. Professional value significantly increases the probability of high income and decreases the probability of low income. Increased professional values considerably reduce the likelihood of healthcare workers being in the low- and middle-income brackets, while increasing their likelihood of being in the upper-middle- and high-income brackets. The Pseudo R2 of 0.197, indicating that the model explains about 19.7% of the variation in the dependent variable (Fig. 3).

Health model

The coefficient of the principal regression on health is 4.939, which is significant at the 1% level. This indicates that the higher the professional values, the greater the likelihood of health workers having better health. Columns (2) to (4) (very unhealthy, unhealthy, average) of Table 3 are negative (−0.062, −0.425, −0.564), implying that the probability of being in these health categories decreases by 6.2%, 42.5%, and 56.4%, respectively, for each 1-unit increase in professional value. In contrast, columns (5) to (6) (healthy, very healthy) of Table 3 are positive (0.775, 0.276), implying an increase in the probability of being in the healthy and very healthy categories by 77.5% and 27.6%, respectively. This suggests that increased professional values significantly reduce the likelihood that healthcare workers will be in poor health, while substantially increasing the likelihood that they will have good health. Pseudo R2 of 0.082, indicating that the model explains about 8.2% of the variation in the dependent variable (Fig. 4).

Robust test and endogeneity solving

Robust test

Endogeneity solving

Moreover, a key methodological challenge in estimating the effect of professional values is the potential for endogeneity due to reverse causality and omitted variable bias. Therefore, we refer to the existing literature and adopt the instrumental variable (IV) method to test endogeneity [47], selecting the average level of professional values of medical staff from the same level and the same type of hospitals other than the individual’s own as the IV. For each individual it is the average professional value score of all other healthcare workers in the same hospital level and type, excluding themselves. Since there is a cohort effect in the professional values of medical staff, and the level of professional values of medical workers from other hospitals does not directly affect their income and health, the requirements of exclusivity and exogeneity are met. This peer-based instrument has been used in similar contexts to identify causal effects in similar settings [48].

Specifically, this paper uses a IV two-stage least-squares estimator (IV-2SLS) for regression analysis [49]. The results are presented in columns (5) to (8) of Table 4. Columns (5) and (7) report that the estimated coefficients of the first stage IV with income and health are significant at the 1% level, suggesting that the IV has good explanatory power for the endogenous variables. The Wald F-statistic of 36.302 and 33.237, respectively, are greater than 10, indicating that the IV satisfies the conditions of relevance and that the problem of weakly instrumented variables does not exist [50]. Columns (6) and (8) of Table 4 report the second-stage regression results, where the coefficient of the explanatory variable, professional values, remains positive and is significantly at the 1% level, reflecting that after controlling for the endogeneity problem, an increase in professional values promotes income and health, further confirming the baseline regression results and ruling out the possibility of reverse causality, which further strengthens the conclusions of this paper.

Discussion

Key finding

Our findings reveal that professional values significantly influence income and health outcomes among Chinese healthcare workers, with substantial implications for healthcare policy and management. The positive association between professional values and these outcomes aligns with theoretical frameworks suggesting that value congruence is a psychological resource within healthcare settings. However, our study extends beyond previous research by demonstrating quantifiable economic benefits of professional values and addressing methodological challenges through instrumental variable approaches.

The strong positive relationship between professional values and income (coefficient = 1.349, p < 0.01) provides empirical evidence for what has previously been theorized in organizational economics literature. As stated before, alignment between personal and organizational values enhances productivity and potentially earnings [30]. Our findings confirm this theoretical proposition within the healthcare context, where the relationship between values and economic outcomes has been underexplored. The marginal effects analysis further reveals that professional values increase the probability of higher income brackets (19.0% for high income) and decrease the likelihood of lower income categories (11.0% for low-to-middle income).

Similarly, the substantial impact of professional values on self-rated health (coefficient = 4.939, p < 0.01) extends previous research that has primarily focused on professional quality of life rather than direct health outcomes [23, 34]. The magnitude of this effect increasing the probability of being in the “healthy” category by 77.5% per unit increase in professional values, suggests that professional values may serve as a more powerful protective factor for healthcare workers’ wellbeing than previously recognized.

Drawing on P-E fit theory, our findings suggest that healthcare workers with stronger professional values may experience greater value-congruence with their occupational roles. This alignment can enhance psychological resilience, reduce emotional exhaustion, and promote sustained job performance, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes and higher income. The perceived value fit is a key driver of well-being and organizational commitment [51]. In the Chinese healthcare context, where extrinsic rewards may be unstable and workloads high, intrinsic value alignment may serve as a critical buffer against burnout.

Differential effects of professional value dimensions

Our heterogeneous analysis reveals essential nuances in how different aspects of professional values contribute to health and outcomes. In general, the health promotion effects are better than the economic promotion effects of professional value among healthcare workers.

Altruistic values demonstrated the strongest effects in both income (coefficient = 0.075) and health (coefficient = 0.248), suggesting that healthcare work’s core humanitarian aspects may yield intrinsic and extrinsic rewards. This finding aligns with Moyo et al.’s identification of altruism as a prominent value across healthcare professions but extends their work by demonstrating its economic and health implications [11]. Altruism is a fundamental component of the professional ethos of healthcare workers, refers to a basic ethical value, holding that medical workers should put the patient before all other interests [52]. Previous studies found that patients of altruistic doctors had dramatically lower rates of hospitalization and emergency room visits, and lower medical expenses (the patients’ annual medical payments were nearly 10% lower on average, 38% fewer potentially preventable hospitalizations, 41% fewer potentially preventable emergency room visits) [53]. Our study for the first time proved that healthcare workers with higher level of altruistic can improve their economic reward. Studies that linking preference estimates to occupational survey measures and yielding that altruistic medical students have lower income expectations and are more likely to choose surgery and pediatrics as their preferred specialty [54, 55]. Our study is different from theirs, and possible reasons are that the different systematic background in China, healthcare workers with higher level altruism are more likely to get the chance of promotion, possible influence their main income but not the specialties they choose. Another possible explanation is that a higher altruism level was associated with a better quality of care, thereby improve the income. Besides, our study also confirms the health benefits of altruistic. There have been alarming reports of high rates of burnout among healthcare workers and worrisome health status [56]. Our results are similar to previous studies which found that altruism is beneficial to health [57]. The health promotion benefits might because altruism leads to an individual sense of well-being and enjoyment resulting from work and therefore lead to better longevity and health. It is hypothesized that healthcare workers who exhibit a low level of altruism may face quiet a few challenges in their professional lives, including reduced support from their colleagues, limited opportunities for career advancement, less equitable remuneration, and a diminished sense of status, influence, and autonomy in the workplace. Though observed benefits, studies find that altruism tends to exhibit a U-shaped pattern: medical students tend to be very altruistic, but this gradually fades during the pre-clinical and clinical stages of their education, then improves slightly in the practical year [52]. Therefore, effective measures should be taken to maintain the level of altruism among healthcare workers.

Moreover, we also found the weakest effect of leisure values on income (coefficient = 0.020) highlighting the potential tension between work-life balance and financial advancement in healthcare settings. This finding contributes to the growing literature on work-life integration in healthcare by suggesting that leisure values may not translate directly into economic benefits. However, social values exhibit the lowest health promotion effects in our study, suggesting that it may not be as effectively integrated as other value dimensions. Possible reasons are the specific pressures within the Chinese healthcare system. The ongoing healthcare reforms in China have created significant workload pressures and structural changes that particularly affect the social dimensions of healthcare work. High patient volumes and administrative burdens may leave limited opportunities for healthcare workers to engage in the community-building and interprofessional collaboration activities that would typically reinforce social values and generate associated health benefits [58].

Heterogeneity in professional values’ impact

The heterogeneity analysis reveals important differences in how professional values influence outcomes across demographic and professional subgroups. The gender disparity in the effect of professional values on income (coefficient = 1.845 for men vs. 1.233 for women) points to potential structural inequalities in how professional values are recognized and rewarded. This finding adds a new dimension to discussions of gender disparities in healthcare by suggesting that even when women embody professional values but receive lower economic returns for doing so [59]. This disparity was also observed in health (coefficient = 5.559 for men vs. 4.745 for women). Many researchers have explored the reasons: first, women healthcare workers often experience different stressors and employ different coping strategies compared to their male counterparts, which may moderate the protective effects of professional values on health. A national survey reveal that female physicians had higher levels of work-family conflict and emotional exhaustion than male physicians [60]. This additional strain may diminish the potential health benefits women derive from their professional values. Furthermore, research has shown that women professions tend to internalize stress more than men, potentially reducing the buffering effect that professional values might otherwise provide against health deterioration [61]. Structural gender inequities that affect how professional values translate into health benefits across genders was considered [62]. When women’s expressions of professional values receive less institutional validation and recognition, the positive health effects of these values may be attenuated.

We divided into the sample into two groups based on their working years (whether have 10 years of working experience or not) as junior group and senior group. Professional values were found to have a positive effect on income and health in both junior and senior healthcare workers, and the differences between the two groups were small and not significant. This suggests that the combined effect of professional values on healthcare workers is stable and does not change significantly depending on the number of years worked. Regardless of the stage of career development, positive professional values can enhance income and health to a certain extent.

The substantially higher income effect for highly educated workers (coefficient = 3.150) compared to those with lower education levels (coefficient = 1.121) suggests that education may serve as a crucial moderator in translating professional values into economic benefits. This interaction between education and professional values has not been extensively explored in previous literature and represents an important contribution to understanding career development in healthcare. Human capital theory suggests that highly educated healthcare workers typically possess more human capital, including specialized knowledge and skills, and that these strengths contribute to a better translation of professional values into tangible financial gains and health rewards. In addition, less-educated healthcare workers may be more likely to be engaged in manual labor or primary care jobs, which are more intensive and have relatively higher health attrition. However, the difference was not statistically significant regarding the health benefits.

In China, the type of employment is an important way to manage personnel in hospitals and other public institutions. Permanent healthcare workers have relatively stable work and benefits. The results show that professional values have a significant positive effect on the income and health of both permanent and non- permanent medical workers, with a greater effect on the income and health of permanent employees. Possible explanations are that they have policy support and financial security, including stable salaries, benefits, and training opportunities, which motivate them to chase opportunities for professional skill enhancement and career development, and this stability in career development may motivate them to take professional values into clinical practice, thus resulting in greater enhancement in income and health approaches.

The differential effects across professional roles are particularly noteworthy. The stronger impact of professional values on income among doctors (coefficient = 1.906) and nurses (coefficient = 1.968) compared to paramedics (coefficient = 1.371) and administrators (coefficient = 1.331) suggests that professional values may be more directly rewarded in clinical roles with direct patient contact. This suggests that the economic recognition of this value may vary by profession, highlighting the importance to enhance the professional value of clinic workers. Conversely, the stronger association between professional values and health among paramedics (coefficient = 6.919) compared to other groups indicates that the protective effect of professional values may be particularly important in high-stress roles. This finding extends previous work on burnout among healthcare workers (Huang et al., 2024) by identifying professional values as a potential protective factor that operates differently across professional groups.

Theoretical and practical implications

Our findings have several important theoretical implications. First, they provide empirical support for conceptualizing professional values not only as ethical guidelines but also as economic and health resources within healthcare settings—extending traditional views by demonstrating their tangible benefits for income and well-being. Second, our instrumental variable approach addresses potential endogeneity, offering methodological rigor that strengthens causal inference beyond prior cross-sectional studies. Third, the differential effects across dimensions and subgroups suggest that professional values are a multifaceted, context-dependent construct rather than a monolithic trait. Notably, these insights may have broad relevance for low- and middle-income countries, where systemic challenges such as high burnout among nurses in Indonesia and severe work-related stress among healthcare workers in India due to excessive workloads and inadequate compensation, highlight the potential of professional values as a protective buffer in resource-constrained systems [63].

From a practical perspective, our results suggest that healthcare organizations should actively foster value-congruent environments. Given the strong association between altruistic values and both income and health, interventions such as recognition programs for ethical behavior, protected time for patient-centered care, and mentorship initiatives could enhance motivation and well-being at relatively low cost. To address disparities, professional development programs should help workers, especially those with lower education, effectively communicate and benefit from their values. Similarly, the gender disparities point to the need for more equitable recognition systems that ensure women receive comparable returns on their professional values. Tailored strategies may also be needed: linking values to career advancement for doctors and nurses, and emphasizing their health-protective role for paramedics. Integrating value-based recognition into performance systems, without substituting fair pay offers a dual-path approach aligned with sustainable healthcare reform that balances economic and ethical imperatives.

Limitations and future research directions

Despite the robust methodology employed, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our instrumental variable approach addresses endogeneity concerns, the cross-sectional nature of our data limits causal inference regarding the long-term development of professional values and their outcomes. Future longitudinal studies could examine how professional values evolve over career stages and how this evolution affects economic and health trajectories. Second, our reliance on self-reported measures, while validated and widely used, may introduce reporting biases. Future research could incorporate objective health measures and actual income data to strengthen the findings. Additionally, qualitative approaches could provide deeper insights into the mechanisms by which professional values translate into tangible outcomes. Third, while our study included a nationally representative sample, it focused exclusively on the Chinese healthcare context. Cross-cultural comparisons would be valuable to determine whether the relationships identified here generalize across different healthcare systems and cultural contexts. Finally, the relatively low Pseudo R2 for our health models suggests that other factors beyond professional values and our control variables influence healthcare workers’ health. Future research should explore potential mediators and moderators of the relationship between professional values and health outcomes, such as workplace environment, leadership styles, and organizational support.

Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that professional values significantly influence both the economic wellbeing and health status of healthcare workers in China. The differential effects across value dimensions, demographic characteristics, and professional roles highlight the complex nature of professional values and their impacts. These findings have important implications for healthcare education, organizational policies, and system-level interventions aimed at improving healthcare worker wellbeing and retention. As China continues to reform its healthcare system, cultivating and supporting professional values among healthcare workers represents a promising approach to addressing the dual challenges of income satisfaction and health maintenance identified in national surveys.
