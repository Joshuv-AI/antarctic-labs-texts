Isothermal miRNA Detection via Ligation-Dependent CRISPR/Cas13a Activation
Authors: Jiyoon Lee, Jihyun Kim, Dong Yeon Jo, Suyeon Lee, Jung Kyoon Choi, Hyun Gyu Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Here, we present a rapid isothermal strategy for miRNA detection based on ligation-dependent generation of Cas13a-activating RNA and CRISPR/Cas13a-mediated signal amplification. The system employs two DNA probes: a hairpin-structured probe containing a double-stranded T7 promoter within its stem and a second probe encoding a template for Cas13a activator RNA. In the presence of a target miRNA, the probes are ligated to form a functional transcription template, enabling the generation of Cas13a-activating RNA. The resulting RNA activates the Cas13a–crRNA complex, inducing collateral cleavage of a fluorescent reporter for signal generation. The method achieves a limit of detection of 2.56 pM for miR-21 and 11.64 pM for miR-10a, with a linear response over 0–500 pM, while maintaining high specificity with negligible responses to nontarget miRNAs. Importantly, the entire assay is completed within 40 min at 37 °C, highlighting its rapid detection capability. The applicability of the platform is validated using miRNA extracted from HepG2, HeLa, and MCF-7 cells, yielding results consistent with RT-qPCR analysis. Owing to its short assay time, operation at 37 °C, and sequence programmability through probe redesign, this strategy provides a useful framework for miRNA detection.

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are short noncoding RNAs that regulate gene expression at the post-transcriptional level and play critical roles in a wide range of biological processes and diseases, including cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and viral infections. − Owing to their close association with disease onset and progression, miRNAs have emerged as promising biomarkers for early diagnosis and precision medicine. Realizing this potential requires accurate and reliable quantification of miRNAs in biological samples, which remains technically challenging. , Their small size, low abundance in biological samples, and high sequence similarity among family members complicate direct detection and discrimination, necessitating sensitive and specific analytical methods. ,

Currently, the most widely adopted method is reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), which is regarded as the gold standard for miRNA detection. In this approach, miRNAs are reverse-transcribed into cDNA and subsequently amplified using target-specific primers, enabling real-time quantification with high sensitivity and sequence discrimination. , Its reliance on thermal cycling, however, necessitates precise temperature control equipment and extends the overall assay time, limiting throughput and operational simplicity. To address these limitations, a variety of alternative strategies have been explored, including isothermal amplification methods, enzyme-assisted signal amplification, and nanomaterial-based biosensors. −

Among these, isothermal amplification methods are particularly attractive because they eliminate the need for thermal cycling, enabling amplification at a constant temperature with simpler and faster workflows. , A key challenge, however, is achieving high sequence specificity, as many isothermal methods amplify targets with limited discrimination among closely related sequences. CRISPR-based detection systems address this challenge by providing an additional layer of sequence verification through programmable crRNA-guided target recognition, and have been increasingly applied to miRNA analysis. Such systems typically exploit target-activated collateral cleavage for signal generation. − Because collateral cleavage is activated only when sufficient activator RNA is present, low-abundance targets such as miRNAs cannot generate a detectable signal without prior amplification. , CRISPR-based detection systems are therefore often coupled with preamplification steps, including probe ligation-based approaches that generate transcription-compatible templates. Ligation-dependent transcription has been widely explored as an efficient nucleic acid amplification strategy, and in CRISPR-coupled systems the resulting transcripts can serve as RNA activators or crRNAs for downstream signal amplification. − Although these combined approaches improve sensitivity, many existing strategies still require multistep procedures or reaction times exceeding 2 h, highlighting the need for simplified and time-efficient detection strategies.

Here, we present a rapid isothermal strategy for miRNA detection based on ligation-dependent generation of Cas13a-activating RNA and CRISPR/Cas13a-mediated signal amplification. This study demonstrates the applicability of these established principles to rapid miRNA detection. The assay operates at 37 °C and is completed within 40 min, achieving sensitive and specific detection of two model miRNAs, miR-21 and miR-10a.

Results and Discussion

Assay Principle of miRNA Detection via Ligation-Dependent CRISPR/Cas13a Activation

As illustrated in Scheme , the strategy consists of three sequential reactions: (1) target miRNA-dependent probe ligation, (2) transcription-driven amplification, and (3) Cas13a-mediated reporter cleavage. The system employs two DNA probes: T7 promoter probe (T7PP), a hairpin-structured probe containing a double-stranded T7 promoter within its stem, and Cas13a recognition probe (CRP), a probe encoding a Cas13a activator sequence. In the presence of a target miRNA, adjacent hybridization of T7PP and CRP probes enables ligation by SplintR ligase, forming a transcription-competent template. The ligated template is transcribed by T7 RNA polymerase, generating multiple copies of Cas13a-activating RNA from a single template. The resulting RNA activators trigger the Cas13a–crRNA complex, inducing collateral cleavage of a FAM- and BHQ1-labeled reporter and producing a fluorescence signal. Because the transcribed RNA contains the target miRNA sequence, it can serve as a new splint for additional probe ligation, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies the signal. In the absence of the target miRNA, ligation does not occur, and the individual probes do not produce functional Cas13a-activating RNA, preventing subsequent Cas13a activation.

Feasibility of the Isothermal miRNA Detection Strategy

Our assay is based on a two-step workflow, in which ligation/transcription is first performed and the resulting product is then transferred to a separate Cas13a detection step (Figure a). To assess the feasibility of the proposed isothermal miRNA detection strategy, we first evaluated its performance using miR-21 and miR-10a as representative model targets, as both are well-characterized cancer-associated miRNAs recognized as potential diagnostic biomarkers. , For both probe sets, fluorescence intensity increased over time in the presence of the target miRNA, whereas negligible fluorescence changes were observed in its absence (Figure b, c).

To confirm that each step of the cascade proceeds as intended, the intermediate and final products were analyzed by PAGE (Figure d). First, the ligated probe was observed in the presence of miR-21, CRP, T7PP, and SplintR ligase (lane 6), whereas no ligation product was detected in the absence of the target miRNA (lane 7). Next, transcription of the ligated template produced RNA products only in the complete reaction containing both ligation and transcription components (lane 8). No corresponding band appeared without the target miRNA (lane 9). The additional bands observed in both lanes are likely attributable to target-independent species, including a short RNA transcript generated from the T7PP probe, probe-transcript complexes, and higher-order probe assemblies formed during the reaction. Finally, upon addition of Cas13a and the FAM–quencher reporter, lower-molecular-weight bands were observed for the complete reaction containing the target miRNA (lane 11), indicating reporter cleavage via Cas13a activation. The reporter band remained intact in the reaction lacking the target miRNA (lane 12).

To determine whether each component is essential for signal generation, fluorescence emission spectra were recorded under various reaction conditions in which individual components were systematically omitted (Figure e, f). In both cases, a strong fluorescence signal was observed only when all components, including CRP, T7PP, SplintR ligase, T7 RNA polymerase, and Cas13a, were present together with the target miRNA (curve 6). Negligible fluorescence was obtained when any component was excluded (curves 1–5) or in the absence of the target miRNA (curve 7). These results demonstrate that all components are required for signal generation and that the detection mechanism relies on the coordinated action of probe ligation, transcription, and CRISPR/Cas13a activation.

To further examine whether the transcribed RNA can function as a secondary splint in the present assay, a purified RNA transcript generated by in vitro transcription from the full-length ligated probe was used as a target input (Figure S1). Compared with the blank control, the observed real-time fluorescence response demonstrates that the transcribed RNA can indeed initiate the proposed positive-feedback process in the present assay. Together, these results confirm that each step of the proposed cascade  probe ligation, transcription, and Cas13a-mediated cleavage  operates as designed, producing a fluorescence signal only in the presence of the target miRNA.

Optimization of Reaction Conditions

After confirming the feasibility of the assay and the essential roles of each component, we optimized the reaction conditions to improve detection performance (Figure S2). The effects of key parameters, including the concentrations of T7PP, CRP, rNTP mix, SplintR ligase, T7 RNA polymerase, and Cas13a, were examined by monitoring the fluorescence response (F – F0).

As shown in Figure S2a,b, the fluorescence signal increased with probe concentration and reached maxima at 25 nM for T7PP and 10 nM for CRP, followed by a decline at higher concentrations. The remaining components exhibited similar concentration-dependent profiles, with optimal signals observed at 0.625 mM for the rNTP mix (Figure S2c), 1.25 U/μL for SplintR ligase (Figure S2d), 0.625 U/μL for T7 RNA polymerase (Figure S2e), and 50 nM for Cas13a (Figure S2f). These optimized conditions were selected for all subsequent experiments.

To further examine nonspecific signals arising from promoter-independent transcription, the CRP probe alone was incubated for different transcription times and subsequently subjected to Cas13a-mediated fluorescence detection (Figure S3). A measurable background signal was observed even in the absence of probe ligation, and the fluorescence intensity gradually increased with increasing transcription time. Under the optimized 20 min transcription condition, however, the background signal remained below approximately 15% of the target-positive signal, allowing clear discrimination of the positive signal (Figure e). These results indicate that optimization of the transcription time is critical for minimizing nonspecific background while maintaining reliable target detection.

Analytical Performance of the Assay

To evaluate the analytical sensitivity of the assay, fluorescence responses were measured over a range of target miRNA concentrations from 0 to 5 nM. For miR-21, the fluorescence intensity at 520 nm (F520) increased with target concentration, with a strong linear relationship observed over the range of 0–500 pM (Figure a,b). Based on the calibration curve, the limit of detection (LOD) for miR-21 was determined to be 2.56 pM using the 3σ/S method, where σ represents the standard deviation of the blank and S is the slope of the calibration curve (F520 = 14.77CmiRNA + 1277.25, R 2 = 0.9982).

A similar trend was observed for miR-10a (Figure c, d). The fluorescence intensity at 520 nm increased with miR-10a concentration, maintaining good linearity over the same range (0–500 pM). The LOD for miR-10a was calculated to be 11.64 pM (F520 = 2.85CmiRNA + 1224.94, R 2 = 0.9907). Although the sensitivity for miR-10a was lower than that for miR-21, likely due to sequence-dependent variations in hybridization and ligation efficiencies, the assay successfully detected both targets by simply redesigning the probe sequences, demonstrating the general applicability of the proposed strategy. As summarized in Table S2, the assay achieves comparable sensitivity to previously reported ligation-transcription or CRISPR-based methods while offering a simplified workflow and shorter assay time.

After confirming the analytical sensitivity of the assay, we evaluated its specificity toward target miRNAs. As shown in Figure a,b, each probe set produced a strong fluorescence signal only in the presence of its corresponding target, while signals from nontarget miRNAs remained comparable to the blank. These results demonstrate minimal cross-reactivity and effective discrimination between closely related miRNA sequences. To examine the selectivity quantitatively, fluorescence responses were normalized to the signal from the corresponding target miRNA (defined as 100%), and the relative responses of nontarget miRNAs are summarized in a heat map (Figure c). Nontarget miRNAs showed negligible responses in both probe sets, confirming high sequence specificity. To further rigorously evaluate the target specificity of the assay, two single-base miR-21 mismatch sequences and one double-base miR-21 mismatch sequence were designed by introducing mismatches at the ligation junction. Using the miR-21 probe set, the perfectly matched target generated a strong fluorescence signal, whereas all mismatch sequences produced fluorescence signals comparable to the blank control (Figure d). These results demonstrate that the ligation-dependent assay can discriminate even single-base differences in the target sequence, supporting its high sequence specificity.

Validation in Biological Samples and Practical Applicability

Having established the sensitivity and specificity of the assay using synthetic miRNA targets, we next evaluated its performance in cell-derived samples. miRNA extracted from HepG2, HeLa, and MCF-7 cell lines was subjected to the Cas13a assay, and the resulting raw fluorescence signals are shown in Figure S4a. For comparison with RT-qPCR, the fluorescence signals were converted into relative miRNA levels using the corresponding standard curve and subsequently normalized to the HepG2 sample, which exhibited the lowest signal among the tested cell lines. RT-qPCR quantification was performed using the corresponding standard curves (Figure S4b), and the resulting values were normalized in the same manner. For both miR-21 and miR-10a, the proposed assay exhibited relative expression patterns consistent with those obtained by RT-qPCR across the three cell lines (Figure ).

(a, b) Relative expression levels of (a) miR-21 and (b) miR-10a in HepG2, HeLa, and MCF-7 cell lines. miRNAs extracted from each cell line were analyzed by RT-qPCR and the proposed assay. Beyond cell-derived RNA, we assessed the assay in a more complex matrix by using human serum spiked with synthetic miR-21 at concentrations ranging from 100 to 400 pM. As shown in Table S3, miR-21 was successfully detected across the tested concentration range. The recovery rates ranged from 98.5% to 102.2%, with standard deviations below 7.93% and coefficients of variation below 3.72%. These results demonstrate that the assay enables reliable miRNA detection in both cell-derived and serum-based samples.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a rapid isothermal strategy for miRNA detection based on probe ligation, transcription of Cas13a-activating RNA, and CRISPR/Cas13a-mediated signal amplification. The assay achieved a low limit of detection, a wide dynamic range, and high sequence specificity. Consistent results were obtained in both cell-derived RNA samples and spiked human serum. By operating entirely at 37 °C with a total assay time of 40 min, the strategy substantially reduces the time compared to existing CRISPR/Cas13a-based approaches. Combined with its sequence programmability through probe redesign, this platform offers a practical solution for rapid and versatile miRNA analysis. Combined with its sequence programmability through probe redesign, this assay may serve as a useful approach for rapid miRNA analysis.

Materials and Methods

Materials

The CRISPR RNA (crRNA) for Cas13a was purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IA). All other oligonucleotides used in this study were synthesized by Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea) with BioRP purification and used as received. Detailed sequence information is provided in Table S1. SplintR ligase and its corresponding buffer were purchased from New England Biolabs (MA). T7 RNA polymerase, 10× T7 RNA polymerase buffer, Proteinase K, and rNTPs (100 mM each) were obtained from Enzynomics (Daejeon, Korea). Cas13a was purchased from Molecular Cloning Laboratories (CA). Cas13a used in this study was LwCas13a (Leptotrichia wadeii Cas13a), purchased from Molecular Cloning Laboratories (CA). Human serum was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (MO). All experiments were performed using DEPC-treated water (DEPC-DW) from Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea).

Assay Procedure

A reaction mixture containing 2 μL of CRP probe (100 nM), 0.5 μL of T7PP probe (1 μM), 0.5 μL of target miRNA at varying concentrations, 0.5 μL of 10× T7 RNA polymerase buffer, and 0.5 μL of 10× SplintR ligase buffer was prepared and adjusted to a final volume of 8 μL with DEPC-DW. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow hybridization.

Ligation and in vitro transcription were initiated by adding a reaction mixture containing 0.5 μL of 10× SplintR ligase buffer, 0.5 μL of 10× T7 RNA polymerase buffer, 0.5 μL of rNTPs (25 mM each), 1 μL of SplintR ligase (25 U/μL), 0.5 μL of T7 RNA polymerase (25 U/μL), and DEPC-DW to a final volume of 12 μL. The total reaction sample (20 μL) was incubated at 37 °C for 20 min.

For CRISPR/Cas13a detection, 2 μL of the reaction product was mixed with 18 μL of a Cas13a reaction mixture containing 1 μL of 10× SplintR ligase buffer, 1 μL of crRNA (500 nM), 1 μL of Cas13a (1 μM), and 1.5 μL of FQ reporter (20 μM). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Fluorescence signals were recorded in real time using a CFX Connect real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, CA) with the FAM channel or measured with an Infinite 200 microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland) at an excitation wavelength of 470 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm. Unless otherwise specified, fluorescence data were obtained as end point measurements using the microplate reader, whereas only the time-course plots were recorded in real time using the CFX Connect system.

For quantitative analysis, end point fluorescence intensities at 520 nm were used to construct calibration curves. Linear regression analysis was performed within the concentration range showing a linear relationship between the miRNA concentration and fluorescence intensity. The limit of detection (LOD) was calculated using the 3σ/S method, where σ is the standard deviation of the blank signal obtained from no-target control reactions and S is the slope of the corresponding calibration curve. Linear regression equations and R 2 values were obtained from the calibration plots. For specificity evaluation, nontarget miRNAs (miR-16, miR-155, miR-200a, miR-200b, and let-7b) as well as two single-base and one double-base miR-21 mismatch sequences were tested at the same concentration under identical assay conditions.

Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

PAGE was performed to analyze the reaction products for miR-21 detection. For PAGE analysis degrade the enzymes present in the reaction mixture. Subsequently, 2 μL of 6× loading dye was added to 10 μL of the reaction mixture, and 10 μL of the resulting mixture was loaded on a 15% polyacrylamide gel. Electrophoresis was carried out at a constant voltage of 120 V for 100 min in 1× Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE) buffer. The gel was stained with GelRed and visualized using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA).

Validation of the Positive-Feedback Mechanism

To verify whether the transcribed RNA product could function as a secondary splint for additional probe ligation, a synthetic full-length ligated probe containing both the T7 promoter and template sequence was transcribed in vitro. The transcription reaction was carried out at 37 °C overnight, and the resulting RNA transcript was purified using the Monarch Spin RNA Cleanup Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purified RNA transcript was then used as the input target in the proposed assay under the same reaction conditions described above. Real-time fluorescence signals were monitored in the absence (blank) or presence of 10 ng of the purified RNA transcript.

Cell Culture, RT-qPCR, and Cas13a Assay

HepG2, HeLa, and MCF-7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM; Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea) supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, NY) and 1% (v/v) penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C.

Total RNA was extracted from 5 × 105 cells using the miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop One microvolume ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA). Reverse transcription and quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) were performed using the HB miR Multi Assay Kit (HeimBiotek Inc., Seongnam, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from 100 ng of total RNA, and RT-qPCR was performed using a CFX Connect real-time PCR detection system (Bio-Rad, CA).

Raw fluorescence intensities obtained from the Cas13a assay were converted into calculated miRNA amounts using the standard curve generated for each miRNA. For miR-21 and miR-10a, linear regression analysis yielded the equations y = 14.77x + 1277.25 and y = 2.85x + 1224.94, respectively, where y is the raw fluorescence intensity and x is the calculated miRNA amount. For each miRNA, the calculated values from all cell lines were normalized to the mean value of the three HepG2 replicates Ri=xix̅HepG2 where R i is the normalized value of the replicate from cell line i. The final relative miRNA level was presented as the mean ± SD of the three normalized replicates. The Cas13a assay and RT-qPCR data were independently normalized to HepG2.

Detection of Spiked miR-21 in Serum Samples

Human serum was filtered using a centrifugal filter unit (MWCO 30 kDa, Millipore, MA) at 6,000×g for 30 min. Synthetic miR-21 at various concentrations was spiked into human serum diluted to 1% (v/v), and the assay was performed as described above.
