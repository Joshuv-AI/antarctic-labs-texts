Pharmacy students’ perceptions of virtual reality-based training in professional identity formation and internship experiences
Authors: Elif Ulutas Deniz, Rumeysa Eren Keskin, Hatice Seda Kaya Alıcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies examining the use of virtual reality (VR)–based simulations in pharmacy education have primarily focused on skill development and general student satisfaction, while research exploring how such interventions relate to internship experiences and professional identity formation is limited. This study aimed to explore pharmacy students’ perceptions and experiences of VR-based pre-internship education in relation to their professional identity formation and real-world internship experiences.MethodsA descriptive qualitative design was employed. A two-phase qualitative study was conducted using individual in-depth interviews to gain insights into the participants’ experiences. The study utilized a previously developed and nationally recognized VR intervention (FarmaVR) designed to introduce the key aspects of community pharmacy practice.ResultsTwenty-one in-depth individual interviews were conducted with the participants. The findings from the individual interviews were organized into three broad themes: positive impact of virtual reality on learning, weaknesses of virtual reality, and perceptions of virtual reality.ConclusionThe findings suggest that VR-based pre-internship education may support pharmacy students’ preparation for internship experiences by facilitating early engagement with their professional roles. Virtual simulations may serve as a complementary educational approach when integrated into comprehensive training strategies that emphasize interpersonal skill development and authentic clinical exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09575-z.

Introduction

Virtual reality is a technical instrument that is highly beneficial and yields favorable outcomes for both students and educators in the realm of education [1]. Recent advancements have rendered virtual reality technology more immersive, adaptable, portable, and economical [2].

In pharmacy education, the development of professional competence requires the acquisition of theoretical knowledge and meaningful experiential learning opportunities. Professional identity formation (PIF) — the process through which students come to internalize the values, roles, and responsibilities of their chosen profession — is now recognized as a central goal of pharmacy education. Practical and experiential learning contexts, including internships, play a particularly important role in this process [3, 4].

In Türkiye, pharmacy students are required to complete a minimum of six months of internship training prior to graduation, as mandated by the Council of Higher Education. This six-month internship is distributed across four periods throughout the five-year undergraduate programme, completed during the summer terms of the third, fourth, and fifth years and the final semester of the fifth year. Students may fulfil their internship requirements in community pharmacies, hospital pharmacies, or the pharmaceutical industry. The learning outcomes associated with each placement period are defined progressively; earlier placements focus on foundational competencies such as familiarisation with pharmacy information systems (Medulla), drug identification, and patient communication, while later placements build towards broader competencies in pharmacy management and operations. Student performance is assessed jointly by the internship committees within the faculty and by the responsible pharmacist at the placement site, who evaluate students based on their professional conduct and the competencies demonstrated throughout the placement period [5]. Within this nationally regulated internship framework, virtual reality–based preparatory simulations may further support students’ readiness for practice and professional identity formation [4].

Experiential learning approaches, including learning by doing, contribute meaningfully to pharmacy education by complementing the foundational knowledge provided through theoretical instruction. In this context, virtual reality (VR) has emerged as a promising tool that may bridge the gap between classroom-based learning and real-world practice by offering safe, immersive, repeatable simulation experiences [6]. Three-dimensional virtual environments allow educators to design specific scenarios in which pharmacy students can practice a range of clinical and professional skills, including patient counselling and medication-related problem solving [7–11]. However, despite these advantages, it has been noted that three-dimensional virtual environments are not as widely used in pharmacy education as they are in medical and nursing education. Most reported programs that include virtual patients in pharmacy education are in the United States and Canada [12].

In particular, the use of virtual reality practices in health education can contribute to better student preparation for internship processes [13]. As the pharmacy profession has evolved from science-based practice to patient-centered practice in recent years, pharmacy faculties are determining an educational program that will provide not only academic success but also practical success for students [14, 15]. Laboratory studies and internship practices are of great importance when examining pharmacy faculty curricula. Although theoretical pharmacy education provides essential foundational knowledge of pharmacy practice, experiential learning is necessary to fully develop the competencies required in real-world professional settings. In this context, internships contribute greatly to the professional development of students [16]. Internships and similar programs are widely implemented in many pharmacy faculties worldwide so that students who have completed their undergraduate education can demonstrate their professional skills and gain the necessary experience. In addition, in many countries, after these practical applications, students’ professional competencies are tested, and assurance is provided that they can perform their professions correctly and safely [17–21].

Internship experiences play a critical role in students’ professional identity development, and the educational tools used in this process can directly affect their perceptions of professional competence [22]. In recent years, interest in professional identity and professionalism in the pharmacy field has significantly increased. The development of a distinct professional identity in pharmacy has been shaped by historical and social changes. Following the industrialization of drug manufacturing in the 20th century, pharmacists’ roles became less visible, negatively affecting the profession’s social reputation. However, as the profession has progressively transitioned toward a patient-centered model of care, pharmacists have reassumed a central role in health outcomes, taking on responsibilities as decision-makers, caregivers, communicators, and lifelong learners. This evolving professional landscape has created complexity for students in forming a coherent professional identity, as they must navigate the clinical, managerial, and patient-facing dimensions of the pharmacist role [23]. In this context, pharmacy students need effective support to develop an identity in society that is appropriate for the goals of their profession. Therefore, strategies for gaining a professional identity in pharmacy stand out as an important alternative in the pre- and post-graduate periods, with experiences that include elements of professionalism. Internships and other practical applications aim to prepare students in accordance with the professional demands [24]. Some factors, such as previous work experience, professional knowledge, social networks, understanding of teamwork, living environment, participation in health facilities, cognitive flexibility, learning environments, and relationships with educators, may affect the formation of basic professional identity [25, 26]. One of these factors is virtual reality-based simulation. Virtual reality (VR) focuses on developing psychomotor skills. Appropriately designed virtual reality experiences can pave the way for identity exploration by offering the opportunity to practice professional skills and interact with the cultural and social aspects of the profession. The structure of collaborative virtual world experiences plays an important role in developing professional identity and a sense of belonging. This type of learning focuses not only on how to do something but also on the individual’s internalization of the social and cultural foundations of the culture in which they are learning [27, 28].

Traditional internships allow students to apply theoretical knowledge in practical settings; however, challenges encountered during this transition, including role ambiguity, clinical decision-making pressure, and patient communication difficulties, may influence the development of professional identity [22]. Emerging educational technologies, such as virtual reality (VR), have the potential to support students’ internship preparedness. Previous studies in the health sciences have reported that VR-supported educational programs may enhance clinical skill development and strengthen students’ perceptions of their professional role. However, research specifically exploring how VR-based pre-internship training contributes to professional identity formation and internship experiences in pharmacy education remains limited.

Unlike previous studies that primarily report general student satisfaction or skill acquisition, an in-depth qualitative exploration of how VR-based pre-internship education shapes pharmacy students’ professional identity formation remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to explore pharmacy students’ perceptions and experiences of VR-based pre-internship education in relation to their professional identity formation and real-world internship experiences.

Methods

This study used a VR intervention (FarmaVR) application that was already designed, tested, and had gained national recognition. FarmaVR was specifically designed to introduce second-year undergraduate pharmacy students to the key operational and clinical aspects of community pharmacy practice, including pharmacy management systems, drug dispensing procedures, and the medulla prescription system. FarmaVR is a computer game that involves maneuvering a robot, watching films at appropriate locations, and navigating a virtual pharmacy. FarmaVR communicates in daily language and plain Turkish. For all participants in this study, the VR intervention represented their first exposure to virtual reality technology. In this application, the student visits a virtual pharmacy with a robot and receives training on pharmacy management and drugs on the shelf with the help of videos prepared by expert teams (Fig. 1). FarmaVR was introduced to eligible second-year undergraduate pharmacy students as part of a research study conducted prior to their compulsory community pharmacy internship. Participation in VR training was voluntary and specific to students who consented to participate in the study, rather than being a mandatory component of the academic curriculum.

Study design

This study used a descriptive qualitative design. According to Kim et al. (2017) [29] and Sandelowski (2000) [30], qualitative description is the least theoretical of all qualitative techniques. This permits researchers to investigate participants’ unvarnished perspectives in their natural conditions without being constrained by pre-existing theories or ideologies. Qualitative descriptions are particularly useful for identifying understudied pharmacy phenomena and answering queries such as, ’ What are people’s concerns about an event? How do people react to incidents (e.g., their ideas, feelings, and attitudes)? What are the reasons people do or do not use a service or procedure? Who utilizes a service and when [30]? . This style of inquiry was in line with the objectives of the current study. This study used the Qualitative Research Reporting Standards Checklist, which improves transparency and maintains an audit trail [31]. The study was conducted between July 2024 and September 2024. Data were collected in two phases: Phase 1 interviews were conducted in July 2024, immediately following students’ completion of the FarmaVR training and prior to their internship; Phase 2 interviews were conducted in September 2024, following the completion of the internship.

Study sample

Eligible participants were second-year undergraduate pharmacy students at a prominent public university in Türkiye who had never worked in a community pharmacy. Second-year undergraduate students were specifically recruited because, at this stage of the curriculum, they were preparing to undertake their first compulsory community pharmacy internship, making them ideally positioned to reflect on the role of pre-internship VR training. Purposive and snowball sampling tactics were used, and eligible participants were encouraged to participate. The study was introduced at the start of a pharmacy course through a 10-minute Microsoft PowerPoint® presentation. Invitation letters containing the researchers’ contact information were distributed throughout the day. A second study presentation was conducted two weeks later to increase participation. A total of 152 s-year undergraduate pharmacy students were invited to participate. Of these, 32 undergraduate students provided consent to participate in this study.

Data collection

A two-phase qualitative study was conducted using individual interviews to comprehend the participants’ experiences. A selected researcher with no prior relationship with the students provided students with information about the study in person and obtained written informed consent from all participants prior to data collection. The students were also given an invitation letter that included information about the study and the researchers’ contact details. Participation was on a ‘first come, first served’ basis, with interested students contacting the researchers directly to schedule a mutually acceptable date and time for data collection. A total of 32 undergraduate students consented to participate in this study. Each student was assigned a one-year FarmaVR license. Prior to training, the students attended an informational meeting on how to use the program. Students who consented to participate in the study were required to complete a total of eight hours of training prior to their internship via the FarmaVR application (Fig. 1). The training covered modules on communication skills, the medulla system, and product introductions, all aligned with the learning outcomes we expected them to achieve during the internship. The eight-hour VR training was completed in a single full-day session in a classroom setting under the supervision of an instructor. Students accessed the application through their own tablets, phones, or computers using their headsets, ensuring consistent and monitored engagement for all participants. Completion of the training was tracked automatically by the application. Of the 32 students who consented to participate, 25 completed the full training and were therefore included in the study. Face-to-face interviews were conducted with students who completed the course immediately after the training to discuss their thoughts on FarmVR in July. The interviews lasted for an average of 25 minutes. During the interview, the participants were guided by a specially prepared semi-structured guide that examined their thoughts on the use of VR in pharmacy education, the perceived benefits and disadvantages of using VR in pharmacy education, and recommendations for the use of VR. A second face-to-face interview was conducted at a specified date and time after the students completed their internships (in September). All students completed their internships during the same period (July–September, 2024). The interviews lasted for an average of 35 minutes. During the interviews, they were asked about the impact of the application on their internship and how it contributed to the process of forming a professional identity. Audio recordings were made during all interviews. The interview guide was developed based on the existing literature exploring professional identity formation processes in health professions education, including conceptual frameworks and empirical findings on how clinical learning environments shape professional roles and perceptions (Appendix-1) [32, 33].

A pilot study

A pilot study was conducted with two students to determine the comprehensibility of the questions asked. All the necessary arrangements were made. Data from the piloted students were not included in the study.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were performed concurrently. Professional transcription services conducted the interviews verbatim. The first author cross-checked the transcripts for accuracy. Data were examined using Braun and Clarke’s (2006) [34] six-step thematic analysis method. Consistent with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive approach, themes were actively constructed by the research team rather than passively emerging from the data. First, the transcripts were reviewed and reread, and the researchers’ ideas were identified. Relevant participants’ verbatims were then extracted and summarized as codes. Similar codes were gathered, compacted, and entered into a coding sheet. Sub-themes linking the various codes were created and cross-checked. A thematic map was then constructed to explain the relationships between the codes and the subthemes. Finally, the subthemes were refined to form the themes. To reduce inconsistencies and improve study credibility and confirmability, the first author performed the data analysis, which was then cross-checked by the second author [35]. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus, and the subthemes and themes were refined accordingly. Maxqda was used to carry out the analysis.

Trustworthiness

The first author handled the coding procedure, which was then cross-checked by the second and third authors to improve credibility and reliability, and ensure analytical consistency [36]. Excerpts were used to support and exemplify numerous topics and subthemes, increasing credibility. Throughout the data collection and analysis, the researchers maintained a constant discussion to verify that the interpretations and findings were true to the data. This is known to improve confirmability [37]. To improve transferability, Saladaña (2021) [38] suggests detailing the data-gathering procedure and sample characteristics. Finally, authenticity was assured using icebreakers prior to data collection, such as questions about participants’ studies and university life in general [36].

Reflexivity

An individual unknown to the participants described the FarmaVR application. One week after exposure to FarmaVR, the first author, a qualitative research expert, conducted interviews. The researcher was a faculty member, although she did not actively teach students.

Ethical consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Atatürk University Faculty of Pharmacy Unit Ethics Committee (Approval No: B.30.2.ATA.0.01.00/213), and written institutional permission was obtained from the institution where the study was conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were adequately informed about the purpose of the study and the data collection procedures.

Results

The following section presents the findings organized according to the three main themes constructed through the reflexive thematic analysis. The themes reflect students’ perceptions of FarmaVR in relation to their professional identity formation and internship experiences.

Interview 18 met the data saturation criteria. Data saturation was achieved when no new codes or themes emerged in three consecutive interviews. Three additional interviews were conducted to confirm data saturation. Twenty-one in-depth individual interviews were conducted with participants. The participants’ ages ranged from 19 to 23 years, with a median age of 20 years. The demographic characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1. None of the participants reported playing any form of video game, and none (n = 21) had ever experienced virtual reality. The findings from the individual interviews were organized into three broad themes: the positive impact of virtual reality on learning, weaknesses of virtual reality, and perceptions of virtual reality (Table 2).

Themes and sub themes

Theme 1. Positive impact of virtual reality on learning

Most students reported that virtual reality contributed to their internships and professional identity formation.

Perceived contribution to professional identity formation

Most students stated that practicing pharmacy management procedures and systems in a virtual reality simulation environment, prior to real-life practice, increased their self-confidence. They argued that this situation made them feel like they were pharmacists.

“It enhanced my self-confidence; I was more confident entering prescriptions because I already had some knowledge — I was not completely unfamiliar with the system anymore.” (S7)

“It enhanced my self-confidence; I was more confident entering prescriptions because I already had some knowledge — I was not completely unfamiliar with the system anymore.” (S7)

Perceived influence on internship experiences

Before their internship, they said that they were nervous about going in without knowing anything, but that they would be more comfortable with the application after the training. Stating that they gained experience through practice, the students argued that they felt familiar with pharmacy operations during their internship.

“On the first day I went, I was confused about what to do. But after watching FarmaVR, I understood a bit better what I needed to do and how to act — it filled that gap.” (S4)

“On the first day I went, I was confused about what to do. But after watching FarmaVR, I understood a bit better what I needed to do and how to act — it filled that gap.” (S4)

Theme 2. Weaknesses of virtual reality

Students stated that the application had several weaknesses, arguing that it was due to technical difficulties, the complexity of the application, and the information they experienced in their internships but could not experience in practice.

Unaddressed knowledge in virtual reality

In the interviews conducted after their internships, most students stated that the practice was inadequate, especially in matters such as patient communication and magistral drug production (referred to as compounding in some countries).

“I learned about magistral drug preparation outside of FarmaVR.” (S9)

“I learned about magistral drug preparation outside of FarmaVR.” (S9)

Complexity

Some students found the application overly complex and expressed a preference for a more interactive design, suggesting that the level of complexity was perceived as a barrier rather than a reflection of real pharmacy practice.

“It was a bit overwhelming for those with no prior knowledge — there was too much detail. The content could have been delivered in a simpler, more guided way.” (S7)

“It was a bit overwhelming for those with no prior knowledge — there was too much detail. The content could have been delivered in a simpler, more guided way.” (S7)

Technical difficulties

Most students stated that the image quality was not good in some places because there were no options to stop, rewind, fast-forward, or speed up the videos in the application.

“There were too many disadvantages. No pause button, no fast-forward, the system felt outdated. There should at least be a way to bookmark the slides you have watched — these do not seem like difficult things to implement.” (S9)

“There were too many disadvantages. No pause button, no fast-forward, the system felt outdated. There should at least be a way to bookmark the slides you have watched — these do not seem like difficult things to implement.” (S9)

Theme 3. Perceptions about virtual reality

Almost all the students stated that the application, which they stated that students with no pharmacy experience should use, was entertaining and educational. Among their favorite aspects, they stated that they liked browsing shelves in the virtual pharmacy environment and walking around the pharmacy with the robot.

“I really liked walking around with the robot inside the pharmacy. The way the pharmacy was brought to life in that environment was great for me.” (S10)

Discussion

This study explored pharmacy students’ perceptions and experiences of FarmaVR, a VR-based pre-internship training program, focusing on its perceived contribution to professional identity formation and preparation for community pharmacy internships. The findings suggest that VR-based training can serve as a meaningful complement to traditional internship preparation, supporting early professional role engagement while also highlighting limitations related to interactivity, patient communication, and technical design.

According to many students, the simulation was perceived as supporting their professional preparation in several ways. Participants reported that the controlled, low-risk environment helped them become more familiar with pharmacy workflows and administrative processes, allowing them to engage with these aspects of practice before real-world internship experiences. More significantly, they emphasized that the simulation seems to encourage the formation of a professional identity. Moreover, many students reported feeling more confident and ready to take on the role of a pharmacist after being acquainted with the design and operation of a pharmacy setting prior to their internship. Some found that experience helped them better visualize themselves in a professional role by bridging the gap between their theoretical understanding and the actual requirements of pharmaceutical practice. Similarly, Qiao et al. conducted a virtual reality study to investigate the experiences of students from different disciplines in a virtual clinical environment and found that it helped students develop their professional identities [39]. Similarly, a study conducted with medical students by Consorti and Consorti (2023) indicated that virtual reality affected the process of constructing a professional identity. In this context, real or simulated activities have been considered effective tools for contributing to the formation of professional identity [26]. Similarly, in a study conducted by Jowsey et al. in 2020 with health students, including pharmacy faculty students, it was determined that simulation learning experiences played an important role in the formation of students’ professional identity. These experiences allowed students to lay the foundations of their future health professional identities by reflecting on their basic professional practices. It was determined that simulations not only developed technical skills for the students but also contributed to them taking on the role of representatives for their future selves as health professionals and gaining insights that strengthened their professional identities [40].

The students also discussed the disadvantages of simulations. A number of students voiced their displeasure from the passive aspect of the learning process, pointing out that it was challenging to completely comprehend the material because they could not pause or replay the videos. The immersive experience was further diminished by technical problems, such as visual blurriness in several areas of the simulation. More importantly, the simulation lacked elements associated with patient contact, such as decision-making in dynamic circumstances or communication abilities. In one study, lack of social interaction, limited topics, and slow processes were also listed as disadvantages [41]. It is also known that virtual reality systems can be difficult to install and can frequently malfunction, preventing their use [42, 43]. In previous studies, in-depth training was required for students to overcome technical difficulties and achieve technical proficiency [12, 44].

In this study, many students characterized the event as inspiring and new, particularly because of its technological aspects. They conceded that the simulation was insufficient on its own to build the entire set of skills required for pharmacy practice. The results suggest that virtual simulations should preferably be combined with more interactive, patient-centered training modalities, even though they may effectively aid in the development of technical knowledge and early professional identity formation. Similarly, in one study, students described VR experiences as “fun,” “novel,” “exciting,” “exceeding expectations,” and “helping to make the pharmacist role easier.” However, some participants said that this “can never be the same” as interacting with real patients [12]. Future studies should examine the efficacy of blended learning strategies that support students’ clinical and interpersonal skill development by combining virtual simulations with real-world scenarios, reflective debriefings, or standardized patient interactions. Future studies in Türkiye and beyond should explore the integration of FarmaVR and similar tools into blended learning frameworks that combine VR pre-training with standardized patient interactions, reflective debriefing sessions, and structured mentorship during internships. Given the mandatory nature of community pharmacy internships in Turkish pharmacy education, there is considerable potential for VR-based tools to be formally embedded within the pre-internship curriculum to better prepare students for the professional demands of practice. In terms of current practice in Türkiye, the findings suggest that pharmacy faculties should consider formally incorporating FarmaVR or similar VR tools into their pre-internship curricula as a structured preparatory component. Given that the community pharmacy internship is a compulsory and regulated element of Turkish pharmacy education, integrating VR-based pre-training into the existing curriculum framework could provide a scalable and standardized approach to enhancing students’ professional readiness before entering the practice environment.

This study has some limitations, even though it offers insightful information on the function of virtual reality in pharmacy education. First, the generalizability of the findings is limited by the small sample size and the fact that each participant was recruited from a single institution. Future research should consider a broader and more varied sample from several universities to investigate whether the experiences and perceptions of virtual reality vary depending on the situation. Furthermore, because the study concentrated mostly on students’ initial perceptions, it is uncertain how virtual reality may affect their performance in real-world pharmacy practice and professional development in the long run. A more thorough understanding of how virtual reality affects skill retention and the formation of professional identity may be possible through longitudinal studies that follow students over time. Additionally, although this study identifies the advantages and disadvantages of virtual simulation, more research is required to improve the technology and investigate how it might be used with other instructional strategies to better prepare students for the reality of pharmacy practice.

Conclusion

This study highlights pharmacy students’ perceptions of the advantages and limitations of a VR-based pre-internship training experience. Participants described the simulation as a supportive preparatory tool that helped them become more familiar with pharmacy operations and engage with professional roles before their internship. Exposure to a virtual representation of pharmacy settings was perceived to support students’ confidence and sense of preparedness for internship. However, limitations related to patient interaction and restricted technical features were also identified, indicating areas for further development of the app. Overall, these findings suggest that VR-based training may serve as a complementary educational approach in pharmacy education when integrated into comprehensive training strategies that emphasize interpersonal skill development and authentic clinical engagement.
