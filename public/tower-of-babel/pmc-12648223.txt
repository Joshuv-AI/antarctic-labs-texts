Nurses’ functional status assessments using the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health across hospital types in Japan: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Kanako Yamamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn Japan, patients receiving treatment in advanced acute care hospitals often cannot return home immediately and require ongoing hospitalization. Sequential support is crucial to facilitate long-term recovery and community reintegration. Therefore, functional assessments should extend beyond physical function to include daily activities and participation. However, how nurses across different hospital types in Japan evaluate patients’ functional status using a standardized framework remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study examined how nurses in hospitals with varying care functions assess patients’ functional status, focusing on the application of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework.MethodsA cross-sectional survey with a qualitative component was conducted in accordance with the STROBE guidelines. Participants were 200 registered nurses in Japan, each with at least three years of clinical experience. Data were collected from February to March 2025 through an online survey using a commercial research firm’s verified panel of licensed nurses. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). In addition, open-ended responses were analyzed qualitatively to capture contextual perspectives on functional assessment.ResultsNo significant differences were observed in discharge planning practices among the four hospital types. The Functional Independence Measure was most frequently used in recovery-phase hospitals (p <0.001). Across all hospital types, assessments focused primarily on body functions and structures, while activities and participation domains received comparatively less attention. Qualitative analysis indicated that the focus of patient handovers varied by hospital function, reflecting institutional roles and priorities.ConclusionAlthough discharge planning practices were comparable across hospital types, functional assessments remained concentrated on physical aspects, with limited attention to broader domains critical for long-term recovery. These findings highlight the need for a more comprehensive and standardized use of the ICF framework to support patients’ social reintegration and continuity of care across healthcare settings.

Background

In recent years, advances in evidence-based multidisciplinary treatment and management have led to shorter intensive care unit (ICU) stays for critically ill patients (Hodgson et al., 2018). However, post-intensive care syndrome (PICS), a condition characterized by physical, cognitive, and psychological impairments following ICU discharge, has emerged as a significant concern (Hiser et al., 2023). To mitigate its effects, early psychological support and rehabilitation have become essential components of post-ICU care (Brown et al., 2019). Despite these efforts, functional status assessments for critically ill patients remain primarily focused on physical performance measures, such as muscle strength tests and basic mobility tasks (Du Plessis et al., 2024).

In contrast, functional evaluations, often based on Activities of Daily Living (ADL), are required for reimbursement under Japan’s medical and long-term care insurance systems in recovery-phase wards and long-term care facilities. These assessments, however, vary depending on the care setting and hospital function, leading to inconsistencies in patient evaluations (Ishihama & Shirahase, 2022). Although regional collaboration pathways and interdisciplinary conferences have been introduced to improve coordination between medical and long-term care facilities, gaps persist in assessing and communicating functional status across institutions (Ministry of Health Labour and Welfare Japan, 2020; WHO, 2002).

During the recovery phase, evaluations still emphasize postural control and physical function rather than providing a comprehensive assessment of overall functional status (Ministry of Health Labour and Welfare Japan, 2022). Additionally, no standardized assessment tool is consistently applied across different care settings, making it challenging to ensure continuity of care. Moreover, variability and a lack of continuity still exist in nursing assessment practices across various settings.

To address these challenges, the World Health Organization developed the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) in 2001 as a standardized framework for assessing disability and functional status. The ICF conceptualizes disability as a universal human experience rather than an individual limitation, offering a common language for healthcare professionals. In Japan, a localized version of the ICF Core Set was introduced in 2013 and has since been the subject of both domestic and international research (Uchigashima, 2021).

The ICF framework consists of two main components: (1) functioning and disability, which includes body functions and structures, activities, and participation, and (2) contextual factors, which encompass environmental and personal influences. These elements are applicable throughout acute, recovery, and long-term care phases (WHO, 2002, 2013). Although the ICF has been widely used in fields such as orthopedics, cardiology, and neurology (Lima et al., 2024; Lima et al., 2022; Uchigashima, 2021), its application in critical care remains limited (Wang et al., 2022). Most existing studies focus on specific conditions such as stroke or acute respiratory distress syndrome, and no known research in Japan has examined ICF-based assessments in ICU settings. Given that recovery from illness or disability is an ongoing process, a standardized assessment framework that spans different care settings is crucial.

To achieve this, it is essential to first understand how nurses, who play a central role in patient assessment and care, evaluate functional status across various phases of treatment, from acute to chronic care. While ICF assessments are typically conducted by rehabilitation professionals (Saleh et al., 2024), nurses also require a standardized framework to ensure continuity of care.

Methods

Study Design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey with a qualitative component, conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Von Elm et al., 2007). The primary design was quantitative, and supplementary qualitative content analysis of open-ended responses was included to provide additional contextual insights.

Sample/Participants

The eligibility criteria for participation included the following: ⅰ) Licensed nurses with at least 3 years of clinical experience, ⅱ) Employment in a hospital classified as advanced acute care, acute care, recovery, or chronic care. The exclusion criteria are as follows: ⅰ) Nurses not directly involved in patient care, ⅱ) Nurses not participating in functional assessments of patients. A priori power analysis for a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; α = 0.05, power = 0.80, effect size = 0.25) determined that a minimum of 45 participants per group was required (Faul et al., 2009). To account for an estimated 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was set at 200 nurses (approximately 50 per group).

Participants were recruited through a commercial research firm maintaining a verified panel of over 5,000 licensed nurses across Japan. This approach ensured broad geographic and institutional representation, maintained participant anonymity, and facilitated efficient and ethically managed recruitment. Considering the limited timeframe of the study and the need to access nurses working in hospitals with different care functions (advanced acute, acute, recovery, and chronic care), this method was deemed the most appropriate and feasible. Moreover, recruitment from this large panel led to purposeful sampling and minimized potential selection bias. All panel members had previously given their consent to participate in health-related research, and participant information was managed in accordance with data protection laws.

Eligible nurses were invited based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Those who expressed interest were screened and selected on a first-come, first-served basis. Researchers reviewed all applications for eligibility before finalizing the participant list. Written study details were provided, and participation was voluntary, with informed consent obtained from all participants.

Instruments

Nurses’ discharge support practices were assessed using the Discharge Planning–Process Evaluation Measurement (DCP-PEM), a validated scale designed to comprehensively evaluate discharge planning by ward nurses (Chiba, 2005). This instrument comprises 26 items across five domains: screening (3 items), assessment (6 items), care planning (5 items), implementation (9 items), and monitoring (3 items). Each item is rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = “not at all” to 5 = “completely”), with higher scores indicating greater adherence to discharge planning practices. The scale’s reliability and validity were confirmed, and permission for its use was obtained from the original developer.

In addition, participants were asked to identify the standardized functional assessment tools they routinely used in clinical practice. These included instruments commonly adopted in Japanese medical and long-term care systems, such as the Functional Independence Measure (FIM), Barthel Index, Lawton and Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale, Berg Balance Scale, Fall Risk Assessment, Water Swallowing Test, and Dysphagia Risk Assessment Tool for the Elderly. Participants were also asked to report whether they used the care-needs level index defined under Japan’s Long-Term Care Insurance system, which classifies assistance requirements from support level to care level 5 (Ministry of Health Labour and Welfare Japan, 2022. Furthermore, ICF-linked instruments such as the All-Japan Geriatric Facilities ICF-based Care Management Staging Tool and the Roken-style Activity Ability Index were also included. Participants could select multiple responses to reflect the diversity of instruments used across hospital functions. The inclusion of these tools enabled examination of institutional variations in the application of ICF-related or function-based measures in nursing practice.

To evaluate the application of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework (WHO, 2001, 2002, 2013), participants estimated the proportion (0%–100%) of their assessments focusing on each ICF component: body functions and structures (e.g., assessments of muscle strength, swallowing, and vital signs), activities (e.g., ability to perform activities of daily living such as bathing, toileting, or meal preparation), participation (e.g., evaluation of patients’ family roles, employment, leisure, or community involvement), environmental factors (e.g., home modifications, caregiver or community support), and personal factors. The participation items used in this survey were developed based on the ICF framework described in the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (WHO, 2001) to ensure consistency with international standards and comparability with prior studies. These proportions and item-level data were later compared among hospital types to determine domain-specific emphasis.

Finally, to explore nurses’ practical use of the ICF framework in greater depth, participants provided open-ended responses describing the content and focus of their functional assessments, particularly regarding activities and participation, and the type of information shared during patient handovers. These qualitative data were subsequently analyzed thematically and mapped onto the ICF domains to capture contextual nuances in assessment practices (WHO, 2002, 2013). Demographic data, including gender, professional qualifications, department, age, and years of clinical experience, were also collected.

Data Collection

Data were collected from February to March 2025 through an online survey using a commercial research firm’s verified panel of licensed nurses across Japan. The firm distributed the questionnaire to eligible nurses who met the inclusion criteria. Participants who consented to the study accessed the web-based questionnaire via an anonymized URL and submitted their responses electronically. All data were automatically stored in a password-protected database accessible only to the author.

Data Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, and percentages) were calculated for each hospital type. For categorical variables, the chi-square test was used to examine differences across hospital types (advanced acute, acute, recovery, and chronic care). When expected cell counts were less than five, Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate. One-way ANOVA was used to compare DCP-PEM scores and the distribution of ICF-based assessments across hospital types. The assumption of homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. If the assumption was met (p > 0.05), Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test was conducted. For variables expressed as proportions (e.g., frequency of assessment items), ANOVA with Tukey HSD post hoc tests was applied to compare mean percentages across hospital types.

If the assumption was not met, Welch’s ANOVA was employed, followed by the Games–Howell post hoc test, which does not assume equal variances and incorporates a built-in correction for multiple comparisons to control type I error risk. Missing data were evaluated for pattern and proportion. If present, listwise deletion was applied when the proportion was <5% and the data were considered missing completely at random. For variables with greater missingness, multiple imputation by chained equations was employed to address potential bias.

In addition to the quantitative analyses, a supplementary qualitative content analysis was conducted on free-text responses regarding nursing priorities during patient handovers across hospital functions. An inductive approach was used to identify meaningful units, which were coded and grouped thematically. Coding was conducted independently by the first author and reviewed by a qualitative research supervisor. Discrepancies were resolved through iterative discussions to ensure credibility and consistency. Subsequently, a framework analysis (Gale et al., 2013) was applied to map the codes onto ICF domains, with particular focus on “activities” and “participation.” This process ensured alignment between the qualitative findings and the ICF framework guiding the study.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of St. Luke’s International University (Approval No. 24A-101) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants received written information outlining the purpose, procedures, voluntary nature, and data confidentiality of the study. After reading the information, participants indicated their consent by checking a box on the questionnaire to confirm their agreement. Participation was voluntary, and participants could withdraw from the study at any time before submitting the questionnaire by choosing not to complete it. However, once the questionnaire was submitted, consent could no longer be withdrawn due to the anonymity of the responses. Thus, written informed consent was explicitly obtained and confirmed before data collection.

Results

All 200 responses were complete and included in the analysis, and no imputation was required. The findings correspond to the instruments and variables described earlier, including the Discharge Planning–Process Evaluation Measurement (DCP-PEM), the range of functional assessment tools, and the ICF-based assessment domains.

A qualitative framework analysis of free-text responses regarding nursing handovers during patient transfers revealed both shared priorities and context-specific emphases across hospital types (Table 6). Across settings, nurses consistently emphasized essential clinical information, treatment and care planning, patient safety, and effective interdisciplinary communication, reflecting a baseline standard in transitional care practices. However, the content and emphasis of handovers varied according to the distinct roles and care objectives of each hospital type. In advanced acute and acute care hospitals, nurses focus on patients’ immediate clinical conditions, rapid detection of changes, and prompt interventions, consistent with the urgency of high-acuity environments. In recovery-phase hospitals, handovers highlighted rehabilitation progress, functional recovery, and psychosocial well-being, reflecting the holistic, patient-centered goals of care during the recovery phase. In chronic care hospitals, handovers are centered on long-term management, coordination of home care services, and sustaining quality of life, emphasizing the integrative nature of chronic care and the importance of continuity beyond the hospital. Collectively, these findings demonstrate both commonalities and context-specific differences in how nurses communicate functional status information across hospital types, suggesting that while core handover elements are consistent, flexible frameworks are needed to accommodate the unique priorities and patient needs inherent in each care setting.

Discussion

Principal Findings

To our knowledge, this is the first cross-sectional study to investigate how nurses assess patients’ functional status across different hospital types in Japan using the ICF framework. The primary objective was to identify potential gaps in assessment practices among these institutions.

The findings revealed no significant differences in discharge planning assessments across hospital types, suggesting that nursing practices related to discharge support are relatively consistent regardless of institutional function. Similarly, the weighting of ICF domains did not vary significantly between hospital types. According to reports, acute care assessments typically emphasize physical function (Saleh et al., 2024), whereas recovery and chronic care settings focus more on activity, participation, and quality of life (Wong et al., 2023). However, our findings reveal a consistent prioritization of physical function across all hospital types (Van Puymbroeck et al., 2014). This convergence across settings suggests that distinctions traditionally observed between acute, recovery, and chronic phases may be narrowing in actual nursing practice, indicating a shared emphasis on physical recovery as a dominant assessment focus. One possible explanation for this trend is the strong link between physical function recovery and a patient’s ability to regain independence in daily life (de Oliveira Almeida et al., 2023; Triantis & Liu, 2024). In acute care settings, shortened hospital stays may cause the emphasis on physical function to extend into the recovery phase, where it continues to be a primary goal of care. Physical function is relatively easy to quantify using objective measures such as walking ability, muscle strength, and respiratory capacity. These metrics enable healthcare professionals to track patient progress and evaluate the effectiveness of rehabilitation and care interventions over time. Despite its relevance in post-discharge life, participation remains under-assessed due to conceptual ambiguity and difficulty in quantification (Amsters et al., 2022). This highlights the urgent need for valid, context-sensitive tools that can capture patients’ potential for social reintegration. The under-assessment of participation can be explained by the time constraints and task-oriented nature of acute and critical care settings, wherein nurses prioritize immediate physical recovery and basic daily functioning. Moreover, a comprehensive assessment of participation often requires interdisciplinary collaboration. It also involves tools that are not routinely employed by nurses, which poses challenges to integration into everyday clinical practice. Nonetheless, comprehensive assessments incorporating participation-related domains are essential for planning a patient’s reintegration into society. The development of simple, reliable tools to assess these aspects remains a key challenge for future research. However, the neglect of participation and contextual factors underscores the need for a balanced application of the ICF framework.

Significant differences were observed in the use of patient assessment tools, including the FIM, care-needs level, and the All Japan Geriatric Facilities ICF-based Care Management Staging Tool. FIM was most frequently used in recovery-phase hospitals but was less common in acute care settings (So et al., 2019). FIM has proven useful for rehabilitation planning, discharge coordination, and evaluating treatment effectiveness (Ministry of Health Labour and Welfare Japan, 2022; Toi et al., 2024). However, its use in acute care is limited by rapid and unpredictable changes in patient condition.

Standardizing assessment timing and resolving interprofessional differences in scoring remain additional challenges. In ICUs, other tools such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment, and the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) are often preferred. These instruments may better capture the complexity of acute-phase conditions than the FIM alone (Denehy et al., 2013). More important than the choice of any single tool is the continuity of patient evaluation across care settings. Because the ultimate goal is not merely discharge or transfer but reintegration into society, assessments should bridge hospital care and post-discharge life. Interestingly, this study found that interdisciplinary discharge planning conferences involving external professionals were less common in recovery and chronic hospitals than in acute care hospitals. This suggests that nurses in recovery and chronic settings may bear greater responsibility for rehabilitation-related assessments, with implications for workload and the need for enhanced training.

This study also examined assessments related to the ICF domain of “participation,” a crucial factor for social reintegration. Significant differences were observed in the evaluation of familial roles and community outings. Familial roles were assessed less frequently in chronic hospitals, likely due to the prolonged hospitalization of patients with limited prospects for returning home. Conversely, the evaluation of outings was less common in acute care hospitals, where time constraints and fast-paced environments hinder broader assessments beyond hospitalization. When patients transition to another healthcare facility, functional assessments may continue. However, when patients are discharged home, critical aspects of social participation may be overlooked. These findings emphasize the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and outpatient follow-up to ensure comprehensive evaluation. Emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, cross-setting data integration, and tele-nursing may also offer innovative solutions for long-term patient support.

Globally, facilitating the social reintegration of patients recovering from high-intensity treatments, such as those with PICS, is a critical objective (Kamdar et al., 2020). Beyond improving survival rates, enhancing post-discharge quality of life and independence is essential. Patients and families often become aware of the challenges to independence only after discharge. However, little is known about the extent to which post-discharge activities and participation, as defined by the ICF, are evaluated in outpatient follow-ups.

As healthcare systems face aging populations and rising demands, a paradigm shift is necessary to better understand post-discharge needs better and develop effective strategies for support. Future studies should clarify the needs of patients and families regarding social reintegration and determine how healthcare systems can meet these needs. Ultimately, a more balanced and interdisciplinary application of the ICF framework, extending beyond physical domains, is required to support patients’ full reintegration into society after hospitalization.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it focuses exclusively on nurses and does not incorporate perspectives from other professionals, such as therapists, physicians, or medical social workers. Second, it does not link assessments to specific patient diagnoses or clinical conditions, making it unclear how practices vary by population. Third, the cross-sectional design precludes the tracking of changes over time or the establishment of causality. Finally, only univariate analyses were performed. This decision aligns with the exploratory aim of the study. However, future research with larger sample sizes and hypothesis-driven designs should incorporate multivariate approaches to control for confounders and strengthen generalizability.

Conclusion

This study found that nurses’ discharge planning practices were consistent across hospital types in Japan; however, functional assessments remained heavily focused on physical domains, with limited attention to “activities” and “participation” as defined in the ICF. Recovery-phase hospitals more frequently used standardized tools and addressed psychosocial factors, but participation-related assessments were generally underutilized. These findings highlight a need to strengthen ICF-based assessments that encompass social and contextual dimensions. Future efforts should focus on developing practical tools and interprofessional strategies to promote comprehensive, patient-centered evaluations during care transitions.
