Untargeted metabolomics reveals remodeling of hepatic metabolic networks and potential tumor-associated metabolic signatures induced by ALV-J in Luhua chickens
Authors: Yajun Wang, Xiangyu Xiao, Ke Ding, Zuhua Yu, Jian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Avian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV-J) represents a major pathogen that severely threatens the poultry industry. The liver contains abundant sinusoidal blood vessels with slow blood flow, which facilitates the enrichment and replication of viral particles; it serves as the primary target organ of ALV-J and the site with the highest tumor incidence. Nevertheless, the hepatic metabolic alterations triggered during ALV-J infection and their potential links to disease progression remain poorly understood. This study aimed to characterize the hepatic metabolic remodeling profile of Luhua chickens upon ALV-J infection using untargeted metabolomics. One-day-old Luhua chickens were divided into an ALV-J-infected group and a control group. Liver tissues were harvested at 60 days post-infection (dpi), and untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Differential metabolites and perturbed metabolic pathways were identified through multivariate statistical analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. In total, 434 differential metabolites were identified, including 241 metabolites detected in the positive ion mode and 193 metabolites in the negative ion mode. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that carbon metabolism (rich factor = 0.060, P = 0.042) and oxidative phosphorylation (rich factor = 0.050, P = 0.021) exhibited the strongest enrichment, followed by biosynthesis of amino acids, tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, riboflavin metabolism and glutathione metabolism. Further annotation of differential metabolites demonstrated that succinate and fumarate, two TCA cycle intermediates positioned at the crossroad of carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, were significantly accumulated in the infected group. Preliminary transcriptional analysis of key metabolic enzyme genes within these pathways, including SDHB, FH, GLS2, ASL and PC, showed that only ASL was significantly upregulated, while the remaining genes displayed no significant differences. This finding implies that metabolite accumulation may not be fully governed at the transcriptional level. In summary, ALV-J infection triggers extensive hepatic metabolic remodeling centered on the reprogramming of carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. This study, for the first time in an avian model, identified the alterations of succinate and fumarate as potential tumor-associated metabolic signatures. These findings provide novel mechanistic insights into the metabolic basis underlying hepatic pathological progression induced by persistent ALV-J infection. The identified key metabolites and pathways also supply candidate targets for developing early disease warning and metabolic intervention strategies. Longitudinal investigations and functional assays are warranted in future work to further validate the direct contribution of these metabolic shifts to ALV-J-associated tumorigenesis.

Introduction

Avian leukosis virus (ALV) is an important retrovirus that infects poultry and inflicts substantial economic losses on the global poultry industry. Among its subgroups, avian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV-J) exhibits high pathogenicity and primarily induces myeloid tumors (myelocytomatosis), hemangiomas, and hepatosplenomegaly in broiler and layer chickens (Fan et al., 2025; Payne and Nair, 2012). This virus possesses robust evolutionary capacity, and recent reports demonstrate that it has spread to indigenous chicken breeds in China, accompanied by prominent mutational trends and diversified pathological manifestations (Liu et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2020; Geng et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). To date, no effective vaccines or therapeutic drugs are available against ALV-J, and disease control relies mainly on costly and time-consuming flock eradication programs.

ALV-J can extensively invade multiple tissues and organs including the liver, spleen, bone marrow, kidney, and blood vessels, triggering systemic neoplastic disease (Cheng et al., 2010; Fan et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2019). Among these organs, the liver, which is characterized by abundant hepatic sinusoids and slow blood flow that facilitates the enrichment and replication of viral particles, represents one of the primary target organs for ALV-J infection and the site with the highest incidence of virus-induced tumorigenesis (Cheng et al., 2010). In ALV-J-positive chickens, gross lesions prior to tumor formation are mostly manifested as hepatomegaly; in severe cases, the enlarged liver may fill the abdominal cavity, presenting a fragile texture prone to rupture (Xu et al., 2022). As the core target organ attacked by ALV-J, the liver displays characteristic lesions that provide important clues for clinical diagnosis. Therefore, dissecting host–pathogen interactions in chicken liver at the metabolic level is critical for exploring novel control strategies and early diagnostic biomarkers.

Metabolomics enables comprehensive profiling of small-molecule metabolites within biological systems and serves as a powerful tool for elucidating disease mechanisms and discovering novel biomarkers (Chen et al., 2021; Wishart, 2016). The metabolome represents the terminal phenotypic readout of gene and protein expression and is highly sensitive to pathological alterations. Viruses frequently hijack host cellular metabolism to support their own replication, thereby inducing metabolic reprogramming in host cells (Xu et al., 2024; Xue et al., 2022). During the replication of enveloped viruses, lipid metabolism is often remodeled to supply membrane structures for viral assembly, while reprogramming of nucleotide and amino acid metabolism provides raw materials for viral genome replication and protein synthesis (Ariav et al., 2021).

Previous metabolomic studies on ALV-J infection have mostly been performed using plasma samples or in vitro cell culture models (Chen et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). Plasma metabolomics reflects systemic responses and cannot accurately capture local metabolic events occurring in key target organs; in vitro cell models lack the complex physiological milieu of living organisms, without neuroendocrine and immune crosstalk. The liver, as the primary target organ of ALV-J, often sustains severe pathological damage, including hepatomegaly, necrosis, and extensive infiltration of myeloid tumor cells (Xu et al., 2022). Given the liver’s central role in systemic metabolism, targeted metabolomic investigation of the liver during ALV-J infection is urgently required.

In this study, untargeted metabolomics based on liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) was applied to profile liver samples from Luhua chickens experimentally infected with ALV-J. By screening key differential metabolites and characterizing perturbed metabolic pathways, we aim to reveal tissue-specific hepatic metabolic response features during the early stage of ALV-J infection, laying a theoretical foundation for identifying potential metabolic early-warning biomarkers prior to the onset of hepatic tumorigenesis in chickens.

Materials and Methods

Animal testing and sample Collection

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the International Guiding Principles for Biomedical Research Involving Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Henan University of Science and Technology (Approval No. HAUST‑025‑P0310018). Two‑day‑old healthy Luhua chickens with similar genetic backgrounds and negative for ALV‑J plasma nucleic acid testing were randomly assigned to two groups: an ALV‑J‑infected group (n = 19) and a healthy control group (n = 19). Chickens in the ALV‑J‑infected group received a single intramuscular injection of 0.2 mL of ALV‑J strain HN24HT02 (isolated from Hetian chicken in Fujian Province, China, 2023; GenBank accession No. PX108887) (10³·⁸ TCID₅₀) per chicken, while those in the control group received an equal volume of sterile phosphate‑buffered saline (PBS). All chickens were reared under identical conditions. On days 15, 30, 45, and 60 post‑infection, all chickens were weighed and anticoagulated blood samples were collected to assess growth performance and viral infection dynamics. At 60 days post‑infection, all chickens were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation. The thymus, bursa of Fabricius, spleen and liver of chickens were collected for organ index statistics. Part of the liver tissues were rapidly excised, rinsed with sterile physiological saline to remove blood, snap‑frozen on dry ice, and stored at –80°C until further analysis.

Metabolite extraction and LC–MS/MS analysis

Approximately 30 mg of each liver tissue sample was weighed into a 2 mL tube, mixed with 200 µL of pre‑chilled water, and homogenized with steel beads using a tissue grinder (55 Hz, 60 s, twice). Then, 800 µL of methanol : acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added, and the mixture was ultrasonicated for 30 min, followed by freezing at –20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, 800 µL of supernatant was collected and vacuum‑dried. The residue was reconstituted in 150 µL of 50% methanol containing 5 ppm 2‑chlorophenylalanine, vortexed for 30 s, and centrifuged again. The final supernatant was filtered through a 0.22‑µm membrane and transferred to vials for analysis. A total of six biological replicates from the control group and six biological replicates from the ALV-J-infected group were randomly selected and used for metabolomic sequencing. A pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing 10–20 µL of each filtered extract (QC omitted if total samples ≤5).

Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) at 40 °C, with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min and injection volume of 2 µL. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B). The gradient program was: 0 min, 5% B; 1 min, 5% B; 4.7 min, 95% B; 6 min, 95% B; 6.1 min, 5% B; 8.5 min, 5% B. Mass spectrometry was carried out on a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 equipped with a HESI source, operating in both positive (spray voltage +3.5 kV) and negative (–3.0 kV) ion modes under data‑dependent acquisition (DDA). Full‑scan MS1 resolution was 60,000 over m/z 70–1000, and the top‑4 precursors were selected for MS/MS fragmentation (HCD, 30% collision energy) at 15,000 resolution. QC samples were injected at the beginning (2–4 injections for equilibration) and every 5–10 samples during the run to monitor system stability.

Data processing and statistical analysis

The raw data files were imported into MS‑DIAL software (version 4.9.221218) for peak picking, alignment, filtering, and metabolite identification. Peaks that were not detected in more than 50 % of the QC samples were removed. Missing values for the remaining peaks were filled using the software’s gap‑filling algorithm, followed by data normalization. Metabolite annotation was performed against the PSNGM (PerSonalbio Next‑Generation Metabolomics Database), which integrates an in‑house standard library, the mzCloud library (https://www.mzcloud.org/), LIPID MAPS (https://www.lipidmaps.org/), HMDB (https://hmdb.ca/), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), NIST_2020_MSMS, and an AI‑predicted MS/MS spectral library. The main search parameters were set as follows: MS1 tolerance for identification, 0.01; MS2 tolerance for identification, 0.05; smoothing level, 3; minimum peak height, 10 000; minimum peak width, 5; mass slice width, 0.05; and identification score cut‑off, 70.

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (BORG BIO, # B619-100), followed by cDNA synthesis with reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, # R333-01). cDNA amplification was performed using SYBR Green (GenStar, # A208-05) with the CFX Connect™ Real-Time System (BIO-RAD, USA). The results were calculated by 2ΔΔCt method with the normalization to GAPDH. All primers were synthesized from General Biotechnology (Anhui, China) and primers sequences were shown in Table S1 .

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). All data in this study were generated from at least three independent replicated experiments. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was tested using the two-tailed Student’s t-test and is indicated by p values; *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001 were considered to indicate significance.

Results

At 60 days post‑infection, all chickens were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, and the thymus, spleen, bursa of Fabricius, and liver were collected for organ index calculation. The results demonstrated that ALV‑J infection induced significant atrophy of the thymus and bursa of Fabricius (Fig. 1C, D, P < 0.05), while the spleen and liver were markedly enlarged (Fig. 1E, F, P < 0.05), indicating that viral infection caused damage to both immune organs and the liver.

Histopathological examination by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that in the control group, hepatocytes showed no degeneration or necrosis, hepatic sinusoids showed no dilation or congestion, and the interstitium showed no pathological changes such as vascular dilation, hemorrhage, or inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1G). In the infected group, mild hepatocellular degeneration and necrosis (black arrows) were observed, along with mild hepatic sinusoidal congestion (blue arrows) (Fig. 1H).

Data quality and multivariate analysis

In this study, quality control (QC) samples were used during LC‑MS analysis to evaluate the stability of the LC‑MS platform. The Pearson correlation coefficients of all QC samples in both positive and negative ion modes were greater than 0.9, confirming the high stability and reliability of the analytical system and the suitability of the data for downstream analysis. To visualize the overall metabolic differences between the ALV‑J‑infected and control groups, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS‑DA) was performed. The score plots showed clear and distinct separation between the two groups in both ion modes (Fig. 2A, B), indicating that ALV‑J infection induced significant and detectable reprogramming of the hepatic metabolome. Permutation tests with 200 permutations were further conducted to assess the robustness of PLS-DA models (Fig. S1A). In the positive ion mode, the model yielded R²X = 0.321, R²Y = 0.990 and Q² = 0.763; in the negative ion mode, R²X = 0.287, R²Y = 0.995 and Q² = 0.722. Identification of differential metabolites

The distribution of differential metabolites is summarized in the volcano plot (Fig. 2C, D). The large number of differential metabolites highlights the profound impact of ALV‑J on hepatic metabolism, with the major top 10 differential metabolites listed in Table S2. A total of 434 differential metabolites were identified, of which 241 were detected in positive ion mode (134 upregulated and 107 downregulated) and 193 in negative ion mode (99 upregulated and 94 downregulated)(Fig. 2E, F). Hierarchical cluster analysis of the differential metabolites between the control and ALV‑J‑infected chicken livers revealed that samples were distinctly separated into two major branches according to treatment groups, with high intra‑group homogeneity and consistent metabolic profiles, as well as pronounced inter‑group discrimination. These results confirm the validity of the experimental grouping and the reliability of sample reproducibility. The clustering outcomes further classified the differential metabolites into two distinct expression patterns: one group of metabolites was enriched in the livers of healthy control chickens but significantly decreased following ALV‑J infection, whereas the other group was specifically accumulated in the livers of ALV‑J‑infected chickens and maintained at lower levels in the normal controls (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these findings indicate that ALV‑J infection dramatically disrupts hepatic metabolic homeostasis in chickens, resulting in characteristic and systematic alterations in the abundance of numerous endogenous metabolites.

ALV-J infection induces hepatic metabolic remodeling centered on carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that carbon metabolism (Rich factor = 0.060, P = 0.042) and oxidative phosphorylation (Rich factor = 0.050, P = 0.021) exhibited the highest enrichment levels in the livers of ALV‑J‑infected chickens, suggesting that cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis may represent the core processes subject to regulation. Additionally, pathways such as amino acid biosynthesis (Rich factor = 0.040), the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Rich factor = 0.030), and riboflavin metabolism (Rich factor = 0.020) were also significantly enriched, further supporting the pivotal role of metabolic reprogramming in the biological processes. Although some pathways (e.g., pyruvate metabolism and ABC transporters) had Rich factor values close to zero, their P values remained significant, which may reflect that only a very small number of genes in these pathways were altered or that the enrichment signal was relatively weak. Furthermore, pathways including cofactor biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism showed a certain degree of enrichment, reflecting a concomitant compensatory response at the levels of antioxidant defense and biosynthesis (Fig. 2H).

A metabolite-pathway interaction network was constructed based on differential metabolites to visualize the interactions between pathways and related differential metabolites (Fig. 3A). Carbon metabolism provides substrates for the TCA cycle, and the reducing equivalents generated by the TCA cycle supply oxidative phosphorylation. Screening of differential metabolites demonstrated that two TCA cycle intermediates at the intersection of carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation, succinic acid and fumaric acid, were significantly accumulated in the liver of ALV-J-infected chickens. The fold change (FC) of succinic acid was 1.56 (P = 0.0128), and the FC of fumaric acid was 7.27 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis showed that the area under the curve (AUC) value of succinic acid was 0.972 and that of fumaric acid was 1.000, indicating that both metabolites could effectively distinguish ALV-J-infected samples from control samples (Fig. S1B). To characterize the transcriptional features underlying these metabolic changes, the mRNA expression levels of metabolic enzyme genes succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit B (SDHB), fumarate hydratase (FH), glutaminase 2 (GLS2), argininosuccinate lyase (ASL) and pyruvate carboxylase (PC) were detected. The qPCR results revealed that only ASL was significantly upregulated (P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in the transcription levels of SDHB, FH, GLS2 and PC (Fig. 3C). ASL participates in arginine metabolism and mediates fumarate production. The transcription levels of SDHB and FH remained unchanged, whereas their catalytic substrates succinic acid and fumaric acid accumulated markedly.

Discussion

Avian leukosis virus subgroup J (ALV‑J), a retrovirus, is highly oncogenic in infected chickens, with liver tumors being the most frequently observed neoplastic manifestation. In this study, we performed the first comprehensive metabolomic analysis of liver tissues from experimentally ALV‑J‑infected Luhua chickens. Our results demonstrate that ALV‑J infection induces profound and systemic reprogramming of the hepatic metabolome, affecting a wide range of metabolites and pathways. This work complements and extends previous metabolomic studies on ALV‑J that utilized plasma or cultured cells(Chen et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2024). By focusing on liver tissue in a live animal model, we provide a more direct and organ‑specific view of the pathological processes. The large number of differential metabolites identified (434) highlights the local and complex nature of the metabolic response within the target organ, compared with the 68 metabolites previously reported in plasma(Chen et al., 2021). Moreover, while earlier studies reported lipid metabolism as the most prominent alteration in infected chicken embryo fibroblasts(Xu et al., 2024), our in vivo liver data reveal a more diversified response, with glutathione metabolism, arginine/proline metabolism, and carbon metabolism being the most significantly enriched pathways. This discrepancy underscores key differences between in vitro and in vivo models: cultured fibroblasts may primarily reflect the virus's direct demand for lipid assembly, whereas the hepatic response integrates viral replication with systemic host immune reactions, endocrine signaling, and the central metabolic functions of the organ. Notably, liver samples in this study were harvested at 60 dpi. At this time point, viremia had declined in some chickens, and no typical neoplastic lesions were observed histologically. Therefore, the metabolic alterations identified herein should be interpreted as adaptive metabolic changes associated with persistent infection, rather than metabolic signatures of mature tumors. Combined with the viremia data and metabolomic findings, 60 dpi most likely represents a pre-neoplastic stage after viremia recession, corresponding to the stage of “compensatory antioxidant response” and early “metabolic priming”. The observed metabolic alterations are most likely induced by the combined effects of persistent viral infection, host immune regulation, and cellular adaptive responses, rather than a single causal factor.

This study’s pathway‑enrichment analysis revealed that carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation represented the two most perturbed pathways. They constitute the core modules of glucose catabolism and mitochondrial energy metabolism, respectively, and are tightly interconnected via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle; hence, they can be regarded as two central hubs for ALV‑J‑triggered hepatic metabolic remodeling. Carbon metabolism showed the highest enrichment (Rich factor = 0.060, P = 0.042), indicating global reprogramming of central carbon metabolism in hepatocytes upon ALV‑J infection. Extensive rewiring of central carbon metabolism is a hallmark of tumors: cancer cells tend to maintain high glycolytic rates under aerobic conditions and convert most glucose into lactate, which is the well‑known Warburg effect (Long et al., 2026). HK2 is a signature molecule mediating the Warburg effect, and the enrichment of the carbon‑metabolism pathway suggests that glycolysis is activated in ALV‑J‑infected livers and contributes to hepatic pathological progression. Reprogrammed carbon metabolism not only supplies ATP and biosynthetic precursors for rapid cellular proliferation but also provides carbon skeletons for other pathways such as the pentose‑phosphate pathway and serine biosynthesis. Accordingly, the prominent enrichment of carbon‑metabolism pathways reflects a fundamental shift in the energy‑metabolism mode of hepatocytes after ALV‑J infection and represents a key metabolic feature associated with disease progression.

Oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS; Rich factor = 0.050, P = 0.021) ranked second in enrichment magnitude and, together with the TCA cycle (Rich factor = 0.030, P = 0.021), forms the core module of mitochondrial energy metabolism. The traditional view holds that tumor cells rely predominantly on aerobic glycolysis rather than OXPHOS for energy production. Nevertheless, accumulating evidence demonstrates that OXPHOS is indispensable for tumorigenesis (Chen et al., 2025). Certain tumor subsets are highly dependent on OXPHOS; dysfunctional OXPHOS generates excessive reactive‑oxygen species (ROS) with oncogenic and mutagenic potential, causing damage to lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids in both mitochondria and the nucleus (Han et al., 2026). As a hub linking carbon metabolism and OXPHOS, impaired TCA‑cycle function can directly trigger abnormal accumulation of metabolic intermediates. In the present differential‑metabolite screening, significant accumulation of the TCA‑cycle intermediates succinate and fumarate was observed in the infected group. In human‑oncology research, these two metabolites are classified as signal‑regulating oncometabolites. In hereditary human tumors driven by loss‑of‑function mutations in SDH or FH, massive accumulation of these metabolites competitively inhibits α‑ketoglutarate‑dependent dioxygenases: succinate inhibits prolyl hydroxylase domain (PHD) proteins to stabilize HIF‑1α and mediates pseudohypoxia and angiogenesis; fumarate suppresses ten‑eleven translocation (TET) demethylases, eliciting DNA hypermethylation and epithelial‑mesenchymal transition (Lanzetti, 2024). Notably, such inhibitory effects depend on high intracellular metabolite concentrations and are not exclusively driven by gene mutations. Transcriptional profiling of key metabolic‑enzyme genes within these pathways revealed significant up‑regulation only of ASL (encoding argininosuccinate lyase), whereas SDHB, FH, GLS2 and PC exhibited no transcriptional alterations. Argininosuccinate lyase encoded by ASL catalyses argininosuccinate cleavage to produce arginine and fumarate and constitutes an important cytosolic source of fumarate. This finding implies that fumarate accumulation in infected hepatocytes may originate partly from ASL‑driven enhanced cytosolic production, besides post‑transcriptional mechanisms such as mitochondrial enzyme‑activity suppression (e.g., ROS‑mediated iron‑sulfur‑cluster damage) and substrate feedback. By contrast, succinate elevation detected in this study cannot be directly explained by ASL‑catalysed reactions, suggesting that its accumulation is mainly governed by mitochondrial post‑transcriptional regulation. Collectively, the coordinated enrichment of carbon metabolism and OXPHOS, coupled with the buildup of succinate and fumarate at their intersection, indicates that ALV‑J may remodel energy‑metabolism hubs to establish a metabolic microenvironment permissive for subsequent hepatic pathological progression. Nevertheless, whether these metabolic changes directly participate in the initiation and progression of ALV‑J‑associated tumorigenesis requires further functional validation including targeted metabolite quantification, enzyme‑activity assays and analysis of samples collected at tumour‑bearing stages.

In addition, amino‑acid biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism showed moderate enrichment in our dataset. The enriched amino‑acid‑biosynthesis pathway (Rich factor = 0.040, P = 0.021) points to elevated amino‑acid demand in ALV‑J‑exposed hepatocytes to furnish raw materials for protein synthesis and nucleotide generation (Liu et al., 2024). Enrichment of glutathione metabolism (P = 0.021) may reflect cellular defensive responses against oxidative stress arising from impaired OXPHOS, and could simultaneously confer metabolic advantages enabling cells to adapt to an oxidative microenvironment (Xue et al., 2025). These pathways interconnect with carbon metabolism and OXPHOS, jointly delineating the coordinated network of hepatocellular metabolic reprogramming induced by ALV‑J.

Conclusion

In summary, ALV-J infection triggers metabolic alterations in chicken livers, centered on the reprogramming of carbon metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation. Significant accumulation of the TCA-cycle intermediates succinate and fumarate occurs at the intersection of these two pathways. This pattern bears similarities to the oncometabolite signatures observed in human tumours, suggesting that ALV-J may remodel energy-metabolism hubs to establish a microenvironment conducive to subsequent pathological progression in hepatocytes. Furthermore, the coordinated alterations in amino-acid biosynthesis and glutathione metabolism further supply raw materials for cellular proliferation and facilitate defence against oxidative stress. Rather than isolated events, the above-mentioned pathway changes represent highly coordinated and interdependent metabolic adaptive processes that may participate in ALV-J-driven hepatic pathological progression in chickens. Nevertheless, whether these metabolic changes are directly involved in the initiation and progression of ALV-J-associated tumorigenesis awaits further longitudinal investigations and functional validation.
