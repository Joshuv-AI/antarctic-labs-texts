Feasibility and long-term outcomes of recanalization for De novo small vessel chronic total occlusions
Authors: Jen-Fang Cheng, Cheng-Ting Tsai, Chien-Lin Lee, Jiun-Yang Chiang, Shih-Chi Liu, Chi-Jen Chang, Chia-Pin Lin, Chi-Hung Huang, Jun-Ting Liou, Chia-Ti Tsai, Yi-Chih Wang, Juey-Jen Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of small vessel size on chronic total occlusions (CTO) interventions remains underexplored. We compared revascularization feasibility and follow-up outcomes between patients with small- and non-small vessel de novo CTOs.MethodsIn total, 493 consecutive patients (64.0 ± 11.4 years, 432 men) undergoing wiring-based de novo CTO interventions were divided into two groups: small vessels (reference vessel diameter < 2.75 mm, n = 245) and non-small vessels (n = 248). Procedural success and guidewire crossing time were compared. Among 449 patients with successful recanalization, long-term outcomes including target lesion failure [cardiac death, target vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI), and target lesion revascularization] were assessed over a median follow-up of 57.4 (30.2–87.3) months.ResultsWith increased J-CTO score, the procedural success rates remained comparable in the small vessel CTO group but declined significantly in non-small vessel CTO group when the score was ≥3 (82 vs. small vessel: 93%, p = 0.008). For lesions with a J-CTO score ≥ 3, multivariate analyses showed that the presence of small vessel was independently associated with procedural success (OR = 8.2, 95% CI = 3.2–21.1, p < 0.001), and a successful guidewire crossing time ≥ 30 min (OR = 3.0, 95% CI = 1.6–5.6, p = 0.001). Cox’ regression model showed that successfully recanalized small vessel CTOs was independently accompanied by a lower long-term risk of TVMI (HR = 0.10, 95% CI = 0.01–0.79, p = 0.029).ConclusionDespite the longer guidewire crossing time for small vessel CTOs with higher complexity, we demonstrated greater procedural feasibility and better long-term TVMI-free survival for small vessel CTO interventions. However, long-term outcomes of small vessels can be clinically silent and therefore might affect the study results.

Introduction

Despite advances in recanalization devices, refined techniques, and multiple practical algorithms [1], [2], [3] for chronic total occlusion (CTO) interventions, percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for CTO lesions remains technically challenging. Among patients undergoing PCI, the presence of lesions with small reference vessel diameter (RVD), which account for 40–50% of all coronary lesions [4], represent a distinct subgroup with different clinical and procedural characteristics compared with those with larger vessels [5]. The success rate of PCI is significantly lower in small vessels than in larger ones (92% vs. 95%) [5]. For CTO-PCI, scoring systems, such as the Japan CTO (Multicenter Chronic Total Occlusion Registry of Japan: J-CTO) score [6], Prospective Global Registry for the Study of Chronic Total Occlusion Intervention (PROGRESS) CTO score [7], and EuroCTO (CASTLE) score [8], incorporate clinical and angiographic factors to assess procedural difficulty. While these tools are widely used, the specific impact of small RVD on CTO revascularization remains unclear.

In the long-term, small vessel disease itself is a known risk factor for major adverse cardiovascular events [9], [10], and has been associated with nearly double the risk of target lesion failure (TLF) at 1 year even with drug-eluting stents (DES) implantation [11], [12]. For CTO-PCI, various predictors of outcomes have been identified, including diabetes mellitus [13], older age [14], chronic kidney disease (CKD) [15], and a higher J-CTO/PROGRESS score [16], [17]. However, few studies compared long-term outcomes between recanalized small and large vessel CTOs.

Given this gap, we investigated whether angiographically small vessel size affects procedural performance and long-term cardiac outcomes. We analyzed data from 493 consecutive patients with de novo CTOs undergoing PCI at a single center.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was based on a prospective registry approved by the Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (201904023RINC) in 2019. Retrospective data were collected from August 2014 (201907064RIND). The retrospective period was determined by the adoption of Gaia series (Asahi Intecc Medical, Japan) guidewires in our hospital, aligned with intraplaque guidewire tracking techniques for CTO-PCI developed by the Taiwan True-Lumen Tracking club [18]. The Gaia series guidewire technology was specifically used for their 1:1 torque response, tip deflection control, and enhanced maneuverability within the CTO segments. Written informed consent was obtained from the participants.

From August 2014 to July 2024, 578 consecutive patients undergoing CTO-PCI using the standardized wiring techniques by our team at the National Taiwan University Hospital were enrolled (Fig. 1). After excluding 85 patients with in-stent CTO, 493 patients with de novo CTO-PCI were included in the analysis. CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate of <60 ml/min/1.73 m2. Procedures and recanalization techniques

CTO was defined as a lesion with thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) grade 0 flow lasting ≥3 months [19]. Multivessel disease was defined as ≥50% stenosis in at least two major epicardial coronary arteries. Small vessel CTOs were defined as target occlusions with an RVD of 2.0 to <2.75 mm [20], [21] determined by quantitative coronary angiography. Distal vessel diameter was assessed using either antegrade or retrograde filling. When preprocedural angiography was inconclusive, the first angiogram after predilation with a small-diameter (1.0–1.5 mm) balloon was used to measure the RVD [22]. RVD and J-CTO score were determined by consensus between two independent operators (Dr. SC Liu and Dr. CL Lee).

The concept of intraplaque guidewire tracking techniques performed in this study, which aimed to minimize the extent of subintima creation and stenting and to preserve the antegrade flow for any side branch ≥1.5 mm from 5 mm before to 5 mm after the occluded segment in final coronary angiography, was developed by seven independent and high-volume CTO interventionists from the medical centers in northern Taiwan [18], [23], [24]. Complete intraplaque course was not required, but the final angiographic result was used to be accepted by consensus among experienced CTO operators. Briefly, antegrade wiring was attempted first in all cases, regardless of anatomical complexity. The timing of guidewire escalation/de-escalation and switching to a parallel-wire technique was left to operator discretion. When retrograde procedures were attempted, direct guidewire crossing with a softer tip load (mainly ≤1 g) or kissing guidewire technique was preferred. When both antegrade and retrograde wiring-based tracking techniques failed, reverse controlled antegrade and retrograde tracking or knuckle wire methods were used as rescue techniques. The total number of guidewires with different tip loads used for each recanalization procedure was counted. If any of “must preserved” side branches ≥1.5 mm was lost during the procedure, the antegrade flow must be restored to fulfil our criteria of successful recanalization. Intravascular imaging is recommended, but not mandatory. “Dissection and reentry” devices were not available during the data collection period. Second-generation drug-eluting stents (DES) were used for all stenting procedures. Additionally, the definition of procedural success also required restoration of TIMI grade 3 anterograde flow, postprocedural stenosis of <30%, and the absence of in-hospital major adverse cardiac or cerebrovascular events [cardiac death, Q-wave MI, stroke, or any repeat target lesion revascularization (TLR)]. The time for successful guidewire crossing and total procedure were recorded.

Follow-up

Among 449 patients with successful recanalization, long-term cardiac events including target lesion failure (TLF: cardiac death, target vessel MI, and clinically-driven TLR), and stent thrombosis were evaluated per Academic Research Consortium definitions [25]. Clinically-driven TLR was defined by the presence of signs/symptoms suggesting myocardial ischemia, positive non-invasive testing or coronary computed tomography angiography, and documented restenosis ≥50% occurring within 5 mm proximal or distal to the occlusion site in the follow-up angiography. Follow-up data were collected through regular clinic visits or telephone contact.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS version 25 software package (IBM Corp.). The distributional properties of continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and categorical variables including procedural success are expressed as percentages. The p-values listed in the last column were calculated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Procedural success rates were compared across J-CTO scores of 0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3 between patients with small and non-small vessel CTOs. Guidewire crossing time were compared among the 449 patients with successful recanalization. Based on the original J-CTO score study for de novo CTO lesions, guidewire crossing time ≥ 30 min was considered indicative of high wiring difficulty [6]. Therefore, we arbitrarily took 30 min as a prolonged time for successful guidewire crossing. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) to identify factors independently associated with procedural success in 241 patients with J-CTO score ≥ 3 and guidewire crossing time ≥ 30 min in 209 patients with J-CTO score ≥ 3 and successful recanalization. In the follow-up study, event-free survival from the index procedure to follow-up was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method among the 449 patients with successful recanalization. Patients lost to follow-up were right-censored at their last follow-up visit. The differences in the Kaplan–Meier curves of the time to event-free survival were initially compared using the log-rank test for patients with small and non-small CTOs. A multivariate Cox proportional hazards model was then used to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI for the effect of risk factors on time to cardiac events. Results

Comparisons of clinical features

In total, 493 consecutive patients (64.0 ± 11.4 years, 432 men) undergoing PCI for de novo CTOs were included in the analyses (Fig. 1). Compared with patients with non-small vessel occlusions, those with small vessel CTOs (N = 245, 49.7%) had lower percentages of male population (83 versus non-small: 92%, p = 0.003) and smoking (30 versus non-small: 44%, p = 0.002), lower body mass index (25.8 ± 3.7 versus non-small: 26.6 ± 3.8 kg/m2, p = 0.015), and higher percentages of diabetes (44 versus non-small: 35%, p = 0.041), CKD (28 vs. non-small: 18%, p = 0.008), and a history of stroke (8 vs. 4%, p = 0.055). Other clinical features were similar between the two groups (Table 1).

Comparisons of angiographic and procedural characteristics

In Table 2, patients with small vessel CTOs had a higher percentage of multivessel disease (93 vs. non-small vessel CTOs: 87%, p = 0.017). The small-vessel CTOs were located more at the left circumflex artery (LCX: 33 vs. non-small: 8%, p < 0.001) but less at the left anterior descending artery (32 vs. non-small: 41%, p = 0.041) and right coronary artery (34 vs. non-small: 50%, p < 0.001). The J-CTO score was lower in patients with small vessel CTOs than that in patients with non-small vessel CTOs (2.2 ± 1.4 versus 2.6 ± 1.3, p = 0.002), mainly due to lower percentages of blunt stump (49 versus 62%, p = 0.006) and presence of calcification (53 versus 65%, p = 0.013). The rate of successful recanalization was much higher for small vessel than non-small vessel CTOs (95 versus 87%, p = 0.002). The total stent length implanted was shorter in the small than non-small vessel group (55 ± 27 versus 62 ± 29 mm, p = 0.011).

Among the 449 patients with successful recanalization, the mean guidewire crossing time was relatively longer in patients with small vessel CTOs, but the difference was not significant when the J-CTO score was 0, 1, or 2 (Fig. 2B). However, for patients with the J-CTO score ≥ 3, the guidewire crossing time was significantly longer for small than non-small vessel CTOs (58 ± 50 vs. 36 ± 37 min, p < 0.001).

Follow-up of long-term cardiac events in 449 patients with successful recanalization

With a median follow-up of 57.4 (30.2–87.3) months, the incidence of cardiac death, target vessel MI, clinically-driven TLR, TLF, and definite/probable stent thrombosis was 2.2%, 2.2%, 8.7%, 9.8%, and 0.4%, respectively (Fig. 3). The Kaplan–Meier curve showed a significant difference in survival-free target vessel MI between recanalized small and non-small vessel occlusions (p = 0.018 by log-rank test, Fig. 4), but the differences in other long-term cardiac events were insignificant between the two groups. In the multivariate Cox’ proportional hazards model, patients with successfully recanalized CTOs had a lower risk of long-term target vessel MI (HR = 0.098, 95% CI = 0.012–0.788, p = 0.029) in the presence of small vessel lesions, but a higher risk of MI for those with CHF (HR = 4.51, 95% CI = 1.24–16.32, p = 0.022) (Table 4).Fig. 3Comparisons of long-term cardiac events between the small and non-small vessel CTO groups among the 449 patients with successfully recanalized CTO. Discussion

Patients undergoing small vessel CTO-PCI in the current study, characterized by more female gender, diabetes, presence of multivessel disease, cerebrovascular disease, CKD, and fewer smokers, were in agreement with most clinical associations with small vessel coronary artery disease reported in the literature [5], [12], [26], [27], [28]. Besides, the lesion complexity presenting as the J-CTO score or the percentages of evident calcification and occlusion length ≧20 mm in the study was also comparable to that in several CTO registries evaluating procedural feasibility of CTO-PCI [29], [30], [31]. The main findings of the study are as follows: (1) Contrary to expectations from non-CTO PCI literature, there was a higher success rate for small vessel CTO interventions. (2) Among occlusions with more lesion complexity as a J-CTO score ≥ 3, the higher procedural success rate for small vessel CTOs persisted. However, small-vessel CTOs were more likely to require longer guidewire crossing times (≥30 min), highlighting the potential challenges to wiring-based recanalization techniques used in the study for small vessel CTOs. (3) Over long-term follow-up, successful recanalization of small vessel CTOs was associated with a lower risk of target vessel MI. These findings merit further investigation to better understand the mechanisms underlying the procedural and prognostic advantages seen in small vessel CTO-PCI.

Previous studies have linked small RVD to lower procedural success rates of PCI [5], a trend not observed in the current CTO-PCI study. Conversely, we found higher success rates in small vessel CTOs, including those with higher lesion complexity. Several factors may account for this observation: First, the higher percentage of ACC/AHA type C lesions could be directly linked to the lower success rate of small vessel PCI [5], [32]. However, the effect of lesion type was minimal in the study because all CTOs, irrespective of vessel size, were type C lesions. Second, the procedural success of non-occluded small vessel PCI was mainly defined as the percent lumen gain and residual stenosis after balloon angioplasty [5]. However, in our study, the lumen gain or residual stenosis could be improved by implantation of DES with smaller sizes. Additionally, angiographic success is primarily determined by guidewire crossing for CTO-PCI. Abundant evidences suggested the association between the J-CTO score and feasibility of recanalization success [6], [29], [30], [33]. Therefore, the lower lesion complexity score in the small vessel group in the current study could partly contribute to the higher success rate of small vessel CTO-PCI.

Small vessel CTOs remained independently associated with procedural success even in lesions with a J-CTO score ≥ 3 (odds ratio: 8.23). However, these cases were also more likely to require a prolonged time (≥30 min) for successful guidewire crossing (odds ratio: 2.97). With a similar J-CTO score between the small and non-small vessel CTOs with a J-CTO score ≥ 3 (small vessel: 3.6 ± 0.6 versus non-small vessel CTOs: 3.5 ± 0.6, p = 0.906), the greater prevalence of LCX occlusions (small vessel: 33% versus non-small vessel CTOs: 10%, p < 0.001) may partly explain this difference. Although the impact of LCX occlusions on CTO-PCI was not evident in the J-CTO score or EuroCTO score [6], [8], the PROGRESS CTO score [7] showed that LCX CTO itself could serve as an independent predictor of procedural failure besides the presence of moderate/severe vessel tortuosity. This suggested more difficulty and potential time-consuming of successful guidewire crossing for LCX CTOs. Additionally, the relatively higher percent use of parallel wire technique for small vessel than non-small vessel group with a J-CTO score ≥ 3 and successful recanalization (small vessel: 24% versus non-small vessel CTOs: 15%, p = 0.096) further underscores the intrinsic wiring challenges of small vessel lesions which were more often at distal locations. Moreover, based on the mean time of 58 min (Fig. 2B) and the 3-fold risk of guidewire crossing ≥30 min required for successful small vessel CTO-PCI with a J-CTO score ≥ 3, the much longer average fluoroscopy time (63 min) used for small vessel CTO-PCI, when compared with 38 min in the PROGRESS-CTO registry for the group with a vessel diameter ≤ 2.75 mm [31], could potentially further contribute to the higher success rate of small vessel CTO-PCI in the study.

When compared with larger vessel interventions, PCI for small vessel was associated with a higher risk of major adverse cardiac events in the long-term, even with the use of newer-generation DES [9], [10], [11], [12]. However, the present study showed a better outcome with a lower rate of target vessel MI among recanalized small vessel CTOs. When considering the lesion-related prognostic factors among the 449 patients with successful recanalization, we found that the J-CTO score was significantly lower (small vessel: 2.2 ± 1.4 versus non-small vessel: 2.5 ± 1.3, p = 0.019) with a lower percentage of the J-CTO score ≥ 3 (small vessel: 42% versus non-small vessel: 51%, p = 0.048) in the small vessel CTO group. Forouzandeh et al. reported that the J-CTO score was an independent predictor of major adverse cardiac events (HR = 1.32), and the HR for J-CTO score ≥ 3 versus 0–2 was 2.04 [17]. Additionally, small vessel CTOs with successful recanalization were characterized by a lower percentage of evident calcification within the occlusions (small vessel: 52% versus non-small vessel: 62%, p = 0.039). In a study evaluating 5-year outcomes of 19,833 patients with DES implantation by Guedeney et al. [34], the presence of moderate or severe calcification was accompanied by a relative 23% increase in target vessel MI, when compared with those with no or mild calcification. Although the percent use of intravascular imaging was similar between the small and non-small vessel occlusions in the present study, the potential non-optimization of stent implantation due to moderate/severe calcification would partly contribute to more long-term adverse events for non-small vessel CTOs with a higher percentage of evident calcification. Indeed, the percentages of diabetes and CKD were higher in the small than non-small vessel group, and the hazard ratio of CKD was 2.8, even not reaching statistical significance, for target vessel MI (Table 4). However, the percentages of both risk factors were relatively lower in the small vessel group, and their prognostic impacts could be partly neutralized by the lower percentage of smoking than that in non-small vessel group (30 versus 44%, p = 0.002) (supplementary Table 5) and therefore possibly outweighed by the lower lesion complexity in the small vessel group. Besides, good distal run-off after CTO-PCI is essential for long-term vessel patency. When compared with non-small vessel CTO, small vessel CTO is usually located at more distal vessel part with fewer downstream branches. Thus, preservation of the sizable branches within the occlusion segment would be rather important to maintain adequate run-off for small vessel than non-small vessel CTO-PCI. As the techniques used throughout the whole study aimed to preserve the antegrade flow for any side branch ≥1.5 mm from 5 mm before to 5 mm after the occluded segment, this could at least minimize the differential prognostic impact of distal run-off on long-term outcomes between the two groups with successful CTO-PCI in the study.

The study has some limitations. First, the definition of small vessel disease varies in literature. Therefore, the results may change if different definitions are applied. However, the comparable clinical features of the current small vessel CTO group still suggested the significance of the cut-off point for vessel size used in the study. Second, the preferred strategy for CTO interventions and recanalization success rates differs among operators and studies. Thus, the study results could change with the recanalization techniques used and respective success rates. For example, a recent large-scale CTO-PCI study derived from the PROGRESS registry suggested that “dissection and reentry” devices and retrograde techniques could be more favored for non-small vessel CTOs with a vessel diameter > 2.75 mm [31]. As the techniques used throughout the whole study mainly focused more on guidewire manipulation, particularly for antegrade approach, the fewer application of retrograde techniques might compromise the procedural success rate of the non-small vessel CTO group in the current study. Because the procedural and prognostic impacts derived by operator-dependent confounding factors usually cannot be completely avoided in CTO-PCI studies, the present study using the same wiring-based recanalization techniques for all patients would potentially minimize the heterogeneity. Third, owing to insurance coverage, the prognostic impact of drug-coated balloons on recanalized CTOs could not be tested in this study. However, the REC-CAGEFREE I trial, investigating de novo, non-complex coronary artery disease with either small or non-small vessel [35], suggests that drug-coated balloon angioplasty with rescue stenting failed to achieve non-inferiority compared with the intended DES implantation in terms of device-oriented composite endpoint at 2 years. Fourth, the application of intravascular imaging was around 50% in the study. The percent use was comparable with most CTO studies. However, the beneficial effect with the use of IVUS-guided compared with angiography-guided for long (≥28 mm) DES implantation [36] still could alter long-term cardiac outcomes in the current study. Fifth, small vessel failure may not lead to significant symptoms. Therefore, the long-term prognostic results of the study, which mainly relied on clinically-driven evaluation, could be somewhat deviated, particular in the small-vessel arm. However, the end-point evaluation of the study was still following the current follow-up methods for most small vessel PCI [9], [10], [11], [12] and CTO-PCI studies [13], [14], [15], [16], [17].

Conclusions

Contrary to previous reports in small vessel PCI, our study demonstrated a higher procedural success rate in small vessel CTO interventions. For small vessel lesions with a J-CTO score ≥ 3, however, a higher probability of a longer time (≥30 min) required for successful guidewire crossing still suggested the underlying challenges to wiring for small vessel occlusions. In the long-term, successfully recanalized small vessel CTOs were associated with a lower risk of target vessel MI. These findings suggest that small-vessel CTO PCI may offer clinical benefits. However, long-term outcomes of small vessels can be clinically silent and therefore might affect the study results, and warrant further study to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.
