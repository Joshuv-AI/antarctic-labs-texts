Educational Interventions for Haemodialysis Patients in the Transplant Process: A Systematic Review
Authors: Ester Oriol‐Vila, Laura Rota‐Musoll, Esmeralda Molina‐Robles, Carme Roure‐Pujol, Emilia Chiverches‐Pérez
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo systematically evaluate empirical studies investigating nursing educational interventions for patients with haemodialysis at the different stages of cadaveric kidney transplantation and synthesise these findings.DesignA systematic review of clinical interventions.MethodsOn clinical trials published in Spanish and English, between January 2005 and July 2022, in the main databases. The number of studies identified is shown in the flowchart of the PRISMA review. For the critical assessment of the scientific quality of the studies, the CASPe checklist and the Cochrane risk of bias assessment tool were applied.ResultsEleven studies were obtained in which nursing educational interventions focused on posttransplantations and one that considered care from the person's admission to hospital were evaluated. The results obtained identified four themes: health education, empowerment, quality of life and holistic patient care with the help of different support methods, including individual and personalised support, group format and informational support via an educational brochure, telephone and website.ConclusionThe reviewed studies suggest that after receiving educational interventions patient dialysis treatment and kidney transplant recipient, have better health outcomes.Patient ContributionLarger studies are needed to promote a comprehensive and continuous self‐care model throughout the kidney transplant process.Implications for Nursing Health EducationNursing educational interventions are necessary throughout the transplantation process for the patient dialysis and the family in a comprehensive and continuous manner. In the future, clinical trial studies are needed at the pretransplantation stage.

Introduction

Patients with Chronic Renal Insufficiency (CRI) in the final stages III–IV–V without dialysis of the CRI classification according to the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative (K/DOQUI) guidelines with a glomerular filtration rate less than 30 mL/min 1.73 m2 are treated in the consultation of advanced chronic kidney disease (Isakova et al. 2017). In this consultation, the patient and his family are prepared to face the evolution of the disease, its progression is predicted, and complications and quality of life aspects are addressed (Arenas, Colladob, and Fernández Chamarro 2024).

The increase in CRI is a consequence of the increase in life expectancy of the population in general, the presence of vascular heart diseases such as hypertension, diabetes and being related to family members with hereditary kidney disease.

When the patient has terminal renal failure or requires renal replacement treatment with a glomerular lower than 15 mL/min 1.73 m2, the patient has different treatments such as haemodialysis, home haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, transplant from a cadaver donor, transplant of living donor and conservative treatment. But, the best option is kidney transplant since it provides a better quality of life, a higher survival rate and less cost than dialysis (Cuevas‐Budhart et al. 2017). In many cases, living donor donation is not possible, so the patient is placed on the cadaver donor waiting list, diagnostic and analytical tests are performed and the patient's informed consent is requested (Díaz Gómez et al. 2015).

After the United States Spain is the country with the most kidney transplant activity (Mahillo 2020). In Catalonia, an autonomous community of Spain, in 2022, 949 kidney transplants were performed. This figure, expressed per million people, represents a transplant rate of 121.8 pmp, higher than the rates of the rest of the world. The average previous time on dialysis for patients who received their first cadaver kidney transplant was 26, 1 months in 2022. It should be borne in mind that kidney transplant is the treatment of choice that provides a greater quality of life, a better survival rate and is less expensive than dialysis (Organització Catalana de Trasplantaments 2022).

The health literacy of the Spanish population is generally low (Romero and Ruiz‐Cabello 2012), and a consensus is needed on a self‐care model for the kidney transplant recipient because it has not yet been established. Coping with chronic illness is different for each person. Health professionals provide knowledge and resources and, through skills, abilities and resilience, patients can develop a healthier lifestyle and an ability to manage stressors (Rivera de los Santos et al. 2011). It has been observed that patients who receive haemodialysis have a high prevalence of depression and anxiety (Al‐Baghdadi and Rajha 2018), they follow a diet with many restrictions, and there is protein‐energy wasting, an inflammatory process and hypercatabolism, all of which entails a decrease in quality of life, an increase in mortality due to cardiovascular diseases and lower survival. Due to all these factors, an assessment and monitoring of the person's nutritional status is necessary to facilitate the recovery process from the kidney transplant (Gracia‐Iguacel, González‐Parra, and Ortiz 2022). Adherence to medication and good control of the regulation of mineral‐bone metabolism is very important to reduce calcifications and to make sure that they do not cause complications in the kidney transplant (Arenas 2020). The COVID‐19 pandemic has had a significant impact on the level of hospitalisation, the activity of external consultations, the kidney transplant programme and the treatment for patients on the haemodialysis programme. Telemedicine and virtual visits are possible, and in this sector, it opens a new path towards the future to guarantee accessibility and equity in healthcare in the face of any contingency plan and to guarantee the continuity of treatment for the population (Soler et al. 2020).

Aim

This review aimed to search for and systematically evaluate empirical studies investigating nursing educational interventions for patient with haemodialysis at the different stages of cadaveric kidney transplant and synthesise these findings.

Methods

A systematic review of clinical interventions was carried out, trying to answer the research question: ‘How should the transplant recipient, on haemodialysis and on the waiting list for a deceased donor kidney, receive nursing education during the transplant process? The PICO framework (Munn et al. 2018) was used to guide the approach of the review.

Population: Transplant recipient.

Intervention: Nurse education.

Comparison: Kidney transplantation process (pretransplant, transplantation and posttransplant).

Outcome: Education for the recipient of the deceased‐donor kidney transplant.

Study design: Clinical trial.

The bibliographic search was carried out in the following databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane and Cinahl. Studies published in Spanish and English in indexed scientific journals were included. The thesauri or descriptors in health sciences Medical Subject Headings were used: (Patients OR Transplant Recipients) AND (Nurse OR Nurse–Patient Relations OR Nursing) AND (Education OR Teaching OR Health Education) AND (Kidney Transplantation OR Pretransplant OR Transplantation OR Posttransplant OR Renal Dialysis) as shown in Table 1.

As a filter, only the publication date was used (January 2005 to 31 July 2022). This period of time was limited in order to observe the evolution of the increase in kidney transplants of deceased donors and the studies that have been conducted. In 2019, 3423 transplants were performed, representing a rate of 72.8 pmp. It should be noted that in 2020 kidney transplant activity decreased by 18.8% due to the COVID‐19 pandemic: 2702 transplants were performed, representing a rate of 56.9 pmp (Mahillo 2020).

Published clinical trials that contemplated nursing education as a strategy to improve the health outcomes of patients over 18 years old awaiting a deceased‐donor kidney transplant were selected.

Those studies in which the educational intervention focused solely on the care of patients receiving transplants from a living donor and those that did not consider the role of nurses in the patient's education were excluded.

After searching the databases with the help of the Mendeley reference manager, the primary and secondary reviewers read and extracted the data from the studies independently. They later shared the data. If there were any doubts, the third reviewer made a decision regarding the quality of the studies. The reviewers were divided into three groups: Ester Oriol‐Vila/ Laura Rota‐Musoll; Ester Oriol‐Vila/ Esmeralda Molina‐Robles; Laura Rota‐Musoll/ Esmeralda Molina‐Robles. Once the data had been obtained, a narrative description was made as it was not possible to carry out a mathematical aggregation.

The methodological quality of the selected studies was carried out using the CASPe (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme in its Spanish form) checklist (Cabello 2015), which helps us to evaluate a clinical trial. This evaluation consists of 11 questions; the first three are for elimination, with each question having three possible answers: ‘YES’, ‘NO’ and ‘I DON'T KNOW’. Only the answer ‘YES’ was taken into account. All studies with a score equal to or greater than nine were included and are presented in the Table S1. The Cochrane criteria (Higgins et al. 2022) were also followed, where it is recommended to assess the biases of each study, classifying them as follows: low risk of bias when all the criteria are met; moderate risk of bias when one or several criteria are partially met; and high risk of bias when one or more criteria are not met (Barrio Lucía and García López 2007) is summarised in Table S2.

The data extracted from the studies analysed are as follows: authors, date and country; participants (intervention group and control group); intervention procedure; duration, frequency and number of interventions; control group; outcomes; results; conclusions; limitations and strengths as shown in Table 2.

Results

The number of studies identified, excluded and included are shown in the flowchart in Figure 1, following the recommendations of the PRISMA review (Page et al. 2021). A total of 5769 study records were identified, of which a large number of studies (n = 5286) were excluded. There were 15 studies that were sought for retrieval and all were retrieved. Out of the 293 studies selected for full‐text reading, 280 were excluded: 210 because they did not correspond to the transplant process, eight because there was no nursing intervention, 12 were not in English or Spanish, 40 were not clinical trials, in seven patients did not participate, one was from a living donor, one was a liver transplant and, finally, one was eliminated because of poor quality (CASPe) score lower than nine. No study with a high risk of bias was found.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram, Page et al. (2021).

The 12 studies included in this review are within the deceased donor transplant process and are representative of seven different countries, none in Spanish, the oldest from 2012. Eleven are based on posttransplant nursing educational interventions, and only one begins with admission of the patient in the hospital before the transplant.

These studies present different educational interventions through various means (individual and personalised, group, an educational brochure, telephone and websites). The collaboration of a dietitian is mentioned in one study and that of a nephrologist in another.

Many of the educational interventions for the patient deal with issues related to the kidney transplantation process, summarised in Table S3.

Health Education

Adherence to Immunosuppressive Treatment

As we already know, pharmacological treatment is key to preventing rejection of the transplanted organ. Developing tests to observe compliance with its correct adherence is very complex. One of the studies used the Medication Event Monitoring System (MEMS) and a prescription refill register. For the intervention, the researchers first proposed a review of the medication and included an explanatory video, phone calls and health advice. They did not obtain different results between the intervention and control groups, but 40% of the participants did not use the MEMS. The percentage of participants who were not using the MEMS was higher in males and in those who had obtained a kidney transplant from a living donor and who had no previous experience of dialysis (Low et al. 2019).

With a study that used SystemCHANGE to improve medication adherence in kidney transplant recipients and that obtained good results (Russell et al. 2016, 2020) a secondary study, called MAGIC, was carried out with the patients that were rejected in this initial study since their adherence was less than 85%. The SystemChange method uses the plan‐do‐check‐act cycle, designed by the patient and guided by interventionists to change and shape the personal environment and daily routines in the aspect of Health. The results were that the patients themselves sought solutions to obtain greater adherence to the medication: alarm systems such as the mobile, time signals (food), restructured physical environment and social support. It should be noted that the patients themselves are able to achieve efficient, easy solutions and behaviour changes to improve adherence to treatment with the help of the nurse (Andrews et al. 2021).

In another study, the participants had both deceased‐donor and living‐donor kidney transplants, and the transplant time was between one and 32 years, with an average of 6.8 years. A web database called Transplant–TAVIE was used, in which interactive sessions of 20 to 30 min were provided by a virtual nurse and unlimited access. There were no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups (Côté et al. 2019).

Physical Exercise and Nutrition

The people in the study were over 60 years of age and kidney transplant recipients. They were able to increase healthy habits. The physical exercise programme lasted 3 months, the SystemCHANGE method was used and it was complemented with a Fitbit (step counter). With a good explanation of the instructions, older people use the devices without problems. The results showed that there is a correlation between the increase in steps and a decrease in heart rate, weight reduction and reduction in waist circumference and body mass index (BMI) (O'Brien and Meyer 2020).

A second study published using the same SystemCHANGE method with the ‘Fitbit’ and with older people, with a 12‐month follow‐up, showed that improvements in physical activity (daily steps) have a direct impact on systolic and diastolic blood pressure and heart rate. They concluded that additional reinforcement is needed to maintain physical activity during the maintenance period (O'Brien et al. 2021).

Another study used a StepWatch 3 activity monitor, with results showing an increase in steps in both the intervention and control groups. During the months of the study, information was provided through an educational brochure, continuous telephone support, face‐to‐face individual advice and motivational interviews. One issue to take into account is posttransplant weight maintenance since, due to medication and an unrestricted diet, patients gain weight. It was observed that low‐weight patients with a BMI < 18.5 were in most cases able to maintain weight, and only those patients with a BMI greater than 30 lost weight. This study showed that the measurements of the waist‐hip ratio were higher due to redistribution of fat away from the abdomen, with the main factor being the use of steroids (Schmid‐Mohler et al. 2019).

Empowerment

In one of the studies, a group of patients was chosen and offered individual and personalised sessions on medication, transplant rejection and lifestyle. Patients recorded morning and night temperature, weight, fluid balance and urine control (protein, glucose, haemoglobin) in a daily diary. They observed that the more control they had the more knowledge they had. The diary was a support to interpret signs that could indicate rejection and other conditions that required help from healthcare professionals. Intrahospital education is scarce because recovery from surgery is becoming shorter and the educational interventions have to continue after the transplant (Urstad et al. 2012).

In another study, education began after the transplant, at the bedside, with three sessions of 30 to 45 min every 2 days with the support of an educational brochure. After 2 months the postintervention assessment was made in which the limitations of the intervention and control groups were observed. The main differences between them resided in the waiting list and the posttransplant time, which had a direct effect on the mental and emotional state of the participants and could have had an impact on the results (Aghakhani et al. 2021).

An effective and cheap method is the use of teach‐back training (TBT), a method used by nurses to receive, understand and retain information. The transmission of concepts is simple and transparent; the key is to repeat short sentences to retain information. First, they evaluated each patient for their educational needs, and they had five 60‐min sessions to increase self‐management. TBT can also help improve posttransplant recovery (Mollazadeh and Hemmati Maslakpak 2018).

The only study found in this review where the educational interventions began before the transplant began at admission, continued through discharge and lasted a total of 90 days. The TBT method was used, together with a telephone follow‐up once a week for a month and also with WeChat. This study made use of advances in technology and information and the use of mobile phones to develop hospital to home care since they valued its greater efficiency and accessibility. This discharge care plan helped to better understand and adapt the knowledge and skills regarding the capacity for self‐care. Hospital admission is becoming shorter, which leads to less preparation time for discharge. With WeChat, when the patient was at home care was continued with instant support and immediate resolution of problems after discharge. The authors concluded that it was very beneficial for developing self‐care skills and confidence. They observed that there was a lower rate of admissions and fewer adverse effects. In addition, by using this system the risk of readmission was detected early so that they could programme and identify high‐risk people that needed more specific and intense interventions (Ren et al. 2019).

Quality of Life

Quality of life is becoming increasingly important in clinical practice. In this review, four studies carried out quality of life questionnaires to assess educational interventions. There are different questionnaires, including the KTQ‐25 questionnaire, which is specifically for patients receiving kidney transplants (Mansouri et al. 2020; Raiesifar et al. 2014; Aghakhani et al. 2021) and the SF‐12 questionnaire, which is a shorter version of the SF‐36 questionnaire that measures the state of both physical and mental health (Urstad et al. 2012).

To increase quality of life, the authors of one of the studies compared whether it was better to do multimedia interventions using a DVD or a 58‐page booklet, and both worked (Mansouri et al. 2020).

In addition, the information given to the patient and the family through questions and answers, supported by an educational brochure and the individualised recommendations of a dietitian, telephone assistance and periodic visits, has an extremely positive effect on quality of life (Raiesifar et al. 2014).

Holistic Care

Social and Family Support

Nurses perform educational interventions to change the behaviours or daily routines of patients. Family members participate to strengthen the social support system to help patients become more involved in self‐care activities (Ren et al. 2019), one example being taking medication to increase treatment adherence (Andrews et al. 2021).

Stress and Anxiety

In one of the studies, it was observed that after the operation the patient is in a critical state, with complications. They experience stress, anxiety and fear of transplant rejection. The length of stay in hospital was six times longer than in the control group (Raiesifar et al. 2014).

Quality of Care and Patient Satisfaction

Preparation for discharge and a good quality of care in the transition to home means fewer hospital readmissions and visits to the emergency unit (Hu, Gu, and Tan 2020).

Discussion

It can be seen that in this review all the interventions are focused on transplantation and posttransplantation. Nursing practice aims to achieve comprehensive care (López Garrigós et al. 2014), to improve health education, empowerment, quality of life and holistic patient care. The transplant process is very complex, hospital stays are getting shorter and the patient needs to acquire all the knowledge, skills and resilience for a better transition to home (Andersen et al. 2019). How a patient copes with chronic disease, the management of stressors and the generation of skills to promote health is different in each individual (Rivera de los Santos et al. 2011).

We can divide the process into different stages:

Pretransplant Stage

The patient haemodialysis on the waiting list for kidney transplant has symptoms of anxiety and stress. There is a relationship between the longer the time on the waiting list for the transplant, the higher the stress level. In the pre‐transplant period, patients with chronic kidney disease should be evaluated, diagnosed, and treated to avoid postoperative complications such as lack of adherence to treatment and understanding of the necessary care that will need to be taken. A multidisciplinary team is required to control all stages of transplantation, and psychological intervention is necessary (Da Silva and Dos Santos Grincenkov 2014).

During the dialysis period, there are food restrictions and, as a consequence, nutritional alterations (Sellares and Rodriguez 2019; Albalate Ramón et al. 2021; Gómez Vilaseca et al. 2017). Transplantation is the best option to increase quality of life since there are no dietary restrictions (Gracia‐Iguacel, González‐Parra, and Ortiz 2022).

In a review of qualitative studies (Koons and Smeltzer 2018) on the experience of patients receiving dialysis (both peritoneal dialysis and haemodialysis) on a deceased donor waiting list, six themes emerged: living with hope, uncertainty, emotions, coping while waiting, restrictions on freedom and support network.

Hope begins when the person enters the waiting list, but over time physical exhaustion increases and there is a possible exit from the waiting list. The calls for the transplant may not come in the end, and the feelings of disappointment and frustration increase. During this period information and psychosocial support should be provided and support programmes should be designed together with the family.

Uncertainty begins when the waiting time is prolonged or when the patient knows that someone has had a transplant rejection. They feel tried and stressed.

Emotions regarding fear of surgery and whether it will bring them a better quality of life are added. Another psychological factor is the anguish caused to the patient by the fact that the kidney donor has to die.

Coping with waiting is channelled by controlling their health through exercise, diet, medication adherence and perhaps working or taking part in leisure activities.

Since they are on dialysis, they have restrictions on freedom, holiday limitations and restrictions on social and family gatherings. They describe moods of boredom and anxiety.

The support network, including family, society and the health team, is an important factor for there to be strength, hope and physical and mental help.

Focused, continuous and individualised education for the patient and family is efficient. It has to be present from the diagnosis of kidney failure and throughout the transplantation process. For education, preparation and educational assessment certain indicators should be established to help to coordinate multidisciplinary care. This can be complemented with support methods such as educational material and technological tools like the web, telephone and social networks (Mahdizadeh et al. 2020).

At this stage, nurses can make educational interventions, for example regarding organ allocation because some mention a lack of contact with the transplant team and having doubts about whether they are still on the waiting list (Koons and Smeltzer 2018). Information, education and advice are tools to strengthen health decision‐making (Andersen, Urstad, and Larsen 2021) and preparation for transplant surgery (Andersen et al. 2022).

In one study, nurses realised the need for ‘health literacy’. Through a film and motivational interviews with patients who received haemodialysis, they were able to reflect on the preparation for the transplant, graft maintenance, how to avoid rejection, lifestyle, medication adherence and their expectations and knowledge (Pelayo Alonso et al. 2017).

Immediate Pretransplant Stage

In a study of patients receiving liver transplants, the patients were given information before surgery via a video with virtual glasses, after which they reported lower levels of anxiety and higher satisfaction with nursing care (Kapikiran and Bulbuloglu 2022).

Providing patients with information and preparing them emotionally before entering the operating room reduces anxiety. The participation of the patient and family, maintaining a dialogue, contributes to the success of the transplant and it is also important in the process of recovery and preservation of the graft (Ferreira, Teixeira, and Branco 2018).

Transplant Stage

In the transplant, very complex multidisciplinary interventions are received and take place in a short period where there is emotional stress greater and demands on self‐care. Many patients are not ready to go home. Nursing interventions increase patient empowerment, and the use of informative brochures can help family participation in self‐care, medication adherence and rejection prevention (Andersen et al. 2019).

Posttransplant Stage

In a qualitative study of a semi‐structured interview, it emerged that the knowledge topics of interest were medication, rejection and lifestyle in a context of daily self‐care (Andersen et al. 2019).

Educational programmes for patients who received a transplant provide them with knowledge, skills and attitudes to promote new health habits and reduce anxiety due to lack of information regarding self‐care, prevention and early detection of complications and adverse effects of immunosuppressive medication (Díaz Jurado et al. 2017).

Promotion of medication adherence is an absolute necessity for graft survival. In one study, the recipients themselves, with the help of experts, developed the content of a video that included motivational messages in an attractive way. The video was suitable for all patients (Low et al. 2016). The important information was the actions and precautions aimed at increasing the life of the transplanted kidney. The most important was not the length of the video but the fact that it could be seen from any place, such as a mobile phone. Education needs to be continuous; the longer the time between health professional visits the more information is lost and the lower the adherence to the immunosuppressive treatment (Kim et al. 2020).

The promotion of physical activity in older people with chronic diseases increases quality of life (O'Brien and Meyer 2020), and encouraging healthy lifestyle habits, diet and adequate physical activity is of great importance in nursing education before and after the transplantation. For example, a diet without dietary restrictions but with immunosuppressive medication can increase weight after a year of the transplant, and excessive weight gain is a cardiovascular risk factor and can lead to lower graft survival (Aksoy 2016). In addition, changing lifestyle to prevent bone fractures is essential since the recipients of kidney transplants have many comorbidities, one of which is osteodystrophy (Arenas 2020). Furthermore, a direct relationship has been observed between people living alone or with low social support and a less healthy lifestyle, stress, anxiety and depression after the transplant. Psychological problems are observed and quality of life decreases. They need psychological care to deal with their emotional problems. Family support is related to favourable health outcomes (Gezginci et al. 2019).

In addition, advanced age and educational level are risk factors for less understanding of the important concepts of kidney transplantation. There is a need to develop educational strategies and follow‐up together with the family due to the greater risk of acute rejection. Older people face problems of ageing, surgical procedure, immunosuppression, infections, malignant neoplasms and a higher incidence of chronic rejection. Patients need psychological and social support and strategies to improve adherence to treatment (Akin and Harmanci Seren 2019).

The main basis of self‐care, apart from physical health, is personalised advice, affective support, self‐management, helping the patient to make shared decisions and always with a relationship of trust between the patient and the professional. This accompaniment builds a relationship of trust, emphasising the comprehensive care of the patient, and can be done in a multidisciplinary way with psychotherapy or with a psychologist (Been‐Dahmen et al. 2019; Ebadzadeh et al. 2019).

Patient education is necessary before and after transplantation to broaden their knowledge. And it is important to contact the nurse when detecting any symptoms of possible viral or bacterial infections and warning signs. When a graft rejection occurs, nurses show empathy, emotional support and educational guidance; in this way, the stress and anxiety of the patient to face the rejection can be reduced. The health professional must provide enough time and care so that the person feels valued (Fernando and Eguren Penin 2015).

Potential Limitations And Biases In the Review Process

The main limitation is the absence of clinical trials in pretransplantation and in most cases, it is not defined what treatment the patient receives. There are limitations in the included studies due to the diversity of instruments used, the variability in the duration of the intervention, small samples, losses in the sample, patients in different replacement treatment and transplantation techniques, when the education intervention began and the different time during the course of the disease in the posttransplantation.

Conclusion

The reviewed studies suggest that after receiving educational interventions patients have better health outcomes. However, larger studies are needed to promote a comprehensive and continuous self‐care model throughout the kidney transplant process.

Implications for Nursing Health Education

In recent years, Spain has seen an increase in kidney transplants from deceased donors, a decrease in the waiting time for the first transplant on dialysis and an increasingly shorter hospital stay, which makes the transition of the patient to their home difficult. Nursing educational interventions are necessary throughout the transplantation process for the patient and the family in a comprehensive and continuous manner. This review has shown that there are many methods to promote health education. In the future, clinical trial studies are needed at the pretransplantation stage.
