Loops, not groups: Long cycles are responsible for discontinuous phase transitions in higher-order network contagions
Leah A. Keating, Laurent Hébert-Dufresne

Abstract
We study a self-consistent approach to introduce higher-order effects in a branching process model of complex contagion on clustered networks. Branching processes operate over an infinite population such that they never circle back and interact with previously exposed parts of the system. This infinite, treelike, structure makes it tricky to account for complex contagion mechanisms such as group effects, peer pressure, or social reinforcement where multiple exposures interact in synergistic ways. Here we present a self-consistent solution that accounts for local group structure and global cycles where the process can feedback on itself. This allows us to distinguish multiple exposures that stem from a single transmission chain, from those occurring at the intersection of different transmission chains. We find that only the latter mechanism can give rise to a discontinuous phase transition in the size of global cascades, which is a defining feature of complex contagions. Group effects alone, without long cycles, produce standard continuous phase transitions.

Simple contagion

In the simple contagion model, following the approach of Newman
16
and Miller
14
, each edge is considered occupied with probability
T
, in which case we assume that this edge will transmit the contagion if given the chance. The probability
T
can be interpreted as a transmission parameter and we thus have a bond percolation model of the contagion dynamics. The resulting cascade or contagion size is then given by the size of the connected components of the percolation network, and a supercritical cascade corresponds to a macroscopic giant component
15
;
10
.

Using Newman-Miller networks
16
;
14
, which describe a network by the distribution of the number
s
of independent edges, and the number
t
of triangles, of a randomly selected node. The joint distribution of
s
and
t
,
p_{s,t}
, can be described by the probability generating function (pgf)

G_{0}(x,y)=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}p_{s,t}x^{s}y^{t}.

(1)

In contagion dynamics on a network, it is also necessary to know the distribution of the excess number of single edges and triangles if we reach a node by traversing a single edge, or by traversing a triangle. The pgf for the excess distribution of single edges and triangles by traversing an single edge is given by

\displaystyle G_{s}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}sp_{s,t}x^{s-1}y^{t}=\frac{1}{\langle s\rangle}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}G_{0}(x,y),

(2)

and by traversing a triangle is

\displaystyle G_{t}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,t=0}^{\infty}tp_{s,t}x^{s}y^{t-1}=\frac{1}{\langle t\rangle}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}G_{0}(x,y).

(3)

In the examples in this letter, we consider the case where
p_{s,t}
follows a doubly-Poisson distribution
16
,

p_{s,t}=e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^{s}}{s!}e^{-\nu}\frac{\nu^{t}}{t!},

(4)

where
\mu
is the mean number of independent edges per node, and
\nu
is the mean number of triangles (groups) per node. For the doubly-Poisson distribution,
G_{0}(x,y)=G_{s}(x,y)=G_{t}(x,y)=e^{\mu(x-1)+\nu(y-1)}
.

We aim to calculate the probability that a randomly selected node
i
is in the giant component of the percolated network. We first calculate
u
, the probability that a randomly selected single edge of
i
does not lead to the giant component, and the probability
v
that a randomly selected triangle of
i
does not lead to the giant component. There are two ways in which a single edge will not connect
i
to the giant component (with probability
u
). (1) With probability
1-T
, transmission does not occur along the edge, or (2) transmission does occur along the edge with probability
T
, but with probability
G_{s}(u,v)
the node at the other end of the edge is not in the giant component,

u=1-T+TG_{s}(u,v).

(5)

There are four ways in which a triangle fails to connect node
i
to the giant component (with probability
v
).
(1) Transmission fails on both edges connecting
i
to the other two nodes in the triangle, with probability
(1-T)^{2}
.
(2) Transmission occurs on either one of two edges, the other two edges fail and the single reached neighbor is not in the giant component, with probability
2T(1-T)^{2}G_{t}(u,v)
.
(3) Transmission occurs on either one of two edges, with the other failing but the third edge transmitting, and neither of the two neighbors are in the giant component, with probability
2T^{2}(1-T)G_{t}(u,v)^{2}
.
(4) Both edges from
i
transmit and neither of the two neighbors are in the giant component, with probability
T^{2}G_{t}(u,v)^{2}
. Summing across the four cases yields:

\displaystyle v=

\displaystyle(1-T)^{2}+2T(1-T)^{2}G_{t}(u,v)

\displaystyle+\left[2T^{2}(1-T)+T^{2}\right]G_{t}(u,v)^{2}.

(6)

Then, the probability that a randomly selected node is
not
in the giant component is
G_{0}(u,v)
, and the probability
R
that a randomly selected node is in the giant component is

R=1-G_{0}(u,v).

(7)

For a simple contagion, Eq. (
7
) also gives the fractional size
S
of the giant component
16
, since the probability that an initial contagion seed is in a specific component is equal to the fractional size of the component. In Fig.
2
, we show that Eq. (
7
) agrees well with Monte Carlo simulations (and exact in the thermodynamic limit).

Complex contagion

We first naïvely introduce a complex contagion and higher-order extension to the self-consistent model by changing the probability of transmission on a second exposure within a given triangle to
T_{\Delta}
, where

T_{\Delta}=1-(1-T)^{\alpha_{\Delta}}.

(8)

If
\alpha_{\Delta}=1
, then
T_{\Delta}=T
and we have a simple contagion, and if
\alpha_{\Delta}>1
, then
T_{\Delta}>T
and we have a complex contagion. Proceeding as in Eqs. (
5
-
7
), and changing every second exposure to transmit with
T_{\Delta}
, the resulting model is

\displaystyle u_{out}=

\displaystyle 1-T+TG_{s}(u_{out},v_{out}),

(9)

\displaystyle v_{out}=

\displaystyle(1-T)^{2}+2T(1-T)(1-T_{\Delta})G_{t}(u_{out},v_{out})

\displaystyle+\left[2TT_{\Delta}(1-T)+T^{2}\right]G_{t}(u_{out},v_{out})^{2},

(10)

and

R=1-G_{0}(u_{out},v_{out})~,

(11)

where
u_{out}
(
v_{out}
) is the probability that a node is not connected to the largest connected component through a randomly-selected edge (triangle), and
R
is the probability that we observe a giant component. We use the subscript
“out”
to refer to the direction of the percolation, where in outward percolation we consider whether a transmission link exists from a node to the giant component.

We need to go beyond our naïve implementation of complex contagion to calculate the size of the giant component by considering the other direction of percolation. We define
u_{in}
(
v_{in}
) as the probability that node
i
selected uniformly at random is not connected to the giant component through a randomly-selected single edge (triangle), where the direction of transmission is from the other node to node
i
(from the other two nodes to node
i
). In the
inward
direction, we get that

u_{in}=1-T+TG_{s}(u_{in},v_{in}),

(12)

which is the same as Eq. (
9
).

In a triangle, we need to count how many of the two neighbors of node
i
are actually part of the giant component, in which case we call them activated. We find that it is necessary to consider the case where a second node can be activated in a triangle from
within
the triangle, but also the case where the second node can become activated through a
separate
transmission chain that also reaches the triangle (see Fig.
4
). We denote
\rho_{in}
the probability that a randomly selected neighbor in the triangle is activated through a transmission chain external to the triangle,

\rho_{in}=1-G_{t}(u_{in},v_{in}).

(13)

T_{\curvearrowright}
.

We add a further probability
T_{\curvearrowright}=T-(1-T)^{\alpha_{\curvearrowright}}
, which is the probability of transmission from a second active node in a triangle if that second node was activated through an external transmission loop. Then,

\displaystyle v_{in}=

\displaystyle 2(1-T)(1-T_{\curvearrowright})\rho_{in}^{2}

\displaystyle+(1-T)\left[T(1-T_{\Delta})+(1-T)\right]2\rho_{in}(1-\rho_{in})

\displaystyle+(1-\rho_{in})^{2},

(14)

and the size of the largest connected component is

S=1-G_{0}(u_{in},v_{in})\;.

(15)

The result of Eq. (
15
) is validated in Fig.

Distinguishing loops and groups

We consider three cases:
groups and loops
(classic complex contagion,
\alpha_{\Delta}=\alpha_{\curvearrowright}=10
);
groups, no loops
(
\alpha_{\Delta}=10
and
\alpha_{\curvearrowright}=1
); and
loops, no groups
(
\alpha_{\Delta}=1
and
\alpha_{\curvearrowright}=10
).

In Fig.
3
, we show the case where
\alpha_{\Delta}=\alpha_{\curvearrowright}=10
; i.e., groups and loops. This increased transmission probability exists regardless of whether the second active node was activated from a node inside the triangle, or through a second transmission loop. We observe a discontinuous phase transition in the size of the giant component, but not in the probability of existence of the giant component. Conversely, for simple contagion, in Fig.
2
, the phase transition is continuous for both the size of the giant component and the probability of existence of the giant component.

In Fig.
5
, we show the case where
\alpha_{\Delta}=10
and
\alpha_{\curvearrowright}=1
; i.e., groups, no loops. In this case, a second active node in a triangle only gets an increased probability of activating the inactive node in the triangle if it was activated itself from within the triangle (
\alpha_{\Delta}=10
).
We find phenomenology reminiscent of simple contagions with continouse phase transitions. The within-group higher-order effect
\alpha_{\Delta}
increases the size and probability of existence of the giant component, but does not alone change the nature of the phase transition.

Conclusions

We have shown that for models of complex contagion on networks with group structure that the existence of a discontinuous phase transition in the size of the giant component can be explained by the existence of transmission cycles (or loops) in the network; i.e., when one transmission chain meets another in a group. This is evidenced through only observing discontinuous phase transitions in cases where
T_{\curvearrowright}>T
(
\alpha_{\curvearrowright}>1
). This result implies that features of hypergraph that hinder (promote) long cycles
13
, such as overlap between hyperedges, are also likely to hinder (promote) bistability and discontinuous transitions.
Understanding the interplay of structure and dynamics is highly important in the growing area of higher-order networks, where it is often claimed that it is the mere existence of groups can cause discontinuous phase transitions.
A further contribution of this work is the incorporation of critical mass effects into a branching process model, which very accurately captures both size and probability of non-linear cascades and could have applications far beyond dynamics on higher-order networks.
