Real‐world application of the International Myeloma Society (IMS)/International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) consensus on genomic staging for high‐risk multiple myeloma: A report from the Australia and New Zealand Myeloma and Related Diseases Registry
Authors: Kenneth J C Lim, Elham Ashrafi, Cameron Wellard, Elizabeth M Moore, Betty Gration, Bradley Augustson, Peter Mollee, Jennifer Zhang, Nicole Wong Doo, Karen Dun, Rachel Cooke, Sabine Ringkowski, Adam Bryant, Georgia McCaughan, Erica Wood, Zoe McQuilten, P Joy Ho, Hang Quach, Andrew Spencer
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

The International Myeloma Society (IMS)/International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) recently proposed the Consensus Genomic Staging (CGS) to provide a data‐driven definition for high‐risk multiple myeloma based on specific genomic and clinical features. This staging system allows for the identification of patients with a poor prognosis in the era of novel agents. 1 The criteria proposed that the presence of at least one of these abnormalities: (1) del(17p), with a cut‐off of >20% clonal fraction, and/or TP53 mutation; (2) an immunoglobulin heavy chain (IgH) translocation including t(4;14), t(14;16) or t(14;20) along with 1q21+ and/or del(1p32); (3) monoallelic del(1p32) along with 1q21+ or biallelic del(1p32); or (4) beta‐2 microglobulin ≥5.5 mg/L with normal creatinine (<1.2 mg/dL), as high‐risk (IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR).

It is important to note that these risk factors were predominantly identified using clinical trial datasets, and the main purpose of the IMS/IMWG CGS criteria was to allow for standardization of risk stratification for the conduct of future clinical trials. Its performance in real‐world populations, particularly in patients who are frequently excluded from clinical trials, such as the frail or those with renal impairment, remains to be determined. Additionally, criterion 4 cannot be applied to patients with creatinine ≥1.2 mg/dL, and the impact of relying solely on criteria 1–3 in this population requires further evaluation.

A limitation of previous prognostic scoring systems such as the International Staging System (ISS), the revised ISS (R‐ISS) and the second revision of the International Staging System (R2‐ISS) was their inability to reliably identify early relapse 2 , 3 , 4 or functional high‐risk (FHR) disease, defined as disease progression within 18 months of commencement of first‐line therapy (progression free survival [PFS]‐1 <18 months). 5 As such, the ability of the new IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria to identify patients at risk of early relapse is also of clinical interest.

We used a real‐world cohort from the Australia and New Zealand Myeloma and Related Diseases Registry (ANZ MRDR) to validate the new IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria. The ANZ MRDR is a clinical registry collecting data for benchmarking and research purposes to improve care for people with MM and related diseases—its methodology has been described previously. 6 , 7 In the absence of routine testing for TP53 mutation and biallelic del(1p32) by next generation sequencing (NGS), a modified set of criteria was used (mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS). We first assessed the impact of the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria on overall survival (OS) using a Cox proportional hazards model. We then evaluated the performance of the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria in pre‐defined subgroups: (1) patients who received autologous haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (AHSCT); (2) patients who did not receive AHSCT; (3) frail patients, defined by an adaptation of the simplified frailty scale using age, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG PS) and components from the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) 8 ; and (4) patients with renal impairment, defined as creatinine ≥1.2 mg/dL. Finally, we compared the discriminative performance of the ISS, R‐ISS, R2‐ISS and mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR in predicting risk of death and the risk of early progression on front‐line therapy (PFS‐1 <18 months).

Given the retrospective nature of the study and the presence of missing data, patients were only classified as standard risk when they did not meet all four of the IMS/IMWG CGS criteria. Patients were classified as high‐risk when at least one high‐risk criterion was satisfied. In the frailty component, a formal CCI assessment was not available. As such, we incorporated available comorbidity variables (including cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, diabetes, liver disease and prior malignancy) to refine frailty classification among patients who were otherwise considered intermediate‐fit (ECOG PS of 1 or age 76–80).

Of 832 evaluated patients diagnosed between January 2012 and October 2025, 567 patients had determinable mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS risk status, of which 166 were mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR and 401 were standard‐risk (mod‐IMS/IMWG‐SR). We excluded 265 with insufficient data to allow for classification. The characteristics between the standard‐risk and high‐risk groups are shown in Table S1 and Figure S1. We next looked at the prevalence and distribution of high‐risk criteria among 515 patients with complete mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS data. There were 112 (22%) patients with mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR disease. Of these 122 patients, the breakdown included 20 (21%), 19 (20%), 24 (25%) and 34 (35%) patients satisfying only one of criterion 1, 2, 3 or 4 respectively. Fifteen (13%) patients met two or more mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria.

Patients with mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR disease had higher rates of ISS stage 3 (p < 0.001), R‐ISS stage 3 (p < 0.001) and R2‐ISS stage 3 or 4 disease (p < 0.001). Among patients receiving AHSCT, the most common front‐line regimen was with bortezomib, lenalidomide and dexamethasone (VRd; 49%), followed by bortezomib, cyclophosphamide and dexamethasone (VCd; 40%). In patients who did not receive AHSCT, VRd and VCd were used with equal frequency (27% each). Frontline treatment selection did not differ significantly between mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR and CGS standard risk (mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐SR) patients.

At a median follow‐up of 44.1 months (95% confidence interval [CI] 41.1–47.1), patients with mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR disease had an estimated median OS of 45.7 months (95% CI 33.6–70.4) compared to 85.5 months (95% CI 78.2–105.2) in patients with mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐SR disease (Figure 1A). The presence of mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR nearly doubled the hazard of death (HR 1.90; 95% CI 1.41–2.55; p < 0.001). Median PFS‐1 was 23.0 months (95% CI 17.2–30.6) in mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR patients compared to 38.9 months (95% CI 30.4–42.3) in CGS‐SR patients (p < 0.001; Figure 1C). Patients who satisfied more than one mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria had an even poorer prognosis (Figure 1B,D) with a median PFS and OS of 15.6 (95% CI 5.5‐NR) and 37.0 (11.0‐NR) months respectively.

We next performed sensitivity analysis using the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria on various subgroups (Table 1A). Among patients receiving AHSCT, mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status was associated with a significantly increased risk of death (HR 2.73, 95% CI 1.63–4.58; p < 0.001). In contrast, mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status was not significantly associated with OS in the transplant‐ineligible cohort (HR 1.32, 95% CI 0.88–1.97; p = 0.18). Similarly, among frail patients, mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status did not confer a statistically significant increase in mortality risk (HR 1.36, 95% CI 0.93–1.98; p = 0.11), whereas a strong association was observed in non‐frail patients (HR 3.22, 95% CI 1.98–5.24; p < 0.001). Among patients with renal impairment, mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR classification was not significantly associated with OS (HR 1.50, 95% CI 0.93–2.41; p = 0.09) in contrast to its significant prognostic impact in patients with normal renal function (HR 2.33, 95% CI 1.59–3.43; p < 0.001).

Early progression (PFS‐1 <18 months) was identified in 154 of 500 evaluable patients, of whom 62 (40%) were also mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR. To evaluate the association between biological high‐risk disease and early progression, a logistic regression model was constructed with early progression as the dependent variable (Table 1B). Mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status was associated with 2.2‐fold increased odds of early progression (95% CI 1.4–3.3; p < 0.001). This is compared to a 2.4‐, 3.3‐ and 3.3‐fold increased odds in ISS stage 3 versus non‐stage 3, R‐ISS stage 3 versusnon‐stage 3 and R2‐ISS stage 3/4 versus non‐stage 3/4, respectively, with R‐ISS 3 demonstrating the highest positive predictive value for early progression (56%). Overall, the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria did not outperform existing staging systems in identifying patients with FHR disease.

This study is among the few to externally validate the IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria using a real‐world cohort, extending generalizability to MM patients in Australia and New Zealand where the use of front‐line anti‐CD38 monoclonal antibody was only just recently approved for transplant‐ineligible patients. 9 , 10 , 11 Strengths of our study include comprehensive cytogenetic/Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH) data, a large sample size, and participation from 21 sites across two countries. Notably, the use of quadruplet regimens was absent from our dataset, which limits its applicability to contemporary treatment settings. Other limitations include the absence of detailed comorbidity data, which may influence frailty classification and transplant eligibility. TP53 mutation testing was not routinely performed as part of standard care and therefore was not included in the analysis. Additionally, biallelic del(1p32) status was assessed by FISH rather than the recommended NGS‐based methods, which may have resulted in under‐detection of cases with smaller deletions. This gap in genomic testing limits full application of IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR in many jurisdictions and highlights the need for broader access to NGS‐based testing methods. Of note, biallelic del(1p) or TP53 mutation as an isolated IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR marker may account for approximately 10% of IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR cases, potentially leading to a modest underestimation of IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR prevalence. 9 Nevertheless, the proportion of IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR patients in our cohort with complete data (22%) was comparable to European real‐world cohorts, with a similarly observed near two‐fold increase in mortality risk. 9 , 10

We also found that the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status was strongly discriminative in patients who were transplant‐eligible, non‐frail, and with normal renal function. However, although a trend towards higher mortality risk was observed in patients with renal impairment, likely owing to a limited sample size, hazard ratios were attenuated and not statistically significant in the transplant‐ineligible, frail and renally impaired subgroups. These findings suggest that the mod‐IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR criteria may have reduced discriminatory value in populations where competing clinical risk factors dominate prognosis. Given that frailty and renal impairment remain common and understudied risk modifiers, this observation is particularly relevant when considering risk‐adapted treatment strategies that incorporate IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR for treatment intensification or stratification. 12 Furthermore, although IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR captures biological high‐risk disease, it does not outperform existing staging systems in patients with FHR disease, highlighting the continued importance of dynamic response assessment during front‐line therapy.

In summary, IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR identifies approximately a quarter of newly diagnosed MM patients as high‐risk in a real‐world setting. IMS/IMWG CGS‐HR status predicts significantly inferior survival in patients who were transplant eligible, non‐frail or had preserved renal function. However, its prognostic impact in transplant‐ineligible, frail and renally impaired populations needs further study. Despite advances in genomic risk stratification, a substantial proportion of patients with FHR disease remain unrecognized at diagnosis, reinforcing the importance of incorporating early treatment response metrics alongside genomic risk models.
