Exploring the potential role of telemedicine in the early diagnosis of skin neglected tropical diseases in remote areas in Afghanistan through the perceptions and experiences of skin NTD health professionals: a qualitative study
Authors: Mette Koopmans, Ricky Janssen, Fabian Schlumberger, Matthew Willis, Anil Fastenau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveSkin neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) are a subset of NTDs that can lead to disability, stigmatisation and socioeconomic problems. Timely diagnosis of skin NTDs is important to prevent morbidity and mortality. The Afghan healthcare system currently faces serious challenges, including gender equity issues that complicate access to healthcare and the diagnosis of skin NTDs. This research aimed to explore the potential role of telemedicine for the diagnosis of skin NTDs in remote areas in Afghanistan.DesignWe conducted a qualitative study using semi-structured interviews and thematic analysis to explore experiences and perspectives of key informants working in the field of NTDs in Afghanistan.SettingNon-governmental organisations and Afghan health centres.ParticipantsTwelve potential key informants were contacted, and eight took part in the study. Entry criteria required a minimum of 20 years of professional experience in Afghanistan’s NTD field. Participants with less experience or those unable to provide informed consent were excluded.ResultsThematic analysis identified four themes: challenges in diagnosing skin-related NTDs, access to healthcare, the role of telemedicine and the implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan. Participants highlighted the lack of a national diagnostic framework, inadequate dermatological knowledge, uninformed prescribing of medication and stigmatisation as the main challenges in the diagnosis of skin NTDs. Geographic barriers and gender issues were mentioned as additional challenges to accessing healthcare and diagnosing skin NTDs in Afghanistan. Although participants perceived telemedicine as a promising avenue for improving access to care through knowledge and image exchange, they highlighted challenges including the absence of physical examination and infrastructure limitations.ConclusionWhile the potential use of telemedicine could help improve access to healthcare and the diagnosis of skin NTDs in the Afghan context, telemedicine should not be the sole method used in diagnosing skin NTDs.Keywords: Public health, Digital Technology, DERMATOLOGY

Introduction

Neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) are a diverse group of conditions estimated to affect over 1 billion people,1 2 primarily in tropical and subtropical regions.3 Among the various categories of NTDs, skin NTDs such as leprosy and cutaneous leishmaniasis (CL) represent a unique public health challenge. Without timely diagnosis and treatment, these conditions can lead to visible differences, disabilities and increased risk of social exclusion through stigma, further marginalising affected individuals.4

While leprosy and CL are a major global health challenge, low-resource settings are disproportionately affected due to poverty and inequality in healthcare access. Afghanistan is among the countries where skin NTDs remain endemic, with leprosy and CL posing significant burdens, particularly in rural and resource-poor settings.5 The country faces an additional layer of complexity due to its fragile healthcare infrastructure, cultural barriers and ongoing humanitarian crises. Inadequate diagnostic systems, a shortage of trained (female) healthcare professionals and geographical barriers further complicate timely access to healthcare.6–9 Afghanistan faces a critical shortage of physicians, particularly dermatologists, with only 2.5 doctors per 10 000 population in a country of over 41 million.10 11 This is substantially below the WHO’s recommended minimum of 2.5 medical professionals per 1000 population.12 The limited availability of dermatologists contributes to long waiting times, making access to dermatological care a major barrier.13 In addition, other challenges related to healthcare access include low health literacy and poverty.14 Poverty leads to an increased risk of contracting CL and leprosy and nearly half of all Afghans live in poverty.15–17

In this challenging context, telemedicine emerges as a potentially transformative tool for bridging the gap in healthcare access and addressing diagnostic barriers.18 19 Teledermatology uses telemedicine to remotely diagnose and treat skin diseases.20 21 Globally, telemedicine has demonstrated success in improving access to healthcare in remote and resource-limited settings.22 Studies from other countries demonstrate that telemedicine could be a valuable tool to address diagnostic challenges in Afghanistan, particularly in overcoming geographic and infrastructural barriers to specialised care.23 24 For instance, a study from French Guiana23 highlighted how teledermatology can provide timely access to specialist expertise, reducing wait times and improving diagnostic accuracy, enabling healthcare providers in remote areas to consult with specialists. Telemedicine offers an opportunity to enhance early detection and improve outcomes for patients with skin NTDs.25

Studies highlight the potential of teledermatology to improve rural healthcare access in Afghanistan.13 However, there are only a few studies on telemedicine initiatives in Afghanistan, with existing studies focusing on mental health26 or COVID-19,27 primarily through systematic reviews and retrospective analysis.28 29 These studies do not focus on skin diseases, and no telemedicine programmes currently appear to exist for the early diagnosis of NTDs of the skin in the country. Therefore, this study is unique in that it provides a qualitative perspective on teledermatology in Afghanistan, a topic that, to our knowledge, has not been studied before.28

This study is also unique in that it focuses on the implementation process of telemedicine. The value of teledermatology depends not only on adoption and implementation by the institutions using it but also on the extent to which its diagnostic use for skin NTDs is tailored to the specific context. For example, while telemedicine has the potential to reduce health inequalities, without addressing infrastructural gaps, its benefits may remain limited to urban populations.30 31 In a context such as Afghanistan, the use of telemedicine may require a unique form of contextual alignment for implementation to be feasible.32 This study therefore seeks to explore how telemedicine should be developed for the Afghan context, what is required to make teledermatology work in practice, what role it should aim to play in the diagnostic process and the potential of telemedicine to improve healthcare equity in Afghanistan.

This study will provide an analysis of key informants’ perspectives in which they share their thoughts on the potential role of telemedicine for the diagnosis of skin NTDs in Afghanistan. Additionally, this study will also investigate the potential implications of telemedicine regarding gender equity and access to healthcare in the Afghan context. By examining the potential role of telemedicine in addressing the diagnostic challenges of skin NTDs, this research aims to inform future interventions and policies that promote equitable access to healthcare. Ultimately, the findings have the potential to guide the development of sustainable telemedicine initiatives that can improve health outcomes for vulnerable populations in Afghanistan and similar settings.

Methods

Study design

A qualitative study was conducted using semi-structured key informant interviews. The potential role of telemedicine for skin NTDs in Afghanistan was investigated, and insights into the implementation challenges of teledermatology were obtained. The study is reported following SRQR guidance—the completed Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist is provided as online supplemental appendix 1.

Sampling and study population

Participants were recruited through the German Leprosy and Tuberculosis Relief Organization’s network based on their expertise in skin NTDs and telemedicine in Afghanistan.33 Eligible participants had at least 20 years of working experience in NTDs in Afghanistan. Those who did not meet this criterion or could not provide informed consent were excluded.

Twelve potential participants were contacted via email, which included study details, a consent form and an option to withdraw at any time. Eight experts participated, two did not respond and two declined. Participants had experience working at local healthcare clinics, various health-focused NGOs operating in Afghanistan and the WHO.

No further recruitment was conducted as no additional key informants met the inclusion criteria.34 For some themes such as barriers in access to healthcare, diagnostic procedures and the use of telemedicine, data saturation was reached after the eighth interview. However, saturation could not be reached for the topic of telemedicine implementation. This was not due to the emergence of new insights in each interview but rather due to the participants’ lack of experience and detailed knowledge of telemedicine implementation, which limited in-depth discussions. Data saturation was achieved regarding participants’ perceptions of the current gender situation in Afghanistan, with no new themes emerging. In contrast, responses to questions about the potential role of telemedicine in promoting gender equality were more diverse. This heterogeneity may indicate the complexity and sociocultural sensitivity of the topic and suggests that data saturation was not fully reached for this topic.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews using a pre-prepared interview guide (online supplemental appendix 2) were conducted to ensure that relevant themes were addressed in an adaptable manner and that data collection was done in a consistent way.35 The guide included open-ended questions on Afghanistan’s health situation, skin NTD diagnosis and improvements, telemedicine use and challenges, implementation, stakeholders and gender considerations.

Interviews were conducted online via Zoom, lasting 20–50 min depending on participant availability and response depth. Data were collected from June to August 2024. The interviews were in English and were recorded using an external device with the consent of the participants, verified both on the consent form and at the start of the interview. They began with an introduction of the researcher, study explanation and participant introduction, followed by a Q&A. Data were stored per Maastricht University’s Data Management Code of Conduct.

Data analysis

The audio recordings of the interviews were transcribed verbatim.36 A codebook was developed using the research questions, literature and themes identified from the data. Thematic analysis was performed using both deductive and inductive coding.37 Deductive coding was applied using the alignment theory of Engel,32 38 while inductive coding incorporated emerging themes.39 Themes were identified to categorise key informants’ experiences and perspectives. Engel’s theory was used as a basis for understanding how the potential use of telemedicine should be aligned with the developer, actors, other telemedicine initiatives, skin NTD diagnostic methods and the Afghan context and how these elements might shape the implementation and actual success of telemedicine in Afghanistan.32 Data analysis was done using Atlas.ti V.24.1.1.

Attention was paid to reflexivity throughout the data analysis process. A reflexivity statement can be found in online supplemental appendix 3.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Eight participants were included in this study. Five participants were based in Afghanistan at the time of the interviews, while three had worked in the field of NTDs in Afghanistan within the past 20 years but were not currently based there. The majority of participants were male (n=7), while only one participant was female (n=1). Five participants had a healthcare background with clinical or epidemiological expertise and three had a health management background with organisational or programmatic roles.

Thematic analysis highlighted four main themes: challenges for the diagnosis of skin NTDs, healthcare accessibility, the role of telemedicine and implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan.

Challenges for the diagnosis of skin NTDs in Afghanistan

Leishmaniasis and scabies are the most prevalent skin NTDs in Afghanistan, with leprosy being less common. Key informants noted that the Afghan Ministry of Health prioritises more common diseases, leading to inconsistent or non-existent diagnostic protocols for skin NTDs. One participant explained:

The big problem here is the diagnostic procedures in Afghanistan, there is no standard protocols to follow…. so, everyone has their own approach for the diagnosis of skin disease. (Key informant #7).

The lack of standardised guidelines underscores the need for a national programme to improve diagnosis and treatment. While not explicitly linked to telemedicine by respondents, the absence of standardised protocols for the diagnosis of skin NTDs may limit its effective implementation. Another challenge to the diagnosis of skin NTDs that emerged was uninformed prescribing of medication. One participant used ‘misuse of medication’ to describe a situation in which (healthcare) staff prescribe medications without clear guidelines. This could result in patients receiving incorrect advice before a proper diagnosis has been made. One participant explains:

In the remote areas, you hardly see a patient who has not caught any corticosteroid ointment from a shop. When you ask from where did you get that ointment; ah I bought it in that and that shop. And in the end, you say well this person sold you this ointment? Yes, they said this will help me. (Key informant #1).

The use of corticosteroids can cause misdiagnosis because the medication masks symptoms of skin NTDs. A participant emphasised the need for clear guidelines on what to do when a patient has skin symptoms and who is allowed to diagnose skin NTDs. This was a recurring concern among the participants, given the limited knowledge of basic dermatology within the Afghan healthcare system. Several key informants point to the need to improve basic dermatology knowledge among health workers to enhance early diagnosis and effective treatment. Participants indicated that teledermatology could potentially play a role in sharing experiences among healthcare providers, thereby promoting accurate diagnosis. Another challenge in diagnosing skin NTDs is related to stigma. Stigma, particularly for leprosy, was felt to deter patients from seeking care. One participant describes the consequences.

Leprosy means if you diagnose a patient, then first of all yes, the stigma is in, this will change his life. Sometimes husband is leaving the patient because of this. (Key informant #2).

Raising community awareness about the treatability of leprosy is crucial to reducing stigma and ensuring timely treatment.

Healthcare accessibility in Afghanistan

Accessibility challenges for diagnosis

Two key themes emerged regarding access to healthcare for patients with skin NTDs. First, operational challenges, such as too many patients, drug shortages and lack of trained personnel in the country. Key informants indicated that future training and the provision of expertise were needed to improve access to healthcare and diagnostics of skin NTDs. Second, almost all key informants identified geographical barriers, recognising that most medical facilities are located in bigger cities, indicating differences in access to diagnosis between people in rural areas and in urban areas.

In the remote areas, this situation is completely different, for example to say you need a skin biopsy, it is a big endeavour for the patient and the family (Key informant #1).

Participants indicated that patients have to travel for hours to reach a clinic and that high costs are involved. This illustrates how difficult getting access to healthcare is and how much effort people living in remote areas must put into going to a clinic where skin NTDs can be diagnosed.

Besides the difficulty of accessing healthcare in remote areas, it is also more difficult for other vulnerable groups to get a skin NTD diagnosis in Afghanistan.

Gender equity and access to healthcare

Participants explained that gender has a significant role in access to healthcare in Afghanistan. In general, men have better access to healthcare compared with women, which also makes the diagnosis of skin diseases more difficult for women than for men. When asked to explain what makes women’s access problematic compared with men’s, the response was cultural norms. Women must be accompanied by a man if they want to go somewhere. One participant explained that men often cannot leave their jobs because doing so would mean losing their income. As a result, women lack a male companion to accompany them. This leaves women unable to access healthcare facilities.

“males/men have to move to the big cities to find work and income for the family and that again, you see, creates or can create a hurdle for accessibility of services” (Key informant #1).

This shows that it is difficult for women to travel to healthcare facilities, resulting in less access to healthcare.

Moreover, participants highlighted that doctors in Afghanistan are often men, and, within this setting, only female doctors are allowed to check a woman’s body. One participant explains that it is not possible for a male doctor to diagnose a woman for skin diseases because women are not allowed to undress in the presence of a male doctor.

Normally they accept only female doctors for this and let’s say in tuberculosis it is not such a big problem, so you can put the stethoscope under the clothes, this is still fine. But if you want to start to tell the lady ; please take out your clothes, this is not possible (Key informant #2).

The lesions of skin diseases are often located in a covered area which requires checking the entire body, something that can only be done by a woman. This related to another challenge mentioned by participants, namely the shortage of female healthcare workers. Participants mentioned that in many clinics, there are no or only a few female healthcare providers present who can examine women, which discourages women from visiting a doctor.

Participants expressed doubt about the potential of telemedicine to improve gender equity in access to healthcare. They stated that they see telemedicine more as something between healthcare providers than for patient use. When asking about the difference between men and women when using telemedicine, one participant indicated that men are more likely to use telemedicine because of the limited access to technology for women and the restrictions on their autonomy.

The following section examines the status of telemedicine in Afghanistan, as well as participants’ perceptions about the potential use of telemedicine to improve and facilitate the diagnosis of skin NTDs.

The role of telemedicine in Afghanistan

Current state of telemedicine in Afghanistan

While many were familiar with telemedicine, only a few participants had used telemedicine in Afghanistan. Telecommunication is used in one Afghan NGO, and some participants knew doctors who have emigrated to other countries but use telecommunications to continue seeing their patients in Afghanistan. Although there have been telemedicine initiatives in Afghanistan, participants mentioned none have focused on skin diseases so far. When asked whether telemedicine initiatives in other healthcare disciplines could serve as a model for implementing telemedicine for skin NTDs in Afghanistan, one participant explained:

Maybe, but it was not perfectly successful. It needs for more support. Especially during previous political transition, some came here, started their work. They finished and they went back after six months (Key informant #6).

This highlights the lack of current telemedicine programmes in Afghanistan and the importance of focusing on proper and sustainable implementation of a new telemedicine project. Participants expressed hope for future development.

Advantages of the potential use of telemedicine

Participants saw telemedicine as potentially useful for the diagnosis of skin NTDs. They noted telemedicine would be suitable for skin NTDs because dermatology is a specialty and, consequently, not every healthcare worker is qualified to diagnose skin NTDs. Because of the lack of dermatologists in the country, telemedicine could provide remote access to specialists. The use of telemedicine could help avoid long trips to clinics and contribute to timely diagnosis. One participant explained:

[Without] The knowledge it [skin NTDs] cannot be diagnosed, so it means telemedicine is the best thing for that because many people can share their experience and to get a right [find the right] way for [to] the diagnosis of leprosy (Key informant #5).

Key informants emphasised the use of telemedicine to share knowledge and obtain second opinions as a significant advantage for the potential use of telemedicine. Participants mainly envision the role of telemedicine between healthcare professionals to share knowledge rather than between a patient and a doctor. A participant explained that suspected leprosy cases are currently referred to Kabul. There, the only two NGOs in Afghanistan working on leprosy conduct in-person assessments. In the future, these organisations plan to use telemedicine. This would help avoid long and difficult travel, which may prevent some patients from seeking care. One participant highlighted that if a patient cannot attend in person, a second opinion should still be obtained. He described how such a remote diagnostic process could be organised:

We try to send this [these] pictures by telemedicine and to get already here a first impression and if they in Kabul think they cannot make a decision, then other people for example in Pakistan or in Germany should be involved here, in this. This is our idea just to make it easier for everybody. And to have more security in making the diagnosis (Key informant #2).

Finally, the use of telemedicine in suspected cases of leprosy could be useful for differential diagnosis between different skin diseases. One participant explained why telemedicine could be helpful, saying:

I think people still spend yes, a lot of money buying medications that are actually useless so I think there is a huge demand for it, that it can be very useful if you can just pick out already fungal disorders, eczema, things like that as well. Because obviously that’s 99 percent (Key informant #3).

Using telemedicine, at least some skin diseases can already be ruled out and follow-up steps determined without a patient having to travel long distances or wait for a dermatologist appointment. According to the participants, leishmaniasis is more common in Afghanistan, so it is more often thought of when diagnosing skin diseases. There is less experience with leprosy in Afghanistan, and telemedicine therefore could support the accurate diagnosis of leprosy.

Challenges to the potential use of telemedicine

Participants acknowledged that telemedicine is not a universal solution and identified several challenges regarding the potential use of telemedicine for diagnosis of skin NTDs. First, diagnosing skin NTDs often requires laboratory tests and physical examination. For leprosy, palpation for nerve enlargement and testing for sensory loss is crucial. One key informant highlighted this saying:

Telemedicine you can maybe send a picture of the integument [skin], but you cannot check its nerve enlargement, and you can also not check hyposensitivity which is quite important for the diagnostics (Key informant #2).

Without physical examinations and lab tests, telemedicine relies solely on images, limiting its diagnostic accuracy for certain conditions.

Participants also had different opinions on which skin diseases are most suitable for the use of telemedicine. One participant indicated that telemedicine could especially be helpful for common diseases. In common diseases, telemedicine would be a valuable addition to current diagnostic procedures. Other participants, in contrast, thought telemedicine is not necessarily useful for a common disease like leishmaniasis because normally every health centre should be able to handle these diseases. When leishmaniasis is diagnosed with telemedicine, treatment cannot be performed immediately. This makes the use of telemedicine no more beneficial to the patient than an in-person diagnosis. These participants mentioned that telemedicine is more suitable for rare diseases such as leprosy because there are only a few leprosy experts in Afghanistan. Using telemedicine, a remote dermatologist could share their knowledge on leprosy, confirm whether it is leprosy and/or rule out other diseases.

Overall, telemedicine was felt to have potential but was not regarded as a perfect solution. Physical exams and tests remain crucial, infrastructure must be improved and more clarity is needed on how best to integrate the use of telemedicine for rare diseases such as leprosy within the Afghan health system.

Implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan

Stakeholders’ involvement and the implementation process

Participants explained that currently only two NGOs are working with skin NTDs in Afghanistan. They indicated that they believe the Ministry of Health of Afghanistan and the Afghan government should also be involved in this field. Furthermore, help from international organisations and local and international NGOs was mentioned but one participant believes that for the successful implementation of telemedicine:

You also need at least a health worker who knows how to deal with it and is part of a network

(Key informant #3).

Key informants highlight that local physicians must also be engaged, as they understand their population’s needs and will be the primary users of telemedicine. Their involvement is essential for effective integration into daily healthcare practices.

Implementation challenges

Participants highlighted several key aspects for the implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan. Sustainability was a major concern, as short-term projects were viewed as ineffective. Integration into local programmes and adaptation of international interventions to the local context were highlighted. Finally, other challenges that were mentioned focused on technical challenges such as poor internet connectivity and limited infrastructure in Afghanistan, insufficient training and issues with the quality of images. When asked what is needed in Afghanistan for the implementation of telemedicine, the participants indicated that a system is needed in Afghanistan. One participant illustrated this saying:

And then of course, there have to be clear structures…. who is making the decision, who is the implementer and afterwards who is monitoring the whole thing. Then it will work. (Key informant #1).

This shows that successful implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan requires investment in the telemedicine system as well as a well-organised and controlled system in which clear standard operating procedures (SOPs) are created.

User preparedness was another important theme; telemedicine technology must be user-friendly, and the end-users must be able to use the telemedicine technology, with adequate training for users and patients. Finally, respondents indicated that funding is needed to financially support telemedicine projects. These findings show the importance of preparing the setting in which the telemedicine project will be implemented, the importance of setting up an underlying system for the implementation of telemedicine, and the importance of ensuring a functioning technological infrastructure if telemedicine were to be implemented in Afghanistan. Discussion

This research explored the potential role of telemedicine in diagnosing skin NTDs in remote areas of Afghanistan, the challenges involved and the key considerations required for implementation. This study highlights the lack of a diagnostic system, infrastructure and geographical barriers to diagnosing skin NTDs.

The use of teledermatology for the diagnosis of skin NTDs in Afghanistan is promising. Telemedicine is a diagnostic tool that is a potentially useful method to control skin NTDs.18 40 Teledermatology offers great potential in improving accessibility to healthcare and consultations between primary care physicians and specialists, allowing patients to be helped more quickly.41 42 Specialists can be consulted remotely, long trips are avoided, costs are saved and through differential diagnosis with teledermatology, diagnoses of skin diseases become more accurate and faster.43 This suggests telemedicine could enhance healthcare access and diagnosis in remote areas. However, the role of teledermatology remains uncertain. While telemedicine can facilitate a second opinion,44 45 it cannot replace physical examinations, which are vital for the diagnosis of many skin NTDs. This limitation may result in reduced accuracy and reliability of diagnoses made via telemedicine. Telemedicine may therefore be more appropriate as an adjunct to current diagnoses rather than a replacement. This finding was consistent with the study conducted on the use of teledermatology in Nepal.46

In this context, training of healthcare workers, protocols and a well-established healthcare system are important so that in uncertain cases of skin NTDs, telemedicine could be used as a helpful tool for diagnosis, but in-person examination could still take place. Implementing telemedicine would thus require similar infrastructure as the current in-person diagnostics. This means that technical problems, as in the current system, namely internet, power and delivery problems as well as healthcare accessibility challenges, would also pose issues for a telemedicine-based approach. It is important to keep this in mind when considering the possible implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan for the diagnosis of skin NTDs. Matching the diagnostic technology to current conditions and resources is crucial for telemedicine to work.32

While telemedicine may not immediately address the structural gender inequalities that limit access to healthcare in Afghanistan, it has the potential to mitigate some of the barriers faced by women. The use of telemedicine could at least allow women to receive remote advice from scarce female doctors. In a context where it is difficult for many women to leave home, telemedicine offers a way to receive needed healthcare without being hindered by travel distance.47 Telemedicine may not address root causes of gender inequality but with the use of telemedicine, women’s access to healthcare could be improved.47

Implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan

To understand how telemedicine could be effectively integrated into the Afghan healthcare system, this study also focused on the challenges participants experienced and expect in the potential implementation of telemedicine. The challenges identified in this study correspond to those frequently cited in the broader literature on the implementation of telemedicine, such as the need for funding, network availability in remote areas and the establishment of a technological infrastructure.48–51 However, one of the main challenges we encountered has not yet been addressed or has been addressed only to a limited extent in previous research on the implementation of telemedicine. The key challenge for the implementation of telemedicine found in this study was the need for a monitored diagnostic system.

The study of Engel52 emphasises that successful implementation of innovations depends on the alignment between developers, users, systems and context. A well-organised healthcare system would support the alignment between these settings. Furthermore, Engel highlights that standards for the implementation of health technologies are made worldwide, whereas specific contexts require tailored guidelines for the implementation of health technology.52 This is consistent with the experiences of participants who indicated that telemedicine can only be successful if telemedicine is implemented in a sustainable way, supported by clear agreements and defined responsibilities. Moreover, local authorities must be involved in the process to adapt telemedicine technology to local programmes and ensure that it fits into the intended setting. This challenge is rarely reported in other telemedicine studies, which raises the question of whether it is genuinely absent in other settings or simply not captured due to differences in research focus. It is possible that these studies primarily highlight the use and benefits of telemedicine while paying less attention to the implementation process, one of the central concerns of the present study.

Study limitations

Limitations of this study include the small group of key informants participating. The small study group makes the generalisability of this research limited. Another limitation is the absence of involvement of patients or governmental officials. This study was based on the experiences and perspectives of key informants working in the field of skin NTDs. Some of the participants were not from Afghanistan and only visited Afghanistan a long time ago or for a short period of time, which may have influenced their experiences and knowledge about the current situation. This study also primarily captured the perspective of healthcare workers within health-focused NGOs in Afghanistan, so the perspective from Afghan government authorities or patients is not represented.

Conclusion

The use of telemedicine for the diagnosis of skin NTDs could play an important role in Afghanistan in improving access to healthcare for people living in remote areas and thereby contribute to the eradication of skin NTDs such as leprosy and CL in Afghanistan. Nevertheless, telemedicine should not be the only component in diagnosing skin NTDs in Afghanistan because physical examination is still important for the diagnosis of skin NTDs. In addition, addressing current technical issues in the current Afghan infrastructure, such as poor internet connectivity, is crucial to the implementation of telemedicine in Afghanistan.
