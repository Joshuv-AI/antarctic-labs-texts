Asymmetric Self-similar Spiral Solutions of 2-D Incompressible Euler Equations
Hyungjun Choi

Abstract
We construct nonradial, self-similar solutions to the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations without assuming rotational symmetry. These solutions extend the study of self-similar algebraic spiral flows, initiated by Elling and further developed by Shao–Wei–Zhang [ 41 ] , where m m -fold symmetry with m ≥ 2 m\geq 2 was assumed. Moreover, they bear resemblance to the numerical simulations of Bressan-Shen [ 10 ] , in connection with the ongoing investigation into non-uniqueness of solutions.



1.  Introduction

We study solutions of the 2-D incompressible Euler equations

\left|\begin{array}[]{rcl}\partial_{t}u+u\cdot\nabla u+\nabla p&=&0,\\
u(t=0)&=&u_{0}.\end{array}\right.

(1.1)

Considering the vorticity
\omega=-\partial_{2}u_{1}+\partial_{1}u_{2}
, we obtain the vorticity equation:

\left|\begin{array}[]{rcl}\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega&=&0,\\
u=\nabla^{\perp}\psi,\penalty\ \Delta\psi&=&\omega,\\
\omega(t=0)&=&\omega_{0},\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

where
\psi
is the stream function and
\nabla^{\perp}=(-\partial_{2},\partial_{1})^{t}
. Since
\omega
is transported by a divergence-free vector field
u
, the distribution of
\omega
is conserved in time. In particular,
\lVert\omega(t,\cdot)\rVert_{L^{\infty}}=\lVert\omega_{0}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}
. This, together with the Beale-Kato-Majda continuation criterion, guarantees the global existence of smooth solutions. On the other hand, the borderline spaces
\omega\in L^{\infty}
or
u\in C^{1}
are ill-posed; see
[
8
,
22
]
.

We now turn to the study of non-smooth solutions. A distribution
u(t,x)\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
is called a
weak solution
to the Euler equation (
1.1
) with initial data
u_{0}
if

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{0}\phi(t=0)\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(u\otimes u):\nabla\phi\,dx\,dt=0,

for all divergence-free test functions
\phi(t,x)\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})
. A distributional weak solution
\omega
to (
1.2
) is defined analogously. Yudovich
[
50
]
proved that there is a unique weak solution
\omega\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2}))
, provided
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{\infty}
and
u_{0}\in L^{2}
. The existence of weak solutions has been extended to broader classes of initial data. DiPerna and Majda
[
19
]
showed the existence of the weak solution of 2-D incompressible Euler equations with initial data
\omega_{0}\in L^{1}\cap L^{p}
,
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{\text{loc}}
that conserves
\lVert\omega\rVert_{L^{1}\cap L^{p}}
. Later, Delort
[
17
]
extended the existence result to bounded, distinguished sign measure
\omega_{0}\in\mathcal{M}\cap H^{-1}_{\text{loc}}
, which includes the vortex sheet case.

For the uniqueness,
L^{\infty}
-control of the vorticity can be relaxed to the so-called Yudovich space
[
51
]
. See
[
46
,
7
,
6
]
for further developments on the well-posedness of weak solutions with unbounded vorticity. The integrability condition of the vorticity can also be altered. Benedeto-Marchioro-Pulvirenti
[
5
]
showed the well-posedness of the class
\omega_{0}\in L^{p}\cap L^{\infty}
,
|u_{0}|\lesssim 1+|x|^{\alpha}
, for
p<2/\alpha
,
\alpha<1
. While Serfaty
[
40
]
established the well-posedness of the class
\omega_{0},u_{0}\in L^{\infty}
, without any integrability condition. More recently, Elgindi-Jeong
[
21
]
showed that
\omega_{0}\in L^{\infty}
with
m
-fold rotational symmetry for
m\geq 3
is sufficient for the well-posedness.

In this work, we are interested in
self-similar solutions
of the 2-D incompressible Euler equation, which take the form

\omega(t,x)=t^{-1}\Omega(t^{-\mu}x),\quad u(t,x)=t^{-1+\mu}U(t^{-\mu}x).

(1.3)

The vorticity profile
\Omega
is singular at the origin, is locally integrable, and could be sign-changing. The initial data is
(-1/\mu)
-homogeneous, placing it outside the scope of the existence and uniqueness results discussed above. In the remainder of the introduction, we review previous work in this direction and present our main result. These self-similar spiral solutions are potentially related to non-uniqueness phenomena in the 2-D incompressible Euler equations in two distinct contexts, which we elaborate on in section
1.2
.

1.1.  Self-similar spiral solutions and main result

Algebraic spirals naturally arise from self-similar solutions of the 2-D incompressible Euler equations. If a self-similar profile (
1.3
) solves the vorticity equation (
1.2
), then
\Omega
satisfies

\left|\begin{array}[]{rcl}(U-\mu x)\cdot\nabla\Omega&=&\Omega,\\
U&=&\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(1.4)

Since the radial vortex
\omega(x)=\omega(|x|)
is a steady state solution to (
1.2
), the function

\overline{\Omega}(x)=C|x|^{-\frac{1}{\mu}},

is a solution to (
1.4
). The characteristic curve of the equation is
\theta=\theta_{0}+C(2-\frac{1}{\mu})^{-1}r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}
, where
(r,\theta)
is the polar coordinates. This illustrates how algebraic spirals emerge as perturbations of
\overline{\Omega}
. In a series of works
[
23
,
24
]
, Elling established the following:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
24
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Given
\mu>\frac{2}{3}
and
M>0
, there is
N_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that a weak solution
u
of (
1.1
) exists with initial data
\omega_{0}=r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}\mathring{\omega}(\theta)
, whenever
\mathring{\omega}
is
N
-fold rotationaly symmetric for
N\geq N_{0}
and

\sum_{n\neq 0}|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{n}|\leq M|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{0}|.

This result was generalized by Shao-Wei-Zhang
[
41
]
, who relaxed the symmetry assumption and extended the range of the parameter
\mu
.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
41
]
, Theorem 1.2)
.

Given
\mu>\frac{1}{2}
and
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{>1}
, there is an
\epsilon>0
, independent of
m
, such that a weak solution
u
of (
1.1
) exists with initial data
\omega_{0}=r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}\mathring{\omega}(\theta)
, whenever
\mathring{\omega}
is
N
-fold rotationaly symmetric and

\lVert\mathring{\omega}-\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{0}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\epsilon N^{\frac{1}{2}}|\mathring{\omega}_{0}|.

Our main result generalizes the above theorems by completely removing the rotational symmetry assumption.

Main Theorem
.

Given
\mu>1
, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for any
\mathring{\omega}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z})
satisfying

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}|n|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{n}|\leq\epsilon|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{0}|,

(1.5)

there is a weak solution
u\in C([0,\infty);L^{2}_{\text{loc}})
to (
1.1
) and
\omega\in C([0,\infty);L^{1}_{\text{loc}})
to (
1.2
) where

•

(initial data)
\omega_{0}(x)=r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}\mathring{\omega}(\theta)
,
u_{0}(x)=\nabla^{\perp}\Delta^{-1}\omega_{0}
;

•

(self-similar)
\omega(t,x)=t^{-1}\Omega(t^{-\mu}x)
for
\Omega\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and
u(t,x)=t^{-1+\mu}U(t^{-\mu}x)
for a continuous div-free vector field
U
.

Remark 1
. The initial stream function
\psi_{0}=\Delta^{-1}\omega_{0}
could be found via Fourier series. Since
2-\frac{1}{\mu}
is never an integer, the equation

\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)^{2}\mathring{\psi}+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\mathring{\psi}=\mathring{\omega},

has a unique solution
\mathring{\psi}
. Setting
\psi_{0}=r^{2-\frac{1}{\mu}}\mathring{\psi}(\theta)
yields
\Delta\psi_{0}=\omega_{0}
.

Remark 2
. Our choice of space for
\mathring{\omega}
is motivated by the Wiener algebra setting used in
[
24
]
. Similar results with assumption (
1.5
), but still with
m
-fold rotational symmetry with large
m
, were obtained in
[
29
]
. The novelty of our result lies in eliminating this symmetry via improved linear analysis.

Remark 3
. When
N
-fold rotational symmetric data is considered, our result recovers that of
[
41
]
(see Theorem
1.2
above), in a different smallness condition:

\sum_{k\neq 0}|k|^{-\frac{1}{2}}|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{kN}|\leq\epsilon N^{\frac{1}{2}}|\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{0}|.

Since the smallness parameter
\epsilon>0
is independent of
N
, taking
N
large can serve as a substitute for the smallness assumption, consistent with the approach in
[
24
]
.

Remark 4
. If
\mathring{\omega}\in L^{p}
for some
p
, then
\omega\in C([0,\infty);L^{q}_{\text{loc}})
for
q=\min\{p,2\mu\}
. Thus, for
p\geq 2
,
\omega
is a renormalized solution in the sense of Diperna-Lions
[
18
]
. Furthermore, the assumption
\mathring{\omega}\in L^{1}
can be relaxed to
\mathring{\omega}
being a bounded measure, via mollification (see Section 8 of
[
41
]
and Corollary
5.11
).

Self-similar spiral solutions without rotational symmetry were also constructed for the generalized SQG equations by García and Gómez-Serrano
[
25
]
, where

\left|\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega=0,\\
u=-\nabla^{\perp}(-\Delta)^{-1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}\omega.\end{array}\right.

To ensure the integrability of the nonlocal operator
(-\Delta)^{-1+\frac{\gamma}{2}}\omega
, they require
\frac{1}{2}<\mu<\frac{1}{2-\gamma}
. This range excludes the Euler case
\gamma=0
. In contrast, our focus on the regime
\mu>1
for the 2-D Euler explores a different region of the parameters. Extending our approach to the surface quasi-geostrophic (SQG) equations would be an interesting direction for future work.

1.2.  Non-uniqueness in incompressible fluid equations

The non-uniqueness of weak solutions of the Euler equations (
1.1
) has been an active field of study. The first explicit examples of non-unique solutions were constructed by Scheffer and Shnirelman
[
39
,
44
]
. A decisive breakthrough came with the introduction of convex-integration techniques by De Lellis and Székelyhidi
[
16
]
, which later powered the celebrated proofs of Onsager’s conjecture
[
28
,
26
]
.

Since Yudovich’s fundamental work
[
50
,
51
]
, it has remained an open question whether uniqueness continues to hold for the vorticity that merely lies in
L^{p}
for
p<\infty
. Brué and Colombo
[
11
]
showed non-uniqueness in the space
\omega\in L^{\infty}_{t}L^{1,\infty}_{x}
. More recently, the same authors, together with Kumar
[
12
]
, introduced a novel convex integration scheme to establish non-uniqueness in
\omega\in L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}_{x}
for some
p>1
, close to
1
.

A complementary line of research considers the forced vorticity equation. In the groundbreaking work
[
47
,
48
]
, Vishik proved non-uniqueness by exploiting unstable self-similar profiles. These ideas have since been streamlined and simplified in a series of works, notably the book
[
2
]
and papers
[
20
,
13
]
.

1.2.1. Bressan-Shen numerics

For vorticities in
L^{p}
with
p\geq\frac{3}{2}
, the two-dimensional Euler flow conserves kinetic energy
[
14
]
; when
p\geq 2
, DiPerna–Lions theory guarantees the ‘renormalised’ transport structure
[
18
]
. Hence, any definitive non-uniqueness mechanism in the regime
p\geq 2
must therefore respect the underlying transport equation.

Bressan and Shen carried out numerical experiments suggesting that the same initial data might evolve into two distinct self-similar solutions: one forming a single algebraic spiral with two wings, and the other splitting into a pair of spirals, each with a single wing. Our analytical results support the plausibility of the single-wing spiral. See
[
9
,
10
,
43
]
for more explanation and codes.

1.2.2. Non-uniqueness in 3-D incompressible Navier-Stokes equation

A similar program pursues the non-uniqueness of Leray–Hopf weak solutions to the 3-D incompressible Navier-Stokes equation via self-similar solutions. Jia and Šverák
[
31
]
produced self-similar solutions for all
-1
-homogeneous initial data, and later showed that a bifurcation arises when the linearised operator around such a profile has a simple kernel
[
32
]
. Numerical evidence for this scenario can be found in
[
27
]
. On the other hand, Albritton, Brué, and Colombo
[
1
]
achieved non-uniqueness for the forced 3-D Navier–Stokes equations, further developing Vishik’s self-similar instability in this setting.

1.2.3. Rolling up and bifurcation in vortex sheet dynamics

A striking feature of the self-similar spirals constructed in
[
23
,
24
,
41
]
, and in the present paper, is that the spiral is absent at the initial time but emerges instantaneously afterward, a phenomenon reminiscent of vortex-sheet ‘roll-up’. This behaviour has long been observed both analytically and numerically in vortex-sheet dynamics
[
45
,
35
,
36
]
. It stands in contrast to the logarithmic spiral solutions
[
30
]
, in which the spiral is already present at the initial time.

Pullin’s numerical simulations
[
37
,
38
]
revealed a bifurcation in which a single sheet, sheared in opposite directions on its two sides, splits into two branches that roll up separately. Similar numerical evidence was reported in
[
33
]
. Recently, Shao, Wei, and Zhang
[
42
]
analytically constructed vortex sheet roll-up with
m
-fold rotational symmetry, where
m
is sufficiently large.

2.  Self-similar Equations and Adapted Coordinates

We study the 2-D incompressible Euler equations in the vorticity form (
1.2
). Given an initial data

\omega_{0}(r,\theta)=r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}\mathring{\omega}(\theta),

(2.1)

we seek a self-similar solution of the form

\omega(t,x)=t^{-1}\Omega(t^{-\mu}x),\quad u(t,x)=t^{-1+\mu}U(t^{-\mu}x),\quad\psi(t,x)=t^{-1+2\mu}\Psi(t^{-\mu}x).

(2.2)

Then, the self-similar profile
\Omega
should satisfy:

\left|\begin{array}[]{rcl}(\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x)\cdot\nabla\Omega&=&\Omega,\\
\Delta\Psi&=&\Omega,\\
\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}r^{\frac{1}{\mu}}\Omega(r,\theta)&=&\mathring{\omega}(\theta).\end{array}\right.

(2.3)

We seek a solution to (
2.3
), using the adapted coordinates, which were introduced by Elling
[
23
,
24
]
. Assume that there is a change of coordinates
(r,\theta)\to(\beta,\phi)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{T}
such that

•

\theta\equiv\beta+\phi\,(\text{mod}\,2\pi)

•

\phi
is constant on the characteristic curve of
\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x
, i.e.,

(\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x)\cdot\nabla\phi=0.

(2.4)

•

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
,
r(\beta,\theta-\beta)
is decreasing in
\beta
,
\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to\infty}r(\beta,\theta-\beta)=0
and
\displaystyle\lim_{\beta\to 0+}r(\beta,\theta-\beta)=\infty
.

This assumption is equivalent to requiring that the characteristic curves originating from
r=\infty
fill all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
without intersecting one another. However, this property may fail for certain self-similar solutions
\Omega
to the equation (
2.3
). In section
2.1
, we examine the radial solution
\overline{\Omega}=Cr^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}
, which generates algebraic spiral trajectories of the form
\beta=\frac{C}{2-\mu^{-1}}r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}
. A key challenge is to construct perturbations of this solution that preserve the non-intersecting spiral structure. From equation (
2.4
), we observe that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=(\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x)\cdot\nabla\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\partial_{r}\Psi\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{r}\partial_{\theta}\Psi\partial_{r}\phi-\mu r\partial_{r}\phi

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}(\partial_{r}\beta\partial_{\theta}\phi-\partial_{\theta}\beta\partial_{r}\phi)\partial_{\beta}\Psi-\mu r\partial_{r}\phi.

Since

\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{r}\beta&\partial_{\theta}\beta\\
\partial_{r}\phi&\partial_{\theta}\phi\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{\beta}r&\partial_{\phi}r\\
\partial_{\beta}\theta&\partial_{\phi}\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}*&*\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},

we have

\partial_{r}\beta\partial_{\theta}\phi-\partial_{\theta}\beta\partial_{r}\phi=-\partial_{r}\phi.

Hence,

\partial_{\beta}\Psi=-\mu r^{2}.

(2.5)

For convenience, we write

\partial_{\rho}=\partial_{\phi}-\partial_{\beta},\quad D_{\beta}=\beta\partial_{\beta},\quad D_{\rho}=\beta\partial_{\rho}.

Since
\frac{d}{d\beta}f(\beta,\theta-\beta)=-(\partial_{\rho}f)(\beta,\theta-\beta)
, the operator
\partial_{\rho}
is a differential in the radial direction. Note that

r\partial_{r}=r\partial_{r}\phi\partial_{\phi}+r\partial_{r}\beta\partial_{\beta}=\frac{r}{\partial_{\rho}r}\partial_{\rho}=\frac{2\partial_{\beta}\Psi}{\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\rho},

(2.6)

and

\partial_{\theta}=\partial_{\theta}\phi\partial_{\phi}+\partial_{\theta}\beta\partial_{\beta}=\frac{-\partial_{\beta}r\partial_{\phi}+\partial_{\phi}r\partial_{\beta}}{\partial_{\rho}r}=\partial_{\phi}-\frac{\partial_{\phi}r}{\partial_{\rho}r}\partial_{\rho}=\partial_{\phi}-\frac{\partial_{\phi\beta}\Psi}{\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\rho}.

(2.7)

We can solve the self-similar Euler equation along the characteristic curve:

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\{(\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x)\cdot\nabla\beta\}\partial_{\beta}\Omega

\displaystyle=\Big\{\frac{1}{r}(-\partial_{\theta}\Psi\partial_{r}\beta+\partial_{r}\Psi\partial_{\theta}\beta)-\mu r\partial_{r}\beta\Big\}\partial_{\beta}\Omega=-2\mu\frac{\partial_{\rho}\Psi}{\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\beta}\Omega.

Hence,

\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\Big(\Omega(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{\frac{1}{2\mu}}\Big)=0.

And thus,

\Omega=(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}\Gamma(\phi).

(2.8)

Combining (
2.5
) and (
2.8
), we can rewrite Poisson’s equation
\Delta\Psi=\Omega
in the form

(r\partial_{r})^{2}\Psi+\partial_{\theta}^{2}\Psi=r^{2}\Omega=-\frac{1}{\mu}\partial_{\beta}\Psi(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}\Gamma(\phi).

We now perform a change of variables according to (
2.6
) and (
2.7
). In the new coordinates, the equation becomes:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=2\mu\partial_{\rho}(r\partial_{r}\Psi)+2\mu\bigg(\frac{\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi}{2\partial_{\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\phi}-\frac{\partial_{\phi\beta}\Psi}{2\partial_{\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\rho}\bigg)(\partial_{\theta}\Psi)+\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}\Gamma(\phi)

\displaystyle=\mu\partial_{\rho}\bigg(2r\partial_{r}\Psi-\frac{\partial_{\phi\beta}\Psi}{\partial_{\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\theta}\Psi\bigg)+\mu\partial_{\phi}\bigg(\frac{\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi}{\partial_{\beta}\Psi}\partial_{\theta}\Psi\bigg)+\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}\Gamma(\phi)

Define
f\triangleq\beta^{2\mu-1}\Psi
and
h\triangleq(-D_{\beta}+2\mu-1)f
. Then,

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\Psi

\displaystyle=-\beta^{-2\mu}h,

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi}\Psi

\displaystyle=\beta^{-2\mu+1}\partial_{\phi}f,

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\Psi

\displaystyle=\beta^{-2\mu}(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f,

\displaystyle\partial_{\phi\beta}\Psi

\displaystyle=-\beta^{-2\mu}\partial_{\phi}h,

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho\beta}\Psi

\displaystyle=-\beta^{-2\mu-1}(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h,

and

\displaystyle r\partial_{r}\Psi

\displaystyle=2\beta^{-2\mu+1}\frac{h(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h},

\displaystyle\partial_{\theta}\Psi

\displaystyle=\beta^{-2\mu+1}\bigg(\partial_{\phi}f-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}\bigg).

Therefore, in the
(\beta,\phi)
coordinate system and in terms of the unknown function
f
, Poisson’s equation takes the form

\mathcal{F}(\Gamma,f)\triangleq\mu(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)\mathcal{N}_{1}(f)+\mu\partial_{\phi}\mathcal{N}_{2}(f)-\mathcal{N}_{3}(f)\Gamma(\phi)=0,

(2.9)

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{N}_{1}(f)&=\frac{4h\cdot(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h}{h}\bigg(\partial_{\phi}f-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h\cdot(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}\bigg)\\
&=\bigg(4h-\frac{(\partial_{\phi}h)^{2}}{h}\bigg)\frac{(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h\partial_{\phi}f}{h},\\
\mathcal{N}_{2}(f)&=\frac{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}{h}\bigg(\partial_{\phi}f-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h\cdot(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f}{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}\bigg)\\
&=\frac{(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h}{h}\partial_{\phi}f-\frac{\partial_{\phi}h}{h}(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f,\\
\mathcal{N}_{3}(f)&=(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h\cdot\big((D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f\big)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}.\end{split}

(2.10)

2.1.  Radial solution in adapted coordinates

A radial profile
\omega(t,x)=c_{0}|x|^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}
is a stationary solution for the two-dimensional Euler equation. When
\mu\neq 1
,

\Omega=c_{0}r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}},\qquad\Psi=c_{0}\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)^{-2}r^{2-\frac{1}{\mu}}.

The characteristic ODE for the vector field
\nabla^{\perp}\Psi-\mu x
is:

\left|\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{r}(s)&=&-\mu r,\\
\dot{\theta}(s)&=&c_{0}\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)^{-1}r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}.\end{array}\right.

Solving these gives

\beta=c_{0}\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)^{-1}r^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}.

And thus

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle=c_{0}^{2\mu}\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)^{-2\mu-1}\beta^{1-2\mu},

\displaystyle\Omega

\displaystyle=\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)\beta.

Since
\Omega=\Gamma(\partial_{\rho}\Psi)^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}
,

\Gamma=c_{0}\mu^{\frac{1}{2\mu}}.

We set

c_{0}=\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)\mu^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}.

Summing up,

\Psi_{0}=\frac{1}{2\mu-1}\beta^{1-2\mu},\qquad\Gamma_{0}(\phi)=2-\frac{1}{\mu},

solves
\Delta\Psi_{0}=\Gamma_{0}(\partial_{\rho}\Psi_{0})^{-\frac{1}{\mu}}
. And thus,
(\Gamma_{0},f_{0})=(2-\frac{1}{\mu},\frac{1}{2\mu-1})
satisfies
\mathcal{F}(\Gamma_{0},f_{0})=0
.

2.2.  Organization of the paper

We begin by analyzing equations (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) in the adapted coordinates
(\beta,\phi)
. The following theorem establishes the existence of solutions in a suitable functional framework. The precise definitions of the function spaces
X
,
Y
, and
Z
are provided in section
3
.

Theorem 2.1
.

There exists a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, such that for any
\lVert\Gamma-\Gamma_{0}\rVert_{X}<\epsilon
, there exists a unique solution
f\in Y
to the equations (
2.9
) and (
2.10
). Moreover,

\lVert f-f_{0}\rVert_{Y}\leq C\lVert\Gamma-\Gamma_{0}\rVert_{X}.

We then translate this result back to the original self-similar variables, yielding the main theorem of the paper:

Theorem 2.2
.

There exists a sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
, such that for any
\mathring{\omega}\in L^{1}
with
\lVert\mathring{\omega}-\widehat{\mathring{\omega}}_{0}\rVert_{X}<\epsilon
, there exists a function
\Psi\in C^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
U=\nabla^{\perp}\Psi
and
\Omega=\Delta\Psi\in L^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfy the followings:

Let
\omega
,
u
, and
\psi

2.3.  The linearized operator

We compute the linearized operator
\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f}
at
(\Gamma_{0},f_{0})
by formally deriving the Gateaux derivative of
\mathcal{N}_{1}
,
\mathcal{N}_{2}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{3}
at
f_{0}=\frac{1}{2\mu-1}
. Recall that
h=(-D_{\beta}+2\mu-1)f
.

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{1}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{1}(f_{0}+\epsilon f)-\mathcal{N}_{1}(f_{0})}{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}D_{\rho}(2\mu f-h)+\Big(4-\frac{2}{\mu}\Big)f,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{2}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{2}(f_{0}+\epsilon f)-\mathcal{N}_{2}(f_{0})}{\epsilon}=\partial_{\phi}(2\mu f-h),

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{3}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{N}_{3}(f_{0}+\epsilon f)-\mathcal{N}_{3}(f_{0})}{\epsilon}=(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h-(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f

\displaystyle=-(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)(2\mu f-h)+2\mu(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f-\beta\partial_{\phi}f.

Summing up, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{(\Gamma_{0},f_{0})}\hskip-15.00002pt(f)

\displaystyle=\mu(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{1}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)+\mu\partial_{\phi}\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{2}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)-\Gamma_{0}\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{3}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)D_{\rho}(2\mu f-h)+(4\mu-2)(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f+\mu\partial^{2}_{\phi}(2\mu f-h)

\displaystyle\quad+\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)(2\mu f-h)-(4\mu-2)(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f+\Big(2-\frac{1}{\mu}\Big)\beta\partial_{\phi}f

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu}\bigg((D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)^{2}(2\mu f-h)+(\mu\partial_{\phi})^{2}(2\mu f-h)+(2\mu-1)\beta\partial_{\phi}f\bigg).

Let
g=2\mu f-h=(D_{\beta}+1)f
, then we have

\mathcal{L}(f)\triangleq\mu\frac{\delta\mathcal{F}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{(\Gamma_{0},f_{0})}\hskip-15.00002pt(f)=(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)^{2}g+(\mu\partial_{\phi})^{2}g+(2\mu-1)\beta\partial_{\phi}f.

(2.11)

The above computation can be interpreted as follows. For
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3},z_{4},z_{5})
, let

\displaystyle F_{1}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\Big(4z_{1}-\frac{z_{2}^{2}}{z_{1}}\Big)\frac{z_{5}}{z_{4}}-\frac{z_{2}z_{3}}{z_{1}},

\displaystyle F_{2}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{3}z_{4}-z_{2}z_{5}}{z_{1}},

\displaystyle F_{3}(\mathbf{z})

\displaystyle=z_{4}z_{5}^{-\frac{1}{2\mu}}.

We set

\mathbf{h}\triangleq(h,\partial_{\phi}h,\partial_{\phi}f,(D_{\rho}+2\mu)h,(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)f),

then
\mathcal{N}_{i}(f)=F_{i}(\mathbf{h})
for each
i=1,2,3
. Note that the functions
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
, and
F_{3}
are analytic in a neighborhood of
\mathbf{h}_{0}=(1,0,0,2\mu,1)
. Then,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{N}_{i}}{\delta f}\bigg|_{f_{0}}(f)=\nabla_{\mathbf{z}}F_{i}(\mathbf{h}_{0})\cdot\mathbf{h},\quad\text{for each}\penalty\ i=1,2,3.

(2.12)

Remark

4.  Invertibility of Linearized Operator

In this section, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
\mu>1
. For any
w\in Z
, there is a unique solution
f\in Y
to the linearized problem
\mathcal{L}(f)=w
. Moreover, we have the estimate

\lVert f\rVert_{Y}\lesssim\lVert w\rVert_{Z}.

The linearized equation is given by

\mathcal{L}f=(D_{\rho}+2\mu-1)^{2}g+(\mu\partial_{\phi})^{2}g+(2\mu-1)\beta\partial_{\phi}f=w,\quad g=(D_{\beta}+1)f.

(4.1)

In section
4.1
, we first establish the injectivity of the linearized operator. We then prove its invertibility using the Fredholm alternative. In section
4.3
, we decompose the operator into a sum of a main part and a relatively compact perturbation.

Remark
.

The Fredholm alternative argument was used in this problem by Shao-Wei-Zhang
[
41
]
, where they inverted the linearized operator for
\mu>\frac{1}{2}
, except
\pm 1
modes. The
\pm 1
modes were excluded mainly due to two issues: the injectivity of the operator and the invertibility of the main part. We circumvent those difficulties by considering different regimes of parameter
\mu
, function spaces, and decomposition of the linearized operator. Specifically, we consider the case
\mu>1
in which
\mathcal{L}
has a trivial kernel. Similar computations for the kernel were carried out in
[
41
]
for the case
\mu>\frac{1}{2}
and modes
|n|\neq 1
case. Additionally, the invertibility of the main part is a tautology from our function space setting. We note that
[
25
,
41
]
used variants of Hölder space while we use Wiener-type spaces for
Y
and
Z
.

4.1.  Uniqueness of the solution

We prove the uniqueness of the solution to the linearized problem (
4.1
) in a function space larger than

. Using the identity

\lVert f\rVert_{\sup}\leq\lVert g\rVert_{\sup}\leq\sum_{n}\lVert\widehat{g}_{n}\rVert_{\sup}\leq\lVert g\rVert_{V}\lesssim\lVert g\rVert_{\widetilde{Y}}=\lVert f\rVert_{Y}

Proof

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\big\{(-D_{\beta}+\textrm{i}n\beta+2\mu-1)^{2}-(|n|\mu)^{2}\big\}\widehat{g}_{n}+(2\mu-1)\textrm{i}n\beta\widehat{f}_{n}=0,\\

is a bounded operator. Let

be the right hand side of (

\lVert\mathcal{T}(f)\rVert_{Z}\lesssim\lVert\mathcal{N}_{3}(f_{0}+f)\Gamma\rVert_{Z}+\lVert\mathcal{N}(f)\rVert_{Z}\leq C(\lVert f\rVert_{Y}\lVert\Gamma\rVert_{X}+\lVert f\rVert_{Y}^{2}).

into itself. Next, again from Lemma

\displaystyle\lVert\mathcal{T}(f)-\mathcal{T}(\widetilde{f})\rVert_{Z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert(\mathcal{N}_{3}(f_{0}+f)-\mathcal{N}_{3}(f_{0}+\widetilde{f}))\Gamma\rVert_{Z}+\lVert\mathcal{N}(f)-\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{f})\rVert_{Z}

\displaystyle\lesssim\lVert\Gamma\rVert_{X}\lVert f-\widetilde{f}\rVert_{Y}+(\lVert f\rVert_{Y}+\lVert\widetilde{f}\rVert_{Y})\lVert f-\widetilde{f}\rVert_{Y}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\prime}(\epsilon+2\delta)\lVert f-\widetilde{f}\rVert_{Y}.

When we take

has exactly one fixed point

Lemma 5.3
.

Suppose
F:U(\subset\mathbb{C}^{k})\to\mathbb{C}
is an analytic function in a neighborhood of a point
\mathbf{f}_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
. And
A
is a unital Banach algebra over
\mathbb{C}
, consisting of functions
f:S\to\mathbb{C}
where
S
is a nonempty set. For
\mathbf{f}=(f_{1},\cdots,f_{k})
with
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}\triangleq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lVert f_{j}\rVert_{V}\ll 1
, we define a function
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}):S\to\mathbb{C}
by

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f})=F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{f})-F(\mathbf{f}_{0})-\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0})\cdot\mathbf{f}.

Then, there is a constant
\epsilon>0
such that

(a)

\lVert F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{f})-F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g})\rVert_{A}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}
, and

(b)

\lVert\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f})-\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{g})\rVert_{A}\lesssim(\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}+\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}})\lVert\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}
,

for all
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}},\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}<\epsilon
.

Since
F
is analytic at
\mathbf{f}_{0}
and
A
is a Banach algebra, there is a
\epsilon>0
such that the Taylor series expansion

F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{f})=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\partial^{\alpha}F(\mathbf{f}_{0})}{\alpha!}\mathbf{f}^{\alpha}

converges in
A
for
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}<\epsilon
. Indeed,
F(\mathbf{f})
,
\nabla F(\mathbf{f})
,
\nabla^{2}F(\mathbf{f})
,
\cdots
are bounded in
A
for
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}<\epsilon
.

F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{f})-F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g})=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{d}{dt}F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g}+t(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g})\cdot\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g}+t(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g})).

Recall that
\lVert\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g}+t(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}))\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}\leq C<\infty
for
\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}},\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}<\epsilon
and

. We get (a) by using the fact that

is an algebra. Similarly,

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{f})-\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g})=\int_{0}^{1}(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g})\cdot\big(\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{g}+t(\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}))-\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0})\big).

Since
\lVert\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0}+\mathbf{h})-\nabla F(\mathbf{f}_{0})\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}\lesssim\lVert\mathbf{h}\rVert_{\mathbf{A}}
by applying part (a) for
\nabla F
, we obtain the estimate (b).
∎

\Phi(t,y)=t^{3\mu-1}\phi(t,x)
for
t>0
and

. Then
\Phi(t,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2})

. From part (a), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u\cdot\partial_{t}\phi+(u\otimes u):\nabla_{x}\phi\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U\cdot\partial_{t}\Phi\,dy-t^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(3\mu-1)U\cdot\Phi+U\cdot(\mu y\cdot\nabla\Phi)-(U\otimes U):\nabla\Phi\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U\cdot\partial_{t}\Phi\,dy=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}U\cdot\Phi\,dy=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u\cdot\phi\,dx,

for each

. Since
u(t,\cdot)\to u_{0}

as
t\to 0+
, we get

The vorticity equation is similarly derived from Lemma
5.6
. Let
\phi(t,x)\in C^{\infty}_{c}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2})

for
t>0
and

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi)\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Omega\cdot\partial_{t}\Phi\,dy-t^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(2\mu-1)\Omega\Phi+\Omega(U-\mu y)\cdot\nabla\Phi\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Omega\partial_{t}\Phi\,dy=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Omega\Phi\,dy=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega\phi\,dx,

for each
t>0
. Since
\omega(t,\cdot)\to\omega_{0}

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega_{0}\phi(t=0)\,dx+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\omega(\partial_{t}\phi+u\cdot\nabla\phi)t\,dx\,dt=0.\qed
