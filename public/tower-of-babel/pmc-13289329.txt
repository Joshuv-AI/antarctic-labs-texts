Improvement of hepatic reserve and changes in liver and spleen volume after balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsImprovement in hepatic reserve after balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) in patients with gastric varices (GVs) has not been fully established. The relationship between increases in liver volume (LV) or splenic volume (SV) after BRTO and prognosis is still unclear. In this study, we aimed to evaluate the factors associated with improvement in hepatic reserve after BRTO in GV patients and the relationship between changes in LV or SV after BRTO and prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively enrolled 258 patients who recieved their first BRTO for GV treatment at 12 institutions between January 2004 and May 2019. Hepatic reserve, LV, and SV were evaluated before and 6 months after BRTO.ResultsChanges in hepatic reserve were evaluated in 160 patients. Albumin levels and prothrombin time-international normalized ratio improved significantly, while platelet counts decreased significantly at 6 months after BRTO. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that history of hepatocellular carcinoma and modified albumin-bilirubin (mALBI) grade were independent factors associated with the improvement of albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score. The ALBI score significantly improved in patients with mALBI grade 2b or 3 (p < 0.001), but not in patients with mALBI grade 1 or 2a. Eighty-three patients who underwent abdominal computed tomography examination 6 months after BRTO had significantly increased LV and SV (LV, p < 0.01; SV, p < 0.01). The patients with a > 10% increase in SV had significantly poorer prognosis than the others (p = 0.03).ConclusionsBRTO for GVs leads to improvement of hepatic reserve. Patients with increased SV after BRTO had poor prognosis.Trial registrationNot aplicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04893-4.Keywords: BRTO, Hepatic reserve, Liver volume, Spleen volume

Introduction

Balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration (BRTO) is widely performed for the treatment of gastric varices (GVs) [1–4]. Improvements in hepatic reserve after BRTO in patients with GV have been reported by several studies, all of which were single-center studies [4–8]. BRTO has also been applied to treat hepatic encephalopathy associated with portosystemic shunt [5, 8]. Previous studies reported that improvement in Child-Pugh score, the model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score, and MELD-sodium (MELD-Na) score after BRTO was observed only in patients with encephalopathy and not in patients with GV [7, 9]. For these reasons, it is still unclear whether significant improvement in hepatic reserve can be expected after BRTO in patients with GV. Furthermore, previous studies have also reported an increase in liver volume (LV) and splenic volume (SV) after BRTO [10, 11]. However, the relationship between changes in LV and SV after BRTO and prognosis has not been investigated. In this multicenter study, we aimed to evaluate whether the improvement in hepatic reserve after BRTO was observed in patients with GV and its associated factors. We also evaluated the relationship between changes in LV and SV after BRTO and prognosis.

Materials and methods

Patients

This study included patients from the cohort of our previous study [12]. These patients underwent their first BRTO for the treatment of GV between January 2004 and May 2019 at 12 institutions. The effective injection of sclerosant into GV and embolization of the GV confirmed by venography was considered as the technical success of BRTO. Exclusion criteria for the assessment of changes in hepatic reserve were: (1) failure of technical success of BRTO; (2) no laboratory data before or after BRTO; (3) death or lost to follow-up within 6 months after BRTO; and (4) patients with acute gastric variceal bleeding due to the temporary worsening of hepatic reserve before BRTO. For the analysis of changes in LV and SV after BRTO, patients without computed tomography (CT) examination before and after BRTO were also excluded. This retrospective multicenter study was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Committee of The University of Osaka Hospital and the institutional review boards of the 11 participating institutions in the Osaka Liver Forum. Clinical data were collected from electronic medical records and anonymized between October 22, 2019 and September 30, 2023. Since this is a retrospective study, the requirement for written informed consent was exempted. This study protocol was performed according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Balloon-occluded retrograde transvenous obliteration procedures

The BRTO procedure was performed according to a method previously reported [13]. A balloon catheter was inserted into the outflow shunt of the GV, and blood flow was blocked by inflating the balloon. To identify the GV and its inflow and outflow vessels, retrograde venography was carried out. After the GV was adequately confirmed by venography, 5% ethanolamine oleate mixed with iopamidol was slowly injected until the GV and its inflow vessels were filled. The technical success of BRTO was defined as the absence of GV delineation on venography after injection of the sclerosing agent.

Clinical examination

As several previous reports had examined improvement in hepatic reserve at 6 months after BRTO, the results of blood tests performed just before and 6 months (within 3 to 9 months) after BRTO were assessed [5, 6]. The albumin-bilirubin (ALBI) score was calculated according to the following formula: ALBI score = (log10 bilirubin [µmol/L] × 0.66) + (albumin [g/L] × −0.085). The ALBI grade was defined as follows: ≤ −2.60, grade 1; > −2.60 to ≤ −1.39, grade 2; and > −1.39, grade 3. The model for end-stage liver disease (MELD) score was calculated according to the following formula: MELD score = (9.57 × loge creatinine [mg/dl]) + (3.78 × loge bilirubin [mg/dl]) + (11.20 × logeprothrombin time-international normalized ratio (PT-INR)) + 6.43. [14] MELD-sodium (MELD-Na) score was calculated according to the following formula: MELD – Na (mmol/L) - (0.025 × MELD × (140 – Na (mmol/L))) + 140. [15] The fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4 index) was calculated according to the following formula: FIB-4 index = (age (years) × aspartate transaminase (AST) (U/l))/((platelet count (109/l)) × (alanine aminotransferase (ALT) (U/l))1/2). In patients who underwent plain or enhanced CT examination before BRTO and 6 months after BRTO, LV, SV, portal vein diameter and splenic vein diameter were measured from 5 mm slice CT images using segmentation software (Slice-O-Matic, Tomovision, Montreal, Canada). Imaging data were centralized at The University of Osaka, and a single gastroenterologist performed LV and SV measurements on all imaging data.

Statistical analysis

As the primary endpoints, the change in hepatic reserve and organ size after BRTO was assessed. To compare the hepatic reserve, LV, and SV before and after BRTO, Wilcoxon signed rank test was used. To evaluate factors associated with improvement of ALBI score after BRTO, logistic regression analysis was used. As the secondary endpoint, overall survival (OS) after BRTO was assesed. The probability of survival after BRTO was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. To assess the factors associated with OS after BRTO, Cox regression analyses was used. To compare patient characteristics, Mann–Whitney test and chi-square test were used. Statistical analyses were performed with Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 23 for Windows (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). For each results, the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients

A total of 258 patients who underwent the initial BRTO for GV were enrolled (Fig. 1). Twenty-three patients in which technical success was not achieved, 13 patients with missing laboratory information before or after BRTO, 3 patients who died within 6 months after BRTO, 15 patients who were lost to follow up within 6 months after BRTO, and 44 patients who underwent BRTO for acute gastric variceal bleeding were excluded. Finally, 160 patients who underwent prophylactic BRTO for GV were included in the analysis for this study. The characteristics of these 160 patients prior to BRTO are summarized in Table 1. Ninety patients (56.3%) were men. Forty-one (25.6%), 30 (18.8%), 84 (52.5%), and 5 (3.1%) patients were classified in mALBI grade 1, 2a, 2b, and 3, respectively. Forty-seven patients (29.4%) had a history of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and 13 patients (8.1%) had HCC at the time of BRTO. There were no patients with viral liver cirrhosis who initiated antiviral therapy during the period from the start of BRTO to 6 months post-treatment.

Changes of hepatic reserve

Compared to the baseline, serum albumin levels and PT-INR were significantly improved at 6 months after BRTO (albumin, 3.5 g/dL vs. 3.8 g/dL, p < 0.001; PT-INR, 1.12 vs. 1.10, p = 0.018) (Fig. 2A and B). Total bilirubin levels were not significantly different between before and after BRTO (1.0 mg/dL vs. 1.0 mg/dL, p = 0.844) (Fig. 2C). In contrast, platelet counts were significantly decreased at 6 months after BRTO (10.0 × 109/L vs. 9.6 × 109/L, p = 0.021) (Fig. 2D). The ALBI score was significantly improved after BRTO (−2.18 vs. −2.39, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2E). The number of patients with mALBI grade 2b decreased from 84 to 54, and that with mALBI grade 1 or 2a increased from 71 to 97 (Fig. 2G). The univariate logistic regression analysis showed that gender, history of HCC, serum albumin level, ALBI score, and mALBI grade were significantly associated with the ALBI score improvement after BRTO (Table 2). In the multivariate analysis, history of HCC (OR 2.55; 95%CI 1.12–5.80; p = 0.03) and mALBI grade 2b or 3 (OR 2.46; 95%CI 1.25–4.87; p = 0.01) were independent factors associated with the improvement of ALBI score after BRTO. When categorized by mALBI grade, ALBI score improved only in patients with mALBI grade 2b or 3 (−1.81 vs. −2.09, p < 0.001, n = 89), and not in patients with mALBI grade 1 or 2a (−2.65 vs. −2.68, p = 0.932, n = 71) (Fig. 2F). Among the patients with low baseline mALBI grades (1 or 2a), the number of patients with mALBI grade 1 was increased from 41 to 46 (Fig. 2H). Likewise, among those with high baseline mALBI grades (2b or 3), the number of patients with mALBI grade 1 and 2a after BRTO increased from 0 to 9, and to 21, respectively. Changes in mALBI grade including those of patients who died or were lost to follow-up within 6 months after BRTO were shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Association of prognosis and changes in liver and spleen volume

Of the 160 patients, 83 patients underwent abdominal CT examination 6 months after BRTO (Fig. 1). Their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 3. The median LV and SV were 925.5 cm3 (range, 578.6–2447.8) and 296.5 cm3 (range, 74.0–1362.0), respectively. The median portal vein diameter and splenic vein diameter were 11.6 mm (range, 5.1–17.5) and 8.0 mm (range, 3.8–18.8), respectively. There was a significant correlation between LV and portal vein diameter, and between SV and splenic vein diameter (Supplementary Fig. 2A, 2B). The median period of observation after BRTO was 2.6 years (range, 0.5–11.8). Of the 83 patients, 11 patients died due to liver failure, 7 patients died due to HCC, and 3 patients died due to non liver disease. In the univariate Cox regression analysis, gender, etiology, history of HCC, Child-Pugh class, MELD-Na score, albumin level, ALBI score, mALBI grade, and FIB-4 index were associated with OS after BRTO (Table 4). In the multivariate analysis, etiology, history of HCC, and mALBI grade were significant prognostic factors after BRTO. Patients with a history of HCC had significantly higher total bilirubin level, lower platelet counts, higher AST, and higher ALT at the baseline. LV and SV at baseline were not associated with OS after BRTO. LV was significantly increased at 6 months after BRTO (925.5 cm3 vs. 993.6 cm3, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3A), and the median rate of LV increase was 3.0% (range, −34.7–34.9). The portal vein diameter was significantly increased at 6 months after BRTO (11.6 mm vs. 12.4 mm, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 2 C). SV was also significantly increased at 6 months after BRTO (296.5 cm3 vs. 311.8 cm3, p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B), and the median rate of SV increase was 6.0% (range, −23.7–96.6). The splenic vein diameter was significantly increased at 6 months after BRTO (8.0 mm vs. 8.8 mm, p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 2D). There was a weak correlation between the change rate of LV and that of SV in all patients (correlation coefficient, 0.31; p < 0.01), and in patients with Child-Pugh class A (correlation coefficient, 0.34; p < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 3A, 3B). On the other hand, there was no significant correlation between the change rate of LV and that of SV in patients with Child-Pugh class B or C. There was no significant correlation between change rate in LV or SV and changes in ALBI score (Supplementary Fig. 4). There was no significant correlation between change in portal vein diameter or splenic vein diameter and changes in organ volumes or ALBI score (data not shown). LV was increased by more than 10% from baseline in 23 patients after BRTO. The prognoses did not differ between the patients with a > 10% increase in LV after BRTO and the others (p = 0.32) (Fig. 3C). There was no significant difference in the baseline characteristics between the two groups (Supplementary Table 1). SV was increased by more than 10% from baseline in 32 patients. Unlike increases in LV, the patients with a > 10% increase in SV had significantly poorer prognosis than the others (p = 0.03) (Fig. 3D). These patients were more likely to be men and have history of HCC and had higher AST level (Supplementary Table 2). Of the 32 patients, 6 patients died due to HCC, 5 patients died due to liver failure, and 1 patient died due to non liver disease. Even when the cut off value of SV increase was set at 5%, patients with increased SV had a poorer prognosis than the other patients (data not shown).

Discussion

The present study showed a significant improvement in serum albumin level and PT-INR at 6 months after BRTO, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [4–7]. Occlusion of portosystemic shunt by BRTO increases hepatic blood flow [7, 9, 16]. The increased hepatic blood flow might induce the sinusoidal endothelium to release various cytokines, such as IL-6, hepatocyte growth factor, and nitric oxide, which may induce hepatocyte proliferation and improvement of hepatic reserve [17], suggesting that BRTO is not only effective in stopping or preventing GV bleeding, but also has the ancillary effect of improving hepatic reserve.

Multivariate analysis revealed that history of HCC and mALBI grade were independent factors associated with improvement in ALBI score after BRTO. In addition, improvement of serum albumin level and total bilirubin level was observed only in patients with impaired liver function of mALBI grade 2b or 3 before BRTO. In this study, patients with a history of HCC had significantly higher total bilirubin level, lower platelet counts, higher AST, and higher ALT at the baseline. Uehara et al. reported that improvement in hepatic reserve after BRTO was observed only in patients with Child-Pugh class B or C and not in the patients with Child-Pugh class A, [6] which may suggest that patients with preserved hepatic reserve have no room for furthur improvement and only patients with substantially impaired hepatic reserve and with advanced liver disease have the potential to improve liver function.

Furthermore, LV was significantly increased after BRTO, consistent with the results of a study by Kobayashi et al. [10] The increase in LV after BRTO is thought to be the result of increased blood flow to the liver due to redistribution of portosystemic shunt blood flow [17]. On the contrary, our study showed an increase in SV and a decrease in platelet counts after BRTO. SV was reported to increase because of advancing liver fibrosis and portal hypertension [18, 19]. Saad et al. reported no change in platelet counts after BRTO, although there was a trend for platelet count reduction among their only 35study patients [20]. Kako et al. reported a significant decrease in platelet count after BRTO, which is consistent with our results. [21] It should be noted that increased blood flow to the portal system after BRTO may not only improve liver function, but may also worsen splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia.

Previous studies have reported that LV is a significant prognostic factor in patients with liver cirrhosis [22, 23]. Larger SV was also associated with poor prognosis in liver disease [24, 25]. However, no study has evaluated the prognostic impact of changes in LV or SV after BRTO. In the present study, Cox regression analysis showed that LV and SV at baseline were not associated with OS after BRTO. On the contrary, patients with a > 10% increase in SV after BRTO had a poorer prognosis than the other patients, whereas no significant difference in prognosis between patients with a > 10% increase in LV and the others was observed. In the patients with a > 10% increase in SV, the causes of death were almost HCC or liver failure. Although it is possible that the higher proportion of patients with HCC history in those cases was related to this result, the further studies are required to examine the association between SV increase and liver-related deaths. We previously reported that patients with early worsening of esophageal varices after BRTO had a poor prognosis [12]. These results suggest that patients who show features of worsening portal hypertension after BRTO require special caution in their prognosis. We should pay attention not only to the splenomegaly in cirrhotic patients before BRTO, but also to the changes in SV that occur in the patients after BRTO.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study. To confirm the results of this study, furthur validation studies are required. Second, there was a survival bias in this study because only patients who were still alive after six months after BRTO were included, and those who had died or lost by then could not be analyzed. Third, the treatment status of patient’s background liver disease varied widely and could not be analyzed in this study. Antiviral therapy before BRTO and after 6 months of BRTO was also not analyzed. And finally, we did not evaluate the relationship between portal vein hemodynamic changes and improvement of hepatic reserve or changes in organ size after BRTO.

Conclusion

Hepatic reserve can be improved after BRTO for GV in the patients with impaired liver function. Patients with increased SV after BRTO had a poor prognosis.
