Sensory network segregation as a predictor of post spaceflight balance impairments and sensory re-weighting
Authors: GD Tays, TD Fettrow, HR McGregor, KE Hupfeld, YE De Dios, PA Reuter-Lorenz, JJ Bloomberg, AP Mulavara, SJ Wood, RD Seidler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExposure to microgravity results in transient sensorimotor performance declines when crewmembers return to Earth, likely due to sensory re-weighting. This poses performance risks following gravitational transitions, such as arriving on the Moon or Mars. Here, we examined whether sensory brain network segregation (how independently a given network functions) in astronauts prior to an International Space Station mission would predict balance post-flight. Intraclass correlation analysis showed high test-retest reliability for all sensory network segregation measures. Indices of the Parietal Operculum 2 network (OP2) segregation pre-flight significantly predicted balance performance. Specifically, greater segregation predicted poorer balance on day one following return to Earth (Left OP2), better balance four days post-flight (Left and Right OP2) and greater balance improvements from one to four days post-flight. Results suggest that OP2 segregation may index plasticity of sensory weighting processes; that is, the degree of vestibular input down-weighting measured initially post-flight and extent of recovery over subsequent four days.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Human behaviour, Predictive markers

Introduction

Behaviors reliant on vestibular inputs such as balance control, mobility and eye-head coordination are impaired for up to approximately two weeks after astronauts return from spaceflight1–4. These sensorimotor performance changes due to microgravity are thought to arise from re-weighting of vestibular inputs inflight5–8. The otoliths in the vestibular system are not able to signal head tilt in the absence of gravity. This results in down-weighting of vestibular inputs inflight and increased reliance on other sensory systems, notably vision3,8–12. This adaptation may be beneficial for performance in the microgravity environment, but when astronauts return to Earth—or, in the future, land on a novel planetary surface—this adaptation becomes maladaptive, resulting in transient sensorimotor declines4,8,13–18. Thus, poorer postural performance during the early postflight period may reflect greater adaptability to the microgravity environment, whereas improved postural performance later postflight would reflect re-adaptability back to normal gravity.

There are large individual differences between astronauts in the magnitude of their post-flight sensorimotor impairments and their rates of re-adaptation to Earth’s gravity2,4,17,19. When future astronauts land on the Moon or ultimately, Mars, they will face immediate performance demands that require sensorimotor skills, such as habitat set-up or walking around the planetary surface. Rapid adaptation to this environment will be necessary to ensure astronaut safety and well-being. Thus, being able to predict which astronauts will have the greatest sensorimotor performance deficits following a gravitational transition will allow for individualized countermeasures and personalized training20.

Prior research has examined predictors of sensorimotor adaptation in young and older adults and patient populations20–22. For instance, studies have shown that better spatial working memory performance was associated with more rapid adaptation23,24, and greater proprioceptive variability predicted better perception of hand location across trials25. Koppelmans et al.19 found that greater cortical thickness in vestibular and sensorimotor brain regions pre-flight was associated with better balance performance following a mission to the International Space Station (ISS).

Brain network segregation is another metric that may be predictive of post-flight performance, in particular for sensory networks given the multisensory re-weighting that occurs inflight. Network segregation describes how independently a given brain network functions26,27. Studies have shown that greater segregation of a network comprising higher-level associative cortices is correlated with better cognitive performance28,29. Network segregation has also been shown to decline with age, and is associated with magnitude of cognitive declines30. Moreover, in individuals with Alzheimer’s disease, higher network segregation is associated with better cognitive function31. Network segregation has been suggested as a biomarker of neuroplasticity32, as greater network segregation is reported to predict greater cognitive gains in response to exercise and/or cognitive training interventions, and it is argued that a more highly segregated network is more “optimal” to respond33–35. Taken together, these studies support the hypothesis that network segregation could be predictive of post-flight sensorimotor performance. The ability to predict which crewmembers will have greater difficulty initially adapting to the novel gravitational environments on the Moon or Mars would allow for targeted training and informed mission task assignments in the first weeks of arrival.

In the present study, we collected resting state functional magnetic resonance images (rsfMRI) on astronauts to assess functional segregation of the OP2 (which receives vestibular projections), visual and default mode networks (DMN). Our first aim was to determine the pre-flight test-retest reliability of these networks, which is essential for their use in predicting future outcomes. The second aim was to examine whether visual and/or OP2 network segregation could predict balance performance post-flight. We included the DMN to serve as a control network; that is, we did not expect DMN segregation to predict post-flight sensorimotor performance.

Results

Our primary goals were to determine whether sensory network segregation was reliable over a roughly 18-month time period (including an intervening mission to the ISS), and if sensory network segregation measured 60 days pre-flight would predict balance performance 1 and 4 days following return (R, Fig. 1; R + 1, R + 4). Astronauts underwent resting state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rsfMRI) scans 180 and 60 days prior to launch (L-180, L-60), and 4, 30, 90 and 180 days following return (R + 4, +30, +90, +180 respectively) to Earth. At 1 and 4 days post-flight (R + 1, +4) astronauts performed a balance task in which they stood upon a sway-referenced platform with their eyes closed and arms crossed on their chest (Sensory Organization Test-5 (SOT-5)) and another balance task with the addition of dynamic head tilts (pitch) at 0.33 Hz (SOT-5M).

Test-retest reliability

We have previously characterized behavioral performance changes in this sample from pre- to post-flight4. We found that SOT-5 and SOT-5M performance significantly declined from pre-flight to R + 14. Here, we investigated whether individual differences in this post-flight performance can be predicted by pre-flight OP2 or visual network segregation. We first determined the test-retest reliability of the segregation scores. For this, we calculated the intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC3,k) across the 6 rsfMRI time points and planned a priori to exclude any sensory networks with an ICC less than 0.5 (other than the DMN which serves as our control network36,37). Due to the potential effects of age, sex and flight experience, we used residual network segregation scores to calculate ICC values instead of raw segregation scores. Of the four networks we investigated, the left and right OP2 and visual networks showed sufficient test-retest reliability over the 6 rsfMRI time points, whereas the DMN was not reliable (Table 1). Based on the cut-offs described by Koo and Li38, the visual network had excellent reliability, the left OP2 had good reliability and the right OP2 had moderate reliability.

Predictors

We then assessed whether pre-flight network segregation at L-60 predicted R + 1 balance performance on the SOT-5 and SOT-5M. We included age, sex and previous flight experience as covariates in these predictive models. We found that higher pre-flight right OP2 network segregation significantly predicted poorer SOT-5M performance on R + 1, and that lower pre-flight right OP2 network segregation predicted better SOT-5M performance (Table 2, Fig. 2). Sex and flight experience also had a significant impact on R + 1 balance performance. Female astronauts had overall worse balance performance (β = 42.089, t(10) = 3.806, p = 0.004 unc.), as did crewmembers with prior flight experience (β = -14.742 , t(10) = -2.250, p = 0.050 unc). Neither left OP2, visual nor DMN segregation significantly predicted R + 1 balance performance on the SOT-5M, and none of the networks predicted R + 1 SOT-5 performance (Table 2). To follow up on the previous flight experience effect in the model, we found that astronauts who were experienced flyers had significantly lower right OP2 network segregation at the pre-flight time point than novice flyers (β = −0.061, t(10) = −3.124, p = 0.001 unc.), regardless of age and sex.

Discussion

We investigated whether sensory network segregation could serve as a predictor of post-flight balance performance. Identifying predictors of post-flight sensorimotor abilities will permit more accurate forecasting of astronauts’ performance efficiency following gravitational transitions (e.g., returning to Earth or upon arrival to a novel planetary surface). An effective predictor would need to have high test-retest reliability across time so that it would not have to be measured immediately before every flight. We examined network segregation for four networks: left and right OP2, visual, and DMN. We found that the visual network has excellent reliability, the left OP2 has good reliability and the right OP2 has moderate reliability across the six time points we examined (two pre-flight and four post-flight). The DMN (which served as a control network) did not have significant test-retest reliability. While this is not what was expected, it may be due to the nature of DMN. The DMN is typically active during rumination and is anti-task correlated. It is certainly possible that the focus of rumination is different postflight, which is known to impact DMN activity39. The transformational experience of spending months in space may make the connectivity patterns of this network different, thereby reducing reliability. Critically, we found that greater right OP2 network segregation predicted worse post-flight balance performance on the SOT-5M task at the R + 1 time point. Further, greater left and right OP2 network segregation scores predicted better balance performance at the R + 4 time point on the SOT-5M task. Finally, the right OP2 network segregation score also predicted the change from R + 1 to R + 4 on the SOT-5M task; those with greater segregation improved the most. Overall, these findings suggest that network segregation can serve as a predictor of astronauts’ early post-flight performance, which is indicative of the extent of inflight adaptation, as well as later post-flight performance, which reflects re-adaptation back to normal gravity.

Due to wide variation in the rate of adaptation to microgravity and re-adaptation upon return to Earth20, identifying predictors for post-flight balance is critical, as future missions to the moon or Mars will require astronauts to perform essential tasks immediately following landing. In one study, we determined that individuals with greater cortical thickness in OP2 and sensorimotor regions had better balance performance post-flight and less pre-to-post-flight changes19. Here, we identified that OP2 network segregation predicted post-flight balance, where higher segregation pre-flight predicted worse performance at R + 1. It has been argued that a network with higher segregation may be in a more “optimal” state to respond positively to training interventions32,35. Thus, astronauts that have a more highly segregated OP2 network adapt more fully to the microgravity environment, and subsequently have worse performance (aftereffects) immediately post-flight. It has also been suggested that those with greater network segregation are more efficiently able to reconfigure their brain to handle task demands35,40. If this is the case, it may be that astronauts with higher preflight network segregation are also better able to handle sensorimotor tasks in the microgravity environment. The OP2 brain region has been posited to serve as a human vestibular cortex, as this region receives vestibular inputs in addition to visual and somatosensory inputs, and is affected by vestibular disorders (e.g. vestibular neuritis41). Thus, it is not surprising that a network connected with this region could play a key role in microgravity adaptation and re-adaptation to Earth’s gravity. This could be investigated in future studies.

We identified that greater right OP2 segregation predicted worse balance at R + 1 on the SOT-5M, and at R + 4, the greater the left and right OP2 network predicted better balance performance on the SOT-5M. To investigate this further, we examined the change in balance performance from R + 1 to R + 4 and its association with right OP2 network segregation. We found that those with the highest right OP2 segregation showed the greatest performance increase from R + 1 to R + 4, consistent with the interpretation that between those two time points astronauts with the highest network segregation reweighted their sensory inputs the most. This suggests that the plasticity of the weighting process may play a key role in astronauts’ adaptation to microgravity, as well as readaptation upon returning to Earth. To further validate the ability of the predictors, we also assessed OP2 network segregation at L-180 (as a post hoc analysis), and found that right OP2 network segregation at this time was also a significant predictor β = −137.691, t(10) = −2.291, p = 0.05 unc.). Greater OP2 network segregation was associated with worse balance performance. There was not a significant association for the left OP2 network segregation.

Moreover, our results indicate that the segregation of the left and right OP2 networks predicts the flexibility of the down-weighting and re-weighting process. This would align with previous investigations that have found that individuals with greater network segregation exhibited greater cognitive gains during cognitive training interventions33–35. A more highly segregated network may be more optimally organized and allow for more rapid adaptive responses, as we observed here over the first several days post-flight. Those that have a more optimally organized OP2 network showed faster re-adaptation to Earth’s gravity. Other investigations have identified evidence of sensory re-weighting post-flight, such as Lowrey et al7. who reported that foot skin receptor sensitivity was decreased immediately post-fight and returned to baseline levels by R + 4, particularly in fast-adapting skin receptors. They further identified that vibration perception was increased at R + 0, and subsequently returned by baseline by R + 242. The present results predict balance performance within that same period post-flight, supporting the argument that pre-flight network segregation is related to responsivity to gravitational transitions. Possibly, individuals with the poorest balance performance also had the greatest room for improvement; however the finding that segregation also predicts R + 4 performance argues that these effects are not solely driven by dynamic range effects. These findings may also indicate that brain network segregation could be a useful outcome metric or predictor of the needs for astronaut training and physical rehabilitation programs. We are not proposing that astronaut selection should be done based on these measures, but rather that they could provide key insights into which astronauts may need additional sensorimotor training or balance support following gravitational transitions. Further, these measures could be tested for their sensitivity to artificial gravity and other potential countermeasures. Overall, our findings indicate that right OP2 network segregation is a significant predictor of sensory re-weighting following gravitational transitions and can be related to both immediate post-flight balance performance, and balance recovery across the first few days post-flight.

Higher network segregation has been linked to greater intervention responsiveness in previous studies32,40,43. Here, higher OP2 network segregation was linked to poorer immediate post-flight balance at R + 1, which is thought to indicate greater in-flight adaptation (resulting in greater aftereffects upon return). Interestingly, we also found that astronauts with previous flight experience had significantly lower right OP2 segregation than those with no previous flight experience. This could suggest that exposure to the microgravity environment, and its concomitant sensory re-weighting, has a persistent effect on the organization of functional brain networks. Evidence has also identified that risk of inflight space motion sickness decreases in those with previous flight experience, supporting some carry-over effects from previous exposure to microgravity44. Brain structural changes have been widely identified to persist post-flight45. Functional brain connectivity changes have also been identified following exposure to microgravity. For instance, one team identified that there was reduced connectivity at post-flight compared to pre-flight in a single cosmonaut between the ventral posterior cingulate cortex and the right insula, which is a part of the OP2 network46. Investigating long-term follow up of resting state functional connectivity also showed that for some regions connectivity returned to pre-flight status by 8 months post-flight, whereas others did not. In particular, the connectivity of the bilateral insular cortex to the rest of the brain decreased post-flight, but returned to pre-flight levels following return to Earth39,45. Whereas, the posterior cingulate cortex and thalamus exhibited decreased connectivity with the rest of the brain even at the 8 month follow and the angular gyrus exhibited increased connectivity with the whole-brain until 8 months post-flight46,47. This research, and our own, suggest that there are long term functional brain changes following exposure to microgravity.

Future studies could address the primary limitation of this study, namely, the limited number of astronaut participants. Re-adaptation to Earth’s gravity will begin as soon as they enter its gravitational field, thus it is possible that by R + 1 those with the most highly segregated networks have already begun to re-adapt. Resting state fMRI scans are relatively straightforward to collect, requiring only ~10 min of data collection. Including this assessment in astronauts’ standard measurement protocol would allow for a more robust data set while also providing information on brain health and potentially persistent functional network changes. Another potential limitation is the low test-retest reliability that we observed for the DMN. While we intended this network to serve as a control (i.e., we hypothesized DMN network segregation would not be related to post-flight performance), the low reliability may have precluded us from observing predictive effects of this network (rather than a lack of functional contributions)45. Further, our analysis did not allow for networks to share nodes between themselves, but many brain networks do overlap in brain regions. It is possible that this portion of the analysis may explain the decreased test-retest reliability of the DMN. Finally, given the lack of significant results that survived Benjamini–Hochberg corrections, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Given the difficulty in collecting data with an astronaut population, and the limited sample size, which is frequent in this population, these results should be viewed as an exploratory analysis and further investigation with a larger sample should be conducted.

In summary, we found that visual and OP2 network segregation scores have moderate to excellent test-retest reliability across 6 time points. Our findings illustrate that right OP2 network segregation predicted R + 1 balance performance on the SOT-5M. Greater segregation predicted worse balance performance, and experienced flyers had significantly lower right OP2 segregation scores. Moreover, experienced flyers and women had poorer balance at R + 1, suggesting that they adapted more inflight, resulting in larger aftereffects. These subgroup effects should be interpreted with caution given the small sample here. Right and left pre-flight OP2 network segregation also predicted R + 4 balance performance on the SOT-5M; in this case, higher segregation predicted better balance. Further, right OP2 network segregation was associated with the change from R + 1 to R + 4 on the SOT-5M; those with higher segregation adapted the most between those time points. Thus, OP2 network segregation provides a biomarker of both post-flight balance and post-flight re-adaptation to gravity, therefore, has promise for predicting which individuals may adapt the fastest when they arrive at the Moon or Mars.

Methods

Participants

We collected data from 15 astronauts with a mean age at launch of 47.5 years (mean ±6.3 years standard deviation); 26% were female, and one astronaut was left handed. ISS mission durations were 188.1 days (±57.5) on average. Six of the astronauts in the study had previous flight experience, spending on average 75.0 days (±131.4) previously in space, with a mean of 2.0 (0.89) previous missions. rsfMRI data were collected across six time points, two pre-flight and four post-flight, as depicted in Fig. 1. The University of Florida and NASA’s Institutional Review Boards approved this study to be within ethical compliance and all astronauts provided informed consent. The data were collected as part of a larger NASA-funded project (NASA # NNX11AR02G) with the overarching aim to investigate the extent, longevity and neural bases of spaceflight-induced neurocognitive changes48. The hypotheses and analyses here were developed as post hoc exploratory questions secondary to the original grant aims.

Balance assessment

Balance performance was assessed using Computerized Dynamic Posturography49 (CDP; Equitest, NeuroCom International, Clackamas, OR). Equilibrium quotients (EQ) were calculated for each astronaut on Sensory Organization Tests 5 and 5 M (SOT-5 and SOT-5M). The human posture control equilibrium quotient (EQ) is calculated by comparing the projection of the center of mass (COM) to the limit of stability (LOS). It is a dimensionless index of postural stability, with a higher EQ indicating greater stability. The SOT-5 requires participants to maintain upright balance with their eyes closed while standing on a sway-referenced platform, while the SOT-5M asks participants to pitch their head up and down, while their eyes are closed, at approximately ±20° at 0.33 Hz paced by an auditory tone49,50. This assesses function of the vestibular system (SOT-5) and with vestibular system challenge (SOT-5M). EQ scores were derived from peak-to-peak excursion of the center of mass (estimated at 55% of total height) over 20-s trials51,52. As in our previous work, we used the median EQ across three trials from each time point in all statistical analyses4,53,54.

rsfMRI acquisition

rsfMRI data were acquired on a 3 Tesla Siemens Biograph MRI scanner located at the University of Texas Victory Lakes facility. A gradient echo T2*-weighted echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters was used to acquire these fMRI data: TR: 3660 ms, TE: 39 ms, flip angle: 90°, FOV: 250 × 250 mm, matrix: 94 × 94, slice thickness: 4.0 mm, voxel size: 2.66 × 2.66 × 5.0 mm, 36 axial slices. We also acquired a T1-weighted gradient-echo pulse sequence with the following parameters: TR: 1.9 s, TE: 2.32 ms, flip angle: 9°, FOV: 250 × 250 mm, matrix: 512 × 512, slice thickness: 0.9 mm, voxel size: 0.488 × 0.488 × 0.9 mm, 176 sagittal slices.

MRI pre-processing

Pre-processing and statistical analyses of the fMRI data were conducted using Statistical Parametric Mapping 1255,56 (SPM12; version 7219), CONN toolbox version 19c57, MATLAB R2019, Advanced Normalization Tools58 (ANTs) and FMRIB Software Library59 (FSL). Functional images were corrected for slice timing, realigned and unwarped, then resliced within CONN. We used the same ANTs normalization pipeline that we employed extensively in our previous spaceflight and spaceflight analog neuroimaging work8,60–63. Briefly, we created a T1 template for each individual using ANTs’ AntsMultivariateTemplateConstruction.sh function. We co-registered each participant’s functional scan to their T1 structural template, and to get resultant transform parameters for the AntsRegistration.sh function. T1 images were normalized to the MNI152 template via AntsRegistration.sh, and the resultant warp parameters were applied to the rsfMRI data to transform them into MNI152 space with AntsTransform.sh. In CONN, rsfMRI runs were spatially smoothed using a 5 mm full-width at half-maximum Gaussian kernel to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. To further assess the quality of the images, we used the Artifact Detection Tool (ART; https://www.nitrc.org/projects/artifact_detect/) in CONN and covaried out volumes with a resultant framewise displacement motion threshold set at or greater than 0.9 mm and a global mean intensity within 5 SD of the mean run intensity.

We performed specialized pre-processing steps for the cerebellum, as we have done in our previous work3,8,60,62,63, due to low cerebellar resolution in standard Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) templates. We used the CEREbellum Segmentation (CERES)64 tool to isolate the cerebellum and then normalized each one to the Spatially Unbiased Infratentorial Template (SUIT)60–63. To do this, we created a binary mask from CERES native space images, then masked out the cerebellum from the cortex using FSL. We then used ANTs to transform the T1 cerebellum to SUIT space, and used the transformation to move the slice time corrected, realigned and resliced cerebellar images to SUIT space. We then applied a 2 mm full-width half-maximum three-dimensional smoothing Gaussian kernel to the cerebellar images in SPM12. This kernel size is compatible with other studies65–67.

We further processed the functional data using standard steps for rsfMRI data in the CONN toolbox57. The data were filtered using a temporal band-pass filter of 0.008–0.09 Hz in order to examine the frequency band of interest and to exclude high frequency sources such as heart rate and respiration. To reduce noise, we used the aCompCor (anatomical component-based noise correction) method68. This leverages anatomical MRI data to define a region composed primarily of white matter and cerebral spinal fluid to model the influence of noise in a voxel specific manner. It uses linear combinations of multiple empirically estimated noise sources from noise regions of interest and includes them as a nuisance parameter region of interest (ROI) in the first level general linear model66.

First-level functional connectivity analysis

Within-network connectivity was determined via first level ROI-to-ROI rsfMRI analyses using the CONN toolbox57. We defined our network nodes using an independent dataset of 110 participants from the Washington University 120 dataset. These data were obtained from the OpenfMRI database, accession number ds000243. We created 4 mm diameter spherical ROIs in FSL using the MNI coordinates published in Power et al.69, as seed coordinates. Left and right OP2 cortex seeds were extracted from zu Eulenburg et al.70. We performed a seed-to-voxel analyses from the Washington University 120 dataset (obtained from the OpenfMRI database, access number: ds00243). First, seed-to-voxel analyses was conducted with the MNI normalized whole brain images as the target without the cerebellum, then a second was performed on SUIT normalized images. The resulting T map with a-values greater than 12.0, and with local maxima separated by 8 mm were identified. We then created a 4 mm radius sphere around these regions for the network segregation analyses. This resulted in a total of 19 networks with 608 nodes total, where no nodes were allowed to overlap with each other and are separated by 8 mm.

For each astronaut in the present study, at each individual time point, the resting state time series was extracted from the network ROIs and nodes in the unsmoothed functional images to avoid “spillage” of BOLD signal from nearby regions, and the mean time course was calculated across the voxels within the ROI and nodes. The average connectivity across nodes within each network was calculated, in addition to each node’s connectivity across networks. Correlation coefficients were converted into z-values using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation71. Negative weighted edges were set to zero in each correlation matrix to avoid misinterpretation28,72,52. The final connectivity matrix for each participant at each time point was a 608 × 608 matrix of z values. For the purposes of this investigation, we only computed network segregation scores for the networks originating from the seed voxels in the visual, left and right OP2 and DMN, for a total of 128 ROIs (Fig. 5). Segregation was calculated for the other networks from the Power69 ROIs, but these values were not used in analyses as predictors of post-flight balance performance.

Within network connectivity is calculated as the mean z-value from each node within a network to each other. Between network connectivity is calculated as mean z-value of each node to all the other nodes. Network segregation is calculated as the difference of mean within network and the mean between network connectivity divided by mean within network connectivity (Eq. (1)).

Segregation analysis

Following Chan et al.28, and our previous work52, network segregation was calculated by subtracting the mean between-network connectivity from the mean within-network connectivity and dividing by the mean within connectivity (Eq. (1)). The within values are the Fisher-z transformed correlation between each ROI within a respective network, whereas the between is the Fisher-z transformed correlation between an ROI in one network, to every ROI in other networks28,52.

Statistical analyses

First, to assess the test-retest reliability of segregation scores over time, we calculated intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC3,k values38) as we have done in our previous work for structural images collected as part of this same study36. We calculated ICCs that tested across all six data collection time points (as prior work suggests that network segregation is a stable metric that changes only slowly across many years of aging28).

We estimated linear mixed effect models within the nlme package73 in R 3.6.174. with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation to determine whether pre-flight network segregation values predicted post-flight balance performance. Based on our hypotheses, we assessed left and right OP2, primary visual, and DMN segregation. Raw EQ score was included as the primary outcomes, figures include the residual values of this score from the statistical models. We entered age, sex and previous flight experience as covariates into the models. Partial correlations were used to identify the strength of the relationship between segregation scores and balance performance controlling for the effects of age, sex and previous flight experience.
