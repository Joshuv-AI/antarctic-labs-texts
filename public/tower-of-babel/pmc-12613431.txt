Barriers and enablers of elderly care specialists towards deprescribing practices in the nursing homes: a focus group study
Authors: Alireza Malek Makan, Man Chun Lee, Hein van Hein, Rob van Marum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPolypharmacy increases as patients age. It is associated with a decline in quality of life and an increased risk of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs). These are highly prevalent among long-term care residents in European countries. The potential benefits of deprescribing, as the process of withdrawing PIM’s are widespread, including improvements in both health and quality of life for patients. An overview of the barriers and enablers of deprescribing is still lacking in national research or policy discussions involving a larger group of Elderly Care specialist (ECS), who aim to maintain or improve the quality of life of elderly and chronically ill patients in the Netherlands. In this study, we explored the barriers and enablers of deprescribing among ECSs.MethodsA focus group study was conducted with two groups comprising a total of 11 ECSs. A theme list was prepared to guide the interviews. The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by one and verified independently by another researcher. Analysis of the reports was carried out through thematic coding using Atlas.TI, performed independently by two researchers. After each step of the coding they were compared, verified and, if necessary, adjusted after consultation.ResultsECSs mentioned that gaining clinical experience and knowledge contributed to a positive attitude towards deprescribing. It is crucial to involve patients and their families and have support from care managers through official policy. Obstacles for deprescribing comprised lack of a medical history, the fear of the negative consequences of reducing medication, the lack of clinical guidelines regarding deprescribing, and the believe that a particular medication can be effective. The factor related to patient and family has a direct positive or negative impact on deprescribing if there is or is not cooperation.ConclusionDeprescribing in nursing home residents remains a challenge. The ECSs are both positive about and aware of its importance. At the same time the collaboration between all parties, including doctor and patient/family and other disciplines such as management and pharmacists and paramedics, was mentioned as a key working method in this regard.

Introduction

Polypharmacy has more than doubled between 1999 and 2014 [1]. Polypharmacy is common among the elderly population because of the development of multimorbidity. Although polypharmacy may be indicated and leading to health improvement, it may also result in negative effects. Polypharmacy has been defined as the use of five or more medications, which is not necessarily inappropriate, “problematic polypharmacy” refers to the use of multiple medications that may not be clinically indicated, have unclear benefit, or pose more risks than benefits. Polypharmacy is associated with a decline of quality of life, serious side effects, increased mortality and high costs [2, 3]. The Benefit-Risk-Balance (BRB) of medications decreases especially for ‘’the very old, with comorbidity, dementia, frailty and limited life-expectancy’’ (VOCODFLEX) [4], a description that fits the nursing home residents [4]. Polypharmacy increases the risk of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) [5]. Deprescribing, the process of withdrawing inappropriate medication, supervised by a healthcare professional [6] may therefore improve patient outcomes and can also lead to cost benefits to the health care system [7–11].

Several studies have identified barriers to deprescribing reported by healthcare providers, such as a lack of evidence that discontinuation of medication would be more beneficial than continuation, uncertainty and fear of negative consequences (such as potential conflict), reluctance of patients and family members, and lack of time or support for deprescribing [12–16]. These studies have examined enablers and barriers to deprescribing in general, while such factors may differ for different drug classes, and for type of patients. Furthermore, most studies focused on clinicians’ perspectives.

The majority of nursing home residents are in the last phase of life. In this phase, the intention of treatment should be more focused on symptom therapy than on prevention [17–20]. Nevertheless, the prevalence of polypharmacy (>5 medications) in the nursing home remains high: 30.3% to 94% [21–23]. More than 18% of the residents in Dutch nursing homes used 4 or more inappropriate medications according to the STOPP-Frail criteria [version2]. In some European countries this percentage is even higher [24]. So it can be noted that deprescribing practice in nursing homes is not optimal. In the Netherlands, medical care for nursing home residents is provided by Elderly Care Specialists (ECS), a medical professional trained to deliver medical care to frail elderly.

Deprescribing attitudes, practices and approaches of geriatricians, general practitioners and community pharmacists across Europe have already been investigated [25]. However, no qualitative research has yet been conducted into such factors among the ECSs, so it is not known whether comparable barriers and enablers factors in comparison with other studies involving other care providers are present regarding deprescribing. In this study, we explored the barriers and enablers of deprescribing among the ECSs.

Methods

Study design, recruitment and setting

A focus group study was executed with two groups of in total 11 ECSs. A theme list was used to guide the interviews.

Focus groups were chosen to stimulate discussion and reveal shared and divergent perspectives among Elderly Care Specialist (ECS) regarding deprescribing. This interactive dynamic, as introduced in [Kitzinger J, 1995 (PMC2550365)] was considered particularly appropriate to explore shared and divergent professional experiences, organizational culture, and peer influences.

A purposive sampling technique was used to recruit participants. The first group consisted of ECSs who also supervise ECSs in training and who work in the nursing homes and were recruited from various regions in the Netherlands. The second focus group worked in the same organization and were colleagues. The moderator had no prior relationship with participants.

The participants were drawn from diverse regions of the Netherlands and various educational institutions, representing a broad spectrum of ages, levels of experience, and patient populations. Consequently, these groups can be considered highly representative of ECSs in the Netherlands. ECSs are a distinct medical specialty in the Netherlands. Unlike general practitioners or geriatricians, ECS are specialists trained specifically in care for long term care residents and geriatric rehabilitation. This typically takes three years of postgraduate training. In contrast to ECS, other medical specialists such as cardiologists or neurologists typically work in hospitals and are not responsible for daily care in nursing homes. They were approached by telephone to participate as part of a training day. The second focus group consisted of ECSs were active in the nursing homes and not associated with resident training. The sessions took 1.5 to 2 h each. The focus groups were led by a moderator.

Elderly Care specialist is a medicine doctor who tries to maintain or improve the quality of life of the elderly and chronically ill. These doctors mainly work in nursing homes in collaboration with paramedics, psychologists and nurses. The main goals are focused on the quality of life based on the wishes of the residents and their families and the informal care givers, where life expectancy plays a major role. The other medical specialists work in the hospital and not in the nursing homes.

The Medicines Act in the Netherlands regulates matters surrounding the production, trade, prescription and dispensing of medicines. The law contains regulations to encourage the safe use of medicines. Nurses are also included in this law. In addition `The Care and Coercion Act` regulates the rights in the event of involuntary care or involuntary admission of people with an intellectual disability and people with a psychogeriatric condition (such as dementia).

Due to the vulnerability of the nursing patients, one of the family members regularly acts as legal representative. For incapacitated residents, medical decisions must always be discussed with the legal representative.

Topic list

To create a topic list on existing literature on barriers to and enablers of deprescribing the Theoretical Domains Framework TDF was used [26, 27]. We used a combination of deductive and inductive approach. Deductive by taking note of previous literature and based on this a topic list, and inductive by having an open conversation and interpreting the data openly. It began with a set of empirical observations, seeking patterns in those observations, and then theorizing about those patterns. We did not conduct a formal pilot. The topic guide was developed based on existing literature and pretested internally by the research team (including an ECS and an ECS resident and also other authors) for clarity and relevance. Using a topic list, four discussion points were debated around the barriers and enablers of stopping medication (deprescribing):

Which factors determine prescribing among nursing home residents?What are your experiences with prescribing for nursing home residents?What is your experience with stopping medication (deprescribing) in nursing home residents?What do you need to promote deprescribing?

Data collection and data analysis

The focus groups took place on October 9 and 17, 2019. Two focus group discussions of 90–120 min duration. The spoken language was Dutch and the sessions were audio recorded with permission. The group discussions were moderated by HvH, assisted by AMM and MM, who made notes in addition to the audio recording. The moderator is experienced with moderating focus group interviews. He had no direct relationship with the participants. No one other than researchers and participants present during the focus group.

After a short introduction, each participant was given the opportunity to respond to the topic list points. Afterwards the basic data of the participants were collected (age, gender, years of experience and main patient group). The audio recordings were verbatim transcribed by one researcher (MM) and verified by the other one (AMM). The transcript have not been returned to the participants for comments.

The group discussions were moderated HvH, assisted by AMM and MM.

The participants were mostly experienced healthcare professionals with a medical background in elderly care. Participants came from various regions in the Netherlands, providing a broad range of perspectives. Those from first group were ECS for many years who also included the trainer for the ECS residents. They had completed training in multiple modules. Participants from the second group had many years’ experience in various disciplines in elderly care, such as psychogeriatrics and rehabilitation and also supervision for GPs. Unfortunately, ethnicity data were not collected, and this limitation is now noted in the Strengths and Limitations section.

For practical reasons, the first group with trainers took place on a “ECS trainer exchange day” which is organized on a regular basis by the medical school for postgraduates for supervision and education of the trainers.

The second focus group worked in the same organization and were colleagues. The moderator had no prior relationship with participants.

We did not include the participants’ IDs during data collection. The contributions of the group members were reasonably balanced (there were no silent or overly dominant participants). This was largely due to the experienced moderator, who maintained a balance in the number and duration of the responses.

The moderator guided the groups in such a way that all participants were invited to contribute. This was done by mixing group discussions with asking all individual members to reflect on a topic. This resulted in a reasonable balance in participation across the group.

Analysis of the reports was performed by open coding, axial coding and selective coding. First, labels (codes) were attached to the text fragments. These codes indicate the main themes of the fragments. Then the codes were compared with each other and similar codes were grouped under a more generic code. Finally, the codes were grouped into main themes and sub-themes. The coding was performed by three researchers (MM, AMM, RvM) using Atlas.TI. After each step of the coding of the main themes and sub-themes, they were compared, verified and, if necessary, adjusted after consultation.

The COREQ guidelines were followed and completed and were added as an attachment to the submission.

Ethical approval

The participants agreed with the audio recording in order to analysis and publication, the name of the participant and the work location were not included. Also no patient data were included. The study protocol was conducted in compliance with Dutch regulations governing qualitative research with healthcare professionals. According to the Dutch Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act (Wet medisch-wetenschappelijk onderzoek met mensen, WMO), ethical approval by a certified medical ethics committee is required only for studies that involve medical-scientific research in which participants are subjected to interventions or required to follow specific behavioural rules. As this study involved focus group interviews with healthcare professionals only, without patients or interventions, it did not fall under the scope of the WMO and therefore did not require ethical approval.

See: https://wetten.overheid.nl/BWBR0009408 and https://english.ccmo.nl/investigators/legal-framework-for-medical-scientific-research/your-research-is-it-subject-to-the-wmo-or-not.

Theme 1: Physician-related factors

Attitude

In general, there was a positive attitude towards deprescribing. This was mainly driven by the fact that ECP treat frail elderly with a high risk of adverse drug reactions and limited life expectancy.

FG1: “because I want to get rid of as much as possible (medication) because I see all the side effects, interactions and misery.”

FG1 “ .but there are often medications among them that I think ’are they still necessary?’. So that is uh particularly there and that limited life expectancy is something that is always very difficult to estimate actually. So uh at the moment when it is very clear that you are really going to treat symptomatically or palliatively uh yes then it is always like ha, can we stop things”.

Clinical experience

Having experience as a physician and using the knowledge and expertise as a skill is frequently mentioned as a enabling factor. The training for ECS plays a supporting role in this.

FG1 “.evidence-based is important, but I believe clinical judgment (‘doctor-based’) also matters, especially because evidence often doesn’t apply to our specific patient group.”

FG1 “new doctors often do not (deprescribing independently), because they think the family decides. Or they are still very stuck in that part of evidence-based medicine, so that part of doctor expertise is not included.”

Emotions

The fear and anxiety of being sued for deviation of clinical guidelines is a serious barrier to doctors stopping a medication, even if they are convinced they should:

FG1 “…I’m just going to say the word fear…that in worse situations you will get a complaint if you stop the medication…”.

FG1 “…I was charged with two years sessions, the inspection with all kinds of investigations. It really had an influence on me (regarding deprescribing)…”.

Theme 2: Medication-related factors

Clinical guidelines

The availability of deprescribing rules in guidelines can be supportive when stopping medication, but if the guideline is not specific enough or has not been developed specifically for the elderly, it sometimes becomes difficult to work outside the guideline.

FG1 “That it is simply stated in the guidelines.”

FG2 “So whenever I deviate from guidelines, I have to go an extra step to explain…”.

FG2 “…but today’s young doctors grew up with guidelines…”.

FG1 “It is not in the guideline if you have no staff or if the family is against it, no, then you are not allowed to give pills, that is not what the guideline says. “.

FG2 “what do you do if a guideline does not mention anything specific about the elderly?”

FG1 “Sometimes you also follow the specialist guideline….if you are indeed unable to come up with your own…”.

FG2 “Well, that’s the aim anyway, with the Care and Coercion Act (a law in the Netherlands to evaluate psychotropic medications dynamically), psychotropic drugs must always be evaluated…”.

Scientific evidence

Scientific evidence for the elderly and frail patient population is limited. There is a lack of evidence about the long-term effect of, for example, preventive medication in older and frail patients. Due to a lack of evidence, determining the benefit-risk balance (BRB) in older and frail patients is experienced as difficult, for example, knowing the Number Needed to Treat (NNT) and Number Needed to Harm (NNH) to substantiate the discontinuation of medication.

FG2 “…they have to be based on something and there is no research…”.

FG2 “…because evidence-based is not necessarily applicable to the current, our target group…”.

FG2 “It often helps if you can explain with figures what percentage risk reduction you are actually working on and what the risk of bleeding is in return. So my experience is that if you can substantiate it with scientific figures, it will help you a lot (it went about anti-coagulation medication)…”.

Expected effect of deprescribing

Uncertainty concerning the effects of deprescribing may function as a barrier. Potential adverse effects of deprescribing were mentioned as barrier. Stopping medication may cause relapse of symptoms or increase the risk of unfavorable events like stroke or cardiac problems. Anticoagulation drugs were medication classes that were frequently mentioned as risk medication.

FG1 “…yes, but that is very annoying because it is not, it is not comfortable to have a stroke in your last part (of life).”

FG2 “.but if someone is indeed chronically depressed and he has already had four (relapses), then I would absolutely not rather not touch it.

Positive experiences with deprescribing were mentioned as facilitator. Situations where a patient improved clinically after deprescribing were mentioned as increasing self-confidence with deprescribing.

FG1 “someone on multiple medications for depression and it turned out there was no indication for it, after stopping the medication the patient improved and then it turned out to be mild hypothyroidism…”.

Adverse drug reactions

Another very relevant theme for ECP’s were presence of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) or the risk of ADRs due to drug-drug and drug disease interactions. Older persons often respond differently to medication due to the changes in their body over time and they also use more medication due to comorbidities and therefore the risk of interactions is higher with more consequences.

FG2 “If there are no side effects or if someone has no visible complaints from the statin, then I think I’ll leave that as it is…”.

FG1 ”.so if, for example, the chance of side effects is much more in the foreground than possibly the benefits then it may be the case that I already decide, with well-reasoned reasoning, not to prescribe something”.

FG1 “…it may sometimes be necessary for certain drugs to be reduced in dosage or to be exchanged for another drug or to be discontinued due to interactions…”.

Adverse drug reactions like constipation, drowsiness and increased risk of falls are frequently mentioned as reason for deprescribing.

FG1 “…fall risk is an important factor for us to assess, whether it is related to medication use, drowsiness, general pain, or other complaints Constipation is a side effect that is often forgotten if you do not pay attention to it. That’s why I actually say that we always prescribe rationally.”

Theme 3: System-level and organizational support

Electronic prescription system (EPS)

The EPS was mentioned as a possible support for deprescribing. The integration of START and STOPP criteria in the EPS was mentioned as an option to promote deprescribing. In this way, doctors can compare theory and clinic to determine whether there is still an indication for a particular medication.

FG2 “but when I hear that you have a supra-regional formulary in which everything is integrated […] and those STOPP and START criteria that you have built into it […] I think that’s nice too.”

FG2 “… because the (medication) indication changes over time…”.

Availability of medical history

Complete and correct exchange of medical information between health care professionals is important for carrying out deprescribing. While ECS request information at the time of admission, the delay is due to procedural and system-level issues with external data sharing (e.g., hospital discharge summaries or GP medication lists). This exchange of information is often experienced as inadequate. This concerned both the (in)completeness of the information and the timeliness of the exchange. For example, medical information following hospital discharge or from the GP/ family doctor often arrives many days after nursing home admission. In addition, the large volume of medical history from the GP’s was also mentioned, while an up-to-date overview would be particularly helpful. Also medical information for instance concerning indications for medication, is often incomplete.

FG2 “We do not always receive the complete GP file …. It sometimes does not arrive until one or two weeks following admission.”

FG1 “…and then I say that we are going to take a critical look at what you need, but then after four or six weeks when I have the first meeting with [uh] with the resident and family, I do not always have all the information…”.

FG1 “…yes, you are faced with the vulnerable patient who makes you think “I need to know a lot more about this before you can even make decisions”.

Cooperation/ teamwork

Collaboration plays a major role in deprescribing among nursing home residents. For example, doctors depend on the observations and reporting of healthcare staff for deprescribing. The competence of healthcare staff in this regard was not always experienced as sufficient, which meant that, for example, symptoms or side effects could be missed. As a result, deprescribing or evaluation of deprescribing cannot be properly carried out.

FG2 “…because then I go to the team manager and say the nurses have to report more…”.

Optimal staffing is an increasing problem in healthcare that is referred to as an enabler.

FG2 “Guidelines are nice if you have employees who can also facilitate them, that you have no barrier, we’re short on staff…”.

Especially when deprescribing psychotropic drugs, the psychologist was seen as an essential collaboration partner.

FG1 “What I especially do when it comes to behavior is to say that the team needs to talk to each other about this problem in order to be on the same page and still involve the psychologist.”

Role of external health care professionals

The role of medical specialists is experienced both positively and negatively. If there is a consensus on the medication policy, it can be very supportive.

Sometimes, patients or their families place more trust in hospital specialists who originally prescribed the medication, which can make deprescribing discussions challenging and increase the risk of disagreement or conflict. Statements made by medical specialists about the lifelong use of medication are also frequently mentioned as barriers.

FG1 “…when you have the discussion about statins, that of course keeps coming up again and again and all cardiologists feel they have to say with great enthusiasm that they (elderly) have to use this for their patients for the rest of their lives.”

FG 1“Well it’s your responsibility or no this patient really needs that medication.”

Using the status of medical specialist and collaborate with them can also facilitate deprescribing.

FG2 “. then I consult a neurologist. Ask for a second opinion if the contacts with the client (patient) or representative are such that I have done my best … and that does not provide a solution and then I would say yes, let me ask an expert who specializes in that field and people see are also taken seriously.”

Pharmacists are mostly perceived as facilitators for deprescribing. Medication reviews performed together with a pharmacist is experienced as positive.

FG1 “What does help is that you otherwise check with the pharmacist per department to see what, for example, psychotropic drugs are prescribed.”

Support from the organization and management

Care staff shortage is a well-known problem in elderly care. There is not only a shortage of healthcare staff, but also of specialists in elderly care and other disciplines such as psychologists. This results in high work pressure and a lack of time.

FG1 “.I just took over an entire department that was all a care home and that is now a nursing home, so you have a lot of work to do.”

These stand in the way of regular consultations, such as pharmacotherapeutic consultations (FTO) and medication reviews, which is actually experienced as a positive factor for deprescribing. The consultations are used by some physicians to discuss the prevalence of medication prescriptions and prescribing behavior. In addition, there is also a high turnover of general practitioners, which hinders continuity in medical care. This continuity is experienced as an important factor in being able to carry out deprescribing.

FG1 “There are many changes in both houses (nursing home wards). So if you can manage a little, you as colleagues will not reverse all kinds of policies of others. Or you will immediately prescribe again at the first…”.

The turnover of doctors was experienced as positive, as the fresh perspective of new doctors was appreciated. At organizational level, the vision of the organization and department was mentioned as a factor that can contribute to deprescribing.

Moreover the internal agreements in an organization regarding medication policy can help doctors to work unambiguously, which was described as an enabler.

FG1 “…also the policy in the organization regarding when do we stop taking pills, such as limited life expectancy of two years, no cholesterol inhibitor etc. gives extra support…”.

Unfortunately, there are cases that lead to a complaint and legal action. the doctors want the mental and legal support of their organization.

FG2 “…that is an organization that supports you and the another doesn’t…”.

Theme 4: Patient-related factors

Patient preferences

The patients belief in medication and the status of medication can both be experienced as a barrier and facilitator.

FG2 “…include what is desired by the client himself. Also, uh, with experience from the past, for example, with certain medications or, uh, for example, they have had side effects.”

FG2 “Then if they really find it valuable, I try to keep as much of it the same and at another time I try to explain in a quiet moment why it is better to use something or not.”

FG1 “.can decide for himself “I want this medicine or not”. … I have too many side effects from this so. I don’t use it.”

Life expectancy

Life expectancy is an item that is not always clear. In the case of a clearly limited life expectancy, it is more flexible for the doctor to adjust medication. For example, it is then more acceptable to stop taking statins. Although determining life expectancy is not always easy and is considered a barrier.

FG1 “So that limited life expectancy is something that is always very difficult to estimate. So the moment it is very clear that you are really going to treat symptomatically or palliatively, then it is always like “ we can stop things”. And then it is always very clear, so then you stop taking cholesterol lowering and ascal and things like that…”.

FG2 “…of course also, for example with statins, if people are admitted and they have a cardiovascular event, but they have a limited life expectancy, then I will of course consider not prescribing that.”

FG1 “…with limited life expectancy, at least consider your goal is to combat complaints or prevent complaints.”

For most ECP’s the decision to deprescribe is not determined by the type of problem causing nursing home admittance (psychogeriatric or somatic problems). In both type of residents, life expectancy is the crucial factor.

FG2 “…you know, I think it would be looked at in the same way with a psychogeriatric patient as with a somatic patient. Absolutely what is the life expectancy, because if someone has profound dementia you have a life expectancy of less than three years.”

FG2 “…the question is whether a patient is a psychogeriatric patient or a somatic resident has a major influence on medication prescription behavior…in principle not, because the medication you prescribe actually has more to do with somatic complaints, quality of life and life expectancy. With psychogeriatrics it is slightly different because you can work more with psychotropic drugs. So there is a change in prescribing behavior.”

Role of relative and informal caregivers

Nursing home residents usually have family involved in their care and therefore also in medication use. Depending on the attitude of the family, deprescribing can be experienced as more or less difficult. Building a relation based on trust is important.

FG1 “…you actually have to have a relationship of trust first, …. but there are also many …. who want to hold on to everything they had and do not have the confidence that you will act properly.”

FG2 “…we’re going to discuss all medical decisions one-on-one with the family. And it seems nice, because you can’t do it with that patient and the law says that he is the representative, so discuss EVERYTHING with the family.” “Because…., but that son who thinks he knows better what is good for his father than I do…”.

FG2 “…but family sometimes finds it very difficult to actually make that decision for that person, especially if the person (medication) has always had to be used by the doctor for life. I think that as a doctor who is now the practitioner, you must clearly indicate what you think is in the interest of that person.”

FG1 “…but then the digital world doesn’t always help. Because family can of course wander around and see that you should or should not prescribe a certain medicine for heart failure… then you will have a nice discussion…”.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the barriers and enablers to deprescribing experienced by elderly care specialists (ECS) who work in nursing homes. In general, ECS are supportive of deprescribing, especially for frail elderly patients, due to concerns about adverse drug reactions and lack of efficacy in patients with limited life expectancy. Studies with other healthcare professionals, such as GPs, pharmacists, and geriatricians, reveal similar points of view. Some GPs hesitate to initiate deprescribing conversations, concerned that patients may feel neglected [28–31]. Others struggle with discussing limited life expectancy or identifying adverse drug effects due to polypharmacy. In contrast, ECS do not mention concerns that patients might feel abandoned or feel uncomfortable discussing life expectancy.

Decisions to deprescribe are often based on clinical experience. Recently trained physicians tend to adhere more strictly to clinical guidelines, despite the fact that these guidelines are often too general and do not align well with elderly care and there is limited scientific evidence to support deprescribing in frail patients Fear of legal repercussions when deviating from these guidelines was a major barrier, even when deprescribing is regarded as appropriate. This lack of evidence and guidelines in nursing home residents is a significant barrier to deprescribing among the doctors working in the nursing homes [28–30].

In accordance with other studies, ECS were hesitant to deprescribe high-risk medications such as anticoagulants and psychotropics due to possible adverse effects [28]. However, their confidence grows following positive outcomes from deprescribing efforts and the presence of guidelines like STOPP/START criteria. Effective deprescribing requires collaboration with other healthcare professionals. Deprescribing was experienced as difficult if another medical specialist is involved in the medication prescription [31]. Medical specialists may prescribe based on their professional guidelines which are not always appropriate for the nursing home setting.

Family involvement is an important element in deprescribing in nursing home residents. Many nursing home residents have cognitive problems [32], which affect their decision-making capacity. Family often steps in as a proxy for the residents’ medical decision making. In some cases, deprescribing may be complicated by the emotions and attitudes of family members, especially when family members disagree or show more faith in hospital specialists. Although family attitudes and emotions were not mentioned as barriers in earlier studies, cognitive limitations in patients were reported as deprescribing challenges for professionals in elderly care [28, 30]. Our findings highlight the importance of involving family perspectives in deprescribing decisions.

ECS faced challenges due to time constraints, collaboration with co-workers, staff continuity, insufficient access to clinical information and limited support from electronic patient systems. These issues were reported earlier [31, 33]. Pharmacists provide valuable support, but barriers may exist in collaboration with nurses. The competence of the nursing staff in this regard was not always experienced as sufficient, which meant that, for example, symptoms or side effects could be missed. As a result, deprescribing or evaluation of deprescribing cannot be properly carried out. Nurses can play an essential role in helping families accept deprescribing decisions, though there is disagreement on whether nurses should be involved in medication review meetings [31, 33]. In short, compared to other studies, this study is the first qualitative exploration of deprescribing barriers/enablers from ECS perspectives in the Netherlands. The ECSs are a unique specialist group of physicians responsible for the medical care of long term care residents, who are well situated to optimize medication prescriptions. Two new themes emerged as particularly relevant, that were not described earlier in the literature: of (I) fear for legal claims (e.g., threat of disciplinary complaints) and (II) the difficulty to deal with hospital specialists as prescribers, who were perceived to be higher in the medical hierarchy. We now highlight these as novel contributions.

This study emphasizes the importance of support from organizational-management as well as from direct colleagues (e.g. collaboration with psychologists, continuity issues).

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study is that the participants were all ECSs with substantial experience with (de)prescribing in very frail older persons in nursing homes.

Peer pressure may have been present. This may mean that socially desirable answers were given, which may result in missed insights and experiences. Nevertheless, several participants shared difficult experiences including official complaints. This indicated a collegial atmosphere of trust. Unfortunately, ethnicity data were not collected, which is a limitation of this study.

Differences between the two groups might be an indication of potential differences in approaches to deprescribing, which were not identified in the results.

In the first focus group, all ECSs were trainers in various areas in the Netherlands. The geographical distribution may have influenced the findings. This also may have caused an overrepresentation of physicians who practice the latest state of the art because they work more closely with the medical residents. However, this can be a strength at the same time: It is possible that they have acquired more knowledge and/or skills, which makes barriers such as fear (reluctance to stop/change the prescribed medication) less present. Finally, no new themes emerged after the second focus group. We acknowledge the possibility that data saturation may not have been fully achieved due to the limited sample size and the lack of heterogeneity among the participants. We acknowledge the limitation of only two groups and now clearly state that saturation was considered but cannot be guaranteed.

Conclusions

Deprescribing among nursing home residents remains challenging. It is a complex and delicate process, hindered by limited evidence-based guidance and requiring the active involvement of patients, family members, and institutional support. Experienced ECSs demonstrated a positive attitude toward deprescribing and recognized its value. This attitude, identified as a newly acknowledged influencing factor, underscores the need to address training for general practitioners. To effectively promote deprescribing as a means of delivering optimal and appropriate care, it is essential to support ECS through improved digital infrastructure—such as user-friendly electronic prescribing and registration systems—and through structural, interdisciplinary collaboration. This includes coordinated efforts with patients, families, management, pharmacists, paramedics, and hospital specialists, all guided by tailored guidelines that address the specific needs of older populations.
