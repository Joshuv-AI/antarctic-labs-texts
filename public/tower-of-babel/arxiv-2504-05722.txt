Well-posedness and L 1 − L p L^{1}-L^{p} Smoothing Effect of the Porous Media Equation under Poincaré Inequality
Lukang Sun

Abstract
We study the Cauchy problem for a weighted porous medium equation on ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} associated with a Gibbs probability measure π = e − V \pi=e^{-V} . Under a Poincaré inequality for π \pi and the convexity assumption on V V , we prove well-posedness and uniqueness of non-negative weak solutions with initial data in L 1 ​ ( ℝ d , π ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi) . We also establish an L 1 L^{1} – L p L^{p} smoothing effect at every positive time. More precisely, for every admissible p > 1 p>1 , we show that the logarithm of the ratio between the L p ​ ( ℝ d , π ) L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi) norm of the solution and its conserved L 1 ​ ( ℝ d , π ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi) mass first decays at a super-exponential rate and then decays exponentially to zero. In particular, even if the initial datum belongs only to L 1 ​ ( ℝ d , π ) L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi) , the solution belongs to L p ​ ( ℝ d , π ) L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi) for every finite p > 1 p>1 and every t > 0 t>0 .

Introduction

We consider the following weighted porous medium equation on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\partial_{t}\mu=\mathcal{L}\mu^{1+\beta},\qquad\beta>0,

(1)

where

\mathcal{L}:=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\rangle}}\right).

Here
V
is the potential associated with the Gibbs probability measure

\pi=e^{-V}
, and throughout the paper we assume, for simplicity, that

V
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. When
V
is constant, equation (
1
) reduces to
the classical porous medium equation.

It is useful to rewrite the equation in terms of the probability density

\rho:=\mu\pi.

A direct computation shows that
\rho
satisfies the continuity equation

\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\rho v_{t}^{\beta}}}\right),

(2)

where

v_{t}^{\beta}:=\frac{1}{\beta}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}}}\right)^{\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}}}\right)^{\beta}\nabla\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\pi}}}\right),\qquad\beta>0.

In the limiting case
\beta=0
, equation (
2
) becomes the
Fokker–Planck equation associated with Langevin dynamics, which is the

L^{2}
-Wasserstein gradient flow of the Kullback–Leibler divergence, or
Boltzmann relative entropy. More generally, equation (
2
) also admits a
gradient-flow interpretation and can be viewed as the gradient flow of an

\alpha
-divergence; see, for instance,
[
20
, Chapter 24]
.

The goal of this paper is to study the well-posedness and uniqueness of the
Cauchy problem for (
1
) with non-negative initial data

\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, and to establish an
L^{1}
–
L^{p}
smoothing effect
for positive times. The main result is the following.

Theorem 1

Assume that
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is convex, namely

\nabla^{2}V\geq 0,

and that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré inequality
(
5
) with constant
\lambda>0
. Let
\beta>0
and let

\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi),\qquad\varsigma\geq 0.

Then the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=\mathcal{L}\mu^{1+\beta}=\frac{1}{1+\beta}L\mu^{1+\beta},\qquad L:=\Delta-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\rangle,

(3)

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma(x),

admits a unique non-negative weak solution in the sense of Definition

2
. Moreover, the solution satisfies

\mu\in\Gamma,

where

\displaystyle\Gamma:=\{\mu:

\displaystyle\mu\in C([0,\infty),L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))\cap L^{\infty}(\tau,\infty;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall p\in(0,\infty),\ \forall\tau>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,\infty;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall\tau>0,

\displaystyle\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\|\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},\quad\forall t\geq 0,

\displaystyle\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,\infty;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\mathcal{L}\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,\infty;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall\tau>0\}.

If, in addition,
\varsigma
is locally strictly positive, in the sense that
for every compact set
Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists
c_{Z}>0
such that

\varsigma(x)\geq c_{Z}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in Z,

then

\mu\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,\infty)),

and the equation is satisfied classically for every positive time.

Let

M:=\|\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

If
M=0
, then
\mu\equiv 0
. If
M>0
, then for every

p>1
satisfying

p+\beta\geq 2,

the quantity

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t)):=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{M}}}\right)

is finite for every
t>0
and satisfies

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\geq 0.

Furthermore, with

t_{0}:=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\geq 0:\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\leq\frac{p-1}{p}}}\right\},

we have

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\leq\begin{cases}-\dfrac{1}{\beta}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}+\dfrac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}M^{\beta}t}}\right),&0<t<t_{0},\\[8.61108pt]
e^{-\frac{2p\lambda M^{\beta}(t-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t_{0})),&t\geq t_{0}.\end{cases}

Here, if
\varsigma\notin L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, we use the convention

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)=+\infty,\qquad e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}=0.

Consequently, for every
t>0
,

\mu(t)\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi),\qquad\forall p\in(1,\infty).

Notably, in the above theorem, we do not require the initial data
\varsigma\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and thus
\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)
could be
+\infty
. If so, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\leq\begin{cases}-\frac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|^{\beta}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}t}}\right)}{\beta}&\text{ if }t\in(0,t_{0}),\\
e^{-\frac{2p\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|^{\beta}_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}(t-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu(t_{0})}}\right)&\text{ if }t\geq t_{0},\end{cases}

(4)

and thus if
t>0
, we always have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}<\infty
. We would like to note that in the first case, where
t\in(0,t_{0})
, the rate of decrease is super-exponential, whereas in the second case, when
t\geq t_{0}
, the rate of decrease is merely exponential. As
t\to\infty
, we observe that
\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\to 0
; equivalently
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}
will monotonically decrease to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}
. By applying Hölder’s inequality, we conclude that the only possible limit is
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu(t)\equiv\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}
.

We say that
\pi=e^{-V}
satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant

\lambda>0
if, for every
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u^{2}\,d\pi-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}u\,d\pi}}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla u|^{2}\,d\pi.

(5)

The above Poincaré inequality plays an important role in the proof of the well-posedness, uniqueness and the
L^{1}-L^{p}
smoothing effect of the equation. It is well-known that, if
\pi
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda
, then, by a simple linearization procedure,
\pi
fulfills also the Poincaré inequality with constant
\lambda
. For example, on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, if
V
is strongly
\lambda-
convex, that is
\nabla^{2}V\geq\lambda\mathrm{I}_{d}
, then
\pi
satisfies the log-Sobolev inequality with constant
\lambda
. It is well-established that according to the Holley-Stroock perturbation lemma, if
V=\bar{V}+\psi
, here
\bar{V}
is strongly
\lambda-
convex and
\psi\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\pi
will satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with some constant
\lambda^{\prime}>0
. Consequently, it also satisfies a Poincaré inequality. The Poincaré inequality is weaker than the log-Sobolev inequality, for example on
\mathbb{R}
,
\pi(x)=e^{-\|x\|^{\beta}}/Z_{\beta}
satisfies a Poincaré inequality if and only if
\beta\geq 1
, however a log-Sobolev inequality if and only if
\beta\geq 2
, see
[
20
, Examples 21.19]
.

We next specify the notion of weak solution used throughout the paper. In the
main theorem, we use the normalization

L:=\Delta-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\rangle,\qquad\Psi(r):=r^{1+\beta},

so that the equation is written as

\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{1+\beta}L\Psi(\mu).

Definition 2
(Weak solution)

Let
T>0
and let
\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
be non-negative. A non-negative
function
\mu
is called a weak solution of

\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{1+\beta}L\Psi(\mu),\qquad\mu(\cdot,0)=\varsigma,

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
if the following conditions hold:

1.

\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

2.

For every
\tau>0
,

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

3.

The initial datum is attained in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, namely

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\|\mu(t)-\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

4.

For every
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
and every

\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T])
, one has

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mu\,\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{1}{1+\beta}\langle\nabla\Psi(\mu),\nabla\phi\rangle}}\right]d\pi dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)\phi(x,t)d\pi(x)\Big|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=0.

We would like to emphasize that much of the existing literature on porous
medium type equations concerns the well-posedness and regularity of
Cauchy–Dirichlet problems, including generalized porous medium equations; see,
for instance,

[
11
,
8
,
10
]
.
The classical porous medium equation is by now well understood, both from the
viewpoint of well-posedness and from that of long-time asymptotics; we refer to

[
6
,
7
,
3
,
19
]

and the references therein.

The equation studied in the present paper differs from the classical porous
medium equation because of the presence of the weighted diffusion operator
associated with the Gibbs measure
\pi=e^{-V}
. To the best of our knowledge,
the well-posedness and long-time behavior of the Cauchy problem (
1
)
under only a Poincaré inequality have not been previously addressed.

A standard approach to the long-time behavior of nonlinear diffusion equations
is the entropy dissipation method, which often relies on functional inequalities
such as Sobolev, logarithmic Sobolev, or Nash inequalities; see, for example,

[
4
,
5
,
1
]
. However, the
structure of (
1
) does not seem to fit directly into the classes of
equations for which these methods yield the desired
L^{1}
-
L^{p}
smoothing
and decay estimates.

Weighted nonlinear diffusion equations with Wasserstein gradient-flow
structure have also been investigated in other exponent regimes. In particular,
[
15
]
studied
weighted ultrafast diffusion equations, corresponding to negative diffusion
exponents. This is different from the present work, which concerns the weighted
porous medium regime
1+\beta>1
. For fast and very fast diffusion equations,
including equations with singular diffusivities, we refer to

[
14
,
12
,
9
,
2
,

]
.

Notation.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a domain. For
p\in(0,\infty)
, we write

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega,\pi)}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\Omega}|f|^{p}\,d\pi}}\right)^{1/p},

and, for a time interval
(t_{1},t_{2})
,

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\Omega\times(t_{1},t_{2}),\pi)}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\Omega}|f|^{p}\,d\pi dt}}\right)^{1/p}.

When
f
is vector-valued,
|f|
denotes its Euclidean norm.

For non-negative integers
k,k^{\prime}
and Hölder exponents

\delta,\delta^{\prime}\in(0,1]
, we use the following norms. If

u:Z\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, then

\|u\|_{k+\delta;Z}:=\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq k}\|D^{\alpha}u\|_{L^{\infty}(Z)}+\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\sup_{x,y\in Z,\ x\neq y}\frac{|D^{\alpha}u(x)-D^{\alpha}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{\delta}}.

If

u:Z\times Z^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}}\to\mathbb{R}
, then

\|u\|_{k+\delta,k^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime};Z\times Z^{\prime}}:=\sup_{y\in Z^{\prime}}\|u(\cdot,y)\|_{k+\delta;Z}+\sup_{x\in Z}\|u(x,\cdot)\|_{k^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime};Z^{\prime}}.

Here
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{m}
is a multi-index and

|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}
. We write

u\in C^{k+\delta,k^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime}}(\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime})

whenever

\|u\|_{k+\delta,k^{\prime}+\delta^{\prime};\Omega\times\Omega^{\prime}}<\infty.

For simplicity, throughout the derivations we often work with the rescaled
equation

\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{1+\beta},\qquad L:=\Delta-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\rangle,

unless explicitly stated otherwise. This is equivalent to equation
(
1
) after a rescaling of the time variable.

2
Main Derivations

This section is dedicated to proving the main theorem. For the well-posedness and uniqueness of the Cauchy problem (
1
), we build upon the ideas presented in
[
19
]
, but with several important differences that we will discuss here. In
[
19
]
, the Barenblatt solution (the fundamental solution) is utilized as a barrier function to derive key estimates for the solution of the classical porous media equation, which are instrumental in demonstrating that the equation is uniformly parabolic. In our situation, however, there is no direct fundamental solution available to serve as a barrier function; therefore, we develop a new barrier function, as detailed in Lemma
4
. Another difference lies in the Aronson-Bénilan estimate used in
[
19
, Proposition 9.8]
, which shows that the solution to the classical porous media equation is
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
t>0
. Due to the presence of the potential
V
in our equation (
1
), the Aronson-Bénilan type estimate can only provide a local upper bound for

\mu
, which is insufficient for proving the well-posedness and uniqueness of our Cauchy problem. Fortunately, we can utilize the novel arguments presented in Theorem
7
to address this challenge.

To establish the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem with non-negative initial data in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, our strategy is to first demonstrate the well-posedness of the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem, and then use its solutions to approximate the solution of the Cauchy problem. The remainder of this paper is structured along these lines.

2.1
Preliminary Lemmas

We first derive the following lemma by direct calculation.

Lemma 3

Assume that
\mu>0
is a classical solution of

\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{1+\beta},\qquad L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

(6)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
. Let

\nu:=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu^{\beta}.

Then
\nu
satisfies

\partial_{t}\nu=\beta\nu L\nu+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}.

(7)

Proof.
Since

\mu^{\beta}=\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\nu,

we have

\mu^{1+\beta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}}.

By the chain rule,

\partial_{t}\nu=(\beta+1)\mu^{\beta-1}\partial_{t}\mu=(\beta+1)\mu^{\beta-1}L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}}.

(8)

Moreover,

\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\nabla\nu=\mu\nabla\nu,

and

\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}}=\mu\Delta\nu+\frac{\mu}{\beta\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}.

Therefore, using
L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>
, we obtain

L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\beta\nu}{\beta+1}}}\right)^{\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}}=\mu L\nu+\frac{\mu}{\beta\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}.

Inserting this identity into (
8
) gives

\partial_{t}\nu=(\beta+1)\mu^{\beta}L\nu+\frac{(\beta+1)\mu^{\beta}}{\beta\nu}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}.

Since

(\beta+1)\mu^{\beta}=\beta\nu,

we conclude that

\partial_{t}\nu=\beta\nu L\nu+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}.

This proves (
7
).

Next, we derive a lower bound for
\nu
.

Lemma 4

Assume that
\Omega
is an open set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\nu>0
be smooth and satisfy equation (
7
) in
\Omega\times(0,T]
with

\nu(x,0)\geq c>0,\forall x\in B_{R}(x_{0})
, where
B_{R}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
. Then we can find some constants
\epsilon,\tau>0
only depending on
c,\beta,R,d,x_{0},V
, such that

\displaystyle\nu(x,t)>\frac{\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-x_{0}}}\right\|^{2})}{t+\tau},\quad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(x_{0})\times[0,T],\text{ or, equivalently,}

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)>\Big(\frac{\beta\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-x_{0}}}\right\|^{2})}{(1+\beta)(t+\tau)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\beta}},\quad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(x_{0})\times[0,T].

Proof.
Without loss of generality, we assume
x_{0}=0
. Define

\mathfrak{L}u:=\partial_{t}u-\beta uLu,\qquad L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>.

Since
\nu
satisfies (
7
), we have

\mathfrak{L}\nu=\partial_{t}\nu-\beta\nu L\nu=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu}}\right\|^{2}\geq 0.

Let

p_{\epsilon}(x,t):=\frac{\delta_{\epsilon}(x)}{t+\tau},\qquad\delta_{\epsilon}(x):=\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2}).

Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}p_{\epsilon}(x,t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta_{\epsilon}(x)}{(t+\tau)^{2}}-\beta\frac{\delta_{\epsilon}(x)L\delta_{\epsilon}(x)}{(t+\tau)^{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\delta_{\epsilon}(x)}{(t+\tau)^{2}}\Big(1+\beta L\delta_{\epsilon}(x)\Big).

Moreover,

L\delta_{\epsilon}(x)=\epsilon\Big(-2d+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V(x),x}}\right>\Big).

Since
V
is smooth on
\overline{B_{R}(0)}
, the quantity

M_{R}:=\sup_{x\in B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|-2d+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V(x),x}}\right>}}\right|

is finite. We choose
\epsilon>0
small enough such that

1-\beta\epsilon M_{R}>0.

Then

1+\beta L\delta_{\epsilon}(x)>0,\qquad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

and therefore

\mathfrak{L}p_{\epsilon}(x,t)\leq 0,\qquad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Next, choose
\tau>0
large enough so that

\frac{\epsilon R^{2}}{\tau}<c.

Then

p_{\epsilon}(x,0)=\frac{\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2})}{\tau}<c\leq\nu(x,0),\qquad\forall x\in B_{R}(0).

On the lateral boundary
\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
, we have

p_{\epsilon}(x,t)=0<\nu(x,t),

because
\nu>0
.

We are left with verifying that we can indeed use the comparison principle (see
[
19
, Section 3.1]
). We write
\mathfrak{L}
in divergence form as

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}u

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u-\beta uLu

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u-\beta u\Delta u+\beta u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla u}}\right>

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}u-\beta\operatorname{div}(u\nabla u)+\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla u}}\right\|^{2}+\beta u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla u}}\right>.

Equivalently,

\mathfrak{L}u=\partial_{t}u-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}a_{i}(x,t,u,\nabla u)-b(x,t,u,\nabla u),

where

a_{i}(x,t,u,p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}):=\beta up_{i},

and

b(x,t,u,p_{1},\ldots,p_{d}):=-\beta\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}^{2}-\beta u\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\boldsymbol{p}}}\right>,\qquad\boldsymbol{p}:=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{d})^{\top}.

The coefficients
a_{i}
and
b
are smooth in their arguments on every set where

u
is bounded above and bounded away from zero. Moreover,
\nu
is smooth,
bounded, and strictly positive on
B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
, while
p_{\epsilon}
is
smooth, bounded, and strictly positive in the interior of
B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
.
Since
p_{\epsilon}=0
on the lateral boundary and
\nu>0
there, any first contact
point between
\nu
and
p_{\epsilon}
must occur in the interior, where

p_{\epsilon}>0
. Hence the operator is locally uniformly parabolic at such a
contact point, and the standard comparison principle applies. Thus,

\nu(x,t)>p_{\epsilon}(x,t)=\frac{\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|^{2})}{t+\tau},\qquad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

For general
x_{0}
, we instead take

\delta_{\epsilon}(x):=\epsilon(R^{2}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x-x_{0}}}\right\|^{2}).

Then

L\delta_{\epsilon}(x)=\epsilon\Big(-2d+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V(x),x-x_{0}}}\right>\Big),

and the same argument applies with

M_{R,x_{0}}:=\sup_{x\in B_{R}(x_{0})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|-2d+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V(x),x-x_{0}}}\right>}}\right|.

Finally, since

\nu=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu^{\beta},

the lower bound for
\mu
follows immediately from the lower bound for
\nu
.

2.2
Cauchy-Dirichlet Problem

We first solve the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem with smooth positive bounded initial-boundary data.

Proposition 5

For small
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
with
\epsilon_{1}<1/\epsilon_{2}
, the following Cauchy-Dirichlet problem has a unique classical solution with values in
[\epsilon_{1},1/\epsilon_{2}]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},\quad L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

(9)

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma_{R}(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)=\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T],

where
\varsigma_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{B_{R}(0)})
,

\varsigma_{R}(x)=\epsilon_{1}
for all
x\in\partial B_{R}(0)
, and

\varsigma_{R}(x)\in[\epsilon_{1},1/\epsilon_{2}]
for all
x\in B_{R}(0)
.

Proof.
We first prove existence. Choose a smooth function

T_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
such that

0<\underline{a}\leq T_{\epsilon}(s)\leq\overline{a}<\infty,\qquad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},

and

T_{\epsilon}(s)=(1+\beta)s^{\beta},\qquad\forall s\in[\epsilon_{1},1/\epsilon_{2}].

Consider the uniformly parabolic problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=\operatorname{div}\Big(T_{\epsilon}(\mu)\nabla\mu\Big)-T_{\epsilon}(\mu)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla\mu}}\right>,

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma_{R}(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)=\epsilon_{1},\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Since
T_{\epsilon}
is smooth, positive, and bounded above and below away from
zero, this is a uniformly parabolic quasilinear problem. By the classical theory
of quasilinear parabolic equations (see
[
19
, Section 3.1]
),
it admits a classical solution.

The constant functions
\epsilon_{1}
and
1/\epsilon_{2}
are respectively a
subsolution and a supersolution of the above truncated problem. Since

\epsilon_{1}\leq\varsigma_{R}(x)\leq 1/\epsilon_{2}\quad\text{in }B_{R}(0),

and the lateral boundary value is
\epsilon_{1}
, the comparison principle gives

\epsilon_{1}\leq\mu(x,t)\leq 1/\epsilon_{2},\qquad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Therefore the solution never enters the region where the coefficient was
modified. Hence

T_{\epsilon}(\mu)=(1+\beta)\mu^{\beta}\qquad\text{on }B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Consequently,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu

\displaystyle=\operatorname{div}\Big((1+\beta)\mu^{\beta}\nabla\mu\Big)-(1+\beta)\mu^{\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla\mu}}\right>

\displaystyle=\Delta\mu^{\beta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla\mu^{\beta+1}}}\right>=L\mu^{\beta+1}.

Thus
\mu
solves (
9
) classically.

Next, we prove uniqueness. Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two classical solutions of
(
9
). Set

w:=\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}.

Then

\partial_{t}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})=Lw.

Define

\eta(x,t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}w(x,s)\,ds,&t\in(0,T),\\
0,&t\geq T.\end{cases}

Since
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\epsilon_{1}
on
\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
, we have

w=0
and hence
\eta=0
on
\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
. Moreover,

\eta(x,T)=0
and
\mu_{1}(x,0)-\mu_{2}(x,0)=0
.

Multiplying
\partial_{t}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})=Lw
by
\eta
and integrating by parts
with respect to
d\pi
and
t
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\eta}}\right>-(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\partial_{t}\eta\,d\pi(x)dt=0.

(10)

Since

\partial_{t}\eta(x,t)=-w(x,t),\qquad\nabla\eta(x,t)=\int_{t}^{T}\nabla w(x,s)\,ds,

equation (
10
) becomes

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})w\,d\pi(x)dt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w(x,t),\int_{t}^{T}\nabla w(x,s)\,ds}}\right>d\pi(x)dt=0.

For the second term, we compute

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w(x,t),\int_{t}^{T}\nabla w(x,s)\,ds}}\right>d\pi(x)dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\nabla w(x,t)\,dt}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x).

Therefore,

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}}}\right)d\pi(x)dt+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\nabla\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}}}\right)dt}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)=0.

Both terms are non-negative. Hence

(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}}}\right)=0\quad\text{a.e. in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T).

Since
r\mapsto r^{\beta+1}
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
, we conclude
that

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\quad\text{in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T).

Thus the classical solution is unique.

We also note that the solution obtained above is smooth in both space and time.
Indeed, spatial smoothness follows from Theorem
23
and the
Schauder estimate in Theorem
24
. To apply Theorem

24
, set

\tilde{\mu}:=\mu^{\beta+1}.

Since
\mu
is bounded above and below away from zero, the equation can be
rewritten as a uniformly parabolic equation for
\tilde{\mu}
with smooth
coefficients. Hence the Schauder interior estimates imply higher spatial
regularity. Smoothness in the time variable then follows by differentiating the
equation repeatedly with respect to time and applying the same interior
regularity argument.

Based on the above proposition, we show that the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem with zero boundary condition also has a unique classical solution in the interior.

Proposition 6

For small
\epsilon_{2}>0
, the following Cauchy-Dirichlet problem has a unique non-negative solution
\mu
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},\quad L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

(11)

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma_{R}(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)=0,\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T],

where
\varsigma_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{B_{R}(0)})
,

\varsigma_{R}(x)\in(0,1/\epsilon_{2}]
for all
x\in B_{R}(0)
, and

\varsigma_{R}(x)=0
for all
x\in\partial B_{R}(0)
. Moreover,

0\leq\mu\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}\quad\text{on }B_{R}(0)\times[0,T],

and

\mu>0\quad\text{in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T].

In particular,
\mu
is smooth in
B_{R}(0)\times(0,T]
and satisfies
(
11
) classically there.

Proof.
For each
i\geq 1
, set

a_{i}:=2^{-i},\qquad\varsigma_{R,i}:=\varsigma_{R}+a_{i}.

By Proposition
5
, applied with lower boundary value
a_{i}
and
upper bound
1/\epsilon_{2}+1
, there exists a unique classical solution

\mu_{i}
of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{i}=L\mu_{i}^{\beta+1},

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,0)=\varsigma_{R,i}(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,t)=a_{i},\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

By the comparison principle,

a_{i}\leq\mu_{i}(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}+1,\qquad\forall(x,t)\in B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Moreover, since

\varsigma_{R,i+1}\leq\varsigma_{R,i},\qquad a_{i+1}\leq a_{i},

another application of the comparison principle gives

\mu_{i+1}\leq\mu_{i}\qquad\text{in }B_{R}(0)\times[0,T].

Hence the pointwise limit

\mu(x,t):=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(x,t)

exists and satisfies

0\leq\mu(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}+1.

We next derive an estimate independent of
i
. Let

\Psi(r):=r^{\beta+1},\qquad\Phi(r):=\frac{r^{\beta+2}}{\beta+2}.

Since
\mu_{i}=a_{i}
on
\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
, the function

\Psi(\mu_{i})-\Psi(a_{i})

vanishes on the lateral boundary. Thus it is an admissible test function.
Multiplying the equation by
\Psi(\mu_{i})-\Psi(a_{i})
and integrating by parts
with respect to
d\pi
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,t))-\Psi(a_{i})\mu_{i}(x,t)}}\right]d\pi(x)

\displaystyle-\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,0))-\Psi(a_{i})\mu_{i}(x,0)}}\right]d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)ds.

Since

0\leq\mu_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}+1,

the first two integrals are uniformly bounded independently of
i
. Hence

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
i
.

Therefore, up to a subsequence,

\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})\rightharpoonup\kappa\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(B_{R}(0)\times(0,T),\pi).

Since
\mu_{i}\to\mu
pointwise and the sequence is uniformly bounded, the
dominated convergence theorem gives

\Psi(\mu_{i})\to\Psi(\mu)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{2}(B_{R}(0)\times(0,T),\pi).

Thus

\kappa=\nabla\Psi(\mu)

in the sense of distributions. Moreover,

\Psi(\mu_{i})-\Psi(a_{i})\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(B_{R}(0))),

and since
\Psi(a_{i})\to 0
, we obtain

\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(B_{R}(0))).

This gives the zero boundary condition in the natural trace sense.

For any test function

\phi\in\mathcal{C}_{c}^{\infty}(B_{R}(0)\times[0,T))
, the solution
\mu_{i}

satisfies

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mu_{i}\partial_{t}\phi+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i}),\nabla\phi}}\right>}}\right]d\pi(x)dt+\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu_{i}\phi\,d\pi(x)\Big|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=0.

Passing to the limit using the strong convergence of
\mu_{i}
and the weak
convergence of
\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})
, we get

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mu\partial_{t}\phi+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\Psi(\mu),\nabla\phi}}\right>}}\right]d\pi(x)dt+\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu\phi\,d\pi(x)\Big|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=0.

Therefore
\mu
is a weak solution of (
11
).

We now prove interior positivity and smoothness. Let
Z\Subset B_{R}(0)
be a
compact set. Since
\varsigma_{R}>0
in
B_{R}(0)
, there exists
c_{Z}>0
such that

\varsigma_{R}(x)\geq c_{Z},\qquad\forall x\in Z.

Applying Lemma
4
locally and covering
Z
by finitely many balls
compactly contained in
B_{R}(0)
, we obtain, for every
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
,

\mu_{i}(x,t)\geq c(Z,t_{1},t_{2})>0,\qquad\forall(x,t)\in Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}],

where the constant is independent of
i
. Together with the uniform upper bound,
this makes the equation uniformly parabolic on
Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Hence, by
Theorem
23
and the Schauder estimates in Theorem
24
,
for every multi-index
k
there exists
\delta\in(0,1)
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|D^{k}\mu_{i}}}\right\|_{2+\delta,1+\delta/2;Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]}\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
i
. Passing to the limit yields

\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(B_{R}(0)\times(0,T])

and
\mu
satisfies (
11
) classically in the interior. The same lower
bound also shows that

\mu>0\quad\text{in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T].

It remains to prove uniqueness. Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two solutions of
(
11
) with the same initial and boundary data. Set

w:=\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}.

Then

\partial_{t}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})=Lw.

Define

\eta(x,t):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int_{t}^{T}w(x,s)\,ds,&t\in(0,T),\\
0,&t\geq T.\end{cases}

Since both solutions have zero boundary data, we have
\eta=0
on

\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T]
. Multiplying by
\eta
and integrating by parts,
we obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\eta}}\right>-(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\partial_{t}\eta\,d\pi(x)dt=0.

Using

\partial_{t}\eta=-w,\qquad\nabla\eta(x,t)=\int_{t}^{T}\nabla w(x,s)\,ds,

we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{B_{R}(0)}(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})w\,d\pi(x)dt

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\nabla w(x,t)\,dt}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)=0.

Both terms are non-negative. Hence

(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{1}^{\beta+1}-\mu_{2}^{\beta+1}}}\right)=0\quad\text{a.e. in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T).

Since
r\mapsto r^{\beta+1}
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
, this implies

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\quad\text{a.e. in }B_{R}(0)\times(0,T).

By continuity in the interior, the equality holds everywhere in

B_{R}(0)\times(0,T)
. Thus the solution is unique.

The following theorem pertains to the large-time behavior of

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(B_{R}(0),\pi)}
, which is crucial for demonstrating the well-posedness, uniqueness, and the

L^{1}-L^{p}
smoothing effect of the equation.

Theorem 7

Assume that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with constant
\lambda>0
,
V
smooth and
p>1,\beta>0,p+\beta\geq 2
. Let

L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>.

For small
\epsilon_{2}>0
, let
\mu
be the classical solution to the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{1+\beta}L\mu^{\beta+1}=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Delta\mu^{\beta+1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla\mu^{\beta+1}}}\right>}}\right],

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma_{R}(x),\quad\forall x\in B_{R}(0),

\displaystyle\mu(x,t)=0,\quad\forall(x,t)\in\partial B_{R}(0)\times[0,T],

where
\varsigma_{R}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{B_{R}(0)})
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{R}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{R}(0),\pi)}=1
,

\varsigma_{R}(x)\in(0,1/\epsilon_{2}]
for all
x\in B_{R}(0)
, and

\varsigma_{R}(x)=0
for all
x\in\partial B_{R}(0)
. Then
K_{p}(\mu(t))
is
monotonically non-increasing in time, where

K_{p}(\mu(t)):=\frac{1}{p-1}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p}(t)d\pi(x)}}\right).

Moreover, with

t_{0}:=\inf\{t\in[0,T]:K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq 1\},

we have

K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq\begin{cases}-\dfrac{p\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\varsigma_{R})}+\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}t}}\right)}{\beta(p-1)}&\text{ if }0<t<t_{0},\\[10.00002pt]
e^{-\frac{2p\lambda(t-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}K_{p}(\mu(t_{0}))&\text{ if }t\geq t_{0}.\end{cases}

(12)

If
K_{p}(\varsigma_{R})\leq 1
, then
t_{0}=0
and only the second estimate is used.

Proof.
For simplicity, write

A_{p}(t):=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p}(t)d\pi(x).

We first prove that
A_{p}(t)
is non-increasing. Since
\mu
solves

\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{1+\beta}L\mu^{1+\beta},\qquad L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}A_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=p\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p-1}(t)\partial_{t}\mu(t)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{p}{1+\beta}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p-1}(t)L\mu^{1+\beta}(t)d\pi(x).

Using integration by parts with respect to
d\pi=e^{-V}dx
, and using that

\mu=0
on
\partial B_{R}(0)
, we get

\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p-1}L\mu^{1+\beta}d\pi=-\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\mu^{p-1},\nabla\mu^{1+\beta}}}\right>d\pi.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}A_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{1+\beta}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\mu^{p-1}(t),\nabla\mu^{1+\beta}(t)}}\right>d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{p}{1+\beta}(p-1)(1+\beta)\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p+\beta-2}(t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu(t)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=-p(p-1)\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p+\beta-2}(t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu(t)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x).

Since

\nabla\mu^{\frac{p+\beta}{2}}=\frac{p+\beta}{2}\mu^{\frac{p+\beta-2}{2}}\nabla\mu,

we have

\mu^{p+\beta-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu}}\right\|^{2}=\frac{4}{(p+\beta)^{2}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu^{\frac{p+\beta}{2}}}}\right\|^{2}.

Thus

\frac{d}{dt}A_{p}(t)=-\frac{4p(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu^{\frac{p+\beta}{2}}(t)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)\leq 0.

Hence
A_{p}(t)
, and therefore
K_{p}(\mu(t))
, is non-increasing.

We also need the mass dissipation property. Since
\mu^{\beta+1}\geq 0
in

B_{R}(0)
and
\mu^{\beta+1}=0
on
\partial B_{R}(0)
, its outward normal
derivative is non-positive on the boundary. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu(t)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\int_{B_{R}(0)}L\mu^{1+\beta}(t)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\int_{\partial B_{R}(0)}e^{-V}\partial_{n}\mu^{1+\beta}(t)dS\leq 0.

Consequently,

\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu(t)d\pi(x)\leq\int_{B_{R}(0)}\varsigma_{R}d\pi(x)=1.

Now set

q:=p+\beta.

Since
q\geq 2
and
\mu=0
on
\partial B_{R}(0)
, the function

\mu^{q/2}
has zero trace on
\partial B_{R}(0)
. Hence its zero extension to

\mathbb{R}^{d}
belongs to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Applying the Poincaré inequality to this zero
extension gives

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu^{\frac{q}{2}}(t)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q/2}(t)d\pi(x)}}\right)^{2}}}\right]

(13)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi(x)-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi(x)}}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{q-1}}}}\right].

We justify the last inequality. Since

\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu(t)d\pi\leq 1,

we may apply Hölder’s inequality with respect to the sub-probability measure

\mu(t)d\pi
. If
q>2
, then

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q/2}(t)d\pi

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{(q-2)/2}(t)\mu(t)d\pi

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu^{(q-2)/2}(t)}}\right)^{\frac{2(q-1)}{q-2}}\mu(t)d\pi}}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2(q-1)}}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi}}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{2(q-1)}}.

Squaring both sides gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q/2}(t)d\pi}}\right)^{2}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi}}\right)^{\frac{q-2}{q-1}}.

When
q=2
, the same estimate reduces to

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu(t)d\pi}}\right)^{2}\leq 1,

which follows from mass dissipation.

We now assume that
A_{p}(t)\geq 1
, equivalently
K_{p}(\mu(t))\geq 0
. Define

B_{q}(t):=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi.

Since
\pi(B_{R}(0))\leq 1
and
q>p
, Hölder’s inequality gives

A_{p}(t)^{q/p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p}(t)d\pi}}\right)^{q/p}\leq\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi=B_{q}(t).

Moreover, using again the sub-probability measure

d\nu_{t}:=\mu(t)d\pi,

we obtain

\displaystyle A_{p}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p}(t)d\pi

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{p-1}(t)d\nu_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu^{q-1}(t)}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{q-1}}d\nu_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q-1}(t)d\nu_{t}}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{q-1}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}1\,d\nu_{t}}}\right)^{1-\frac{p-1}{q-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mu^{q}(t)d\pi}}\right)^{\frac{p-1}{q-1}}

\displaystyle=B_{q}(t)^{\frac{p-1}{q-1}}.

Hence

A_{p}(t)^{1/(p-1)}\leq B_{q}(t)^{1/(q-1)}.

Equivalently,

B_{q}(t)^{-1/(q-1)}\leq A_{p}(t)^{-1/(p-1)}.

Therefore,

1-B_{q}(t)^{-1/(q-1)}\geq 1-A_{p}(t)^{-1/(p-1)}.

Since
A_{p}(t)\geq 1
, the factor

1-A_{p}(t)^{-1/(p-1)}

is non-negative. Combining this with
B_{q}(t)\geq A_{p}(t)^{q/p}
, we get

\displaystyle B_{q}(t)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-B_{q}(t)^{-\frac{1}{q-1}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq A_{p}(t)^{q/p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-A_{p}(t)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}}}\right)

\displaystyle=A_{p}(t)^{\frac{p+\beta}{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-A_{p}(t)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}}}\right).

Substituting this into (
13
), we obtain

\int_{B_{R}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\mu^{\frac{p+\beta}{2}}(t)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi\geq\lambda A_{p}(t)^{\frac{p+\beta}{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-A_{p}(t)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}}}\right).

Returning to the differential identity for
A_{p}(t)
, we conclude that

\frac{d}{dt}A_{p}(t)\leq-\frac{4p(p-1)\lambda}{(p+\beta)^{2}}A_{p}(t)^{\frac{p+\beta}{p}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-A_{p}(t)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}}}\right).

Since

K_{p}(\mu(t))=\frac{1}{p-1}\log A_{p}(t),

we have

A_{p}(t)=e^{(p-1)K_{p}(\mu(t))}.

Also,

\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))=\frac{1}{p-1}\frac{A_{p}^{\prime}(t)}{A_{p}(t)}.

Dividing the previous differential inequality by
(p-1)A_{p}(t)
gives

\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq-\frac{4p\lambda}{(p+\beta)^{2}}A_{p}(t)^{\beta/p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-A_{p}(t)^{-1/(p-1)}}}\right).

Using

A_{p}(t)^{\beta/p}=e^{\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\mu(t))}

and

A_{p}(t)^{-1/(p-1)}=e^{-K_{p}(\mu(t))},

we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq-\frac{4p\lambda}{(p+\beta)^{2}}e^{\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\mu(t))}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-e^{-K_{p}(\mu(t))}}}\right),

whenever
K_{p}(\mu(t))\geq 0
.

We now distinguish two regimes. First suppose
K_{p}(\mu(t))\geq 1
. Then

1-e^{-K_{p}(\mu(t))}\geq 1-e^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Therefore, as long as
K_{p}(\mu(t))\geq 1
,

\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq-\frac{2p\lambda}{(p+\beta)^{2}}e^{\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\mu(t))}.

(14)

Set

\alpha:=\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}.

Multiplying (
14
) by
-\alpha e^{-\alpha K_{p}(\mu(t))}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\alpha K_{p}(\mu(t))}=-\alpha e^{-\alpha K_{p}(\mu(t))}\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))\geq\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}.

Integrating from
0
to
t<t_{0}
, we obtain

e^{-\alpha K_{p}(\mu(t))}\geq e^{-\alpha K_{p}(\varsigma_{R})}+\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}t.

Taking logarithms and using
\alpha=\beta(p-1)/p
, we get

K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq-\frac{p}{\beta(p-1)}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\varsigma_{R})}+\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}t}}\right),\qquad 0<t<t_{0}.

Next suppose
0\leq K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq 1
. Since

1-e^{-x}\geq\frac{x}{2},\qquad 0\leq x\leq 1,

and

e^{\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\mu(t))}\geq 1,

we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq-\frac{2p\lambda}{(p+\beta)^{2}}K_{p}(\mu(t)).

(15)

Applying Grönwall’s lemma from
t_{0}
to
t
gives

K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq e^{-\frac{2p\lambda(t-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}K_{p}(\mu(t_{0})).

This estimate holds as long as
K_{p}(\mu(s))\geq 0
on
[t_{0},t]
. If
K_{p}

becomes negative at a later time, the same upper bound remains true trivially,
because the right-hand side is non-negative while
K_{p}(\mu(t))<0
. Therefore
(
12
) follows.

Remark 8

As a consequence of Theorem
7
, we obtain the uniform estimate

K_{p}(\mu(t))\leq\max\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{-\frac{p}{\beta(p-1)}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}t}}\right),1}}\right\},\qquad\forall t>0.

In particular, for every
t>0
,
\beta>0
,
p>1
, and
p+\beta\geq 2
,
there exists a constant
C=C(p,\beta,\lambda,t)
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(B_{R}(0),\pi)}\leq C(p,\beta,\lambda,t).

Importantly, this bound is independent of

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{R}}}\right\|_{L^{p}(B_{R}(0),\pi)}
.

Remark 9

Theorem
7
also applies to the classical porous medium equation
on
B_{R}(0)
. Indeed, one may choose a smooth probability measure

\pi=e^{-V}
such that
V
is constant on
B_{R}(0)
and such that
\pi

satisfies a Poincaré inequality with some constant
\lambda>0
, possibly
depending on
R
. On
B_{R}(0)
, the weighted operator

\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>
then coincides with the usual Laplacian.
Hence the conclusion of Theorem
7
gives the corresponding
estimate for the classical porous medium equation on
B_{R}(0)
.

2.3
Cauchy Problem

We will now utilize the solutions obtained from the Cauchy-Dirichlet problem to construct a solution for the Cauchy problem.

2.3.1
Cauchy Problem with Smooth Positive Bounded Initial Data

We first consider the Cauchy problem with smooth positive bounded initial data.

Proposition 10

Assume that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and that
V
is smooth. For small

\epsilon_{2}>0
, the following Cauchy problem has a unique bounded positive
classical solution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},\quad L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

(16)

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

where
\varsigma\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and

0<\varsigma(x)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Moreover,

0<\mu(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],

and

\mu\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

In addition,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

is non-increasing in time,

\mu\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall p\in(0,\infty),

and

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\Psi(\mu):=\mu^{1+\beta}.

Finally, for every
\tau>0
,

\partial_{t}\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad L\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Proof.
Let
R_{i}:=2^{i}
. Choose functions

\chi_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\overline{B_{R_{i}}(0)})
such that

0\leq\chi_{i}\leq 1,\qquad\chi_{i}=1\text{ on }B_{R_{i}-1}(0),\qquad\chi_{i}>0\text{ in }B_{R_{i}}(0),

and

\chi_{i}=0\text{ on }\partial B_{R_{i}}(0).

Set

\varsigma_{i}:=\varsigma\chi_{i}.

Then

0<\varsigma_{i}(x)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}\quad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0),\qquad\varsigma_{i}=0\quad\text{on }\partial B_{R_{i}}(0).

By Proposition
6
, there exists a unique solution
\mu_{i}
of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{i}=L\mu_{i}^{\beta+1},\qquad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0)\times(0,T],

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,0)=\varsigma_{i}(x),\qquad x\in B_{R_{i}}(0),

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,t)=0,\qquad(x,t)\in\partial B_{R_{i}}(0)\times[0,T].

The comparison principle gives

0\leq\mu_{i}(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},\qquad(x,t)\in B_{R_{i}}(0)\times[0,T].

Let
Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
. For all sufficiently large
i
, we have

Z\Subset B_{R_{i}}(0)
and
\chi_{i}=1
on a neighbourhood of
Z
. Since

\varsigma>0
and is continuous, there exists
c_{Z}>0
, independent of
i
, such
that

\varsigma_{i}(x)\geq c_{Z},\qquad x\in Z.

Applying Lemma
4
locally and covering
Z
by finitely many balls,
we obtain

\mu_{i}(x,t)\geq c(Z,t_{1},t_{2})>0,\qquad(x,t)\in Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}],

with a constant independent of
i
. Together with the uniform upper bound, this
makes the equation uniformly parabolic on
Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
, uniformly in
i
.
Therefore, by Theorem
23
and the Schauder estimate in Theorem

24
, for every multi-index
k
there exist
\delta\in(0,1)
and a
constant
C
, independent of
i
, such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|D^{k}\mu_{i}}}\right\|_{2+\delta,1+\delta/2;Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]}\leq C.

By a diagonal argument, there exists a function
\mu
such that, along a
subsequence,

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{locally smoothly in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Hence
\mu
is positive and smooth in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
, satisfies

\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},

and obeys

0<\mu(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}.

We now show that
\mu
attains the initial datum. Fix
Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

t_{2}>0
. For all sufficiently large
i
, we have
\chi_{i}=1
on a neighbourhood
of
Z
, and hence

\varsigma_{i}=\varsigma\quad\text{on }Z.

Moreover, Lemma
4
gives a positive lower bound for
\mu_{i}
on

Z\times[0,t_{2}]
, independent of
i
. Thus the equation is uniformly parabolic
there up to the initial time. The local Schauder estimates up to the initial
time (see for example
[
16
, Theorem 1996]
) imply local compactness on
Z\times[0,t_{2}]
. Passing to the limit gives

\mu(x,0)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(x,0)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\varsigma_{i}(x)=\varsigma(x),

locally uniformly in
x
. Therefore

\mu(x,t)\to\varsigma(x)\quad\text{for every }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\text{as }t\downarrow 0.

Since

0\leq\mu(x,t)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},\qquad 0\leq\varsigma(x)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},

and
\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
, dominated convergence gives

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

For
t>0
, the same dominated convergence argument gives continuity in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
. Thus

\mu\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Next, we derive the energy estimate. For each
\mu_{i}
, testing the equation
with
\Psi(\mu_{i})=\mu_{i}^{\beta+1}
gives

\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,t))d\pi(x)-\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\Phi(\varsigma_{i}(x))d\pi(x)=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)ds,

where

\Phi(r):=\frac{r^{\beta+2}}{\beta+2}.

Since

0\leq\varsigma_{i}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}},

we obtain

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)ds\leq C(t,\beta,\epsilon_{2}),\qquad 0<t\leq T,

where
C
is independent of
i
. Passing to the limit and applying Fatou’s
lemma gives

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)ds\leq C(t,\beta,\epsilon_{2}),\qquad 0<t\leq T.

Hence

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Since

0\leq\mu\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}

and
\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
, we immediately have

\mu\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall p\in(0,\infty).

The
L^{1}
norm is non-increasing. Indeed, applying Proposition
12

with
\mu_{1}=\mu
and
\mu_{2}=0
yields

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{2})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{1})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},\qquad 0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T.

We now estimate
\partial_{t}\mu
. Fix
t>0
and
h>0
, and choose
\eta>1
such
that

\eta^{\beta}t=t+h.

Define

\tilde{\mu}(x,s):=\eta\mu(x,\eta^{\beta}s).

Then
\tilde{\mu}
solves the same equation with initial datum
\eta\varsigma
.
By Proposition
12
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\tilde{\mu}(t)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\eta\varsigma-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=(\eta-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Since
\tilde{\mu}(t)=\eta\mu(t+h)
, we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq 2(\eta-1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Moreover,

\eta=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1+\frac{h}{t}}}\right)^{1/\beta},

so

\lim_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\eta-1}{h}=\frac{1}{\beta t}.

Thus

\limsup_{h\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{h}\leq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{\beta t}.

Since
\mu
is classical for positive times, Fatou’s lemma gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{\beta t}.

Because
\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu)
, we also have

L\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0.

Finally, uniqueness follows from Proposition
12
. If
\mu_{1}
and

\mu_{2}
are two solutions with the same initial datum, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(0)-\mu_{2}(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

Hence
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
, and the proof is complete.

Proposition 11

Assume, in addition to the hypotheses of Proposition
10
, that

\pi=e^{-V}
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with constant
\lambda>0
.
Let
\mu
be the solution of (
16
) constructed in Proposition

10
. Then, for every
p\in(0,\infty)
and every
\tau>0
,

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

with a bound depending only on

p,\beta,\lambda,\tau,T,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},

and not on
\epsilon_{2}
or on
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}
.

Moreover,

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

with

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),

where the constant is independent of
\epsilon_{2}
.

Proof.
Let
\mu_{i}
be the Cauchy–Dirichlet approximations used in the proof of
Proposition
10
. Set

M_{i}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(B_{R_{i}}(0),\pi)}.

Then

M_{i}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Applying Theorem
7
to the normalized solution

\bar{\mu}_{i}(x,t):=M_{i}^{-1}\mu_{i}(x,M_{i}^{-\beta}t),

and then scaling back, gives, for every
p>1
with
p+\beta\geq 2
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(B_{R_{i}}(0),\pi)}\leq C(p,\beta,\lambda,t,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),\qquad t>0,

where the constant is independent of
i
and
\epsilon_{2}
. Passing to the limit
and applying Fatou’s lemma yields

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq C(p,\beta,\lambda,t,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}).

Taking the supremum over
t\in[\tau,T]
gives

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))

for all
p>1
satisfying
p+\beta\geq 2
.

If
p>1
but
p+\beta<2
, choose
q>p
such that
q+\beta\geq 2
. Since

\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
, Hölder’s inequality gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Thus the same conclusion holds for every
p>1
. For
0<p\leq 1
, using

\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
and the non-increasing property of the
L^{1}
norm, we have

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Therefore

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall p\in(0,\infty),\ \forall\tau>0.

It remains to prove the gradient estimate independent of
\epsilon_{2}
. For each

\mu_{i}
, the energy identity gives

\displaystyle\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,T))d\pi(x)-\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,\tau))d\pi(x)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt,

where

\Phi(r):=\frac{r^{\beta+2}}{\beta+2}.

Hence

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\Phi(\mu_{i}(x,\tau))d\pi(x).

Using the
L^{\beta+2}
estimate already obtained at time
\tau
, we get

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{B_{R_{i}}(0)}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),

with a constant independent of
i
and
\epsilon_{2}
. Passing to the limit and
using Fatou’s lemma yields

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}).

This proves the proposition.

The following proposition establishes an
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
contraction property.

Proposition 12

Let

L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,\qquad\Psi(r):=r^{1+\beta}.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two non-negative classical solutions of

\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu)

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
, such that

\mu_{i}\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad i=1,2,

and

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt<\infty,\qquad i=1,2.

Then, for every
0\leq t_{1}\leq t_{2}\leq T
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{1}(t_{2})-\mu_{2}(t_{2}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{1}(t_{1})-\mu_{2}(t_{1}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Consequently,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t_{2})-\mu_{2}(t_{2})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t_{1})-\mu_{2}(t_{1})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

In particular, if
\mu_{1}(0)=\mu_{2}(0)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, then

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
.

Proof.
Set

y:=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\qquad w:=\Psi(\mu_{1})-\Psi(\mu_{2}).

Since
\Psi(r)=r^{1+\beta}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
, we have

w>0\Longleftrightarrow y>0,\qquad w=0\Longleftrightarrow y=0.

Subtracting the two equations gives

\partial_{t}y=Lw.

Let
\theta_{\delta}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
be such that

0\leq\theta_{\delta}\leq 1,\qquad\theta_{\delta}(s)=0\text{ for }s\leq 0,\qquad\theta_{\delta}^{\prime}(s)\geq 0,

and

\theta_{\delta}(s)\to\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(s)\quad\text{as }\delta\downarrow 0.

Here

\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(s)=\begin{cases}1,&s>0,\\
0,&s\leq 0.\end{cases}

Let
\zeta_{1}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy

0\leq\zeta_{1}\leq 1,\qquad\zeta_{1}(x)=1\text{ if }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|\leq 1,\qquad\zeta_{1}(x)=0\text{ if }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|x}}\right\|\geq 2,

and define

\zeta_{n}(x):=\zeta_{1}(x/n).

Then

0\leq\zeta_{n}\leq 1,\qquad\zeta_{n}\uparrow 1,\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\frac{C}{n}.

Fix
0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
. Multiplying

\partial_{t}y=Lw

by
\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n}
and integrating over

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(t_{1},t_{2})
gives

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Lw\,\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt.

Using integration by parts with respect to
d\pi=e^{-V}dx
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}Lw\,\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla(\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n})}}\right>d\pi dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\delta}^{\prime}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla w}}\right\|^{2}\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt

\displaystyle-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\delta}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right>d\pi dt.

Since
\theta_{\delta}^{\prime}\geq 0
, the first term on the right-hand side is
non-positive. Hence

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\theta_{\delta}(w)\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\delta}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right>d\pi dt.

Letting
\delta\downarrow 0
, and using the fact that
w
and
y
have the same
sign, we get

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(y)\zeta_{n}\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right>d\pi dt.

Since

\partial_{t}y\,\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(y)=\partial_{t}y^{+},

the left-hand side becomes

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{2})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{1})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{2})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{1})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right>d\pi dt}}\right|.

Using
0\leq\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(w)\leq 1
and Cauchy’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\operatorname{sign}_{0}^{+}(w)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla w,\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right>d\pi dt}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla w}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt}}\right)^{1/2}.

Moreover,

\nabla w=\nabla\Psi(\mu_{1})-\nabla\Psi(\mu_{2}),

and therefore

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla w}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt\leq 2\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt<\infty.

Also,

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{n}}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt\leq\frac{C(t_{2}-t_{1})}{n^{2}}.

Hence

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{2})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{1})\zeta_{n}(x)d\pi(x)+\frac{C}{n}.

Letting
n\to\infty
and using monotone convergence gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{2})d\pi(x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}(x,t_{1})d\pi(x).

Thus

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{1}(t_{2})-\mu_{2}(t_{2}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{1}(t_{1})-\mu_{2}(t_{1}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

By the
L^{1}
-continuity of
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
, the same estimate also holds for

t_{1}=0
.

Exchanging the roles of
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{2}(t_{2})-\mu_{1}(t_{2}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|(\mu_{2}(t_{1})-\mu_{1}(t_{1}))^{+}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Adding the two inequalities yields

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t_{2})-\mu_{2}(t_{2})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t_{1})-\mu_{2}(t_{1})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

If
\mu_{1}(0)=\mu_{2}(0)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, then the right-hand side is zero
with
t_{1}=0
, and therefore
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

2.3.2
Aronson-Bénilan Estimate

Under the additional convexity assumption on the potential
V
, we obtain an
Aronson–Bénilan type estimate analogous to the one for the classical porous
medium equation.

Proposition 15

Assume in addition that
V
is convex, namely

\nabla^{2}V\geq 0.

Let
\mu
be the positive classical solution from Proposition
10
,
and define

\nu:=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu^{\beta}.

Then

L\nu\geq-\frac{1}{\beta t},\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],

where

L:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>.

Proof.
We prove the estimate first for smooth strictly positive approximating
solutions, for which all applications of the comparison principle are justified.

We begin by constructing smooth convex approximations of
V
with bounded
derivatives. Let
V^{*}
denote the Legendre transform of
V
, and define

W_{i}(x):=\sup_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|p}}\right\|\leq i}\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<p,x}}\right>-V^{*}(p)\}.

Then
W_{i}
is convex and globally Lipschitz, with

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla W_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq i

in the weak sense. Moreover, since
V
is smooth and convex, for every compact
set
K\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all sufficiently large
i
, we have

W_{i}=V\quad\text{on a neighbourhood of }K.

Indeed, on such a compact set the quantity
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla V}}\right\|
is bounded, and
the identity

V(x)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V(x),x}}\right>-V^{*}(\nabla V(x))

shows that the restricted supremum defining
W_{i}
agrees with
V
once

i>\sup_{K}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla V}}\right\|
.

Let
\rho_{\varepsilon}
be a standard smooth mollifier and choose

\varepsilon_{i}\downarrow 0
. Set

V_{i}:=W_{i}*\rho_{\varepsilon_{i}}.

Then

V_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\nabla^{2}V_{i}\geq 0,

and
V_{i}
has bounded derivatives of every order for each fixed
i
. Moreover,

V_{i}\to V\quad\text{in }C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Define

L_{i}:=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V_{i},\nabla}}\right>.

Let
S_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a cutoff such that

0\leq S_{i}\leq 1,\qquad S_{i}=0\text{ on }B_{i}(0),\qquad S_{i}=1\text{ outside }B_{2i}(0).

Set

\varsigma_{i}(x):=\varsigma(x)(1-S_{i}(x))+2^{-i}.

Then
\varsigma_{i}
is smooth, strictly positive, bounded, and equal to the
constant
2^{-i}
outside a compact set. Let
\mu_{i}
be the smooth solution of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{i}=L_{i}\mu_{i}^{\beta+1},\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T],

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,0)=\varsigma_{i}(x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For each fixed
i
, this problem is uniformly parabolic, since the comparison
principle gives

2^{-i}\leq\mu_{i}(x,t)\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T].

Because
V_{i}
has bounded derivatives of all orders and
\varsigma_{i}
is smooth
with bounded derivatives, the solution
\mu_{i}
is smooth and has bounded
derivatives on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
for each fixed
i
.

Define the pressure variable

\nu_{i}:=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu_{i}^{\beta}.

By Lemma
3
, applied with
L_{i}
in place of
L
, we have

\partial_{t}\nu_{i}=\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}\nu_{i}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}.

Set

q_{i}:=L_{i}\nu_{i}.

Since
L_{i}
is independent of time,

\partial_{t}q_{i}=L_{i}\partial_{t}\nu_{i}=L_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}\nu_{i}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}}}\right).

Using the product rule

L_{i}(\phi\psi)=\phi L_{i}\psi+\psi L_{i}\phi+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\phi,\nabla\psi}}\right>,

we get

\displaystyle L_{i}(\nu_{i}L_{i}\nu_{i})

\displaystyle=\nu_{i}L_{i}^{2}\nu_{i}+(L_{i}\nu_{i})^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla L_{i}\nu_{i}}}\right>

\displaystyle=\nu_{i}L_{i}q_{i}+q_{i}^{2}+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla q_{i}}}\right>.

Therefore,

L_{i}(\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}\nu_{i})=\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}q_{i}+\beta q_{i}^{2}+2\beta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla q_{i}}}\right>.

We compute the term
L_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}
using the weighted Bochner
identity. Since

L_{i}=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V_{i},\nabla}}\right>,

we have

L_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}=2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla L_{i}\nu_{i}}}\right>+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla^{2}\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}+2\nabla^{2}V_{i}(\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla\nu_{i}).

Recalling that
q_{i}=L_{i}\nu_{i}
, this identity can be rewritten as

L_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}=2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla q_{i}}}\right>+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla^{2}\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}+2\nabla^{2}V_{i}(\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla\nu_{i}).

Combining the previous identities, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}q_{i}

\displaystyle=\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}q_{i}+2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla q_{i}}}\right>+\beta q_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle+2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla^{2}\nu_{i}}}\right\|^{2}+2\nabla^{2}V_{i}(\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla\nu_{i}).

Since
\nabla^{2}V_{i}\geq 0
, the last two terms are non-negative. Thus

\partial_{t}q_{i}-\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}q_{i}-2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla q_{i}}}\right>-\beta q_{i}^{2}\geq 0.

Define

\mathcal{P}_{i}r:=\partial_{t}r-\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}r-2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla r}}\right>-\beta r^{2}.

Then

\mathcal{P}_{i}q_{i}\geq 0.

For
\tau>0
, define

Q(t):=-\frac{1}{\beta(t+\tau)}.

Since
Q
is independent of
x
, we have

L_{i}Q=0,\qquad\nabla Q=0.

Therefore,

\mathcal{P}_{i}Q=Q^{\prime}(t)-\beta Q(t)^{2}.

But

Q^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{\beta(t+\tau)^{2}},\qquad\beta Q(t)^{2}=\frac{1}{\beta(t+\tau)^{2}}.

Hence

\mathcal{P}_{i}Q=0.

For each fixed
i
, the function
q_{i}(\cdot,0)=L_{i}\nu_{i}(\cdot,0)
is bounded
from below. Indeed,
\varsigma_{i}
is smooth, strictly positive, and constant
outside a compact set, while
V_{i}
has bounded derivatives. Hence there exists

M_{i}\geq 0
such that

q_{i}(x,0)\geq-M_{i},\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since

Q(0)=-\frac{1}{\beta\tau}\to-\infty\qquad\text{as }\tau\downarrow 0,

we can choose
\tau>0
sufficiently small so that

Q(0)\leq-M_{i}.

Therefore,

q_{i}(x,0)\geq Q(0),\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We now compare
q_{i}
and
Q
. Set

z_{i}:=q_{i}-Q.

Since
\mathcal{P}_{i}q_{i}\geq 0
and
\mathcal{P}_{i}Q=0
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{P}_{i}q_{i}-\mathcal{P}_{i}Q

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}z_{i}-\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}z_{i}-2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla z_{i}}}\right>-\beta(q_{i}^{2}-Q^{2})

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}z_{i}-\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}z_{i}-2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla z_{i}}}\right>-\beta(q_{i}+Q)z_{i}.

Thus
z_{i}
satisfies the linear differential inequality

\partial_{t}z_{i}-\beta\nu_{i}L_{i}z_{i}-2(\beta+1)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\nu_{i},\nabla z_{i}}}\right>-\beta(q_{i}+Q)z_{i}\geq 0.

For each fixed
i
, the coefficients are smooth and bounded on

\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T]
, and
\nu_{i}
is bounded above and below away from zero. Hence
the operator is uniformly parabolic. Since

z_{i}(x,0)=q_{i}(x,0)-Q(0)\geq 0,

the parabolic comparison principle on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
gives

z_{i}(x,t)\geq 0,\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T].

Equivalently,

q_{i}(x,t)\geq Q(t)=-\frac{1}{\beta(t+\tau)}.

Letting
\tau\downarrow 0
gives

L_{i}\nu_{i}(x,t)=q_{i}(x,t)\geq-\frac{1}{\beta t},\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

It remains to pass to the limit. By the local compactness argument used in
Proposition
10
, after passing to a subsequence,

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{locally smoothly in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Therefore,

\nu_{i}=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu_{i}^{\beta}\to\nu=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu^{\beta}

locally smoothly in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
. Since
V_{i}\to V
in

C^{\infty}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we also have

L_{i}\nu_{i}=\Delta\nu_{i}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V_{i},\nabla\nu_{i}}}\right>\to\Delta\nu-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla\nu}}\right>=L\nu

locally uniformly in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
. Passing to the limit in

L_{i}\nu_{i}\geq-\frac{1}{\beta t}

yields

L\nu\geq-\frac{1}{\beta t}\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

This proves the proposition.

As a consequence of the preceding proposition, we have

L\nu\geq-\frac{1}{\beta t},\qquad\nu=\frac{\beta+1}{\beta}\mu^{\beta}.

Since
L
is linear, this implies

L\mu^{\beta}\geq-\frac{1}{(1+\beta)t}.

Therefore, for every
t>0
, every
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and every non-negative

\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(B_{1}(x_{0}))
, integration by parts with respect to
d\pi

gives

\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}\langle\nabla\mu^{\beta}(t),\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi(x)\leq\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}\frac{1}{(1+\beta)t}\varphi\,d\pi(x).

Applying Theorem
25
to
u=\mu^{\beta}(t)
and

g=(1+\beta)^{-1}t^{-1}
, we obtain, for every
r\geq 1
,

\sup_{B_{1/2}(x_{0})}\mu^{\beta}(t)\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d,r,\max_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V,\min_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\mu^{\beta}(t)\|_{L^{r}(B_{1}(x_{0}),\pi)}+\frac{1}{(1+\beta)t}}}\right\}.

We now distinguish two cases. If
0<\beta\leq 1
, then
1/\beta\geq 1
, and we
choose
r=1/\beta
. This gives

\|\mu^{\beta}(t)\|_{L^{1/\beta}(B_{1}(x_{0}),\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}\mu(t)d\pi}}\right)^{\beta}\leq\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}^{\beta}.

Hence

\sup_{B_{1/2}(x_{0})}\mu^{\beta}(t)\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d,\beta,\max_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V,\min_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}^{\beta}+\frac{1}{(1+\beta)t}}}\right\}.

If
\beta>1
, we choose instead
r=1
. Then

\|\mu^{\beta}(t)\|_{L^{1}(B_{1}(x_{0}),\pi)}=\int_{B_{1}(x_{0})}\mu^{\beta}(t)d\pi\leq\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}^{\beta}.

Using the
L^{1}
–
L^{\beta}
smoothing estimate, we have, for every
t>0
,

\|\mu(t)\|_{L^{\beta}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta,\lambda,t,\|\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}}\right).

Therefore,

\sup_{B_{1/2}(x_{0})}\mu^{\beta}(t)\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d,\beta,\lambda,t,\|\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},\max_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V,\min_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V}}\right).

Combining the two cases, we obtain

\sup_{B_{1/2}(x_{0})}\mu(t)\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(d,\beta,\lambda,t,\|\varsigma\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},\max_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V,\min_{B_{1}(x_{0})}V}}\right)<\infty.

2.3.3
Cauchy Problem with Positive Initial Data in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)

In this subsection, we assume that the local upper bound (
17
) is
available. In particular, this holds under the convexity assumption on
V
used
in the Aronson–Bénilan estimate above. We also assume that

\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with
constant
\lambda>0
.
We proved the following proposition.

Proposition 16

Assume that
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is convex, that is,

\nabla^{2}V\geq 0,

and assume that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré
inequality with constant
\lambda>0
. Let
\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
be
locally strictly positive in the sense stated in the Introduction.

Then the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},\qquad L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma(x),

admits a unique positive weak solution
\mu\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\prime}:=\{\mu:

\displaystyle\mu\in\mathcal{C}([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))\cap L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall p\in(0,\infty),\ \forall\tau>0,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall\tau>0,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\text{ is non-increasing in }t,

\displaystyle\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad L\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\quad\forall\tau>0\}.

Moreover,

\mu\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]).

In particular, the equation is satisfied classically on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
, and
the initial condition is attained in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
.

Proof.
Let
\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
be non-negative and locally strictly positive,
in the sense that for every compact set
Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists a constant

c_{Z}>0
such that

\varsigma(x)\geq c_{Z}\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in Z.

Choose a sequence

\varsigma_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that

\varsigma_{i}>0,\qquad\varsigma_{i}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\qquad\varsigma_{i}\to\varsigma\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi),

and

\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}<\infty.

Moreover, we choose the approximation so that for every compact set

Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there exist constants
c_{Z}>0
and
i_{Z}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\varsigma_{i}(x)\geq c_{Z},\qquad\forall x\in Z,\quad\forall i\geq i_{Z}.

Such an approximation can be obtained by truncating
\varsigma
, mollifying,
and adding a small positive constant.

For each
i
, Proposition
10
gives a unique bounded positive
classical solution
\mu_{i}
of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{i}=L\mu_{i}^{\beta+1},

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(x,0)=\varsigma_{i}(x),

where

L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>.

We first prove local compactness. Let
Z\Subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
. By the local upper bound (
17
), applied to

\mu_{i}
, we have

\sup_{Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]}\mu_{i}\leq C(Z,t_{1},t_{2},\beta,V,\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),

where the constant is independent of
i
. On the other hand, by the local lower
bound in Lemma
4
, using the uniform positivity of
\varsigma_{i}
on
compact sets, there exists

c(Z,t_{1},t_{2})>0

such that

\mu_{i}(x,t)\geq c(Z,t_{1},t_{2}),\qquad\forall(x,t)\in Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}],

for all sufficiently large
i
. Thus the equation is uniformly parabolic on

Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]
, with constants independent of
i
.

Consequently, by Theorem
23
and the Schauder estimate in Theorem

24
, for every multi-index
k
there exist
\delta\in(0,1)
and a
constant
C
independent of
i
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|D^{k}\mu_{i}}}\right\|_{2+\delta,1+\delta/2;Z\times[t_{1},t_{2}]}\leq C.

By a diagonal argument, there exists a function
\mu
such that, up to a
subsequence,

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{locally smoothly in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Therefore,

\mu\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T])

and

\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1}\quad\text{classically in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Moreover, by the lower bound above,

\mu>0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Next we pass the a priori estimates to the limit. Since

\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}<\infty,

the estimates from Proposition
11
give, for every

p\in(0,\infty)
and every
\tau>0
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq C(p,\beta,\lambda,\tau,T,\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),\qquad t\in[\tau,T],

with a constant independent of
i
. Passing to the limit and applying Fatou’s
lemma gives

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall p\in(0,\infty),\quad\forall\tau>0.

Similarly, the energy estimate gives

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}),

where

\Psi(r):=r^{1+\beta}.

Passing to the limit and applying Fatou’s lemma yields

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}).

Hence

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0.

For the time derivative, Proposition
10
gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|L\Psi(\mu_{i}(t))}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{\beta t}\leq\frac{C}{t}.

Since
\mu_{i}\to\mu
locally smoothly in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]
, we have

\partial_{t}\mu_{i}\to\partial_{t}\mu\quad\text{pointwise in }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Therefore, by Fatou’s lemma,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{C}{t}

for every
t>0
. Since
\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu)
classically for positive
times, we also obtain

L\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0.

It remains to prove that
\mu
attains the initial datum in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and
that
\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
.

By Proposition
12
, for all
i,j
and all
t\in[0,T]
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu_{j}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma_{j}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

We first pass to the limit in this estimate for positive times. Fix
i
and

t>0
. Since, along the chosen subsequence,

\mu_{j}(\cdot,t)\to\mu(\cdot,t)\quad\text{a.e. in }\mathbb{R}^{d},

Fatou’s lemma gives

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mu_{i}(x,t)-\mu(x,t)|\,d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\mu_{i}(x,t)-\mu_{j}(x,t)|\,d\pi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma_{j}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Thus, for every fixed
i
and every
t>0
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

We now prove the initial trace. For every fixed
i
and every
t>0
, by the
triangle inequality and the estimate above,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

For fixed
i
, Proposition
10
gives

\mu_{i}\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\mu_{i}(0)=\varsigma_{i}.

Hence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\to 0\qquad\text{as }t\downarrow 0.

Therefore,

\limsup_{t\downarrow 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Letting
i\to\infty
and using

\varsigma_{i}\to\varsigma\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi),

we obtain

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

Thus the initial datum is attained in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
. We henceforth define

\mu(0):=\varsigma.

It remains to prove continuity at positive times. Let
t\in(0,T)
and let
h

be such that
t+h\in(0,T)
. For fixed
i
, using the estimate above at the two
positive times
t+h
and
t
, we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu_{i}(t+h)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t+h)-\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t+h)-\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

For fixed
i
, since
\mu_{i}\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t+h)-\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\to 0\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

Therefore,

\limsup_{h\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq 2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Letting
i\to\infty
, we obtain

\lim_{h\to 0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t+h)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

Together with the continuity at
t=0
proved above, this yields

\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

The
L^{1}
norm of
\mu
is non-increasing. Indeed, for any

0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
, the assumptions of Proposition
12
are satisfied
on the interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Applying Proposition
12
with

\mu_{1}=\mu
and
\mu_{2}=0
, we obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{2})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{1})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

By the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
-continuity of
\mu
at
t=0
, this inequality also extends
to
t_{1}=0
.

We now prove uniqueness. Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be two solutions in the same
class with the same initial datum
\varsigma
. For every
\tau>0
, we have

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt<\infty,\qquad i=1,2.

Hence Proposition
12
can be applied on
[\tau,T]
. Therefore, for
every
t>\tau>0
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(\tau)-\mu_{2}(\tau)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Letting
\tau\downarrow 0
and using

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
, we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(0)-\mu_{2}(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

Thus
\mu_{1}(t)=\mu_{2}(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
for every
t\in[0,T]
, and the
solution is unique.

Remark 17

The comparison principle is preserved in the passage to the whole-space
limit. Let
\varsigma_{1},\varsigma_{2}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfy

0<\varsigma_{2}\leq\varsigma_{1}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon_{2}}.

Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be the corresponding solutions constructed in
Proposition
10
. We claim that

\mu_{1}(x,t)\geq\mu_{2}(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T].

Indeed, choose the same cutoff functions for both initial data and set

\varsigma_{k,i}:=\varsigma_{k}\chi_{i},\qquad k=1,2.

Then

\varsigma_{1,i}\geq\varsigma_{2,i}\qquad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0).

Let
\mu_{k,i}
be the zero-boundary Cauchy–Dirichlet solution on

B_{R_{i}}(0)
with initial datum
\varsigma_{k,i}
.

Since
\mu_{k,i}
may vanish on the boundary, the equation is degenerate
there, and the uniformly parabolic comparison principle cannot be applied
directly to
\mu_{1,i}
and
\mu_{2,i}
. To justify the comparison, we first
approximate the zero-boundary problems by strictly positive boundary
problems. For
\delta>0
, let
\mu_{k,i}^{\delta}
solve

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu_{k,i}^{\delta}=L\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{k,i}^{\delta}}}\right)^{\beta+1},\qquad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0)\times(0,T],

\displaystyle\mu_{k,i}^{\delta}(x,0)=\varsigma_{k,i}(x)+\delta,\qquad x\in B_{R_{i}}(0),

\displaystyle\mu_{k,i}^{\delta}(x,t)=\delta,\qquad(x,t)\in\partial B_{R_{i}}(0)\times[0,T].

For each fixed
\delta>0
, these problems are uniformly parabolic, because
the solutions stay bounded above and below away from zero. Moreover,

\varsigma_{1,i}+\delta\geq\varsigma_{2,i}+\delta

and the boundary data are equal. Hence the classical comparison principle
gives

\mu_{1,i}^{\delta}\geq\mu_{2,i}^{\delta}\qquad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0)\times[0,T].

Passing to the limit
\delta\downarrow 0
, and using the construction of the
zero-boundary solutions in Proposition
6
, we obtain

\mu_{1,i}\geq\mu_{2,i}\qquad\text{in }B_{R_{i}}(0)\times[0,T].

Finally, letting
i\to\infty
in the whole-space approximation yields

\mu_{1}(x,t)\geq\mu_{2}(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,T].

The same argument applies to initial data satisfying the assumptions of
Proposition
16
. Such solutions are obtained as limits of
bounded, positive, smooth initial data, and the ordering is preserved at
each approximation level. Passing to the limit yields the comparison
principle for the limiting solutions.

2.3.4
Cauchy Problem with Non-negative Initial Data in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)

We now remove the local strict positivity assumption on the initial datum. We proved the following proposition.

Proposition 18

Assume that
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is convex, that is,

\nabla^{2}V\geq 0,

and assume that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré
inequality with constant
\lambda>0
. Then the following Cauchy problem has a
unique non-negative weak solution in the function class
\Gamma^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=L\mu^{\beta+1},\qquad L=\Delta-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla V,\nabla}}\right>,

(18)

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma(x),

where
\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and
\varsigma\geq 0
. Here
\Gamma^{\prime}
is the
class defined in Proposition
16
. The equation is satisfied in the
weak sense on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
, and the initial condition is attained in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
.

Proof.
For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\varsigma_{i}:=\varsigma+2^{-i}.

Then
\varsigma_{i}
is locally strictly positive in the sense of Proposition

16
. Moreover, since
\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=1
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=2^{-i}\pi(\mathbb{R}^{d})=2^{-i}\to 0.

Thus

\varsigma_{i}\to\varsigma\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi).

Also,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+1.

Let
\mu_{i}
be the solution corresponding to the initial datum
\varsigma_{i}
,
as constructed in Proposition
16
. Then

\mu_{i}\in\Gamma^{\prime},\qquad\mu_{i}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T]).

Since

\varsigma_{i}\geq\varsigma_{i+1},

the comparison principle in Remark
17
gives

\mu_{i}(x,t)\geq\mu_{i+1}(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Hence there exists a pointwise monotone limit

\mu_{\mathrm{pt}}(x,t):=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(x,t),\qquad(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T].

Clearly,

\mu_{\mathrm{pt}}\geq 0.

We next prove convergence in
C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
. Fix
i,j
and let

0<\tau<t\leq T
. Since
\mu_{i},\mu_{j}\in\Gamma^{\prime}
, we have

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi ds<\infty,\qquad\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{j})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi ds<\infty.

Therefore Proposition
12
can be applied on the time interval

[\tau,T]
. Hence

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu_{j}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(\tau)-\mu_{j}(\tau)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Since each
\mu_{i}
is continuous from
[0,T]
into
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and satisfies

\mu_{i}(0)=\varsigma_{i},\qquad\mu_{j}(0)=\varsigma_{j}

in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, we may let
\tau\downarrow 0
and obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu_{j}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma_{j}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)},\qquad 0<t\leq T.

For
t=0
, the same estimate is immediate:

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(0)-\mu_{j}(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma_{j}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Consequently,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu_{j}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma_{j}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Since
\varsigma_{i}\to\varsigma
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, the sequence

\{\varsigma_{i}\}
is Cauchy in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
. Therefore
\{\mu_{i}\}
is Cauchy
in the Banach space

C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Hence there exists a function

\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))

such that

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{in }C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Equivalently,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\to 0.

We now identify this
L^{1}
limit with the pointwise monotone limit. For each
fixed
t>0
, the convergence

\mu_{i}(\cdot,t)\to\mu(\cdot,t)\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)

implies that a subsequence converges to
\mu(\cdot,t)
almost everywhere.
However, the full sequence
\mu_{i}(\cdot,t)
converges pointwise monotonically
to
\mu_{\mathrm{pt}}(\cdot,t)
. Therefore the two limits agree:

\mu(\cdot,t)=\mu_{\mathrm{pt}}(\cdot,t)\quad\text{for a.e. }x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Thus, after modifying
\mu
on a set of
\pi
-measure zero if necessary, we
identify
\mu
with the pointwise monotone limit and simply write

\mu(x,t)=\lim_{i\to\infty}\mu_{i}(x,t),\qquad t>0.

Evaluating the convergence in
C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
at
t=0
, we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(0)-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(0)-\mu_{i}(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}-\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Since
\mu_{i}(0)=\varsigma_{i}
and both terms on the right-hand side tend to
zero, we obtain

\mu(0)=\varsigma\quad\text{in }L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi).

Therefore the initial condition is attained in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
.

We now pass the a priori estimates to the limit. Since

\sup_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}<\infty,

the estimates obtained in Proposition
16
give, for every

p\in(0,\infty)
and every
\tau>0
,

\sup_{t\in[\tau,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p,\beta,\lambda,\tau,T,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}}\right),

where the constant is independent of
i
. By Fatou’s lemma, for every

t\in[\tau,T]
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\liminf_{i\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Hence

\sup_{t\in[\tau,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p,\beta,\lambda,\tau,T,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}}\right).

Thus

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall p\in(0,\infty),\ \forall\tau>0.

Similarly, the energy estimates for
\mu_{i}
give

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}}\right),

where

\Psi(r):=r^{1+\beta}.

Hence, after passing to a subsequence if necessary,

\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})\rightharpoonup\kappa\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

We claim that

\kappa=\nabla\Psi(\mu)

in the sense of distributions.

Let

E_{\tau}:=(\tau,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Since

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{in }C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

we first have

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{strongly in }L^{1}(E_{\tau},dtd\pi).

Indeed,

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{1}(E_{\tau})}

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau}^{T}\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq(T-\tau)\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu(t)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\to 0.

We now upgrade this convergence to higher integrability. Fix any finite

q>1
. Choose
s>q
. By the uniform estimates, we have

\sup_{i}\|\mu_{i}\|_{L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))}<\infty.

Moreover, by Fatou’s lemma,

\mu\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

and hence

\sup_{i}\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{s}(E_{\tau})}<\infty.

Interpolating between
L^{1}(E_{\tau})
and
L^{s}(E_{\tau})
gives

\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{q}(E_{\tau})}\leq\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{1}(E_{\tau})}^{\theta}\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{s}(E_{\tau})}^{1-\theta},

where
\theta\in(0,1)
is determined by

\frac{1}{q}=\theta+\frac{1-\theta}{s}.

Since

\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{1}(E_{\tau})}\to 0

and

\sup_{i}\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{s}(E_{\tau})}<\infty,

we obtain

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(E_{\tau})

for every finite
q>1
. Together with the already proved
L^{1}
convergence,
this gives

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{strongly in }L^{q}(E_{\tau}),\qquad\forall q\in[1,\infty).

We now prove the strong convergence of the nonlinear term. Let

m:=1+\beta.

Since
m<\infty
, the previous conclusion implies

\mu_{i}\to\mu\quad\text{strongly in }L^{m}(E_{\tau}).

For non-negative numbers
a,b
, we have

|a^{m}-b^{m}|\leq m|a-b|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{m-1}+b^{m-1}}}\right).

Applying this with
a=\mu_{i}
and
b=\mu
, and then using Hölder’s inequality,
we get

\displaystyle\|\mu_{i}^{m}-\mu^{m}\|_{L^{1}(E_{\tau})}

\displaystyle\leq m\int_{E_{\tau}}|\mu_{i}-\mu|\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{i}^{m-1}+\mu^{m-1}}}\right)\,d\pi dt

\displaystyle\leq m\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mu_{i}\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}^{m-1}+\|\mu\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}^{m-1}}}\right).

The factor

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\mu_{i}\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}^{m-1}+\|\mu\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}^{m-1}}}\right)

is uniformly bounded, while

\|\mu_{i}-\mu\|_{L^{m}(E_{\tau})}\to 0.

Therefore,

\mu_{i}^{m}\to\mu^{m}\quad\text{strongly in }L^{1}(E_{\tau}).

Since
m=1+\beta
and
\Psi(r)=r^{1+\beta}
, this gives

\Psi(\mu_{i})=\mu_{i}^{1+\beta}\to\mu^{1+\beta}=\Psi(\mu)\quad\text{strongly in }L^{1}((\tau,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi).

Therefore, for every test function

\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\tau,T))
,

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi(\mu_{i})\,\partial_{x}\phi\,d\pi dt\to\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\Psi(\mu)\,\partial_{x}\phi\,d\pi dt,

while

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})\phi\,d\pi dt\to\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\kappa\phi\,d\pi dt.

Thus
\kappa=\nabla\Psi(\mu)
in the distributional sense. Consequently,

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0,

and, by weak lower semicontinuity,

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi(x)dt\leq C\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tau,T,\beta,\lambda,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}}\right).

We now verify that
\mu
satisfies the equation weakly. For every

0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
and every

\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[t_{1},t_{2}])
, the smooth solution
\mu_{i}
satisfies

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mu_{i}\,\partial_{t}\phi+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i}),\nabla\phi}}\right>}}\right]d\pi dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu_{i}(x,t)\phi(x,t)d\pi(x)\Big|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=0.

Using the strong convergence of
\mu_{i}
in
C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
and the weak
convergence of
\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})
in
L^{2}(t_{1},t_{2};L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
, we may pass
to the limit and obtain

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[-\mu\,\partial_{t}\phi+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left<\nabla\Psi(\mu),\nabla\phi}}\right>}}\right]d\pi dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)\phi(x,t)d\pi(x)\Big|_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}=0.

Thus

\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu)

in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
.

We also obtain a time-derivative estimate away from
t=0
. For each
i
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|L\Psi(\mu_{i}(t))}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma_{i}}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{\beta t}.

Hence, for every
0<\tau<T
,

\sup_{i}\sup_{t\in[\tau,T]}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\partial_{t}\mu_{i}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau}.

Equivalently,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{i}(t)-\mu_{i}(s)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau}|t-s|,\qquad s,t\in[\tau,T],

with
C
independent of
i
. Passing to the limit in

C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
, we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)-\mu(s)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\frac{C}{\tau}|t-s|,\qquad s,t\in[\tau,T].

Thus

\mu\in W^{1,\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Since the distributional equation has already been identified, we conclude that

\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu)\quad\text{in }L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0.

Equivalently,

L\Psi(\mu)\in L^{\infty}(\tau,T;L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),\qquad\forall\tau>0.

The
L^{1}
norm of
\mu
is non-increasing. Indeed, for any

0<t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T
, the conditions of Remark
13
are satisfied
on the interval
[t_{1},t_{2}]
. Applying Remark
13
with

\mu_{1}=\mu
and
\mu_{2}=0
, we obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{2})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t_{1})}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

By the
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
-continuity of
\mu
at
t=0
, this inequality also extends
to
t_{1}=0
.

Finally, we prove uniqueness. Let
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
be two solutions in the
same class with the same initial datum
\varsigma
. For every
\tau>0
, we have

\int_{\tau}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu_{i})}}\right\|^{2}d\pi dt<\infty,\qquad i=1,2.

Hence Remark
13
can be applied on
[\tau,T]
. Therefore, for
every
t>\tau>0
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(\tau)-\mu_{2}(\tau)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

Letting
\tau\downarrow 0
and using

\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

we get

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(t)-\mu_{2}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu_{1}(0)-\mu_{2}(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=0.

Thus
\mu_{1}(t)=\mu_{2}(t)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
for every
t\in[0,T]
, and the
solution is unique.

2.4
Large Time Asymptotics

We first show that the weak solution obtained above preserves its

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
mass. This improves the non-increasing property proved earlier.

Lemma 19
(Mass conservation)

Let
\mu
be a non-negative weak solution of

\partial_{t}\mu=L\Psi(\mu),\qquad\Psi(\mu)=\mu^{1+\beta},

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
such that

\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

and, for every
\tau>0
,

\nabla\Psi(\mu)\in L^{2}(\tau,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(s)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\qquad\forall\,s,t\in[0,T].

In particular, if
\mu(0)=\varsigma
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}\qquad\forall\,t\in[0,T].

Proof.
Let
\zeta_{R}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be a cutoff function such that

0\leq\zeta_{R}\leq 1,\qquad\zeta_{R}=1\text{ on }B_{R}(0),\qquad\zeta_{R}=0\text{ outside }B_{2R}(0),

and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{R}}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leq\frac{C}{R}.

Fix
0<s<t\leq T
. Using the weak formulation with the spatial test function

\zeta_{R}
and a standard approximation of the indicator function of
[s,t]
in
time, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,s)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)=-\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla\Psi(\mu),\nabla\zeta_{R}\rangle d\pi d\tau.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,s)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\Psi(\mu)}}\right\|^{2}d\pi d\tau}}\right)^{1/2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{R}}}\right\|^{2}d\pi d\tau}}\right)^{1/2}.

Since
\nabla\zeta_{R}
is supported in
B_{2R}(0)\setminus B_{R}(0)
, we have

\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{R}}}\right\|^{2}d\pi d\tau\leq\frac{C(t-s)}{R^{2}}\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{2R}(0)\setminus B_{R}(0)}}\right).

Because
\pi
is a probability measure,

\pi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{2R}(0)\setminus B_{R}(0)}}\right)\to 0\qquad\text{as }R\to\infty.

Hence

\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\nabla\zeta_{R}}}\right\|^{2}d\pi d\tau\to 0\qquad\text{as }R\to\infty.

It follows that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,s)\zeta_{R}(x)d\pi(x)}}\right|=0.

Since
\mu(\cdot,t),\mu(\cdot,s)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
and

\zeta_{R}\uparrow 1
, the monotone convergence theorem gives

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,t)d\pi(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu(x,s)d\pi(x),\qquad 0<s<t\leq T.

Finally, since
\mu\in C([0,T],L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
, letting
s\downarrow 0
yields

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(0)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

This proves the lemma.

Therefore, the solution constructed in Proposition
18
belongs to
the class
\Gamma
appearing in Theorem
1
; namely, its
L^{1}
mass is
conserved rather than merely non-increasing.

We now prove the large-time estimate.

Proposition 20

Assume that
\pi=e^{-V}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies the Poincaré inequality
with constant
\lambda>0
. Let
\mu\in\Gamma
be the non-negative weak solution
of

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mu=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta\mu^{1+\beta}-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\mu^{1+\beta}\rangle}}\right),

\displaystyle\mu(x,0)=\varsigma(x),\qquad\varsigma\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi),\quad\varsigma\geq 0.

Let

M:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\varsigma}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}.

If
M=0
, then
\mu\equiv 0
. If
M>0
, then for every

p>1
,
\beta>0
, and
p+\beta\geq 2
, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\leq\begin{cases}-\dfrac{\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}+\dfrac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}M^{\beta}t}}\right)}{\beta},&0<t<t_{0},\\[8.61108pt]
e^{-\frac{2p\lambda M^{\beta}(t-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t_{0})),&t\geq t_{0},\end{cases}

where

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t)):=\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}}{M}}}\right)\geq 0,

and

t_{0}:=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\geq 0:\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t))\leq\frac{p-1}{p}}}\right\}.

If
\varsigma\notin L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
, then we use the convention

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)=+\infty,\qquad e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}=0.

Proof.
If
M=0
, then
\varsigma=0
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
. By uniqueness,
\mu\equiv 0
.
Thus we assume
M>0
.

Define the normalized solution

\bar{\mu}(x,t):=\frac{1}{M}\mu\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,M^{-\beta}t}}\right).

Then
\bar{\mu}
satisfies

\partial_{t}\bar{\mu}=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta\bar{\mu}^{1+\beta}-\langle\nabla V,\nabla\bar{\mu}^{1+\beta}\rangle}}\right),

with initial datum

\bar{\mu}(x,0)=\frac{\varsigma(x)}{M}.

By the mass conservation lemma,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bar{\mu}(t)}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}=1,\qquad t\geq 0.

We first prove the estimate for the normalized solution. Applying Theorem

7
to the normalized Cauchy–Dirichlet approximations used in the
construction of
\bar{\mu}
, and then passing to the limit by Fatou’s lemma,
gives

K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(t))\leq\begin{cases}-\dfrac{p}{\beta(p-1)}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\frac{\beta(p-1)}{p}K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(0))}+\dfrac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}t}}\right),&0<t<\bar{t}_{0},\\[8.61108pt]
e^{-\frac{2p\lambda(t-\bar{t}_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(\bar{t}_{0})),&t\geq\bar{t}_{0},\end{cases}

where

K_{p}(f):=\frac{1}{p-1}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f^{p}\,d\pi}}\right),

and

\bar{t}_{0}:=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t\geq 0:K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(t))\leq 1}}\right\}.

The passage to the limit is justified as follows. The approximating solutions
converge to
\bar{\mu}
in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}((0,T];L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi))
and
almost everywhere along a subsequence. Hence Fatou’s lemma yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{\mu}^{p}(t)d\pi\leq\liminf_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{\mu}_{i}^{p}(t)d\pi

for every
t>0
. Thus the upper bounds for the approximations pass to the
limit. For the initial datum, the approximations may be chosen so that their

L^{p}
norms converge monotonically to the
L^{p}
norm of

\varsigma/M
, with the value
+\infty
allowed. Therefore the convention

e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}=0
is consistent when

\varsigma\notin L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
.

Now observe that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\bar{\mu}^{p}(t)d\pi=\frac{1}{M^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mu^{p}(M^{-\beta}t)d\pi.

Hence

K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(t))=\frac{1}{p-1}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\mu(M^{-\beta}t)}}\right\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)}^{p}}{M^{p}}}}\right)=\frac{p}{p-1}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu(M^{-\beta}t)}}\right).

Equivalently,

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(s))=\frac{p-1}{p}K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(M^{\beta}s)).

The condition

K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(M^{\beta}s))\leq 1

is equivalent to

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(s))\leq\frac{p-1}{p}.

Thus

\bar{t}_{0}=M^{\beta}t_{0}.

Substituting
t=M^{\beta}s
in the estimate for
K_{p}(\bar{\mu}(t))
, and then
multiplying by
(p-1)/p
, gives

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(s))\leq-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(e^{-\beta\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\varsigma)}+\frac{2\beta\lambda(p-1)}{(p+\beta)^{2}}M^{\beta}s}}\right)

for
0<s<t_{0}
, and

\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(s))\leq e^{-\frac{2p\lambda M^{\beta}(s-t_{0})}{(p+\beta)^{2}}}\mathop{\mathrm{D}_{\rm{p}}}\nolimits(\mu(t_{0}))

for
s\geq t_{0}
. Renaming
s
as
t
proves the proposition.

3
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied a weighted porous medium equation associated with a
Gibbs probability measure
\pi=e^{-V}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Under the assumptions that

V
is smooth and convex and that
\pi
satisfies a Poincaré inequality, we
proved the well-posedness of the Cauchy problem for non-negative initial data in

L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
. The solution is constructed by first solving suitable
Cauchy–Dirichlet problems on bounded domains and then passing to the
whole-space limit. The
L^{1}
contraction principle plays a central role in
establishing uniqueness, stability, and convergence of the approximating
solutions.

A key difficulty is that, unlike the classical porous medium equation, the
presence of the drift term generated by the potential
V
prevents the direct
use of standard Barenblatt-type barriers. To overcome this, we introduced a
local lower barrier for the pressure variable and combined it with an
Aronson–Bénilan type estimate. This yields local upper and lower bounds at
positive times, which are sufficient to obtain local uniform parabolicity and
hence higher regularity for locally strictly positive data.

We also established mass conservation for weak solutions and proved an

L^{1}
–
L^{p}
smoothing effect. More precisely, for every admissible

p>1
, solutions with merely
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
initial data become

L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
at every positive time. Moreover, the logarithmic ratio between
the
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
norm of the solution and its conserved
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)
mass
satisfies a two-phase decay estimate: it first decays at a super-exponential
rate and then decays exponentially to zero. This shows convergence, in the
corresponding
L^{p}
sense, toward the constant equilibrium determined by the
initial mass.

Several natural questions remain open. One possible direction is to weaken the
convexity assumption on
V
, which is used in the Aronson–Bénilan estimate.
Another direction is to investigate whether analogous smoothing and decay
estimates hold under functional inequalities weaker than the Poincaré
inequality, or for more general weighted nonlinear diffusion equations. These
questions will be addressed in future work.

\partial_{t}y=Lw

in the weak sense, namely

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\partial_{t}\varphi\,d\pi dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt

for every
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T))
.
Then

\partial_{t}y^{+}\leq Lw^{+}

in the sense of distributions. Equivalently, for every

\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)),\qquad\varphi\geq 0,

one has

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}\,\partial_{t}\varphi\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w^{+},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Proof.
Set

\chi_{y}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{y>0\}},\qquad\chi_{w}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{w>0\}}.

Since

y=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\qquad w=\Psi(\mu_{1})-\Psi(\mu_{2}),\qquad\Psi(r)=r^{1+\beta},

and since
\Psi
is strictly increasing on
[0,\infty)
, we have

\mu_{1}>\mu_{2}\iff\Psi(\mu_{1})>\Psi(\mu_{2}),

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}\iff\Psi(\mu_{1})=\Psi(\mu_{2}),

and

\mu_{1}<\mu_{2}\iff\Psi(\mu_{1})<\Psi(\mu_{2}).

Therefore

y>0\iff w>0,\qquad y=0\iff w=0,\qquad y<0\iff w<0.

Hence

\chi_{y}=\chi_{w}\qquad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

We denote this common function by

\chi:=\chi_{y}=\chi_{w}.

Since

\mu_{i}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

we have

y=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Because the map
r\mapsto r^{+}
is Lipschitz, the chain rule gives

y^{+}\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

and

\partial_{t}y^{+}=\mathbf{1}_{\{y>0\}}\partial_{t}y=\chi\,\partial_{t}y\qquad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

Similarly, since

\Psi(\mu_{i})\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

we have

w\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)).

Again by the Sobolev chain rule,

w^{+}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;H^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

and

\nabla w^{+}=\mathbf{1}_{\{w>0\}}\nabla w=\chi\nabla w\qquad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

Now let

\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)),\qquad\varphi\geq 0.

By assumption, for every

\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)),

one has

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y\,\partial_{t}\zeta\,d\pi dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w,\nabla\zeta\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Since

y\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

we may integrate by parts in time. Because
\zeta
is compactly supported
in
(0,T)
, no boundary term appears. Hence

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\zeta\,d\pi dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w,\nabla\zeta\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Let
\theta_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
satisfy

0\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}\leq 1,\qquad\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\geq 0,

\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)=0\quad\text{for }r\leq 0,\qquad\theta_{\varepsilon}(r)=1\quad\text{for }r\geq\varepsilon.

Then

\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\to\mathbf{1}_{\{w>0\}}=\chi\qquad\text{a.e. on }\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T).

We take

\zeta_{\varepsilon}:=\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi.

This function is not necessarily smooth, but it is admissible by density.
Indeed,

\zeta_{\varepsilon}\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,T;H^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\pi)),

and

\nabla\zeta_{\varepsilon}=\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(w)\varphi\nabla w+\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\nabla\varphi.

Approximating
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
by smooth compactly supported functions
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1})
, the right-hand side passes to the limit by

L^{2}
-convergence of the gradients, while the left-hand side passes to
the limit because
\partial_{t}y\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and

\zeta_{\varepsilon}
is bounded and compactly supported.

Therefore we may use
\zeta_{\varepsilon}
as a test function. We get

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi\,d\pi dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\nabla w,\nabla\bigl(\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi\bigr)}}\right\rangle d\pi dt.

Expanding the gradient gives

\nabla\bigl(\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi\bigr)=\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(w)\varphi\nabla w+\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\nabla\varphi.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi\,d\pi dt

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(w)\varphi|\nabla w|^{2}\,d\pi dt

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Since

\theta_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(w)\geq 0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\varphi\geq 0,

the first term on the right-hand side is nonpositive. Therefore

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y\,\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\varphi\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Letting
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
, we obtain by dominated convergence

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi\,\partial_{t}y\,\varphi\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Indeed, on the support of
\varphi
,

|\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\partial_{t}y\,\varphi|\leq|\partial_{t}y|\,|\varphi|,

which is integrable, and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\theta_{\varepsilon}(w)\langle\nabla w,\nabla\varphi\rangle}}\right|\leq|\nabla w|\,|\nabla\varphi|,

which is integrable because
\nabla w\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}
and

\nabla\varphi\in L^{\infty}_{c}
.

Using

\partial_{t}y^{+}=\chi\,\partial_{t}y,\qquad\nabla w^{+}=\chi\nabla w,

we get

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y^{+}\,\varphi\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w^{+},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

Finally, since
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T))
,
integration by parts in time gives

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\partial_{t}y^{+}\,\varphi\,d\pi dt=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}\,\partial_{t}\varphi\,d\pi dt.

Therefore

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}y^{+}\,\partial_{t}\varphi\,d\pi dt\leq-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\langle\nabla w^{+},\nabla\varphi\rangle\,d\pi dt.

This proves

\partial_{t}y^{+}\leq Lw^{+}

in the sense of distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(0,T)
.

Theorem 25

Suppose that

(20)
