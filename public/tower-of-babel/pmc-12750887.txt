Parathyroidectomy for secondary hyperparathyroidism in chronic kidney disease: a retrospective study of predictors of persistence and postoperative outcomes
Authors: Sanda Mrabet, Amyra Sakay, Radhouane Mani, Raja Boukadida, Sidina Emah, Nawres Thabet, Dorsaf Zellama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSecondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) is a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD), especially in the later stages. Parathyroidectomy (PTX) is recommended for patients who do not respond to medical treatment. However, in some cases, SHPT may persist due to incomplete gland resection or recur as a result of hyperplasia of remnant tissue. The aim of this study was to evaluate PTX outcomes in CKD patients treated at our center and identify predictors of persistent disease.MethodsA retrospective, descriptive and analytical study was conducted on CKD patients who underwent PTX for SHPT between January 2014 and January 2024 at Sahloul University Hospital in Tunisia. The analysis covered demographic, clinical, biochemical, radiological and surgical data. SHPT diagnosis was defined as intact parathormone (iPTH) > 585 pg/mL on at least two separate occasions, associated with hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia or normocalcemia, and resistance to ≥ 6 months of optimized medical therapy (phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, calcimimetics), in line with KDIGO guidelines. Vitamin D deficiency was corrected before confirming the diagnosis. Persistent SHPT was defined as a iPTH level over 585 pg/mL three to six months after surgery.ResultsA total of 100 patients were included. The mean age was 44.9 ± 14.1 years; 96% were in stage 5 CKD, of whom 83% were on hemodialysis, 11% on peritoneal dialysis, and 6% were pre-dialysis. Four patients (4%) had late stage 4 CKD with imaging and histological confirmation of secondary origin, excluding primary hyperparathyroidism. Bone pain was present in 26% of patients and hypoalbuminemia in 42%. Scintigraphy detected enlarged glands in 97% of cases. Subtotal PTX was performed in 82% of cases, total PTX in 17%, and total PTX with autotransplantation in 1%. Persistent SHPT occurred in 30% of patients. Mean resected gland volume was 1.8 ± 0.7 cm³ in persistent SHPT vs. 1.1 ± 0.5 cm³ in remission (p = 0.01), with a positive correlation between gland volume and postoperative iPTH levels (r = 0.41, p = 0.003). Predictors of persistence included bone pain, hypoalbuminemia, metabolic acidosis, upper right gland hyperplasia, and incomplete resection.ConclusionPTX is an effective treatment for refractory SHPT, but persistence occurs in one-third of cases. Preoperative clinical and biochemical factors may help identify patients at risk. A multidisciplinary evaluation and complete resection are crucial.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Secondary hyperparathyroidism (SHPT) is a common and serious complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD), particulary in its advanced stages. The pathophysiological cascade begins with phosphate retention, which stimulates fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) production. This, in turn, suppresses calcitriol synthesis and leads to hypocalcemia. The resulting calcium deficit triggers compensatory parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion, promoting parathyroid gland hyperplasia and progressive SHPT [1, 2].

Klotho deficiency, frequently observed in CKD, further exacerbates these disturbances by impairing phosphate excretion and calcitriol synthesis [3]. Together, these mechanisms contribute to the development of CKD–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD), which manifests clinically as bone pain, fractures, vascular calcification, and increased cardiovascular risk [4, 5].

Management of SHPT initially relies on dietary phosphate restriction, phosphate binders, adequate dialysis, vitamin D analogues, and calcimimetics [3, 6].However, a subset of patients remains refractory to medical therapy, necessitating surgical intervention. Parathyroidectomy (PTX) has demonstrated efficacy in reducing PTH levels and improving bone symptoms [7, 8], yet persistence or recurrence of SHPT may occur. These outcomes are often linked to incomplete resection, ectopic or supernumerary glands, and nodular hyperplasia [9–11].

Despite the widespread use of PTX, data on postoperative outcomes and predictors of persistence remain limited in North African populations. This study aims to evaluate the surgical outcomes of PTX for SHPT in CKD patients treated at our center and to identify clinical, biochemical, and radiological predictors of persistent disease.

Materials and methods

We conducted a retrospective, descriptive and analytical study in the Nephrology and Maxillofacial Surgery departments at Sahloul University Hospital (Tunisia), spanning a ten-year period from January 2014 to January 2024.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible patients were aged ≥ 16 years, had CKD stages 3 to 5, and were referred for PTX due to medically refractory SHPT. In line with KDIGO guidelines, SHPT was defined as an intact parathormone (iPTH) level exceeding nine times the upper limit of normal, associated with hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia or normocalcemia, and resistance to ≥ 6 months of optimized therapy (phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs, calcimimetics). Vitamin D deficiency was systematically corrected prior to diagnosis confirmation.

Definitions

Persistent SHPT was defined, in accordance with KDIGO guidelines, as a serum iPTH level of > 585 pg/mL measured between three and six months post-surgery. Recurrence was defined as an iPTH level > 585 pg/mL after an initial postoperative remission, occurring beyond 6 months post-PTX. Hungry bone syndrome (HBS) was defined as corrected serum calcium < 2.0 mmol/L within 3 days postoperatively, associated with hypophosphatemia and elevated alkaline phosphatase, requiring prolonged calcium supplementation. Corrected calcium was calculated using the formula: Corrected calcium (mmol/L) = measured calcium + 0.02 × (40 − serum albumin [g/L]).

Exclusion criteria

We excluded patients with primary or tertiary hyperparathyroidism, acute kidney injury, rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis, prior parathyroid surgery, or incomplete medical records.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation or, for variables not following a normal distribution (assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test), as median (interquartile range). Qualitative variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Paired comparisons of pre- and postoperative biochemical parameters (iPTH, calcium, phosphate) were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed variables (iPTH) and the paired t-test for normally distributed variables (calcium and phosphate).

Bivariate analysis (Chi-squared test and Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney test, as appropriate) was used to compare patients with and without persistent SHPT. Variables with p < 0.10 in univariate analysis and those considered clinically relevant were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent predictors of persistence. The model adhered to the rule of at least 10 events per variable to avoid overadjustment, and model fit and collinearity were verified.

Missing laboratory data (e.g., vitamin D status in 12% of patients) were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations to reduce bias. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study cohort included 100 patients with advanced CKD and medically refractory SHPT. The mean age was 44.9 ± 14.1 years. Most patients (96%) were in stage 5 CKD: 83% were receiving hemodialysis, 11% peritoneal dialysis, and 6% were pre-dialysis. Four patients (4%) had late stage 4 CKD (eGFR 15–20 mL/min/1.73 m² with imaging and histological confirmation of secondary origin, excluding primary hyperparathyroidism.

Comorbidities were frequent, particularly diabetes mellitus (86%) and arterial hypertension (40%). Only 3% had received a kidney transplant previously. The most common etiologies of nephropathy were chronic interstitial nephritis (38%) and undetermined origin (34%).

Preoperative symptoms included bone pain in 26% of patients and spontaneous fractures in 3%. Biochemical profiles showed markedly elevated iPTH levels (mean 1522 ± 1162 pg/mL), hyperphosphatemia in 68%, and hypocalcemia in 60%. Hypoalbuminemia was present in 42% of cases. Based on corrected calcium values, 36% of patients had hypocalcemia, compared to 54% using uncorrected calcium. Metabolic acidosis was present in 21% of patients.

Imaging findings

Cervical ultrasound was performed in 59 patients and identified enlarged parathyroid glands in 66% of cases, predominantly involving the inferior glands. Technetium-99 m sestamibi scintigraphy was performed in 96 patients and showed a higher detection rate (97%) compared to ultrasound. In seven patients, SPECT/CT was required to localize ectopic glands, most commonly in the mediastinum. The dominant site of scintigraphic uptake was the inferior left gland (74%) consistent with the anatomical distribution of hyperplasia. Imaging findings are summarized in Table 1.

Surgical procedure and pathology

Surgical findings mirrored the imaging results, with predominant hyperplasia of the inferior glands. Subtotal PTX was performed in 82% of cases, total PTX in 17%, and total PTX with autotransplantation in 1%. In subtotal PTX, at least one superior gland was routinely preserved according to surgical protocol.

The most frequently resected glands were the inferior right (84%) and inferior left (70%).

Histopathological analysis revealed simple parathyroid hyperplasia in 93 cases, nodular hyperplasia in five, and parathyroid adenoma in one. When available, gland volumes were recorded and later compared between patients with and without persistent disease (see “Predictors of Persistence”).

Postoperative biochemical evolution

On postoperative day 3, iPTH levels decreased significantly from a preoperative median of 1522 pg/mL (IQR 940–2230) to 199 pg/mL (IQR 40–550). Serum calcium declined from 2.23 ± 0.29 to 1.89 ± 0.30 mmol/L, and serum phosphate from 1.67 ± 0.45 to 1.38 ± 0.30 mmol/L. These changes were all statistically significant (p < 0.001). A summary of pre- and postoperative biochemical parameters is presented in Table 2a. HBS occurred in 26% of patients, particularly those with elevated preoperative alkaline phosphatase and longer dialysis duration.

Complications

Surgical site infection was documented in 1% of patients. Hypocalcemia due to HBS occurred in 26% of patients, with serum calcium dropping to 1.89 ± 0.30 mmol/L. Mild hyperkalemia (> 5.5 mmol/L) occurred in 9% and was managed medically. hypomagnesemia (< 0.65 mmol/L) occurred in 7% and was corrected with supplementation.The mean hospital stay was 5.3 ± 3.1 days.

Predictors of persistent and recurrent SHPT

Within 6 months post-PTX, 30 patients (30%) had persistent SHPT (iPTH > 585 pg/mL).

Recurrence was observed in three additional patients after initial remission with a median time to recurrence of 24 months (range 12–48 months).

Reintervention was required in 25 cases, primarily due to incomplete resection (50%), undetected ectopic glands (26%), and de novo hyperplasia (23%).

Discussion

SHPT remains a complex and progressive complication of CKD, particularly in patients undergoing long-term dialysis.In our cohort, biochemical profiles were consistent with advanced disease: iPTH levels exceeded 1500 pg/mL, hyperphosphatemia was present in 68% of patients, and hypocalcemia in 60%. Hypoalbuminemia affected 42% of cases likely reflecting the malnutrition–inflammation complex, which has been associated with poorer surgical response. Metabolic acidosis, present in 21% of patients, may contribute to bone resorption and stimulate PTH secretion, thereby exacerbating SHPT. n several cases, radiological signs of high-turnover bone disease — including brown tumors and spontaneous fractures — were observed, suggesting a biologically resistant phenotype often associated with nodular hyperplasia and systemic inflammation [8, 9].

Preoperative imaging played a central role in surgical planning. Cervical ultrasound demonstrated moderate sensitivity (≈ 66%) for detecting hyperfunctioning glands, while technetium-99 m sestamibi scintigraphy identified abnormal glands in 97% of cases. In 7% of patients, SPECT/CT was required to localize ectopic glands, most commonly in the thymus or mediastinum.

This anatomical distribution aligns with the predominance of inferior gland hyperplasia. In subtotal PTX, surgical protocol typically mandated preservation of at least one superior gland. Recent advances in AI-assisted imaging, including dual-phase CT and deep learning algorithms, have further improved localization accuracy, with reported sensitivities exceeding 94% [10–12].

Subtotal PTX was the preferred technique in 82% of cases. Histological analysis revealed simple hyperplasia in 93% of cases, nodular hyperplasia in 5%, and adenoma in 2% consistent with multicentre registry data [13, 14].

Postoperative outcomes were generally favourable: iPTH levels decreased to 199 pg/mL by day 3, and calcium and phosphate levels normalized in most patients. HBS occurred in 26%, particularly in those with elevated preoperative alkaline phosphatase and longer dialysis duration. HBS likely reflects more complete removal of hyperfunctioning tissue, although its long-term prognostic value remains debated [15, 16].

Three to six months postoperatively, 30% of patients exhibited persistent SHPT (iPTH > 585 pg/mL), and three additional cases relapsed after initial remission. Multivariate analysis identified six independent predictors of persistent SHPT: preoperative bone pain, serum albumin < 35 g/L, metabolic acidosis, upper right gland hyperplasia on ultrasound, limited gland resection, and absence of postoperative vitamin D therapy.

Low serum albumin and metabolic acidosis may reflect the malnutrition–inflammation complex frequently observed in end-stage renal disease. This state promotes bone resorption, impairs calcium–phosphate balance, and alters the response of parathyroid tissue to calcitriol and calcimimetics. Consequently, it may contribute to resistance to medical and surgical treatment. Optimising nutritional status, correcting acidosis, and ensuring adequate vitamin D levels preoperatively may improve postoperative calcium homeostasis and reduce the risk of persistent SHPT [17–19].

Recurrence was observed in only three patients limiting statistical power.However, shared features included: preoperative iPTH >1800 pg/mL, dialysis duration >7 years, nodular hyperplasia, imaging localisation failure, and incomplete resection. These factors are consistent with prior studies studies. The observed association with postoperative calcium levels < 1.8 mmol/L should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect early postoperative bone remineralisation rather than a direct causal mechanism. Larger prospective cohorts are needed to confirm these associations. These findings nevertheless underscore the importance of comprehensive preoperative imaging, systematic thymectomy, and, where available, intraoperative iPTH monitoring to minimise recurrence [20–22].

Reintervention was required in 25 cases. The absence of intraoperative PTH monitoring and frozen section histology in our center likely contributed to recurrence. These tools have demonstrated utility in improving surgical precision and reducing reoperation rates [23, 24].

Postoperative management, including the use of vitamin D analogues and calcium supplementation, was critical in preventing HBS and supporting bone remodeling. Patients receiving early vitamin D therapy had significantly lower recurrence rates, supporting recent evidence on endocrine modulation [25, 26].

Beyond the dialysis population, CKD–MBD is increasingly recognized as a progressive complication that begins in the early stages of chronic kidney disease, often from stage G2 onwards [27]. At these early stages, PTH levels generally remain within normal limits, while FGF 23 concentrations are already elevated, representing the earliest biochemical alteration of mineral metabolism. As kidney function declines further, additional disturbances emerge, including reduced levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, and phosphate retention. These factors collectively stimulate parathyroid gland activity before overt hypocalcemia develops [28].

Recent cohort studies have demonstrated that SHPT independently contributes to CKD progression and cardiovascular events, even after adjusting for serum calcium and phosphate levels [29]. The NEFRONA study confirmed that elevated PTH and hyperphosphatemia were associated with an increased risk of renal decline and cardiovascular morbidity, regardless of dialysis status [30]. These findings highlight the systemic impact of SHPT on processes beyond bone metabolism, including vascular calcification, myocardial fibrosis and left ventricular hypertrophy [31, 32].

In transplant recipients, persistent SHPT post-transplantation remains concern despite restored renal function. Long-standing hyperplasia and receptor downregulation may impair recovery. Surgical PTX has shown benefits in reducing PTH and improving graft outcomes, though timing remains debated [33–35].

The optimal therapeutic management of SHPT in non-dialysis CKD is still evolving. Although calcimimetics such as cinacalcet are effective in dialysis patients, concerns over hypocalcemia and a lack of long-term outcome data limit their use in earlier CKD stages [36]. Extended-release calcifediol has emerged as a promising alternative, providing gradual increases in 25(OH)D and suppression of PTH without causing significant hypercalcemia or hyperphosphatemia [37]. However, randomized trials with hard endpoints, such as fracture prevention and cardiovascular outcomes, are still needed to validate these approaches [9].

Strengths and limitations

This study presents a relatively large cohort of patients with advanced SHPT. This allows for meaningful statistical analysis and the identification of clinically relevant predictors of persistence and recurrence. Integrating clinical, biochemical, radiological, and surgical data provides a multidimensional perspective on postoperative outcomes. Using KDIGO-aligned thresholds to define persistence and applying multivariate logistic regression enhances the study’s methodological rigor.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design may introduce selection and information biases. The absence of intraoperative PTH monitoring and frozen section histology may have affected surgical precision. Additionally, access to advanced imaging modalities and vitamin D formulations was limited, which could affect generalizability to other settings. Despite these limitations, the findings are consistent with the existing literature and provide valuable insights into managing refractory SHPT.

Conclusion

PTX remains a cornerstone in the management of refractory SHPT in CKD patients. However, persistent and recurrent SHPT are common and can be predicted by a combination of clinical, biochemical, radiological, and surgical variables. A multidisciplinary evaluation, comprehensive imaging, and complete resection are crucial to improving long-term outcomes. The Integration of advanced imaging modalities, intraoperative monitoring, molecular biomarkers, and personalized postoperative care into future strategies has the potential to further optimise outcomes and reduce the burden of SHPT in dialysis and transplant populations.
