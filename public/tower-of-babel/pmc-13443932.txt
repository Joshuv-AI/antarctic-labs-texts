Identifying key diaphragm ultrasound predictors for early screening of respiratory sarcopenia
Authors: Liulei Wu, Liangliang Qiu, Siyao Gao, Congni Ye, Jie Chen, Jing Xu, Li Kang, Xiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early screening for respiratory sarcopenia lacks sensitive tools. We evaluated whether diaphragm ultrasound parameters can independently predict respiratory and overall muscle strength in healthy adults. In this cross-sectional study, 149 healthy adults underwent handgrip strength (HGS), maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP), and right (“R-”) and left (“L-”) diaphragm ultrasound. Diaphragm ultrasound included thickness at functional residual capacity (ThFRC) and total lung capacity (ThTLC), diaphragm thickening ratio (DTR), excursion (EFRC and ETLC), and dynamic indices during maximum voluntary (EVS, VVS, and ECHO-MRR). Statistical methods included spearman correlation, univariable and multivariable regression, stepwise regression, and paired comparisons. Multivariable analysis revealed that RVVS and LThTLC were associated with MIP, MEP, and HGS, right-sided parameters (RDTR, REVS, RECHO-MRR) with MIP and MEP, while RThTLC with MIP and HGS. Stepwise regression pinpointed the most predictive parameters: RVVS and LThTLC together for MIP, while RDTR alone for MEP and RThTLC alone for HGS. Bivaria regression and paired comparisons confirmed significant bilateral heterogeneity in both diaphragm parameters and their associations with muscle strength (all p < 0.05). This study establishes a precise predictive model in which RThTLC, RVVS+LThTLC, and RDTR to overall, inspiratory, and expiratory strength, respectively. Introduction

Sarcopenia, an age-related syndrome characterized by progressive loss of overall muscle mass and function, is a major determinant of frailty, disability, and mortality in older adults1,2. A distinct yet often underrecognized subtype termed respiratory sarcopenia, involves the specific deterioration of respiratory muscle mass and strength3. Early identification of respiratory sarcopenia is clinically paramount, as it serves as an independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes in various chronic cardiopulmonary conditions and aging itself2,4. However, respiratory sarcopenia lacks standardized and sensitive method for its early identification.

Current screening paradigms of respiratory sarcopenia are indirect and have limitations. Handgrip strength (HGS) is widely used to screening overall sarcopenia, which does not specifically capture respiratory muscle impairment5. While maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP and MEP) are the standard measurements of global respiratory muscle strength, they are effort-dependent and can mask early or subclinical diaphragm dysfunction due to compensatory use of accessory muscles6,7. Given that the diaphragm is responsible for majority of inspiratory work, early screening of its function is crucial for respiratory sarcopenia. However, a validated, noninvasive screening tool remains an unmet clinical need.

Diaphragm ultrasound has emerged as a practical, radiation-free, non-invasive bedside technique capable of evaluating both structure and function of respiratory muscle8,9. While conventional parameters such as thickness and excursion demonstrate established correlations with respiratory muscle strength, their predominantly static or displacement-based nature often lacks the sensitivity required to detect subtle functional decline, underscoring the necessity for advanced methodological protocols10. To address this limitation, recent advancements emphasize the critical evaluation of dynamic contractility and relaxation11. Consequently, dynamic parameters derived from the sniffing maneuver—a rapid, forceful nasal inhalation, provoking near-maximal diaphragm contraction, analogous to phrenic nerve stimulation—provide a more sensitive measurement of respiratory muscle strength8, especially the excursion velocity during maximum voluntary sniffing (VVS) and the diaphragm maximal relaxation rate obtained with ultrasound (ECHO-MRR). However, the independent predictive value of these advanced parameters for both respiratory and overall muscle strength remains underexplored.

Therefore, this study was conducted in a generally healthy cohort to: (1) investigate the associations between diaphragm ultrasound parameters (with a focus on the understudied dynamic parameters of VVS and ECHO-MRR) and key muscle strength indicators (HGS, MIP, MEP); and (2) identify the most robust independent diaphragm predictors through a sequential analytic strategy. Our findings seek to establish the utility of diaphragm ultrasound as a potential auxiliary tool for the early screening of respiratory sarcopenia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional and observational study was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University, from May 2024 to May 2025. Generally healthy volunteers aged 18–70 years were consecutively recruited. Given the healthy nature of the cohort, baseline screening relied primarily on rigorous medical history taking and comprehensive physical examinations. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of major diseases (respiratory, neuromuscular, cardiac, metabolic); (2) any reported history of neuromuscular disorders, systemic autoimmune diseases, unexplained muscle weakness, or abnormal physical examination findings; (3) prior thoracic or abdominal surgery; (4) pregnancy; (5) professional athletic status or regular high-intensity respiratory training; (6) abnormal pulmonary function (FEV1/FVC < 0.7); (7) low handgrip strength (defining sarcopenia); (8) body mass index (BMI) < 16 or > 30 kg/m2. From the original recruitment of 158 individuals, we excluded four individuals with a body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2 and five professional athletes due to the potential confounding effects on respiratory muscle assessment. The final study population therefore consisted of 149 participants. All participants provided informed written consent to this study, which was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University for Medical Research and Clinical Technology Application (Approval No.: MRCTA, ECFAH of FMU [2019]195).

Overall muscle strength measurement

Overall muscle strength was evaluated by measuring HGS with a CAMRY EH101 digital hand dynamometer (CAMRY, China)12. Following standardized verbal instruction, each participant squeezed the dynamometer with maximum effort for three seconds, and the maximal voluntary contraction force in kilograms (kg) was recorded per trial. After obtaining three measurements for each hand with one-minute rest intervals, we calculated the average value across these trials for subsequent statistical analysis.

Pulmonary function tests

Pulmonary function tests were conducted to assess ventilatory function and respiratory muscle strength in this cohort with preserved overall muscle function. Forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in the first second (FEV1), maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP), and maximal expiratory pressure (MEP) were measured using the COSMED Pony FX portable spirometry system (COSMED, Italy). All participants were seated and wore a nose clip during the testing.

For FVC and FEV1, participants performed at least five maneuvers until two acceptable efforts (variation < 10%) were achieved according to standard criteria. The best result was recorded, and both FVC and FEV1 were expressed as percentages of their predicted values (FVC%predicted, FEV1%predicted) based on sex, height, and age. The FEV1/FVC ratio was also calculated13.

For MIP and MEP, participants performed at least five maneuvers for each parameter, with a one-minute rest intervals between efforts. The highest value obtained from maneuvers demonstrating acceptable reproducibility (variation < 20%) was recorded14.

Diaphragm ultrasound imaging acquisition and analysis

To assess diaphragm morphology and function, a board-certified physician performed all diaphragm ultrasound examinations using a Resona A20S system (Mindray, China), adhering to established scanning protocols14.

Briefly, we quantify diaphragm excursion and thickness using M-mode and B-mode ultrasound imaging (Fig. 1)15–17. The following parameters were measured on each hemidiaphragm according to a scanning protocol as published previously18,19: thickness at functional residual capacity (ThFRC), thickness at total lung capacity (ThTLC), diaphragm thickening ratio (DTR; calculated as ThTLC/ThFRC), excursion at functional residual capacity (EFRC), excursion at total lung capacity (ETLC), excursion during maximum voluntary sniffing (EVS), excursion velocity during maximum voluntary sniffing (VVS), and maximal relaxation rate obtained with ultrasound (ECHO-MRR)20. Parameters from the right and left hemidiaphragms are denoted with the prefixes “R-” and “L-“, respectively (e.g., RThFRC, LThFRC).

Statistical analysis

Non-normal continuous variables are presented as median (interquartile range) and Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare these variables between sexes in the study. Spearman’s rank correlations were used to evaluate the bivariate associations among demographic characteristics (sex, age, and BMI), diaphragm ultrasound parameters (ThFRC, ThTLC, DTR, EFRC, ETLC, EVS, VVS and ECHO-MRR; analyzed separately for both right and left) and muscle strength indicators (HGS, MIP and MEP).

Univariable linear regression analyses were first performed to identify factors associated with each muscle strength indicator (HGS, MIP, and MEP). These analyses were conducted in two stages: (1) assessing the crude associations of the demographic confounders (age, sex, and BMI) with each outcome; and (2) assessing the crude associations of individual diaphragm ultrasound parameters (analyzed separately for the right and left hemidiaphragm) with each outcome. Subsequently, for each muscle strength outcome (HGS, MIP, and MEP), we constructed separate multivariable linear regression models for each diaphragm parameter that showed a significant association (p < 0.05) in the univariable analyses. Each of these models included the specific diaphragm parameter and was adjusted for only those pre-specified demographic confounders (age, sex, and BMI) that were significantly associated (p < 0.05) with the outcome in their respective univariable analyses.

Furthermore, we used stepwise regression to develop integrative models for overall muscle strength outcome of HGS and respiratory muscle strength outcomes of MIP and MEP, respectively. The goal was to identify the key independent predictors from the diaphragm parameters that were significant in the univariable analyses. The stepwise algorithm began with an empty model and iteratively added or removed predictors based on predefined and strict statistical criteria: a p-value < 0.05 for entry and a p-value > 0.10 for removal. This process continued until no more variables met the entry or removal criteria.

To identify which hemidiaphragm was a stronger predictor of muscle strength (HGS, MIP, MEP), we constructed bivariable models. For parameters where both the left and right measurements were significantly correlated with a given strength outcome in univariable analysis, the corresponding bilateral parameters were simultaneously entered into a single model. This approach allowed us to assess the independent contribution of each side and identify the dominant predictor. Given the anatomical differences between the left and right hemidiaphragms, as well as the variations in ultrasound measurement techniques for each side, we further compared paired measurements for each ultrasound parameter using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 149 participants (87 [58%] males) were included in this study. The overall cohort had a median age of 25 years (IQR, 23–31) and a median BMI of 22.4 kg/m2 (IQR, 21.2–23.4). As expected, males had significantly greater height, weight, and BMI than females (all p < 0.001, Table 1). For pulmonary function, the median FVC%predicted was 90.2% (IQR, 88.8–92.1), the median FEV1%predicted was 85.1% (IQR, 82.4–87.2), and the median FEV1/FVC ratio was 87.3% (IQR, 85.8–89.6). For overall muscle strength, the median HGS was 38.1 kg (IQR, 30.6–41.9). For respiratory muscle strength, the median MIP was 81.6 cmH₂O (IQR, 78.3–85.2), and the median MEP was 94.4 cmH₂O (IQR, 89.7-100.3).

Sex-based differences were observed in several diaphragm ultrasound parameters. Males demonstrated greater values in right side of RThFRC, RThTLC, RETLC, RVVS, and RECHO-MRR (all p < 0.05). Similarly, on the left side, LThFRC and LECHO-MRR were higher in males (all p < 0.05). In contrast, REFRC was significantly lower in males compared to females (p = 0.019). Regarding overall and respiratory muscle strength, males exhibited significantly higher HGS, MIP, and MEP, all p < 0.05) than females (p < 0.001). Associations between diaphragm ultrasound parameters and HGS

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed weak to moderate associations between HSG and several diaphragm parameters. The strongest correlation was observed with RThTLC (ρ = 0.32, p < 0.05), while other significant correlations (RThFRC, REFRC, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, LThFRC, LThTLC, LDTR) were weak (|ρ| range, 0.16–0.29, all p < 0.05) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Univariable linear regression identified seven parameters—RThFRC, RThTLC, RETLC, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, LThFRC, and LThTLC—as significant predictors of HGS (all p < 0.05). Among these, RThTLC (β = 3.17, p < 0.001) demonstrated the strongest crude associations, with explaining 14% of the variance in HGS (Table 2).

Associations between diaphragm ultrasound parameters and MIP

Spearman’s correlation analysis revealed that MIP was significantly associated with multiple diaphragm parameters. The moderate correlations were observed with RThTLC (ρ = 0.32, p < 0.05), RVVS (ρ = 0.31, p < 0.05), and RECHO-MRR(ρ = 0.32, p < 0.05); while other significant correlations (right side: RDTR, RETLC, and REVS; left side: LThFRC, LThTLC, LETLC, LVVS, and LECHO-MRR were weak (ρ range: 0.19–0.29, all p < 0.05) (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Subsequent univariable linear regression confirmed significant associations for all correlated parameters (RThTLC, RDTR, RETLC, REVS, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, LThTLC, LETLC, LVVS, and LECHO-MRR; all p < 0.05) with exception of LThFRC (p = 0.203). RVVS (β = 0.18, p < 0.001) and RECHO-MRR (β = 0.17, p < 0.001) emerged as the strongest univariable predictors, explaining 16% and 17% of the variance in MIP, respectively. Given demographic factors (age and sex, all p < 0.05) were also correlated with MIP, we proceeded to multivariable models adjusted for these confounders (Table 2).

After adjusting for the significant demographic confounders of age and sex, six parameters—RThTLC, RDTR, REVS, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, and LThTLC—remained statistically significant with MIP (all p < 0.05) (Table 3). These six parameters were then entered into a stepwise regression model, which identified RVVS and LThTLC as the most robust, independent predictors of MIP in the final, parsimonious model (all p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Associations between diaphragm ultrasound parameters and MEP

Spearman’s correlation analysis identified weak associations between MEP and several diaphragm parameters, including RThTLC, RETLC, REVS, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, and LETLC (ρ range, 0.17–0.27, all p < 0.05). RDTR was not correlated with MEP in this initial analysis (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

Univariable linear regression confirmed RThTLC, REVS, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, and LETLC as significant predictors. RDTR also emerged as a significant predictor (β = 3.37, p = 0.014, R2 = 0.04), whereas the association with RETLC (p = 0.218) was no longer statistically significant (Table 2).

After adjusting for the significant confounder of sex, multivariable models showed that RDTR, REVS, RVVS, RECHO-MRR, and LThTLC remained independently associated with MEP (all p < 0.05) (Table 3). These five variables were subsequently entered into a stepwise regression model, which identified RDTR as the sole, most robust independent predictor of MEP in the final model (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Comparison of bilateral hemidiaphragm ultrasound parameters and their associations with muscle strength

To determine which hemidiaphragm provides superior predictive information, we employed bivariable regression models that included corresponding parameters from both sides. For HGS, while bilateral thickness parameters (ThFRC and ThTLC) were correlated in Spearman’s analyses, only RThTLC remained an independent predictor in the bivariable model (β = 0.47, p < 0.001). Similarly, for MIP, several bilateral parameters were correlated (ThTLC, ETLC, VVS, and ECHO-MRR). However, the bivariable models revealed that right-sided parameters—specifically RThTLC, RVVS, and RECHO-MRR—were the dominant and independent correlates (RThTLC, β = 0.27, p = 0.017; RVVS, β = 0.39, p < 0.001; and RECHO-MRR, β = 0.38, p < 0.001), whereas their left-sided counterparts were not. In contrast, for MEP, while bilateral parameters (ThFRC, ThTLC, ETLC and ECHO-MRR) showed significance in initial univariable analyses, only RECHO-MRR remained an independent predictor in the bivariable model (β = 0.33, p < 0.001) (Table 5).

Discussion

Our study systematically evaluated both conventional and dynamic diaphragm ultrasound parameters for predicting muscle strength in healthy adults. We not only confirmed the value of established measures like thickness, but more importantly identified novel dynamic sniff-derived parameters—specifically sniffing velocity (RVVS), diaphragm thickening ratio (RDTR), and the maximal relaxation rate (RECHO-MRR)—as key independent predictors of muscle strength. Through stepwise regression modeling, we established a parsimonious predictive framework: RVVS, together with left-sided thickness (LThTLC), best predicts inspiratory strength (MIP); RDTR alone optimally predicts expiratory strength (MEP); and right-sided thickness (RThTLC) remains the strongest predictor of overall muscle strength (HGS). These findings underscore that incorporating dynamic sniff-derived measures—particularly RVVS and RDTR, with RECHO-MRR also demonstrating significant predictive value in initial models—can enhance the sensitivity of diaphragm ultrasound as a practical bedside tool for the early screening of respiratory sarcopenia, linking diaphragm function to broader overall muscle health in aging.

Building on our identification of sniff-derived dynamic parameters of excursion and velocity (EVS, VVS) and ECHO-MRR as key predictors, multivariable linear regression (adjusted for demographic confounders) results delineated their specific functional significance. Crucially, right-sided sniffing velocity (RVVS) maintained significant independent associations with MIP, MEP, and HGS, confirming its role as a broad marker of neuromuscular performance. Similarly, the independent associations of right-sided sniffing excursion (REVS) and maximal relaxation rate (RECHO-MRR) with both MIP and MEP highlight their relevance to respiratory muscle contractile properties. This clinical relevance is supported by our previous study, which demonstrated RVVS and RECHO-MRR can assessed diaphragm dysfunction and further to serve as sensitive biomarkers for respiratory involvement and disease severity in patients with facioscapulohumeral muscular dystrophy type 1 (FSHD1)16. This link between these dynamic parameters (particularly RVVS and RECHO-MRR) and respiratory pressures can be attributed to the diaphragm primary role in inspiration21. Specifically, these metrics directly reflect the diaphragm contraction capability, which depends on the integrity of phrenic nerve drive and the efficiency of fast-twitch fiber recruitment—factors that are fundamental to generating maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures22,23. The association between VVS and HGS further implies shared neuromuscular determinants, including central motor drive and fast motor unit function, may underlie rapid force production across different muscle groups24. Therefore, sniff-derived dynamic parameters are not merely correlates but effort-independent, physiologically grounded markers that may detect subclinical or early neuromuscular alterations prior to muscle weakness or atrophy.

Beyond the dynamic sniff-derived parameters, our bilateral and confounder-adjusted analysis provides refined insights into conventional diaphragm metrics. While previous studies have identified associations such as diaphragm thickness with respiratory function in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Smargiassi et al., 2014) and RDTR/RThTLC with MIP in healthy subjects (Cardenas et al., 2018)25–27, they largely relied on unilateral right-sided assessments and often lacked rigorous adjustment for confounders like age, sex, and BMI. In contrast, our study confirms and extends these observations by demonstrating that RDTR is independently associated with both MIP and MEP, and RThTLC is independently associated with both MIP and HGS. Crucially, we identify a novel and physiologically informative finding that left-sided thickness at total lung capacity (LThTLC) also maintains independent associates with MIP, MEP, and HGS. This suggests that LThTLC provides complementary structural information, potentially reflecting diaphragm muscle mass and force-generating reserve28, and may offer particular clinical value when right-sided evaluation is limited or asymmetric is suspected. In contrast, other left-sided parameters (e.g., LETLC, LEvs, LVVS, LECHO-MRR) did not show significant associations with muscle strength. Collectively, these results underscore that specific conventional parameters—particularly RThTLC, RDTR, and LThTLC—retain important and distinct roles in profiling muscle strength, thereby strengthening the overall utility of a multimodal diaphragm ultrasound assessment for early screening.

Further, the stepwise regression analysis refined our understanding by identifying the most parsimonious and clinically informative predictive models after accounting for demographic factors. For MIP, the final model retained RVVS and LThTLC, alongside age and sex. This dual-parameter framework underscores that diaphragm speed and structural reserve are complementary, fundamental determinants of inspiratory capacity. It thus provides a practical imaging-based strategy for monitoring inspiratory muscle health, potentially detecting early dysfunction—where declines in speed or thickness may precede measurable strength loss—before MIP alone becomes abnormal29,30. For MEP, RDTR emerged as the key independent predictor. This highlights that the efficiency of diaphragm thickening during inspiration is a key functional marker of expiratory strength, positioning RDTR as a targeted indicator for expiratory muscle assessment. Together, these models translate diaphragm ultrasound into a structured clinical assessment: RVVS with LThTLC for inspiratory and RDTR for expiratory. This approach offers an objective alternative where conventional, sustained volitional maneuvers (MIP or MEP) are impractical, such as in patients with cognitive impairment, facial weakness, or poor cooperation31. For HGS, RThTLC was retained as a significant predictor alongside age, sex, BMI. This independent association reinforces the concept of overall muscle aging4, suggesting that the diaphragm, due to its continuous activity and metabolic sensitivity, may serve as an accessible and effort-independent proxy for global muscle reserve32, particularly in individuals where HGS assessment is compromised (e.g., pain, neurological deficits, or cognitive impairment)33,34. Therefore, diaphragm thickness can be positioned not only as a respiratory parameter but also as a practical, objective indicator of overall muscle health, especially where conventional strength testing is limited.

Our analysis identified RThTLC as the strongest overall predictor of muscle strength. Physiologically, the diaphragm reaches its maximal active contraction at total lung capacity. Based on skeletal muscle mechanics, this maximal structural thickening reflects the muscle’s functional cross-sectional area and the extent of active fiber recruitment, which directly determine peak force-generating capacity. This premise is robustly supported by classical respiratory physiology studies. Ueki et al. demonstrated a strong positive correlation between ThTLC and MIP, highlighting this parameter as a highly sensitive functional window for assessing contractile reserve35. Furthermore, Wait et al. established a linear coupling among diaphragm thickening, lung volume changes, and inspiratory force36. Consequently, RThTLC serves not merely as a static morphological index, but as a robust functional biomarker integrating diaphragm contractile potential and neuromuscular recruitment.

Our bilateral assessment revealed that most right-sided parameters demonstrated stronger associations with muscle strength than their left-sided counterparts. For instance, RVVS correlated more robustly with inspiratory strength (MIP) than its left-sided counterpart (r = 0.31 vs. r = 0.22), and RThTLC emerged as the strongest overall predictor. This observed asymmetry is driven by both anatomical and technical factors. Anatomically, the right hemidiaphragm is generally thicker and supported by the larger right diaphragmatic crus. Technically, the liver provides a stable, homogeneous acoustic window that significantly minimizes artifacts from gastrointestinal gas. This superior image resolution increases the precision of dynamic tracking and structural measurements, whereas visualization of the left hemidiaphragm—often obscured by stomach or bowel gas despite the splenic window—is less consistent37,38. This technical disparity probably introduces greater measurement variability on the left, attenuating its true physiological associations. Notably, LThTLC constituted an exception, maintaining significant associations likely because it is measured under static conditions and is less susceptible to such motion-related artifacts. Therefore, we recommend prioritizing the right hemidiaphragm for routine screening of systemic, symmetric conditions (like age-related respiratory sarcopenia) due to its superior technical reproducibility. However, in cases of suspected focal or asymmetric pathology (e.g., unilateral trauma or phrenic nerve palsy), rigorous bilateral assessment remains mandatory, supplemented by alternative imaging modalities if left-sided ultrasound proves inconclusive.

Despite the promising predictive value of advanced dynamic parameters such as VVS and ECHO-MRR, we acknowledge that globally standardized protocols for these parameters are currently lacking in routine clinical practice. However, these parameters exhibit substantial operational feasibility. M-mode ultrasound provides the superior temporal resolution essential for accurately capturing rapid diaphragm biomechanics. Furthermore, the sniffing maneuver is a natural, reflex-like action that requires minimal patient coordination compared to traditional spirometry8. Supported by well-defined anatomical landmarks, such as the liver acoustic window on the right side, these dynamic parameters feature a relatively short operator training curve. Therefore, while future consensus guidelines are required to harmonize measurement protocols, the intrinsic operational simplicity and non-invasiveness of these parameters strongly support their potential for large-scale clinical reproducibility.

Regarding clinical translation, it is essential to consider the distinct diaphragm biomechanics in specific patient populations, particularly those with COPD. Unlike the healthy cohort in our study, COPD patients frequently exhibit chronic pulmonary hyperinflation, which flattens the diaphragm dome and alters its zone of apposition39. This geometric alteration fundamentally changes the baseline thickness and excursion-force relationship40. Consequently, future studies should focus on establishing disease-specific cut-off values and incorporating markers of hyperinflation severity, such as the residual volume to total lung capacity (RV/TLC) ratio, to calibrate the predictive model for accurate respiratory sarcopenia screening in this population41.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional and single-center design precludes the establishment of temporal causality between ultrasound morphological changes and functional decline, while also limiting generalizability due to specific local demographics. Second, regarding external validity, our cohort deliberately consisted of young asymptomatic adults to investigate foundational physiological associations independent of age-related pathological confounders. Consequently, this physiological baseline cannot be directly extrapolated to geriatric populations or patients with established chronic muscle wasting (e.g., COPD or ICU-acquired weakness); future prospective validation in these specific clinical cohorts is essential. Third, while we rigorously excluded neuromuscular diseases by comprehensive clinical evaluation, the lack of confirmatory tests, such as serum creatine phosphokinase or electromyography, means that subclinical myopathy cannot be definitively ruled out. Finally, our ultrasound assessment was confined to the diaphragm; the compensatory contributions of other key respiratory muscles (e.g., intercostal and abdominal muscles) were not evaluated and remain to be elucidated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes a novel, ultrasound-based framework for the early screening of respiratory muscle impairment. We demonstrate that dynamic sniff-derived parameters (notably RVVS and RECHO-MRR) and specific conventional measures (RThTLC, RDTR, and LThTLC) serve as objective, effort-independent biomarkers. These parameters collectively provide complementary insights into respiratory muscle function, structural reserve, and their link to overall muscle strength. This integrated approach offers a practical bedside alternative, particularly valuable when conventional volitional tests are limited, thereby advancing the toolkit for early detection and monitoring of respiratory muscle impairment in aging and at-risk populations.
