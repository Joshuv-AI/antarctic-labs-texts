Medicaid Enrollees’ Access to Contraceptive Services at Community Health Centers
Authors: Yoon Hong Park, Qian Luo, Sara Luckenbill, Risa Griffin, Julia Strasser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study examines the scope of contraception services and their rural-urban distribution for Medicaid enrollees at community health centers.

Introduction

Community health centers (CHCs) play a critical role in providing contraception care to low-income individuals in the US. Of the 31 million patients who obtain care at CHCs annually, most (90%) live at or below 200% of the Federal Poverty Level, and half (51%) are covered by Medicaid.1 While nearly all CHCs provide at least 1 contraceptive method, few offer a wide range of methods, with differences by urban vs rural location.2,3 Numerous factors contribute to the availability of contraception services at CHCs, including barriers to stocking contraceptive methods and variation in the availability of clinicians who provide contraception.4 Using a comprehensive, national Medicaid claims dataset, we studied the scope of contraception services and the rural-urban distribution of contraception clinicians for Medicaid enrollees at CHCs.

Methods

This cross-sectional study used the 2021 Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System analytical files, the most comprehensive and standardized source of Medicaid claims and enrollment data available at the national level. We used diagnosis codes, procedure codes, and National Drug Codes from the other services and pharmacy files to identify prescription contraception (pill, patch, and ring), injectable contraception (DMPA), intrauterine devices (IUDs), and contraceptive implants provided to Medicaid enrollees (eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 1). To identify services rendered through CHCs, we constructed a dataset that links individual clinicians to CHC grantees, using National Provider Identifiers and Bureau of Primary Health Care Health Center Management information system identifiers.5

We constructed our own clinician specialty classification file for health care professionals using the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System and the Medicare Provider Enrollment, Chain, and Ownership System data. Additionally, we constructed rurality data for CHCs using the 2021 uniform data system dataset merged with the zip code level rural urban commuting area data provided by the US Department of Agriculture Economic Research Service (eMethods 1-3 in Supplement 1).

We estimated the number and proportions of CHCs that provided each of 4 contraception services (prescription, DMPA, IUD, and implant) and the number of clinicians providing these methods by profession or specialty, as well as the number of Medicaid enrollees served. We conducted χ2 tests to compare the distribution of workforce profession or specialty by rurality of CHCs, with Bonferroni correction and significance set at P < .05. Further post hoc analyses with 2-tailed tests using adjusted standardized residuals were completed to compare each component (eMethods in Supplement 1). Analysis occurred from December 2024 to November 2025. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. The study was approved by the George Washington University institutional review board (NCR202330), which waived informed consent because this study uses secondary data (eMethods in Supplement 1).

Results

Our CHC National Provider Identifier list covered 1257 of the total 1271 (98.9%) CHCs in the 2021 uniform data system data. Nearly all (1188 [98.5%]) provided prescription contraception, 1132 (96.3%) provided DMPA, 832 (67.9%) provided implants, and 823 (67.1%) provided IUDs (Table 1). Out of all Medicaid enrollees who received any of the contraception services in our interest in 2021, 609 172 received contraception at a CHC overall: by contraceptive method type, between 41 086 (10.7%) and 127 991 (18.8%) did so at a CHC (Table 1).

Family medicine physicians were the most common practitioner type for IUDs (1576 [36.7%]) and implants (1853 [36.6%]), whereas nurse practitioners (NPs) were for prescription methods (10 551 [35.4%]) and DMPA (4752 [35.7%]). Results of post hoc analyses from χ2 tests showed that rural CHCs had a higher proportion of NPs and physician assistants (PAs) for all 4 method types (P < .001 for all results except NPs for IUD [P = .004] and NPs for implant, P = .002), in comparison with urban CHCs (Table 2). For implant, rural CHCs had lower proportions of obstetricians or gynecologists (92 [11.9%] vs 732 [17.1%]; P = .01) and midwives (47 [6.1%] vs 409 [9.5%]; P = .01), compared with urban CHCs. For IUD, no statistically significant difference between urban and rural CHCs was found for any of the other practitioner types.

Discussion

We found significant variation in the scope of contraception services provided to Medicaid enrollees through CHCs across the US, with approximately two-thirds of CHCs providing IUDs and implants, indicating Medicaid enrollees’ incomplete access to the full range of contraception care in CHCs. Though our study does not include services through Title X due to the nature of Medicaid claims data, this finding is consistent with previous research on the scope of contraception services at CHCs.4,6

We also found differences in workforce distribution by urban vs rural location. These findings highlight the important role of NPs and PAs in providing contraception care at CHCs, particularly in rural settings where OBGYNs and other specialists may be less accessible. Rural Medicaid enrollees face multiple barriers to care, including travel distance to care.7 Growing the rural NP and PA workforce could increase Medicaid enrollees’ access to a wider range of contraception services.

The Medicaid program is likely to undergo substantial changes in eligibility criteria and therefore in enrollment in the coming years.8 Furthermore, previous research has found that CHCs would be forced to reduce their health care workforce in response to uncertain Medicaid revenue.9 These trends would potentially worsen access to contraception among a population already facing multiple barriers to care.
