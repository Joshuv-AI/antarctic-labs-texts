LncRNA MIR210HG promotes the proliferation, migration, and invasion of lung cancer cells by inhibiting the transcription of SH3GL3
Authors: Fang Zou, Zhi‐Hua Zhang, Shuang‐Shuang Zou, Zhong‐Bao Zhuang, Qiang Ji, Rui Chang, Jia‐Huan Cao, Bu Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung cancer (LCa), the most frequent malignancy worldwide, causes millions of mortalities each year. Overexpression of the long noncoding RNA MIR210HG in LCa has been established; however, a more comprehensive investigation into its biological role within LCa is imperative. This study aimed to validate the MIR210H levels in LCa tissues and cells. The expression of indicated genes was evaluated using quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR) and/or Western blotting. The viability, proliferation, migration, and invasion of LCa cells were measured using the 3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT), colony formation, wound healing, and transwell assays, respectively. The methylation levels of LCa cells were determined via methylation‐specific PCR; additionally, chromatin immunoprecipitation or RNA immunoprecipitation assays were performed to determine the targeting relationship between DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) and the SH3‐domain containing CRB2 like 3 (SH3GL3) promoters and the interaction between DNMT1 and MIR210HG, respectively. Our findings revealed the upregulation of MIR210HG, coupled with a diminished expression of SH3GL3 in LCa tissues and cells. Knockdown of MIR210HG or overexpression of SH3GL3 suppressed the proliferative, migratory, and invasive capacities of the cells. DNMT1 bound to the SH3GL3 promoter region, and MIR210HG inhibited the transcription of SH3GL3 by recruiting DNMT1. These findings indicate that MIR210HG facilitates LCa cell growth and metastasis by repressing SH3GL3 transcription via the recruitment of DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter region.Keywords: DNMT1, LncRNA MIR210HG, lung cancer, SH3GL3

BACKGROUND

Lung cancer (LCa) is the most prevalent malignancy, claiming the lives of millions worldwide every year. 1 The development of LCa is complex, arising from intricate genetic, and environmental interplays that prompt cell malignancy and irreversible genetic shifts. These changes entail the abnormal expression of oncogenes and tumor suppressors, which disrupt vital cellular functions such as proliferation, metastasis, and apoptosis. 2 , 3 Despite emerging advances in the early diagnosis of LCa, the number of patients who can benefit from molecularly targeted therapy remains low. 4 Thus, delving into the molecular processes of LCa is essential for developing novel treatment techniques.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs; ncRNAs that are >200 nt long) are rapidly emerging as vital factors associated with multiple cellular and biological processes, including tumorigenesis and metastasis. 5 , 6 The lncRNA CASC11 was suggested to be upregulated, whereas the knockdown of this lncRNA attenuated the proliferation of LCa cells. 7 Similarly, the knockdown of the lncRNA NNT‐AS1 decreased the aggressive activity of the LCa cells and increased their apoptosis. 8 The lncRNA MIR210HG acted as an oncogenic gene across diverse malignancies, such as colorectal adenocarcinoma, 9 glioma, 10 and LCa. Bu et al. indicated elevated MIR210HG levels in LCa; the silencing of this lncRNA repressed cancer cell proliferation and metastasis and facilitated both radiosensitivity and chemoresistance. 11 Thus, investigating the potential mechanism of MIR210HG in LCa appears promising.

Previous studies have demonstrated the role of abnormal promoter methylation in tumor suppressor genes, mediated by DNA methyltransferase families like DNMT1, in promoting carcinogenesis in humans. 12 For instance, LINC00337 was suggested to accelerate the advancement of lung cancer by downregulating TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 2 (TIMP2) through the recruitment of DNMT1 to the promoter region of TIMP2. 13 Moreover, the lncRNA NEAT1 interacted with DNMT1 to regulate cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration in LCa. 14 These findings highlight the significant role of lncRNA‐mediated DNA methylation in cell progression. Data from RNA‐Protein Interaction Prediction (RPISeq) predicted the potential binding relationship between MIR210HG and DNMT1. In addition, CpG islands were identified by MethPrimer in the promoter region of SH3‐domain containing CRB2 like 3 (SH3GL3). Decreased SH3GL3 expression has been reported in glioblastoma, whereas increased expression was correlated with a worse prognosis in glioblastoma patients. 15 Lin et al. indicated that the low expression of SH3GL3 enhanced the proliferative and migratory abilities of LCa cells. 16 Based on these studies, we speculated that MIR210HG promoted the development of LCa by decreasing the levels of SH3GL3 via the recruitment of DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter.

The aim of this study was to validate the MIR210H and SH3GL3 levels in LCa tissues and cells. Mechanistically, MIR210H was found to repress SH3GL3 levels by recruiting DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter, thereby accelerating LCa progression.

METHODS

LCa tissue samples and cell lines

Human LCa samples and paracancerous tissues (n = 38) were collected from The First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University between July 2018 and October 2020 and stored at −80°C until further analysis. The Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Hebei North University authorized all the trials, and informed consent was obtained from all the patients involved in this study. Associations between the expression levels of MIR210HG and SH3GL3 and the clinicopathological features of the patients are presented in Tables 1 and 2, respectively. All the LCa cell lines, including CALU‐1, HCC827, A549, and NCI‐HI299, and the control cell line BEAS‐2B were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA) and cultured according to their recommendations.

Cell transfection and reagents

The MIR210HG or SH3GL3 fragments were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) (oe‐MIR210HG or oe‐SH3GL3). Short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeted MIR210HG, DNMT1, SH3GL3 (sh‐MIR210HG, sh‐DNMT1, and sh‐SH3GL3) or scrambled oligonucleotides (purchased from GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were inserted into the pGLVH1 vector. LCa cells were transfected with the above‐mentioned vectors at a final concentration of 2 μg/mL. The cells were treated with 5 μM of the DNA methyltransferase inhibitor 5‐aza‐2′‐deoxycytidine (5‐Aza‐dc, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 48 h, as described previously. 17 Genomic DNA (0.1 μg/μL) from the LCa cell lines was treated with M. SssI methylase (New England Biolabs, MA, USA). 18

Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR)

The total RNAs from the LCa tissues and cells were extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen), and the RNA quality was assessed using NanoDrop2000c (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, USA).

After reverse‐transcription into cDNA using the Prime‐Script Mix Kit (Takara, Tokyo, Japan), qRT‐PCR was performed with the SYBR Green qPCR Kit (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA). GAPDH served as an internal control. The following primer sets were used in this study: GAPDH, F: CCAGGTGGTCTCCTCTGA, R: GCTGTAGCCAAATCGTTGT; MIR210HG, F: GCTTTGTGATTCAGGGTGGT, R: TGCAGACATGGAGGAGACAG; SH3GL3, F: CTGCAAGGAGGACGAGTACC, R: CATTTGGCACTGCAGACACT; DNMT1, F: AGATCAAGCTCGCGACTCTC, R: GGCTTTCTGAACGAGTCCTG. The gene expression was presented as fold change relative to the expression levels in the controls using the 2−ΔΔCt method. 19

Western blot

After isolating the proteins using RIPA buffer (Invitrogen), 50 g of proteins from each group were subjected to purification using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gels. The purified proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes, followed by the blocking of non‐specific sites using 5% skimmed milk. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibodies SH3GL3 (1:5000, ab184008, Abcam) and DNMT1 (1:1000, ab19905, Abcam) overnight at 4°C, and the corresponding HRP‐conjugated secondary antibody was used to probe the related primary antibodies. The antibody‐reactive bands were measured with an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Millipore, Bedford, MA, USA), and GAPDH was used as the internal control.

3‐(4,5‐Dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay

Cells were seeded into 96‐well plates and grown overnight. MTT reagent was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 4 h at 37°C. The absorbance was detected at 490 nm using an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay reader (BioRad, Richmond, CA, USA).

Colony formation assay

Approximately 5 × 103 cells were seeded onto six‐well plates and placed in an incubator at 37°C until the appearance of colonies. The colonies were fixed in methanol after washing with PBS. Crystal violet (0.1%, Sigma) was used to stain the colonies, followed by washing with running water. Finally, the number of colonies was counted using a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Wound healing assay

Cells were seeded into six‐well plates and grown to confluence. The cell layer was scratched with a 10 μL sterile tip, and the wound width was determined 48 h after scratching using a phase‐contrast microscope. The migration abilities of the LCa cells were analyzed by evaluating the area occupied within the wound.

Transwell assay

The invasion abilities of LCa cells were examined using a transwell assay kit (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA). 20 Serum‐free culture medium containing 2 × 105 cells was added into the top chamber (with Matrigel), and medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum was added as a chemoattractant to the bottom chamber. After 24 h of incubation, the cells on the upper surface of the Matrigel were removed, stained with crystal violet, and counted under a microscope.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and immunofluorescence (IF)

The FISH assay was conducted to localize MIR210HG, and the combination of FISH and IF experiments was used to analyze the colocalization and interactions of MIR210HG and DNMT1 in A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells. Briefly, cells grown on the slides were washed with PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. After dehydration, cells were incubated with fluorescence‐conjugated probes (RiboBio) in a hybridization buffer at 37°C overnight. Subsequently, the cells were counterstained with 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) for 10 min, and images were observed under an immunofluorescence microscope (Nikon).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

A Magna RIP™ Kit (Millipore) was utilized for the RIP. Harvested cells were lysed in the RIP lysis buffer, and the lysate was incubated with magnetic beads conjugated with immunoglobulin G antibody (anti‐IgG), anti‐DNMT antibody, and anti‐EZH2 antibody. The coprecipitated RNAs were extracted, enriched, and measured using qRT‐PCR. 21 Additionally, the total RNAs (input control) were detected.

MS‐PCR

DNA isolation (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and methylation (Zymo, CA, USA) kits were utilized to isolate the genomic DNA and modified methylated residues, respectively. PCR analysis of the bisulfite‐modified genomic DNA, which triggers the chemical conversion of unmethylated but not methylated cytosine to uracil, was used to determine the DNA methylation status.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay (ChIP)

A Magna ChIP Kit (Millipore) was used to determine whether the SH3GL3 promoter could bind to DNMT1. After 10 mins of fixation in 1% formaldehyde to obtain the protein‐DNA complexes, the chromatin fragments were extracted from the sonicated cell lysates. The chromatin supernatants were subjected to immunoprecipitation with rabbit anti‐DNMT1 or normal rabbit IgG antibodies, and the precipitated DNA was purified and analyzed by PCR using specific primers.

Statistical analysis

All data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis of the results was performed using the Student's t‐test (for two groups) or one‐way analysis of variance (for multiple groups). A p‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

MIR210H was elevated in LCa

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data (UALCAN: http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) was used to predict the MIR210HG levels in LCa. The results revealed a higher expression of MIR210HG in LCa samples than in control samples (Figure 1A). The MIR210HG levels were significantly increased in LCa tissues and cell lines (CALU‐1, HCC827, A549, and NCI‐H1299) compared to those in the control tissue samples and BEAS‐2B cells (Figure 1B,C). As shown in Figure 1D, patients with high MIR210HG expression levels exhibited a worse prognosis. The invasion of A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells was markedly higher than that of the CALU‐1 and HCC827 cells (Figure 1E); therefore, the A549, and NCI‐H1299 cells were selected for subsequent experiments.

MIR210HG was upregulated in LCa tissues and cells. (A) MIR210HG levels in LCa and control samples were obtained from TCGA. (B and C) Relative levels of MIR210HG in LCa and normal tissues and the LCa cell lines (CALU‐1, HCC827, A549, and NCI‐H1299). BEAS‐2B cells were used as control. (D) The correlation between MIR210HG expression and survival in LCa patients. (E) The invasiveness of LCa cells was measured using transwell assays. Experimental data are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). N = 3. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001.

Knockdown of MIR210HG inhibited LCa progression in vitro

The biological function of MIR210 HG was evaluated by in vitro silencing experiments. A529 and NCI‐H1299 cells were transfected with different MIR210 HG shRNAs and the level of MIR210 HG was detected. It was found that after transfection of sh‐MIR210HG‐3 plasmid, the expression of MIR210HG was down‐regulated most significantly (Figure 2A), and sh‐MIR210HG‐3 was used for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, MIR210H knockdown significantly reduced the cell viability (Figure 2B), number of colonies (Figure 2C), wounded area (Figure 2D), and invasiveness (Figure 2E).

Knockdown of MIR210HG inhibited LCa progression in vitro. (A) Analysis of the knockdown efficiencies of different MIR210HG shRNA. (B) The activities of A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells were analyzed using the MTT assay. (C) Cell proliferation was measured using the colony formation assay. (D) Wound healing assay was used to detect the cell migration capability. (E) The invasive ability of LCa cells was measured using the transwell assay. Experimental data are shown as mean ± SD. N = 3. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

SH3GL3 was downregulated in LCa tissues and cells

The results from the TCGA‐Pancreatic Adenocarcinoma (TCGA‐PRAD) data suggested low expression levels of SH3GL3 in the LCa samples (Figure 3A). Additional qRT‐PCR examinations confirmed the downregulation of SH3GL3 in the LCa cells (Figure 3B). Moreover, MIR210H expression was negatively correlated with SH3GL3 expression (Figure 3C), and the SH3GL3 mRNA levels were remarkably downregulated in LCa cells relative to those in the BEAS‐2B cells (Figure 3D). This finding was confirmed by the decrease in the protein expression of SH3GL3 in the LCa cells (Figure 3F). Taken together, these results indicated that SH3GL3 was poorly expressed in LCa and exhibited a negative correlation with MIR210H.

(D and E) The relative mRNA and protein levels of SH3GL3 in the LCa cell lines. The experimental data are shown as the mean ± SD. N = 3. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Overexpression of SH3GL3 suppressed LCa cell growth and metastasis in vitro

We examined the transfection efficiency of oe‐SH3GL3 and sh‐SH3GL3 in LCa cell lines to determine the influence of SH3GL3 overexpression on LCa cell growth. SH3GL3 expression was dramatically increased in the oe‐SH3GL3 group, whereas transfection with sh‐SH3GL3 decreased SH3GL3 expression (Figure 4A). SH3GL3 overexpression significantly suppressed the cell viability (Figure 4B), colony numbers (Figure 4C), migration ability (Figure 4D), and invasiveness (Figure 4E) in vitro; conversely, SH3GL3 knockdown promoted cancer cell viability and metastasis (Figure 4B–E). These findings indicated the inhibitory effect of SH3GL3 on LCa cell growth and metastasis.

Overexpression of SH3GL3 suppressed LCa cell growth and metastasis in vitro. (A) Measurements of the transfection efficiencies of oe‐SH3GL3 and sh‐SH3GL3. (C–E) Analysis of the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LCa cells in response to transfection with oe‐NC, oe‐SH3GL3, sh‐NC, or sh‐SH3GL3. The experimental data are shown as the mean ± SD. MIR210HG inhibited the transcription of SH3GL3 by recruiting DNMT1

The localization of MIR210HG was determined to investigate whether there was an interaction between MIR210HG and SH3GL3 in LCa tumorigenesis. MIR210HG mainly resided in the nucleus of the A549 and NCI‐H1299 cells (Figure 5A) and was overexpressed by transfection with oe‐MIR210HG. The most apparent upregulation of MIR210HG was obtained after transfection with 2 μg/mL of oe‐MIR210HG (Figure 5B). SH3GL3 was markedly reduced in the oe‐MIR210HG group compared to the control and oe‐NC groups (Figure 5C). Methylation was observed in the oe‐MIR210HG and M. Sssl (methylase) treatment groups, whereas treatment with the DNMT1 inhibitor 5‐aza‐dc or knockdown of DNMT1 led to decreased methylation levels of the SH3GL3 promoter (Figure 5D). In addition, a CpG island was identified around the SH3GL3 promoter region using the CpG island search software MethPrimer (Figure 5E). The results of the RIP assay indicated interactions between MIR210HG and DNMT1 (Figure 5F). MIR210HG overexpression increased the binding of DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter region, whereas no binding of IgG to the SH3GL3 promoter was observed after MIR210HG overexpression (Figure 5G). Additionally, colocalization between MIR210HG and DNMT1 was noted in the LCa cells (Figure 5H). In summary, MIR210HG repressed SH3GL3 expression by recruiting DNMT1 to the promoter of SH3GL3.

MIR210HG facilitated LCa progression by downregulating SH3GL3

Rescue experiments were designed and carried out to assess whether the growth of LCa cells was controlled by the interaction between MIR210HG and SH3GL3. We first observed that knockdown of MIR210HG accelerated the expression of SH3GL3, while transfection of sh‐SH3GL3 reversed this effect (Figure 6A). The knockdown of MIR210HG repressed the growth and metastasis of LCa cells, whereas these inhibitory effects were blocked after the simultaneous knockdown of MIR210HG and SH3GL3 (Figure 6B–E). Thus, MIR210HG contributed to the proliferative and metastatic abilities of LCa cells by inhibiting SH3GL3 expression.

DISCUSSION

The risk of relapse and metastasis is heightened due to the low early diagnosis rate of LCa, resulting in a poor prognosis. Thus, it is imperative to conduct comprehensive research and develop relevant early diagnostic and prognostic molecular markers for effective clinical application. 22 Thousands of lncRNAs were demonstrated to be abnormally mutated or dysregulated in various cancers, 23 indicating that lncRNAs play an important role in several tumorigenesis processes, including carcinogenesis, progression, and metastasis. 24 , 25 , 26 MIR210HG was reported to act as an oncogene in several tumors, including LCa. Kang et al. suggested that elevated levels of MIR210HG were positively associated with lower disease‐free survival and overall survival in non‐small cell lung cancer. 27 In the present study, MIR210HG was overexpressed in LCa, indicating a poor prognosis in the patients. Furthermore, MIR210HG silencing inhibited the LCa cell aggressive phenotypes. These data suggested that MIR210HG might function as an oncogene in LCa carcinogenesis.

The SRC domain is a 50‐amino‐acid domain that plays a vital role in signal transduction and the initiation and development of many diseases. 28 , 29 SH3GL3 was identified as a risk factor for Huntington's disease in 1998. 30 In recent years, it has been demonstrated to have an antitumor effect in several malignancies, such as multiple myeloma 31 and glioblastoma. 15 These studies underscored the pivotal role of SH3GL3 on tumor cell proliferation and migration. In the current study, the expression of SH3GL3 was low in LCa. Consistent with the findings of a previous study, 16 the overexpression of SH3GL3 suppressed the proliferation, migration, and invasion of LCa cells, indicating an inhibitory effect of SH3GL3 on LCa cell growth and metastasis. Furthermore, CpG islands were detected in the promoter region of SH3GL3; this discovery holds promise for uncovering the mechanisms by which SH3GL3 contributes to cancer suppression.

DNMT1, identified by Gold and Hurwit in 1964, 32 plays a key role in establishing and maintaining DNA methylation patterns. 33 , 34 A previous study suggested that gene transcription was inhibited through the hypermethylation of CpG islands. 35 Moreover, the function of DNMT1 has been widely characterized; it facilitates the proliferation and differentiation of tumor cells and inhibits the expression of tumor suppressor genes, thereby accelerating tumor progression, 36 as well as in LCa. 37 Furthermore, recent studies have emphasized that lncRNA interacts with DNMT1 to induce DNA methylation and promote tumor cell progression. For instance, lncRNA NEAT1 interacted with DNMT1 to regulate cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration in LCa by repressing cGAS/STING. 14 In the current study, MIR210HG promoted the binding of DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter region. Firstly, through RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP), we demonstrated the binding sites between MIR210HG and DNMT1. Secondly, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays revealed that overexpression of MIR210HG led to an increased association between DNMT1 and the SH3GL3 promoter region. Moreover, we supplemented our findings with RNA‐FISH combined with IF, which indicated the co‐localization of MIR210HG and DNMT1 within the cells. Thus, DNMT1 was identified as a mediating factor that promotes the reduction of SH3GL3 expression by MIR210HG. Furthermore, MIR210HG silencing decreased the proliferative and metastatic abilities of LCa cells, and these effects were reversed by transfection with sh‐SH3GL3. Overall, these findings demonstrated for the first time that MIR210HG served as an oncogene in LCa carcinogenesis by inhibiting SH3GL3 levels via interactions with DNMT1. Although this study demonstrated the oncogenic effect of MIR210HG in vitro, additional in vivo studies are being planned to validate the findings.

CONCLUSION

The overexpression of MIR210H was linked to the pathogenesis of LCa via the promotion of the proliferation, migration, and invasion of the cells, which was mediated by the recruitment of DNMT1 to the SH3GL3 promoter region. These observations provided potential implications for LCa therapy.
