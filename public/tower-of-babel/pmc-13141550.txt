Navigating fatherhood and identity reconstruction among fathers with nasopharyngeal carcinoma: A qualitative study
Authors: Xiaohui Su, Ziqi Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is highly prevalent in East Asia among working-age men, and chemoradiotherapy causes severe side effects and late effects threatening cancer survivors' quality of life and parenting practices. This study aims to examine the reconstruction of fatherhood and paternal identity among male NPC survivors.MethodsThis qualitative narrative inquiry design, with in-depth narrative interviews conducted with 20 middle-aged Chinese fathers with NPC. These participants are recruited through snowball strategies and are located in a regional cancer center in southern China. Thematic analysis was applied for data interpretation.ResultsThe findings demonstrate that embodied disruption from treatment undermines the traditional breadwinner role. This crisis is amplified by three major themes: (1) embodied disruption and identity continuity, (2) silent institutions: an amplifier of the cancer-related life disruption, and (3) reconstructing fatherhood: from breadwinner to emotional companion.ConclusionsFathers’ experiences reflected an institutionally magnified disruption, in which treatment-related impairment was intensified by gaps in social and institutional support, producing a broader crisis of livelihood and identity. In response, fatherhood was reconstructed not as a free choice, but as a constrained adaptation under structural and gendered pressures. These findings extend biographical disruption theory in the Chinese context and highlight the need for improved sick-leave protection and family-oriented psychosocial care for male cancer survivors.Keywords: Fathers, Male, Cancer survivors, China, Parenting, Nasopharyngeal carcinoma

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) is a regionally concentrated malignancy with a particularly high burden in southern China and other parts of East and Southeast Asia.1,2 NPC treatment, particularly chemoradiotherapy, frequently leads to severe, long-term side effects such as xerostomia, dysphagia, and hearing loss.3 These treatment-related impairments can contribute to ongoing uncertainty regarding future functioning, work capacity, social acceptance, and the ability to fulfill family roles, while also increasing stigma, anxiety, and disruption to health-related quality of life.4,5 However, these consequences are often framed primarily as biomedical outcomes, with less attention to the structural and social conditions that shape how they are experienced in everyday life.6 This omission is particularly important in NPC, which has a high incidence peak among men aged 45–59,7,8 a life stage often characterized by intensive work and family responsibilities, making illness-related disruptions especially consequential for family and role functioning.9

In the Chinese context, fatherhood is closely related to ideals of masculinity, emphasizing financial provision, professional success, and physical strength.10 For fathers with NPC, this contextual shaping is particularly salient because treatment-related functional and communication difficulties intersect with a life stage of intensive work and family responsibilities, while insurance reimbursement arrangements, employment protections during treatment, and uneven access to psychosocial support may substantially condition how disruption is experienced and managed.11

While existing sociological research acknowledges the subversive effects of chronic illness on everyday life, critics point out that these theories tend to be individual-centered, overlooking how the illness experience is conditioned by social structures.12 However, the evidence remains limited in three respects. First, much of this literature is not specific to NPC and therefore provides insufficient attention to how NPC-related sequelae, particularly those affecting eating and communication, shape uncertainty and everyday role performance. Second, studies of men's cancer experiences have often emphasized individual coping or role loss, with less empirical attention to the dynamic reconstruction of fatherhood practices in family life over time. Third, in the Chinese context, there is relatively limited process-oriented research that links institutional conditions to the trajectories through which illness disruption is amplified or mitigated in men's fatherhood identities.13, 14, 15 Specifically, the limited medical insurance coverage, strict labor policies, and absent psychosocial support collectively constitute a significant social vulnerability.16,17 These gaps motivate the present study.

Therefore, this study aims to systematically trace the dynamic process of illness disruption and identity reconstruction experienced by Chinese fathers with NPC, with particular attention to how treatment-related impairments, culturally salient expectations of fatherhood, and institutional conditions interact over time. We examine how institutional arrangements may intensify or constrain illness-related uncertainty and role disruption, and how fathers negotiate changing responsibilities and meanings of fatherhood in everyday family life.

In this article, we use the term “institutionally magnified disruption” as an analytic formulation to describe situations in which institutional arrangements intensify illness-related disruption across livelihood, family roles, and self-understanding, particularly when access to reimbursement, employment protections, and psychosocial support is constrained.

Fatherhood and illness

In the Chinese context, fatherhood practices are further refined into dimensions such as interactive supervision, emotional expression, and academic support.18 However, paternal practice is closely intertwined with cultural notions of masculinity.19 Traditional male identity is often constructed around occupational achievement, financial provision, and physical strength.20,21

Despite the contemporary advocacy of the “New Father”, emphasizing emotional engagement and active caregiving, tensions between traditional and modern ideals remain substantial.19 Fathers often encountered a “double burden”, as they strive to maintain professional excellence as breadwinners while simultaneously dedicating time and emotional engagement to child-rearing.22 This conflict is particularly pronounced in China, where societal expectations for male career achievement often clash with the demands of parental responsibilities, especially in children's educational support (i.e., “non-absent fatherhood”, the role of a father who is physically and emotionally present and actively involved in his children's lives).23

Biographical disruption

The Biographical Disruption Theory (BDT) is a foundational sociological framework for understanding how chronic illness reshapes individual experience. It interprets how disease undermines patients' taken-for-granted structures of everyday life and forms of knowledge.12 This threat to the integrity of the life story compels patients to engage in profound identity reconstruction.

Despite its widespread application, a central debate concerns the role of social context in mediating this disruption.24 Critics argue that the theory was overly individual-centered, insufficiently addressing how illness experiences are shaped by broader social structure.25 For middle-aged fathers, for instance, disruptive impact cannot be separated from their socio-economic responsibilities and family roles.11,26 Consequently, contemporary applications of the theory emphasize situating biographical disruption within its specific sociocultural contexts.27,28

Structural vulnerability

While biographical disruption highlights individual experience, the severity and manifestation are largely predetermined by one's position within the social structure.24,29 The SVF posits that individual health outcomes and disease burden stem from institutional arrangements shaped by socio-economic, legal, political, and policy regimes.29,30 Vulnerability, therefore, is not the result of personal deficit or choices, but an institutional consequence produced through social inequality and power relations.31

In this study, the structural vulnerability perspective is used to examine how sociocultural and institutional conditions shape the experience and consequences of illness beyond individual-level adjustment alone. Specifically, it analyzes how specific institutional factors in China, such as health care, social assistance, and labor policies, directly create or amplify the hardships faced by fathers with NPC. Integrating the SVF with BDT provides an analytical framework for examining how institutional conditions may intensify illness-related disruption and shape the multilayered experiences of fathers negotiating illness under structural constraints in the Chinese context.

Methods

Study design

This study used a qualitative design informed by narrative inquiry as a methodological orientation to guide the interview design and interpretation of temporality, while the interview transcripts of fathers diagnosed with NPC were analyzed using inductive thematic analysis. The research was conducted at a specialized cancer hospital in Southern China, which serves as a major treatment center for NPC patients in the region. The first author conducted fieldwork at the hospital in the capacity of a research assistant. This study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) to ensure the reporting quality.

This study was situated within an interpretive qualitative paradigm. Narrative inquiry informed the design of the interviews because the study sought to understand how fathers narrated illness, work, family roles, and identity across time, with particular attention to temporality and biographical sequence.32 Narrative inquiry is particularly suitable for examining lived experience because it explores how people make sense of events through stories that connect past, present, and future. However, because our analytic aim was not to reconstruct each participant's full life story but to identify recurring patterns across participants, we used inductive thematic analysis as the primary analytic method. Thematic analysis was appropriate because it offers a flexible and systematic approach for identifying patterned meanings across a dataset, including narrative interview material.33 This combination allowed us to retain sensitivity to temporality and sequence while also generating cross-case themes relevant to illness-related life disruption.

Sampling

Participants were recruited from December 2024 to March 2025 through clinician-facilitated identification and snowball sampling. The first author accompanied the attending physician on rounds twice a week to identify eligible male patients who had been diagnosed with NPC before the age of 45, were married, and had at least one child under the age of 15 at the time of diagnosis. We focused on fathers of children under 15 because this life stage is more likely to involve active caregiving, close daily involvement, and substantial financial responsibility. The age criterion referred to participants' age at the time of diagnosis rather than at the time of interview. Potential participants were first informed about the study either by the attending physician during ward rounds or by a member of a patient support organization in collaboration with the supervising physician. The first author approached patients only after this initial introduction and only if they had expressed interest in receiving further information. For those who agreed, contact details were shared with the first author, who then explained the study in detail via WeChat and arranged the interview. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that refusal would not affect their treatment. Eligible participants were then invited to take part in an interview about their experiences of NPC survivorship and fatherhood practices.

Data collection

Narrative interviews were conducted for data collection from March to May 2025. The data collection procedure consisted of three flexible parts to balance open narration and topic comparability across interviews. Each interview began with an open-ended question: “Can you tell me what your life was like when you first noticed symptoms of nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and how it continued from there? Please take your time and explain how one thing led to another.” This question prompted uninterrupted, mostly chronological narratives. After participants completed their narratives, specific topics mentioned during the interview were explored in greater detail. The second part of the interview was guided by an interview protocol designed to capture the subjective experiences of fathers negotiating fatherhood after an NPC diagnosis. The protocol was developed based on a literature review, covering topics on parenting expectations, fatherhood imaginations, work arrangements, and handling parenting tasks. The third part explored how fathers with NPC reconstructed their sense of self and rediscovered the meaning in life.

The interview duration ranged from 60 to 120 minutes. All interviews were conducted by two PhD-level researchers (one male and one female, both without medical backgrounds) in mixed settings, including face-to-face and online, depending on participants’ availability and health conditions. Both forms of interview were conducted one-on-one using the same interview guide. Both forms of interview are conducted one-on-one. The face-to-face interviews were conducted in a private meeting room located away from the ward area, and the online interviews utilized the Tencent meeting, which required a password. All interview data in Mandarin were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim, imported into NVivo for data management and coding, and the participants' identities were pseudonymized to protect their confidentiality. The transcripts were cross-checked for accuracy by the first author. Each participant was invited to review their transcript and remove any sections they did not wish to include for research purposes. Participants were informed that the interviews were not a form of counseling or therapy, but an opportunity to reflect on their experience of fatherhood and role adaptation after cancer diagnosis.

Data analysis

Thematic analysis was conducted collaboratively by the first and second authors. Narrative inquiry informed the interpretation of temporality and sequence in participants’ accounts, but the analytic method used was thematic analysis. The analysis employed strategies of researcher triangulation to ensure the trustworthiness of the findings. First, the transcripts reviewed by participants were independently analyzed by two authors to identify an initial coding framework that reflected recurring experiences and central concepts related to fatherhood. Second, two authors collaborate on a systematic comparison and contrast of the participants' narratives to refine the analytical framework for a consensus.34 Finally, the data interpretation synthesizes the emerging themes of this framework and aligns with the principle of data saturation in developing broader themes, such as “Embodied Disruption and Identity Continuity”. Data saturation was achieved by 15 interviews, and 5 additional interviews were conducted to ensure all relevant themes were adequately represented. This inductive process aimed to capture the complexity of how cancer reshapes fatherhood and to situate participants' narratives within their broader sociocultural context for interpretation.35 Additionally, participant quotes were translated from Chinese to English by the first and second authors. The first author conducted the English translation, and the second author reviewed the translated quotations independently with the original Chinese transcript. All translations were verified through back-translation. All discrepancies were discussed through comparison til consesus, the presented text preserved both semantic accuracy and contextual meaning.

Trustworthiness was enhanced through researcher triangulation, participant transcript checking, and detailed documentation of coding and theme development, with attention to credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. Specifically, two interviewers were PhD-level qualitative researchers without clinical responsibility for participants, which helped reduce role confusion during recruitment and interviewing. The first author's fieldwork position in the hospital facilitated access but also required continuous reflection on how institutional proximity might shape participants' responses. The second author is both a researcher and a cancer survivor, with sensitivity to the nuanced experiences of different participants. These reflexivities are informed by medical sociology, structural context, and personal cancer survivorship experience, providing multiple perspectives throughout the investigation. It could shape iterative coding and interpretation of these qualitative findings. This study acknowledges these reflexivities in coding and strengthens the thematic framework through iterative consensus.

Results

A total of 20 men were recruited in this study. All participants had children under the age of 15 at the time of diagnosis. This developmental stage is when children typically require significant financial and caregiving involvement from parents. The recruited participants' ages at diagnosis ranged from 30 to 40 years old. Sixty percent of them were between 36 and 40 years old, most of these fathers were married, representing a transitional period between young adulthood and middle age. Additionally, these fathers are raising one or more children under the age of 15, indicating that they also face multiple social and family responsibilities. More than half of the participants had been living with NPC for over 3 years. Five (25%) of them had been diagnosed more than 10 years ago. Embodied disruption and identity continuity

The unpredictable body

Radiotherapy, as an essential treatment for NPC, often causes severe and lasting side effects, including xerostomia (severe dry mouth), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), hearing loss, and skin fibrosis. For these fathers, these symptoms are not merely medical terms but concrete impediments to their parenting.

I have also lost all my natural teeth and now have a full set of dentures. Since my dentures are fitted as a complete set, they sometimes become dislodged when I speak passionately, which is truly embarrassing (20250326HSX, diagnosed at age 38, 1 child).

I perceived a significant loss of social value due to the illness, resulting in deep sadness that is difficult to articulate. It felt as if the world around me had turned gray (20250321XYL, diagnosed at age 36, 2 children).

I am currently unable to drive, which may have psychological effects on my child. Other children's fathers can drive and are often perceived as successful and attractive, which may contribute to my child's sense of difference or inadequacy (20250317HW, diagnosed at age 37, 1 child).

When fathers lose control of their bodies, they realize they can no longer embody the previous ideal of fatherhood. Yet this disruption does not erase paternal identity altogether. Rather, continuity is preserved through their persistent efforts to remain responsible, maintain decision-making agency, and re-establish everyday order under altered bodily conditions. In this sense, illness interrupts the embodied form of fatherhood, but not fathers’ commitment to being a father.

The collapse of daily routine: from controllable to disordered

Cancer is not merely a medical event but a profound disruption of the lifeworld. In response, the fathers we interviewed sought to reimpose order through highly instrumental planning after their diagnosis. Their strategy was a way to navigate the chaotic and unfamiliar “world of illness”. By quickly constructing a predictable order within the new domain, they attempted to counter the loss of control stemming from the interruption of everyday roles. This problem-solving orientation functioned as an everyday practice to sustain the pre-illness masculine role as “the one in control”, even with this entirely new context.

At first, I had been thinking it was a benign meningioma, so when it was finally confirmed to be malignant, I felt both tense and deeply disappointed. I did not know much about nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and in the north, where I was living, I had hardly encountered anyone with this disease before. Everything suddenly became uncertain (20250318WG, diagnosed at age 37, 1 child).

Economic incapacity

Suspending work for cancer treatment is not only an economic loss for these fathers. It represents the collapse of the daily routines that defined their role as the family breadwinner. This collapse directly undermines the paternal identity. As hospital visits or home convalescence replace the core routine of going to work, they experience a profound sense of uselessness, feeling an inability to perform fatherhood through familiar daily actions.

My wife is the only earner in the whole family, but she has to take care of me, so the family is practically without financial income. And then the thought that if I don't make it through this, the whole family will be paralyzed makes me feel very anxious (20250324FXJ, diagnosed at age 29, 1 child).

The pain and struggle experienced by fathers with NPC are not merely individual physiological or psychological problems. They constitute a lived condition shaped by the intertwining of biological suffering and structural vulnerability. Structural vulnerability refers to systemic gaps in social structures designed to provide support during a crisis in one's biological life, including health insurance, labor policies, and psychosocial care.

Our findings reveal a clear dynamic. The diseased body first undermines fathers’ capacity to fulfill everyday responsibilities. Institutions' failure, including insufficient insurance coverage, stringent workplace norms, and absent psychosocial support, fails to provide an adequate safety network. This gap magnifies a biological crisis into a comprehensive crisis of livelihood and identity.

Silent institutions: an amplifier of the cancer-related life disruption

As a structural mechanism, “silent institutions” intensify individual suffering by transforming bodily impairment into prolonged livelihood insecurity and pressure on paternal identity through sick-leave regulations, insurance design, and the lack of psychosocial care.

Restricted sick leave rights

The financial toxicity of cancer, intensified by limited sick-leave entitlements and income loss, directly threatens the participants' breadwinner identity. As income models built over time working collapsed in treatment began.

At that time, I still had one month’s salary that had not been paid, but I wanted to return to work after completing my treatment, so I did not pursue the outstanding wages (20250326HZX, diagnosed at age 42,1 child).

Consequently, to avoid jeopardizing re-employment, they chose not to pursue unpaid wages. This forced prioritization of financial survival over health during sick leave. This choice weakened their bargaining power, pushing fathers to entrench pre-existing labor–management power asymmetries.

I also rely heavily on overtime for my income, and it's hard to go and support the family if I'm only getting a base salary. If I take a month off, either my parents or my loved ones will have to cover these expenses, which is stressful for me and for them (20250327XH, diagnosed at age 36, 2 children).

I returned to work one month after completing treatment. Although my company granted me sick leave, the pay during leave would have been very low (20250322CCQ, diagnosed at age 36, 2 children).

Limitations of health insurance

Although China's health insurance system provides basic protection for cancer survivors, significant coverage gaps in the long-term survivorship phase exacerbate financial strain. This transforms recovery into a state of ongoing economic vulnerability.

I am about to face the expiration of my “special outpatient coverage,” (a specific medical insurance scheme designed for cancer patients in China). Once it expires, I will have to pay all follow-up examination costs out of pocket (20250327XH, diagnosed at age 38, 2 children).

Insurance benefit design favors active treatment over surveillance, as special outpatient benefits are time-limited and require renewal. Once coverage lapses, combined with deductibles, coinsurance, and reimbursement caps, follow-up services quickly become substantial out-of-pocket expenses. Income instability during the early return-to-work phase and constrained household cash flow further magnify trade-offs between health care and livelihood, creating a compounded effect of institutional barriers and financial pressure.

The absence of psychosocial care

Participants commonly reported a lack of formal psychosocial support within the health system, which compelled them to manage substantial emotional distress on their own or to rely on fragile informal support networks.

During treatment, I went through many things psychologically. The hardest part was that there was basically no one I could really talk to. No one was checking in on me emotionally, and even when I called home, there was very little that could be said in terms of comfort or support (20250322CCQ, diagnosed at age 36,2 children).

I had heard from other patients that the hospital next door had a psycho-oncology clinic, and I had thought about going for a consultation, but in the end I never went (20250326SKG, diagnosed at age 36,1 child).

The health care system's disregard for men's mental health reinforces traditional Chinese expectations that fathers should bear pain in silence. This legitimizes and entrenches traditional masculinity within the medical system.

Under the combined pressure of bodily vulnerability and institutional constraints, fathers were compelled to move from a public, traditional breadwinner role to a private, companion role centered on emotion. This reconstruction of fatherhood is not a simple or emancipatory personal choice. Rather, it is a resilient adaptation undertaken to maintain the continuity of paternal identity, together with a contingent reorientation shaped by biological and structural limits.

Reconstructing fatherhood: from breadwinner to emotional companion

From breadwinner to companion: establishing new parenting practices

One father reported that after treatment, he shifted from coming home and merely lying down with his phone to cherishing bedtime companionship with his child. This change exemplifies a broader shift in the practice of fatherhood, moving from the economic to the emotional sphere. When illness interrupted his grand narrative as a breadwinner, he began to construct new paternal meanings within the small segments of daily life. As the future becomes uncertain and the body is no longer reliable, investing resources in a high-quality present becomes the most feasible and meaningful way to enact fatherhood.

Before I became ill, I spent very little time with my children because I was almost always at work. After the illness, however, I had much more time to be with them. At the very least, I could be with them every day and have meals together (20250321XYL, diagnosed at age 36, 2 children).

Similarly, another father, who daily cares for his newborn, including bathing and changing clothes, is no longer a simple household chore. In the midst of bodily illness and pain, it becomes a new everyday practice through which he reaffirms his value as a father by means of repetitive, embodied intimate contact. These practices are no longer about the accumulation of future wealth but about emotional connection and the creation of memories in each present moment. This marks a shift from a dominant model of fatherhood to a caring one.

One month after I returned home following radiotherapy, my daughter was born. Her arrival was a tremendous source of support for me and brought immense joy. Many people report significant suffering and loss of appetite after treatment, but in retrospect, my experience was relatively manageable, likely because my attention was diverted. After her birth, I was constantly occupied with bathing her, changing her clothes, and playing with her (20250327JOHN, diagnosed at age 32, 2 children).

The meaning of cancer on parenting: “gift” and future planning

The participant came to regard the illness as a “gift,” which drew his attention to many sources of happiness and signaled a fundamental shift in his evaluation of the meaning of everyday life. When the “major disruption” of illness forced him off the “fast track” of pursuing career success, he gained an opportunity to pause and reexamine the ordinary. He began to find joy and value in daily interactions that had previously been overlooked. This was not merely a change in mindset but a profound process of meaning reconstruction grounded in a renewed discovery and appreciation of everyday life itself.

Before my illness, I was often extremely anxious, sometimes due to work and other times because of concerns over my child's academic performance. After becoming ill, however, I began to notice many sources of happiness. It all depends on how you interpret your circumstances. Illness can feel like a gift from above, allowing you to perceive these meaningful and joyful aspects of life (20250326HSX, diagnosed at age 39, 1 child).

Confronting life's finitude and economic precarity, the fathers redefined the concept of legacy. Property was reframed as love and a means of securing future stability. In this view, a father's legacy becomes the foundation for the child's future.

My bottom line is that I will not go into debt or sell my house, even if it means spending all my income on medical treatment. As a member of the “first urban generation” in my family, the first to settle in the city after leaving the countryside. I do not want my child to return to where I started. If I am forced to sell my house, it will be even harder for him to regain his footing in the future. I cannot afford to lose both my health and my assets. At first, I told no one, but after the recurrence, everyone found out, and I felt as though I had become a symbol of tragedy in the eyes of others (20250326LZF, diagnosed at age 34, 1 child).

Under the premise of a possible future absence of parenting, the fathers see property and money as a commitment in their role as breadwinners. Although such arrangements carry profound emotional meaning, the coexistence of illness risk and gaps in institutional support compels individuals to build their own safety nets against uncertainty.

Discussion

Main findings

This study explores the complex existential challenges faced by middle-aged fathers diagnosed with NPC. The analysis suggests that this experience is not just a personal tragedy or psychological adaptation, but a dynamic process of identity negotiation co-constructed by the biological impact of the disease and the structural deficiencies of the social system. Building on this, we first elaborate on how the illness precipitates a profound biographical disruption.12 We subsequently argue that this disruption is exacerbated by the specific social policy environment in China, consequently leading to an institutionally amplified vulnerability.30 Given China's non-universal health care system, medical expenses are only partially covered by health insurance. Nevertheless, for major illnesses, including cancer, individuals still bear a heavy financial burden for medical expenses, even after insurance reimbursement.36 This structural financial pressure, combined with the effects of the disease, constitutes the dual challenge fathers face in reconstructing their paternal roles. The research analyzes the specific practices of reconstructing fatherhood under this dual pressure, framing the analysis in dialogue with theories on caring masculinity and the return to everyday life framework for cancer and chronic illness patients.37, 38, 39 Finally, we summarize the theoretical contributions, practical implications directions for future research.

The first theme reveals how NPC and its aggressive treatment transform the father's body from a reliable instrument into an ontological barrier to the enactment of fatherhood. Radiation therapy side effects, such as dry mouth, tooth loss, and extreme fatigue, are not merely clinical symptoms but constitute a fundamental erosion of the father's embodiment. The concept of biographical disruption offers a classic paradigm for understanding and asserting that chronic illness shatters individuals' taken-for-granted assumptions about life, compelling a fundamental reassessment of identity and relationships.12 For these cancer-stricken fathers, the disruption extends beyond their life trajectory to the corporal foundation of their paternal role.38,40 The body ceases to be a transparent medium, becoming a “problem” that demands continuous attention and fine-tuned management.41 Loss of physical function directly dismantles ordinary, embodied paternal practices.42 This physical disintegration poses a direct ontological challenge to masculinity, which is fundamentally predicated on physical strength and functional capacity.43,44 Hegemonic masculinity emphasizes strength, competitiveness, and emotional restraint as key elements of male authority and familial status.45 Fathers' reactions, such as emphasizing masculine rationality and control, can be interpreted as an attempt to compensate for feelings of powerlessness and sustain a threatened gender identity.46 This behavior aligns with the effect of Stoicism in male cancer survivors, which is characterized by perceived less social support and optimism,47 and a difficulty expressing emotions for fear of appearing weak or “unmanly”.48, 49, 50 Thus, the bodily disintegration is not merely physiological but constitutes a dual crisis of gender identity.

The second theme suggests inadequate social institutions deliver a second, more structurally influential, strike, exacerbating the plight of the survivor and their family.51,52 The fathers' vulnerability stems not only from the illness but from a social environment that fails to provide sufficient support. This aligned with the SVF, which posits that social structure predetermines an individual's health outcomes and experiences of suffering.30 Participants' anxieties regarding expiring medical insurance, lack of sick leave wages, and absent psychological support are vivid manifestations of this vulnerability. This is not personal misfortune but institutionally produced suffering.30 Indeed, research indicates that Chinese cancer survivors report rarely receiving psychological support at the community level and experience significant economic pressure due to insufficient treatment costs and insurance.53,54 Amending these structural barriers requires system-level intervention and resource allocation,55 rather than reducing the problem to individual biomedical symptoms.56 The institutional gap, failing to provide even “a small room for pouring out one's heart”, constitutes a systemic neglect of survivors' psychosocial needs. Although cancer distress screening is considered a component of quality oncology care, requiring all survivors to be screened and referred, implementation is often constrained by systemic barriers related to personnel and processes.54,57 It reinforces gender norms of masculine ideals, including self-reliance and the breadwinner role, which inhibit men from seeking help and lead them to view expressing vulnerability as “unmanly”.58 Simultaneously, financial pressure that caused feelings of failure and anxiety declined in family quality of life.51 Consequently, social structure is complicit in reinforcing a masculine ideal that places immense stress on these fathers.

Under the dual pressure of bodily disintegration and institutional failure, fathers do not yield passively. Instead, they demonstrate significant agency by profoundly reconstructing fatherhood, an experience echoed in other studies.59 Suggesting fathers with cancer examine their paternal role through interaction with their children and self-reflection. This reconstruction embodies Giddens' concept of the “reflexive project of the self”.60 Facing life's finiteness, fathers are forced to reflect on their priorities, shifting their focus from a work-centered, future-oriented trajectory to a family-centered, present-oriented one. This transition is not a simple substitution but a strategic adaptation implemented under extreme constraints. It is significant to acknowledge that masculinity is not a fixed construct, but a flexible social practice shaped by context.61 This adaptability is referred to as the “chameleonisation” of masculinity,62 enabling men to modify their gender scripts to protect self-image confronted with challenges such as illness or caregiving roles.63 The increased childcare time is less a free choice than an expression of pragmatic resilience.59,64 This reconstruction process echoes the rise of caring masculinities, which challenge traditional masculine ideals of emotional detachment and competitive success, instead advocating care, affection, and interdependence as core elements of masculinity.37 Consequently, Chinese fathers must negotiate and reconcile their masculine roles with caregiving responsibilities in their narratives, emphasizing the identity of multitasking and the pursuit of ensuring family well-being.10 This transformation is fraught with tension. It is often accompanied by guilt over the inability to perfectly fulfill traditional roles, constituting a paradoxical reconstruction. It is simultaneously a transcendence of traditional masculinity and an unavoidable response to structural limitations. One study captured this intense role threat, describing the dilemma as moving “out of the frying pan into the fire”.44

Integrating the three themes, our findings suggest not only that illness disrupts biography, but that disruption becomes intensified when bodily decline intersects with weak institutional protection. In this sense, the study extends Bury's concept of biographical disruption by showing that, for fathers with NPC, disruption is not produced by illness alone; rather, it is co-shaped by the interaction between embodied impairment, masculine role expectations, and structurally insufficient support.65,66 Building on SVF, our analysis further shows that social vulnerability does not merely worsen outcomes after disruption occurs, but actively shapes how disruption is experienced, interpreted, and negotiated in everyday paternal life.12 We therefore conceptualize the fathers' experience as an institutionally magnified disruption: a form of biographical disruption in which the erosion of bodily capacity is intensified by gaps in insurance coverage, rigid work arrangements, and absent psychosocial care. This formulation moves beyond treating BDT as an individual illness trajectory and instead repositions disruption as a culturally and structurally mediated process.67

The findings broadly supported the conceptual framework presented in Fig. 1, while also refining it in two ways. First, the data showed that institutional conditions did not merely form a background context but actively intensified disruption by translating bodily impairment into prolonged insecurity around work, finance, and paternal identity. Second, the reconstruction of fatherhood did not appear as a linear outcome, but as an ongoing and oscillating process, with fathers moving between continuity, loss, adaptation, and reorientation over time.

This study makes significant contributions to both theory and practice. First, by applying the biographical disruption to the cohort of fathers with NPC, we uncovered the origins of their psychological distress in the dynamic interaction between the physical collapse and the dismantling of social roles. This concept demonstrates the fathers' entire trajectory, from their encounter with disease and struggle against institutional flaws to the attempted reconstruction of fatherhood. Our analysis contributes to research on male cancer survivors' active efforts to rewrite illness narratives in resistance to clinical reductionism and gender normative constraints.68

On the one hand, this study demonstrates how masculinity mediates individuals' interpretation of bodily change. Fathers with NPC are not only confronting a medical condition but also engaged in a struggle against the cultural expectation of being male. Failure to fulfill the breadwinner role, they are compelled to reintegrate a sick body into paternal identity. This adjustment is a form of micro-resistance against dominant masculine norms. Our findings align with existing research showing that gender perspectives shape pathways of biographical repair,69, 70, 71, 72 indicating that sociocultural backgrounds profoundly influence the interpretation of bodily changes and their capacity for reflective adaptation.27,73

This study demonstrates a non-linear, continuously oscillating adaptation process.27 Recent studies suggest that biographical repair is not a stable return to normality, but a non-linear and fluctuating process.27,74,75 Fathers therefore continuously renegotiate their narratives in response to ongoing instability and future uncertainty.69,76 Our findings suggest that fathers neither simply return to a pre-illness “normality” nor arrive at a stable “new normal”.68 Instead, they negotiate a new identity by shifting the focus of fatherhood from material provision to emotional companionship, aligning with the emphasis on caring masculinities in relationships and interdependence. However, this reconstruction frequently generates new challenges, causing the fathers to oscillate between hope and frustration, control and powerlessness. Therefore, adapting to instability itself becomes central to their biographical repair.27,28,68 Furthermore, these cases highlight the disruption of the anticipated future. Facing the possibility of early mortality, fathers with cancer must rework their current narratives to accommodate and prepare for future uncertainties.75

Implications for nursing practice and research

These findings suggest several concrete actions for oncology nurses. During routine assessment, nurses can ask fathers brief role-oriented questions, such as whether treatment has affected their work, income, or ability to care for their children. When fathers express concern about explaining illness-related bodily changes to children, nurses can provide brief, age-appropriate communication guidance or refer families to psycho-oncology or social work services. During discharge planning or follow-up consultations, nurses can identify fathers at risk of financial and role-related distress, such as those returning to work early or facing follow-up costs, and provide information on available insurance, hospital social support, or community resources. Where possible, nurses may also invite spouses into short educational conversations to help redistribute caregiving and communication responsibilities within the family.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited during ward rounds by the first author, which may have introduced recruitment bias. Fathers who were more communicative, more engaged with care, or more willing to disclose personal experiences may have been more likely to participate, while those with greater distress, heavier treatment burden, or stronger reluctance to discuss family role strain may have remained underrepresented. Second, although this study focused on fathers as a conceptually relevant group, the sample does not allow for full comparisons across life stages, limiting transferability to younger fathers, older fathers, or grandfathers. In particular, if the sample did not adequately capture the full range of men within the 45–59 age group, an established peak-incidence group for NPC, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of those at the epidemiological center of disease burden. Third, the sample was predominantly urban, which may underrepresent fathers from rural or socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds, who are likely to face more severe constraints in medical access, employment protection, and out-of-pocket treatment costs. Finally, because the study was cross-sectional, it could not fully capture longer-term turning points, reversals, or fluctuations in paternal identity reconstruction over time. Longitudinal research would be valuable for tracing how coping strategies and fatherhood trajectories continue to evolve after treatment.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that middle-aged Chinese fathers with NPC-telling illness experience is not only a personal medical event but also a profound social crisis. We found that the embodied disruption from treatment interacts with silent institutions, including inadequate health care, labor, and social policies. It is to create what we have termed “institutionally magnified disruption.” This mechanism transforms a biomedical shock into a comprehensive crisis of livelihood and identity. Faced with this dual pressure, fathers are compelled to reconstruct their paternal roles, shifting from a “breadwinner” to an “emotional companion.” Crucially, our findings show this is not an emancipatory choice but a constrained adaptation, forged at the intersection of structural pressures and entrenched gendered expectations. Theoretically, this research extends the concept of biographical disruption by foregrounding the critical and often damaging interaction between institutions and masculine identity in a Chinese context. These findings underscore an urgent need to move beyond individual support and reform the structural conditions, such as sick-leave standards and the provision of family-oriented psychosocial care, that fundamentally shape the cancer experience.
