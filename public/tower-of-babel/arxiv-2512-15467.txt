Abstract
. Replacing operators with continuous operator-valued functions, we prove time-dependent versions of well-known results on compressions and diagonals of bounded operators. The setting of smooth functions is also addressed. Our results have no analogues in the literature and rely on a new technique. The results are especially transparent for selfadjoint operators.

1.
Introduction

To introduce the objects of our study,
let
L(H)
be the space of bounded linear operators on a separable Hilbert space
H,
and
A\in L(H).

If
(e_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
is an orthonormal basis in
H,1\leq N\leq\infty,
then
A
admits a matrix
representation
M_{A}=(\langle Ae_{i},e_{j}\rangle)_{i,j=1}^{N}.

If
N<\infty
then
M_{A}
is a finite matrix, and, given
A,
it is
a central problem of matrix theory to
find
M_{A}
most suitable for revealing the structure of
A

and convenient for dealing with specific tasks depending
on
M_{A}.

While this topic is classical, there are still many problems in understanding
M_{A}

e.g. the existence of prescribed zero patterns in
M_{A}
,
awaiting their complete solutions.
For an infinite-dimensional space
H
the relations between
A
and
M_{A}

become even more involved and depend on advanced methods and techniques
stemming from various domains of analysis.
As shown in
[
36
]
,
the structure of
M_{A}
can be rather arbitrary if one is allowed to exclude
elements of
M_{A}
corresponding to thin subsets of
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}.

On the other hand,
M_{A}
may have a very particular form,
and for example, (block) tridiagonal and pentadiagonal forms
are standard tools in the study of selfadjoint and unitary operators,
respectively. As another, striking instance, recall that
a large class of bounded selfadjoint operators on
H
having a Hankel matrix
with respect to some basis can be characterized using spectral terms,
as shown in
[
30
]
.

In the study of
M_{A}
the main diagonal plays a distinguished role,
as it carries a lot of information about
A.

However, extracting of this information is often very challenging
if
A
is far from being compact.
To facilitate such a study one may consider
the whole set of diagonals
\mathcal{D}(A):=\{\langle Ae_{n},e_{n}\rangle_{n=1}^{\infty}\}\subset\ell_{\infty}(\mathbb{N}).

While the study of diagonals has received a substantial attention last years,
especially for selfadjoint and normal
A,

the structure of
\mathcal{D}(A)
and its interplay with
A

are still far from being understood.
First, note that the study of diagonals can proceed
as either the study of
\mathcal{D}(A)
for a class of operators
A
,
or as the study of
\mathcal{D}(A)
for a fixed operator
A.

Most of the relevant research has been concentrated on the first, easier problem,
but there have been several papers addressing the second problem as well.
Since the entries constituting diagonals of
A
belong to the numerical range
W(A)

of
A
, the numerical range appears
to be very natural tool for describing, at least, a part of
\mathcal{D}(A)
.
Moreover, the essential numerical range
W_{e}(A)
of
A,

consisting of the limits of elements in
\mathcal{D}(A),

whenever they exist, looks like an appropriate substitute or
useful addition.
Both
W(A)
and
W_{e}(A)
will be basic in our considerations.

Until Kadison’s papers
[
23
,
24
]
, the research on
\mathcal{D}(A)

was rather sporadic. To note several landmark results preceding
[
23
,
24
]
,
recall that in a deep article by Stout
[
39
]
the set

\mathcal{D}(A)
appeared in a natural way in the study of Schur algebras.
Stout related
\mathcal{D}(A)

to
W_{e}(A),
and proved that
0\in W_{e}(A)

implies that for any
(\alpha_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\not\in\ell_{1}
there is
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
|d_{n}|\leq|\alpha_{n}|
for all
n,

thus clarifying fine structure of
\mathcal{D}(A).

The existence of zero diagonals in
\mathcal{D}(A)

for arbitrary
A\in L(H)
was studied by P. Fan in
[
15
]

He proved that
A
admits a zero diagonal
if and only if there exists an orthonormal basis
(e_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset H

such that
\sum_{k=1}^{n_{m}}\langle Ae_{k},e_{k}\rangle\to 0
as
m\to\infty

for a subsequence
(n_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}.

Clearly, the same statement holds with any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
in place of
0
, thereby describing constant diagonals.
The paper
[
15
]
inspired a closer look at the nature of
\mathcal{D}(A),

and Herrero showed in
[
21
]
that if
(d_{n})^{\infty}_{n=1}
belongs to the interior
{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
of
W_{e}(A)

and
(d_{n})^{\infty}_{n=1}
has a limit point again in
{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
, then
(d_{n})^{\infty}_{n=1}\in\mathcal{D}(A)
.
However, there is, in general, a certain arbitrariness if one restricts to the study of diagonals for class
of operators. For instance, as shown in
[
18
]
, any bounded sequence can be realized as a diagonal
of some nilpotent operator (of order
2
).

In
[
6
]
, Bourin put the results of Herrero into a general context of operator-valued
diagonals. It was proved in
[
6
]
that if
W_{e}(A)
contains the open unit disc, then for every

(D_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset L(H)

satisfying

\sup_{n\geq 1}\|D_{n}\|<1
,
there exist mutually orthogonal subspaces
M_{n}\subset H
such that

\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}M_{n}=H
and, denoting by
P_{M_{n}}
the orthogonal projection onto
M_{n},

the operator
P_{M_{n}}A\upharpoonright_{M_{n}}
is unitarily equivalent to
D_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.

The direct sums
\bigoplus_{n\geq 1}P_{M_{n}}A\upharpoonright_{M_{n}},
called “pinchings” of
A
in
[
6
]
, can be considered as operator counterparts of elements from
\mathcal{D}(A),
and they essentially coincide with those elements when
D_{n}
act on one-dimensional spaces. A complementary discussion and related results can be found in
[
7
]
.
A close, but weaker statement was obtained much earlier in
[
5
, Theorem 4.8]
.
See also
[
1
, p. 440]
or
[
14
, Lemma 2.5]
.

A different perspective has been opened since
Kadison’s striking results on diagonals of selfadjoint projections.
Given tuples of reals
d=\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
\lambda=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}

in non-increasing order, Schur and Horn proved that there is a Hermitian matrix with diagonal values
\{d_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
if and only if
\lambda
majorizes
d
, in the sense that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
for
k=1,\dots,n-1
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}.

Looking for similar results in
infinite dimensions,
Kadison discovered in
[
23
,
24
]

that a sequence
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is
a diagonal of some
selfadjoint
projection if and only if it takes values in
[0,1]
and
the sums
a:=\sum_{d_{n}<1/2}d_{n}
and
b:=\sum_{d_{n}\geq 1/2}(1-d_{n})
satisfy either
a+b=\infty
or
a+b<\infty
and
a-b\in\mathbb{Z}.

This is in sharp contrast with a result by A. Neumann
[
37
]
describing

l^{\infty}
-closure of diagonals of a diagonalizable selfadjoint operator as the closure
of all permutations of the sequence of its eigenvalues. If the kernel and range of
a projection
P
are infinite-dimensional, then one only gets
\overline{\mathcal{D}(P)}^{\|\cdot\|_{\infty}}=[0,1]^{\bf N}.

The Kadison integer condition was recognized as the Fredholm index obstruction in the subsequent paper by Arveson
[
3
]
,
where Kadison’s dichotomy was studied for normal operators with finite spectrum.
Later on, the nature of the integer was identified and clarified by Kaftal and Loreaux
in
[
26
]

and in the subsequent paper

[
28
]
,
as a so-called essential codimension of a pair of projections.

Developing their ideas in various directions, Arveson and Kadison
recast in
[
2
]
generalizations of the Schur-Horn theorem
in the setting of operator algebras, where matrix algebras and diagonals are replaced, respectively, by finite factors and maximal Abelian self-adjoint subalgebras (MASAs),
and the Schur-Horn majorisation
is generalised properly.
They gave also rise to an intensive activity around
the structure of
\mathcal{D}(A)
for classes of

A\in L(H).

Without mentioning all of the substantial contributions,
we give only a few samples related to this discussion.
Pairs of null real sequences realized as sequences of eigenvalues and diagonals of positive compact operators with a trivial kernel were characterized in
[
25
]
.
A description of diagonals for selfadjoint operators
A
with finite spectrum was given in
[
9
]
.
For a class of unitary operators
A,
the set
\mathcal{D}(A)
was characterized in
[
22
]
.
Very recently, an (almost) complete description of diagonals of arbitrary bounded
selfadjoint operators was obtained in the important papers
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
.

The main bottleneck in the study of diagonals described above
was a lack of techniques taking into account presence of continuous spectrum.
Thus, even the description of elements of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{M})
for the toy multiplication operator

(Mf)(t)=tf(t),

f\in L^{2}([0,1]),
was out of reach.
To remedy this problem, a new approach to the study the diagonals of bounded operators
T

(and their tuples

\mathcal{A}=\langle A_{1},\dots,A_{k}\rangle\in B(H^{k})
)
was proposed in
[
34
]
.
Arguing in terms of geometry of numerical ranges,

[
34
]
introduced
a

Blaschke-type
condition

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\rm dist}\,(d_{n},\mathbb{C}\setminus W_{e}(A))=\infty

on the size of

(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)

near the boundary of

W_{e}(A)

and offered, in particular, the following two results
given in Theorem
1.1
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
A\in L(H).

(i)

If
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
satisfies

(1.1)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\rm dist}\,(d_{n},\mathbb{C}\setminus W_{e}(A))=\infty,

then
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathcal{D}(A).

(ii)

Let
W_{e}(A)
contain the open unit disc.
Let
H_{n},n\in\mathbb{N},
be separable Hilbert spaces (finite or infinite-dimensional)
and
D_{n}\in L(H_{n})
be strict contractions, i.e.,
\|D_{n}\|<1

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.
If

(1.2)

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-\|D_{n}\|)=\infty,

then there is
a sequence
(M_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
mutually orthogonal subspaces of
H
such that

\bigoplus_{n=1}^{\infty}M_{n}=H
and
P_{M_{n}}A\upharpoonright_{M_{n}}

is unitarily equivalent to
D_{n}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}.

(The statement in (ii) was formulated in
[
34
]
somewhat imprecisely.)
Theorem
1.1
,(i) substantially generalized Herrero’s theorem,
and the approach in
[
34
]
led to a general and new method for constructing
a big part of diagonals of operators
A\in L(H)
that works in a variety of different and general settings,
for instance in
[
11
]
and
[
12
]
mentioned above.
For its pertinent discussion and implications, see e.g.
[
29
]
.
The relevance of (
1.1
) is illustrated by the fact that for the unilateral shift
S
on
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
and for the multiplication operator
M

as above the sets
\mathcal{D}(S)
and
\mathcal{D}(M)
are in fact characterized by (
1.1
),
see
[
21
]
and
[
29
]
.
Moreover, it allowed one to construct diagonals for fixed operators rather than operator classes.
It is crucial that the same type of technique yields the results in the context of operator diagonals
thus replacing the uniform contractivity condition
from
[
6
]
on operator diagonals
by a more general assumption (
1.2
)
of Blaschke’s type.
Finally, the approach from
[
34
]
was developed in

[
35
]

and
[
36
]
for describing the properties of the whole matrices

M_{A}
for
A\in L(H),
although the main diagonals played a distinguished role there.

The present paper takes the next step ahead in
understanding the properties of matrices of
A\in L(H)

and related concepts.
It is a frequent and very fruitful idea in analysis to clarify whether a certain important property is
stable under perturbations in a natural sense. One of the ways to realise this is to obtain its time-dependent version. In this spirit one may mention, for instance,
the study of the existence of regular curves of eigenvalues for families
(A(t))_{t\in[0,1]}\subset L(H),

see e.g.
[
4
]
or
[
27
, Chapter VII]
,
or a recent work
[
17
]
providing a time-dependent, “continuous” version of an important estimate
by Bourgain and Tzafriri.
Although time-dependent versions of results on diagonals and matrix representations look
natural as such, they are essentially missing in the literature.
The only paper in this direction,
[
10
]
, provides a time-dependent
version of Kadison’s theorem.
It shows that if
d_{n}:X\to[0,1],

n\in\mathbb{N},
are measurable functions on a measure space
X,

then there exists a measurable projection-valued function
P:X\to L(H)
with

{\rm diag}\,(P(x))=(d_{n}(x))_{n=1}^{\infty}
for almost all
x\in X
if and only if for almost all
x\in X

either
a(x)=\infty
or
b(x)=\infty,
or
a(x),b(x)<\infty
and
a(x)-b(x)\in\mathbb{Z},

where
a
and
b
are defined similarly to the above. The statement yielded a description of spectral functions
of shift-invariant subspaces in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and will surely spark an interest in the near future.
However,
the arguments in
[
10
]
are tailored for the concrete setting of Kadison’s theorem,
and cannot be used in the general case of selfadjoint or, more generally, arbitrary bounded operators.
Moreover,
measurability is a rather
weak property and if one aims at more regular functions
d_{n}
, then a different technique is required.
This and other similar problems will be addressed in this paper.

First, we obtain the next time-dependent, “continuous” version of a very partial but important case
of Bourin’s result mentioned above. It deals, in fact, with continuity of classical compressions,
under the assumptions ensuring that such compressions exist.
See Remark
4.9
below.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
be such that
0\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Let
Z
be a separable Hilbert space, and let
D\in C([0,1],L(Z))

satisfy

(1.3)

\max_{t\in[0,1]}\|D(t)\|<\min_{t\in[0,1]}{\rm dist}\,\bigl(0,\partial W_{e}(A(t))\bigr).

Then there exists
S\in C([0,1],L(Z,H))
such that
S
is isometry-valued and

(1.4)

S^{*}(t)A(t)S(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Moreover, for a special type of compression with finite-dimensional
Z

we are able to control rather general moduli of continuity, see Theorem
4.4
below.
If the compression
D
is scalar, then we prove
a stronger version of Theorem
1.2
, dealing with
A
and
D

of prescribed smoothness.
Here differentiability is understood in the
real sense
, so that all derivatives are
only
\mathbb{R}
-linear.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
A\in L(H)
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be an open set. For
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\},
let
d:\Omega\to{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
be a
C^{r}
-function. Then there exists a sequence

(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{r}(\Omega,H),
such that

(i)

\|u_{n}(\omega)\|=1,\,n\in\mathbb{N},\omega\in\Omega
;

(ii)

If
n\neq m,

n,m\in\mathbb{N},
then
u_{m}(\omega)\perp u_{n}(\omega^{\prime})
for all
\omega,\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega
;

(iii)

\langle Au_{n}(\omega),u_{n}(\omega)\rangle=d(\omega),\,n\in\mathbb{N},\omega\in\Omega
;

(iv)

There exists a Hilbert space
X
and an isometry-valued function
S\in C^{r}(\Omega,L(X,H))

satisfying

S^{*}(\omega)AS(\omega)=d(\omega)I,\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Second, we prove the next non-stationary version of Herrero’s theorem that was a prototype
for Theorem
1.1
, (ii).

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
and
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C([0,1])
be such that

d_{n}(t)\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t)),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in[0,1].

If

\inf\{{\rm dist}\,(d_{n}(t),\partial W_{e}(A(t)):n\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,1])\}>0,

then there exists a sequence
(v_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\in C([0,1],H)
such that

(v_{n}(t))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
H
for every
t\in[0,1],

and

\langle A(t)v_{n}(t),v_{n}(t)\rangle=d_{n}(t),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in[0,1],

Moreover, the family
(v_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equicontinuous if
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is so.

The next statement is a direct corollary of Theorem
1.4
, which is worth noting.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
and
d\in C([0,1],\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N}))
satisfy

W_{e}(A(t))\supset\{z:|z|\leq\|d(t)\|_{\infty}+\theta\}

for some
\theta>0
. Then there exists a continuous function
v:[0,1]\to\ell^{\infty}(\mathbb{N},H)

such that

(v_{n}(t))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
H
for all
t\in[0,1]
and

\left\langle A(t)v_{n}(t),v_{n}(t)\right\rangle=d_{n}(t)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

Our approach uses several ideas from
[
34
]
,
[
33
]
and
[
32
]
.
However, methods from these papers cannot be applied directly,
and new tools are required.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
is based on
factorization of (
1.4
)
into simpler germs,
where the regularity properties are easier to control,
and on inductive arguments adapted to the time-dependent
case. In the case of scalar and smooth
D
,
we employ a different argument depending on partitions
on unity.
To show Theorem
1.4
, we combine the idea from
[
34
]

with a reasoning similar in spirit to the proof of Theorem
1.2
,
and rely on inductive arguments again.

We give versions of our results for the important case when operators
A(t)
are selfadjoint.
The case of selfadjoint
A
received a special attention in the study of compressions and diagonals
due to its applications to frames theory, theory of von Neumann algebras and
majorization, and we find it useful to address it as well.
Since then
{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))=\emptyset
for all
t\in[0,1]
we have to adjust
our proofs and the corresponding formulations accordingly.
See Theorems
7.5
and
7.6
for counterparts of Theorems
1.2

and
1.4
in this setting.

At the moment, it is not clear whether
Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
can be shown
under Blaschke type assumptions of Theorem
1.1
.
We even do not know whether one can get a time-dependent variant of Bourin’s formulation,
and we suspect that Blaschke type assumptions would probably require
additional new ideas.

We formulated Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
, as well as the related statements below, for continuous functions on the interval
[0,1]
. It is plausible that similar results hold for continuous functions on an arbitrary compact metric space. However, we restrict our attention to the unit interval in order to keep the complexity of the proofs and the length of the paper within reasonable limits.

2.
Notation and conventions

Let
H
be an infinite-dimensional complex separable Hilbert space, and
let
L(H)
stand for the algebra of bounded linear operators on
H.

The space of bounded linear operators between Hilbert spaces
K
and
H

is denoted by
L(K,H).

For bounded unitarily equivalent linear operators
A
and
B
acting on the corresponding Hilbert spaces we write

A\smash{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{u}}B.

For a metric space
G
and a Banach space
X
we denote by
C(G,X)

the space of continuous functions on
G
with values in
X.
For
f\in C(G,X)
we denote
Z(f)=\bigvee_{g\in G}f(g)
, where
\bigvee
stands for the closed linear span.

Similarly,
C^{r}(\Omega,X)
denotes the space of
X
-valued
r
-times continuously differentiable functions on an open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}.

For
r>0,
we let
\mathbb{D}_{r}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<r\},
and write

\mathbb{D}
instead of
\mathbb{D}_{1}
to follow the established convention.

The restriction of a linear operator
A\in L(H)
to a subspace
M
of
H
is denoted by

A{\upharpoonright}_{M},
and
P_{M}
stands for the orthogonal projection from
H
onto
M.

For a subset
S
of a Banach space, we denote by
{\rm conv}\,S
its convex hull, by
{\rm Int}\,S
its interior,
by
\overline{S}
its closure and
by
\partial S
its topological boundary.

We deal only with
separable complex
Hilbert spaces and we assume
that a subspace of a Hilbert space is always closed.

3.
Preliminaries

Our arguments will rely on the notions of essential numerical range and essential spectrum
of
A\in L(H).

To put them into a proper context, recall that the numerical range of
A
is defined
as

W(A):=\left\{\langle Ax,x\rangle:x\in H,\|x\|=1\right\}.

It is well-known that
W(A)
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{C}
and
\sigma(A)\subset\overline{W(A)}
.
At the same time,
W(A)
may be neither closed, nor open, and its geometry may be highly complicated.
While
W(A)
contains a lot of information on
A
and would be a natural candidate to use
for the study of continuity properties of matrix representations of
A,
it is highly unstable, even under rank-one perturbations.
This makes it difficult to use
W(A)
and motivates one to invoke
counterparts of
W(A)
more suitable for various inductive constructions.

Thus, we will use essential numerical range of
A,
having no such a drawback.
The set
W_{e}(A)
can be considered as an approximate version of
W(A),

and it can be defined in several equivalent ways.
Following one of them, we define the essential numerical range
W_{e}(A)
as the set of
all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that there exists an orthonormal sequence
(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset H
with

\lim_{n\to\infty}\langle Au_{n},u_{n}\rangle=\lambda.

As shown in
[
39
]
, orthonormal sequences in this definition can be replaced by bases in
H.

Recall that
W_{e}(A)
is a compact and convex subset of
\overline{W(A)}

and it is non-empty if
H
is infinite-dimensional.
Moreover,
W_{e}(A+K)=W_{e}(A)
for every compact operator
K.

It is easy to show that for any
A,B\in L(H)
one has

\rho(W_{e}(B),W_{e}(A))\leq\|B-A\|,

where
\rho
stands for the Hausdorff metric on compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}.

Thus the mapping

A\mapsto W_{e}(A)
is continuous. Note that
W_{e}(A)
is related to the spectral properties of
A,

and, in particular, if
\sigma_{e}(A)
stands for the essential spectrum
of
A
, then
{\rm conv}\sigma_{e}(A)\subset W_{e}(A).

The next lemma will be indispensable in our arguments and be applied many times
in the sequel.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
A\in L(H).

(a)

One has
\lambda\in W_{e}(A)
if and only if
for every
\varepsilon>0
and every subspace
M\subset H
of finite codimension
there is a unit vector
x\in M
such that

|\langle Ax,x\rangle-\lambda|<\varepsilon.

Thus,

W_{e}(P_{M}A{\upharpoonright}_{M})=W_{e}(A).

(b)

If
\lambda\in{\rm Int}\,\,W_{e}(A),

then for every subspace
M\subset H
of finite codimension there is
x\in M
such that
\|x\|=1
and

\langle Ax,x\rangle=\lambda.

Moreover, there exists an infinite-rank orthogonal projection
P

such that

PAP=\lambda P.

If
A=A^{*},
then the same properties hold for
\lambda\in{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}W_{e}(A),

where
{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}W_{e}(A)
stands for the interior of
W_{e}(A)
relative to
\mathbb{R}.

The proof of the first property is easy and can be found e.g. in
[
33
, Proposition 5.5]
.
The second property is more involved,
see e.g.
[
32
, Corollary 4.5]
and
[
34
, Proposition 4.1]
for its proof and other related statements.
The properties (a) and (b) are very useful in inductive constructions of
sequences in
H.
In particular,
by absorbing all of the elements constructed after a finite number of induction steps
into a finite-dimensional subspace
F
,
one may still use
W_{e}(A)
when dealing with vectors from
F^{\perp}.

In particular, given
\lambda\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
one may find a unit vector
x\in F^{\perp}
with
\langle Ax,x\rangle=\lambda.

These properties will play a role in this paper as well.
They will be often combined with the following simple observation.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
K\subset H
be a compact set.
Then for every subspace
M\subset H
with
{\rm codim}\,M<\infty
and every
\epsilon>0
there exists
a subspace
L\subset M,{\rm codim}\,L<\infty
such that for all
f\in L,\|f\|=1,

and
x\in K
one has

|\langle x,f\rangle|\leq\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
\{x_{k}:1\leq k\leq n\}
be a finite
\varepsilon
-net for
K
.
If
L=M\cap\left(\bigvee_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right)^{\perp},

then
L\subset M,{\rm codim}\,L<\infty,
and for every
f\in L
with
\|f\|=1,

\sup_{x\in K}|\langle x,f\rangle|=\sup_{x\in K}\min_{1\leq k\leq n}|\langle x-x_{k},f\rangle|\leq\epsilon.

∎

The next result, crucial for our studies, is a particular case of
[
6
, Theorem 2.1]

mentioned in the introduction.
It will be of substantial use, and is thus formulated
as a separate statement.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
A\in L(H)
be such that
W_{e}(A)\supset\mathbb{D}_{1}.

Assume that
X
is a separable Hilbert space, and
D\in L(X)
satisfies
\|D\|<1
.
Then there exists a subspace
M\subset H
such that the compression
P_{M}A{\upharpoonright}_{M}
is unitarily equivalent to
D
.

Finally, we will introduce the notation pertaining to regularity properties of operator-valued functions
and relevant for our approach.
Given Hilbert spaces
X
and
H,
for
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H)),
write

{\rm Lip}\,(V):=\sup\left\{\frac{\|V(t^{\prime})-V(t)\|}{|t^{\prime}-t|}:\,0\leq t,t^{\prime}\leq 1,t^{\prime}\neq t\right\}.

Recall that a modulus of continuity is a non-decreasing function
\delta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
\delta(0)=\lim_{s\to 0_{+}}\delta(s)=0
.

If
M
and
M^{\prime}
are metric spaces, then a function
f:M\to M^{\prime}
is said to admit
\delta
as a modulus of continuity if
{\rm dist}\,(f(m),f(m^{\prime}))\leq\delta({\rm dist}\,(m,m^{\prime}))
for all
m,m^{\prime}\in M
.
A set of functions
f_{n}:M\to M^{\prime},\ n\in\mathbb{N},
is equicontinuous if there exists a modulus of continuity
\delta
such that all
f_{n}
admit it as a modulus of continuity.

If
M
is compact and
f
is continuous, then
f
admits the modulus of continuity
\delta_{f}
defined by

\delta_{f}(\varepsilon)=\max\{{\rm dist}\,(f(m),f(m^{\prime})):{\rm dist}\,(m,m^{\prime})\leq\varepsilon\}.

Note that if
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}:M\to M^{\prime}
are continuous functions then
\delta:=\max\{\delta_{f_{1}},\dots,\delta_{f_{k}}\}
is a modulus of continuity for each of
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}
.

4.
Compressions of operator-valued functions

In the following we let
H
be an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space.
In this section we will prove Theorem
1.2
as well as related statements of independent interest.
The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies on the factorization of (
1.4
) into
three similar relations separating time-dependent left and right hand sides of (
1.4
).
First, we construct a stationary, diagonal compression
D_{{\rm diag}}
of
A(t)
depending continuously
on
t
, see Proposition
4.5
. Second, we show that the bilateral shift
U
(of infinite multiplicity) can be compressed continuously
to
D(t),t\in[0,1]
, see Proposition
4.6
. It remains to find
U
among compressions of
D_{{\rm diag}}
, see Proposition
4.8
.
However, realization of this scheme is rather involved, and it will be divided into several incremental steps.

We start with constructing stationary diagonal compressions of
A,
and to this aim the next simple lemma will be crucial.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
A\in L(H)
, and assume that

\mathbb{D}_{r}\subset W_{e}(A)
for some
r>0
.
Let
X
be a separable Hilbert space and
D\in L(X)
satisfy

\|D\|<r
. If
L\subset H
is a subspace of finite codimension, then there exists
a subspace
M\subset L
such that the compression
P_{M}A{\upharpoonright}_{M}
is unitarily equivalent to
D
. Equivalently, there exists an isometry
V:X\to H
with
{\rm Im}\,V=M
such that
V^{*}AV=D
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
, (a),

W_{e}(P_{L}A{\upharpoonright}_{L})=W_{e}(A)\supset\mathbb{D}_{r}.

Hence
W_{e}(r^{-1}P_{L}A{\upharpoonright}_{L})\supset\mathbb{D}_{1}
and, by Theorem
3.3
,
there exists a subspace
M\subset L
such that
r^{-1}P_{M}A{\upharpoonright}_{M}\smash{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{u}}r^{-1}D
. So
P_{M}A{\upharpoonright}_{M}
is unitarily equivalent to
D
.
∎

The construction of stationary compressions for
A
will be achieved via an approximation argument involving inductive
construction. To be able to implement the induction
step we need several preparations.
First we prove an approximate version of (
1.4
) for finite-dimensional
D
,
with the modulus of continuity
\delta_{V}
controlled by

\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
satisfy

0\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
t\in[0,1].

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and
D\in C([0,1],L(X))

be such that

\|D(t)\|<{\rm dist}\,(0,\partial W_{e}(A(t)))

for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Then for every
\varepsilon>0
and every subspace
L\subset H
of finite codimension there exists

V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that:

(i)

V(t)
is an isometry for all
t\in[0,1]
;

(ii)

{\rm Lip}\,(V)<\infty,
and
{\rm Lip}\,(V)
depends only on
\varepsilon
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
.

(iii)

Z(V)\subset L
and
\dim Z(V)<\infty
;

(iv)

\bigl\|V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)-D(t)\bigr\|\leq\varepsilon
for all
t\in[0,1]
;

Proof.

Let
k
be the smallest positive integer such that

\|A(t^{\prime})-A(t)\|\leq\varepsilon/4

and

\|D(t^{\prime})-D(t)\|\leq\varepsilon/2

whenever
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,
|t-t^{\prime}|\leq k^{-1}
.

Set

t_{j}=j/k,\qquad 0\leq j\leq k.

By Lemma
4.1
, there exists an isometry
V(t_{0}):X\to H
satisfying
V^{*}(t_{0})A(t_{0})V(t_{0})=D(t_{0})

and
{\rm Im}\,V(t_{0})\subset L
.

We construct isometries
V(t_{j}),j=1,\dots,k,
such that
V^{*}(t_{j})A(t_{j})V(t_{j})=D(t_{j})

by induction on
j
.

Let
j\geq 1
and suppose that the isometries
V(t_{0}),\dots,V(t_{j-1}):X\to H
have already been constructed. Let

M_{j}=\bigl(V(t_{j-1})X\cup A(t_{j-1})V(t_{j-1})X\cup A^{*}(t_{j})V(t_{j-1})X\bigr)^{\perp},

and note that
{\rm codim}\,M_{j}<\infty
. By Lemma
4.1
, there exists an isometry
V(t_{j}):X\to H
such that

V^{*}(t_{j})A(t_{j})V(t_{j})=D(t_{j})
and
{\rm Im}\,V(t_{j})\subset L\cap M_{j}.
The constructed isometries
V(t_{0}),\dots,V(t_{k})
satisfy

V(t_{j+1})X\perp V(t_{j})X,A(t_{j})V(t_{j})X,A^{*}(t_{j+1})V(t_{j})X

for all
j=0,1,\dots,k-1
.

For
j=0,1,\dots,k-1,
define a function
f_{j}:[t_{j},t_{j+1}]\to[0,\pi/2]
by

f_{j}(t):=\frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{t-t_{j}}{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}=\frac{k\pi(t-t_{j})}{2},\qquad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

Then, for
t_{j}\leq t\leq t_{j+1},
set

V(t)=V(t_{j})\cos(f_{j}(t))+V(t_{j+1})\sin(f_{j}(t)),\qquad t\in[t_{j},t_{j+1}].

Clearly,
{\rm Im}\,V(t)\subset L.
Note that for every
x\in X,
we have
V(t_{j})x\perp V(t_{j+1})x
, and so

\|V(t)x\|^{2}=\cos^{2}(f_{j}(t))\|V(t_{j})x\|^{2}+\sin^{2}(f_{j}(t))\|V(t_{j+1})x\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Hence
V(t)
is an isometry for each
t\in[0,1],
and (i) holds.

By construction, the function
V:[0,1]\to L(X,H)
is norm-continuous.
Moreover, in each interval
(t_{j},t_{j+1}),

V
is differentiable and

\frac{{\rm d}V}{{\rm d}t}=-V(t_{j})\sin(f_{j}(t))\frac{k\pi}{2}+V(t_{j+1})\cos(f_{j}(t))\frac{k\pi}{2}

with

\Bigl\|\frac{{\rm d}V}{{\rm d}\,t}\Bigr\|\leq\frac{k\pi(|\sin(f_{j}(t))|+|\cos(f_{j}(t))|)}{2}\leq k\pi.

Hence
\|V(t^{\prime})-V(t)\|\leq k\pi|t^{\prime}-t|
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1],
and
{\rm Lip}\,(V)\leq k\pi.

In particular,
{\rm Lip}\,(V)
is finite and depends only on
\varepsilon
and moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D},

implying (ii).

Clearly

\displaystyle\dim Z(V)=

\displaystyle\dim\bigvee\{V(t)X:0\leq t\leq 1\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dim\bigvee\{V(t_{j})X:j=0,1,\dots,k\}<\infty,

and (iii) follows.

It remains to prove (iv).
For all
t_{j}\leq s\leq t_{j+1}
and
x,x^{\prime}\in X
such that
\|x\|=\|x^{\prime}\|=1

we have

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle V^{*}(s)A(s)V(s)x-D(s)x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle A(s)V(t_{j})x\cdot\cos(f_{j}(s))+A(s)V(t_{j+1})x\cdot\sin(f_{j}(s)),V(s)x^{\prime}\right\rangle-\left\langle D(s)x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|\left\langle\cos(f_{j}(s))A(t_{j})V(t_{j})x,V(s)x^{\prime}\right\rangle+\left\langle\sin(f_{j}(s))A(t_{j+1})V(t_{j+1})x,V(s)x^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle D(s)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\Bigr|+\|A(s)-A(t_{j})\|\cdot|\cos(f_{j}(s))|+\|A(s)-A(t_{j+1})\|\cdot|\sin(f_{j}(s))|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|\cos^{2}(f_{j}(s))\Bigl\langle A(t_{j})V(t_{j})x,V(t_{j})x^{\prime}\Bigr\rangle+\sin^{2}f_{j}(s)\Bigl\langle A(t_{j+1})V(t_{j+1})x,V(t_{j+1})x^{\prime}\Bigr\rangle

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\langle D(s)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\Bigr|+\varepsilon/2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|\cos^{2}(f_{j}(s))\bigl\langle(D(t_{j})-D(s))x,x^{\prime}\bigr\rangle+\sin^{2}(f_{j}(s))\bigl\langle(D(t_{j+1})-D(s))x,x^{\prime}\bigr\rangle\right|+\varepsilon/2

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\cos^{2}(f_{j}(s))\|D(t_{j})-D(s)\|+\sin^{2}(f_{j}(s))\|D(t_{j+1})-D(s)\|+\varepsilon/2\leq\varepsilon,

and (iv) follows.
∎

Now we show how to improve our approximation of
D

by choosing an appropriate compression of
A
.
Apart from making approximation sharper, we keep control over the continuity of compression.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H)).

For
\theta>0
and a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
X,

let

D\in C([0,1],L(X))
be
such that

W_{e}(A(t))\supset\{z:|z|\leq\|D(t)\|+\theta\},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,\theta),
a subspace
L\subset H
with
{\rm codim}\,L<\infty,
and
l>0,
assume that
an isometry-valued
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
is such that

Z(V)\subset L,\quad\dim Z(V)<\infty,\quad{\rm Lip}\,(V)\leq l,

and

\left\|V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)-D(t)\right\|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0

there exist
\tilde{l}>0
(depending only on
l
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
)
and an isometry-valued

\widetilde{V}\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
satisfying

Z(\tilde{V})\subset L,\quad\dim Z(\widetilde{V})<\infty,\quad{\rm Lip}\,(\tilde{V})\leq\tilde{l},

(4.1)

\left\|\widetilde{V}^{*}(t)A(t)\widetilde{V}(t)-D(t)\right\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},\qquad t\in[0,1],

and

(4.2)

\left\|\widetilde{V}(t)-V(t)\right\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\theta}},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Denote by
B_{X}
the closed unit ball in
X
.
Clearly the sets

\left\{A(t)V(t)B_{X}:0\leq t\leq 1\right\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\{A^{*}(t)V(t)B_{X}:0\leq t\leq 1\right\}

are compact.
So there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and vectors
x_{1},\dots x_{m}\in H
such that

\min\{\|A(t)V(t)x-x_{j}\|:1\leq j\leq m\}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2

and

\min\{\|A^{*}(t)V(t)x-x_{j}\|:1\leq j\leq m\}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2

for all
t\in[0,1]
and
x\in B_{X}
. Let
a={\varepsilon}{\theta}^{-1},

and

\widetilde{D}(t)=\frac{D(t)+(a-1)V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)}{a},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then for every
t\in[0,1],

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{D}(t)\|\leq

\displaystyle\|D(t)\|+a^{-1}(1-a)\bigl\|D(t)-V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|D(t)\|+\frac{(1-a)\varepsilon}{a}<\|D(t)\|+\frac{\varepsilon}{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|D(t)\|+\theta.

Let

M=Z(V)^{\perp}\cap\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}^{\perp}.

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists a norm-continuous isometry-valued function
R:[0,1]\to L(X,H)
such that

Z(R)\subset M\cap L
,
\dim Z(R)<\infty
,
and

\left\|R^{*}(t)A(t)R(t)-\widetilde{D}(t)\right\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}

for all
t\in[0,1]
. In particular,

Z(R)\perp Z(V),\qquad Z(R)\perp\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\},

Moreover,
{\rm Lip}\,(R)<\infty,
and
{\rm Lip}\,(R)
depends only on
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{\widetilde{D}}
, which in turn depends only on
\varepsilon^{\prime}
,
l
,
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
.

Define

(4.3)

\widetilde{V}(t)=\sqrt{1-a}\,V(t)+\sqrt{a}\,R(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

By construction,
Z(\widetilde{V})\subset L.
Moreover, for every
t\in[0,1],
taking into account that
R(t)x\perp V(t)x,x\in X,
we have

\|\widetilde{V}(t)x\|^{2}=(1-a)\|V(t)x\|^{2}+a\|R(t)x\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}

for all
x\in X
. So
\widetilde{V}(t)
is an isometry on
X
.
In addition,
Z(\widetilde{V})\subset Z(V)+Z(R),
so
\dim Z(\widetilde{V})<\infty
.

To prove (
4.1
), observe that for all
x,x^{\prime}\in X
such that
\|x\|=\|x^{\prime}\|=1,

\displaystyle\Bigl|\left\langle\bigl(\widetilde{V}^{*}(t)A(t)\widetilde{V}(t)-D(t)\bigr)x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl\langle\sqrt{1-a}A(t)V(t)x+\sqrt{a}A(t)R(t)x,\sqrt{1-a}V(t)x^{\prime}+\sqrt{a}R(t)x^{\prime}\Bigr\rangle-\langle D(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|(1-a)\bigl\langle A(t)V(t)x,V(t)x^{\prime}\bigr\rangle+a\bigl\langle A(t)R(t)x,R(t)x^{\prime}\bigr\rangle-\langle D(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sqrt{a(1-a)}\left|\langle A(t)V(t)x,R(t)x^{\prime}\rangle\right|+\sqrt{a(1-a)}\left|\langle A(t)R(t)x,V(t)x^{\prime}\rangle\right|,

where

\left|\langle A(t)V(t)x,R(t)x^{\prime}\rangle\right|\leq\min\left\{\left\|A(t)V(t)x-x_{j}\right\|:1\leq j\leq m\right\}\cdot\|R(t)x^{\prime}\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2,

and, similarly,

\bigl|\langle A(t)R(t)x,V(t)x^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/2.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\bigl|(1-a)\langle A(t)V(t)x,V(t)x^{\prime}\rangle+a\langle A(t)R(t)x,R(t)x^{\prime}\rangle-\langle D(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigl|(1-a)\langle A(t)V(t)x,V(t)x^{\prime}\rangle+a\langle\widetilde{D}(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle-\langle D(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle\bigr|+{\varepsilon^{\prime}/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}/2.

Hence, since
\sqrt{a(1-a)}\leq 1/2
,

\|\widetilde{V}^{*}(t)A(t)\widetilde{V}(t)-D(t)\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},\qquad t\in[0,1],

i.e., (
4.1
) holds.

To show (
4.2
), we note that for all
t\in[0,1]
and
x\in H
,
\|x\|=1,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{V}(t)x-V(t)x\|^{2}=

\displaystyle(1-\sqrt{1-a})^{2}\|V(t)x\|^{2}+{a}\|R(t)x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\sqrt{1-a})^{2}+{a}=2-2\sqrt{1-a}\leq 2{a}.

Therefore,

\|\tilde{V}(t)-V(t)\|\leq\sqrt{2a}=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\theta}},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Finally, if
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1],
then

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{V}(t^{\prime})-\widetilde{V}(t)\|\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-a}\|V(t^{\prime})-V(t)\|+\sqrt{a}\|R(t^{\prime})-R(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle l|t^{\prime}-t|+\sqrt{a}\cdot{\rm Lip}\,(R)|t^{\prime}-t|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\widetilde{l}|t^{\prime}-t|

for some constant
\widetilde{l}
depending only on
l
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
.
∎

Before implementing the factorization argument in Proposition
4.5
below,
we note a related statement with
D
acting on a finite-dimensional space.
In this case, we are able to provide better control
for regularity of
V,
which will be crucial in
construction of diagonals for

in the next section.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let

X
be a Hilbert space with
\dim X<\infty
,

A\in C([0,1],L(H))
,
D\in C([0,1],L(X))

and

W_{e}(A(t))\supset\{z:|z|\leq\|D(t)\|+\theta\}

for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then for every subspace
L
of
H
with
{\rm codim}\,L<\infty,
there exists
an isometry-valued function
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H)),

Z(V)\subset L,
with modulus of continuity
\delta,

depending only on
\theta
and the moduli of continuity

\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
,
such that

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists
l_{1}>0
(depending only on
\theta
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
) and an isometry-valued function
V_{1}\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that
Z(V_{1})\subset L
,
\dim Z(V_{1})<\infty
,
{\rm Lip}\,(V_{1})\leq l_{1}
and

\bigl\|V^{*}_{1}(t)A(t)V_{1}(t)-D(t)\bigr\|\leq\theta/2

for all
t\in[0,1]
.
By
an inductive argument, choosing
\varepsilon=2^{-n}\theta

and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2^{-n-1}\theta
in Proposition
4.3
for every
n\in\mathbb{N},
we
find
l_{n}>0
(depending only on
\theta
,
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
) and an isometry-valued function
V_{n}\in C([0,1],L(X,H)),n\geq 2,
such that

(4.4)

Z(V_{n})\subset L,\quad\dim Z(V_{n})<\infty,\quad{\rm Lip}\,(V_{n})\leq l_{n},

(4.5)

\bigl\|V_{n}^{*}(t)A(t)V_{n}(t)-D(t)\bigr\|\leq\theta/2^{n},

and

(4.6)

\|V_{n+1}(t)-V_{n}(t)\|\leq 2^{\frac{1-n}{2}}

for all
n\geq 2
and
t\in[0,1]
.

From (
4.6
) it follows that the series
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\|V_{n+1}(t)-V_{n}(t)\right\|

converges uniformly on
[0,1].
Hence setting

V(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{n}(t),\qquad t\in[0,1],

we infer that
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H)).
Since
V_{n}
is isometry-valued and
Z(V_{n})\subset L
for every
n\in\mathbb{N},
the same holds
for
V.
Moreover, in view of (
4.5
), we have

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{n}^{*}(t)A(t)V_{n}(t)=D(t)

for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
. If
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]
,
|t-t^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon,
then

\displaystyle\|V(t)-V(t^{\prime})\|\leq

\displaystyle\|V(t)-V_{n_{0}}(t)\|+\|V_{n_{0}}(t)-V_{n_{0}}(t^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|V_{n_{0}}(t^{\prime})-V(t^{\prime})\|\leq 2\cdot\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{\infty}2^{\frac{1-n}{2}}+l_{n_{0}}\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{(5-n_{0})/2}}{2-\sqrt{2}}+l_{n_{0}}\varepsilon

Set

\delta(\varepsilon)=\inf_{n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\frac{2^{(5-n_{0})/2}}{2-\sqrt{2}}+l_{n_{0}}\varepsilon\right).

Then
V
admits
\delta
as a modulus of continuity.

∎

Now we have all the required tools to realize the first step of
our factorization by finding a diagonal operator among
continuous compressions of
A.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
and assume that
0\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Let
X
be an infinite-dimensional separable Hilbert space and let
D\in L(X)
be a diagonal operator satisfying

\|D\|<\min_{t\in[0,1]}{\rm dist}\,(0,\partial W_{e}(A(t))).

Then there exists an isometry-valued mapping
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=D,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Choose
\theta>0
such that

W_{e}(A(t))\supset\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|\leq\|D\|+\theta\},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Let
(x_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}
be an orthonormal basis in
X
and let
(d_{j})_{j=1}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{C}
be such that

Dx_{j}=d_{j}x_{j}
for all
j\geq 1
.
Set

X_{k}=\bigvee\{x_{j}:j\leq 2^{k}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D_{k}=P_{X_{k}}D{\upharpoonright}_{X_{k}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, we first use Lemma
4.2
and then apply Proposition
4.3
successively with

\varepsilon=2^{-(n-1)}\theta
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=2^{-n}\theta
,
n\geq 2
, in order to obtain a sequence
(l_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset(0,\infty)

depending only on
\theta
and
\delta_{A}
.

We now construct inductively isometry-valued continuous mappings

V_{k,n}:[0,1]\to L(X_{k},H),\qquad n\leq k,

such that

(i)

\dim Z(V_{k,n})<\infty
;

(ii)

\bigl\|V_{k,n}^{*}(t)A(t)V_{k,n}(t)-D_{k}\bigr\|\leq 2^{-n}\theta(2-2^{-k})
;

(iii)

\bigl\|V_{k,n}(t)-V_{k,n-1}(t)\bigr\|\leq 2^{\frac{2-n}{2}}
;

(iv)

{\rm Lip}\,(V_{k,n})\leq l_{n}
;

(v)

V_{k+1,n}
is an extension of
V_{k,n}
.

First, Lemma
4.2
yields
V_{1,1}
with properties (i)–(v).

Assume now that for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have constructed
V_{k^{\prime},n}
satisfying (i)–(v)
for all
n
and
k^{\prime}
with
n\leq k^{\prime}\leq k
.
Set

Y_{k}=X_{k+1}\ominus X_{k}\qquad\text{and}\qquad D^{\prime}_{k}=P_{Y_{k}}D{\upharpoonright}_{Y_{k}}.

Define

L_{k}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{k}Z(V_{k,n})^{\perp},

so that
{\rm codim}\,L_{k}<\infty.

Using the compactness of the set

\left\{A(t)V_{k,n}x,A^{*}(t)V_{k,n}(t)x:t\in[0,1],1\leq n\leq k,x\in X_{k},\|x\|=1\right\}

and Lemma
3.2
, we find a subspace
M_{k}\subset L_{k}
with
{\rm codim}\,M_{k}<\infty
such that

\left|\langle A(t)V_{k,n}x,m\rangle\right|<\theta\cdot 2^{-2k-2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\langle A^{*}(t)V_{k,n}x,m\rangle\right|<\theta\cdot 2^{-2k-2}

for all
t\in[0,1],1\leq n\leq k,
and
x\in X_{k},m\in M_{k},
with
\|x\|=\|m\|=1
.

Step 1: construction of
V_{k+1,1}
.

By Lemma
4.2
there exists an isometry-valued mapping
R_{k,1}:[0,1]\to L(Y_{k},H)
such that

Z(R_{k,1})\subset M_{k}
,

\dim Z(R_{k,1})<\infty,\qquad{\rm Lip}\,(R_{k,1})\leq l_{1},

and

\|R^{*}_{k,1}(t)A(t)R_{k,1}(t)-D^{\prime}_{k}\|\leq\theta/2,\qquad t\in[0,1].

For
t\in[0,1]
set

V_{k+1,1}(t)=V_{k,1}(t)\oplus R_{k,1}(t).

Arguing as in Proposition
4.3
, one checks that
V_{k+1,1}
satisfies (i)–(v) (for
n=1
).

Step 2: construction of
V_{k+1,j}
for
2\leq j\leq k
.

Assume that
V_{k+1,1},\dots,V_{k+1,j-1}
have already been defined for some
2\leq j\leq k
.
Using Proposition
4.3
, we obtain a continuous isometry-valued mapping

R_{k,j}:[0,1]\to L(Y_{k},H)
such that

Z(R_{k,j})\subset M_{k},\qquad\dim Z(R_{k,j})<\infty,\qquad{\rm Lip}\,(R_{k,j})\leq l_{j},

\|R^{*}_{k,j}(t)A(t)R_{k,j}(t)-D^{\prime}_{k}\|\leq\theta/2^{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\|R_{k,j}(t)-R_{k,j-1}(t)\|\leq 2^{\frac{2-j}{2}}

for all

.
We then set

V_{k+1,j}(t)=V_{k,j}(t)\oplus R_{k,j}(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Again

satisfies (i)–(v).

Step 3: construction of
V_{k+1,k+1}
.

Once
V_{k+1,1},\dots,V_{k+1,k}
are available, Lemma
4.2
provides
V_{k+1,k+1}
satisfying
(i)–(v).
This completes the inductive step in

.

We have thus constructed continuous functions
V_{k,n}:[0,1]\to L(X_{k},H)
satisfying (i)–(v)
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\leq k
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
. For
k\geq n
the operator
V_{k+1,n}(t)
extends

.
Define an isometry

by

V_{n}(t)x=V_{k,n}(t)x\quad\text{if }x\in X_{k},

and extend

by continuity to an isometry

, keeping the same notation.
Moreover, for

and

with

we have

\|(V_{n}(t)-V_{n}(t^{\prime}))x\|\leq l_{n}|t-t^{\prime}|,

and hence the same inequality holds for all

with
\|x\|=1
.
Thus
V_{n}
is continuous on
[0,1]
and
{\rm Lip}\,(V_{n})\leq l_{n}
.

By construction we also have, for all
t\in[0,1]
,

(4.7)

\|V^{*}_{n}(t)A(t)V_{n}(t)-D\|\leq 2\cdot\theta/2^{n}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|V_{n+1}(t)-V_{n}(t)\|\leq 2^{\frac{1-n}{2}}.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem
4.4
and using the second estimate in
(
4.7
), we define

V(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}V_{n}(t),\qquad t\in[0,1],

and infer that
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
and that

is isometry-valued.
Finally, the first estimate in (

) implies that

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=D
for all

.
∎

We proceed with the second step of our factorization and construct
D

as a continuous compression of a fixed unitary operator
U,

being a bilateral shift of infinite multiplicity.
In other words,
U
will be a dilation of
D(t)
for every
t\in[0,1],

on a space depending continuously on
t.

This will require to impose additional restriction
on the size
D,
which is only of a technical character.
For the arguments employed in the next proposition,
recall that a unitary operator
U
on a Hilbert space
Y
is said to be a bilateral shift of multiplicity
\mu
if there exists a subspace
Z
of
Y
with
\dim Z=\mu,
a wandering subspace
of
U,
such that

Y=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}U^{j}Z,
where
\bigoplus
denotes the orthogonal sum.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be separable Hilbert spaces such that
\dim X=\dim Y=\infty.

Suppose that
U\in L(Y)
is the bilateral shift of infinite multiplicity
and
D\in C([0,1],L(X))
satisfies
\max\{\|D(t)\|:t\in[0,1]\}<1
. Then there exists
W\in C([0,1],L(X,Y))
such that

W
is isometry-valued and

W^{*}(t)UW(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Let
Z\subset Y
be a wandering subspace for
U
, i.e.,
Y=\bigoplus_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}U^{j}Z
.
Let
W_{0}:X\to Z
be a surjective isometry. For every
t\in[0,1]

consider the defect operator
\Delta_{D(t)}:=\left(I-D^{*}(t)D(t)\right)^{1/2},

and define the operator
W(t):X\to Y

by

W(t)x:=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}U^{-j}W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x,\qquad x\in X.

By the binomial series,

(1-z)^{1/2}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}z^{n},
z\in\mathbb{D},

where

\alpha_{n}=\binom{1/2}{n}(-1)^{n}=-\left|\frac{(-1/2)(1-1/2)\dots(n-1-1/2)}{n!}\right|\leq 0,\qquad n\geq 1.

Thus we have
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{n}|<\infty
and
\Delta_{D(t)}
can be defined by the Riesz-Dunford calculus for every
t\in[0,1].

Note that
\Delta_{D(\cdot)}\in C([0,1],L(X))
as the sum of the series converging uniformly on
[0,1],
Moreover,
from the series expansion,
\Delta_{D(t)}
is nonnegative
for every
t\in[0,1],
since

\langle\Delta_{D(t)}x,x\rangle\geq(1-\|D(t)\|^{2})^{1/2}\geq 0,\qquad x\in X,\|x\|=1.

In addition, estimating the series, we infer that
\|\Delta_{D(t)}\|\leq 1
.

We have

\displaystyle\|W(t)x\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|U^{-j}W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x\|^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(\|D^{j}(t)x\|^{2}-\|D^{j+1}(t)x\|^{2})=\|x\|^{2}-\lim_{j\to\infty}\|D^{j+1}(t)x\|^{2}=\|x\|^{2}.

So
W(t)
is an isometry for each
t\in[0,1]
.

Let
c=\max_{t\in[0,1]}\|D(t)\|,
so that
c<1
by assumption.
Given
\eta>0,
there exists
\delta>0
such that
\|D(t^{\prime})-D(t)\|<\eta
and
\|\Delta_{D(t^{\prime})}-\Delta_{D(t)}\|<\eta
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1]
with
|t^{\prime}-t|<\delta
.
Then

\displaystyle\|W(t^{\prime})-W(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\Delta_{D(t^{\prime})}D^{j}(t^{\prime})-\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\Delta_{D(t^{\prime})}-\Delta_{D(t)}\|\cdot\|D(t^{\prime})\|^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\|\Delta_{D(t)}\|\cdot\|D^{j}(t^{\prime})-D^{j}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}c^{j}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\Bigl\|\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}D^{j-1-r}(t^{\prime})(D(t^{\prime})-D(t))D^{r}(t)\Bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{1-c}+\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\sum_{r=0}^{j-1}\bigl\|D^{j-1-r}(t^{\prime})(D(t^{\prime})-D(t))D^{r}(t)\bigr\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{1-c}+\eta\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}jc^{j-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta}{1-c}+\frac{\eta}{(1-c)^{2}}.

So
W\in C([0,1],L(X,Y)).

Moreover, for all
t\in[0,1]
and
x,x^{\prime}\in X,

\displaystyle\langle W^{*}(t)UW(t)x,x^{\prime}\rangle=

\displaystyle\left\langle U\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}U^{-j}W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x,\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}U^{-j}W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\langle W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j+1}(t)x,W_{0}\Delta_{D(t)}D^{j}(t)x^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\langle\left(I-D^{*}(t)D(t)\right)D^{j+1}(t)x,D^{j}(t)x^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left\langle\left(D^{*j}(t)D^{j}(t)-D^{*(j+1)}(t)D^{j+1}(t)\right)D(t)x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle D(t)x,x^{\prime}\right\rangle,

i.e.,
W^{*}(t)UW(t)=D(t),
as required.
∎

To carry out the third step of our factorization argument,
we show how to obtain preassigned unitary compressions
of an appropriate diagonal operator.
We will need the next useful statement given in
[
38
, Proposition 1.1]
,
allowing us to identify a convex combination of a finite number of operators with a compression of their direct sum.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
(A_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
and
(\tilde{A}_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
be elements of
L(H)^{n}
such that
A_{j}\smash{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{u}}\tilde{A}_{j},1\leq j\leq n,
and let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}\geq 0
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=1
. Then there exists
a subspace
M\subset\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}H
such that

P_{M}\Bigl(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{n}A_{j}\Bigr)\upharpoonright_{M}\smash{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{u}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}\tilde{A}_{j}.

(For a generalization of Lemma
4.7
for operator tuples see
[
33
, Lemma 6.1]
.)

The following statement is essentially known,
see e.g. a remark following
[
14
, Theorem 2.2]
and
[
20
, Theorem 2.1]
.
As the constants are crucial here, we provide a detailed and explicit proof.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
Y
be a separable infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, and let
U\in L(Y)
be
a unitary operator.
For every
\varepsilon>0,
there exist a separable Hilbert space
X,
a diagonal operator
D_{{\rm diag}}\in L(X)

with
\|D_{{\rm diag}}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon,

and an isometry
V:Y\to X
such that
V^{*}D_{{\rm diag}}V=U
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed.
By the Weyl-von Neumann theorem (see e.g.
[
13
, Theorem 39.4]
), we can write
U=\widetilde{D}+K
where
\widetilde{D}
is a diagonal operator on
Y
,
\widetilde{D}={\rm diag}\,[\widetilde{d}_{j}],
where
\sup_{j\geq 1}|\widetilde{d}_{j}|\leq 1,
and
K
is a compact operator on
Y
with
\|K\|\leq\varepsilon/2
.
Then
K={\rm Re}\,K+i{\rm Im}\,K,
where
{\rm Re}\,K,{\rm Im}\,K
are selfadjoint compact operators on
Y
. So
{\rm Re}\,K
and
{\rm Im}\,K
are diagonal (in some orthonormal but possibly different bases):

{\rm Re}\,K={\rm diag}\,\left[d_{j}^{(1)}\right],\qquad{\rm Im}\,K={\rm diag}\,\left[d_{j}^{(2)}\right],\qquad\sup_{j\geq 1}\left\{|d_{j}^{(1)}|,|d_{j}^{(2)}|\right\}\leq\varepsilon/2.

Let

D_{1}=(1+\varepsilon)\widetilde{D},\qquad D_{2}=\frac{2(1+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}{\rm Re}\,K,\qquad D_{3}=\frac{2i(1+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}{\rm Im}\,K,

and define
X=Y\oplus Y\oplus Y
and
D_{{\rm diag}}=D_{1}\oplus D_{2}\oplus D_{3}\in L(X)
. Then
D_{{\rm diag}}
is a diagonal operator on
X
such that
\|D_{{\rm diag}}\|\leq 1+\varepsilon,
and
U
is a convex combination of
D_{1},D_{2}
and
D_{3}:

U=\frac{1}{1+\varepsilon}D_{1}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2(1+\varepsilon)}D_{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2(1+\varepsilon)}D_{3}.

By Lemma
4.7
, there exists an isometry
V:Y\to X
satisfying
V^{*}D_{{\rm diag}}V=U
.
∎

Combining Propositions
4.5
,
4.8
and
4.6

and factorizing (
1.4
) accordingly,
we derive Theorem
1.2
, one of the main results of this paper.
Now it becomes a direct consequence of the propositions mentioned above.

Proof of Theorem
1.2

Consider
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
with
0\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
We have to show that if
D\in C([0,1],L(Z))

and
D
is so small that (
1.3
) holds, then
there exists a norm continuous, isometry-valued function
S:[0,1]\to L(Z,H)

satisfying

S^{*}(t)A(t)S(t)=D(t)
for all
t\in[0,1].

Choose
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
such that

\max_{t\in[0,1]}\|D(t)\|<b_{1}<b_{2}<\min_{t\in[0,1]}{\rm dist}\,(0,\partial W_{e}(A(t))).

Let
U\in L(Y)
be a bilateral shift of infinite multiplicity and
D_{{\rm diag}}\in L(X)
be the diagonal operator
constructed by Proposition
4.8
such that

\|D_{{\rm diag}}\|\leq b_{2}/b_{1}
and
J^{*}D_{{\rm diag}}J=U
for an isometry
J:Y\to X
.

By Proposition
4.5
, there exists an isometry-valued function
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=b_{1}D_{{\rm diag}},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Moreover, by Proposition
4.6
, there exists an isometry-valued function
W\in C([0,1],L(Z,Y))

satisfying

W^{*}(t)UW(t)=b_{1}^{-1}D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Let
S(t)=V(t)JW(t),t\in[0,1]
.
By construction,
S\in C([0,1],L(Z,H)),

S
is isometry-valued, and
for all
t\in[0,1],

(4.8)

\displaystyle S^{*}(t)A(t)S(t)=

\displaystyle W^{*}(t)J^{*}V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)JW(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{1}W^{*}(t)J^{*}D_{{\rm diag}}JW(t)=b_{1}W^{*}(t)UW(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(t).

This completes the proof.

\hfill\qed

Remark 4.9
.

It might be instructive to clarify that Theorem
1.2

addresses compressions of
A,
which are continuous in a natural sense.
Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
,
denoting the unitary operator
S(t):Z\to{\rm Im}\,S(t)
by
S_{Z}(t)

and observing that
P(t)=S(t)S^{*}(t):H\to H
is the projection onto
{\rm Im}\,S(t)
for every
t\in[0,1],
the relation (
4.8
) rewrites as

(4.9)

S_{Z}^{-1}(t)\left(P(t)A(t)\upharpoonright_{{\rm Im}\,S(t)}\right)S_{Z}(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

So
P(t)A(t)\upharpoonright_{{\rm Im}\,S(t)}\smash{\mathop{\sim}\limits^{u}}D(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Moreover, the spaces
{\rm Im}\,S(t)
change continuously in the following sense.
If
\mathcal{G}(H)
stands for the metric space of subspaces of
H,
and
g
denotes the gap metric in
\mathcal{G}(H),

then

(4.10)

\|P(t)-P(s)\|=g({\rm Im}\,S(t),{\rm Im}\,S(s))\leq\|S(t)-S(s)\|,\qquad t,s\in[0,1],

(see e.g.
[
31
, Lemma II.10.12 and p. 145, C.10.5]
), so that
P,S_{Z},S_{Z}^{-1}
as well
as
G_{S}:[0,1]\to\mathcal{G}(H),

G_{S}(t)={\rm Im}\,S(t),
are continuous and have modulus of continuity dominated by the modulus of continuity of
S.

5.
Smooth compressions

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.3
,
where we replace continuous compressions by smooth ones at the price
of assumptions much stronger than those of Theorem
1.2
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed.
Given
A\in L(H),
an open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
, and
d\in C^{r}(\Omega)
with
d(\Omega)\subset{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A),
we construct
(u_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{r}(\Omega,H)
such that

(u_{n}(\omega))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal sequence in
H
for every
\omega\in\Omega,

and

\langle Au_{n}(\omega),u_{n}(\omega)\rangle=d(\omega),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\omega\in\Omega.

Moreover, we show
that there exists an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
X
and an isometry-valued function
S\in C^{r}(\Omega,L(X,H))

satisfying

S^{*}(\omega)AS(\omega)=d(\omega)I,\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

For
a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
let
T(a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}):=\hbox{Int}\,{\rm conv}\,\{a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\}
.
Clearly
\{d^{-1}(T(a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime})):a,a^{\prime},a^{\prime\prime}\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)\}

is an open cover of
\Omega
. Then there exists a
C^{\infty}
-partition of unity subordinate to this cover, i.e., there exists a sequence
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{\infty}(\Omega,[0,1])
such that

(a)

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)=1
for all
\omega\in\Omega
;

(b)

for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exist
a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)
such that

{\rm supp}\,f_{k}\subset d^{-1}\left(T\left(a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}\right)\right)
;

(c)

for each
\omega\in\Omega
there exists a neighbourhood
\Omega_{\omega}
of
\omega
such that the set
\{k:{\rm supp}\,f_{k}\cap\Omega_{\omega}\neq\emptyset\}
is finite.

Using Lemma
3.1
,(a) find inductively unit vectors
v_{n,k}^{(i)}\in H,n,k\in\mathbb{N},i=1,2,3
, satisfying

(5.1)

\langle Av_{n,k}^{(i)},v_{n,k}^{(i)}\rangle=a_{k}^{(i)}

for all
n,k,i,
and

(5.2)

v_{n,k}^{(i)}\perp v_{m,l}^{(j)},\ Av_{m,l}^{(j)},\ A^{*}v_{m,l}^{(j)},

whenever
(n,k,i)\neq(m,l,j)
.

If
z\in T\left(a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}\right)
for some
k\in\mathbb{N},
then
z
can be written uniquely as a convex combination of
a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}:

z=\beta_{k}^{(1)}(z)a_{k}^{(1)}+\beta_{k}^{(2)}(z)a_{k}^{(2)}+\beta_{k}^{(3)}(z)a_{k}^{(3)},

where
\beta_{k}^{(1)},\beta_{k}^{(2)},\beta_{k}^{(3)}
are barycentric coordinates
of
z
with respect to
a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},
and
a_{k}^{(3)}.

It is easy to see that
\beta_{k}^{(1)},\beta_{k}^{(2)}
and
\beta_{k}^{(3)}
are linear functions
of the Cartesian coordinates of
a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},
and
a_{k}^{(3)}
(see e.g.
[
16
, Section 2]
)
thus

\{\beta^{(j)}_{k}:1\leq j\leq 3\}\subset C^{\infty}\Bigl(T\left(a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}\right),[0,1]\Bigr)

and
\sum_{j=1}^{3}\beta_{k}^{(j)}(z)=1
for all

z\in T\left(a_{k}^{(1)},a_{k}^{(2)},a_{k}^{(3)}\right)
.

Define now the functions
u_{n}:\Omega\to H,n\in\mathbb{N},
by

u_{n}(\omega)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{f_{k}(\omega)}\left(\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{(1)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)}v_{n,k}^{(1)}+\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{(2)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)}v_{n,k}^{(2)}+\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{(3)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)}v_{n,k}^{(3)}\right).

By construction,
u_{n}\in C^{r}(\Omega)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}.

Moreover, using (
5.2
), we infer that (ii) holds. Since

\|u_{n}(\omega)\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)\left(\beta_{k}^{(1)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)+\beta_{k}^{(2)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)+\beta_{k}^{(3)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)=1

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega\in\Omega,
condition (i) follows.
Moreover, in view of (
5.1
), we have

\displaystyle\langle Au_{n}(\omega),u_{n}(\omega)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)\left(\beta_{k}^{(1)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)\langle Av_{n,k}^{(1)},v_{n,k}^{(1)}\rangle+\beta_{k}^{(2)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)\langle Av_{n,k}^{(2)},v_{n,k}^{(2)}\rangle+\beta_{k}^{(3)}\bigl(d(\omega)\bigr)\langle Av_{n,k}^{(3)},v_{n,k}^{(3)}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)\left(\beta_{k}^{(1)}(d(\omega))a_{k}^{(1)}+\beta_{k}^{(2)}(d(\omega))a_{k}^{(2)}+\beta_{k}^{(3)}(d(\omega))a_{k}^{(3)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\omega)d(\omega)=d(\omega)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega\in\Omega
. Thus, (iii) is satisfied as well.

Let
\omega\in\Omega
.
If
X
is a Hilbert space with the orthonormal basis
(e_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},

then setting

S(\omega)e_{n}=u_{n}(\omega),\qquad\omega\in\Omega,

we obtain an isometry-valued function
S
on
\Omega
such that

S^{*}(\omega)AS(\omega)=d(\omega)I,\qquad\omega\in\Omega.

Finally, let us show that
S
has the required smoothness. Recalling that for
\omega\in\Omega
there is a neighborhood
\Omega_{\omega}

of
\omega
such that the set

F_{\omega}:=\{k:{\rm supp}\,f_{k}\cap\Omega_{\omega}\neq\emptyset\}
is nonempty and finite, we let for every
n\in\mathbb{N}:

M_{n}:=\bigvee\{v_{n,k}^{(1)},v_{n,k}^{(2)},v_{n,k}^{(3)}:\,k\in F_{\omega}\}.

Then
\dim M_{n}=3\,\text{card}\,F_{\omega}<\infty
for all
n,
and the subspaces
M_{n},n\in\mathbb{N},
are mutually orthogonal.
Let
U_{n}:M_{1}\to M_{n}
be the unitary operator defined by
U_{n}v_{1,k}^{(i)}=v_{n,k}^{(i)},
for all

k\in F_{\omega}
and
i=1,2,3.

Then for all
\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega_{\omega}
and
x\in X,

we have

S(\omega^{\prime})(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle u_{n}(\omega^{\prime})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle U_{n}u_{1}(\omega^{\prime}).

Hence, arguing inductively,
for every
k\in\mathbb{N},1\leq k\leq r,

(5.3)

S^{(k)}(\omega^{\prime})(x)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle x,e_{n}\rangle U_{n}u_{1}^{(k)}(\omega^{\prime}),\qquad\omega^{\prime}\in\Omega,

with
S^{(r)}\in C(\Omega,L(X,H)),
i.e.
S\in C^{r}(\Omega,L(X,H))
since the choice of
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
was arbitrary.

The cases
r=\infty
and
r=0
can be considered completely analogously.

\hfill\qed

Remark 5.1
.

It is easy to see that one cannot replace smoothness of
u
and
d
in
Theorem
1.3
by their analyticity.
Indeed, if
A\in L(H)
and, for instance, one has

\langle Au(z),u(z)\rangle=d(z)
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
, with
u
and
d

analytic in
\mathbb{D}
, then differentiating gives

2\,{\rm Re}\,\langle Au^{\prime}(z),u(z)\rangle=d^{\prime}(z)
for
z\in\mathbb{D}
.
The open mapping property then yields that
d^{\prime}
is constant, so
d
is
necessarily a linear function.

Remark 5.2
.

Along the lines of the proof above, one can show
that if
A\in L(H),

r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0,\infty\},

and
D:\Omega\to L(X)
is a
C^{r}
-function such that
{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A)\supset\{z:|z|\leq\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\|D(\omega)\|\},
then there exists an isometry-valued
C^{r}
-function
S:\Omega\to L(X,H)
satisfying

S^{*}(\omega)AS(\omega)=D(\omega)

for all
\omega\in\Omega
. However, this statement is technically more demanding
and, apart from Bourin’s theorem, relies on an appropriate modification of Proposition
4.6
.
To keep this paper within reasonable limits, its proof will be given elsewhere.

6.
Diagonals of operator-valued functions

In this section we will prove Theorem
1.4
, one of the main results of this paper.
We start with the next auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
.
Then there exist mutually orthogonal subspaces
M_{k}\subset H,k\in\mathbb{N},
such that

(6.1)

W_{e}\bigl(P_{M_{k}}A(t){\upharpoonright}_{M_{k}}\bigr)=W_{e}(A(t))

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set of all rational numbers in
[0,1]
.
For each
q\in Q
choose a countable set
S_{q}
dense in
W_{e}(A(q))
.
Let
S=\left\{(q,l):q\in Q,l\in S_{q}\right\}
.
Let
f:\mathbb{N}\to S\times\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be a bijection. Let

f_{1}:\mathbb{N}\to Q,\,f_{2}:\mathbb{N}\to\cup_{q\in Q}S_{q},\,f_{3},\,f_{4}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}

be the functions satisfying

f(n)=\bigl((f_{1}(n),f_{2}(n)),f_{3}(n),f_{4}(n)\bigr)

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Using Lemma
3.1
, (a), construct inductively mutually orthogonal unit vectors
x_{n}\in H,n\in\mathbb{N},
such that

\left|\left\langle A(f_{1}(n))x_{n},x_{n}\right\rangle-f_{2}(n)\right|<\frac{1}{f_{3}(n)}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}.
For every
k\in\mathbb{N},
set
M_{k}=\bigvee\{x_{n}:f_{4}(n)=k\}
. Clearly the subspaces
M_{k},k\in\mathbb{N},
are infinite-dimensional and mutually orthogonal. Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
q\in Q
and
l\in S_{q}
.
For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
n_{j}
with

f_{1}(n_{j})=q
,
f_{2}(n_{j})=l
,
f_{3}(n_{j})=j
and
f_{4}(n_{j})=k
. So

|\langle A(q)x_{n_{j}},x_{n_{j}}\rangle-l|<j^{-1},\qquad j\geq 1,

and the vectors
(x_{n_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}
form an orthonormal sequence.
Therefore,
l\in W_{e}(P_{M_{k}}A(q){\upharpoonright}_{M_{k}})
. Since
S_{q}
is dense in
W_{e}(A(q)),

we have
W_{e}(A(q))=W_{e}(P_{M_{k}}A(q){\upharpoonright}_{M_{k}}).
Hence, as
Q
is dense in
[0,1]
and the map

A\to W_{e}(A)
is continuous (see Section
3
),
we infer that (
6.1
) holds.
∎

We proceed
with the next version of Proposition
4.3
. Here, identifying
v(t)
with rank one isometries
V(t)
, we do not assume that
\dim Z(V)<\infty
as in Proposition
4.3
, which causes some technical complications.
Following the strategy of the proof of Theorem
1.2
, this statement will be needed to prepare the inductive step in the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

It will be convenient to denote by
S_{H}
the unit sphere in
H
, i.e.
S_{H}=\{x\in H:\|x\|=1\}
.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H)),

and let
d\in C([0,1])

be such that
d(t)\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Let

\theta=\min\left\{{\rm dist}\,(d(t),\partial W_{e}(A(t))):0\leq t\leq 1\right\}.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,\theta)
suppose that
v\in C([0,1],S_{H})
satisfies

\bigl|\langle A(t)v(t),v(t)\rangle-d(t)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\varepsilon)
and every
subspace
M\subset H
such that

W_{e}(P_{M}A(t){\upharpoonright}_{M})=W_{e}(A(t)),\qquad t\in[0,1],

there exists
\widetilde{v}\in C([0,1],H)
satisfying for all
t\in[0,1],

(i)

\bigl|\langle A(t)\widetilde{v}(t),\widetilde{v}(t)\rangle-d(t)\bigr|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},

(ii)

\|\widetilde{v}(t)-v(t)\|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}},

(iii)

\widetilde{v}(t)\in\left(v(t)\vee M\right).

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
d\equiv 0
(if not, then replace
A
by
A-d
).
We can also assume that
\|A(t)\|\leq 1
for all
t\in[0,1]
and

\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 4\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}
.

The set
\left\{v(t),A(t)v(t),A^{*}(t)v(t):0\leq t\leq 1\right\}
is compact.
So by Lemma
3.2
, there exists a subspace
\widetilde{M}\subset M
such that
\dim M/\widetilde{M}<\infty
, and for all
m\in\widetilde{M}
with
\|m\|=1
and all
t\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle|\langle v(t),m\rangle|\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}/8,

\displaystyle|\langle A(t)v(t),m\rangle|\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}/8,

\displaystyle|\langle A^{*}(t)v(t),m\rangle|\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\prime}/8.

Let

a=\varepsilon\theta^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\widetilde{d}(t):=a^{-1}(a-1)\left\langle A(t)v(t),v(t)\right\rangle,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Clearly
\widetilde{d}\in C([0,1])

and

|\widetilde{d}(t)|\leq\frac{(1-a)\varepsilon}{a}<\frac{\varepsilon}{a}=\theta,\qquad t\in[0,1].

By Lemma
4.2
, there exists
r\in C([0,1],S_{\widetilde{M}})
such that for all
t\in[0,1],

\bigl|\bigl\langle A(t)r(t),r(t)\bigr\rangle-\widetilde{d}(t)\bigr|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8}.

For every
t\in[0,1]
let

v^{\prime}(t)=\sqrt{1-a}\,v(t)+\sqrt{a}\,r(t).

Then
v^{\prime}\in C([0,1],H)

and for all
t\in[0,1],

\displaystyle\bigl|\langle A(t)v^{\prime}(t),v^{\prime}(t)\rangle\bigr|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigl|(1-a)\bigl\langle A(t)v(t),v(t)\rangle+a\langle A(t)r(t),r(t)\bigr\rangle+2\sqrt{a(1-a)}{\rm Re}\,\bigl\langle A(t)v(t),r(t)\bigr\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|(1-a)\langle A(t)v(t),v(t)\rangle+a\widetilde{d}(t)\Bigr|+\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8}+\sqrt{a(1-a)}\cdot\frac{2\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{4},

since
\sqrt{a(1-a)}\leq 1/2
.
Moreover, for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[0,1],

\|v^{\prime}(t)-v(t)\|\leq 1-\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{a}\leq 2\sqrt{a}\leq 2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}.

Fix
t\in[0,1].
We have

\displaystyle\|v^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}=

\displaystyle(1-a)\|v(t)\|^{2}+a\|r(t)\|^{2}+2\sqrt{a(1-a)}{\rm Re}\,\langle v(t),r(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+2\sqrt{a(1-a)}{\rm Re}\,\langle v(t),r(t)\rangle.

So

\bigl|1-\|v^{\prime}(t)\|\bigr|\leq\bigl|1-\|v^{\prime}(t)\|^{2}\bigr|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{8}.

Let
\widetilde{v}(t)=\frac{v^{\prime}(t)}{\|v^{\prime}(t)\|}
. Then

\widetilde{v}(t)\in S_{H}
and

\|\widetilde{v}(t)-v^{\prime}(t)\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon^{\prime}/8}-1\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/4.

Hence

\|\widetilde{v}(t)-v(t)\|\leq\|\widetilde{v}(t)-v^{\prime}(t)\|+\|v^{\prime}(t)-v(t)\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}/4+2\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}

and

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle A(t)\widetilde{v}(t),\widetilde{v}(t)\right\rangle\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle A(t)(\widetilde{v}(t)-v^{\prime}(t)),\widetilde{v}(t)\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle A(t)v^{\prime}(t),\widetilde{v}(t)-v^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\right|+\left|\left\langle A(t)v^{\prime}(t),v^{\prime}(t)\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{4}+\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon^{\prime}}{4}=\varepsilon^{\prime},

so that (ii) and (i) hold.
Moreover, by construction, it is direct that
\widetilde{v}(t)\in\left(M\vee v(t)\right)
for all
t\in[0,1],
and (iii) holds as well.
∎

The next statement will complement Theorem
4.4
in realizing an inductive step
in the proof of Theorem

. At the same time, its proof
also relies on an inductive argument, which is made possible by Proposition

.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H)),

d\in C([0,1]),
and
v\in C([0,1],S_{H})
satisfy the assumptions of Proposition
6.2
.
If
M\subset H
is a subspace such that
W_{e}(P_{M}A(t){\upharpoonright}_{M})=W_{e}(A(t)),

t\in[0,1],

then there exists
u\in C([0,1],H)
such that for all
t\in[0,1],

(i)

\langle A(t)u(t),u(t)\rangle=d(t),\quad\|u(t)\|=1;

(ii)

\|u(t)-v(t)\|\leq 12\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}};

(iii)

u(t)\in\left(v(t)\vee M\right);

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
d\equiv 0
.

Setting
v_{0}=v,
we use Proposition
6.2
to construct inductively continuous mappings
v_{n}:[0,1]\to S_{H},n\in\mathbb{N},
such that

\bigl|\langle A(t)v_{n}(t),v_{n}(t)\rangle\bigr|\leq\varepsilon 2^{-n},\qquad\|v_{n+1}(t)-v_{n}(t)\|\leq 3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta 2^{n}}}

and
v_{n}(t)\in\left(v(t)\vee M\right)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

Let

u(t)=\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{n}(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then, for every
t\in[0,1],

\langle A(t)u(t),u(t)\rangle=0
and

\displaystyle\|u(t)-v(t)\|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\|v_{n+1}(t)-v_{n}(t)\|\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta 2^{n}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 3\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}2^{-n/2}\leq 12\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\theta}}.

Moreover, by construction,
u(t)\in\left(M\vee v(t)\right)
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
∎

Now we are ready to prove Theorem

via an inductive argument based
on Lemma

.

Proof of Theorem
1.4

Consider
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
and
d_{n}\in C([0,1]),n\in\mathbb{N},
satisfying
d_{n}(t)\in{\rm Int}\,W_{e}(A(t))
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]

and

\theta:=\inf\{{\rm dist}\,(d_{n}(t),\partial W_{e}(A(t))):n\in\mathbb{N},t\in[0,1]\}>0.

We will construct

v_{n}\in C([0,1],H),n\in\mathbb{N},
such that
(v_{n}(t))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
H
for every
t\in[0,1],

and

\langle A(t)v_{n}(t),v_{n}(t)\rangle=d_{n}(t),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in[0,1].

Moreover, our proof will reveal that the equicontinuity of
\{d_{n}:n\geq 1\}

implies
the equicontinuity of
\{v_{n}:n\geq 1\}.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\|A(t)\|\leq 1
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
Fix a sequence
(y_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
of unit vectors in
H
such that
\bigvee_{m=1}^{\infty}y_{m}=H
.
Note that each
n\in\mathbb{N}
can be written uniquely as

n=2^{r(n)-1}(2s(n)-1)

with integers
r(n),s(n)\in\mathbb{N}.

Fix
\eta\in(0,\sqrt{\theta}/2\sqrt{2}),
and
let

\eta_{k}=\frac{\eta}{2^{r(k)/2}{s(k)^{1/2}}},\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

The functions
v_{k}:[0,1]\to S_{H},k\geq 1,
will be constructed inductively.

Using Lemma
6.1
, find mutually orthogonal subspaces
M_{k},k\in\mathbb{N},
of
H
such that
W_{e}(P_{M_{k}}A(t){\upharpoonright}_{M_{k}})=W_{e}(A(t))
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N},
and suppose that we have already constructed continuous functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1}:[0,1]\to S_{H}
such that

(1)

the vectors
v_{1}(t),\dots,v_{k-1}(t)
are mutually orthogonal for each
t\in[0,1]
;

(2)

\langle A(t)v_{j}(t),v_{j}(t)\rangle=d_{j}(t)
for all
j\leq k-1,t\in[0,1]
;

(3)

if
j\leq k-1,t\in[0,1],
then
v_{j}(t)\in\bigvee_{i=1}^{j}\left(M_{i}\vee y_{r(i)}\right)
;

(4)

if
H_{j}(t)=\bigvee_{i=1}^{j}v_{i}(t)
, then for all
j\leq k-1
and
t\in[0,1],

{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{r(j)},H_{j}(t))\leq\prod_{i=1}^{s(j)}\left(1-\eta^{2}_{2^{r(j)-1}(2i-1)}\right).

Moreover,
if
\{d_{n}:n=1,2,\dots\}
are equicontinuous with a modulus of continuity
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
is the modulus of continuity constructed in Theorem
4.4
, then we also assume that

(5) there exist functions
u_{j}:[0,1]\to S_{H},

1\leq j\leq k-1,
admitting the modulus of continuity
\delta^{\prime}
such that

\|u_{j}(t)-v_{j}(t)\|\leq 27\sqrt{2}\eta_{k}{\theta}^{-1/2}.

for all
t\in[0,1]
and
1\leq j\leq k-1.

Observing that if
k=1
, then (1)–(5) are formally satisfied,
we construct a function
v_{k}
in the following way.

For each
t\in[0,1]
write

y_{k}(t)=(I-P_{H_{k-1}(t)})y_{r(k)}.

Let

b_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{s(k)}(1-\eta_{2^{(r(k)-1)}(2i-1)})>0\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{k}(t)=\max\{b_{k},\|y_{k}(t)\|\}

for all

Finally, choose
\delta_{k}>0
such that

12\sqrt{\frac{2\delta_{k}}{\theta}}\leq\eta_{k}/2.

Note that the set

\left\{\frac{A(t)y_{k}(t)}{b_{k}(t)},\,\frac{A^{*}(t)y_{k}(t)}{b_{k}(t)}:\,t\in[0,1]\right\}

is compact. Therefore by Lemma
3.2
there exists a subspace

\widetilde{M}_{k}\subset M_{k}
such that

\dim M_{k}/\widetilde{M}_{k}<\infty,\qquad\widetilde{M}_{k}\perp\{y_{r(j)}:1\leq j\leq k\},

and, moreover,

\left|\left\langle\frac{A(t)y_{k}(t)}{b_{k}(t)},m\right\rangle\right|\leq\delta_{k},\qquad\left|\left\langle\frac{A^{*}(t)y_{k}(t)}{b_{k}(t)},m\right\rangle\right|\leq\delta_{k},

for all
m\in\widetilde{M}_{k}
with
\|m\|=1
and
t\in[0,1]
.

By Theorem
4.4
, there exists a continuous function
u_{k}:[0,1]\to S_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}
such that

\langle A(t)u_{k}(t),u_{k}(t)\rangle=d_{k}(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Observe that if

\{d_{n}:n\geq 1\}
are equicontinuous and admit the same modulus of continuity
\delta
,
then we may assume that
u_{k}
admit
\delta^{\prime}
, where
\delta^{\prime}
is the modulus of continuity constructed in Theorem
4.4
.

Let

\widetilde{d}_{k}(t)=\frac{b_{k}^{2}(t)}{b^{2}_{k}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}d_{k}(t)-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}\langle A(t)y_{k}(t),y_{k}(t)\rangle}{b_{k}^{2}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}.

We have

\displaystyle\bigl|\widetilde{d}_{k}(t)-d_{k}(t)\bigr|\leq

\displaystyle\left|1-\frac{b_{k}^{2}(t)}{b^{2}_{k}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}\right|+\left|\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}\langle A(t)y_{k}(t),y_{k}(t)\rangle}{b_{k}^{2}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}\leq\frac{2\eta_{k}^{2}}{1-\eta_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4\eta_{k}^{2}\leq 4\eta^{2}\leq\theta/2.

So

\left|\langle A(t)u_{k}(t),u_{k}(t)\rangle-\widetilde{d}_{k}(t)\right|\leq 4\eta_{k}^{2}\leq\theta/2

and

{\rm dist}\,(\widetilde{d}_{k}(t),\partial W_{e}(A(t)))\geq\theta/2

for all
t\in[0,1]
.

By Theorem
6.3
, there exists a continuous function
u_{k}^{(1)}:[0,1]\to S_{\widetilde{M}_{k}}
such that

\bigl\langle A(t)u_{k}^{(1)}(t),u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\bigr\rangle=\widetilde{d}_{k}(t)

for all
t\in[0,1]
and

\|u_{k}^{(1)}(t)-u_{k}(t)\|\leq\frac{24\sqrt{2}\eta_{k}}{\theta^{1/2}}.

Define

u_{k}^{(2)}(t)=\sqrt{1-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)}}u_{k}^{(1)}(t)+\frac{\eta_{k}y_{k}(t)}{b_{k}(t)},\qquad t\in[0,1].

Since
u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\in\widetilde{M}_{k}
and

y_{k}(t)\in\left(\bigvee_{j=1}^{k-1}u_{j}(t)\vee y_{r(k)}\right)\subset\bigvee_{j=1}^{k-1}M_{j}\vee\bigvee_{j=1}^{k}y_{r(j)},

we have
y_{k}(t)\perp u_{k}^{(1)}(t)
and

\|u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\|=1
for all
t\in[0,1]
.
In addition, in view of

u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\in\left(\widetilde{M}_{k}\vee y_{k}(t)\right),

we also have
u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\perp H_{k-1}(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

Thus,

\displaystyle\left|\Bigl\langle A(t)u_{k}^{(2)}(t),u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\Bigr\rangle-d_{k}(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|\Bigl(1-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b^{2}_{k}(t)}\Bigr)\langle A(t)u_{k}^{(1)}(t),u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\rangle+\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}}{b^{2}_{k}(t)}\langle A(t)y_{k}(t),y_{k}(t)\rangle-d_{k}(t)\Bigr|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{k}}{b_{k}(t)}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{b_{k}^{2}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)}}\Bigl|\langle A(t)y_{k}(t),u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\rangle+\langle A(t)u_{k}^{(1)}(t),y_{k}(t)\rangle\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Bigl|\frac{b_{k}^{2}(t)-\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)}\widetilde{d}_{k}(t)+\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)}\langle A(t)y_{k}(t),y_{k}(t)\rangle-d_{k}(t)\Bigr|+2\eta_{k}\delta_{k}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\delta_{k}.

Moreover,

\|u_{k}^{(2)}(t)-u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\|\leq 1-\sqrt{1-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}^{2}(t)}}+\frac{\eta_{k}\|y_{k}(t)\|}{b_{k}(t)}\leq\Bigl(1-\sqrt{1-\eta_{k}^{2}}\Bigr)+\eta_{k}\leq 2\eta_{k}.

By Theorem
6.3
, there exists a continuous function
v_{k}:[0,1]\to S_{H}
such that
for all
t\in[0,1],

v_{k}(t)\in u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\vee\widetilde{M}_{k}\subset\bigvee_{j=1}^{k}\left(M_{j}\vee y_{r(j)}\right),

\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\|\leq 12\sqrt{\frac{2\delta_{k}}{\theta}}\leq\eta_{k}/2,

and

\langle A(t)v_{k}(t),v_{k}(t)\rangle=d_{k}(t).

In particular,

v_{k}(t)\perp v_{1}(t),\dots,v_{k-1}(t),\quad t\in[0,1].

We conclude that (1)–(3) hold, and it remains to establish (4).

To this aim, observe that if
\|y_{k}(t)\|<b_{k},
then (4) is satisfied trivially.
If otherwise
\|y_{k}(t)\|\geq b_{k},
then
b_{k}(t)=\|y_{k}(t)\|
and

{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{k}(t),H_{k}(t))=\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}-|\langle y_{k}(t),v_{k}(t)\rangle|^{2},

where

\displaystyle|\langle y_{k}(t),v_{k}(t)\rangle|\geq

\displaystyle|\langle y_{k}(t),u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\rangle|-\|y_{k}(t)\|\cdot\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\|

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{\eta_{k}\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}}{b_{k}(t)}-\|y_{k}(t)\|\cdot\eta_{k}/2\geq\frac{\eta_{k}\|y_{k}(t)\|}{2}.

So

{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{k}(t),H_{k}(t))\leq\|y_{k}(t)\|^{2}\Bigl(1-\frac{\eta_{k}^{2}}{4}\Bigr)\leq\prod_{i=1}^{s(k)}\left(1-\frac{\eta^{2}_{2^{r(k)}(2i-1)}}{4}\right)

by the induction assumption.

As a result of this inductive construction, we obtain the sequence
(v_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset C([0,1],H)

such that
(v_{k}(t))_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal system for each
t\in[0,1]

and

\langle A(t)v_{k}(t),v_{k}(t)\rangle=d_{k}(t)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
.

Moreover, by construction, if the functions

\{d_{k}:k\geq 1\}
are equicontinuous with modulus of continuity
\delta,
then the functions

\{u_{k}:k\geq 1\}
are equicontinuous with modulus of continuity
\delta^{\prime}
constructed in Theorem
4.4
. In addition, for all
t\in[0,1],
we have

\displaystyle\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}^{(2)}(t)\|+\|u_{k}^{(2)}(t)-u_{k}^{(1)}(t)\|+\|u_{k}^{(1)}(t)-u_{k}(t)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta_{k}}{2}+2\eta_{k}+\frac{24\sqrt{2}\eta_{k}}{\theta^{1/2}}\leq 27\sqrt{2}\eta_{k}\theta^{-1/2}.

So
\sup\{\|v_{k}(t)-u_{k}(t)\|:t\in[0,1]\}\to 0
as
k\to\infty,
hence
\{v_{k}:k\geq 1\}
are equicontinuous with modulus of continuity
\delta^{\prime}.

It remains to prove that the sequence
(v_{k}(t))_{k=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
H
for every
t\in[0,1]
. To show this property, let
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
t\in[0,1]
be fixed. Then

{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{m},\bigvee_{j=1}^{\infty}v_{j}(t))=\lim_{s\to\infty}{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{m},H_{2^{m-1}(2s-1)}(t))\leq\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(1-\frac{\eta^{2}_{2^{m-1}(2i-1)}}{4}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle\ln{\rm dist}\,^{2}(y_{m},\bigvee_{j=1}^{\infty}v_{j}(t))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\ln\left(1-\frac{\eta^{2}_{2^{m-1}(2i-1)}}{4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^{2}_{2^{m-1}(2i-1)}}{4}=-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\eta^{2}}{i2^{m+2}}=-\infty,

so that
y_{m}\in\bigvee_{k=1}^{\infty}v_{k}(t)
for each
t\in[0,1]
.
Since
\bigvee_{m\in\mathbb{N}}y_{m}=H
, the property follows,
and thus the proof is complete.

\hfill\qed

7.
Compressions and diagonals of operator-valued functions with selfadjoint values

In this section, we show how to obtain results analogous to those in Sections
4

and
6
for selfadjoint
A.

Here we formulate the results using more explicit spectral terms, which make them particularly
attractive.
Recall that for a selfadjoint operator
A\in L(H)
we have
\overline{W(A)}={\rm conv}\,\sigma(A)
and
W_{e}(A)={\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A),
where
\sigma_{e}(A)
denotes the essential spectrum of
A

and all of the sets are subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. Thus,
{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}W_{e}(A)={\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A),
where
{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}
stands for the interior relative to
\mathbb{R}.

Lemma
3.1
remains valid after replacing
{\rm Int}\,W_{e}
by
{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}W_{e}.

We start with obtaining a counterpart of Theorem
1.2

for selfadjoint
A
and
D.

In the selfadjoint case, the factorization approach from Section
4

simplifies and requires just two factors to separate the two time-dependent sides of
(
1.4
).

The next results are analogues of Lemma
4.2
, Proposition
4.3
and Proposition
4.5
,
respectively, and have similar, and in fact more direct, proofs
(using relative interiors instead of interiors).
The statements are formulated for selfadjoint
A(t)
,
t\in[0,1]
, with
{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma_{e}(A(t))\supset[a,b]
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
,
a<b
, and all
t\in[0,1]
.
To deduce them from the corresponding statements of the preceding sections, it suffices to pass to
A(t)-(a+b)/2
and
D(t)-(a+b)/2
in place of
A(t)
and
D(t)
for
t\in[0,1]
.
For similar situations with general
A
and selfadjoint
A
considered
in a time-independent setting, see e.g.
[
34
]

and
[
29
]
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
satisfy
A^{*}(t)=A(t),t\in[0,1],
and assume that there exist
a,b\in\mathbb{R},a<b,
such that
[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A(t))

for all
t\in[0,1]
. For a Hilbert space
X
with
\dim X<\infty
, let
D\in C([0,1],L(X))

be such that

D^{*}(t)=D(t)
and
\sigma(D(t))\subset[a,b]
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then for every subspace
L\subset H
,

{\rm codim}\,L<\infty,
and every
\varepsilon>0,

there exists
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that for all
t\in[0,1],

(i)

V(t)
is an isometry;

(ii)

\dim Z(V)<\infty
and
Z(V)\subset L
;

(iii)

\|V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)-D(t)\|\leq\varepsilon
;

(iv)

{\rm Lip}\,(V)<\infty,
and
{\rm Lip}\,(V)
depends only on
\varepsilon
and the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
.

Proposition 7.2
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
satisfy
A^{*}(t)=A(t),t\in[0,1],
and
[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A(t))
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R},a<b,
and all
t\in[0,1].

Let
X
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and let
D\in C([0,1],L(X))
be such that

D^{*}(t)=D(t)

and
\sigma(D(t))\subset[a,b]
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Set

\theta=\min_{t\in[0,1]}\min\{\|b-D(t)\|,\|D(t)-a\|\}.

Given
\varepsilon,l>0,
and a subspace
L\subset H,{\rm codim}\,L<\infty,
suppose that

V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
is isometry-valued,

\dim Z(V)<\infty
,
Z(V)\subset L,

{\rm Lip}\,(V)\leq l,
and

\|V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)-D(t)\|\leq\varepsilon,\qquad t\in[0,1].

Then for every
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
there exist
\tilde{l}
depending only on
\varepsilon^{\prime},l
and
the moduli of continuity
\delta_{A}
and
\delta_{D}
and

\tilde{V}\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that

(i)

\tilde{V}
is isometry-valued,

(ii)

Z(\tilde{V})\subset L
,
\dim Z(\tilde{V})<\infty
and
{\rm Lip}\,(\tilde{V})\leq\tilde{l},

(iii)

\|\tilde{V}^{*}(t)A(t)\tilde{V}(t)-D(t)\|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime},\,t\in[0,1],

(iv)

\|\tilde{V}(t)-V(t)\|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\theta}},\,t\in[0,1].

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
be such that
A^{*}(t)=A(t),t\in[0,1],
and there are
a,b\in\mathbb{R},a<b,
satisfying
[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A(t))

for all
t\in[0,1].
Assume that
X
is a separable Hilbert space,
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
with
\dim X_{1}=\dim X_{2}=\infty
, and
D\in L(X)
is given by

D=a^{\prime}I\oplus b^{\prime}I,

a<a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<b
. Then there exists
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
such that
V
is isometry-valued,
and

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=D,\qquad t\in[0,1].

We will need the next auxiliary statement serving a role similar to Proposition
4.6

in the particular setting of selfadjoint operators.
It is proved in a generality greater than needed for our reasoning,
but its proof does not deviate much from the proof of a simpler version.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
A\in L(H)
be such that
A=A^{*}
and
[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A)

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R},a<b.

If
Z
is a separable Hilbert space and
D\in C([0,1],L(Z))

satisfies
D^{*}(t)=D(t)
and
\sigma(D(t))\subset[a,b]

for all

t\in[0,1]
,
then there exists an isometry-valued
W\in C([0,1],L(Z,H))
such that

(7.1)

W^{*}(t)AW(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Note first that there exist orthogonal infinite-dimensional subspaces
H_{a},H_{b}\subset H
such that

P_{H_{a}\oplus H_{b}}A{\upharpoonright}_{H_{a}\oplus H_{b}}=\begin{pmatrix}aI&0\cr 0&bI\end{pmatrix}.

Indeed, using Lemma
3.1
we construct inductively orthonormal vectors
e_{1},f_{1},

e_{2},f_{2},e_{3},\dots
in
H
such that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\langle Ae_{j},e_{j}\rangle=a
,
\langle Af_{j},f_{j}\rangle=b
,

e_{j}\perp\{e_{1},\dots,e_{j-1},f_{1},\dots,f_{j-1},Ae_{1},\dots,Ae_{j-1},Af_{1},\dots,Af_{j-1}\},

and

f_{j}\perp\{e_{1},\dots,e_{j},f_{1},\dots,f_{j-1},Ae_{1},\dots,Ae_{j},Af_{1},\dots,Af_{j-1}\}.

Set
H_{a}=\bigvee\{e_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
H_{b}=\bigvee\{f_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
Then the compression
P_{H_{a}\oplus H_{b}}A{\upharpoonright}_{H_{a}\oplus H_{b}}
has the required form.

Choose now isometries
W_{a}:Z\to H_{a}
and
W_{b}:Z\to H_{b},
and for every
t\in[0,1]

define

W(t):Z\to H_{a}\oplus H_{b}
by

\displaystyle W(t)x=\frac{W_{a}(b-D(t))^{1/2}x}{(b-a)^{1/2}}\oplus\frac{W_{b}(D(t)-a)^{1/2}x}{(b-a)^{1/2}},\qquad x\in Z.

Then for all
x\in Z
and
t\in[0,1],
we have

\displaystyle\|W(t)x\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{\|(b-D(t))^{1/2}x\|^{2}}{b-a}+\frac{\|(D(t)-a)^{1/2}x\|^{2}}{b-a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\langle(b-D(t))x,x\rangle}{b-a}+\frac{\langle(D(t)-a)x,x\rangle}{b-a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(b-a)\|x\|^{2}}{b-a}=\|x\|^{2},

i.e.
W(t)
is an isometry.
Moreover, an argument similar to the one for
\Delta
in the proof of Proposition
4.6

shows that
W\in C([0,1],L(Z,H)).

Finally, for every
x\in Z,

\displaystyle\langle W^{*}(t)AW(t)x,x\rangle=\langle AW(t)x,W(t)x\rangle=

\displaystyle\frac{a}{b-a}\langle(b-D(t))x,x\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{b}{b-a}\langle(D(t)-a)x,x\rangle=\langle D(t)x,x\rangle,

so that
W^{*}AW=D
for all
t\in[0,1],
as required.
∎

The next statement is a counterpart of Theorem
1.2

in the case of operator functions
A
with selfadjoint values.

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
be such that
A^{*}(t)=A(t),t\in[0,1],
and
[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A(t)),

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R},a<b,
and all
t\in[0,1].

Assume that
Z
is a separable Hilbert space,
and
D\in C([0,1],L(Z))
satisfies
D^{*}(t)=D(t)
and
\sigma(D(t))\subset(a,b).

Then there exists
S\in C([0,1],L(Z,H))
such that
S
is isometry-valued and

S^{*}(t)A(t)S(t)=D(t),\qquad t\in[0,1].

Proof.

Choose
a<a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}<b
such that
\sigma(D(t))\subset[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
for all
t\in[0,1].

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces,
X=X_{1}\oplus X_{2}
and

D_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}=\begin{pmatrix}a^{\prime}I&0\cr 0&b^{\prime}I\end{pmatrix}.

Using Lemmas
7.3
and
7.4
, one
obtains isometry-valued functions
V\in C([0,1],L(X,H))
and
W\in C([0,1],L(Z,X))
satisfying

V^{*}(t)A(t)V(t)=D_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad W^{*}(t)D_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}W(t)=D(t),\quad t\in[0,1].

Now it suffices to set
S(t)=V(t)W(t),t\in[0,1].

∎

We finish this section with the following variant of Theorem
1.4

for selfadjoint
A,
where again spectral terms makes the formulation
more transparent.

Theorem 7.6
.

Let
A\in C([0,1],L(H))
have selfadjoint values, and
d_{n}\in C([0,1]),n\in\mathbb{N}.

Assume that there are
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that

d_{n}(t)\in[a,b]\subset{\rm Int}\,_{\mathbb{R}}{\rm conv}\,\sigma_{e}(A(t)),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in[0,1].

Then there exist
(v_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C([0,1],H)
such that

(v_{n}(t))_{n=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
H
for every
t\in[0,1],

and

\langle A(t)v_{n}(t),v_{n}(t)\rangle=d_{n}(t),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\quad t\in[0,1].

Moreover, the sequence
(v_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is equicontinuous if the sequence
(d_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
is so.

The proof of this result is completely analogous to the proof of Theorem
1.4
,
with substantial simplifications caused by the selfadjointness of
A
.
