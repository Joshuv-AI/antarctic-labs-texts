Holistic Pelvic Floor: Pelvic Floor Stress Dysfunction Diseases
Authors: Xingqi Wang, Zhenhua Gao, Ling Li, Daoming Tian, Yuan Li, Jihong Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFemale pelvic floor dysfunction diseases (PFD) comprise pelvic floor stress dysfunction diseases (PFSD) and non-pelvic floor stress dysfunction diseases (non-PFSD), with PFSD accounting for the majority of pelvic floor issues. PFSD is a group of diseases caused by pelvic floor stress support function injury and stress imbalance, mainly associated with pelvic floor support structure defects, weaknesses, injuries, pregnancy, transvaginal delivery, age, etc. A key to resolving PFSD lies in establishing the concept of holistic pelvic floor and therapeutic strategies based on functional pelvic floor biomechanics, etiology, and a holistic view of the disease. This article presents our perspective on current advances, challenges, and future directions in understanding and treating PFSD, emphasizing the need for a paradigm shift from symptom-oriented to mechanism-based management.Keywords: Holistic pelvic floor, Pelvic floor stress dysfunction diseases, Pelvic organ prolapse, Stress urinary incontinence, Biomechanic, Etiology

Introduction

Female pelvic floor dysfunction diseases (PFD) [1, 2] constitute a collective of disorders affecting all pelvic floor organs and encompassing syndromes in the disciplines of urology, gynecology and obstetrics, and proctology. Traditionally, pelvic floor disorders have been approached as distinct entities, which can hinder a holistic understanding of the variously named conditions that comprise this group. With the development of medical subspecialties, pelvic floor diseases with different clinical manifestations are artificially classified for diagnosis and treatment in different departments, e.g., urology, gynecology, anorectal surgery, orthopedics, proctology, and plastic surgery. This classification results in the complete isolation of pelvic floor diseases. The complexity of pelvic floor diseases often results in symptoms dominated by specific clinical manifestations, such as stress urinary incontinence (SUI), pelvic organ prolapse (POP), vaginal laxity syndrome (VLS), frequent and urgent urination, pelvic pain, etc. Therefore, the most direct therapy is to resolve major clinical symptoms that bother patients to the greatest extent possible, such as raising urethral pressure for SUI, restoring cervix to its normal anatomical position for POP. Such diagnosis and treatment methods definitely can be efficacious to some extent under specific historical conditions over a period of time. However, owing to the limited understanding of the nature and interrelationships of pelvic floor diseases, a treatment approach based on a rigid pattern of thinking cannot restore the pre-natal functional status of the female pelvic floor. In addition, technological advancements can only incrementally improve upon existing therapeutic outcomes, and a significant breakthrough in overall therapeutic efficacy is unlikely to occur beyond a certain plateau. Therefore, it is important to find out the core cause of pelvic floor diseases by thinking outside the box and analyzing manifestations layer by layer. In addition, logical reasoning facilitates the deduction of the development and progression of pelvic floor diseases, as well as the intrinsic connections between pelvic floor diseases with different names. This understanding is essential for fundamentally renewing the comprehension of the pelvic floor and for developing new treatment models. This clinical perspective article proposes a novel theoretical framework and classification system for PFD. It is likely that the concept of holistic pelvic floor and therapeutic strategies based on functional pelvic floor biomechanics, etiology, and a holistic view of the disease will suggest new directions for the resolution of functional pelvic floor diseases.

The Concept of Pelvic Floor Stress Dysfunction Diseases

The recognition of PFD has highlighted the importance of mechanical equilibrium in symptom development. From this perspective, PFD comprises pelvic floor stress dysfunction diseases (PFSD) and non-pelvic floor stress dysfunction diseases (Non-PFSD), with PFSD accounting for the majority of pelvic floor issues.

PFSD

PFSD is a group of diseases caused by pelvic floor stress support function injury and stress imbalance, mainly associated with pelvic floor support structure defects, weaknesses, injuries, pregnancy, transvaginal delivery, age, etc. Clinically, pelvic floor stress imbalance is manifested as SUI, POP, VLS, and dysporia such as fecal incontinence (FI) caused by some tissue injuries, which often occur in combination with others. These conditions represent diverse clinical phenotypes of a shared underlying stress imbalance, varying according to the specific injured anatomical structures [3]. It is essential to explicitly state that “PFSD” is introduced herein as a novel conceptual framework and etiological taxonomy, rather than an established diagnostic entity. This terminology is not intended to replace the standardized, symptom- and anatomy-based diagnostic criteria defined by the International Continence Society (ICS) or the International Urogynecological Association (IUGA), such as SUI, POP, or VLS, which remain the gold standard for clinical diagnosis. Rather, these specific disorders are collectively categorized within the overarching spectrum of PFSD. This etiology-oriented framework is designed to shift clinical perspective from isolated, symptom-based management toward a holistic, mechanism-based understanding.

Non-PFSD

Non-PFSD is not solely caused by pelvic floor stress imbalance, and mainly comprises cervical elongation (CE), urge urinary incontinence (UUI), pseudo urgency syndrome (PUS), chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS), female sexual dysfunction (FSD), among others. Each of these conditions has distinct underlying causes. CE patients show clinical manifestations that are consistent with POP patients, involving causes such as changes in hormone levels, POP, surgical interventions, parity, delivery, pelvic infections, age, etc. [4–6]. Currently, its pathogenesis remains controversial, so that CE cannot be blindly treated as uterine prolapse. UUI [7] is a group of syndromes accompanied by urinary urgency or immediately followed by involuntary urination from the external urethral orifice. Its pathogenesis is mainly neurogenic and infectious, which cannot be explained merely by changes in pelvic floor stress. PUS [8] is a condition in which SUI patient urinates immediately upon the first urge to urinate, so as to avoid leakage. This behavioral habit of repeated urination contributes to the symptoms of UUI, which is most often triggered by psychiatric and behavioral factors rather than physiological causes. CPPS [9, 10] is characterized by chronic or persistent pain (lasting more than 6 months) perceived in relation to the pelvis and surrounding structures. It is often associated with psychological and emotional subjective feelings, and has a very low association with pelvic floor stress injuries. FSD is mainly manifested as dysaphrodisia, sexual arousal disorders, orgasmic disorders, and dyspareunia, involving a variety of causes, including biological, socio-cultural, racial, religious, and psycho-emotional factors. In summary, while the entities within the non-PFSD category possess distinct underlying causes from one another, their individual etiologies are inherently multifactorial. The pathogenesis of each condition involves a complex interplay of neurological, infectious, or psychobehavioral factors, which cannot be adequately explained solely from a biological stress perspective. Pathogenesis of PFSD

Female pelvic floor biomechanical homeostasis is a system that involves a complex combination of mechanical equilibrium factors. In most cases, a single factor causing functional mechanical imbalance does not lead to symptomatic PFSD; rather, it is typically the outcome of a combination of etiological factors contributing to the mechanical imbalance. The pathogenesis of pelvic floor stress injury can be divided into acute and chronic injuries. Acute injuries occur mainly in the second stage of labor during vaginal delivery, while chronic injuries occur primarily due to “aging” of support structures, chronic increase in abdominal pressure, uterine atrophy after decline of ovarian function, and decrease in elasticity of the vagina, etc. [11–13]. Therefore, the varying degrees and symptoms of PFSD are closely related to their pathogenesis and age.

During transvaginal delivery, pelvic floor tissues undergo significant dilation, which can lead to muscle fiber breakage or decreased fiber elasticity. A study has indicated that the levator ani muscle is stretched to 3.26 times its original length to accommodate the delivery of the fetal head [14]. Injuries to the levator ani muscle resulting in more than 50% muscle loss lead to a 40% reduction in the strength of pelvic floor muscles during contraction [15]. At the same time, the six-point tearing of levator plate and the blunting of vaginal folding angle will cause axial transfer of uterine moment, thus resulting in the “rammer” effect and the displacement of pelvic organs (Fig. 2). Such injury further leads to VLS, which is often accompanied by SUI or POP. In women having undergone transvaginal delivery, however, the hyperelastic nature of pelvic floor support structures allows for the resolution of most symptomatic PFSD within a year as tissues return to a functional mechanical equilibrium, yet the potential effects of the injury persist. In addition, compression of pudendal nerves by fetal head leads to fibrosis by denervation of partial muscles, and weak contractions aggravate this process [16].

With age, women inevitably experience pelvic floor collagen loss, muscle atrophy, muscle strength loss, and reduced vaginal elasticity and closure, leading to the destruction of support structure components and weakening of mechanical properties, which may further disrupt mechanical homeostasis of normal pelvic floor. Some other etiologic factors potentially related to PFSD are also superimposed factors in close association with functional mechanical imbalance. Damage to female pelvic floor support structures caused by various factors will inevitably provoke axial changes in moments, ultimately disrupting the overall mechanical equilibrium of the pelvic floor in both its static and dynamic aspects. This will also lead to a series of clinical manifestations and pathological processes in PFSD. Therefore, PFSD is actually a category of functional mechanical imbalance diseases involving multiple factors and stages.

Intrinsic Connection of PFSD Syndromes

Correlation Between POP and SUI

The most common PFSD features the simultaneous development of POP and SUI. The majority of POP patients experience varying degrees of anterior vaginal wall bulging or prolapse, while simple posterior vaginal wall prolapse is relatively rare. Most SUI patients also have anterior vaginal wall prolapse, characterized by the downward displacement of the cervix and bladder. However, such downward displacement is not synchronized with downward displacement of posterior bladder wall. This occurs because anterior bladder wall and anterior urethra are attached to pubic symphysis with connective tissue or lax ligaments, whereas the support structure of posterior bladder wall and posterior urethra primarily derives from the levator plate and perineal body, which contract anteriorly toward the ventral side. Once the levator plate is weakly attached or the perineal body is torn, the cervix vesicae will be poorly closed, and the length of functional urethra will be shortened, thereby inducing SUI. However, when the cervix vesicae and anterior wall are synchronized with posterior urethra to move downward, such problems will not occur, so does SUI.

Correlation Between VLS and SUI

SUI and VLS share common epidemiologic risk factors, with the majority of SUI patients also presenting with VLS. During vaginal delivery, a series of dynamic processes, such as fetal head flexion, rotation, and extension, cause extreme dilatation of the vagina. Under the continuous extrusion by the fetal head, the nerves innervating vaginal and pelvic floor muscle fibers are damaged, with consequent weakening of neuromodulation, decline in pelvic floor muscle contraction function, loss of neurotrophy of muscle fibers, and degenerative changes [16]. In addition, iatrogenic injury, aging, and other factors contribute to wide and lax vaginal orifice or walls, reduced vaginal elasticity, decreased vaginal support for urethra. A torn and shortened perineal body, along with a weakly contracted levator plate, fails to adequately support the cervix and urethra during increased abdominal pressure. This results in poor closure of the cervix and a shorter functional urethra, ultimately triggering SUI and vesicocele [17].

Correlation Between VLS and POP

Moderate to severe POP is a dynamic mechanical imbalance caused by a combination of multiple factors, including diameter and morphology of vagina, morphology of perineal body, morphology and contractility of levator plate, degree of uterine atrophy, biomechanical properties of uterine appendage, degree of pelvic floor nerve injury, and integrity of arcus tendineus fasciae pelvis. Of these, diameter and morphology of vagina is a necessary factor, while morphology of perineal body, morphology and contractility of levator plate are core factors, and other mechanical structures are key factors.

In moderate to severe POP, the vagina’s narrowest segment is wider than its normal diameter, indicating the presence of VLS. VLS is a necessary but not sole factor in the development of POP. The anatomical position of the functional pelvic floor organs is maintained by various factors, and moderate to severe POP arises when multiple injuries are present. Thus, the correction of VLS is a critical technical consideration for enduring therapeutic outcomes.

Both SUI and POP or VLS fall under the same category of PFSD, but they are divided into different diseases that belong to the same category by artificial classification. As a result, this classification of diseases is superficial, leading to equally superficial treatment measures. A treatment model that only focuses on the symptoms of SUI or POP will definitely not achieve the satisfactory outcomes of holistic and long-term treatment.

Misconceptions of PFSD

Theories of pelvic floor have been developing slowly until the 1990s. During that period, the “integral theory” proposed by Petros [18] and the “three-level theory” proposed by Delancy [19] contributed to the rapid development of pelvic floor treatment, and various surgical models for PFSD treatment were proposed to restore the normal anatomy of the pelvic floor by slinging vaginal fornix, uterus, and rectum. However, long-term observations have revealed that these surgeries come with problems such as high recurrence rate, many complications, high reoperation rate, and unsatisfactory diagnostic and therapeutic results [20]. Subsequently, the evolution of related theories has reached an impasse, and it becomes clear that numerous misconceptions regarding PFSD are at the root of this stagnation.

Disconnection Between PFSD Classification and the Most Essential Pathogenesis

Etiology is essential for grasping the core of diseases, and eliminating the disease’s cause underpins the logic of diagnostic and therapeutic approaches. Existing diagnostic and treatment strategies for PFSD have been designed on the basis of disease presentation, and their clinical efficacy has been widely documented in clinical practice. Therefore, to improve the clinical treatment effect of PFSD, the most direct way is to elucidate the mechanisms by which pathogenic factors induce pathological damage and how the superimposition of different pathological damage factors leads to clinical symptoms.

Overlooking the Unity of Pelvic Floor Organs and Support Structures

According to the core idea of current pelvic floor theories and clinical classifications, pelvic floor diseases are anatomically subdivided into anterior, middle, and posterior pelvic floor diseases. Their clinical manifestations are described separately in terms of urinary, reproductive, and anorectal systems. In addition, their therapeutic strategies are tailored to repair deficiencies of anterior, middle and posterior pelvic floor. However, the organs and support structures of the pelvic floor are a whole in themselves and are completely inseparable from each other. Breaking the intrinsic connection of PFSD and overlooking the unity of mechanical imbalance in PFSD will inevitably lead to poor clinical outcomes and insurmountable complications.

Current Status of Modern Pelvic Floor Theories

Several theories and hypotheses on pelvic floor have been developed in modern times; however, these theories are restricted to two-dimensional planes and simple understanding of static morphology, and have not really entered into the study of dynamic functional pelvic floor biomechanics. Current theories suggest that female pelvic organs are fixed in the pelvic cavity by the sling and support of ligaments and fascia of pelvic tissues and organs, and the levator ani muscle plays an auxiliary role. However, these ligaments are relatively weak, consisting primarily of thickened peritoneum, which lacks the tensile strength necessary to support the weight of pelvic floor organs and counteract abdominal pressure. The anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments of knee joints have a tensile strength 408 times higher than that of sacral and cardinal ligaments, yet they bear loads of similar magnitude. Other ligaments are merely connecting structures between organs or between muscles and bony structures. It is logically impossible to explain all pelvic floor diseases by ligament injuries or to deduce the relationship between symptoms and pathogenesis of PFSD. Luo et al. [21, 22] found that the stiffness of cardinal and sacral ligaments were 0.20 ± 0.06 and 0.12 ± 0.04 N/mm, respectively, and that the strain of the two was close to 100% under the maximum load (17.8 N). In contrast, the levator ani muscle is only a skeletal muscle with a thickness of 2–3 mm [16], and it is unable to support the tension exerted by abdominopelvic cavity. Our research team [23, 24] found that the cervical position was restored after sacral sling was performed in both the normal (0°) and pathological (90°) state of a simulated pelvic floor system. However, in compound pelvic floor injuries, the mesh sling device predominantly bears the load, with the force direction perpendicular to the axis of the surgical mesh sling and vaginal orifice (Fig. 3). This suggests that current repair and reconstruction techniques for the apical sling are ineffective in achieving an axial moment reset. It is easy to find that the cervix itself is not fixed if carefully analyzed from the perspective of mechanics and the mystery of human pelvic floor support. The cervix exhibits significant mobility— superior-inferior and anterior–posterior—during supine positioning, maximum Valsalva, and maximum contraction (Fig. 4). Owing to the overall backward movement of morphologic features of the uterine–vagina axis after suspension, axial changes occur in pelvic floor biomechanics. While anatomical replacement of the cervix is achieved, the repair of pelvic floor mechanical imbalance to establish a new pelvic floor mechanical equilibrium may introduce new mechanical imbalances, potentially resulting in additional complications. Minor structural alterations may induce additional or even greater imbalances in the process of restoring equilibrium. Similarly, any endeavor to modify the physiological structure or mechanical axis is unlikely to yield entirely satisfactory outcomes or to prevent associated complications. Therefore, a key breakthrough point in tackling the current treatment bottleneck of PFSD may be to analyze and delve into the biomechanical characteristics of the female pelvic floor from the viewpoint of dynamic functional biomechanics.

Treatment Strategies for PFSD

Surgical treatment is required for approximately 11.1% of PFSD patients [25], and novel theories, ideas, and methods are inevitable to solve the current dilemma of PFSD. New theories must be consistent with the most basic axiom of pelvic floor—“force,” which includes various mechanical equilibrium problems at the physiological level, mechanical imbalance in disease states, and restoration of mechanical equilibrium during reconstruction. Effective treatment must align with the underlying cause of the disease; any theory or surgical method that deviates from physiological reconstruction is unlikely to be a valid approach. At the core of the new idea of the pelvic floor is establishing the concept of an integral pelvic floor from a holistic view, which classifies SUI, POP, and other PFSDs as the same disease category while analyzing and treating them with a comprehensive, holistic, yet individualized mechanistic concept, recognizing that physiological pelvic floor morphology is the optimal functional morphology. New methods are surgical models and therapeutic patterns built on the idea of physiological reconstruction and repair in line with the new design innovated from the root. Their ultimate objective is the biomechanical reconstruction of the pelvic floor to its prenatal morphology.

Recent clinical studies from our team have demonstrated the efficacy of this mechanism-based approach. A multicenter retrospective study indicated that physiologic pelvic floor reconstruction significantly improved anatomical parameters (Aa, Ba, C, D, Ap, Bp, Gh, Pb, TVL) and quality of life in patients with moderate to severe POP over a 2-year follow-up period (P < 0.001), with a low incidence of complications [26]. Furthermore, for patients with SUI and concurrent VLS, a T-shaped sling combined with modified posterior pelvic floor reconstruction achieved a 97.3% continence rate at 1 year postoperatively, while significantly improving sexual function (P < 0.01) [27]. Another unpublished cohort study involving 135 patients with POP complicated by SUI showed that physiological pelvic floor reconstruction similarly yielded a postoperative continence rate of 96.3%. Despite these promising preliminary results, we recognize that establishing universally standardized diagnostic criteria requires broader consensus. Future research will focus on long-term follow-ups and the implementation of rigorous randomized controlled trials to provide higher-level evidence supporting this paradigm shift from symptom-oriented to mechanism-based management.

Prospect

A key to resolving PFSD lies in establishing the concept of holistic pelvic floor and therapeutic strategies based on functional pelvic floor biomechanics, etiology, and a holistic view of the disease. The evolutionary history of mankind has informed the ultimate therapeutic goal of PFSD, namely physiological reconstruction, with mechanical reconstruction and aesthetic reconstruction recognized as the essence. However, to achieve this goal, it holds considerable significance to re-understand the anatomy of the pelvic floor from the viewpoint of stress, to recognize the pathological process of pelvic floor injury from the viewpoint of biological stress imbalance, and to utilize engineering mechanics to deduce and validate physiological, pathological, and therapeutic models, thereby securing the most reliable and scientific clinical data. The precise treatment of PFSD is anticipated in the future.
