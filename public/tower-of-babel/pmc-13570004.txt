Telitacicept for primary angiitis of the central nervous system: A real-world retrospective study
Authors: Jiangyi Lyu, Yifan Wang, Fangfang Li, Shangtian Xiang, Jing Zhao, Yu He, Weizhou Zang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrimary central nervous system vasculitis (PACNS) is a rare inflammatory disease with limited treatment options. Telitacicept and high-resolution vessel wall imaging (HR-VWI) may offer new strategies for therapy and disease monitoring.ObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of telitacicept in patients with PACNS and to explore the value of HR-VWI in assessing disease activity.DesignA single-center retrospective study.MethodsEight patients with PACNS who received telitacicept and completed HR-VWI follow-up were retrospectively enrolled. Neurological outcomes assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), HR-VWI vessel wall enhancement characteristics, and immunoglobulin and complement levels were compared at baseline and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after treatment. Relapse and adverse events were recorded during follow-up.ResultsFollowing telitacicept treatment, neurological outcomes progressively improved, with modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores showing a gradual decline over time. At 12 months, all patients remained at a level of mild functional disability (mRS ≤ 2). HR-VWI demonstrated reduced vessel wall enhancement, shortened enhancement length, and decreased circumferential involvement of the affected vessels after treatment. In some patients, the degree of luminal stenosis improved or remained stable, with no evidence of progressive worsening. Immunological analyses showed a progressive decline in serum IgG and IgM levels, whereas IgA levels changed modestly. Complement C3 and C4 levels remained generally stable throughout treatment. Three patients experienced disease relapse, all occurring within 3 months after treatment initiation; however, the relapse frequency was lower than that observed before telitacicept therapy. One patient developed mild allergic cheilitis that resolved with symptomatic therapy.ConclusionTelitacicept treatment was associated with improvements in neurological outcomes and reductions in HR-VWI inflammatory parameters in patients with PACNS. HR-VWI may provide valuable information for assessing disease activity and evaluating longitudinal vascular changes during follow-up.

Introduction

Primary angiitis of the central nervous system (PACNS) is a rare inflammatory vasculitic disorder confined to the brain and spinal cord, predominantly affecting the arteries of the brain parenchyma, spinal cord, and leptomeninges.1,2 The clinical manifestations of PACNS are nonspecific, with subacute or insidious-onset headache being the most common presentation (50%–60%), 3 followed by cognitive impairment. Patients may also present with seizures, stroke-like episodes, or other focal neurological deficits. Current treatment strategies generally consist of induction therapy with high-dose glucocorticoids in combination with cyclophosphamide, followed by maintenance immunosuppressive therapy with agents such as mycophenolate mofetil or azathioprine for at least one year. 4 However, treatment responses remain variable, and a proportion of patients respond poorly to conventional immunosuppressive regimens.5,6 Moreover, long-term use of glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide is associated with substantial adverse effects. In addition, treatment response is currently assessed primarily through clinical manifestations and parenchymal imaging findings, which may not directly reflect the activity of vascular wall inflammation.

PACNS is a heterogeneous disorder that can be broadly classified into small-vessel PACNS (SV-PACNS) and large-/medium-vessel PACNS (LV-PACNS) according to the size of the affected vessels. The diagnostic criteria proposed by Calabrese and Mallek in 1988 remain the most widely used in clinical practice and require the presence of an acquired, otherwise unexplained neurological deficit, evidence of vasculitis within the central nervous system, and exclusion of alternative causes. 7 In 2009, Birnbaum and colleagues further distinguished biopsy- or autopsy-proven cases as definite PACNS, whereas cases with characteristic angiographic findings in the absence of histopathological confirmation were classified as probable PACNS. 8 Because LV-PACNS predominantly involves intracranial large and medium-sized arteries, the diagnostic yield of brain biopsy is often limited; therefore, its diagnosis relies primarily on clinical presentation and vascular imaging findings.

In recent years, HR-VWI has emerged as a promising imaging modality for the direct visualization of vessel wall morphology and enhancement characteristics and has gained increasing attention in the diagnosis and follow-up of PACNS. Previous studies have shown that PACNS typically manifests on HR-VWI as concentric vessel wall thickening and enhancement, often accompanied by negative remodeling, although eccentric wall thickening may also be observed in some cases.9,10 Compared with conventional vascular imaging techniques, such as magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which primarily depict luminal abnormalities, HR-VWI enables direct assessment of vessel wall inflammation and disease activity. Therefore, it has increasingly become an important adjunctive tool for the diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring of PACNS.11,12

Growing evidence suggests that, in addition to T-cell–mediated inflammation, B cells may also contribute to the pathogenesis of PACNS. Immune cell profiling of cerebrospinal fluid has demonstrated increased proportions of B cells in a subset of patients with PACNS, suggesting a potential role for B-cell–mediated immunity in disease pathogenesis. 13 Furthermore, in several biopsy-proven lymphocytic PACNS cases, perivascular infiltration by CD20+ B lymphocytes has been observed, providing additional evidence for the involvement of B cells in the disease process. 14 However, unlike other antibody-mediated neuroimmunological disorders, no disease-specific autoantibody has yet been identified in PACNS, and its underlying immunopathological mechanisms remain incompletely understood. B-cell activating factor (BAFF, also known as BLyS) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are key cytokines involved in the regulation of B-cell survival, maturation, and plasma-cell differentiation and play important roles in a variety of autoimmune and neuroinflammatory diseases. BAFF primarily promotes the survival and expansion of mature B cells, whereas APRIL is more closely associated with the maintenance and function of plasmablasts and plasma cells. 15 In addition to antibody production, B cells can modulate T-cell activation through antigen presentation and cytokine secretion. 16 Therefore, targeting the BAFF/APRIL pathway may simultaneously influence both humoral and cellular immune responses.

Telitacicept is a recombinant transmembrane activator and calcium-modulator and cyclophilin ligand interactor (TACI)-Fc fusion protein, consisting of the extracellular domain of TACI linked to the Fc fragment of human IgG1. By simultaneously binding and neutralizing BAFF and APRIL, telitacicept suppresses the survival of mature B cells as well as the differentiation of plasmablasts and plasma cells. 17 Telitacicept has demonstrated favorable efficacy and safety in several autoimmune diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus, myasthenia gravis, and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder.18–20 However, clinical evidence supporting its use in PACNS remains extremely limited, and its effects on vascular wall inflammation and imaging characteristics have not been systematically evaluated. Therefore, in this real-world study, we used high-resolution magnetic resonance vessel wall imaging (HR-VWI) to dynamically assess changes in vessel wall inflammation and investigate the efficacy and safety of telitacicept in patients with PACNS, as well as its impact on vascular wall imaging features.

Materials and methods

Research design

This study is a single-center, retrospective, observational investigation. By retrospectively collecting and analyzing clinical and imaging data from patients with PACNS who received telitacicept treatment and completed HR-VWI follow-up during prior clinical management, we systematically evaluated the application value of HR-VWI in dynamically monitoring changes in vascular wall inflammatory activity.

Participants

This retrospective study included 8 patients with PACNS who were hospitalized or followed up in the Department of Neurology at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital and received treatment with telitacicept from January 2020 to December 2025. All included patients fulfilled the 1988 Calabrese and Mallek diagnostic criteria and were classified as LV-PACNS based on characteristic vascular imaging findings. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients were included if they met all of the following conditions: (1) fulfilled the diagnostic criteria for PACNS proposed by Calabrese in 1988 or were confirmed by pathological examination of brain tissue, with the diagnosis independently reviewed and agreed upon by at least two senior neurologists; (2) demonstrated stenosis of at least one large- or medium-sized vessel on DSA or MRA and exhibited HR-VWI imaging features of PACNS, including diffuse, concentric vessel wall thickening, absence of plaque, and circumferential, homogeneous enhancement; (3) received telitacicept treatment for at least 3 months; (4) underwent HR-VWI examinations before treatment and at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months after initiation of therapy, with qualified imaging data available; and (5) were aged between 18 and 75 years. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) CNS vasculitis secondary to systemic autoimmune diseases (e.g., systemic lupus erythematosus, ANCA-associated vasculitis, Sjögren’s syndrome), infection, malignancy, or drug-related factors; (2) intolerance to immunosuppressive therapy, including but not limited to severe osteoporosis, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, active hepatitis or severe liver disease (ALT or AST >3 times the upper limit of normal), or renal insufficiency (creatinine clearance <60 mL/min); (3) contraindications to immunosuppressive therapy, such as acute infection, pregnancy, or lactation; or (4) incomplete clinical or imaging data, or imaging quality insufficient for analysis.

Research methods

In all included patients, initiation of telitacicept was based on routine clinical decision-making after comprehensive evaluation by the treating neurologists. Telitacicept was administered at a dose of 160 mg by subcutaneous injection once weekly. All patients received telitacicept treatment for at least 3 months, while the duration of treatment varied according to disease activity, clinical response, and physician judgment during follow-up. Depending on disease activity and clinical manifestations, some patients received concomitant glucocorticoid therapy, including intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy or oral prednisone-equivalent regimens, primarily for induction of remission during the active phase of the disease. Following clinical stabilization or remission, glucocorticoid doses were gradually tapered according to improvements in clinical symptoms and serial HR-VWI findings. Previous treatment with glucocorticoids, cyclophosphamide, rituximab, mycophenolate mofetil, or other immunosuppressive agents before initiation of telitacicept was permitted and was not considered an exclusion criterion.

Relapse was defined as the emergence of new or worsening central nervous system (CNS) symptoms following prior disease remission or stabilization, unexplained by infection, metabolic abnormalities, drug adverse reactions, or other non-vasculitic factors, and meeting at least one of the following objective criteria: (1) neuroimaging demonstrating new or worsening vasculitis-related changes; (2) increased glucocorticoid dosage due to disease activity, or reinitiation or intensification of immunomodulatory therapy to control disease progression. All relapse events were independently assessed by two neurologists experienced in diagnosing and treating inflammatory CNS diseases. Discrepancies in assessment were resolved through discussion to reach consensus. The time of relapse was defined as the point in time when the first clinical symptom or imaging change meeting the above criteria occurred.

All patients underwent systematic safety monitoring during treatment, including adverse event recording, assessment of infection-related symptoms, and laboratory tests (including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, and relevant immunological parameters). If severe adverse reactions occur or if the investigator determines that continued treatment may pose a significant risk, treatment with telitacicept may be suspended or discontinued, with appropriate clinical management provided based on the patient’s condition.

Data collection and follow-up

All research data were retrospectively collected through the hospital’s electronic medical record system, including: (1) baseline demographic information, age at treatment initiation, primary clinical manifestations, and specific treatment regimens; (2) neuroimaging characteristics and HR-VWI-assessed vascular wall inflammatory activity metrics, including dynamic changes in vascular wall enhancement length, enhancement intensity, and luminal stenosis degree; (3) laboratory test results and changes in immunological markers, including immunoglobulins (IgG, IgM, IgA) and complement components C3 and C4; (4) changes in mRS scores during follow-up; (5) adverse events occurring during treatment and overall safety assessment.

Imaging examination and evaluation methods

All subjects underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations on the same 3.0 T MRI scanner using a standard head phased-array coil. Conventional MRI sequences included T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences, and diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) to assess brain parenchymal lesions. HR-VWI employed black-blood imaging to suppress blood flow signals, primarily acquiring a three-dimensional T1-weighted vascular wall imaging sequence. The scan coverage encompassed the circle of Willis and its major arterial branches. HR-VWI images were acquired both before and after intravenous administration of a gadolinium contrast agent (0.1 mmol/kg) to evaluate vascular wall enhancement characteristics.

All HR-VWI images were independently assessed by two research physicians unaware of patient clinical data and follow-up outcomes, following a randomized sequence. The analysis focused on the primary responsible vessel deemed clinically symptomatic, measuring the following imaging parameters: (1) degree of vascular wall enhancement; (2) length of vascular wall enhancement (mm); (3) circumferential extent of vascular wall enhancement (0–360°, in 90° increments); (4) Vessel lumen stenosis on the 3D-TOF MRA sequence (0–100%, in 10% increments). 9

The degree of vascular wall enhancement is graded using the signal intensity of the enhanced pituitary stalk as a reference standard: - Vascular wall signal intensity lower than the pituitary stalk is defined as mild enhancement; - Signal intensity equal to or higher than the pituitary stalk is defined as marked enhancement; - No significant change in vascular wall signal intensity before and after enhancement is defined as no enhancement. Perform direct measurements using electronic measurement tools on the image workstation and record the longitudinal length of the vascular wall enhancement in that segment. The circumferential enhancement range was assessed on SPACE sequences through multiplanar reconstruction along the vessel course, evaluating the circumferential enhancement angle across the vascular cross-section. If circumferential enhancement varied across longitudinal segments of the same vessel, the observed maximum value was used for statistical analysis. The degree of lumen stenosis was measured on 3D-TOF MRA sequences. Images were magnified to 400%, and the narrowest point of the affected vessel lumen was selected as the measurement site. The diameter at this point was measured relative to the proximal, relatively normal vessel lumen. The degree of stenosis was calculated using the following formula: Degree of Stenosis (%) = [1 − (Diameter at the narrowest point of the affected segment/Diameter of the normal vessel proximal to the stenosis)] × 100%.

In patients with multiple vessel involvement, HR-VWI imaging parameters are derived from the most severely affected vessel. The most severely affected vessel is defined as the one exhibiting the highest wall enhancement signal intensity. If multiple vessels meet this criterion, the vessel with the longest enhanced segment is selected. If still indistinguishable, the vessel with the greatest degree of luminal narrowing is chosen. Follow-up HR-VWI measurements are performed on the same target vessel identified at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Given the small sample size and the non-normal distribution of repeated measurements, changes in clinical outcomes, laboratory parameters, and HR-VWI variables across different time points were analyzed using the Friedman test. When the overall Friedman test was significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni adjustment were performed. All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 8 patients with PACNS, 4 of whom were female. The median age at onset was 41.5 years (range 29–52 years), and the median time from initial onset to initiation of telitacicept treatment was 124 days (range 15–1628 days). Laboratory findings revealed elevated inflammatory markers in only a minority of patients, with both ESR and CRP elevated in 12.5% of cases. The median neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) was 1.86. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis revealed elevated white blood cell counts and protein levels in 12.5% and 25% of cases, respectively. The CSF-to-serum albumin quotient (Qalb) was 6.1, suggesting impaired blood-brain barrier function in some patients. The most common clinical manifestations were limb weakness or Numbness (75.0%), followed by speech impairment and cognitive impairment (50.0% each). Dizziness occurred in three patients (37.5%), whereas headache and epilepsy were each observed in two patients (25.0%). Imaging revealed a high incidence of ischemic cerebral infarction (87.5%), with 37.5% of patients exhibiting intracranial hemorrhage. HR-VWI demonstrated abnormal vascular wall enhancement in all patients, primarily presenting as concentric enhancement accompanied by varying degrees of luminal narrowing. Among the initial treatment regimens prior to telitacicept therapy, 3 patients (37.5%) received glucocorticoid pulse therapy alone, 3 patients (37.5%) received mycophenolate mofetil, 1 patient (12.5%) received glucocorticoids combined with cyclophosphamide, and another 1 patient (12.5%) received glucocorticoids combined with rituximab. The mRS score was 2.5 (IQR, 1.75), indicating a moderate degree of neurological disability at treatment initiation. Clinical efficacy

Improvement in symptoms and functional outcomes

Neurological disability improved over the course of follow-up. The median mRS score decreased from 2.5 (IQR, 1.75) at baseline to 1.5 (IQR, 1.0) at Month 1. Friedman analysis demonstrated a significant overall difference in mRS scores across the five assessment time points (χ2 = 21.277, df = 4, P < 0.001). Further pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction showed that the mRS score at 12 months was significantly lower than that at baseline (adjusted P = 0.012) and at 1 month (adjusted P = 0.027), while no significant differences were observed between the remaining time points. Imaging changes

Imaging analysis revealed that brain parenchymal lesions were primarily distributed in the parietal lobe (4 cases, 50.0%), periventricular region (3 cases, 37.5%), basal ganglia (3 cases, 37.5%), corona radiata (2 cases, 25.0%), and frontal lobe (2 cases, 25.0%). The lesions primarily manifested as multiple cerebral infarcts (7/8, 87.5%). Three patients (37.5%) had concomitant cerebral hemorrhage, and one (12.5%) showed leptomeningeal enhancement. Analysis of HR-VWI images revealed that the affected vessels were predominantly in the anterior circulation. The middle cerebral artery (MCA) was involved in all patients (8/8, 100%), predominantly affecting the M1–M2 segments. Involvement of the intracranial internal carotid artery (C7 segment) was identified in three patients (37.5%), and two patients (25.0%) additionally exhibited vertebral artery V4 segment involvement. Overall, the vascular lesions demonstrated a multifocal and bilateral distribution pattern. The longitudinal changes in HR-VWI parameters are presented in Figure 3. Longitudinal HR-VWI assessments demonstrated significant changes in vessel wall enhancement intensity (χ2 = 16.000, P = 0.003), enhancement length (χ2 = 28.764, P < 0.001), circumferential extent of enhancement (χ2 = 20.800, P < 0.001), and luminal stenosis (χ2 = 24.500, P < 0.001). Post hoc analysis showed that enhancement length was significantly reduced at Month 6 compared with baseline (P = 0.002), at Month 12 compared with baseline (P < 0.001), and at Month 12 compared with Month 1 (P = 0.004). Luminal stenosis was significantly lower at Month 12 than at baseline (P = 0.020) and Month 1 (P = 0.020). Although significant overall differences were observed for enhancement intensity and circumferential extent, no significant pairwise differences were identified in post hoc analyses. Immune changes

Regarding immunological markers, longitudinal changes in serum immunoglobulin and complement levels were assessed in the eight patients with PACNS before treatment and during follow-up. Significant overall differences were observed in serum IgA (χ2 = 15.000, P = 0.005), IgG (χ2 = 13.610, P = 0.009), and IgM levels (χ2 = 23.200, P < 0.001) over time. Compared with baseline, serum IgA levels were significantly lower at Month 3 (P = 0.044), Month 6 (P = 0.027), and Month 12 (P = 0.005). Serum IgG levels were significantly reduced at Month 12 compared with baseline (P = 0.004). For IgM, levels at Month 6 were significantly lower than those at baseline (P = 0.001) and Month 1 (P = 0.044), and levels at Month 12 were also significantly lower than those at baseline (P = 0.001) and Month 1 (P = 0.044). No significant longitudinal changes were observed in serum C3 (χ2 = 2.100, P = 0.717) or C4 levels (χ2 = 4.790, P = 0.310). Recurrence

Prior to telitacicept initiation, all patients had active disease. Five patients remained relapse-free during follow-up. Patient 1 relapsed 15 days after switching to mycophenolate mofetil monotherapy and resumed telitacicept treatment thereafter. One patient had received intravenous methylprednisolone pulse therapy and cyclophosphamide before telitacicept initiation. Patient 2 and Patient 6 experienced relapse 28 and 53 days after treatment initiation, respectively. Safety assessment

No serious treatment-related adverse events, severe infections, or deaths were observed during treatment and follow-up. One patient developed mild allergic cheilitis, which improved after symptomatic treatment without discontinuation of telitacicept. Apart from this case, no other drug-related allergic reactions were reported.

Discussion

This real-world cohort study, combined with HR-VWI, systematically evaluated the clinical efficacy, imaging changes, immunological characteristics, and safety of telitacicept in patients with PACNS. The results demonstrated that neurological outcomes improved continuously after telitacicept treatment, with a significant time-dependent reduction in mRS scores. HR-VWI revealed a gradual decrease in vessel wall enhancement intensity, enhancement length, and circumferential involvement, and in some patients, luminal stenosis improved or remained stable. In terms of immunological parameters, serum IgG and IgM levels showed a progressive decline, while complement levels remained overall stable, suggesting that telitacicept effectively modulates humoral immunity without causing significant complement consumption. Taken together, the consistent changes in clinical, imaging, and immunological indicators provide preliminary real-world evidence supporting the potential therapeutic role of telitacicept in PACNS.

In this study, most patients did not show a significant elevation in peripheral inflammatory markers, whereas HR-VWI revealed clear signs of vascular wall inflammation in all patients. This suggests that inflammatory activity in PACNS may be primarily confined to the vascular walls of the central nervous system and is therefore difficult to fully reflect through systemic inflammatory markers.5,21 This illustrates the limitations of traditional inflammatory markers in assessing disease activity in PACNS and further highlights the core value of imaging tools in disease evaluation. At the same time, some patients exhibited impaired blood-brain barrier function (elevated Qalb) and abnormal cerebrospinal fluid protein levels, suggesting that activation of the intrathecal immune microenvironment may be involved in the onset and progression of the disease, thereby providing a basis for targeted immunomodulatory therapy. During follow-up, the degree of vascular wall enhancement, the length of enhancement, and the circumferential extent of involvement gradually decreased with treatment; these trends generally aligned with clinical improvement, further supporting the potential value of HR-VWI as a tool for longitudinal assessment of PACNS. 12

Previous pathological and immunological studies have demonstrated the presence of T-cell infiltration within inflammatory lesions of PACNS, particularly CD4+ T cells, which contribute to blood–brain barrier disruption and the maintenance of local inflammatory responses.22–24 However, the clinical improvement, attenuation of vessel wall inflammation, and reduction in immunoglobulin levels observed in our study suggest that B-cell–related immune pathways may also play an important role in sustaining disease activity. Dalakas et al. highlighted that, 16 beyond antibody production, B cells possess potent antigen-presenting capabilities, with antigen presentation efficiency reported to be several hundred-fold greater than that of macrophages or dendritic cells under certain conditions. In addition, B cells can regulate T-cell activation and effector functions through the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.25,26 Experimental studies have further shown that B-cell depletion is associated with significant reductions in both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell populations, with memory CD8+ T cells being suppressed by up to tenfold.27,28 Therefore, although telitacicept does not directly target T cells, it may indirectly suppress vascular wall inflammation by disrupting B-cell–T-cell interactions. Collectively, these findings suggest that even in the absence of clearly identifiable pathogenic autoantibodies, B cells may contribute to the immunopathogenesis of PACNS through antibody-independent mechanisms.

Within the molecular network regulating B-cell development and survival, B-cell activating factor (BAFF) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) are recognized as key survival factors. BAFF primarily promotes the survival and differentiation of transitional and mature B cells through binding to BAFF receptor (BAFF-R), whereas APRIL mainly supports the survival of plasmablasts and plasma cells via transmembrane activator and CAML interactor (TACI) and B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) signaling pathways.15,29 In addition to peripheral immune cells, astrocytes under inflammatory conditions are also capable of producing BAFF and APRIL. In multiple sclerosis lesions, expression of both BAFF and APRIL is markedly upregulated, facilitating local B-cell expansion and sustained survival within the central nervous system.30,31 Furthermore, B cells can form ectopic lymphoid follicle-like structures within the meninges, where they undergo activation, clonal expansion, and differentiation into plasma cells, thereby contributing to the persistence of local inflammatory responses.32,33 Although direct evidence of dysregulated BAFF/APRIL expression in PACNS is currently lacking, these observations suggest that the BAFF/APRIL signaling axis may participate in maintaining the local immune microenvironment within the central nervous system. This provides a theoretical rationale for targeting the BAFF/APRIL pathway as a therapeutic strategy in PACNS.

Telitacicept is a recombinant TACI-Fc fusion protein that simultaneously neutralizes BAFF and APRIL, thereby blocking their interactions with BAFF-R, TACI, and BCMA receptors. Compared with other B-cell–targeted therapies, telitacicept exerts a broader spectrum of action across the B-cell lineage. Anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies, such as rituximab, primarily deplete mature and memory B cells but have limited effects on plasmablasts and long-lived plasma cells because these cells generally lack CD20 expression.34,35 In addition, compensatory increases in serum BAFF levels have been observed following rituximab treatment, suggesting a potential limitation in sustaining long-term immune control.29,36 Anti-CD19 monoclonal antibodies, such as inebilizumab, target a broader range of the B-cell lineage, including a proportion of plasmablasts; however, their effects on long-lived plasma cells remain limited. 37 Belimumab selectively inhibits BAFF signaling and thereby reduces B-cell survival, but APRIL-mediated plasma cell survival pathways are largely preserved. 38 In contrast, by simultaneously inhibiting both BAFF and APRIL, telitacicept can target multiple stages of B-cell differentiation, affecting mature B cells, memory B cells, plasmablasts, and plasma cell–related pathways. Theoretically, this dual-pathway blockade may provide broader and more profound regulation of the B-cell lineage than strategies targeting a single molecule or cell population.

Relapse remains a significant challenge in the clinical management of PACNS. 39 In this study, 37.5% (3/8) of patients experienced a relapse during treatment with telitacicept, with one case occurring shortly after discontinuation of telitacicept; however, the overall relapse rate was lower than before treatment, and all relapse events occurred within 3 months of treatment initiation. These findings suggest that telitacicept may play a role in reducing the frequency of disease activity, but it has limitations in completely controlling relapses. Given the protracted course and high relapse rate characteristic of PACNS, these results underscore the importance of long-term monitoring of PACNS patients in clinical practice and suggest that longer treatment durations or combination therapy strategies may be necessary to maintain disease stability.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it is subject to potential selection and information biases, limiting causal inference. Second, the sample size was small, with only eight patients included, which restricts the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Third, none of the included patients underwent histopathological confirmation. Although all patients fulfilled the 1988 Calabrese and Mallek diagnostic criteria, underwent comprehensive exclusion of secondary causes, and demonstrated characteristic DSA/MRA and HR-VWI findings consistent with LV-PACNS, the possibility of diagnostic misclassification cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, the follow-up duration was relatively limited, precluding a comprehensive assessment of the long-term effects of telitacicept on relapse prevention, neurological outcomes, and safety. Finally, the absence of a control group makes it difficult to distinguish the treatment effect of telitacicept from the potential influence of concomitant therapies and the natural course of the disease. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and further investigate imaging, immunological, and molecular biomarkers for patient stratification and individualized treatment in PACNS.

Conclusion

This real-world retrospective study suggests that telitacicept treatment was associated with improvements in neurological outcomes and reductions in HR-VWI inflammatory parameters in patients with PACNS. HR-VWI may provide valuable information for assessing disease activity and evaluating longitudinal vascular changes during follow-up, complementing clinical and laboratory assessments. These findings suggest that telitacicept may represent a potential therapeutic option for PACNS, particularly for patients with inadequate response to conventional immunosuppressive therapies. Future large-scale prospective studies are needed to further validate these observations and establish optimal treatment strategies for PACNS.
