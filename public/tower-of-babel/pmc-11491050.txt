Temporal trend in markers of nutritional status and food consumption of non-village indigenous people in Brazil
Authors: Brena Barreto Barbosa, Greyceanne Cecília Dutra Brito, Larissa Loures Mendes, Lia Silveira Adriano, Aline Martins de Carvalho, Antonio Augusto Ferreira Carioca
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-village indigenous people may face urban lifestyle influences, impacting their nutritional profile. Therefore, this study aimed to analyze the temporal trends in the nutritional status (2008–2023) and food consumption (2015–2023) of non-village indigenous people in Brazil, using data from the Food and Nutrition Surveillance System (SISVAN).MethodsIn this time series study, secondary data available on the SISVAN online platform were used. All records of non-village indigenous people from all regions of Brazil were evaluated. Nutritional status was assessed using height-for-age (H/A) and Body Mass Index-for-age (BMI/A) for children and adolescents, and BMI for adults and the elderly. Food consumption trends were analyzed using food consumption screeners, based on specific food groups consumed the day before the assessment. An annual percentage change (APC) was calculated using Prais-Winsten regression models.ResultsThere was a reduction in severe stunting among children aged 2 to 4 years old (APC = -0.80%; p = 0.025) and 5 to 9 years old (APC = -4.02%; p < 0.001). Adolescents showed an increase in thinness (APC = 0.91%; p = 0.016) and obesity (APC = 4.38%; p < 0.001). In adults and the elderly, there was a decrease in underweight (APC = -5.59%; p = 0.002 and APC = -3.12%; p < 0.001, respectively) and an increase in obesity grade 1 and overweight (APC = 3.81%; p < 0.001 and APC = 1.82%; p < 0.001, respectively). There was an increase in vegetable consumption among children, adolescents and the elderly, and a rise in the consumption of hamburgers and sausages across all age groups.ConclusionThere was an improvement in the nutritional status of children and adolescents, with reduced stunting, but a rise in obesity among adolescents, adults and the elderly. Food consumption trends showed increased consumption of vegetables and ultra-processed foods.

Introduction

The indigenous peoples of Brazil, who have inhabited the territory since before European colonization [1] comprise around 305 indigenous ethnicities, totaling approximately 1.7 million people, or 0.83% of the population. Notably, 63.27% of these individuals live outside officially demarcated Indigenous Lands, known as non-village indigenous people [2]. To assess the diverse nutritional needs of the Brazilian population, the Ministry of Health established the Food and Nutritional Monitoring System (SISVAN), which tracks the nutritional status and dietary habits of individuals using the National Unified Health System (SUS). SISVAN covers all stages of life, irrespective of gender, education, race/color or whether they belong to traditional peoples and communities [3].

A systematic review revealed high rates of overweight and obesity in the Brazilian adult indigenous population, with a prevalence of 33% and 16% of these conditions, respectively [4]. This trend has also been observed among indigenous children [5], along with the occurrence of severely stunted [6] and the persistence of thinness and malnutrition in children of some indigenous ethnicities in the country [6, 7]. Furthermore, anthropometric data from the 2015 National School Health Survey (PeNSE) revealed that the highest prevalence of being overweight was observed among adolescents who declared themselves black or indigenous [8].

In a study that analyzed food consumption in Brazil, segmented by race/skin color based on data from the Family Budget Survey (2017–2018), a greater consumption of ultra-processed products was observed among black, mixed-race and indigenous people compared to whites and asians [9]. In another research, which focused on the food consumption of indigenous Yanomami children under five years of age, a prevalence of 32% in the consumption of ultra-processed foods was identified [10]. Although this result is considerably lower compared to the 80.5% identified among children in the general Brazilian population [11], the prevalence is still high and may indicate a decrease in the consumption of traditional foods, which are predominantly fresh and minimally processed, among indigenous children [10].

Studies on the nutritional status and food consumption of the indigenous population focus on groups that reside in demarcated territories, leaving a gap in knowledge about non-village indigenous people. According to the 2022 Demographic Census, there was an 88% increase in the indigenous population compared to the last count carried out in 2010 [2]. This significant growth during the interval between censuses is mainly due to methodological changes, which included the use of the question “Do you consider yourself indigenous?” outside the territories officially demarcated by the National Foundation of Indigenous Peoples (Funai). The question also covers indigenous groupings identified by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in other locations, which consist of occupied households distributed in urban or rural areas with the proven or potential presence of indigenous people [12].

It is worth considering that non-village indigenous people face different realities, with possible exposure to urban lifestyle habits, which can influence their nutritional profile differently. Therefore, the objective of this study was to analyze the temporal trends in the nutritional status (2008–2023) and food consumption (2015–2023) of non-village indigenous people in Brazil, using data from SISVAN.

Methods

Study design

This time series study uses secondary data from the SISVAN online platform, covering the period from 2008 to 2023 and encompassing all regions of Brazil. The data was obtained from the system’s online platform in March 2024.

Context

SISVAN, a system managed by the Ministry of Health collects and compiles data on the nutritional status and food consumption of individuals assisted by Primary Health Care (PHC) within Brazil’s SUS. Accessible online since 2008 with anthropometric assessments and, since 2015, with food consumption data, SISVAN records are regularly updated and integrated with other health systems, including e-SUS AB and the Bolsa Família Program (PBF). The platform provides public access to annual reports detailing this collected data [3]. The difference in the data collection periods for nutritional status (2008–2023) and food consumption (2015–2023) in this study is due to SISVAN’s expansion in 2015 to include food consumption data, which was not part of the system when it was initially established in 2008.

In Brazil, non-village indigenous people are served by the SUS service network. Their nutritional status and food consumption patterns are monitored through SISVAN in the same manner as for the broader population. The only distinction made for this group occurs during the preparation of reports, when filling in the “race/color” field on the platform, where the “indigenous” option is selected.

In contrast, PHC for indigenous people in villages is provided through the Indigenous Health Care Subsystem (SasiSUS). To monitor the nutritional situation of these people, there is the Indigenous Food and Nutritional Surveillance System (SISVAN-I), an adaptation of SISVAN that meets the specificities of the indigenous village communities. This population is monitored by Indigenous Family Health Teams, which collect and record weight and height data to calculate nutritional diagnosis throughout life cycles [13].

Participants

The nutritional status of non-village indigenous people was analyzed using data from SISVAN’s publicly available reports (https://sisaps.saude.gov.br/sisvan/relatoriopublico/index). The analysis focused on individuals who identified as indigenous in the “race/color” category, which is a mandatory field. The study included different life stages: children (2 to 4 years and 5 to 9 years old), adolescents (10 to 19 years old), adults (20 to 59 years old) and elderly people (60 years or older), based on SISVAN’s age classification [14].

Study variables

The height-for-age (H/A) and Body Mass Index for age (BMI/A) indices, as proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and utilized by the Ministry of Health, were collected to assess the nutritional status of children and adolescents [15].

For the H/A index, children and adolescents with a Z-score below − 3 were classified as severely stunted; those with a Z-score between − 3 and − 2 were classified as moderately stunted; and those with a Z-score of -2 or higher were classified as having adequate height. Regarding the BMI-for-age index, children and adolescents with a Z-score below − 3 were classified as extremely thin; between − 3 and − 2, as thin; between − 2 and + 1, as eutrophy; between + 1 and + 2, as a risk of being overweight; between + 2 and + 3, as overweight; and greater than + 3, as obesity [15].

Weight status in adults was classified according to BMI, following WHO guidelines. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (in kilograms) by height squared (in meters). The classification criteria were as follows: underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²), adequate weight (BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 kg/m²), overweight (BMI between 25 and 29.9 kg/m²), obesity grade 1 (BMI 30–34.9 kg/m²), obesity grade 2 (BMI 35–39.9 kg/m²) and obesity grade 3 (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²) (WHO, 2000). The following BMI cutoff points, specific for the elderly, were used to categorize this index: low weight (BMI < 22 kg/m²); adequate weight (BMI between 22 kg/m² and 27 kg/m²); and overweight (BMI > 27 kg/m²) [16].

In addition to anthropometric variables, SISVAN Web also provides reports with food consumption data, collected through food consumption markers. These markers analyze specific aspects of the previous day’s diet. SISVAN offers three types of forms for assessing food consumption: the first targets children up to six months old, focusing on breastfeeding and the early introduction of food; the second is for children aged six months to less than two years, aiming to evaluate the appropriate and timely introduction of quality foods; and the third is for children over two years of age, adolescents, adults, the elderly and pregnant women, and is based on the guidelines of the current Food Guide for the Brazilian Population [17].

The Food Guide for the Brazilian Population, published in 2014, categorizes food based on processing levels into four groups. The first group includes unprocessed or minimally processed foods, which are edible parts of plants or animals that have not undergone processes involving the addition of other substances. The second group consists of culinary ingredients, which are substances extracted directly from foods in the first group or from nature. The third group comprises processed foods, which are industrial products made by adding ingredients from the second group to foods from the first group. The fourth group is made up of ultra-processed foods, which are industrial formulations characterized by the presence of additives. It advocates for a diet primarily composed of natural or minimally processed foods and advises against the consumption of ultra-processed foods. In this study, food intake is analyzed by examining the proportion of individuals who consumed specific food groups on the day before the assessment. The analysis includes both healthy food groups, such as vegetables, and unhealthy foods, like ultra-processed items. The study considers all individuals within the analyzed age group who reported consuming a food group on the previous day [17].

The form aimed at people over two years of age, including children, adolescents, adults, the elderly and pregnant women, was created to identify healthy and unhealthy eating patterns. For healthy habits, the focus is on the consumption of three categories of food: (1) beans; (2) fruits; and (3) vegetables and/or legumes. Conversely, unhealthy eating habits are characterized by the consumption of four types of food: (1) hamburgers and/or sausage products; (2) sweetened drinks; (3) instant noodles, packaged snacks or savory biscuits; and (4) filled biscuits, sweets or other treats [17].

The Ministry of Health, in its document “Guidelines for the Evaluation of Dietary Consumption Markers in Primary Care”, developed a specific indicator for each food category that represents the proportion of individuals who consumed foods from these groups on the day before the assessment. An indicator of ultra-processed foods was also analyzed, assessing whether individuals had consumed at least one of the following unhealthy foods the previous day: (1) hamburgers or sausage products; (2) sugary drinks; (3) instant noodles, packaged snacks, or savory biscuits; and filled biscuits, sweets or other treats. All individuals within the relevant age group who reported consuming a food group on the previous day were considered in this analysis [17].

Data source and analysis

Data on nutritional status and food consumption were obtained using the SISVAN Web platform and subsequently organized in an Excel® spreadsheet. All information available on the platform was incorporated into the analyses.

Time trend analysis was conducted using Prais-Winsten regression models, a recommended approach for time series studies, in order to control the autocorrelation of regression residuals over the years investigated. The annual percentage change (APC) of each category of nutritional status and food consumption was obtained by the following formula: [-1 + (10β)] x 100, where β represents the base 10 logarithm resulting from the Prais-Winsten regression. Non-significant p-values (p ≥ 0.05) indicated a stable trend, while significant p-values (p < 0.05) denoted an increasing or decreasing trend, depending on the positive or negative annual variation, respectively. The regression models were conducted on Stata - statistical software package, version 12.1, was used, and results with P < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

The total number of nutritional status records for non-village indigenous people continued to grow over time, rising from 59,538 in 2008 to 253,205 in 2023. There was also an increase in the number of records of food consumption markers, rising from 2,605 in 2015 to 18,371 in 2023. 80% of the population was made up of women, with the highest percentage being adults (95.0%) and the lowest being children aged 2 to 4 years old (52.0%). In relation to the geographic region of Brazil, the largest audience came from the North region, with around 48.0% and the smallest was from the Southeast region (6.0%). Regarding the age group, in descending order, adults (41.6%), adolescents (19.7%), children aged 5 to 9 years (19.6%), children aged 2 to 4 years (16.9%) and elderly people (2.3%).

Among children aged 2 to 4 years old, there was a decrease in the prevalence of severe stunting (APC = -1.59%; p < 0.001), moderate stunting (APC = -0.80%; p = 0.025), severe thinness (APC = -2.32%; p = 0.047), risk of becoming overweight (APC = -1.83%; p = 0.010), overweight (APC = -2.00%; p = 0.011) and obesity (APC = − 3.70%; < 0.001), accompanied by an increase in adequate height for age (APC = 0.44%; p < 0.001) and eutrophy (APC = 1.26%; p < 0.001) (Table 1). For children aged 5 to 9 years old, there was a decrease in severe stunting (APC = -4.02%; p < 0.001), moderate stunting (APC = -1.94%; p < 0.001), severe thinness (APC = -3.27%; p < 0.001), being overweight (APC = -1.63%; p = 0.002), obesity (APC = -0.80%; p = 0.034) and severe obesity (APC = -3.29%; p < 0.001), and an increase in adequate height for age (APC = 0.82%; p < 0.001) and eutrophy (APC = 0.81%; p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Discussion

The study reveals a complex set of nutritional transitions. There has been a decline in the prevalence of severe and moderate stunting, thinness, being overweight and obesity among children aged 2 to 4 and 5 to 9 years old, indicating positive improvements in their nutritional status. Conversely, the rise in thinness and obesity rates among adolescents suggests a concerning trend towards the adoption of unhealthy dietary patterns. The increase in vegetable consumption by children, adolescents and the elderly is encouraging, as it reflects a move towards healthier dietary choices. However, the concurrent rise in the consumption of ultra-processed foods, such as hamburgers and sausages in all age groups, suggests a mixed trend in food consumption, where traditional diets coexist with the increasing intake of unhealthy options.

In general, the non-village indigenous population in Brazil faces nutritional and food consumption difficulties similar to those of the village dwelling indigenous population and the Brazilian population in general. A highlight is the increase in obesity among adolescents, adults and the elderly, as well as the growth in the consumption of ultra-processed foods in all age groups. These factors indicate a growing problem of excess weight in this population and a reduction in the consumption of traditional foods. Obesity, a global public health problem, affects populations at all socioeconomic levels, and non-village indigenous people are no exception to this trend [18]. An important difference is the reduction in the prevalence of severe and moderate stunting, thinness, being overweight and obesity among non-village indigenous children. Studies indicate that indigenous children tend to weigh less and be smaller in height compared to children of other ethnicities [6, 19].

Improvements in the nutritional status of children and adolescents, with significant reductions in conditions such as stunting in children and adolescents and reduced thinness in children, are positive indicators for the health of this population. These trends can be attributed to several factors, including better access to health services [20], child nutrition programs such as the National School Feeding Program (PNAE) [21], and programs that include conditional income aid, such as the PBF [22].

The simultaneous increase in obesity and thinness among adolescents suggests the occurrence of a global syndemic, which can be understood as the coexistence of obesity, malnutrition and climate change [23]. Climate change influences food systems, affecting water availability, agricultural productivity and food prices, which, in turn, negatively impacts food access and food security [24]. Due to their close relationship with the land and natural resources, indigenous populations and traditional peoples are especially vulnerable to the effects of climate change [25, 26]. Furthermore, children and adolescents are also among the groups most exposed to the negative repercussions of this global syndemic [23].

The increase in thinness and obesity among adolescents may also be related to the history of public food and nutrition policies in Brazil, which prioritize actions aimed mainly at children. Despite advances, there are still many challenges to fully consolidate these actions. Most of these policies focus on solving problems of malnutrition [27] and have insufficiently addressed adolescents’ nutritional issues, especially with regard to obesity [28].

The increasing trend in obesity among adolescents, adults and the elderly indicates that the obesity epidemic also affects non-village indigenous people. This result is consistent with previous studies, which showed a high prevalence of obesity in Brazilian indigenous populations [4, 5, 29, 30]. Obesity can increase the risk of chronic non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, which have already been identified among indigenous populations in Brazil, although these conditions were not previously significantly prevalent among this population [31–33].

Food consumption is an important factor in understanding trends observed in nutritional status. The consumption markers used in this study from SISVAN facilitate the identification of both healthy and unhealthy eating practices, based on the Food Guide’s recommendations. The increase in the consumption of vegetables in children, adolescents and the elderly are an indication that guidelines for promoting healthy eating are being followed by the Brazilian population. The Food Guide for the Brazilian Population, in its current version published in 2014, recommends that natural or minimally processed foods be the basis of one’s diet, while ultra-processed foods should be avoided because they can contribute to nutritional deficiencies, obesity and diabetes [34].

However, the growing trend of consuming unhealthy foods, such as hamburgers and sausages, indicates that the diet is being combined with traditional and ultra-processed foods. This trend towards a heterogeneous pattern of food consumption may be the result of the reduction in the participation of traditional foods in the diet of non-village indigenous people and the adoption of urban eating habits, which are marked by the greater presence of ready-to-eat, processed and ultra-processed foods in the daily diet [35].

As indigenous people move to urban areas, they do not find space and conditions to produce their own food, which induces them to adopt non-indigenous eating habits [36]. This change is accentuated by the presence of “food swamps” in urban areas, characterized by the predominance of establishments that sell ultra-processed foods to the detriment of healthier options. This reality, already identified in cities in Brazil [37, 38], makes it even more difficult to maintain a traditional diet that respects the cultural habits of indigenous people.

It is also worth noting that, when living in villages, the indigenous population still accesses foods that are part of the natural food environment, part of traditional food systems, and that are derived from natural ecosystems and are therefore considered sustainable [39]. However, when integrating into the urban space, the indigenous population becomes exposed to the constructed food environment and all the complexity of the current modern food system that operates under the logic of greater availability and commercialization of ultra-processed foods [40].

A study that analyzed changes in the Brazilian population’s diet and its determinants during the Covid-19 pandemic observed an increase in the consumption of sausages, which was attributed to a decrease in family income [41]. Furthermore, the ultra-processed food industry invests heavily in marketing and advertising. A study on television food advertising in Brazil identified that 60.7% of food and drink advertisements on terrestrial television channels are for ultra-processed products, which can influence food choices [42]. Although these results are related to the Brazilian population in general, they may be applicable to non-village indigenous peoples, since they have predominantly urban habits.

The trends observed in this study are in line with the concerns expressed in the base document of the 6th National Conference on Food and Nutritional Safety. The document highlights the importance of implementing Brazilian food guides and the difficulties faced due to the influence of advertising and marketing strategies for ultra-processed foods. It also addresses concerns about the global syndemic of obesity, malnutrition and climate change, and how these factors affect different populations, especially the most vulnerable. Furthermore, it points to the need for political interventions and changes in the food system to improve the health and food and nutritional safety of the population [43].

This was the first study to evaluate the temporal trend in the nutritional status and food consumption markers of non-village indigenous people across the country, covering all age groups. This approach provided an updated view of the nutritional situation of this population, which can serve as a basis for the development of public health policies. The increase in obesity in all age groups except children suggests the need for new strategies to deal with the interaction between food availability, the marketing of ultra-processed foods, and urban lifestyles. However, the study has some limitations. The use of secondary data may restrict the quality and completeness of the information. Furthermore, the classification of non-village indigenous people covers different cultural and social situations that may influence nutritional status and food consumption, and possible regional variations in observed trends may not have been captured.

Conclusion

The findings revealed improvements in the nutritional status of children and adolescents, with significant reductions in severe and moderate stunting and thinness, which can be attributed to effective public policies. However, there was a concerning rise in obesity among adolescents, adults and the elderly, signaling nutritional transition and possibly the impact of the global syndemic of obesity, malnutrition and climate change among non-village indigenous peoples.

The combination of traditional foods with a diet increasingly influenced by ultra-processed foods reflects changes in the food culture and consumption patterns of non-village indigenous peoples, which must be considered when attempting to promote a healthy diet and prevent chronic non-communicable diseases. Therefore, it is essential to strengthen food and nutritional monitoring systems to monitor and respond to these trends efficiently.
