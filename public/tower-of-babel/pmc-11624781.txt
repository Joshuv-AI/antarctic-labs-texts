Enhancing provider–patient connection through low-effort interventions: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Claire Brickson, Dagan Hammar, Ben Harnke, Vineet Chopra, Tianjing Li, Amiran Baduashvili
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionNudges, or low-effort interventions aimed at influencing behaviour, can improve provider medical decision-making through promoting adherence to practice guidelines. Strengthening provider–patient humanistic connection through nudges may increase provider satisfaction, mitigate burnout and improve patient satisfaction and outcomes. This scoping review aims to map the literature investigating provider-directed, low-effort interventions aimed at enhancing the provider–patient connection.Methods and analysisThe authors conducted a preliminary search to identify terms that will be used in the final search strategy. The search follows Joanna Briggs Institute guidelines and will be used to query Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO and Cochrane databases. Two authors will work independently to screen abstracts and full text. We will summarise the findings using descriptive statistics and narrative synthesis and will use SEIPS 2.0 as the anchoring framework.Ethics and disseminationAs a secondary analysis of published data, this study does not require ethics approval. The results will be submitted to relevant academic peer-reviewed journals and conferences. We anticipate that the interventions surveyed in this scoping review will have the potential to be incorporated into clinical practice at an individual and systemic level with the goal of enhancing providers’ sense of meaning connected to their work, mitigating burnout and improving the patient experience.

Introduction

Nudges, defined as focused interventions that shape behaviour without restricting choice, have been applied in healthcare settings to influence a range of outcomes such as adherence to cancer screening recommendations and appropriate antibiotic prescribing.1 Previous systematic reviews have investigated nudges aimed at cognitive domains of professional competence, such as knowledge acquisition, information management and clinical reasoning, with examples including electronic medical record default settings for opioid prescriptions and peer-comparison feedback on lab test ordering.16 However, nudges targeting other crucial aspects of professional competence, such as humanism, empathy and curiosity, which play key roles in fostering more meaningful provider–patient connections, have not been systematically examined in healthcare settings.2

The provider–patient connection, empathy and curiosity are aspects of humanistic care that contribute to medical providers’ satisfaction and sense of meaning in their work.7 These factors carry the potential to improve patient experience in the medical system, adherence to recommendations and health outcomes.8 Demanding workloads may compromise clinicians’ ability to provide humanistic care9 and contribute to clinician burnout.10 Re-establishing a sense of meaning through curiosity and patient connection has been proposed as a potential strategy to mitigate burnout.1113

Interventions targeting provider-patient relationships typically require substantial time and financial investments,8 potentially limiting a wider uptake.14 Leaders in medical education and hospital operations may be interested in low-cost and low-effort alternatives aimed at enhancing provider–patient relationships. Although nudges are prototypical examples of such interventions, the scope of applicable strategies is broader. The objective of this scoping review is to identify studies in inpatient and outpatient adult healthcare settings that assess provider-directed low-effort strategies and interventions aimed at improving provider–patient relationships by examining outcomes such as providers' sense of meaning in their work, burnout and patient experience. Through this review, we aim to map the characteristics of such interventions, along with the metrics and tools used for assessment, and identify possible gaps and opportunities for future research.

Methods and analysis

Protocol

The protocol was drafted following methods outlined by the Joanna Briggs Institute15 and adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) checklist.16 Scoping review methodology is selected to assess the broad range of studies addressing the topic of advancing the provider–patient connection, anticipating that this will include studies of varying target populations, intervention types, study designs and outcome measures.17

Our aim is to characterise studies that have investigated low-effort interventions to improve the provider–patient connection, map the studied interventions and describe the types of assessment tools and outcomes used to assess their efficacy. We use the term ‘low-effort’ to refer to interventions that do not impose a significant burden on healthcare systems and individual providers, although our exploration of this field may also help refine this definition. These interventions require minimal resources, training and time to implement. Examples may include checklists, simple prompts, environmental redesign (ie, making chairs at the bedside more accessible), brief training modules or other tools that could be integrated into routine clinical practice without substantial monetary or temporal demands.18 19

The review team is comprised of frontline inpatient hospital medicine providers, leaders in medical education and critical appraisal of medical literature, a leader within the department of medicine, a world expert in evidence synthesis and an experienced information specialist. The planned study dates are 4 December 2024 to 30 September 2025.

The authors (CB, AB) reviewed 1000 randomly selected articles from the preliminary search to explore language used in literature to describe provider–patient connection. Keywords and phrases identified through the preliminary search informed the development of the final search strategy (table 1) and helped refine the eligibility criteria.

Search strategy

We will search Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO and Cochrane databases. The full search strategy translated to all included databases is provided in online supplemental appendix. The search results will be uploaded to Covidence, a web platform for managing screening and data abstraction for systematic reviews, and duplicates will be removed. We will supplement the search with citation chaining. Two independent reviewers will screen all titles and abstracts and all full texts. Disagreements at full-text review phase will be resolved by a third author. We will create a search instruction document that will detail examples of included and excluded studies with an accompanying rationale.

Eligibility criteria

The full inclusion and exclusion criteria presented in a PICO (Patient, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome) format are outlined in table 2. We aim to include randomised or non-randomised intervention assessment studies, which involve low-effort provider-directed interventions intended to enhance the connection, trust or relationship between patients and their medical providers. Interventions aimed at promoting general patient satisfaction or a clinical behaviour or metric (such as medication adherence, appropriate use of imaging or goals of care conversation) without a component of strengthening the provider–patient connection will be excluded. Studies involving interventions directed solely at patients will also be excluded to align with our goal of identifying actions that can be readily adopted by providers. To identify low-effort interventions, interventions that take longer than 2 hours of provider time commitment or are judged to be costly by the study authors will be excluded. All interventions that meet the above criteria will be included regardless of their mode of delivery, such as paper-based or electronic. No geographic or language-based exclusion will be applied.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Our target population consists of medical providers, defined as physicians, medical trainees (house staff and fellows) and advanced practice providers (physician assistants/associates and nurse practitioners) working in inpatient and outpatient settings. We will exclude interventions that target providers exclusively caring for paediatric patients, defined as below the age of 16, as these interventions may not be applicable to adult populations given differences in social dynamics and maturity between children and adults.

To be included in the review, studies must include a patient-centred outcome measure. These measures could involve assessments from patients or caregivers pertaining to their connection with their medical provider. We define ‘connection’ as the bond formed between provider and patient that is grounded in a deeper understanding of the patient beyond their medical illness, encompassing values, preferences and unique life circumstances. Such a bond may enhance trust, empathy, respect, effective communication, cultural competence and holistic care, among other aspects. While studies may include outcomes measuring provider perspectives, they will be excluded if they only include provider outcomes to align with our goal of patient-centred interventions. There will be no restriction on the type of outcome measurement (quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods). We will include primary studies published in peer-reviewed academic journals without constraints based on the year of publication or language.

Data extraction

We will extract data from the included studies using a prebuilt template using a data extraction guide to help standardise the process. We plan to extract the following variables: first author, year of publication, country of origin, language, study design, primary study aim, intervention, comparator (if any), target provider population, patient population, sample size (provider and patient), clinical context, provider outcomes, patient outcomes and the measure for effectiveness. We will pilot-test the extraction form by having all reviewers extract data from three included studies. If significant disagreements are noted, we will revise the extraction form and guide to improve clarity and coherence. Given the nature of the study design, we may not anticipate all relevant variables to extract prior to conducting the comprehensive search and full-text review. Therefore, we anticipate the data extraction to be an iterative process as we may add more relevant variables after the initial full-text review. We expect to finalise the variables after extracting data from the first 10 included studies. We will track and report all amendments to the data extraction form. Once the extraction form is finalised, data will be extracted and coded by one reviewer and verified by another.

Analysis of evidence

We will construct a PRISMA diagram outlining the number of titles and abstracts screened, the number of papers advanced to full-text review, and the number of studies included in the scoping review. For manuscripts excluded at the level of full-text review, we will report the reasons for their exclusion. We will use narrative synthesis to summarise included studies. We will provide a map and simple frequency-based descriptive statistics outlining the study settings, population characteristics, intervention categories and approaches used to assess the impact on provider–patient connection.

Given the lack of pre-existing frameworks for mapping the concepts we intend to review, we will adopt the SEIPS 2.0 framework to structure our presentation of each included study.20 The SEIPS 2.0 framework consists of three overarching categories: work systems, processes and outcomes. The framework subdivides work systems into several components, namely, persons (provider and/or patient), tools and technology, tasks, organisation, internal environment and external environment. We will categorise the intervention targets based on these work systems elements. For instance, interventions addressing provider knowledge (ie, cultural competence) will fall under the person category, while interventions prompting providers to ask specific questions to patients (ie, patient dignity questionnaire) will be classified under tasks. Furthermore, interventions aimed at making sitting next to a patient easily accessible will be deemed to target the internal environment, and the interventions allowing structural changes to allocate more time for physicians to spend with patients (ie, easing documentation requirements) will be classified under the organisation element.

The processes component of SEIPS 2.0 includes cognitive, physical and social and behavioural components. We will use these processes to classify the intended effect of the interventions. Interventions making chairs more accessible, for example, will impact the physical component, whereas brief training in non-verbal communication will target the social and behavioural component of the provider–patient encounter process.

The outcomes component includes patient, professional and organisational elements, which will assist in categorising the assessment methods for the intervention. For example, a postprovider encounter questionnaire examining patient satisfaction will be categorised under the patient subcomponent, whereas a questionnaire evaluating physician burnout postintervention will be categorised under the professional subcomponent.

Presentation of results

We will tabulate the types of study designs. Using an iterative process, we will develop and tabulate categories for the studied interventions and outcome measures based on the extracted data. We will provide descriptive characterisation of the approaches and dimensions used to assess provider–patient connection. The evidence will be structured using the SEIPS 2.0 model as previously described. We will include all essential reporting items outlined by the PRISMA extension for Scoping Reviews checklist.

The results of this scoping review will offer a comprehensive overview of the existing landscape and are expected to help facilitate further research on strengthening provider–patient connection using studied or innovative approaches in clinical and educational settings. Our overarching goal is to help mitigate provider burnout, deepen the meaning clinicians find in their work, improve patients’ satisfaction and potentially health outcomes by strengthening their interpersonal connection with their healthcare providers.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this study aims to synthesise previously published literature, local ethics approval for this study will not be sought. The included articles will undergo a review process to ensure adherence to local ethical standards. The final results will be submitted to a peer-reviewed academic journal and considered for presentation at academic conferences.
