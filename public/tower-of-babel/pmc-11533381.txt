More children, more “efficacy”: the relationship between fertility and self-efficacy among the post-80 s and post-90 s generations
Authors: Haibin Wei, Qiaoqi Wang, Yibo Wu, Peng Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Fertility punishment is a powerful interpretation of the low birth rate in contemporary China, but it is not consistent with the fact that the proportion of second children has been increasing year by year for some time. From the perspective of Bandura’s “Self-efficacy”, based on the post-80 s and post-90 s youth samples in the Psychology and Behavior Investigation of Chinese Residents 2021, this paper explores the quantitative relationship between the number of children and individual self-efficacy. The data indicates that: (1) Fertility is closely linked to self-efficacy, with young people who have two children exhibiting higher self-efficacy; (2) The quantitative relationship between the number of children and self-efficacy varies based on parental roles—men/fathers show almost no change in self-efficacy when moving from having no children to one child, but demonstrate a significant increase in self-efficacy when they have two children. In contrast, women/mothers experience the lowest self-efficacy when having one child, which only shows a significant improvement when they have two children. These findings provide a new perspective for theoretically explaining the current fertility situation and expand the application of self-efficacy. In terms of policy efforts, it is necessary to provide support for women’s one-child fertility, and to increase the publicity of the view of more children and more efficacy to resist the potential energy of low fertility that arises from the view of more children and more burdens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-024-01894-y.

Research background

Since ancient times, “more children, more blessings” has been the dominant birth view in traditional Chinese culture. This view has boosted the birth of more children, and the blessings of having more children have further reinforced the view itself. According to some relevant studies, the intergenerational support and well-being of parents rise with the increase in the number of children [1], and the number of children reflects distinctive features of cultural and institutional differences [2]. However, statistics of the seventh National Census show that the total fertility rate in China has dropped to a historically low level of 1.3 [3]. Scholars have not reached a consensus on how low the fertility rate has been in the past 20 years or so [4], but indisputably, the fertility rate has long been kept below the replacement level in China since the turn of the new century [5]. How to understand the inconsistency between the traditional “more children, more blessings” view and the fact of low fertility rate in China? Scholars have tried to explain it from the perspective of fertility cost (“more children, more burdens”). Fertility costs are mainly incurred from providing daily care, education input, housing purchase, and even raising grandchildren [6–10]. Having more children signifies greater stress, burden, and loss of well-being for parents. In addition, the view of fewer and better births has changed that of “more children, more blessings” [11]. Therefore, it is possibly a harsh reality for parents that more children may not necessarily bring more blessings [12, 13] but more burdens.

Since 2011, China (mainland) has successively implemented the restricted two-child policies (i.e. two children are allowed when two parents or one parent is from a one-child family), the universal two-child policy, and the three-child policy. The adjustment and perfection of the fertility policy is an important strategic measure to adapt to the new situations of population, economic and social development and promote the long-term and balanced development of the population. Statistics from the National Health Commission show that the proportion of non-first children in the newly-born population of China continued to rise from 45% in 2016 to 59.5% in 2019 [14]. In the face of the low fertility rate affected by the view of “more children, more burdens”, how can we explain the continuous rise of the proportion of two-child families? Up to now, scholars fail to give convincible explanations for China’s demographic changes, specifically, the low fertility rate but the increasing proportion of two-child families. Conversely, is it possible to seek explanatory factors in two-child families to increase the fertility rate of fertile women? Answers to these questions will undoubtedly help to explain the current fertility phenomenon and determine the motivating factors for solving the low fertility crisis in contemporary China.

Among the numerous theories, the Theory of Self-efficacy can give a powerful explanation that the number of children has close correlations with parents’ self-efficacy. Self-efficacy, proposed by Bandura, refers to an individual’s judgment and confidence in whether he can successfully perform a task or a specific behavior [15]. In family life, parents’ self-efficacy is a major manifestation of efficacy that influences how people adapt to their parental role and perform their parental duties [16]. Human’s sense of happiness takes root in higher self-efficacy [17]. Enhancing self-efficacy can improve health outcomes for adolescent mothers [18], and self-efficacy has a stronger explanatory power regarding the impact of the number of children on parental stress, burden, and health [19–21]. Such impacts can exist throughout an individual’s life course [22]. An increase in the number of children can make fathers more confident about their careers and futures and mothers happier and more satisfied with their lives [23]. Busy individuals are often seen as having competence, ambition, and other human capital characteristics [24], which are conducive to increase individual efficacy [25]. Therefore, the impact of the number of children on parents cannot be simply equated with an increase in caring responsibilities and financial burdens, nor an increase or decrease in blessings. It is evident that, in addition to “blessings” and “burdens,” the number of children is closely related to parents' self-efficacy. The concept of “more children, more efficacy” may also be an important manifestation for contemporary young parents in China.

In summary, this research differs from previous studies mainly in the following three aspects. Firstly, it offers a new self-efficacy perspective to explain fertility situations in contemporary China. The punitive effect of having many children and the explanation for the low fertility rate in China do not quite match with the reality that the proportion of two-child families has been increasing in China. Focusing on this issue, this paper gives a new explanation from the perspective of self-efficacy, which, to a certain extent, enriches the connotation and extension of “blessing” in the traditional view of “more children, more blessings”. Secondly, it enriches the application context of self-efficacy theory. Although self-efficacy theory has been widely applied in individual actions and decision-making, there is a noticeable lack of literature connecting it to specific reproductive behaviors. This article applies self-efficacy theory to the domain of individual reproductive behaviors and decision-making, thereby expanding the scope of the theory’s application. Thirdly, this paper adopts the cutting-edge technology of “Multivalued Treatment Effects”. The traditional propensity-score matching method is highly effective in exploring causality. However, it mainly applies to the case when the independent variable is dichotomous (for example, whether one receives training and whether one smokes), but cannot apply to the case when the independent variable is multivariate (such as the case in this paper when the number of children has multiple values, 0, 1, and 2, etc.). In this paper, the “Multivalued Treatment Effects” technique (for more details on this technique, please refer to the “Empirical Strategy” in the paper) is applied to address the problem.

Theoretical framework

Bandura believed that four principal sources of information (enactive mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, verbal persuasion, and physiological and affective states) were factors influencing an individual’s self-efficacy [26] and proposed a mechanism of self-efficacy regulation and control, arguing that human behaviors were influenced by the behavioral outcomes as well as one’s cognition-based expectations for his/her behavioral abilities and outcomes [27]. The post-80 s and post-90 s were born in the context of the one-child policy but have to decide the number of children in the context of the multi-child policy. In adulthood, the post-80 s and post-90 s have to cope with new issues and social demands arising from their parenthood. Therefore, the post-80 s and post-90 s must constantly enhance their competencies and skills and re-judge and re-assess their efficacy to adapt to and contribute to their work and family lives, for example, post-80 s and post-90 s parents will strengthen their child-raising abilities by elevating their socio-economic status (income, education, professional prestige). If parents perceive themselves to be capable of successfully fulfilling their parental duties, it will be very critical for subsequent parenting behavior and child development as those with high parental self-efficacy are more persistent in the face of challenges [16]. Moreover, doing more for their children can enhance Chinese parents’ self-efficacy. As Chinese parents always give top priority to the interests of their children, doing more for their children can increase their parental self-efficacy consequently [28]. It reflects the Chinese fertility view: focusing on giving birth to children and more on raising children [29]. For example, in the context of housing-and-household integration, post-80 s and post-90 s parents often purchase house property before having children or their children’s school age in the purpose of transferring their agricultural hukou (registered permanent residence) to an urban hukou to guarantee better educational resources for their children, even if they will be in debts for purchasing house property.

Based on the self-efficacy regulation and control mechanism, this paper mapped the relationships between self-efficacy and childbearing behaviors of the post-80 s and post-90 s parents by integrating the abilities (socio-economic status, including income level, educational level, and occupational prestige), the conditions (hukou, housing, debt, and whether there is assistance of grandparental care1), and the three information sources (enactive mastery experiences, vicarious experiences, and physiological and affective states2) affecting self-efficacy of post-80 s and post-90 s parents (as shown in Fig. 1). It should be noted that in line with the connotations of the three information resources, the paper uses whether one has children as direct experiences, considers the number of siblings as vicarious experiences, and measures physiological and emotional states using the BMI and depression index. Since the childbearing behavior is congruent with the behavioral outcome of number of children, the behavior and behavioral outcomes in the self-efficacy regulation and control mechanism are merged into the number of children. Thus, the current demographic change can be interpreted as follows: In consideration of childbearing costs and burdens, the post-80 s and post-90 s will determine the number of their children and speculate on their self-efficacy in childbearing according to their abilities, conditions, and sources of information; meanwhile, theoretically speaking, their self-efficacy will rise with an increase in the number of children.Fig. 1Mechanism diagram of the number of children and self-efficacy of post-80 s and post-90 s parents

Research design: data, variables and methods

Data sources and description

This paper uses the Psychology and Behavior Investigation of Chinese Residents 2021 as the database, covering 11,707 families in 120 cities (with only one member of each family interviewed) [30–32]. In line with the contents of this research, married post-80 s and post-90 s are set as the research samples. Correspondingly, 8568 cases under age 25 or over age 40 are excluded from this research. Moreover, some cases that did not answer the relevant questions and may fail to satisfy the “common area assumption” (as 26 cases with three children) are also excluded. Finally, 1385 cases are included in the analysis of this paper.

Variable selection and setting

To explore the quantitative relationship between the number of children and parents’ self-efficacy, the paper treats the self-efficacy of post-80 s and post-90 s as the dependent variable and the number of children as the independent variable. Additionally, based on the research requirements, demographic characteristics (gender, age), capability factors (monthly per capita income, education level, occupational prestige), condition characteristics (hukou, number of properties, debt situation, whether living with grandparents), vicarious experiences (number of siblings), as well as physiological and emotional characteristics (BMI and levels of depression) are included as control variables, totaling five categories with twelve specific indicators.

Measurement tools

In this research, the New General Self-Efficacy Scale (NGSES) [33] is chosen as a measurement tool for the self-efficacy of post-80 s and post-90 s parents. The scale consists of the following eight question items: (1) I will be able to accomplish most of the goals I set for myself; (2) When faced with harsh tasks, I have confidence that I can tackle the challenges; (3) Generally speaking, I believe that I can achieve the results critical for me; (4) I believe that I can succeed in any endeavor as long as I make up my mind; (5) I will successfully overcome many challenges; (6) I have confidence that I can fulfill many tasks efficiently; (7) Compared with other people, I can better achieve most tasks; (8) I can perform well even when tasks are tough. There are three reasons for choosing this Scale design. First, compared with the General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES) with 17 questions, this Scale has only 8 questions, making the questionnaire easier to retrieve. Second, the NGSES scale has higher internal validity and predictive validity than the GSES scale, and both of them have rather high reliability. Lastly, the unidimensional NGSE scale is more applicable to this research, compared with the multidimensional GSES scale.

Quantitative strategy

This research sets three cases of the independent variables, namely, 0 child, one child, and two children, therefore the Multivalued Treatment Effects Model is applied for testing. On the one hand, the number of children may be affected by age, education, the number of siblings, etc. For example, only when someone reaches certain age can he have two or more children. Therefore, age must be controlled at a similar level to study the impact of the number of children on self-efficacy. However, in the traditional regression, the unrealistic scenario of discussing “the self-efficacy of a 20-year-old individual with two children” (that is, the so-called sample selection bias) may arise. On the other hand, the traditional propensity score matching model only applies to dichotomous variables, such as whether or not to participate in training, whether or not to vaccinate, etc., while the Multi-valued Treatment Effects Model can apply to analyze the case where the independent variable has multiple values (Imbens, [34]; Cattaneo, [35]). “Inverse probability weighted regression adjustment (ipwra)” is further chosen to estimate multi-valued treatment effects. Ipwra is adopted because the sample size is small. Therefore, this paper gives smaller weights to those extreme values instead of excluding these values directly and uses all control variables except gender, BMI index, and depression index as predictor variables for inverse probability weighting (ipw) to predict the assignment of individuals to a certain group. Gender is excluded from the ipw, given that the number of children of each respondent does not change after gender changed. BMI and depression index are the time-point survey results, so they will not influence individual fertility behaviors according to the “cause appears and effect follows” principle. Results

Basic information of respondents with different numbers of children

As shown in Table 2, zero-child respondents (respondents with no children) account for 46% of the 1385 young people; one-child respondents (respondents with one child) account for 34.73%; two-child respondents (respondents with two children) account for 19.35%. From the perspective of age distribution, the proportion of zero-child respondents drops while the proportion of one-child respondents gradually rises with age; the 26–30-aged group account for 14.18% of the two-child respondents, and the 36–40-aged group account for 57.09%; zero-child respondents account for only 8.81% of the 36–40-aged group. From the perspective of hukou, the two-child respondents account for the highest proportion in the agricultural hukou group but the lowest proportion in the non-agricultural hukou group; the agricultural hukou group and the non-agricultural hukou have roughly equal proportions of the zero-child and one-child respondents. From the perspective of per capita household income, the two-child respondents account for the highest proportion in the low-income group, while zero-child and one-child respondents account for the roughly equal proportions in the low-income group; the high-income group has roughly equal proportions of zero-child, one-child, and two-child respondents (with a difference of less than 2%). From the perspective of educational level, two-child respondents make up the lowest proportion in those with higher educational levels (bachelor, master and above); on the contrary, two-child respondents make up the highest proportion in those with college or lower educational levels (the only slightly exceptional situation is that the proportion of one-child respondents in the high school group is about 1.5% higher than that of two-child respondents). From the perspective of occupational prestige, zero-child respondents have the highest occupational prestige, followed successively by one-child and two-child respondents; and the differences in occupational prestige of zero-child, one-child, and two-child respondents are not significant. Observed from the perspective of housing property, one-child respondents account for the lowest proportion in both the no-property group (respondents with no housing property) and three-or-more-property group (respondents with three or more housing properties). From the perspective of debt, zero-child respondents make up the highest proportion in the no-debt group and two-child respondents make up the highest proportion in the in-debt group. From the perspective of the number of siblings, zero-child respondents account for the highest proportion in the no-sibling group and the one-sibling group, while two-child respondents account for the lowest proportion in the no-sibling group and the one-sibling group; in contrast, two-child respondents account for the highest proportion of the two-or-more-sibling group, followed successively by one-child respondents and no-child respondents. The mean value of the dependent variable self-efficacy for the full sample is 3.669; the self-efficacy value of one-child respondents is the lowest (3.647), followed by that of zero-child respondents (3.668), and that of two-child respondents is the highest (3.710). But are these small differences in self-efficacy really associated with the number of children? For example, as mentioned above, the proportion of respondents with higher educational levels in the two-child group (respondents with two children) is much lower than that of respondents with lower educational levels. Is it possible that people with higher educational levels are younger and haven’t completed childbirth because of longer education duration as a result of higher education popularization? Therefore, a matching approach should be applied to exclude confounding factors or avoid sample selection bias.

POM of self-Efficacy for respondents with different numbers of children

As shown in Fig. 2, the potential mean self-efficacy of respondents with different numbers of children is estimated using propensity score matching. The term “potential outcome means” (POM) is used because various scenarios, including the zero-child, one-child, and two-child cases, can be found in the observed data. POM refers to the self-efficacy of a group if all the group members are assumed to have no children, one child, or two children, which are counterfactual outcomes. When the full sample is observed, if all respondents are assumed to have no children, the average self-efficacy of the group is about 3.656; this figure decreases slightly to about 3.621 if all respondents have one child; and this figure increases substantially to about 3.759 if the group have two children. If the female group is assumed to have no children, their self-efficacy is about 3.685; if the female group is assumed to have one child, the self-efficacy decreases substantially by about 0.086%; if the female group is assumed to have two children, their self-efficacy increases to about 3.731. If the male group is assumed to have no children, their self-efficacy is the lowest (about 3.614). However, in stark contrast to women, men's self-efficacy shows almost no change when they have one child. Of course, similar to women, when men have two children, their self-efficacy also increases, but the increase is more pronounced, reaching a level of approximately 3.828.Fig. 2Self-efficacy of Respondents with Different Number of Children. Overall, there is a gender difference in the quantitative relationship between the number of children and self-efficacy. Comparison of potential self-efficacy among respondents with different numbers of children

As shown in Table 3, this part compares the potential self-efficacy of respondents with different numbers of children and checks its statistical significance at the confidence intervals of 90% and above. According to the comparison results of the full sample, the full sample has a statistically significant increase in self-efficacy when changing from having no children to two children or from having one child to having two children; the full sample has a slight decrease in self-efficacy when changing from no children to having one child, although there is not a significant probability (confidence level/probability below 90%) to infer the sample results to the overall population. The results for the female group indicate that, compared to women who have not given birth, those who have one or two children find it difficult to observe an increase in self-efficacy with a high probability (confidence level/probability of 90% or above); on the contrary, compared to women who have not given birth, the female have a high probability (of over 90%) of experiencing an about 0.079 decrease in self-efficacy when changing from having no children to having one child; but the female have an about 0.123 increase when changing from having one child to two children, and the difference is statistically significant at the 95% confidence level. According to the comparison results of the male group, the male group have a statistically significant increase in self-efficacy when changing from having no children to one child and from having one child to two children; having only one child increases the male self-efficacy slightly by about 0.009, which is not statistically significant; having the second child increases the male self-efficacy significantly by about 0.216. The difference coefficient is statistically significant at the 99% confidence interval, so this increase is considered to be real. Compared with the two-valued treatment effects matching method (just setting experimental and control groups, two-valued score matching can be judged by if the p value of two groups is greater than 0.1. Conclusions and discussion

“More children, more blessings” or “more children, more burdens”, which is true? This question can explain why many scholars have great interest in explaining the fertility rate changes in China, and it is also a hot issue for the long-term balanced development of China’s population. The punitive effect of having many children and the explanation for the low fertility rate discussed in existing studies do not quite match the reality that the proportion of two-child families has been rising in China. This paper gives a new explanation from the self-efficacy perspective and uses the cutting-edge technology “Multivalued Treatment Effects” to explore the quantitative relationship between the number of children and individual self-efficacy. The main conclusions derived from the data analysis are as follows.

Firstly, the number of children is closely related to individual’s self-efficacy, and having two children can effectively increase the self-efficacy of young parents. According to the results of the full sample, changing from having no children to two children and changing from having one child to two children will bring a statistically significant increase in self-efficacy; changing from having no children to one child will bring a slight decrease in self-efficacy, although the difference is not statistically significant at the 95% confidence interval. Overall, the number of children is closely related to individual’s self-efficacy, and two-child post-80 s and 90 s parents have greater self-efficacy than zero-child and one-child parents. As proved by some studies, besides young post-80 s and post-90 s parents, rural older adults with two children tend to have better mental conditions and more blessings than those with one child [36]. This research offers more evidence for the traditional Chinese view that having more children brings more blessings and enriches the connotations of blessing, that is, blessing does not necessarily mean an increase of wealth but an increase of self-efficacy.

Moreover, gender differences exist in the phenomenon of “more children, more efficacy”. According to the gender-specific results, when men/fathers have one child, their self-efficacy shows almost no change, but when they have two children, a significant increase in self-efficacy can be observed. In contrast, women/mothers experience their lowest self-efficacy when having one child, with a significant improvement in self-efficacy only after having two children. Clearly, this gender difference is an important reflection of the clash and intertwining of deeply rooted traditional culture and the dramatic changes of modern thought. On one hand, China has a strong tradition of lineage continuation and a “male breadwinner, female caregiver” family division of labor, which makes it deeply ingrained for women to become mothers and take on the role of family caregivers. While men are also expected to be fathers, they are not required to bear the burden of family caregiving, which is reflected in the data as showing almost no change in self-efficacy when having one child. On the other hand, the new China’s practice of gender equality has significantly increased women's educational attainment and has resulted in an exceptionally high female labor participation rate that is rare among countries worldwide (Yang Fan, He Yuchen, [37]). This clash has resulted in Chinese women of childbearing age feeling the necessity to have one child, which is accompanied by a high level of “work-family conflict.” This may reflect the reality observed in the data that women's self-efficacy is at its lowest when having one child. However, when people choose to have two children, both men and women are strongly motivated by a sense of responsibility and commitment, which starkly contrasts with the largely passive mindset tamed by traditional culture that is often associated with having only one child. As we heard in our case interviews, many young people with only one child described those who have two children as “superheroes,” “incredibly strong,” or “truly amazing.” Consequently, the data shows a significant increase in self-efficacy for both men and women. In summary, whether it is the non-linear relationship observed between the number of children and women’s/mothers’ self-efficacy in this study, or other complex relationships identified in existing literature, it indicates that the gender roles and differences shaped by the intertwining of traditional and modern social changes should not be overlooked.

Overall, based on existing studies, this paper takes on the new perspective of self-efficacy to explain the demographic changes in China, that is, the low infertility rate and the ever-rising proportion of two-child families. The paper also incorporates individuals’ reproductive behaviors and decisions into the theoretical framework of self-efficacy, thereby expanding and enriching the application scenarios of self-efficacy theory. The research has the following inspirations. To begin with, this research can provide a reference for the post-80 s and post-90 s parents to make scientific and rational fertility decisions (by suggesting that having two children does not necessarily bring burdens; it is also associated with an important role in enhancing self-confidence). In addition, this research can provide a reference for the government to formulate fertility policies and the related supporting measures (For example, the government should publicize the positive effects of fertility to encourage the post-80 s and post-90 s parents to have two children and strengthen their willingness to have more children and the government should adopt policies to increase the efficacy of women when they have their first child).

This paper still has some limitations. In particular, the use of cross-sectional data means that the paper cannot make strict determinations about causal relationships—after all, as mentioned earlier, self-efficacy and reproductive behavior are more likely to influence each other and be mutually causal. In possible follow-up longitudinal studies, mediation variables or structural equation modeling could be employed to more comprehensively reveal the interactive relationships. Besides, this paper does not discuss the impact of having three children on self-efficacy, because the number of three-child cases in the full sample is too small to allow for matching control variables. As a result, the multivalued treatment effects model fails to satisfy the “common area assumption”. Worse still, this paper does not collect information on the gender of the children, so it is impossible to investigate the impact of the gender of children on self-efficacy. However, all these limitations can provide the basis for further research.
