Development and validation of a UPLC-MS/MS method for the quantification of asciminib and its pharmacokinetic interaction and metabolic stability with shikonin
Authors: Chenjian Zhou, Hailun Xia, Yingying Hu, Xiaohai Chen, Que Zou, Xuegu Xu, Zhe-li Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAsciminib has been approved for use in patients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) in the chronic phase. Shikonin has multiple pharmacological effects and is a mixed inhibitor of CYP450 enzymes. In clinical practice, the combination of asciminib and shikonin may lead to drug interactions. This study developed and validated a highly efficient and accurate ultra-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) method for the quantitative determination of asciminib and investigated the interaction between asciminib and shikonin. Chromatographic separations of asciminib and fluconazole (internal standard, IS) were accomplished by a 2.0-min gradient elution with acetonitrile and water (with 0.1% formic acid) as the mobile phase. Quantification was conducted in positive ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). The results showed that the linear correlation of asciminib was satisfactory within 5-1000 ng/mL with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 5 ng/mL. Moreover, the validation of required recoveries, precision, accuracy, stability, and matrix effects were achieved in accordance with FDA guidelines. Subsequently, the interaction between asciminib and shikonin in rats was investigated. Pharmacokinetic results showed that shikonin reduced the CLz/F and increased the AUC(0−t). Metabolic stability results showed that shikonin significantly prolonged the in vitro t1/2 from 178.9 ± 14.0 to 748.0 ± 208.7 min and significantly reduced intrinsic clearance (Clint) from 12.9 ± 1.0 to 3.1 ± 0.8 µL/min/mg protein. The UPLC-MS/MS method established in this experiment was confirmed to be capable of accurately quantifying asciminib and was successfully used in both in vivo and in vitro studies of asciminib in combination with shikonin.Keywords: Asciminib, Shikonin, UPLC-MS/MS, Herb, Interaction

Introduction

Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML), a myeloproliferative neoplasm, is driven by a tyrosine kinase known as BCR/ABL1 [1]. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) were shown to effectively interfere with the interaction between the BCR/ABL1 oncogene and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), thereby inhibiting the proliferation of malignant clones. TKIs have emerged as powerful agents to treat Philadelphia chromosome (Ph)-positive CML [2]. This targeted therapeutic approach was reported to have transformed the natural course of CML, with the 10-year remission rate increasing from approximately 20% to 80–90% over two decades of follow-up. However, a subset of patients was still found to experience treatment failure as a result of intolerance to conventional TKIs or the development of CML resistance [3].

Asciminib is an allosteric inhibitor approved for the treatment of chronic-phase CML (CML-CP) patients who are refractory or intolerant to traditional TKIs, as well as patients with T315I mutations [4, 5]. It restores inhibition of kinase activity by mimicking myristate and binding to the myristoyl site [6]. Furthermore, asciminib is also selective for inhibition of unmutated BCR/ABL1, ABL1 kinase, and all variants of the clinically observed adenosine triphosphate (ATP) -site mutants, including T315I [7]. Therefore, it is expected to overcome the therapeutic hurdles of traditional ATP-competitive TKIs due to its novel specificity (myristoyl site), which minimizes off-target effects with reduced adverse events [8]. However, during the treatment, there can also be AEs such as neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and headache [9, 10].

A recently conducted study evaluating the pharmacokinetic profiles of asciminib in male healthy participants demonstrated that the drug was rapidly absorbed after oral intake, reaching maximum plasma concentrations 2 h after dosing, and that direct oxidation and glucuronidation have been determined to be the main metabolic routes [11]. It was mainly catalyzed by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4, whose relative contribution was estimated at 36% based on in vitro and in vivo data [12]. Asciminib is a substrate of CYP3A4, and there may be an increased risk of toxicity when combined with a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor [13, 14].

In recent years, herbal and botanical medicines have gained widespread popularity worldwide as complementary and alternative therapies. Unlike modern pharmaceuticals, which require rigorous laboratory testing, clinical trials, and approval by regulatory authorities before entering the market, most commercially available herbal products lack robust scientific evidence to support their efficacy and safety. Therefore, the possibilities of herb-drug interactions during and before dosing should be considered [15].

Shikonin is a lipophilic hydroxynaphthoquinone found predominantly in the root epidermis of over 150 species of traditional herbs from the Boraginaceae family [16, 17]. It has antithrombotic, anti-inflammatory, antifungal, antibacterial, antioxidant, and antitumor properties [17, 18]. It is often explored in complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) for various therapeutic purposes [19, 20]. Although these two compounds have distinct therapeutic indications, the increasing trend of patients using complementary and CAM alongside conventional cancer therapies has raised the possibility of their co-administration [21]. Furthermore, both asciminib and shikonin have been shown to share common pharmacokinetic pathways, including metabolism via cytochrome P450 enzymes, which underscores the relevance of investigating their potential interaction. However, no detailed reports have been found in the literature regarding interactions between asciminib and shikonin. As both drugs are metabolized by enzymes such as CYP3A4, the potential for competitive inhibition or enzyme induction may alter the pharmacokinetic profiles of these compounds, potentially leading to changes in drug efficacy or increased toxicity [14, 22]. This could pose significant concerns, particularly for patients already on complex medication regimens.

Compared with previously reported methods for asciminib analysis, the ultra performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) analytical method was optimized to enhance operational convenience and to enable rapid and precise determination of asciminib concentrations in plasma [23–27]. This method was subsequently applied to investigate the drug-drug interactions between asciminib and shikonin.

Experimental

Reagents and chemicals

Reference standards of asciminib (over 98% purity, Fig. 1A), fluconazole (used as internal standard, IS, over 98% purity, Fig. 1B) and shikonin (over 98% purity) were supplied by Beijing sunflower and technology development CO., LTD (Beijing, China). Formic acid (FA, 98% purity) was supplied by Beijing Sunflower and technology development CO., LTD (Beijing, China). LC-grade acetonitrile and methanol were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). The water for the entire experiment was ultrapure water, produced by Millipore Purification Systems (Millipore, Bedford, Massachusetts, USA). Except for the mentioned, other chemicals and solvents were provided in analytical grade. Liver tissues were collected from six rats. Rat liver microsomes (RLM) were prepared in the laboratory according to previously published protocols [28], and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Analytical conditions of UPLC-MS/MS

The initial mobile phase for the liquid chromatographic separation was made up of 90% solvent A (water with 0.1% FA) and 10% solvent B (acetonitrile). The analyte and IS were then eluted at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min in a gradient as follows: 0–0.5 min maintained at 90% solvent A, 0.5–1.0 min linearly decreased to 10% solvent A, 1.0–1.4 min kept at 10% solvent A, 1.4–1.5 min steeply reversed back to 90% solvent A, and 1.5–2.0 min kept at 90% solvent A. Additionally, a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) was used, with a temperature setting of 40℃.

Mass analysis was conducted on a Waters Acquity I-Class and XEVO TQS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters Corp., Milford, MA, USA). Determination of the precursor and product ions of asciminib and fluconazole (IS) was performed with a dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) system. The mass spectrometry parameters were optimally tuned for asciminib and IS, including cone voltages of 10 V and 6 V, and collision energies of 20 eV, and 16 eV, respectively. The mass spectrometry was performed (m/z 450.84 → 432.99 for asciminib, m/z 307.13 → 220.11 for IS) in positive ionization. Data acquisition was performed by Masslynx 4.1 software (Waters Corp.).

Formulation of working solutions and quality control (QC) samples

Asciminib (1 mg/mL) and IS (1 mg/mL) were dissolved in methanol and prepared separately as standard stock solutions. The working solutions were prepared in a range of concentrations (50, 100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000 and 10000 ng/mL) after gradual dilution in methanol. Different concentrations of 10 µL asciminib working solution were mixed with 90 µL of drug-free rat plasma to obtain calibration solutions (5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 ng/mL).

Quality control (QC) samples at three concentration levels (10, 400, 800 ng/mL) were prepared using the same approach. All stock and working solutions prepared beforehand were preserved at -80 °C and kept for further use.

Sample processing

For the preparation of the samples, acetonitrile was added to achieve rapid and efficient protein precipitation. In a 1.5 mL polypropylene centrifuge tube, 100 µL of plasma sample was mixed with 10 µL of 500 ng/mL IS solution and 300 µL of acetonitrile. After thorough vortexing of the mixture for 3 min, the tubes were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Finally, the obtained 100 µL of supernatant was transferred into a new autosampler vial and 2 µL was injected into the analytical system for quantification.

Method validation

The assay was performed based on the FDA guidance for bioanalytical method validation, including evaluations of selectivity, calibration curves, lower limit of quantification (LLOQ), accuracy, precision, recovery, matrix effects, and stability [29]. Five replicates were analyzed at each QC level. Although this number was slightly below the six replicates recommended by the FDA, the obtained accuracy and precision values met the FDA acceptance criteria, demonstrating the reliability of the method.

Selectivity

The selectivity of the method was evaluated by examining potential interferences in blank plasma (from six different rats), standard solutions, and real rat plasma samples after dosing.

Sensitivity and linearity

The eight-point calibration solutions of asciminib were 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1000 ng/mL, which were conducted over 3 days (n = 3). The weighted (1/x2) least squares regression model was used to assess linearity by plotting the ratio of analyte peak area to IS peak area relative to the nominal analyte concentration. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) is the lowest point of the calibration curve needed to meet certain precision and accuracy expectations (accuracy within ± 20%, precision ≤ 20%).

Accuracy and precision

Accuracy was measured as the percentage of the measured concentration relative to the nominal concentration (relative error RE, %) and precision was expressed as relative standard deviation (RSD, %). It was examined for accuracy and precision by analyzing samples at three QC levels (10, 400 and 800 ng/mL) over a period of three days. According to the requirements, the variation in precision and accuracy should be within ± 15%.

Matrix effect and extraction recovery

The recovery of the preparation method was examined by comparing the ratio of analyte peak areas before and after extraction. Matrix effect (ME) was also investigated by comparing the response of asciminib in plasma after extraction with that in a pure solution (n = 5). Both recovery and ME were evaluated using QC levels (10, 400 and 800 ng/mL).

Stability

The experiment was investigated at three QC levels (10, 400 and 800 ng/mL) under different storage conditions (n = 5). Evaluation of the samples stability at the three QC levels was carried out under different storage conditions, including long-term storage (-80 °C for 3 weeks), short-term storage (3 h at room temperature), three freeze-thaw cycles (-20 °C to room temperature), and 4 h in an autosampler (10 °C). These time points were selected based on the maximum storage duration of samples under each condition.

Pharmacokinetic study

Eight male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (weight 250–280 g) were supplied by the Laboratory Animal Centre of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China) and were allowed to drink and eat freely for two weeks in an environmental controlled rearing room.

Subsequently, SD rats were grouped randomly into A (single group, n = 4) and B (co-administered group, n = 4). These animals were not fed for 12 h but had free access to water before the experiment. Asciminib was suspended in 0.5% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and shikonin was dissolved in corn oil. The dosage of asciminib was determined based on body surface area conversion at 8 mg/kg [30], while the dosage of shikonin was established at 20 mg/kg according to prior literature [31]. At the start of the experiment, corn oil was administered to Group A, and shikonin (20 mg/kg) was administered to Group B via gavage. Thirty min later, asciminib (8 mg/kg) was administered to both groups by gavage. Samples of approximately 0.3 mL of caudal venous blood were collected into heparin-containing polyethylene tubes at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h post-dose. The plasma was immediately separated from the whole blood after centrifugation at 8,000 rpm for 10 min and stored at -80 °C until analysis. The experimental process of the animals strictly adhered to the regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China).

Animals were euthanized using the anesthesia method according to the AVMA Guidelines for the Animal Euthanasia. All experimental animals were euthanized with intravenous pentobarbital (150 mg/kg) after completion of the experiment. After ensuring that the animals were free of life pointers, they were packaged and cremated.

Metabolic stability experiment in rat liver microsomes

The metabolic stability of asciminib was determined in rat liver microsomes in the presence or absence of shikonin. In a 200 µL enzyme incubation system, the following components were included: PBS (pH 7.4), asciminib, 0.3 mg/mL RLM, and 1 mM NADPH. Based on previous literature, the concentrations of asciminib and shikonin were determined to be 1 µM and 10 µM, respectively [32]. Asciminib and shikonin were dissolved in DMSO, and the concentration of DMSO was maintained at ≤ 1%. Based on previously published studies and our experimental conditions, asciminib and shikonin are considered to be fully soluble in the incubation system at the concentrations used [33, 34]. Shikonin was added to the experimental group, while an equal volume of DMSO was added to the control group. The incubation system was maintained at a temperature of 37 °C. At 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, and 210 min after incubation began, the incubation systems were placed at -80 °C to terminate the reaction. After the reaction was completely terminated, 400 µL acetonitrile and 20 µL IS working solution were added. The mixture was thawed and vortexed for 2 min, then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. 100 µL of the supernatant was aspirated and transferred to a sample vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis. The metabolic stability of asciminib was determined, and the in vitro half-life (t1/2) and intrinsic clearance (Clint) were calculated.

The in vitro t1/2 was obtained using the equation: t1/2 = 0.693/k; V (µL/mg) = volume of incubation (µL) /protein in the incubation (mg); Clint (µL/min/mg protein) = V × 0.693/t1/2 [32].

Statistical analysis

Pharmacokinetic parameters were obtained by noncompartmental analysis using Drug and Statistics (DAS) software (version 3.0, Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, China), and the parameters were statistically analyzed using the independent samples t-test. Mean plasma concentration-time curves were generated with Origin 8.0. All data were shown as mean ± SD and analyzed using SPSS 24.0.

Results and discussions

Method validation

Various liquid chromatographic conditions, such as the mobile phase and column type, were optimized for obtaining favorable peak shapes, short retention time and high detection sensitivity. Different combinations of mobile phases, including acetonitrile, methanol, and water (with FA or not) were evaluated. At last, a gradient elution was performed using acetonitrile and water containing 0.1% formic acid (FA). In addition, the Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) was used, with the column temperature maintained at 40℃, resulting in a good separation with symmetrical peak shapes. Furthermore, the mass spectrometry parameter conditions were optimized according to the values of resolution and response.

Selectivity

To examine the selectivity of the method, blank plasma samples without analytes (Fig. 2A), blank plasma samples with analytes (Fig. 2B) and real rat plasma samples (Fig. 2C) were analyzed. It was evident that asciminib and IS achieved effective separation from endogenous substances without interference.

Linearity of calibration curve and LLOQ

The linear regression equation of the peak ratio (y) versus the matched concentration (x) was y = 0.0105117x + 0.0311626 (r2 = 0.998), showing a good linear relationship between 5.0 and 1000 ng/mL. The LLOQ achieved was 5.0 ng/mL, which had an RE below ± 20% and an RSD ≤ 20% (shown in Table 1).

Shikonin remarkably changed the major pharmacokinetic profiles of asciminib in sprague-dawley rats

After analysis by DSA 3.0, the main pharmacokinetic parameters and mean plasma concentration-time curves of Group A (asciminib administered alone) and Group B (co-administered with 20 mg/kg shikonin) were displayed in Table 4; Fig. 3, respectively. The findings revealed a dramatic increase in AUC (0−t) by 1.42-fold for asciminib when co-administered with shikonin (p < 0.05). Moreover, there was a decrease in the clearance rate of Group B, suggesting that shikonin could reduce the metabolism rate of asciminib in rats (p < 0.05).

According to previous studies, a cohort study showed a slight increase in systemic exposure to asciminib when it was combined with itraconazole capsules [12]. In addition, it was demonstrated that concomitant administration of rifampicin, a strong CYP3A inducer, slightly reduced exposure to asciminib, as evidenced by a mean reduction in AUC (0–∞) and AUC (0−t) of 14.9% and 12.7%, respectively [12]. In the present study, inhibition of CYP3A4 by shikonin could be a potential cause of the increase in AUC (0−t) up to 1.42-fold, which may lead to more serious adverse events of asciminib.

Shikonin increased the metabolic stability of asciminib in rat liver microsomes and other potentially involved mechanisms

To explore the potential mechanisms and metabolic stability of asciminib, the metabolism of asciminib was investigated in rat liver microsomes. As shown in Fig. 4, shikonin delayed the metabolism of asciminib. The in vitro t1/2 of asciminib in liver microsomes was 178.9 ± 14.0 min, while the in vitro t1/2 was significantly prolonged to 748.0 ± 208.7 min after co-administration with shikonin (p < 0.05). For Clint, the value was 12.9 ± 1.0 µL/min/mg protein when asciminib was used alone, and decreased significantly to 3.1 ± 0.8 µL/min/mg protein when used in combination with shikonin (p < 0.05). These results indicated that shikonin inhibited the metabolism of asciminib in RLM, thereby affecting its elimination. This finding provided a potential mechanism for the significant increase in asciminib pharmacokinetic parameters in rats when the two drugs are used in combination. Previous studies have demonstrated that shikonin influences drug metabolism by inhibiting the activity of CYP1A2, CYP2B6, CYP2C9, CYP2D6, CYP2E1, and CYP3A4 enzymes [22, 31]. It has been suggested that shikonin may increase plasma drug exposure through modulation of CYP450 enzyme activity. Additionally, other studies have reported that shikonin enhances the efficacy of anticancer drugs in drug-resistant cells by reducing drug efflux via the P-glycoprotein (P-gp) pathway [35, 36]. Another study was reported in which shikonin was shown to upregulate P-gp, multidrug resistance-associated protein 2/3, organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) 1B1, and OATP 2B1 in primary rat hepatocytes [37]. The effect of shikonin on transport proteins is considered one of the potential mechanisms leading to increased plasma drug exposure. Although the results indicated potential for drug-drug interactions, it should be noted that, due to the limited sample size, further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.

This study found that shikonin may increase asciminib exposure by inhibiting its metabolism. Shikonin may increase the risk of adverse reactions, and clinicians and patients should exercise caution when co-administering these agents. Further studies are needed to clarify the potential interaction between shikonin and asciminib. These could include investigating the inhibitory effect of shikonin on CYP3A4 and other relevant CYP enzymes, as well as developing human pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic models. Moreover, given the species differences and the limited number of animals used in the present study, extrapolation of these results to humans may have certain limitations. Therefore, the effect of shikonin on asciminib metabolism requires further investigation, and the underlying inhibitory mechanism warrants more in-depth exploration.

Conclusions

In summary, a highly reliable and sensitive UPLC-MS/MS method for the determination of asciminib in rat plasma was established and validated. This method was subsequently applied to investigate the potential herb-drug interaction between asciminib and shikonin both in vitro and in vivo. The results indicated that shikonin may increase plasma exposure by affecting asciminib metabolism in rats. Further research is warranted to clarify the likelihood of this interaction occurring in humans.
