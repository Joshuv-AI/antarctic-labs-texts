Enhanced sensorimotor cortex responsiveness to nonplegic hand stimulation and motor network assembly during recovery after spinal cord injury in primates
Authors: Takamichi Tohyama, Reona Yamaguchi, Naokazu Goda, Tetsuya Yamamoto, Norihiro Sadato, Tadashi Isa, Masaki Fukunaga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have reported changes in cortical responses to stimulation of the plegic or nonplegic hand after unilateral neuronal injury. We aimed to investigate the relationship between these responsiveness changes in central sensorimotor systems and functional recovery. Five macaque monkeys underwent lower cervical cord subhemisection surgery. Their hand movements were observed, and longitudinal functional MRI (fMRI) was performed under anaesthesia to assess responses to tactile stimulation of each hand and resting-state connectivity. Eigenvector centrality was computed from resting-state fMRI data to examine network assembly. Following severe paralysis of the ipsilesional hand, four monkeys showed recovery of grasping movements, with an average success rate improving until 16 weeks post-injury. Bilateral sensorimotor hand areas exhibited increased responses to contralesional (nonplegic hand) stimulation 2–5 weeks post-injury, which declined over time. These changes correlated with the centrality of the bilateral premotor cortices and supplementary motor areas. An inverse correlation was observed between responses in the ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex and success rates. These findings suggest that cortical responsiveness enhancement may be caused by post-injury disinhibitory mechanisms involving motor network assembly. Our results indicate that disinhibitory mechanisms can drive large-scale sensorimotor reorganisation, and disinhibition reduction leads to long-term recovery after spinal cord injury in primates.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54528-7.

Introduction

Spinal cord injury disrupts the integrity of sensorimotor system, leading to sensory deficits and muscle weakness below the lesion level. Reorganisation of the brain can promote recovery of hand dexterity after partial spinal cord injury1, which is of the utmost importance for patients with plegic hands following cervical cord injury2,3. Understanding the relationship between reorganisation processes and hand motor recovery is essential for developing effective therapies.

Previous studies have reported that increased responses of the sensorimotor cortex to stimulation of fingers or nerves of the affected hand correlate with recovery of the hand and upper limb functions after incomplete cervical cord injury or stroke4–6, suggesting that responsiveness in the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) can be an indicator of motor recovery after damage to the central nervous system. The mechanisms underlying the relationship between changes in responsiveness and motor recovery remain unclear, yet a study reported that reorganisation of cortical motor activity was extended to a posterior location during affected finger movements in patients with cervical cord injury, suggesting an important role of the S1 in the recovery process after the injury7. It has also been reported that cortical areas exhibiting increased responsiveness to nonplegic forepaw stimulation are expanded in the intact sensorimotor cortex of rats after injury to the unilateral cervical cord8,9. The authors proposed that the expansion suggested increased sensorimotor interactions driven by acquisitions of new movement strategies after the injury8. However, it is unclear whether changes in the cortical responsiveness contribute to central sensorimotor reorganisation and recovery of severely affected hand movement after the injury. In a rat study, no recovery of plegic forepaw function was observed after the injury8. These findings highlight the importance of investigating the relevance of the cortical responsiveness changes to central sensorimotor reorganisation and recovery of hand function after unilateral cervical cord injury in primates.

One of the graph-analytic measures, eigenvector centrality10, has been introduced to resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) connectivity11 over the past decade12. Eigenvector centrality quantifies the relative importance (hub-like role) of regions of interest (ROIs) by weighting connections to other highly central nodes, capturing the global organisation of the connectome through its recursive definition13. It is also thought to inform the hierarchical organisation of the connectome and the underlying network assembly, which have been shown to be altered in individuals with disease14–16. The theoretical and conceptual basis for the relationship between eigenvector centrality and hierarchical network organisation has been articulated within a generalised framework17. Recently, eigenvector centrality mapping has been used to depict the functional connectivity during early motor learning, and a network including the primary motor cortex (M1) and premotor cortex (PM) was found to be related to the accuracy of movements in humans18. Because global brain network architecture might be modulated after injury to the spinal cord19, we focused on the eigenvector centrality of sensorimotor areas to examine the areas’ network assembly in the reorganisation process likely across whole-brain networks after injury. In contrast, classic seed-based correlation analyses enabled the identification of changes in the strength of functional connectivity within sensorimotor areas, including the S1, M1 and supplementary motor area (SMA), in patients with complete cervical cord injury compared to healthy controls20. However, it remains unclear to what extent these changes contribute hierarchically to the reorganisation of whole-brain networks following injury. Compared with conventional correlation analyses, eigenvector centrality analyses should offer insight into hierarchical organisation and assembly of the connectome17.

We hypothesised that the cortical responsiveness change may correlate with the assembling of sensorimotor network and the recovery of hand movements after unilateral cervical cord injury. In this study, five macaque monkeys underwent surgeries of lateral subhemisection of the lower cervical cord21,22, and their hand movements were observed. The monkeys underwent intensive longitudinal task-based blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) fMRI to examine responses of the sensorimotor cortex to tactile stimulation of each hand and the resting-state fMRI under light anaesthesia by isoflurane inhalation. The eigenvector centrality of sensorimotor areas was computed from the resting-state fMRI data. Correlations between task-based responses and the centrality of sensorimotor areas or grasping task performance were examined using ROI and voxel-wise analyses to evaluate the contribution of changes in cortical responsiveness to the reorganisation of sensorimotor systems and recovery of hand movements.

Results

Recovery process of ipsilesional hand movements after spinal cord injury

Immediately after injury, all the monkeys exhibited severe paralysis of their ipsilesional hands. Ipsilesional hand movements gradually recovered in the food retrieval task until 16 weeks after injury, except in monkey H (Fig. 1a–g and Supplementary Fig. 1). The 4-week pre-injury period was referred to as ‘Pre’, and then the recovery time course after injury was divided into three periods depending on the recovery process: periods of 2–5 weeks, 7–10 weeks, and 13–16 weeks post-injury, referred to as ‘Post1’, ‘Post2’, and ‘Post3’, respectively. Representative reaching and grasping performances for each period are shown in Fig. 1a, b. Monkey I grasped a small piece of food (sweet potato cube) with a precision grip before injury. During the Post1 period, ipsilesional reaching was rare; when attempted, a hooking strategy was used and was often unsuccessful, with frequent drops, particularly in the final week. In Post2, the monkey was able to grasp a piece of food using a power grip, although fine grasping movements had not fully recovered. By the end of Post3, it successfully grasped a piece of food using the thumb and index finger. Performance accuracy, defined as success rate, increased for retrieval during the Post1 period, followed by a return to baseline over the observation period, except in monkey H (Fig. 1c). In contrast, success rates of grasping movements remained variable across monkeys during the Post1 and Post2 periods, returning to baseline in three monkeys (I, Y and R) (Fig. 1d). In monkey F, success rates of grasping movements showed a marked increase from 10 weeks post-injury but remained below baseline throughout the observation period (Fig. 1d). Performance efficiency, defined as movement time (the interval between finger insertion into the slit and withdrawal in successful trials), decreased during retrieval in the Post1 period (indicating improved performance), followed by a further gradual improvement that did not reach baseline within the observation period in four monkeys (I, Y, F and R) (Fig. 1e). Movement time during grasping movements also decreased gradually but did not return to baseline (Fig. 1f). Group analyses (n = 4; monkeys with good recovery) showed that performance accuracy recovered well within 16 weeks post-injury (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. 1). In these monkeys, there were significant relationships between performance accuracy and time from injury (χ2 [15] = 6795.6, P < 0.001, retrieval; χ2 [15] = 4295.9, P < 0.001, grasping movements). Post-hoc residual analyses indicated that these monkeys either did not attempt to reach for food or failed to retrieve it without dropping it up to 4 weeks post-injury, but this tendency diminished by 6 weeks post-injury (Fig. 1g). Furthermore, the monkeys tended to experience difficulties in grasping food until 6 weeks post-injury, with this tendency diminishing by 9 weeks post-injury (Fig. 1g). These findings suggest that monkeys I, Y, F and R struggled to perform the task especially during Post1, the early recovery period (2–5 weeks post-injury). In summary, performance accuracy showed substantial recovery over 16 weeks after injury, with retrieval recovering in four of the five monkeys and grasping in three, while performance efficiency remained limited (Fig. 1a–g and Supplementary Fig. 1). Monkey H could stand using all the four limbs 2–3 weeks after injury, however its ipsilesional hand function did not recover during the observation period. The monkey did not attempt to reach and grasp a piece of food with the ipsilesional hand even 16 weeks after injury (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 1). None of the monkeys exhibited any objective functional impairments in the use of the contralesional nonplegic hand, such as grasping food or supporting a weight.

Estimation of lesions

The reconstructed lesions are shown in Fig. 1h. Lesions were made at the C6–C7 segment. The cervical cords were subhemisected while sparing the medial side of the ipsilesional dorsal columns. In all monkeys, the descending corticospinal tract and the ascending spinothalamic tract were mostly lesioned, and the ascending dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathway was partially injured but mostly spared. The extent of the lesion in monkey H (83.5%) was markedly larger than that in monkey Y (48.2%). While the other monkeys had comparable lesion sizes, the lesion in monkey H extended into the dorsal column, involving approximately the lateral half of the unilateral dorsal column. This larger lesion size, together with damage to the dorsal column, likely contributed to the severe deficit observed in this animal.

Changes in activation of sensorimotor hand areas in response to hand stimulation

We set ROIs in the sensorimotor cortex contralateral to the stimulated hand to determine the hand area (Fig. 2a, b and Supplementary Fig. 2). The centres of the ROIs were located in the subdivision of S1, areas 3a/b, and the ROIs included parts of M1. There were considerable differences in the recovery process between the four monkeys with good recovery (I, Y, F and R) and the monkey with poor recovery (H) (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 1). Thus, we excluded monkey H from the following group analyses. The activations of the ipsilesional and contralesional sensorimotor hand areas in response to stimulation of the ipsilesional (plegic) or contralesional (nonplegic) hand during each period are shown in Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 3. There were no significant changes in the activation of the bilateral sensorimotor hand areas in response to ipsilesional hand stimulation after injury (ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area: ANOVA-type statistic = 0.855, df = 1.734, P = 0.822; contralesional sensorimotor hand area: ANOVA-type statistic = 0.639, df = 1.757, P = 0.999) (Fig. 2c), likely due to the inconsistent response patterns observed in the monkeys (Supplementary Fig. 3a). In contrast, there were significant changes in the activation of the bilateral sensorimotor hand areas in response to contralesional nonplegic hand stimulation after injury (ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area: ANOVA-type statistic = 4.295, df = 1.519, P = 0.044; contralesional sensorimotor hand area: ANOVA-type statistic = 5.878, df = 2.077, P = 0.002) (Fig. 2d), highlighting response consistency across monkeys after injury (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Post-hoc tests revealed that the activation magnitudes of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area in response to contralesional hand stimulation in Post1 and Post3 were significantly larger than those in Pre (P < 0.001, Post1; P < 0.001, Post3), and those in Post3 were significantly smaller than those in Post1 (P < 0.001). Similarly, the activation magnitudes of the contralesional sensorimotor hand area in response to contralesional hand stimulation in all post-injury periods were significantly larger than those in the pre-injury period (P = 0.009, Post1; P < 0.001, Post2; P < 0.001, Post3). We focused on the responses to contralesional nonplegic hand stimulation in the following analyses.

ROI-based correlations between activation of the sensorimotor hand areas and the centrality of sensorimotor areas

The ROIs for eigenvector centrality were set as the bilateral M1, dorsal PM (PMd), ventral PM (PMv), SMA and areas 3a/b in S1 (Fig. 3a), following the results of previous studies20,23–25. In the four monkeys with good recovery (I, Y, F and R), activation of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area in response to contralesional hand stimulation significantly correlated with the centrality of the bilateral PMd (r = 0.889, P = 0.000, ipsilesional PMd; r = 0.722, P = 0.036, contralesional PMd), the PMv (r = 0.886, P = 0.000, ipsilesional PMv; r = 0.813, P = 0.005, contralesional PMv) and the SMA (r = 0.825, P = 0.004, ipsilesional SMA; r = 0.820, P = 0.004, contralesional SMA) (Fig. 3b, left). This activation also significantly correlated with the centrality of the ipsilesional S1 (r = 0.760, P = 0.016), but not with that of the contralesional M1 (r = 0.589, P = 0.138) or S1 (r = 0.477, P = 0.310) (Fig. 3b, left). There was a trend of correlation with the centrality of the ipsilesional M1, but this did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.685, P = 0.056) (Fig. 3b, left). The centrality of the bilateral PMv and SMA tended to peak in the Post1 period, followed by a decrease. Similarly, activation of the contralesional sensorimotor hand area in response to contralesional hand stimulation significantly correlated with the centrality of the ipsilesional PMd (r = 0.808, P = 0.007) and PMv (r = 0.803, P = 0.007), but not with that of the contralesional PMd (r = 0.485, P = 0.296) or PMv (r = 0.632, P = 0.101) (Fig. 3b, right). There were no significant correlations with the bilateral M1 (r = 0.513, P = 0.252, ipsilesional M1; r = 0.379, P = 0.527, contralesional M1) or S1 (r = 0.571, P = 0.160, ipsilesional S1; r = 0.337, P = 0.626, contralesional S1) (Fig. 3b, right). Additionally, there were trends of correlations with the centrality of the bilateral SMA, but these did not reach statistical significance (r = 0.678, P = 0.060, ipsilesional SMA; r = 0.677, P = 0.060, contralesional SMA) (Fig. 3b, right). Similar correlation analyses in each monkey revealed consistency in the correlations between activation of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area in response to contralesional hand stimulation and centrality of the ipsilesional PMv in a subset of monkeys (Supplementary Figs. 4–7).

Voxel-wise correlations between responsiveness and ipsilesional hand performance

In the four monkeys with good recovery (I, Y, F and R), there was no positive correlation between the responses to contralesional hand stimulation and the success rates of the ipsilesional grasping movements. Conversely, the ipsilesional sensorimotor cortex and cerebellum responses to contralesional hand stimulation were correlated inversely with the success rates (Fig. 4a; Table 1). In the monkey with poor recovery (H), a significant inverse correlation of the responses with the success rates, which dropped sharply after injury and stayed at the bottom (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1b), was extensively distributed across the temporal, parietal and occipital cortices (Fig. 4b; Table 1).

Discussion

The results of this study are summarised in Fig. 5. In the intact state (Fig. 5a), the sensorimotor cortex receives inputs from and sends outputs to the contralateral hand. In the early recovery period (Post1), 2–5 weeks after C6–C7 spinal cord subhemisection, which caused the disruption of sensory and motor pathways and ipsilesional hand impairments (Fig. 5b; see also Supplementary Information Discussion for details), the responses of the contralesional sensorimotor cortex to ipsilesional plegic hand stimulation, mediated by a partially injured but mostly spared dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathway, remained unchanged. Conversely, responses of the bilateral sensorimotor cortices to contralesional nonplegic hand stimulation were significantly increased. The increased responses of the bilateral sensorimotor cortices correlated with the centrality of the bilateral PM and SMA. In the post-early recovery period (Post2 and Post3) (Fig. 5c), the responses declined as recovery progressed, in association with changes in the centrality of these non-primary motor areas. These findings deepen our understanding of the changes in cortical responsiveness during the process of central sensorimotor reorganisation and recovery after unilateral cervical cord injury in primates.

Previous studies have focused mainly on the relationship between S1 responsiveness to affected hand stimulation and functional recovery4–6. In this study, however, there were no significant changes in the responses of the contralesional sensorimotor hand area to ipsilesional hand stimulation after injury (Fig. 2c, right), which is likely due to inconsistent response patterns across monkeys (Supplementary Fig. 3a, right). The post-injury responses did not clearly increase in monkeys I and F, unlike in monkeys Y and R (Supplementary Fig. 3a, right). The smaller increase in responses to ipsilesional hand stimulation was likely associated with the relatively larger lesion extent (79.0%–82.4% vs. 48.2%–65.9%, monkeys I and F vs. monkeys Y and R; Fig. 1h), suggesting that preserving sensory pathways play a role in cortical responses after injury. In monkeys I and F, who had relatively large lesions, recovery of ipsilesional grasping movements was slow (Fig. 1d). Although performance efficiency did not return to baseline, performance accuracy showed substantial recovery over 16 weeks after injury, with retrieval recovering in all four monkeys and grasping in three (Fig. 1a–g and Supplementary Fig. 1). Given the absence of response changes (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 3a, right), the recovered performance is unlikely to be explained by ‘unchanged’ cortical responsiveness to ipsilesional hand stimulation in the four monkeys who exhibited functional recovery after cervical cord subhemisection.

By contrast, the response patterns of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area to contralesional hand stimulation were consistent across monkeys (Supplementary Fig. 3b, left). This may be due to the integrity of the dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathway, which is the primary somatosensory pathway on the contralesional side (Figs. 1h and 5b and c). The enhanced responses of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area to contralesional nonplegic hand stimulation (Figs. 2d, left, 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 3b, left) are consistent with previous findings in rats with a unilateral injury to the cervical cord8,9. Additionally, we observed the increased responses of the contralesional sensorimotor hand area to cotralesional hand stimulation (that is, ipsilateral hand inputs to the S126; Figs. 2d, right, 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 3b, right). These findings align with evidence for the changes in responsiveness of homotopic regions of S1 in the two hemispheres after a small deafferentation in animals27. In our primate model, a partial deafferentation was caused by injury to the spinothalamic and part of the dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathways (Figs. 1h and 5b). As previously mentioned by the authors27, the early increased responsiveness of the corresponding hand areas in this study appears to be caused by a disinhibition or enhanced plasticity, in part through the interhemispheric processing of inputs following the partial deafferentation. A reduction in gamma-aminobutyric acid immunoreactivity in the deafferented S1 has been reported as the mechanism of increased cortical responsiveness28. Recently, our group has shown that non-selective disinhibition of bilateral sensorimotor cortices and massive reorganisation of motor systems can occur in monkeys after lateral subhemisection of the mid-cervical cords29. Notably, the enhanced responses observed at 2–5 weeks post-injury (Post1) declined progressively over time; however, they were still larger at 13–16 weeks post-injury (Post3) than before injury (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3b). A previous study demonstrated the changes in S1 responses linked to enhanced transcription of several genes from the early phase after spinal cord injury30, whereas structural plasticity in the spinal-to-brainstem circuit and the contribution of brainstem plasticity to S1 map rearrangement were reported in the chronic phase31,32. Thus, neural mechanisms underlying the sensory plasticity seem to depend on the timing after injury.

ROI-based correlation analyses revealed significant associations between the enhanced sensorimotor responses and the centrality of the PMd, PMv and SMA (Figs. 3b and 5b, and Supplementary Figs. 4b and 5b). These observations suggest that the assembly of PM- and SMA-centric networks may involve changes in bilateral responsiveness, likely through disinhibitory mechanisms after injury. It is noteworthy to observe the consistent correlations with centrality of the ipsilesional PMv (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Figs. 4b, 5b), suggesting a robust relationship between cortical responsiveness and PMv network assembly after injury. This finding is supported by a previous report that observed an increase in anatomical connections between PMv and S1 in monkeys after M1 lesions33. The early increase and subsequent decline in centrality may result from the expansion and/or strengthening of the network involved in the assembly, followed by its refinement. The dynamic nature of the correlative cortical responsiveness and motor network assembly would reflect a sensorimotor relearning process after injury. At the microscopic level, a study on motor cortex activity during forelimb motor learning reported the expansion and refinement of movement-related neuronal population in the sensorimotor cortex in rodents34. It is also possible that local cortical activity in motor areas contributes to the changes in centrality. Previous neuroimaging studies in patients and monkeys with cervical cord injury showed movement-related activations of the ipsilateral M1 and higher-order motor areas, including the PM and SMA, which were not observed in control individuals, followed by a decrease of the activation23,25. However, when calculating its network assembly at the global level, the changes in the centrality of M1 did not have a clear peak at the Post1 period and did not reach significance in correlation with the responsiveness of the sensorimotor hand areas (Fig. 3b). This highlights the hub-like role of non-M1, hierarchically higher-order motor areas, including PM and SMA, in sensorimotor reorganisation, revealed by intra- and interhemispheric correlations, which may support functional recovery after injury.

In addition to the finding of an early increase in the responsiveness of the ipsilesional sensorimotor hand area (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 3b, the Post1 period), its inverse correlation with ipsilesional hand performance (Fig. 4a; Table 1) suggests that the early increased responsiveness may be related to the initial severe motor impairment, followed by the recovery (Fig. 1a–g and Supplementary Fig. 1). However, the initial enhancement in responsiveness may contribute to subsequent recovery. This idea is supported by evidence for the contribution of increased movement-related activation of the ipsilesional M1 to ipsilesional hand movements during the early stage of recovery in monkeys after partial cervical cord injury23. This was noted in the four monkeys, who showed a positive attitude during the task after relatively area-restricted lesions (Fig. 1h), likely due to adaptation to the body schema involving the plegic hand. Although the following finding came from only a single monkey, it was observed that the responses inversely correlated with the performance were distributed across the temporal, parietal and occipital cortices in monkey H (Fig. 4b; Table 1), suggesting abnormally increased responsiveness in the case of more severe injury involving the dorsal column. The severity of the injury, including involvement of the primary somatosensory pathway, may have led to prolonged maladaptation to the task with the ipsilesional hand in the monkey. Even long after the injury, the monkey did not attempt to reach and grasp a piece of food (Fig. 1c, d and Supplementary Fig. 1), even though its ipsilesional reaching function was not greatly affected by the lower cervical cord lesion. This behaviour appeared to be learned non-use of the affected limb after deafferentation in monkeys35. It is worth noting that these highly responsive areas in monkey H were different from the areas commonly activated by noxious stimuli, such as the secondary somatosensory cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, insula, prefrontal cortex, thalamus and cerebellum36,37. Further studies with detailed imaging analyses of a larger number of animals will clarify the neural correlates of the maladaptive behaviour after the sever injury.

Our primate model of unilateral cervical cord injury may be suitable for exploring the mechanisms underlying the recovery of hand movement through the observation of cortical responses. This is because the primary afferent, dorsal column-medial lemniscal pathway, was partially injured on the ipsilesional side but completely spared on the contralesional side (Figs. 1h and 5b). The monkeys used in this study may be clinically classified as having Brown-Séquard syndrome, which is classically characterised by ipsilesional hemiplegia accompanied by partial hyperesthesia and contralesional analgesia following damage to the unilateral cervical cord38–41 related to the laterality of cervical cord lesions. Although the syndrome is well known for its unique features, ‘pure’ Brown-Séquard syndrome has rarely been diagnosed. A more liberal definition, relative ipsilesional hemiplegia and proprioceptive loss accompanied by relative contralesional hemianalgesia, has been adopted as the international standards42. Patients with Brown-Séquard syndrome are suggested to have a favourable prognosis for functional recovery43. Considering our fMRI findings of the correlative cortical responsiveness and motor network assembly, the syndrome may not be simply a combination of fixed symptoms in the sensory and motor domains but rather may result from dynamic central processes in the reorganisation of the sensorimotor system after injury. The early enhancement and subsequent decline of cortical responses (Figs. 2d and 5, and Supplementary Fig. 3b) suggest a critical time window for the sensorimotor system to adapt to the post-injury body schema. To prevent maladaptive behaviour and promote appropriate recovery after unilateral cervical cord injury, a novel rehabilitative strategy for facilitating sensorimotor interactions would be required from the early period when the cortical23 or spinal compensatory mechanism44 may start working and when application of rehabilitative training may determine the outcome of hand function45.

This study has several limitations. First, BOLD fMRI of the monkeys was performed under anaesthesia using isoflurane inhalation. Anaesthesia reduces stimulation-evoked cortical responses in animals46. However, a previous study used the same method of anaesthesia during fMRI of monkeys with cervical cord injury4 and reported seemingly reasonable results. Second, we consistently performed task-based fMRI scans on the monkeys (see Methods for details). However, there was variability in the evoked BOLD fMRI signals across sessions. To minimise the potential impact of this variability, ROI analysis was performed on a period basis. Finally, the monkeys received different types of antibodies (see Methods for details). This could affect the extent to which ipsilesional hand movements recovered22. Because the effect of antibody administration on recovery patterns was unclear in this study owing to the small number of monkeys in each group and variability in lesion extent, we focused on the degree of recovery over the observation period rather than differences between antibody types. This is unlikely to affect our conclusions, which are based on correlation analyses rather than group comparisons.

In conclusion, through observation of cortical responses to contralesional nonplegic hand stimulation, we successfully clarified sensorimotor reorganisation following cervical cord subhemisection in primates. The increased responses in the sensorimotor cortex declined as recovery progressed, in association with changes in motor network assembly. Changes in cortical responsiveness to stimuli from the nonplegic hand may be caused by post-injury disinhibitory mechanisms involving motor network assembly, demonstrating large-scale sensorimotor reorganisation during recovery after unilateral spinal cord injury in primates.

Methods

This study involved five adult macaque monkeys (body weight: 5.5, 5.3, 6.9, 7.5, and 5.7 kg for monkeys I, Y, F, R, and H, respectively; see also Supplementary Information for details). All the procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of National Institutes of Natural Sciences (Reference numbers: 16A181, 17A031). All experiments were conducted according to the National Institutes of Natural Sciences Regulations of Animal Experiments (revision on 1st October 2015). This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Behavioural testing

To assess dexterous hand movements, the monkeys were trained to perform a food retrieval task (Fig. 1a), which was modified from a task developed by our group23,47(see Supplementary Information for details). The monkeys grasped small pieces of sweet potato through vertical (length: 40 mm; width: 22 mm) or horizontal slits (length: 14 mm; width: 40 mm) with their left (ipsilesional) hand. Reaching and grasping movements were filmed from the side at 60 frames/s, and the first 42 trials per day were analysed offline. We defined two types of errors: ‘error of retrieval’ and ‘error of grasping movements’. ‘Error of retrieval’ means that the monkey did not reach for a piece of food or could not retrieve it without dropping it. ‘Error of grasping movements’ means that the monkey could not grasp the food with a grip using the index finger and thumb, in addition to the performance which met the criterion of ‘error of retrieval’. The success rates of retrieval and grasping movements were defined as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{(\mathit{total\ number\ of\ trials})-(\mathit{total\ number\ of\ error\ trials})}{(\mathit{total\ number\ of\ trials})} \times 100\ (\%)$$\end{document}

We calculated the total success rate five days per week for further analysis.

To examine behavioural outcomes after injury, Pearson’s chi-squared test and residual analysis were performed using R ver.3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria; https://www.R-project.org/). A single time point just before injury was used for the pre-injury data. In all group analyses, data obtained from four monkeys, excluding monkey H, were used because the recovery process of the monkey was entirely different from that of the other monkeys, as described in detail in the Results section.

Additionally, movement time was defined as the interval between finger insertion into the slit and its withdrawal. It was measured in successful retrieval trials, including those involving grasping movements. Mean weekly movement time was analysed, with inclusion restricted to weeks containing at least two successful trials to ensure reliability.

Rehabilitation of ipsilesional grasping movements

Within a few weeks after injury, monkeys preferentially reached for pieces of sweet potato using the contralesional nonplegic hand during the task. To discourage this and promote use of the ipsilesional plegic hand, pieces of sweet potato were provided as a reward whenever the monkeys attempted to use it. Immediately after behavioural testing, monkeys were retrained to reach for and grasp sweet potato using the ipsilesional hand through a hole in the cage panel, with or without slits depending on performance. The maximum number of trials per session, including testing, was limited to 142 to ensure consistency across monkeys.

Lesions of the spinal cords

Lesions of the spinal cords were made according to a published protocol21,22(see Supplementary Information for details). Briefly, monkeys were anaesthetised with a combination of ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg, i.m.), xylazine hydrochloride (1 mg/kg, i.m.) and atropine (0.05 mg/kg, i.m.), followed by isoflurane inhalation (1%–2%). After the laminectomy of the C3–C7 vertebrae, a lesion was created at the C6–C7 segment using a surgical blade and a needle. The left side of the cervical cord was transected from the ventral root entry zone to the vertebral level. The lesion was extended laterally to the lateral-most part of the lateral funiculus using the needle. After surgery, the monkeys were administered an analgesic (Ketoprofen, 25 mg, i.m., 2 times/day, 3 days) and an antibiotic (Ampicillin Sodium, 40 mg/kg, i.m., 2 times/day, 5 days). For another study on regenerative medicine, the anti-human repulsive guidance molecule-a antibody (mouse IgG; IBL, Japan)22 was administered to monkeys I and Y for 4 weeks after injury, and the control antibody (IgG from mouse serum; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was administered to monkeys F, R and H. We did not distinguish the two groups based on antibody type because of the small number of monkeys in each group and the main purpose of this study.

MRI data acquisition and analysis

Procedures

The monkeys were initially sedated with a combination of ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg, i.m.), xylazine hydrochloride (1 mg/kg, i.m.) and atropine (0.05 mg/kg, i.m.), followed by isoflurane inhalation (0.8%–2%) through MRI scans. The monkeys were stabilised in the supine position with head fixation using a custom-made acrylic stereotaxic frame. To minimise an effect of the initial sedation by injection of ketamine hydrochloride and xylazine hydrochloride on functional imaging and to keep the consistency of recording conditions across monkeys and sessions, fMRI data were acquired one hour later since the initial sedation. Vital changes were carefully monitored by measuring the oxygen saturation, heart rate, respiratory rate and concentration of end-tidal carbon dioxide. The isoflurane concentration was preserved at 0.8%–1% during the fMRI scans.

Stimulus protocol

To investigate cortical responsiveness related to the grasping task, all fingers, including the palm of the left (ipsilesional) or right (contralesional) hand, were repetitively brushed by an experimenter using a plastic brush at approximately 2 strokes/s. While the behavioural task mainly evaluated the functions of the index finger and thumb, the other fingers and the palm of the hand also played a role in preshaping, which highlights the importance of stimulating the whole hand. This procedure allowed us to obtain the sufficiently evoked activation of the sensorimotor hand area. Each of these stimulation paradigms was performed for a period of 25 fMRI volumes (37.5 s) following a baseline period of 25 fMRI volumes (37.5 s). This block was repeated for five stimulations and six baseline periods in task-based fMRI. Consistent brushing pressure and pace were maintained across sessions by carefully observing bristle deflection and using MRI scanner sounds as pacing cues.

Data acquisition

MRI data were acquired weekly for 16 weeks after injury using a 3T MAGNETOM Allegra head-only MRI scanner (Siemens, Germany) with a 4-channel surface array coil designed for the macaque brain (Takashima Seisakusyo Co. Ltd., Japan). In combination with T1-weighted and T2-weighted whole-brain images, BOLD signals were acquired with gradient-echo T2* echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence (voxel size, 2.5 × 2.5 × 2.0 mm; repetition time [TR]/echo time [TE], 1500/30 ms; 24 axial slices; flip angle [FA], 72° for task-based fMRI; voxel size, 1.4 × 1.4 × 1.7 mm; TR/TE, 2200/30 ms; 32 axial slices; FA, 80° for resting-state fMRI). One task-based fMRI scan was performed in each session using the stimulus paradigm for each hand described above. Three or four resting-state fMRI scans, each consisting of 285 volumes, were performed during each session.

Statistical analysis

Task-based and resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using customised Nonhuman Primate version of the Human Connectome Project (NHP-HCP) pipelines48,49(see Supplementary Information for details).

For analysis of the task-based fMRI, the EPIs were further temporally high-pass filtered (cutoff, 100 s) and spatially smoothed (full width at half maximum [FWHM], 4 mm). The activations in response to the hand stimulation in each session were estimated using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL)’s FEAT with NHP-HCP TaskfMRIPipeline49. Contrast of parameter estimates (COPE) images were obtained as BOLD signal magnitudes maps for further analysis.

ROI analyses were performed to examine BOLD signal magnitudes of the sensorimotor cortex in response to hand stimulation during the pre- and post-injury periods (Pre, Post1, Post2 and Post3). The sensorimotor hand area ROIs were functionally defined individually based on the mean activation among the three task-based fMRI localiser sessions, with the exception of the pre- and post-injury periods. ROIs centred on the peak of the mean activations (cluster-forming threshold: Z > 3.1, P < 0.01, cluster-wise corrected, one-sample t-test with the FSL’s FLAME fixed-effects modelling) were created using the Analysis of Functional NeuroImages 3dUndump (radius, 4 mm). The mean COPE value within the ROI for each hand stimulation was computed for each period. The statistical significance of the BOLD signal magnitudes of the ROIs among periods was examined using the nonparametric analysis50 of longitudinal data in R software packages and p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons using Bonferroni’s method (see Supplementary Information for details).

For analysis of the resting-state fMRI data, artefacts and nuisance signals were removed from the EPIs for each session by manual classification of independent components estimated using the multi-run FIX method with the HCP ICAFIX pipeline51. Then, the EPIs were temporally band-pass filtered (0.01–0.1 Hz) and spatially smoothed (FWHM, 2 mm). ROI-based correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between BOLD activation of the sensorimotor cortex in response to contralesional hand stimulation and eigenvector centrality of the sensorimotor areas. Definition of ROIs: area 3a/b in S1, M1, PMd, PMv and SMA were based on level 5 of the Cortical Hierarchy Atlas of the Rhesus Macaque53,54. The mean eigenvector centrality of each area ROI and the mean BOLD signal magnitudes of the sensorimotor hand area ROI were computed in each session during the pre- and post-injury periods. The data of the eigenvector centrality and BOLD signal magnitudes were temporally smoothed using the moving average of four sessions, and the valid length of the data was used. The Pearson’s correlation coefficient between the mean eigenvector centrality value and BOLD signal magnitudes of the four monkeys (I, Y, F and R) was calculated for each pair of ROIs. Statistical significance was computed using a permutation test and corrected for multiple comparisons based on the maximum statistical method55(P < 0.05).

An additional whole-brain analysis was performed to examine the spatial distribution of the relationship between changes in response to hand stimulation and ipsilesional hand performance. Correlations between the activation of BOLD signals in response to contralesional hand stimulation and the success rates of grasping movements in all sessions were analysed individually using the second-level FSL’s FLAME fixed-effects modelling. The group mean activation of the four monkeys (I, Y, F and R), which was correlated positively or inversely with the success rates, was tested using the third-level FSL’s FLAME 1 + 2 (one-sample t-test). The obtained T-scores were converted to Z-scores and corrected for multiple comparisons based on cluster size (cluster-forming threshold: Z > 3.1, P < 0.01, cluster-wise corrected).

Histological assessment of lesions

The monkeys were anaesthetised deeply with intravenous injections of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with 0.05 M phosphate-buffered saline and 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. The fixed spinal cord was removed and immersed in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline containing 30% sucrose and then sectioned at a thickness of 40 μm on a freezing microtome. To evaluate the location of the lesion in the spinal cord, sections were Nissl stained with 1% cresyl violet. The lesions were reconstructed by manual visualisation using a digital microscope.
