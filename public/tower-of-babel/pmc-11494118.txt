Spatiotemporal variability and foraging behavior of bee visitors to a rare long-lived iteroparous forb, Silene spaldingii (Caryophyllaceae)
Authors: Timothy D Hatten, Terry Griswold, Jason Gibbs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSilene spaldingii S Watson is a rare long-lived forb (Caryophyllaceae) found primarily in open native grasslands of the Inland Pacific Northwest and is putatively pollinated by one key bumble bee pollinator, Bombus fervidus (Fabricius). However, populations of bumble bees and their visitation patterns can vary dramatically, and some species are in decline including B. fervidus. Understanding the role of co-pollinators such as sweat bees (Halictidae) could be crucial as the plight of rare plants and pollinators intensifies. We collected data across three seasons (2015–2017) on the Bombus-S. spaldingii pollination system, focusing on three Key Conservation Areas in the Channeled Scablands of eastern Washington. Bee visitors to S. spaldingii were monitored and the pool-of-pollinators was surveyed with blue vane traps. Nine species of bees were observed foraging on the plant, while 2211 bees comprised of five families, 22 genera and 81 taxa were captured in blue vane traps, meaning only 11.1% of species in the pollinator pool visited S. spaldingii. Halictus tripartitus Cockerell, a sweat bee, was a common visitor to the plant, but this was the first record of visitation for several other species, including Lasioglossum buccale (Pérez) which has never before been recorded in the Americas. These sweat bees appear to vector S. spaldingii pollen, suggesting they are co-pollinators of the plant. Weather and patch characteristics affected visitation patterns and the pool-of-pollinators. We conclude that sweat bees are likely co-pollinators of S. spaldingii and that they could become increasingly important if B. fervidus populations continue to decline.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75836-w.Keywords: Pollination, Bombus, Halictidae, Silene spaldingii, Spalding’s catchfly, Pacific NorthwestSubject terms: Biodiversity, Ecosystem services

Introduction

The number of nearctic studies focused on variability of pollinators (or “visitors”) rather than composition or effectiveness of them is rather limited1–4. This gap extends to critically important groups of pollinators including bees (Hymenoptera: Apoidea) whose baseline monitoring data are often too limited to get a picture of their spatiotemporal dynamics5. Even less is known about the interactions of key pollinators and less common co-pollinators across space and time.

During normal years co-pollinators visit plants concomitantly with key pollinators albeit patterns of visitation may differ by year, season, day and location1. During abnormal years co-pollinators may visit without key pollinators or in greater numbers should environmental (e.g., weather) and biological factors (timing of flowering or senescence) favor this6.

Until recently, sweat bees (Halictidae) have been overlooked in pollinator and pollination studies, due in part to the large number of sweat bees found in most regions and difficulty in distinguishing species. However, sweat bees have been shown to visit many plants and to be pollinators of cultivated plants7,8, rare-wild plants9–11 and co-pollinators in highly specialized systems6.

Silene spaldingii S Watson is a rare long-lived perennial forb (Caryophyllaceae) found primarily in open native grasslands (or “prairie”) of the Inland Pacific Northwest12. It blooms relatively late in the summer producing moderately long (1–2 cm), white, aromatic, radially symmetrical tubular flowers with hidden nectaries and dorsally located anthers and stigmas12,13. Silene spaldingii is geitonogamous, capable of self-fertilization via pollen dispersal among flowers on the same plant, and protandrous with male reproductive organs developing before female organs13. During the last two centuries, S. spaldingii has become increasingly rare concomitant with the anthropogenically-induced loss of native habitat. Remnant populations of S. spaldingii are small, fragmented and unevenly distributed, covering only a small fraction of the species’ historic range (Columbia Plateau and Northern Rockies). In the Channeled Scablands ecoregion of eastern WA, losses of native habitat are estimated at 47%14. Consequently, S. spaldingii was listed in 2001 as Threatened under the U.S. Endangered Species Act15 and Threatened or Endangered by U.S. State agencies including Washington State16. Extant populations are managed in Key Conservation Areas within five physiographic regions of the species; the Channeled Scablands (WA and ID), Palouse Grasslands (eastern WA & ID), Blue Mountain Basins (northeast OR including Zumwalt Prairie), Canyon Grasslands (ID) and Intermontane Valleys (western MT)15.

We chose the Bombus-S. spaldingii pollination system of the Inland Pacific Northwest USA to examine spatiotemporal patterns of pollinators and co-pollinators. Silene spaldingii is primarily pollinated by just one species of bumble bee, Bombus fervidus (Fabricius), a hypothesis that is supported by field17,18 and experimental13 data. For example, Lesica13 showed that the number of seeds, germination and growth of seedlings of S. spaldingii were all greatly diminished for plants in which pollinators had been excluded. During this 2-year experiment Bombus nevadensis Cresson was identified as the only pollinator entering S. spaldingii blossoms and to carry pollen (importantly, B. nevadensis was subsequently reidentified as B. fervidus, pers com with Lesica). Similar evidence for B. appositus as a key pollinator is lacking, but it has been shown to forage on S. spaldingii18, and like B. fervidus it is a long-tongued species19 capable of reaching the nectar rewards of the plants deep tubular flowers.

Studies from other regions and pollination systems show, however, that populations of bumble bees and their visitation patterns can vary dramatically among sites1,4 and years1,4,20–22 and co-pollinators can be common. Intriguingly, Lesica and Heidel17 observed sweat bees on S. spaldingii, but at fewer sites and in lower numbers than bumble bees. Their sweat bees were not examined for pollen, and thus their potential as co-pollinators remains unclear. These authors also observed small moths (Noctuidae) visiting S. spaldingii but such bouts were uncommon. Developing visitation and behavioral data on co-pollinators seems increasingly crucial as the plight of rare plants and pollinators intensifies23.

Fine-scale variables such as patch size and floral context can influence pollinators. In many foraging studies a patch is defined as an individual plant, or as number or size of flowers on a plant24, or by number of conspecific plants and their blossoms within a defined area, i.e., local density, or density sensu stricto25, as in this study. Patch-scale characteristics can affect abundance and foraging behavior of bee pollinators26 and flowering species like S. spaldingii18 and other rare plants of the region27. There is evidence, for example, showing that lower density patches have fewer visitors28 and, because of pollen limitation or other factors, have lower reproductive success or fitness of offspring27,29 increasing chances of local extinction known as an Allee effect24,29. Patch context, such as flowering species richness at the patch scale, can differentially affect pollination services of focal species by facilitating or competing for these services30, or by provoking a more nuanced density-dependent or pollinator-specific effect28,31,32.

In this study we endeavored to develop more rigorous data on the S. spaldingii pollination system. Emphasis was placed on elucidating the identities and spatiotemporal dynamics and foraging behavior of dominant pollinators and co-pollinators over a 3-yr period. Overarching questions we sought to answer included: (1) Are sweat bees common visitors to S. spaldingii in the study area, if so what species of sweat bee, and did they actively forage on S. spaldingii and vector the plant’s pollen (i.e., likely co-pollinators); (2) Are the large annual dynamics so typical of bumble bees observable in the study area, and do they influence or co-vary with bumble bee or co-pollinator visitation rates (visitors/ hour) to S. spaldingii; (3) Do visitation rates or pool-of-pollinators differ by Key Conservation Area and year and are these affected by annual weather patterns or fine-scale variables.

Methods

Study area

The study area is situated in eastern Washington within the Channeled Scablands region (Fig. 1). The dramatic topography of the Channeled Scablands with its coulee channels, buttes, loess islands and scoured areas (“scablands” and “channeled scablands”) is the consequence of cataclysmic flood events that swept across the region as Lake Missoula repeatedly filled and subsequently burst during the last glacial maximum33. Review of Northwest Region Existing Vegetation Type maps34 indicates that the study area supports diverse habitats, ranging from mesic woodlands within the higher north and northeastern elevations (e.g., Fishtrap and Hawkcreek-Telford Key Conservation Areas) to xeric sage-steppe habitat within the lower southern and southeastern elevations (e.g., Lamona Key Conservation Area). Some of the largest populations of S. spaldingii are found here (≈ 10,000 plants)35, but only one S. spaldingii-pollinator study has been conducted in the region15.

Study area location map showing baseline monitoring sites and S. spaldingii-pollinator observation plots in the Channeled Scablands ecoregion of eastern Washington, USA.

Weather

Monthly precipitation (in mm) and temperature (in °F) data were downloaded from three weather stations: Davenport, Spokane 17 SSW and Odessa36. These stations were chosen based on proximity to Hawkcreek-Telford, Fishtrap, and Lamona Key Conservation Areas, respectively, and provided cumulative monthly data for precipitation and maximum, minimum and average temperature (Tmax, Tmin and Tavg, respectively). Data were subsequently averaged across month by year and analyzed via Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) using SAS Software (SAS 2001). Independent variables were year and Key Conservation Areas, dependent variables were the weather variables listed above.

Plots

At the onset of the study in 2014, Bureau of Land Management (BLM) botanists provided GPS coordinates for known populations of S. spaldingii and accompanied the lead author during visits. Plots for observing pollinator visits to S. spaldingii (hereafter “S. spaldingii-pollinator plots”, “plots”) were subsequently selected based on ease-of-access and apparent health of S. spaldingii plants within them, and these plots provided the designated sampling units for the study. Plots were all within the Hawkcreek-Telford, Fishtrap, and Lamona (aka Coal Creek) Key Conservation Areas with estimated S. spaldingii populations of 4500, 1000 and 700 plants, respectively35. All plots were situated on land managed by BLM, but some lands here are also managed by private or state agencies. The number of plots and number of S. spaldingii within them (i.e., S. spaldingii density) varied among Key Conservation Areas, with the greatest number of plots suitable for the study found in Lamona (seven plots) followed by Hawkcreek-Telford (six) and then Fishtrap (four) for a total of seventeen plots. Because these plots contained a group of conspecific plants (S. spaldingii and its blossoms) within a defined area, they also represent patches25 of S. spaldingii.

In 2015, plots were delimited by cordoning off a 10 × 10 m square area (i.e., 100 m2) around a center group of plants. These plots were also used in 2016, but in 2017 we converted to a circular plot area with 5 m radius (i.e., 78.5 m2); these circular plots seemed easier to delineate and monitor ocularly, the eyes sweeping more naturally around an arc than along a line and into corners. Because S. spaldingii is a perennial iteroparous species13,17, with vegetative, flowering and dormant stages, it was necessary to swap in other sites with apparent flowering plants when it was dormant in original sites. New plots were chosen for their similarity and proximity to the original plots. Teams ranging in size from one person (in 2016) to three to four people (2017 and 2015, respectively) collectively spent time in these 17 plots, observing bee visitors during peak S. spaldingii bloom i.e., mid-July to early-August. The number of plots and time spent in them varied among years and Key Conservation Areas (Additional file Table S1).

Plant community

During S. spaldingii bloom of 2015, we surveyed the plant community within plots to describe patch and fine-scale characteristics. Identity and abundance of forbs and woody perennials within 3 m radii of plot centers were recorded. Silene spaldingii abundance and S. spaldingii characteristics (total number of stems and flowers per plant) were recorded within the entire plot (100 m2). Mean and standard deviation was calculated for the S. spaldingii and S. spaldingii characteristics data per Key Conservation Area, while mean forb richness (no perennials) was calculated per Key Conservation Area. In 2016, no data on S. spaldingii or the plant community was collected. In 2017, greater visitation rates and a larger field crew enabled us to recommence the survey work of 2015, with S. spaldingii and S. spaldingii characteristics data collected within for the entire circular plot (78.5 m2) each time a plot was entered (sampled) as was the number of forb species in flower. These data were averaged across samples per Key Conservation Area.

Identity and foraging behavior of bee visitors

Monitoring of bee visitors to S. spaldingii was conducted at 1 m to 3 m from plants. Plots with a higher density of plants were on occasion assigned more than one observer, and in such cases observation data was pooled and hours summed. We recorded species identities of visiting bees and observed their nectar and pollen collecting behaviors and movement patterns among blossoms and plants. Visitation events were considered complete when a foraging bee was no longer visible in a plot. Importantly, bees (or other insects) that touched a blossom but did not forage on it were not tallied as visitors, thus herein the term “forager” and “visitor” is synonymous.

A percentage of foraging bees were captured, killed, and subsequently frozen (the evening of capture) until pollen analysis and taxonomic work on specimens could be performed. More-often-than-not we simply observed bees without collecting them; high bumble bee visitation rates during the first week of 2017 prompted a no-capture policy so long as visitors could be clearly identified with the naked eye. For highly trained observers, with years of field experience, this policy was extended to the sweat bee Halictus tripartitus Cockerell and the subgenus Dialictus of the sweat bee genus Lasioglossum. Extensive video records and pictures also helped to confirm the identity of visitors.

Pollen analysis

Pollen from two dehiscing S. spaldingii plants was collected in July 2017. It was subsequently added to a vial with a mixture of 10 ml of glycerin and 2–5 drops of Safranin O stock-stain. After mixing, one drop was added to a glass slide, covered with a slip and sealed with clear nail polish per LM Technique 5 of Jones37. This pollen was examined and photographed with a Fisherbrand™ compound microscope (100-1000×). Mounting of pollen from our specimens followed the same procedure, after picking pollen from them with an insect pin under a Nikon™ SMZ645-50× dissecting microscope. Pollen from the S. spaldingii anthers was compared to that of the bees for presence of like-pollen. This was done for both safe- and available-pollen locations on the bees, i.e., “safe” pollen is tucked away in the bee’s scopae for transport to the nest, “available” pollen is located on a bee’s face, head or prothorax and potentially available for pollination.

Pollinator-visitation rates

A mixed-model with random effects was used to analyze differences in mean visitation rates of bumble bees and sweat bees to S. spaldingii (PROC MIXED; SAS Institute 2001). Class variables were Year (N = 3), Key Conservation Area (N = 3), and plot (N = 27). Model terms were Year, Key Conservation Area and Year*Key Conservation Area with Plot nested within Key Conservation Area and designated as a random effect. Response variables were visitation rates of bumble bees and sweat bees to the plant, calculating rates as number of visitors/ hour and averaging these by site and week. However, as visitation rates are proportions, rates were first arcsine square root-transformed to better meet the expectations of a normal distribution pattern. For 2017 data, which was more complete than 2015 and 2016 data, relationships among mean number of S. spaldingii plants, stems and flowers per plot and grand mean arcsine square root-transformed visitation rates of bees were examined with regression analysis (PROC GLM, SAS Institute, 2001).

Sites

In 2015, we established two baseline monitoring sites per Key Conservation Area to begin monitoring the pool-of-pollinators within each Key Conservation Area. These sites differed from our plots, as plots were for observing bee visitors to S. spaldingii, whereas sites were for collecting information on the background pool-of-pollinators. Monitoring was conducted from mid-July to early-August to coincide with peak S. spaldingii bloom. In 2016 and 2017, the number of monitoring sites per Key Conservation Area was increased to three. Sites were positioned around pollinator observation plots, generally maintaining distances of approximately 1.6 km from plots and 3 to 5 km between sites (Fig. 1). The idea was to maintain enough distance from S. spaldingii-pollinator plots as to not affect visitation rates within them, but not so distant as to yield an unrepresentative sample of the pool-of-pollinators available to them. The United States Fish and Wildlife Service’s criterion for spatial independence of S. spaldingii populations is 1.6 km, based on their accepted foraging range for some bumble bees15. This guided the lead author’s placement of traps, believing this to be a conservative spatial arrangement because bumble bees often forage at distances far less than this38.

A pair of Blue Vane (BV) traps (SpringStar™ LLC) was placed at each site. Individual BVs per pair were spaced 50–100 m apart, suspended from vegetation (≈ 1 m to 2 m above ground surface) or self-supporting tripods (traps ≈ 0.75 m to 1 m above ground surface), and placed in habitat as representative of our plots as possible. The traps reflect ultraviolet light and are highly attractive to bees39. During sampling events traps were left out for 48 h. Most bees captured in them were dead or difficult to field identify and thus were euthanized during collection, stored in containers with 70% ethanol, transported to the lab, processed, and identified. However, bumble bees captured in BVs that were easily identifiable in the field and in good condition were recorded and released unharmed (≈ 50% of the bumble bees).

Baseline monitoring data for bumble bee and sweat bee data were converted to rates (48 h monitoring period), natural log (x + 1) transformed, averaged by trap/ site (1 n = no. bees/2 traps), aggregated by Key Conservation Area (n = 3) and year (i.e., 3 sites/Key Conservation Areas x 3 Key Conservation Areas/year, N = 9) and analyzed with a two-way ANOVA (Proc GLM, SAS Institute 2001). Relationships among trap- and plot-level-visitation rates across the study (i.e., 3 Key Conservation Areas x 3 years, N = 9) were examined with regression analysis (Proc GLM, SAS Institute 2001) as was the effect of individual temperature and precipitation variables on visitation rates.

Results

Weather

Temperature variables were positively correlated with each other (most P values < 0.01) and negatively correlated (weakly) with precipitation (PRCP) (strongest, Tmin, R = -0.73 P = 0.03), and although degrees of freedom (df) were insufficient to assess Key Conservation Area x year interactions effects the reduced model detected significant differences in weather by year and Key Conservation Area; e.g., Prcp by Key Conservation Area (F2,8 = 25.67, P < 0.01 ) and year (F2,8 = 5.86, P = 0.06), Tmax by Key Conservation Area (F2,8 = 18.94, P < 0.01 ) and year (F2,8 = 12.03, P = 0.02), Tmin by Key Conservation Area (F2,8 = 9.64, P < 0.03 ) and year (F2,8 = 4.36, P = 0.10), and Tavg by Key Conservation Area (F2,8 = 10.73, P < 0.02 ) and year (F2,8 = 6.94, P = 0.05) (Fig. 2). Clearly, precipitation and temperature varied more strongly among Key Conservation Area than year with Lamona experiencing lower precipitation and higher temperatures than Fishtrap or Hawkcreek. Year effects were detected too but not always significantly with 2015 experiencing lower precipitation and higher temperature than 2016 or 2017.

Plots

We generally observed our 17 plots twice per week during the two to three weeks-long seasons, but some plots randomly received more or less than this. Across the study we visited the 17 plots 201 times and spent 193.72 h in them, averaging 3.94 observations/ plot/ year and 1.04 h/ observation/ visit. During observations, 167 bee visitors to S. spaldingii were documented, comprised of 105 sweat bees (Halictidae), 61 bumble bees (Bombus), and 1 megachilid bee (Dianthidium). See Supplementary material, Table S1 online for more details. We also observed many other insect taxa within plots including wasps (Pompilidae, Vespidae, Sphecidae), robber flies (Asilidae), ant lions (Myrmeleontidae) and a short-tongued bumble bee (Bombus huntii Greene). Sometimes these flew close to S. spaldingii, but they did not forage on it (40).

Plant community

Average S. spaldingii and plant community metrics/ plot/ Key Conservation Area are shown in Table 1. While these data are qualitative due to inconsistent measurements among years, the density of S. spaldingii/ plot (same as number of plants/ plot) in 2015 and 2017 appeared to be greater at Lamona than Fishtrap or Hawkcreek while the number of S. spaldingii flowers/ plant seemed greater at Fishtrap than Lamona or Hawkcreek. It can also be seen that in 2015 during S. spaldingii bloom average richness of the plant community/ sub-plot (3-m radius of plot center) for flowering and non-flowering plants combined ranged from 7.7 ± 1.3 species at Lamona to 9.7 ± 1.8 species at Fishtrap, while average number of forb species in flower across the whole plot in 2017 was approximately 1/3 this richness. More weed species (non-natives) were documented in Fishtrap than in Lamona or Hawkcreek (4 sp. versus 1 sp. and 1 sp., respectively) (see Supplementary material online, Table S2).

Identity & foraging behavior

Of the 167 bee visitors to S. spaldingii, 24 were captured for ID and pollen analysis. These captured visitors, all female, were comprised of nine Apidae (eight B. fervidus, one B. appositus), 12 Halictidae (one Agapostemon virescens (Fab.) and one A. subtilior Cockerell, three Lasioglossum (Hemihalictus) buccale (Pérez), four L. Dialictus nevadense (Crawford), three H. tripartitus) and three Megachilidae (Dianthidium pudicum (Cresson)). The detection of L. (Hemihalictus) buccale (Pérez) provides the first record of the species in the Americas. The principal author observed another long-tongued bumble bee visitor to S. spaldingii in the Channeled Scablands, B. californicus Smith, but this occurred outside of the study area40. See Fig. 3 and refer to Supplementary materials (Figure S3, Figure S5) online for images of bees foraging on S. spaldingii and discussion of their foraging behavior during the study.

Pollen analysis

The palynological characters of S. spaldingii pollen as detectable with our methods and equipment revealed a morphology similar to many other Silene species, e.g., pantoporate and spheroid and quite distinct from other pollen observed in this study. Of the 24 collected S. spaldingii visitors, 18 had either available or unavailable S. spaldingii pollen on their bodies (see Supplementary material Table S3). The purity of pollen loads was variable among visitors, with available and unavailable loads consistently more mixed on bumble bees than sweat bees (see Supplementary material Table S3, Figure S1, Figure S2 and Figure S4).

Pollinator visitation rates

The Mixed Model’s results show that visitation rates differed significantly among years and Key Conservation Areas for sweat bees and bumble bees (df = 2, 51 for year comparisons, df = 2, 24 for Key Conservation Area comparisons). Significant year*Key Conservation Area interaction effects were detected too (Table 2, Fig. 4). For sweat bees, visitation rates across Key Conservation Areas were significantly lower in 2015 than 2016 (t = -3.22, P < 0.01) or 2017 (t = -2.31, P = 0.03), but not significantly different between 2016 and 2017 (t = 1.39, P = 0.17); visitation rates across Years were not significantly different between Lamona and Hawkcreek (t = 1.95, P = 0.06) nor Lamona and Fishtrap (t = -0.88, P = 0.39) but differences were detected between Fishtrap and Hawkcreek (t = 2.66, P = 0.01). For bumble bees, visitation rates across Key Conservation Areas were greater in 2017 than in 2015 (t = 3.68, P < 0.01) and 2016 (t = 3.23, P = 0.01); visitation rates across years were greater in Lamona than Fishtrap (t = 3.24, P < 0.01) and Hawkcreek (t = 3.28, P < 0.01), but not different between Fishtrap or Hawkcreek (t = 0.16, P = 0.88).

Sites

BV traps captured a diverse bee fauna, 2211 bees comprised of five families, 22 genera and 81 taxa (Table 4; Fig. 5). Discussion

We show that during three consecutive years sweat bees were regular visitors to S. spaldingii in the Channeled Scablands physiographic region of the plant. Sweat bees were also found to commonly forage on the plant and carry S. spaldingii pollen on their bodies. This confirms and broadens the findings of Lesica and Heidel17 from two decades ago, providing the first evidence for S. spaldingii pollen collection by sweat bees and showing them to be common visitors to S. spaldingii across the study area. Collectively, short of conducting pollinator exclusion or effectiveness studies, these findings strongly suggest sweat bees are co-pollinators of S. spaldingii.

This hypothesis is strengthened by the striking annual interaction pattern of bumble bee and sweat bee visitors to S. spaldingii. Sweat bees appear to complement the bumble bee’s foraging niche on S. spaldingii, potentially providing pollination services when bumble bees cannot. Visitation rates by long-tongued bumble bees were erratic, swinging by orders of magnitude among years and Key Conservation Areas. Sweat bee visitation rates were also erratic, but were seemingly complementary to the bumble bees, such that when rates of bumble bees were high those of sweat bees were low and vice versa, or when one was present the other was absent (e.g., all Key Conservation Areas in 2016, and at Fishtrap in 2015 and 2017). However, it is not likely that sweat bees are as efficient pollinators of S. spaldingii as bumble bees given their smaller foraging ranges41, slower and shorter interplant movement (this study), less than optimal body sizes for pollinating the plant, and the attraction to other flowering plants within S. spaldingii patches. Nevertheless, plants essentially hedge their bets, insuring they receive maximum pollination services from pollinators available to them42. And, as some species of bumble bees are in decline across the region43, it is important to understand the role of sweat bees in this system.

The sweat bee family (Halictidae) is speciose, with no less than 90 genera44 and 3500 described species45, and discoveries or taxonomic revisions for this group are far from exhausted46. Many species of North American L. (Dialictus), for example, have only recently been described even though this subgenus of Lasioglossum is the most commonly collected taxon in North America47. When examined at the family level Halictidae seem ubiquitous across the landscape, but a similar analysis at the sub-family or guild-level often uncovers inter- and intra-regional as well as temporal differences in composition of this taxon21,39–41. The abundance and diversity of the Halictidae coupled by the vast number of plants pollinated by them makes it one of the most important group of pollinators in North America48.

Results from baseline monitoring sites revealed a diverse pollinator pool potentially available to S. spaldingii. However, only 11.1% of all captured taxa were also found to visit S. spaldingii, consistent with previous findings17,18 showing the S. spaldingii pollination system to be dependent on remarkably few pollinators. Agapostemon and Dianthidium species are new additions to the list of S. spaldingii visitors, as are a couple of Lasioglossum (Dialictus) and Lasioglossum (Hemihalictus) species. The Agapostemon and Dianthidium visitors were rare compared to other Halictus, Lasioglossum and Bombus visitors, but they were observed foraging on the plant and found to carry S. spaldingii pollen. Data from our monitoring sites is far from a robust measure of diversity because different sampling methods capture different bees49 and ours was restricted to BV traps deployed for only 2-days per year during S. spaldingii bloom. For more information on bee faunae of the S. spaldingii physiographic regions see Rhoades et al.21 and Hatten et al.20 for the Palouse Hills, and Tubbesing et al.18 and Kimoto et al.39 for Blue Mountain Basins (Zumwalt Prairie specifically).

Halictus tripartitus was the most common co-pollinator followed by L. (Dialictus) and Agapostemon. Halictus tripartitus has a western- to mid-western distribution in the USA and is broadly polyphagous50. Nesting in the species has been described as solitary46, but more recently as primitively eusocial51. Some L. (Dialictus) also make primitively eusocial colonies52 capable of producing overlapping generations per season but see53 for literature and discussion on complexity and plasticity of sweat bee social behavior. In contrast, Agapostemon are solitary (like A. subtilior) or communal (A. virescens), producing only a single generation per season48.

Whether our visitation rates are typical is unknown because baseline data for the Channeled Scablands is lacking. Spatiotemporal variability of plant-pollinator systems tends to be pronounced1, and, like other studies4, we found visitation rates to be more variable temporally (among years) than spatially (among Key Conservation Areas). Current and past data for the Channeled Scablands is more robust for bumble bees than sweat bees, and comparison of our results to that of Lesica and Heidel17 suggests that visitation rates to S. spaldingii have declined over the last two decades. These authors reported having only to “wait an hour” to observe bumble bee visitors, contrasting with our findings of having to wait over three hours per bumble bee visitor across the 3-year study, and a far lower visitation rate than this across the first two years of the study (see Supplementary material, Table S1). This pattern is consistent with trends showing that some bumble bees have become less common in recent decades, including in the United Kingdom54,55, Europe56 and North America57–59. Hatfield et al.60 provide evidence that B. fervidus is in decline, prompting the International Union for the Conservation of Nature to list the species as vulnerable. Any decline in B. fervidus populations should be viewed with concern because specialized pollination systems may be more vulnerable to extinction than generalized systems61.

Our analysis points to a relationship between precipitation and background bumble bee abundance in the three Key Conservation Areas, showing that their numbers increased with increased precipitation (a variable inversely correlated with temperature). Ogilvie et al.62 found similar effects for this variable on subalpine bumblebees, but this was due to indirect effects of precipitation on floral resources, highlighting the complexity of such relationships. In 2015, the Channeled Scablands study area was exposed to a heat wave and drought-like conditions, e.g., it was significantly warmer than 2017 (> 3 °F or + 6%) and drier than 2016 (8.03 mm or -21%) and 2017 (6.81 mm or -18%). Not surprisingly, flora and fauna were noticeably affected by this35, with the plant community almost completely senesced during S. spaldingii-bloom, and only a few bees detected at sites or plots. In 2016, conditions seemed much improved, more verdant, but bumble bee visitation rates to S. spaldingii did not increase. It was not until 2017, when activity at Blue vane sites more than quadrupled, and more species were captured, that numbers of bumble bee visitors to S. spaldingii dramatically increased while that of sweat bees remained more constant.

Climate models predict there will be heat waves and increases in annual temperature (up 1.1 °F to 5.3 °F by 2080) in the Pacific Northwest. They also predict slightly wetter annual conditions (+ 1 to + 2%), wetter autumns and winters, earlier snow melt and drier summers63,64. How pollinators respond to such conditions remains to be seen. Evidence indicates that climate change will have consequential effects on plants and pollination systems65, plant-pollinator interactions66 and bumble bees67. Without modeling we cannot predict how bees will respond to climate change in the Channeled Scablands, but response of bees (and plants) to 2015 conditions suggest that future conditions may present challenges to them.

At Fishtrap and Hawkcreek-Telford Key Conservation Areas, we captured more bumble bee species in BVs than at Lamona. In contrast, Lamona supported a higher proportion of the S. spaldingii pollinator B. fervidus. Lamona Key Conservation Area is in a narrow coulee in sage-steppe habitat bordered by range- and cropland, and S. spaldingii habitat found within it is largely contiguous, extending for several miles along the toe-slope of the upper southern walls (i.e., north aspect) of Coal Creek Coulee. Our 2015 plant survey revealed Lamona plots to be in good condition, having a lower incidence of weeds compared to Fishtrap and Hawkcreek. These factors likely provided habitat and nesting sites for this late emerging grassland species19,68, and food resources given the relatively dense patches of S. spaldingii found therein (pers. obs. Hatten). In contrast, Fishtrap tended to support a greater abundance of sweat bees. Fishtrap (Watermelon Hill area) was burned in 2014, resulting in an increased incidence of weeds or missing S. spaldingii plants within and among plots (pers. comm. Kim Frymire, Botanist, BLM) and S. spaldingii patches were very scattered. Flowering weed species often support bees and sweat bees were observed to forage on them in these plots. At Hawkcreek-Telford, S. spaldingii patches were situated on the northern aspect of eroded walls or along Telford Road nestled in at the base and on toe-slopes of walls. In both these Key Conservation Areas the coulees are many miles wide and braided with woodland habitats, yielding landscape conditions suitable for a diverse bumble bee fauna. However, conditions and habitat varied even within Key Conservation Areas, with the Sam Knack Rd sites appearing to dry out earlier than the other sites potentially affecting phenology of S. spaldingii, other flowering species and pollinators as well.

Croft27 identified livestock grazing as a threat to S. spaldingii and identified other factors including habitat conversion, lack of fire, invasive species and pesticide spray and drift, many of these factors being threats to other rare plants of the region as well. We did not assess land use in this study and thus draw no conclusions about their effects. We point out, though, that at the time of the study grazing had been restricted at Lamona since 2012, and seasonally permitted at Fishtrap and Hawkcreek-Telford’s Sam Knack Rd sites (pers. comm. Kim Frymire, BLM Botanist). We noted signs of grazing (trails and cow patties) in or adjacent to these latter plots. Targeted grazing can be effective at managing native annual forbs69 and moderate or even high intensity grazing can be beneficial for flowering species and native bee diversity70, but the latter can also be detrimental to abundance and diversity of native plants or bees26,71.

Fishtrap and Hawkcreek-Telford are larger Key Conservation Areas than Lamona and harbor larger S. spaldingii populations, but in monitoring plots S. spaldingii densities were highest at Lamona. S. spaldingii density, as well as S. spaldingii flower and stem densities, affected visitation rates at the plot level because sweat bees and bumble bees were positively correlated with it. Influence of these variables could not be separated, however, due to collinear relationships. Our findings are similar to those of Tubbesing et al.18 who found a relationship between B. fervidus visitation rates and S. spaldingii density at a local scale, and similar to results in other pollination systems24,26,72–74. Apparently, patches with high local densities may be more visible and readily encountered by bees than patches with low density and foraging within them might be more economical42.

We detected an inverse correlation between number of species in flower and visitation rates of sweat bees and bumble bees to S. spaldingii. Unfortunately, these effects were confounded because flowering species and S. spaldingii density were inversely correlated. It is notable that sweat bees were commonly observed foraging on coflowering species in our plots, yet those collected on S. spaldingii yielding available pollen had relatively pure S. spaldingii loads indicating high floral constancy per foraging trip when foraging on S. spaldingii. In contrast, bumble bees collected on S. spaldingii carried more mixed pollen loads even though within plots they exhibited a high degree of S. spaldingii fidelity. Attraction to a focal species with deep tubular flowers by bumble bees can be strong as observed herein and by Hegland and Boeke31 and Tubbesing et al.18. Tubbesing et al.18 suggests that once in S. spaldingii patches B. fervidus stays relatively faithful to S. spaldingii, i.e., “facultative specialist”75, likely due to the large nectar rewards afforded by such flowers. Our observations combined with pollen analysis would seem to support this hypothesis, suggesting that the bumble bees foraged opportunistically across the landscape but became specialists within S. spaldingii patches.

Sweat bees foraged differently on S. spaldingii in our plots, with considerably more intra- and inter-flower foraging and movement on the same plant or closely spaced ones than B. fervidus. Such behavior on the protandrous, geitonogamous S. spaldingii could be expected to contribute to self-fertilization by moving like-pollen within or among flowers of the same plant. Plants that display multiple flowers at once, a common trait for S. spaldingii and as observed in this study (Table 2), are more vulnerable to geitonogamous selfing than those with fewer flowers42. The sweat bees also moved among plants and as pollen vectors likely contributed to cross-fertilization.

Conclusions

We found sweat bees to commonly forage on S. spaldingii in the Channeled Scablands, complementing the foraging niche of B. fervidus in this bumble bee-S. spaldingii pollination system. There is an indication that precipitation positively affected background abundance of bumble bees, and only when their numbers greatly increased did visitation rates to S. spaldingii increase. Patches with higher density of S. spaldingii attracted more pollinators to a patch. Those found in the more contiguous and high-quality habitat of Lamona had higher visitation rates by B. fervidus, while those in lower quality habitats of Fish Trap had higher visitation rates by sweat bees. These data, combined with our documentation of sweat bee foraging when bumble bee foraging was rare or absent, suggests that sweat bees are co-pollinators of S. spaldingii and could become more important if B. fervidus populations or S. spaldingii habitat continues to decline. A study to examine the pollinator efficiencies of sweat bees on S. spaldingii would be a logical next step for future research.
