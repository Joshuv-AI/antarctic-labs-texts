Spatial distribution and determinants of the knowledge of reproductive age women about prevention of mother to child transmission (PMTCT) of HIV in Ethiopia
Authors: Abebe Gedefaw Belete, Genanew Kassie Getahun, Kedir Seid, Chala Getaneh Jaleta, Sintayehu Samuel Lorato, Mitiku Desalegn, Dawit Tesfaye Rundasa, Yohannes Godie Ashebir, Olyad Kuma Getahun, Gebeyehu Lakew, Mathewos Mekonnen Gemmechu, Mulualem Endeshaw Zeleke, Yibeltal Assefa Atalay, Amlaku Nigusie Yirsaw, Eyob Ketema Bogale, Abiy Hailu Tikuneh, Abas Ali Hassen, Eyasu Alem Lake, Tadesse Nigussie, Manaye Yihune Teshale, Nigus Kabtu Belete, Samuel Hailegebreal, Lovel Fornah, Abakundana Nsenga Ariston Gabriel, Mulugeta Shegaze Shimbre, Bushra Bibi, Fikiru Yigezu Jaleta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLimited knowledge about preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV contributes to the high prevalence of pediatric HIV caused by vertical transmission. Given the lack of sufficient information, this study aimed to identify hotspot and cold spot areas as well as the factors influencing reproductive-age women’s knowledge about preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV in Ethiopia.MethodThe study utilized data from the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey, comprising a weighted sample of 15,683 reproductive-age women. Spatial analysis was conducted to identify significant clusters, and determinants were assessed using adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.ResultSignificant hotspot regions were identified in Somali, Gambela, Afar, Benishangul Gumuz, the northern part of SNNP, and certain areas of Oromia. Key determinants included education level (primary: AOR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.02–1.40; secondary: AOR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.08–1.85; higher: AOR = 2.22, 95% CI = 1.52–3.25), religious affiliation (Muslim: AOR = 0.57, 95% CI = 0.47–0.68; Protestant: AOR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.61–0.93; Other: AOR = 0.54, 95% CI = 0.33–0.86), having an employed partner (AOR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.08–1.71), higher wealth index (rich: AOR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.11–1.63), media exposure (AOR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.12–1.57), antenatal care attendance (AOR = 1.46, 95% CI = 1.25–1.70), institutional childbirth (AOR = 1.18, 95% CI = 1.01–1.37), urban living (AOR = 1.36, 95% CI = 1.01–1.83), residence in larger central regions (AOR = 1.38, 95% CI = 1.12–1.70), and living in communities with high media exposure (AOR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.13–1.75).ConclusionIn Ethiopia, knowledge about preventing mother-to-child transmission of HIV among women of reproductive age varies significantly across regions. Therefore, implementing targeted interventions in the identified hotspot areas is essential to support the WHO’s goal of eliminating new pediatric HIV infections by 2030.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12981-025-00829-2.Keywords: Knowledge, Spatial distribution, Prevention of mother-to-child transmission, PMTCT, Reproductive age women, Ethiopia

Introduction

HIV infection is one of the world’s most serious public health problem and remains to be a major cause of childhood illness and death in Africa [1]. Globally, in 2023, 1.4 million children were living with HIV and Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) accounts for more than 80% of these cases [2]. In the same year 52,000 of new HIV infections were occurred among children living in east and south Africa [3]. Similarly in Ethiopia there were 1,491 new HIV infections among children of the same age [4]. Most of these pediatrics and child HIV infections were primarily due to vertical transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery and breast feeding and Prevention of mother-to-child HIV transmission (PMTCT) is the best way to halt new HIV infection among children [3, 5, 6].

The PMTCT program is designed to support reproductive-age women living with or at risk of HIV by promoting their health and preventing vertical transmission to their infants. It provides comprehensive services for HIV prevention, care, and treatment for pregnant, laboring, and breastfeeding women, as well as their infants and partners [1]. In 2021, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced global guidelines for the Elimination of Mother-to-Child Transmission (EMTCT) of HIV, aiming for zero new HIV infections among infants and young children by 2030 [7]. The program’s impact targets include reducing new pediatric HIV infections from MTCT to fewer than 50 cases per 100,000 live births and achieving an MTCT rate of less than 2% in non-breastfeeding populations or below 5% in breastfeeding populations [7].

Effective PMTCT has reduced HIV transmission to less than 1%. It is done by early initiation of ART for all pregnant women infected with HIV to minimize vertical transmission to her infant during pregnancy and breastfeeding period [8]. Globally in 2023, 1.2 million pregnant women were living with HIV and 84% of them were receiving effective antiretroviral medication for PMTCT and four million new HIV infections due to vertical transmission have been averted. It is a great progress in PMTCT of HIV; however, there is still much work to be done to achieve the zero new HIV infection by 2030. Most of vertical infections are due to mothers not receiving ART, those dropping off from ART, and those infected during pregnancy and breastfeeding [3] and one of the reason behind this is poor awareness and knowledge about PMTCT of HIV [9].

According to a study done in SSA, only 56.21% of women on the reproductive age have good knowledge about PMTCT [10]. Similarly based on a study done in Ethiopia on EDHS 2011 and 2016, women who had adequate knowledge about PMTCT were 34.9% and 41.1% respectively [9, 11] and factors like age, residence, education, occupation, media exposure, wealth index, parity, history of ANC visit, place of delivery, perceived distance from health facility were identified as determinants of knowledge levels of reproductive age women about PMTCT of HIV [9–12]. Although many studies were tried to assess the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT, in Ethiopia there is no any study that tries to assess the knowledge levels of women using Geographic Information System (GIS) data. So, this study aimed to identify the hotspot and cold spot areas regarding the knowledge levels of women on PMTCT of HIV in different geographical locations of Ethiopia to make evidence based interventions.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

The study utilized data from the 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS), a nationally representative survey. Ethiopia, the second most populous country in Africa, have a population of 107,334,000 in 2023 [13]. Located in the Horn of Africa, the country spans an area of 1.1 million square kilometers and lies between latitudes 3˚ and 15˚ N and longitudes 33˚ and 48˚ E [14, 15]. Currently, Ethiopia is divided into twelve administrative regions (Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, Central Ethiopia Regional State, Sidama, South Ethiopia Regional State, South West Ethiopia Peoples’ Region, Gambella, and Harari) and two city administrations (Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa). However, at the time of the EDHS 2016 survey, the country was organized into nine regions and two city administrations [16].

Source and study population

The source population for the study included all Ethiopian women aged 15–49 years, while the study population was drawn from women residing in the selected enumeration areas (EAs). Clusters without GPS coordinates were excluded from the geospatial analysis.

Sample size and sampling technique

The study employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling method to select participants. In the first stage, 645 EAs were randomly selected with probability proportional to size, including 202 urban and 443 rural EAs. n the second stage, a household listing was conducted in all selected EAs, and 28 households per cluster were systematically chosen with equal probability. This process resulted in the inclusion of 15,683 eligible women of reproductive age in the analysis [17]. The spatial analysis excludes 401 individual from 21 clustersn as they have no GPS coordinates. The exclude clusters were from four regions (Somali, Oromia, Gambela and Diredawa).

Data collection procedures

The study utilized data from the 2016 EDHS, accessed via the DHS program website (http://www.dhsprogram.com) after obtaining permission through an online request on december 16, 2024. Aggregated data were downloaded on december 17, 2024, and files labeled “IR (Individual (Women’s) Recode)” and “GE (Geographic Data)” were used for the spatial analysis.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study is the knowledge about PMTCT of HIV. It was assessed using five key questions: (1) Have you heard about HIV or AIDS? (2) Is HIV transmitted from mother to child during pregnancy? (3) Is HIV transmitted from mother to child during delivery? (4) Is HIV transmitted from mother to child during breastfeeding? (5) Does ART reduce the risk of vertical transmission? Women who responded “yes” to all these questions were categorized as having “Good knowledge,” while those who did not were classified as having “Poor knowledge.” [10].

Independent variables

The study included both individual-level and community-level independent variables. Individual-level variables comprised women’s age, marital status, education level, occupation, religion, partner’s education and occupation, media exposure, wealth index, household head’s gender, parity, age at first pregnancy and first birth, place of delivery, antenatal care (ANC) history, and perceived distance to a health facility. Community-level variables included type of residence, region, community media exposure, community wealth index, community women’s education level, and community partners’ education level.

Operational definitions

Region: Regions were grouped into three categories: small peripheral regions (Afar, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, and Gambela), large central regions (Tigray, Amhara, Oromia, and the Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’ Region), and metropolitan areas (Harari, Dire Dawa, and Addis Ababa) [18].

Media exposure: The EDHS surveys measure media exposure based on three components: reading newspapers or magazines, listening to the radio, and watching television, with response options of not at all, less than once a week, and at least once a week. Women who accessed at least one of these media outlets at least once a week were categorized as “exposed,” while those who did not were classified as “not exposed.” [19].

Wealth index The data on EDHS were classified in to five categories (poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest). We make it three categories by merging poorer with poorest, and richer with richest.

Antenatal care visit (ANC) a woman who had at least one ANC visit for her last pregnancy was considered as having ANC visit.

Community media exposure was created by aggregating women’s individual-level exposure to media. Then we compute the proportion of women exposed to at least one of the three media using Stata and categorized into low and high value based on the median media exposure of the women [18].

Community women’s education and partner’s education These variables were derived by aggregating the individual education levels of women and their partners. The proportion of individuals with at least a secondary education was calculated using Stata and categorized into low or high values based on the median education level within the community [18].

Community poverty was created by aggregating the combined house hold wealth index. Then we compute the proportion of poorer and poorest households together using Stata and categorized into low and high value based on the median house hold wealth index [18].

Data management and analysis

The downloaded data were imported into Stata version 18 for cleaning, coding, and statistical analysis. To account for the sampling design, data weighting was performed prior to analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, and means with standard deviations, were calculated. Given the hierarchical structure of the data, multilevel binary logistic regression analysis was used to address the clustering effect.

Initially, a bivariable analysis was conducted to identify candidate variables for the final model, with variables showing a p-value < 0.25 included in the multivariable analysis. The final analysis employed multivariable mixed-effects binary logistic regression at both individual and community levels to determine factors influencing reproductive-age women’s knowledge about PMTCT. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a p-value < 0.05 were used to evaluate the strength and significance of associations. Multi-collinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a median VIF < 5 serving as the cutoff.

Four models were constructed for the analysis. The null (empty) model, with no covariates, was used to assess cluster-level variability. The second model included only individual-level factors to evaluate their impact on the outcome, while the third model incorporated only community-level factors. The full model combined both individual- and community-level factors to assess their combined effects on knowledge about PMTCT. Model comparison was performed using Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) and Deviance (−2LL), with the model having the lowest AIC and deviance considered the best fit.

Cluster-level variation was assessed using the likelihood ratio test, intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC), median odds ratio (MOR), and proportional change in variance (PCV) [20].

MOR quantifies the variation or heterogeneity in outcomes between clusters. It is defined as the median value of the odds ratio when comparing a cluster with good knowledge to one with poor knowledge, selected randomly from two clusters [20].

Spatial autocorrelation analysis

Arc GIS Pro 3.3.0 was used for spatial data analysis. The Global Moran’s I was used to measure the general patterns of the knowledge level of women about PMTCT in the country. Moran’s I value varies between − 1 and + 1. A values close to − 1 indicates a dispersed pattern of women with poor knowledge about PMTCT, a value close to + 1 indicates a clustered pattern of women with poor knowledge about PMTCT and a value of zero indicates a random pattern [19].

Hot spot analysis

Hot spot analysis (Gettis-Ord Gi*) statistics was used to identify areas with “poor women’s knowledge” and areas with “good women’s knowledge”. Z-score and p-value in Hot spot analysis shows the statistical significance of the clustering. values with high GI* indicates a “hotspot” (high proportion of women with poor knowledge level) and low GI* a “cold spot” (low proportion of women with poor knowledge level) [21].

Spatial interpolation

It is used to predict the unsampled areas using the data from the sampled locations. We used Ordinary Kriging spatial interpolation method to predict the knowledge level of women about PMTCT in unobserved areas by using the actual collected data [20].

SaTscan statistics

A purely spatial analysis using the Bernoulli probability model was conducted with Kulldorff’s SaTScan software version 10.1.3 to identify local clusters. Women with poor knowledge were classified as cases, while those with good knowledge served as controls. The analysis employed the default maximum spatial cluster size of the population with a circular scanning window. Significant SaTScan clusters were determined using the standard Monte Carlo p-value with 999 replications. The scanning window with the highest likelihood was identified as the most likely cluster [21].

Ethics approval and consent to participate

We used data from the EDHS 2016 after getting a permission letter from the DHS program. The EDHS collect the data from each respondent after obtaining a written consent from all of the study participants. For spatial analysis the GPS coordinates were randomly displaced (2 km and 5 km for urban and rural clusters respectively) to protect the confidentiality of respondents [17].

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

A total of 15,683 weighted reproductive age women were included in the study. The mean age of the study participants (mean with SD) was 27.93 ± 0.07 years and more than 21% (3381) of them were aged between 15 and 19 years and the majority 12,207 (77.84%) of them resides in rural areas. Among the total study participants more than half 10,223 (65.19%) were married and around half of the woman and their partner 47.81% and 46.59% respectively were uneducated. Half of the women 7819 (49.86%) and almost all of their partners 9416 (92.11%) had occupation. Among the total participants 6786 (43.27%) were orthodox religion follower and the majority 13,832 (88.20%) were from the large central regions. Half of the study participants 7263 (46.31%) were from rich households and half of (50.31%) them had a big problem to get health facility due to distance. The majority 11,543 (73.60%) had no any media exposure and around 42.11% were from a community with high media exposure. Similarly 39.73%, 39.12%, and 51.91% of them were from a community with high level of women’s education, partner’s education and poverty respectively (refer to Table 1).

Spatial analysis

Spatial autocorrelation

Based on 999 permutations, the analysis revealed statistically significant positive spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.188587, z-score = 80.206991, p = 0.000000). This indicates that the knowledge level of reproductive-age women about PMTCT in Ethiopia exhibited a clustered distribution. The probability of this clustering occurring by random chance is less than 1%, supported by a z-score of 80.206991 (refer to Fig. 1).

Spatial autocorrection analysis of the distribution of the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT in Ethiopia, in 2016. Statistically significant p-value and a positive z-score indicates a clustered distribution, statistically significant p-value and negative z-score indicates a dispersed distribution and non-statistically significant p-value indicates a randon distribution

Hotspot analysis

Spatial clusters of high and low values were identified using the Getis-Ord Gi* statistic. Statistical significance was assessed at the 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence levels, corresponding to absolute Z-score thresholds of > 1.65 (p < 0.1), > 1.96 (p < 0.05), and > 2.58 (p < 0.01), respectively. To account for multiple testing and spatial dependence, which reduces the risk of false positives, the False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied. The resulting significant clusters are visualized as hot spots (high values) and cold spots (low values) at these confidence levels. In the visual representation, red areas indicate hotspots, while blue areas represent cold spots. According to the findings, statistically significant hotspots (regions with a high prevalence of poor knowledge) were observed in Somali, Gambela, Afar, Benishangul-Gumuz, the northern part of SNNP, and parts of Oromia. Conversely, significant cold spots (regions with a low prevalence of poor knowledge) were identified in Addis Ababa and most areas of Tigray and Amhara (refer Fig. 2).

Unsampled areas were predicted with the sampled areas using the ordinary kriging interpolation method. The predicted women’s poor knowledge level about PMTCT increases from green to red-coloured areas. The red colour shows high-proportion predicted areas (poor knowledge) and the green colour indicates low-proportion predicted areas (good knowledge). Cluster analysis

In SatScan analysis a significant one primary and seven secondary (p < 0.05) clusters were identified. The primary cluster was centred at (6.023458 N, 44.807507 E) in 467.18 km radius, with a Relative risk of 1.54 and a Log likelihood ratio of 248.33. It was located in Somali region. Women inside the cluster were 1.54 times more non-knowledgeable than those outside the cluster. Secondary clusters were found in Gambela, SNNP, Benishangul Gumuz, some parts of Oromia, Amhara and Afar (refer to Fig. 4).

SaTScan analysis of the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT in Ethiopia. Coloured areas areas are significant clusters and the reds are the most significant ones.

Multilevel analysis

Among the candidate variables, age at first birth and sex of the household head were excluded from the final multivariable analysis as they were not significant in the bivariable analysis. Additionally, marital status and parity were automatically dropped by Stata software due to collinearity. The final multilevel analysis included individual-level variables such as women’s age, education, occupation, partner’s education, partner’s occupation, religion, wealth index, distance to the health facility, media exposure, age at first marriage, ANC visits, and place of delivery for the most recent birth. Community-level variables included residence, region, community media exposure, community women’s education, community partner’s education, and community poverty.

The random effect results in a multilevel analysis

In the null model, the ICC was 27.4%, indicating that 27.4% of the variability in knowledge levels was attributable to differences between clusters, while the remaining 72.6% was due to individual differences. The MOR value of 2.89 in the null model reflects the variation in knowledge levels between clusters. This means that if two women were randomly selected from different clusters, the woman from the cluster with better knowledge would have 2.89 times higher odds of being knowledgeable about PMTCT compared to the woman from the cluster with poorer knowledge. A 67% PCV demonstrates the proportion of variance in the null model accounted for by the final model. Model fitness was assessed using deviance, LL, and AIC, and Model III were the best-fitting model (refer to Table 4).

The fixed effect analysis results

Four models were constructed for the analysis. The null model with 15,683 observation and 643 clusters, and with no covariates, was used to assess cluster-level variability. The second model with 6,662 observation and 642 clusters included only individual-level factors (Age, Women’s and Partner’s education, Women’s and Partners occupation, Religion, Wealth index, Distance from the health facility, Media exposure, Age at first marriage, ANC visit, and Place of delivery for recent birth), while the third model with 15,683 observation and 643 clusters incorporated only community-level factors (Residence, Region, Community media exposure, Community women’s education, Community partner’s education, and Community poverty) and the full model with 6,662 observation and 642 clusters combined both individual- and community-level factors. The study identified several statistically significant factors influencing the knowledge level of reproductive-age women about PMTCT. These included women’s education, partner’s occupation, religion, wealth index, media exposure, ANC attendance for the most recent pregnancy, place of delivery for the most recent birth, residence, region, and community media exposure (refer to Table 4).

Women with higher education levels had significantly greater odds of having good knowledge about PMTCT. Specifically, women with primary, secondary, and tertiary education had 1.19 times (AOR = 1.19; 95% CI = 1.02–1.40), 1.41 times (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI = 1.08–1.85), and 2.22 times (AOR = 2.22; 95% CI = 1.52–3.25) higher odds, respectively, compared to women with no formal education. Similarly, women whose partners were employed had 1.36 times (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI = 1.08–1.71) higher odds of having good knowledge than those whose partners were not employed (refer to Table 4).

Religious affiliation also influenced knowledge levels. Compared to Orthodox women, those identifying as Protestant, Muslim, or other religions had lower odds of being knowledgeable, with AORs of 0.76 (95% CI = 0.61–0.93), 0.57 (95% CI = 0.47–0.68), and 0.54 (95% CI = 0.33–0.86), respectively. Women from wealthier households were more likely to have good knowledge, with an AOR of 1.35 (95% CI = 1.11–1.63) compared to women from poorer households (refer to Table 4).

Media exposure played a crucial role, as women with media exposure had 1.32 times (AOR = 1.32; 95% CI = 1.12–1.57) greater odds of being knowledgeable compared to those without exposure. Women who attended ANC visits during their most recent pregnancy had 1.46 times (AOR = 1.46; 95% CI = 1.25–1.70) higher odds of being knowledgeable, and those who delivered in health facilities had 1.18 times (AOR = 1.18; 95% CI = 1.01–1.37) greater odds compared to those who gave birth at home (refer to Table 4).

Urban residence was associated with 1.36 times (AOR = 1.36; 95% CI = 1.01–1.83) higher odds of knowledge compared to rural areas. Women from larger central regions were 1.38 times (AOR = 1.38; 95% CI = 1.12–1.70) more likely to be knowledgeable than those from smaller peripheral regions. Lastly, women from communities with high media exposure had 1.41 times (AOR = 1.41; 95% CI = 1.13–1.75) higher odds of having good knowledge compared to those in communities with low media exposure (refer to Table 4).

Discussion

The main objective of this study is to assess the spatial variation and determinants of the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT in Ethiopia. The spatial autocorrelation analysis found a spatially clustered knowledge levels among the reproductive age women. Statistically significant hotspots (areas with poor knowledge level about PMTCT) were found in Somali, Gambela, Afar, Benishangul Gumuz, north part of SNNP and some part of Oromia and statistically significant cold spots were found in Addis Ababa, and most parts of Tigray and Amhara. Similarly, with SatScan analysis statistically significant clusters were observed in Somali, Gambela, SNNP, Benishangul Gumuz, and some parts of (Oromia, Amhara and Afar). The disparities in knowledge about PMTCT across different regions of Ethiopia can be largely attributed to socio-economic, cultural, and geographical factors that vary significantly between regions. Many of the areas with significant hotspots (knowledge gaps) are located in the peripheral regions of Ethiopia. These areas often characterized by remote and rural settings, face considerable challenges such as limited access to healthcare, education, and media, all of which are crucial for spreading information about maternal and child health, including HIV prevention. Additionally, many of the people living in these regions are nomadic or semi-nomadic, which presents its own set of challenges. Nomadic populations tend to move frequently in search of pasture for livestock, which limits their ability to access fixed healthcare services, schools, and media outlets. The nomadic lifestyle further complicates efforts to provide continuous healthcare education and services, as these populations may miss critical ANC visits or health campaigns [22, 23].

According to this study women’s education, Partners occupation, Religion, Wealth index, Media exposure, ANC visit for recent pregnancy, Place of delivery for recent birth, Residence, Region, and Community media exposure were found significantly associated with the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT. Women with primary, secondary, and tertiary education had good knowledge about PMTCT than those who had no education. It is consistent with previous studies done in Sub-Saharan Africa, East-Africa, Ethiopia, Ghana, South Sudan, and Pakistan [9–12, 24–27]. This may be because educated women may get information regarding PMTCT in school and they can easily understand health related knowledge transmitted through different media outlets including magazine, newspaper and books [28–30]. Women with protestant, Muslim and other religion follower had poor knowledge about PMTCT than those who were orthodox religion followers. This is similar with studies done in Ethiopia and Nigeria [9, 31]. This may be due to the active involvement of orthodox church in teaching its follower about HIV/AIDS [32]. Women from rich households had good knowledge about PMTCT than those from poor households. This is consistent with studies done in Ethiopia, East-Africa, Sub-Saharan Africa and eight HIV prevalent countries [9–12, 33]. This is because of women from rich household had easy access to different medias that they can get information regarding PMTCT easily. Additionally it is more easy for them to reach health facility and get information from health care providers even if it is far away from them [34, 35]. Similarly, women whose partner was working were more knowledgeable about PMTCT than those whose partners were not working. This is in line with a study done in Ethiopia [36] and it may be because women who had working partner may get enough income for their households that enables them to easily access health care services and different media platforms than enables them to get knowledge about PMTCT [35]. Further partners with working status may be educated and may provide information regarding PMTCT for their wives that makes them knowledgeable [28].

Women who had media exposure and those from a community with high media exposure were more knowledgeable about PMTCT than counterparts, which is in line with studies done in Ethiopia, Sub-Saharan Africa and eight HIV prevalent countries [9, 11, 12, 33]. It may be because women who had media exposure can get health related information easily than those who had not an exposure [37, 38]. Women who had ANC visit for her recent pregnancy and those who delivered their recent chid at health facility were more knowledgeable about PMTCT than those who had not ANC visit for their last pregnancy and those who delivered their last child at home. It is consistent with studies done in Ethiopia, South Sudan and East-Africa [10, 24, 26, 36]. It may be because women who visit health facility either during ANC or delivery may had an opportunity to get information regarding PMTCT from health care providers [39]. Women who reside in urban area had more knowledge about PMTCT than those who resides in rural area. It is supported by studies done in Ethiopia, East-Africa, and Pakistan [9–11, 24, 27, 33]. Easy accessibility of media and health facility in urban areas may results women to have a better knowledge about PMTCT and women in urban areas may be more educated [40–42]. Similarly, women who reside in large central regions were more knowledgeable about PMTCT than those who reside in small peripheral regions which is similar with a study done in Ethiopia [9]. It may also be due to the differences in availability and accessibility of health care and media among large central regions and small peripheral regions [22, 23].

Although our study assessed the spatial variation of women’s knowledge on PMTCT using a national representative data it has its own limitation. First, the cross-sectional design of the EDHS, while valuable for establishing associations and spatial patterns, inherently limits our ability to infer causal relationships between the identified factors and PMTCT knowledge. Second, the operationalization of PMTCT knowledge as a binary construct (good/poor), though necessary for analysis, potentially oversimplifies a multifaceted concept. This dichotomization may obscure variations across distinct knowledge domains and introduces a risk of non-differential misclassification, which could attenuate the strength of the observed associations. Third, our spatial analysis is subject to the demographic and health surveys’ protocol of randomly displacing GPS coordinates for confidentiality up to 2 km in urban clusters and 5 km in rural clusters. This intentional spatial imprecision likely results in an attenuation of cluster boundaries in our hot spot maps, meaning the identified geographical clusters of high and low knowledge are probably more diffuse and less sharply defined than in reality. Consequently, our spatial results should be viewed as a conservative estimate of the true clustering. Fourth, the exclusion of any clusters with missing geospatial data raises the possibility of selection bias, particularly if these omitted clusters were systematically different from those included in the analysis. Further a recall bias may be introduced during interview and that may affect our result.

Conclusion

In Ethiopia the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT varies across different regions. Statistically significant hotspots (areas with poor knowledge about PMTCT) were found in Somali, Gambela, Afar, Benishangul Gumuz, north part of SNNP and some part of Oromia. Women’s education, Partners occupation, Religion, Wealth index, Media exposure, ANC visit for recent pregnancy, Place of delivery for recent birth, Residence, Region, and Community media exposure were statistical significant factors that affect the knowledge level of reproductive age women about PMTCT. Given these findings, targeted interventions in the identified hotspot areas are crucial to improving the knowledge and awareness of reproductive-age women about PMTCT. Such efforts will help prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV and contribute to achieving the WHO’s 2030 goals.
