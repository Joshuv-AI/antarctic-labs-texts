Voluntary running exercise is associated with metabolic shifts linked to adult hippocampal neurogenesis
Authors: Alejandra Vazquez-Medina, David Ruíz-Bolivar, Patricia Morales-Iglesias, Karina Marín-Hernandez, Danniela Rivera-Ortiz, Francisco Vizcarrondo-Fornaris, Briana Bello-Rivera, Brendimar Sierra-Medina, Sebastian Rodríguez-Soto, Nataliya Chorna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdult hippocampal neurogenesis is a metabolically demanding process requiring tight coordination between energy production and biosynthetic flux. Although voluntary running is a potent stimulus for this plasticity, the metabolic landscape sustaining the neurogenic niche remains incompletely defined. Using untargeted gas chromatography/mass spectrometry-based metabolomics to characterize the hippocampal metabolome of mice following eight weeks of voluntary running, we identified metabolic changes consistent with coordinated metabolic reprogramming that suggest an adaptive metabolic stress response. A significant catabolic shift, marked by depletion of glutamic and aspartic acids, is associated with increased bioenergetic utilization and possible integration of neurotransmitter-derived substrates into central carbon metabolism. The exercise-induced elevation of CoA-related metabolites and tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates is indicative of increased mitochondrial bioenergetic demand. Simultaneously, elevated nitrogenous metabolites, such as asparagine and glycine, coincide with increased availability of biosynthetic precursors for nucleotide synthesis, redox balance, and structural remodeling linked to neurogenesis. Enrichment of one-carbon metabolism is compatible with integration of metabolic pathways involved in biosynthetic and regulatory processes related to neurogenic remodeling. Together, these findings align with the interpretation that voluntary running may act as a metabolic hormetic stimulus, linked to reconfiguration of hippocampal metabolic networks to support a permissive environment for neurogenic plasticity and cognitive resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54888-0.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Voluntary running is known to enhance cognitive function and support brain health, though the mechanisms through which it influences neural processes are still being actively investigated1,2. Among the brain regions influenced by exercise, the hippocampus emerges as a pivotal center for learning and memory. Located within the medial temporal lobe, this structure plays a crucial role in various cognitive and neurological functions, including memory formation, spatial navigation, emotional regulation, pattern separation, and both spatial and episodic memory3.

Notably, the hippocampus retains the ability to generate new neurons throughout adulthood4,5. These newly generated neurons play a crucial role in replacing neural populations damaged by disease or injury, and they integrate into pre-existing neural circuits6. This process, known as adult neurogenesis, is critically involved in various hippocampus-dependent forms of learning and memory7,8.

In the hippocampus, the generation of new neurons initially occurs from neural stem cells (NSCs) residing in the subgranular zone (SGZ) of the dentate gyrus9. The microenvironments of the SGZ support the proliferation of NSCs and their subsequent neuronal differentiation. These stages include the exit of NSCs from quiescence, the initiation of cell proliferation, migration, terminal differentiation, and eventual incorporation into functional neuronal circuits9,10. Hormones, growth factors, neurotransmitters, and environmental factors such as physical activity all contribute to the regulation of neurogenesis11–14. However, the intrinsic metabolic state plays an equally critical role in modulating these fate decisions15.

Extensive research has been undertaken to elucidate the influence of energy metabolism on NSCs fate. It is recognized that neurogenic activity depends on a balance between quiescent and proliferative states. Consistent with previous studies, quiescent NSCs exhibit low energetic demands, relying predominantly on ATP production from glycolysis and partially on mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO)16,17. However, entry into a proliferative state requires a shift toward anabolic metabolism. Previous studies demonstrated that neural stem cells upregulate lipid synthesis through FASN-mediated de novo lipogenesis, and genetic or pharmacological inhibition of this pathway significantly reduces proliferation, highlighting the essential role of lipid biosynthesis in NSCs proliferation18,19.

Consistent with previous reports linking lipogenesis to NSC proliferation, our earlier research demonstrated a critical role of de novo lipogenesis in NSCs proliferation18. Specifically, using a mouse model of voluntary running exercise, we provided direct evidence of the impact of a pro-neurogenic stimulus on the lipid metabolism of NSCs. Notably, this effect was abolished by the pharmacological inhibition of FASN, which attenuates exercise-induced benefits such as heightened NSCs proliferation and cognitive enhancement18. While these findings establish a mechanistic role for lipogenesis in NSCs activation, they do not address how broader metabolic pathways are coordinated in response to exercise. Previous studies further demonstrated that during the later stages of neuronal differentiation, maturation, and integration into neuronal circuitry, oxidative phosphorylation becomes increasingly important as a primary ATP supplier, given the need for a more oxidized environment at these developmental stages10.

Despite increasing recognition of metabolic regulation in neurogenesis, the coordination of carbon substrates and nitrogen balance required to support protein and nucleotide synthesis during neuronal differentiation remains largely unexplored. While the lipid requirements are well-established, the upstream metabolic pathways, specifically regarding amino acid catabolism and the TCA cycle, that fuel this anabolic demand during exercise remain unknown. Thus, the broader metabolic landscape supporting exercise-induced neurogenesis, beyond lipid metabolism, remains incompletely defined.

Therefore, this study investigated the impact of exercise on the broader hippocampal metabolome to elucidate the nitrogen and carbon metabolic pathways underlying neurogenesis. Rather than testing a specific mechanistic hypothesis, this work expands the investigation in an exploratory manner to characterize global metabolic adaptations associated with voluntary running. Using untargeted GC/MS-based metabolomics, we identified distinct metabolic signatures, including alterations in nitrogen homeostasis, amino acid and one-carbon metabolism, and CoA-related pathways, consistent with increased availability of biosynthetic substrates associated with exercise-induced structural plasticity.

Materials and methods

Mouse model of voluntary running exercise

We used an established model of voluntary running exercise in 20-week-old male mice (C57BL/6 J, Jackson Lab) housed individually in standard cages in temperature-controlled (21 °C) quarters with a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle. Animals were given water and food (Purina Chow) ad libitum as previously described18. Briefly, mice were randomized into two groups: sedentary control (SED, n = 7) and running experimental (RUN, n = 7). They were housed with free access to a wireless running wheel (Med Associates) for 8 weeks. For the SED group, the wheels were locked for the duration of the experiment; therefore, this group could not perform running exercises. The RUN group’s running activities were recorded for each animal to ensure each mouse remained physically active. The recording was conducted using an automatic counter and Med Associates software. The mice engaged in daily running sessions from 6:00 pm to 5:00 am. After completion of the experiment, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital solution (Fatal Plus or Euthanasia III; 0.22 mL/kg) using a 25 gauge needle. Deep anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of toe pinch and corneal reflexes prior to decapitation. All procedures were approved by the University of Puerto Rico Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) under protocol number A660121.

Extraction of hippocampal metabolites

Mouse brains were dissected and washed with ice-cold saline for 30 s to remove excess blood. Hippocampi were rapidly isolated, quenched in liquid nitrogen, and weighed. The processing of frozen hippocampi followed the procedures outlined in a prior publication20. Briefly, approximately 20–40 mg of frozen tissue was resuspended in 4 volumes of Mammalian Protein Extraction Reagent (MPER, Thermo Fisher Sci) and homogenized on ice. 130 µL of tissue homogenate was transferred, followed by 20 s of vortexing and a fast spin on a table centrifuge at RT. The flow-through was diluted 1:1 with deionized water, followed by sonication for 10 s using a Sonic Dismembrator on ice, vortexing for 10 s, and centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C21. For GC/MS analysis, 800 µL of chloroform: methanol: water (v/v 2:5:2) was added to 100 µL of eluate, centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen gas stream, and stored at −80 °C.

Derivatization of samples for GC/MS analysis

Hippocampal samples from the SED and RUN groups underwent two steps of derivatization. Briefly, dried samples were first derivatized by methoxyamination by adding 30 µL of 20 mg/mL methoxyamine hydrochloride solution in pyridine (Sigma-Aldrich) and incubating at 37 °C for 2 h. Trimethylsilylation was subsequently performed by adding 30 µL of N-methyl-N-trimethylsilyl-trifluoroacetamide (MSTFA + 1% TMCS, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubating for 1 h at 65 °C. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min at RT. 20 µL of each sample supernatant was added to analytical glass vials with inserts and processed by GCMS/MS-TQ8050 (Shimadzu Inc.) using analytical conditions as previously described20. Briefly, the chromatography conditions were as follows: RXI-5MS (0.25 mm inner diameter, 0.25 μm D.F., 30 m) (Restek), split injection (ratio = 15), injection volume of 1 µL. The inlet temperature was 280 °C, the ion source temperature was 200 °C, and the interface temperature was 150 °C. The oven temperature was set to 100 °C for 1 min, then programmed to 290 °C at 8 °C/min, held at 290 °C for 16 min. Helium was the carrier gas at a constant linear velocity of 39 cm/s. MS conditions: electrospray ionization (ESI) source, full scan mode, electron energy of 70 eV, quadrupole scan range of m/z 35–700. Quality assessment and quality control were performed using several blank samples, including system suitability blanks and derivatization processing blanks. To mitigate systematic bias, the order of sample analysis was randomized, and blanks and quality control samples were evenly distributed among the injections to monitor instrument stability.

GC/MS raw data analysis

The GC/MS raw data obtained from hippocampal samples were processed using LabSolutions PostRun analysis software (Shimadzu Inc.), equipped with the NIST14/2014/EPA/NIH database, to identify metabolites via peak integration and multiple searches of the mass spectral library. Metabolite identification was performed using NIST14 spectral library matching with a minimum similarity score of 90% combined with retention index matching. Confirmation with authentic standards was not performed22.

Differential analysis of identified metabolites

To evaluate significant differences in metabolites detected by GC/MS across both the SED and RUN groups, the peak intensities for each sample were extracted and processed using MetaboAnalyst 6.023. Briefly, the data were subsequently normalized by weight to improve comparability across variables. Fold change (FC) analysis was performed to evaluate the magnitude of change between groups, using an unpaired method with a cut-off threshold of 1.5. To identify group-specific metabolic profiles and distinguish between experimental conditions, principal component analysis (PCA) with PERMANOVA was performed. Pathway enrichment analysis was conducted to determine whether specific metabolic pathways were statistically overrepresented among significantly altered metabolites, with enriched pathways identified based on FDR-adjusted p-values (α < 0.05).

Univariate analysis was performed using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test to compare individual metabolite intensities between SED and RUN groups using GraphPad Prism version 10.6 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). To correct for multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Krieger–Yekutieli two-stage step-up method to control the false discovery rate (FDR), with statistical significance defined as an FDR-adjusted p-value (q-value) < 0.05. Bar plots were generated to visualize group differences.

Results and discussion

Inter-individual variability in running activity does not influence the hippocampal metabolomic profile

Running activity was recorded during week 1, week 2, and subsequently in 2-week intervals through week 8. Individual running distances ranged from 3.1 to 11.6 km during week 1 (0.4 to 1.7 km/day) and from 20.0 to 96.0 km during the final 2-week interval (weeks 7 to 8; 1.4 to 6.9 km/day), reflecting the well-documented inter-individual variability inherent to this model (Supplementary Figure S1). Spearman correlation analyses between individual running distance and each hippocampal metabolite in the RUN group revealed no significant correlations after Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction (all adjusted p values > 0.05), suggesting that inter-individual differences in running distance were not a major driver of the hippocampal metabolomic profile.

Hippocampal metabolic shifts induced by running

Our study focused on investigating the impact of running exercise on hippocampal metabolism using untargeted metabolomics. The metabolic extracts obtained from the hippocampi of the SED and RUN groups were analyzed via GC/MS. We identified a total of 37 metabolites categorized into 14 main classes, including amino acids (43%), saturated fatty acids (11%), unsaturated fatty acids (8%), TCA acids (8%), amino fatty acids (FA, 3%), cholesterols (3%), hydroxy FA (3%), hypoxanthines (3%), N-acyl-alpha-amino acids (5%), nicotinic acid alkaloids (3%), other pyrimidines (3%), primary amides (3%), short-chain acids (3%), xanthines (3%) (Fig. 1, A).

PCA analysis revealed clear clustering of samples by experimental group (Fig. 1, B). The first principal component (PC1) explained 55.2% of the total variance, while the second principal component (PC2) accounted for 29%, together capturing 84.2% of the overall metabolic variation. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) confirmed that the metabolic profiles differed significantly between groups (F = 19.288, R² = 0.616, p = 0.002; 999 permutations). The Mann–Whitney U test identified 22 metabolites that were significantly altered (Fig. 1, C). Among these, pyroglutamic acid, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and N-acetylglutamic acid were notably downregulated, while the remaining 18 metabolites were significantly upregulated in response to running exercise (Supplementary Table 1).

Metabolomics enrichment analysis identified 12 metabolic pathways as significantly enriched in the hippocampus of RUN mice compared with SED controls (Fig. 1, D). These include one-carbon metabolism, glutathione biosynthesis, glutamine-glutamate cycle, nitrogen metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, CoA biosynthesis, tyrosine metabolism, tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), purine metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and fatty acid biosynthesis.

Nitrogen metabolism in neurogenesis: balancing TCA and neurotransmitters

Nitrogen metabolism was identified as the most significantly enriched pathway (ER = 10.88, FDR = 5.72 × 10− 13) (Fig. 1, D). This enrichment was driven by significant alterations in key nitrogen-related metabolites, including the depletion of glutamic and aspartic acids and the accumulation of glutamine and asparagine. This distinct profile highlights the complex nitrogen reallocation required to support hippocampal function during physical activity. The observed divergence, in which some substrates are depleted while others accumulate, likely reflects metabolic heterogeneity within hippocampal tissue. In this coupled system, we interpret the data as potentially consistent with complementary metabolic processes within hippocampal cell populations, including metabolic patterns potentially consistent with anabolic processes previously described in astrocytes, involving production of nitrogenous precursors such as glutamine and asparagine via pyruvate carboxylase-dependent anaplerosis24, associated with a coexisting neuronal catabolic shift consistent with increased TCA cycle metabolism to meet the bioenergetic costs of differentiation25 (Fig. 2). Thus, the observed metabolic profile may reflect coordinated metabolic interactions among multiple hippocampal cell types to balance biosynthetic supply with the oxidative demands of structural remodeling24.

Catabolic shift

Our data indicate that voluntary running prompts an energy metabolic realignment in the hippocampus. We observed a significant depletion of glutamic acid (FC = 0.3, −log10(q) = 3.2) and aspartic acid (FC = 0.2, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C), suggesting these amino acids are likely associated with increased entry into the TCA cycle (glutamate to 2-oxoglutarate, aspartate to oxaloacetate) to replenish intermediates and sustain ATP production. Consistent with the extensive depletion of the glutamic acid pool, we observed a significant downregulation of its downstream metabolite, N-acetylglutamic acid (FC = 0.4, −log₁₀(q) = 2.6) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C). N-acetylglutamic acid is synthesized from glutamate and acetyl-CoA by the mitochondrial enzyme N-acetylglutamate synthase; because glutamate is the obligate substrate for this reaction, the reduction in N-acetylglutamic acid levels likely reflects a direct consequence of substrate limitation26. This observation is consistent with altered metabolic prioritization in the exercised hippocampus, in which glutamic acid may be preferentially associated with TCA cycle metabolism during increased bioenergetic demands. Consequently, metabolite patterns suggest altered activity of secondary biosynthetic pathways, including decreased N-acetylglutamic acid levels. While N-acetylglutamic acid is an essential activator of carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1, the rate-limiting step of the urea cycle, its depletion in this context may be consistent with altered nitrogen handling associated with nitrogen conservation rather than excretion. In the hippocampus, ammonia detoxification has been reported to occur primarily through glutamine synthetase rather than ureagenesis27. Thus, decreased N-acetylglutamic acid levels align with the observed glutamine accumulation, consistent with altered nitrogen handling associated with nucleotide biosynthesis rather than disposal. At the systems level, this broad reduction in excitatory amino acids, combined with unchanged GABA levels, complements our previous finding of upregulated serotonin20. In this framework, voluntary running acts as a metabolic hormetic stimulus that promotes plasticity and cognitive resilience28.

Anabolic transformation

In contrast to the depletion of TCA substrates, asparagine accumulated dramatically (FC = 7.1, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C). While asparagine serves as a biochemical precursor to aspartate, its accumulation here suggests a specific role in supporting rapid cellular proliferation. A recent study demonstrated that increased asparagine is critical for B-cell proliferation in germinal centers, driving nucleotide production necessary for DNA replication29. While this metabolic route has yet to be fully characterized in hippocampal neural stem cells, the mechanistic parallels suggest that asparagine similarly supports the biosynthetic demands associated with proliferation.

Parallel to this nitrogen buffering, we observed a notable accumulation of glycolic acid (FC = 3.1, −log10(q) = 3.2), a metabolite intimately linked to the regulation of the glycine pool (FC = 2.0, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C). Biochemically, this accumulation is consistent with potential involvement of the glyoxylate pathway, where glycolate serves as a direct alpha-keto acid precursor for the synthesis of glycine via transamination30. Given the essential role of glycine in purine biosynthesis and glutathione production, the elevation of glycolic acid suggests an adaptive metabolic buffering mechanism. This accumulation may reflect increased availability of carbon substrates associated with one-carbon metabolism and redox defense mechanisms.

The accumulation of asparagine and glutamine (FC = 1.6, −log₁₀(q) = 2.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) suggests they may contribute as donors of carbon and nitrogen associated with NSCs survival and growth31, or may facilitate the uptake of other essential nutrients32.

Taken together, our data suggest a coordinated dual-mode metabolic response consistent with the principles of metabolic hormesis33 (Fig. 2). Within this framework, the persistent depletion of TCA-linked substrates (glutamate and aspartate) reflects an adaptive metabolic stress signal representing a bioenergetic challenge that transiently perturbs homeostasis. This metabolic pressure coexists with metabolic patterns suggestive of a compensatory adaptive response, evidenced by the concurrent accumulation of asparagine and glycine. In contrast to chronic metabolic stress that impairs cellular function, this voluntary metabolic challenge appears consistent with a metabolically altered state associated with proliferation. Thus, the metabolic realignment observed here likely represents an adaptive mechanism that promotes plasticity and cognitive resilience, consistent with recent models of neuroprotection28.

Role of glutathione biosynthesis and glutamate-glutamine cycle in neurogenesis

Beyond the primary nitrogen pathways, we identified significant enrichment in Glutathione Metabolism (ER = 10.75, FDR = 3.83 × 10− 9) and the Glutamate-Glutamine Cycle (ER = 9.54, FDR = 3.83 × 10− 9) (Fig. 1, D). This suggests that the observed depletion of glutamic acid is not solely driven by bioenergetic demands but may reflect involvement in downstream biosynthetic processes, specifically, the maintenance of the antioxidant glutathione pool and neurotransmitter recycling. Glutamic acid serves as the central hub bridging nitrogen metabolism with the γ-glutamyl cycle. In this pathway, the intermediate pyroglutamic acid is enzymatically reconverted to glutamic acid by 5-oxoprolinase to replenish the pool for glutathione synthesis34. Concurrently, glutamine acts as the immediate precursor to sustain the glutamate pool via the glutamate-glutamine cycle between neurons and astrocytes35 (Fig. 2).

Our data show a significant downregulation of both pyroglutamic acid (FC = 0.2, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) and glutamic acid, concomitant with an upregulation of glycine. Since glycine and glutamate are the essential substrates for de novo glutathione synthesis36, this pattern suggests that glycine availability is maintained to support metabolic activity associated with glutathione metabolism with glutamate potentially utilized in pathways associated with sustaining glutathione levels. Given that Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) are crucial regulators of signaling pathways for neuronal differentiation37, this metabolic shift implies that voluntary running may be associated with metabolic adaptations linked to redox homeostasis. Thus, our results indicate a metabolic realignment consistent with enhanced glutathione biosynthesis and a cellular environment associated with neurogenesis.

Bioenergetic support of neurogenesis: CoA biosynthesis, TCA cycle enrichment, and OXPHOS

Significant enrichments were observed in closely interconnected metabolic pathways, including CoA biosynthesis (ER = 9.12, FDR = 4.43 × 10⁻⁶), the TCA cycle (ER = 9.04, FDR = 3.83 × 10⁻⁹), and OXPHOS (ER = 6.71, FDR = 3.81 × 10⁻³) (Fig. 1, D). Notably, steady-state levels of the essential branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) isoleucine (FC = 2.7, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) and valine (FC = 4, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) increased significantly, while leucine remained stable. These amino acids function as critical upstream substrates for CoA formation, a process vital for sustaining energy metabolism, protein synthesis, and maintaining a nitrogen balance38. Taken together, their availability may support the metabolic plasticity required for brain health and cognitive functions, specifically the demands of neurogenesis39 (Fig. 3).

Our observation of elevated BCAAs is consistent with previous 13 C-isotopomer studies demonstrating the metabolic contributions of these amino acids to the mitochondrial carbon pool40. Isoleucine and leucine contribute carbon atoms to acetyl-CoA, driving the initial entry into the TCA cycle and progression toward 2-oxoglutarate. Subsequently, isoleucine and valine further support the cycle by feeding into the succinyl-CoA node. We interpret the observed elevation of these amino acids as consistent with increased availability of carbon substrates associated with the mitochondrial carbon pool. By maintaining high steady-state levels of these anaplerotic substrates, hippocampal tissue may be metabolically positioned to accommodate the bioenergetic challenge of neurogenesis, ensuring that acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA availability is not rate-limiting during periods of high metabolic turnover.

Additionally, we identified a likely convergent anaplerotic route involving methionine (FC = 1.7, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) (via the trans-sulfuration pathway) and threonine (FC = 3.2, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C). In human physiology, unlike murine models, which possess functional threonine dehydrogenase (TDH), threonine is predominantly catabolized via threonine dehydratase to α-ketobutyrate and ammonium41. The subsequent oxidative decarboxylation of α-ketobutyrate yields propionyl-CoA, which enters the TCA cycle at the succinyl-CoA junction. This establishes threonine as a putative anaplerotic substrate capable of replenishing TCA intermediates. Our observation of a significant elevation in succinic acid (FC = 1.9, −log₁₀(q) = 2.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) is consistent with potential contributions from this threonine-dehydratase pathway, suggesting it may contribute to maintenance of TCA cycle metabolite levels during exercise-induced neurogenesis. Tryptophan may also support this anaplerotic pathway, as its intermediate, anthranilic acid, participates in the formation of acetoacetyl-CoA, linking amino acid catabolism to both energy production and lipid biosynthesis42 (Fig. 4).

Crucially, this enrichment of TCA cycle intermediates may reflect metabolic adaptations associated with increased energetic demand and Oxidative Phosphorylation (OXPHOS). We interpret the significant elevation of the succinate pool not as a driver of respiration per se, but as a marker of increased anaplerotic input and metabolic patterns associated with mitochondrial activity. This expanded pool may indicate increased substrate availability for mitochondrial metabolism without substrate exhaustion. While quiescent neural stem cells primarily rely on glycolysis, the transition to differentiation and neurite outgrowth necessitates a metabolic shift toward sustained ATP generation and mitochondrial membrane potential stability17[,43. The concurrent enrichment of TCA cycle metabolites and OXPHOS pathways suggests that the running-induced metabolic program is associated with increased availability of carbon substrates and metabolic support linked to the survival and maturation of newborn neurons10,]44.

Metabolic and epigenetic interactions through one-carbon metabolism

One-carbon metabolism emerged as one of the most significantly enriched pathways (ER = 9.73, FDR = 1.61 × 10− 5) (Fig. 1, D), highlighting its central role associated with coordination of metabolic and epigenetic processes underlying hippocampal neurogenesis (Fig. 3). This interconnected network, comprising the folate and methionine cycles, generates one-carbon units required for methylation reactions, nucleotide synthesis, and redox balance45[,46. As mentioned above, methionine was significantly upregulated within this pathway. Methionine serves as a precursor for S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the principal methyl donor for DNA, RNA, protein, and lipid methylation reactions that regulate cellular function and epigenetic programming47–49[,50. The concurrent elevation of tryptophan (FC = 2.2, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) further supports one-carbon pathway enrichment. Specifically, tryptophan metabolism integrates with folate-dependent reactions via the kynurenine pathway to supply formate units to the one-carbon pool42[,51.

The observed upregulation of serine (FC = 5.0, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) and glycine (FC = 2.0, −log₁₀(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) links one-carbon metabolism to both structural and functional demands of neurogenesis. Serine plays a pivotal role in lipid biosynthesis, including sphingolipids and phospholipids which are essential for membrane formation, dendritogenesis, and myelination, thereby supporting neuronal structural development and differentiation47[,48. These lipid pathways also influence synaptic function and neuronal signaling, reinforcing the contribution of serine to hippocampal plasticity52. Glycine complements these structural roles through functional modulation of synaptic activity, acting as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors and thereby facilitating synaptic transmission and learning processes associated with neurogenesis53. Together, the coordinated elevation of serine and glycine suggests that exercise-induced remodeling of one-carbon metabolism may support both neuronal architecture and synaptic function (Fig. 4).

Beyond biosynthetic support, one-carbon metabolism also provides a direct link between metabolic remodeling and epigenetic regulation during neurogenesis. Running exercise has increasingly been recognized as influencing hippocampal neurogenesis through metabolic and epigenetic interactions54,]55. In the present study, enrichment of one-carbon metabolism, CoA biosynthesis, and TCA cycle activity is consistent with a metabolically permissive environment associated with chromatin remodeling for chromatin remodeling during the transition from NSCs to differentiated neurons. Elevated methionine may support increased substrate availability for SAM production, potentially enhancing DNA and histone methylation56. SAM-dependent methylation plays a central role in regulating chromatin states, including histone marks such as H3K4me3 that regulate pluripotency and differentiation programs57. Notably, differentiation requires locus-specific redistribution rather than global accumulation of these marks; activation of Eph-B signaling has been shown to downregulate DHFR and selectively reduce H3K4me3 at progenitor genes such as Sox2 and Nestin, facilitating exit from the stem-like state and promoting neuronal differentiation58. These findings support the concept that exercise may enhance metabolic conditions associated with dynamic epigenetic remodeling rather than uniformly increasing methylation.

Parallel to increased methyl-donor availability, elevated isoleucine, threonine, and tryptophan suggest a complementary mechanism supporting histone acetylation. While nuclear acetyl-CoA is primarily derived from citrate or acetate59, these amino acids provide an auxiliary substrate reservoir via catabolic convergence on acetyl-CoA. Although their direct contribution to hippocampal acetylation remains to be elucidated, the simultaneous enrichment of methyl- and acetyl-donor precursors indicates that exercise may induce metabolic conditions associated with enhanced epigenetic plasticity and neuronal differentiation (Figs. 3 and 4).

Collectively, these findings support a model in which voluntary running is associated with remodeling of one-carbon metabolism to integrate biosynthetic, structural, and epigenetic functions. By coordinating methyl-donor availability, nucleotide synthesis, lipid production, and acetyl-CoA-dependent chromatin modification, exercise may establish a metabolic framework that supports neural stem cell proliferation, differentiation, and long-term hippocampal plasticity.

Tryptophan metabolism and its role in hippocampal neurogenesis

Consistent with the elevated levels of tryptophan, an essential amino acid significantly increased in the RUN group, we identified a robust enrichment of Tryptophan Metabolism (ER = 9.18, FDR = 2.11 × 10− 4) (Fig. 1, D). Since tryptophan is obtained exclusively from the diet, it must cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to influence central nervous system function60. Using the same mouse model of voluntary running exercise, we previously demonstrated increased systemic TRP availability for BBB transport, leading to elevated TRP and serotonin levels in the hippocampus and brainstem, along with increased 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) levels in the brainstem20. Because 5-HIAA is the principal metabolite of serotonin and a recognized marker of serotonergic activity61[,62, these findings are consistent with enhanced serotonergic metabolism associated with voluntary running exercise.

Elevated hippocampal tryptophan availability may be associated with two complementary neurogenic pathways. First, it may support serotonin synthesis via tryptophan hydroxylase 2 in the raphe nuclei63. Serotonergic projections arising from the raphe nuclei innervate the hippocampal molecular layer and hilus, where they synapse with principal neurons and interneurons. Importantly, hilar interneurons interact with immature, newly generated granule cells and act as modulators of neurogenesis46,]64.

Second, tryptophan metabolism generates indole derivatives that contribute to neuronal maturation. Indole has been reported to promote cell cycle exit in neural stem cells (NSCs) by activating the Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling pathway. This signaling has been associated with NSC differentiation and may support the functional integration of newborn neurons into existing hippocampal circuits65. Together, the running-induced increase in tryptophan availability is consistent with serotonergic and indole-associated signaling pathways linked to hippocampal neurogenesis.

Link between tyrosine metabolism and neurogenesis

We observed a marked enrichment in the Tyrosine Metabolism (ER = 9.34, FDR = 2.9 × 10− 5), (Fig. 1, D) accompanied by a significant elevation of both phenylalanine (FC = 5.1, −log10(q) = 3.2) and tyrosine (FC = 2.2, −log10(q) = 2.0) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) levels in the RUN group. This pattern is consistent with altered catecholaminergic metabolism and metabolic adaptation associated with exercise. Phenylalanine is converted to tyrosine by phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) in the liver and kidneys66. Both phenylalanine and tyrosine can cross the BBB67 and subsequently enter multiple metabolic pathways, contributing to catecholamine synthesis and, to a lesser extent, to energy metabolism through the generation of fumarate.

Dopamine’s impact on mammalian brain development is well recognized, as it regulates the proliferation of neural precursor cells68,]69, in part through activation of D3 receptors that are enriched in neuroepithelial zones involved in neurogenesis70. In the adult brain, dopamine signaling has been shown to increase the production of new neurons in the hippocampus, supporting roles in both proliferation and differentiation of neural stem cells71. While dopamine is known to modulate neurogenesis, its specific contribution to exercise-induced NSC activation remains incompletely understood.

Metabolic integration of fatty acid metabolism and neurogenesis

In a previous study, we presented compelling evidence that a pro-neurogenic stimulus increases the production of saturated fatty acids in NSCs18. Saturated fatty acids have a dual origin, arising from both exogenous dietary sources and endogenous metabolic pathways. While obtained directly from the diet, they are also synthesized via fatty acid synthase (FASN), which orchestrates the biosynthesis of palmitic acid from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA. Additionally, the body synthesizes stearic acid and other long-chain saturated fatty acids by elongating existing fatty acids72. This dual mechanism highlights a crucial adaptation that allows mammals to meet physiological requirements despite insufficient dietary intake.

Using a mouse model of voluntary running, we previously demonstrated a significant increase in de novo lipogenesis within NSCs. This was confirmed by the central administration of C75, an irreversible FASN inhibitor, which led to a prominent reduction in exercise-induced NSCs proliferation in the SGZ. These findings suggest a functional connection between fatty acid metabolism and neurogenesis, indicating that metabolic state influences the brain’s response to environmental stimuli, such as physical activity18.

Consistent with these previous findings, we observed a significant enrichment in Fatty Acid Metabolism (ER = 8.59, FDR = 7.8 × 10− 5) (Fig. 1, D), along with an increase in the levels of other saturated fatty acids, specifically myristic acid (FC = 15, −log10(q) = 3.2) and behenic acid (FC = 1.9, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C), in the hippocampus of the RUN group. Myristic acid plays a crucial role in post-translational modifications, such as N-myristoylation73, which influences the subcellular localization of signaling proteins. It is also implicated in neurotransmitter receptor trafficking74, a process essential for maintaining synaptic strength and plasticity. Behenic acid, a long-chain saturated fatty acid, influences membrane fluidity and stability75. Alterations in these properties can modulate receptor interactions fundamental to neuronal development.

Notably, a recent clinical study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with fish oil led to a significant rise in behenic acid in the erythrocyte plasma membranes of patients with probable Alzheimer’s disease. This increase was part of a beneficial lipid profile change, accompanied by elevated omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, that correlated with reduced oxidative stress76. This suggests that the elevation of behenic acid observed in our running mice may similarly reflect a neuroprotective metabolic adaptation.

In addition to saturated fats, we identified a significant elevation in adrenic acid (FC = 2, −log10(q) = 3.2) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C), an ω−6 polyunsaturated fatty acid derived from arachidonic acid. Adrenic acid is one of the most abundant fatty acids in the early human brain, required for neuronal development and the enrichment of myelin lipids77. Like other polyunsaturated fatty acids, it is metabolized by cytochrome P450 enzymes to form epoxydocosatrienoic acids. These metabolites have been shown to exhibit advantageous bioactivities, including analgesic effects and the mitigation of endoplasmic reticulum stress78, suggesting a potential mechanism by which exercise may support cellular resilience.

Finally, we observed a significant increase in hippocampal oleamide levels (FC = 2.8, −log10(q) = 2.0) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) in running mice. Oleamide, an amide derived from oleic acid, possesses endocannabinoid activity79 and is acknowledged for its anti-inflammatory and sleep-inducing properties80[,81. Furthermore, oleamide influences the activation of serotonin and GABA receptors82. Recent research highlights oleamide’s ability to enhance the expression of doublecortin, a microtubule-associated protein crucial for hippocampal neurogenesis, by activating of PPAR-α in hippocampal NSCs83.

Glutamine and xanthine coordinate purine metabolism during neurogenesis

We identified a significant increase in xanthine levels (FC = 2.3, −log10(q) = 2.4) (Supplementary Table S1, Fig. 1, C) in the RUN group, indicating elevated purine turnover. This aligns with recent evidence that enhanced purine metabolism drives neurogenesis by replenishing the nucleotide pool required for DNA replication and energy transfer during neural stem cells (NSCs) proliferation84. Central to this metabolic pathway, glutamine serves as the obligate nitrogen donor for de novo purine biosynthesis, supporting the anabolic demands of cell cycle progression in early neurodevelopment85[,86. Concurrently, the accumulation of xanthine, generated via xanthine oxidoreductase, is consistent with altered nucleotide turnover and recycling pathways associated with redox signaling. This signaling modulates oxidative stress, a critical regulator of progenitor fate decisions87. Thus, the integration of glutamine-driven synthesis and xanthine-associated catabolism may contribute to a metabolic environment associated with hippocampal neurogenesis.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that voluntary running is associated with distinct metabolic remodeling in the hippocampus consistent with an adaptive metabolic stress response. Using untargeted metabolomics, we identified metabolite patterns compatible with a biphasic metabolic response characterized by depletion of excitatory amino acids associated with bioenergetic demands, together with accumulation of nitrogen-rich metabolites, specifically asparagine and glycine, associated with biosynthetic and neurogenic processes. These findings suggest that the metabolic effects of exercise extend beyond increased energy metabolism and may align with a framework of metabolic hormesis in which transient metabolic stress contributes to a hippocampal environment associated with plasticity and neurogenic activity.

Furthermore, our results highlight associations between mitochondrial carbon and nitrogen metabolism, one-carbon metabolism, and pathways linked to the structural and epigenetic support of neural stem cell activity. Although the precise mechanisms connecting these metabolite shifts to neural stem cell dynamics remain to be defined, this work provides a systems-level metabolic perspective for understanding how exercise may contribute to hippocampal resilience. Future studies integrating metabolomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and functional analyses will be essential for defining the regulatory networks underlying these metabolic adaptations and for identifying metabolic targets associated with preservation of cognitive function across the lifespan.

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides novel insights into exercise-induced metabolic remodeling, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the sample size (n = 7 per group) enabled robust statistical differentiation, replication in larger cohorts will be important for further validation. Second, the exclusive use of GC/MS limits detection of complex lipids and larger macromolecules compared with complementary LC/MS-based approaches. Third, untargeted GC/MS metabolomics was performed using relative quantification without internal standards, which may limit absolute quantification accuracy and inter-batch comparability. Accordingly, subsequent studies will incorporate targeted LC/MS/MS analyses with internal standards to improve quantitative precision and metabolite coverage. Fourth, our interpretation of metabolic pathway activity is inferred from steady-state metabolite levels; direct quantification would require stable isotope tracing as well as direct assessment of mitochondrial function. Fifth, the present study was performed using whole hippocampal tissue and therefore lacks cellular resolution and cell-type-specific metabolic characterization. In addition, functional neurogenesis measurements were not directly evaluated in the current study. Finally, although we propose metabolic and epigenetic interactions as a mechanistic framework, chromatin modifications were not directly measured. Future studies integrating stable isotope tracing with transcriptomic, proteomic, epigenomic, spatial metabolomic, and functional analyses will be essential to define how these metabolic inputs spatially and temporally regulate neurogenic cell fate.
