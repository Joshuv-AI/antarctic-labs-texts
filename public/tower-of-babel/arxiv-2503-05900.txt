Generalized Ginsparg-Wilson relations: Fermionic anomalies on the lattice
*

Abstract
The Ginsparg-Wilson ( gw ) relation elegantly captures how the anomalous chiral symmetry of a Dirac fermion manifests on the lattice. In this talk, we discuss how the GW relation and its closed-form solution, the overlap operator, can be generalized to Majorana or Dirac fermions in any dimension for finite symmetry transformations (continuous or discrete). We find an exact symmetry which reproduces both perturbative and global anomalies on the lattice. These generalized GW fermions are boundary theories of various bulk symmetry-protected topological phases and thus provide an explicit lattice realization of the fermionic bulk-boundary correspondence central to recent proposals for chiral gauge theories on the lattice.

Dirac fermions and the chiral anomaly

Although the problem for a
gauged
chiral symmetry is more severe,
there is in fact already a problem with the
global
chiral symmetry for a Dirac fermion on the lattice.
Simply put, naive attempts to formulate Dirac fermions with an exact chiral symmetry on the lattice lead to additional unwanted fermions, called “doublers”.
This can be seen by a straightforward discretization of the continuum Dirac action
S=\int\!d^{d}x\ \overline{\psi}\not{\partial}\psi
, where
we simply replace the continuum derivative with a difference operator on a
d
(even)-dimensional hypercubic Euclidean spacetime lattice. In the absence of gauge fields, this becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\psi=\gamma_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}\psi\xrightarrow[\text{\hskip 7.97224ptdiscretization \hskip 7.97224pt}]{\text{naive}}D\psi=\frac{1}{2a}\textstyle\sum_{\mu}\gamma_{\mu}(\delta_{\mu}+{\delta}^{*}_{\mu})\psi=\frac{1}{2a}\textstyle\sum_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\left(\psi_{x+\hat{\mu}}-\psi_{x-\hat{\mu}}\right)

(1)

where
a
is the lattice spacing,

\gamma_{\mu}
are the (Hermitian) Dirac matrices,
and
\delta_{\mu},{\delta}^{*}_{\mu}
are forward and backward difference operators, respectively, acting on lattice Dirac fields
\psi_{x}
as

\delta_{\mu}\psi_{x}=\psi_{x+\hat{\mu}}-\psi_{x}
and
{\delta}^{*}_{\mu}\psi_{x}=\psi_{x}-\psi_{x-\hat{\mu}}
, with

\hat{\mu}
being a unit vector in the direction
\mu=1,\dotsc,d
.
In momentum space, the continuum Dirac operator
\mathcal{D}(p)=\gamma_{\mu}p_{\mu}
becomes the lattice Dirac operator
D(p)=\frac{i}{a}\sum_{\mu}\gamma^{\mu}\sin( a p_{\mu} )
, where
ap_{\mu}\in(-\pi,\pi]
is now a periodic variable.
We see that
D(p)
has
2^{d}
zeros, which result in propagator poles.
These are additional massless fermions which survive the continuum limit, but are clearly not present in the continuum theory we started with.
This is the fermion doubling problem.
At this point, though, the problem seems to depend on our choice of the naive discretization scheme in
Eq.

1
.
So we may wonder: is there a clever discretization?

This is where the no-go theorem of
Nielsen-Ninomiya (
nn
)

[
35
,
36
,
37
]
comes in.
The
nn
no-go theorem shows that a local
free
-fermion lattice theory with the right continuum limit and an exact chiral symmetry cannot be free of doublers.
Therefore, we must ask a sharper question: which of the conditions of the no-go theorem should we give up to satisfactorily formulate a single massless Dirac fermion on the lattice?

As an example of how to remove the doublers, we can add a momentum-dependent mass term to the naive discretization of
Eq.

1
.
The mass term is chosen so that the doublers at the corners of the Brillouin zone become heavy, while the fermion at the origin stays massless.
This can be achieved with the Wilson-Dirac operator,

\displaystyle D_{w}(m,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mu}\frac{1}{2a}\left[\gamma_{\mu}(\delta_{\mu}+\delta_{\mu}^{*})-r\delta^{*}_{\mu}\delta_{\mu}\right]+m

(2)

where
m
is a bare fermion mass,
r
is called the Wilson parameter, and

\Delta={\delta}^{*}_{\mu}\delta_{\mu}
is a lattice discretization of the Laplacian operator.
In momentum space, this operator is

D_{w}(p)=\sum_{\mu}\frac{1}{a}\left[i\gamma_{\mu}\sin(a p_{\mu})+r(1-\cos ap_{\mu})\right]+m
.
With
m=0
and
r\sim O(1)>0
, all the doublers acquire a heavy mass of cutoff scale
\sim 1/a
, while the fermion at the origin stays massless.
The problem with this approach is that while it removes the doublers, it unfortunately also breaks chiral symmetry since
\{D_{w},\gamma_{5}\}\neq 0
.
The chiral symmetry is only recovered in the continuum limit.
This becomes a practical problem when we turn on gauge fields, because in the absence of chiral symmetry, fermion masses get additively renormalized and require fine tuning.
Thus, we sacrifice chiral symmetry to eliminate the doublers, consistent with the
nn
no-go theorem.
There are, of course, numerous other ways to violate one of the conditions of the
nn
theorem, but is there an “optimal” approach?

The Ginsparg-Wilson relation
.
To understand the nature of chiral symmetry on the lattice, Ginsparg and Wilson
[

asked the question: what becomes of the exact chiral symmetry when a continuum theory of massless Dirac fermion with an exact chiral symmetry

Denoting the resulting lattice Dirac operator by

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{D},\gamma_{5}\}=0\xrightarrow{\text{\lx@glossaries@gls@link{acronym}{RG}{{{}}{rg}} blocking}}\{D,\gamma_{5}\}=2aD\gamma_{5}D,

It reveals that the lattice Dirac operator violates chiral symmetry, but in a rather specific manner.
Despite formulating this relation,

The relation was later rediscovered when Hasenfratz serendipitously found that perfect actions satisfy the

Following this, it was quickly realized that overlap

This cemented the fundamental role of the

The overlap operator and Lüscher symmetry
.
The overlap operator, in particular, provides an elegant closed-form solution to the
gw
relation (
3

In terms of the Wilson-Dirac operator

[

2

\displaystyle aD_{\mathrm{ov}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+V),\quad V=A/{\left[{A}^{\dagger}A\right]}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(4)

where

is a Wilson-Dirac operator [

2

It is a straightforward exercise to check that the overlap operator

satisfies the

Unlike the Wilson-Dirac operator, the overlap operator has a remarkable property by the virtue of being a solution to the

In fact, for any

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle\to\psi(1-i\varepsilon\gamma_{5}V),\quad\overline{\psi}\to\overline{\psi}(1+i\varepsilon\gamma_{5})

Note that the presence of

The continuum limit arises when
V\to-1

3

Note that
\psi

We shall return to this point later when we look at Majorana fermions.

This symmetry protects the masslessness of Dirac fermions against additive mass renormalization, thus eliminating the fine-tuning problem that plagues the Wilson-Dirac formulation.

Anomaly on the lattice
.
We have seen that the overlap action has an exact (modified) chiral symmetry.
But recall that the
U(1)_{\chi}
chiral symmetry has a mixed anomaly with the
U(1)_{V}
vector symmetry.
That is, in the presence of background
U(1)_{V}
gauge fields, the
U(1)_{\chi}
chiral symmetry is broken.
If the lattice action is invariant under the modified chiral symmetry of
Eq.

5
, how does this anomaly emerge?
It turns out that the
gw

[
39
]

The Jacobian associated with transformation
Eq.

5

e^{-i\varepsilon\tr\gamma_{5}V}

where
\tr\gamma_{5}V=\operatorname{ind}D_{\mathrm{ov}}
is in fact the index of the overlap Dirac operator, defined as the difference between the number of negative and positive chirality zero modes. This is precisely the expected anomaly.

A continuum solution to the
gw
relation
.
Much intuition can be gained about the overlap operator, and its generalizations which we will discuss in the next section, by examining a “continuum” solution to the
gw
relation.
Let us write a (regulated) Dirac operator as
D=h/(1+h)
,
where
h
is some operator.
Substituting this into the
gw
relation (with lattice spacing
a=1
) yields
\{h,\gamma_{5}\}=0
.
Thus, any operator

One natural choice is the continuum Dirac operator
\not{D}/m
itself, where
m
is a regulator mass scale. This yields a continuum solution to the

D={\not{D}}/{(\not{D}+m)}.

But this is just a

)
regulated Dirac fermion! A

\phi

S_{\text{ghost}}=\int\!d^{d}\!x\ \overline{\phi}(\not{D}+m)\phi

Therefore, we find that a continuum

In a sense, the overlap operator is a nonperturbative lattice version of the perturbative continuum

Interestingly, the

5

While the transformation in
Eq.

5
looks mysterious for the overlap operator, it has simple interpretation for the

]

In this section, we illustrate a generalization of the

To motivate the anomaly, let us consider the Fidkowski-Kitaev chain. This is a 1+1-dimensional Hamiltonian model of single-component Majorana fermions on an

Our interest in this system lies in the fact that it exhibits a nontrivial

This system has a second-order critical point at

However, this is an important distinction between

This can be seen by considering the limits

At

In contrast, at

The decoupled Majoranas at sites

This degeneracy is robust against any

The underlying physics here is analogous to the anomaly-inflow mechanism

Indeed, this

Physically, this means that any system in a nontrivial

Both domain-wall fermions and the Majorana chain discussed earlier are examples of this.
In this language, the 5-dimensional domain-wall fermion realizes a nontrivial

This is why we get robust massless Weyl fermions at the 4-dimensional boundary.

The edge mode of the 1+1-dimensional Majorana chain is simply a 0+1-dimensional Majorana fermion, with the continuum action

Generalizing the GW relation to Majorana fermions
.
In Ref.
[
32
]
, it was shown that the original derivation of
gw
can be generalized beyond even-dimensional Dirac fermions and infinitesimal (continuous) symmetries to include odd dimensions, Majorana fermions, and finite symmetry transformations (including discrete symmetries).
We refer the reader to the paper for details.
Here, we sketch the essential features of the procedure and highlight some subtleties.

When employing the
gw
construction,
we need to add a Gaussian term to the original continuum action, coupling the continuum fields to the block-averaged lattice fields.
Upon integrating out the original continuum fields, one obtains a lattice theory.
This Gaussian term has the dimensions of a mass term, which is how a regulator scale enters the problem and how the standard (or continuum) version of chiral symmetry (corresponding to
\{\mathcal{D},\gamma_{5}\}=0
) is broken by the regulator.

A mass term is easy to write down for a Dirac fermion.
But Fermi statistics prohibit adding such a term to the Majorana action
S=\int\!dt\,\chi\partial_{t}\chi
.
Consequently, we cannot formulate a
gw
relation for a single 0+1-dimensional Majorana fermion.
6
6

6

This, incidentally, is the same problem with formulating a
gw

This becomes possible, however, for two Majorana fermions, where one can introduce a mass term of the form
\chi_{a}\varepsilon_{ab}\chi_{b}
, with flavor indices
a,b=1,2
and
\varepsilon_{ab}
being the totally antisymmetric Levi-Civita tensor.
The continuum action with a mass term thus takes the form

S=\int\!dt\,\chi^{T}(\partial_{t}+\mu\tau_{2})\chi,

where
\chi
is now a two-flavor one-dimensional Majorana field,
and
\tau_{2}
is the second Pauli matrix.

Reflection symmetry
\mathsf{R}

[

\chi(t)\to\mathsf{R}\chi(t)=i\chi(-t),

such that
\mathsf{R}^{2}=-1
.
One can verify that this transformation preserves the kinetic term but not the mass term.
This is a hint that this symmetry might be anomalous.
At this point, we have all the ingredients to apply the

[
32
]

Summarizing the results:
if

\displaystyle\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}}-\mathsf{D}=2a\mathsf{D}\tau_{2}\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}},

(6)

where
\mathsf{D}_{\mathsf{R}}=\mathsf{R}^{T}\mathsf{D}\mathsf{R}
.
This is the

\mathsf{R}

Importantly, an explicit overlap solution exists:

\displaystyle a\mathsf{D}_{\mathrm{ov}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{2}}{2}(1+V_{\rm m}),\quad V_{\rm m}=A_{\rm m}/[A_{\rm m}^{\dagger}A_{\rm m}]^{-\frac{1}{2}},

where
A_{\rm m}=D_{w}^{\text{m}}(-\mu,a\mu)
and

Lüscher symmetry for Majorana fermions
.
Does the

At the outset, we note that a transformation like

This is precisely the mod-2 index of the Dirac operator which appears in the continuum anomaly.
Since we find a

The connection between
spt
phases in the bulk and ’t Hooft anomalies on the boundaries has proven remarkably fruitful over the past few decades.
From this point of view, domain-wall fermions constitute a bulk system in an
spt
phase, whereas the overlap operator and the
gw
relation furnish an explicit lattice realization of the boundary theory with an exact ’t Hooft anomaly.
In this work, we investigated whether a
gw

spt
phases).
We found that while the
gw
construction admits substantial generalization, certain crucial elements remain missing.
For example, it is unclear how the complete
\mathbb{Z}_{8}
Dai-Freed anomaly for the 1-dimensional Majorana emerges in this formulation, which only makes a
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
anomaly manifest.
A satisfactory Hamiltonian formulation remains another open question — one that would be crucial for quantum computing applications and would help bridge the divide between this approach and the condensed matter literature.
Given the prominent role of the bulk-boundary correspondence in recent approaches to constructing lattice chiral gauge theories, one is led to wonder:
Could a complete picture of lattice fermionic anomalies be the missing piece needed to solve the longstanding puzzle of non-abelian chiral gauge theories?
This remains to be seen.
