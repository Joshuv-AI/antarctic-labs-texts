Optimal-Transport Stability of Inverse Point-Source Problems for Elliptic and Parabolic Equations
Lingyun Qiu, Shenwen Yu

Abstract
. We establish quantitative global stability estimates, formulated in terms of optimal transport (OT) cost, for inverse point-source problems governed by elliptic and parabolic equations with spatially varying coefficients. The key idea is that the Kantorovich dual potential can be represented as a boundary functional of suitable adjoint solutions, thereby linking OT geometry with boundary observations. In the elliptic case, we construct complex geometric optics solutions that enforce prescribed pointwise constraints, whereas in the parabolic case we employ controllable adjoint solutions that transfer interior information to the boundary. Under mild regularity and separation assumptions, we obtain estimates of the form 𝒯 c ​ ( μ , ν ) ≤ C ​ ‖ u 1 − u 2 ‖ L 2 ​ ( ∂ Ω ) and 𝒯 c ​ ( μ , ν ) ≤ C ​ ‖ u 1 − u 2 ‖ L 2 ​ ( ∂ Ω × [ 0 , T ] ) , \mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])}, where μ \mu and ν \nu are admissible point-source measures. These results provide a unified analytical framework connecting inverse source problems and optimal transport, and establish OT-based stability theory for inverse source problems governed by partial differential equations with spatially varying coefficients.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, increasing attention has been devoted to environmental pollution, particularly to the transport of contaminants in aquatic and atmospheric media. In this work, we consider the following parabolic equation to model the spatiotemporal distribution of a pollutant:

\partial_{t}u(x,t)-\nabla\cdot\Big(\kappa(x)\nabla u(x,t)\Big)+q(x)u(x,t)=F(x,t),

where
\kappa(x)
and
q(x)
denote the spatially dependent diffusion and reaction coefficients, respectively, and
F
represents the (possibly) time-dependent source term associated with the pollutant. The primary objective of this study is to establish quantitative stability for the unknown source term
F
from boundary measurements, leading to an inverse source problem for parabolic equations. As a preliminary step toward the parabolic case, we first examine the associated stationary problem

-\nabla\cdot\Big(\kappa(x)\nabla u(x)\Big)+q(x)u(x)=F(x),

where several analytical ingredients and auxiliary propositions developed for the elliptic framework will subsequently be utilized in the parabolic analysis.

For general source terms, uniqueness for both inverse source problems fails. To ensure identifiability while retaining physical relevance, we restrict attention to source terms represented as finite superpositions of point sources. This point-source model has been extensively studied in the inverse problems literature.
A seminal contribution was made by A. El Badia and collaborators
[
5
]
, who developed an algebraic reconstruction method based on harmonic polynomials
v(x_{1},x_{2})=(x_{1}+ix_{2})^{n}
. These functions solve the adjoint equations with constant coefficients, and evaluation of the associated boundary functionals
\mathcal{R}(v)
yields a linear system from which both source locations and amplitudes can be recovered.
Following this idea, inverse point-source reconstruction has been investigated for a variety of governing equations, including Helmholtz equations
[
6
]
, parabolic equations
[
4
,
3
]
, and wave equations
[
21
]
, as well as moving-source scenarios
[
22
,
2
]
.
The recent work
[
25
]
introduced explicit time-dependent complex geometric optics (CGO) solutions
v(x,t)=e^{\alpha t}e^{\rho\cdot x}
as test functions, yielding a well-conditioned reconstruction operator and avoiding the unstable rank estimation inherent in the harmonic-polynomial approach.
Alternative methodologies have also been developed, ranging from direct sampling
[
24
]
and boundary integral formulations
[
30
]
to machine-learning-based approaches
[
15
]
and optimization via data-misfit minimization
[
16
]
, all targeting the reconstruction of inverse point-source models.
While the works mentioned above provide valuable insights, they primarily address uniqueness and reconstruction algorithms; quantitative stability results remain limited. Existing approaches typically rely on explicit test functions available only in constant-coefficient settings, and thus encounter substantial obstacles when extended to partial differential equations (PDEs) with spatially varying parameters.

In this paper we address this more general and technically demanding setting, where
\kappa(x)
and
q(x)
are spatially dependent, and we establish global stability estimates expressed in terms of optimal transport (OT) cost. More precisely, given two admissible sources
\mu
and
\nu
(elliptic) or two admissible space-time sources (parabolic), we measure their discrepancy via an optimal-transport cost
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)
for a bounded cost function
c(x,y)
.
Our goal is to bound
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)
by the discrepancy of the corresponding boundary data:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\quad

\displaystyle\text{(elliptic)},

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times[0,T])}\quad

\displaystyle\text{(parabolic)}.

The mechanism behind these estimates is that the Kantorovich dual potentials can be realized as boundary linear functionals of suitably constructed adjoint solutions.
In the elliptic case, we employ CGO-based interpolation functions that enforce prescribed pointwise conditions at source locations. In the parabolic case, we design controllable adjoint solutions that transport interior information to the boundary in space-time. These constructions link OT duality with the boundary observation operator and provide a unified analytic route to stability for both stationary and time-dependent problems.

Over the past two decades, OT theory has undergone significant development; see the monographs
[
33
,
32
,
1
]
.
Within inverse problems, there has been increasing interest in replacing traditional
L^{2}
-based misfit with Wasserstein distances in least-squares formulations. As shown in
[
11
,
12
]
, OT-based metrics can offer improved robustness and favorable convexity properties with respect to translations and dilations in seismic full waveform inversion.
From a numerical and analytical standpoint,
[
23
,
27
]
provide systematic analysis and efficient algorithms for gradient-based methods employing Wasserstein misfits. In
[
19
]
, the authors employ the OT metric to derive error estimates for numerical solutions to inverse initial value problems for parabolic equations. To the best of our knowledge, however, rigorous OT-based stability results for inverse problems have not been available; one of the aims of this work is to fill this gap.

To accommodate spatially varying coefficients, correction terms arise in the construction of CGO solutions, and suitable estimates for these terms become essential. CGO solutions form a fundamental class of test functions in inverse problems: the celebrated work
[
31
]
established global uniqueness for the Calderón problem; subsequent developments extended CGO constructions to Schrödinger-type equations with advection terms
[
20
,
28
]
and to solutions vanishing on parts of the boundary
[
18
,
17
]
. Our analysis relies on
C^{k}
-norm estimates for the correction term as in
[
7
]
, where such bounds were used to obtain stability in photoacoustic tomography; see also
[
26
,
10
,
13
]
.
Related results for the variable-coefficient Helmholtz equation, obtained under suitable smallness assumptions, can be found in
[
8
]
.
Complementing these CGO-based constructions, a second key ingredient in our analysis is boundary control theory: in the parabolic case, we use boundary controllability to transfer interior information to the boundary and to convert the relevant interior integrals into boundary terms, with quantitative bounds provided by the observability estimates in
[
14
]
.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the fundamental concepts of OT and present several key lemmas that will be used later. Sections 3 and 4 are devoted to the main global stability theorems for elliptic and parabolic equations, respectively. In Section 5, we conclude the paper and discuss possible directions for future work. Finally, Section 6 contains the proofs of the lemmas introduced in Section 2.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Optimal transport and Kantorovich duality

We recall basic notions from optimal transport required in what follows, including the optimal transport cost, the Kantorovich dual formulation, and a useful reduction of the dual search space. The presentation follows
[
32
]
.

Theorem 1

(Optimal transport cost and Kantorovich duality)
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Polish spaces, let
\mu\in P(X)
and
\nu\in P(Y)
, and let
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
be a lower semicontinuous cost function.

For
\pi\in P(X\times Y)
and
(\varphi,\psi)\in L^{1}(d\mu)\times L^{1}(d\nu)
, define

I[\pi]=\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)d\pi(x,y),\quad J(\varphi,\psi)=\int_{X}\varphi d\mu+\int_{Y}\psi d\nu.

Let
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
denote the set of all Borel probability measures
\pi
on
X\times Y
such that for every measurable
A\subset X
and
B\subset Y
,

\pi[A\times Y]=\mu[A],\qquad\pi[X\times B]=\nu[B].

The optimal transportation cost between
\mu
and
\nu
is then defined by

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}I[\pi].

Define
\Phi_{c}
as the set of all measurable pairs
(\varphi,\psi)\in L^{1}(d\mu)\times L^{1}(d\nu)
satisfying

\varphi(x)+\psi(y)\leq c(x,y)\quad\text{for }\mu\text{-a.e. }x\in X,\ \nu\text{-a.e. }y\in Y.

Then the following duality holds:

(1)

\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}I[\pi]=\sup_{(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}}J(\varphi,\psi).

Moreover, the infimum in the primal problem is attained.

If, in addition, there exist nonnegative measurable functions
c_{X}\in L^{1}(d\mu)
and
c_{Y}\in L^{1}(d\nu)
such that

(2)

c(x,y)\leq c_{X}(x)+c_{Y}(y)\quad\forall(x,y)\in X\times Y,

then the supremum in (
1
) is also attained.

In our setting,
X=Y
is either
\Omega
or
Q
, depending on whether the underlying problem is elliptic or parabolic. Throughout, we assume that the cost function
c:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
is bounded. This boundedness allows reduction of the dual search space, as noted in
[
32
, Remark 1.13]
and formalized below. We also observe that boundedness of
c
implies (
2
), ensuring existence of a maximizer in the dual formulation.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\|c\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x,y}c(x,y)
and define

\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}=\{(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}:\ 0\leq\varphi\leq\|c\|_{\infty},\ -\|c\|_{\infty}\leq\psi\leq 0\}.

Then

\max\{J(\varphi,\psi):(\varphi,\psi)\in\Phi_{c}\}=\max\{J(\varphi,\psi):(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}\}.

A classical example of a cost function is
c(x,y)=d(x,y)^{p}
on a metric space
(X,d)
with
p\geq 1
. In this case, the optimal transport cost induces the Wasserstein-
p
distance:

W_{p}(\mu,\nu)=\Bigl(\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times X}d(x,y)^{p}\,d\pi\Bigr)^{1/p},\qquad\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{p}(X),

where
\mathcal{P}_{p}(X)
denotes the set of probability measures on
X
with finite
p
-th moments.

2.2.
CGO solutions and test function spaces

We now introduce the test function spaces used throughout the analysis. These consist of adjoint solutions and will later be specialized using CGO constructions.

For the elliptic case, define

\mathcal{V}:=\{v\in H^{2}(\Omega):-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x)\nabla v)+q(x)v=0\text{ in }\Omega\}.

For the parabolic case, we use time-dependent functions

\mathcal{V}_{t}:=\{v\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)):\ \partial_{t}v+\Delta v-qv=0\text{ in }Q\}.

A key step in the reconstruction is the construction of test functions
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{V}
associated with distinct points
\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\subset\Omega
, satisfying the interpolation property

v_{j}(s_{\ell})=\delta_{j\ell}.

The following lemma ensures the existence of auxiliary functions with linearly independent point evaluations.

Lemma 3
.

For any collection of
n
distinct points
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\Omega
, let

\eta_{1}:=\min_{i\neq j}|s_{i}-s_{j}|,\quad\eta_{2}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq n}|s_{j}|.

Then there exist
n
test functions
\tilde{v}_{1}(x),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{V}
such that the matrix
A=[a_{l,j}]_{1\leq l,j\leq n}
, defined by

(3)

a_{l,j}:=\tilde{v}_{j}(s_{l}),

is invertible. Moreover, the following estimates hold:

(4)

\|A^{-1}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\exp(-\tilde{r}\eta_{2}^{2})}{1-n\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{r}\eta_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}\eta_{2}}\right)},

and

(5)

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\|\tilde{v}_{j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right\}\leq C_{2}\tilde{r}e^{\tilde{r}R_{0}^{2}}\left(1+\frac{C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}\eta_{2}}\right),

where
C_{1}=C_{1}(\Omega,p)
and
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Omega,d)
are constants independent of the point set
\{s_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
,
R_{0}=\max_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}|x|
and

\tilde{r}:=2n\left(\frac{2}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1+C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\eta_{2}}\right),\quad\tilde{q}(x):=\frac{q(x)}{\kappa(x)}+\frac{\Delta\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}.

Lemma
3
will be proved in
A.1
, where CGO solutions are used in the construction.

2.3.
Boundary control theory

To eliminate interior contributions arising in the parabolic setting, we make use of boundary controllability results, whose proof is deferred to
A.2
. Relying on the observability estimates in
[
14
]
, we obtain the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
. Then there exists a boundary control

\omega\in L^{2}\bigl(T^{*},T;H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)\bigr)

such that the system

(6)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\psi+\Delta\psi-q\psi=0&&\text{in }[T^{*},T]\times\Omega,\\
&\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial n}=\omega&&\text{on }[T^{*},T]\times\partial\Omega,\\
&\psi(\cdot,T^{*})=v(\cdot,T^{*})&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&\psi(\cdot,T)=0&&\text{in }\Omega\end{aligned}\right.

admits a solution
\psi
. Moreover, the control satisfies the estimate

(7)

\|\omega\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{+})}\leq C(\Omega,q)\,\|v(\cdot,T)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\left\|\tfrac{\partial v}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{+})}.

3.
Case for time-independent equations

3.1.
Problem formulation

We first consider the inverse source problem associated with the stationary elliptic equation

(8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} -\nabla\!\cdot\!\bigl(\kappa(x)\nabla u\bigr)+q(x)\,u&=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\,\delta(x-s_{j})&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}&=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a smooth bounded domain. We assume
\kappa\in H^{p+2}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
q\in H^{p}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, and
p>1+\frac{d}{2}
. In addition,
\kappa
is strictly positive on
\overline{\Omega}
. The available data consist of boundary measurements of the solution, that is,
u|_{\partial\Omega}
.

We impose the following assumptions on the source term:

H1

The point locations
s_{j}\in\mathring{\Omega}
are distinct and the number of points satisfies
m\leq M
for a prescribed integer
M
.

H2

The amplitudes satisfy
a_{j}>0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}=1
.

Under
H1
-
H2
, the measure

\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\delta(x-s_{j})

defines a probability measure on
\Omega
. We denote by

\Theta_{M}:=\Bigl\{\mu=\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{j}\delta(x-s_{j}):\mu\text{ satisfies \ref{H1}--\ref{H2}}\Bigr\}

the collection of all admissible source measures.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\Theta_{M}
, and write

\mu=\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}\delta(x-s),\qquad\nu=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}\delta(x-s^{\prime}),

where
N,N^{\prime}
are finite subsets of
\mathring{\Omega}
. Let
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
denote the solutions to (
8
) corresponding to
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.

Given a bounded cost function
c(x,y)
, our objective is to establish a global stability estimate of the form

(9)

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

where
\mathcal{T}_{c}
denotes the optimal-transport cost associated with
c
.

3.2.
Construction of test functions

For any
v\in\mathcal{V}
, define the linear functionals

(10)

\mathcal{R}_{i}(v):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\,u_{i}\,ds,\qquad i=1,2,

where
u_{i}
solves (
8
) with source
\mu
(for
i=1
) or
\nu
(for
i=2
).
Multiplying (
8
) by
v\in\mathcal{V}
and integrating by parts yields the following identity.

Theorem 5
.

For all
v\in\mathcal{V}
,

\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)=\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}\,v(s)=\int_{\Omega}v\,d\mu,\qquad\mathcal{R}_{2}(v)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}\,v(s^{\prime})=\int_{\Omega}v\,d\nu.

The functionals
\mathcal{R}_{i}
have been used in earlier reconstruction approaches (see, e.g.,
[
5
,
25
]
) to form linear systems for recovering point-source parameters. Their structure also mirrors the Kantorovich dual formulation and therefore provides a natural bridge to optimal-transport stability estimates.

Indeed, if
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
and both functions belong to
\mathcal{V}
, then

(11)

J(\varphi,\psi)=\mathcal{R}_{1}(\varphi)+\mathcal{R}_{2}(\psi).

For general
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
, relation (
11
) may fail since
\varphi,\psi
need not lie in
\mathcal{V}
. However, due to the atomic structure of
\mu
and
\nu
, it suffices to construct functions
v_{\varphi},v_{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}
satisfying

(12)

v_{\varphi}(s)=\varphi(s)\quad\forall s\in N,\qquad v_{\psi}(s^{\prime})=\psi(s^{\prime})\quad\forall s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}.

Under this condition, the identity

J(\varphi,\psi)=\mathcal{R}_{1}(v_{\varphi})+\mathcal{R}_{2}(v_{\psi})

holds. The existence of
v_{\varphi}
and
v_{\psi}
is shown in Theorem
7
.

To construct such interpolating functions, we first build a basis
\{v_{s}\}_{s\in N}\subset\mathcal{V}
satisfying

v_{s}(s^{\prime})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }s=s^{\prime},\\
0&\text{if }s\neq s^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Lemma
3
guarantees the existence of auxiliary CGO-based functions with linearly independent evaluations, from which the interpolating basis can be constructed. Moreover, the estimates of
\|\tilde{v}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
and
\|A^{-1}\|_{2}
provide bounds on the norms of these basis functions, which will be used in establishing the main stability result.

Theorem 6
.

There exist functions
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
such that

(13)

v_{i}(s_{j})=\delta_{ij}\quad\text{for all }1\leq i,j\leq n.

and

(14)

\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{3}\,\sqrt{n}\,\tilde{r}\,e^{\tilde{r}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})},

where
C_{3}=C_{3}(\Omega,d)
is independent of the point set
\{s_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
.

Proof.

Let
\{\tilde{v}_{j}(x)\}_{1\leq j\leq n}
be the test functions constructed in Lemma
3
, and define

\begin{pmatrix}v_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
v_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix}:=A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{v}_{1}(x)\\
\vdots\\
\tilde{v}_{n}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

Then the collection
\{v_{j}(x)\}_{1\leq j\leq n}\subset\mathcal{V}
satisfies the interpolation condition (
13
).

To estimate the norms, we use the bound

(15)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\leq\|A^{-1}\|_{2}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|\tilde{v}_{j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}.

From the definition of
\tilde{r}
, we observe that

n\left(\exp\left(-\frac{\tilde{r}\eta_{1}^{2}}{2}\right)+\frac{C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\tilde{r}\eta_{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Substituting the estimates from (
4
) and (
5
) into (

14
).
∎

Theorem 7
.

For all

.

3.3.
Global OT stability for elliptic point sources

We now turn to the proof of the stability estimate (
9

Instead of using a pair of test functions
(v_{\varphi},v_{\psi})\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}

In particular, points in

Lemma 8
.

Assume that

Then, for any

Moreover, the following inequality holds:

(16)

J(\varphi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v).

Proof.

The existence of

More precisely, we first construct a collection

Then the desired test function is given by

v=\sum_{s\in S_{1}\cup S_{3}}\varphi(s)\,v_{s}-\sum_{s\in S_{2}\cup S_{4}}\psi(s)\,v_{s}.

Since
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}

(17)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{s\in S_{1}\cup S_{3}}|\varphi(s)|+\sum_{s\in S_{2}\cup S_{4}}|\psi(s)|\right)\,\,\max_{s\in S}\left\|\frac{\partial v_{s}}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|c\|_{\infty}|S|\,\,\max_{s\in S}\left\|\frac{\partial v_{s}}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C(\Omega)\,\|c\|_{\infty}|S|\,\,\max_{s\in S}\|v_{s}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}(\Omega,d)\,\|c\|_{\infty}|S|\,\tilde{r}\,e^{\tilde{r}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})},

where now

\tilde{r}:=2|S|\left(\frac{2}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1+C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\eta_{2}}\right),

and
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
are as in Lemma
3
and Theorem
6
.

Next, for any
s\in S_{3}\cup S_{4}
, the admissibility condition

\varphi(s)+\psi(s)\leq c(s,s)=0

implies
\varphi(s)\leq-\psi(s)
on
S_{3}
and
\psi(s)\leq-\varphi(s)
on
S_{4}
.
Define

w(s):=\begin{cases}\varphi(s),&\text{if }s\in S_{3},\\[1.99997pt]
\psi(s),&\text{if }s\in S_{4}.\end{cases}

By the definition of
v
, we then have

a_{s}v(s)-b_{s}v(s)=|a_{s}-b_{s}|\,w(s),\qquad\forall s\in S_{3}\cup S_{4}.

We now rewrite
J(\varphi,\psi)
using the above decomposition:

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S_{1}\cup S_{3}\cup S_{4}}a_{s}\varphi(s)+\sum_{s\in S_{2}\cup S_{3}\cup S_{4}}b_{s}\psi(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S_{3}\cup S_{4}}\Bigl[\min\{a_{s},b_{s}\}\bigl(\varphi(s)+\psi(s)\bigr)+|a_{s}-b_{s}|\,w(s)\Bigr]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in S_{1}}a_{s}\varphi(s)+\sum_{s\in S_{2}}b_{s}\psi(s).

By the inequality
\varphi(s)+\psi(s)\leq 0
on
S_{3}\cup S_{4}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in S_{3}\cup S_{4}}|a_{s}-b_{s}|\,w(s)+\sum_{s\in S_{1}}a_{s}\varphi(s)+\sum_{s\in S_{2}}b_{s}\psi(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}v(s)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}v(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v),

which proves (
16
).
∎

Remark 2
.

The inequality (
16
) may be strict even if
(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})
is an optimal pair attaining the supremum of
J(\varphi,\psi)
.
However, when
c
is a distance function on
\Omega
, one can choose an optimal pair
(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
such that

\varphi^{*}(s)+\psi^{*}(s)=0\quad\text{for all }s\in S_{3}\cup S_{4},

so that equality holds in (
16
).
This follows, for instance, from Theorem 1.14 in
[
32
]
.

We are now in a position to state the main stability theorem.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be two probability measures in
\Theta_{M}
.
Then there exists a test function
v^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
such that

(18)

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq\bigl|\mathcal{R}_{1}(v^{*})-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v^{*})\bigr|\leq C_{3}(\Omega,d)\,\|\kappa\|_{C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})}\,\|c\|_{\infty}\,M\,\tilde{r}\,e^{\tilde{r}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})}\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},

where

\tilde{r}=4M\left(\frac{2}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1+C_{1}\|\tilde{q}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\eta_{2}}\right),

and
C_{1}
is as in Lemma
3
.

Proof.

By Kantorovich duality and Lemma
2
, we have

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}}J(\varphi,\psi).

Let
(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
be a maximizer, and apply Lemma
8
to obtain a test function
v^{*}\in\mathcal{V}
such that

(19)

J(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\leq\mathcal{R}_{1}(v^{*})-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v^{*}).

On the other hand, from the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{i}(v)
in (
10
), we have, for every
v\in\mathcal{V}
,

(20)

|\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v)|\leq\|\kappa\|_{C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})}\left\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

Moreover, for
v=v^{*}
, the uniform bound on
\left\|\frac{\partial v^{*}}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
is provided by (
17
).
Combining (
19
), (
20
), and (
17
), we obtain

J(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})\leq C_{3}(\Omega,d)\,\|\kappa\|_{C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})}\,\|c\|_{\infty}\,|S|\,\tilde{r}\,e^{\tilde{r}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})}\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

Since
\mu,\nu\in\Theta_{M}
, we have
|S|\leq 2M
, and the choice of
\tilde{r}
with
4M
in its definition yields (
18
).
Finally, using
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)=J(\varphi^{*},\psi^{*})
completes the proof.
∎

4.
Case for parabolic equations

We now consider the time-dependent setting and study the inverse point-source problem for a parabolic partial differential equation. Our goal is to derive a global stability result, in the sense of optimal transport, analogous to the elliptic estimate (
9
).

4.1.
Problem setting

We begin with the following forward model:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccll}u_{t}-\Delta u+q(x)\,u&=&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t)\,\delta(x-s_{j})&\text{in }Q:=\Omega\times(0,T),\\[3.99994pt]
\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial n}&=&0&\text{on }\Sigma:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[3.99994pt]
u(x,0)&=&0&\text{in }\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Remark 3
.

A more general model with a spatially varying diffusion coefficient,

u_{t}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x)\nabla u)+q(x)\,u=\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t)\,\delta(x-s_{j}),

may also be considered. Since the arguments below change only at the level of notation and not conceptually, we restrict to the case
\kappa(x)\equiv 1
for clarity of exposition.

We impose the following assumptions on the space–time source.

H3

The point source locations
s_{j}\in\mathring{\Omega}
are distinct, and
m\leq M
, where
M
is a fixed integer.

H4

The source intensities satisfy
g_{j}(t)\geq 0
and
g_{j}\in L^{2}(0,T)
for
j=1,\dots,m
.
There exists
T^{*}\in(0,T)
such that

g_{j}(t)=0\quad\text{for }t\in[T^{*},T],

and the total intensity is normalized:

\sum_{j=1}^{m}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}g_{j}(t)\,dt=1.

H5

Each intensity function
g_{j}(t)
belongs to the finite-dimensional subspace

(24)

G_{K}:=\Bigl\{g\in L^{2}(0,T^{*}):\int_{0}^{T^{*}}g(t)\,e^{\frac{2k\pi\mathrm{i}}{T^{*}}t}\,dt=0\ \text{for all }k\in\mathbb{Z},\,|k|>K\Bigr\}.

Equivalently, the time dependence of each source is band-limited to Fourier frequencies
|k|\leq K
.

Under assumptions
H3
–
H5
, the source term defines a probability measure on the space–time domain
Q
.
We denote the admissible class by

\Theta_{M,K}:=\Bigl\{\,\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{m}g_{j}(t)\,\delta(x-s_{j})\ \text{such that }\mu\text{ satisfies assumptions \ref{H3}--\ref{H5}}\Bigr\}.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\Theta_{M,K}
.
We write the corresponding decompositions

\mu=\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}(t)\,\delta(x-s),\qquad\nu=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}(t)\,\delta(x-s^{\prime}),

and denote by
u_{1},u_{2}
the solutions of (
4.1
) associated with
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively.

In the parabolic setting, the underlying optimal transport space is
Q
.
Let
c:Q\times Q\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded cost function satisfying the assumptions stated in Section
2.1
.
For two admissible sources
\mu,\nu\in\Theta_{M,K}
, the corresponding optimal transport cost will be denoted by
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)
.

Our objective is to establish a global stability estimate relating the boundary discrepancy of the parabolic solutions to the transport geometry:

(25)

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)\leq C\,\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma)},

where
C>0
depends only on
\Omega
,
M
,
K
, and the coefficients of the equation.

4.2.
Construction of time-dependent test functions

We introduce the following notation associated with the intermediate time
T^{*}
:

\displaystyle Q^{-}:=\Omega\times(0,T^{*}),\quad\Sigma^{-}:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T^{*}),

\displaystyle Q^{+}:=\Omega\times(T^{*},T),\quad\Sigma^{+}:=\partial\Omega\times(T^{*},T).

For any
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
, and for the two solutions
u_{1},u_{2}
corresponding to
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively, we define

(26)

\mathcal{R}_{i}(v):=\int_{\Omega}u_{i}(x,T^{*})\,v(x,T^{*})\,dx+\int_{\Sigma^{-}}u_{i}\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\,d\sigma,\qquad i=1,2.

Multiplying (
4.1
) by a test function
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
and integrating by parts over
Q^{-}
yields the following representation of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
.

Theorem 10
.

For all
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in N}\int_{0}^{T}a_{s}(t)\,v(s,t)\,dt=\int_{Q}v\,d\mu,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{T}b_{s^{\prime}}(t)\,v(s^{\prime},t)\,dt=\int_{Q}v\,d\nu.

The identities above show that, as in the elliptic case, the functionals
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
depend only on the values of
v
at the spatial source locations, weighted by the corresponding time-dependent intensities.
We now construct time-dependent test functions by combining the spatial basis from Theorem
6
with a truncated temporal Fourier expansion.

Theorem 11
.

Let
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n}\in G_{K}
and let
s_{1},\ldots,s_{n}\in\Omega
be distinct points. Then there exists a test function
v(x,t)\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
such that

v(s_{j},t)=h_{j}(t),\quad\text{for all }1\leq j\leq n\text{ and }t\in(0,T^{*}).

Moreover, there exists a constant

C_{4}=C_{4}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},n,\Omega,q,K)>0,

where

\eta_{1}:=\min_{i\neq j}|s_{i}-s_{j}|,\qquad\eta_{2}:=\min_{1\leq j\leq n}|s_{j}|,

such that

(27)

\left\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{-})}\leq C_{4}\,\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}.

Proof.

Let
e_{k}(t):=e^{\frac{2\pi ikt}{T^{*}}}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
denote the Fourier modes on
(0,T^{*})
.
Since
h_{j}\in G_{K}
, each
h_{j}
admits a truncated Fourier expansion

h_{j}(t)=\sum_{|k|\leq K}c_{k,j}\,e_{k}(t).

For each Fourier mode
k
and each index
j
, Theorem
6
can be applied to the modified potential

q_{k}(x):=q(x)-\frac{2\pi ik}{T^{*}}

to construct spatial test functions
v_{k,j}(x)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying

v_{k,j}(s_{\ell})=\delta_{\ell,j},\qquad(\Delta-q_{k})v_{k,j}(x)=0.

Moreover, from (
14
) we have

\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|v_{k,j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{3}\,\sqrt{n}\,\tilde{r}_{k}\,e^{\tilde{r}_{k}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})},

where

\tilde{r}_{k}:=2n\left(\frac{2}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1+C_{1}\|q_{k}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}}{\sqrt{2}\eta_{2}}\right),

and
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
are the constants from Lemma
3
and Theorem
6
, respectively.

For
|k|\leq K
, we further observe that

\|q-\tfrac{2\pi ik}{T^{*}}\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}\leq\|q\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}+\frac{2\pi K}{T^{*}}\sqrt{|\Omega|},

so that

\tilde{r}_{k}\leq r_{K}:=2n\left(\frac{2}{\eta_{1}^{2}}+\frac{1+C_{1}\bigl(\|q\|_{H^{p}(\Omega)}+\frac{2\pi K}{T^{*}}\sqrt{|\Omega|}\bigr)}{\sqrt{2}\,\eta_{2}}\right).

Consequently,

(28)

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
|k|\leq K\end{subarray}}\|v_{k,j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{3}\sqrt{n}\,r_{K}\,e^{r_{K}(R_{0}^{2}-\eta_{2}^{2})}.

We now define the time-dependent test function

(29)

v(x,t):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{|k|\leq K}c_{k,j}\,e_{k}(t)\,v_{k,j}(x).

By construction and the choice of
q_{k}
, we have

\partial_{t}v+\Delta v-q(x)v=0\quad\text{in }Q^{-},

so
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
. Moreover, at the interpolation points,

v(s_{j},t)=\sum_{|k|\leq K}c_{k,j}e_{k}(t)=h_{j}(t),\qquad 1\leq j\leq n,\ t\in(0,T^{*}).

To estimate the spatial Sobolev norm of
v
, we use Cauchy-Schwarz and the orthogonality of the Fourier modes:

\displaystyle\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{|k|\leq K}|c_{k,j}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{|k|\leq K}\|v_{k,j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{T^{*}}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|h_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\sum_{|k|\leq K}\|v_{k,j}\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{n^{2}(2K+1)}{T^{*}}}\,\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\,\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
|k|\leq K\end{subarray}}\|v_{k,j}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}.

Combining this with (
28
) and using standard trace estimates on
\partial\Omega
yields

\left\|\frac{\partial v}{\partial n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{-})}\leq C_{4}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2},n,\Omega,q,K)\,\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\|h_{j}\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})},

which is precisely (
27
).
∎

Remark 4
.

The band-limited assumption is essential in the above construction.
When
h_{j}
are arbitrary functions in
L^{2}(0,T^{*})
, the above construction does not apply directly. In that case, the series (
29
) need not converge in
L^{2}(0,T^{*};H^{1}(\Omega))
: the constant
C_{4}
in (
27
) grows exponentially as
K\to\infty
, and the resulting bound becomes ineffective.
If one were able to construct
v
for general
h\in L^{2}(0,T^{*})
with uniform control on the boundary norm, then the band-limiting assumption
g_{j}\in G_{K}
could be removed.

Now we relate the optimal transport cost
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)
to the boundary functionals
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

Theorem 12
.

Let
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
. Then, using the basis functions constructed in Theorem
11
, one can construct test functions
v_{\varphi},v_{\psi}\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
such that

(30)

J(\varphi,\psi)=\mathcal{R}_{1}(v_{\varphi})+\mathcal{R}_{2}(v_{\psi}).

Moreover, the optimal transport cost satisfies

(31)

\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)=\max_{(v_{\varphi},v_{\psi})}\left\{\mathcal{R}_{1}(v_{\varphi})+\mathcal{R}_{2}(v_{\psi})\right\},

where the maximum is taken over all pairs
(v_{\varphi},v_{\psi})\in\mathcal{V}_{t}\times\mathcal{V}_{t}
corresponding to admissible dual potentials
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
through (
30
).

Proof.

By the definitions of
\mu
and
\nu
, we may write

(32)

J(\varphi,\psi)=\sum_{s\in N}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\varphi(s,t)\,a_{s}(t)\,dt+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\psi(s^{\prime},t)\,b_{s^{\prime}}(t)\,dt.

Let
\mathcal{P}:L^{2}(0,T^{*})\to G_{K}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
G_{K}
.
Then, for any
g\in L^{2}(0,T^{*})
and any
g^{\prime}\in G_{K}
,

\langle g-\mathcal{P}g,\,g^{\prime}\rangle_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}=0,\qquad\|\mathcal{P}g\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\leq\|g\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}.

Since
a_{s},b_{s^{\prime}}\in G_{K}
, we can replace
\varphi(s,\cdot)
and
\psi(s^{\prime},\cdot)
by their projections without changing the pairing:

(33)

\sum_{s\in N}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\varphi(s,t)\,a_{s}(t)\,dt=\sum_{s\in N}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\mathcal{P}\bigl(\varphi(s,\cdot)\bigr)(t)\,a_{s}(t)\,dt,

and similarly for the terms involving
\psi
and
\nu
.

Applying Theorem
11
, we can construct the corresponding test functions
v_{\varphi}
and
v_{\psi}
such that

v_{\varphi}(s,t)=\mathcal{P}(\varphi(s,t)),\quad v_{\psi}(s^{\prime},t)=\mathcal{P}(\psi(s^{\prime},t))\quad\text{for all }s\in N,\;s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}.

Substituting these identities into (
32
) and using (
33
), together with the representation of
\mathcal{R}_{i}
in terms of the sources, we obtain (
30
). The characterization (
31
) then follows directly from Kantorovich duality combined with Lemma
2
.
∎

Remark 5
.

As in Theorem
7
for the elliptic case, Theorem
12
shows that the values of the functionals
\mathcal{R}_{1}
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}
uniquely determine the optimal transport cost
\mathcal{T}_{c}(\mu,\nu)
within the admissible class
\Theta_{M,K}
.
Although the test functions
v_{\varphi}
and
v_{\psi}
realizing (
30
) are not unique, the corresponding values
\mathcal{R}_{1}(v_{\varphi})
and
\mathcal{R}_{2}(v_{\psi})
are invariant.

4.3.
Global OT stability for parabolic point sources

We now establish the global stability estimate (
25
) for parabolic point sources.
Let
\mu,\nu\in\Theta_{M,K}
with spatial supports

N
and
N^{\prime}
and write

S_{1}:=N\setminus N^{\prime},\quad S_{2}:=N^{\prime}\setminus N,\quad S_{3}:=N\cap N^{\prime},

and

S:=S_{1}\cup S_{2}\cup S_{3}.

We also introduce the separation parameters

\eta_{1}:=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\neq s^{\prime}\\
s,s^{\prime}\in S\end{subarray}}|s-s^{\prime}|,\qquad\eta_{2}:=\min_{s\in S}|s|.

Lemma 13
.

Assume that the cost function satisfies
c((x,t),(x,t))=0
for all
(x,t)\in Q^{-}
.
For any
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
, define

\tilde{v}(s,t):=\begin{cases}\varphi(s,t),&s\in S_{1}\ \text{or}\ \bigl(s\in S_{3},\ a_{s}(t)\geq b_{s}(t)\bigr),\\[3.00003pt]
-\psi(s,t),&s\in S_{2}\ \text{or}\ \bigl(s\in S_{3},\ a_{s}(t)<b_{s}(t)\bigr),\end{cases}\qquad s\in S,\ t\in(0,T^{*}).

Then there exists a test function
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
such that

v(s,\cdot)=\mathcal{P}\bigl(\tilde{v}(s,\cdot)\bigr)\quad\text{for all }s\in S,

where
\mathcal{P}:L^{2}(0,T^{*})\to G_{K}
denotes the orthogonal projection onto
G_{K}
.
Moreover,

(34)

J(\varphi,\psi)\leq\mathcal{R}_{1}(v)-\mathcal{R}_{2}(v).

Proof.

Since
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
, both
\varphi
and
\psi
are bounded on
Q
. In particular,

\|\varphi\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq\|c\|_{\infty},\qquad\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq\|c\|_{\infty}.

Hence, by the definition of
\tilde{v}
,

\|\tilde{v}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\leq\sqrt{T^{*}}\,\|c\|_{\infty}\quad\text{for all }s\in S.

Since
\mathcal{P}
is the orthogonal projection onto
G_{K}
, we also have

\|\mathcal{P}(\tilde{v}(s,\cdot))\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\leq\|\tilde{v}(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,T^{*})}\leq\sqrt{T^{*}}\,\|c\|_{\infty},\quad s\in S.

Applying Theorem
11
, we can construct a test function
v\in\mathcal{V}_{t}
such that

(35)

v(s,t)=h_{s}(t)=\mathcal{P}\big(\tilde{v}(s,\cdot)\big)(t),\quad\text{for all }s\in S,\ t\in(0,T^{*}).

Moreover, from (
27
) and the bound on
\mathcal{P}(\tilde{v})
, we obtain

(36)

\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C_{4}\,\sqrt{T^{*}}\,\|c\|_{\infty},\quad\text{for all }t\in(0,T^{*}).

Next, for any
s\in S_{3}
, the admissibility condition
(\varphi,\psi)\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{c}
implies

\varphi(s,t)+\psi(s,t)\leq c\big((s,t),(s,t)\big)=0.

Define

w(s,t):=\begin{cases}\varphi(s,t),&\text{if }a_{s}(t)\geq b_{s}(t),\\[3.00003pt]
\psi(s,t),&\text{if }a_{s}(t)<b_{s}(t),\end{cases}\qquad s\in S_{3}.

By the definition of
\tilde{v}
, we then have, for all
s\in S_{3}
,

a_{s}(t)\tilde{v}(s,t)-b_{s}(t)\tilde{v}(s,t)=|a_{s}(t)-b_{s}(t)|\,w(s,t),\quad t\in(0,T^{*}).

We now estimate
J(\varphi,\psi)
. Using the definition of
J
and splitting the sums over
S_{1},S_{2},S_{3}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S_{1}\cup S_{3}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}a_{s}(t)\varphi(s,t)\,dt+\sum_{s\in S_{2}\cup S_{3}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}b_{s}(t)\psi(s,t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in S_{3}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\Big(\min\{a_{s}(t),b_{s}(t)\}\big(\varphi(s,t)+\psi(s,t)\big)+|a_{s}(t)-b_{s}(t)|\,w(s,t)\Big)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in S_{1}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}a_{s}(t)\varphi(s,t)\,dt+\sum_{s\in S_{2}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}b_{s}(t)\psi(s,t)\,dt.

Since
\varphi(s,t)+\psi(s,t)\leq 0
for
s\in S_{3}
, the terms involving
\min\{a_{s},b_{s}\}
are nonpositive. Hence

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in S_{3}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}|a_{s}(t)-b_{s}(t)|\,w(s,t)\,dt+\sum_{s\in S_{1}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}a_{s}(t)\varphi(s,t)\,dt

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{s\in S_{2}}\int_{0}^{T^{*}}b_{s}(t)\psi(s,t)\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\left(\sum_{s\in S_{3}}|a_{s}(t)-b_{s}(t)|\,w(s,t)+\sum_{s\in S_{1}}a_{s}(t)\varphi(s,t)+\sum_{s\in S_{2}}b_{s}(t)\psi(s,t)\right)dt.

By the definition of
\tilde{v}
, the right-hand side can be rewritten as

\displaystyle J(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\left(\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}(t)\tilde{v}(s,t)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}(t)\tilde{v}(s^{\prime},t)\right)dt.

Since
a_{s},b_{s^{\prime}}\in G_{K}
and

and similarly for the terms involving

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T^{*}}\left(\sum_{s\in N}a_{s}(t)\mathcal{P}\big(\tilde{v}(s,\cdot)\big)(t)-\sum_{s^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}}b_{s^{\prime}}(t)\mathcal{P}\big(\tilde{v}(s^{\prime},\cdot)\big)(t)\right)dt

where in the last step we used the representation of

We are now ready to state and prove the main stability estimate.

The same strategy also yields OT-type stability for inverse point-mass
initial data problems for parabolic equations; see

Proof.

We first show that for any

By Lemma

there exists a pair

solving the adjoint system (

and

Therefore,

where we have used the above control estimate for

∎

5.
Concluding remarks

This work develops an optimal-transport framework for the stability analysis of inverse point-source problems for variable-coefficient elliptic and parabolic equations. The central observation is that suitably constructed adjoint solutions allow Kantorovich dual potentials to be realized as boundary linear functionals, thereby linking OT geometry directly to boundary measurements. In the elliptic setting, this is accomplished through CGO-based interpolation functions with quantitative control of correction terms. In the parabolic setting, the same spatial construction is combined with boundary controllability to transfer interior information to the boundary. This yields global stability bounds that depend only on boundary data misfits. A parallel argument also applies to point-mass initial data; see
B
.

Several limitations and directions for further research remain. First, our analysis focuses on finitely many point sources with positive amplitudes and, in the parabolic case, band-limited temporal profiles. Extending the framework to more general classes of measures (e.g., measures with bounded densities, sparse superpositions of localized blobs, or signed sources) is an interesting open problem. Second, the present work assumes full boundary measurements; if one could obtain
C^{k}
-bounds for CGO solutions vanishing on portions of the boundary, analogous OT stability estimates for partial boundary data should be within reach. Third, it would be natural to investigate extensions to more general models, such as equations with advection terms, coupled systems, or Helmholtz-type equations at fixed frequency, where CGO constructions and controllability results are also available. Finally, on the numerical side, it would be of interest to explore how the OT-based stability estimates derived here can inform the design and analysis of OT-type misfit functionals in reconstruction algorithms, and to what extent the theoretical constants and separation conditions can be reflected in practical regularization strategies.
