Hijacking pre-tRNA enables LTR-retrotransposon–initiated constitutive heterochromatin formation
Authors: Yazi Deng, Yu Liang, Yi Ni Luo, Luoqi Ma, Wei Wu, Wei Wu, Lu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pericentric heterochromatin serves as a fundamental component of eukaryotic chromosomes, endowing specialized genomic architecture with broad functional consequences. Although it is universally marked by H3K9me3 modification, the underlying pericentric DNA sequences diverge substantially across species. Here, by leveraging a transposition reporter system combined with a genome-wide RNA interference (RNAi) screen, we identified a specialized mechanism for recruiting SUV39H methyltransferase to initiate pericentric heterochromatin formation. This pathway depends on a highly ordered complex comprising the Puf68, pre–transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and the primer binding site (PBS). Puf68 binds with high affinity to poly-U tracts in pre-tRNA 3′ trailer, forming a Puf68/pre-tRNA complex that subsequently base-pairs with the PBS of nascent long terminal repeat (LTR)–retrotransposons. Through direct interaction, Puf68 recruits Su(var)3-9 to these regions, catalyzing H3K9 trimethylation. Notably, Puf68 is sufficient to initiate de novo heterochromatin assembly both at pericentric and ectopically integrated LTR-retrotransposon regions. Our findings not only uncover a previously unrecognized mechanism of heterochromatin initiation but also resolve a long-standing question of how hosts harness nascent LTR-retrotransposon transcripts.

INTRODUCTION

The term “heterochromatin” was coined by Emil Heitz in 1928 to describe chromosomal regions that maintain compacted morphology throughout the cell cycle (1). Pericentric heterochromatin, known as constitutive heterochromatin, represents a functional and fundamental domain of the eukaryotic chromosomes in species ranging from yeast to human, endowing critical roles in regulating gene expression, restraining transposon activity, and preserving genomic integrity (2–5). The core conserved features of pericentric heterochromatin comprise (i) the characteristic H3K9me2 and H3K9me3 epigenetic marks (3, 6), (ii) the evolutionarily conserved SUV39H-family H3K9-specific histone methyltransferases (7, 8), and (iii) the heterochromatin protein 1 (HP1) binding platform that mediate heterochromatin formation and maintenance (9, 10).

The assembly of pericentric heterochromatin proceeds through three discernible phases: nucleation of repressive complexes at initiation sites, cis spreading of repressive histone modifications, and heterochromatin establishment (5, 11, 12). While SUV39H methyltransferase–catalyzed H3K9 methylation represents the principal hallmark of pericentric heterochromatin, the mechanisms governing SUV39H nucleation and heterochromatin initiation exhibit remarkable divergence across species. In the fission yeast Schizosaccharomyces pombe, Ago1-associated small RNAs (sRNAs) recognize homologous nascent transcripts of repetitive sequences, which subsequently nucleates Clr4 methyltransferase complex (CLRC) to initiate pericentric heterochromatin formation (13–16). Additionally, in the absence of sRNAs, the MTREC/PAXT complex directs a heterochromatic long noncoding RNA to nucleate Clr4-mediated heterochromatin assembly (17). While the mechanisms initiating pericentric heterochromatin in mammalian cells remains less clear, recent study indicates that the zinc-finger proteins (ZNFs) specifically bind pericentric DNA and recruit SUV39H methyltransferases to establish de novo pericentric heterochromatin (18).

However, the factor that nucleates SUV39H homologs at pericentric regions to initiate de novo heterochromatin formation in Drosophila somatic tissues remains unidentified. Comparative analysis of genomic DNA composition revealed that, compared to other species, the DNA sequences at pericentric regions in Drosophila genome are highly unique (fig. S1, A and B). More than 73% of the pericentric DNAs comprised transposable elements (TEs), with around 44.7% of them coming from long terminal repeat (LTR)–retrotransposons (fig. S1B). These data suggest that Drosophila has likely evolved a specialized mechanism to initiate SUV39H methyltransferase-dependent pericentric heterochromatin assembly.

RESULTS

Identify the suppressors of transposon mobilizations via a genome-wide RNAi screen

In S. pombe, small interfering RNA (siRNA)–directed recruitment of SUV39H methyltransferase establishes a precise mechanism for nucleating constitutive heterochromatin (13, 16). During Drosophila and mouse gametogenesis, the nuclear PIWI proteins are proposed to bind nascent transposon RNAs through guiding the base-pairing between Piwi-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) and transposon transcripts, which subsequently recruit cosuppressors to transcriptionally silence transposon activity (19–23). To this end, we first tested whether the piRNA and siRNA pathways contribute to establishing H3K9me3-dependent constitutive heterochromatin in Drosophila somatic tissues. Based on our recent identification of Drosophila hindgut as an ideal model system for monitoring constitutive heterochromatin assembly and transposon mobilization (24, 25), we tested above hypothesis in this tissue. By performing H3K9me3 immunostaining, we found that neither the piRNA (piwi, aub, and ago3 depletion) nor the siRNA pathway (ago2 depletion) contribute significantly to H3K9me3-mediated heterochromatin assembly in the Drosophila hindgut (fig. S1C). These results suggest that Drosophila somatic tissues use a distinctive mechanism to initiate pericentric heterochromatin formation.

Defective heterochromatin assembly is likely to cause aberrant transposon activation and mobilizations, leading to genomic instability. Recently, we have engineered a green fluorescent protein (GFP)–based reporter system capable of tracking de novo insertions of retrotransposons during both Drosophila germline and somatic development (24–27). This GFP reporter exclusively produces fluorescent protein upon retrotransposon transcription and subsequent integration into new genomic loci (Fig. 1A). Here, we used a GFP transposition reporter for HMS-Beagle, a very active LTR-retrotransposon in fly germline and hindgut. Consistent with our finding that piRNA and siRNA pathways play minimal roles in heterochromatin establishment, genetic ablation of these pathways in Drosophila hindguts did not result in detectable retrotransposition events of HMS-Beagle (fig. S1D). Next, to systematically identify the factors required for suppressing transposon mobilizations and initiating pericentric heterochromatin formation, we performed a genome-wide RNA interference (RNAi) screen (Fig. 1B). Through this screen, we identified multiple factors capable of sufficiently controlling HMS-Beagle mobilizations during Drosophila hindgut development (Fig. 1B).

Given the well-documented role of pericentric nascent RNAs in initiating constitutive heterochromatin formation in S. pombe, here, we focused on the RNA binding protein Puf68 (also known as Hfp in Drosophila) as a candidate regulator (Fig. 1B). Puf68, the Drosophila ortholog of human PUF60, was initially characterized as a poly-U binding factor that regulates both constitutive and alternative RNA splicing in vivo (28). The holometabolous development of Drosophila involves metamorphic remodeling during pupation, when larval tissues undergo apoptosis-driven degeneration while imaginal cells proliferate to form adult structures, including the hindgut (27, 29). Our results indicate that Puf68 executes its function throughout Drosophila hindgut development, effectively suppressing HMS-Beagle transpositions in both larval and adult stages (Fig. 1, C and D). While HMS-Beagle was highly mobile in puf68-depleted hindguts, control (white RNAi) samples maintained strong transposon suppression (Fig. 1, C and D). To rule out potential off-target effects from this RNAi construct, we validated our findings with three independent RNAi alleles, all of which produced consistent results (fig. S2A). Additionally, using a Puf68-specific antibody, we demonstrated that Puf68 exhibited exclusive nuclear localization pattern and activating puf68 RNAi efficiently depleted Puf68 protein levels (fig. S2B).

Puf68 facilitates the deposition of H3K9me3 at pericentric chromatin

Next, we endeavor to investigate whether the aberrantly activated LTR-retrotransposon in puf68-depleted Drosophila hindguts was caused by compromised heterochromatin establishment. To this end, we examined the distribution of H3K9me3, the key marker of constitutive heterochromatin, by immunofluorescence. During wild-type (white RNAi) Drosophila development, we clearly observed strong H3K9me3 enrichment at constitutive heterochromatin regions (Fig. 1E). However, upon puf68 depletion, the foci of H3K9me3 at constitutive heterochromatin was markedly abolished (Fig. 1E). As reported previously, HP1 is capable of recognizing H3K9me3 to reinforce heterochromatin maintenance (9, 10, 30). In wild-type hindguts, H3K9me3 and HP1 are colocalized, with robust HP1 accumulation at constitutive heterochromatin (Fig. 1F). In contrast, Puf68 depletion resulted in dramatically decreased enrichment of HP1 at heterochromatic foci (Fig. 1F).

To further validate the immunofluorescence results, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) to obtain a quantitative, genome-wide assessment of H3K9me3 distribution. Whole-chromosome mapping identified pericentromeric heterochromatin as the primary site of H3K9me3 accumulation in wild-type Drosophila (Fig. 1G and fig. S2C), consistent with these regions’ low gene content and high transposon density (31). However, in contrast to controls, genetic ablation of Puf68 substantially compromised H3K9me3 deposition at pericentric domains (Fig. 1G and fig. S2C). Having established the global H3K9me3 pattern, we then focused on analyzing the incorporation of H3K9me3 at transposon DNA sequences. Our data indicated that the deposition of H3K9me3 at TEs was dramatically decreased upon puf68 depletion (fig. S2D). Furthermore, in Puf68-deficient fly hindguts, nearly all of the transposon families showed diminished H3K9me3 incorporation (fig. S2, E and F). It has been known that the heterochromatin established at transposon loci exhibits spreading capacity, leading to position-effect variegation of nearby genes (5, 32). Accordingly, our locus-specific analysis data indicated that Puf68 deficiency not only reduced deposition of H3K9me3 at transposon DNAs, but also impaired H3K9me3 spreading to adjacent upstream and downstream regions of transposon loci (Fig. 1H). Together, these findings establish Puf68 as a critical regulator of constitutive heterochromatin assembly.

Puf68 initiates heterochromatin formation at ectopically integrated LTR-retrotransposons

To determine whether Puf68 initiates heterochromatin formation at ectopic site, we examined H3K9me3 deposition at the genomic locus of the integrated HMS-Beagle transposition reporter. In wild-type flies, we detected a clear enrichment of H3K9me3 near the reporter’s integration site (fig. S3A). As expected, depletion of puf68 dramatically reduced H3K9me3 levels at this locus (fig. S3A), indicating that Puf68 is necessary for the de novo initiation of heterochromatic silencing. Concomitant with this loss of H3K9me3, the HMS-Beagle reporter became significantly more active at transcriptional level in puf68-depleted hindguts (fig. S3, B and C).

We next investigated whether Puf68’s role in initiating H3K9me3 heterochromatin extends to other TEs. Using the same reporter system, we tested four LTR-retrotransposons (diver, blood, roo, and copia) and one non–LTR-retrotransposon (Doc) (fig. S3, B and C). Our results demonstrate that Puf68 effectively initiates ectopic H3K9me3 heterochromatin to suppress the expression of LTR-retrotransposons, but not the non–LTR-retrotransposon Doc (fig. S3, B and C).

Puf68 preferentially suppresses the transcription of LTR-retrotransposons

As a key guardian of genomic stability, pericentric heterochromatin enforces transcriptional repression of repetitive elements, including satellite repeats and TEs (18). To examine the role of Puf68 in this process, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) to quantify the expression of all transposons during Drosophila hindgut development. Compared with sh-white control flies, 22 and 18 transposon families showed at least a twofold increase in transcription from puf68-depleted larval and pupal hindguts, respectively (Fig. 2A and fig. S4, A and B). Notably, LTR-retrotransposons accounted for 7 of the 10 most highly activated families at the larval stage and 9 of the 10 at the pupal stage, with five families consistently activated across both stages (Fig. 2A and fig. S4, A and B). Notably, a subset of LTR-retrotransposons displayed precise developmental timing in their activation patterns (Fig. 2A and fig. S4C). For example, copia showed higher transcriptional activity during larval stage, whereas accord became more active at pupal stage (Fig. 2A and fig. S4C). Consistent with the RNA-seq data, single-molecule RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA FISH) assay revealed strong copia signals in puf68-depleted hindguts at both stages, but not in control hindguts (fig. S4D).

To identify the key transposons in driving heterochromatin formation and better understand the effects of reduced H3K9me3 on increased transposon expression, we performed assay for transposase-accessible chromatin (ATAC) sequencing on early-stage regenerating hindguts. Our results demonstrated that only specific LTR-retrotransposons and their flanking genomic regions exhibited increased chromatin accessibility upon puf68 depletion, while LINE, DNA, and Helitron transposons remained unaffected (Fig. 2B). The multiomics integration results revealed strong concordance among H3K9me3 deposition (ChIP-seq), chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq), and transcriptional activity (RNA-seq) at LTR-retrotransposon loci (Fig. 2C and fig. S4E). These findings suggested that Puf68 deficiency in Drosophila hindguts disrupted proper H3K9me3 deposition at 5′ LTR and upstream regions of LTR-retrotransposons, leading to chromatin decompaction and subsequent transposon derepression (Fig. 2C and fig. S4E). Collectively, we propose that LTR-retrotransposons provide special genomic anchors for Puf68 to initiate H3K9me3-dependent constitutive heterochromatin assembly.

Puf68 interacts with Su(var)3-9 methyltransferase

Drosophila genome has three genes, dsetdb1, su(var)3-9, and G9a, encoding histone methyltransferases for catalyzing H3K9 methylation. We systematically analyzed the expression patterns and subcellular localization of these three H3K9 methyltransferases during Drosophila hindgut development at both larval and pupal stages. All three H3K9 methyltransferases exhibited stable expression and exclusive nuclear localization throughout hindgut development (Fig. 3, A and B, and fig. S5, A and B). Notably, only Su(var)3-9 showed precise colocalization with H3K9me3 at constitutive heterochromatin domain, unlike other methyltransferases (Fig. 3B). Moreover, we found that Puf68 is specifically required for targeting Su(var)3-9 to constitutive heterochromatin, as its depletion completely abolished this localization while sparing other H3K9 methyltransferases (Fig. 3, A and B, and fig. S5, A and B). Consistent with these results, only Su(var)3-9, but not dSetdb1 and G9a, is essential for establishing H3K9me3 at constitutive heterochromatin during Drosophila hindgut development (Fig. 3C and fig. S5, C and D). Additionally, similar to puf68-depleted Drosophila hindguts, activating su(var)3-9 RNAi also reduced the accumulation of HP1 in constitutive heterochromatin (fig. S5E). To assess the specificity of Puf68’s epigenetic regulation, we performed immunostaining for multiple histone modifications. However, Puf68 deficiency leaves other histone modifications unaltered (fig. S5F), underscoring the specific function of Puf68 in regulating H3K9 trimethylation.

The loss of Su(var)3-9 from heterochromatin upon Puf68 depletion could result from reduced protein levels or defective recruitment. Western blotting confirmed that Puf68 deficiency did not affect the production of Su(var)3-9 methyltransferase in Drosophila hindguts (fig. S5G). However, impaired Puf68-dependent recruitment of Su(var)3-9 to heterochromatin leads to a marked reduction in H3K9me3 levels in puf68-depleted hindguts (fig. S5H). In this case, Puf68 is likely to interact and recruit Su(var)3-9 methyltransferase to the pericentric region to catalyze H3K9 trimethylation, for licensing constitutive heterochromatin formation. To test this, we conducted coimmunoprecipitation assay. In vivo coimmunoprecipitation revealed a strong physical interaction between Puf68 and Su(var)3-9 (Fig. 3D). Next, to identify the domain of Puf68 interacting with Su(var)3-9, we performed coimmunoprecipitation assays in human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells expressing full-length Puf68 or its truncation mutants alongside Su(var)3-9 (Fig. 3E and fig. S6, A and B). Our results indicated that both full-length Puf68 and its N-terminal fragment (residues 2 to 129) efficiently coprecipitated Su(var)3-9 (Fig. 3E and fig. S6A), suggesting this region as the minimal interaction domain. Deleting this region completely abolished the interaction between Puf68 and Su(var)3-9 (fig. S6B).

To functionally characterize Puf68 domains in vivo, we inserted the GFP tag into endogenous Puf68 and generated transgenic Drosophila lines expressing Puf68 truncation mutants. Consistent with previous immunostaining result (fig. S2B), the GFP-tagged full-length Puf68 is exclusively localized to the nucleus, and deletion of either RRM2 or RRM3 domain did not alter its cellular localization (fig. S6C). However, removal of N-terminal fragment (residues 2 to 120) resulted in Puf68 protein leaking to the cytoplasm (fig. S6C). Next, we investigated the role of Puf68 domains in assisting its binding to chromatin by isolating the proteins from nucleoplasm and chromosomes. Our results indicated that depletion of RRM2 domain alone dramatically reduced Puf68 association to chromatin (fig. S6D). In summary, these results demonstrated that while the N-terminal domain is required for recruiting Su(var)3-9 methyltransferase, RRM2 domain mediates Puf68 binding to pericentric chromatin.

Since the N-terminal fragment is essential for the interaction between Puf68 and Su(var)3-9, and the RRM2 domain is required for Puf68 chromatin binding, we propose that these two domains are critical for recruiting Su(var)3-9 to pericentric chromatin. To test this hypothesis, we generated short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) specifically targeting the transcripts encoding the Puf68 N-terminal fragment and RRM2 domain (fig. S7A). Both shRNAs efficiently depleted endogenous Puf68 expression, as validated in fig. S2B. We then separately crossed flies expressing N terminus–truncated or RRM2-deleted Puf68 with the corresponding domain-specific RNAi lines. Our results demonstrate that both N terminus–deficient and RRM2-deleted Puf68 fail to recruit Su(var)3-9 to pericentric chromatin and consequently cannot support H3K9me3 catalysis (fig. S7, B and C). H3K9me3 signals were markedly reduced in the hindgut nuclei of these depletion backgrounds (fig. S7, B and C).

Puf68 recruits Su(var)3-9 to pericentric chromatin to catalyze H3K9 trimethylation

We then performed Su(var)3-9 ChIP-seq to further evaluate the function of Puf68 in nucleating Su(var)3-9 and initiating H3K9me3-mediated constitutive heterochromatin formation. Consistent with the H3K9me3 enrichment pattern, in the control group, Su(var)3-9 also shows significant accumulation at pericentric regions (Fig. 3F and fig. S8A). By contrast, Puf68 deficiency impaired the recruitment of Su(var)3-9 to these regions, resulting in reduced deposition of H3K9me3 (Fig. 3F and fig. S8A). Inspired by these data, we next examined the Su(var)3-9 incorporation at transposon DNA sequences. Su(var)3-9 is significantly enriched at transposon sequences, and puf68 depletion markedly reduced its deposition at these repetitive elements (fig. S8, B, C and D). Meanwhile, loss of Puf68 not only decreased deposition of Su(var)3-9 at transposon DNAs, but also diminished spreading into adjacent upstream and downstream regions (Fig. 3G). Moreover, our analysis uncovered that Su(var)3-9 binding sites showed extensive overlap with H3K9me3-marked regions across chromosomes, with 93.82% of Su(var)3-9 binding peaks colocalizing with H3K9me3 (fig. S8E). Puf68 deficiency similarly disrupted both Su(var)3-9 incorporation and H3K9me3 deposition at TEs (fig. S8, F to H). Collectively, our results suggest that Puf68 serves as a critical factor to recruit Su(var)3-9 methyltransferase to pericentric regions for catalyzing the trimethylation of H3K9.

Puf68 binds poly-U sites of pre-tRNA

To elucidate how the RNA binding protein Puf68 functions as a epigenetic suppressor to license Su(var)3-9/H3K9me3–mediated constitutive heterochromatin initiation, we performed linear amplification of complementary DNA ends with high-throughput sequencing (LACE-seq) to identify its RNA targets. LACE-seq has been established as an effective method for characterizing the in vivo RNA targets of RNA binding proteins in low-abundance cells (33), making it particularly suitable for detecting Puf68-binding RNAs in Drosophila hindguts (fig. S9A). LACE-seq analysis of Drosophila hindguts yielded highly reproducible data across two biologically independent replicates (fig. S9B). Motif enrichment analysis revealed strong enrichment of U-rich sequences in Puf68-binding RNAs, further supporting its role as a poly-U binding factor (fig. S9C). Although 4% of the Puf68 LACE-seq reads were mapped to repetitive elements, we found that nearly half of them were aligned with LTR-retrotransposons (fig. S9D), the most abundant transposons in pericentric regions. During LTR-retrotransposon replication, the transcribed retrotransposon mRNA hijacks tRNA for serving as a primer to reverse transcribe 5′ LTR RNA to cDNA (fig. S9E). Unexpectedly, analysis of Puf68 binding sites across LTR-retrotransposons revealed that majority of the reads were specifically mapped to the LTR-retrotransposons’ primer binding site (PBS), the sequence essential for initiating reverse transcription (fig. S10A). For instance, LACE-seq reads clearly and exclusively base-paired with PBS of HMS-Beagle were readily detected (Fig. 4A). Next, we focused on analyzing Puf68 binding to pre-tRNAs, which harbor poly-U tracts at their 3′ trailer. Our data revealed that Puf68 interacts with approximately half of all pre-tRNA genes, with LACE-seq signals showing strong enrichment at the 3′ trailer of pre-tRNAs (Fig. 4, B to D). Additionally, the pre-tRNAs significantly enriched in association with Puf68 could base-pair with the PBS sequences of 88.5% (54/61) of the LTR-retrotransposon families (fig. S10, A and B). We observed that a single LTR-retrotransposon PBS can be targeted by multiple pre-tRNAs, and one pre-tRNA can potentially base-pair with different LTR-retrotransposons’ PBS sequences (fig. S10, A and B), indicating that the pairing relationship between pre-tRNA and PBS is versatile. To test whether PBS–pre-tRNA pairing is required for transposon silencing, we therefore generated a Copia GFP transposition reporter in which the PBS sequence was mutated (fig. S10C). Transcriptional quantification revealed that the PBS-mutated Copia reporter exhibited significantly higher transcriptional activity compared with the wild-type reporter (fig. S10D). This elevated transcription depends on Puf68-mediated heterochromatin formation, as the repressive effect on the PBS-mutant Copia reporter was substantially compromised upon puf68 depletion (fig. S10E).

Furthermore, Puf68 also bound both sense strands of Flea and Mdg3 elements and the antisense strand of roo elements, with LACE-seq peaks consistently exhibiting poly-U motifs at these binding sites (fig. S11, A to C). Together, our findings suggested that Puf68 predominantly binds to the nascent LTR-retrotransposon RNAs in pericentric regions through two mechanisms: primarily via pre-tRNA base-pairing and, to a lesser extent, through direct binding. This interaction subsequently recruits and nucleates Su(var)3-9 and initiates H3K9me3-mediated constitutive heterochromatin formation.

To validate the in vivo interaction between Puf68 and pre-tRNA, we therefore performed in vitro electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA). We expressed and purified the full-length of Puf68 fused to a His-tag. Puf68 specifically binds pre-tRNAs of both Tyr-GTA and Lys-TTT (Fig. 4E and fig. S12A). This interaction depends critically on the poly-U tracts at the 3′ trailer of pre-tRNAs, as mature tRNAs lacking these poly-U sequences show no detectable binding (Fig. 4E). Additionally, as depleting the sequence of PBS resulted in complete loss of the interaction between pre-tRNA and transposon mRNAs, we concluded that the interaction critically depends on the PBS (fig. S12B). Our previous results indicated that the RRM2 domain of Puf68 is essential for its deposition onto chromatin (fig. S6D). Consistent with this, when examining pre-tRNA interaction with all three of Puf68’s RNA-binding domains, we found that the RRM2 domain of Puf68 dominantly binds pre-tRNA (fig. S12, C and D). Furthermore, our data indicated that this association is specific to pre-tRNA but not mature tRNA (fig. S12D). To validate the essential role of the Puf68/pre-tRNA-PBS interaction in recruiting Puf68 to LTR-retrotransposons, we performed CUT&Tag for Puf68. The results demonstrated specific enrichment of Puf68 at LTR-retrotransposon sequences, with no significant enrichment observed at LINE, DNA, or Helitron transposons (Fig. 4F).

DISCUSSION

Given the high diversity of DNA composition in pericentric regions across species, the mechanisms initiating pericentric heterochromatin formation are highly organism-specific. Tremendous efforts have been conducted to elucidate the initiation mechanisms of constitutive heterochromatin in model systems, including S. pombe and mammalian cell lines. The yeast S. pombe uses the sRNA amplification loops and interaction networks to nucleate CLRC at noncoding centromeric RNAs for initiating constitutive heterochromatin assembly (5, 15, 34). While in mammalian cells, as they contain highly concentrated satellite DNAs in pericentric regions, ZNF512 and ZNF512B directly bind these repeats and recruit SUV39H methyltransferases to establish H3K9me3-associated heterochromatin (18). Additionally, ZNF512 and ZNF512B also license SUV39H accumulation to LINE elements, enforcing H3K9me3 deposition and transcriptional silencing (18). A coevolutionary relationship has been proposed between retrotransposons and ZNFs in metazoans: ZNFs preferentially bind TEs to suppress their activation, while TEs serve as a central driver for ZNF diversification (35). However, this coevolution is absent in Drosophila melanogaster (35), implying the existence of a specialized transposon regulatory mechanism in dipterans. Here, we identified that, in D. melanogaster, the nascent LTR-retrotransposon RNAs serve as a platform to mediate the base-pairing between PBS and pre-tRNA/Puf68 complex, which subsequently recruit and nucleate Su(var)3-9 to initiate H3K9me3-marked pericentric heterochromatin formation (fig. S12E).

Transfer RNA–derived fragments (tRFs) represent a class of highly abundant and evolutionarily conserved small noncoding RNAs found across diverse organisms (36). Upon LTR-retrotransposon activation, tRFs perfectly anneal to the PBS of these transposons, priming reverse transcription to facilitate their replication and propagation (37–39). However, the highly conserved tRNA PBS in LTR-retrotransposons also allows tRFs to engage the RNAi pathway, enabling specific targeting, posttranscriptional silencing, and suppression of LTR-retrotransposon mobilizations (40, 41). The aforementioned tRFs base-paired with LTR-retrotransposons are predominantly derived from mature tRNAs, which are located in the cytoplasm. Nevertheless, the function of nuclear pre-tRNA in regulating LTR-retrotransposon activity remained to be elucidated. Our findings indicate that Drosophila genome harbors a high density of LTR-retrotransposons at pericentric regions, creating an ideal environment for host to harness pre-tRNA to establish constitutive heterochromatin. This specialized mechanism occurring in Drosophila effectively contributes to transposon silencing and genomic stability maintenance.

TEs have successfully colonized the genomes of nearly all eukaryotes, constituting ∼50% of the human genomic DNA (42, 43). Among them, LINE elements represent around 17%, while LTR-retrotransposons [or endogenous retroviruses (ERVs)] account for about 8%. To repress their activity in somatic cells, the human silencing hub (HUSH) complex recognizes nascent RNAs of evolutionarily young LINE-1 through its subunit Periphilin and recruits SETDB1 methyltransferase to promote of H3K9me3 deposition and establish transcriptional silencing (44–46). Although, in mouse stem and tumor cells, certain ERVs are silenced by KAP1/SETDB1-mediated heterochromatin, whether their nascent RNAs can serve as platforms to recruit repressive factors remains unknown. In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that the poly-U binding factor Puf68 licenses Su(var)3-9 to nascent LTR-retrotransposon RNAs through its associated pre-tRNA fragments, thereby initiating H3K9me3-marked heterochromatin formation (47). While the underlying mechanism remains unclear, recent study has revealed that 5′ tRFs (Gly-GGC) in mouse sperm contribute to paternal epigenetic inheritance, suppressing offspring gene driven by MERVL LTR promoter activity (47). Although detailed mechanisms may differ, the recruitment of epigenetic repressors for establishing heterochromatin via the interaction between pre-tRNA and nascent LTR-retrotransposon RNA could potentially be a recurrent theme from flies to mammals.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Fly strains and husbandry

Generally, all flies used in this study were maintained at 25°C and grown on standard agar-corn medium. The flies used for RNAi screening were maintained at 18°C as HMS-Beagle becomes most active at mobilization level at this temperature. Fly alleles used for RNAi screening were bought from Vienna Drosophila Resource Center (VDRC) and stored in Core Facility of Drosophila Resource and Technology, CEMCS, CAS. We used byn-Gal4 to specifically deplete gene expression in Drosophila hindguts. As activating su(var)3-9 and dsetdb1 RNAi using byn-Gal4 resulted in embryonic lethality, we depleted these two genes specifically at third-instar larvae and early pupae by using gal80ts. To deplete su(var)3-9 and dsetdb1 at third-instar larvae, embryos and early stages of larvae were raised at 18°C, and then, 6-day-old larvae were transferred to 29°C and wandering larvae were dissected for experiments. To deplete su(var)3-9 and dsetdb1 at early pupae, embryos and larvae were raised at 18°C, and then, 0-hour pupae (white pupae) were transferred to 29°C and 40-hour pupae were dissected for experiments. Information on the fly alleles used is provided in table S1.

Plasmid construction

We used the Counter-Selection BAC Modification Kit (GENE BRIDGES, catalog no. K002) to construct the plasmid of HMS-Beagle transposition reporter (HMS-Beagle-TR). The BAC clone containing the full length of HMS-Beagle (p[acman]-CH322-33A08) was served at the template to generate the GFP reporter. The full length of HMS-Beagle is 7059 base pairs (bp), the sequence encoding Gag is from 1529 to 2927 bp, and the sequence encoding Pol is from 2867 to 6046 bp. The GFP reporter was inserted into HMS-Beagle between 6198 and 6199 bp, which will not disrupt the coding sequence.

To construct the plasmid for puf68 and G9a silencing, the DNA fragments of shRNAs were synthesized and cloned into the Nhe I and Eco RI sites of VALIUM22. The constructs of su(var)3-9–GFP (for making transgenic fly), puf68ΔRRM2-GFP (for making transgenic fly), puf68ΔRRM3-GFP (for making transgenic fly), puf68Δ2-120-GFP (for making transgenic fly), puf68-GFP (for making knock-in fly), and GFP-g9a (for making knock-in fly) were generated by Gibson Assembly and cloned into the Eco RI site of pCaSpeR3 vector.

To construct the plasmids for producing sgRNAs, the DNA fragments containing sgRNA sequences for generating knock-in flies were synthesized and cloned into pEASY-Blunt simple U6 vector. The constructs for in vitro transfection of puf68-GFP, puf68ΔRRM1-GFP, puf68ΔRRM2-GFP, puf68ΔRRM3-GFP, puf68Δ2-129-GFP, puf68Δ306-536-GFP, and su(var)3-9–FLAG were generated by Gibson Assembly and cloned into the Eco RI and Hind III sites of pUC57 vector.

To construct the plasmids for in vitro transcription (IVT), the DNA templates for transcribing HMS-Beagle 5′ RNA [+1 to 501 nucleotides (nt)], HMS-Beagle 3′ RNA (+6562 to 7062 nt), 3S18 5′ RNA (+1 to 501 nt), and 3S18 3′ RNA (+5626 to 6126 nt) were obtained by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and then generated by Gibson Assembly and cloned into the Eco RI and Hind III sites of pUC57 vector. The constructs of plasmids to express and purify the proteins of Puf68–6× His, Puf68–RRM1–6× His, Puf68–RRM2–6× His, and Puf68–RRM3–6× His in bacteria were generated by Gibson Assembly and cloned into the Nco I site of pET28b.

All of the constructs were verified by colony PCR and Sanger sequencing. The plasmids were sent to Core Facility of Drosophila Resource and Technology, CEMCS, CAS for generating transgenic or knock-in flies. The plasmids for generating transgenic flies were site-specifically landed into fly genome at the attP2 and attP40 sites. Oligos used for constructing plasmids are listed in table S2.

Visualizing mobilization events from HMS-Beagle transposition reporter

To examine enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP)–positive cells from HMS-Beagle mobilization reporter in larval and adult hindguts, wandering larvae and 1-day-old adult flies were collected. As the GFP expression from the reporter system is driven by heat-shock promoter, to induce GFP production for visualization, larvae and flies were heat-shocked at 37°C for 45 min and then recovered at room temperature for 3 to 5 hours. Drosophila hindguts were dissected in cold phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with 4% PFA at room temperature for 10 min. Fixed hindguts were then washed three times with PBST (phosphate-buffered saline containing 0.1% Triton X-100), stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and mounted with Vectashield Mounting Medium (VWR, catalog no. 101098-042). Given that GFP is fused with a nuclear localization signal, the cells showing GFP and DAPI colocalization were considered harboring real HMS-Beagle transposition events. Larval males and females were dissected and showed similar number of GFP-positive cells in hindguts. Adult female flies were dissected to visualize GFP-positive cells. Representative images were taken by the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

LACE-seq

LACE-seq was performed according to published study (33). Immunoprecipitation was conducted using the anti-DDDDK tag antibody (2 μg per sample), and rabbit immunoglobulin G (IgG) served as the negative control.

Sample preparation

About 30 Drosophila hindguts from third-instar larvae (puf68–3× Flag–5× Myc knock-in fly) were dissected and collected. Hindguts were then placed in 60-mm cell culture dishes on ice and irradiated by UV-C light twice at 400 mJ, with a 1- to 2-min pause between two rounds of irradiations. Cross-linked hindguts were lysed with wash buffer (1× phosphate-buffered saline, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate) and sonicated using Q-Sonic bioruptor (on 20 s, off 40 s; for 2 min). After sonication, RQ1 DNase (Promega, catalog no. M6101) and SUPERase·In ribonuclease (RNase) inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. AM2696) were added to digest genomic DNA.

Beads preparation

Ten microliters of protein A magnetic beads were washed twice with bovine serum albumin (BSA)/phosphate-buffered saline solution (0.1% BSA in 1× phosphate-buffered saline) and blocked with blocking buffer (1× phosphate-buffered saline, 0.2 mg/ml glycogen, and 0.2 mg/ml BSA) at room temperature for 1 hour with gentle rotation. After blocking, the blocked beads were washed once with Na-phosphate buffer (93.2 mM Na2HPO4, 6.8 mM NaH2PO4, and 0.05% Tween 20, pH 8.0), and the beads were resuspended with Na-phosphate buffer containing 2 μg of antibody. Then, the tubes were rotated at 20 rpm for 1 hour at room temperature. After incubation, beads were washed twice with wash buffer and resuspended in 10 μl of wash buffer for each sample.

RNA immunoprecipitation

Deoxyribonuclease (DNase)–treated samples were centrifuged (7500g, 10 min, 4°C), and supernatant was transferred to the beads with antibody. The sample was incubated at 4°C for 1 hour with gentle rotation. Then, the beads were washed three times with ice-cold wash buffer, once with high-salt wash buffer (5× phosphate-buffered saline, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% NP-40, and 0.5% sodium deoxycholate), and once with PNK buffer (50 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 10 mM MgCl2, and 0.5% NP-40). MNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EN0181) was diluted to 1:5,000,000 in 1× MN reaction buffer (50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 5 mM CaCl2), and then, 10 μl of the diluted MNase was added to the beads and incubated at 37°C for 3 min. After MNase treatment, 1× PNK + EGTA buffer (50 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 20 mM EGTA, and 0.5% NP-40) was added to inactivate MNase. Then, the beads were washed once with 1× PNK + EGTA buffer, twice with wash buffer, and twice with PNK buffer. After that, samples were treated with 1 μl of FastAP alkaline phosphatase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EF0651) for RNA dephosphorylation. Samples were incubated in ThermoMixer C at 37°C for 10 min.

Library preparation

The beads were washed twice with 1× PNK + EGTA buffer, twice with PNK buffer, and twice with BSA solution (0.2 mg/ml BSA in DEPC-treated water). To ligate 3′ linker, 12 μl of water, 2 μl of 10× ligation buffer, 1 μl of 3′ linker (1 μM), 1 μl of T4 RNA ligase 2 (NEB, catalog no. M0242), and 4 μl of 50% PEG8000 were added, and then, samples were incubated in ThermoMixer C at 25°C for 2.5 hours. The beads were washed three times with PNK buffer. For reverse transcription, the beads were resuspended in 8.5 μl of nuclease-free water and 1 μl of T7-RT primer and then heated at 65°C for 5 min and snap-chilled on ice for 2 min. Then, 3 μl of 5× first-strand buffer, 0.5 μl of 0.1 M DTT, 0.5 μl of Superscript II (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 18064014), 0.5 μl of RNase inhibitor, and 1 μl of dNTP mix (10 mM) were added, and the samples were placed on thermal cycler to run the RT program (42°C for 50 min, 70°C for 15 min). Then, 2 μl of 10× Exonuclease I buffer and 3 μl of Exonuclease I mixture (NEB, catalog no. M0293) were added directly into the tube. This was mixed well, and samples were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour and 80°C for 20 min. After that, to release the first-strand cDNA from protein A beads, 3 μl of 10× RNase H buffer, 1 μl of RNase H (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EN0202), and 6 μl of water were added, and the samples were incubated on a thermal cycler at 37°C for 30 min and 65°C for 20 min. Then, the supernatant was transferred to new tubes. The streptavidin beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 650002) were washed three times with 1× B&W buffer (5 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.5, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 1 M NaCl) and then transferred to the supernatant from previous step, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After incubation, beads were washed twice with 1× B&W buffer and once with BSA solution. To ligate 3′ cDNA linker, 2 μl of 10× ligation buffer, 2 μl of ATP (10 μM, NEB, catalog no. P0756S), 10 μl of 50% PEG8000, 4.5 μl of water, 1 μl of T4 RNA ligase 1 truncated (NEB, catalog no. M0204S), and 0.5 μl of 3′ cDNA linker were added to the samples and incubated at 25°C for 24 hours in ThermoMixer C. After that, to create the dsDNA template for IVT, the DNA was preamplified by PCR with KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (KAPA Biosystems, catalog no. KK2601). The PCR program was set as follows: 98°C 3 min; 98°C 15 s, 62°C 20 s, and 72°C 30 s (16 cycles); 72°C 5 min, then hold at 12°C. After amplification, PCR products were purified by 46.8 μl of Ampure XP beads (1.8:1 ratio; Beckman Coulter, A63881) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and eluted with 13 μl of water. The eluted DNA was transferred to a new PCR tube, and 2 μl of 10× reaction buffer, 2 μl of NTP mix (25 mM), 1 μl of 0.1 M DTT, 0.5 μl of RNase inhibitor, 2 μl of T7 polymerase (NEB, catalog no. M0251) were added and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours for reverse transcription. Samples were then treated with TURBO DNase and purified by Agencourt RNA Clean beads (Beckman Coulter, catalog no. A63987). One microliter of 10 μM P7 primer was added and incubated at 65°C for 5 min. Then, 4 μl of 5× first strand buffer, 1 μl of 0.1 M DTT, 0.5 μl of Superscript II, 0.5 μl of RNase inhibitor, and 1 μl of dNTP mix (10 mM) were added to each sample. Samples were incubated on a thermal cycler, and the program (42°C 50 min; 70°C 15 min; and hold at 12°C) was run. P7 primer (1.5 μl; 10 μM), 1.5 μl of P5 index primer, 6 μl of 5× buffer, 0.6 μl of dNTP mix (10 mM), and 0.4 μl of SuperFi DNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 12351010) were added. This was mixed well, the samples were placed on thermal cycler, and the PCR program (98°C 1 min; 10 cycles of 98°C 10 s, 62°C 15 s, and 72°C 30 s; 72°C 10 min, then hold at 12°C) was run. PCR products were run on agarose gel, and the gel was cut between 130and 300 bp to purify the DNA using a gel extraction kit (QIAGEN, catalog no. 28604). The LACE-seq library was single-end sequenced using NextSeq-75SE.

LACE-seq data mapping and identification of peak, cluster, and motif were performed following previous study (33). Briefly, the adapter sequences at the 3′ end of raw reads were removed using Cutadapt (v4.1) (Cutadapt removes adapter sequences from high-throughput sequencing reads) with the following parameters: --discard-untrimmed -j 12 -a ATCTCGTATGCCGTCTTCTGCTT -q 30,0 -m 18 --max-n 0.25 --trim-n. Clean reads were first aligned to human or mouse pre-rRNA using Bowtie software (v1.2.1.1) (48), and the remaining unmapped reads were subsequently aligned to the D. melanogaster reference genome (dm6), along with TE consensus sequences and pre-tRNA sequences. For LACE-seq data mapping, two mismatches were allowed (Bowtie parameters: -v 2 -m 10 -best -strata; -v 2 -k 10 -best -strata). The genomic positions of tRNAs were retrieved from the GtRNAdb (49). To define pre-tRNA sequences, downstream 20-nt flanks of each tRNA gene were extracted with “bedtools getfasta -s” to maintain strand-specific orientation. Within this extended region, we searched for the “tetra-thymine” motif (TTTT) from the last annotated mature tRNA nucleotide to 3′ end. If this TTTT sequence was present, the 3′ end of the pre-tRNA was defined as the final T of the most 3′-proximal TTTT when present. To find the PBS sequence of LTR transposons, pairwise comparisons between pre-tRNA and the flanking region of the 5′ LTR end site (including 5 bp upstream and 30 bp downstream flanking sequences) were performed using BLASTn (v2.6.0) with the parameters “-task blastn-short -outfmt 6 -strand minus.” The sequence that best matches the tRNA is defined as the PBS.

Reverse transcription qPCR

The total RNA from Drosophila hindguts was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 15596018) following the standard protocol in this kit. Total RNA was eluted in RNase-free water (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 10977035), and RNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop. To remove any contaminated genomic DNA in RNA samples, 2 μl of Turbo DNase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. AM2239) was added to 10 μg (or less) of total RNA, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After Turbo DNase treatment, RNA was purified using RNA Clean and Concentrator-5 (Zymo Research, catalog no. R1016), and the concentration was measured by NanoDrop.

One microgram of purified RNA was used for reverse transcription using PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa, catalog no. RR047A) following the protocol from this kit. For each reaction, 1 μl of cDNA was used for quantitative PCR (qPCR) using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, catalog no. 11202ES03). qPCR reaction was performed using Roche LightCycler 96 Instrument Real Time PCR System. rp49 was used for normalizing Drosophila samples. P values were calculated from at least three independent biological replicates using a two-tailed, paired t test. The error bars on the graphs report the standard deviation for at least three independent biological replicates. Oligos used for qPCR are as follows: rp49: forward, 5′-CCGCTTCAAGGGACAGTATCTG-3′; reverse, 5′-ATCTCGCCGCAGTAAACGC-3′ and gfp: forward, 5′-CAATGGGTGTGTTCTGTTGG-3′; reverse, 5′-AGGAGGATGGCAACATTCTG-3′.

RNA-seq library preparation

Purified RNA after DNase treatment was used for preparing the RNA-seq library. These RNA libraries were prepared using TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, catalog no. 20020596) following the standard protocol in this kit. Briefly, 1 μg of total RNA was diluted in RNase-free water to a final volume of 10 μl and used for library preparation. Five microliters of RBB (rRNA binding buffer) and 5 μl of GRM (globin removal mix) were added to diluted RNA and mixed well, then the samples were placed on the thermal cycler to run the RNA denaturation program (68°C, 5 min). After denaturation, 35 μl of RRB (rRNA removal beads) was mixed with each sample to remove rRNA, then the RNA was transferred to a new tube. Next, 99 μl of RNAClean XP beads (Beckman Coulter, catalog no. A63987) was added to purify RNA. After purification, RNA was eluted with 8.5 μl of EPH (elute, prime, fragment high mix) and then fragmented by running the Elution 2-Frag-Prime program (94°C, 9 min) on the thermal cycler. After fragmentation, 7.2 μl of FSA (First Strand Synthesis Act D Mix) and 0.8 μl of SuperScript III (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 18080044) were added to each sample and then placed on the thermal cycler, and the Synthesize 1st Strand Program (25°C, 10 min; 42°C, 15 min; 70°C, 15 min) was run. After synthesizing first-strand cDNA, 5 μl of RBS (resuspension buffer), 5 μl of diluted CTE (end repair control, 1:50 dilution in RBS), and 20 μl of SMM (second strand marking master mix) were added to each sample and mixed well. The samples were placed on the thermal cycler, and the temperature was held at 16°C for 1 hour to synthesize second-strand cDNA. To purify DNA, 90 μl of RNAClean XP beads was added to each sample and then eluted with 17.5 μl of RSB, and 15 μl of DNA was transferred to a new tube (−20°C). The next day, DNA was thawed on bench, and 2.5 μl of diluted CTA (A-tailing control, 1:100 diluted in RBS) and 12.5 μl of ATL (A-tailing mix) were added to each sample. This was mixed well and placed on the thermal cycler (37°C, 30 min; 70°C, 5 min) to adenylate the 3′ end. After adenylation, 2.5 μl of diluted CTL (ligation control), 2.5 μl of LIG (ligation mix), and 1 μl of RNA adapters (10 μM) were added to each tube. Then, the samples were placed on thermal cycler, and the LIG program (30°C, 10 min) was run. After ligation, 5 μl of STL (stop ligation buffer) was added to stop ligation. Then, DNA was purified twice with two rounds of RNAClean XP beads (round 1: 42 μl; round 2: 50 μl), and the DNA was eluted with 52.5 and 22.5 μl of RSB, respectively. A total of 19 μl of second round eluted DNA was transferred to a new tube, and 5 μl of PPC (PCR primer cocktail), 25 μl of PMM (PCR master mix), and 1 μl of index were added. This was mixed well, the samples were placed on the thermal cycler, and the PCR program (98°C 30 s; 15 cycles of 98°C 10 s, 60°C 30 s, and 72°C 30 s; 72°C 5 min) was run to amplify DNA fragments. Enriched DNA fragments were purified using 50 μl of RNAClean XP beads and eluted with 20 μl of RSB, and 19 μl was then transferred to a new tube. The libraries were sent to Novogene and Sequanta for quality control and sequencing.

The expression of transposons was analyzed and quantified using piPipes (50) by family and SQuIRE (v0.9.9.92) (51) “Map” and “Count” function by locus. Sequencing reads were initially trimmed using Cutadapt with parameters -e 0.1 -O 3 -m 55 --quality-cutoff 25, and then, the adapter-trimmed FASTQ files were used as input for both tools. SQuIRE calls STAR (52) with options suited for TE analysis, quantification of reads per gene, and estimation of reads per TEs in a locus-specific manner. Differential expression analysis was done by DESeq2 (53) using the raw count matrix of TEs from SQuIRE.

RNA FISH

Stellaris RNA FISH probe sets for Copia was designed and purchased from LGC Bioresearch Technologies, and FISH experiment was performed following the standard protocol from this kit. Briefly, four to six hindguts from third-instar larvae and 4-day-old pupae were dissected in cold phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. After fixation, hindguts were washed with phosphate-buffered saline for three times to remove formaldehyde. Hindguts were then immersed in 70% (v/v) ethanol at 4°C for 8 hours. After that, hindguts were washed once with 1 ml of prepared Wash Buffer, containing 20% Wash Buffer A (LGC Biosearch Tech, catalog no. SMF-WA1-60), 10% formamide, and 70% nuclease-free water, rotating at room temperature for 5 min. Then, samples were incubated with 50 μl of Hybridization Buffer (LGC Biosearch Tech, catalog no. SMF-HB1-10) containing probe sets (125 nM) and 10% formamide and were placed in dark at 37°C overnight. The next day, hindguts were washed twice with prepared Wash Buffer for 30 min at 37°C and once with Wash Buffer B (LGC Biosearch Tech, catalog no. SMF-WB1-20) for 5 min at room temperature. Larval and pupal hindguts were then mounted with Vectashield Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories Inc., catalog no. 101098-042). Images were taken by the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Immunostaining

Four to six hindguts from third-instar larvae, 3- to 5-day-old pupae and adults were dissected in cold phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with 4% PFA in Buffer A (15 mM PIPES, pH 7.4, 80 mM KCl, 20 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 0.5 mM spermidine, and 0.15 mM spermine) for 10 min at room temperature. Hindguts were then washed twice with BAT buffer (Buffer A and 0.1% Triton X-100), 15 min each time. After washing, hindguts were blocked with BAT buffer containing 10% Normal Donkey Serum (Absin, catalog no. abs935) at room temperature for 1 hour. Primary antibodies were diluted with NDS-BAT buffer, and samples were incubated with diluted primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. The next day, hindguts were washed with BAT buffer containing 2% BSA (Biosearch, catalog no. B-1000-100) for three times, 30 min each time. Then, hindguts were incubated with secondary antibody (1:400) at 4°C overnight. The next day, hindguts were washed once with BAT buffer for 30 min, once with BAT buffer containing DAPI for 30 min, and another once with BAT buffer for 30 min. The hindguts were mounted from third-instar larvae and pupae with Vectashield Mounting Medium, and the images were taken with the Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope. The antibodies used for immunostaining are listed in table S3.

ChIP-seq library preparation and analysis

Around 400 third-instar larval hindguts were dissected and used for ChIP-seq library preparation. Hindguts were dissected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline and transferred to 500 μl of cold phosphate-buffered saline. A total of 63.4 μl of 16% PFA was added to fix the hindguts, and the samples were rotated at room temperature for 10 min. After fixation, 29.7 μl of 2.5 M glycine was added to a final concentration of 125 mM and incubated at room temperature with rotation for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and hindguts were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline and once with Sonication Buffer (50 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, and 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktail). After washing, 250 μl of Sonication Buffer was added to each sample, and samples were sonicated using Q-Sonic bioruptor for four rounds (each round: duration time of 15 min; on 20 s, off 40 s), shearing genomic DNA to an average fragment size of 200 to 1000 bp. To keep the samples cool, after each round of sonication, wait for a few of min and let the water in Q-Sonic bioruptor cool to 4°C. After sonication, the samples were centrifuged at 16,000g for 15 min at 4°C, and then, the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. The sonicated lysate was diluted with nine volumes of 1× Dilution Buffer (16.7 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 1.1 mM EDTA, 1.1% Triton X-100, and 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktail), and 80 μl of diluted lysate was saved as Input.

Equal volume of Dynabeads Protein A (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 10002D) and Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 10004D) was mixed, and 15 μl of mixed beads was used for each immunoprecipitation. Before using the beads, these were washed twice with PBST (0.02% Tween 20) and resuspended in 600 μl of PBST. One microgram of H3K9me3 antibody (Active Motif, catalog no. ab39161) or GFP antibody (Abcam, catalog no. ab290) was added to each tube, and the antibody with beads was incubated at 4°C with gentle rotation for at least 2 hours. After antibody incubation, beads were washed twice with PBST, diluted hindguts lysate was added, and samples were incubated at 4°C with gentle rotation overnight.

The next day, the samples were washed twice with Wash Buffer A (20 mM tris HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, and 1% Triton X-100), twice with ice-cold Wash Buffer B (20 mM tris HCl, pH 8.0, 2 mM EDTA, 500 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, and 1% Triton X-100), Wash Buffer C (10 mM tris HCl, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, 0.25 M LiCl, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 1% NP-40), and TE Buffer (10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, and 1 mM EDTA) sequentially. Eighty microliters of Direct Elution Buffer (10 mM tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA, and 0.5% SDS) was added to each IP samples. Three microliters of 5 M NaCl was added into saved Input samples. The IP and Input samples were incubated at 65°C heat block for 6 hours or overnight for de-crosslinking. After de-crosslinking, 0.5 ng of mouse gDNA was added to IP sample as spike-in. One microliter of 10 mg/ml RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. EN0531) was added to each sample and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. A total of 2.5 μl of 20 mg/ml Proteinase K was added to each sample and incubated at 55°C for 2 hours. DNA was purified using phenol/chloroform and precipitated using ethanol. DNA was then eluted with 10 μl of nuclease-free water, and the concentration was measured by Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. Q32851).

ChIP-seq libraries were prepared using TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme, catalog no. TD502/TD503) following the standard protocol in this kit. Briefly, 5 ng/1 ng of purified DNA from the last step was diluted to a final volume of 11 μl and used for DNA library preparation. Four μl of TTBL (TruePrep Tagment Buffer L) and 5 μl of TTE (TruePrep Tagment Enzyme) Mix V5/V1 were added to each sample. Samples were mixed well and incubated at 55°C for 10 min. After incubation, 5 μl of TS (Terminate Solution) buffer was added to each sample, and the samples were placed at room temperature for 5 min to stop the reaction. TAB (TruePrep Amplify Buffer), TAE (TruePrep Amplify Enzyme), and indexes were to each sample, and the tubes were placed on thermal cycle to amplify the DNA fragments. The libraries were purified by RNAClean XP beads and sent to Novogene and Sequanta for quality control and sequencing. The ChIP-seq library was paired-end sequenced using Nova-PE150.

Data analysis

Adapters were trimmed from ChIP-seq reads via Cutadapt with parameters -e 0.1 -O 3 -m 55 --quality-cutoff 25, and then using bowtie2 (v2.3.1) (54) with default parameters aligned reads to dm6 and TE sequences. High-quality alignments were kept into sorted.bam files via samtools (v1.9) (55) “view” function with parameters -h -b -f 3 -F 12 -q 20 -F 256 and “sort” function. Duplications were removed using Picard (v2.27.5) (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) MarkDuplicates and the .bam files were further converted to .bed files using the bedtools (v2.25.0) (56) bamToBed command. To perform spike-in normalization, the proportion of reads mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) was calculated for each library. A spike-in scaling factor was then derived based on the assumption that the spike-in proportion should remain constant across libraries. The spike-in scaling factor was used to adjust reads per million (RPM). Last, the BedGraph files were generated using bedtools genomecov, normalized by adjusted RPM, and then converted into .bigWig files using bedGraphToBigWig from UCSC utilities (57).

ATAC-seq

Drosophila 3-day-old pupal hindguts were used for ATAC-seq. Briefly, the hindguts were dissected in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline within 1 hour. Forty hindguts per sample were used for ATAC-seq library preparation using Hyperactive ATAC-Seq Library Prep Kit (Vazyme, catalog no. TD711). Hindguts were washed twice with ice-cold TW Buffer (Tagment DNA and Wash Buffer). Then, ice-cold lysis buffer (resuspension buffer, 10% NP-40, 10% Tween 20, and 1% digitonin) was added to resuspend hindguts, and the samples were kept on ice for 5 min. The samples were centrifuged at 500g for 10 min at 4°C to collect cells. Supernatant was discarded and ice-cold Fragment Mixture (16.5 μl of TW Buffer, 0.5 μl of 10% Tween 20, 0.5 μl of 1% digitonin, 18.5 μl of nuclease-free ddH2O, 10 μl of 5× TTBL, and 4 μl of TTE Mix V50) was added. This was mixed well, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. Stop Buffer was added to stop fragmentation. Fragmented DNA was purified by ATAC DNA Extract Beads. Five microliters of N5XX index, 5 μl of N7XX index (Vazyme, catalog no. TD202), and 30 μl of 2× CAM were added to DNA samples, these were placed on the thermal cycler, and the the PCR program was run (72°C 3 min; 95°C 3 min; 16 cycles of 98°C 10 s, 60°C 5 s; 72°C 1 min; 4°C hold). The libraries were purified by RNAClean XP beads and sent to Sequanta for quality control and sequencing.

In vivo

Zero to 24 hours of su(var)3-9–GFP transgenic embryos were collected and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. One milliliter of 20% bleach solution was added, and the samples were shaken for 5 min to remove the vitelline membrane of embryos. Lysis buffer (600 μl) was added, the embryos were homogenized to make cells fully accessible to the lysis buffer, and the samples were then placed on ice for 30 min.

In vitro

HEK293T cell line was purchased from the Core Facility for Stem Cell Research, CEMCS, CAS (catalog no. SCSP-502). HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11960500CP) supplemented with 10% FBS (Ausbian, catalog no. WS500T), 1% Glutamax (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 35050061), 1% Sodium Pyruvate 100 mM Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 11360070) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 15140122). Cells were placed at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. The plasmids that produce proper proteins were transfected to HEK293T cells by Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. L3000008) following the manufacturer’s instruction. Plasmids and lipo3000 reagents were diluted in Opti-MEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific–Invitrogen, catalog no. 31985070). Forty eight hours posttransfection, the transfected cells were harvested by digesting them with trypsin. Cells were then washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline. The phosphate-buffered saline was discarded, and 600 μl of lysis buffer was added. This was mixed well, and the samples were placed on ice for 30 min.

After lysing, the samples were spun at 16,000g for 15 min at 4°C. The pellets were discarded, and the supernatant was transferred to a new tube. Five percent of the lysate was saved and 6× SDS protein loading buffer was added. The samples were placed on 95°C heat block and boiled for 10 min, then stored at −20°C as Input. The rest of the lysate was then transferred to the tubes containing 10 μl of anti-GFP Magarose Beads (AlpalifeBio, catalog no. KTSM1366) or Protein A/G beads with FLAG antibody. Samples were incubated at 4°C with gentle rotation overnight. The next day, IP samples were washed five times with ice-cold cell lysis buffer and 2× SDS protein loading buffer was added to samples, and then, the IP samples were boiled at 95°C for 10 min.

Fractionation of the cell nucleus

To examine the subcellular localization of full-length Puf68 and truncated mutants in nuclear, 0 to 24 hours of puf68-GFP knock-in embryos and puf68ΔRRM2-GFP, puf68ΔRRM3-GFP, puf68Δ2-120-GFP transgenic embryos were collected and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline. Bleach solution (20%) was used to remove the vitelline membrane of embryos. Nuclei extraction buffer (20 mM Hepes-KOH, pH 7.9, 10 mM KCl, 0.5 mM spermidine, 0.1% TritonX-100, 20% glycerol, and 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktail) was added to embryos. Embryos were homogenized and centrifuged at 600g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellets (nuclei) were dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (supplemented with 1× proteinase inhibitor cocktail). The nuclei was sonicated using Q-Sonic bioruptor (on 20 s, off 40 s; for 3 min) and centrifuged at 16,000g for 10 min at 4°C. The proteins of the supernatant were the soluble nuclear fraction. The pellets were chromatin fractions. Six times SDS protein loading buffer was added to each fraction and boiled for 10 min. Samples were run on 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels, and the remaining steps follow the Western blot.

Protein expression and purification

Puf68 fused with a C-terminal 6× His tag was cloned into pET-28b expression vectors and transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta2 (DE3). One hundred microliters of of E. coli Rosetta2 was inoculated in 1 liter of LB medium and cultured at 37°C with shaking until OD value reaching to 0.6 to 0.8. IPTG was added to a final concentration of 0.3 mM, and E. coli was then transferred to an 18°C incubator and cultured for 20 hours with shacking. After inducing Puf68 expression, the bacteria were harvested by centrifuging the samples at 5000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C. Supernatant was discarded, and the pelleted E. coli was resuspended with 20 ml of ice-cold lysis buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.3 M NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 10% glycerol, and 10 mM imidazole). E. coli was then sonicated and centrifuged at 20000 rpm for 1 hour at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred and incubated with 1 ml of Ni Smart Beads (Smart-Lifesciences) for 1 hour at 4°C. After incubation, the beads were washed with 100 ml of wash buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.3 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 20 mM imidazole). Puf68 protein was then eluted with 5 to 10 ml of Elution Buffer (20 mM tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 0.3 M NaCl, 10% glycerol, and 500 mM imidazole). The flowthrough fraction was concentrated using Amicon Ultra 30 kDa centrifugal filters (Millipore). Protein concentration was quantified by Bradford assay, and the A280 was measured using NanoDrop One. The purity of expressed Puf68 proteins was examined by 12% SDS-PAGE gels and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue.

CUT&Tag

Drosophila S2 cells were cultured in Schneider’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 21720001) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Absin, catalog no. abs935) at 25°C. Two hundred thousand S2 cells were collected for CUT&Tag library preparation using the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (Vazyme, catalog no. TD904) following standard protocol in this kit. Briefly, S2 cells were washed once with phosphate-buffered saline and fixed with Enhancer Buffer. Termination Buffer was added to stop the reaction of cross-linking, then the samples were treated with permeabilization buffer. Concanavalin A–coated Magnetic Beads Pro (ConA Beads Pro) were prepared by washing them with Binding Buffer. Cells were then incubated with the prewashed ConA beads. After that, the samples were incubated with primary antibody (Anti-Puf68 and Anti-IgG) at 4°C overnight. The next day, samples were incubated with secondary antibody (Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG H&L, 1:100 dilution) for 1 hour at room temperature. After incubation, samples were washed twice with Dig-Wash Buffer (Wash Buffer containing 5% digitonin). After washing, the samples were incubated with pA/G-Tnp Pro (Hyperactive pA/G-Transposon Pro) for 1 hour at room temperature and then washed with Dig-300 Buffer. Then, TruePrep Tagment Buffer L (TTBL) was added, and the samples were incubated at 37°C for 60 min. Ten percent SDS was added into the samples to stop the tagmentation reaction. Samples were then incubated at 80°C for 2 hours. DNA was extracted using DNA Extract Beads Pro, and libraries were amplified with Amplification Mix (2× CAM, Illumina Primer 1, and Illumina Primer 2). Data analysis

Adapters were trimmed from Cut&Tag-seq reads via Cutadapt with parameters -e 0.1 -O 3 -m 55 --quality-cutoff 25 and then, using bowtie2 (v2.3.1) with default parameters, aligned reads to dm6 and TE sequences. High-quality alignments were kept into sorted.bam files via samtools (v1.9) “view” function with parameters -h -b -f 3 -F 12 -F 256 and “sort” function. Duplications were removed using Picard (v2.27.5) MarkDuplicates, and the .bam files were further converted to .bed files using the bedtools (v2.25.0) bamToBed command. BedGraph files were generated by bedtools genomecov, normalized by RPM, and then converted to bigWig files using bedGraphToBigWig. Normalized coverage of each TE was resampled into 100 bins to obtain class-level mean profiles. Treatment-control differences were tested using a curve-based KS-like statistic, defined as the maximum deviation between cumulative mean profiles, with significance estimated from 10,000 random group-label permutations (right-tailed).
