Plan for Speed: Dilated Scheduling for Masked Diffusion Language Models
Omer Luxembourg, Haim Permuter, Eliya Nachmani

Abstract
Masked diffusion language models (MDLM) have shown strong promise for non-autoregressive text generation, yet existing samplers act as implicit planners, selecting tokens to unmask via denoiser confidence or entropy scores. Such heuristics falter under parallel unmasking - they ignore pairwise interactions between tokens and cannot account for dependencies when unmasking multiple positions at once, limiting their inference time to traditional auto-regressive (AR) models. We introduce the Dilated Unmasking Scheduler (DUS), an inference‐only, planner‐model‐free method that requires no additional training. DUS leverages a first‐order Markov assumption to partition sequence positions into dilation‐based groups of non‐adjacent tokens, enabling independent, parallel unmasking steps that respect local context that minimizes an upper bound on the joint entropy of each iteration step. Unlike semi‐AR block approaches (e.g., LLADA and Dream) that still invoke the denoiser per block, DUS reduces the number of denoiser calls to O ⁡ ( log ⁡ B ) O\bigl(\log B\bigr) per generation block - yielding substantial speedup over the O ⁡ ( B ) O(B) run time of state-of-the-art diffusion models, where B B is the block size in the semi-AR inference process. In experiments on math (GSM8K) and code completion (Humaneval, MBPP) benchmarks - domains suited to non-ordinal generation - DUS improves scores over parallel confidence‐based planner, without modifying the underlying denoiser. DUS offers a lightweight, budget‐aware approach to efficient, high‐quality text generation, paving the way to unlock the true capabilities of MDLMs.

Introduction

Diffusion-based language models have emerged as a promising alternative to traditional AR large language models, offering potential advantages in parallel generation and controllability
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
;
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
. In a discrete diffusion paradigm for text, generation is formulated as an iterative denoising process: starting from a fully noised (e.g., masked) sequence, the model gradually reconstructs the original text over multiple timesteps. This approach enables any-order generation, in contrast to the strictly left-to-right dependency of AR models, and recent results show that sufficiently large diffusion LMs can achieve performance on par with AR LLMs of similar scale. For example, the open-source Dream 7B diffusion model matches the capability of comparable AR models
(
Ye et al. 2025b
;
Ye et al. 2025a
)
, and LLaDA (8B)
(
Nie et al. 2025b
)
has demonstrated competitive language understanding and generation quality against AR baselines. These advances establish masked discrete diffusion as a viable paradigm for language modeling. However, a critical challenge remains: inference speed and unmasking planners.
Diffusion LMs typically perform one denoising step per token - so generating a sequence of length
N
requires
N
denoising passes to reach reasonable quality, making naive decoding prohibitively slow. Cutting this sampling overhead without sacrificing output fidelity is therefore essential for practical diffusion-based language modeling.

Several inference strategies have been proposed to accelerate or improve the diffusion decoding process, but each comes with limitations
(
Liu et al. 2025
;
Peng et al. 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. Confidence-based unmasking heuristics, for instance, attempt to hasten convergence by greedily unveiling tokens in order of the model’s confidence. At each iteration, the model identifies the tokens for which it is most certain of the prediction and fixes (unmasks) them, then continues denoising the remaining masked positions. However, it may suffer from error propagation: if the model becomes overconfident in an incorrect token and unmasks it too early, it can introduce irreversible mistakes that degrade the final output. Moreover, purely confidence-driven schedules are myopic, lacking a global plan; they do not guarantee optimal ordering.

Another line of work introduces semi-autoregressive diffusion strategies that interpolate between fully parallel diffusion and sequential generation. Notably, block diffusion models
(
Arriola et al. 2025
)
divide the sequence into blocks and generate these blocks in a sequential manner (one block after another) while employing parallel diffusion within each block. This blockwise diffusion paradigm leverages the power of diffusion to fill in multiple tokens at once (within a block) and uses autoregressive context across blocks to handle flexible-length text and maintain coherence. Such models have achieved state-of-the-art likelihoods among diffusion LMs and can handle arbitrary-length generation beyond fixed token positions. LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025b
)
can be seen as a related approach: it is a masked diffusion LM trained at scale, and while its core generation process is parallel masked token prediction, it integrates an unmasking policy module to determine the order in which tokens should be revealed during decoding. In practice, this introduces a sequential decision-making component (a form of planning or blockwise reveal) into the diffusion sampling loop. These semi-autoregressive or planner-based techniques indeed improve generation quality and efficiency – for example, using caching can reduce redundant computations
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
. Nonetheless, these strategies partly sacrifice the parallelism of diffusion and add complexity to the inference pipeline. Block diffusion still requires multiple autoregressive steps (proportional to the number of blocks, e.g. generating a long sequence in chunks), and methods like Dream’s policy module effectively rely on the denoiser’s training or on an external decision mechanism to guide sampling, which is not a dedicated unmasking policy.

A more recent thread of research focuses on adaptive scheduling of diffusion steps to accelerate inference. Conventional discrete diffusion uses a fixed schedule of
T
timesteps (often on the order of tens or hundreds) to gradually denoise the sequence.
Park et al. 2024
highlight that we need not use all intermediate noising levels linearly; one can “jump” across diffusion steps to speed up sampling. Their Jump Your Steps (JYS) approach optimizes the selection of a non-uniform, reduced set of timesteps, effectively skipping certain denoising steps while controlling the approximation error introduced by these jumps. By minimizing what they call Compounding Decoding Error (CDE), JYS significantly accelerates generation, achieving faster sampling in discrete diffusion models. This result underlines an important insight: with a smart schedule, diffusion models can sample in far fewer steps, but care must be taken to avoid a loss in quality. However, JYT is given for log-likelihood ratios as in SEDD
(
Lou, Meng, and Ermon 2024
)
which has score-based loss compared to MDLMs’ cross-entropy. Moreover, it does not guarantee to avoid unmasking high correlated tokens together.
In a similar vein,
Peng et al. 2025
address the problem from a planning perspective. They show that the order in which masked tokens are revealed can be optimized by treating it as a planning problem and introduce
Path Planning
(P2), which uses a guiding model (like a pre-trained BERT or even the diffusion model itself) to decide which tokens to unmask next. By expanding the evidence lower bound to include such a planner, they generalize existing sampling strategies (including random, confidence-based, and others) and report improved generation performance across various domains. P2 lays the baseline for MDLM planner-based approach, but does not suggest a method to increase inference complexity.

In summary, current approaches to improve diffusion LM inference either introduce heuristics which can be suboptimal, impose a token-by-token unmasking, undoing some advantages of parallel generation capabilities of diffusion models, and some require an explicit planner (adding overhead
(
Peng et al. 2025
)
or training complexity
(
Liu et al. 2025
)
). There remains a clear need for an inference strategy that retains the simplicity and parallelism of the diffusion process, avoids heavy additional machinery, and still substantially reduces sampling time without sacrificing drastically output quality.

In this work, we introduce the
Dilated Unmasking Strategy
(DUS), a purely inference-time planner method for MDLMs that requires no changes to the model or training procedure. By leveraging a Markovian assumption, we unmask tokens in a deterministic, coarse-to-fine (“dilated”) pattern - starting from a sparse subset and roughly doubling the revealed tokens each iteration - achieving
\mathcal{O}(\log B)
denoising steps for
B
tokens without sacrificing output quality. In summary, the main contributions of this work are as follows:

•

Inference-Only, Planner-Free Decoding:

Drop-in method with zero modifications to model architecture or training.

•

Deterministic Dilated Schedule:

Coarse-to-fine unmasking under a Markovian assumption - no confidence scores or learned policies required.

•

Logarithmic Iterations:

Generation in
\approx\log_{2}B
denoising steps vs.
\mathcal{O}(B)
for traditional token-by-token methods.

•

Theoretical Quality Improvement:

While reducing denoising steps typically trades off quality, we prove that our method maintains or improves output compared to baselines at the same step budget.

•

Empirical Validation:

Comparable or superior performance to strong samplers (e.g. LLaDA, Dream) on complex language tasks, with dramatically fewer diffusion iterations - faster inference.

Method

In this section, we present our new dilated unmasking scheduling (DUS) for masked diffusion language models.
Masked diffusion samplers consist of two core components:

•

Denoiser.
A pretrained network
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
that, at each reverse diffusion step, takes a partially masked sequence and predicts the values of all masked tokens based on the current unmasked context
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Austin et al. 2023
)
.

•

Planner.
A strategy
\mathcal{P}_{t}
that, at each iteration
t
, selects which positions in the sequence to unmask next. Common planners include ancestral diffusion sampling which it’s selection is random, confidence‐based selection, entropy‐based selection, and pre-trained planners such as in
(
Peng et al. 2025
;
Liu et al. 2025
)
.

Most large-scale discrete diffusion models perform inference in a semi-autoregressive, block-wise fashion: the sequence is partitioned into consecutive blocks of size
B
, and each block is unmasked and denoised before moving to the next
(
Nie et al. 2025b
)
.
Block diffusion training masks entire spans of length
B
and learns to recover them jointly, yielding stronger local coherence, while other method train on unmasking the whole sequence.

Our DUS method slots in as a
inference-only
planner. It requires no retraining of the denoiser and can serve as a drop-in planning strategy for any standard discrete diffusion model - MDLM
(
Sahoo et al. 2024
)
, MD4
(
Shi et al. 2025
)
, LLaDA
(
Nie et al. 2025b
)
, Dream
(
Ye et al. 2025b
;
Ye et al. 2025a
)
, and others - reducing per-block complexity from
O(B)
to
O(\log B)
.

Notation

Let
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{K}\}
be a sequence of random variables forming a stationary, ergodic, first-order Markov chain with fast-mixing properties. At each decoding iteration step
t
, a masked version of the sequence is denoted by
\mathcal{M}=\{M_{1},\dots,M_{K}\}
, where
M_{i}
replaced by
X_{i}
if token
i
has been unmasked, and
M_{i}=\texttt{[MASK]}
otherwise. Let
\mathcal{S}_{t}\subset\{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{M}\}
denote the state at denoiser iteration
t
, i.e., the collection of all currently known (unmasked) tokens and remaining masked positions.

Define a
planner

\mathcal{P}_{t}
that chooses
k\leq K
candidate indices
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subset\{1,\dots,K\}
of masked tokens to be unmasked at iteration
t
, and a
denoiser

\mathcal{D}
that maps each of those masked tokens
M_{i_{j}},j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
to a prediction of
X_{i_{j}}
conditioned on the current state:

\hat{X}_{i_{j}}=\mathcal{D}(M_{i_{j}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t}).

(1)

Our objective is to minimize the joint entropy of the full sequence, i.e.,

H(\mathcal{X}).

(2)

For our case, with conditioned generation on a prompt, which is an initial state
S_{0}
, our minimization objective is
H(\mathcal{X}\mid S_{0})
.

Since the denoiser is trained in each diffusion reverse iteration to recover masked tokens (by variated rate) to minimize the ELBO loss, the denoising step during inference struggles to unmask the whole sequence at once. Hence, unmasking the sequence is done in multiple iterations
(
Campbell et al. 2022
;
Nie et al. 2025a
)
.
Recent large-scale diffusion LLMs extend this by partitioning the sequence into semi-AR blocks of length
B\leq K
, applying several denoiser iterations within each block. Accordingly, at iteration
t
we define the state
S_{t}
to include (1) all previously unmasked blocks, (2) the current block (with both masked and unmasked tokens), and (3) the remaining masked blocks. Under this formulation, inference reduces to minimizing the joint conditional entropy

H(X_{b},\dots,X_{b+B}\mid S_{t})
, where
\{b,\dots,b+B\}
are indices in the current inferred block, and parallel
k
-tokens unmasking seek to minimize the joint entropy gain

H(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{k}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t}),

(3)

where
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{b,\dots,b+B\}
. Due to the nature of the model, each token is unmasked independently of the other
k-1
, hence the term we practically minimize is

\sum_{j=1}^{k}H(X_{i_{j}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t}).

(4)

The following sections compares multiple planner strategies including our new method and evaluates their effectiveness in minimizing the block conditional entropy across iterations of unmasking.

Self-Planners Denoiser Guided

Next both planners are referred as self-planners - using the denoiser’s signal to plan the unmasking token and denoise the tokens in order to chose one.

•

Self-Planner by Confidence

Define the output probabilities of the denoiser
\mathcal{D}
as
p_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{i_{j}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})
for the candidate token at sequence index
i_{j}
given state
\mathcal{S}_{t}
.
At each timestep
t
, the planner selects the top-
k
tokens according to the denoiser’s confidence, i.e.
\arg\max p_{\mathcal{D}}(X_{i_{j}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t})
for
j\in\{1,\dots,k\}
. This strategy tends to select tokens that are predictable, often resulting in unmasking tokens that are highly correlated with previously unmasked tokens (e.g., adjacent in the sequence), leading to redundancy and higher entropy gain:

\displaystyle H(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{k}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}H(X_{i_{j}}\mid X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{j-1}},\mathcal{S}_{t})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}H(X_{i_{j}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t}),

(5)

where (a) valid since conditioning reduces entropy. Clearly, self-planner by confidence has a lower bound on the entropy gain, which is the joint entropy, while there is no guarantee for the bound equality.

•

Self-Planner by Entropy

This variant selects the
k
tokens with the minimal individual conditional entropy
H(X_{i_{j}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t})
. While it avoids highly uncertain positions, it does not account for mutual information between candidates, leaving again the entropy gain suboptimal.

Dilated Unmasking Scheduler as Predefined Planner

We introduce our Dilated Unmasking Scheduler - DUS, a deterministic planner that, for a block of size
B
with exponent base
a
, predefines an unmasking strategy fixing which tokens are revealed at each of the
R=\lceil\log_{a}B\rceil
iterations. Let

R=\bigl\lceil\log_{a}B\bigr\rceil,\quad s_{t}=\bigl\lfloor\tfrac{B}{a^{t}}\bigr\rfloor,\quad t=1,\dots,R.

(6)

Hence we define the iterative planner
\mathcal{P}_{t}
and the incremental unmasked group at each iteration
\mathcal{U}_{t}
as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{0}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bigl\{\,k\in\{1,\dots,B\}\setminus\mathcal{U}_{t-1}\mid(k-1)\bmod s_{t}=0\bigr\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{t-1}\,\cup\,\mathcal{P}_{t}.

If
|\mathcal{P}_{R}|<|\mathcal{P}_{R-1}|
, merge
\mathcal{P}_{R}
into
\mathcal{P}_{R-1}
to balance coverage. This completes in
R\approx\lceil\log_{a}B\rceil
steps, where
|\mathcal{P}_{t}|=\max(a,a^{t-1})
is an incremental series.

For example, let
B=8
and
a=2
, then
R=3,s_{1}=4,s_{2}=2,s_{3}=1
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\,k\mid(k-1)\bmod 4=0\}=\{1,5\},\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{\,k\notin\{1,5\}\mid(k-1)\bmod 2=0\}=\{3,7\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{3}

\displaystyle=\{\,k\notin\{1,5,3,7\}\mid(k-1)\bmod 1=0\}=\{2,4,6,8\}.

We use the following lemma to prove that in each iteration in our method, the mutual information (MI) between two tokens is low.

Lemma 1

Define
\rho
as the Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi maximal correlation coefficient between random variables
(
Rényi 1959
)

\rho=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(P_{X_{i_{n}}}),g\in L^{2}(P_{X_{i_{m}}});\\
\mathbb{E}[f(X_{i_{n}})]=0,\ \mathbb{E}[f(X_{i_{n}})^{2}]=1;\\
\mathbb{E}[g(X_{i_{m}})]=0,\ \mathbb{E}[g(X_{i_{m}})^{2}]=1\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\bigl[f(X_{i_{n}})\,g(X_{i_{m}})\bigr].

(7)

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a first-order Markov chain with fast-mixing. Define
d=|i_{m}-i_{n}|
as the index distance between two random variables from the sequence,
X_{i_{n}},X_{i_{m}}
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
, then exist
\delta_{d}>0
such that

I\bigl(X_{i_{m}};X_{i_{n}}\bigr)\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\bigl(1-\rho^{2d}\bigr)\leq\delta_{d}<\varepsilon.

(8)

Proof:
Under fast-mixing assumption,
\rho<1
and decays exponentially
(
Asoodeh, Alajaji, and Linder 2016
)

\rho^{d}\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{as}\quad d\to\infty.

(9)

Moreover, the MI between
X_{i_{n}},X_{i_{m}}
s.t.
d=|i_{m}-i_{n}|
satisfies

I\bigl(X_{i_{m}};X_{i_{n}}\bigr)\;\leq\;-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\!\bigl(1-\rho^{2d}\bigr)\;=\;O\!\bigl(\rho^{2d}\bigr).

(10)

Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
\rho<1
, there exists some integer
D
s.t.
\forall d\geq D
and
k\geq 1
that satisfies

\displaystyle I\bigl(X_{i_{m}};X_{i_{n}}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\bigl(1-\rho^{2d}\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho^{2d}}{2\bigl(1-\rho^{2}\bigr)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{k}

since from (
10
) , the MI decays.
In conclusion, as the distance
d=|i_{m}-i_{n}|
grows,
I(X_{i_{m}};X_{i_{n}})\to 0
.
\square

Without loss of generality, let
i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}
be the indices of the
k
chosen tokens by DUS planner, and
\forall m,n\in\{1,\dots k|m\neq n\},d\leq|i_{m}-i_{n}|
. The joint conditional entropy decomposes to

\displaystyle H(X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{k}}\mid\mathcal{S}_{t})=

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}H(X_{i_{j}}|X_{i_{1}},\dots,X_{i_{j-1}},\mathcal{S}_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big[H(X_{i_{j}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})-I(X_{i_{j}};X_{i_{1}},\dots X_{i_{j-1}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big[H(X_{i_{j}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})-I(X_{i_{j}};X_{i_{1}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})\big]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\big[H(X_{i_{j}}|\mathcal{S}_{t})\big]-\varepsilon\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}H(X_{i_{j}}|\mathcal{S}_{t}).

(11)

Transition (a) follows from the first-order Markov chain, and (b) justified because our dilated pattern maximizes distances, hence, by Lemma
1
, each mutual-information term is negligible.
Overall, we have shown that DUS minimizes the independent conditional entropy and attains the joint entropy bound, whereas other planners are unbounded from below.
This scheduled dilation planner thus integrates three core features:

•

Maintained Spacing Across Iterations:

By preserving the same sparse dilation at every round, we guarantee that distant‐token spacing - and thus the bound in (
8
) holds throughout all
R
iterations, unlike schedulers that cluster adjacent tokens and break the independence assumption, mainly for later iterations. Hence, (
11
) approximation preserved across the block iterations.

•

Contextual Conditioning:

By revealing tokens spread across the sequence early, later predictions are conditioned on richer adjacent context, further reducing remaining entropy.

•

Skip Mechanism:

As an interleavable feature atop our deterministic schedule, tokens whose denoiser signal (e.g. low confidence or low negative entropy) falls below a threshold are deferred to the next iteration
\mathcal{P}_{t+1}
- enabling DUS to preserve its fixed dilation while still adapting to model uncertainty and avoiding the exposure of fully uncurtained tokens.

Under a fast-mixing, first-order Markov process, DUS achieves lower cumulative conditional entropies (the sum of per‐iteration entropy gains) than denoiser-guided planners in parallel unmasking - by explicitly reaching the joint entropy bound at each step and distributing informative context most efficiently across iterations.

Experiments

In this section we evaluate the generative and downstream capabilities of our new planner for MDLMs on two families of tasks: mathematical reasoning and code generation. We compare two pretrained diffusion‐based LLMs - LLaDA‐8B and Dream‐7B - under a unified benchmarking protocol. We report results on three representative datasets:

GSM8K

(
Cobbe et al. 2021
)
- grade‐school math word problems. We set the generation length to
G=256
;

HumanEval

(
Chen et al. 2021
)
- Python function synthesis.
G=512
;

MBPP

(
Austin et al. 2021
)
- Python programming by example.
G=512
.

For each model and dataset, we decode with semi-AR masked diffusion using block size
B\in\{8,16,32,64\}
. Decoding proceeds in
n_{\rm blocks}=G/B
rounds, in each of which the model predicts all currently masked tokens before moving to the next block.
The Number of Function Evaluations (NFE) of a block set to
\mathrm{NFE}_{\rm block}=\log_{2}(B)
iterations in average due to the nature of our DUS planner and either for the self-confidence planner for fair comparison. The total number of denoiser calls (total NFE) is

\mathrm{NFE}=n_{\rm blocks}\times\log_{2}B=\frac{G}{B}\log_{2}B,

(12)

so that larger blocks trade off more parallelism against fewer diffusion evaluations. We additionally report the average NFE per denoising block, and the effective NFE which is the total NFE needed to complete the generation sequence completely. Practically, the planner can early stop the generation if the
[EOS]
token is unmasked and all previous tokens are unmasked too, since iterating afterwards will generate non-related content.

These results establish a training‐free method for accelerating MDLMs inference (both pre‐trained and instruction‐tuned) without sacrificing generation quality.

Conclusions

We have introduced the Dilated Unmasking Scheduler, a purely inference‐time, model‐free planner for masked diffusion language models. In extensive experiments on large diffusion LLMs, DUS consistently outperforms the traditional denoiser‐confidence planner-improving downstream task accuracy by up to 5 on challenging math and code benchmarks - while simultaneously reducing the number of denoising iterations by an order of magnitude. Unlike typical speed–quality trade‐offs, our method both accelerates inference and enhances output quality. By unlocking the parallelism inherent in diffusion decoding without any modifications to model architecture or training, DUS demonstrates a new path toward diffusion‐based LMs that surpass autoregressive approaches in both efficiency and performance, and inspires the design of future inference‐only planners to fully exploit this potential.
