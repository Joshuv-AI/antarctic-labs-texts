Revascularization Gaps in Diabetic Foot Disease in Nigeria: Health-System Constraints and the Need for a National Limb-Salvage Strategy
Authors: Oladimeji Adedeji Junaid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Diabetic foot ulceration remains a major cause of non-traumatic lower-limb amputation, disability, prolonged hospitalisation and excess mortality. Nigerian studies document substantial burdens of ulceration, late presentation and amputation, but national data on vascular assessment, referral and revascularization remain limited.Methodology:PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar and African Journals Online, together with relevant guideline, epidemiology, health-financing and programme websites, were searched from inception to 15 July 2026. Approximately 250 potentially relevant records were identified; about 74 full-text articles, guidelines and policy documents were assessed, and 36 sources were retained for thematic synthesis. Nigerian evidence was prioritised. Selection considered direct relevance, methodological clarity and contribution to prespecified clinical and health-system themes; no formal risk-of-bias instrument or meta-analysis was used.Results:The available literature is consistent with a probable, but currently unquantified, revascularization gap within Nigerian diabetic-foot services. Potential contributors include delayed recognition of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), inconsistent objective perfusion assessment, scarce and geographically concentrated vascular expertise, limited imaging and procedural infrastructure, fragmented referral pathways and high out-of-pocket costs. Revascularization remains the central focus of this review because restoration of perfusion is indispensable when ischaemia threatens healing; however, it is one component of comprehensive multidisciplinary care in diabetic foot disease.Conclusion:Nigeria should strengthen objective vascular assessment and timely access to revascularization within an integrated limb-salvage pathway that also prioritises infection control, offloading, glycaemic and cardiovascular risk optimisation, wound care, nutrition, smoking cessation and rehabilitation. National data systems are needed to quantify service gaps and evaluate outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetic foot disease is one of the most visible expressions of the global diabetes epidemic. It lies at the intersection of chronic metabolic disease, neuropathy, infection, peripheral arterial disease (PAD), poverty, delayed presentation and weak health systems. Globally, diabetic foot ulceration is associated with prolonged hospitalisation, recurrent infection, impaired quality of life, high mortality, ulcer recurrence and major lower-limb amputation [1,2]. In Nigeria, late presentation, prolonged hospitalisation, major amputation and mortality are recurrently documented [3–6].

Nigeria is entering a period in which the diabetic-foot burden is likely to grow. The International Diabetes Federation estimated that approximately 3.0 million Nigerian adults aged 20 - 79 years were living with diabetes in 2024 and projected this number to rise to about 6.6 million by 2050; Nigeria already has the largest number of adults with diabetes in the IDF Africa Region [7,8]. Without deliberate prevention and service reform, growth in diabetes, obesity, hypertension, chronic kidney disease and population ageing is likely to increase both neuropathic and ischaemic foot disease.

The Nigerian literature has rightly emphasised ulcer burden, late care-seeking, self-medication, use of unorthodox treatment and high amputation rates [3–6,9]. Diabetic foot ulcers complicated by PAD are not merely infected wounds; they may represent chronic limb-threatening ischaemia (CLTI), in which impaired perfusion substantially reduces the probability of wound healing and may necessitate revascularization when technically feasible and consistent with the patient’s overall treatment goals [10–13]. The central question is therefore whether patients with suspected ischaemia are identified early enough and offered appropriate vascular evaluation and revascularization when clinically indicated. Accordingly, this review examines whether the available evidence is consistent with a probable but currently unquantified revascularization gap in Nigerian diabetic-foot care.

Methods

This focused narrative review addressed the question: what clinical, infrastructural, workforce, financing and policy factors may limit access to revascularization for diabetic foot ulceration and PAD in Nigeria, and what health-system responses are feasible? PubMed/MEDLINE, Google Scholar and African Journals Online were searched from database inception to 15 July 2026. The websites of the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot, International Diabetes Federation, World Bank, Federal Government of Nigeria and World Diabetes Foundation were also searched. Search terms included combinations of “diabetic foot”, “diabetic foot ulcer”, “peripheral arterial disease”, “chronic limb-threatening ischaemia”, “revascularization”, “limb salvage”, “amputation”, “vascular surgery”, “health insurance”, “Nigeria”, “West Africa” and “sub-Saharan Africa”. Reference lists of key articles and guidelines were hand-searched.

The searches were iterative, particularly in Google Scholar. Approximately 250 potentially relevant records were identified across bibliographic databases, reference-list searches and grey-literature sources. After removal of duplicates and clearly irrelevant records, about 74 full-text articles, guidelines and policy documents were assessed for relevance. Thirty-six sources were retained in the final synthesis because they directly informed one or more prespecified review domains. Sources were excluded when they were duplicate reports, lacked adequate bibliographic or methodological detail, did not materially address the review question, or repeated evidence already represented by a more authoritative or recent source. Because no prospective retrieval log was maintained, the reported search and screening numbers are approximate rather than formal PRISMA counts. Eligible sources addressed predefined domains, including diabetic-foot burden and outcomes, PAD prevalence and recognition, vascular assessment and revascularization, limb salvage, workforce and infrastructure, multidisciplinary care, financing, and relevant Nigerian policies. Nigerian studies were prioritised, supplemented by Sub-Saharan African evidence where local data were limited, and international guidelines, systematic reviews, and comparative studies were used to contextualise standards and policy options. Data extracted included study characteristics, setting, population, key findings, vascular-assessment methods, revascularization and amputation outcomes, service barriers, financing considerations, and policy recommendations. Evidence was organised in a matrix and narratively synthesised under predefined themes. Quality was assessed pragmatically based on design, methodological clarity, relevance, consistency, and source authority. No formal risk-of-bias tool, duplicate screening or extraction, or meta-analysis was undertaken; these limitations are acknowledged.

The revascularization gap: an unmet vascular-care need

The central contribution of this review is to propose that preventable diabetic-foot amputation in Nigeria may partly reflect a probable revascularization gap, an under-recognised weakness in the vascular limb-salvage pathway. Wound care, debridement, antimicrobial therapy, offloading, glycaemic optimisation, nutritional care, smoking cessation and rehabilitation remain indispensable. Nevertheless, these interventions cannot fully compensate for critically inadequate blood flow. In patients with ischaemic ulcers, a pathway that repeatedly treats infection or tissue loss without objective perfusion assessment remains incomplete.

This framing shifts attention from individual blame towards shared system accountability. Patient delay is influenced by poverty, low awareness, fear of hospital costs, previous healthcare experiences and weak referral networks [9]. Missed ischaemia may be more likely where objective vascular testing and defined referral pathways are not embedded in routine care [10,11]. West African workforce reports and Nigerian service descriptions suggest that specialist availability and infrastructure are constrained, although national data linking these constraints directly to individual amputation decisions are not available [14,15]. Accordingly, the review identifies a plausible system-level shortfall that requires direct measurement rather than assuming its magnitude.

Peripheral arterial disease and the biological case for revascularization

The diabetic foot is commonly understood as the interaction of neuropathy, infection and ischaemia. Neuropathy permits repetitive unnoticed trauma; infection accelerates tissue destruction; and PAD restricts oxygen delivery, immune function and tissue repair [1,2]. In the EURODIALE cohort, ischaemia, infection and serious comorbidity frequently coexisted, illustrating why isolated wound-focused care is insufficient [16]. Patients with diabetes may also have severe arterial disease without classic claudication or rest pain, so the first recognised manifestation of PAD may be a non-healing ulcer or established gangrene [1,10,12].

The interaction between infection and ischaemia is clinically important. Ulcer depth, tissue injury and host-related factors increase the risk of diabetic-foot infection and progression to limb-threatening disease [17]. In poorly perfused tissue, infection may spread rapidly, debridement may leave a wound unable to granulate and antimicrobial delivery may be impaired. Perfusion assessment is therefore integral to infection management. At the same time, restoration of blood flow cannot substitute for prompt source control, culture-directed antimicrobial therapy, pressure relief, metabolic stabilisation and appropriate wound care.

International guidance recommends objective vascular assessment and management in centres with expertise in, or rapid access to, revascularization [10]. Earlier IWGDF guidance recommends vascular imaging when an ulcer fails to heal within four to six weeks despite good standard care and consideration of revascularization when healing remains inadequate [11]. The Global Vascular Guidelines emphasise objective haemodynamic testing, anatomic assessment and evidence-based planning for CLTI [12]. Updated systematic-review evidence describes wound-healing and limb-salvage outcomes following revascularization in diabetic foot ulcers complicated by PAD, but concludes that the available evidence is heterogeneous, predominantly observational and of very low certainty, preventing firm conclusions regarding the comparative effectiveness of specific revascularization strategies [13]. These recommendations support selective, patient-centred revascularization within multidisciplinary care rather than indiscriminate intervention.

The Nigerian evidence: burden, PAD and amputation

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported a substantial pooled burden of diabetic foot ulceration and high amputation prevalence across Nigerian studies [3]. The multicentre evaluation of diabetic foot ulcer in Nigeria documented prolonged hospitalisation, high amputation rates and substantial mortality, while identifying poor foot-care knowledge, self-medication, use of unorthodox treatment and late presentation as important gaps [4]. The MEDFUN analysis identified predictors of lower-extremity amputation, confirming that limb loss is a recurrent outcome in tertiary diabetic-foot care [6]. Prospective evidence from Lagos similarly documented serious sequelae after hospitalisation [5]. Together, these studies demonstrate a substantial burden of advanced disease but do not constitute national population-based surveillance and do not quantify vascular-assessment or revascularization rates.

PAD-specific Nigerian studies report clinically important disease among people with type 2 diabetes and higher frequencies among those with established diabetic foot ulcers [18–20]. Wider sub-Saharan African evidence also indicates that PAD is common and frequently under-recognised in diabetic populations [21]. Collectively, these findings make ischaemia a central consideration in Nigerian diabetic-foot care. However, the national proportion of patients receiving objective perfusion assessment, vascular imaging, specialist review or revascularization remains unknown; the apparent vascular-access shortfall is therefore inferred rather than measured.

Barriers that may limit timely revascularization Late presentation

Late presentation narrows the window for salvage. Delayed care-seeking reflects patient beliefs, perceived severity, previous healthcare experiences, financial constraints and use of alternative care [9]. By the time some patients reach tertiary care, infection and tissue loss may be advanced. Meta-analytic evidence has identified clinical factors associated with lower-extremity amputation, supporting early risk stratification and timely escalation of patients at greatest risk [22]. Earlier referral should therefore combine infection management, offloading and wound care with prompt assessment for ischaemia, rather than treating revascularization as a late rescue measure.

Failure of vascular assessment

PAD in diabetes may be clinically silent, masked by neuropathy or misinterpreted as infection-related pain. Pulse palpation alone is inadequate, particularly when medial arterial calcification or below-knee disease complicates assessment. Guidelines recommend combining clinical examination with objective tests such as ankle pressure, ankle-brachial index (ABI), toe pressure, Doppler waveform analysis and transcutaneous oxygen pressure where available [10,11]. Nigerian PAD studies demonstrate that ABI can identify disease missed by symptoms or pulse examination [18,20]. Although national testing rates are unavailable, limited access to objective assessment may contribute to delayed recognition of ischaemia and delayed vascular referral.

Workforce shortages

Revascularization requires vascular surgeons, interventional radiologists or other appropriately trained endovascular specialists, anaesthetic support, imaging, theatre or angiography-suite staff, postoperative surveillance and follow-up. West African literature describes shortages of skilled vascular personnel and limited specialist capacity [14,15]. These reports support concern about uneven access, particularly outside urban tertiary centres, but do not establish how often workforce constraints determine amputation decisions. National workforce mapping and prospective referral audits are therefore needed.

Infrastructure and consumable limitations

Endovascular and open surgical revascularization depend on vascular laboratories, duplex ultrasonography, computed tomography angiography or magnetic resonance angiography, angiography suites, guidewires, balloons, stents, bypass materials and reliable perioperative care. Equipment downtime, maintenance failures and consumable shortages may interrupt services where capacity is already limited [14,15]. A South-South Nigerian retrospective report suggests that coordinated limb-preserving care is feasible when specialties integrate perfusion management, infection control, offloading and metabolic care [23]. International observational evidence similarly associates successful revascularization with improved wound healing and limb preservation, but such findings should not be extrapolated uncritically to all Nigerian settings [24].

Financial barriers and weak insurance protection

Financial barriers likely constrain access to vascular imaging and intervention. World Bank indicators show that Nigerian households finance a large share of healthcare directly and that per-capita health expenditure remains limited [25,26]. Vascular imaging, endovascular consumables, bypass surgery, hospitalisation, antibiotics, offloading devices, wound care and rehabilitation can exceed what families can mobilise. At the point of care, major amputation may appear less expensive than a prolonged limb-salvage pathway, even though its downstream consequences include disability, rehabilitation needs, prosthetic costs, unemployment and caregiver burden. Nigerian studies are needed to determine how often cost directly alters vascular referral or treatment choices.

The National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) Act 2022 provides an important policy opening by creating a framework for mandatory insurance and progress towards universal health coverage [27–29]. Limb-salvage benefit packages should cover the complete pathway: objective perfusion assessment, imaging, revascularization when indicated, infection treatment, debridement, offloading, wound care, nutritional support and rehabilitation. Financing revascularization in isolation would leave other essential components inaccessible and would not produce an effective limb-salvage service.

Fragmented multidisciplinary care

Diabetic foot disease requires coordinated care involving diabetology, vascular surgery, interventional radiology, infectious diseases, orthopaedic or plastic surgery, wound nursing, podiatry where available, nutrition, orthotics and rehabilitation. International guidance emphasises structured prevention, rapid assessment, multidisciplinary management and timely escalation [30,31]. Coordinated systems have been associated with reductions in major amputation [32]. Revascularization should therefore stand out as the

neglected vascular component of a broader pathway, not as a competing alternative to infection control, offloading, metabolic optimisation, wound care or rehabilitation.

Nigeria has nevertheless developed important foundations. The Diabetes Podiatry Initiative Nigeria and subsequent World Diabetes Foundation-supported projects have strengthened foot-risk screening, clinics, training and patient education [33,34]. These programme reports indicate that coordinated diabetic-foot screening, training and clinic strengthening can be implemented in Nigerian settings. Their next development should include consistent perfusion assessment, clear vascular referral criteria and reliable access to revascularization for patients with CLTI.

The economic case for integrated limb salvage

The immediate cost of revascularization is visible; the lifetime cost of amputation is often hidden. In England, diabetic foot ulceration and amputation consumed an estimated GBP 837 - 962 million in 2014 - 2015, approaching 1% of the National Health Service budget [35]. Although Nigerian costs, prices and financing structures differ, the principle is relevant: economic evaluation should compare an integrated limb-salvage pathway with the full downstream costs of disability, repeated hospitalisation, revision procedures, prosthetic care and lost productivity, rather than comparing only the price of an angiogram, angioplasty or bypass with an amputation episode.

Nigeria needs local cost and cost-effectiveness analyses of prevention, early vascular assessment, infection management, offloading, wound care and selective revascularization. These analyses should account for disease severity, procedural success, repeat intervention, survival, quality of life, household expenditure

and productivity. Until such evidence exists, it would be inappropriate to claim that revascularization is invariably cost-saving; the defensible argument is that its value should be assessed within the full multidisciplinary pathway and over an appropriate time horizon.

A proposed national performance indicator: the Amputation-to-Revascularization Ratio

National improvement requires measures that make vascular assessment and limb-salvage activity visible. We propose the Amputation-to-Revascularization Ratio (ARR) as a hypothesis-generating surveillance indicator intended for prospective validation, not as a standalone measure of quality or a basis for interfacility ranking. The numerator would be the number of major lower-limb amputations attributable to diabetes-related ulceration, infection or ischaemia during a defined period. The denominator would be the number of index lower-limb revascularization episodes undertaken for diabetic-foot PAD or CLTI during the same period.

For the numerator, “major amputation” should be defined as amputation through or proximal to the ankle and attributed after clinical review. Minor amputations should be reported separately. For the denominator, multiple procedures performed during one planned treatment episode on the same limb should count as one index episode; a later reintervention for restenosis or disease progression should be recorded separately. Bilateral procedures should be counted by limb, while analyses should also report unique patients to avoid inflation by repeat treatment.

Interpretation would require adjustment for referral patterns, tertiary-centre case concentration, disease severity, anatomical complexity, sepsis, frailty, life expectancy, technical feasibility and patient preference. A tertiary referral centre receiving advanced cases could have a high ARR despite appropriate practice, whereas a low ratio could reflect poor case ascertainment rather than high-quality care. ARR should therefore always be interpreted alongside the number of eligible CLTI cases, objective-assessment rates, raw procedure counts, and Wound, Ischemia and Foot Infection stage where available, time to vascular review, proportion objectively assessed, revascularization success, minor and major amputation, mortality and loss to follow-up.

Validation should proceed prospectively in hospitals representing different levels of care and geopolitical regions. Initial studies should test completeness of case capture, inter-rater reliability of attribution, linkage of repeat procedures, sensitivity to case-mix and association with wound healing, disability, mortality and quality of life. Only after such validation should ARR be considered for benchmarking or funding decisions.

Tiered Implementation framework for diabetic-foot limb salvage

Implementation roadmap

Short term (0–2 years): require documentation of vascular assessment for diabetic foot ulcers in tertiary hospitals; introduce ABI and Doppler training; establish local referral algorithms; strengthen infection control, offloading, nutritional assessment and wound-care protocols; hold multidisciplinary threatened-limb conferences; audit major amputations; and begin prospective data capture.

Medium term (2–5 years): develop regional limb-salvage hubs with vascular imaging and endovascular capacity; create formal multidisciplinary clinics; define NHIA and state-insurance benefit packages covering the complete care pathway; standardise interfacility transfer; expand vascular and endovascular training positions; and undertake Nigerian cost-effectiveness studies.

Long term (5 years and beyond): establish a national diabetic-foot registry; integrate foot prevention and cardiovascular risk reduction into primary care; publicly report vascular-assessment, revascularization, amputation, mortality and functional outcomes; validate ARR and other quality measures; and use case-mix-adjusted outcomes to guide funding and regional service planning.

Potential challenges and pragmatic responses

A national programme will face workforce shortages, high device costs, uneven insurance implementation, urban-rural inequity, equipment-maintenance problems, weak data systems and difficulty retaining trained personnel. These constraints are substantial and mean that service expansion must be phased, evaluated and matched to local capacity. They do not, however, justify a pathway in which suspected ischaemia is never assessed or referral options are unclear.

A pragmatic model should distinguish what can be delivered at each level. Basic facilities can prioritise prevention, infection triage, offloading, wound care, metabolic stabilisation, smoking cessation and rapid referral. Intermediate centres can add ABI or Doppler assessment, debridement, nutritional support and coordinated transfer. Regional hubs can concentrate vascular imaging, selective endovascular or open revascularization, reconstruction, rehabilitation and registry reporting. Implementation should be accompanied by maintenance plans, procurement safeguards and workforce-retention strategies.

Discussion

Revascularization is neither a luxury nor a competitor to conservative and multidisciplinary management. It is the perfusion-restoring component of evidence-based care for selected patients whose healing is threatened by PAD or CLTI [10–13]. Limb salvage also depends on prompt infection control, appropriate debridement, pressure offloading, glycaemic and cardiovascular risk optimisation, wound care, nutritional support, smoking cessation, rehabilitation and patient-centred decision-making [30,31]. Some limbs will remain non-salvageable despite timely care, particularly in overwhelming sepsis, extensive tissue destruction, prohibitive procedural risk or limited life expectancy.

The policy priority is therefore not indiscriminate angioplasty or bypass. It is a reliable pathway in which every ulcer receives timely perfusion assessment, suspected CLTI prompts rapid vascular review, and revascularization is considered alongside the complete multidisciplinary treatment plan when technically feasible and aligned with the patient's goals. Nigerian implementation studies should test whether such pathways improve healing, reduce major amputation and remain affordable across different resource settings.

Lessons from other settings are relevant but must be adapted rather than copied. Multidisciplinary programmes in high-income settings have been associated with reduced major amputation [32]. For Nigeria, regional hub-and-spoke models are a plausible response to scarce expertise, but their effectiveness should be tested through prospective implementation studies assessing referral time, equity, procedural outcomes, costs and patient-reported outcomes.

The ethical dimension is also important. Major amputation remains necessary and potentially life-saving in overwhelming sepsis, irreversible tissue destruction or a non-salvageable limb. However, when a patient has no access to objective perfusion assessment, timely referral or a clinically indicated revascularization option, the resulting disability may also reflect inequity in access to evidence-based care. Diabetes-related lower-extremity complications contribute substantially to global disability, strengthening the public-health case for an integrated limb-salvage strategy [36].

Limitations

This policy-oriented narrative review was designed to provide a clinically and policy-relevant synthesis rather than a systematic estimate of effect. Approximately 250 records were identified, about 74 full-text sources were assessed, and 36 were retained, but these numbers are approximate because searches were iterative and a prospective record-level log was not maintained. Screening and extraction were undertaken by one author, and no formal risk-of-bias instrument, meta-analysis or PRISMA flow diagram was used.

Relevant publications may therefore have been missed, and interpretation remains susceptible to author judgement.

The Nigerian evidence is heterogeneous in setting, case definition, vascular-assessment method and outcome reporting. Most importantly, no national registry reports objective vascular-assessment rates, referral intervals, revascularization procedures, case severity or limb-salvage outcomes. The magnitude of the probable revascularization gap cannot therefore be quantified from current literature; it is inferred from converging evidence on PAD prevalence, advanced presentation, amputation outcomes, workforce limitations and health financing. Several recommendations are adapted from international guidelines and require local feasibility testing, costing and prospective evaluation. The proposed ARR is unvalidated and should not be used for benchmarking until its definitions, reliability, case-mix sensitivity and associations with patient outcomes have been established.

Conclusion

Nigerian studies demonstrate substantial burdens of diabetic foot ulceration, late presentation, PAD, major amputation and mortality [3–6,9,18–21]. They do not, however, provide national estimates of vascular-assessment rates, referral intervals or revascularization. Current evidence is therefore consistent with a probable revascularization gap and an unmet vascular-care need, but cannot define its national size or the proportion of amputations that timely revascularization could prevent.

The next phase of Nigerian diabetic-foot care should strengthen the entire multidisciplinary limb-salvage pathway: prevention, infection control, offloading, glycaemic and cardiovascular risk optimisation, wound and nutritional care, smoking cessation, rehabilitation and patient-centred decision-making. Within that pathway, objective perfusion assessment and timely revascularization should no longer be exceptional services. Regional vascular hubs, explicit insurance coverage, workforce development, standard referral pathways and a national registry are practical priorities for testing and closing the current evidence and service gaps.
