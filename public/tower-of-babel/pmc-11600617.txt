Analysis of vaginal microbiota during postpartum and postmenopausal periods based on metagenomics
Authors: Xiaocui Li, Zhan Zhang, Huihui Bai, Zhaohui Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostmenopausal and the postpartum periods are essential physiological phases that result in low estrogen levels in women; however, they are important to female reproductive health. Traditional as well as new detection methods (such as 16 S RNA sequencing) have limitations in detecting the composition of vaginal microbiota. Therefore, in this study, we used metagenomic detection technology to study the composition of vaginal microbiota in postmenopausal and postpartum women. Six women were randomly selected from each group (healthy women of childbearing age, postmenopausal group, and postpartum) for vaginal microecology, composition, α-diversity, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe), and Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) analyses.ResultsWe discovered that Lactobacillus dominance disappeared in postpartum and postmenopausal group women and that diversity increased. However, the proportions of Atopobium vaginae, Escherichia coli, and Streptococcus agalactiae significantly increased. Diversity was the highest in the postpartum period, with a significant increase in the proportions of A. vaginae, Gardnerella vaginalis, Prevotella, and occasionally, Chlamydia trachomatis. Linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis revealed that Lactobacillus crispatus and L. iners enrichment in the postpartum and menopausal periods was much lower than that in the childbearing age group. CARD analysis revealed that ABC-F ATP-binding cassette ribosomal protection protein subfamily gene abundance was significantly lower in the menopausal than in the childbearing age group, whereas the gimA family macrolide glycosyltransferase gene abundance was significantly higher.ConclusionsThe dominance of vaginal Lactobacillus in postpartum and menopausal women disappeared, while their diversity increased. In addition, the reproductive tract of postpartum women was susceptible to invasion by pathogenic microorganisms, which deserves clinical attention. When menopausal women receive treatment for vaginal infections, the likelihood that certain bacterial communities develop antibiotic resistance through ribosomal protection mechanisms is lower than that of women in the childbearing age, while the possibility of developing resistance to macrolides through glycosylation may increase. This, however, requires further research.

Background

Vaginal microbiota refers to the collective community of microorganisms that colonize the female vagina at a specific time. These microorganisms influence the innate and adaptive immunity of the host through co-evolution, co-development, co-metabolism, and co-regulation, thereby directly inhibiting viruses and bacteria and enhancing the epithelial cell barrier function [1, 2]. The postmenopausal period is an essential physiological phase for women, characterized primarily by ovarian function failure. Hormonal changes lead to degenerative changes in multiple organ systems, such as atrophy of the vulva, pain during sexual intercourse and urinary tract irritation, and they become prone to recurrent infections and inflammation. The postpartum period, a special period in a woman’s life, is affected by pregnancy and childbirth, which disrupt physical barriers. It is also a period of low estrogen levels but important to female reproductive health. Historically, our understanding of the vaginal microbiome has been primarily grounded in traditional methods, which rely on bacterial culture and long cultivation cycles and are influenced by various factors, leading to a low success rate and an inability to discern the intricate makeup of the vaginal microbiome. With the rapid advancement of molecular biology, sequencing of bacterial 16 S RNA has initiated more comprehensive and in-depth study of the composition and function of the vaginal microbiome and enabled theoretical innovations [3]; however, this approach cannot accurately distinguish microbial species. Recently, metagenomic technology has emerged, bypassing traditional methods and directly extracting total DNA from environmental samples. New functional genes and bioactive substances are identified by constructing and screening a metagenomic database. Therefore, it is possible to quickly and accurately obtain genetic information on specific microbial groups and explore their diversity, metabolic functions, and interactions with hosts. Furthermore, it has been applied to study vaginal microbial composition and gene functions [4, 5]. Therefore, we used metagenomic detection technology to study the composition of the vaginal microbiota in postmenopausal and postpartum women, provide a theoretical basis for changes in these microbiota, and determine whether or how to reasonably intervene.

Methods

Sample sources and collection

Sample sources

Women who underwent physical examination at the Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital between March 2023 and May 2023 were selected. The control group comprised healthy women in the childbearing age (n = 6). The menopausal group consisted of healthy women who had experienced menopause for > 1 year (n = 6). The postpartum group consisted of women who underwent full-term delivery at the Beijing Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital and returned for re-examination 6–7 weeks after delivery.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: for the childbearing age group, participants included healthy women in the childbearing age who had a healthy physical examination for vaginal secretions, no sexual history within 30 days, and were not pregnant or post-abortion. For the menopausal group, participants included healthy women who had been menopausal for 1 + years, with no history of systemic antibiotic use or vaginal local medication within 2 weeks and no sexual history within 30 days. Participants in the postpartum group did not systemically use antibiotics, did not have vaginal lavage, and did not use local vaginal medication within 2 weeks, and no lochia or sexual activity had yet resumed.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with intrauterine devices; those undergoing hormone replacement therapy after menopause or taking hormones or immunosuppressants due to other illnesses; individuals with diseases such as heart, liver, kidney, or endocrine disorders; and individuals with tumors or cervical lesions.

Sample collection

For enrolled patients, a survey questionnaire was completed, vaginal secretions were collected, and sterile cotton swabs were used to collect 1/3 of the vaginal sidewall secretions, obtaining two samples. One sample underwent wet slide and Gram staining for observation through an oil microscope, while the other was placed in a 2-ml Eppendorf tube and stored at -80 ℃ until metagenomic testing.

Standards for vaginal microbiota detection

The diagnostic criteria for bacterial vaginosis (BV) are as follows: a Nugent score of 4–6 indicates intermediate BV and a Nugent score of ≥ 7 indicates BV [6].

Macrogenomic detection and analysis

DNA extraction

A trace sample genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen, centrifuge column type) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions, and the samples were stored in a -20 ℃ freezer.

DNA database construction

The extracted DNA products were pretreated and subjected to DNA fragmentation, adaptor ligation, purification, and double-end screening before quality inspection. After passing the database detection, the data from each sample were mixed according to the requirements of the effective concentration and target data volume, and the Illumina NovaSeq sequencing platform was used for sequencing.

Analysis process

Quality control: all raw metagenomic sequencing data were subjected to quality control using the MOCAT2 software. The Cutadapt software was used to de-splice all the original sequence tags (reads) and filter out reads with quality < 20 or length < 30 bp. The SolexaQA package was used to obtain clean reads. SOAPaligner was used to compare the host genome and filter out contaminated host reads to obtain high-quality clean data.

Species annotation: clean reads obtained from quality control were used as input data. Meta PhlAn3 was used for species classification to obtain the relative abundance of microbial communities at various levels, from phylum to species. Statistics on species abundance were conducted at the classification levels of kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. Only the top nine species with the highest abundance rankings were labeled, whereas the remaining species were labeled as other. The relative abundance of different groups at different levels was calculated by summing the abundance of all samples in each group, calculating the relative abundance (percentage), and drawing a bar chart. Based on the abundance results at the species level, after data standardization, a heatmap was drawn to intuitively reflect the distribution of species within the sample using color changes.

Statistical analysis

Alpha-diversity analysis refers to the diversity within a specific region or ecosystem, and was used to describe the richness, diversity, and uniformity of species within each sample.

Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) variance analysis compares two or more groups and identifies biomarkers with significant differences in abundance between the groups. Based on the relative species abundance data, we first used the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis rank sum test to detect species with significant differences in abundance between two or more groups. The significantly different species obtained in the previous step were analyzed for inter-group differences using the grouped Wilcoxon rank sum test. Finally, linear discriminant analysis (LDA) was used to reduce the dimensionality of the data and evaluate the impact of species with significant differences (LDA score).

Non-redundant gene sets with resistance genes were annotated using the official Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; v3.0.8) software RGI (Resistance Gene Identification, v4.0.2), after which a non-parametric difference test was used for inter-group comparison and the Benjamin–Hochberg method was used to correct the P-value. The corrected P-value threshold was set to 0.05.

Metagenomic analysis

Analysis of vaginal microbiota composition

A heatmap (Fig. 1) visually displays the species-level composition of the vaginal microbiota in the three groups. Combined with Table 1, we found significant differences between menopausal and postpartum women. Unlike women in the childbearing age, who had lactobacilli (especially Lactobacillus crispatus and Lactobacillus iners) as the dominant bacteria, the dominant bacteria during the menopausal and postpartum periods differed, with only occasional samples having L. iners as the dominant bacteria. In the postpartum group, the proportions of Atopobium vaginae, Gardnerella vaginalis, and Prevotella were significantly elevated, and some samples showed a significant increase in the proportion of the pathogen C. trachomatis. In the menopausal group, the proportion of intermediate BV increased, whereas in the postpartum group, both the BV and BV intermediate types were significantly elevated.

α-Diversity analysis

Commonly used α-diversity indices include richness, Shnnon’s index, and evenness index (Fig. 2). The Shannon index borrows knowledge from information theory. The larger the Shannon index, the greater the uncertainty in predicting what kind of sample will be taken next from the microbial community. The greater the uncertainty, the more unknown factors there are in this microbial community, which indicates higher diversity. The postpartum group had the highest diversity of the vaginal microbiota, while the childbearing age group has the lowest diversity. However, the differences in vaginal microbiota diversity between the three groups were insignificant (P = 0.28; Fig. 2-a). The evenness index refers to the distribution of the total number of species and individuals in a community or environment and is a measure of the degree of similarity between different species in terms of quantity. The postmenopausal group had the highest evenness, while the childbearing age group had the lowest evenness. The difference between the two groups was significant (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2-b). The richness index refers to the number of species observed in each sample. The childbearing age group had the highest richness, and the postmenopausal group had the lowest richness, while the postpartum group had the highest dispersion degree. However, the differences among the three groups were insignificant (P = 0.30; Fig. 2-c).

CARD

The core of CARD is antibiotic resistance ontology, which includes genes and mechanisms related to antibiotic resistance. The average gene abundance of the ABC-F ATP-binding cassette (ABC) ribosomal protection protein was significantly lower in the menopausal group than in the childbearing age group (Fig. 5a), whereas the average gene abundance of the gimA family macrolide glycosyltransferase was significantly higher in the menopausal group compared to the childbearing age group (Fig. 5b).

Discussion

Under normal circumstances, lactobacilli that produce H2O2 are the dominant bacteria in vaginal microbiota. In addition to maintaining an acidic environment in the vagina, lactobacilli secrete bacteriocins and other antimicrobial factors that inhibit or kill pathogenic microorganisms. Concurrently, they prevent pathogenic microorganisms from adhering to vaginal epithelial cells through competitive exclusion mechanisms, thereby maintaining the balance of the vaginal microbiota. Therefore, the diversity of the microbiota is low but is easily affected by factors such as race, genes, age, hormone levels, menstrual cycle, pregnancy and postpartum status, sexual activity, and other external factors [7, 8]. Women in the childbearing age and those in the postpartum period are at the same age stage; postpartum and menopausal women are in a state of lower estrogen levels and a susceptible state than women in the childbearing age [9, 10]. The composition of the vaginal microbiota in menopausal and postpartum women should be investigated to identify whether we need to intervene in the composition of the vaginal microbiota in women during these two special periods.

In this study, we found that the proportion of lactobacilli in the vaginal microbiota of postpartum and menopausal women was significantly decreased, losing their dominant position. The proportion of A. vaginae in the postpartum group was significantly higher than that in the childbearing age group. The lowest richness and highest evenness were observed in the menopausal group, with higher diversity. The diversity of the vaginal microbiota in postpartum women were the highest, with higher richness and evenness. Recent studies in South Korea showed that the abundance of the Lactobacillus genus decreased from 63.2 to 23.7% after menopause [11]. This was similar to an earlier study in American women, which showed that the genus Lactobacillus decreased from 83 to 54% after menopause [12]. This finding implies that the decreasing trend in lactobacilli after menopause may not be closely related to geography and dietary habits. The vaginal microbiota composition of menopausal and postpartum women is similar to that of Ehrlich’s “Rivet” model. The number of bacteria of the same species is equivalent, functionally related, and evenly intersecting. Small fluctuations in the number of certain bacterial species will not have an impact on the entire ecosystem until community structure changes reach a threshold, and a significant change in the ecosystem’s function occurs [13]. It is widely recognized that the composition of vaginal microbiota, which can be dominated by lactobacilli, is more conducive to resisting the invasion of harmful pathogenic microorganisms and maintaining the stability of the vaginal microbiota. However, the menopausal and postpartum women included in this study had no obvious symptoms of external genital itching, pain, or increased secretion. Therefore, it is worth exploring whether it is necessary to actively supplement women with non-lactobacilli as the dominant bacteria to achieve a microbial community state with lactobacilli as the dominant bacteria, or whether blindly supplementing lactobacilli will break the original " Rivet " model and cause an iatrogenic vaginal microbiota imbalance.

Although there were significant differences in each sample within the menopausal and postpartum groups and no biomarkers with significant differences were found between the two groups, the vaginal microbiota of menopausal women was in a suppressed state, with a decrease in richness. In contrast, the vaginal microbiota of postpartum women had greater richness and diversity, and there was a significant increase in strains such as A. vaginae and G. vaginalis, which may be related to BV; even a case of infection with C. trachomatis was present. Other studies also found that the number of bacterial species involved in the pathogenesis of BV in postpartum women significantly increased and that the bacterial composition became more complex [14, 15]. Hu et al. found that the number of lactobacilli in postpartum women decreased, whereas that of pathogenic bacteria (such as Prevotella, Megamonas, Anaerococcus, and peptide bacteria) increased; however, the diversity of vaginal microbiota in postpartum women was lower [16]. However, this study as well as the study by Zhang et al. found that the vaginal diversity of postpartum women was increased [15]. This also indicates that the composition of the vaginal microbiota in postpartum women is complex, and further research is required. The Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines for Bacterial Vaginitis in China (2021) [6] recommend treatment of symptomatic women with BV. Some studies have also reported that the natural protection mechanism of the vagina during the postpartum period is damaged by an increase in vaginal pH, which is not conducive to the growth of lactobacilli and reduces their richness. It is suggested that lactobacilli and probiotics should be supplemented during the postpartum period to improve the vaginal microenvironment and reduce postpartum infections [17]. However, in our clinical work, we found that women who underwent postpartum re-examination rarely complained of external genital itching, increasing leukorrhea, or unusual smell. Therefore, it is rare to check for vaginal secretions, especially during traditional gynecological examinations, which rarely detect increased or abnormal vaginal secretions. However, in the present study, there was a significant increase in the vaginal microbiota composition of postpartum women, which may be related to BV, such as A. vaginae, G. vaginalis, and C. trachomatis. In 2016, the WHO estimated 128 million new cases of C. trachomatis infection worldwide, with a total female prevalence rate of 4.2% [18]. Chlamydia trachomatis infection is a sexually transmitted disease caused by C. trachomatis in the female reproductive organs, which has a significant impact on female reproductive health. Long-term complications can have a huge impact on the physical and mental health of patients. Therefore, C. trachomatis infection has become a global public health problem. In this study, we found one case of C. trachomatis infection in six patients, which may suggest that the infection rate of C. trachomatis in postpartum women is higher than that in the general population, and that clinical attention should be paid to the detection of C. trachomatis in postpartum women. We will also pay attention to this in our future research. Consideration should be taken to determine whether further invasion of pathogenic microorganisms and infection will occur if appropriate intervention is not undertaken, especially when the proportion of pathogenic microorganisms such as C. trachomatis is high. Moreover, recent studies suggest that the postpartum period is suitable to investigate the ecology of L. crispatus and other species—their capacity to disperse, colonize, persist, and diversify—in relation to important changes taking place in the host and local microbial community. In addition to human-population-adapted interventions, we might also consider those that are life-stage-adapted in efforts to support vaginal health and improve outcomes for women [19].

Antimicrobial drug resistance is one of the greatest threats to human health. Elucidating the mechanisms underlying microbial resistance to antibiotics is crucial for drug resistance research. In this study, we found significant differences in ABC-F protein levels between the menopausal and reproductive age groups. ABC ribosomal protection proteins belong to the family of evolutionarily conserved membrane transport proteins. The ABC-F subfamily can participate in ribosome synthesis, antibiotic resistance, and transcriptional regulation [20, 21], and is an important source of antibiotic resistance in Staphylococcus aureus, Mycobacterium abscesses, and other bacteria [22, 23]. Members of the ABC-F family (including lsa [A], msr [A], optr [A], and vga [A]), differ from other members of the ABC family, which actively pump drugs out of the cell. The ABC-F protein lacks the transmembrane domain characteristics of transporters and is believed to confer antibiotic resistance through ribosome protection mechanisms by interacting with ribosomes and replacing binding drugs [24]. This mechanism can confer resistance to antibiotics, such as macrolides, lincomamides, and streptococcins [25, 26]. In this study, ABC-F abundance in the vaginal secretions of postmenopausal women was lower than that in the childbearing age group. This indicates that the possibility of resistance in the vaginal microbiota of postmenopausal women through ribosomal protection mechanisms is lower than in women in the childbearing age. For multi-resistant bacteria in postmenopausal women with vaginal infections, using drugs with a higher affinity for ribosomes may not necessarily improve treatment efficacy. However, we found in this study that the abundance of gimA family macrolide glycosyltransferase was significantly higher in the menopausal group than in the childbearing age group. The gimA family of macrolide glycosyltransferases is found in Streptomyces [27] and is a natural biocatalyst that can transfer sugar groups from activated sugar donors to different receptors. There has been limited research on this topic; however, previous studies show that some pathogenic bacteria can inactivate various macrolide antibiotics through glycosylation, including erythromycin and tylosin [28]. This suggests that when treating vaginal infections in menopausal women, development of resistance to widely used macrolide drugs is possible. This will become an issue requiring attention in the future treatment of female reproductive tract infections, especially in menopausal women and research on antibiotic resistance. However, this study was limited by a small sample size; thus, further research is needed to confirm our findings.

Conclusions

The vaginal microbiota of women is complex and diverse and is influenced by various factors that constrain and regulate each other. The reproductive health issues of women during the two special periods of menopause and postpartum are worth exploring. The increasingly mature technology of metagenomic testing provides us with powerful tools for a more comprehensive study of vaginal microbiota composition. In the future, we will strive to increase the sample size, deeply explore metabolic pathways, and virulence factors, to more comprehensively grasp the vaginal microbiota situation during menopause and postpartum periods, avoid or timely correct vaginal microbiota disorders, and provide more targeted treatment, so as to maximize the benefits of women in these two special periods.
