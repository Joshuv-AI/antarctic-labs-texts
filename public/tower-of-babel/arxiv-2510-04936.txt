Discrete scalar curvature as a weighted sum of Ollivier-Ricci curvatures Thanks: AH was supported by NSF grant DMS-2303402. AJB was partially supported by ONR grant N00014-22-1-2679.
Abigail Hickok 1 and Andrew J. Blumberg 1,2,3

Abstract
. We study the relationship between discrete analogues of Ricci and scalar curvature that are defined for point clouds and graphs. In the discrete setting, Ricci curvature is replaced by Ollivier-Ricci curvature. Scalar curvature can be computed as the trace of Ricci curvature for a Riemannian manifold; this motivates a new definition of a scalar version of Ollivier-Ricci curvature. We show that our definition converges to scalar curvature for nearest neighbor graphs obtained by sampling from a manifold. We also prove some new results about the convergence of Ollivier-Ricci curvature to Ricci curvature.



1.
Introduction

The curvature of a Riemannian manifold
M
is a fundamental way of characterizing its geometry. The curvature of a manifold roughly speaking encodes the way that the tangent plane is moving as we move around on the manifold. Unlike Euclidean space (which is a “flat” manifold with zero curvature), manifolds in general may be curved either positively or negatively. Curvature affects properties such as whether parallel geodesics (locally-shortest paths) curve either towards or away from each other, and whether geodesic balls are smaller or larger than their Euclidean analogs. It also constrains the global topology of a manifold.

In the case of a curve, the curvature is essentially encoded in the second derivative. But in general, the curvature is a much more complicated quantity. There are several distinct but intimately related notions of curvature; we consider two of them in this paper. Arguably the simplest notion of curvature is the
scalar curvature
, which is a function
S\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
that quantifies the local curvature at each point
x\in M
. Scalar curvature is an isometry-invariant, and manifolds with positive scalar curvature have been extensively studied; this condition puts constraints on the possible topology of the manifold, and moreover arises naturally in the theory of general relativity. A richer notion of curvature is captured by the
Ricci curvature
tensor, a linear transformation
\text{Ric}_{x}\colon T_{x}M\to T_{x}M
on the tangent space at
x
. Ricci curvature controls the local deformations of shapes along geodesic paths on the manifold, and plays an essential role in our understanding of the geometry of
3
-manifolds (as indicated by Perelman’s proof of Thurston’s geometrization conjecture).
Scalar and Ricci curvature measure curvature in different ways, but they are related by the fact that scalar curvature is the trace of Ricci curvature.

In recent years, there has been growing interest in discrete analogues of curvature for graphs. There are several definitions of “discrete Ricci curvature” that take the form of functions
\kappa\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
on the edges of a graph (e.g., see
[
11
,
5
,
2
]
).
We focus on Ollivier-Ricci curvature (ORC), a version of discrete Ricci curvature that is based on optimal transport. For a weighted graph
G
, the ORC of an edge
(x,y)
is

\kappa_{G}(x,y)=1-\frac{W_{1}(\mu_{x},\mu_{y})}{w(x,y)}\,,

where
\mu_{x},\mu_{y}
are uniform probability measures on neighbors of nodes
x,y
respectively, and
W_{1}
denotes the
1
-Wasserstein metric. In
Figure

1
, we show three prototypical examples of edges with negative, zero, and positive ORC. Edges that are present in highly interconnected “communities” tend to have positive ORC, while “local bottlenecks” tend to have negative ORC.

Negative

1
and calculate
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
using the formulation in
Definition

2.4
.

ORC has been used in numerous applications. In
[
15
,
10
]
, ORC was applied to the problem of community detection
[
12
]
. Recently, Saidi et al. used ORC to
improve low-dimensional embeddings of high-dimensional point cloud data
[
13
,
8
]
. Within machine learning, it has been used to mitigate over-smoothing and over-squashing
[
9
]
in graph neural networks, to encode structural properties
[
21
,
4
]
, and to evaluate graph generative models
[
16
]
.

From a theoretical perspective, Ollivier-Ricci curvature is particularly appealing since it is the only form of discrete Ricci curvature with formal guarantees about its convergence to traditional Ricci curvature. When ORC was first introduced by Ollivier, it was defined not only on graphs, but also on any metric space
M
equipped with a Markov kernel
\{\mu_{x}\}_{x\in M}
. For example, in the manifold version of ORC,
M
is a Riemannian manifold and
\mu_{x}
is the uniform probability measure on a geodesic ball centered at
x
. Ollivier proved that his manifold version of ORC (see
Definition

2.2
) converges to the Ricci curvature in an appropriate limit. Van der Hoorn et al.
[
20
]
extended this result to graph ORC on random geometric graphs, where nodes are sampled uniformly at random from a manifold
M
and edges connect points within a small connectivity threshold
\epsilon
of each other. They proved that for pairs of points at prescribed distance
\delta\to 0
, a scaled version of ORC converges in probability to a constant multiple of the Ricci curvature of
M
. Trillos and Weber
[
18
]
strengthened these results by proving non-asymptotic bounds on the rate of convergence. These theoretical results establish that one can regard ORC as a sensible way to approximate the Ricci curvature of an underlying manifold from samples.

In many applications, it is desirable to have a definition of discrete scalar curvature
S\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
on the nodes
V
of a graph, so that one can study the node-wise properties of the graph. Given the relationship between Ricci and scalar curvature, it is natural to ask whether one can use discrete Ricci curvature to define discrete scalar curvature. Sandhu et al.
[
14
]
and Sreejith et al.
[
17
]
did just this by defining the discrete scalar curvature of a node
x
to be the sum (or mean, depending on which version one uses) of the discrete Ricci curvatures of the incident edges; the former used Forman-Ricci curvature, and the latter used Ollivier-Ricci curvature. However, it is not known whether either of these definitions converge to the scalar curvature of the underlying manifold.

Contributions

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a new definition of discrete scalar curvature and study its relationship to the scalar curvature of manifolds. Given a weighted graph
G
with edge weights
w(x,y)
, we define scalar Ollivier-Ricci curvature

(1.1)

\text{SORC}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\deg(x)}\sum_{y\text{ adjacent to }x}w(x,y)^{2}\kappa_{G}(x,y)

For an unweighted graph, one can calculate
\text{SORC}(x)
by setting
w(x,y)=1
for each edge. We use the version of Ollivier-Ricci curvature
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
that is defined in
Definition

2.4
.

This definition uses a weighted sum of the discrete Ricci curvatures of the incident edges, in contrast to the definitions of
[
14
]
and
[
17
]
. In particular, when the nodes are points on a manifold and the graph weights
w(x,y)
are their pairwise geodesic distances
d_{M}(x,y)
, our weighted sum puts higher weight on pairs of points that are farther away from each other.

There are two motivations for this definition. The first is that this weighting scheme is the correct scaling factor in order to obtain convergence to a constant multiple of scalar curvature, as we explain. The second is that we show that the relationship between
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
and Ricci curvature tends to deteriorate as
d_{M}(x,y)\to 0
; see
Proposition

4.9
.

Our main theorem says that a scaled version of
\text{SORC}(x)
converges to scalar curvature
S(x)
in probability. We assume that the nodes of our graph are sampled from a compact Riemannian manifold
M
of dimension
n
. Details about our random geometric graphs are given in
Section

2.2
.

Theorem 1.1

(
Theorem

5.8
)
.

As the number of nodes
N\to\infty
and the connection threshold
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
(at an appropriate rate) in a random geometric graph
G_{N}
, we have

\Big|\frac{2(n+2)^{2}}{\epsilon_{N}^{4}}\text{SORC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0

for a random node
x_{N}\in G_{N}
.

As part of establishing the main theorem, we prove two new results that clarify the convergence of Ollivier-Ricci curvature to Ricci curvature. In
Proposition

4.9
, we prove a non-asymptotic upper bound on the error between Ricci curvature and scaled Ollivier-Ricci curvature for all edges in a random geometric graph.

Proposition 1.2

(
Proposition

4.9
)
.

For a constant
\theta>0
that is sufficiently small, there are constants
C_{1,\theta},C_{2,\theta}
such that with probability at least
1-C_{1,\theta}N^{-\theta}
,

|\epsilon_{N}^{-2}\kappa_{G}(x,y)-\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})|\leq C_{2,\theta}\Big(\epsilon_{N}+\frac{\log(N)^{p_{n}}}{d_{M}(x,y)N^{1/n-2\alpha}}\Big)

for all edges
(x,y)\in E(G_{N})
, where
v_{xy}=\log_{x}(y)/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\log_{x}(y)\right\rVert}
.

This improves on the analogous upper bounds in
[
18
]
, which hold only for edges that are sufficiently short and sufficiently long. Similarly, the results in
[
20
]
hold only for a given edge with prescribed distance
\delta_{N}
; moreover, they only proved convergence in mean.

Additionally, we prove in
Proposition

4.11
that the scaled Ollivier-Ricci curvature of a random edge converges in probability to Ricci curvature. Again, we do not restrict to edges of certain lengths, unlike in
[
20
,
18
]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
x_{N}
be a node chosen uniformly at random from
G_{N}
and let
y_{N}
be an adjacent node chosen uniformly at random. As the number of nodes
N\to\infty
,

\epsilon_{N}^{-2}\kappa_{G}(x_{N},y_{N})\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\text{Ric}_{x_{N}}(v_{N},v_{N})\,,

where
v_{N}=\log_{x_{N}}(y_{N})/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\log_{x_{N}}(y_{N})\right\rVert}
.

Numerical experiments on samples from manifolds of known scalar curvature validate our theoretical results and give a sense of the rate of convergence.

2.
Background and Assumptions

The purpose of this section is to give a terse review of the key definitions used in the paper.

2.1.
Riemannian geometry and curvature

Let
M
be a compact
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold, where
n\geq 2
. The Riemannian metric allows us to define the norm
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert v\right\rVert}
of any vector
v
in the tangent space
T_{x}M
, for any point
x
in
M
. This in turn allows us to define the
geodesic distance

d_{M}(x,y)
between every pair
x,y
of points on the manifold.

A closed geodesic ball of radius
\epsilon
, centered at
x\in M
, is defined as

B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{y\in M\mid d_{M}(x,y)\leq\epsilon\}\,.

We denote its volume by
\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon}))
. We use
v_{n}
to denote the volume of an
n
-dimensional unit Euclidean ball.

The Ricci curvature at
x
is a bilinear map

\text{Ric}_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{x}M\times T_{x}M\to\mathbb{R}\,.

Equivalently, we can associate
\text{Ric}_{x}
with a linear transformation

\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}T_{x}M\to T_{x}M\qquad\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(v)=\sum_{i}\text{Ric}_{x}(v,e_{i})e_{i}\,,

where
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal basis for the vector space
T_{x}M
. Observe that the two definitions are related by the formula
\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(v)\cdot w=\text{Ric}_{x}(v,w)
for all
v,w\in T_{x}M
.

The scalar curvature at
x
is the trace of the Ricci curvature:

(2.1)

S(x)=\text{tr}(\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x})=\sum_{i}\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(e_{i})\cdot e_{i}\,.

2.2.
Random geometric graphs

Definition 2.1
.

A
random geometric graph
(RGG) on a manifold
M
is a weighted graph
G
where the nodes are labeled by points in
M
and an edge
(x,y)
exists if and only
d_{M}(x,y)
is less than a
connectivity threshold

\epsilon
. The weight of an edge
(x,y)
is the geodesic distance
d_{M}(x,y)
.

We denote the edge set of a graph
G
by
E(G)
. When two nodes
x,y
are adjacent, we write
x\sim y
.

Throughout this paper, let
G_{N}
be an RGG with
N
nodes and connection threshold
\epsilon_{N}
, whose vertices are sampled uniformly at random from
M
, where
\epsilon_{N}=N^{-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{6n})
. Let
\{x_{N}\}
be any sequence of nodes
x_{N}\in G_{N}
.

The shortest-path distance
d_{G}(x,y)
between two nodes
x,y\in G
is the (weighted) length of a shortest path between
x
and
y
.

2.3.
Ollivier-Ricci curvature

It is helpful to first define Ollivier-Ricci curvature for pairs of points on a manifold, and then to extend the definition to a discrete notion of curvature for edges in a graph. See
[
11
]
for more details.

Definition 2.2
.

[Ollivier-Ricci curvature on a manifold] Let
M
be a manifold, and let
\epsilon>0
. For every point
x\in M
, let
\mu_{x}^{M}
be the uniform probability measure on the ball
B^{M}(x,\epsilon)
. The
manifold Ollivier-Ricci curvature
of a pair
x,y
is

\kappa_{M}(x,y)=1-\frac{W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})}{d_{M}(x,y)}\,,

where the
1
-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}^{M}
is calculated with respect to geodesic distance
d_{M}
.

Ollivier
[
11
]
proved the following theorem, which says that manifold Ollivier-Ricci curvature converges to Ricci curvature as
\epsilon\to 0
and
d_{M}(x,y)\to 0
.

Theorem 2.3
.

\epsilon^{-2}\Big|\kappa_{M}(x,y)-\frac{\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})}{2(n+2)}\Big|=\mathcal{O}\Big(\epsilon+d_{M}(x,y)\Big)\,,

where
v_{xy}=\log_{x}(y)\in T_{x}M
.

Definition 2.4

(Ollivier-Ricci curvature on a graph)
.

Let
G
be a weighted graph with edge weights
w(x,y)
. For each node
x\in G
, let
\mu_{x}^{G}
be the uniform probability measure on its
1
-hop neighborhood, excluding
x
itself. The
Ollivier-Ricci curvature
of an edge
(x,y)
is

\kappa_{G}(x,y)=1-\frac{W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})}{w(x,y)},

where the
1
-Wasserstein distance
W_{1}^{G}
is calculated with respect to the shortest-path metric on the graph.

If
G
is unweighted, one can define
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
by taking the weight of each edge to be
1
. In our paper, the graphs that we consider are random geometric graphs, so the edge weights are the geodesic distances
d_{M}(x,y)
.

Remark 2.5
.

Ollivier-Ricci curvature is defined much more generally in
[
11

\{\mu_{x}^{G}\}

is
\kappa_{G}(x,y)=1-\frac{W_{1}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})}{w(x,y)}

are specifically the formulation in
Definition

2.4

3.
The mean Ricci curvature at a node converges to scalar curvature

We know from Riemannian geometry that scalar curvature
S(x)
is the trace of Ricci curvature at node
x
. (See
Equation

2.1
.) This leads us to define “discrete scalar Ricci curvature”

\text{SRC}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{\deg(x)}\sum_{y\text{ adj. to }x}\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(\log_{x}y)\cdot\log_{x}y\,,

for every vertex
x
. Unfortunately, the edges incident to a node
x
do not necessarily form an orthonormal basis for the tangent space
T_{x}(M)
, so
\text{SRC}(x)
is not equal to the trace of the Ricci curvature at
x
.

In this section, we use a modification of “Hutchinson’s trick”
[
7
]
—a method for estimating the trace of a linear operator—to prove that
\text{SRC}(x)
converges to
\text{tr}(\text{Ric}_{x})=S(x)
as
N\to\infty
(
Lemma

3.5
). Hutchinson’s trick says that if
A
is a symmetric matrix and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
are i.i.d. random variables with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, then
\mathbb{E}[\vec{u}^{T}A\vec{u}]=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(A)
. In this section, the linear transformation
\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}
plays the role of
A
, and
u_{i}=\log_{x}(z)\cdot e_{i}
, where
\{e_{i}\}
is a fixed choice of orthonormal basis and
z
is a point sampled uniformly at random from the ball
B^{M}(x,\epsilon)
. However, our random variables
u_{i}
do not satisfy Hutchinson’s assumptions. Below, we modify Hutchinson’s trick so that it works in our setting.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
A
is a symmetric matrix and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a random vector (not necessarily sampled uniformly at random) in
B^{E}(0,\epsilon)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\qquad\text{for all }i\,,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\qquad\text{for all }i\neq j

for some
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
as
\epsilon\to 0
. Then, as
\epsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u^{T}Au]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(A)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{1,1}\,,

\displaystyle\text{var}(u^{T}Au)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{1,1}^{2}\,.

Proof.

We begin by calculating
\mathbb{E}[u^{T}Au]
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u^{T}Au]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij}A_{ij}\Big(\delta_{ij}\sigma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}\text{tr}(A)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\cdot\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{1,1}\,.

Now we consider
\text{var}(u^{T}Au)
:

\displaystyle\text{var}(u^{T}Au)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(u^{T}Au)^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[u^{T}Au]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[(u^{T}Au)^{2}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,r,s}A_{ij}A_{rs}\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}u_{r}u_{s}]\,.

We have
\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}u_{r}u_{s}]\leq\epsilon^{4}
because
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
. Therefore,

\text{var}(u^{T}Au)\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\mathinner{\!\left\lVert A\right\rVert}_{1,1}^{2}\,.

∎

Let
x\in M
and let
z
be a point sampled uniformly at random from
B^{M}(x,\epsilon)
. Assume that
\epsilon
is smaller than the injectivity radius of
M
. Let
u=\log_{x}z\in T_{x}M
, and let
c_{M}(x,\epsilon)=v_{n}\epsilon^{n}/\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon}))
. We fix an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
for
T_{x}M
and define
u_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{i}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For sufficiently small
\epsilon
,

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]=c_{M}(x,\epsilon)\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{n+2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\,,

where
c_{M}(x,\epsilon)\to 1
as
\epsilon\to 0
(uniformly in
x
as long as
M
is compact).

Proof.

The expectation is

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}\int_{B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{i})^{2}\,dV(z)\,.

For sufficiently small
\epsilon
, the exponential map
\exp_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})\to B^{E}(0,\epsilon)\subseteq T_{x}M
and its inverse
\log_{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}B^{E}(0,\epsilon)\to B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})
are diffeomorphisms. In the geodesic normal coordinates
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
, we have

\int_{B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{i})^{2}dV(z)=\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}\sqrt{\det(g)}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}\,.

It is standard that

(3.1)

\sqrt{\det(g)}=1-\frac{1}{6}\text{Ric}_{x}(e_{k},e_{\ell})u_{k}u_{\ell}+\mathcal{O}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}^{3})\,.

Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}\Bigg[\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}du_{1}\cdots du_{n}-\frac{1}{6}\text{Ric}_{x}(e_{k},e_{\ell})\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}u_{k}u_{\ell}\,du_{1}\cdots u_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}\mathcal{O}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}^{3})\,du_{1}\cdots u_{n}\Bigg]\,.

Because
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
, we have
|u_{i}^{2}u_{k}u_{\ell}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})
and
|u_{i}^{2}\mathcal{O}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}^{3})|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{5})
, so

\displaystyle\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}u_{k}u_{\ell}\,du_{1}\cdots u_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\cdot\text{vol}(B^{E}(0,\epsilon))=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+4})

\displaystyle\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}\mathcal{O}(\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}^{3})\,du_{1}\cdots u_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{5})\cdot\text{vol}(B^{E}(0,\epsilon))=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+5})\,.

Therefore

(3.2)

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]=\frac{1}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}\Bigg(\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}\Bigg)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})

because
\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon}))=v_{n}\epsilon^{n}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+2})
.
Let
\mathcal{I}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}^{2}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}
. The integral
\mathcal{I}_{i}
does not depend on
i
, so

n\mathcal{I}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathcal{I}_{j}=\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}^{2}du_{1}\cdots du_{n}=\int_{0}^{\epsilon}r^{2}\cdot S_{r}\,dr\,,

where
S_{r}
is the surface area of an
(n-1)
-dimensional sphere of radius
r
. Therefore,

\mathcal{I}_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\epsilon}nv_{n}r^{n+1}\,dr=\frac{v_{n}}{n+2}\epsilon^{n+2}\,.

By
Equation

3.2
,

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}^{2}]=c_{M}(x,\epsilon)\cdot\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{n+2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\,.

The last statement of the lemma follows because

c_{M}(x,\epsilon)=\frac{v_{n}\epsilon^{n}}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon}))}=\frac{v_{n}\epsilon^{n}}{v_{n}\epsilon^{n}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+2})}\,.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For
i\neq j
,

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}]=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\,.

Proof.

The expectation is

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}]=\frac{1}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}\int_{B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{i})((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{j})\,dV(z)\,.

In the geodesic normal coordinates
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{B^{M}({x},{\epsilon})}((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{i})((\log_{x}z)\cdot e_{j})\,dV(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}u_{j}\sqrt{\det(g)}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}u_{j}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+4})

because
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert u\right\rVert}\leq\epsilon
. Because
u_{i}
and
u_{j}
are odd functions,

\int_{B^{E}(0,\epsilon)}u_{i}u_{j}\,du_{1}\cdots du_{n}=0\,,

so

\mathbb{E}[u_{i}u_{j}]=\frac{\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{n+4})}{\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon}))}=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{4})\,.

∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\{x_{N}\}
be a sequence of random nodes, with
x_{N}\in G_{N}
. Let
k_{N}=\deg(x_{N})
. Then

\lim_{N\to 0}\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k]=0

for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

The degree
k_{N}
has a binomial distribution
B(N-1,p_{N})
, where

p_{N}=\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon_{N}}))/\text{vol}(M).

Using the fact that
\text{vol}(B^{M}({x},{\epsilon_{N}}))=v_{n}\epsilon_{N}^{n}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{N}^{n+2})
as
N\to\infty
, we see that

\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]=(N-1)p_{N}\geq\frac{v_{N}}{2\,\text{vol}(M)}\cdot N\epsilon_{N}^{n}=N^{1-n\alpha}

for sufficiently large
N
. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]\to\infty
because we have stipulated that
1-n\alpha>0
.

By applying a Chernoff bound, one obtains
\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq(1-\delta)\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]]\leq\exp(-\delta^{2}\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]/2)
for any
0\leq\delta<1
(see e.g.,
[
19
]
). Substituting
\delta=1-k/\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]
yields

\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k]\leq\exp\Big(-\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]\Big(1-k/\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]\Big)^{2}/2\Big)

when
N
is sufficiently large such that
k<\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]
. Therefore,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k]=0
because
\mathbb{E}[k_{N}]\to\infty
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

As
N\to\infty
, we have

\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\Big]-S(x_{N})\to 0

and
\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\to 0
in probability.

Proof.

If
\deg(x_{N})=0
, then
\text{SRC}(x_{N})=0
. If
\deg(x_{N})=k>0
, the random variable
\text{SRC}(x_{N})
is a sample mean of
\{\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x_{N}}(\log_{x_{N}}z)\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
, where
{z_{i}}
are points sampled uniformly at random from
B^{M}({x},{\epsilon_{N}})
, so

(3.3)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})>0\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x_{N}}(\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{1}\Big]\,,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\text{var}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{(n+2)^{2}}{\epsilon_{N}^{4}}\cdot\frac{\text{var}(\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x_{N}}(\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})}{k}\,,

By
Lemma

3.1
,
Lemma

3.2
, and
Lemma

3.3
,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x_{N}}(\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{1}\Big]

\displaystyle=c_{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}{n+2}\cdot S(x_{N})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{N}^{2})\,,

(3.6)

\displaystyle\text{var}\Big(\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{N}^{4})\,,

where
c_{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})\to 1
as
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
. Together,
Equation

3.3
and
Equation

3.5
imply that

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\Big]-S(x_{N})\Big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\widehat{\text{Ric}}_{x}(\log_{x_{N}}z_{1})\cdot\log_{x_{N}}z_{1}\Big]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})>0]-S(x_{N})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq S(x_{N})\big|c_{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})\,\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})>0]-1\big|

as
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
, where
c_{M}(x_{n},\epsilon_{N})\to 1
as
N\to\infty
. By
Lemma

3.4
,
\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})>0]\to 1
as
N\to\infty
, so the right-hand side converges to
0
, which proves convergence of the expectation as
N\to\infty
.

Let
\delta>0
and
\xi>0
. We wish to show that

\mathbb{P}\Big[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\Big]<\xi

for sufficiently large
N
. In the next steps, we condition on the value of
\deg(x_{N})
.
Equation

3.3
and
Equation

3.5
imply that

\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Big]-S(x_{N})\Big|<\delta/2

for sufficiently large
N
and all
k>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-\mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Big]\Big|>\delta/2\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Bigg]\,.

By apply Chebyschev’s inequality to the right-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Bigg]\leq\frac{4}{\delta^{2}}\cdot\text{var}\Big(\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Big)\,.

By
Equation

3.4
and
Equation

3.6
, there is a constant
C
such that if
k>0
, then

(3.7)

\text{var}\Big(\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\text{SRC}(x_{N})\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Big)\leq\frac{C}{k}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\Bigg]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\mid\deg(x_{N})=k\Bigg]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})=k]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})=0]+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{4C}{\delta^{2}k}\cdot\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})=k]\,.

Choose
k_{*}
such that
C\delta^{-2}k_{*}^{-1}<\xi/2
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\Big|\frac{n+2}{\epsilon_{N}^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x_{N})-S(x_{N})\Big|>\delta\Bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})=0]+\frac{C}{\delta^{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k_{*}]+\sum_{k>k_{*}}\frac{C}{\delta^{2}k}\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}=k]

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\delta^{-2})\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k_{*}]+C\delta^{-2}k_{*}^{-1}\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}>k]

\displaystyle\leq(1+C\delta^{-2})\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k_{*}]+\xi/2\,.

By
Lemma

3.4
,
(1+C\delta^{-2})\cdot\mathbb{P}[k_{N}\leq k_{*}]<\xi/2
for sufficiently large
N
. Consequently,
\frac{n+2}{\epsilon^{2}}\cdot\text{SRC}(x)
converges in probability to
S(x)
as
N\to\infty
.
∎

4.
Convergence rate of Ollivier-Ricci graph curvature

The primary aim of the paper is to prove that a node’s scalar Ollivier-Ricci curvature (
eq.

1.1
) converges to scalar curvature in a suitable sense (
Theorem

5.8
). In
Section

3
, we completed the first step by showing that a node’s mean incident Ricci curvature
\text{SRC}(x)
converges to scalar curvature
S(x)
. To prove
Theorem

5.8
, we will show that
\text{SORC}(x)
converges to
\text{SRC}(x)
, up to a multiplicative constant. To do this, we must show that Ollivier-Ricci graph curvature is a sufficiently good approximation to Ricci curvature. For a pair
x,y
of adjacent nodes,
Theorem

2.3
shows that the error in the approximation is

|\epsilon^{-2}\kappa_{G}(x,y)-\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})|=\frac{|W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})|}{\epsilon^{3}}\cdot\frac{\epsilon}{d_{M}(x,y)}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon+d_{M}(x,y))\,,

where
v_{xy}=\log_{x}(y)\in T_{x}M
. Our proof of
Theorem

5.8
requires us to

(1)

prove that

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}\to 0

with high probability and

(2)

control the ratio
\epsilon/d_{M}(x,y)
, which may be arbitrarily large.

In this section, we focus on (1), which we prove in
Lemma

4.8
. In addition to showing that this quantity converges to
0
with high probability, we additionally give a bound on the rate of convergence. As immediate consequences of this intermediate work, we prove two new results (
Proposition

4.9
and
Proposition

4.11
) related to the convergence of
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
to the underlying manifold’s Ricci curvature, although we do not directly use these results in our proof of
Theorem

5.8
.

Our proof of
Lemma

4.8
is broken up into two main parts. First, we control the error that is due to the discrepancy between the geodesic metric on
M
and the shortest-path metric on
G_{N}
. We bound
|W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|
by applying results from
[
1
]
to bound the graph-distance discrepancy
|d_{G}(u,v)-d_{M}(u,v)|
. Second, we bound
|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})|
by bounding
\max_{z\in G}W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{z}^{G},\mu_{z}^{M})
, the
1
-Wasserstein distance between the measure
\mu_{z}^{G}
on neighboring nodes and the measure
\mu_{z}^{M}
on the manifold ball
B^{M}(z,\epsilon_{N})
.

4.1.
Bounding
|W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{x}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|

We first recall two results from Bernstein et al
[
1
]
, which together quantify the distortion in the graph-distance
d_{G}
approximation to geodesic distance
d_{M}
. Let
V
denote the volume of
M
.

Lemma 4.1
.

[Sampling Lemma in
[
1
]
]
Let
G
be an RGG with connection threshold
\epsilon
, and let
\beta>0
,
\lambda>0
. Then
M\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in G}B^{M}(z,\lambda)
with probability at least
1-\beta
, provided that

\frac{N}{\text{vol}(M)}>\log(\text{vol}(M)/\beta V_{\min}(\lambda/4))/V_{\min}(\lambda/2)\,,

where
V_{\min}(r)=\min_{x\in M}\text{vol}(B^{M}(x,r))
.

As noted in
[
1
]
, the quantity
V_{\min}(r)
is positive because
M
is compact.

Theorem 4.2
.

[Theorem 2 in
[
1
]
]
Let
G
be an RGG with connection threshold
\epsilon
. Suppose that
4\lambda<\epsilon
. If
M\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in G}B^{M}(z,\lambda)
, then

d_{M}(u,v)\leq d_{G}(u,v)\leq(1+4\lambda/\epsilon)d_{M}(u,v)

for all pairs
u,v\in G
.

Bernstein et al
[
1
]
apply
Lemma

4.1
and
Theorem

4.2
to obtain a probabilistic bound on the distortion of the graph-distance approximation to geodesic distance.

In
Lemma

4.3
and
Lemma

4.4
below, we apply the results of Bernstein et al
[
1
]
to obtain a probabilistic bound on the graph-distance discrepancy as
N\to\infty
and
\epsilon_{N}=N^{-\alpha}\to 0
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\beta_{N}=N^{-\theta}
and
\lambda_{N}=N^{-\omega}
, where
\theta,\omega>0
and
\theta+2n\omega<1
. Then for sufficiently large
N
, we have that
M\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in G_{N}}B^{M}(z,\lambda_{N})
with probability at least
1-\beta_{N}
.

Proof.

In order to apply
Lemma

4.1
(Sampling Lemma in
[
1
]
), we must show that

(4.1)

\frac{\log(\text{vol}(M)/\beta_{N}V_{\min}(\lambda_{N}/4))}{N\cdot V_{\min}(\lambda_{N}/2)}<\frac{1}{\text{vol}(M)}

for sufficiently large
N
. It suffices to show that the left-hand side is
o(1)
as
N\to\infty
.

For any
x\in M
, the volume of a geodesic ball
B^{M}(x,r)
is characterized by the scalar curvature
S(x)
:

\frac{\text{vol}(B^{M}(x,r))}{v_{n}r^{n}}=1-\frac{6}{n+2}S(x)r^{2}+\mathcal{O}(r^{4})

as
r\to 0
, where we recall that
n=\dim(M)
. Because
M
is compact, the scalar curvature is bounded, so
\text{vol}(B^{M}(x,r))\geq\frac{1}{2}v_{n}r^{n}
for all
x\in M
and sufficiently small
r
. Therefore there are positive constants
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
such that

(4.2)

\frac{\log(\text{vol}(M)/\beta_{N}V_{\min}(\lambda_{N}/4))}{N\cdot V_{\min}(\lambda_{N}/2)}\leq C_{1}\cdot\frac{\log(C_{2}/\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{n})}{N\lambda_{N}^{n}}

for sufficiently large
N
. We have

\log(C_{2}/\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{n})=o(1/\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{n})

as
N\to\infty
because
\log(x)/x\to 0
as
x\to\infty
and
\beta_{N}^{-1}\lambda_{N}^{-n}=N^{\theta+n\omega}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Therefore,

C_{1}\cdot\frac{\log(C_{2}/\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{n})}{N\lambda_{N}^{n}}=o\Big(\frac{1}{N\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{2n}}\Big)\,.

By choice of
\theta
and
\omega
, we have
N\beta_{N}\lambda_{N}^{2n}=N^{1-\theta-2n\omega}\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
. Together with
Equation

4.2
, this shows that
Equation

4.1
holds for sufficiently large
N
. Applying
Lemma

4.1
completes the proof.
∎

In
Lemma

4.4
, we apply the previous results to obtain a bound on the graph-distance discrepancy for pairs
u,v
of nodes that are neighbors of adjacent nodes
x,y
, respectively. These are the only relevant graph distances when we are calculating Ollivier-Ricci curvature. In the statement below, the parameter
\alpha<\frac{1}{6n}
controls the connection threshold
\epsilon_{N}=N^{-\alpha}
, and the parameter
\beta_{N}=N^{-\theta}
, where
\theta\in(0,1-6n\alpha)
, is the probability that the bound does not hold. The parameter
\lambda_{N}=N^{-\omega}
, where
\omega\in(3\alpha,\frac{1-\theta}{2n})
, controls the bound itself. The interval for
\theta
is nonempty because
\alpha<\frac{1}{6n}
, and the interval for
\omega
is nonempty because
\theta<1-6n\alpha
. Increasing the probability
1-\beta_{N}
(equivalently, increasing
\theta
) comes at the cost of increasing the bound
\lambda_{N}
(equivalently, decreasing
\omega
).

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\beta_{N}=N^{-\theta}
and
\lambda_{N}=N^{-\omega}
, where
\theta\in(0,1-6n\alpha)
and
\omega\in(3\alpha,\frac{1-\theta}{2n})
. Then for sufficiently large
N
,

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in B^{G}(x,\epsilon_{N})\\
v\in B^{G}(y,\epsilon_{N})\end{subarray}}|d_{M}(u,v)-d_{G}(u,v)|\leq 12\lambda_{N}\,,

with probability at least
1-\beta_{N}
, where the right-hand side is
o(\epsilon_{N}^{3})
because
\omega>3\alpha
.

Proof.

For sufficiently large
N
and probability at least
1-\beta_{N}
, we have
M\subseteq\bigcup_{z\in G_{N}}B^{M}(z,\lambda_{N})
by
Lemma

4.3
. Additionally,
4\lambda_{N}<\epsilon_{N}
for sufficiently large
N
because
\omega>\alpha
. By
Theorem

4.2
(Theorem 2 in
[
1
]
), we have

(4.3)

d_{G}(u,v)\leq(1+4\lambda_{N}/\epsilon_{N})d_{M}(u,v)

for all
u,v\in G_{N}
. For any adjacent vertices
x,y\in G_{N}
and any
u\in B^{G}(x,\epsilon_{N})
,
v\in B^{G}(y,\epsilon_{N})
, we have

d_{M}(u,v)\leq d_{M}(x,u)+d_{M}(x,y)+d_{M}(y,v)\leq 3\epsilon_{N}\,.

Therefore,

|d_{M}(u,v)-d_{G}(u,v)|\leq 12\lambda_{N}

by
Equation

4.3
.
∎

Lemma

4.5
, below, shows that
W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})
is a good approximation to
W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})
, which concludes the first part of
Section

4
. The work here is analogous to Appendix A.1 of
[
20
]
, with some minor corrections.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\beta_{N}=N^{-\theta}
, where
\theta\in(0,1-6n\alpha)
, and let
\omega\in(3\alpha,\frac{1-\theta}{2n})
. Then for sufficiently large
N
,

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}\leq\frac{12}{N^{\omega-3\alpha}}=o(1)

with probability at least
1-\beta_{N}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda_{N}=N^{-\omega}
. By
Lemma

4.4
,

(4.4)

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in B^{G}(x,\epsilon_{N})\\
v\in B^{G}(y,\epsilon_{N})\end{subarray}}|d_{M}(u,v)-d_{G}(u,v)|\leq 12\lambda_{N}\,,

with probability at least
1-\beta_{N}
. Therefore,

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}\leq\frac{12\lambda_{N}}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}=\frac{12}{N^{\omega-3\alpha}}\,.

∎

4.2.
Bounding
|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{x}^{G})-W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})|

As outlined at the beginning, the second part of this section is to bound the error that is introduced by the Wasserstein distance between
\mu_{z}^{G}
and
\mu_{z}^{M}
, where
z
is any node in
G
. We start by recalling the following result from
[
6
]
, where we define
p_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\begin{cases}3/4\,,&n=2\\
1/n\,,&n\geq 3\end{cases}
.

Theorem 4.6

(Theorem 2 in
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mu
be the uniform probability measure on
M
and
\mu_{N}
the empirical probability measure
\mu_{N}
on the nodes of
G_{N}
. Then for any
s>0
, there are constants
A_{M,s}^{\prime}
,
C_{M,s}
and a transport map
T_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}M\to X_{N}
such that

\sup_{x\in M}d(x,T_{N}(x))\leq A_{M,s}^{\prime}\frac{\log(N)^{p_{n}}}{N^{1/n}}

with probability at least
1-C_{M,s}N^{-s}

The benefit of
Theorem

4.6
is that we can leverage the transport map
T_{N}
to obtain a bound on
W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{z}^{M},\mu_{z}^{G})
. However, note that
T_{N}
does not necessarily map

Lemma 4.7
.

For any

\displaystyle\leq 1-\mu_{z}^{M}(B_{N}^{-})+|\hat{\mu}_{z}^{M}(B_{N}^{-})-\mu_{z}^{M}(B_{N}^{-})|

We are now ready to bound

Lemma 4.8
.

Let

We have

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}+\frac{W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{x}^{M})+W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{y}^{G},\mu_{y}^{M})}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}+2\max_{z\in G_{N}}W_{1}(\mu_{z}^{G},\mu_{z}^{M})\,.

\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}\leq\frac{12}{N^{\omega-3\alpha}}+2A_{M,\theta}\frac{\log(N)^{p_{n}}}{N^{1/n-3\alpha}}

∎

4.3.
Bounds on the error between Ollivier-Ricci curvature and Ricci curvature

Lemma

4.8
is all that we need for

, the main result of our paper. However, we can also use it to derive two new convergence results about Ollivier-Ricci curvature.

The first,

4.9
, proves that we can bound the rate of convergence
\kappa_{G}(x,y)\to\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})
, in terms of
d_{M}(x,y)
, for all edges. For
d_{M}(x,y)
close to
\epsilon_{N}
, the largest possible value, the bound is strongest. As
d_{M}(x,y)\to 0
, the bound becomes weaker; in other words,
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
has little relation to Ricci curvature when
(x,y)
is very short relative to the connection threshold
\epsilon_{N}
. Our intuition is that when
d_{M}(x,y)
is very small, the
1
-hop neighborhoods of
x
and
y
will have substantial overlap, leading to positive Ollivier-Ricci curvature regardless of the manifold’s curvature.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\theta\in(0,1-6n\alpha)
. There are constants

such that with probability at least

|\epsilon_{N}^{-2}\kappa_{G}(x,y)-\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})|\leq C_{2,\theta}\Big(\epsilon_{N}+\frac{\log(N)^{p_{n}}}{d_{M}(x,y)N^{1/n-2\alpha}}\Big)

for all edges
(x,y)\in E(G_{N})
, where
v_{xy}=\log_{x}(y)/\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\log_{x}(y)\right\rVert}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

2.3
,

|\epsilon_{N}^{-2}\kappa_{M}(x,y)-\text{Ric}_{x}(v_{xy},v_{xy})|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{N})\,.

By
Lemma

4.8
, there are constants
A_{M,\theta}
,
C_{M,\theta}
such that

\displaystyle\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\epsilon_{N}^{-2}|\kappa_{G}(x,y)-\kappa_{M}(x,y)|

\displaystyle=\max_{(x,y)\in E(G_{N})}\frac{|W_{1}^{M}(\mu_{x}^{M},\mu_{y}^{M})-W_{1}^{G}(\mu_{x}^{G},\mu_{y}^{G})|}{\epsilon_{N}^{3}}\cdot\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leq 3A_{M,\theta}\frac{\log(N)^{p_{n}}}{d_{M}(x,y)N^{1/n-2\alpha}}

with probability at least
1-(1+C_{M,\theta})N^{-\theta}
.
∎

showed that a bound holds for

5.
Main Results

Finally, we prove that
\text{SORC}(x)
converges in a suitable sense to the scalar curvature
S(x)
. As we sketched at the beginning of
Section

4
, the last remaining ingredient is to control the size of the ratio
\epsilon/d_{M}(x,y)
for pairs
x,y
of adjacent nodes. The ratio is a random variable that takes values in
[1,\infty)
. We prove in
Lemma

5.6
that for a random node

, the mean ratio
\frac{1}{deg(x_{N})}\sum_{y\sim x_{N}}\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x_{N},y)}
converges to a constant as
N\to\infty
. For dimensions
n\geq 3
, we do this by applying a concentration-inequality argument. Unfortunately, in dimension
n=2
, the variance of the ratio is infinite (see
Lemma

5.5
), so the proof requires more care.

5.1.
The ratio
\epsilon/d_{M}(x,y)

Suppose that
x_{N}
is a node chosen uniformly at random from the graph
G_{N}
. We define

T_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\deg(x_{N})}\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x_{N},y_{N}^{i})}\,,

where
y_{N}^{1},\ldots,y_{N}^{\deg(x_{N})}
are the neighbors of
x_{N}
.

The degree of
x_{N}
is a random variable, so it is easier to study the sum of a fixed number
k
of random variables with the same probability distribution. Because the neighbors
y
of
x_{N}
are uniformly distributed in
B^{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})
, we have that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x_{N},y)}\leq z\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x_{N},p)}\leq z\Big]\,,

where
p
is a point sampled uniformly at random from
B^{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})
. Letting
\{p_{N}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of points sampled uniformly at random from
B^{M}(x_{N},\epsilon_{N})
, we define

\displaystyle Z_{N}^{i}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\epsilon_{N}}{d_{M}(x_{N},p_{N}^{i})}

\displaystyle S_{N,k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Z_{N}^{i}\,.

We define
\mu_{N}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{N}^{1}]
to be the mean of one of the ratios. Recall that we defined the random variable
Z
with CDF defined by
Equation

4.12
. Let
\mu=\mathbb{E}[Z]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mu_{N}=\mu=\frac{n}{n-1}\,.

Proof.

Because
Z_{N}^{1}
and
Z
are non-negative,

|\mu_{N}-\mu|=\Big|\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{Z}(z)-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]\,dz\Big|\,,

where
F_{Z}(z)
is the cdf for
Z
defined by
Equation

4.12
. We have
Z_{N}^{1}\geq 1
, so
\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]=0=F_{Z}(z)
for

. Therefore,

|\mu_{N}-\mu|=\Big|\int_{1}^{\infty}F_{Z}(z)-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]dz\Big|\leq\int_{1}^{\infty}|F_{Z}(z)-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]|\,dz\,.

By
Lemma

4.10
, there is a constant
C>0
such that

|\mu_{N}-\mu|\leq C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}z^{-n}\,dz=\frac{C\epsilon_{N}^{2}}{n-1}

for sufficiently large

. The right-hand side approaches

as

because
\epsilon_{N}\to 0
.

The mean

\mathbb{E}[Z]=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-F_{Z}(z))\,dz=1+\int_{1}^{\infty}z^{-n}\,dz=\frac{n}{n-1}\,.

Lemma 5.2
.

If

Proof.

By

|\mathbb{E}[(Z_{N}^{1})^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[Z^{2}]|=\Big|\int_{1}^{\infty}\Big(F_{Z}(\sqrt{z})-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq\sqrt{z}]\Big)\,dz\Big|\,.

Lemma 5.3
.

If

Proof.

Lemma 5.6

Proof.

Let
\eta>0
and

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[|T_{N}/\deg(x_{N})-\mu|>\eta]<\xi\,.

for sufficiently large

\mathbb{P}[|T_{N}/\deg(x_{N})-\mu|>\eta

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|T_{N}/\deg(x_{N})-\mu_{N}|>\eta]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[|T_{N}/\deg(x_{N})-\mu_{N}|>\eta\,|\,\deg(x_{N})=k]\cdot\mathbb{P}[\deg(x_{N})=k]

, there is a constant

and integers

Lemma 5.7
.

As

7.
Conclusions

In this paper, we defined “scalar Ollivier-Ricci curvature,” a function
\text{SORC}(x)
on the nodes of a weighted graph. The scalar Ollivier-Ricci curvature at a node
x
is a weighted sum of the Ollivier-Ricci curvature of each incident edge, where higher-weight edges are given higher weight in the
\text{SORC}(x)
sum. We proved (
Theorem

5.8
) that
\text{SORC}(x)
converges in probability to a scaled version of scalar curvature, as the number of nodes
N\to\infty
in a random geometric graph.

Somewhat counterintuitively, our definition of
\text{SORC}(x)
places higher weight on
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
when
x
and
y
are farther apart (but still connected) in a random geometric graph. This reflects what we see in
Proposition

4.9
(and also
[
20
,
18
]
); when
x
and
y
are too close together, the error grows between Ollivier-Ricci curvature and Ricci curvature. If
x
and
y
are very close, then their
1
-hop neighborhoods have significant overlap, so their edge is likely to have positive Ollivier-Ricci curvature
\kappa_{G}(x,y)
, regardless of the true underlying curvature of the manifold. On the other hand, if
x
and
y
are a little farther apart, but still close enough to be connected, then curvature plays a larger role in the connectivity of their respective neighborhoods, so that Ollivier-Ricci curvature is a good approximation to Ricci curvature.

Our theoretical analysis assumes a framework that is not always true in real-world networks, so we suggest the following pre-processing steps when our assumptions are violated. First, our theoretical analysis assumes that edge weights are geodesic distances, so that edges with lower weight correspond to points that are closer to each other. However, it is often the opposite case in real-world networks where higher weight indicates a stronger connection. In those cases, we suggest applying a monotonically decreasing function
f(w)
to the weights
w
in order to obtain weights
\tilde{w}
in which lower weight indicates a closer connection. Second, our theoretical analysis assumes that there is a connectivity threshold
\epsilon
at which points
x,y
are not connected by an edge if their geodesic distance is greater than
\epsilon
. In real-world networks, one should apply a threshold so that each node is only connected to other nodes that have a reasonably strong connection with it—or to nodes that are reasonably close to it, depending on context. Otherwise, our definition of
\text{SORC}(x)
will put very high weight on edges that are not truly relevant to
x
. With these two pre-processing steps, one can sensibly calculate scalar Ollivier-Ricci curvature on real-world networks that are not necessarily random geometric graphs.

Technical Details

Define the random variables
Z_{N}^{i},Z
and their respective means
\mu_{N},\mu
as in
Section

5.1
. For every integer
k
, define the truncated random variables

\displaystyle\overline{Z_{N,k}^{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z_{N}^{i}\mathbf{1}_{|Z_{N}^{i}|\leq k}\,,

\displaystyle\overline{Z_{k}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Z\mathbf{1}_{|Z|\leq k}

and their respective means

\displaystyle\mu_{N,k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}[Z_{N,k}^{1}]\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}[\overline{Z_{k}}]\,.

Define the sum
S_{N,k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}Z_{N}^{i}
as in
Section

5.1
, and define the analogous truncation sum

\overline{S_{N,k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\overline{Z_{N,k}^{i}}\,.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\eta>0
. There are
N_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
and
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
N\geq N_{*}
and
k\geq k_{*}
, we have

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu|<\eta\,.

Proof.

For any
N
and
k
,

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{N}|\leq|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{k}|+|\mu_{k}-\mu|\,.

The second term corresponds to convergence in the Euclidean case as the truncation parameter
k\to\infty
. There is no
N
dependence. Because
Z
is a non-negative random variable,

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[Z>z]\,dz

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z\leq k}>z]\,dz\,.

We note that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z\leq k}>z]=\mathbb{P}[Z>z]
, and the latter is an upper bound at each
k
. By the dominated convergence theorem,
|\mu_{k}-\mu|\to 0
as
k\to\infty
.

We now turn to the first term,
|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{N}|
. Because
\overline{Z_{N,k}}
and
\overline{Z_{k}}
are non-negative random variables, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{N,k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{N,k}}>z]\,dz\,,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{k}}>z]\,dz\,.

Therefore,

\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{k}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{k}}\leq z]-\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{N,k}}\leq z]\,\,dz.

For any non-negative random variable
Y
and its truncation
\overline{Y_{k}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=Y\mathbf{1}_{|Y|\leq k}
, we have

\mathbb{P}[\overline{Y_{k}}\leq y]=\begin{cases}\mathbb{P}[Y\leq k]-\mathbb{P}[Y\leq y]\,,&y\leq k\,,\\
1\,,&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Therefore,

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{k}|=\int_{0}^{k}|\mathbb{P}[Z\leq k]-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq k]|+|\mathbb{P}[Z\leq z]-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]|\,dz\,.

Because
Z_{N}^{1}\geq 1
and
Z\geq 1
,

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{k}|=\int_{1}^{k}|\mathbb{P}[Z\leq k]-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq k]|+|\mathbb{P}[Z\leq z]-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]|\,dz\,.

By
Lemma

4.10
, there is a constant
C
such that for sufficiently large
N
,

|\mathbb{P}[Z\leq z]-\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}\leq z]|\leq C\epsilon_{N}^{2}/z^{n}

for all
z\geq 1
(including
z=k
). Therefore,

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu_{k}|\leq C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\Big(k^{-n+1}+\int_{1}^{k}z^{-n}\,dz\Big)=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon_{N}^{2}k^{-n+1})\,,

which implies the lemma.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

\lim_{z\to\infty}z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]=0\,.

Proof.

This is a standard proof (see e.g., Theorem 2.2.14 in
[
3
]
), which we include here for convenience. For any
z
,

z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>z}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>\lfloor z\rfloor}]\,.

Therefore, it suffices to show that
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>m}]=0
, where
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let
f_{m}(z)=\mathbb{P}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>m}>z]
and let
g(z)=\mathbb{P}[Z>z]
. Because
Z
is non-negative,
\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>m}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{m}(z)\,dz
. The function
g(z)
is an upper bound for
f_{m}(z)
, and it is integrable because
\int_{0}^{\infty}\,g(z)\,dz=\mathbb{E}[Z]<\infty
. Additionally,
\lim_{m\to\infty}f_{m}(z)\leq\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[Z>m]=0
, so by the dominated convergence theorem,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[Z\mathbf{1}_{Z>m}]=0
.
∎

.

Let

. There are

and

Lemma A.4
.

Let
\xi>0
. Then there is a constant
C
, an integer
N_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, and an integer
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for

and

Proof.

By Lemma 2.2.13 in

k^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}2z\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{N,k}}>z]\,dz=k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[\overline{Z_{N,k}}>z]\,dz\leq k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]\,dz\,.

We note that

k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]\,dz\leq k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z|\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]-\mathbb{P}[Z>z]|\,dz+k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]\,dz\,.

Putting the equations above together,

(A.1)

k^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Z_{N,k}})^{2}]\leq k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z|\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]-\mathbb{P}[Z>z]|dz+k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]dz\,.

When
z\in[0,1]
, we have
\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]=\mathbb{P}[Z>z]=1
because
Z_{N}^{1}\geq 1
and
Z\geq 1
. By
Lemma

4.10
, there is a constant
C
such that

|\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]-\mathbb{P}[Z>z]|\leq C\epsilon_{N}^{2}z^{-n}

for sufficiently large
N
and all
z\geq 1
. Therefore,

k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z|\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]-\mathbb{P}[Z>z]|\,dz=k^{-1}\int_{1}^{k}2z|\mathbb{P}[Z_{N}^{1}>z]-\mathbb{P}[Z>z]|\,dz\leq 2C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\int_{1}^{k}z^{-n+1}\,dz\,.

The worst-case scenario is
n=2
, in which case the right-hand side is

2C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\log(k)\,,

so by
Equation

A.1
,

k^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Z_{N,k}})^{2}]\leq 2C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\log(k)+k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]\,dz\,.

By a change of variables to the integral on the right-hand side,

k^{-1}\int_{0}^{k}2z\mathbb{P}[Z>z]\,dz=\int_{0}^{1}2kz\mathbb{P}[Z>kz]\,dz\,.

The right-hand side is independent of
N
and goes to
0
as
k\to\infty
by the dominated convergence theorem, so it is bounded above by
\xi
for sufficiently large
k
and all
N
.

∎

Lemma A.5
.

Let
\eta>0
and
\xi>0
. Then there is a constant
C
, an integer
N_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
, and an integer
k_{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for
N\geq N_{*}
and
k\geq k_{*}
,

\mathbb{P}[|S_{N,k}/k-\mu|>\eta]<\xi+C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\log(k)\,.

Proof.

By
Lemma

A.1
, there are
N_{1}
and
k_{1}
such that if
N\geq N_{1}
and
k\geq k_{1}
, then

|\mu_{N,k}-\mu|<\eta/2\,,

so

\mathbb{P}[|S_{N,k}/k-\mu|>\eta]<\mathbb{P}[|S_{N,k}/k-\mu_{N,k}|>\eta/2]\,.

Then we have

\mathbb{P}[|S_{N,k}/k-\mu_{N,k}|>\eta/2]\leq\mathbb{P}[S_{N,k}\neq\overline{S_{N,k}}]+\mathbb{P}[|\overline{S_{N,k}}/k-\mu_{N,k}|>\eta/2]\,.

We can upper bound
\mathbb{P}[S_{N,k}\neq\overline{S_{N,k}}]
by observing that
S_{N,k}\neq\overline{S_{N,k}}
only if
|Z_{N}^{i}|>k
for some
1\leq i\leq k
. By applying a union bound, we have that

\mathbb{P}[S_{N,k}\neq\overline{S_{N,k}}]\leq k\mathbb{P}[|Z_{N}^{1}|>k]
. Therefore, by
Lemma

A.3
, there are
N_{2}>N_{1}
and
k_{2}>k_{1}
such that

\mathbb{P}[S_{N,k}\neq\overline{S_{N,k}}]<\xi/2

for all
N>N_{2}
and
k>k_{2}
.

By Chebyschev’s inequality and straightforward simplification,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|\overline{S_{N,k}}/k-\mu_{N,k}|>\eta/2]

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta^{-2}\text{var}(\overline{S_{N,k}}/k)

\displaystyle=4\eta^{-2}k^{-1}\text{var}(\overline{Z_{N,k}})

\displaystyle\leq 4\eta^{-2}k^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Z_{N,k}})^{2}]\,.

By
Lemma

A.4
, there is a constant
C
and integers
N_{3}\geq N_{2}
,
k_{3}\geq k_{2}
such that

4\eta^{-2}k^{-1}\mathbb{E}[(\overline{Z_{N,k}})^{2}]<\xi/2+C\epsilon_{N}^{2}\log(k)\,.

for all
N\geq N_{3}
and
k\geq k_{3}
, which concludes the proof.
∎
