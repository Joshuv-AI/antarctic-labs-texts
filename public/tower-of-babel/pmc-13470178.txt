Metastatic pulmonary calcification mimicking refractory pneumonia in an end stage renal disease patient: A diagnostic pitfall
Authors: Jiun-Chi Chuang, Si-Wei Liou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetastatic pulmonary calcification may mimic infectious lung disease in patients with end-stage renal disease, resulting in delayed diagnosis and repeated ineffective treatment. We report a 71-year-old hemodialysis patient with persistent dyspnea and progressive pulmonary infiltrates repeatedly treated as pneumonia without improvement. CT demonstrated high-attenuation consolidation and diffuse ground-glass opacities, while preserved oxygen saturation despite extensive radiographic abnormalities argued against severe infection. Technetium-99m methylene diphosphonate bone scintigraphy later confirmed metastatic pulmonary calcification associated with secondary hyperparathyroidism. This case highlights the importance of recognizing clinical–radiologic mismatch and the diagnostic value of Technetium-99m methylene diphosphonate bone scintigraphy in metastatic pulmonary calcification.Keywords: Metastatic pulmonary calcification, End stage renal disease, Secondary hyperparathyroidism, Technetium-99m MDP, Pulmonary infiltrates

Introduction

Metastatic pulmonary calcification (MPC) is an uncommon disease mainly seen in end-stage renal disease patients, especially those with severe secondary hyperparathyroidism [1]. Most patients with MPC are asymptomatic and are found incidentally [2]. Although high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT) has improved recognition of MPC by demonstrating characteristic imaging findings, these features are nonspecific and frequently overlap with those of pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or malignancy, making diagnosis challenging [1,3,4]

Here, we report a patient with end-stage renal disease who presented with persistent pulmonary infiltrates repeatedly treated as pneumonia. The diagnosis of MPC was delayed for several months and was ultimately confirmed by bone scintigraphy. This case highlights the importance of keeping MPC in mind when pneumonia remains repeatedly unresponsive in ESRD patients.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old female with a history of end-stage renal disease on regular hemodialysis with secondary hyperparathyroidism was admitted due to persistent dyspnea. Three months just before her current admission, she had been hospitalized for the same reason. At that time, antibiotic therapy was initiated for pneumonia due to bilateral pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography. CT was also done then for further evaluation. A 5.1 cm area of consolidation with high attenuation in the right upper lobe and scattered ground-glass opacity in the bilateral lungs was noticed. However, since her symptoms did not gradually worsen, she was still discharged, though radiographic abnormalities and clinical symptoms persisted. After that hospitalization, the patient experienced repeated admissions and discharges up to 3 times, and each time she was treated as having pneumonia. However, no significant clinical improvement was observed.

During this admission, antibiotics were prescribed again. Due to paradoxical breathing, an oxygen supplement was given this time; a respiratory rate reached 40 times. However, her oxygen saturation remained 99% on room air, and she was alert and oriented, making severe pneumonia less likely. Follow-up chest radiography demonstrated worsening diffuse bilateral infiltrates compared to past imaging (Fig. 1). Microbiological investigations, including blood and sputum cultures, cryptococcus and aspergillus antigens, sputum acid fast stain for TB were all negative. A repeat chest CT showed that the 5.1 cm high-attenuation consolidation in the right upper lobe remained unchanged in size; malignancy became less likely since the lesion remained stable in size. In addition, diffuse bilateral ground-glass opacities became more extensive compared to the CT done 3 months ago (Fig. 2). Despite the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics such as meropenem and vancomycin, there was no significant clinical or radiographic improvement.

Due to the lack of clinical and radiographic response to repeated antimicrobial therapy, a noninfectious etiology was then suspected. Considering her secondary hyperparathyroidism history with recent laboratory data revealing elevated parathyroid hormone levels (755.4 pg/mL), hyperphosphatemia (7.4 mg/dL), and a corrected serum calcium level of 10.3 mg/dL, a metabolic disorder was then suspected. Reviewing the image, the areas of high-attenuation consolidation, particularly in the right upper lobe, were noted to be suggestive of calcific deposition rather than typical inflammatory consolidation. In addition, the progressive worsening of diffuse ground-glass opacities without corresponding clinical deterioration further argued against an infectious process. Hence, Technetium-99m methylene diphosphonate (Tc-99m MDP) bone scintigraphy was subsequently performed and demonstrated diffuse radiotracer uptake in both lung fields (Fig. 3), consistent with metastatic pulmonary calcification.

Tc-99m MDP bone scintigraphy confirming metastatic pulmonary calcification. Whole-body Tc-99m MDP bone scintigraphy demonstrated diffuse radiotracer uptake in both lungs, consistent with metastatic pulmonary calcification.

The patient was subsequently evaluated for management of refractory secondary hyperparathyroidism. As there was no urgent indication for surgery, she was referred to general surgery for evaluation of parathyroidectomy.

Discussion

MPC is an uncommon, underdiagnosed complication in patients with end-stage renal disease. According to autopsy studies, up to 60%-75% of these patients receiving dialysis may have MPC, suggesting that the condition is much more prevalent [1,2]. The nonspecific clinical and radiologic presentation of MPC is one of challenges in diagnosis, since imaging results may resemble more common conditions like pneumonia, pulmonary edema, or even cancer [1,3]. In this case, the patient was repeatedly treated for presumed pneumonia. However, little radiographic improvement and the lack of response to antibiotic therapy should have prompted an earlier reevaluation of the diagnosis. Similar diagnostic delays have been reported in previous studies, emphasizing the need for more clinical awareness [4,5].

The discrepancy between severe radiographic abnormalities and comparatively preserved oxygen saturation with a maintained stable mental status was a significant breakthrough point of this case. This clinical–radiologic mismatch is atypical for severe infectious pneumonia and may serve as a useful clue to alternative diagnoses such as MPC [6]. Limited impairment of gas exchange is thought to contribute to the preserved oxygen saturation observed in MPC [7,8]. On the other hand, dyspnea in some MPC cases including this case may be better explained by a restrictive ventilatory pattern and increased work of breathing resulting from diffuse pulmonary calcification, which reduces lung compliance rather than primarily impairing oxygen exchange [1].

Another pitfall causing this case to be difficult to diagnose is the non-specific characteristics of MPC on computed tomography. Although CT is more sensitive than chest radiography for detecting pulmonary calcification [9,10], its imaging findings may still mimic other respiratory diseases. Common patterns of MPC on CT include ground-glass opacities, centrilobular nodules, and high-attenuation consolidation, mainly in the upper lobes [1,3]. Our patient demonstrated 2 of these characteristic patterns, namely persistent high-attenuation consolidation in the right upper lobe and progressive bilateral ground-glass opacities. However, these characteristic features are not unique to MPC. Ground-glass opacities are nonspecific and may mimic infectious pneumonia, including COVID-19, influenza, RSV, or fungal pneumonia [9,10]. Similarly, centrilobular nodules may be mistaken for respiratory bronchiolitis (commonly seen in smokers), or hypersensitivity pneumonitis [11,12]. As for increased attenuation, while calcification was thought to be the main etiology, it may also result from iodine, barium, or other radiopaque foreign materials [13]. In addition, high-attenuation pulmonary lesions should also be differentiated from calcified pulmonary tumors, such as pulmonary hamartoma, calcified metastases (particularly from osteosarcoma or chondrosarcoma), carcinoid tumors, and calcified primary lung cancer, as well as healed granulomatous disease [14,15]. Although contrast-enhanced CT was performed in our patient because pulmonary malignancy was initially suspected, noncontrast CT is generally preferred when pulmonary calcification is suspected. Calcifications are more easily recognized on noncontrast CT [16], while contrast-enhanced blood vessels may obscure subtle calcifications or make them difficult to differentiate from adjacent vascular structures [17]. Despite these differences, neither contrast-enhanced nor non-contrast CT findings are diagnostic of MPC by themselves. Imaging findings should always be interpreted in conjunction with the patient's clinical presentation and metabolic abnormalities. Therefore, recognition of MPC on CT requires a high index of suspicion, particularly in patients with known abnormalities in calcium–phosphate metabolism.

The pathogenesis of MPC is multifactorial and is closely related to disturbances in calcium–phosphate homeostasis associated with chronic kidney disease–mineral and bone disorder (CKD-MBD). In patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, persistent hyperphosphatemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and an elevated calcium–phosphate (Ca × P) product promote precipitation of calcium phosphate crystals in otherwise normal tissues [1]. Intermittent metabolic alkalosis following hemodialysis may further facilitate calcium salt deposition, while uremia itself may alter tissue proteins and increase their susceptibility to calcification. Vitamin D supplementation has also been reported as a potential contributing factor in some dialysis patients [1]. These metabolic disturbances not only lead to pulmonary calcification but are also associated with renal osteodystrophy, vascular calcification, and metastatic calcification involving various soft tissues [18]. The lungs are particularly susceptible because the upper lobes have a relatively alkaline microenvironment resulting from a higher ventilation–perfusion ratio and lower carbon dioxide tension, which favors calcium phosphate precipitation and explains the characteristic upper-lobe predominance of MPC [2]. Consequently, the calcium–phosphate (Ca × P) product serves as a simple and useful predictor of metastatic calcification. When the Ca × P product exceeds 70 mg²/dL², metastatic calcification should be strongly suspected [19]. In our patient, the calculated Ca×P product was 76.2 mg²/dL², exceeding the threshold associated with an increased risk of metastatic calcification.

Tc-99m MDP bone scintigraphy, in contrast, provides a more sensitive role for detecting metastatic calcification [20,21]. Unlike CT, which identifies structural changes, bone scintigraphy detects active calcium deposition and may demonstrate diffuse pulmonary uptake even when CT findings are inconclusive [22]. Furthermore, since CKD-MBD is a systemic metabolic disorder, the whole-body imaging capability of bone scintigraphy enables identification of calcification in multiple organs, supporting the diagnosis of a systemic metabolic process rather than an isolated pulmonary disease [23]. These advantages make bone scintigraphy a valuable diagnostic tool in suspected cases of MPC.

Management of secondary hyperparathyroidism includes optimization of dialysis, control of calcium–phosphate balance, use of calcimimetic agents, and parathyroidectomy in some refractory cases [24]. Since MPC is a metabolic complication of this condition, treatment primarily focuses on correcting the underlying metabolic disorder [7]. The prognosis for MPC is generally benign and reversible if the underlying metabolic imbalance is identified and treated quickly. While often asymptomatic, untreated cases can progressively worsen, leading to severe respiratory failure and a poorer prognosis. Previous reports have shown that MPC may be partially relieved after effective control of hyperparathyroidism, although irreversible changes may persist in some patients [[25], [26], [27]].

In conclusion, MPC should be considered in patients with end-stage renal disease who present with persistent or progressive pulmonary infiltrates that do not respond to conventional therapy. Recognition of clinical–radiologic mismatch, awareness of metabolic risk factors, and appropriate use of bone scintigraphy are essential for timely diagnosis and management.
