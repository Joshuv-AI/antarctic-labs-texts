Pre-mRNA splicing regulates cellular dedifferentiation via lipid metabolism in a cytokinin-dependent manner in Arabidopsis
Authors: Ami Takeuchi, Toshiki Ishikawa, Toshihiro Arae, Junichi Togami, Kenji Nagamiya, Koji Koizumi, Takuyuki Ikeda, Shingo Nagawa, Iwai Ohbayashi, Munetaka Sugiyama, Misato Ohtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlant cells exhibit high plasticity for proliferation and differentiation, and pre-mRNA splicing makes an important contribution to this plasticity. Here, we show that the gene responsible for root redifferentiation defective4-1 (rrd4-1), a temperature-sensitive Arabidopsis thaliana mutant with defects in adventitious rooting and callus formation from hypocotyls, encodes a homolog of yeast Ntr1, which is involved in pre-mRNA splicing. Defective callus formation in rrd4-1 at the restrictive temperature of 28 °C depended on the presence of kinetin (a synthetic cytokinin) in the callus-inducing medium. RNA-seq analysis revealed that genes involved in secondary cell wall biogenesis were upregulated, whereas those involved in cell cycle progression were downregulated, in a cytokinin-dependent manner in rrd4-1 at 28 °C. Moreover, kinetin and the rrd4-1 mutation had broad effects on alternative splicing, particularly on lipid metabolism genes such as PTPLA, SPHK2, and ATNCER1. Consistent with this result, levels of very-long-chain fatty acid (VLCFA)-type sphingolipids were reduced in rrd4-1, and kinetin affected their contents during callus induction. Kinetin enhanced the inhibitory effect of the lipid synthesis inhibitor cerulenin on callus formation, and rrd4-1 was hypersensitive to cerulenin. Together, our data suggest that pre-mRNA splicing regulates cytokinin-mediated cellular dedifferentiation through the regulation of lipid metabolism gene splicing.

Introduction

Plant organs have a remarkable ability to regenerate, due to the high plasticity of plant cell proliferation and differentiation. Previous studies have suggested that plant regeneration proceeds through 3 stages: cellular dedifferentiation, cellular redifferentiation, and organogenesis, which are characterized by different phytohormone requirements (eg, Christianson and Warnick 1985). Callus formation is a critical early step in plant organ regeneration and is regulated by the balanced application of 2 phytohormones, auxin and cytokinin, in the culture medium (Skoog and Miller 1957).

Callus-inducing medium (CIM) contains auxin, typically 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D), which does not undergo polar transport, and cytokinin, typically kinetin. In this medium, auxin initiates cellular dedifferentiation for cell division by activating the lateral root initiation program through AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR 7 (ARF7) and ARF19, which in turn induce the expression of LATERAL ORGAN BOUNDARIES DOMAIN (LBD) transcription factors such as LBD16 (Atta et al. 2009; Sugimoto et al. 2010; Fan et al. 2012). By contrast, cytokinin promotes cell division and stem cell maintenance by inducing the expression of genes such as CYCLIN D3 (CYCD3) and WUSCHEL (WUS) through the cytokinin-responsive type-B ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR (ARR) transcription factors ARR1 and ARR12 (Riou-Khamlichi et al. 1999; Dewitte et al. 2007; Meng et al. 2017; Zhang et al. 2017). In light of these findings, callus, once thought to be an unorganized mass of entirely undifferentiated cells, is now considered an organized cell mass regulated by various factors, including phytohormones, with the process being similar to lateral root primordium formation in auxin-rich CIM culture.

Molecular genetics research on Arabidopsis mutants has highlighted the roles of lipid metabolism in callus formation. Very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs), which are synthesized via the fatty acid elongase complex that includes 3-ketoacyl-CoA synthase 1 (KCS1), appear to act as negative regulators of callus induction, as the kcs1 mutant exhibits enhanced callus formation (Shang et al. 2016). Consistent with this notion, mutants of cytosolic glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, the critical enzyme for NADPH supply in VLCFA biosynthesis, show enhanced callus formation and reduced VLCFA levels (Li et al. 2025). Furthermore, the AP2/ERF family transcription factor PUCHI, which regulates the expression of VLCFA biosynthesis genes, also contributes to this regulation, as puchi-1 mutants display disorganized callus structures (Trinh et al. 2019). Together, these findings support a model in which lipid biosynthesis, including VLCFA biosynthesis, regulates cell dedifferentiation and subsequent cell proliferation/organization for callus formation. Because VLCFA-containing ceramides have been proposed to act as signaling molecules that determine protoderm tissue development through interaction with a tissue-differentiation-related transcription factor (Nagata et al. 2021), it is possible that VLCFAs may also function as signaling molecules that regulate callus formation. A link between VLCFAs and phytohormones has also been suggested; VLCFA synthesis in epidermal cells suppresses cytokinin biosynthesis in the vasculature to fine-tune cell division activity in Arabidopsis (Nobusawa et al. 2013). Furthermore, defective biosynthesis of VLCFAs increases the contents of cytokinins such as isopentenyladenine and cis-zeatin by upregulating cytokinin biosynthesis genes in rice (Zhou et al. 2025). Moreover, defects in VLCFAs alter the polarity and trafficking of PIN1 plasma membrane auxin carriers, leading to an abnormal auxin distribution in tissues (Babić et al. 2025). Thus, VLCFAs may contribute to callus formation and organ regeneration through multiple mechanisms, including phytohormone signaling.

Our previous work has revealed the importance of post-transcriptional RNA metabolism in callus formation. A number of temperature-sensitive Arabidopsis mutants with defects in cell dedifferentiation and redifferentiation in tissue culture have been isolated, including shoot redifferentiation defective2-1 (srd2-1), root initiation defective1-1 (rid1-1), and root redifferentiation defective4-1 (rrd4-1) (Yasutani et al. 1994; Konishi and Sugiyama 2003; Sugiyama 2003; Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005; Ohtani et al. 2013). These mutants show high-temperature sensitivity in the dedifferentiation of hypocotyl tissue fragments and fail to form callus from the hypocotyl at a restrictive temperature of 28 °C (Sugiyama 2003; Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005; Ohtani et al. 2013). The gene responsible for srd2-1 encodes a zinc-finger protein involved in the transcription of small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), which are core components of spliceosomal snRNPs, while the gene responsible for rid1-1 encodes a nucleolus-localized DEAH-box RNA helicase that is presumably involved in snRNP biogenesis (Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005; Ohtani et al. 2013). Both are implicated in pre-mRNA splicing, indicating an essential role for pre-mRNA splicing in cellular dedifferentiation (Ikeuchi et al. 2013; Ohtani 2015, 2017, 2018). Quantification of snRNA levels revealed that in vitro cell dedifferentiation and organogenesis require a much higher level of snRNA than does seedling development (Ohtani et al. 2015). Thus, processes such as dedifferentiation and organ regeneration, which involve drastic changes in gene expression, are likely to require higher splicing activity than normal developmental processes. SRD2 and RID1 expression are induced by auxin and/or cytokinin during CIM culture, where their spatiotemporal expression patterns are differentially regulated by these phytohormones (Takayanagi et al. 2022). These observations suggest that the capacity for pre-mRNA splicing may be actively regulated by auxin and cytokinin to promote the dedifferentiation of plant cells. However, the mechanisms by which phytohormones regulate cellular dedifferentiation through pre-mRNA splicing remain poorly understood.

In this study, we identified the gene responsible for rrd4-1 as encoding a homolog of yeast NineTeen Complex (NTC)-related protein1 (Ntr1), which is involved in pre-mRNA splicing (Sugiyama 2003; Tsai et al. 2005). The corresponding gene is also known as SPLICEOSOMAL TIMEKEEPER LOCUS1 or AtNTR1 (Jones et al. 2012; Dolata et al. 2015). While the pre-mRNA splicing-related mutants srd2-1 and rid1-1 exhibit similar organ regeneration abnormalities, rrd4-1 responds differently from srd2-1 and rid1-1 on root-inducing medium (RIM) that includes indole-3-butyric acid as an auxin precursor but not cytokinin. In srd2-1 and rid1-1, the initiation of adventitious root primordia is completely inhibited at 28 °C on RIM (Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005; Ohtani et al. 2013), whereas rrd4-1 produces abnormal and short adventitious roots under these conditions (Sugiyama 2003). Because CIM and RIM differ in the presence or absence of kinetin, we analyzed the molecular function of RRD4 with special attention to its dependence on cytokinin. The results indicated that the RRD4 function is required for callus formation in the presence of cytokinin. RNA-seq analysis during the early stages of CIM culture revealed that the effect of cytokinin on alternative splicing (AS) was greater in rrd4-1 than in the wild type. The AS of lipid biosynthesis genes was significantly affected by both cytokinin and the rrd4-1 mutation, and lipid analyses and inhibitor treatments demonstrated that RRD4- and cytokinin-mediated lipid metabolism are critical for cell dedifferentiation during callus formation. These findings suggest that cytokinin may modulate lipid metabolism through RRD4-mediated pre-mRNA splicing of lipid biosynthesis genes and that such regulation is one of the molecular pathways by which phytohormones regulate cellular dedifferentiation and organ regeneration.

Results

RRD4 encodes a pre-mRNA splicing factor

The rrd4-1 mutant was isolated by screening for temperature-sensitive mutants with defects in tissue culture response (Sugiyama 2003). To identify the gene responsible for the rrd4-1 mutation, we performed chromosome mapping analysis (Figure S1). The rrd4-1 mutation was mapped to a 64-kb region at the 23-cM position of chromosome 1 (Figure S1a). This region contains 21 protein-coding genes, and subsequent sequence analysis of the rrd4-1 genome identified a single guanine-to-adenine substitution in the At1g17070 exon (Fig. 1a; Figure S1b). This mutation was predicted to introduce a premature stop codon, possibly leading to production of a truncated protein that lacked the latter half of the amino acid sequence (Fig. 1a). For a complementation test, a 9.46-kb wild-type genomic fragment containing At1g17070 was introduced into rrd4-1 (Figure S1b). The resulting plants were examined for defects in callus formation, and successful complementation was observed with the wild-type genomic fragment (Figure S1c). Based on these results, we concluded that RRD4 corresponds to At1g17070. Western blotting with an anti-RRD4 antibody revealed the presence of a 97-kDa band in samples from wild-type seedlings, as predicted from the amino acid sequence of At1g17070. However, this band was absent in rrd4-1 samples (Fig. 1b). No additional specific bands were detected in rrd4-1, including at the 51.7-kDa position corresponding to the predicted truncated protein in rrd4-1 (Figure S1d), suggesting that the rrd4-1 mutation causes a loss of functional RRD4 protein.

RRD4/At1g17070, also known as STIPL1 or NTR1, has been reported to participate in pre-mRNA splicing (Jones et al. 2012; Dolata et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019; He et al. 2023). Thus, the abnormal tissue culture responses reported for rrd4-1 may be attributable to defects in the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing.

Mutants defective in pre-mRNA splicing are hypersensitive to cytokinin-dependent inhibition of callus formation

We next examined the effects of cytokinin on callus formation in rrd4-1, as well as in srd2-1 and rid1-1 (Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005; Ohtani et al. 2013), 2 other previously reported temperature-sensitive pre-mRNA splicing-related mutants. In the wild type, callus formation was inhibited on CIM containing 10 mg L−1 of kinetin at 22 °C and 0.5 mg L−1 of kinetin at 28 °C (Fig. 1c). By contrast, in rrd4-1, the inhibitory effects of kinetin were observed at concentrations of 0.5 mg L−1 or higher at 22 °C and 0.1 mg L−1 or higher at 28 °C (Fig. 1c). Callus inhibition also occurred at lower kinetin concentrations in srd2-1 and rid1-1 compared to the wild type, and even compared to rrd4-1: 0.5 and 0.1 mg L−1, respectively, at 22 °C and 0.02 mg L−1 at 28 °C (Fig. 1c; Figure S2). At 22 ˚C, the wild-type callus was gradually inhibited as the kinetin concentration increased, as indicated by the level of callus formation (for the details, please see Figure S2). This inhibitory effect of kinetin was enhanced at 28 ˚C and by the rrd4-1 mutation (Figure S2), suggesting synergistic effects of kinetin and splicing defects on callus formation.

When wild-type hypocotyl explants were cultured on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin for 4 d, there were no clear differences in callus size or morphology at 22 °C; however, calli formed on CIM with kinetin were slightly smaller and had a smoother surface than those on CIM without kinetin at 28 °C (Fig. 1d). In the mutants, the effect of kinetin on callus morphology was evident at 22 °C: calli were distinctly uneven in the absence of kinetin but had a smooth surface on CIM with kinetin (Fig. 1d). Mutant calli, particularly in srd2-1 and rid1-1, were smaller than wild-type calli at 22 °C, and there was no clear sign of callus formation on CIM with kinetin at 28 °C (Fig. 1d). These results indicate that mutants with defects in pre-mRNA splicing are hypersensitive to the negative effects of kinetin on callus formation.

RT-qPCR analysis of hypocotyl explants cultured on CIM with and without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin at 28 °C revealed that RRD4 expression was not significantly altered by kinetin in the wild type and was significantly reduced in rrd4-1 compared to the wild type, regardless of the presence of kinetin (Fig. 1e). Thus, cytokinin in the CIM does not appear to affect callus formation by altering RRD4 expression. We also performed RT-PCR analysis of FOLYLPOLYGLUTAMATE SYNTHASE3 (FPGS3) and SERINE-ARGININE PROTEIN 30 (SR30), whose AS patterns in response to cellular stresses are well documented (Takahashi et al. 2025). Wild-type and rrd4-1 explants exhibited distinct patterns of AS, regardless of the presence of kinetin (Fig. 1f). These results indicate that RRD4 functions in pre-mRNA splicing during callus formation.

Time-specific requirement for cytokinin-responsive pre-mRNA splicing during callus formation

To determine the time window during which RRD4 function is required for callus formation, we performed kinetin- and temperature-shift experiments for callus induction using wild-type and rrd4-1 hypocotyl explants (Fig. 2; Figure S3). The kinetin and temperature conditions were shifted from the absence to the presence of kinetin and from 22 °C to 28 °C, respectively, at 0, 12, 24, 36, or 48 h of CIM culture, and explants were then cultured for a total of 4 d. Microscopic observations showed that callus formation occurred in the wild type under all conditions (Fig. 2b; Figure S3, b and c). By contrast, no signs of resumed cell proliferation were observed in rrd4-1 when the kinetin- and temperature-shift occurred at 0 or 12 h of CIM culture (Fig. 2b; Figure S3, b and c). Cell proliferation resumed in rrd4-1 when the shift occurred after 24 h of culture, although rrd4-1 explants formed smaller calli than the corresponding wild-type explants (Fig. 2b; Figure S3, b and c). In our CIM culture system, the first cell division is observed after 24 to 36 h. Thus, RRD4 function is necessary for acquiring cell division competency and/or resumption during the early stages of CIM culture, ie, before 24 h. This result is similar to that reported for temperature-shift experiments with srd2-1 (Ohtani and Sugiyama 2005). Therefore, the cytokinin-responsive regulation of pre-mRNA splicing, which is important for the resumption of cell division, appears to occur within the first 24 h of CIM culture.

Transcriptome analysis reveals differences in the responses of rrd4-1 and the wild type to kinetin in the CIM

We next performed RNA-seq analysis to examine the transcriptomic dynamics associated with hypocotyl dedifferentiation. Wild-type and rrd4-1 hypocotyl explants from 12-d-old seedlings were cultured on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin at 28 °C for 0, 12, 24, or 48 h (Fig. 3a). After processing and quality control of the RNA-seq data, we obtained expression data for 32,678 genes (for details, please see Materials and Methods). Four hundred forty-five genes were differentially expressed between rrd4-1 and the wild type before CIM culture, and more than 5,000 genes were differentially expressed between rrd4-1 and the wild type after CIM culture (Table 1). Kinetin application affected the expression of approximately 1,000 genes in the wild type during CIM culture. By contrast, only 133 genes were differentially expressed in response to kinetin in rrd4-1 at 12 h of CIM culture, and this number increased to 1,747 genes at 48 h (Table 1). Thus, transcriptomic responses to cytokinin differed between the wild type and rrd4-1.

Principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that PC1 separated samples from before and after callus induction with the relation to gene ontology (GO) terms “plant-type cell wall modification” and “response to mechanical stimulus” (Fig. 3b; Data Sets S1 and S2). The term “ribosome biosynthesis” was enriched in the genes that contributed to negative values of PC2 (Fig. 3, b and c; Data Sets S3 and S4). Wild-type and rrd4-1 samples were separated along PC3, with rrd4-1 samples from 12 and 24 h placed on the negative side (Fig. 3c). Genes that contributed to negative values of PC3 were associated with responses to hydrogen peroxide and heat, as well as protein folding (Fig. 3c; Data Sets S5 and S6). These results imply that rrd4-1 responds to CIM culture differently than the wild type, in part by inducing certain stress responses (Fig. 3c; Data Sets S5 and S6).

Unexpectedly, samples were not separated by the presence or absence of cytokinin along any of the PCA axes (Fig. 3, b and c). Consistent with this result, hierarchical clustering analysis of log2-transformed and z-score-normalized average TPM values from the top 1,000 most variable genes showed similar patterns of gene expression between samples with and without kinetin (Fig. 3d). These findings suggest that the inhibitory effect of cytokinin on callus formation in rrd4-1 may involve the altered expression of only a small subset of genes.

Misregulation of cell division activity and promotion of xylem vessel differentiation in rrd4-1 cultured on CIM with cytokinin

Time-course expression analyses (Fig. 4a) identified 689 and 830 cytokinin-dependent differentially expressed genes (DEGs; false discovery rate [FDR] < 0.05) during CIM culture of the wild type and rrd4-1, respectively (Fig. 4b). GO enrichment analysis revealed enrichment of cell-wall-related genes in the shared DEGs (Fig. 4b; Data Set S7). By contrast, the 558 DEGs specific to the wild type and the 699 DEGs specific to rrd4-1 were associated with stress responses and developmental processes, respectively (Fig. 4b; Data Sets S8 and S9). These findings suggest that the effects of cytokinin on gene expression differ between the wild type and rrd4-1.

Next, we performed k-means clustering of the cytokinin-dependent DEGs and classified them into 5 and 4 clusters in the wild type and rrd4-1, respectively (Fig. 4, c and d, Figure S4; Data Sets S10 to S17). Wild-type cluster 1 and rrd4-1 cluster 1 both contained genes that were strongly upregulated on CIM with kinetin at 48 h, and both contained genes related to the secondary cell wall (Fig. 4, c and d). Their expression increased after 24 h of CIM culture with kinetin in both the wild type and rrd4-1, and the upregulation was more pronounced in rrd4-1 than in the wild type, especially at 48 h (Fig. 5a; Figure S5, a to c). These secondary-cell-wall-related genes were closely related to xylem vessel differentiation, and we therefore examined the expression pattern of the programmed-cell-death-related gene XYLEM CYSTEINE PEPTIDASE1 (XCP1), which is specifically expressed during xylem vessel differentiation (Avci et al. 2008). XCP1 was also markedly upregulated in rrd4-1 in the presence of kinetin at 48 h (Figure S5d), suggesting that xylem vessel differentiation is promoted in rrd4-1 on CIM with kinetin. Notably, enhanced production of de novo xylem vessel elements was also observed in rrd4-1 hypocotyls cultured on CIM with kinetin (Fig. 5, b and c). rrd4-1 cluster 1 was uniquely enriched in the GO term “response to cytokinin” (Fig. 4d; Data Set S15) but contained only 7 genes with this annotation, including ARABIDOPSIS HISTIDINE KINASE 4 (AHK4), ARABIDOPSIS RESPONSE REGULATOR4 (ARR4), ARR6, ARR7, and ZINC FINGER PROTEIN 5 (ZFP5), as well as VASCULAR-RELATED NAC-DOMAIN6 (VND6) and VND7, master transcription factors for xylem vessel differentiation (Kubo et al. 2005). Cytokinin-related genes showed no clear differences in expression patterns between the wild type and rrd4-1, whereas VND6 and VND7 were significantly upregulated at 48 h only in rrd4-1 cultured on CIM with kinetin (Figure S6). We therefore concluded that the rrd4-1 mutation did not affect overall cytokinin response during callus induction.

Wild-type cluster 2 and rrd4-1 cluster 2 contained genes that were upregulated in the absence of kinetin (Fig. 4, c and d; Data Sets S11 and S16). The GO terms “response to auxin” and “regulation of cell cycle” were enriched only in the rrd4-1 cluster 2 (Fig. 5d; Figures S7 and S8). Although several auxin-related genes showed differential expression patterns, the overall expression patterns of auxin-related genes were almost indistinguishable between the wild type and rrd4-1 (Figure S7). By contrast, the expression of genes associated with cell cycle regulation was reduced in rrd4-1 compared with the wild type at 12 h of CIM culture (Fig. 5d; Figure S8). In particular, A-, B-, and D-type cyclin genes important for the cell cycle phase transition were downregulated in rrd4-1 at 12 and 24 h of culture regardless of kinetin presence; however, they were strongly upregulated at 48 h of culture in the absence of kinetin (Fig. 5d; Figure S8). These expression patterns of cell-cycle-related genes may be directly related to the division of hypocotyl cells.

Cluster 3 contained genes that were downregulated regardless of kinetin presence and were enriched in genes related to the immune response, such as glucosinolate biosynthetic pathway genes, in both the wild type and rrd4-1 (Fig. 4, c and d; Data Sets S12 and S17). Wild-type cluster 3 also contained a wide range of stress response genes, including those associated with jasmonic acid and ethylene signaling (Fig. 4c; Data Set S12). Immune-responsive genes are transiently activated at the early stages of callus induction involving physical wounding (Ikeuchi et al. 2017), and jasmonic acid signaling pathways promote wound-induced cell division (Asahina et al. 2011). However, the genes involved in the response to wounding or jasmonic acid showed no clear differences between the wild type and rrd4-1 (Figure S9). Thus, the callus formation defects of rrd4-1 cannot be attributed to such signaling pathways.

Cytokinin-dependent splicing dynamics during hypocotyl dedifferentiation

The kinetin- and temperature-shift experiments indicated that the critical time for cytokinin and RRD4 function occurred before 24 h of CIM culture (Fig. 2; Figure S3). Because the DEG analysis did not clarify the molecular basis of the rrd4-1 phenotype, we next focused on AS dynamics by performing differential AS (DAS) analysis using rMATS (Shen et al. 2014) with RNA-seq data from 12 h (Fig. 6, a and b). Pairwise comparisons of DAS events or genes (FDR < 0.05 and an absolute change in percent spliced in [|ΔPSI|] > 0.05) showed that the rrd4-1 mutation and the presence of kinetin affected all types of AS events, especially mutually exclusive exons and retained introns (RIs) (Fig. 6b). Before callus induction, 2,774 DAS genes were detected between the wild type and rrd4-1 (Fig. 6b; Figure S10a), and these genes were significantly enriched in the GO terms “mRNA processing” and “circadian rhythm” (Figure S10a; Data Set S18), consistent with previous reports that RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1 is involved in AS for the circadian clock (Jones et al. 2012).

After CIM culture, the presence of kinetin affected 1,625 AS events (1,016 genes) in the wild type and 3,995 events (2,279 genes) in rrd4-1 (Fig. 6b; Figure S10b). GO enrichment analysis indicated that the presence of kinetin affected AS of genes related to protein metabolism in the wild type (Fig. 6c; Data Set S19) and genes related to tRNA metabolism, DNA repair, and membrane organization in rrd4-1 (Figure S10b; Data Set S20). A comparison of DAS genes between the wild type and rrd4-1 at 12 h of CIM culture with kinetin revealed that 2,950 genes were differentially spliced between the wild type and rrd4-1, and that these genes significantly enriched the genes related to pre-mRNA splicing, RNA degradation, and protein modification (Fig. 6d; Data Set S21), suggesting that the rrd4-1 mutation affected AS of numerous genes involved in the regulation of gene expression processes during callus induction. The comparison of our DAS data with previously reported data for 7-d-old seedlings of the ntr1-1 mutant, a different allele of RRD4 (Wang et al. 2019), identified 1,633 genes as RRD4-regulated DAS genes unique to hypocotyl dedifferentiation (Figure S10c).

We next compared the 1,016 genes that were alternatively spliced in response to kinetin in the wild type with the 2,950 genes that were alternatively spliced between the wild type and rrd4-1 on CIM with kinetin (Fig. 7a), and identified 652 DAS genes that were common to both gene sets and were therefore affected by both cytokinin and the rrd4-1 mutation (Fig. 7a). The same comparisons for DEGs at 12, 24, and 48 h of CIM culture identified 363 cytokinin-dependent DEGs (Figure S11a to f; the 363 genes are the sum of the overlapping genes in S11A to F). A large number of GO terms enriched in these 363 genes were related to the cell wall and glucosinolate metabolism (Figure S11, a to f). Only one gene was shared between the cytokinin-dependent DAS genes and DEGs (Figure S11g), suggesting that the rrd4-1 mutation has independent effects on gene expression levels and AS.

Cytokinin- and RRD4-dependent splicing of sphingolipid biosynthesis genes may be key for callus formation

The common 652 DAS genes in Fig. 7a were significantly enriched in the GO terms “mRNA processing,” “chromatin remodeling,” “inositol metabolic process,” and “membrane lipid biosynthetic process” (Fig. 7a; Data Set S22). The GO terms “mRNA processing” and “sphingosine biosynthesis process” were also enriched in the RRD4-regulated DAS genes unique to hypocotyl dedifferentiation (Figure S10c). We therefore examined the splicing pattern of the splicing-factor genes SR30 and SR45a and the sphingolipid biosynthesis genes PROTEIN-TYROSINE PHOSPHATASE-LIKE (PTPLA), SPHINGOSINE KINASE2 (SPHK2), and NEUTRAL CERAMIDASE 1 (ATNCER1) (Data Set S22). For these genes, the RNA-seq data revealed significant alterations in the expression of intron regions in rrd4-1 (Fig. 7, b and c; Figure S12, a to c), and RT-PCR analysis confirmed the accumulation of RI-type transcripts in rrd4-1 (Fig. 7, d and e; Figure S12d). RI events in these genes were presumed to generate premature stop codons, but the genes showed no drastic reductions in expression levels (Figure S13). Thus, the RI forms of these genes are not targets of nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, and their presence is permitted in plant cells. Notably, the splicing efficiency of sphingolipid biosynthesis genes calculated from RNA-seq data appeared to be markedly reduced by the rrd4-1 mutation and further altered by kinetin (Fig. 7, b and c; Figure S12c). These results provide evidence that sphingolipid biosynthesis genes are cytokinin- and RRD4-dependent splicing regulatory targets during callus formation.

We then performed lipidomic analysis of ceramides (Cers), glucosylceramides (GlcCers), and glycosylinositolphosphoceramides (GIPCs) in wild-type and rrd4-1 hypocotyl explants cultured for 0 or 24 h on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin. Lipidomic analysis revealed that total lipid content was lower in rrd4-1 hypocotyls than in wild-type hypocotyls before callus induction (Figure S14a). Before CIM culture, LCFA-Cer levels were higher and VLCFA-GIPC levels were lower in rrd4-1 than in the wild type (Figure S14b), suggesting that lipid biosynthetic activity was defective in rrd4-1. Callus induction caused drastic changes in the lipid contents of wild-type hypocotyls: total lipid content decreased, the content of Cer species increased, and the contents of GlcCer and GIPC species decreased (Figure S14, a and c), suggesting that sphingolipid metabolism was activated as part of the lipid remodeling associated with cellular dedifferentiation in CIM culture. By contrast, such patterns were disturbed in rrd4-1, especially for lipid species with VLCFAs (Figure S14c). Cytokinin-dependent changes in lipid contents were also significantly altered in rrd4-1 (Fig. 7f; Figure S14c). In the wild type, the presence of kinetin in the CIM increased the content of GlcCer species but had no effect on other species. However, in rrd4-1, kinetin significantly reduced the levels of VLCFA-Cer and VLCFA-GlcCer (Fig. 7f). Taken together, these results suggest that the rrd4-1 mutation affects lipid biosynthesis in the hypocotyl, altering lipid composition, especially that of VLCFA-type sphingolipids, in response to cytokinin during callus induction.

Finally, we examined the relationships among cytokinin, pre-mRNA splicing, and lipid biosynthesis using cerulenin and fumonisin B1 (FB1), the inhibitors of fatty acid synthesis and VLCFA-specific ceramide synthesis, respectively (Vance et al. 1972; Koo et al. 2005; Markham et al. 2011). Hypocotyl explants from 12-d-old wild-type and rrd4-1 seedlings were cultured on CIM with and without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin with 0, 1, or 10 μM cerulenin or 0, 0.1, 0.5, or 1.0 µM FB1 at 22 °C or 28 °C for 24 d (Fig. 7g; Figure S15). In the wild type, 10 μM cerulenin and 1.0 µM FB1 strongly inhibited callus formation, and 1 μM cerulenin and 0.1 µM FB1 inhibited callus formation only in the presence of kinetin (Fig. 7g; Figure S15), suggesting that kinetin and the lipid biosynthesis inhibitors may act synergistically to inhibit callus formation. These inhibitory effects of cerulenin were more severe in rrd4-1 particularly in the presence of kinetin and at high temperature: callus formation was severely inhibited in rrd4-1 hypocotyls cultured on CIM with kinetin and 1 μM cerulenin at 22 °C and on CIM without kinetin and 1 μM cerulenin at 28 °C (Fig. 7g). Taken together, these findings suggest that cytokinin negatively regulates cell proliferation through lipid biosynthesis and that splicing mediated by RRD4 plays a key role in this regulation.

Discussion

RRD4 encodes a homolog of yeast Ntr1 required for regulation of pre-mRNA splicing

In this study, we revealed that the gene responsible for rrd4-1 encodes a pre-mRNA splicing factor homologous to yeast Ntr1 and human TFIP11, which are involved in spliceosome disassembly (Tsai et al. 2005; Tannukit et al. 2009; Jones et al. 2012) (Fig. 1a; Figure S1). In Arabidopsis, this gene is also known as STIPL1 or NTR1 and has been implicated in developmental and physiological phenomena, including the regulation of circadian rhythms, seed dormancy, leaf morphology, and heat stress response (Jones et al. 2012; Dolata et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019; He et al. 2023). A previous study characterized the target genes of RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1-mediated pre-mRNA splicing in 7-d-old seedlings (Wang et al. 2019), but the abnormally-spliced genes in the rrd4/ntr1 mutant only partially overlapped between seedlings and hypocotyls cultured on CIM (Figure S10c), suggesting that RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1 regulatory targets may differ among tissues and/or physiological processes. Despite their multifaceted developmental abnormalities, knockout mutants of RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1 are viable and fertile under normal growth conditions (Sugiyama 2003; Jones et al. 2012; Dolata et al. 2015; Wang et al. 2019; He et al. 2023). However, they cannot survive under high-temperatures and heat stress, and suppression of cell proliferation is observed under these conditions (Sugiyama 2003; Dolata et al. 2015; He et al. 2023). Together with our observation that the resumption of cell division was inhibited in rrd4-1 at 28 °C, these findings suggest that RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1 are essential for maintenance of cellular functions under high temperatures. Dolata et al. (2015) revealed that NTR1 functions not only in AS by acting on strong alternative splice sites (ie consensus-sequence-like splice sites) but also in the control of transcript elongation through the pausing of RNA polymerase II. Such observations suggest that high temperatures might increase the need for RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1-dependent spliceosome disassembly and transcription elongation adjustment, perhaps owing to the acceleration of chemical reactions. Future analyses will focus on the temperature-dependent mode of action of RRD4/STIPL1/NTR1 on specific targets for cellular dedifferentiation.

Cytokinin influences pre-mRNA splicing for hypocotyl dedifferentiation

Pre-mRNA splicing mutants exhibited severe abnormalities in hypocotyl dedifferentiation especially in the presence of cytokinins (Fig. 1, c and d; Figure S2). A high concentration of kinetin inhibits callus formation even in the wild type (Fig. 1, c and d; Figure S2), and pre-mRNA splicing mutants are highly susceptible to the inhibitory effect of cytokinin on callus formation. Recently, the seventh intron of the cytokinin receptor gene CRE1/AHK4 (Inoue et al. 2001) was reported to be regulated in a cytokinin-dependent manner, generating a truncated receptor lacking the domain for signal transduction (Králová et al. 2024). This truncated protein can function as a “decoy” receptor that acts as a negative regulator of cytokinin signaling, presumably constituting a feedback system for cytokinin response (Králová et al. 2024). However, we did not detect any signs of kinetin-dependent AS of cytokinin receptor genes in our data (Fig. 6d), indicating that this feedback regulation system is not involved in cellular dedifferentiation for callus formation. In addition, the overall expression patterns of auxin- and cytokinin-related genes were similar between the wild type and rrd4-1 (Figures S6 and S7). Rather, AS of genes associated with pre-mRNA splicing, RNA degradation and protein modification was significantly affected in rrd4-1 (Fig. 6d), indicating that RRD4 may indirectly modulate cytokinin signaling through such post-transcriptional regulation.

Lack of clear differences in cytokinin-related gene expression in rrd4-1 (Figures S6, S7, and S9) also raises the possibility that the defective cellular dedifferentiation in pre-mRNA splicing mutants enhanced by cytokinin may occur through a mechanism distinct from the authentic cytokinin signaling pathway. In human studies, kinetin and its derivatives have been identified as recovery agents for mis-splicing of IκB kinase complex-associated protein (IKBKAP), which is responsible for the genetic disease familial dysautonomia (Slaugenhaupt et al. 2004; Hims et al. 2007; Yoshida et al. 2015; Salani et al. 2019; Maková et al. 2025). Because humans do not have the cytokinin signaling pathway, these findings strongly suggest that kinetin directly interacts with splicing factors and/or mRNAs to influence splicing regulation in human cells. Similarly, during hypocotyl dedifferentiation, cytokinin might directly influence such molecules and pre-mRNA splicing. This hypothesis would explain the high sensitivity of splicing mutants to cytokinin and should be tested in future research.

Cytokinin- and RRD4-dependent splicing regulates VLCFA biosynthesis for cellular dedifferentiation

Through RNA-seq and lipidomic analysis, we identified sphingolipid metabolism as a regulatory target of cytokinin- and RRD4-dependent splicing during hypocotyl dedifferentiation (Fig. 7, a to e; Figure S12, c and d). The sphingolipid composition of hypocotyls was significantly altered during callus induction (Fig. 7f; Figure S14), and inhibition of lipid synthesis affected callus formation (Fig. 7g: Figure S15). The effects of lipid synthesis inhibition on callus formation were enhanced in rrd4-1 (Fig. 7g; Figure S15), supporting the view that RRD4 functions in the splicing regulation of sphingolipid metabolism-related genes to promote cellular dedifferentiation. The effects of rrd4-1 mutation on VLCFA-type sphingolipids were particularly pronounced (Fig. 7f; Figure S14), and abnormal VLCFA-type sphingolipid metabolism may therefore be one of the molecular bases of defective callus formation in rrd4-1, possibly through the generation of truncate proteins of lipid metabolism enzymes by the intron retention (Fig. 7, b to e; Figure S12, c and d). The existence of a “lipid checkpoint” has been proposed in human cell cycle regulation (Köberlin et al. 2024). In this model, a defect in lipid biosynthesis during the G1 phase inhibits progression of the cell cycle, resulting in cell cycle arrest (Köberlin et al. 2024). We propose a similar mechanism in plant cell cycle regulation, especially for cellular dedifferentiation, during which non-dividing cells acquire competency for cell division (Fig. 8). In rrd4-1, defects in lipid metabolism may trigger the lipid checkpoint and prevent cells from resuming cell proliferation, but cell differentiation (particularly xylem vessel formation) may be promoted instead.

Proposed working model for cytokinin-dependent pre-mRNA splicing regulation during hypocotyl dedifferentiation in Arabidopsis. During hypocotyl dedifferentiation, cytokinin influences the regulation of pre-mRNA splicing, in which RRD4 participates, thereby altering lipid biosynthesis and ultimately determining the direction of cell division or differentiation.

Mutual regulatory interactions have been reported between cytokinin and VLCFAs; VLCFAs negatively regulate cytokinin biosynthesis in Arabidopsis (Nobusawa et al. 2013) and rice (Zhou et al. 2025). In addition, Arabidopsis mutants deficient in cytokinin signaling exhibit extensive reprogramming of metabolism, including the biosynthesis of membrane lipids and flavonoids (Abdelrahman et al. 2021), suggesting that cytokinin regulates VLCFA biosynthesis. Here, we found that the transcripts of some lipid biosynthesis genes were alternatively spliced possibly in a cytokinin-dependent manner (Fig. 7, a to e; Figure S12c). Unlike the regulation of gene expression at the transcriptional level, AS enables plant cells to flexibly adjust mRNA/protein isoform production, possibly helping them to respond more precisely to environmental signals (Alhabsi et al. 2025). In particular, the biosynthesis of essential biomolecules for cell activity, such as lipids, cannot be stopped entirely, and regulation at the mRNA isoform level might therefore be more suitable for rapid and fine response than an increase or decrease in transcription itself. The lipid metabolism-related genes identified here likely represent only one set of targets controlled by cytokinin-dependent pre-mRNA splicing regulation. Future work should therefore examine other pathways, such as mRNA processing and chromatin remodeling (Fig. 7a), to determine their contributions to cellular dedifferentiation.

In summary, cytokinin-dependent pre-mRNA splicing regulation plays a crucial role in the early stages of hypocotyl dedifferentiation, especially at a high temperature condition, through its effects on lipid metabolism (Fig. 8). Specifically, we found that: (1) RRD4 functions in the splicing regulation of lipid-metabolism-related genes during callus initiation through spliceosome disassembly and/or transcription elongation regulation, and (2) the disruption of this regulation at a high temperature condition causes abnormalities in lipid biosynthesis, ultimately leading to failure of the reactivation of cell cycle progression (Fig. 8). In other words, this suggests that the splicing of lipid-metabolism-related genes would be a key crosstalk point between cytokinin and RRD4 for cellular dedifferentiation (Fig. 8). This model offers a view of how plants integrate internal (ie cytokinin) and external (ie temperature) stimuli through post-transcriptional regulation to flexibly regulate cell proliferation and cell differentiation. Further characterization of detailed molecular mechanisms will lead to a more comprehensive understanding of plant-specific cellular dedifferentiation mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana ecotype Landsberg erecta (Ler) was used as the wild-type in this study. All mutant lines were derived from this ecotype and were generated as described previously: the srd2-1 mutant by Yasutani et al. (1994) and Ohtani and Sugiyama (2005), the rid1-1 mutant by Konishi and Sugiyama (2003) and Ohtani et al. (2013), and the rrd4-1 mutant by Sugiyama (2003).

Seeds were surface-sterilized with 1% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution containing 0.1% (w/v) Triton X-100 for 10 min and washed 5 times with sterile distilled water. Sterilized seeds were sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium containing 1% (w/v) sucrose and 0.05% (w/v) 2-morpholinoethanesulfonic acid (MES). The medium was solidified with either 1.5% (w/v) agar (referred to as GMA medium) or 0.25% (w/v) gellan gum (referred to as GM medium) and adjusted to pH 5.7. The plates were maintained in the dark at 4 °C for 2 d and then incubated under continuous light (15 to 20 μmol m−2 s−1) at 22 °C.

Tissue culture for callus induction

Tissue culture was performed as described previously (Ozawa et al. 1998). Hypocotyl segments (5 mm long) were excised from 12-d-old seedlings at a position 5 mm above the hypocotyl–root junction and cultured on CIM for callus induction. Tissue culture was carried out at 22 °C or 28 °C under continuous light (15 to 20 μmol m−2 s−1). CIM consisted of B5 medium supplemented with 2.0% (w/v) glucose, 0.5 mg L−1 2,4-D, 0 to 10 mg L−1 kinetin, 0 to 10 μM cerulenin, and 0 to 1.0 µM fumonisin B1 (FB1) buffered with 0.05% (w/v) MES to pH 5.7 and solidified with 0.25% (w/v) gellan gum. Callus induction was performed for 24 d without cerulenin and for 14 d with cerulenin.

Kinetin- and temperature-shift experiments

For the kinetin concentration- and temperature-shift experiments, hypocotyl explants of wild-type and rrd4-1 mutant Arabidopsis plants were cultured on CIM to induce dedifferentiation and callus formation. In the concentration-shift assay, explants were initially placed on CIM containing 0 mg L−1 kinetin and transferred to CIM containing 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin after 12, 24, 36, or 48 h of culture at 28 °C. The “0 h” condition refers to continuous culture on 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin throughout the period.

In the temperature-shift assay, explants were cultured on CIM containing 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin. Cultures were started at 22 °C and shifted to 28 °C after 12, 24, 36, or 48 h. The “0 h” condition refers to continuous culture at 28 °C. Explants from both experiments were collected after 4 d of culture and observed under a microscope.

Positional cloning and complementation test

The rrd4-1 mutant was crossed with the wild-type Columbia (Col) strain, and the resultant F2 plants were selfed to produce F3 lines. The F3 lines were assessed for the ability of callus formation at 28 °C on CIM that contained 0.5 mg L−1 kinetin and for DNA polymorphisms between Ler and Col to genotype their parental F2 plants. The chromosomal position of the RRD4 locus was determined on the basis of linkage of the rrd4-1 mutation to Ler alleles of polymorphic marker loci. A complementation test was performed as described in Ohtani and Sugiyama (2005), with complementation assessed by observing callus formation of the transformants at 28 °C.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Seven-day-old seedlings were harvested and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, then ground into a fine powder using a pre-chilled Shake Master Neo disruptor (Biomedical Science, Tokyo, Japan) at 1,000 rpm for 1 min. The powdered tissue was suspended in protein extraction buffer containing 70 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 3 mM EDTA (pH 8.0), 250 mM sucrose, 15 mM 2-mercaptoethanol, 5 mM DTT, 2 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 50 μg ml−1 leupeptin. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was centrifuged again under the same conditions. The final supernatant was collected as the total protein extract. Protein concentrations were determined using the TaKaRa Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan).

Protein samples were mixed with an equal volume of 2× Laemmli buffer containing 125 mM Tris–HCl (pH 6.8), 4% (w/v) SDS, 20% (w/v) glycerol, 0.004% (w/v) bromophenol blue, and 200 mM DTT, then incubated at 95 °C for 5 min. The denatured protein samples were separated by SDS–PAGE using a 7.5% (w/v) resolving gel with a 3% (w/v) stacking gel and Tris–glycine SDS running buffer (25 mM Tris, 190 mM glycine, 0.1% [w/v] SDS). After electrophoresis, the proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore) using a semi-dry transfer system with Tris–glycine SDS transfer buffer (48 mM Tris, 39 mM glycine, 0.04% [w/v] SDS) containing 20% (v/v) methanol. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk in TBST (20 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween-20, pH 7.5) for 4 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies (anti-RRD4, 1:1,000) for 1 h at room temperature. The anti-RRD4 antibody was a custom-made rabbit polyclonal antibody generated by Operon Biotechnology (Tokyo, Japan) using a mixture of 2 synthetic peptides corresponding to the C-terminal sequences NH2-DNDYEGGRWEGDEF-Cys-COOH and NH2-KRRKDRDSGRKADL-Cys-COOH. These peptides were conjugated to keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) using m-maleimidobenzoyl-N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (MBS) chemistry and subsequently used for immunization. After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked antibody; Cell Signaling Technology, Cat#7074S; 1:10,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using western Blot Chemiluminescence HRP Substrate (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) and visualized with a luminescence image analyzer (ImageQuant LAS 500; GE Healthcare Technologies Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis of RRD4

Total RNA was extracted from hypocotyl explants of wild-type and rrd4-1 plants cultured for 24 h on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin using the RNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration and quality were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Residual genomic DNA was removed using 10× RQ1 RNase-Free DNase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of DNase-treated RNA using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and oligo(dT)15 primers (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. qPCR was performed using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara Bio) on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 60 s, followed by 50 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene expression levels were normalized to those of UBQ10 as an internal control. The sequences of all primers are provided in Data Set S23. Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method.

RT-PCR for analysis of splicing patterns

RT-PCR was performed using cDNA synthesized in the previous section. Amplification was performed using KOD One PCR Master Mix (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) for 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 55 to 56 °C for 5 s, and 68 °C for 3 to 10 s. UBQ10 was used as an internal control for RT-PCR. The sequences of all primers and PCR cycling parameters are provided in Data Set S23.

Microscopy observations

For callus observation, hypocotyl explants were fixed overnight at 4 °C in a 9:1 (v/v) ethanol: acetic acid solution, then sequentially rehydrated in 70% (v/v), 50% (v/v), and 30% (v/v) ethanol (5 min each) and immersed in sterile water for 5 min. For concentration- and temperature-shift experiments, samples were fixed overnight at −30 °C in 90% (v/v) acetone; rehydrated through 70% (v/v), 50% (v/v), and 30% (v/v) acetone (5 min each); and transferred to sterile water for 5 min. All samples were mounted in a clearing solution consisting of chloral hydrate, glycerin, and water (8:1:2, w/v/v) and observed using a light microscope equipped with DIC optics (BX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from hypocotyl explants of wild-type and rrd4-1 seedlings cultured for 0, 12, 24, and 48 h on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin using the procedure described above. Three biological replicates were prepared for each condition. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA integrity was confirmed with a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 150-bp paired-end reads.

FastQC (version 0.11.9; Andrews 2010) was used for quality control of raw sequencing reads. Adapters were trimmed using Cutadapt (version 4.1; Martin 2011). 3ʹ-end adapter sequences were removed by specifying the adapter sequences for read 1 (“-a AGATCGGAAGAGCACACGTCTGAACTCCAGTCA”) and read 2 (“-A AGATCGGAAGAGCGTCGTGTAGGGAAAGAGTGT”), and reads shorter than 50 nucleotides after trimming were discarded (“–minimum-length 50”). Low-quality bases were trimmed using fastp (version 0.23.2; Chen et al. 2018), disabling adapter trimming (“–disable_adapter_trimming”), polyG tail trimming (“–disable_trim_poly_g”), and length filtering (“–disable_length_filtering”), because adapters had already been removed using Cutadapt. Reads were aligned to the Arabidopsis thaliana reference genome (TAIR9) using STAR (version 2.7.9a; Dobin et al. 2013). Genome indices were generated with non-default settings (“–genomeSAindexNbases 12 –sjdbOverhang 149 –sjdbGTFtagExonParentTranscript Parent –sjdbInsertSave All”). Read mapping was performed with “–outFilterMismatchNmax 1 –outSAMtype BAM Unsorted”. The resulting SAM files were converted to BAM format, sorted, and indexed using SAMtools (version 1.16.1; Li et al. 2009). Default parameters were used otherwise. Gene features were annotated using the TAIR10 GTF file obtained from The Arabidopsis Information Resource (TAIR) in December 2022. RNA read counts were obtained using subread featureCounts (version 2.0.3; Liao et al. 2014).

DEGs between 2 groups were identified using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) in DESeq2 (version 1.38.3; Love et al. 2014) with a reduced model of ∼1. Genes with FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 were considered to be DEGs.

Time-course analysis of DEGs was performed with DESeq2. Raw count data were filtered to retain only genes with an average count greater than 10 across all samples. Sample information, including cytokinin conditions and time points, was incorporated into a generalized linear model using DESeq2. DEGs were identified by LRT, comparing a full model (∼ Cytokinin + Time + Cytokinin:Time) with a reduced model (∼ Cytokinin + Time). Genes with an FDR-adjusted P-value < 0.05 were considered to be differentially expressed. The expression profiles of DEGs were subjected to k-means clustering to classify their temporal expression patterns. Clustering analysis was performed using the k-means algorithm implemented in R (version 4.2.2). The optimal number of clusters (k) was determined using the elbow method by evaluating the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS) for different values of k. DESeq2-normalized expression values, averaged across biological replicates, were standardized to z-scores and used for clustering analysis.

GO enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (version 4.6.2; Yu et al. 2012). GO terms for the genes of interest were retrieved using the org.At.tair.db R package (version 3.16.0; Carlson 2022). Enrichment analysis was performed with “pvalueCutoff = 0.05, qvalueCutoff = 0.1, pAdjustMethod = “BH” (Benjamini–Hochberg method). After enrichment, redundant GO terms were removed using the simplify() function with the parameters “cutoff = 0.7, by = “p.adjust”, and select_fun = min”.

AS events were identified using rMATS (version 4.1.2; Shen et al. 2014). Reads were aligned and analyzed with the following non-default parameters: –readLength 150 –libType fr-firststrand –t paired –mil 50 –mel 3000 –novelSS. The workflow was divided into 3 separate steps using the –task option (prep, post, and stat) to enable efficient processing and quality control.

RIs in genes of interest were identified and quantified using IRFinder-S (v2.0.1; Lorenzi et al. 2021). First, genome indexing was performed using STAR (v2.7.10b) with the following parameters: –runMode genomeGenerate –genomeSAindexNbases 12 –sjdbOverhang 150 –sjdbInsertSave All. The resulting STAR genome index was then converted into an IRFinder-compatible reference using the IRFinder BuildRefFromSTARRef command. Subsequently, intron retention was analyzed with the IRFinder BAM command using the -R 0.01 option. This enabled the detection of RIs and calculation of PSI values. The calculated PSI values, along with read coverage data, were visualized using a custom R script developed in-house. Read coverage profiles were normalized on a per-gene basis by scaling all positions within a gene to the highest read count observed for that gene (set to 1). The same normalization procedure was applied separately to each sample.

Lipidomics profiling by LC–MS/MS

For lipidomics profiling, hypocotyl explants of the wild type and rrd4-1 were cultured on CIM with or without 0.1 mg L−1 kinetin for 0 or 24 h at 28 °C. Samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen after collection and then lyophilized. Sphingolipids were prepared as described by Ishikawa et al. (2018). In brief, lyophilized plant tissues were homogenized and extracted with 1-butanol/methanol (2:1, v/v). The extract was subjected to alkaline hydrolysis with KOH to remove glycerolipids, followed by a two-phase partitioning using acidic 1-butanol to recover all sphingolipids in the organic phase. After solvent evaporation, the residue was reconstituted in tetrahydrofuran/methanol/water (2:1:2, v/v/v) with 0.1% formic acid. Sphingolipid species were analyzed by LC–MS/MS (LCMS-8030, Shimadzu GLC, Kyoto, Japan) and quantified on the basis of internal standards as described by Fang et al. (2016).
