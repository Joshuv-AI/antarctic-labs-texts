Transcriptomic profiling of select genes associated with cervical cancer and its preneoplastic lesions in Nigerian patients: an in vivo and in silico study
Authors: Saviour God’swealth Usin, Gershom Akanmu Olajire, Joshua Osekponor Olumese, Emeka Ogwa Idenyi, Olukayode Oluwafemi, Timothy Abiola Oluwasola, Ayodeji A. Salami, Olaposi Idowu Omotuyi, Gabriel O. Ogun, Michael Adedapo Gbadegesin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveCervical cancer remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women in Nigeria, primarily due to late detection. Genetic alterations in host genes contribute significantly to its pathogenesis. This study aims to validate the differential expression, mutational status, and clinical significance of SPP1, ANXA1, ANXA3, TMEM45A, IL-18, IGHG2, KRT10, CD177, ERO1α, ANKRD31, GSTA4, and MREG genes in patients presenting with symptoms of cervical cancer.MethodsA total of twenty-four (24) consenting female patients were recruited for this study; twenty-one (21) of them were those presenting with symptoms of cervical cancer, and the remaining three (3) were those who were clinically and histologically confirmed not to have any symptoms of cervical cancer. Tissue samples were obtained surgically and examined histopathologically. Total RNA was isolated, and gene expression levels were quantified using RT-qPCR. Gene expression across normal, tumour, and metastatic tissues, and their association with cancer hallmarks, were analysed using TNMPlot, muTarget, and Cancer Hallmark Enrichment Plot. Gene ontology and functional enrichment analyses were also performed.ResultsHistopathological analysis identified eight morphological categories: normal control (NC), mild infiltration/inflammation (MI), low-grade (LSIL) and high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL), chronic cervicitis (ChC), well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (WISCC), moderately-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (MSCC), and poorly-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (PISCC). Most of the genes were upregulated in MI, LSIL, MSCC, HSIL, WISCC, and ChC, indicating roles in proliferation, immune evasion, and metastasis. Notably, ERO1α, IGHG2, IL-18, TMEM45A, GSTA4, ANXA1, and ANXA3 were downregulated in early-stage lesions, supporting their potential as early diagnostic biomarkers.ConclusionsThe present study findings highlight the studied genes as key contributors to cervical cancer progression and underscore their potential utility as biomarkers for early detection and targeted therapy in Nigerian women.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a major global public health challenge, ranking as the fourth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1]. It is also one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies in women of reproductive age (15–44 years), second only to breast cancer [2, 3]. According to recent global cancer estimates, about 662,301 new cases and more than 348,874 deaths were recorded in 2022 [4, 5], with a disproportionate burden borne by nearly 90–94% by low- and mddle-income countries. Sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria, continues to experience high incidence and mortality rates, largely due to persistent high-risk HPV infection, limited screening coverage, and delayed diagnosis. The incidence of cervical cancer in Nigeria has been reported to be about 10.7% of its populaion [6, 7], with 44,699 deaths and 73,417 new cases of cervical cancer reported in 2020 alone. It has also been reported that cervical cancer contributed 12,075, which is 9.7% of new cases and 7,68 (10.1%) cancer deaths in Ngeria in 2020 [8].

Although national prevalence estimates are limited due to under-reporting and gaps in cancer registry coverage, available data suggest a considerable population of women living with the disease, particularly in reproductive age groups. However, despite advances in prevention and therapy, these epidemiological trends underscore the urgent need for novel, accessible, and mechanism-driven therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, despite being highly preventable, cervical cancer is among the leading causes of death in developing countries where access to early detection and treatment is limited, and it ranks as the second leading cause of cancer mortality in women after breast cancer [9, 10]. Additionally, genetic alterations also contribute to the pathogenesis of cervical cancer without any association with HPV infection [11]. These alterations consist of mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, including gene amplifications and deletions, and chromosomal rearrangements, which ultimately confer a proliferative and survival advantage to cells [12].

Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) is a spectrum of premalignant epithelial lesions of the cervix and represents the established precursor to invasive cervical cancer [13, 14]. In contrast to cervical cancer, CIN is confined to the epithelial layer and progresses through histopathologically distinct stages; CIN I (low-grade), CIN II, and CIN III (high-grade), based on the proportion of epithelial thickness exhibiting dysplasia [15]. This stepwise progression provides a critical biological window for early detection and intervention, underscoring the clinical importance of understanding molecular events that drive CIN persistence and progression. It has been demonstrated by several studies that persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary etiological factor underlying cervical neoplasia [16–18]; however, only a subset of HPV-infected individuals develops high-grade CIN or invasive disease [19]. This observation highlights the role of host- and lesion-specific molecular alterations in determining whether CIN lesions regress, persist, or progress. More so, infection with high-risk HPV (HR-HPV) is inadequate in causing cervical cancer as its manifestation is hinged on other genetic events that can either be dependent or independent of HPV infection [20, 21].

Furthermore, the malignancy of cervical cancer is characterised by multistage and heterogeneity due to genetic and epigenetic modifications, thereby posing a significant challenge to the effective diagnosis and monitoring of the progression of cancer, even at an early stage [22]. Currently, diagnostic techniques in cancer, cervical cancer inclusive, involve invasive biopsy procedures that can lead to further infections and bleeding. This calls for novel, non-invasive diagnostic methods that exhibit high accuracy, safety, and early detection in cancer diagnosis. One such method involves cancer biomarkers based on nucleic acids [22]. Through improvements in screening methods, especially in genomics and transcriptomics, there has been a reduction in cervical cancer mortality rates [23]. Also, accumulating evidence indicates that CIN progression is accompanied by dynamic transcriptomic changes affecting pathways involved in cell cycle control, DNA damage response, apoptosis, immune regulation, and epithelial differentiation [24–26]. While low-grade lesions often reflect transient HPV infection with limited transcriptional disruption, high-grade CIN (CIN II-III) is characterised by pronounced gene expression reprogramming that mirrors early oncogenic transformation [27, 28]. Profiling these transcriptomic alterations is therefore essential for elucidating the molecular basis of CIN progression and for identifying biomarkers capable of stratifying lesions according to progression risk.

Nevertheless, the mutation pattern and differential expression of several genes implicated in cervical cancer and its precancerous lesions in Nigeria have not been uncovered. This has hindered effective screening, early detection, and treatment, leading to a high mortality and morbidity rate amongst women of the Nigerian population [6]. Moreover, phenocopies might exist in cervical cancer diagnosis, where different patients diagnosed with the same symptoms exhibit different underlying genetic heterogeneity, leading to varying prognoses [29]. This underscores the need to decipher the molecular diversity of cervical cancer, identifying various molecular signatures that may aid in the discovery of diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers, ultimately leading to precision-based therapeutic strategies and improved responses [30]. Advancements in science and technology have made tremendous improvements in identifying biomarkers for cervical cancer, thus improving their survival rate [31], including HPV status, TP53 mutations, and CDKN2A, among others. We explore some of these biomarkers on the cervical cancer biopsies collected from among individuals of reproductive age in Ibadan, Nigeria especially since genetic alterations with/without HPV infection can result in cervical cancer and/or its preneoplastic lesions.

Methods

Ethical approval study site, and sample collection

The ethical approval was obtained from the UI/UCH Ethics Committee, Institute for Advanced Medical Research and Training (IAMRAT), with UI/UCH EC assigned number: UI/EC/23/0381. Furthermore, all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals prior to the collection of the sample. A total of twenty-four (24) consenting female patients (screened patients) were recruited for this study. Twenty-one (21) participants presented with symptoms suggestive of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN), confirmed by cytological and/or histopathological examination, while the remaining three (3) participants served as controls, showing no clinical or histological evidence of CIN. Inclusion criteria were strictly defined as follows: (1) all consenting patients were female presenting with symptoms of cervical dysplasia or cervical intraepithelial neoplasia; (2) patients within the age of 25–50 years. However, individuals who experienced tumour recurrence or had other types of cancer were excluded. Collection of samples, gynaecological and histological analyses were conducted at the Departments of Obstetrics & Gynaecology and Pathology, respectively, University College Hospital (UCH), Ibadan. The gynaecological analysis and collection of samples were carried out between January 2023 and November 2023. A portion of the collected samples was preserved in a DNA/RNA shield™ and stored in a -80 °C freezer at the Professor Abideen O. Oluwasola Special Diagnostics Centre, UCH. Ibadan, while the remaining portion was blotted and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological investigation. The molecular biology procedures were conducted at Mols and Sims, Ado-Ekiti, Ekiti State, Nigeria.

Histological analysis

Cervical tissue biopsies obtained from the patients were analysed at the Department of Pathology for histological examination and classification of tumour stages. This involved histological staining, sectioning, and examination under a microscope by a competent pathologist. After fixation, the specimens were examined, trimmed, and placed into embedding cassettes. The tissues were dehydrated, cleared, and infiltrated with paraffin wax using an automated tissue processor. Subsequently, the processed tissues were embedded in paraffin, oriented correctly, and solidified. Thin Sects. (3–5 micrometres) were cut using a microtome and floated on a warm water bath before being mounted onto glass slides. The slides underwent Haematoxylin and Eosin (H & E) staining, which includes deparaffinisation, rehydration, staining, dehydration, and clearing. Following staining, coverslips were applied with a mounting medium, and the slides were examined under a microscope to evaluate histological features such as the epithelial layer, transformation zone, and stromal components by professional personnel in the Department of Pathology, University of Ibadan, Ibadan, Oyo State.

RNA Isolation

Total RNA was isolated from cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and normal cervix (non-tumour) tissues by adapting the extraction protocol by Omotuyi et al. [32]., using RNA Snap kit (Invitrogen™ TRIzol™ Reagent) (Catalog number: 15596026; Lot No. 563909; ThermoFisher Scientific., Vilnius, Lithuania), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genomic DNA contamination was removed by DNase I RNase Free (Catalog Number: EN0521; Packaging Lot: 3388861; ThermoFisher Scientific., Vilnius, Lithuania), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer. Subsequently, the resulting DNA-free RNA was reverse-transcribed and amplified in a single step using the Luna® Universal One-Step RT-qPCR Kit (Lot No.: NEB E3005S; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA).

Gene expression data retrieved from the GEO database

The microarray gene expression profiles of GSE120691 [33] and GSE223804 [34] used in this study to identify investigated genes were obtained from the publicly available NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database of cervical cancer (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). GSE120691 comprises samples from 11 cervical cancer patients, 21 individuals with CIN, and 19 healthy controls, allowing comparisons across normal, precancerous, and cancerous cervical tissues [33], while GSE223804 includes data from 16 high-risk HPV-positive patients, consisting of 5 cases of CIN (grades I-III), 9 cases of invasive squamous cell carcinoma (including 4 microinvasive), and 2 samples of morphologically normal epithelium, enabling transcriptomic profiling across HPV-associated disease stages [34]. Validation of differentially expressed genes using quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

A gene selection from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database was studied using quantitative PCR. Primer sequences of selected DEGs are given in Table 2. The primers were synthesised by Inqaba Biotec West Africa Ltd (Ibadan, Nigeria). The kit used in the qPCR reaction was the Luna Universal probe qPCR master mix. Briefly, 1 µg of total RNA from each sample (tumour and non-tumour) was reverse-transcribed by the iScript™ cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, Cat No: 1708891). From that, 50 ng of cDNA (2.5 µl) was used to perform the expression analysis of selected genes on the Applied Biosystems real-time PCR detection system, with a final volume of 20 µl reaction in each well. The protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min (hot start), followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, and annealing/ extension at 60 °C for 60 s, as recommended by the manufacturer’s instructions. For reference, the GapDH gene was used as an endogenous control. The results were normalised with endogenous control using the Livak method (2−ΔΔCt) [35] with a little modification. A schematic illustration of the experimental design employed in this study is displayed in Fig. 1.

Differential expression of studied genes during cancer formation using TNMplot

The TNMplot (https://www.tnmplot.com/) was used to analyse the differential expression of the studied genes from GEO samples in normal (15,648 samples), tumour (40,442 samples), and metastatic (848 samples) tissues [36]. The analysis was conducted after the specific gene was fed into the web tool, and ‘Cervix’ was selected as the tissue to be investigated.

Identification of plausible mutations associated with altered expression of the studied genes using muTarget

muTarget (https://mutarget.com/) was used to identify potential associations between gene mutations and altered expression of the studied genes in this study [37]. The ‘Target’ option was selected, and ‘Cervix cancer’ was the tumour type used to investigate the studied genes. The threshold was set at a default value, and ‘All somatic mutations’ was selected as the somatic mutation type. More so, the analysis was based on the Mann-Whitney p-value, and mean FC and default thresholds were set at p ≤ 0.01 and 0.714 > FC > 1.4.

Identification of the association of studied genes with cancer hallmarks using Cancer Hallmark Enrichment Plot

The Cancer Hallmark Enrichment Plot (https://cancerhallmarks.com) was used to validate the association of the studied genes with the hallmarks of cancer [38]. The input query was the compilation of the studied genes.

Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG functional enrichment analysis

Gene ontology and functional enrichment analysis of the studied genes were performed using Web Gestalt (https://www.webgestalt.org/) [39]. The method of interest selected was Over-Representation Analysis (ORA). The GO functional enrichment was performed in the biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). The pathway analysis was processed in the KEGG pathway.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis for qRT-PCR data was performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 for Windows (www.graphpad.com; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA), mean ± standard deviation (SD), where one-way ANOVA calculated all the values with a significant p-value < 0.05, and a post hoc (Tukey) test was done on the results.

Results

Histopathological investigation

The histopathological results of the different grades of cervical cancer examined in this study are depicted in Fig. 2A-G. Eight (8) distinct morphological grades were observed in this study, namely: normal cervical epithelium (NC), mild inflammation (MI), chronic cervicitis (ChC), low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL; CIN I), moderately differentiated squamous cell carcinoma (MSCC), high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL; CIN II), well-differentiated invasive squamous cell carcinoma (WISCC), and poorly-differentiated invasive squamous cell carcinoma (PISCC). The normal cervical epithelium showed orderly stratification, intact maturation, uniform nuclei, and a continuous basement membrane without stromal inflammation (Fig. 2A), while, mild inflammation was characterised by preserved epithelial architecture with focal stromal lymphocytic infiltration (Fig. 2B). In chronic cervicitis, dense lymphoplasmacytic infiltrates, stromal edema, and vascular congestion were evident, accompanied by reactive epithelial changes without dysplasia (Fig. 2C). Low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (CIN I) exhibited dysplastic changes confined to the lower one-third of the epithelium, including basal cell hyperplasia, mild nuclear enlargement, hyperchromasia, and koilocytic features, with an intact basement membrane (Fig. 2D).

In high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (CIN II), dysplasia extended into the lower two-thirds of the epithelium, showing marked nuclear pleomorphism, increased mitotic activity, and loss of epithelial maturation, while stromal invasion was absent (Fig. 2E). Well-differentiated invasive squamous cell carcinoma displayed stromal invasion by malignant squamous cells forming keratin pearls and intercellular bridges, with relatively preserved differentiation (Fig. 2F). Moderately differentiated carcinoma showed invasive nests of tumour cells with reduced keratinisation, increased nuclear atypia, and frequent mitoses (Fig. 2G). In contrast, poorly differentiated squamous cell carcinoma was characterised by sheets of highly atypical cells with minimal keratinisation, severe pleomorphism, numerous atypical mitotic figures, necrosis, and aggressive stromal invasion (Fig. 2H).

Validation of DEGs in CIN patients

The differential expression of the studied genes was validated in this study, as depicted in Fig. 3A-L. Our findings reveal that SPP1 was upregulated in MSCC and HSIL compared to the NC (Fig. 3A). In contrast, ANKRD31 was upregulated in LSIL, MSCC, HSIL, and WISCC (Fig. 3B). More so, ERO1α was upregulated in MSCC, but downregulated in WISCC and PISCC (Fig. 3C), whereas, upregulation of CD177 was seen in the LSIL, MSCC, and HSIL, its expression of downregulation was in the MI, ChC, WISCC, and PISCC groups (Fig. 3D). Furthermore, the upregulation of IGHG2 was seen in the WISCC, and HSIL, but was downregulated in PISCC and ChC (Fig. 3E). The expression of KRT10 displayed upregulation in MSCC, HSIL, and WISCC (Fig. 3F). Nonetheless, IL-18 and TMEM45A genes had the same expression patterns; upregulation in MSCC, downregulation in all morphological grades compared to NC (Fig. 3G-H). GSTA4 was upregulated in MI, MSCC, and ChC; its expression was downregulated in HSIL and PISCC (Fig. 3I). ANXAI was upregulated in MSCC, WISCC, and ChC (Fig. 3J), while ANXA3 was upregulated in MSCC (Fig. 3K). However, they were downregulated in PISCC, while ANXA3 was further downregulated in ChC. MREG was seen to be upregulated in MSCC and PISCC (Fig. 3L), with downregulations seen in HSIL, WISCC, and ChC.

The differential expression of the validated genes was analysed across normal, tumour, and metastatic cervical biopsy samples using the TNM plot and is depicted in Fig. 4A-K. The result of the analysis showed that CD177 was upregulated in tumour samples but was downregulated in metastatic tissues (Fig. 4A). KRT10 was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic tissues, but was more upregulated in the latter (Fig. 4B). TMEM45A was upregulated in tumour samples (Fig. 4C). GSTA4 was downregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4D). MREG was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4E). SPP1 was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4F). ANKRD31 was downregulated in tumour samples and upregulated in metastatic samples (Fig. 4G). ERO1α was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4H). IL18 was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4I). ANXA3 was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4J). ANXA1 was upregulated in both tumour and metastatic samples (Fig. 4K).

Association of gene expressions with crucial gene mutations

The Mann-Whitney U test was performed to identify gene mutations associated with the differential expression of the selected genes in this study, highlighting potential targets for further investigation in pre-cervical cancer lesions. The condition of selected genes was FC > 1.4, p < 0.01. As shown in Fig. 5A-K, the 5 top genes associated with the expression of the selected genes were described. Notably, SPP1 expression was higher in tumour samples containing mutations of ANO4, JARID2, MDN1, NFE2L2, and NAV3; On the other hand, upregulation of ERO1α was associated with a mutation in CDCA2, JMJD1C, and downregulated in CREBBP (Fig. 5B). ANKRD31 expression was lower in tumour samples containing mutations of AKNA, ALPK2, TENM1, ZC3H7B, and USH2A (Fig. 5C); GSTA4 expression was higher in tumour samples containing mutations of NFE2L2, HTT, HSPG2, CDH12, and lower in STK11 (Fig. 5D); and MREG expression was lower in tumour samples containing mutations of BACH1, and TGM4, and higher in MYH2, NCOA5, and TGFBR2 (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

Cervical cancer is a major health concern globally, often linked to HPV infection [40], and involves a multi-step process from normal epithelium to pre-neoplastic (CIN) and invasive cervical cancer [41]. In Nigeria, it is the second leading cause of cancer mortality and morbidity [42]. However, in the course of conducting this study, we observed that patients with symptoms of cervical cancer, sometimes identify with locally advanced diseases, due to several factors such as delay in seeking medical intervention which has some underlying causes like disregard for subtle symptoms which include abnormal vaginal bleeding or pelvic pain, lack of awareness, illiteracy, amongst others. Investigating abnormal expression of certain genes in the CIN study thus has the potential to serve as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers for early detection and treatment of cervical cancer [43, 44].

Numerous studies have demonstrated that SPP1 promotes cell proliferation, immune evasion, and metastasis [45, 46]. Its aberrant expression can result in tumour progression; thus, in this study, SPP1 was found to be upregulated in both MISCC and HSIL (Fig. 3A), suggesting that SPP1 may play a significant role in the progression and aggressiveness of cervical squamous cell carcinoma via promoting cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis. This is in agreement with previous studies that associated the upregulation of SPP1 with the progression of cervical cancer and reduced overall survival [46, 47]. This finding also corroborates the result from the TNMplot, where SPP1 was found to be upregulated in both tumour and metastatic states (Fig. 4F). Its role in enhancing cellular survival and proliferation makes it a potential biomarker for the progression of squamous cell carcinoma from a less severe to a more severe stage. TNMplot helps to understand cancer and find causes, early signs, treatment targets, and ways to fight metastasis [36]. It is usually used to visualise gene expression across different stages of cancer. This study investigated the gene expression levels between paired normal, tumour, and metastatic tissues using RNA-seq data using TNMplot.

muTarget shows the connection between gene mutations and how other genes respond by changing their expression levels [37]. This innovative approach analyses large datasets of tumours, revealing how mutations in one gene might cause up- or downregulation of other genes. It can identify genes whose altered expression is driven by mutations, not random chance; it uncovers potential new targets for therapies; and it aids in discovering biomarkers. muTarget unlocks a deeper understanding of cancer, paving the way for more precise diagnoses and helping develop personalised treatment strategies. In our study, SPPI was upregulated with mutations in ANO4, JARID2, MDNI, NFE2L2, and NAV3 genes using muTarget (Fig. 5A), which play a major role in tumour growth and metastasis [48]. In addition, this upregulation results in a mutation that can plausibly affect the activity, stability, and localisation of Nrf2 protein, thus disrupting its signalling activity and leading to tumour progression and resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [49] Therefore, SPP1 is a plausible prognostic biomarker in cervical cancer patients in Nigeria.

ERO1α has been proven to play a key role in oxidative protein folding and cellular stress responses, potentially reprogramming energy metabolism in cancer cells [50]. Its overexpression has been associated with more tumour progression and poor prognosis [51]. In addition, recent studies have associated upregulation of ERO1α with several aspects of tumour progression such as angiogenesis, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [50]. In this study, ERO1α exhibited a more complex expression pattern. It was upregulated in MISCC but downregulated in WISCC and PSICC (Fig. 3C). Upregulation of ERO1α in MISCC may indicate support for tumour growth at this stage, possibly through its role in oxidative protein folding and maintaining cellular redox balance. However, the downregulation in WISCC and PSICC suggests a shift in cellular requirements or a response to different stress conditions in more differentiated or aggressive cancer stages. This differential expression pattern underscores the complexity of ERO1α in cancer biology, potentially reflecting changes in the tumour microenvironment or cellular metabolism as cancer progresses. This finding aligns with the result obtained from the TNMplot, where ERO1α was upregulated in the tumour state (Fig. 4H). Further analysis using muTarget confirmed the upregulation of ERO1α in conjunction with mutations in CDCA2, JMJDIC, and CREBBP, which play a major role in promoting cell division (Fig. 5B). This finding also corroborates the study by Lin et al. [52] on cervical cancer.

Another gene, ANKRD31, has been studied to be implicated in the early stages of cancer progression, showing upregulation in various grades of squamous intraepithelial lesions and squamous cell carcinomas [53, 54]. We observed that ANKRD31 displayed a wider range of upregulation across various stages and grades, i.e., it was upregulated in LSIL, MSICC, HSIL, and WISCC (Fig. 3B). This consistent upregulation across different stages suggests that ANKRD31 might be involved in the early and continuous development of squamous cell carcinoma. This expression pattern indicates that ANKRD31 could be a crucial player in the initial transformation of normal cells into cancerous cells and their subsequent progression to more advanced stages. However, it was observed that the TNMPlot analysis revealed an upregulation in metastatic tissues and downregulation in tumour tissues (Fig. 4G). Bioinformatics analysis using muTarget showed the decreased expression of ANKRD31 with mutations notably in USH2A, TENMI, ZC3H7B, ALPK2, and AKNA genes (Fig. 5C).

GSTA4 is downregulated in cisplatin-resistant cervical cancer [55] and has been identified as a gene that promotes proliferation and chemoresistance in colon cancer [56]. It has also been proposed as a biomarker for Enterococcus faecalis-induced inflammation [57]. In this study, GSTA4 was up-regulated in the MISCC and thus poses a potential biomarker for early-stage CIN (Fig. 3I). It has been studied that the upregulation of GSTA4 genes in certain cervical cancer cells is associated with the promotion of cellular proliferation by interaction with growth factors. Certain polymorphisms have been connected to changed amounts of GSTA4 expression, especially those in the gene’s promoter region [58]. Also, activation of GSTA4 in colorectal cancer cells has been shown to promote tumorigenesis and contribute to chemoresistance [56]. However, it was observed that the TNMPlot analysis revealed a downregulation in metastatic and tumour tissues (Fig. 4D). More so, analysis using muTarget revealed the upregulation of GSTA4 in the NFE2L2, HTT, HSPG2, CDH12, and STK11 mutations (Fig. 5D). For instance, CDH12 has been demonstrated to play an essential role in maintaining tissue architecture. It promotes cell adhesion [59]. Thus, the mutation of CDH12 modulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition, a process implicated in the invasion and migration of cancer cells [60]. On the other hand, low expression of GSTA4 is associated with somatic mutations in STK11. Mutations in STK11 have been reported to be associated with cervical squamous cell carcinoma as well as adenocarcinomas [61], causing poor prognosis in affected patients.

Studies by Damek-Poprawa et al. [62]. and Butler et al. [63]. demonstrated that MREG has been implicated in regulating lysosome function in pigment epithelial cells. This dysregulation of lysosomal function has been linked to various age-related diseases, including cancer [64, 65], suggesting that MREG may play a role in developing or progressing certain cancers through its modulation of lysosomal activity. In addition, it has been studied that MREG is usually involved in pigmentation and cellular trafficking, although its role in CIN has not been fully studied yet; however, changes in its expression can influence the progression of CIN by affecting key cellular processes involved in cancer. In this study, MREG was upregulated in MSICC and downregulated in PSICC (Fig. 3L), suggesting the importance of MREG in maintaining cellular differentiation and how it can contribute to a less aggressive tumour. On the other hand, downregulated MREG expression in PSICC might be correlated with loss of differentiation and increased malignancy. The result of this study aligns with the study of Meng et al. [66]., which also found significant down-regulation of MREG in thyroid cancer. However, it was observed that the TNMPlot analysis revealed an upregulation in metastatic and tumour tissues (Fig. 4E). Further bioinformatics analysis revealed that mutations in BACH1, MYH2, NCOA5, TGFBR2, and TGM4 correlate with an increase in expression of MREG (Fig. 5E). TGFBR2 is a protein product of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which suppresses the activity of human telomerase reverse transcriptase (hTERT) implicated in cell survival, proliferation, invasion, as well as G1/S transition, while decreasing cell apoptosis. TGRBR2 is known to function as a cancer suppressor via the TGF-β/Smad pathway [67]. Thus, therapies targeted at TGFBR2 might play a role in intervening in CIN cases in the Nigerian population. Furthermore, KRT10 is primarily expressed in differentiated keratinocytes and plays a crucial role in maintaining the integrity of the epidermis [68]. It has been reported to be upregulated in cervical cancer [69]. Furthermore, high expression of KRT10 has been associated with poor prognosis in cervical cancer patients [70–72]. In this study, KRT10 was upregulated in HSIL (Fig. 3F), which suggests its plausible role in the proliferation of cervical keratinocytes in cervical cancer and its potential as an important biomarker in the diagnosis of CIN [73]. Nevertheless, the TNMPlot analysis revealed an upregulation of KRT10 in tumour and metastatic tissues (Fig. 4B). Further analysis using muTarget revealed that KRT10 was reduced in mutations involving BRE, DNAH9, MED14, STARD9, and TRIM9 (Fig. 5F). It has been proven that MED14 functions as a subunit of the mediator complex that plays a role in regulating the expression of genes involved in cell development, growth, and metabolism [74]. So, a mutation in MED14 can consequently affect the expression of KRT10 and other associated genes with tumour progression and growth. Moreover, it has been shown to possess varying expression levels in several tumours.

TMEM45A has been studied to be implicated in the development of tumours and the resistance of cancer to chemotherapy drugs in hypoxic conditions [75]. This study was upregulated in MSICC and downregulated in other grades (Fig. 3H). This is in tandem with previous reports that TMEM45A is highly expressed in several tumours and plays a key role in enhancing proliferation, metastasis, and resistance to chemotherapy [75, 76]. As observed in the study, the upregulation of TMEM45A in the early stage of CIN serves as a potential biomarker for early detection in the Nigerian population. Correspondingly, additional study using TNMPlot revealed an upregulation of TMEM45A in tumour tissues (Fig. 4C). Moreover, muTarget revealed that TMEM45A expression was downregulated in mutations associated with ERBB2, LAMA2, MICALL2, PTPRS, and SYCP1 (Fig. 5G), corroborating reports from Ikushima et al. [77] that cervical squamous cell carcinoma is associated with amplification of these genes, especially ERBB2, as well as integration of HPV16. More so, CD177 is well-recognised as an immunological molecule and plays unique roles in cancer pathophysiology. It also functions as an indication of prognosis in a variety of malignancies [78, 79]. In this study, CD177 was significantly upregulated in LSIL, MSICC, and HSIL (Fig. 3D). This is in support that the higher the expression of CD177, the higher the tumour burden of the patient and positive expression of CD177 [79, 80], which denotes poor prognosis. Also, this indicates the major role of neutrophils in response to acute inflammation and aberrant cellular alterations, predisposing factors to cancer [81]. The dysregulated expression of CD177 could serve as a potential biomarker of CIN in the Nigerian population. In addition, a study using TNMPlot revealed a downregulation of CD177 in metastatic tissues (Fig. 4A). Moreover, muTarget revealed that CD177 expression was upregulated in mutations associated with DNAH8, NIPBL, PCDHGA6, SLC24A1, and SORCS3 (Fig. 5H). Thus, gene therapy targeting these genes may pose a novel therapeutic intervention in cervical cancer patients in the Nigerian population.

It is well-established that β-cell-derived immunoglobulins (Ig) function as antibodies in the humoral immune response. However, there is increasing evidence that many cell types express different forms of Ig [82]. Cancer-derived Ig has been discovered to promote tumorigenesis via several mechanisms, such as inducing inflammation, activating the aggregation of platelets, mediating tumour immune escape, and promoting the malignant behaviours of cancer cells. They serve as promising diagnostic and therapeutic targets in cancer patients [83]. In this study, IGHG2 was significantly upregulated in HSIL compared to the NC and other diseased groups (Fig. 3E). This may be suggestive of an attempt by precancerous cells to avoid immune destruction by interacting with Fc gamma receptors (FcγR) on immune cells, leading to invasive cervical cancer [84], suggesting that IGHG2 can be used as a prognostic marker for this cancer subtype among pre-cervical cancer lesion patients in Nigeria. IL-18 belongs to the IL-1 family, and it is the best-characterised member. Beyond serving as a critical regulator of signalling pathways in many immune responses, IL-18 also plays an important role in the anti-tumour process of many cancers [85]. IL-18, in this study, was significantly downregulated across all stages compared with NC (Fig. 3G); this expression pattern confirms the tumour-suppressive role of IL-18 in many cancer types [85, 86]. This agrees with a previous study by Matamoros et al. [87], where it was discovered that downregulation in IL-18 expression is associated with high-grade lesions and cancer. This is a validation that downregulated IL-18 expression is associated with the prognosis of cervical cancer, and IL-18 might thus be a potential therapeutic target for cervical cancer patients. Possible somatic mutations responsible for the alteration in the expression of IL-18 were identified using the muTarget web-based tool. Notably, downregulation of IL-18 was found to be associated with mutations in BRINP3, FLT4, MAP3K15, MGAM, and PHKA1 (Fig. 5I). These genes, especially, FLT4 plays a vital role in lymphangiogenesis in malignant tumours, leading to tumour progression, more so, MAP3K15 is a gene well-known for its immune-related activities against cancer [88]. In contrast to our finding, TNMPlot analysis revealed an upregulation of IL-18 in tumour and metastatic tissues (Fig. 4I).

On the other hand, ANXA1 is best known for its anti-inflammatory role and shows varying expression patterns in several cancer types [89, 90]. In the progression of cancer, it has been shown to switch roles from anti-inflammatory to pro-inflammatory [90]. In this study, it was upregulated in MSCC and WISCC, while it was downregulated in PISCC (Fig. 3J). The high expression of ANXA1 in this study in MSCC and WISCC suggests its role in maintaining cell differentiation and contributing to less aggressive tumour behaviour. This is in line with Pedrero et al. [91] study on head and neck cancers, which reported that the downregulation of ANXA1 was associated with increased aggressiveness of head and neck cancers. Conversely, its downregulation in PISCC suggests its correlation with increased tumour aggressiveness and potential for metastasis. This corroborates a study by Wei et al. [92], which reported that downregulated ANXA1 expression correlates with increasing progression of cervical neoplasia, indicating its potential as a relevant biomarker in detecting CIN lesions and cell differentiation in squamous cell carcinoma. Correspondingly, analysis from TNMPlot shows an upregulation of IL-18 in tumour and metastatic tissues (Fig. 4K), thereby confirming the expression patterns seen. Nonetheless, more analysis using muTarget revealed that ANXA1 expression was downregulated in mutations of AMOT, EIF5B, MYO15A, PIK3R4, and TP53 (Fig. 5J). TP53 encodes for the p53 tumour suppressor protein, widely known as the guardian of the genome, and influences the prognosis of most cancer types, including cervical cancer [93]. Most cases of cervical cancer are caused by human papillomavirus-mediated degradation of p53 via its E6 oncoprotein [6, 94]. Therefore, targeting these genes can pose therapeutic strategies for CIN in the Nigerian population. Furthermore, ANXA3 is involved in developing several human diseases, and its aberrant expression has been linked to the emergence and metastasis of many malignant tumours [95]. In this study, ANXA3 was upregulated in MSCC and WISCC, while it was downregulated in PISCC and CC grades (Fig. 3K). Its downregulation in PISCC might indicate its role in metastasis and tumour progression, while its upregulation in MSCC and WISCC suggests its contribution to a less aggressive tumour status. In contrast to our finding, TNMPlot analysis revealed an upregulation of ANXA3 in tumour and metastatic tissues (Fig. 4J). muTarget analysis revealed that ANXA3 expression is reduced with mutations in ALMS1, BIRC6, CDH12, PLCL1, and ZNF611. CDH12 is essential in maintaining tissue architecture and promoting cell adhesion [59]. The loss or mutation of CDH12 modulates epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process implicated in the invasion and migration of cancer cells [60].

This study evaluated the cancer hallmark enrichment plot, which reveals biological processes, like evading growth suppressors or inducing angiogenesis in cancer samples [88]. This plot is primarily used as a visual tool in cancer research and depicts enrichment scores for each hallmark pathway. High scores indicate a cancer that heavily relies on that pathway, providing clues for targeted therapies. In this study, it was revealed that ANXA1, ANXA3, CD177, ERO1α, GSTA4, IL-18, and SPP1 (Table 2; Fig. 6) are notably associated with multiple cancer hallmarks, such as sustaining proliferative signalling, evading growth suppressors, evading immune destruction, tissue invasion and metastasis, and resisting cell death. Despite having significant odds ratios and combined scores, the adjusted p-values for these associations are not statistically significant, indicating that these findings might be due to chance rather than true biological relevance. This enrichment analysis validates that these genes may play an important role in the progression and aggressiveness of cervical squamous cell carcinoma. While the hallmark enrichment analysis did not show significant associations for ANKRD31, IGHG2, KRT10, MREG, and TMEM45A, their consistent upregulation implies they may have a crucial role in the cellular mechanisms underlying cervical carcinogenesis, possibly in processes not captured by the current hallmark categories.

In addition, gene ontology and functional enrichment analysis using WebGestalt were evaluated. This was used for enrichment analysis against two key databases: Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG). GO enrichment helps classify genes based on their function (e.g., DNA repair and cell signalling). If a set of genes is enriched for a specific GO term, it suggests those genes play a crucial role in that biological process. This can provide insights into how the genes might be working together. Together, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis in WebGestalt offer a powerful lens to interpret gene expression data, guiding researchers towards a deeper understanding of biological phenomena [39]. This study evaluated the GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis of the studied genes in cervical cancer (Fig. 7A-D). The study findings suggest that the GO analysis categorises the functions of the studied genes into three main aspects: biological process, cellular component, and molecular function. The biological process encompasses the roles and processes these genes are involved in, ranging from cellular processes, metabolic processes, biological regulation, development process, localisation, cell communication, cellular component organisation, reproduction, response to stimulus, cell population proliferation, growth and multi-cellular organisational process (Fig. 7A). Also, the cellular component aspect identifies the specific locations within the cell where these genes’ products (proteins) are active, such as the cytoplasm, membrane, or nucleus (Fig. 7B). More so, the molecular function category highlights the biochemical activities of the gene products, including binding activities (like protein, ion, lipid and nucleotide bindings or enzyme binding) and catalytic activities (Fig. 7C). It provides insights into the molecular mechanisms through which these genes exert their effects on cervical cancer. The KEGG pathways analysis reveals the involvement of the studied genes in critical cancer-related pathways, according to the differential expression level (p ≤ 0.05). These pathways may include lipid and atherosclerosis, GnRH secretion, inflammatory bowel disease, drug metabolism, chemical carcinogenesis, legionellosis, glutathione metabolism, vibrio cholera infection, malaria, and African trypanosomiasis (Fig. 7D), relevant to cancer progression and metastasis. Further studies, involving large cohorts and diverse regions of the country to ensure that epigenetic factors do not play a role in these studied gene expression patterns, are recommended.

Conclusion

In this study, we validated the expression and prognostic potential of CD177, SPP1, ERO1α, IL18, IGHG2, KRT10, TMEM45A, MREG, GSTA4, ANKRD31, ANXA1, and ANXA3 in pre-cancerous CIN lesions and invasive squamous cell carcinoma. This array of genes investigated in this study was differentially expressed in pre-cancerous cervical lesions as validated by qPCR. Bioinformatics analysis of publicly available datasets also revealed the differential expression of these genes across tumour and metastatic states as well as identified plausible mutations that could alter the expression of these genes in cervical cancer. These genes thus serve as potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for the studied population.

Limitations of study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, HPV genotyping was not performed for the patients recruited, despite HPV infection being the primary driver of cervical cancer and its precancerous lesions. Second, the study cohort was relatively small, comprising only twenty-four patients, which limits the generalisability of the findings and underscores the need for validation in larger populations. Third, the study included only three non-CIN control samples, which precludes robust statistical analyses such as one-way ANOVA, limits the ability to perform paired comparisons, and may not fully capture biological variability, particularly for transcriptomic analyses. Consequently, caution should be exercised when interpreting comparisons with the patient cohort. Finally, some analyses relied on publicly available datasets (TNMplot, muTarget) that primarily represent invasive cervical cancer rather than CIN lesions, which may affect the direct relevance of these findings to precancerous stages. These limitations highlight the need for future studies with larger, well-characterized cohorts, comprehensive HPV typing, and more representative control groups to strengthen statistical power, validate results, and provide more reliable biological insights.
