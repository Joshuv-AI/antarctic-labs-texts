Machine learning-based sex estimation using palatal morphometry acquired from digital impressions
Authors: Kale Taib Karim, Dler Abdulrahman Mohammed, Mohammed Taha Ahmed Baban
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstimating sex is a key aspect of identifying unknown human remains. Among the anatomical structures used for this purpose, the human palate is particularly valuable due to its structural integrity during the lifetime and marked sexual dimorphism, making it an interesting structure for scholars to investigate. In recent years, innovative technologies such as intraoral scanners (IOS) and machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful tools, widely applied in the field of forensics, offering high accuracy and efficiency. This study aims to combine the previously mentioned technologies to estimate sex from morphometric features of the palate in the Iraqi subpopulation.MethodsMorphometric features of the palate were extracted from digital impressions of 100 male and 100 female subjects using various software. These features were analysed to predict sex using six different ML models.ResultsThe width of the palate in the canine area (WPC) and the area of the palate (APA) demonstrated the highest discriminative power, with areas under the curve (AUC) of 0.983 and 0.868, respectively. Among all ML models, the Support Vector Classifier (SVC) and Logistic Regression (LR) achieved the highest accuracy in sex prediction, reaching 95% as estimated by nested cross-validation (NCV).ConclusionThe results indicate that the methodology used for sex estimation in this study is promising. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and inclusion of a broader range of population groups are needed to confirm and strengthen the validity of these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07291-6.

Introduction

The identification of the deceased person is a critical step in nearly all processes related to the resolution of an individual's death [1]. It is important for both criminal investigations and civil matters. Beyond its legal and procedural significance, accurately determining the identity of the deceased person has a profound psychological impact by offering closure to the friends and family members of the deceased person [2].

In cases where the primary means of identification are not feasible, the identification process is initiated with sex estimation. It is considered the most important step in establishing the identity of a deceased person among the four primary components of the biological profile [3]. Sex estimation significantly narrows the pool of potential individuals, reducing the number of suspects by approximately half. This step is typically followed by estimating age, ancestry, and stature as part of the overall identification process [4]. However, on some occasions, estimating sex poses significant challenges for the investigating team when only limited parts of the human body are available [5], as the human body has the potential to undergo considerable disfigurement in various forensic scenarios [6].

The palate holds significant forensic value in the identification of human remains, particularly when antemortem data, which is recorded medical data of the person obtained before death, is available for comparison. Like fingerprints, palatal rugae exhibit significant individuality, with no two palates displaying identical patterns, even among identical twins [7]. Furthermore, palatal rugae retain a consistent morphology throughout an individual's life due to their composition of fibrous connective tissue covered by keratinised epithelium [8, 9]. Additionally, their unique anatomical location within the oral cavity, which is enclosed by numerous soft and hard tissues, offers considerable resistance to trauma and thermal damage and can resist putrefaction for up to seven days [10]. Moreover, evidence of sexual dimorphism in rugae patterns provides an additional dimension in forensic analysis, assisting in sex estimation, especially in cases where antemortem data are not available [8].

In recent years, dentistry has seen significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of IOS [11], which enables the precise capture of three-dimensional (3D) images of the dental arch, producing comprehensive details of both hard and soft tissues [12]. In forensic odontology, IOS offers several advantages over traditional impression methods. One of the main benefits of using digital impressions is their practicality, allowing for streamlined electronic storage and reliable comparisons between ante-mortem and post-mortem data, with the latter referring to records or conditions documented after death. [13].

Contrary to traditional impressions, digital files can be securely archived without occupying physical space and can be readily shared, facilitating collaborative efforts among forensic odontologists from different geographic locations and enabling remote consultation, including expert opinions from professionals. Such remote capabilities are particularly beneficial in circumstances where onsite forensic examination is not feasible [14].

Furthermore, digital impressions demonstrate higher accuracy than conventional methods and eliminate the need for plaster model fabrication, thereby expediting the process. They enable the detailed visualisation of non-metric dental traits, such as crown morphology, pits, pigmentation, and ridge details [15, 16], which are often crucial for identification. If a physical model is required, the digital data can be used to produce a high-resolution 3D-printed resin replica suitable for further analysis or presentation in court [16]. Additionally, the noninvasive nature of IOS ensures the preservation of human remains' original condition by avoiding physical contact or damage. IOS also has the capability to capture full-colour, high-quality 3D images, contributing to the creation of a comprehensive visual archive of dental structures for future reference in forensic investigations [14].

ML, a branch of artificial intelligence that entails creating a model to learn from current data and use recognised patterns within this data to make predictions [17]. Supervised ML is a subtype of ML that relies on training models using labelled datasets, wherein predefined input–output associations guide the learning process. This structured approach enables the model to establish relationships between variables, thereby facilitating the accurate prediction of outcomes for previously unseen data. ML has many applications in forensic dentistry. Research indicates that ML is applicable for age estimation [18], sex estimation [19], and dental identification, yielding encouraging outcomes [19, 20]. The use of ML overcomes the limitations of human subjective bias in the traditional approach of the forensic sciences [21].

To date, limited research has explored sex estimation based on palatal morphometry derived from digital impressions and analysed using ML techniques. Moreover, existing studies have demonstrated significant variations in palatal dimensions across different population groups, highlighting the importance of population-specific validation [22, 23]. Notably, there is no evidence in the literature that this integrated approach has been applied to an Iraqi subpopulation. Therefore, the present study aims to assess the accuracy of sex estimation using morphometric measurements of the palate, analysed through various ML algorithms in a sample of the Iraqi subpopulation. The null hypothesis of this study is that ML classifiers cannot predict sex with high accuracy based on the morphometry of the palate obtained from digital impressions.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study was conducted between January and June 2025 at the postgraduate clinic of the College of Dentistry at the University of Sulaimani. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Ethics Committee at the College of Dentistry/University of Sulaimani, No. 292/24 on December 16, 2024. This study was conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines. Participants were selected based on the study criteria from individuals referred for digital impressions acquisition as part of their dental treatment. The study details were thoroughly explained to all participants, and written informed consent (Appendix A) was obtained before conducting the clinical examination, filling out case sheets, and initiating data acquisition.

The nQuery sample size calculator, developed by Statsols in Boston, USA, was used to determine the sample size for this study. In a two-sided test comparing the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve to a reference value for continuous response data using the z-test approximation, with a test significance level of 5%, a sample size of 91 males and 91 females is required to achieve 95.11% power when the AUC under the null hypothesis is 0.8, as this threshold is widely regarded as the minimum level of reliable and acceptable accuracy in forensic for sex estimation [24, 25].

All participants were over 18 years old, residents of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, of both sexes, all maxillary teeth were present from right 2nd molar to the left 2nd molar, had no history of orthodontic treatment, or any palatal pathology, and excluding individuals with severe malocclusions, smokers, developmental anomalies of the palate, those who wear partial dentures or any appliances of maxillary arch, subjects with a history of palatal surgery, bony and soft-tissue protuberance, ulcer or swelling, any deformity, scars and trauma to the maxillofacial area. Following the clinical examination, 65 cases were excluded, and 200 digital impressions were included in this study (100 males and 100 females). All digital impressions of the palate were acquired using a 3Shape TRIOS 3 intraoral scanner, developed by 3Shape in Copenhagen, Denmark, with pre-embedded settings in the system.

Image analysis

All digital impressions were anonymised, and a single examiner conducted all procedural steps. Digital scans were exported as stereolithography (STL) files, with the images cropped posteriorly, distal to the second molar, to standardise the dataset. Segmentation of the palate is performed using the Mesh Mixer version 3.5.0 software developed by Autodesk Corporation, San Francisco, USA. The area of interest was selected using a brush with smooth boundaries and separate tools within the software. The segmentation was conducted anteriorly from the most anterior part of the incisive papilla and laterally while preserving all interdental papillae. A zero-degree angulation view was maintained throughout the process. The APA was then automatically obtained using the software’s analysis tool. Following this, the palatal rugae were segmented from the palate using the brush tool (size 25), along with smooth boundaries and separate functions within the software. The surface area of the rugae (AR) was determined using the same method as the APA measurement. The STL files of the segmented palate were imported into 3D Slicer software version 5.6.2, developed by the National Alliance for Medical Image Computing in Boston, USA, to obtain the WPC. The WPC was measured as the linear distance between two anatomically estimated points corresponding to the mid-palatal surfaces of the right and left canines at the gingival margin level, as shown in Fig. 2A. The STL files of segmented palatal rugae were imported into the same software to obtain morphometric measurements of the palatal rugae. Measurements were conducted by recording the distance from the most medial point to the most lateral point of each ruga (as shown in Fig. 2B). According to the classification described by Thomas and Kotzé [26], the palatal rugae were categorised into three types based on their length: Primary rugae: ≥ 5 mm; Secondary rugae: 3–5 mm; and Fragmentary rugae: < 3 mm.Fig. 2A screenshot of 3D Slicer software showing morphometric measurements: (A) WPC, (B) Palatal rugae

A screenshot of 3D Slicer software showing morphometric measurements: (A) WPC, (B) Palatal rugae

The following measurements were obtained: the length of the primary rugae on the right side (LPRR), the length of the primary rugae on the left side (LPRL), the length of the secondary rugae on the right side (LSRR), the length of the secondary rugae on the left side (LSRL), the length of the fragmentary rugae on the right side (LFRR) and the length of the fragmentary rugae on the left side (LFRL). All previously mentioned image analysis procedures were conducted on 20 randomly selected samples, with a three-month interval, by the same examiner to assess reliability. The total length of primary rugae (TLPR), the mean length of primary rugae (MLPR), the total length of secondary rugae (TLSR), the mean length of secondary rugae (MLSR), the total length of fragmentary rugae (TLFR), and the mean length of fragmentary rugae (MLFR) were calculated using Microsoft Excel 2021, developed by Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, USA.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 25, IBM Corp., New York, USA. The intraclass correlation coefficient was applied to assess intra-examiner consistency. Descriptive statistics were calculated, including mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range (IQR).

To evaluate data distribution, the Shapiro–Wilk test was utilised. Independent t-tests were used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied to non-normally distributed data. ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the ability of each parameter to distinguish between males and females. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ML algorithms

All ML models in this study were developed using Python version 3.13.5, by the Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, Delaware, USA. The ML algorithms were executed on a Lenovo laptop (Model: 20M3) equipped with an 8th-generation Intel Core i5 processor. The dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% testing sets. The analysis included six ML algorithms: LR, SVC, Decision Tree (DT), Random Forest (RF), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Gaussian Naive Bayes (GNB), which were applied to estimate sex on the 20% testing subset, with all models applied to the complete set of predictor parameters. The models’ performance was evaluated using recall, precision, F1 score, AUC, training, and testing accuracy. To evaluate classifier performance across thresholds, the precision–recall curves were used, and the average precision (AP) was calculated for each classifier.

Besides classification performance, the calibration of models was evaluated to assess their reliability in predicting probabilities. Three key metrics were utilised: the Brier Score which measures the mean squared variance between predicted probabilities and actual results, the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) that summarises the variation between predicted probabilities and frequencies observed across bins, and the Brier Skill Score (BSS) to differentiates the Brier Score of the model against a baseline, with positive values and higher scores illustrating superior performance.

To evaluate the reliability of the ML, tenfold cross-validation (TFCV) and NCV were used. TFCV was performed by shuffling the dataset and running the ML algorithms ten times, after which the mean and SD of each algorithm’s accuracy were calculated. NCV was applied to provide an unbiased estimate of generalisation performance while simultaneously optimising hyperparameters, and it consisted of two levels:Outer loop (model evaluation): A tenfold stratified cross-validation was used to split the dataset into training and test folds, generating unbiased estimates of model performance on unseen data.Inner loop (hyperparameter tuning): Within each outer training fold, a fivefold stratified cross-validation was applied to optimise model hyperparameters for all ML classifiers except for GNB.

Average classification accuracy and SD for each model were calculated across folds, providing reliable and robust performance estimates while minimising overfitting. All preprocessing, threshold optimisation, and calibration were performed strictly within the training folds to prevent data leakage. Feature scaling was fitted on training data and applied to validation/test sets. Hyperparameters were tuned through inner cross-validation, with the optimal model retrained on the full training fold. The probability calibration used Platt scaling fitted on training predictions. Calibration performance was evaluated with ECE using 10 equal-frequency bins.

Results

The mean ± SD age of the male subjects was 29.40 ± 5.32, while that of the mean ± SD age of the female subjects was 28.75 ± 5.59. In contrast, APA, AR, WPC, LPRR, TLPR, MLPR, LSRR, LSRL, TLSR, MLSR, LFRR, LFRL, TLFR, and MLFR were non-normally distributed. Intra-examiner reliability was excellent across all measured parameters, with intraclass correlation coefficient values ranging from 0.97 to 0.99. Discussion

Sex estimation is a fundamental step in identifying human remains and represents a critical component of forensic science, particularly when primary methods such as DNA profiling, fingerprint analysis, or dental records are unavailable. These primary approaches may be impractical in situations such as mass disasters, fires involving charred bodies, severe decomposition, airplane crashes, or when antemortem dental records are missing. In such cases, investigators rely on other techniques to construct a biological profile, with sex estimation serving as a cornerstone of this process [7, 27, 28]. Within forensic odontology, palatal rugae offer notable advantages for this purpose. Their protected position inside the oral cavity and the nature of the tissue make them resistant to trauma, decomposition, and high temperatures, while their uniqueness and postmortem stability render them reliable anatomical markers. Consequently, rugoscopy can serve as a practical, non-destructive, and cost-effective method in comparison to molecular methods in specific forensic contexts [7, 27, 28]. This study aimed to evaluate the efficiency of morphometric parameters derived from digital impressions of the palate, analysed using ML models for reliable sex estimation in the Iraqi subpopulation.

In this study, IOS was used, a technology widely employed in forensic applications to produce distortion-free, high-resolution digital impressions. Unlike conventional impression techniques, IOS enables immediate data acquisition, long-term storage, and electronic transmission, thereby enhancing reproducibility and efficiency [29–31]. However, this technology requires high acquisition, careful adherence to scanning protocols, and examiner training [32].

In the present study, image analysis was performed exclusively using Meshmixer and 3D Slicer software, which are primarily designed for 3D reconstruction, visualisation, and basic morphometric measurements [33, 34]. Consequently, only direct morphometric measurements were considered. Advanced feature engineering techniques, such as interaction terms, dimensionality reduction (e.g., principal component analysis), or automated feature selection (e.g., recursive feature elimination), were not applied because they may be unsuitable for small datasets, as they can lead to overfitting, compromised model stability, and reduced interpretability [35]. Focusing on direct morphometric measurements ensured that the features remained easily interpretable and reproducible.

The application of ML methods in forensic odontology has shown considerable promise for improving predictive performance. ML has been successfully applied to human identification, sex estimation, age estimation, analysing morphometric features of the skull, palate, and teeth to estimate sex with high accuracy [19, 31, 36–41]. Nonetheless, key limitations remain, including the need for large, representative datasets and the limited interpretability of complex models, both of which may affect accuracy and generalisability [42].

In this study, six ML algorithms were selected to balance predictive performance with interpretability and to ensure suitability for relatively small datasets. More advanced approaches, such as Convolutional Neural Networks, XGBoost, and LightGBM, were not employed, as they generally require larger datasets, are more susceptible to overfitting when applied to limited samples, and provide lower interpretability [43, 44].

Seven palatal parameters (APA, AR, WPC, LPRL, TLPR, MLPR, and LSRR) showed statistically significant differences between sexes (p < 0.05), with males having larger mean values. This sexual dimorphism can be attributed to various factors, such as sex hormones and muscular tension, which contribute to accelerated and prolonged bone growth in males. As a result, craniofacial dimensions in males are typically 5% to 9% larger than those in females, resulting in more pronounced facial and cranial structures [45, 46].

ROC and AUC analyses revealed WPC, APA, and AR as the most predictive parameters, with AUCs of 0.983, 0.868, and 0.807, respectively. Sensitivity and specificity for WPC were both 0.95, APA was 0.77 for both, and AR was 0.74 for both. In terms of ML, NCV was utilised as the primary analytical approach because it separates hyperparameter tuning from model evaluation, providing more robust performance estimates. This method reduces estimation bias and minimises overfitting compared with simpler validation schemes, thereby improving generalisability [47]. The results of NCV revealed that among the algorithms tested, SVC and LR achieved the highest accuracy (95%), while KNN exhibited the lowest predictive accuracy at 78%.

These results surpass those of earlier studies that used conventional statistics or discriminant function analysis, highlighting the advantage of utilising ML, which likely contributed to improved predictive performance [40, 48]. The use of specialised software for tracing and measurement, as computer-assisted measurements, is less affected by human error and offers greater precision than manual approaches [49]. Differences with prior studies may also reflect population variability, landmark selection, and the distortion-free nature of IOS impressions [17, 23, 50].

While some previous studies support the findings of the present study, others report contrasting results. Such discrepancies may reflect variations in methodology, morphometric measurement techniques of the palate, population characteristics, sample size, and data analysis approaches. The null hypothesis of this study was rejected, as sex could be predicted with an accuracy of up to 95% [24, 25] using multiple morphometric measurements of the palate and palatal rugae analysed through ML algorithms. To the best of our knowledge, WPC, in particular, represents a novel and highly discriminative parameter that has not been previously reported. Comparisons with prior studies are limited, as most literature provides descriptive analyses rather than quantitative data, and no WPC measurements are available for sex estimation. To ensure the reliability of the findings, intra-examiner assessment confirmed measurement consistency.

Beyond methodological accuracy, the practical application of this approach in forensic casework is essential. IOS combined with ML offers a non-invasive, cost-effective, and rapid tool for sex estimation, particularly when advanced forensic techniques such as molecular analysis are unavailable, especially in the event of war and mass disasters, where a large number of unidentified human remains are found. Furthermore, digital impressions can be securely stored, shared electronically, and potentially integrated with national forensic databases, enhancing accessibility in large-scale investigations. However, its implementation requires examiner training, standardised scanning protocols, and consideration of legal admissibility in forensic courts.

This study is subject to certain limitations, most notably the absence of external validation. Since palatal morphology may differ across population groups, the findings may have limited generalisability beyond the Iraqi subpopulation examined in this study. Future studies should be conducted across diverse populations to enhance external validity and facilitate broader forensic applicability. These considerations must be addressed before implementing such methods routinely in operational forensic practice.

Conclusions

Identifying the sex of human remains is a critical phase in the human identification process, as it significantly narrows the search for identity by eliminating nearly half of the potential cases. Consequently, scholars have studied different methodologies to develop accurate, rapid, and cost-effective techniques for sex estimation. The findings of this study suggest that the width of the palate in the canine area and the area of the palate parameters are the most reliable indicators for sex prediction. By using machine learning algorithms, specifically the Support Vector Classifier and Logistic Regression, sex was predicted with an accuracy of up to 95% as estimated through nested cross-validation. Although these results are promising, the absence of external validation limits the generalisability of the findings beyond the studied population. Future research involving larger and more diverse samples is recommended to validate and enhance the forensic applicability of this approach.
