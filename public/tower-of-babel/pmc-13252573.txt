Outcomes after inhospital, subacute, and elective TAVI in COMPARE-TAVI 1
Authors: Troels Thim, Henrik Nissen, Phillip Freeman, Jordi Sanchez Dahl, Rebekka Vibjerg Jensen, Evald Høj Christiansen, Ashkan Eftekhari, Bjarne Linde Nørgaard, Jonas Agerlund Povlsen, Steen Hvidtfeldt Poulsen, Kristian Altern Øvrehus, Christian Juhl Terkelsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWe aimed to compare procedural, echocardiographic, and clinical outcomes within the first year after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) among inhospital and subacute patients to elective patients in the COMPARE-TAVI 1 cohort.Methods and resultsAmong 1030 patients treated with TAVI, 927 (90%) had an elective procedure, 63 (6.1%) had a subacute procedure, and 40 (3.9%) had an inhospital procedure. Procedural characteristics and outcomes were comparable. Among elective, subacute, and inhospital patients, mortality at 30 days was 15 (1.6%), 0 (0%), and 0 (0%) and at 1 year 54 (5.8%), 4 (6.3%), and 2 (5.0%). Stroke at 30 days was 28 (3.0%), 0 (0%), and 3 (7.5%) and at 1 year 42 (4.5%), 5 (7.9%), and 3 (7.5%). New pacemaker at 30 days was 119 (14%), 12 (21%), and 6 (17%) and at 1 year 130 (16%), 14 (25%), and 7 (19%). Elective, subacute, and inhospital patients with LVEF ≤40%, improved their LVEF from 33% (28–37), 33% (27–37), and 28% (22–33) before TAVI to 46% (35–60), 43% (33–52), and 42% (33–55) at 30-days, and 54% (45–62), 53% (43–61), and 49% (39–63) at 1-year. Differences between the groups were not statistically significant.ConclusionAfter transfemoral TAVI with balloon-expandable valves in COMPARE-TAVI 1, procedural, echocardiographic, and clinical outcomes were comparable among elective, subacute, and inhospital patients within the first year.

Introduction

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is well-documented for the treatment of aortic stenosis in elective patients.1–8

In clinical practice, TAVI is also performed in patients with urgent or emergent treatment needs. The outcomes of such patients are documented in registries that indicate worse outcomes at 1 year despite procedural outcomes being comparable to those of elective patients.9–14 While preprocedural technical evaluation of subacute and inhospital patients, almost always, can be performed in the same manner as for elective patients, the assessment of comorbidities and frailty can be difficult in the face of severe aortic stenosis with cardiac decompensation and general deconditioning. Increased comorbidity may explain, at least in part, the observed worse outcomes at 1 year among patients with urgent and emergent treatment needs in these registries.9–14 Limitations in these registry studies may be the reliance on retrospective data and the lack of systematic clinical and echocardiographic evaluation after 1 year.9–14 In the COMPARE-TAVI 1 trial, inhospital, subacute, and elective patients were included, and clinical and echocardiographic outcomes were evaluated after one year according to a defined protocol. This provides an opportunity to evaluate these questions in prospectively collected data.

In this study, we use the COMPARE-TAVI 1 trial patient cohort to compare the outcomes after TAVI in patients with inhospital or subacute procedures with those in patients with elective procedures.15,16

Methods

Study design

This observational study divided patients undergoing TAVI into three groups based on procedure priority as registered in the COMPARE-TAVI 1 database, i.e. patients with elective, subacute, and inhospital procedures. Patients were categorized as inhospital when the heart team during an admission, most often acute, recommended that TAVI was performed before discharge. Patients were categorized as subacute when the heart team recommended TAVI within two to three weeks. The remaining patients were categorized as elective. Outcomes among patients with subacute or inhospital procedures were compared to outcomes among patients with elective procedures.

We used a cohort of patients defined by the COMPARE-TAVI 1 trial that included 1031 patients scheduled for treatment with transfemoral TAVI.16 One patient died before TAVI and was not included in this study.

Setting and participants

The COMPARE-TAVI protocol and 1-year primary outcomes of the COMPARE-TAVI 1 trial have been published.15,16 In brief, patients referred from the heart team for transfemoral TAVI were eligible for COMPARE-TAVI 1 if they were eligible for treatment with the two study valves. There were no exclusion criteria, so patients were eligible across all procedure priorities. Patients were randomized to treatment with one of two balloon-expandable transcatheter heart valves (THVs), i.e. Sapien 3 (29 mm)/Sapien 3 Ultra (20, 23, 26 mm) THVs (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, California, USA) or Myval/Myval Octacor THVs (Meril Life Sciences Pvt. Ltd., Vapi, Gujarat, India).

Patients were included between June 2020 and November 2023.16 Of all patients treated with transfemoral TAVI at the participating centres during periods with active inclusion, 77% were included in the trial. Participating centres were Aarhus University Hospital, Odense University Hospital, and Aalborg University Hospital (all in Denmark).

Variables

We report baseline patient and procedure characteristics and outcomes after 30 days and one year. Among the reported outcomes are death, stroke, and a new pacemaker after TAVI. We also report procedural outcomes, bleeding events (within 30 days), valve regurgitation, and endocarditis (at one year only) according to VARC-3 criteria.17 Outcome data were collected through follow-up visits, patient files, and health registries.15,16 Some endpoints were adjudicated by two independent cardiologists (acute myocardial infarction, endocarditis, bleeding, readmission with congestive heart failure) or a neurologist (stroke). All endpoints were monitored by trained study monitors.15,16

Protocols for computed tomography and echocardiography have been published.15,16

Ethical statement

The Central Denmark Region Ethics Committee approved the COMPARE-TAVI 1 trial.15,16 Participants gave oral and written informed consent before participation and the study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki principles.

Statistical methods

Numerical data are presented as median (interquartile range) and were compared using the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Categorical variables are presented as count (frequency) and were compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test when expected cell counts were ≥5 and Fisher's exact test when expected cell counts were <5. Patients with subacute and inhospital procedures were compared with patients with elective procedures (reference). We constructed cumulative risk curves for death, and cumulative incidence curves for stroke and new pacemaker with death as a competing risk. Hazard ratios for death were estimated with Cox proportional hazards models and for stroke and pacemaker with Fine–Gray models as unadjusted as well as adjusted for age, LVEF, and EUROSCORE-II.

Results

The cohort comprised 1030 patients treated with TAVI, of which 927 (90%) had an elective procedure, 63 (6.1%) had a subacute procedure, and 40 (3.9%) had an inhospital procedure.

Patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. Congestive heart failure was more frequent among patients undergoing subacute and inhospital procedures than among patients undergoing elective procedures, and their New York Heart Association functional class was higher and LVEF was lower. Among subacute patients, the aortic valve area was lower, while gradients were comparable to those of elective patients. Among inhospital patients, the valve area was comparable, but gradients were lower compared to those of elective patients. Inhospital patients more often had bicuspid valves and bioprosthetic valves compared to elective patients. The EuroSCORE II and STS-PROM were lowest in elective patients, higher in subacute patients, and highest in inhospital patients.

A particular concern treating subacute or inhospital patients with heart failure and reduced LVEF is related to the potential for LVEF improvement after TAVI. To address this concern, we made an analysis limited to patients with reduced LVEF defined as a LVEF of ≤40% (98 elective, 30 subacute, and 26 inhospital). In this subgroup of patients, LVEF was 33% (28–37), 33% (27–37), and 28% (22–33) among elective, subacute, and inhospital patients, respectively, before TAVI. At 30 days, LVEF was 46% (35–60), 43% (33–52), and 42% (33–55), and at 1 year, LVEF was 54% (45–62), 53% (43–61), and 49% (39–63). These changes in LVEF were not statistically different between groups.

Discussion

In the COMPARE-TAVI 1 cohort, patients undergoing subacute and inhospital TAVI had procedural, echocardiographic, and clinical outcomes that were comparable to those of elective patients despite presenting more often with heart failure. Importantly, elective, subacute, and inhospital patients with reduced LVEF improved their LVEF after TAVI.

Procedure priority definitions

In the STS/ACC TVT Registry procedure, priority definitions are based on the timing of intervention as well as the clinical status of the patient before the procedure.9 Urgent procedures are required during the same hospitalization, emergent procedures allow no delay, and salvage procedures are also mentioned. These categories are also used in other registry studies.10,11 The treating physicians categorize the procedures, and the categories urgent and emergent are often reported together as urgent/emergent or similar.9–11 This combined category (urgent/emergent), in definitions, corresponds to what we refer to as inhospital patients. The subacute patients in this study have an urgency level lower than that of this category but higher than that of elective patients. Based on the practise of the centres reporting to the registries used in the registry studies, this group of patients can be in either category. In this study, inhospital patients constituted 3.9% of the cohort. In registry studies, this number varies between 2.3% and 24%.9–14 Such variation can reflect variations in case mix and classification differences and can make comparison of outcomes in different studies difficult.

Outcomes and comorbidity

The outcomes among patients treated with TAVI are highly dependent on patient selection. In patient selection, the heart team-based evaluation of comorbidities plays a central role.18 For elective patients, centres have guideline-based, established, and tested evaluation pathways. However, these pathways are often unsuitable for subacute patients, and particularly inhospital patients due to the urgency of their condition. As a result, tests may be omitted. Other times tests are performed, but their reliability in comorbidity assessment, e.g. pulmonary function tests, may be less due to the impact of aortic valve disease.

In larger registries and cohorts from single or few centres, urgent/emergent patients are often reported to have higher comorbidity levels than elective patients.9–14 This does not translate into higher procedural risk.9–13 In some studies, it translates into increased short-term mortality and 1-year mortality,10–14 and in others, the 30-day mortality is comparable but the 1-year mortality is higher.9 This increased mortality is often attributed to the higher comorbidity levels of urgent/emergent patients under the assumption that valve performance is similar. In this study, procedural and echocardiographic outcomes among subacute and inhospital patients were comparable to those of elective patients, which agrees with these registry studies and their interpretation. In this study, however, the comorbidity levels were, overall, not different from those of elective patients, and the outcomes were also comparable after 1 year. This may also agree with the findings in the registry studies and their interpretation.

Patients in critical condition

Even in critically ill patients, the procedural risk does not seem to be increased, and favourable outcomes have been reported among patients in cardiogenic shock, although the outcomes were not as favourable as outcomes in patients without cardiogenic shock.19,20 In COMPARE-TAVI 1, patients in critical condition, including patients admitted to intensive care with cardiogenic shock, were also included. Our data, in contrast to these earlier studies, suggest that even inhospital patients with LVEF ≤ 40% may have great potential for improvement in LVEF and a good 1-year clinical outcome to parallel the overall outcomes in the cohort.16,21 Inhospital critical condition, as such, should therefore not exclude patients from TAVI.

Strengths and limitations

The results obtained in this study are highly dependent on the heart team process for selection of patients to TAVI and differences between the current study and the referenced registry and cohort studies may well relate to this factor. This and other factors can contribute to differences in outcomes of subacute and inhospital patients between centres.22

In COMPARE-TAVI 1, the patients were treated with one of two balloon-expandable valves and COMPARE-TAVI 1 only included patients planned for femoral access TAVI. The results may not extend to treatment with other valves and alternative access. In one registry, the use of balloon-expandable valves was associated with a lower risk than the use of self-expandable valves in urgent/emergent patients, although this finding may be biased by selection.9 Also, the results are obtained at three Danish centres with relatively high volumes per centre and operator.

The data from COMPARE-TAVI 1 are detailed, prospectively collected, and validated in core labs and end-point committees. These data should, thus, be of at least the same quality as those used in registry and retrospective cohort studies. While 77% of all patients treated with TAVI at the participating centres were enrolled in COMPARE-TAVI 1, we cannot account for procedure priority in the same way for patients not included in COMPARE-TAVI 1 and therefore whether elective patients were more or less likely to be included in the study than subacute or inhospital patients. Also, we cannot account for patients not selected for TAVI or give detailed information on conditions of inhospital patients (such as how many were in intensive care or on inotropes), which introduces uncertainty in relation to generalisability.

This study is observational, so clinical, procedural, and unregistered characteristics that may affect outcomes were not perfectly balanced between study groups. The sample size was limited to the patients included in COMPARE-TAVI 1 and the number of patients with subacute and inhospital procedures was relatively low, which increases the risk of chance findings and, at the same time, indicates equivalence in case of lack of statistical power to show a difference. However, the numbers in this report were not very different from the numbers in similar reports. Also, with three groups and a comparison of multiple characteristics and outcomes, differences could appear by chance. We performed a sensitivity analysis in which the subacute and inhospital patients were combined in one group, which was compared to the group of elective patients. The results of this sensitivity analysis were very similar to the reported results. Overall, our results should be interpreted with caution and indications of differences between groups are therefore compared to findings of other studies here and should preferably be investigated in other cohorts where these findings can be questioned or supported.

Conclusions

In COMPARE-TAVI 1, patients undergoing subacute and inhospital TAVI had procedural, echocardiographic, and clinical outcomes up to one year that were comparable to those of patients undergoing elective TAVI despite higher baseline risk and reduced LVEF. These findings support subacute and inhospital TAVI in selected patients but may only apply to transfemoral TAVI with balloon-expandable valves at experienced centres with structured heart team pathways.
