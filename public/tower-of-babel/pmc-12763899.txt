Bedside needle arthroscopy for native joint bacterial arthritis in a real-world clinical practice setting: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Alex B. Walinga, Stein J. Janssen, Tobias Stornebrink, Rover Krips, Sander W. Tas, Arthur J. Kievit, Gino M.M.J. Kerkhoffs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBacterial (i.e., septic) arthritis requires prompt source control, including drainage of the infected synovial fluid, often through arthrocentesis (needle aspiration) or surgical intervention, in combination with antibiotics to prevent joint damage; however, when surgical intervention is required, conventional arthroscopy can lead to treatment delays and anesthesia-related complications. To overcome these delays, needle arthroscopy was recently developed to offer the possibility of 2-mm diameter arthroscopy lavage under local anesthesia. The purpose of this study was to prospectively evaluate bedside needle arthroscopy under local anesthesia to demonstrate its potential as an effective, minimally invasive alternative for timely diagnosis and joint lavage in patients with (suspected) native bacterial arthritis in a real-world clinical practice setting.MethodsOver a 30-month period, this prospective, double center cohort study included patients with either confirmed (positive synovial fluid culture) or highly suspected (≥ 2 local signs and ≥ 1 systemic sign) native joint bacterial arthritis. The primary outcome was the need for reoperation (conventional arthroscopy or arthrotomy) within 30 days. Bivariate analysis assessed differences in patient and treatment characteristics between successful and failed needle arthroscopic debridement.ResultsForty-two patients (44 native joints) underwent needle arthroscopy. The mean age was 67 years (SD 16), the mean BMI was 26.8 kg/m2 (SD 3.9), and 69% were male. The knee (n = 34, 77%) was the most commonly involved joint. Within 30 days, 14% (6/44; 95% CI 5–27%) required a reoperation (conventional arthroscopy or arthrotomy). Two parameters were identified as risk factors for failure of a single debridement: the baseline level of ESR (112 mm/hr vs. 57 mm/hr, p = 0.027) and purulent synovial fluid (67% vs. 11%; p = 0.011). No serious procedure-related complications were observed.ConclusionsA single bedside needle arthroscopy was effective in treating 86% of patients with confirmed or suspected native joint bacterial arthritis in a real-world practice, avoiding the need for general anesthesia or conventional surgery. This approach represents a safe and effective, minimally invasive alternative that can be rapidly implemented, enabling early joint lavage and potentially reducing the risk of secondary osteoarthritis.Trial registrationWe pre-registered this trial on the Dutch Trial Register, later called CCMO (NTR 21076, CCMO NL78387.018.21).

Background

Bacterial (i.e., septic) arthritis of a native joint requires prompt diagnosis and treatment to control the infection, prevent joint destruction and life-threatening sepsis, and reduce the long-term risk of secondary osteoarthritis [1]. Treatment normally consists of drainage -with or without rinsing- of the affected joint followed by antibiotic therapy. Despite (repeated) arthrocentesis or surgical debridement and systemic antibiotics, some cases may require more than one debridement to control the infection, resulting in reoperations, prolonged hospital stays, and increased risk of anesthesia. A systematic review reported failure rates of 5–72%, with a pooled reoperation rate of 26% for conventional arthroscopy (n = 2.619) [2]. The diagnostic pathway for bacterial arthritis encompasses clinical assessment, laboratory tests, imaging, and often diagnostic bedside aspiration [3, 4]. In a real-world setting, when there is a high suspicion of bacterial arthritis (even if not yet confirmed), the patient often undergoes prompt surgical intervention, either arthroscopically or via arthrotomy, to lavage the joint, after which empiric antibiotics are started. Nearly all guidelines recommend repeated arthrocentesis or invasive surgical washout for bacterial arthritis, depending on the involved joint [5]. Nevertheless, surgery requires anesthesia and the availability of an operating room, potentially causing treatment delays as well as risking anesthesia-related complications [6]. To overcome these delays, bedside needle arthroscopy was recently developed to offer the possibility of 2-mm diameter arthroscopy under local anesthesia, an uncommon alternative worldwide [7, 8]. Hence, compared to standard arthroscopy, it is portable within the hospital and can be performed at the patient’s bedside outside the operating room [7, 8]. Currently, needle arthroscopy is more commonly being used in orthopedic care worldwide, both in the operating room and at the patient’s bedside under local anesthesia [9–11]. A small pilot study (n = 11) recently demonstrated the feasibility and safety of bedside needle arthroscopy for suspected bacterial arthritis [7]. However, larger prospective data is lacking, and the need for reoperations is unclear. Consequently, we performed a multicenter, prospective cohort study to assess whether bedside needle arthroscopy can be effective in the treatment of bacterial arthritis without the need for subsequent conventional surgery. Here, we report our findings and establish needle arthroscopy as a potentially valuable new tool to aid diagnosis and lavage of a joint in a timely fashion, minimizing morbidity from surgery and general anesthesia in real-world clinical practice.

Patients and methods

This prospective, two-center, observational cohort study was conducted in the Netherlands over a 30-month period. Ethical approval (METC 2021_197) and amendments were granted by the Medical Ethics Committee of the University of Amsterdam, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was monitored by the Amsterdam UMC clinical research unit (Amsterdam, The Netherlands). We followed the ethical standards set by the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013, the STROBE statement, and we pre-registered this trial on the Dutch Trial Register, later called CCMO (NTR 21076, CCMO NL78387.018.21) [12]. The study was conducted at the Departments of Orthopedic Surgery and Sports Medicine and Rheumatology & Clinical Immunology of Amsterdam UMC, locations AMC and VUmc, and the Department of Orthopedic Surgery of Flevoziekenhuis.

Patient selection

All patients with a suspected bacterial arthritis of a native joint (i.e., patients with confirmed or with high suspicion of a bacterial arthritis of the ankle, elbow, knee, shoulder, or wrist) that underwent needle arthroscopic debridement were included in this study and were followed for a minimum of three months. Patients were enrolled regardless of referral source (emergency department, outpatient clinic, or inpatient ward) and were followed for at least three months. Patients with (suspected) bacterial arthritis were included based on criteria that align with standard clinical decision-making, thereby reflecting real-world treatment practices:

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had suspected bacterial arthritis of the hip (due to the steep learning curve and operator dependence of hip needle arthroscopy), a foreign body in the affected joint (e.g., hardware or prosthesis), recent (< 3 months) arthrotomy with osteosynthesis involving the joint, an acute traumatic open joint fracture or dislocation, local osteomyelitis, or patients with a clear pre-existing diagnosis of non-infectious inflammatory arthritis of the affected joint at baseline.

Surgical technique

The surgical technique has been described previously [8]. All interventions were conducted at the patient’s bedside (emergency department, in-patient ward, or preoperative holding area) using a 2 mm needle arthroscope (NanoScope [n = 40] or NanoNeedle [n = 4]; Arthrex, Naples, USA) (Figs. 1 a and b).Fig. 1a: Bedside needle arthroscopic (NanoScope) lavage of bacterial arthritis of a native knee under local anesthesia. b; Bedside needle arthroscopic (Nano Needle) lavage of bacterial arthritis of a native knee under local anesthesia

Antibiotic regimen

All patients received empiric intravenous antibiotics after joint aspiration, in accordance with our institutional guidelines. Some patients had already received antibiotics prior to the joint aspiration. The choice of antibiotic regimen, route of administration (intravenous versus oral), and total treatment duration were determined according to clinical factors such as the severity and duration of infection, comorbidities, and the results of microbiological cultures. Patients were started on broad-spectrum antibiotics (usually intravenous flucloxacillin, which was administered alone or in combination with a short course of gentamicin). Regimens were tailored to preoperative aspiration and intraoperative culture sensitivities, with selection, duration, and route determined by a multidisciplinary team of orthopedic surgeons, rheumatologists, infectious disease specialists, and microbiologists.

Outcome measures and explanatory variables

The primary outcome was the 30-day reoperation rate (i.e., need for conventional arthroscopy or arthrotomy to control infection). The secondary outcomes were the rate of any intervention rate (i.e., needle aspiration, needle arthroscopy, conventional arthroscopy, arthrotomy, or other surgical procedures), mortality within three months, procedure failure (i.e., need for conversion to loco-regional or general anesthesia, device failure, joint not accessible), procedure metrics (e.g., intervention duration, maximal Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) score during the intervention, amount of fluid used during the intervention), hospital metrics (e.g., length of hospital stay and time between patient presentation and procedure), patient-reported outcomes at three-month follow-up (i.e., EQ-5D-5 L for all patients, sub scores of the Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score [KOOS] for bacterial knee arthritis, Foot and Ankle Outcome Score [FAOS] for bacterial ankle arthritis, SHORT-PROMIS Upper Extremity for bacterial shoulder, elbow, and wrist arthritis), the number and type of adverse events within three months post-procedure. We also recorded patient demographics and diagnostic laboratory values, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), white blood cell (WBC) count, blood culture results, and synovial fluid analyses (including crystal analysis, Gram stain, and culture).

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the reoperation rate within 30 days after the initial needle arthroscopy. Statistical analyses were performed with Stata 15.1 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA, 2017). Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages; continuous variables are presented by mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]), depending on distribution.

Bivariate analysis was performed to assess differences in patient and treatment characteristics between groups (i.e., successful and failed debridement), using a two-tailed t-test for normally distributed continuous data and a Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous data. Fisher’s exact test was used to test categorical data. Preoperative and 3-month follow-up patient-reported outcome data were compared using a paired t-test in case of normally distributed data and a Wilcoxon signed rank test in case of non-normally distributed data. A p-value below 0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were repeated for patients with a positive synovial culture (i.e., culture-proven bacterial arthritis).

A prospective sample size calculation assumed a 20% failure rate and a margin of error of ± 12% (95% CI 8%–32%). This margin was chosen based on feasibility and prior studies reporting failure rates between 9% and 33% for needle arthroscopy and a pooled estimate of 26% (95% CI 19–34%) after conventional arthroscopy [2, 7]. We determined that 44 joints were required. To correct for a potential loss to follow-up of 10%, the required sample size was 49 joints.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 49 eligible joints (in 47 patients), five joints (in 5 patients) were excluded due to loss to follow-up at three months. Therefore, we conducted an analysis involving a cohort of 42 patients with 44 (suspected) native bacterial joints treated with needle arthroscopy. The mean age was 67 years (SD 16), and 69% were male (Table 1). The knee was affected in 34 joints (77%). Blood cultures were obtained in 36 patients, of which nine (25%) were positive. Synovial fluid cultures were positive in 14 of 44 joints (32%). Five patients with a positive blood culture had a negative synovial fluid culture. Overall, 43% of the joints were diagnosed with a confirmed bacterial arthritis, and the other 57% had a high clinical suspicion of bacterial arthritis. Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome, the reoperation rate, was 14% (6/44 joints, 95% CI: 5%−27%). The overall reintervention rate was 23% (10/44 joints, 95% CI: 11%−38%). The median interval between the initial procedure and any reintervention was 6 days (IQR, 4–11) (Table 3).

Risk factors for procedure failure

Two parameters were identified as risk factors for failure of a single debridement: the baseline level of ESR (112 versus 57 mm/hr, p = 0.027) and the aspect of the synovial fluid (67% was purulent versus 11%, p = 0.011) after bivariate analysis (Tables 1 and 2).

Patient reported outcomes measures

Among all patients in which EQ-5D-5 L scores were collected before and after treatment (n = 33), the EQ-5D-5 L score improved significantly (p < 0.001) from a median baseline score of 0.073 (IQR − 0.025–0.45) to 0.81 (IQR 0.74–0.91) at the 3-month follow-up. The KOOS-subscales and the FAOS-subscales also improved significantly (p < 0.001 and p = 0.043, respectively), whereas the SHORT-PROMIS upper extremity (UE) scale also improved but not significantly (p = 0.11) from a median baseline to the 3-month follow-up. Antibiotic regimen

All 44 patients received systemic antibiotics in addition to joint lavage. The empiric regimen most frequently consisted of intravenous flucloxacillin, which was administered alone or in combination with a short course of gentamicin in the majority of cases. Alternative empiric regimens included clindamycin, benzylpenicillin, cefazolin, moxifloxacin, or ceftriaxone, depending on allergy, comorbidity, or clinical suspicion of Gram-negative infection. In patients with available culture results, antibiotic therapy was subsequently tailored to pathogen susceptibilities. The total duration of treatment varied between 0.5 and 12 weeks, depending on the diagnosis, with a median duration of 4 weeks (IQR 2–6). In patients with a positive synovial fluid culture, the most frequently identified pathogen was Staphylococcus aureus (n = 5), followed by Streptococcus agalactiae (n = 4) and Streptococcus dysgalactiae (n = 2), and these patients were treated for at least 4 weeks. Single cases were caused by Haemophilus influenzae (n = 1), Cutibacterium acnes (n = 1), and Streptococcus pyogenes (n = 1). Median antibiotic duration varied by pathogen: 5 weeks for S. aureus (range 4–8), 5 weeks for S. agalactiae (range 4–6), and 8 weeks for S. dysgalactiae (range 4–12). The single cases of H. influenzae, C. acnes, and S. pyogenes were treated for 4–6 weeks.

Sub-analysis of needle arthroscopy of patients with a positive synovial fluid culture

Among the 14 joints with a positive synovial fluid culture, the reoperation rate was 21% (3/14), and the reintervention rate was 36% (5/14). The median time between the index and reintervention was 5 days (IQR 4–6). 64% of the patients with a positive synovial fluid culture (9/14) were satisfied with the bedside needle arthroscopic intervention as the primary treatment for suspected bacterial arthritis. After univariate analysis, no risk factors were identified and associated with failure of a single debridement in this subgroup.

Discussion

The main finding of this real-world clinical practice prospective cohort study in patients with (suspected) native joint bacterial arthritis is that only 14% of joints required reoperation within 30 days, which indicates that bedside needle arthroscopy successfully controlled infection in 86% of cases under local anesthesia. This is of high clinical relevance, as timely treatment not only reduces the immediate risk of joint destruction but also prevents the development of secondary osteoarthritis. The use of needle arthroscopy, under local anesthesia, as a minimally invasive technique represents a safe and well-tolerated alternative for conventional arthroscopy or arthrotomy in case of (suspected) bacterial arthritis of a native joint.

The outcomes of this prospective cohort study are promising and justify further experimental study designs, such as a randomized controlled trial, to directly compare needle arthroscopy to other treatment modalities (i.e., repeated arthrocentesis or conventional arthroscopy/surgery at the OR). A recent systematic review of 2,619 conventional arthroscopy procedures reported a pooled reoperation rate of 26% (range 5–72%), with three high-quality studies specifically noting 33–38% reoperation [2, 13–15]. In contrast, our 14% reoperation rate (95% CI: 5–27%) suggests a potential advantage of needle arthroscopy under local anesthesia. In a pilot study by Stornebrink et al. [7], eleven joints with (a suspicion of) bacterial arthritis underwent needle arthroscopy. Only one of these patients (8%) required a second and a third needle arthroscopic lavage to control the infection. Of note, no conventional surgery was needed to control the infection in these patients. In that study, 27% (3/11) of the joints cultured positive for a micro-organism, and only one of these joints needed a second and third needle arthroscopic lavage (33%), which is comparable with our reported reintervention rate of 36% (5/14) in joints with positive synovial fluid culture. This further supports the need for follow-up research and an experimental study design to compare needle arthroscopy to other treatment modalities.

Our findings indicated two significant risk factors for single debridement failure: increased baseline ESR levels (112 vs. 57 mm/1st hour, p = 0.027) and the presence of purulent synovial fluid during the procedure (p = 0.011). Notably, 50% of patients with purulent synovial fluid (4/8) required reoperation using conventional surgery to control their infection. These findings show that such factors could be useful for identifying individuals who are more likely to require extra procedures. However, given the sample size and the lack of a control group, one should be cautious in ascribing too much significance to the few markers that achieved statistical significance (i.e. ESR and purulent synovial fluid). Furthermore, all measures of severity likely track together, and some may achieve significance in small samples by chance. Similarly, a recent systematic review found minimal to moderate evidence associating synovial white blood cell count, sepsis, large joint infection, and irrigation volume with conventional surgical failure [2]. These findings highlight the need for additional studies to improve risk stratification in the management of (suspected) bacterial arthritis.

Our data show significant improvements in patient-reported outcomes at the 3-month follow-up. The overall EQ-5D-5L score improved significantly (p < 0.001), reflecting a positive impact on health and quality of life. Patients with (suspected) bacterial arthritis of the knee and ankle had significant increases across all KOOS and FAOS subscales (p < 0.001 and p = 0.042–0.043, respectively). While upper extremity cases showed increases in SHORT-PROMIS scores, these were not statistically significant (p = 0.11), likely due to the small sample size. While controlling the infection and avoiding joint damage are the most important factors in the treatment of bacterial arthritis, determining patient outcomes in terms of physical function, pain, and quality of life is relevant for informing patients about their expected recovery. We are not aware of other prospective studies that evaluated patient-reported outcome measures in (suspected) bacterial arthritis.

Needle arthroscopy appears to have a low complication rate, and its minimally invasive nature may lower risks compared to conventional arthroscopy [16, 17]. A recent systematic review reported only minor complications ranging from 0% to 10%, with no major complications observed [18]. In our study, we observed only one minor complication (2.3%), a vasovagal response immediately after the procedure. Similarly, McMillan et al. [19] found no major complications in a review of 1,419 cases treated with needle arthroscopy, with only minor complications such as the occurrence of vasovagal events (1.9%) and transient pain lasting more than 24 h (0.3%). By contrast, several reviews have reported higher complication rates after conventional arthroscopy. Salzler et al. [20] reported an overall complication rate of 4.7% (4,305/92,565) following knee arthroscopy, with infection (0.84%) as the most frequent complication. Likewise, Arshad et al. [21] found a pooled complication rate of 4.1% (325/7,942) after ankle arthroscopy. Open arthrotomy is associated with even higher morbidity compared to arthroscopy. A meta-analysis of 20 studies including more than 10,000 patients demonstrated lower risks of reinfection and complications, as well as shorter hospital stay, in favor of arthroscopic over open procedures. Taken together, these findings suggest that needle arthroscopy, due to its minimally invasive nature, may carry the lowest complication profile of all procedures.

Multiple studies have shown that needle arthroscopy can be performed outside the operating theatre under local anesthesia, with high patient satisfaction [7, 18, 22]. As mentioned above, local anesthesia was also sufficient for a well-tolerated procedure in patients with (suspected) bacterial arthritis in our study. The median maximal NRS score during our procedures was 5, which is comparable to that of arthrocentesis based on a study by Sibbitt et al. [23] who reported a mean Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) pain score of 5.8 for palpation-guided arthrocentesis in a cohort of 22 patients. 83% of patients in our study expressed satisfaction with the intervention. However, the dissatisfied group’s median maximal NRS score was 7, while the satisfied group’s was 4. This result emphasizes the inverse correlation between pain levels and patient satisfaction. Even if other components of care were appropriate, poorly controlled pain might cause patients to have a negative overall experience. In addition, to minimize excessive pain scores, we advocate that the procedure is carried out by an experienced, well-trained physician.

Significant cost savings can be achieved by avoiding conventional surgery, especially in acute care settings where surgical expenses account for a considerable amount of total treatment costs [24]. Furthermore, the need for general anesthesia and access to an operating room can cause therapeutic delays due to preoperative testing or a lack of available resources. In cases with (suspected) bacterial arthritis, expedient drainage is crucial to prevent complications; thus, preventing treatment delays is very important. To thoroughly evaluate this, both groups should be compared to assess cost-effectiveness, which is outside the scope of the current study.

All of these findings suggest that needle arthroscopy could represent a safe and effective minimally invasive treatment option for managing bacterial arthritis, particularly in settings where prompt intervention is required. We advocate for the integration of this new approach into existing treatment algorithms within orthopedic and rheumatology diseases, and aim to contribute to comparative trials and guideline development to support its evidence-based implementation.

Limitations

Limitations include the modest sample size and the fact that the study was conducted in only two hospitals, limiting generalizability and warranting replication elsewhere. In addition, the potential bias from the relatively low threshold for performing lavage could influence treatment outcomes. Cultures were positive in 43% of patients, raising critical considerations about diagnostic accuracy and microbiological evaluation in bacterial arthritis. However, bacterial arthritis can be challenging to diagnose because of overlapping symptoms with other joint pathologies, previous antibiotic usage, or culture-negative arthritis, and therefore, the prevailing adage is “better safe than sorry,” including timely initiation of antibiotics and joint drainage. This approach mirrors real-world clinical practice settings, where patients are frequently treated empirically for suspected bacterial arthritis, often without microbiological confirmation, to prevent progression and complications. Thus, despite significant clinical suspicion of bacterial arthritis, it cannot be avoided that some patients with other joint pathologies, due to symptom overlap (i.e., gout), are included in these types of studies and, therefore, treated with needle arthroscopy. These cases did not “need” a reoperation. Though, some patients with culture-negative joints received a second intervention due to persistent physical and/or systemic symptoms in combination with persistent high clinical suspicion of bacterial arthritis. This is a potential study limitation, as culture-negative results could mask real pathogen identification, making it difficult to determine the full range of infections. Nonetheless, the clinical outcomes in both culture-positive and culture-negative cases were similar, implying that needle arthroscopy may be beneficial regardless of microbiological proof. We believe that excluding these cases retrospectively would reduce the generalizability of our findings. Given these considerations, we recognize the need for a randomized controlled trial to more rigorously compare needle arthroscopy with conventional surgery and/or repeated arthrocentesis. Such a trial would provide more solid information on the best strategy to manage (suspected) bacterial arthritis and could be more cost-effective. In addition, there was no standardized protocol for antibiotic selection, due to varying culture sensitivities and patient allergies, nevertheless this is a realistic representation of clinical practice in this patient group.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that 86% of patients with high suspected bacterial arthritis treated with bedside needle arthroscopy were successfully treated without having to go to the operating room, demonstrating the potential of this minimally invasive method. This strategy is consistent with real-world clinical practice decision-making, where early treatment is often deemed necessary despite pending or negative culture results. Furthermore, needle arthroscopy seems safe and tolerable, with minimal complication and mortality rates. This technique offers a compelling alternative to conventional surgical methods, which warrants further investigation via randomized controlled trials to solidify its place in the management of (suspected) bacterial arthritis.
