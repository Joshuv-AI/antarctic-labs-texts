Excess of post-resistance exercise energy expenditure: a comparison between morning and evening
Authors: Nan An, Daiki Watanabe, Motohiko Miyachi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeExcess post-exercise energy expenditure (EPEEE) refers to sustained elevation in energy expenditure above resting levels after exercise. This study compared the effect of morning (AM) and evening (PM) resistance exercise on EPEEE, with and without circadian rhythm (CR) adjustments.MethodsIn this randomized crossover study, 13 men with prior resistance training experience performed six resistance exercises, each with three sets of 10 repetitions at 60% one-repetition maximum, in separate AM and PM sessions. Resting energy expenditure (REE) and post-exercise energy expenditure (PEEE) were measured via exhaled gas analysis; EPEEE was calculated by subtracting REE from PEEE. CR-adjusted values were calculated using a sinusoidal model of energy expenditure across the day to derive REE-CR, PEEE-CR, and EPEEE-CR.ResultsREE did not differ significantly between AM and PM. Mean REE-CR values for AM and PM were similar. PEEE-CR in AM (194.4 ± 27.5 kcal/2 h) was significantly higher than that in PM (183.5 ± 32.2 kcal/2 h; P = 0.030), though insignificant in unadjusted PEEE. EPEEE was significantly higher in AM (49.8 ± 21.0 kcal/2 h) than in PM (30.1 ± 18.8 kcal/2 h; P = 0.049). CR adjustment attenuated EPEEE enhancement at AM; moreover, the difference in EPEEE-CR between AM and PM was no longer significant (AM: 47.3 ± 20.8 kcal/2 h; PM: 31.4 ± 18.9 kcal/2 h; P = 0.100).ConclusionCompared to evening exercise, morning resistance exercise increases EPEEE; however, this difference diminishes when CRs are considered. These findings offer practical implications for optimizing resistance exercise prescriptions aimed at improving metabolism and cardiometabolic health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-026-06268-9.Keywords: Circadian rhythm, Energy metabolism, Exercise timing, Resistance training

Introduction

Excess post-exercise energy expenditure (EPEEE) refers to the sustained increase in energy expenditure above resting levels following exercise, a phenomenon conceptually similar to excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC). EPEEE and EPOC are elevated energy expenditure and oxygen uptake occurring after exercise as the body works to restore balance (Borsheim and Bahr 2003). Mechanistically, exercise, especially at high intensity, shifts energy production toward anaerobic pathways, leading to the accumulation of lactate, depletion of ATP and glycogen, increased body temperature, and heightened sympathetic activity (Gaesser and Brooks 1984). After exercise, extra oxygen is required to resynthesize ATP (Gaesser and Brooks 1984), convert lactate back to glycogen, and restore oxygen levels in the blood and muscle (LaForgia et al. 2006). This process also fuels protein synthesis, enabling repair and adaptation of muscle tissue damaged during exercise (Phillips et al. 1997). Elevated oxygen use further supports hormonal regulation, particularly balancing stress hormones, such as epinephrine and norepinephrine, which influence metabolism (Imamura et al. 2004; Zouhal et al. 2008; Greiwe et al. 1999). Additionally, elevated core temperature is perhaps the most important factor contributing to an increased post-exercise metabolic rate (Gaesser and Brooks 1984). Overall, EPEEE and EPOC represent a vital component of the body’s recovery and adaptation, extending the benefits of exercise by enhancing energy efficiency, metabolic function, and long-term training outcomes (Gaesser and Brooks 1984).

Both EPEEE and EPOC are influenced by factors such as exercise type, volume, intensity, and muscle mass. For example, resistance exercise elicits greater EPOC than does aerobic exercise (Burleson et al. 1998), and high-intensity resistance exercise results in higher EPOC than does low-intensity resistance exercise (Thornton and Potteiger 2002). Moreover, performing resistance exercise at 8-repetition maximum (8RM) for three sets generates approximately three times the EPOC of a single set (Haddock and Wilkin 2006), and individuals with higher lean body mass exhibit greater EPOC than that of those with lower lean body mass (Tahara et al. 2008; Campos et al. 2012). In addition to these exercise-related factors, non-exercise variables, such as diet and menstrual cycles, also influence EPEEE and EPOC (Thornton et al. 2011; Lyons et al. 2007; Farinatti et al. 2016; Jarvinen et al. 2022; Fukuba et al. 2000).

Although the type, intensity, and duration of exercise are well-documented determinants of energy expenditure, the influence of exercise timing within a day remains less understood. Given that circadian rhythms (CRs) regulate core physiological systems, such as hormone secretion, thermogenesis, and substrate metabolism, the timing of exercise may be a critical but underappreciated factor influencing post-exercise energy expenditure (PEEE). The interaction between energy metabolism and CRs has received increasing attention in recent years (Zitting et al. 2018). Ruddick-Collins et al. (2022) demonstrated that diet-induced thermogenesis (DIT) is higher after breakfast than after lunch or dinner. However, after adjusting for CRs, no significant differences in DIT were observed among the three meals. Similarly, energy expenditure during aerobic exercise is higher in the morning than in the evening (Ezagouri et al. 2019). Furthermore, several studies evaluating the effects of exercise timing on weight loss suggest that morning training may provide additional benefits compared to evening training (Martinez-Montoro et al. 2023). Despite these findings, studies examining the effects of CRs on PEEE and EPEEE remain limited. Notably, to the best of our knowledge, no study has directly compared EPEEE following resistance exercise performed in the morning versus that in the evening.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the effects of performing resistance exercise in the morning and evening on EPEEE. We hypothesized that EPEEE would be higher in the morning than in the evening; however, this difference would diminish after adjusting for CRs. To test this hypothesis, morning and evening EPEEE were compared both before and after CR adjustment. In addition to energy metabolism, resistance exercise induces thermogenic, cardiovascular, hormonal, and oxidative stress responses, which may also be modulated by CRs. To ensure accurate interpretation of these physiological and biochemical markers, tympanic temperature, cardiovascular variables, and urinary concentrations of cortisol and 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) (Graille et al. 2020) were simultaneously measured. Specifically, tympanic temperature was used to evaluate thermoregulatory adaptations; cardiovascular measures (heart rate and stroke volume) reflected autonomic nervous system activity; urinary cortisol levels served as indicators of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis activation; and 8-OHdG was used as a biomarker of oxidative stress. Collectively, these assessments allowed for a multidimensional evaluation of potential circadian influences beyond energy metabolism.

Materials and methods

Participants

In this randomized crossover study, 14 men aged 18–35 years were recruited. Based on a previous study (Ruddick-Collins et al. 2022), a power analysis was conducted using G*Power based on a two-tailed paired t-test, considering the repeated-measures design of the study, with an effect size of 1.21, power of 0.8, and a significance level of 0.05. The required sample size was 12, and 14 participants were recruited to account for potential data loss. The inclusion criteria were as follows: men aged 18–35 years with body fat percentages between 10% and 30% and prior resistance training experience. This body fat range was chosen to reflect the typical distribution observed in healthy young men who engage regularly in resistance exercise. It allowed for the recruitment of participants who were neither under fat nor had obesity, thus maintaining generalizability while minimizing health-related confounds. Although we initially aimed to recruit individuals with consistent resistance training habits, this proved challenging owing to the limited availability of such participants within the recruitment timeframe. Consequently, we broadened the inclusion criteria to include individuals with prior resistance training experience, regardless of current training regularity. Completely untrained individuals were excluded, as their low neuromuscular adaptation and heterogeneous training backgrounds could introduce excessive variability in physiological responses. This pragmatic approach allowed for adequate recruitment while maintaining the reliability and interpretability of outcome measures. The exclusion criteria included a history of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, stroke, or skeletal muscle disorders; food allergies; smoking; an average sleep duration of < 5 h or > 9 h; use of medications or supplements affecting energy metabolism; inability to refrain from resistance training or high-intensity aerobic exercise within 72 h before the experiment; and inability to abstain from caffeine consumption within 24 h before the experiment.

Experimental design

The study included one preliminary measurement session (approximately 3 h) and two main experimental sessions (4 h 10 min each). Participants followed controlled lifestyle habits for 24 h before each session. The experimental sessions were conducted in the morning (AM: 8:30–12:40 h) and evening (PM: 17:00–21:10 h), with the AM and PM conditions randomly assigned after the preliminary measurements. To minimize the influence of potential confounding factors, participants refrained from strenuous physical activity for 72 h and from caffeine, alcohol, and supplements for 24 h prior to each session. They consumed a standardized meal (dinner before AM sessions and breakfast before PM sessions), along with 2,000 mL of mineral water, and fasted for at least 10 h before arriving at the laboratory. The standardized meal was a commercially available bento (demi-glace Hamburg steak with steamed rice), providing 545 kcal per 330 g serving, including 16.5 g protein (12.1%), 15.5 g fat (25.6%), 84.8 g carbohydrates (62.2%), and 1.6 g salt. The same meal was provided before both AM and PM trials to minimize dietary variability. All sessions were scheduled according to each participant’s habitual sleep–wake schedule. A 5–10-day interval was maintained between trials to minimize potential carryover effects.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the Waseda University Ethics Committee (approval number: 2022 − 350). Participants received verbal and written explanations of the study’s purpose and procedures and provided written informed consent. Before providing consent, the participants completed a screening questionnaire (Online Resource 1) to confirm their eligibility based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study followed strict coronavirus disease 2019 infection control guidelines.

Preliminary measurements

During the preliminary session, the following assessments were conducted: anthropometric measurements (height, weight, and body composition), one-repetition maximum (1RM) testing for six resistance exercises, and a questionnaire on sleep schedules.

AM condition (morning session)

The participants consumed 1,500 mL of water before dinner the previous evening, ate dinner between 21:30 and 22:30, and consumed an additional 500 mL of water between dinner and their arrival at the laboratory the following morning. They arrived at the laboratory at 8:30 in a fasted state and underwent anthropometric measurements and urine collection from 8:30 to 8:35.

The laboratory environment was maintained at a temperature of 22 °C and relative humidity of 55% using air-conditioning and climate-control equipment to ensure consistency. The participants rested in a seated position for 30 min before undergoing continuous measurement of resting energy expenditure (REE) from 9:05 to 9:35 using a respiratory gas analyzer. REE was recorded every 3 s, and averages were calculated. They then completed a light warm-up from 9:35 to 9:55, followed by six resistance exercises from 9:55 to 10:30, during which exercise energy expenditure (EEE) was measured. EPEEE was subsequently measured in a seated position from 10:30 to 12:30, and urine samples were collected again from 12:30 to 12:40.

PM condition (evening session)

On the day of the experiment, the participants consumed a standardized breakfast between 6:00 and 7:00 and refrained from additional meals or snacks before arriving at the laboratory by 17:00. They consumed 500 mL of water between dinner and breakfast and an additional 1,500 mL between breakfast and their arrival at the laboratory. Upon arrival, anthropometric measurements were taken, and urine samples were collected from 17:00 to 17:05.

The laboratory conditions (temperature and humidity) were identical to those in the AM session and were maintained using air-conditioning and climate-control equipment. The participants rested in a seated position for 30 min before undergoing continuous measurement of REE from 17:35 to 18:05 using the same respiratory gas analyzer, with measurements recorded every 3 s and averages calculated. They then completed a light warm-up from 18:05 to 18:25, followed by six resistance exercises from 18:25 to 19:00, during which EEE was measured. Subsequently, EPEEE was measured in a seated position from 19:00 to 21:00, and urine samples were collected again from 21:00 to 21:10. Apart from the timing, the procedures and conditions for the PM session were identical to those of the AM session. The experimental protocol for the AM/PM conditions is shown in Fig. 1.

Measurements

Height was measured using a digital stadiometer (SECA213, Medical Measuring Systems and Scales, Hamburg, Germany). Body weight and composition, including muscle mass and body fat percentage, were assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (MC-780MA-N; Tanita Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

The one-repetition maximum (1RM) for six resistance exercises (arm curl, lunge, lat pulldown, seated row pulley, bench press, and squat) was determined using direct methods with free weights or pulleys. A rating of perceived exertion scale specific to resistance exercises was used, with each exercise rated on a 10-point scale (1 = very light, 10 = maximal effort) (Zhao et al. 2022).

Energy expenditure was calculated based on oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production data obtained using a respiratory gas analyzer (AE310s, Minato, Tokyo, Japan). The Weir equation [16] was used to calculate the energy expenditure in kilocalories. The primary outcome, EPEEE, was determined by subtracting REE from PEEE. REE, EEE, and PEEE values were averaged over 10-s intervals based on the continuous respiratory measurements.

Body temperature was measured using an infrared ear thermometer (CE Thermo2, NEPRO, Osaka, Japan) and recorded every 5 min.

Circulatory function was assessed at rest, during, and after exercise. Heart rate was measured using the chest bipolar lead method, while stroke volume and cardiac output were recorded beat-by-beat using the thoracic impedance method (PHYSIOFLOW PF07 ENDURO, Manatec Biomedical). Data were averaged per min for 10 min at rest, 34 min and 40 s during exercise, and 120 min post-exercise.

Urinary cortisol and 8-OHdG were analyzed as markers of hormonal and oxidative stress, respectively, before and after the experiments. All analyses were outsourced to LSI Medience Corporation. The ratios of 8-OHdG and cortisol to creatinine were calculated to correct for urine concentration or dilution. Cortisol was measured using the Cortisol Abbott assay kit (Abbott Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Levels of 8-OHdG were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay using the New 8-OHdG Check ELISA kit (Japan Institute for the Control of Aging, Shizuoka, Japan). Urinary creatinine was measured using the L-Type Wako CRE·M kit (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Resistance exercise protocol

The resistance exercise protocol included six exercises: arm curl, lunge, lat pulldown, seated row pulley, bench press, and squat. Exercise intensity was set at 60% of the 1RM determined beforehand. The 1RM test used to determine training loads was conducted during the familiarization session in the morning from 9:00–11:00, and the same absolute weights were applied in both AM and PM trials to ensure comparable exercise workloads across conditions. Each exercise consisted of 10 repetitions per set, with three sets. Each repetition lasted 4 s, with a 1 min and 20 s rest period between sets. The total duration of all six exercises was 34 min 40 s. Participants were instructed to avoid holding their breath during exercises. Safety measures included the use of safety bars and the presence of spotters on both sides of the weights. Continuous respiratory data were collected during exercise using a gas analyzer connected to a respiratory mask and tubing.

The six exercises were performed as follows:

Arm curl: Standing with elbows fully extended, participants lifted a barbell to chest level by flexing the elbows.

Lunge squat: Standing with a barbell on the shoulders, participants performed alternating forward lunges with knee flexion.

Lat pulldown: Seated, participants pulled a lat pulldown bar from overhead to chest level.

Seated row pulley: Seated, participants pulled a bar toward the torso while leaning slightly backward.

Bench press: Lying supine, participants raised and lowered a barbell above their chest.

Squat: Standing with feet shoulder-width apart, participants performed squats with a barbell on their shoulders at a knee angle of 90°.

Data analysis and statistical procedures

REE values, measured every 10 s over a 30-min period, were summed to calculate the cumulative REE for each 30-min interval (expressed in kcal/30 min). Similarly, cumulative values for EEE, PEEE, and EPEEE were calculated over their respective measurement periods. The primary outcome measure, EPEEE, was calculated using two approaches:

EPEEE: Subtracting REE measured immediately before resistance exercise from PEEE obtained after resistance exercise under morning and evening conditions.

EPEEE-CR: Adjusting EPEEE values for CR effects based on established findings and the timing of morning and evening experimental conditions.

The average REE corrected for CR (REE-CR) was calculated over 30 min by adding the correction values derived from the formula to the 10-s REE measurements. Similarly, PEEE values measured after resistance exercise were adjusted for CR (PEEE-CR), and their 2-h averages were calculated. EPEEE-CR was then determined by subtracting REE-CR from PEEE-CR, with the final values expressed as cumulative energy expenditure over 2 h (kcal/2 h). This CR adjustment approach falls under the category of statistical correction using mathematical modeling rather than relying on direct physiological markers. The sine function used was derived from established circadian patterns in resting metabolic rate and has been validated in previous studies (Zitting et al. 2018). By applying this correction, we aimed to reduce the confounding effects of CR–induced variations in resting metabolic rate and more accurately isolate the time-of-day influence on post-exercise energy expenditure.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0.2 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The distributional characteristics of all dependent variables were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (n = 13). All primary energy expenditure variables met the normality assumption and were analyzed using two-tailed paired t-tests. Cohen’s d for paired samples, also referred to as dz, was calculated for PEEE and EPEEE as the mean difference between the AM and PM conditions divided by the standard deviation of the paired differences. Effect sizes were interpreted using conventional thresholds of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 for small, medium, and large effects, respectively. In contrast, urinary biomarkers of hormonal and oxidative stress did not meet this assumption and were analyzed using non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Outliers in the primary energy expenditure data were evaluated using the conventional threshold of mean ± 3 standard deviations; all values fell within this range and no data were excluded. Normally distributed variables were reported as means and standard deviations, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians and interquartile ranges. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05. Shapiro–Wilk test results are presented in Online Resource 2, and Wilcoxon signed-rank test outcomes are presented in Online Resource 3.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to provide novel insights into the effect of CRs on PEEE and EPEEE by directly comparing resistance exercises performed in the morning and evening. The primary findings of this study are summarized as follows: First, REE did not differ significantly between morning and evening conditions, and the mean REE-CR values in the morning and evening were very similar. Second, PEEE-CR in the morning was significantly higher than that in the evening, but not significant in unadjusted PEEE. Third, EPEEE was significantly higher in the morning than in the evening. Adjustment for CRs attenuated the enhancement of EPEEE in the morning, and the statistical significance of the difference in EPEEE-CR between morning and evening disappeared. These results suggest that EPEEE following resistance exercise is higher in the morning than in the evening; however, this difference diminishes upon considering the CR.

Our findings suggest that the timing of resistance exercise may modulate PEEE, with modest advantages observed in the morning. For individuals targeting fat loss or metabolic health improvements, especially those with sedentary lifestyles or impaired metabolic flexibility, morning training may offer a slight enhancement in daily energy expenditure (Willis et al. 2020). While the magnitude may not translate into considerable clinical effects in the short term, it may be meaningful over time when combined with dietary and behavioral interventions. In contrast, for athletes prioritizing performance, exercise timing should align with peak neuromuscular output, which often occurs later in the day (Chtourou and Souissi 2012). Nonetheless, morning sessions may serve as an adjunct strategy during weight-management phases. Ultimately, the integration of circadian considerations into exercise prescription may enhance intervention efficacy when tailored to individual goals and physiological contexts.

Zitting et al. (2018) conducted a 37-day study in humans to investigate the effects of a 3-week CR variation induced by controlling diet, activity, and sleep. They demonstrated that REE was lowest at approximately 5:00 AM and peaked at approximately 5:00 PM 12 h later, with an amplitude of 55.2 kcal/day. Moreover, Kräuchi and Wirz-Justice (1994) highlighted that the fluctuations paralleled the changes in proximal skin and rectal temperatures. Consistent with these findings, the estimated difference in REE between approximately 8:00 AM and 5:00 PM was 108 kcal/day in the present study (Table 3). Additionally, tympanic temperature was significantly lower in the morning by 0.32 °C compared with the temperature in the evening (Table 2), aligning with the results of previous studies.

Ruddick-Collins et al. (2022) examined the influence of CRs on DIT, referred to as the thermic effect of food in their study, using the standard method of calculating DIT based on pre-meal REE, and demonstrated that breakfast DIT (60.8 ± 5.6 kcal) was 1.6 times higher than that of lunch DIT (36.3 ± 8.4 kcal) and 2.4 times higher than that of dinner DIT (25.2 ± 9.6 kcal). However, when REE was standardized to account for CRs, no significant differences in DIT were observed among the three meals [breakfast, 54.1 kcal ± 30.8; lunch, 49.5 kcal ± 29.4; dinner, 49.1 kcal ± 25.7 (P = 0.680)]. These findings suggest that the differences in DIT across breakfast, lunch, and dinner are attributable to CR-induced variations in the resting metabolic rate rather than to the acute effects of meal timing. Contrary to these findings, our study revealed that the EPEEE following resistance exercise was approximately 20 kcal/2 h higher in the morning than in the evening. Notably, even after adjusting for CRs, the difference in EPEEE between the morning and evening did not completely disappear. This suggests that the magnitude of CR contributions differs between postprandial DIT and EPEEE following resistance exercise.

Morales-Palomo et al. (2024) demonstrated that high-intensity aerobic exercise performed in the morning offers unique advantages in improving cardiometabolic risk factors. Their findings revealed that, compared with that in the afternoon exercise group, the morning exercise group achieved significantly greater reductions in systolic blood pressure (–4% vs. − 1%, P = 0.019), fasting plasma insulin levels (–12% vs. − 5%, P = 0.001), and insulin resistance (–14% vs. − 4%, P = 0.006). Additionally, the MetS Z score was significantly lower in the morning exercise group compared to that in the afternoon group (–52% vs. − 19%, P = 0.021). These results suggest that exercising in the morning may have greater potential for metabolic regulation and improvements in cardiometabolic health. Despite the modest difference (~ 20 kcal) in EPEEE between morning and evening resistance exercise observed in the present study, this time-of-day effect may have practical relevance for exercise scheduling. For individuals aiming to optimize energy expenditure or fat loss, scheduling resistance exercise in the morning may offer incremental benefits when consistently applied over time. Additionally, individuals with limited time availability, such as shift workers or office employees, may benefit from adjusting their training sessions based on individual chronotype or physiological responsiveness. The greater effects of morning exercise training compared with evening training on the improvement in risk factors for metabolic syndrome observed in the previous study may be partly explained by the higher EPEEE in the morning than that in the evening observed in our study.

Based on the results of this study, it is challenging to determine the exact reasons for the higher PEEE and EPEEE values in the morning than in the evening. The potential contributing factors include differences in cardiovascular functions (Paschos and FitzGerald 2010), body temperature (Windred et al. 2024), and inflammation (Wang and Li 2021). In this study, both heart rate and stroke volume showed no significant differences between morning and evening conditions, suggesting a minimal contribution from cardiovascular functions. Tympanic temperature prior to exercise was, on an average, 0.32 °C lower in the morning than in the evening. During exercise, this difference decreased to 0.27 °C, and it further diminished to 0.12 °C during recovery. It is plausible that the greater PEEE and EPEEE observed in the morning may be attributed to the metabolic demands of increasing the body temperature from a state of lower baseline temperature in the morning to the levels required for exercise and subsequent recovery (Online Resource 4) (Carmo et al. 2023; Racinais and Oksa 2010). Moreover, the urinary 8-OHdG/creatinine ratio, a marker of oxidative stress and inflammation, demonstrated significantly greater changes from rest to post-recovery in the morning than in the evening. This finding suggests that resistance exercise performed in the morning is associated with greater oxidative stress than that performed in the evening. Moreover, antioxidant defense mechanisms are weaker in the early morning, producing stronger oxidative stress responses (Wang and Li 2021; Budkowska et al. 2022; Wilking et al. 2013). Notably, the morning urinary cortisol concentrations before the experiment were higher than those in the evening, and that this high level continued even after the experiment. It is a well-known phenomenon that cortisol levels are high upon waking and then decrease to normal once fully awakened (Bhake et al. 2019). The increased secretion of cortisol, an indicator of stress, is speculated to be partly responsible for the increased energy metabolism in the morning. The fact that no significant differences were observed in post-exercise values or in the changes from pre- to post-exercise may suggest that acute exercise-induced cortisol responses were not strongly influenced by time of day or that the pre-exercise morning peak masked the detection of exercise effects. Further studies are needed to clarify the interaction between exercise timing and hormonal rhythms. Taken together, the higher PEEE and EPEEE observed in the morning in this study may be related to enhanced metabolic responses to exercise under conditions of lower core temperature values and greater oxidative stress during resistance exercise in the morning than in the evening (Kanabrocki et al. 2002). Further research is required to elucidate the physiological mechanisms underlying these findings.

This study underscores the importance of CRs in understanding energy expenditure variations following resistance exercise, thus providing novel insights into this underexplored field. However, our findings should be interpreted cautiously owing to some limitations. First, the measurement period for EPEEE was restricted to 2 h, which may be insufficient to capture its full magnitude. Future studies should employ long-term metabolic measurements using human calorimeters to address this limitation. Second, although key lifestyle factors such as pre-experiment meals, physical activity, caffeine and alcohol consumption, and sleep were controlled to the extent possible, other variables influencing energy metabolism may have persisted. Consequently, complete control beyond morning-evening differences remains challenging. Third, strictly replicating identical conditions is inherently difficult, despite assessing the same participants in both morning and afternoon sessions in randomized order. This limitation hinders the comprehensive confirmation of the reproducibility of the findings. Fourth, although the 1RM test used to determine training loads was conducted in the morning, the same absolute weights were used in both AM and PM sessions. It is known that 1RM differs between morning and evening (Robertson et al. 2024). If the 1RM measured in the morning had not been used, the results could have possibly been different. Fifth, the study’s exclusive inclusion of young, healthy men raises concerns about sex bias and limits the generalizability of the results to broader populations, including women and older adults. Sixth, although a ~ 20-kcal difference in EPEEE between morning and evening sessions was observed, its practical implications, particularly for health promotion or weight management, remain uncertain. In addition, the relatively small sample size (n = 13) may have limited the statistical power to detect differences in some outcomes, particularly EPEEE-CR. As illustrated in Fig. 2, increasing the number of participants might reveal clearer time-of-day effects. Therefore, future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm these findings. Seventh, the influence of CRs could not be completely eliminated when comparing energy metabolism in the morning and evening. Although CR-adjusted indicators such as PEEE-CR and EPEEE-CR were employed in the present study, the discrepancies in individual responses before and after adjustment, as illustrated in Fig. 2, suggest that some participants may have experienced a mismatch between their intrinsic circadian phase and the scheduled exercise time, potentially contributing to metabolic variability. Ideally, by setting two conditions in which energy expenditure was continuously measured without resistance exercise session in the morning and evening followed by calculating EPEEE from the amount of differences with the exercise conditions to attempt a comparison between the morning and evening periods, it may have been possible to more accurately eliminate the influence of CRs. Moreover, validated chronotype assessment tools, such as the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire, were not used to enhance the interpretability of our findings and participant selection. Finally, the focus on moderate-intensity resistance exercise restricts the broader applicability of our study. Future research should investigate a wider range of exercise modalities and intensities to elucidate the role of CRs in exercise-induced energy expenditure.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the EPEEE was found to be higher in the morning than in the evening; however, this difference diminished when CR was considered. These findings highlight the importance of considering CR when evaluating exercise-induced energy expenditure.
