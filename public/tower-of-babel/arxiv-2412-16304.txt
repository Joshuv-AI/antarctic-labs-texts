Estimation of the frequency-shift between two photons by time-sampling measurements Preprint: APS/123-QED
Luca Maggio, Danilo Triggiani, Paolo Facchi, Vincenzo Tamma

Abstract
We present a sensing scheme for estimating the frequency difference of two non-entangled photons. The technique consists of time-resolving sampling measurements at the output of a beam splitter. With this protocol, the frequency shift between two photons can be estimated with the ultimate precision achievable in nature, overcoming the limits in precision and the range of detection of frequency-resolving detectors employed in standard direct measurements of the frequencies. The sensitivity can be increased by increasing the coherence time of the photons. We show that, already with ∼ 1000 \sim 1000 sampling measurements, the Cramér-Rao bound is saturated independently of the value of the difference in frequency.



I
Introduction

When two photons impinge on a balanced beam splitter from its two input ports, a well-known two-photon interference effect occurs
Hong et al. 1987
;
Shih and Alley 1988
. In particular, if the photons are completely identical in all their degrees of freedom, they will always reach one of the two output ports simultaneously (they will always bunch). This phenomenon has been widely applied in quantum information, e.g., quantum computing
Kok et al. 2007
;
Barz et al. 2012
, quantum key distribution
Tang et al. 2014
;
Guan et al. 2015
, quantum repeater
Sangouard et al. 2011
;
Hofmann et al. 2012
, and quantum coherence tomography
Teich et al. 2012
.

When the two photons are not completely identical, the probability of them reaching two different output ports (the probability of having a coincidence event) is not zero and depends on the overlap between the two single-photon states. It is thus possible to retrieve the value of a physical property encoded in such overlap by measuring the coincidence rate. Indeed, this interference effect has several applications in quantum metrology, and it has been used for evaluating time delays between the photons
Lyons et al. 2018
, frequency
Gerrits et al. 2015
;
Jin et al. 2015
;
Gianani et al. 2018
;
Fabre and Felicetti 2021
, and polarization
Harnchaiwat et al. 2020
;
Sgobba et al. 2023
.

More recently, it was shown that it is possible to maximize the sensitivity in estimating relative photonic parameters, such as time delay or transverse displacement, by resolving their frequencies or their transverse momenta
Triggiani et al. 2023
;
Triggiani and Tamma 2024
. The ultimate precision is achieved under the assumption of unit detector efficiency and perfect overlap of all the photonic degrees of freedom but the one to measure.

However, achieving the ultimate quantum precision in the measurement of the photonic frequencies remains an experimental challenge. Indeed, standard techniques that rely on the direct measurement of such frequencies are limited by the precision of the employed frequency-resolved detectors.

In this work, we present a two-photon interferometry technique for measuring the frequency shift between two photons with ultimate quantum precision. The sensing scheme is based on sampling time-resolving measurements of these photons after they impinge on a beam splitter. The precision that it is possible to reach with such a scheme is independent of the values of the relative photonic frequency we wish to estimate. This sensing protocol can have applications in vibrometry
Rehain et al. 2021
, characterization of biological material, or optical coherence tomography
Kolenderska et al. 2020
.

In this article, we describe the experimental setup in detail and evaluate its efficiency in a quantum metrology framework. With this setup, the frequency shift between two photons can be estimated with the ultimate precision, under the assumption of unit detector efficiency and perfect overlap of all the degrees of freedom of the two photons but the frequency. Also, we discuss the regime in which the ultimate precision is not achieved. We provide a comparison between this setup and a non-resolving protocol, proving that time-resolution measurements improve the estimation, especially in a high frequency-shift regime. Already with
\sim 1000
sampling measurements, the Cramér-Rao bound is saturated, independently of the value of the frequency shift.

III
Ultimate quantum sensitivity

An unbiased estimator of the frequency shift
\widetilde{\Delta\omega}
is affected by an error described by the variance
\mathrm{Var}[\widetilde{\Delta\omega}]
. This variance will always be bounded below by the Cramér-Rao bound, which represents the maximum precision achievable by the sensing protocol and it is a function of the Fisher information
F_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)
and the number of iterations of the experiment
N
Cramér 1999
;
Rohatgi and Saleh 2015
. This bound is always saturated in the asymptotic limit of large
N
. Ultimately, the Cramér-Rao bound is bounded from below by the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, which is the maximum precision achievable by any scheme, independently of the chosen type of measurement, and it depends on the quantum Fisher information
H\left(\Delta\omega\right)
and
N
Helstrom 1969
;
Holevo 2011
. Therefore, the following inequalities hold

\mathrm{Var}\left[\widetilde{\Delta\omega}\right]\geq\frac{1}{NF_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)}\geq\frac{1}{NH\left(\Delta\omega\right)}.

(6)

In particular, by using the probabilities in Eq. (
5
), the Fisher information assumes the form

F_{\nu=1}\left(\Delta\omega\right)=\gamma^{2}H\left(\Delta\omega\right)=2\gamma^{2}\tau^{2},

(7)

proving that under the condition of unit detection efficiency, this sensing protocol reaches the ultimate precision for
\nu=1
. This happens because for
\nu=1
the detectors cannot distinguish at all the two input photons, enhancing the two-photon interference. Interestingly, the overlap of the density distributions of the two photons in the frequency domain does not affect the asymptotic sensitivity of the sensing scheme. Also, the dependence of the Fisher information only on the coherence time is an advantage with respect to the standard direct measurements, since these operate only in a specific range of frequencies and their precision is limited by their finite resolution. Remarkably, our technique allows us to obtain a precision of already the order of
0.1
MHz (which corresponds, for example, in the visible range to a precision of the order of 20 am for wavelengths of the order of 800 nm) for
\tau^{2}N=10^{7}\mathrm{ns}^{2}
, achievable for example with a coherence time
\tau=100
ns
McKeever et al. 2004
;
Wilk et al. 2007
;
Keller et al. 2004
and just a number of sampling measurements
N\sim 10^{3}
, or increasing accordingly the number of measurements if photons of smaller coherence times are employed. Therefore, this technique outperforms the precision of most standard spectrometers in the state-of-the-art
Davis et al. 2017
;
Hiemstra et al. 2020
.

VI
Conclusion

We demonstrate the efficiency of an interferometric scheme based on time-resolving sampling measurements for the estimation of the frequency shift
\Delta\omega
of two photons. We show that in the regime of
N\sim 1000
the Cramér-Rao bound is approximately saturated and the bias is inferior to
1\%
of the value of
\Delta\omega
, independently of the overlap between the photons and
\Delta\omega
itself.

In fact, the efficiency of the detection does not depend on
\Delta\omega
. This is more evident when the sensing scheme is compared with a non-resolving interferometric technique, which is not efficient for large values of
\Delta\omega
. Instead, the precision of the estimation is dependent on the coherence time of the photons. That makes the precision virtually without an upper limit, especially considering techniques that can increase the coherence time of the photons.

Furthermore, this technique is not constrained by the detector frequency resolution required to directly measure the difference in frequency between the two photons. Instead, it is dependent on the efficiency of the detector in time. However, this requirement is considered only for imposing the photons indistinguishability at the detector. Having only this constraint in the detection allows us to outperform the standard spectrometers on a broader range.

&\left\langle 0\right|\hat{E}_{1C_{1}}(t_{1})^{+\left(d\right)}\hat{E}_{2C_{2}}(t_{2})^{+\left(a\right)}\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle=0,\\
\end{split}

(22)

The non-zero terms, instead, assume the forms

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle 0\right|\hat{E}_{1C_{1}}(t_{1})^{+\left(a\right)}\hat{E}_{2C_{2}}(t_{2})^{+\left(d\right)}\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}dt^{\prime}_{1}dt^{\prime}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)}\times\\
&\times\psi_{1}\left(t^{\prime}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t^{\prime}_{2}\right)\left\langle 0\right|\hat{a}_{1}(t_{1})\hat{d}_{2}(t_{2})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1})\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2})\left|0\right\rangle=\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{1-\nu}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)}\psi_{1}\left(t_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t_{2}\right),\end{split}

(23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle 0\right|\hat{E}_{2C_{1}}(t_{1})^{+\left(d\right)}\hat{E}_{1C_{2}}(t_{2})^{+\left(a\right)}\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}dt^{\prime}_{1}dt^{\prime}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{2}\right)}\times\\
&\times\psi_{1}\left(t^{\prime}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t^{\prime}_{2}\right)\left\langle 0\right|\hat{d}_{2}(t_{1})\hat{a}_{1}(t_{2})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1})\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2})\left|0\right\rangle=\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{1-\nu}}{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)}\psi_{1}\left(t_{2}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t_{1}\right),\end{split}

(24)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle 0\right|\hat{E}_{s_{1}C_{1}}(t_{1})^{+\left(a\right)}\hat{E}_{\sigma\left(S_{1}\right)C_{2}}(t_{2})^{+\left(a\right)}\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}dt^{\prime}_{1}dt^{\prime}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(S_{1},C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(\sigma\left(S_{1}\right),C_{2}\right)}\times\\
&\times\psi_{1}\left(t^{\prime}_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t^{\prime}_{2}\right)\left\langle 0\right|\hat{d}_{s_{1}}(t_{1})\hat{a}_{\sigma\left(S_{1}\right)}(t_{2})\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1})\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2})\left|0\right\rangle=\\
&=\frac{\sqrt{\nu}}{2}\Big(\delta_{1S_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)}\psi_{1}\left(t_{1}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t_{2}\right)+\\
&+\delta_{2S_{1}}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{1}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{2}\right)}\psi_{1}\left(t_{2}\right)\psi_{2}\left(t_{1}\right)\Big).\end{split}

(25)

Using these results in eq. (
20
), all the second-order correlation functions are

\displaystyle\begin{split}G^{\left(2\right)}_{C_{1}C_{2}}\left(d,t_{1};d,t_{2}\right)&=0,\\
G^{\left(2\right)}_{C_{1}C_{2}}\left(a,t_{1};d,t_{2}\right)&=G^{\left(2\right)}_{C_{1}C_{2}}\left(d,t_{1};a,t_{2}\right)=\frac{1-\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2},\\
G^{\left(2\right)}_{C_{1}C_{2}}\left(a,t_{1};a,t_{2}\right)&=\frac{\nu}{4}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\left|\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)+i\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{1}t_{1}-i\omega_{2}t^{\prime}_{2}}+\mathrm{e}^{i\Phi\left(2,C_{1}\right)+i\Phi\left(1,C_{2}\right)}\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}-i\omega_{2}t_{1}}\right|^{2}=\\
&=\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\left(1+\mathrm{Re\left(\mathrm{e}^{i\left[\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)+\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)-\Phi\left(2,C_{1}\right)-\Phi\left(1,C_{2}\right)\right]}\mathrm{e}^{i\Delta\omega\Delta t}\right)}\right)=\\
&=\frac{\nu}{2}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\left(1+\left(2\delta_{C_{1}C_{2}}-1\right)\cos\Delta\omega\Delta t\right),\end{split}

(26)

where
\Phi\left(1,C_{1}\right)+\Phi\left(2,C_{2}\right)-\Phi\left(2,C_{1}\right)-\Phi\left(1,C_{2}\right)=\pm\pi
in case of coincidence event (
C_{1}\neq C_{2}
) and it is equal to 0 in case of a bunching event (
C_{1}=C_{2}
).

A.2
Evaluation of the probabilities

After evaluating the second-order correlation functions we can retrieve the probabilities of coincidence (labelled with
A
) and bunching (labelled with
B
) as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}P_{\nu}\left(t_{1},t_{2},A|\Delta\omega\right)&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta=a,d}G^{\left(2\right)}_{12}\left(\alpha,t_{1};\beta,t_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\left(1-\nu\cos\Delta\omega\Delta t\right),\\
P_{\nu}\left(t_{1},t_{2},B|\Delta\omega\right)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=a,d}G^{\left(2\right)}_{11}\left(\alpha,t_{1};\beta,t_{2}\right)+G^{\left(2\right)}_{22}\left(\alpha,t_{1};\beta,t_{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left|\psi\left(t_{2}\right)\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}\left(1+\nu\cos\Delta\omega\Delta t\right),\end{split}

(27)

where the factor
1/2
is used for taking into account the symmetry
t_{2}\leftrightarrow t_{1}
. Since the sensing protocol is designed for detecting only the time delay, we can define
\tau_{tot}=t_{1}+t_{2}
and
\Delta t=t_{2}-t_{1}
, and we can integrate the probabilities over
\tau_{tot}
, obtaining

P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t,X|\Delta\omega\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau_{tot}P_{\nu}\left(\frac{\tau_{tot}-\Delta t}{2},\frac{\tau_{tot}+\Delta t}{2},X\right),\qquad X=A,B.

(28)

By solving the integral, the probabilities in eq (
3
) are,

P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t,X|\Delta\omega\right)=\frac{C\left(\Delta t\right)}{2}\left(1+\nu\alpha\left(X\right)\cos\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)\right),\qquad X=A,B,

(29)

where
C\left(\Delta t\right)
is the envelope defined as follows:

C\left(\Delta t\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\tau_{tot}\left|\psi\left(\frac{\tau_{tot}-\Delta t}{2}\right)\psi\left(\frac{\tau_{tot}+\Delta t}{2}\right)\right|^{2}.

(30)

If we take into account the efficiency of the detectors
\gamma\in\left[0,1\right]
(which is the same for both the detectors), it is possible to register events where 0 photons or only one photon is detected. By defining these probabilities respectively as
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
and by using eq. (
27
), we can write

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&P_{0}=\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}\\
&P_{1}\left(t_{1}\right)=\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt_{2}\left(P_{\nu}\left(t_{1},t_{2},A\right)+P_{\nu}\left(t_{1},t_{2},B\right)\right)\\
&P\left(t_{1},t_{2},X\right)=\gamma^{2}P_{\nu}\left(t_{1},t_{2},X\right),\qquad X=A,B\end{cases}.

(31)

Therefore, integrating with respect to
\tau_{tot}
, the probabilities become

\displaystyle\begin{cases}&P_{0}=\left(1-\gamma\right)^{2}\\
&P_{1}\left(\Delta t\right)=2\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right)\int d\tau_{tot}\left|\psi\left(t_{1}\right)\right|^{2}=2\gamma\left(1-\gamma\right)\\
&P\left(\Delta t.X|\Delta\omega\right)=\gamma^{2}P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t.X|\Delta\omega\right),\qquad X=A,B\end{cases}.

(32)

Here, the one-photon event and the zero-photons event have a probability that depends only to the efficiency of the detectors. The two-photons event has a probability that is proportional to the square of the efficiency.

Appendix B
Evaluation of the quantum Fisher information in eq.
6
and eq.
7

In this section we evaluate the quantum Fisher information
H
for the estimation of
\Delta\omega=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}
that is encoded in the input state
\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle
defined in eq (
15
). In order to do that, we evaluate first the quantum Fisher information for the single parameters
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. Each photon encode respectively of these parameter. We define for the i-th photon the single-photon state
\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle
,
i=1,2
, such that
\left|\bm{\psi}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle\otimes\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt^{\prime}_{1}\left|\psi\left(t^{\prime}_{1}\right)\right|\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{1}t^{\prime}_{1}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1})\left|0\right\rangle,\\
\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle=&\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt^{\prime}_{2}\left|\psi\left(t^{\prime}_{2}\right)\right|\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{2}t^{\prime}_{2}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2})\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(33)

therefore, the quantum Fisher information is the sum of two matrices, i.e.
H=H_{1}+H_{2}
, where

H_{i}\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)=4\mathrm{Re}\left(\left\langle\partial_{x}\psi_{i}|\partial_{x}\psi_{i}\right\rangle-\left|\left\langle\psi_{i}|\partial_{x}\psi_{i}\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right),\qquad x=\omega_{1},\omega_{2}.

(34)

Since each photon encode only one parameter,
\left|\partial_{\omega_{2}}\psi_{1}\right\rangle=\left|\partial_{\omega_{1}}\psi_{2}\right\rangle=0
. Therefore, the quantum Fisher information will be diagonal:

H\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}H_{1}\left(\omega_{1}\right)&0\\
0&H_{2}\left(\omega_{2}\right)\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

We find the i-th diagonal term by using
\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle
in eq. (
33
) and
\left|\partial_{\omega_{i}}\psi_{i}\right\rangle
, as shown in eq (
34
). The kets
\left|\partial_{\omega_{i}}\psi_{i}\right\rangle
are the following:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\partial_{\omega_{1}}\psi_{1}\right\rangle=&-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{1}\left|\psi\left(t^{\prime}_{1}\right)\right|\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{1}t^{\prime}_{1}}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{1}(t^{\prime}_{1})\left|0\right\rangle,\\
\left|\partial_{\omega_{2}}\psi_{2}\right\rangle=&-i\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt^{\prime}_{2}t^{\prime}_{2}\left|\psi\left(t^{\prime}_{2}\right)\right|\mathrm{e}^{-i\omega_{2}t^{\prime}_{2}}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{2}(t^{\prime}_{2})\left|0\right\rangle.\end{split}

(36)

So, by using the eqs. (
33
), (
34
) and (
36
), we find the i-th diagonal term of the quantum Fisher information

H_{i}\left(\omega_{i}\right)=4\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt_{i}\left|t_{i}\psi\left(t_{i}\right)\right|^{2}-\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}dt_{i}t_{i}\left|\psi\left(t_{i}\right)\right|^{2}\right|^{2}\right)=4\tau^{2},

(37)

where
\tau
is the variance of the temporal distribution
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right|^{2}
of each photon. Thus the quantum Fisher information for the parameters
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
assumes the form

H\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)=4\tau^{2}I,

(38)

where
I
is the
2\times 2
identity matrix. For evaluating the quantum Fisher information for
\Delta\omega
, we define
\omega_{M}=\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}
. We evaluate the quantum Fisher information for
\left(\omega_{M},\Delta\omega\right)
by applying a transformation on
H
with the Jacobian

J=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}

(39)

in the following way:

H\left(\omega_{M},\Delta\omega\right)=JH\left(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right)J^{T}=\tau^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{pmatrix}=2\tau^{2}I.

(40)

This quantum Fisher information proves that it is possible to estimate separately
\omega_{M}
and
\Delta\omega
, and that the quantum Fisher information for
\Delta\omega
is
H\left(\Delta\omega\right)=2\tau^{2}
.

Appendix C
Evaluation of the Fisher information

C.1
Fisher information in eq.
9

In this section we evaluate the Fisher information starting from its definition

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)&=E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta\omega}\log P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t,X|\Delta\omega\right)\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

(41)

Here, by using the probabilities formula in eq
3
, we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)&=\sum_{X=A,B}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta t\frac{1}{P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t,X|\Delta\omega\right)}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta\omega}P_{\nu}\left(\Delta t,X|\Delta\omega\right)\right)^{2}=\\
&=\nu^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta tC\left(\Delta t\right)\frac{\Delta t^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)}{1-\nu^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta tC\left(\Delta t\right)\Delta t^{2}\beta_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right),\end{split}

(42)

where we define the function
\beta_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)
as follows:

\beta_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)=\frac{\nu^{2}\sin^{2}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)}{1-\nu^{2}\cos^{2}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)}.

(43)

It is worth to notice that for
\nu=1
,
\beta_{\nu}=1\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)=1
. So, in this case the Fisher information assumes the form

F_{\nu=1}\left(\Delta\omega\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta t\Delta t^{2}C\left(\Delta t\right)=2\tau^{2}=H\left(\Delta\omega\right).

(44)

Therefore, for
\nu=1
, the fisher information saturates the quantum Cramér-Rao bound, and the maximum precision is achieved.

C.2
For
\tau\Delta\omega\gg 1

the Fisher information assumes a simpler form in the regime
\tau\Delta\omega\gg 1
. In fact, in this regime, we can approximate the value of the Fisher information by splitting the interval of integration in eq. (
42
) in intervals of length
\pi/\Delta t
. In each of this intervals, the term
C\left(\Delta t\right)\Delta t^{2}
can be considered approximately constant, and thus we can substitute it with a constant value:

F_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)\simeq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)^{2}C\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)\int_{\frac{n\pi}{\Delta\omega}}^{\frac{\left(n+1\right)\pi}{\Delta\omega}}d\Delta t\beta_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)^{2}C\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)\frac{\Delta\omega}{\pi}\bar{\beta}_{\nu},

(45)

where
\bar{\beta}_{\nu}
is the mean of the function
\beta_{\nu}\left(x\right)
in each interval:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{\beta}_{\nu}=\frac{\Delta\omega}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{\Delta\omega}}d\Delta t\beta_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\Delta t\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\pi}dx\beta_{\nu}\left(x\right)=1-\sqrt{1-\nu^{2}}.\end{split}

(46)

By summing again all the terms of the Fisher information in eq (
45
), we can rewrite the Fisher information as an integral, by using the approximations
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{\Delta\omega}{\pi}\bar{\beta}_{\nu}\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\xi
and
\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)^{2}C\left(\frac{\pi n}{\Delta\omega}\right)\rightarrow C\left(\xi\right)\xi^{2}
,

F_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)\simeq\bar{\beta}_{\nu}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\xi C\left(\xi\right)\xi^{2}=2\tau^{2}\bar{\beta}_{\nu}.

(47)

In this way we prove that in the regime
\tau\Delta\omega\gg 1
, the Fisher information is proportional to the quantum Fisher information by a function of only
\nu
.

C.3
Fisher information in eq.
11

If the distribution in time of the two input photons
\left|\psi\left(t\right)\right|^{2}
is Gaussian, we can show that the envelope in eq.
30
can be expressed as follows:

C\left(\Delta t\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\tau^{2}}}\mathrm{exp}\left[-\frac{\Delta t}{4\tau^{2}}\right].

(48)

Therefore, by substituting

in eq (

(49)

C.4
Fisher information in eq.
12

If the detectors at the output of the beam splitter represented in fig.
1
do not resolve the time delay, the output can be only of two types: bunching and coincidence. We find their probabilities by integrating with respect to
\Delta t
the probabilities in eq. (

):

\displaystyle P\left(X|\Delta\omega\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta tP_{\nu}\left(X,\Delta t|\Delta\omega\right)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\nu\alpha\left(X\right)}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d\Delta tC\left(\Delta t\right)\cos^{2}\left(\Delta t\Delta\omega\right),\qquad X=A,B.

(50)

In particular, if the photons are Gaussian in time, by using eq. (

P\left(X|\Delta\omega\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\nu\alpha\left(X\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\tau^{2}\Delta\omega^{2}}\right),\qquad X=A,B.

(51)

We evaluate the Fisher information using its definition, that here is written for clarity:

\displaystyle\begin{split}F^{G,NR}_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)&=E\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\Delta\omega}\log P_{\nu}\left(X|\Delta\omega\right)\right)^{2}\right].\end{split}

(52)

Therefore, by using eq. (

), the Fisher information assumes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}F^{G,NR}_{\nu}\left(\Delta\omega\right)&=\sum_{X=A,B}\frac{1}{P_{\nu}\left(X|\Delta\omega\right)}\left(\frac{\partial P_{\nu}\left(X|\Delta\omega\right)}{\partial\Delta\omega}\right)^{2}=\\

(53)

For

we can simplify it by substituting
\mathrm{e}^{2\tau^{2}\Delta\omega^{2}}\rightarrow 1+2\tau^{2}\Delta\omega^{2}
. In this case, the Fisher information becomes equal to the Quantum Fisher information:

F^{G,NR}_{\nu}=1\left(\Delta\omega\right)=2\tau^{2}=H\left(\Delta\omega\right).

(54)
