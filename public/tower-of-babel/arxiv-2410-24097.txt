A space-adiabatic approach for bulk-defect correspondences in lattice models of topological insulators
Danilo Polo Ojito, Emil Prodan, Tom Stoiber

Abstract
. In space-adiabatic approaches one can approximate Hamiltonians that are modulated slowly in space by phase-space functions that depend on position and momentum. In this paper, we establish a rigorous relation between this approach and the operator-theoretic approach for topological insulators with defects, which employs C ∗ C^{*} -algebras and operator K-theory. Using such tools, we show that by quantizing phase-space functions one can construct lattice Hamiltonians which are gapped at certain spatial limits and carry protected states at defects such as boundaries, hinges, and corners. Moreover, we show that the topological invariants that protect the latter can be computed in terms of the symbol functions. This enables us to compute boundary maps in K-theory that are relevant for bulk-defect correspondences.

It is therefore important to be able to determine the boundary maps for spaces
\overline{{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)}
which are non-trivial compactifications of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/\Gamma
. Despite the simple standard form of
{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)
it is generally difficult to compute such a boundary map
\partial_{\omega}
using topological methods alone. Indeed, there is no general theory to perform those computations and, at best, one can attempt to find a basis of the K-groups represented by Hamiltonians gapped at
\Omega_{\infty}
and compute their defect invariants. This is a difficult task to carry out analytically, especially in higher dimensions and for general geometric defects. After all, one has to rigorously prove that specific Hamiltonians have spectral gaps when restricted to
\Omega_{\infty}
and compute their defect invariants both of which is generally only feasible for models that can be diagonalized exactly or have a very particular algebraic form.

In this paper, we explore a space-adiabatic Ansatz to help with those computations. The idea is that if a Hamiltonian
h=\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}a_{q}u^{q}\in M_{N}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}))
is modulated slowly in space then the coefficient functions
a_{q}
approximately commute with the unitaries
u^{q}
and therefore the topological content should hopefully be roughly the same as that of a continuous function in
M_{N}(C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
, its
adiabatic symbol
. In particular it should sometimes be possible to compute the defect invariants using the boundary map
K_{i}(C(\Omega_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{1-i}((C\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\infty})\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
between topological spaces which is usually much more amenable to computation by algebraic-topologial methods such as spectral sequences. This is similar to the semiclassical limit for the corresponding situation in continuous space where Hamiltonians are differential operators whose spectral and topological properties become for
\hbar\to 0
more and more determined by their symbol, a phase-space function on the cotangent bundle. More closely related is the space-adiabatic approach
[
44
,
45
]
which works similarly, except that the semi-classical parameter
\hbar
is replaced by the inverse of a characteristic length-scale such that one considers instead the limit of infinitely slow modulation in real-space. In principle space-adiabatic methods can also be used for lattice operators, however, a problem for topological methods is that the lattice itself cannot be rescaled continuously, hence one cannot canonically define an adiabatic limit for general configurations spaces
\Omega
. And indeed, the commutative algebras
C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
C(\Omega)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
in general will behave very differently from each other in K-theory, even in the simple cases of Example
1.2
and Example
1.3
.

The solution that we propose for this problem is to make the adiabatic Ansatz using slightly different models for the configuration spaces
\Omega
than commonly used, but for which it is then possible to establish a good relation between the crossed product algebra and the adiabatic phase-space
\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
. Let us therefore assume that we have a configuration space
\Omega
whose
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-action is the restriction of an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action and which moreover comes equipped with a compatible action of the multiplicative group
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
implementing a scale transformation
(t,\omega)\mapsto t\cdot\omega
. This allows us to consider rescaled crossed products
C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
for the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-action
\alpha^{(t)}_{x}=\alpha_{tx}
, which are for
t>0
isomorphic to each other. Inspired by
[
23
,
24
]
, we then obtain a continuous field of
C^{*}
-algebras

\left(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\right)_{t\in[0,1]}

(8)

with trivial action at the end-point
t=0
, where the crossed product therefore becomes the commutative algebra of phase-space functions
C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
. A quantization of a phase-space function
f\in M_{N}(C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
shall be a continuous section of the field (
8
) whose evaluation at
t=0
reproduces
f
. The machinery of continuous fields ensures the existence of a large number of quantizations, even without having to specify a concrete quantization map. The existence of a scaling action on
\Omega
will be crucial to ensure that one can construct moreover quantizations with additional properties, such as spectral gaps, see Section
2
. For the typical continuous section
(h^{(t)})_{t\in[0,1]}
consisting of self-adjoint Hamiltonians one will find that their representations
\pi_{\omega}(h^{(t)})
on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
are modulated more and more slowly in real-space as
t\to 0
. Thus the continuous field allows us to rigorously define the space-adiabatic limit on an operator-algebraic level. Moreover, quantization does induce a natural homomorphism on the level of K-theory
Q:K_{i}(C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{i}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
which behaves naturally with respect to boundary maps.

To apply this formalism to the computation of boundary maps of a
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-space
\Omega
as in Example
1.2
or
1.3
one has to embed it into a larger space which has an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
- and an
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
-action. This can be done by embedding each codimension
n
defect
{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}/\Gamma
into a copy of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
which carries an affine action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. For a configuration space which decomposes like (
7
) the natural model for an adiabatic configuration space is therefore a CW-complex with actions of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
which act affinely on the cells. Moreover, the affine actions should leave certain lattice directions invariant, which imposes a rationality condition. A CW-complex like this will be called flat and rational in this work, see Section
3
.

We can now announce our main result:

Theorem 1.10
.

Let the compact configuration space
\Omega
be a CW-complex which is flat and rational at level
n
, in which case the
n
-skeleton decomposes as
\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}\simeq\sqcup_{j}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}
with some labeled copies of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. If
H:\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\mapsto M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
is a self-adjoint function whose restriction to
\Omega_{n-1}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
has a spectral gap, then there exists an associated adiabatic quantization,
(H^{(t)})_{t\in[0,1]}\in\left(M_{N}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}
, i.e. a continuous self-adjoint section, with the following properties:

(i)

The evaluation
H^{(1)}\in M_{N}(C(\Omega_{n-1})\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
has a spectral gap.

(ii)

If
H
is invariant under a finite linear group action then each
H^{(t)}
can also be chosen invariant.

(iii)

Let
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}
be an element of one of the
n
-cells. Then the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-orbit
{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)
in
\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}
is closed and discrete, with the orthogonal complement of the isotropy group
\Gamma_{\omega}
generated by unit vectors
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, hence it represents a codimension
n
defect.

(iv)

For
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}
, all pairings of
\Theta_{1-i}(\pi_{\omega}(H^{(1)}))
with the complete set of Chern cocycles of Proposition
1.9
can be computed from the adiabatic symbol as

\displaystyle\langle\pi_{\omega}(\Theta_{1-i}(H^{(1)})),{\rm Ch}^{\omega}_{v_{1},...,v_{m}}\rangle\;

(9)

\displaystyle=\;\tfrac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}({\mathbb{R}}_{j}^{n}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})}\langle\Theta_{1-i}(H))\rvert_{{\mathbb{R}}_{j}^{n}},{\rm Ch}_{v_{1},\dots,v_{m},\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}}\#{\rm Ch}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}}\rangle

where one takes the cup product with the canonical Chern cocycle on the
n
-cell
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}
and normalizes by dividing by the Lebesgue volume of a fundamental domain for the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-action. Here one makes the identification

K_{1-i}(C_{0}(\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1})\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\;\simeq\;\bigoplus_{j}K_{1-i}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\otimes C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}))

and restricts
\Theta_{1-i}(H)\in K_{1-i}(C_{0}(\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1})\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
to one of the cells.

Theorem
1.10
provides us with a direct method to construct lattice Hamiltonians that have known asymptotic limits, spectral gaps and Chern numbers, which is invaluable information for K-theoretic computations. The fundamental domain appears in (
9
) since the right-hand side of (
9
) turns out to be an average of the left-hand side over all
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{j}
in the same
n
-cell. Given a concrete problem with a (preferred)
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-space
\Omega
one can make an adiabatic Ansatz for the construction of Hamiltonians whenever
\Omega
(or at least some smaller closed orbit
\overline{{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)}
) embeds into a flat CW-complex, which need not be unique. After all, the defect Chern numbers of any lattice Hamiltonian resulting from an adiabatic construction, i.e. the left-hand side of (
9
), engage only a single
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-orbit.

The CW-structure is also convenient in that it allows us to efficiently construct interesting Hamiltonians in terms of gapped adiabatic symbols on the asymptotic part
\Omega_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
of the phase-space, since their defect invariants (
9
) are determined by a boundary map
K_{i}(C(\Omega_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{1-i}((\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\infty})\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
which is comparatively easy to compute by reducing to the K-theoretic boundary map between the boundary and the interior of a disk, see Section
6
.

In section
7
, we illustrate the method for different geometries. After demonstrating that the usual bulk-interface correspondence can be derived easily through the adiabatic method, we show that one can also obtain information about higher-order boundary maps describing bulk-hinge or bulk-corner correspondence.

Our approach bears strong similarities to the use of effective models in the form of matrix-valued phase-space functions for the study of topological insulators and their gapless defect states which goes back to

[

]
. That approach is appealing for physical applications because it avoids the intricacies of the operator-algebraic approach and, with some limitations, it can also be used for crystalline topological phases
[
56

57
]
. Our work supplies some rigorous foundations and justifications to those ideas since it shows that those effective models can often be systematically translated back into actual lattice models while behaving consistently on the level of K-theoretical topological invariants.

2.
Continuous fields for adiabatic quantization

As described in the introduction, our main result involves configuration spaces
\Omega
which have an action of
\underline{\rm Sim}(d):={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, a subgroup of the group
{\rm Sim}(d)
of similarity transformations on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. In the rest of this work all spaces
\Omega
will be assumed to have that extra structure, whereas the discrete variants such as the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-spaces from Example
1.2
, Example
1.3
only appear as the restrictions to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-orbits
\overline{{\mathscr{O}}(\omega)}
of points
\omega\in\Omega
anymore.

Definition 2.1
.

A configuration space
\Omega
shall be a compact Hausdorff space gifted with a continuous
\underline{\rm Sim}(d)
-action. The actions by translations
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are written as
(\omega,x)\mapsto x\triangleright\omega
and the scaling
\mathbb{R}_{+}
is written as
(t,\omega)\mapsto t\cdot\omega
. Obviously,

t\cdot(x\triangleright\omega)\;=\;(tx)\triangleright(t\cdot\omega),\quad(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

This results in a strongly continuous
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action
\alpha
and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
-action
\gamma
on
C(\Omega)
and
\gamma
of
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that

\gamma_{t}\circ\alpha_{x}\;=\;\alpha_{tx}\circ\gamma_{t}.

We recall that our interest is in the discrete crossed product
C(\Omega)\rtimes{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, where the action of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
on
C(\Omega)
comes from the restriction of
\alpha
. This algebra encodes lattice models that depend parametrically on
\omega\in\Omega
. Even though we work with lattice models, it is fruitful to engage a hybrid approach between the usual descriptions of continuous and lattice Hamiltonians, where we have an
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
action on the configuration space and a
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
action on the physical space.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a compact configuration space.
Consider the rescaled translation action

\alpha^{(t)}_{x}(f)\;=\;\alpha_{tx}(f)

on
C(\Omega)
with component-wise scaling by
t\in T:=[0,1]
. The adiabatic quantization of the symbol space
C(\Omega)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
is represented by the continuous field of
C^{*}
-algebras

{\mathcal{A}}\;:=\;({\mathcal{A}}_{t})_{t\in T}\;=\;\left(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\right)_{t\in T}.

By
{\mathcal{A}}
we also denote the total algebra of the field and
{\rm ev}_{t}\colon{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
stands for the evaluation at
t\in T
. The fiber at
t=0
is isomorphic to
C(\Omega)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
.

Recall that a continuous field of
C^{*}
-algebras
({\mathcal{A}}_{t})_{t\in T}
is itself a
C^{*}
-algebra obtained by completing a set of distinguished sections of
\sqcup_{t\in T}{\mathcal{A}}_{t}
with certain properties
[
21
, Chapter 10]
in the natural
C^{*}
-norm. In the present case, any family of crossed products with continuously varied action by an amenable group has a canonical structure as a continuous field for which the total algebra of the field satisfies (e.g.
[
23
, Theorem 2.5]
)

\left(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\right)_{t\in T}\;=\;\left(C(T)\otimes C(\Omega)\right)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(T)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

(10)

with the action
\alpha^{(T)}_{x}(f)(t)=\alpha^{(t)}_{x}(f(t))
.
Hence a dense subset of continuous sections is precisely given by the formal Fourier series

t\in T\;\mapsto\;\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f_{q}(t)u_{t}^{q},\qquad f_{q}\in C(T,C(\Omega))

whose finitely many non-vanishing coefficient functions depend continuously on a parameter and with unitary generators satisfying
u_{t}^{q}fu_{t}^{-q}=\alpha^{(t)}_{q}(f)
. In the present case, the field is trivial away from
0
in the sense that all fibers for
t>0
are isomorphic to each other due to the scaling action of
\gamma
, moreover:

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
0<t_{0}<1
the field

\left(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\right)_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}

is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{A}}
as a continuous field.

Proof.

For any
s,t\neq 0
the isomorphism
\rho_{s,t}:C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(s)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
is given by

\rho_{s,t}\Big(\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}a_{q}u_{s}^{q}\Big)\;=\;\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\gamma_{t/s}(a_{q})u_{t}^{q}.

Choosing any continuous increasing function
g:[0,t_{0}]\to[0,1]
which is equal to the identity on
[0,\frac{1}{2}t_{0}]
the isomorphisms
\rho_{s,g(s)}
together define an isomorphism of the continuous fields.
∎

Definition 2.4
.

Consider a continuous field as in Definition
2.2
. We say that a continuous section
\hat{\xi}=(\xi_{t})_{t\in T}
of the field with
{\mbox{\rm ev}}_{0}(\hat{\xi})=\xi_{0}=\xi
is an adiabatic quantization of
\xi\in{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
. For the particular case when
\xi
is a projection/unitary/self-adjoint an adiabatic quantization shall be a continuous section which consists of projections/unitaries/self-adjoints.

For any continuous field there is by definition at least a norm-dense subalgebra of elements of
{\mathcal{A}}_{0}
which have an adiabatic quantization and for self-adjoint elements the section can be made self-adjoint. To construct sections with additional properties we recall that sections satisfy spectral continuity, i.e. given any self-adjoint continuous section
(\xi_{t})_{t\in T}
the spectra
\sigma(\xi_{t})\subset{\mathbb{R}}
will depend on
t
continuously in the sense that if
\sigma(\xi_{t})
is contained in an open set for some
t
, then this will also be true for an open neighborhood of
t

[
21
, Proposition 10.3.6]
. In particular, if
\xi_{t}
is invertible then this will also hold for a small neighborhood of
t
. It is well-known that a selfadjoint element of a
C^{*}
-algebra with a spectral gap can be deformed to a projection via functional calculus and also that continuous functional calculus maps continuous sections to continuous section
[
21
, Proposition 10.3.3]
. Likewise, any invertible which commutes with its adjoint up to a small enough error
\epsilon
is close to an actual unitary by polar decomposition. Therefore one can replace any adiabatic quantization of a projection/unitary
\xi_{0}
with another adiabatic quantization such that
\xi_{t}
is a projection/unitary at least for small enough
t
. This is enough since triviality away from
0
implies that one can stretch any such section to cover all of
[0,1]
, see Lemma
2.3
. Hence any projection/unitary has an adiabatic quantization consisting of projections/unitaries. With this argument, one can show that, for any continuous field over
[0,1]
that is trivial away from
0
, there exists a canonical homomorphism
[
23
]

Q_{i}\colon[\xi_{0}]_{i}\in K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\;\mapsto\;[\xi_{1}]_{i}\in K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{1})

(11)

which does not depend on the continuous section used. In general, it need not be injective or surjective.

As motivated in the introduction, we want to quantize functions on the phase space
\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}
to obtain lattice Hamiltonians that have spectral gaps at specified asymptotic limits and computable Chern numbers.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a configuration space. An adiabatic symbol is a self-adjoint function
H
in
C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})\otimes M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
. For a compact invariant subset
\Omega_{\infty}
of
\Omega
let
R^{(0)}_{\Omega_{\infty}}\colon C(\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})\to C(\Omega_{\infty}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d})
be the restriction map. We say that
H
is gapped on
\Omega_{\infty}\subset\Omega
if
H|_{\Omega_{\infty}}:=R^{(0)}_{\Omega_{\infty}}(H)
has a spectral gap around
0
, i.e. there is a compact interval
\Delta
containing
0
so that
\sigma\big(H|_{\Omega_{\infty}}\big)\cap\Delta=\emptyset
.

Here and in the remainder of this work, we say that a subset of
\Omega
is invariant if it is mapped to itself under both the
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
- and
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
-actions.

Proposition 2.6
.

Given an adiabatic symbol
H
and any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a continuous section
(H^{(t)})_{t\in T}
of the field
(M_{N}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}))_{t\in T}
consisting of self-adjoint operators such that
\left\lVert H^{(0)}-H\right\rVert<\epsilon
. Let
\Omega_{\infty}\subset\Omega
be closed and invariant. If
H
is gapped on
\Omega_{\infty}
, then the section can be chosen such that:

(i)

H^{(t)}
is gapped in
M_{N}(C(\Omega_{\infty})\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
for all
t
;

(ii)

Let
\Omega
carry a linear
G
-action for some finite subgroup
G
of
O(d)
that leaves
\Omega_{\infty}
invariant and induces an action
\eta:C(\Omega)\times G\to C(\Omega)
, then the quantization can additionally be chosen to also be
G
-invariant. Here, linear means that

Proof.

Remark 2.7

Proposition

For a general configuration space
\Omega
we just saw that it is not difficult to prove the existence of adiabatic quantizations with nice properties. Let us make the construction of such a quantization more explicit. For simplicity, let
H:\Omega\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\to M_{N}({\mathbb{C}})
have uniformly finite hopping range in the sense that there is a Fourier expansion

H(\omega,k)\;=\;\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f(\omega)e^{\imath k\cdot q}

with only finitely many non-vanishing coefficient functions
f_{q}\in M_{N}(C(\Omega))
. An adiabatic quantization is generated by the family of finite Fourier series

H^{(t)}\;=\;\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f^{(t)}_{q}u_{t}^{q}\in M_{N}(C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})

with coefficients given by finitely many non-vanishing continuous functions
t\in[0,1]\mapsto f^{(t)}_{q}\in C(\Omega)
,
f^{(0)}_{q}=f_{q}
, and
(u^{q}_{t})_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
the unitary generators of the crossed product
C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha^{(t)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, hence satisfying the commutation relation
u_{t}^{y}f^{(t)}_{q}u_{t}^{-y}=\alpha_{y}^{(t)}(f^{(t)}_{q})
. After symmetrizing one can read off functions
f_{q}^{(t)}
which define a self-adjoint continuous section from

H^{(t)}\;=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}f_{q}u_{t}^{q}+u_{t}^{q}f^{*}_{-q}\Big)=\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left(f_{q}+\alpha^{(t)}_{q}(f^{*}_{-q})\right)u_{t}^{q}\;.

In any representation on
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
the associated matrix elements take the form

\displaystyle\langle\delta_{x},\pi^{(t)}_{\omega}(H^{(t)})\delta_{y}\rangle\;

\displaystyle=\;f^{(t)}_{x-y}\big(tx\triangleright\omega\big)

\displaystyle=\;\tfrac{1}{2}\left(f_{x-y}\big(tx\triangleright\omega)+f^{*}_{y-x}\big(ty\triangleright\omega)\right),\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

Thus, they depend on the real-space location, but any characteristic length scale becomes proportional to
t^{-1}
. Hence it is justified to identify the multiplication operator
H^{(0)}\in M_{N}(C(\Omega)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
as the adiabatic limit of
H^{(1)}\in C(\Omega)\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. To make sure that
H^{(1)}
varies slowly enough in space for the additional properties discussed in Proposition
2.6
, one may have to rescale the coefficient functions as in Lemma
2.3
such that
t=1
represents a large enough scale.

3.
Flat configuration spaces

Recall that a CW-complex structure on a topological space
\Omega
is given by an increasing filtration
\Omega=\bigcup_{-1\leq d}\Omega_{n}
by closed sets,
\Omega_{-1}=\emptyset
, such that every
\Omega_{n}
is obtained as an iterated pushout of cells

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod_{i}{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{n-1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\coprod_{i}{\mathbb{D}}^{n}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Omega_{n}}

In this paper, it will be most convenient to identify the interior of the
n
-dimensional closed ball with
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We also give
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
a canonical vector space structure. An action on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
can then be called affine if it acts via affine transformations.

Definition 3.1
.

Let a configuration space
\Omega
be a CW-complex with skeleton filtration
(\Omega_{n})_{0\leq n\leq d}
, hence there is a homeomorphism

\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho_{n}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\;\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{N_{n}}{\mathbb{R}}_{i}^{n}

with labeled copies of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. We say that
\Omega
is flat at level
n
if
\rho_{n}
can be chosen equivariant w.r.t. the translation and scaling action for affine actions on the copies
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}
, i.e. for every
i
there is a
n\times d
matrix
\Lambda
such that

\displaystyle x\triangleright\rho_{n}^{-1}(y)\;=\;\rho_{n}^{-1}(y+\Lambda x),\quad t\cdot\rho_{n}^{-1}(y)\;=\;\rho_{n}^{-1}(ty),\quad(t,x)\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

for
y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}_{i}
. We also impose that
\Lambda
has maximal rank. We will say, moreover, that the action is rational if the kernel of
\Lambda
is spanned by
d-n
linearly independent vectors in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
or, equivalently,
\Lambda
is proportional to a matrix in
M_{d\times n}(\mathbb{Q})
.

If
\Omega
is flat at every level, we will simply call it a flat configuration space. The rationality condition is important in view of Definition
1.7
, since it implies that the
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
-orbit of any
\omega\in\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}
is discrete with finite co-volume in
\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1}
and, therefore, it represents a codimension
n
defect for the pair
\Omega=\Omega_{n}
and
\Omega_{\infty}=\Omega_{n-1}
. Note that the rationality condition is un-affected by scaling.

As a simple example the configuration space from Example
1.3
includes into the disk
{\mathbb{D}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}\sqcup{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
with the affine action of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
on itself. This obviously has a CW-structure, which is flat at the top level. That will also be true for the generalizations in section
7.2
.

In the rest of the section, we show that the structure introduced in Definition
3.1
appears quite naturally when considering geometric boundaries defined by polyhedral cones.

Let us first consider the compact metric space
\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
of closed subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
endowed with Fell topology
[
26
]
, i.e. the topology induced by the Hausdorff metric on
\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{S}}^{d})
upon including
\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})\to\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{S}}^{d})
via stereographic projection
[
27
,
39
]
. If
\mathcal{L}\in\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
we consider its orbit space
\mathscr{O}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\mathcal{L}):=\{\mathcal{L}-x\;|\;x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
. If
{\mathcal{L}}
is scale-invariant then a configuration space is defined by the compact space

\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}\;:=\;\overline{\mathscr{O}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\mathcal{L})}

(12)

where the closure is taken with respect to the Fell topology and the translation and scaling action are inherited from
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.

For each
v\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d}
, we define the half-plane
\mathcal{L}_{v}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\;|\;v\cdot x\geq 0\}
. A convex cone can be defined as a non-empty intersection of half-spaces
\mathcal{L}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{L}_{\lambda_{i}}
. If
m=d
and the normal vectors
\lambda_{i}
are linearly independent, then one speaks of a simplicial cone.

Proposition 3.2
.

For
{\mathcal{L}}
a simplicial cone
\Omega_{\mathcal{L}}
is homeomorphic to
\tilde{\Omega}:=[0,1)^{d}\cup\{*\}
, the one-point compactification of a half-open parallelepiped.

Proof.

W.l.o.g. we may assume that
{\mathcal{L}}=({\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0})^{d}
since any simplicial cone takes that form after a change of coordinates. Choose an increasing homeomorphism
t\colon[0,1)\to\{-\infty\}\cup{\mathbb{R}}
and define

\imath\colon x\in[0,1)^{d}\;\mapsto\;[t(x_{1}),\infty)\times...\times[t(x_{d}),\infty)\in\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})

and
\imath(*)=\emptyset
the empty set. The map
\imath\colon\tilde{\Omega}\to\mathscr{C}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is clearly injective and the interior
(0,1)^{d}
is mapped bijectively to
{\mathscr{O}}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}({\mathcal{L}})
. Moreover,
\imath
is continuous since its image consists of cartesian products of intervals and it is easy to verify sequential continuity using the well-known convergence criteria for sequences in the Fell topology (e.g.
[
7

). From the compactness of
\tilde{\Omega}
, one concludes that
\imath
is a homeomorphism onto its image. This implies that

is a homeomorphism onto

because the image of

has

∎

One can give

the structure of a CW-complex such that each

-cell, which also gives

the structure of a CW-complex. By reducing to a subcomplex this also applies more generally to non-pointed cones which are intersections of

half-spaces:

Let

(13)

Moreover,

is a CW-complex in such a way that each

W.l.o.g. one can assume

4.
Connes-Thom isomorphism at work

The Connes-Thom isomorphism
[
15
]
is a natural isomorphism between the K-theory of a crossed product algebra
{\mathscr{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with that of
{\mathscr{C}}
. It comes into play due to the following key observation:

Proposition 4.1
.

If
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is equipped with the affine
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
action

{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\ni(x,y)\mapsto x\triangleright y=y+\Lambda^{T}x=y+\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\dots&\lambda_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{T}x,

with column vectors
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, then one can rewrite

C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\;\simeq\;C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

with the
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
-action
\hat{\alpha}
induced by the action
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{T}}^{d}(y,k)\mapsto k-\Lambda y
on
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

Proof.

The crossed product
C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the non-unital
C^{\ast}
-algebra defined by norm-closure of the linear span of operators of the form
\rho(f)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g(x)U_{x}\textup{d}{x}
on
L^{2}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\otimes L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})

[
50
]
where
f\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
,
g\in C_{c}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and the so-called regular covariant representation is

\displaystyle(\rho(f)\psi)(k,y)

\displaystyle\;=\;f(k+\Lambda y)\psi(k,y)

\displaystyle(U_{x}\psi)(k,y)

\displaystyle\;=\;\psi(k,y-x)\qquad\forall\ k\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d},\ x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

One can write
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}g(x)U_{x}\textup{d}{x}=\hat{g}(p)
, where
\hat{g}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is the Fourier transform of
g
and
p:=(p_{1},...,p_{n})
are the self-adjoint generators of the unitary translation group
(U_{x})_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}
. Since the unitary operators
u^{q}=\rho(k\in{\mathbb{T}}^{d}\mapsto e^{\imath k\cdot q})
with
q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, are dense in
\rho(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
, one gets that a norm-dense subset of
\rho\rtimes U\big(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\big)
is given by finite series

\sum_{q\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}u^{q}\hat{g}_{q}(p),\quad\hat{g}_{q}\in C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

Note the commutation relation
u^{q}U_{x}u^{-q}=e^{\imath x(\Lambda^{T}q)}U_{x}
implies that
u^{q}
acts on functions
\hat{g}(p)
by translation of the argument by
\Lambda^{T}q
. Due to the universal property of crossed products
[
50
]
, this commutation relation shows that there is a homomorphism
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. It clearly has dense range and, since the subalgebra
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is embedded isometrically, it is injective (after all, the conditional expectation
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\to C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
is faithful).
∎

In the following we will write the dual crossed product from the Proposition as
C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
to specify the underlying affine action. One obtains a continuous field whose fibers can be written as

{\mathcal{A}}_{t}\;:=\;C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda^{T}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:=\;C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}.

Proposition 4.2
.

For each
t
, there is a Connes-Thom isomorphism

\partial_{t\Lambda}:K_{i-n}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{i}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

(14)

A continuous section
(\xi_{t})_{t\in T}
,
\xi_{t}\in M_{N}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
of projections/unitaries defines classes
[\xi_{t}]_{i}\in K_{i}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
(\partial_{t\Lambda})^{-1}([\xi_{t}]_{i})\in K_{i-n}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
does not depend on
t
.

Proof.

The Connes-Thom isomorphism is a natural map
K_{i}({\mathscr{C}})\simeq K_{i+1}({\mathscr{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}})

[
15
]
and by composing
n
Connes-Thom isomorphisms one has
K_{i}({\mathscr{C}})\simeq K_{i+n}({\mathscr{C}}\rtimes{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The total algebra of the field
{\mathcal{A}}\simeq(C(T)\otimes C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n}))\rtimes_{\alpha^{(T)}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
can also be rewritten as a crossed product
{\mathcal{A}}\simeq(C(T)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}^{(T)}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. There is therefore also a Connes-Thom isomorphism of the entire field

\partial^{(T)}_{CT}\colon K_{i-n}(C(T)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{i}((C(T)\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\rtimes_{\hat{\alpha}^{(T)}}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}).

(15)

Due to the naturalness property of the Connes-Thom map under equivariant homomorphism
[
15
, Axiom 2]
, restriction to a fiber
t
supplies a commuting square between (
15
) and (
14
), with surjective vertical arrows. One can therefore represent the pre-images
(\partial_{t\Lambda})^{-1}([\xi_{t}]_{i})\in K_{i-n}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
by a norm-continuous family
(\zeta_{t})_{t\in T}
in
M_{N}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
representing the pre-image
[\zeta]_{i-n}=(\partial_{CT}^{(T)})^{-1}([\xi]_{i})
. Hence they all define the same
K
-theory class.
∎

For the special case of discrete crossed products over
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
, it is also known
[
23
]
that the composition
\partial_{\Lambda}\circ(\partial_{0\cdot\Lambda})^{-1}
is precisely the natural homomorphism
K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\to K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{t})
mentioned in Section
2
, which in this case is therefore an isomorphism. For an affine configuration space, one will always be able to reduce the calculations of defect Chern numbers from Proposition
1.9
to one on the torus, which paves the way to relate the invariants with Chern numbers of an adiabatic symbol.

5.
Chern numbers of adiabatic quantizations

All elements of
K_{i}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
can be distinguished by numerical pairings with a set of cyclic cocycles, called Chern cocycles. Throughout we identify the torus with
{\mathbb{T}}^{d}={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, therefore there is for any direction
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
a
*
-derivation
\nabla_{v}:C^{1}({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\to C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
given by

\nabla_{v}f\;:=\;\sum_{i=1}^{d}v_{i}\nabla_{i}f.

For any tuple
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})
of vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
(m+1)
-tuple
(f_{0},\ldots,f_{m})
of functions
f_{j}\in C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
one defines the Chern cocycle

{\rm Ch}_{v}(f_{0},\dots,f_{m})\;=\;\,\sum_{\rho\in S_{m}}(-1)^{\rho}\,{\mathcal{T}}\big(f_{0}\nabla_{v_{\rho(1)}}f_{1}\ldots\nabla_{v_{\rho(m)}}f_{m}\big)\;,

(16)

with
{\mathcal{T}}
the usual trace on
C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})
normalized such that
{\mathcal{T}}({\bf 1})=1
. As a consequence, all non-trivial pairings will exactly have
{\mathbb{Z}}
as their range whenever
v
is made up of standard basis vectors of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(see Appendix
A
).

Proposition 5.1
.

Consider all tuples of unit vectors
v_{I}=(e_{i_{1}},\dots,e_{i_{n}})
,
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{m}
with subsets
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}\}
of elements in
\{1,\dots,d\}
. We say a class
\xi_{I}\in K_{i}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
is dual to
{\rm Ch}_{v_{I}}
if

\langle[\xi_{I}]_{i},{\rm Ch}_{v_{J}}\rangle\;=\;\delta_{I,J}

(17)

for all subsets
I,J\subset\{1,\dots,d\}
whose length has the same parity as
i
.

A dual element
\xi_{I}
exists for each subset
I\subset\{1,..,d\}
and the even respectively odd subsets provide a basis for
K_{0}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{2^{d-1}}
and
K_{0}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}^{2^{d-1}}
respectively.

We recall the proof in Appendix
A
.

Let us now consider the basic building block
C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rtimes_{t\Lambda^{T}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\simeq C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})\rtimes_{t\Lambda}{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with affine action as given in Proposition
4.1
. A basis of the K-groups
K_{i}(C_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})\rtimes_{t\Lambda^{T}}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\simeq K_{i+n}(C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))
is given by applying the Connes-Thom isomorphism to the generators
\xi_{I}

Theorem 5.2

At

Proof.

In the case

The sign factor can be simplified as given.
∎

For general

We can now state our main result on the Chern numbers of adiabatic quantizations:

.

Let

Proof.

By Theorem

If we want to compute the pairing of one of the Chern cocycles

therefore almost finishes the proof of Theorem

Having finished the proof of Theorem
1.10
we now have all the necessary tools to compute the Chern numbers for lattice realizations of adiabatic symbols. For the remainder of this section, we will comment on possible generalizations. The key part of the argument that allows us to describe the K-theoretic quantization homomorphism
K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{0})\to K_{i}({\mathcal{A}}_{1})
explicitly is that it is the composition of a Connes-Thom isomorphism and its inverse. This is crucial because there are well-known duality results for the Chern cocycles involved. It appears feasible to generalize this approach to other instances of K-theory where an analogue of the Connes-Thom isomorphism is available, for instance also for real K-theory, which includes various forms such as KO-, KR-, or van Daele K-theory. All of these frameworks can be employed to describe topological insulators with internal symmetries of the Altland-Zirnbauer type
[
12
,
36
,
1
]
. One can then, however, no longer distinguish all generators using the pairing with cyclic cohomology, especially if torsion components are involved. Instead one must use the more general duality pairing with the respective K-homology given by the Kasparov product, which has similar properties. In particular, the homology classes can be built from exterior products of one-dimensional Dirac operators in a similar way that our Chern cocycles are cup products of
1
-cocycles. The boundary map in terms of the Kasparov product leads to similar dualities as we used for the cocycles (see e.g.
[
12
,
13
]
). This approach could have been used for the complex K-groups that we study in this paper, but it is technically much more complicated than the approach via cyclic cohomology and would involve additional steps to turn the pairings with K-homology into concrete, computable expressions. To some extent, there are also Connes-Thom isomorphisms for equivariant K-theory (e.g.
[
35
]
), but they take a more complicated form (unless the additional group action commutes with the
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
-action, but for crystalline symmetries there are many interesting cases where this is not true). How useful those are to explore K-theoretic invariants for equivariant adiabatic quantizations remains to be seen.

6.
Boundary maps in the adiabatic picture

In this section, we assume that
\Omega
is a CW-complex with skeleton filtration
\Omega_{0}\subset\dots\subset\Omega_{d}=\Omega
given by closed invariant subsets. For any sort of bulk-boundary or bulk-defect correspondence, one will consider Hamiltonians that are gapped at some asymptotic limit
\Omega_{\infty}\subset\Omega
. Generally, this
\Omega_{\infty}
will take the form
\Omega_{\infty}=\Omega_{n-1}
for some

, but possibly bind topologically protected states at defects of higher codimension. We then have for each

\partial_{n}\colon K_{i}(C(\Omega_{n-1})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\to K_{1-i}(C_{0}(\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1})\rtimes_{\alpha}{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}).

This map can, for general
\Omega_{n}
and
\Omega_{n-1}
, often be difficult to describe explicitly due to a lack of explicitly known numerical invariants like the Chern cocycles that one can use to distinguish different K-theory classes.

We will use the adiabatic picture to obtain at least some and sometimes complete information about this map. Let us therefore now consider the case of a trivial
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
-action, which is relevant for the endpoint

of our continuous fields. The relevant boundary maps now take the form

\partial_{n}\colon K_{i}(C(\Omega_{n-1})\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d}))\to K_{i}(C_{0}(\Omega_{n}\setminus\Omega_{n-1})\otimes C({\mathbb{T}}^{d})).

By construction of a CW-complex one can easily compute those once one understands the boundary maps for a single sphere, i.e. those of the exact sequences

in

, but which are not topologically protected anymore when localized to a single realization. Those can still be interesting as approximate boundary modes which become more and more robust the closer the system is to the adiabatic limit.

7.
Applications

This section is devoted to the construction of the explicit examples of Theorem
1.10
to demonstrate that by correctly choosing configuration spaces one ends up with lattice Hamiltonians which model non-trivial geometries and have interesting boundary or defect modes.

7.1.
Bulk-interface correspondence

As our first application, we consider bulk-interface correspondence for two bulk materials with a common spectral gap separated by a dividing hyper-surface. If the two materials have different Chern numbers then there may be in-gap interface states localized to the hyper-surface which carry Chern numbers depending on the differences of the bulk Chern numbers and the angles of the surface. This setting has been studied before both with and without K-theory

The configuration space for an interface is in the simplest case given by

Let us note that in

Let

-algebra, i.e. a

It is often more convenient to describe K-theoretic invariants in terms of numerical invariants and one of the prime methods for that is the pairing with cyclic cohomology
[

]

\varphi(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m})\;=\;(-1)^{m}\varphi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m},a_{0})

\displaystyle(b\varphi)(a_{0},\ldots,a_{m+1})\;=\;

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\varphi(a_{0},\ldots,a_{j}a_{j+1},\ldots,a_{m+1})

\displaystyle\;+\;(-1)^{m+1}\varphi(a_{m+1}a_{0},a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}).

The cohomology classes of cyclic

(38)
