Theory-to-Practice Gap for Neural Networks and Neural Operators
Philipp Grohs \ORCID 0000-0001-9205-0969, Samuel Lanthaler \ORCID 0000-0003-1911-246X, Margaret Trautner* \ORCID 0000-0001-9937-8393

Abstract
This work studies the sample complexity of learning with ReLU neural networks and neural operators. For mappings belonging to relevant approximation spaces, we derive upper bounds on the best-possible convergence rate of any learning algorithm, with respect to the number of samples. In the finite-dimensional case, these bounds imply a gap between the parametric and sample complexities of learning, known as the theory-to-practice gap . In this work, a unified treatment of the theory-to-practice gap is achieved in a general L p L^{p} -setting, while at the same time improving available bounds in the literature. Furthermore, based on these results the theory-to-practice gap is extended to the infinite-dimensional setting of operator learning. Our results apply to Deep Operator Networks and integral kernel-based neural operators, including the Fourier neural operator. We show that the best-possible convergence rate in a Bochner L p L^{p} -norm is bounded by rates of order 1 / p 1/p .

Introduction

Deep learning has had remarkable success in a wide range of tasks such as speech recognition, computer vision, or natural language processing
[
17
]
. Increasingly this methodology is also used in the scientific domain, with applications to protein folding
[
22
]
, plasma physics
[
13
]
and numerical weather prediction
[
27
,
43
,
5
]
. However, the theoretical underpinnings of this field remain incomplete, and significant advances are still required to understand the empirical success of neural networks in these diverse applications. In many of these tasks, the goal is to approximate an unknown mapping based on a dataset consisting of input- and output-pairs, so-called
supervised learning
. This has motivated a surge of theoretical work aimed at deepening our understanding of supervised learning with neural networks.

Theoretical error estimates for supervised learning are usually obtained by splitting the overall error into two contributions
[
10
]
: the approximation error and the generalization error. The approximation error measures the best-possible error that can be achieved by a given architecture. The generalization error bounds the difference between this best-possible error and the error achieved by empirical risk minimization, i.e. optimization based on a finite number of empirical data samples. This decomposition captures a trade-off between
model expressivity
(or parametric complexity), and generalization from a finite amount of data, i.e. the
sample complexity
.

The study of the expressivity of neural networks dates back several decades to foundational work such as that of Cybenko
[
11
]
and Hornik, Stinchcombe, and White
[
20
]
, which focused on qualitative universality theorems. Motivated by the need to better explain the empirical efficiency of deep neural networks in applications, there has recently been increased interest in deriving quantitative approximation guarantees. These relate achievable approximation errors to key factors, such as the depth, width or choice of activation function
[
6
,
47
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
. What is striking about these results is that the derived approximation rates are generally far superior to the rates that are achievable by classical numerical representations. This fact makes deep learning methods potentially appealing for applications in numerical analysis where a high convergence rate is often desired.

The Theory-to-Practice Gap.

Despite these encouraging results, any actual numerical approximation algorithm must operate with limited information on the function to be approximated – typically in the form of a finite number of samples of the function itself, or samples of a local (differential) operator applied to the function. It is therefore a key question whether the theoretically established approximation rates can be retained under such limited information.

For this reason, the sample complexity of deep learning has also been of recent focus. Of particular relevance to the present work are the articles
[
18
,
4
,
1
]
. An informal summary of their results is as follows. First, define by
U^{\alpha}([0,1]^{d})
the set of functions on
[0,1]^{d}
which, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, can be approximated by a neural network
\psi_{n}
with at most
n
non-zero weights, and with approximation error
\|f-\psi_{n}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq n^{-\alpha}
. We refer to the approximation rate
\alpha
as the
parametric convergence rate
, since this rate holds with respect to the number of neural network parameters
n
.

Second, to address the sample complexity of computing such an approximation,
[
18
]
considers (optimal) reconstruction methods aiming to reconstruct
f\in U^{\alpha}([0,1]^{d})
from point samples
f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{N})
, and examines limits on the best possible guaranteed convergence rate –
not in terms of the number of parameters but in the number of samples
N
.

More formally,
[
18
]
asks for the optimal convergence rate
\beta_{\ast}>0
for which there exists a reconstruction method
A:f\mapsto Q(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{N}))
defined in terms of a reconstruction mapping
Q:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
, with a convergence guarantee of the form
\sup_{f\in U^{\alpha}}\|f-A(f)\|_{L^{p}}\leq CN^{-\beta_{\ast}}
. Compared to the parametric convergence rate
\alpha
, which describes the optimal convergence rate in terms of the number of parameters, the rate
\beta_{\ast}
is now in terms of the available information – the
N
function samples. This is of practical relevance, since a single function evaluation requires a certain minimal computational time, any upper bound on
\beta_{\ast}
yields a corresponding lower bound on the time to compute an accurate approximation for
f\in U^{\alpha}
.

A naive counting argument based on the number of degrees of freedom would suggest that
\beta_{\ast}=\alpha
coincides with the optimal parametric convergence rate
\alpha
. Indeed, it might be hoped that the determination of
n
parameters of the approximating neural network
\psi_{n}
requires
N=n
point evaluations, implying that for
A(f):=\psi_{n}
, we have
\|f-A(f)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq n^{-\alpha}=N^{-\alpha}
. As is well-known, this intuition is indeed correct for reconstruction by several popular methods, including polynomials, trigonometric polynomials and certain kernel methods
[
42
]
. However, the results of
[
18
]
show that this intuition does not carry over to the sample complexity of neural network approximation spaces: In this case, there is a gap between
\beta_{\ast}
and
\alpha
. In fact, even in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
, the optimal sample convergence rate
\beta_{\ast}
remains uniformly bounded and, for
p=\infty
it even holds that
\beta_{\ast}\lesssim\frac{1}{d}
which implies the existence of a curse of dimension. The gap between
\alpha
and
\beta_{\ast}
is termed the
theory-to-practice gap
as it describes the discrepancy between the complexity of a theoretically possible approximation and one that can actually be computed from the information at hand.

A first contribution of this work is Theorem
2.3
which further extends and sharpens the bounds from
[
18
]
in the setting of finite-dimensional function approximation. Roughly speaking, we show that in a general
L^{p}
-setting,
\beta_{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}
which means that for high input dimensions (i.e., large
d
), no actual algorithm is capable of beating the standard Monte-Carlo rate
\frac{1}{p}
– irrespective of the parametric convergence rate
\alpha
. Compared to results in
[
18
]
which, roughly speaking, established bounds of the form
\beta_{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{p}+1
, this constitutes a significant improvement if the input dimension
d
is large.

Operator Learning.

In addition to the aforementioned works on neural networks in finite-dimensions, there has also been increasing interest in operator learning
[
24
]
. The aim of operator learning is to approximate non-linear operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, mapping between infinite-dimensional function spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
. In applications, such operators often arise as solution operators associated with a partial differential equation, but more general classes of operators can be considered. To approximate such
\mathcal{G}
, operator learning frameworks generalize neural networks to this infinite-dimensional setting. Empirically, the most successful approach is usually based on supervised learning from training data, and hence the same questions as discussed above also arise in this context.

Early work on operator learning dates back to a foundational paper by Chen and Chen
[
9
]
. Without any claim of completeness, we mention a number of works studying the parametric complexity of operator learning which aim to relate the model size to the achieved approximation accuracy, including both upper bounds on the required number of parameters, e.g.
[
19
,
25
,
16
,
37
]
, as well as lower bounds, e.g.
[
39
,
31
,
30
,
32
]
.
The data (or sample) complexity of operator learning has e.g. been studied in
[
2
,
35
,
3
]
.
While the present work is restricted to the noiseless setting, some related work has investigated the sample complexity of operator learning in the setting of noisy data
[
8
,
12
,
34
,
44
,
45
]
.
In connection with the present work, we also highlight
[
23
]
, where approximation spaces for the Fourier neural operator are introduced, and upper bounds on the sample complexity of learning on these spaces are discussed. Closely related spaces will be discussed in this work. These spaces, which are an infinite-dimensional generalization of the approximation spaces
U^{\alpha}
introduced in
[
18
]
(and described above), represent classes of Deep Operator Networks and kernel-integral based neural operators with a parametric approximation rate
\alpha
. They will be introduced and studied in Sections
3.4
and
3.5
.

In the context we are able to generalize the theory-to-practice gap to the infinite-dimensional setting: Assuming access to an optimal algorithm for the reconstruction of the underlying mapping from
N
samples, we show that the best achievable convergence rate
N^{-\beta_{\ast}}
on the relevant approximation spaces is upper bounded by
1/p
, if the reconstruction error is measured in a Bochner
L^{p}
-norm. These results are provided in Theorems
3.12
and
3.14
(Deep Operator Networks) and Theorems
3.20
and
3.22
(kernel-integral based neural operators) and they uncover fundamental limits on the convergence guarantees that are possible in the context of operator learning: one cannot improve on the standard Monte-Carlo rate, irrespective of how high the parametric convergence rate may be.

In summary, this work makes the following contributions:

•

We give a unified treatment of the theory-to-practice gap in a general
L^{p}
-setting, valid for arbitrary
p\in[1,\infty]
, sharpening available bounds in the literature.
Specifically, the bounds we achieve in the finite-dimensional setting is sharper than that of
[
18
]
.

•

Going beyond the setting of
[
18
]
,
we extend the theory-to-practice gap to the infinite-dimensional setting of operator learning. Our results apply to Deep Operator Networks and integral kernel-based neural operators, including the Fourier neural operator.

•

For these architectures, we show that the optimal convergence rate in a Bochner
L^{p}
-norm is bounded by
\beta_{\ast}\leq 1/p
, for any
p\in[1,\infty)
.

•

We furthermore show that
\beta_{\ast}=0
for
uniform
approximation over a compact set of input functions, i.e. no algebraic convergence rates are possible.

Ultimately, the results of this work suggest that the practical limitation of learning operators arises from an information-theoretic barrier rather than a model expressivity barrier. In other words, one is limited by the ability to extract information from finite data samples.

2
A generalized gap in finite dimension

The goal of this section is to derive new lower bounds on the sample complexity of ReLU neural network approximation spaces, in a finite-dimensional setting. To describe the mathematical setting, we recall relevant notions from
[
18
, Sect. 2.1 and 2.2]
, below.

2.1
ReLU neural networks

Following
[
18
]
, a
neural network
is defined as a tuple
\psi=\left((A_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(A_{L},b_{L})\right)
, consisting of matrices
A_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}\times d_{j-1}}
and bias vectors
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
. The number of layers
L(\psi):=L
is the
depth
of
\psi
, and
W(\psi):=\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\|A_{j}\|_{\ell^{0}}+\|b_{j}\|_{\ell^{0}}\right)
is used to denote the
number of (nonzero) weights
of
\psi
. Here, the notation
\|A\|_{\ell^{0}}
refers to the number of nonzero entries of a matrix (or vector)
A
. Finally, we write
d_{\text{in}}(\psi):=d_{0}
and
d_{\text{out}}(\psi):=d_{L}
for the
input and output dimension
of
\psi
, and we set
\|\psi\|_{\mathsf{NN}}:=\max_{j=1,\ldots,L}\max\{\|A_{j}\|_{\infty},\|b_{j}\|_{\infty}\}
, where
\|A\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i,j}|A_{i,j}|
.

The function
R_{\sigma}\psi
computed by
\psi
, is defined via an activation function
\sigma
. In this paper, we restrict attention to ReLU networks, corresponding to
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ x\mapsto\max\{0,x\}
, and acting componentwise on vectors. The
realization

R_{\sigma}\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
is then given by

R_{\sigma}\psi:=T_{L}\circ(\sigma\circ T_{L-1})\circ\cdots\circ(\sigma\circ T_{1})\quad\text{where}\quad T_{j}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\mathbb{R}^{d_{j-1}}&\to&\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}\\
x&\mapsto&A_{j}x+b_{j}\end{array}\right..

With slight abuse of notation, we usually do not distinguish notationally between
\psi
and its ReLU-realization
R_{\sigma}\psi
. Thus, we simply say that
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{L}}
is a (ReLU) neural network, when referring to the realization associated with a specific setting of the weights matrices and biases
\psi=\left((A_{1},b_{1}),\ldots,(A_{L},b_{L})\right)
.

2.2
Neural network approximation spaces

We next summarize the relevant approximation spaces
A^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
for a domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
; they depend on a generalized ‘smoothness’ parameter
\alpha>0
and a depth-growth function
\mathbf{\ell}=\mathbf{\ell}(n)
. In short,
A^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
contains all functions
f:D\to\mathbb{R}
that can be uniformly approximated at rate
n^{-\alpha}
, by ReLU neural networks with at most
n
non-zero weights and biases, depth at most
\mathbf{\ell}(n)
, and weight magnitude at most
1
.
The approximation spaces formulated below are similar to the approximation classes described in the seminal work
[
14
]
in Section 11.2. For the purposes of this work, we take the definitions a step further by putting additional depth and weight magnitude bounds on the ReLU neural network class
\Sigma_{d,n}^{\ell}
defined below and defining a quasi-norm on the approximation class.

More precisely, given an input dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
and a non-decreasing depth-growth function
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
, we
define

\Sigma_{d,n}^{\mathbf{\ell}}:={\left\{\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,\begin{array}[]{l}\psi\text{ NN with }d_{\text{in}}(\psi)=d,d_{\text{out}}(\psi)=1,\\
W(\psi)\leq n,L(\psi)\leq\mathbf{\ell}(n),\|\psi\|_{\mathsf{NN}}\leq 1\end{array}\right\}}.

Then, given a measurable subset
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},\ p\in[1,\infty],
and
\alpha\in(0,\infty),
for each
measurable
f:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we define

\Gamma^{\alpha,p}(f):=\max\left\{\|f\|_{L^{p}(D)},\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left[n^{\alpha}\cdot d_{p,D}\left(f,\Sigma_{d,n}^{\mathbf{\ell}}\right)\right]\right\}\in[0,\infty],

where
d_{p,D}(f,\Sigma):=\inf_{g\in\Sigma}\|f-g\|_{L^{p}(D)}.

As pointed out in
[
18
]
,
\Gamma^{\alpha,p}
is not a (quasi-)norm. However, one can define a neural network
approximation space quasi-norm

\|\cdot\|_{A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}}
by

\|f\|_{A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}}:=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\Gamma^{\alpha,p}(f/\theta)\leq 1\right\}\in[0,\infty],

giving rise to the
approximation space

A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}:=A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}(D):=\left\{f\in L^{p}(D):\|f\|_{A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}}<\infty\right\}.

We denote by
U^{\alpha,p}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)\subset A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}(D)
the unit ball,

U_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}:=U_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}(D):=\left\{f\in L^{p}(D):\|f\|_{A_{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\alpha,p}}\leq 1\right\}.

As shown in
[
18
]
,
U^{\alpha,p}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
consists precisely of those
f
for which
d_{p,D}(f,\Sigma_{d,n}^{\mathbf{\ell}})\leq n^{-\alpha}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We will focus on the special case
p=\infty
, in the following.

The following quantity is crucial in characterizing the sample complexity of neural network approximation spaces
[
18
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\ell}^{*}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{\ell}(n)\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

(2.1)

It describes the maximal depth that is allowed for a neural network approximant of a given function.

Remark 2.1
.

We will later extend the definition of the approximation spaces
A^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
to the infinite-dimensional operator learning setting. Specifically, in Section
3.4
we define
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
for a class of DeepONets and in Section
3.5
, we define
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}
for (integral-kernel based) Neural Operators.

2.3
Sample Complexity

By definition, the approximation of a function
f\in A^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
by neural networks can be achieved at a rate
n^{-\alpha}
, in terms of the required number of neural network parameters. This rate is fast, when
\alpha\gg 1
, thus allowing for efficient approximation in principle. Despite this fact, it has been shown in
[
18
]
that such high rates, in terms of the parameter count
n
, do not imply correspondingly high convergence rates when considering the required number of samples to train such neural networks. This phenomenon is referred to as the
theory-to-practice gap
.

Below, we recall mathematical notions from
[
18
]
, which quantify the sample complexity of a set of continuous functions
U\subset C(D)
, where
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a subdomain. We are mostly interested in the setting where
U=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
is the unit ball in the relevant neural network approximation space.

2.3.1
The Deterministic Setting

Given
U\subset C(D)
, we now consider the approximation of
f\in U
with respect to the
L^{p}(D)
-norm.

A map
A:U\rightarrow L^{p}(D)
is called a
deterministic method using
N\in\mathbb{N}
point measurements
, if there exists
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in D^{N}
and a map
Q:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow L^{p}(D)
such that

A(f)=Q\left(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{N})\right)\quad\forall\,f\in U.

The set of all deterministic methods using
N
point measurements will be denoted by
\mathrm{Alg}^{\text{det}}_{N}(U,L^{p}(D))
.

We define the error of
A
for approximation in
L^{p}
as

\displaystyle e(A,U,L^{p}(D)):=\sup_{f\in U}\|f-A(f)\|_{L^{p}(D)}.

The
optimal error
for (deterministic) approximation in
L^{p}
using
N
point samples is then

\displaystyle e_{N}^{\text{det}}(U,L^{p}(D)):=\inf_{A\in\mathrm{Alg}^{\text{det}}_{N}(U,L^{p}(D))}e(A,U,L^{p}(D)).

Finally, the
optimal order of convergence
for (deterministic) approximation in
L^{p}
using
N
point samples is

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}^{\text{det}}(U,L^{p}(D)):=\sup\left\{\beta\geq 0:\begin{array}[]{ll}\exists\,C>0\,\text{s.t. }\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\\
e_{N}^{\text{det}}(U,L^{p}(D))\leq C\cdot N^{-\beta}\end{array}\right\}.

Example 2.2
.

As an example, for a fixed set of points
\{x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\}
, the optimal recovery algorithm is known, although difficult to compute in practice. It is obtained by returning the center of the smallest
L^{p}
ball in
U
containing the set
\{\widehat{f}:\;f\in U,\widehat{f}(x_{i})=f(x_{i}),\;i\in[N]\}
. This ball is known as the Chebyshev ball
[
40
]
. Furthermore, returning any function in this set incurs error only up to a factor of
2
greater than the optimal error. So the deterministic setting decomposes into two parts; first, choosing sample points, and second, computing the center of the Chebyshev ball
[
14
, Section 10.2]
.

2.3.2
The Randomized Setting

Generalizing the deterministic setting above, we consider randomized algorithms. A
randomized method using
N\in\mathbb{N}
point measurements (in expectation)
is a tuple
(A,\mathsf{N})
consisting of a family
A=(A_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
of maps
A_{\omega}:U\rightarrow L^{p}(D)
indexed by a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and a measurable function
\mathsf{N}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
with the following properties:

1.

for each
f\in U
, the map
\Omega\rightarrow L^{p}(D),\omega\mapsto A_{\omega}(f)
is measurable with respect to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
L^{p}(D)
,

2.

for each
\omega\in\Omega
, we have
A_{\omega}\in\mathrm{Alg}^{\text{det}}_{\mathsf{N}(\omega)}(U,L^{p}(D))
,

3.

\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\mathsf{N}(\omega)]\leq N
.

We say that
(A,\mathsf{N})
is
strongly measurable
if the map
\Omega\times U\rightarrow L^{p}(D),(\omega,f)\mapsto A_{\omega}(f)
is measurable, where
U\subset C(D)
is equipped with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra induced by the uniform norm. We denote the set of all strongly measurable
(A,\mathsf{N})
satisfying the above properties by
\mathrm{Alg}_{N}(U,L^{p}(D))
.

The
expected error
of a randomized algorithm
(A,\mathsf{N})
for approximation in
L^{p}
is defined as

\displaystyle e((A,\mathsf{N}),U,L^{p}(D)):=\sup_{f\in U}\mathbb{E}_{\omega}\left[\|f-A_{\omega}(f)\|_{L^{p}(D)}\right].

(2.4)

The
optimal randomized error
for approximation in
L^{p}
using
N
point samples (in expectation) is

\displaystyle e_{N}^{\text{ran}}(U,L^{p}(D)):=\inf_{(A,\mathsf{N})\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}(U,L^{p}(D))}e((A,\mathsf{N}),U,L^{p}(D)).

(2.5)

Finally, the
optimal randomized order
for approximation in
L^{p}
using point samples is

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(U,L^{p}(D)):=\sup\left\{\beta\geq 0:\begin{array}[]{ll}\exists\,C>0\;\text{s.t. }\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\\
e_{N}^{\text{ran}}(U,L^{p}(D))\leq C\cdot N^{-\beta}\end{array}\right\}.

We point out that a deterministic method is a special case of a randomized method, and hence

\displaystyle\beta^{\mathrm{det}}_{\ast}(U,L^{p}(D))\leq\beta_{\ast}(U,L^{p}(D)).

(2.8)

Since our aim is to derive upper bounds on these convergence rates, we may restrict attention to
\beta_{\ast}(U,L^{p}(D))
, implying corresponding bounds also for
\beta_{\ast}^{\mathrm{det}}(U,L^{p}(D))
.

In the following, we in particular derive upper bounds for the exponents

\beta_{\ast}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{p})
,
where
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}([0,1]^{d})
and
L^{p}=L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
with
1\leq p\leq\infty
.

2.4
Theory-to-practice gap

It was shown in
[
18
, Theorem 1.1 and 1.2]
, that the best possible convergence rate of any reconstruction method on
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}:=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}([0,1]^{d})
based on point samples is upper bounded as follows. When the reconstruction error is measured with respect to the
L^{\infty}([0,1]^{d})
norm, the rate is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{\infty})\leq\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}.

(2.9)

When measuring the reconstruction error with respect to the
L^{2}([0,1]^{d})
norm, the bound becomes

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{2})\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}.

(2.10)

These are the tightest known upper bounds on the convergence rates for the most relevant range
\alpha\geq\max(2,{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor})
; for smaller values of
\alpha
refined estimates are given in
[
18
, Theorems 5.1 and 7.1]
.
Astonishingly, even in the limit
\alpha\to\infty
, the best possible reconstruction rates are upper bounded by
1/d
and
3/2
, respectively.

Our first goal in the next subsection is to extend and sharpen the bounds in (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) for arbitrary
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, with a unified proof.

Main result in finite dimension

We prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. Let
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be non-decreasing with
\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}\geq 3
. Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, consider

U:=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|_{[0,1]^{d}}:={\left\{f|_{[0,1]^{d}}\,\middle|\,f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}},

such that
U\subset C([0,1]^{d})
. Then

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(U,L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}.

(2.11)

Since the restriction
U=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|_{[0,1]^{d}}
is a subset of
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}([0,1]^{d})
, Theorem
2.3
implies the following corollary:

Corollary 2.4
.

Denote
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}([0,1]^{d})
. Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, we have

\beta_{\ast}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}.

Remark 2.5
.

The upper bound (
2.11
) implies in particular, that the best possible convergence rate of any randomized or deterministic reconstruction method is upper bounded by
\beta\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}
. In high-dimensional applications, this upper bound is approximately
\frac{1}{p}
, implying that algorithms cannot be expected to converge at substantially faster than Monte-Carlo rates.
It is an open problem whether the bound in (
2.11
) is sharp or whether the bound may be tightened further towards
\frac{1}{p}
.

2.5
Proof of Theorem
2.3

Our proof of Theorem
2.3
is based on several technical lemmas and ideas from
[
18
]
. The main novelty here is a different approach to combining these ingredients: While the derivation in
[
18
]
relies on a linear combination of (many) hat functions and combinatorial arguments, our proof will be instead be based on a random placement of a single hat function, and a probabilistic argument.
Thus, the novelty of our contribution in the finite-dimensional setting is twofold; (i) the obtained bounds are sharper and (ii) the probabilistic proof is new. In obtaining this result, we have lifted Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
with minor modifications from
[
18
]
, and Lemmas
2.6
and
2.9
are entirely new.

Outline

Before detailing the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we first outline the general idea on the domain
[0,1]^{d}
. Our first observation is that, for any choice of evaluation points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in[0,1]^{d}
, there exists a void with inner diameter of order
N^{-1/d}
; more precisely, we show that, independently of the choice of
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
, for a randomly drawn
y\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|\geq\frac{1}{4}N^{-1/d},

(2.12)

with probability at least
1/2
.

Given the inevitable presence of such a void, we are then tempted to place a function
g
with support inside this void. If we assume that
\|g\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 1
, or indeed that
g
is the characteristic function of a cube in this void, then such
g
can have an
L^{p}
-norm as large as
\|g\|_{L^{p}}\sim N^{-1/p}
. Given only point values at
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
, such
g
will be indistinguishable from the zero-function. Thus, if
A
is a reconstruction method relying only on the point values at
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
, then
A(g)=A(0)
. It follows that
\|g\|_{L^{p}}\leq\|g-A(g)\|_{L^{p}}+\|0-A(0)\|_{L^{p}}
and hence at least one of
\|g-A(g)\|_{L^{p}}
or
\|0-A(0)\|_{L^{p}}
must be on the order of magnitude of
\|g\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\sim N^{-1/p}
. As a consequence, the achievable convergence rate
\beta
of a reconstruction method on characteristic functions is fundamentally limited to
\beta\leq\frac{1}{p}
.

To link the above observation with reconstruction methods on neural network approximation spaces, we will recall (cp. Lemma
2.7
, below) that ReLU neural networks can efficiently approximate certain localized functions
\vartheta_{M,y}
. These functions are locally supported inside a cube of side-length
r:=1/M
with center
y\in[0,1]^{d}
. In our proof, the
\vartheta_{M,y}
with
M\sim N^{1/d}
will act as a replacement of the characteristic function
g
of the outline, above. The main difference with the characteristic function is that the gradient of
\vartheta_{M,y}
cannot be arbitrarily large, if we constrain it to belong to the unit ball
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
in the approximation space. This limit on the gradient introduces an additional correction in our upper bound, which finally will take the form
\beta_{\ast}\leq\frac{1}{p}+\text{(correction depending on $\alpha,\mathbf{\ell}$)}
.

Details

We now proceed with the detailed proof of Theorem
2.3
. For any placement of evaluation points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in[0,1]^{d}
, we first show that a point
y\in[0,1]^{d}
picked uniformly at random has a positive chance of sitting in a “void” with interior diameter
\sim N^{-1/d}
:

Lemma 2.6

(Existence of a void)
.

Let
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in[0,1]^{d}
be given. Consider
y\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
drawn uniformly at random. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}>\frac{1}{4}N^{-1/d}\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

(2.13)

Proof.

Let us fix
r>0
for the moment. Applying a union bound, we note that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}\leq r\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[y\in\textstyle\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\left(x_{j}+[-r,r]^{d}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{Vol}\left(x_{j}+[-r,r]^{d}\right)=N(2r)^{d}.

It thus follows that, if
r:=\frac{1}{4}N^{-1/d}\leq\frac{1}{2}(2N)^{-1/d}
, then

\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}\leq r\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Hence, we must have
\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}>r\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}
, as claimed.
∎

We now state the following fundamental result, which follows from
[
18
, Lemma 3.4]
. The main insight of this lemma is that ReLU neural networks can efficiently approximate a localized function
\vartheta_{M,y}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
, which is supported in a cube of side-length
2/M
around
y
, and which “fills in” a significant fraction of this cube.

Lemma 2.7

(Localized networks)
.

Given
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
M\geq 1
and
y\in[0,1]^{d}
, there exists a function
\vartheta_{M,y}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,1]
with the following properties:

•

\vartheta_{M,y}(x)
depends continuously on
x
and
y
; in fact, we have that
\vartheta_{M,y}(x)=\vartheta_{M}(x-y)
is a shift of a neural network
\vartheta_{M}
.

•

\vartheta_{M,y}(x)=0
whenever
|x-y|_{\infty}\geq 1/M
,

•

For any
p\in[1,\infty]
, there exists
C=C(d,p)>0
satisfying,

\|\vartheta_{M,y}\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\geq CM^{-d/p},

•

There exists a constant
\kappa=\kappa(\gamma,\alpha,d,\mathbf{\ell},c)>0
, such that

g_{M,y}:=\kappa M^{-\alpha/(\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor})}\vartheta_{M,y}\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Proof.

The existence of
\vartheta_{M,y}
and the other properties are shown in
[
18
]
, see in particular
[
18
, Lemma 3.4]
.
∎

We also state the following result, which is a minor variant of
[
18
, Lemma 2.3]
:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\emptyset\neq U\subset C(D)
be bounded, and let
1\leq p\leq\infty
. Assume that there exists
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
,
\kappa>0
, such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability space
(\Xi,\mathbb{P})
and a random variable
\psi:\Xi\to U
,
\xi\mapsto\psi_{\xi}
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}(D)}\right]\geq\kappa N^{-\lambda},\quad\forall\,A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U,L^{p}(D)).

Then
\beta_{\ast}(U,L^{p}(D))\leq\lambda
.

Proof.

This follows by the same reasoning as
[
18
, Lemma 2.3]
.
∎

Our main interest in this result is when
U=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|_{[0,1]^{d}}
. Combining the results from Lemmas
2.6
,
2.7
and
2.8
, we now come to the proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Let
d,N\in\mathbb{N}
be given. Recall that
U:=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|_{[0,1]^{d}}
. Our goal is to apply Lemma
2.8
to deterministic reconstruction methods based on
N
point-values,

A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U,L^{p}([0,1]^{d})).

To construct a suitable probability space
(\Xi,\mathbb{P})
and random function
\Xi\to U
, we first define a probability space as
\Xi:=[0,1]^{d}\times\{-1,+1\}
endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-algebra and define
\mathbb{P}:=\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})\otimes\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,+1\})
. We will denote elements of
\Xi
as
\xi=(y,\sigma)
. This defines our probability space.

We next fix

\displaystyle M:=4N^{1/d}.

(2.14)

With this choice of
M
, we then define
\psi_{\xi}:=\psi_{(y,\sigma)}:=\sigma g_{M,y}\in U
, where
g_{M,y}
is the function of Lemma
2.7
. Thus,
\psi_{\xi}=\psi_{(y,\sigma)}
is a random function, given by a random shift of a localized neural network
\vartheta_{M}
by
y\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
and a random choice of sign
\sigma\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\{-1,+1\})
. This defines our random variable

\displaystyle\psi:\Xi\to U.

(2.15)

Invoking Lemma
2.8
, it will suffice to prove the following claim, formulated as a lemma for later reference:

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\psi:\Xi\to U
,
\xi\mapsto\psi_{\xi}
be the random variable defined above (
2.15
), where
U:=U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})|_{[0,1]^{d}}
. There exists a constant
\kappa
, independent of
N
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right]\geq\kappa N^{-\lambda},

(2.16)

for all
A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U,L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))
and where
\lambda:=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}
.

The claim of Theorem
2.3
is then immediate from Lemma
2.8
and
2.9
.

Proof of Lemma
2.9
.

Let
A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))
be given. Let
Q:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
be the reconstruction mapping associated with
A
and let
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the associated evaluation points, such that
A(f)=Q(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{N}))
. We first observe that – whatever the choice of
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}
– we have, by Lemma
2.6
and our choice of
M=4N^{1/d}
in (
2.14
),

\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}>1/M\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

Now consider the random event

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle={\left\{(y,\sigma)\in\Xi\,\middle|\,\psi_{(y,\sigma)}(x_{1})=\dots=\psi_{(y,\sigma)}(x_{N})=0\right\}}

\displaystyle={\left\{(y,\sigma)\in\Xi\,\middle|\,\sigma g_{M,y}(x_{1})=\dots=\sigma g_{M,y}(x_{N})=0\right\}},

where the randomness is introduced by
y\sim\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
and
\sigma\sim\mathrm{Unif}\{-1,+1\}
. Since
g_{M,y}
is supported in the shifted cube
(y+[-1/M,1/M]^{d})
(cp. Lemma
2.7
), it follows that

\mathrm{Prob}_{y,\sigma}[E]\geq\mathrm{Prob}_{y}\left[\min_{j=1,\dots,N}|y-x_{j}|_{\infty}>1/M\right]\geq\frac{1}{2}.

We can now finish the proof. To this end, we first observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}

\displaystyle\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]\,\mathrm{Prob}_{\xi}[E]

\displaystyle\qquad\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(0)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right].

(2.17)

We next note that the random variable
\psi_{\xi}=\sigma g_{M,y}
, conditioned on
E
, has the same distribution as
-\psi_{\xi}=-\sigma g_{M,y}
conditioned on
E
; this follows from the fact that
E
and
\mathbb{P}
are invariant under replacement
\sigma\to-\sigma
. Furthermore, it follows from Lemma
2.7
that there exists a constant
C=C(\alpha,d,p,\mathbf{\ell})>0
, such that

\displaystyle\|\psi_{\xi}\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}=\|g_{M,y}\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\geq CM^{-d/p-\alpha/(\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor})},

(2.18)

for all
M\geq 1
and
y\in[0,1]^{d}
.
Hence, it follows with

\lambda:=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}},

that

\displaystyle 2CM^{-d\lambda}

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]

(by (
2.18
))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-(-\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(0)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|-\psi_{\xi}-A(0)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]\quad

(triangle ineq.)

\displaystyle=2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(0)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\;|\;E\right]

(invar.
\sigma\to-\sigma
)

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right].

(by (
2.17
))

Thus, we have shown that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right]\geq\frac{C}{2}M^{-d\lambda}.

This implies a bound of the desired form (
2.16
), since

\frac{C}{2}M^{-d\lambda}=\frac{C}{2}\left(4N^{1/d}\right)^{-d\lambda}=:\kappa N^{-\lambda},

where we recall
\lambda=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}
,
and
where the constant
\kappa
depends on
\alpha,d,p,\mathbf{\ell}
, but is independent of
N
. This is (
2.16
) and concludes our proof.
∎

3
Extension to operator Learning

We now consider the extension of the theory-to-practice gap to operator learning, i.e. the data-driven approximation of operators mapping between infinite-dimensional function spaces. The finite-dimensional upper bound (
2.11
) suggests that in the infinite-dimensional limit,
d\to\infty
, the best convergence rate in
L^{p}
should be upper bounded by
\frac{1}{p}
,
independently of
\alpha
and
{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}
.

Our goal in this second half of the paper is to rigorously state and prove such a theory-to-practice gap for two prototypical classes of neural operators: deep operator networks, as discussed in
[
36
,
29
]
, and a class of integral-kernel based neural operators
[
26
,
28
]
. Before stating our main results in the operator-learning setting, we will first summarize the overall objective of operator learning, and discuss suitable infinite-dimensional replacements of the spaces
L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
, for
1\leq p\leq\infty
in this context. This is followed by a definition of the aforementioned prototypical neural operator frameworks, their associated operator approximation spaces, and the statement of our main results, establishing a theory-to-practice gap in infinite dimensions.

3.1
Operator Learning

In the following, we will be interested in the sample complexity of operator learning. To simplify our discussion, we will only consider the special case
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
, i.e. we consider the data-driven approximation of non-linear functionals
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Since our analysis concerns
lower bounds
on the sample complexity (or
upper bounds
on the optimal convergence rates), the results continue to hold if the output space is replaced by a more general
\mathcal{Y}
(finite-dimensional or infinite-dimensional). Thus, this reduction can be made without loss of generality. For our analysis, only the fact that the input space
\mathcal{X}
is infinite-dimensional will be relevant.

3.1.1
Sample Complexity of Operator Learning

With this operator learning setting in mind, we first point out that the discussion of the sample complexity of Section
2.3
carries over with only minor changes. For convenience, we repeat the main elements here.

We now consider a set of continuous operators
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\subset C(\mathcal{X})=C(\mathcal{X};\mathbb{R})
on a separable Banach space
\mathcal{X}
. We fix a Banach space of operators
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
, equipped with a norm
\|{\,\cdot\,}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}}
. In analogy with the finite-dimensional setting, a map
\mathcal{A}:\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\to\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
will be called a deterministic method using
N\in\mathbb{N}
point measurements, if there exists
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{N})\in\mathcal{X}^{N}
, and a map
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
, such that

\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{N})),\quad\forall\,\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}.

We recall that each
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
is a continuous operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and hence the above expression is well-defined. Consistent with our earlier discussion, the set of all deterministic methods using
N
point measurements will be denoted by
\mathrm{Alg}^{\mathrm{det}}_{N}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})
. It will be assumed that
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\subset\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
with a canonical embedding.

The approximation error of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
is defined as

e(\mathcal{A},\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})=\sup_{\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}}\|\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})-\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}},

and the optimal error is
e^{\mathrm{det}}_{N}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})=\inf_{\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})}e(\mathcal{A},\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})
. The optimal order of convergence for deterministic approximation in
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
using
N
point samples is defined as in (
2.3.1
).

Randomized methods for operator learning, as well as the corresponding errors and the optimal randomized order (
2.3.2
) are similarly defined, in analogy with Section
2.3
; we here only recall that a randomized method using
N
point measurement (in expectation) is a tuple
(\mathcal{A},\mathsf{N})
consisting of a family
\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{A}_{\omega}),{\omega\in\Omega}
of maps
\mathcal{A}_{\omega}:\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\to\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
indexed by a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and a measurable function
\mathsf{N}:\Omega\to\mathbb{N}
with the same properties as described there, and with
\mathcal{A}_{\omega}\in\mathrm{Alg}^{\mathrm{det}}_{\mathsf{N}(\omega)}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})
. We continue to denote by
\mathrm{Alg}_{N}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})
the set of all strongly measurable randomized methods with
\mathbb{E}_{\omega}[\mathsf{N}(\omega)]\leq N
expected point evaluations.

In the following, we will derive upper bounds on
\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\mathsf{\mathbf{V}})
, where
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
is the unit ball in an operator learning approximation space, and
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}
is a space of operators with distance measured by either an
L^{p}
-norm with
1\leq p<\infty
, or by a sup-norm. We describe the relevant setting in the next section.

3.2
Input functions in infinite dimensions

In the finite-dimensional case, the domain
[0,1]^{d}
is widely considered as a canonical prototype, and results obtained for
[0,1]^{d}
usually extend to more general bounded domains
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, under mild assumptions. In the infinite-dimensional setting, there is no longer such a canonical choice. Therefore, we preface our discussion of the infinite-dimensional theory-to-practice gap with the introduction of suitable alternatives to the spaces
L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
and
C([0,1]^{d})
(the latter corresponding to the limit case
p=\infty
) to be considered in this work.

Approximation in
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}=L^{p}(\mu)

When the approximation error is measured in an
L^{p}
-norm for
1\leq p<\infty
, we consider the following prototypical setting: We are given a probability measure
\mu
on the input function space
\mathcal{X}
. We then consider the Banach space of
p
-integrable (real-valued) operators
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}=L^{p}(\mu)
, with the following
L^{p}
-norm:

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}:=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[|\mathcal{G}(u)|^{p}\right]^{1/p}.

Thus, in the infinite-dimensional setting, the probability measure
\mu
replaces the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
.
In order to obtain the hardness result, we must ensure that
\mu
is “truly infinite-dimensional”.
We thus assume that the following
d
-dependent assumption holds for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
:

Assumption 3.1
.

There exist linearly independent
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
with bi-orthogonal
\{e^{\ast}_{1},\dots,e^{\ast}_{d}\}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
, such that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is the law of

\displaystyle u=\xi+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}e_{j},\quad(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\sim\mu_{d},\;\xi\sim\mu_{d}^{\perp},

(3.1)

where

[label=
()
]

1.

The law of
(y_{1},\dots y_{d})
is of the form
\mu_{d}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\rho_{j}(z_{j})\;\mathsf{d}z_{j}
, for
\rho_{j}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

2.

The law of
\xi
is of the form
\mu_{d}^{\perp}\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega_{d})
for
\Omega_{d}:={\left\{\xi\in\mathcal{X}\,\middle|\,e^{\ast}_{1}(\xi)=\dots=e^{\ast}_{d}(\xi)=0\right\}}\subset\mathcal{X}.

3.

There exist intervals
I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\essinf_{z_{j}\in I_{j}}\rho_{j}(z_{j})>0
for
j=1,\dots,d
.

We can thus think of
\mu
in Assumption
3.1
as essentially equivalent to the product measure
\mu_{d}\otimes\mu_{d}^{\perp}
. This structure will be convenient for our derivation of the infinite-dimensional theory-to-practice gap. Below we provide an illustrative example of a basic setting where Assumptions
3.1
hold.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the space
\mathcal{X}=L^{2}([0,1])
and the Dirichlet Laplacian sine basis
e_{j}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(j\pi x)
for
j\in\{1,2,\dots\}
and
x\in[0,1]
. Then we have that
e_{j}^{*}(u)=\int_{0}^{1}u(x)e_{j}(x)\;\mathsf{d}x
. It is straightforward to check that biorthogonality and linear independence hold. Draw independent
Z_{j}\sim N(0,\sigma_{j}^{2})
with
\sum_{j}\sigma_{j}^{2}<\infty
, for instance, with
\sigma_{j}=\frac{1}{j}
, and set
u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Z_{j}e_{j}
. Then
\mu
, the law of
u
, is the trace-class Gaussian measure
N(0,C)
on
L^{2}([0,1])
with covariance
C=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{j}^{2}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}
. Splitting at
d
with
y_{j}=Z_{j}
for
j\leq d
and
\xi=\sum_{j>d}Z_{j}e_{j}
yields the form (
3.1
), where independence of the
Z_{j}
gives
\xi\perp(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
. The law of
(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})
is
\mu_{d}=N(0,\Sigma_{d})
where
\Sigma_{d}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\dots,\sigma_{d}^{2})
with product density
\mu_{d}=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\rho_{j}(z_{j})\mathsf{d}z_{j}
for
\rho_{j}
the
N(0,\frac{1}{j^{2}})
density. Each Gaussian density satisfies
\essinf_{z\in I_{j}}\rho_{j}>0
on any bounded interval
I_{j}
. The law of
\xi
is
\mu_{d}^{\perp}=N(0,C_{d}^{\perp})
with
C_{d}^{\perp}=\sum_{j>d}\sigma_{j}^{2}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}
, supported on
\Omega_{d}
since
e_{k}^{*}(\xi)=0
for
k\leq d
.

This concludes our discussion of the
L^{p}(\mu)
-setting considered in this work.

Approximation in
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}=C(\mathcal{K})

When the goal is to approximate
\mathcal{G}\in\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
uniformly, i.e. with respect to the
L^{\infty}
-norm, we restrict attention to a compact set
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
. In this case, we consider the Banach space of continuous operators
\mathsf{\mathbf{V}}=C(\mathcal{K})
, endowed with the sup-norm:

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{C(\mathcal{K})}:=\sup_{u\in\mathcal{K}}|\mathcal{G}(u)|.

Thus, in the infinite-dimensional setting, the compact set
\mathcal{K}
replaces the unit cube
[0,1]^{d}
. To ensure that
\mathcal{K}
is truly infinite-dimensional, and we make the following assumption:

Assumption 3.3
.

We assume that the set
\mathcal{K}
is (i)
convex
and (ii) there does
not
exist a finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{X}_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
containing
\mathcal{K}
.

We can relate this uniform setting to the
L^{p}(\mu)
-setting described above:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
satisfy Assumption
3.3
. Then for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, with
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
, satisfying Assumption
3.1
; more specifically,
\mu
is the law of

u=e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}e_{j},\qquad(y_{1},\dots,y_{d})\sim\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]).

Here
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\in\mathcal{X}
are linearly independent elements for which there exist bi-orthogonal elements
e^{\ast}_{1},\dots,e^{\ast}_{d}\in\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
, and
e_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
is such that
e^{\ast}_{1}(e_{0})=\dots=e^{\ast}_{d}(e_{0})=0
.

Proof.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be given. Our aim is to construct
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
as in the claim of Proposition
3.4
. By assumption,
\mathcal{K}
is infinite-dimensional. It follows that there exist
v_{0},\dots,v_{d}\in\mathcal{K}
which are linearly independent. Given such a choice, we set
e_{0}:=v_{0}
, and
e_{j}:=\frac{1}{d}(v_{j}-v_{0})
for
j=1,\dots,d
. Since
v_{0},\dots,v_{d}
are linearly independent, it follows that also
e_{0},\dots,e_{d}
are linearly independent. Furthermore, for any finite set of linearly independent vectors, we can find bi-orthogonal elements
e^{\ast}_{0},\dots,e^{\ast}_{d}\in\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
, such that
e^{\ast}_{j}(e_{k})=\delta_{jk}
,
j,k=0,\dots,d
. In particular, for this choice we then have
e^{\ast}_{1}(e_{0})=\dots=e^{\ast}_{d}(e_{0})=0
.

We now define
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
as the law of
u:=e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}e_{j}
, with
y_{1},\dots,y_{d}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathrm{Unif}([0,1])
. This
\mu
trivially satisfies Assumption
3.1
. To prove Proposition
3.4
, it thus only remains to show that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
. To see this, we observe that

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=e_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}e_{j}=v_{0}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}(v_{j}-v_{0})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}\right)v_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{y_{j}}{d}v_{j}=:\sum_{j=0}^{d}\lambda_{j}v_{j},

where
\lambda_{0}:=1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}
,
\lambda_{j}:=\frac{1}{d}y_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,d
.
The last sum identifies
u
as a convex combination of
v_{0},\dots,v_{d}\in\mathcal{K}
: Indeed, since
y_{j}\in[0,1]
, for
j=1,\dots,d
, it follows that
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{d}\geq 0
and we also have
\sum_{j=0}^{d}\lambda_{j}=1
by definition of
\lambda_{0}
. It now follows from the convexity of
\mathcal{K}
that
u\in\mathcal{K}
for any choice of
y_{1},\dots,y_{d}\in[0,1]
, and hence
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

A consequence of Proposition
3.4
is that approximation of a continuous operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{K}\to\mathcal{Y}
with respect to the
C(\mathcal{K})
-norm is at least as difficult as approximation with respect to the
L^{p}(\mu)
-norm, for
any

p\in[1,\infty)
, and where
\mu
satisfies Assumption
3.1
. Indeed, for any reconstruction method
\mathcal{A}:\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\to C(\mathcal{K})
, we have

\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\leq\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}\leq\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})\|_{C(\mathcal{K})},

where the last inequality follows from
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
.
This implies that the optimal convergence rate with respect to
C(\mathcal{K})
can be at most as large as the optimal convergence rate with respect to
L^{p}(\mu)
:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
be compact. If
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is a probability measure such that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
, then

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},C(\mathcal{K}))\leq\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu)).

(3.2)

This simple observation will allow us to deduce results about the uniform setting from corresponding results in the
L^{p}(\mu)
setting, and derive estimates on
\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},C(\mathcal{K}))
by passing to the limit
p\to\infty
.

3.3
From Finite to Infinite Dimensions

Neural operators
\Psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, approximating
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, can often be interpreted as a composition of two mappings,
\Psi(u)=\psi\circ\mathcal{E}(u)
. Here,
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an encoder, which maps the infinite-dimensional input into a finite-dimensional latent space, and
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the realization of a finite-dimensional neural network. The latent dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
is a hyperparameter of the architecture.

The general idea behind our proof of the infinite-dimensional theory-to-practice gap is the following: If the relevant approximation space
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\subset C(\mathcal{X})
contains compositions of the form
f\circ\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
where
f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
belongs to the
d
-dimensional neural network approximation space, then the mapping

U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}\to\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},\quad f\mapsto f\circ\mathcal{E},

defines an embedding of
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
into
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
. Thus, the sample complexity of
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
should be at least as large as that of
U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
. As a consequence, any
d
-dimensional upper bound on convergence rate implies a corresponding bound in the infinite-dimensional case. This general intuition is confirmed and made precise by the following proposition:

Proposition 3.6

(
L^{p}
setting)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a separable Banach space, let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
, and let
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\subset C(\mathcal{X})
be a set of continuous operators. Assume that there is an encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a constant
c>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\cdot\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d}).

(3.3)

If for some
\alpha>0
and
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle{\left\{f\circ\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}}\subset\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},

(3.4)

then

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}.

(3.5)

Our proof of Proposition
3.6
will be based on the following two lemmas. The first lemma shows that, under the assumptions of Proposition
3.6
any deterministic reconstruction method
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu))
in infinite dimensions induces an associated finite-dimensional reconstruction method
A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))
.

Lemma 3.7
.

With the notation and under assumptions (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) of Proposition
3.6
, the following holds: For any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu))
, there exists
A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))
, such that

\displaystyle\|f\circ\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{A}(f\circ\mathcal{E})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\geq c\|f-A(f)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})},\quad\forall\,f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}.

(3.6)

Here
c>0
is the constant of (
3.3
).

Proof Sketch.

The detailed proof of Lemma
3.7
is included in Appendix
B.2
. The basic idea of the proof is the following: By definition,
\mathcal{A}
is of the form
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{G})=\mathcal{Q}(\mathcal{G}(u_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{G}(u_{N}))
for some sample points
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}(\mu)
. We want to construct
A:U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
, of the form
A(f)=Q(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{N}))
, where
Q:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
. The canonical choice of the sample points
x_{1},\dots,x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is via composition with the encoder,
x_{j}:=\mathcal{E}(u_{j})
. The main remaining question is then how to construct the mapping
Q:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
from
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}(\mu)
. A first idea is that this reconstruction could satisfy,

Q(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(\mathcal{E}(u)):=\mathcal{Q}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(u),\quad\forall\,u\in\mathcal{X},\;\forall\,(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

However, this is not well-defined, since different
u
will generally map to the same
x=\mathcal{E}(u)
. The improved guess is the following: Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider a random variable
u\sim\mu
. We now condition on the event that
\mathcal{E}(u)=x
. This gives a conditional distribution on the input function space. We then
average
the reconstruction
\mathcal{Q}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(u)
in
u
over this conditional distribution, i.e. define

Q(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(x):=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\mathcal{Q}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right].

This is well-defined, and due to Jensen’s inequality the conditional averaging on the right-hand side turns out to reduce the reconstruction error of
A
compared to
\mathcal{A}
. The detailed calculations are provided in Appendix
B.2
.
∎

The last lemma is in anticipation of our next result, Lemma
3.8
. The final result Proposition
3.6
will then be an immediate consequence.

Lemma 3.8
.

With the notation and under assumptions (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) of Proposition
3.6
, the following holds.
There exists
\kappa>0
, such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability space
(\Xi,\mathbb{P})
and a random variable
\Psi:\Xi\to\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
,
\xi\mapsto\Psi_{\xi}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\Psi_{\xi}-\mathcal{A}(\Psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\right]\geq\kappa N^{-\lambda},\quad\forall\,\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu)),

(3.7)

where
\lambda=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.9
, there exists a constant
\kappa>0
, such that for any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability space
(\Xi,\mathbb{P})
and random variable
\psi:\Xi\to U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
,
\xi\mapsto\psi_{\xi}
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right]\geq\kappa N^{-\lambda},\quad\forall\,A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}},L^{p}([0,1]^{d})),

and where
\lambda:=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}
. We use
\psi_{\xi}
to define a new random variable
\Psi:\Xi\to\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}
,
\Psi_{\xi}:=\psi_{\xi}\circ\mathcal{E}
. We claim that (
3.7
) holds for this
\Psi_{\xi}
.

To see this, let
\mathcal{A}\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}(\mu))
be given. By Lemma
3.7
, there exists
A\in\mathrm{Alg}_{N}^{\mathrm{det}}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}},L^{p}([0,1]^{d}))
, such that

\|\psi_{\xi}\circ\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{A}(\psi_{\xi}\circ\mathcal{E})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\geq c\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\Xi.

Here
c>0
is the constant appearing in (
3.3
) (and is independent of
N
).
Taking expectations over
\xi
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\Psi_{\xi}-\mathcal{A}(\Psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\right]\geq c\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\psi_{\xi}-A(\psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}\right].

By construction of
\psi_{\xi}
, the right hand side is lower bounded by
c\kappa N^{-\lambda}
, with
\kappa>0
independent of
N
and
\lambda=\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+{\lfloor\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}}
. Thus it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\|\Psi_{\xi}-\mathcal{A}(\Psi_{\xi})\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}\right]\geq c\kappa N^{-\lambda},

where
c,\kappa>0
are independent of
N
. Replacing
c\kappa
by
\kappa
, the claimed bound (
3.7
) follows.
∎

The proof of Proposition
3.6
is now immediate:

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Proposition
3.6
follows from Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
2.9
.
∎

3.4
Deep Operator Networks (DeepONet)

In this section, we state and prove a theory-to-practice gap for a general family of “DeepONet” architectures. We recall that we are interested in the approximation of operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. In this setting, we define these DeepONet architectures to be of the form
\Psi=\psi\circ\mathcal{L}
, combining a linear encoder
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a feedforward neural network
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. The next three paragraphs provide a precise description of the considered architecture, define relevant approximation spaces, and prove an infinite-dimensional theory-to-practice gap for these architectures.

Architecture

Fix a sequence of continuous linear functionals
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
d_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\mathcal{L}_{d_{0}}
the linear encoder
\mathcal{L}_{d_{0}}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}
,
\mathcal{L}_{d_{0}}(u):=(\ell_{1}(u),\dots,\ell_{d_{0}}(u))
. The
encoder-net

\Psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with a neural network
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{0}}\to\mathbb{R}
is a mapping of the form
\Psi(u)=\psi\circ\mathcal{L}_{d_{0}}(u)
.

To ensure universality of the resulting operator learning architecture, we will make the (minimal) assumption that

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}{\left\{\ell_{j}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}\,\middle|\,j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\ast}\text{ is dense},

(3.8)

where we recall that
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
denotes the continuous dual of
\mathcal{X}
. Throughout the following discussion, we will consider the sequence
(\ell_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
fixed, and it will be assumed that (
3.8
) holds without further mention.

DeepONet Approximation Space

We can now define spaces
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
for DeepONets. To this end, we introduce,

\Sigma^{\mathbf{\ell}}_{n,\mathrm{DON}}:=\left\{\Psi=\psi\circ\mathcal{L}_{d_{0}}:\begin{array}[]{l}\psi\text{ NN with }d_{\text{in}}(\psi)=d_{0},d_{\text{out}}(\psi)=1,\\
\max\{W(\psi),d_{0}\}\leq n,L(\psi)\leq\mathbf{\ell}(n),\|\psi\|_{\mathsf{NN}}\leq 1\end{array}\right\}.

Then, given
\alpha\in(0,\infty),
for each continuous (non-linear) operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define

\Gamma^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathrm{DON}}(\mathcal{G}):=\max\left\{\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{G}(u)\|,\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left[n^{\alpha}\cdot d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{G},\Sigma_{n,\mathrm{DON}}^{\mathbf{\ell}}\right)\right]\right\}\in[0,\infty],

where
d_{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\Sigma):=\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{G}(u)-\Psi(u)\|.

We can define a DeepONet
approximation space quasi-norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}}
by

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}}:=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\Gamma_{\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}(\mathcal{G}/\theta)\leq 1\right\}\in[0,\infty],

giving rise to the DeepONet
approximation space

\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}:=\left\{\mathcal{G}\in C(\mathcal{X}):\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}}<\infty\right\}.

Encoder Construction

The following is a useful technical lemma, which will be applied to construct suitable encoders
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. It shows that if a finite-dimensional map
F:V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sufficiently close to the identity, then the image of
V
must “fill out” a non-empty open set
V_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-empty domain. There exist constants
\epsilon_{0},c_{0}>0
and a non-empty open subset
V_{0}\subset V
with the following property: For any Lipschitz-continuous function
F:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, satisfying

\|F-\mathrm{id}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(V)}\leq\epsilon_{0},

where
\mathrm{id}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathrm{id}(y)=y
denotes the identity mapping,
it follows that
F(V)\supset V_{0}
, and

F_{\#}\mathrm{Unif}(V)\geq c_{0}\mathrm{Unif}(V_{0}).

The result of Lemma
3.9
follows as a consequence of the contraction mapping theorem; we provide a detailed proof in Appendix
B.1
. Our goal in this section is to construct encoders
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
which “fill out” the set
[0,1]^{d}
. The link with the finite-dimensional setting of Lemma
3.9
is made by identifying
\mathcal{X}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega_{d}
, as in the decomposition of
\mu
made in Assumptions
3.1
, and by considering the second factor as a parameter. This leads us to study parametrized mappings,
F:V\times\Omega_{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(y,\xi)\mapsto F_{\xi}(y)=F(y;\xi)
, with the parameter
\xi\in\Omega_{d}
a random variable. The following result derives a similar result as Lemma
3.9
in this parametrized setting:

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a non-empty domain, and let
\epsilon_{0},c_{0}>0
and
V_{0}\subset V
denote the constants and set of Lemma
3.9
, respectively. Let
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, and assume that
F:V\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
(y,\xi)\mapsto F(y;\xi)
is measurable. Assume furthermore, that there exists
K\subset\Omega
, such that the mapping
F_{\xi}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
y\mapsto F_{\xi}(y):=F(y;\xi)
is Lipschitz for each
\xi\in K
, and

\displaystyle\|F_{\xi}-\mathrm{id}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(V)}\leq\epsilon_{0},\quad\forall\,\xi\in K.

(3.9)

Then the push-forward under
F
of the product measure
\mathrm{Unif}(V)\otimes\mathbb{P}
on
V\times\Omega
satisfies,

F_{\#}(\mathrm{Unif}(V)\otimes\mathbb{P})\geq c_{0}\mathbb{P}(K)\,\mathrm{Unif}(V_{0}).

A detailed proof of Lemma
3.10
is given in Appendix
B.1
. Let now
\{\ell_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
be a set of encoding functionals, such that
\mathrm{span}\{\ell_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
is dense.
Our first goal is to use the
\{\ell_{k}\}
to construct an encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
.

To this end, let us momentarily fix
\delta>0
. Then by density, there exists
d_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and coefficients
c_{jk}
for
j\in[d]
,
k\in[d_{0}]
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{\ast}_{j}-\sum_{k=1}^{d_{0}}c_{jk}\ell_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\ast}}\leq\delta,\quad\forall\,j=1,\dots,d.

(3.10)

Since
e_{j}^{\ast}(u(y;\xi))=y_{j}
, (
3.10
) allows us to approximate a “projection” onto
y_{j}
. Motivated by this, we now define the encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathcal{E}(u):=(\mathcal{E}_{1}(u),\dots,\mathcal{E}_{d}(u))
, via

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{j}(u)=b_{j}+\sum_{k=1}^{d_{0}}a_{jk}\ell_{k}(u),

(3.11)

for coefficients
a_{jk}
and bias
b_{j}
, for
j\in[d]
and
k\in[d_{0}]
, to be determined.

Proposition 3.11
.

Assume that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies Assumption
3.1
for
d\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then there exists an encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
of the form (
3.11
) and constant
c>0
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\,\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d}).

(3.12)

The proof of Proposition
3.11
is a straight-forward, albeit somewhat tedious, consequence of Lemma
3.10
and the fact that encoders of the form (
3.11
) are dense in the space of all affine encoders with
d
-dimensional range. The proof relies on the assumption that the linear functionals
\{\ell_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are dense in
\mathcal{X}^{\ast}
. We include the detailed argument in Appendix
B.4
.

Theory-to-Practice Gap

We can now state a theory-to-practice gap for the unit ball
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
in the DeepONet approximation space
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
:

Theorem 3.12

(DeepONet theory-to-practice gap)
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
. Let
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be non-decreasing with
\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}\geq 4
. Assume that
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
satisfies Assumption
3.1
. Then for any
\alpha>0
, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}.

(3.13)

We recall that the typical Monte-Carlo (MC) approximation rate in the
L^{p}
-norm is
\beta_{MC}=1/p
, reducing to the well-known
1/2
-rate with respect to the
L^{2}
-norm. Theorem
3.12
shows that,
independently of
\alpha>0
and the depth
\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}\geq 4
, it is not possible to achieve better-than-MC rates by any approximation method on the relevant approximation spaces
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
.

Proof.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be a non-decreasing function with
\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}\geq 4
. We denote
\widetilde{\mathbf{\ell}}:=\mathbf{\ell}-3
, so that
\widetilde{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\ast}\geq 3
, as in the assumptions of the
finite dimensional
theory-to-practice gap, Theorem
2.3
. Let
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the encoder of Proposition
3.11
, such that
\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\,\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
. Let
\gamma_{d}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
denote re-scaling of
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
by a constant scaling factor
\gamma_{d}
. By Lemma
3.13
, which we state below after the proof, there exists a constant
\gamma_{d}\geq 1
, such that we have

\displaystyle{\left\{f\circ\mathcal{E}\,\middle|\,f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\widetilde{\mathbf{\ell}}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\right\}}\subset\gamma_{d}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}.

(3.14)

Assuming (
3.14
), the claim of Theorem
3.12
then follows immediately from Proposition
3.6
. Indeed, defining
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}:=\gamma_{d}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}}
, that proposition implies that

\beta_{\ast}(\gamma_{d}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\lfloor\widetilde{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\ast}/2\rfloor}.

However, it follows from the definition that
\beta_{\ast}
is invariant under re-scaling,

\beta_{\ast}(\gamma_{d}\cdot\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))=\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu)).

Thus, recalling also
\widetilde{\mathbf{\ell}}^{\ast}=\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}-1
, we have

\displaystyle\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\lfloor({\mathbf{\ell}}^{\ast}-1)/2\rfloor}.

(3.15)

Since
d\in\mathbb{N}
was arbitrary and the left-hand side is independent of
d
, we can take the infimum over all
d\in\mathbb{N}
on the right to conclude that

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}.

This is (
3.13
).

∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
non-decreasing and
f\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(D)
. Then for every
e\in\mathbb{N}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times e}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there is
R\in(0,\infty)
with
f(C\cdot+b)\in R\cdot U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}(E)
, where
E:=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{e}:\ Cx+b\in D\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{e}
.

The detailed proof of Lemma
3.13
is given in Appendix
B.5
. We also state the following theory-to-practice gap for uniform approximation over compact
\mathcal{K}
:

Theorem 3.14

(DeepONet; uniform theory-to-practice gap)
.

Let
\mathbf{\ell}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
be non-decreasing with
\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}\geq 4
. Assume that
\mathcal{K}\subset\mathcal{X}
is a compact set satisfying Assumption
3.3
. Then for any
\alpha>0
, we have

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},C(\mathcal{K}))=0.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
, for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, with
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
, and
\mu
satisfies Assumption
3.1
. By Proposition
3.11
, there exists a DeepONet encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constant
c>0
, such that

\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\,\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d}).

Following the steps in the proof of Theorem
3.12
, leading up to (
3.15
), it follows that for any
p\in[1,\infty]
, we have

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\lfloor(\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}-1)/2\rfloor}.

We now recall that,

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},C(\mathcal{K}))\leq\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},L^{p}(\mu)).

This inequality is (
3.2
) and follows from the fact that uniform approximation over
\mathcal{K}
is a more stringent criterion than
L^{p}(\mu)
approximation with respect to
\mu
, owing to the fact that
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)\subset\mathcal{K}
. Thus, we have

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},C(\mathcal{K}))\leq\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{d}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\lfloor(\mathbf{\ell}^{\ast}-1)/2\rfloor}.

This holds for any
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. The convergence rate
\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{DON}},C(\mathcal{K}))
on the left depends on
\alpha
and
\mathbf{\ell}
, but is independent of
p
and
d
. Thus, upon letting
d,p\to\infty
, the claim follows.
∎

3.5
Integral-kernel Neural Operators

In this section, we state and prove a theory-to-practice gap for a general family of integral-kernel neural operator (NO) architectures
[
26
,
33
]
. Again, we are interested in the approximation of operators
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. In this setting, we define integral-kernel NO architectures to be of the form
\Psi=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}
, combining a lifting layer
\mathcal{R}
, hidden layers
\mathcal{L}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{L}_{L}
and an output layer
\mathcal{Q}
. The next three paragraphs provide a precise description of the considered architecture, define relevant approximation spaces, and prove an infinite-dimensional theory-to-practice gap.

Architecture

The following is a minimal architecture shared by all/most variants of integral kernel-based neural operators
[
28
]
. For notational simplicity, we focus on real-valued input and output functions. All results extend readily to the more general vector-valued case.

Definition 3.15

(Averaging Neural Operator)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}(D;\mathbb{R})
,
\mathcal{Y}(D;\mathbb{R})
, and
\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{{d_{c}}})
be spaces of functions on Lipschitz domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{D}}
. An averaging neural operator (ANO)
\Psi:\;\mathcal{X}(D;\mathbb{R})\to\mathcal{Y}(D;\mathbb{R})
of depth
L
takes the form

\Psi(u)=\mathcal{Q}\circ\mathcal{L}_{L}\circ\dots\mathcal{L}_{1}\circ\mathcal{R}(u)

(3.16)

where
u\in\mathcal{X}(D;\mathbb{R})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In addition, the pointwise lifting operators
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are obtained by composition with shallow ReLU neural networks; i.e. there exist neural networks
R:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
and
Q:\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}\to\mathbb{R}
, of depth
L(R)=L(Q)=2
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(u)(x)=R(u(x),x),\qquad\mathcal{Q}(v)(x)=Q(v(x)).

(3.17)

Finally, the hidden layers
\mathcal{L}_{j}:\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}})\to\mathcal{V}(D;\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}})
take the form

\mathcal{L}_{j}(v)(x)=\sigma\left(W_{j}v(x)+b_{j}+\fint_{D}v(y)\;\mathsf{d}y\right),

(3.18)

where
W_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}\times d_{c}}
is a matrix and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{c}}
a bias.

Generalizing our definitions of quantities of interest from Section
2.1
, we will denote by
L(\Psi):=L
the depth (number of hidden layers) of an ANO, and we denote by
W(\Psi)=W(R)+\sum_{j=1}^{L}\left(\|W_{j}\|_{\ell^{0}}+\|b_{j}\|_{\ell^{0}}\right)+W(Q)
the total number of non-zero parameters of the architecture. Furthermore, we define
\|\Psi\|_{\mathsf{NN}}:=\max\left\{\|W_{j}\|_{\infty},\|b_{j}\|_{\infty},\|R\|_{\mathsf{NN}},\|Q\|_{\mathsf{NN}}\right\}
as the maximal weight magnitude.

Remark 3.16
.

The ANO introduced above is a special case of a more general family of kernel-based neural operators introduced in
[
26
]
. Its theoretical significance is that most instantiations of such neural operators contain the ANO as a special case, with a specific tuning of the weights. For example, the popular Fourier neural operator (FNO)
[
33
]
uses the same general structure, but employs hidden layers of the form

\mathcal{L}(v)(x)=\sigma\left(Wv(x)+b+\int\kappa(x-y)v(y)\,dy\right),

where the integral kernel
\kappa
is convolutional, and

\kappa(x)=\sum_{|k|\leq k_{\mathrm{max}}}\widehat{\kappa}_{k}e^{ik\cdot x}

is parametrized by the coefficients
\widehat{\kappa}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{d_{c}\times d_{c}}
in its (truncated) Fourier expansion. Thus, the ANO can be obtained from the FNO upon setting
\widehat{\kappa}_{k}\equiv 0
for
k\neq 0
and
\widehat{\kappa}_{0}=I_{d_{c}\times d_{c}}/\mathrm{vol}(D)
.

NO Approximation Space

We now define the relevant approximation spaces
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}
for (averaging) neural operators. Assume we are given function spaces
\mathcal{X}(D)=\mathcal{X}(D;\mathbb{R})
and
\mathcal{Y}(D)=\mathcal{Y}(D;\mathbb{R})
. In our discussion, we will assume that
\mathcal{X}(D)\subset L^{\infty}(D)
is an infinite-dimensional Banach space on Lipschitz domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{D}}
, and we will assume that
\mathcal{Y}(D)
contains all constant functions. We now introduce,

\Sigma^{\mathbf{\ell}}_{n,\mathrm{NO}}:=\left\{\Psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}:\begin{array}[]{l}\Psi\text{ is an ANO with }W(\Psi)\leq n,\\
L(\Psi)\leq\mathbf{\ell}(n),\|\Psi\|_{\mathsf{NN}}\leq 1\end{array}\right\}.

Remark 3.17
.

Part of the definition of
\Sigma^{\mathbf{\ell}}_{n,\mathrm{NO}}
is that for any
\Psi\in\Sigma^{\mathbf{\ell}}_{n,\mathrm{NO}}
, we must have
\Psi(\mathcal{X})\subset\mathcal{Y}
. Non-trivial
\Psi
exist, since we can readily construct averaging neural operators
\Psi
of the form (
3.16
), for which the output
\Psi(u)
is a
constant-valued function
, for any input
u\in\mathcal{X}
. Since
\mathcal{Y}
contains constant functions by assumption, this implies that such
\Psi
defines a map
\Psi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
. In our proofs, we will only ever consider
\Psi
of this form, thus our results hold even when
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
.

Given
\alpha\in(0,\infty),
for each continuous (non-linear) operator
\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, we define

\Gamma^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathrm{NO}}(\mathcal{G}):=\max\left\{\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{G}(u)\|,\ \sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left[n^{\alpha}\cdot d_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{G},\Sigma_{n,\mathrm{NO}}^{\mathbf{\ell}}\right)\right]\right\}\in[0,\infty],

where
d_{\infty}(\mathcal{G},\Sigma):=\inf_{\Psi\in\Sigma}\sup_{u\in\mathcal{X}}\|\mathcal{G}(u)-\Psi(u)\|_{\mathcal{Y}}.

We define a NO
approximation space quasi-norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}}
by

\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}}:=\inf\left\{\theta>0:\Gamma_{\mathrm{DON}}^{\alpha,\infty}(\mathcal{G}/\theta)\leq 1\right\}\in[0,\infty],

giving rise to the NO
approximation space

\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}:=\left\{\mathcal{G}\in C(\mathcal{X};\mathcal{Y}):\|\mathcal{G}\|_{\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}}<\infty\right\}.

We again denote by
\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}
the unit ball in
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}
.

Remark 3.18
.

As pointed out in Remark
3.16
, the ANO can be obtained by a special setting of the weights in the FNO. As a consequence, it can be shown that
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}\subset\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{FNO}}^{\alpha,\infty}
, where
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{FNO}}^{\alpha,\infty}
denotes the relevant approximation space for FNO, which can be defined in analogy to
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty}
. Based on this relationship, it could be shown that

\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{FNO}}^{\alpha,\infty},L^{p}(\mu))\leq\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty},L^{p}(\mu)),

and hence any upper bound on
\beta_{\ast}(\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}_{\mathbf{\ell},\mathrm{NO}}^{\alpha,\infty},L^{p}(\mu))
implies a corresponding upper bound for the FNO.

Encoder Construction

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
be a probability measure on
\mathcal{X}
. We recall that
L^{1}(D)
is a subset of the dual of
L^{\infty}(D)
under the natural pairing,

\langle u,e^{\ast}\rangle=\int_{D}u(x)e^{\ast}(x)\,\;\mathsf{d}x,\quad\forall u\in L^{\infty}(D),\;e^{\ast}\in L^{1}(D).

The following proposition constructs an encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, whose existence will imply a theory-to-practice gap for the averaging neural operator.

Proposition 3.19
.

Let
\mu
satisfy Assumption
3.1
for
d\in\mathbb{N}
, with bi-orthogonal elements
e^{\ast}_{j}\in L^{1}(D)
. Then there exists an encoder
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{X}(D)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)=\fint_{D}R(u(x),x)\,\;\mathsf{d}x,

(3.19)

with
R:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{D}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
a shallow ReLU neural network,
and constant
c>0
dependent on
d
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\cdot\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d}).

(3.20)

Proof.

It will suffice to show that there exists a neural network
R:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{D}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and encoder of the form (
3.19
), such that for
some
non-empty open set
V_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and constant
c>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\cdot\mathrm{Unif}(V_{0}).

(3.21)

Indeed, given such
V_{0}
, there exists a scaling factor
\gamma>0
and shift
b\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
[0,1]^{d}\subset\gamma\cdot V_{0}+b
. Replacing the neural network
R(\eta,x)
by
\widetilde{R}(\eta,x):=\gamma\cdot R(\eta,x)+b
, it is then immediate that the encoder
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}:\mathcal{X}(D)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(u)=\fint_{D}\widetilde{R}(u(x),x)\;\mathsf{d}x
satisfies a lower bound of the form (
3.20
).

To prove the existence of an encoder
\mathcal{E}
satisfying (
3.21
), we recall that, by Assumption
3.1
,
u\sim\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
is of the form

\displaystyle u(x;y,\xi)=\xi(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}y_{j}e_{j}(x),

where
e_{1},\dots,e_{d}
are linearly independent with bi-orthogonal elements
e^{\ast}_{1},\dots,e^{\ast}_{d}
, and the coefficients
y_{j}\sim\rho_{j}(y)\;\mathsf{d}y
are independent. Furthermore,
\xi\sim\mu_{d}^{\perp}
is a random function such that
e^{\ast}_{1}(\xi)=\dots=e^{\ast}_{d}(\xi)=0
. In the following we will consider
\xi
a random “parameter” and denote the law of
\xi
by
\mathbb{P}:=\mu_{d}^{\perp}
. By assumption the dual elements
e^{\ast}_{j}
are represented by a function in
L^{1}
.

Under our assumptions, there exist non-empty intervals
I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}
and constant
c_{\rho}>0
, such that
\essinf_{I_{j}}\rho_{j}(z)\geq c_{\rho}
for all
j=1,\dots,d
.
We may assume without loss of generality that
I_{j}
is a bounded interval, and fix a constant
c_{V}>0
such that
I_{j}\subset[-c_{V},c_{V}]
for all
j=1,\dots,d
. Let
V=\prod_{j=1}^{d}I_{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We now choose
B>0
, such that
\mathrm{Prob}(\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq B)>0
. This is possible, because of the assumed inclusion
\mathcal{X}\subset L^{\infty}
. For this choice of
B>0
, we define the random event

\mathcal{K}:=\{\|\xi\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq B\},

so that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K})>0
. Note that this bound on
B
implies that for all
y\in V
and
\xi\in K
, we have

\|u({\,\cdot\,};y,\xi)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq B+dc_{V}\max_{j=1,\dots,d}\|e_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}=:B^{\prime}.

For fixed
\xi\in K
, define the maps
F_{\xi}^{\dagger},F_{\xi}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\left\{\;\begin{aligned} F_{\xi}^{\dagger}(y)&:=\left\{\fint_{D}u(x;y,\xi)\,e^{*}_{k}(x)\;\mathsf{d}x\right\}_{k=1}^{d}=y\\
F_{\xi}(y)&:=\fint_{D}\;R\left(u(x;y,\xi),x\right)\;\mathsf{d}x\end{aligned}\right.

(3.22)

where
R
is a ReLU neural network mapping
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{D}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
R^{\dagger}
be defined by
R^{\dagger}(\eta,x)=\{\eta\,e^{*}_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{d}
. Then by Corollary
A.2
, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a ReLU neural network
R
such that

\displaystyle\fint_{D}\|R(\cdot,x)-R^{\dagger}(\cdot,x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}];\mathbb{R}^{d})}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon.

(3.23)

Identify
R
with a ReLU neural network achieving this bound.
Note that
F_{\xi}^{\dagger}\equiv\mathrm{id}
is exactly the identity on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
\xi\in K
. Given the constant
\epsilon_{0}>0
of Lemma
3.10
, We seek to show that for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
in (
3.23
), we can ensure that

\displaystyle\|F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(V)}=\|F_{\xi}-\mathrm{id}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(V)}\leq\epsilon_{0},\quad\forall\,\xi\in K.

(3.24)

By Lemma
3.10
, this entails that there exist
V_{0}\subset V
and
c_{0}>0
, such that

F_{\#}\left(\mathrm{Unif}(Y)\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)\geq c_{0}\mathbb{P}(K)\,\mathrm{Unif}(V_{0}),

where
\mathbb{P}
denotes the law of
\xi
. The claim then follows by observing that

\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu=F_{\#}\left(\mu_{d}\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)\geq c_{\rho}^{d}\,F_{\#}\left(\mathrm{Unif}(Y)\otimes\mathbb{P}\right)\geq c_{\rho}^{d}c_{0}\mathbb{P}(K)\,\mathrm{Unif}(V_{0}).

Since
\mathbb{P}(K)>0
by construction, the claim then follows with constant
c:=c_{\rho}^{d}c_{0}\mathbb{P}(K)
. It therefore remains to show that (
3.23
) for sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
implies (
3.24
).

In the remainder of this proof, we will show that this holds for
\epsilon:=\epsilon_{0}/2d
. By (
3.23
), we have
\|\partial_{\eta}(R-R^{\dagger})(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}<\epsilon
, where
\eta
refers to the first argument of
R
and
R^{\dagger}
. Then for
\xi\in K
and
y,y^{\prime}\in V
, we have

\|u({\,\cdot\,};y,\xi)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)},\;\|u({\,\cdot\,};y,\xi)\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}\leq B^{\prime},

and hence

\displaystyle\|F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}\|R({\,\cdot\,},x)-R^{\dagger}({\,\cdot\,},x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}/2d.

To estimate
\|DF_{\xi}-DF_{\xi}^{\dagger}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}
, we recall that, due to the convexity of the
d
-dimensional cube
V
, the
W^{1,\infty}(V)
seminorm is equal to the Lipschitz seminorm:

\|DF_{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}=\sup_{y,y^{\prime}\in V}\frac{|F_{\xi}(y)-F_{\xi}(y^{\prime})|}{|y-y^{\prime}|}.

We now bound, for
y,y^{\prime}\in V
:

\displaystyle|(F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger})(y)-(F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger})(y^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\left|\fint_{D}(R-R^{\dagger})(u(x;y,\xi),x)-(R-R^{\dagger})(u(x;y^{\prime},\xi),x)\;\mathsf{d}x\right|

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{D}\left|(R-R^{\dagger})(u(x;y,\xi),x)-(R-R^{\dagger})(u(x;y^{\prime},\xi),x)\right|\;\mathsf{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{D}\left|u(x;y,\xi)-u(x;y^{\prime},\xi)\right|\|\partial_{\eta}(R-R^{\dagger})(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}\;\mathsf{d}x

\displaystyle=\fint_{D}\left|\sum_{j=1}^{d}(y_{j}-y_{j}^{\prime})e_{j}(x)\right|\|\partial_{\eta}(R-R^{\dagger})(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}\;\mathsf{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{D}\sum_{j=1}^{d}|y_{j}-y_{j}^{\prime}|\|\partial_{\eta}(R-R^{\dagger})(\cdot,x)\|_{L^{\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}\;\mathsf{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}|y-y^{\prime}|\epsilon.

With our choice of
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}/2d
, this implies that

\displaystyle\|DF_{\xi}-DF^{\dagger}_{\xi}\|_{L^{\infty}(V)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{y,y^{\prime}\in V}\frac{|(F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger})(y)-(F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger})(y^{\prime})|}{|y-y^{\prime}|}\leq\epsilon\sqrt{d}\leq\epsilon_{0}/2.

Combining both estimates, we have shown that

\fint_{D}\|R({\,\cdot\,},x)-R^{\dagger}({\,\cdot\,};x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}([-B^{\prime},B^{\prime}])}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon,

for
\epsilon=\epsilon_{0}/2d
, implies

\|F_{\xi}-F_{\xi}^{\dagger}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(V)}\leq\epsilon_{0}.

This is what we set out to show, and concludes our proof of Proposition
3.19
.
∎

4
Discussion and Conclusion

This work has rigorously examined the theory-to-practice gap in both finite-dimensional and infinite-dimensional settings, resulting in rigorous bounds on achievable convergence rates for general reconstruction methods based on point-values. By deriving upper bounds on the optimal rate
\beta_{\ast}
, we have uncovered the inherent constraints of learning on relevant neural network and neural operator approximation spaces. In the finite-dimensional case, our contributions include a unified treatment of the theory-to-practice gap for approximation errors measured in general
L^{p}
-spaces for arbitrary
p\in[1,\infty]
and dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore, we extend the theory-to-practice gap to infinite-dimensional operator learning frameworks, and derive results for prominent architectures such as Deep Operator Networks and integral kernel-based neural operators, such as the Fourier neural operator (FNO). Notably, for operator learning we establish that the optimal convergence rate in a Bochner
L^{p}
-norm satisfies
\beta_{\ast}\leq 1/p
, while no algebraic convergence is possible (
\beta_{\ast}=0
) for uniform approximation on infinite-dimensional compact input sets. These findings highlight some intrinsic limitations of these data-driven methodologies and provide a clearer understanding of the theoretical bounds shaping practical applications.
In particular, the practical barrier to accurate operator learning models is an information-theoretic barrier rather than a model expressivity barrier. Ultimately, the parameters of any model must be identified from finite data samples, and the present work shows that in the worst case, the sample convergence rate will not match the parametric one. We hypothesize that the sample complexity limitation identified in this work provides an explanation for why sample convergence rates can be seen in practice; e.g.
[
21
]
, while to our knowledge it is rare to match empirical parametric rates to rates obtained via theory in operator learning.

A natural response to the hardness results obtained in this work is what modifications to the problem setting would allow this limitation to be bypassed. In particular, the results of the present work state that in the worst case, no algebraic rate is possible for uniform approximation in operator learning, even in the setting of arbitrarily good parametric rates. As a closing discussion, we attempt to place this result in context of some other relevant results in the literature. In the nonparametric statistics literature, it has been proved that no algebraic rates exist for finite-data regression of Lipschitz functionals on a Hilbert space
[
38
]
. A similar result holds for approximation of Lipschitz operators between Banach spaces; no method based on finite data samples can achieve algebraic sample rates
[
3
,
23
]
. A complementary result to the present work restricts the notion of the parametric space to efficient parametric approximation by a particular architecture (FNO), and finds that for error measured in
L^{2}
, algebraic parametric convergence rates imply algebraic sample convergence rates
[
23
]
. On the other hand, for holomorphic functions, existing work has provided both lower and upper bounds on the algebraic approximation rates that hold in this setting
[
2
]
. While it is in general difficult to directly compare rates results as the particular settings tend to differ, recent work has made an attempt to present existing sample rates for operator learning in a general setting
[
7
]
.

As an alternative to modifying smoothness assumptions on the function space, another direction could be to modify the character of the data. While our results hold for any possible algorithm, deterministic or randomized or adaptive, using finite data samples, we restricted to the setting of point evaluations
f(x_{j})
. An examination of the proofs of Lemma
2.6
and Lemma
2.9
reveals that the situation does not improve if one instead has access to point samples of derivatives because the argument relies on detection of a localized bump function, and higher order derivatives of the localized bump function are still indistinguishable from those of the zero function outside the support of the localized bump function. However, it is an open question whether our lower bounds still hold if the data samples provide some
nonlocal
information about the function. While we expect that they do in general, it is possible that simultaneously restricting the function class to be approximated and elevating the data samples to include nonlocal functionals could yield improved rates. Additionally, while the present work focused on the noiseless setting, if the approximation classes remain noiseless, we expect the lower bounds on sample complexity rates to hold in the setting of noisy data samples. However, it is unclear whether the same would hold true if the approximation classes that determine the parametric complexity were defined with some notion of noise from the onset.

There are several additional interesting avenues for future work, two of which we briefly mention in closing.
One open problem is to study the theory-to-practice gap under additional constraints, e.g. on spaces of the form
\mathsf{\mathbf{A}}^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}\cap\mathrm{Lip}
. We expect that the theory-to-practice gap will persist essentially unchanged even when introducing additional regularity constraints. Another open problem is to extend this gap beyond ReLU activations. This is specifically relevant for operator learning, where popular implementations of e.g. FNO usually use a smooth variant of ReLU, such as GeLU. To date, even in finite dimensions, no theory-to-practice gap is known for such smooth activation functions,
though such results do hold for the ReQu activation
[
1
]
.
Since our proofs rely on the homogeneity of ReLU, the path to such an extension for smooth activations is not immediately obvious.

Appendix A
A result on neural network approximation

We here derive a technical result, which shows that ReLU neural networks can approximate
C^{1}
functions in the
W^{1,\infty}
-norm over compact subsets. This result follows from well-known techniques, but we couldn’t find a reference. We hence include a statement and proof (sketch), below.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\sigma(x)=\max(x,0)
denote the ReLU activation. Let
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. For any
f\in C^{1}(D)
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a shallow ReLU-neural network
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, of the form,

\psi(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}\sigma(w_{j}^{T}x+b_{j}),\quad\text{with }a_{j},b_{j}\in\mathbb{R},\;w_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\text{ for }j=1,\dots,N,

(A.1)

such that
\|\psi-f\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}\leq\epsilon
.

Proof.

Step 1:
Fix a compactly supported smooth function
\rho:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)\subset(-1,1)
,
\rho\geq 0
, and such that
\rho
is even, i.e.
\rho(x)=\rho(-x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
\sigma_{\rho}(x):=(\sigma\ast\rho)(x)
denote the convolution. We note that
\sigma_{\rho}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
is smooth and monotonically increasing and that
\sigma_{\rho}
is equal to ReLU on
\mathbb{R}\setminus(-1,1)
, i.e.

\sigma_{\rho}(x)=\begin{cases}0,&(x\leq-1),\\
x,&(x\geq 1).\end{cases}

We first note that for any
\epsilon>0
, we can find
N\in\mathbb{N}
and coefficients
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j},\omega_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, for
j=1,\dots,N
, such that

\left\|\sigma_{\rho}(x)-\textstyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\sigma(\omega_{j}x+\beta_{j})\right\|_{W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\epsilon.

To see this, we temporarily fix
M\in\mathbb{N}
, introduce an equidistant partition
x_{m}:=-1+2m/M
of
[-1,1]
, for
m=0,\dots,M
, denote
c_{m}:=\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{m})-\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{m-1})
and note that

\displaystyle\left|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}1_{[x_{m},\infty)}(x)\right|=\begin{cases}|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)|,&x\in(-\infty,-1),\\
|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{m_{0}})|,&x\in[x_{m_{0}},x_{m_{0}+1}),\\
|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{M})|,&x\in[1,\infty).\end{cases}

(A.2)

Since
\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)\equiv 0
for
x\leq-1
and
\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)\equiv 1
for
x\geq x_{M}=1
, it follows that

\left|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}1_{[x_{m},\infty)}(x)\right|=0,\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus[-1,1).

On the other hand, we also have

\|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}=\|\sigma^{\prime}\ast\rho^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\sigma^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}.

For any
x\in[-1,1)
, we can find
m_{0}\in\{0,\dots,M-1\}
, such that
x\in[x_{m_{0}},x_{m_{0}+1})
, and from (
A.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}1_{[x_{m},\infty)}(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq|\sigma^{\prime}_{\rho}(x)-\sigma^{\prime}_{\rho}(x_{m_{0}})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\sigma^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}|x-x_{m_{0}}|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}}{M}.

Given
\epsilon>0
, choose
M
sufficiently large so that
2\|\rho^{\prime}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}/M\leq\epsilon/2
. Then, noting that
\sigma^{\prime}(x-x_{m})=1_{[x_{m},\infty)}(x)
pointwise a.e., it follows that

\left\|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma^{\prime}(x-x_{m})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\epsilon/2.

Taking into account that
\sigma_{\rho}(x)\equiv 0\equiv\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma(x-x_{m})
for
x<-1
and
\sigma_{\rho}(x)\equiv x\equiv\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma(x-x_{m})
for
x>1
, this in turn implies that,

\left\|\sigma_{\rho}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma(x-x_{m})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq\int_{-1}^{1}\left\|\sigma_{\rho}^{\prime}(x)-\sum_{m=1}^{M}c_{m}\sigma^{\prime}(x-x_{m})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\,dx^{\prime}\leq\epsilon.

Since
\epsilon
was arbitrary, we have shown that
\sigma_{\rho}
belongs to the
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R})
-closure of the set of shallow
\sigma
-neural networks, in one spatial dimension. In turn, this implies that any shallow
\sigma_{\rho}
-neural network in
d
dimensions can be approximated by a shallow
\sigma
-neural network to any desired accuracy in the
W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
-norm.

Step 2:
Given a compact domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it follows from
[
41
, Theorem 4.1]
and the fact that
\sigma_{\rho}
is smooth and non-polynomial, that the set of shallow
\sigma_{\rho}
-neural networks is dense in
C^{1}(D)
. Thus, for any
f\in C^{1}(D)
and given
\epsilon>0
, we can first find a shallow
\sigma_{\rho}
-neural network
\psi_{\rho}
, such that

\|f-\psi_{\rho}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}=\|f-\psi_{\rho}\|_{C^{1}(D)}\leq\epsilon/2.

Second, as a result of Step 1 we can find a
\sigma
-neural network
\psi
, such that

\|\psi_{\rho}-\psi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}\leq\epsilon/2.

By the triangle inequality, we conclude that, for this
\psi
, we have

\|f-\psi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}\leq\|f-\psi_{\rho}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}+\|\psi_{\rho}-\psi\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D)}\leq\epsilon.

∎

In the following corollary, we weaken the
C^{1}
requirements for Lemma
A.1
for chosen inputs.

Corollary A.2
.

Let
D_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
D_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
be compact domains. Let
f:D_{1}\times D_{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(\eta,x)\mapsto f(\eta,x)
be a measurable function such that
f
is
C^{1}
in
\eta
and integrable in
x
, such that

\int_{D_{2}}\|f({\,\cdot\,},x)\|_{C^{1}(D_{1})}\;\mathsf{d}x<\infty.

The for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a shallow ReLU-neural network
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
of the form
A.1
such that

\int_{D_{2}}\|\psi(\cdot,x)-f(\cdot,x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D_{1})}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon.

(A.3)

Proof.

The assumption says that
f
belongs to
L^{1}_{x}(D_{2};C^{1}_{\eta}(D_{1}))
. Clearly, we have
C^{1}(D_{1}\times D_{2})\subset L^{1}_{x}(D_{2};C^{1}_{\eta}(D_{1}))
. Upon mollifying
f(\eta,x)
in the second variable, one checks that
C^{1}(D_{1}\times D_{2})
is in fact dense in
L^{1}_{x}(D_{2};C^{1}_{\eta}(D_{1}))
. Thus, there exists
f_{\epsilon}\in C^{1}(D_{1}\times D_{2})
, such that

\int_{D_{2}}\|f({\,\cdot\,},x)-f_{\epsilon}({\,\cdot\,},x)\|_{C^{1}(D_{1})}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon/2.

From Lemma
A.1
, there exists a shallow ReLU-neural network
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\|\psi-f_{\epsilon}\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D_{1}\times D_{2})}\leq\epsilon/(2|D_{2}|).

This in turn implies that

\int_{D_{2}}\|\psi(\cdot,x)-f_{\epsilon}(\cdot,x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D_{1})}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon/2.

Combining the above estimates, and using the triangle inequality, we conclude that

\int_{D_{2}}\|\psi({\,\cdot\,},x)-f({\,\cdot\,},x)\|_{W^{1,\infty}(D_{1})}\;\mathsf{d}x\leq\epsilon/2+\epsilon/2=\epsilon.

∎

\displaystyle F_{\#}\mathrm{Unif}(V)

B.2
Proof of Lemma

Proof of Lemma
3.7
.

Before discussing our construction of
A
, we recall that, by definition,
\mathcal{A}:\mathsf{\mathbf{U}}\to L^{p}(\mu)
is of the form

\mathcal{A}(\Psi)=\mathcal{Q}(\Psi(u_{1}),\dots,\Psi(u_{N})),

where
u_{1},\dots,u_{N}
are fixed and
\mathcal{Q}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to L^{p}(\mu)
is a reconstruction from point-values. Given
\psi\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
, we now define
A(\psi)\in L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
by the conditional expectation,

A(\psi)(x):=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\mathcal{A}(\psi\circ\mathcal{E})(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right],\quad\,\forall\,x\in[0,1]^{d},

This conditional expectation is well-defined for
\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu
almost every

. By assumption (
3.3
), we have
\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\cdot\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
, and hence
A(\psi)(x)
is well-defined for (Lebesgue-) almost every

.

We also note that
A(\psi)(x)
is of the form
A(\psi)=Q(\psi(x_{1}),\dots,\psi(x_{N}))
: indeed, by definition, we have
\mathcal{A}(\psi\circ\mathcal{E})(u)=\mathcal{Q}(\psi(\mathcal{E}(u_{1})),\dots,\psi(\mathcal{E}(u_{N})))(u)
. Hence, upon defining

, and

Q(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(x):=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\mathcal{Q}(y_{1},\dots,y_{N})(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right],

we then have
A(\psi)=Q(\psi(x_{1}),\dots,\psi(x_{n}))
for all

.

To simplify notation for the following calculations, we define
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathcal{A}(\Psi)
with
\Psi:=\psi\circ\mathcal{E}
. We can then write

A(\psi)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{u}[\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x].

Here
\mathbb{E}_{u}[\ldots\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x]
is the conditional expectation over a random variable
u\sim\mu
, with conditioning on
\mathcal{E}(u)=x
.

For

, we then have

\displaystyle|A(\psi)(x)|^{p}

\displaystyle=\big|\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right]\big|^{p}\leq\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[|\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)|^{p}\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right],

(B.1)

by conditional Jensen’s inequality. It follows that

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|A(\psi)(x)|^{p}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq c^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|A(\psi)(x)|^{p}\,\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=c^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu}\Big[|A(\psi)(x)|^{p}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq c^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu}\left[\mathbb{E}_{u}\Big[\left|\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\right|^{p}\,\Big|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\Big]\right]

\displaystyle=c^{-1}\,\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\Big[\left|\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\right|^{p}\Big].

The first inequality is by assumption
\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu\geq c\cdot\mathrm{Unif}([0,1]^{d})
, the second inequality on the third row is (
B.1
) above. The final equality follows from basic properties of the conditional expectation. Thus, recalling that
\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}(\Psi)\in L^{p}(\mu)
, it follows that

\int_{[0,1]^{d}}|A(\psi)(x)|^{p}\,dx\leq c^{-1}\,\|\mathcal{A}(\Psi)\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}^{p}<\infty.

This shows that
A:U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}\to L^{p}([0,1]^{d})
is well-defined.

It remains to show that

satisfies the claimed lower bound (
3.6
). To see this, we once more apply conditional Jensen’s inequality, to obtain

\displaystyle\|\Psi-\mathcal{A}(\Psi)\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}^{p}

\displaystyle=\|\Psi-\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}^{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u\sim\mu}\left[\left|\psi(\mathcal{E}(u))-\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu}\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[\Big|\psi(\mathcal{E}(u))-\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\Big|^{p}\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu}\left[\Big|\psi(x)-\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[\Psi_{\mathcal{A}}(u)\,|\,\mathcal{E}(u)=x\right]\Big|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu}\left[\left|\psi(x)-A(\psi)(x)\right|^{p}\right]

\displaystyle=\|\psi-A(\psi)\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{E}_{\#}\mu)}^{p}.

Recalling (
3.3
), this implies that

\|\Psi-\mathcal{A}(\Psi)\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}^{p}\geq c\|\psi-A(\psi)\|_{L^{p}([0,1]^{d})}^{p}.

Since
\psi\in U^{\alpha,\infty}_{\mathbf{\ell}}
was arbitrary and
\Psi=\psi\circ\mathcal{E}
, the proof of (
3.6
) is complete.
∎
