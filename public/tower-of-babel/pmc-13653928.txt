Time, gravity, and the relativity of tumor position in free-breathing lung stereotactic body radiation therapy
Authors: Paul Retif, Barbara Knäusl, William H. Nailon
Published: 2026

Stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) has transformed the treatment of early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and continues to become more precise as imaging and dose-delivery techniques evolve, supporting high rates of local control [1], [2]. Advanced motion-management strategies, such as respiratory gating or real-time tracking decrease the dose to the surrounding normal tissue but may increase treatment time and, depending on the technique, require fiducial marker placement. Consequently, many patients are still treated in free breathing in combination with an internal target volume concept because of the inherent simplicity of this approach [3], [4]. However, despite decades of lung SBRT, important lessons remain as Merring-Mikkelsen et al. recently showed that respiratory motion is not the only time-dependent geometric uncertainty in free-breathing lung SBRT: “gravity” may also influence tumor position [5].

In a large, homogeneous single-institution cohort of 97 patients (102 peripheral lung tumors), all treated with 67.5 Gy in three fractions, the authors implemented a two-step strategy. First, during online cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) matching, a pre-treatment vertical tumor-to-bone offset exceeding 5 mm prompted an additional CBCT after a 10 min waiting period to allow a potential gravity-induced shift to stabilize. Second, during offline analysis, a ≥3 mm change between pre- and post-treatment CBCTs triggered a longer waiting period (up to 20 min) before imaging in subsequent fractions. Treatment-log timestamps and serial tumor positions were used to estimate patient-specific drift rates, identifying 16 cases (15.7%) projected to exceed the 4 mm posterior planning target volume margin within 20 min, and for which worst-case dose recalculation showed a decrease in mean gross tumor volume D90% from 99.9% to 74.2%. It is worth noting that the delivery used several static open fields distributed as an arc over the ipsilateral hemithorax, therefore extrapolation to volumetric modulated arc therapy workflows would require caution. Importantly, the authors systematically evaluated multiple patient- and tumor-related characteristics, yet none predicted clinically relevant drift.

Consistent with earlier reports, this study reported a time-dependent posterior displacement, the exact mechanism of which remains unclear. Besides gravity, patient relaxation, respiratory-baseline changes, tissue deformation, and CBCT temporal averaging may be contributing factors [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Treatment time should therefore be considered a geometric quality-assurance variable: here, reducing the treatment time from 20 to 10 min lowered the modeled proportion exceeding the margin from 15.7% to 1.0%. As current SBRT guidance does not explicitly address treatment time or systematic intra-fractional tumor-shift testing, standardized imaging intervals and timestamps, time-to-delivery and posterior-drift checks, as well as predefined escalation strategies when drift is detected, should be considered [1], [12], [13], [14]. Post-treatment CBCT may guide subsequent fractions but cannot prevent within-fraction underdosage. Because systematic analysis did not identify any reliable predictor, these safeguards should initially apply to all patients rather than selected subgroups. Prospective multicenter studies should define optimal timing thresholds and determine whether these workflow changes improve delivered dose and clinical outcomes.

Modern radiotherapy increasingly relies on sub-millimetric precision, but geometric accuracy must extend to intra-fraction monitoring. In this context, whatever its mechanism, posterior tumor drift is no longer merely an observation but an actionable quality assurance problem.
