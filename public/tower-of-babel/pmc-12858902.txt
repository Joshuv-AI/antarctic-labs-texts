External multicenter validation of the eSAH score for predicting outcomes after subarachnoid hemorrhage
Authors: Natália Vasconcellos de Oliveira Souza, Rohan Sharma, Otavio Frederico de Toledo, Ingrid Pereira Marques, Salvador F. Gutierrez-Aguirre, Lara-Velazquez Montserrat, Eric Sauvageau, Naval Neeraj, Amin Aghaebrahim, Feres Chaddad Neto, Ricardo A. Hanel, Gisele Sampaio Silva, William David Freeman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Reliable assessment of prognosis after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) is essential to inform clinical decision-making and prevent premature assumptions of poor outcome. The enhanced SAH (eSAH) score combines three routinely available variables—age, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and subarachnoid hemorrhage blood volume (SAHV)—to provide an objective framework for early outcome stratification. SAHV is estimated using the ABC/2-derived ellipsoid method applied across five major cisternal compartments on non-contrast CT. While the eSAH score has shown promise in initial derivation studies, its external performance and calibration have not yet been validated across diverse healthcare settings. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using consecutive data from two high-volume tertiary centers: one in a high-income country (the United States) and one in a middle-income country (Brazil). Non-aneurysmal SAH cases were excluded. The eSAH score was validated to predict discharge functional outcomes (modified Rankin Scale, mRS) and mortality, using discrimination (area under the ROC curve, AUC) and calibration metrics and was compared to the World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) score. A total of 472 patients were included. The eSAH score demonstrated moderate discriminative performance, with AUCs of 0.78 for unfavorable functional outcome and 0.799 for in-hospital mortality. Each one-point increase in the score was associated with a 2.14-fold increase in the odds of poor outcome (95% CI, 1.84–2.50; p < 0.0001) and a 2.14-fold increase in the odds of mortality (95% CI, 1.78–2.57; p < 0.001). The WFNS score yielded slightly lower AUCs (0.76 and 0.75, respectively). The eSAH score provides a simple, generalizable framework for early prognostication after aneurysmal SAH, performing consistently across two markedly different healthcare systems. Although its discriminative power remains moderate, its objective structure and reliance on measurable imaging features make it ideally suited for integration with artificial intelligence to enable automated, precise, and scalable SAHV quantification. Such integration may substantially enhance prognostic accuracy and support more equitable, data-driven neurocritical care worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34326-3.

Introduction

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) accounts for only 5% of all strokes but disproportionately affects younger individuals, particularly women around the age of 50, and remains a leading cause of long-term disability and socioeconomic burden1. Despite advances in risk-factor control2,3, diagnostic imaging4, treatment5, and neurocritical care6 that have led to reductions in both incidence and case fatality6,7, reliable prognostication after SAH remains a major challenge8.

While no individual variable can reliably predict outcome, accurate and standardized prognostication remains crucial for informed clinical decision-making and for preventing premature assumptions of poor prognosis9,10. The enhanced SAH (eSAH) score was developed to improve outcome prediction at hospital discharge by incorporating three easily imputed variables: age, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), and subarachnoid hemorrhage blood volume (SAHV). In the original development cohort, the eSAH score demonstrated excellent discriminative performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.89 for unfavorable discharge outcomes and 0.88 for in-hospital mortality11. However, it has not yet been externally validated across diverse populations and healthcare settings.

Numerous prognostic models have been proposed, but many suffer from limited methodological rigor or lack external validation8. Consistent predictors of poor outcomes include advanced age, initial clinical severity12, and SAHV on non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT)13,14, which correlates with mortality risk15, vasospasm, and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), a complication affecting around 30% of SAH patients and a major contributor to cortical infarction and poor neurological outcomes16. Furthermore, endovascular treatment has been associated with a reduced risk of death and dependency5,17 and is often included in prognostic models alongside variables such as World Federation of Neurological Surgeons (WFNS) grade, blood burden (e.g., modified Fisher scale, mFs), and aneurysm size8. Notably, aneurysm size may itself correlate with the hemorrhage volume, suggesting possible collinearity among these predictors18.

The modified Fisher scale (mFs)14, originally designed to predict delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), provides a visual and semi-quantitative estimate of subarachnoid blood burden, which itself has been correlated with mortality and poor clinical outcomes15.In contrast, models incorporating quantitative SAHV, especially using machine learning (ML), have demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting DCI and clinical outcomes19,20. The eSAH score estimates SAHV using the ellipsoid ABC/2 method, originally described for intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) quantification21, and subsequently adapted for SAH assessment, where A represents width, B is length, and C is vertical extent. Applied across five major brain cisterns on noncontrast CT, this method yields an eSAH score ranging from 0 to 5 (Table 1) and closely approximates gold-standard manual segmentation while being faster and less resource-intensive22.

This study aims to externally validate the eSAH score for predicting in-hospital mortality and discharge functional outcomes in aneurysmal SAH patients. Using a multicenter cohort from a high-income (United States) and a middle-income country (Brazil), we hypothesize that the score maintains predictive performance across diverse settings and offers a practical tool for resource-constrained settings.

Methods

Study population and data collection

This external validation cohort included adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with aneurysmal SAH at two high-volume academic institutions: one in São Paulo, Brazil, and the other in Jacksonville, United States. The Brazilian cohort was retrospectively collected from 2016 to 2024, while the U.S. cohort was prospectively registered between 2014 and 2024 using a REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) database dedicated to endovascular procedures (Fig. 1). Both centers independently manage over 45 cases of aneurysmal SAH annually; however, case volumes declined during the COVID-19 pandemic, averaging approximately 35 cases per year per site during the study period23. Importantly, neither center was involved in the development or internal validation of the original eSAH score, ensuring the independence of this external validation effort.

Study flowchart.

The Brazilian site is a publicly funded, non-stroke-certified teaching hospital, whereas the U.S. site is a privately funded, Joint Commission-certified comprehensive stroke center.

The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. This multicenter validation used de-identified retrospective data. Institutional Review Board/Ethics Committee approval was obtained at all participating sites, with a waiver of informed consent in accordance with applicable regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study used de-identified retrospective data from the Baptist Intracranial Aneurysm Registry and participating centers. IRB approval was obtained from the Baptist Health Institutional Review Board (IRB #24 − 023; Expedited Continuing Review Approval, approved May 20, 2025; valid through May 19, 2026) and from the Research Ethics Committee of the Universidade Federal de São Paulo (CAAE: 26768619.8.0000.5505). As per both approvals, the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and anonymized nature of the study.

Eligibility criteria

Patients were included if they had a diagnosis of aneurysmal SAH confirmed by NCCT or lumbar puncture, with aneurysm verification through CT angiography (CTA), MR angiography (MRA), or digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Exclusion criteria were traumatic SAH or non-aneurysmal etiologies, lack of NCCT images within 5 days of ictus, or CT slice thickness greater than 5 mm when reconstruction to thinner slices was not possible. A flow diagram outlining inclusion is provided in Fig. 1.

Data sources and variables

Clinical and radiographic data were obtained from electronic medical records and institutional prospective databases by trained local teams. Variables included demographics (sex, age), SAH severity (GCS and WFNS), and medical history (hypertension, past or active smoking, excessive alcohol use). Excessive alcohol use was defined as > 8 drinks/week for women or > 15 drinks/week for men. Radiological data included mFs14 and aneurysm location. Treatment was categorized as surgical, endovascular, or conservative/clinical management (e.g., patients who died before aneurysm repair). In the U.S. cohort, all patients underwent endovascular treatment due to the database source. Admission CT scans were evaluated locally by trained personnel.

eSAH Score, variable Selection, and imaging assessment

The eSAH score (Table 1), as previously described11, incorporates age, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, and SAHV. SAHV was calculated using the ABC/2 method in three dimensions: A = width at the thickest portion in cross-section; B = length along the long axis of the hemorrhage; and C = vertical extent determined by the number of CT slices (Fig. 2). For this calculation, only cisternal blood was considered, excluding intraparenchymal and subdural components. This method approximates the star-shaped distribution of hemorrhage in the basal cisterns using subsegmental ellipsoid volumes22. Only CT scans with a slice thickness ≤ 5 mm were included for volumetric analysis. To reduce measurement bias across centers, regular training sessions were conducted by the principal investigator to standardize SAHV assessments.

Outcomes

Functional outcomes were assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at baseline, hospital discharge, and the last available follow-up. The latter was defined as the most recent documented in-person or telehealth clinical evaluation. Consistent with the original eSAH publication, the mRS at discharge was used for score validation. A favorable outcome was defined as mRS 0–3 and an unfavorable outcome as mRS > 3.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using skewness, kurtosis, and graphs. Normally distributed data are reported as means ± standard deviations and compared using independent t-tests; non-normal data as medians with interquartile ranges and compared with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation24; however, the main outcome variables were not imputed to avoid introducing bias or inflating statistical significance, as imputing outcomes can compromise the validity of effect estimates (see Supplement Content for additional information regarding multiple imputation).

The outcome prediction model was developed following Sharma et al.’s methodology, which employed multivariable logistic regression with predictors available at initial presentation11: sex, age, SAHV, GCS, mFs, WFNS grade, and presence of intraparenchymal or intraventricular hemorrhage. GCS cutoffs were literature-based25–27; age cutoffs were derived from mean differences between favorable and unfavorable outcome groups, rounded to the nearest decade. SAHV cutoffs were derived from a previously reported analysis using Youden’s index22.

Performance of eSAH and WFNS scores was assessed by area under the receiver operator characteristic curve (AUC) for discrimination28, calibration slope, and Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A calibration slope of 1 indicates perfect calibration; <1 indicates overfitting. AUCs: 0.5 = no discrimination; >0.7 = acceptable; >0.8 = good; >0.9 = excellent; 1 = perfect29. AUCs were compared using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves, which evaluates whether the difference between two AUCs derived from the same sample is statistically significant.

Calibration was assessed by the calibration slope and intercept, as well as the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. In addition, a mixed-effects logistic regression model with site as a random intercept was used in sensitivity analyses to account for potential clustering effects by center. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed with SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, 2025) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, 2025).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of 472 patients included, 302 were from Jacksonville, USA, and 170 from São Paulo, Brazil (Fig. 1). The mean age was 55.9 ± 16.0 years, and 73.9% were female. Baseline characteristics across centers are detailed in Table 2. Hypertension was more prevalent in the U.S. cohort (OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.1–2.5; p < 0.001), whereas smoking was more common among patients in the Brazilian cohort (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.1–2.1; p = 0.0203). Most patients presented with a good clinical grade, with 59.3% having a WFNS score of 1–3. The proportion of patients with an mFs of 3–4 was similar between the two groups (p = 0.9937). Median blood volume was 6.7 mL (IQR 0.6–29.7) for good clinical outcomes (mRS 0–3), and 24.4 mL (6.7–55.0) for poor clinical outcomes (mRS > 3), mean difference 9.2 mL, CI 95% 4.7–15, p < 0.0001.

Variables associated with poor clinical outcomes (mRS > 3) and mortality

Age consistently emerged as a predictor of both poor clinical outcomes (mean difference = 8.7 years, 95% CI: 6–11; p < 0.0001) and mortality (mean difference = 5.9 years, 95% CI: 2–9; p = 0.0016) across both cohorts. Higher WFNS and mFs scores were strongly associated with both poor outcomes and mortality (p < 0.0001 for both). Increased total SAHV (mean difference = 9 mL, 95% CI: 4.7–15) and cisternal blood volume (mean difference = 7.3 mL, 95% CI: 3.9–13) were also significantly associated with poor outcomes and mortality (p < 0.0001 for all). Intraparenchymal and subdural hemorrhages showed a non-significant trend toward association with poor outcomes (p = 0.10), but were not associated with mortality (p = 0.1456). The mean differences between groups were minimal and did not permit calculation of a reliable 95% CI.

Posterior circulation aneurysms demonstrated a trend toward association with poor functional outcomes (OR = 1.5, 95% CI: 0.9–2.4; p = 0.0902), but not with mortality (p = 0.0946). Hypertension was significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes in the U.S. cohort (OR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.3–3.5; p = 0.0048), but not in the Brazilian cohort (p = 0.5471). Neither smoking nor alcohol use was associated with poor outcomes or mortality. Hypertension was not a significant predictor of mortality in either cohort (p = 0.6098). (Table 3)

Outcomes

Discharge disposition varied significantly between groups. In Jacksonville, 42.1% of patients were transferred to rehabilitation or nursing facilities, whereas no patients in São Paulo were discharged to such settings (p < 0.0001). Hospital length of stay was similar between cohorts (mean 20.9 ± 21 days). In-hospital mortality was significantly higher in São Paulo (34.7%) compared to Jacksonville (9.9%) (OR = 4.8, 95% CI 2.9–7.9, p < 0.0001). Long-term follow-up data (median 12 months, IQR 6–28) were available for 98.3% of the Brazilian cohort and 78.5% of the U.S. cohort. At last follow-up, mortality remained higher in São Paulo (35.3%) versus Jacksonville (8.9%) (p < 0.0001). Additionally, a greater proportion of Jacksonville patients achieved a favorable functional outcome (mRS 0–3) at follow-up compared to those in São Paulo (71.7% vs. 58.7%; p < 0.0001). (Table 2).

eSAH external validation

The eSAH score demonstrated good predictive performance for clinical outcomes and mortality, with an AUC of 0.78 and 0.799, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). Each one-point increase in the score was associated with a 2.14-fold increase in the odds of poor outcome (95% CI, 1.84–2.50; p < 0.0001) and a 2.14-fold increase in the odds of mortality (95% CI, 1.78–2.57; p < 0.001).

To benchmark this new model, we evaluated the performance of the WFNS score in the same cohort. The WFNS score yielded an AUC of 0.76 for unfavorable outcome and 0.75 for mortality (Fig. 3C and D). The eSAH score showed numerically higher discrimination, although the differences were not statistically significant according to the DeLong test (p = 0.15 for unfavorable outcome; p = 0.21 for mortality).

Discussion

In this multicenter external validation cohort, the eSAH score demonstrated moderate discriminative performance across diverse clinical settings. The predictive accuracy and excellent calibration, reflected by an AUC of 0.78 for unfavorable functional outcomes and 0.799 for in-hospital mortality, reaffirm the utility of this score, especially given its simplicity and ease of use. These findings underscore the eSAH score’s capability to offer valuable prognostic information at the point of care, potentially enabling more informed clinical decisions, optimized resource allocation, and enhanced patient-family communication10,30–32. Importantly, mixed-effects logistic regression confirmed that the predictive association of the eSAH score with both outcomes remained robust after accounting for clustering by site. No significant interaction between site and score was detected, suggesting consistent performance across the U.S. and Brazilian cohorts.

A critical strength of this study lies in its inclusion of cohorts from distinctly different healthcare contexts: high-resource (United States) and resource-constrained (Brazil) settings. The disparity in clinical management observed, such as the predominant use of endovascular treatment in the U.S. versus surgical clipping in Brazil, as well as significantly different median treatment times (3.7 h vs. 48 h), emphasizes the score’s resilience and adaptability to varied clinical practices and healthcare infrastructures. Importantly, despite these considerable variations in practice patterns and resource availability, the eSAH score maintained consistent performance and calibration, reinforcing its applicability globally, including regions with limited access to advanced diagnostic tools or specialized neurocritical care resources.

An ideal clinical prognostic scale should be easy to apply, yield consistent results across observers, and demonstrate clear stratification of outcome risk across score categories27. The eSAH score’s reliance on objective, easy, and routinely obtained clinical data (age and Glasgow Coma Scale) represents another pivotal strength. Compared to conventional grading scales such as Hunt-Hess33 which can introduce subjective interpretation biases (e.g., “drowsy,” “stupor”), the eSAH score’s quantifiable components reduce interobserver variability and promote reproducibility12,25.

While the WFNS scale remains popular due to its widespread familiarity and use of the GCS34, the eSAH score outperformed the WFNS in predicting both unfavorable outcomes (AUC 0.78 vs. 0.76) and mortality (AUC 0.79 vs. 0.75), underscoring the advantage of objective measurements like GCS35 and SAHV15. However, these differences did not reach statistical significance when formally tested using the DeLong method, indicating comparable discriminative ability between the two scores in this external validation cohort. Noteworthy, the prognostic accuracy of the WFNS score has been questioned. Many patients initially classified as WFNS grade I ultimately experience poor outcomes36, while others categorized as grade IV or V may recover well and be discharged with favorable outcomes37, highlighting the presence of “false good” and “false poor” grades.

Central to the enhanced prognostic accuracy of the eSAH score is the incorporation of quantitative SAHV measurement38 using the ABC/2-derived ellipsoid formula22. Unlike the mFs14, which provides only semi-quantitative assessment, the ABC/2 method facilitates rapid and reliable estimation of cisternal SAHV, aligning closely with gold-standard manual segmentation methods while remaining practical for emergency clinical application22. Interrater variability with this approach remains a potential limitation and was not accounted for in this cohort, however, our experience indicates that measurement proficiency can typically be achieved quickly (after approximately five training cases). Moreover, the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of this method make it particularly attractive in resource-limited settings11.

Nonetheless, while the eSAH score demonstrated consistent performance across heterogeneous healthcare systems, its discriminative ability remains moderate, indicating that it should not yet be considered a robust standalone prognostic tool. Rather, its true value lies in its simplicity, transparency, and potential for technological integration. The score’s quantitative nature and reliance on objective imaging metrics make it particularly amenable to automation through artificial intelligence (AI). Automated SAHV quantification could markedly enhance precision, reproducibility, and clinical utility, transforming the eSAH from a pragmatic clinical score into a scalable, data-driven prognostic framework applicable across global healthcare settings39,40. Although initial costs may present barriers, particularly in lower-resource environments, the long-term economic and clinical benefits, such as reduced misclassification risk, optimized resource allocation, and improved patient outcomes, justify further exploration and development of these advanced computational tools41–43.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of part of our cohort may have introduced biases related to data completeness and recording quality. Second, the manual calculation of SAHV is inherently subject to variability. Although raters underwent standardized training, potential sources of inconsistency remain, including CT slice thickness, gantry angle, and imaging artifacts such as patient motion, vascular calcifications, or indwelling implants. Interobserver reliability was not formally assessed in this study. Although consistent proficiency with the ABC/2-derived ellipsoid method is typically achieved after limited practice, its subjective nature represents a methodological limitation and requires improvement. Third, exclusions due to missing baseline imaging in the Brazilian cohort might have introduced selection bias, although included and excluded patients did not differ significantly in baseline characteristics. Finally, the marked differences between cohorts —in treatment modality, treatment delays, and cultural practices regarding withdrawal of care— inevitably introduce heterogeneity that may influence outcomes beyond the eSAH score itself.

In accordance with recent Neurocritical Care Society recommendations, which caution against relying on any single predictor for definitive prognostication yet emphasize the value of structured and reproducible tools, the eSAH score should be interpreted as a complementary aid to clinical judgment rather than a stand-alone determinant of outcome44,45. Despite inherent variability in SAHV quantification, the score demonstrated consistent and moderate discriminative performance across two highly distinct healthcare systems.

These contrasting contexts—Brazil’s publicly funded system and Florida’s high-resource private setting—highlight the real-world diversity in infrastructure, access, and care pathways. Longer treatment delays and the predominance of surgical clipping in Brazil likely reflect systemic limitations such as restricted 24/7 endovascular availability and delayed intensive care access, whereas the Florida cohort benefited from immediate neurointerventional services and structured rehabilitation pathways. Yet, the eSAH score maintained stable calibration and performance in both environments, reinforcing its applicability as a standardized and objective framework for early outcome assessment across variable healthcare settings.

Finally, its design, grounded in simple, quantitative, and reproducible variables, positions it well for future integration with AI–driven systems for automated SAHV measurement46. Such integration could substantially enhance precision, reproducibility, and clinical applicability, bridging the gap between advanced neuroimaging analytics and real-world bedside decision-making.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the eSAH score provides a simple, generalizable, and clinically practical framework for outcome prediction after aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage. Although its current discriminative power is moderate, its design, grounded in objective variables, makes it uniquely suited for future integration with AI systems for automated SAHV measurement, a step consistent with current NCS recommendations for standardized, data-driven prognostic assessment. Such integration could substantially improve prognostic accuracy, standardize risk assessment, and bridge the gap between advanced neuroimaging analytics and real-world bedside decision-making.
