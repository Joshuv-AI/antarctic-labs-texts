Characterization of motor unit activities during isometric elbow flexion with different speeds
Authors: Chen Chen, Xiaodong Liu, Fang Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe neural mechanisms regulating muscle activity exhibit variations under different contraction levels and speeds. This study aims to systematically characterize motor unit (MU) behavior during isometric elbow flexion tasks performed at different force levels and contraction speeds, using high-density surface electromyography (HDsEMG) decomposition.MethodsHDsEMG signals were recorded from the biceps and triceps brachii muscles of 61 participants during force-tracking tasks at four force levels (10%, 30%, 50%, and 70% of maximum voluntary contraction, MVC) and three contraction speeds (5%, 10%, and 20%MVC/s). A convolution kernel compensation algorithm was used to decompose the sEMG signals into MU spike trains. Motor unit features were extracted and analyzed, including recruitment thresholds, discharge rates, action potential amplitude, common drive measures, and force-tracking performance.Main resultsAcross varying contraction levels and speeds, the number of identified MUs from each condition ranged from 9 to 15 for the biceps brachii and 3 to 9 MUs for triceps brachii. Motor unit recruitment and action potential amplitudes increased with force, consistent with the size principle. Faster contractions led to higher discharge rates at recruitment and derecruitment, greater common drive (as indicated by principal component analysis), and higher force-tracking error. These adaptations are more pronounced in the biceps brachii, which was the primary muscle involved in the contraction.Conclusion and SignificanceMotor unit behavior is modulated by contraction force and speed, with faster contractions eliciting higher discharge rates and common drive. These findings underscore the neuromechanical adaptability of the motor unit system and have implications for motor function assessment, rehabilitation, and the development of myoelectric interfaces.

Introduction

Human voluntary movement is orchestrated by the central nervous system (CNS) through the precise activation of motor units–the fundamental units of neuromuscular control. Each motor unit (MU) consists of a single alpha motor neuron and the muscle fibers it innervates [1]. During movement, motor units are recruited and modulated in their firing rates to generate muscle force, enabling a wide range of motor behaviors, from fine manipulation to powerful exertion [2]. Understanding how motor units behave under different neuromechanical demands is essential to uncovering the principles of motor control and to informing neurorehabilitation and neuroprosthetic design [3]. Motor unit behavior is shaped not only by the magnitude of force output but also by the temporal characteristics of the contraction [4, 5]. Motor units are typically recruited in a size-dependent order, with smaller, fatigue-resistant units activated first, a phenomenon known as the size principle [6]. As force increases, larger and more fatigable units are recruited. Discharge rates also tend to increase with force, up to a certain limit, after which additional force is primarily achieved via further recruitment [7, 8]. This relationship has been well characterized in isometric contractions, where force is held steady or gradually modulated [9].

However, motor behaviors in daily life often require generating force quickly and under time pressure. In such conditions, the CNS must adapt its recruitment strategy to meet temporal demands, often relying on rapid recruitment of higher-threshold units and modulation of discharge rates [10]. Recent investigations have demonstrated that the rate of force development exerts a substantial influence on MU recruitment thresholds, discharge rates, and synchronization patterns. Typically, during slow voluntary contractions in which torque or force is generated at relatively low rates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le $$\end{document}10% maximum voluntary contraction (MVC)/second), MU activation is characterized by a progressive and gradual increase in both recruitment and discharge rates [11, 12]. In contrast, during rapid (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge $$\end{document}20%MVC/second) or explosive contractions, MU activation patterns shift toward rapid recruitment and elevated discharge rates [13–16]. This modulation of MU behavior in response to varying contraction rates has been consistently documented in young adults across several muscles, including the tibialis anterior [14], masseter [17], soleus [17], gastrocnemius [18], and vastus lateralis [15].

Despite these insights, comprehensive analyses of how motor unit behavior varies systematically across both force levels and contraction speeds are still limited. Prior studies mainly focused on a single contraction condition [19, 20]. Moreover, sample sizes have often been small, limiting the generalizability of the findings. There is also a lack of studies that quantify how contraction speed modulates motor unit recruitment, firing dynamics, and the common drive [21], particularly in a unified experimental framework. Advances in high-density surface electromyography (HDsEMG) and signal decomposition algorithms, such as convolution kernel compensation (CKC), now allow for the noninvasive identification of individual motor unit spike trains in vivo [22–24]. This technique enables reliable tracking of motor unit activity during voluntary contractions, providing access to discharge patterns, recruitment order, motor unit action potentials (MUAPs), and temporal synchronization across different task conditions [25–27]. These developments open new possibilities for analyzing how the neuromuscular system adapts to dynamic motor demands at the level of individual motor neurons.

In this study, we leveraged HDsEMG decomposition to investigate motor unit behavior in a large cohort of participants performing isometric elbow flexion at multiple force levels (10–70%MVC) and contraction speeds (5–20%MVC/s). By simultaneously recording from the biceps and triceps brachii, we analyzed a comprehensive set of motor unit features, including recruitment thresholds, discharge rates, MUAP amplitudes, common drive measures, and force-tracking performance. Our aims were: (1) to quantify how contraction speed influences motor unit recruitment and discharge behavior across different force levels, (2) to evaluate the common drive of neural input under varying task demands, and (3) to examine differences between task-relevant (agonist) and less-involved (antagonist) muscles. The findings provide new insights into the temporal adaptability of motor unit control and offer a foundation for applications in neuromuscular diagnostics, rehabilitation, and intuitive myoelectric control.

Methods

Subjects

61 participants (34 males and 27 females), aged between 8 and 80 years (20 subjects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le $$\end{document} 18 years old and 21 subjects >60 years old), were recruited for this study. All individuals were free from neurological disorders and provided written informed consent prior to participation. The consent of minors was obtained from their legal guardians. The study protocol, including the informed consent procedure, was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the local ethics committee of Shanghai Jiao Tong University (approval number E20240248I).

Experiments

The experiment required participants to perform isometric elbow flexion tasks while following a force trajectory at different rates (Fig. 1). Experimental protocol

Before data collection, the support structure and sensor placement were adjusted to optimize relaxation conditions. Furthermore, we monitored the HDsEMG signals to verify the absence of detectable muscle activity during non-task periods. Each participant was required to perform force-tracking tasks at four contraction levels, each executed at three different rates of force development. Prior to the main experiment, the MVC for elbow flexion was assessed. Participants were instructed to perform maximal elbow flexion efforts three times, with a one-minute rest between trials. The average of the three trials was used as the MVC value.

Subsequently, participants were asked to follow a trapezoidal force trajectory by controlling their elbow flexion force. Each trapezoidal trajectory consisted of three phases: a rising phase, during which force increased linearly from rest to the target level; a plateau phase, during which the target force was maintained steadily; and a falling phase, during which force decreased linearly back to rest. The trapezoidal trajectories were defined by four contraction levels (10%, 30%, 50%, and 70%MVC) and three rates of force development (S1: 5%MVC/s, S2: 10%MVC/s, and S3: 20%MVC/s). Figure 1B illustrates the 12 trapezoidal force trajectories that participants were required to follow. For each trajectory, participants performed two repeated trials. Each trial lasted between 16 and 34 seconds. Throughout the experiment, both the target force trajectory and the participant’s generated force were displayed in real-time for visual feedback.

Force signal recording

During the experiment, participants were comfortably seated facing a custom-designed apparatus that secured both the force sensor and the participant’s arm. A hand grip dynamometer (Biometrics Ltd, UK) was firmly mounted on a fixed platform (Fig. 1A). Participants extended their dominant arm forward, maintaining an approximately 90-degree angle at the elbow, with the midpoint of the forearm consistently positioned against the force sensor. To minimize undesired movements, the elbow was stabilized with a strap, and participants were instructed to primarily activate the upper arm muscles during force generation.

A graphical user interface developed in MATLAB enabled real-time data acquisition from the force sensor via a serial connection and provided continuous visual feedback to participants. The force sensor operated at a sampling rate of 200 Hz.

High-density sEMG recording

HDsEMG signals were recorded using two 64-channel electrode arrays (8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times $$\end{document}8 configuration; Grid8X8P-8mm, Onesense, China), with each electrode having a diameter of 3 mm and an inter-electrode spacing of 8 mm in both the horizontal and vertical directions (Fig. 1A). The arrays were positioned to cover the muscle bellies of the biceps brachii (grid 1) and triceps brachii (grid 2). Each array was connected to a multi-channel acquisition module that wirelessly transmitted the data via Wi-Fi to a base station interfaced with a laptop (Oct-HD, Onesense, China).

The HDsEMG signals were acquired in monopolar mode with a gain of 1,000 and sampled at 2,000 Hz. Signals were hardware bandpass filtered between 3 Hz and 900 Hz and digitized using a 24-bit analog-to-digital converter. Real-time signal visualization and recording were performed using the Oct-HD PC Client software, and all data were stored for subsequent offline analysis. A custom synchronization module based on an Arduino microcontroller was developed to synchronize the force and sEMG recordings. At the beginning and end of each force acquisition trial, the microcontroller generated trigger signals that were sent to the HDsEMG base station to ensure precise temporal alignment.

Preprocessing

The sEMG signals were bandpass filtered using a 4th-order Butterworth filter with 20 Hz and 500 Hz cutoff frequencies. To further suppress powerline interference, a 50 Hz comb filter was applied. Channels exhibiting abnormal amplitudes–typically fewer than five and likely resulting from poor electrode-skin contact–were identified and excluded from subsequent analysis. For the force signals, the data were first up-sampled to 2,000 Hz to align with the sEMG sampling rate and then low-pass filtered at 2.5 Hz to reduce high-frequency noise.

MUAP extraction and matching

After decomposing each trial individually, the separation vectors from the two trials were combined. Using these combined vectors, both trials were re-decomposed according to Equation (4). Based on the identified MUSTs from both trials, the multichannel MUAP waveforms for each motor unit were obtained using spike-triggered averaging [33]. For each channel, a 64-ms window centered on the discharge timings of each MU, which sufficiently encompasses the entire waveform range [34], was extracted and averaged to compute the MUAP waveform.

Each MUST and its corresponding MUAP obtained from the same separation vector were retained only if the multichannel MUAPs from both trials matched. If the MUAPs did not match, the associated MUST was discarded. When matching the MUAPs, the channels with significant peak amplitude (e.g., higher than two times the standard deviation of the MUAP’s peak amplitude of all channels) were selected to calculate the normalized cross-correlation coefficients between MUAP waveforms from pairs of MUs [27, 35]. When comparing MUAP waveforms, the channels were strictly paired. Two MUs were matched if the average correlation coefficient of all paired channels was >0.8, the average difference in peak amplitude for all paired channels was < 50%, and the distance between the center of gravity of the MUAPs was < 32 mm [36].

By filtering the MUSTs with PNR and MUAP matching, the remaining MUSTs were regarded as with high reliability, as shown in Fig. 3B and C. Due to position maintaining or force control, several MUs were activated before the contraction or lost during the contraction and also reserved. Features were extracted from all retained MUSTs and their corresponding MUAPs for subsequent analysis. Feature extraction

Four categories of features were extracted from each task, corresponding to (1) force-tracking performance, (2) common drive among motor units, (3) motor unit size, and (4) motor unit discharge frequency. The first two categories of features, related to force-tracking performance and common drive, were extracted at the trial level. In contrast, the latter two categories, associated with motor unit size and discharge frequency, were computed for each individual motor unit.

Force tracking features

For the force signals, root mean square error (RMSE) and Bias error were extracted to assess the force tracking performance across different groups [37, 38]. The force tracking accuracy was obtained through analysis of the entire signal, encompassing the rising, steady-state, and declining phases.

Common drive to muscles

We first extracted features related to the cumulative spike train (CST) by merging all MUSTs within each trial and computing the corresponding discharge rate profile [39]. The instantaneous discharge rate of the CST was obtained using a 400-ms Hanning window applied to the spike trains for moving average smoothing. In addition, the discharge rate profiles of individual MUSTs were calculated, and principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on these profiles within each trial. The first principal component (PC1) was extracted to represent the common drive among motor units [25]. To evaluate the linear relationship between neural features and force output, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between both the CST and PC1 and the force signal. Furthermore, the variance explained by PC1 was computed to quantify the extent to which the common drive accounts for variability in motor unit discharge behavior [40].

MU size

By simultaneously analyzing of force signals and MUST, we further extracted the recruitment and derecruitment thresholds for each MU, defined as the contraction force at the time of the MU’s first and last discharge [19, 41]. For the characterization of MUAPs, the maximum peak-to-peak amplitude (PPV) across all channels of the MUAP waveform was extracted.

MU discharge rates

For the characterization of MU discharge properties, the mean discharge rate (DR) at recruitment (DR-recruitment), plate-phase (DR-plate), and decruitment (DR-derecruitment) were extracted [42]. The recruitment discharge rate of each motor unit was calculated as the inverse of the average inter-spike interval of its first five discharges. Similarly, the derecruitment discharge rate was derived from the last five discharges. The discharge rate during the plateau phase was obtained by averaging the discharge rates throughout the steady-force segment of the contraction. Notably, these three discharge rate features were computed only for motor units whose first five discharges occurred entirely within the force-rising phase and whose last five discharges occurred within the force-declining phase.

Given the wide age range of the recruited participants, we further analyzed the effect of age on the extracted features. For this analysis, results across all tasks were aggregated. To mitigate the influence of inter-individual variability, participants were grouped by age in 5-year intervals, and the feature values were averaged within each age group. A linear regression analysis was performed using the least squares method to fit the relationship between ages and features.

Motor unit decomposition performance

High-density sEMG decomposition was successfully conducted across all force levels and contraction speeds for both the biceps brachii (Grid 1) and triceps brachii (Grid 2). Fig. 3 gives the example decomposition results in a 30%MVC-S2 trial. As shown in Table 1, the number of MUs identified in the biceps brachii increased with contraction intensity, peaking at 15±8 MUs at 70%MVC and 10%MVC/s. The PNR values remained consistently high across all conditions, averaging around 27 dB for Grid 1 and 24 dB for Grid 2, indicating high decomposition quality. Motor unit properties

Features representing the common drive among motor units are summarized in Fig. 6. The correlation between CST and force (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{CST-force}$$\end{document}) increased with force level in both muscles (Figs. 6A, 5D), suggesting stronger neuromuscular coupling at higher contraction levels. Likewise, the correlation between the PC1 and force (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{PC1-force}$$\end{document}) also rose (Figs. 6B, E), as did the percentage of variance explained by PC1 (Figs. 6C,F), reaching >80% at the highest force levels. These results collectively indicate that the common drive received by motor units became stronger and was tightly linked to force output as force increased. For the biceps brachii at the high force level (70%MVC), the correlation between the common drive and force and the variance explained by PC1 increased significantly with contraction speed. This suggests that faster force generation enhances the synchronization among motor units. In contrast, these trends were less consistent in the triceps brachii, which may be attributed to its role as a non-primary muscle during the elbow flexion task.

The recruitment and derecruitment thresholds of MUs increased systematically with force level (Figs. 7A–B, D–E), reflecting orderly recruitment. In both muscles, MUAP peak-to-peak voltage (PPV) also increased with force (Figs. 7C, F), suggesting recruitment of larger motor units at higher contraction levels. Statistically significant effects of contraction speed were also observed on both thresholds and PPV. An increase in contraction speed led to a significant elevation in both recruitment and derecruitment thresholds of motor units decoded from the biceps brachii. Similar trends were also observed in the triceps brachii under certain conditions.

Fig. 8 shows the discharge rate of motor units across tasks. The discharge rate at recruitment and derecruitment phases consistently decreased with target force in both muscles (Fig. 8), while the discharge rate in the plate phase remains stable. Comparable patterns were observed in the triceps brachii (Figs. 8D–F). The contraction speed also affects the discharge rates. The discharge rate at recruitment or derecruitment tends to increase with the contraction speed, while the discharge rate decreased, which might be caused as MUs with larger size were recruited during fast contraction.

Discussion

Motor unit decomposition performance

This study demonstrated the feasibility and reliability of using HDsEMG and CKC algorithms to track motor unit activities across a wide range of contraction intensities and speeds. The decomposition accuracy, as evidenced by consistently high PNR and MUAP matching scores, is consistent with prior validations of the CKC approach in both simulation and experimental settings [19, 43]. The number of identified motor units increased with force, demonstrating that higher force levels enable more motor units to surpass the detection threshold due to their increased recruitment and larger MUAPs. Compared to earlier studies focused on fixed contraction rates, the current work extends these observations to a dynamic modulation of speed, highlighting the robustness of the decomposition pipeline even under time-varying force trajectories.

Notably, the number of decodable motor units in the biceps brachii exhibits a more pronounced increase with rising force levels, suggesting that the coordinated involvement of antagonist muscles plays a critical role in maintaining joint mechanical stability during high-level contractions. MUAP tracking across tasks (Fig. 4) demonstrated a higher degree of consistency in the biceps brachii relative to the triceps brachii. This discrepancy aligns with previous findings that task-relevant muscles exhibit more stable MU recruitment patterns, likely due to their central role in force generation and more consistent motor pool activation.

It should be noted that the present study encompassed a wide age range among the recruited participants. As illustrated in Fig. 9, while age was found to exert a modest influence on the decomposition outcomes, this effect was quantitatively negligible. Our preliminary analyses revealed no statistically significant interaction between age and contraction mode. As the primary objective of this study is to investigate the influence of contraction paradigms on MU behavior, rather than examining age-related effects, we opted to perform pooled analyses across all participants to enhance the generalizability of our findings.

The decomposition results revealed significantly fewer MUs were decoded in the triceps brachii compared to the biceps brachii across all contraction intensities. For example, at 10% MVC, the number of decoded MUs in the triceps (3-4 MUs) was less than 50% of those identified in the biceps (7-11 MUs). However, this activation ratio exhibited a progressive increase with contraction intensity. At higher force levels (70% MVC), the triceps demonstrated greater MU recruitment, reaching approximately 65% of biceps activation. This pattern likely reflects the increasing neuromuscular demand for antagonist co-activation to maintain joint stability during high-intensity contractions [44]. Regarding the features, MUs from the two muscles exhibited comparable ranges as these features were mainly determined by intrinsic physiological properties rather than muscle-specific differences [1]. In addition, all presented results are derived from the subset of MUs that were successfully decoded using the current methodology, which might introduce biases in the interpretation.

Motor unit activities across contraction levels

Our findings reaffirm the classical size principle of motor unit recruitment. As the contraction level increased, recruitment thresholds, MUAP amplitudes, and the number of active motor units all rose systematically (Fig. 7), consistent with the progressive engagement of higher-threshold, larger motor units. This aligns with prior observations from intramuscular and sEMG decomposition studies [4]. Additionally, the common drive to the motor neuron pool, as reflected by the cumulative spike train–force correlation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{CST-force}$$\end{document}) and PC1 variance, increased with force level. These results mirror previous reports demonstrating stronger neuromuscular coupling during high-effort contractions, indicating that force scaling is not only a function of recruitment and rate coding but also increased common drive among motor units [8, 25].

Interestingly, while discharge rates at recruitment and derecruitment phases decreased with increasing force (Fig. 8), the plateau discharge rate remained relatively constant. These findings indicate that at higher contraction levels, a larger proportion of high-threshold, larger-sized motor units can be decoded, typically exhibiting lower initial firing rates. Similar observations have been made in earlier studies, suggesting that the CNS may rely more heavily on MU recruitment at higher force levels rather than further increasing firing rates, especially once plateau force has been reached [2]. Several MUs exhibited low firing rates of < 5 Hz even during the plateau phase (Fig. 8B and E). This phenomenon might result from the calculation method employed. We characterized firing frequency by computing the reciprocal of the mean inter-spike interval. Consequently, when motor units exhibited intermittent firing interruptions during contraction phases, this could lead to artificially low frequency values.

Motor unit activities across contraction speeds

A major contribution of this study is the detailed characterization of how contraction speed modulates motor unit control strategies. Faster rates of force development led to significant increases in force-tracking error (Fig. 5), especially at higher force levels, which aligns with the known speed-accuracy tradeoff in motor performance. Concomitantly, both recruitment thresholds and discharge rates at recruitment increased with speed, suggesting that the nervous system compensates for rapid force demands by accelerating MU recruitment and firing. This supports prior findings where similar firing adaptations in strength training studies with rapid contractions were observed [42].

Moreover, the enhancement of common drive features (higher PC1 variance and CST-force correlation) at higher speeds (Fig. 6) highlights the increased neural synchronization during fast contractions. The differences observed between the biceps and triceps brachii are also worth noting. In contrast to the biceps, the triceps showed more modest speed effects, which is likely attributable to its antagonistic role in this elbow flexion task. The MU discharge variability and force modulation were more pronounced in task-relevant muscles. These findings suggest that the neuromuscular system adapts to faster contraction speeds by increasing the efficiency and common drive, particularly in primary mover muscles. Such adaptations are essential for rapid, precise force output and may underlie training-induced improvements observed in athletic populations.

Limitations and future work

While this study benefits from a large, diverse participant pool and a comprehensive range of contraction conditions, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although CKC-based decomposition has demonstrated strong validation, the identification of deeper or smaller motor units remains challenging, particularly during high-speed or high-force contractions. Extending the analysis to dynamic or functional movements (e.g., reaching, grasping) would provide valuable insight into real-world motor control. Additionally, while age-related analyses showed weak-to-moderate trends (Fig. 9), future work with age-stratified cohorts and longitudinal follow-up could further clarify how aging affects neuromuscular speed adaptation. Given the increasing interest in neural biomarkers for motor function and aging, understanding the interaction between contraction dynamics and motor unit control in older adults has potential implications for rehabilitation and motor decline prevention.

Conclusion

This study characterized motor unit behavior during isometric elbow flexion under varying force levels and contraction speeds using HDsEMG decomposition. The results demonstrate that both force amplitude and speed significantly influence motor unit recruitment, discharge rates, and common neural drive, with faster contractions eliciting higher discharge rates and greater common drive, particularly in task-relevant muscles. These findings highlight the adaptive nature of neuromuscular control in response to time-varying demands and underscore the importance of considering contraction speed in both research and clinical applications. The decomposition framework used here offers a reliable basis for future studies and potential applications in neurorehabilitation and myoelectric control.
