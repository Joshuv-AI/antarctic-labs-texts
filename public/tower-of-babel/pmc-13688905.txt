The impact of educational video about preeclampsia on pregnant women’s knowledge: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Sumayah Alrehaili, Hanan Badr, Amal Sijini, Mohammed Ali Saghir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreeclampsia is a severe hypertensive condition that may occur during pregnancy, posing significant risks to both the mother and the fetus. Educating pregnant women about this condition is essential for its recognition and the prevention of complications. This study aimed to examine the impact of educational videos about preeclampsia on pregnant women’s knowledge.MethodsA quasi-experimental design (one group pre- & post-test) was employed to evaluate the effectiveness of an educational video developed to enhance pregnant women’s basic knowledge about preeclampsia. A purposive sample of 100 pregnant women participated by completing a validated 39-item Preeclampsia Knowledge Questionnaire at three time points: prior to the educational intervention, immediately after watching the video in the antenatal clinic, and again two weeks later via WhatsApp.ResultsPre-intervention knowledge scores indicated limited knowledge of pre-eclampsia in pregnant women. Knowledge scores significantly improved immediately after the intervention and at the two-week follow-up compared to pre-intervention levels (p < 0.001). The mean knowledge score increased from 17.40 ± 8.98 prior to the intervention to 33.00 ± 4.35 post-intervention. After two weeks, it decreased slightly to 31.89 ± 4.73.ConclusionsThese findings support the effectiveness of educational videos in enhancing pregnant women’s knowledge of preeclampsia and recommend their incorporation of educational videos into routine prenatal care. Further research is required to find out the long-term effects of utilizing educational videos for the early detection of preeclampsia in pregnant women.Trial RegistrationTCTR20250606007 (2025-06-06) Retrospectively registered.

Introduction

Preeclampsia is the greatest common hypertensive disorder of pregnancy; it is a major cause of maternal mortality and morbidity [1]. characterized by new-onset hypertension, defined as a persistent systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mm Hg or higher or a diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mm Hg or higher, which appears in the second half of pregnancy (the second 20 weeks) or postpartum, associated with either proteinuria greater than 300 mg/24 hours or another maternal organ dysfunction [2]. Additionally, Preeclampsia can adversely impact maternal and fetal health, leading to several difficulties for both the mother and the infant [3]. Maternal consequences of preeclampsia may include eclampsia, HELLP syndrome, placental abruption, postpartum hemorrhage, acute kidney injury, and liver dysfunction [4]. Neonatal complications may include intrauterine growth restriction, preterm, lower birth weight, score lower on the APGAR scale, and require admission to the critical care unit [4, 5].

Globally, preeclampsia is a major cause of mother and newborn morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [6]. Furthermore, Preeclampsia affects about 5–7% of pregnancies worldwide, with annually, responsible for over 70,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 fetal deaths [7]. While in Saudi Arabia, according to the annual statistical book from Ministry of Health, the total number of preeclampsia cases among Saudi women in 2023 is 4,507 and eclampsia cases 414 [8].

According to previous studies in Saudi Arabia have identified significant gaps in women’s knowledge and awareness of preeclampsia. For example, a cross-sectional study estimates the level of awareness and knowledge of preeclampsia found that 66.7% of 390 women exhibiting insufficient knowledge [9]. Another study assessed 541 women from the western region of Saudi Arabia demonstrated that general knowledge of preeclampsia and its risk factors was inadequate [10]. Similarly, a study conducted in the Al Baha region with 485 pregnant women, revealing that while many had heard of pre-eclampsia, their understanding of its symptoms, risks, and consequences was limited [11]. Also, a study Carried out among 385 women of reproductive age illustrated that limited knowledge and awareness of preeclampsia [12].

Therefore, Educational interventions designed to improve pregnant women’s knowledge have the potential to significantly reduce complications by facilitating early diagnosis and appropriate management of preeclampsia [13]. These educational interventions often cover key topics such as the stages, etiology, clinical manifestations, diagnostic methods, complications, preventive measures, and management strategies related to preeclampsia [13]. Consequently, delivering information through various media formats has become increasingly important for patient education [14–16].

Additionally, multiple studies have demonstrated the efficacy of educational interventions in enhancing pregnant women’s knowledge of pre-eclampsia. For example, a study conducted with 96 women diagnosed with preeclampsia to evaluate the effect of educational sessions on their knowledge. The findings revealed a considerable improvement in participants’ knowledge following the session compared to their pre-intervention levels [17]. Similarly, 102 pregnant women were selected to assess the impact of a comprehensive educational program on knowledge, risk perception of cardiovascular disease, self-efficacy, and adherence to healthy lifestyle practices among women with preeclampsia. The results of the study showed that participants’ awareness and knowledge were greatly enhanced by the educational program [18]. Furthermore, a study conducted to investigate how a preeclampsia intervention program affected high-risk women’s awareness and pregnancy outcomes. Following the educational intervention, the experimental group demonstrated a statistically significant enhancement in preeclampsia awareness levels relative to the control group [19].

Recent quasi-experimental studies indicate that video-based educational interventions can enhance pregnant women’s knowledge about preeclampsia and its prevention. A study examined the impact of a video-assisted training program, revealing that knowledge increased to 60%, good practices rose to 88%, and positive attitudes reached 70% post-program [20]. A study conducted in Indonesia with 32 participants revealed that viewing animated videos resulted in a statistically significant enhancement in knowledge [21]. Another study conducted in Indonesia utilized videos and pamphlets to educate 74 pregnant women, resulting in a significant improvement of their knowledge [22].

As a result, the current study aims to examine the impact of an educational video about preeclampsia on pregnant women’s knowledge. Specifically, the primary objectives are to assess the existing knowledge of pregnant women regarding preeclampsia and to evaluate the impact of the educational video immediately after watching the video and again two weeks later. This study intends to enhance maternal and fetal health outcomes in Saudi Arabia and similar regions by addressing knowledge gaps and providing women with essential health information. The following hypotheses were proposed:

Materials and methods

Study design

Quasi-experimental design (one group pre-& post-test).

Participants and setting

The sample consisted of 100 pregnant women selected using a purposive sampling method. Recruitment was carried out through antenatal clinics at primary health centers in Al-Madinah Al-Munawwarah, Saudi Arabia. Inclusion criteria were: Saudi pregnant women who were either multiparas or primiparas, aged between 18 and 60 years, Arabic speakers, and without any auditory, speech, or visual impairments. Exclusion criteria included non-Saudi women, women under 18 years of age, and those with visual or hearing impairments.

Sample size was determined using a power analysis table with a G power with a 0.05 alpha error, 95% power for the study, and a 0.6 effect size from a previous study [22]. resulting in a sample size of at least 32 patients. To prevent the effects of drops and bias, the researcher increased the sample size to include 100 patients.

Instrument

The questionnaire that was used consisted of two parts:Part 1: The demographic data and obstetric history, derived from [23], consisted of multiple-choice and yes/no questions. Sociodemographic data included age, residence, educational level and family income. Whereas obstetric history about gravidity, parity, number of abortions, gestational age, antenatal follow-up, family or personal history of pre-eclampsia, and familiarity with pre-eclampsia. Permission was obtained to use this demographic data and obstetric history in the current study. All responses were coded and entered Excel for analysis.Part 2: A pre-post-test Preeclampsia Knowledge Questionnaire adapted from Fondjo et al. [23] was used to assess participants’ knowledge of preeclampsia. The original questionnaire was selected because it is a published instrument that assesses the major domains of preeclampsia knowledge. To better align the instrument with the educational content and objectives of the present intervention, the questionnaire was modified by adding items assessing the definition of preeclampsia and preventive measures, thereby providing a more comprehensive evaluation of participants’ knowledge. The additional items were developed following a review of the relevant literature and current clinical recommendations and were evaluated during the content validation process.

The original questionnaire was selected because it is a published instrument that assesses the major domains of preeclampsia knowledge. To better align the instrument with the educational content and objectives of the present intervention, the questionnaire was modified by adding items assessing the definition of preeclampsia and preventive measures, thereby providing a more comprehensive evaluation of participants’ knowledge. The additional items were developed following a review of the relevant literature and current clinical recommendations and were evaluated during the content validation process.

The adapted questionnaire assessed participants’ knowledge regarding the definition of preeclampsia, timing of occurrence, risk factors, signs and symptoms, diagnostic methods, potential complications, and preventive measures. The tool consisted of 39 closed-ended questions with predefined response options, such as identifying risk factors with answers of “Yes,” “No,” or “I do not know.” Each correct response was awarded one point, whereas incorrect or unanswered responses received zero points. Total scores were converted to percentages and categorized according to Bloom’s cut-off criteria as low (0–23, < 60%), moderate (24–31, 60–80%), or high (32–39, 80–100%) levels of knowledge. Permission to adapt and use the original questionnaire was obtained from the original authors.

Validity and reliability

The content validity of the data collection tool was ensured by a review of four experts in the field of obstetrics and gynecology who assessed the relevance and appropriateness to meet the study’s aim and suggested modifications were made then the completed form was utilized to collect data. A pilot study was conducted with 10 participants to evaluate the reliability of the questionnaire. The questionnaire’s internal consistency was assessed based on the Cronbach alpha coefficient for questionnaire scales. The Cronbach’s alpha for knowledge regarding preeclampsia was 0.91.

Data collection

The data was collected in antenatal clinics at primary health centers from November 2024 to February 2025. Following the ethics committee’s approval, the researcher reviewed clinic registration records to identify individuals who satisfied the inclusion criteria. Recruitment and data collection occurred from Sunday to Thursday, between 8:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m. At the beginning of the study, all 100 participants received both verbal and written information regarding it and they gave their written informed consent prior to participation. Following consent, each participant was assigned a distinct identification number to facilitate statistical comparison of their responses across the pre-test, immediate post-test, and follow-up test conducted two weeks later.

In order to assess the existing knowledge levels among pregnant women regarding preeclampsia, initially 100 participants complete the Preeclampsia Knowledge Questionnaire as a pre-test. Following this, each participant individually viewed an educational video about preeclampsia. Immediately after watching the video, a post-test using the same questionnaire was conducted in the clinic to evaluate the immediate effectiveness of the video on participants’ knowledge. Subsequently, the educational video was shared with participants via WhatsApp, allowing them to review the content at home over a two-week period. Additionally, WhatsApp served as a platform for open questions and answers (Q&A). Participants were permitted to submit their inquiries, which the researcher would address. After two weeks, the researcher resumed contact with the participants via WhatsApp and requested them to complete the Preeclampsia Knowledge Questionnaire once more to assess their retention of the information acquired.

Intervention

The educational video was carefully developed to ensure clarity, accessibility, and engagement. Three primary strategies were employed: (1) the use of simple and clear language to accommodate varying levels of health literacy among pregnant women; (2) the incorporation of illustrative images and visual aids to enhance understanding and retention of information; and (3) the presentation of concise and focused content to minimize cognitive overload and maintain viewer engagement.

The educational video, with a duration of approximately five minutes, was developed by the researcher following an extensive review of the relevant medical, nursing, and healthcare literature. The content was generated through comprehensive searches of MEDLINE, PubMed, and CINAHL, as well as the official websites of the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Saudi Ministry of Health (MOH), using the keywords preeclampsia, eclampsia, educational video, hypertension, and knowledge. The literature review informed the development of evidence-based educational content covering the definition of preeclampsia, timing of onset, risk factors, signs and symptoms, diagnostic methods, preventive measures, and potential maternal and fetal complications.

The video provided participants with essential information about preeclampsia, including its definition, methods of diagnosis, risk factors, clinical manifestations, potential maternal and fetal complications, and preventive measures. The warning signs emphasized included high blood pressure, persistent headache, sudden swelling of the face, hands, and feet, blurred vision, severe pain in the upper abdomen or shoulders, and vomiting occurring after the fifth month of pregnancy. The preventive recommendations presented in the video included following a low-salt diet, drinking 6–8 glasses of water daily, avoiding fried and processed foods, obtaining adequate rest, engaging in regular physical activity, maintaining a healthy body weight, and adhering to scheduled antenatal follow-up visits and healthcare providers’ recommendations. The educational content was supported by illustrations, animations, motion graphics, subtitles, and audio narration to facilitate understanding and improve learner engagement.

The educational content and the video, developed based on the literature review, were evaluated for content validity by four experts specializing in women’s health, midwifery nursing, and obstetric medicine. Although no formal content validity instrument was used, the experts independently reviewed the educational material using predefined evaluation criteria, including scientific accuracy, relevance to the study objectives, comprehensiveness of the content, clarity of the information, appropriateness of the language for pregnant women, logical sequence of the topics, and suitability for the target population. The experts provided qualitative feedback and recommendations regarding both the educational content and its presentation. Their comments were reviewed by the research team, and the necessary revisions were incorporated into the final version of the educational video.

Additionally, two media experts evaluated the educational video to assess the quality and effectiveness of its multimedia components, including still images, motion graphics, cartoons, animations, subtitles, audio narration, and the overall visual presentation to ensure clarity, engagement, and ease of understanding.

Prior to data collection, a pilot study was conducted with 10 pregnant women (10% of the study sample) to assess the clarity, acceptability, and feasibility of the educational video and study procedures, as well as the reliability of the knowledge questionnaire. Participants reported that the educational content was clear, easy to understand, and appropriately presented, and that the questionnaire items were straightforward. Therefore, no modifications were required. The participants included in the pilot study were subsequently incorporated into the final study sample.

Data analysis

Data was entered, organized and analyzed by Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 28. Tables and graphs presented the data. Categorized variables were shown as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented in the form of mean and standard deviation. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the Shapiro-Wilk test were used to test the data’s normality and report that it did not follow a normal distribution; subsequently, non-parametric tests were used for analysis. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to test the difference between knowledge before the intervention with the after intervention, before the intervention with two weeks after intervention and after intervention with two weeks after intervention. The Kruskal-Wallis test and Mann-Whitney U test were used to test the relationship between knowledge with binary and nominal independent variables, respectively. A p-value (≤ 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics committee of the Faculty of Nursing College at University (Certificate of Approval No. 1 M.23, date: September 3, 2024) and the Institutional Review Board (IRB), General Directorate of Health Affairs in AL-Madinah Region (Certificate of Approval No. 24–112, date: November 5, 2024). The study was registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry: Registration ID number : TCTR20250606007 (2025-06-06) Retrospectively registered.

Discussion

Preeclampsia is a pregnancy-associated condition that results in significant morbidity and mortality for both the mother and the fetus. Identifying women at elevated risk for preeclampsia early facilitates the prevention and management of the condition in its early stages [24]. Preeclampsia represents a significant global health issue for mothers. It results in severe illness and mortality in both mothers and newborns, along with premature birth and chronic heart disease in mothers [25].

The current study aimed to examine the impact of an educational video on pregnant women’s knowledge of pre-eclampsia. The results clearly indicated a significant increase in total knowledge scores following the intervention. Women performed significantly better on the post-test and two-week follow-up compared to the pre-test, indicating the efficacy of the educational video. Therefore, the study supports the hypothesis that " Pregnant women who receive the educational video will exhibit higher post-test knowledge scores than at pre-test.”

According to the study’s findings, (59%) of the participants were middle-aged (29–39 years), 33% were between the ages of 18 and 29, and the remaining participants were above 40 years old. Approximately (70%) of the pregnant women possessed a university degree, while 24% had completed high school. Approximately (84%) were housewife, while merely (14%) were employed. In terms of income, (63%) of women reported earning an average monthly income between 5,000 and 10,000 RS. The findings align with Radwan et al. (2023) study, which revealed that the majority of women (77%) were unemployed, and approximately (68%) owned a university degree [12]. Another study revealed that (60%) of pregnant women obtained a college degree, while (53%) had a moderate monthly income [23]. These social and demographic characteristics enhance our comprehension of the sample population.

The present study identified a statistically significant correlation between women’s pre-intervention knowledge scores and their levels of education. Women holding a university degree got the highest overall knowledge score. This finding was supported by two studies conducted in Canada and Egypt, in which it was revealed that knowledge of preeclampsia increased with increasing the educational level [26, 27]. This increase may be due to the fact that women with a higher educational level had better knowledge and concern about their pregnancy and its associated complications.

According to pre-intervention knowledge, the current study revealed that about three-quarters of the participants lacked sufficient knowledge regarding preeclampsia prior to the intervention. This aligned with previous studies examining women’s knowledge and awareness of preeclampsia in Saudi Arabia. That showed (66.7%) percent of the total sample exhibited a deficiency in knowledge [9]. Another study highlights the insufficient knowledge and awareness of preeclampsia among Saudi women of reproductive age [12].

Moreover, A study was done to evaluate the knowledge of Saudi Arabian women regarding preeclampsia and its associated risk factors. The findings revealed that their overall knowledge of preeclampsia was insufficient [10]. Similarly, A study was conducted to find out the knowledge of women in the Al Baha region of Saudi Arabia regarding preeclampsia and the associated factors. The study’s findings indicated that the majority of participants were aware of pre-eclampsia; however, they lacked a comprehensive understanding of its symptoms, risk factors, and consequences [11].

The current study observed a significant transformation following the intervention. The percentage of women exhibiting low knowledge decreased sharply, whereas those demonstrating high knowledge increased significantly. This is backed by a study done in Mansoura, Egypt, which showed that educational sessions greatly improved knowledge after the intervention compared to before [17]. Similarly, research was conducted to look at how a preeclampsia intervention program affects pregnancy outcomes and awareness of high-risk preeclampsia in Jordanian women and discovered a significant difference in preeclampsia awareness mean scores [19].

Furthermore, a study in India looked at a structured teaching program meant to help first-time mothers learn more about how to deal with high blood pressure during pregnancy. The mothers’ overall knowledge significantly improved after the program [28]. Also, a study conducted at Minia, Egypt mentioned that the educational intervention which aimed to identify the awareness of women regarding sign and symptoms of preeclampsia had dramatically increased the knowledge score from 24% to 96% [27]. In addition, a study in Tanta, Egypt, revealed that the intervention greatly increased the women’s knowledge [29].

The current study findings were supported by the results of Reda et al. (2024), who examined the impact of video-assisted training programs on the attitudes, behaviors, and knowledge of pregnant women at risk for pre-eclampsia. The study found A significant difference (p < 0.001) existed in women’s overall knowledge, practices, and attitudes regarding preeclampsia before and after the intervention [20]. This study corroborates the findings of Ermiati et al. (2021) regarding the impact of videos and leaflets on pregnant women’s knowledge of preeclampsia. The results demonstrated that both videos and leaflets markedly enhanced maternal knowledge regarding pre-eclampsia, with a p-value of 0.000 (< 0.05) [22].

Moreover, the results of this study are in line with those of Ernawati et al. (2024), which aimed to evaluate the efficacy of animated videos in enhancing pregnant women’s knowledge about preeclampsia. The results indicated a significant improvement in knowledge levels, with a P-value of 0.00 < 0.005 [21]. Hermawati et al. (2024) conducted a study to examine the impact of animated videos on pregnant women’s knowledge and motivation regarding preeclampsia prevention. The mean differences between the pretest and post-test (p = 0.000, p = 0.001, respectively) indicated a significant enhancement in knowledge and motivation levels [30]. In conclusion, both current and previous study indicates that educational videos are essential for increasing awareness of preeclampsia among pregnant women.

Limitation

This study is limited by its small sample, drawn only from women attending antenatal clinics at primary health centers in Al Madinah Al Munawwarah, which may affect generalizability. The two-week intervention captured only immediate outcomes, without assessing long-term behavioral changes. Additionally, differences in participants’ baseline knowledge, education, and digital literacy may have influenced the video’s effectiveness.

Recommendations

Implications of educational videos in nursing practice by incorporating videos into routine prenatal care, nurses can improve maternal health outcomes by giving pregnant women easily accessible, consistent, and clear information. Furthermore, these videos complement conventional in-person instruction by enhancing women’s comprehension, retention, and ability to review the material as necessary. Nurses play a crucial role in promoting the utilization of these technologies and should advocate for their integration into hospital educational policies.

Moreover, further research is required to determine the long-term effects of utilizing educational videos for the early detection of preeclampsia in pregnant women. Furthermore, subsequent research should employ larger sample sizes to enhance the generalizability of the findings. It is also essential to examine the impact of various instructional methods (such as videos, applications, and brochures) on knowledge acquisition and change in behavior. Further investigation is required to ascertain the efficacy of interactive and AI-driven educational tools in enhancing retention of learned material. It is essential to identify the challenges healthcare professionals encounter when educating women about preeclampsia in various obstetric environments.

Conclusions

This study’s findings indicate that an educational video on preeclampsia enhances pregnant women’s knowledge and awareness of the condition, potentially resulting in improved maternal outcomes. Video-based education empowers women to understand warning signs, risk factors, and the appropriate timing for seeking medical assistance. The incorporation of video-based education into maternal health care remains highly beneficial and significant, particularly as the utilization of digital technologies increases.
