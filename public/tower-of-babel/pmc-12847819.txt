Assessing sustainability effects of tobacco use and control in global supply chains
Authors: Bolin Yu, Yuling Pan, Jing Meng, Hao Wu, Zounachuan Sun, Congcong Du, Chenyang Shuai, Ningkang Chen, Kun Wang, Zhenci Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tobacco use presents substantial challenges for the environment, social and economic development along global supply chains. Here we assess the global sustainability impacts of tobacco use and control across global supply chains in 164 countries and regions. We find that tobacco use in the top ten downstream countries drove over 50% of global upstream impacts. High-income countries tended to have larger per capita impacts than low-income countries, displacing 42% of health burden and 34% of greenhouse gas emissions to upstream low-income countries. We also observe declines in environmental stressors, disease burden, and employment during 2010-2020, driven by impact intensity, production structure, and tobacco prevalence. Reducing the prevalence of tobacco use as appropriate by 2027 could generate substantial benefits to the environment, health, and economy, albeit with a reduction in employment relative to 2020 levels. Our analyses contribute to a just and sustainable transition towards a healthy and tobacco-free world.

Introduction

Despite economic and employment benefits, the tobacco industry imposes substantial burdens on human health and the environment, such as air pollution, climate change, freshwater depletion, cropland occupation and food insecurity1–3. Tobacco use introduces additional challenges to the global sustainable development agenda. There have been 168 signatories and 183 parties to the World Health Organization (WHO) Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), a treaty that includes measures such as Article 17 regarding supporting economically viable alternative activities and Article 18 regarding protecting the environment and the health of persons4,5. Recently, the tenth Conference of the Parties to the WHO FCTC adopted a historic decision to strengthen implementation of Article 18, including through national policies and international cooperation6. The United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) emphasize strengthening the implementation of the WHO FCTC in all countries, as appropriate (SDG 3.a)7. However, as of 2020, the number of global adult tobacco users remained notably high, reaching 1.3 billion8. Thus, mitigating the adverse effects of tobacco use is critical to achieving the global sustainability agenda.

We are now facing the challenge of squaring the circle between reducing the prevalence of tobacco use, while maintaining a sustainable and just transition. Tobacco control can bring about both benefits and risks in economic, social, and environmental aspects, such as environmental pressure, health burden, and employment. The SDG 3.a regarding tobacco control can directly and indirectly influence the achievement of several SDGs1, such as SDG1’no poverty’9, SDG6 ‘clean water’10, SDG12 ‘responsible consumption and production’11, and SDG 15 ‘life on land’12. In fact, SDGs are highly interconnected13–16 through global supply chains. In the context of globalization, understanding the upstream impacts embodied in global tobacco supply chains, as well as the potential effects of the SDG regarding tobacco control, is crucial for moving towards a more sustainable and equitable future.

Previous studies have overlooked the integral upstream production activities prior to the delivery of tobacco to final demand, which include growing17, curing18, manufacturing and distribution19, as well as inputs from other production sectors such as fertilizer, plastic, and cigarette paper10, thereby underestimating the impacts of tobacco use. Such information is of growing concern because tobacco use could lead to far-reaching impacts within and between regions through global supply chains, as the world economy is increasingly interconnected than ever before. In addition, lots of studies have analyzed the impacts of tobacco use on a single dimension of health20–22, employment23, economy24,25, or environment2,26–29. However, quantitative studies on the global upstream impacts of tobacco use in all environmental, social, and economic dimensions across global supply chains are limited. Such studies should be conducted within a uniform system boundary defined as a consistent analytical framework that applies identical sectoral classifications and considers their sectoral connections across all economies globally, enabling cross-country comparability of the upstream impacts. Moreover, previous studies rarely investigate the underlying drivers of global upstream impacts changes and ignore the potential sustainability implications of promoting tobacco control-related SDGs. These gaps hinder a comprehensive understanding of the environmental, social, and economic impacts of tobacco use and control policies, the sustainable transition away from tobacco production, and the formulation of targeted tobacco control policies.

Here, we provide a detailed assessment of global upstream environmental, social, and economic impacts of tobacco use and control in 164 countries and regions during 2010-2020 along the global supply chain, using a demand-driven environmentally extended multi-regional input-output (EEMRIO) framework based on GLORIA (Global Resource Input-Output Assessment), a global MRIO database30,31. This approach establishes a uniform system boundary for assessing the upstream impacts of tobacco use in different regions. Eight indicators, highly related to tobacco use, were selected to represent socioeconomic impacts (employment, disease burden and value-added) and environmental pressure, including greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, agriculture and non-agriculture blue water consumption (i.e., use of freshwater in surface water bodies like lakes and rivers, and groundwater in aquifers32), permanent and annual cropland use. These indicators correspond to environmental, social and economic effects of sustainability, and SDGs (e.g., SDGs 3, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 13). The breakdown of the tobacco industry includes two sectors: the growing tobacco sector refers to activities involving cultivation and primary processing of tobacco leaves, and the tobacco products sector refers to activities covering manufacturing and distribution of finished goods. We detail the differences in global upstream impacts of tobacco use across sectors, regions and income groups. Then, we look into potential socioeconomic drivers that are closely related to tobacco production and consumption to reveal the key factors driving changes in individual impact indicators from 2010 to 2020 through the structural decomposition analysis (SDA), an input-output-based factor decomposition approach. To quantify the potential impacts of future tobacco control following UN SDG 3.a and the ambitious tobacco-free target on environmental, social, and economic development, we conduct a scenario analysis based on projected population growth and anticipated reductions in tobacco use prevalence among adults in the targeted year. Our results could promote a just and sustainable transition away from tobacco production for affected countries, thereby benefiting humanity and the planet.

Results

Global upstream impacts of downstream tobacco use throughout global supply chains

Our results show that global tobacco use poses substantial sustainability challenges in environmental, social, and economic dimensions. We visualize the global upstream impacts of country-specific tobacco use to identify the key hubs for improving sustainability (Fig. 1). As the largest emerging economy, China ranks top in all impact indicators associated with tobacco use. In 2020, China was responsible for 28% of global disease burden, 14% of global GHG emissions, 18% of global annual cropland use, 15% of agriculture blue water consumption, and 30% of global value-added. Especially, we find that compared with other regions, China has a larger impact on some adjacent regions (such as Pakistan, Thailand, and India) and distant regions (such as the US and Brazil), due to close trade relationships related to tobacco. In addition, other emerging economies, such as India, Indonesia, Brazil, and South Africa, are also major contributors. In terms of environmental consequences, during the period 2010-2020, the average annual GHG emissions associated with global tobacco use reached 221.4 million tons of CO2 equivalent (Supplementary Fig. 1), which is equivalent to the total GHG emissions of the Philippines in 2020. In addition, global tobacco use is associated with 5.4 billion m3 of total blue water use (exceeding Australia’s agricultural water use in 2020) and 4.6 million ha of cropland use (approximately Japan’s agricultural land in 2020) annually. In terms of social impacts, although global tobacco use is associated with 30.8 million jobs, the negative health impacts of emissions generated during tobacco production and distribution throughout global supply chains have been overlooked. Here, we unveil a substantial emissions-related disease burden of 0.6 million disability-adjusted life years annually. Economically, although approximately 320.9 billion US$ of value-added is associated with tobacco use annually, the excess economic costs of increased healthcare expenditures and productivity losses are far greater33,34. These losses are due to the historical and continued consumption of tobacco products, despite progress in reducing smoking prevalence among adults (Supplementary Fig. 2). More than one billion smokers consume almost 5 trillion cigarettes each year, while others use substantial quantities of smokeless tobacco products and suffer from the effects of widespread waterpipe use35.

We observe substantial changes in global upstream impacts from 2010 to 2020 (Supplementary Fig. 3). While global final tobacco consumption decreased by 1.6% (5.8 billion US$) from 2010 to 2020, global value-added rose by 3.6% (12.1 billion US$). Our results highlight a decoupling status between tobacco use and economic growth, that is, less tobacco consumption can bring about more economic benefits. In contrast, all other environmental, employment, and health indicators exhibit varying degrees of decline, indicating marked synergies between reduced tobacco prevalence and related impacts. Noticeably, a 1% decrease in final tobacco consumption is associated with a 42% decrease in non-agriculture blue water consumption and a 30% decrease in GHG emissions.

Our results also reveal the notable differences and interactions between regions of different income groups. For example, high-income countries contributed to 42% and 34% of disease burden and GHG emissions in low-income countries, respectively. In addition, the substantial disparities in the upstream impacts of tobacco use across countries and regions could be attributed to differences in the population of tobacco users and the unequal distribution of impacts among them (Supplementary Figs. 4–11). Globally, more than 80% of global permanent cropland use is concentrated in the top 20% of tobacco users with higher impact intensity, and the Gini coefficient is as high as 0.77. By contrast, we observe that the distribution of disease burden is relatively even, with a Gini coefficient of 0.34. Our analysis suggests that the upstream impacts associated with tobacco use are unevenly distributed among tobacco users. Therefore, in addition to regional measures, the impacts in terms of each individual tobacco user should be meticulously assessed.

We show that high-income countries have a higher per capita impact than low-income countries, as indicated by the overall distribution and median values (Supplementary Fig. 12). For instance, in Luxembourg, an adult tobacco user can contribute to 5 tons of GHG emissions, 20 m2 of permanent cropland use, and 4495 US$ of value-added, on a global scale. However, as the number of adult tobacco users in each region is far higher than the number of jobs creation related to tobacco use, the per capita employment is minimal, and accordingly, there is only a minor disparity in per capita employment among the four income groups. Compared with low-income countries, per capita tobacco consumption in high-income countries is generally higher. This is because for each adult tobacco user in these areas, more tobacco is grown, and more tobacco products are produced, packaged, transported, and sold, resulting in higher environmental stressors and economic gains as well.

The tobacco products sector was found to have notably higher GHG emissions, non-agricultural blue water consumption, and permanent cropland use than the growing tobacco sector, indicating that the former bears the primary responsibility for reducing these environmental stressors. Additionally, the global value-added associated with final demand from the tobacco products sector was eight times larger than that of the growing tobacco sector (Fig. 2(h)). That means the economic benefits of the tobacco industry are mainly derived from tobacco products.

While the growing tobacco sector primarily impacts land and water use directly, the tobacco products sector exerts influence through both direct and indirect means in the supply chain. We find that both tobacco products and growing tobacco sectors are important in governing agriculture blue water consumption and annual cropland use associated with tobacco use (Fig. 2(b) and Fig. 2(d)). In addition, growing tobacco can provide almost the same employment opportunities as tobacco products, as there is no statistically significant difference in employment between them (Fig. 2(f)). Surprisingly, both tobacco products and growing tobacco are responsible for a heavy disease burden owing to tobacco use (Fig. 2(g)). Although most tobacco leaves are used as intermediate inputs for tobacco product manufacturing, in some cases, tobacco leaves may be sold as final products for various applications such as pipe tobacco and chewing tobacco20. Our results suggest that the health harms associated with tobacco use from the growing tobacco sector are largely overlooked.

Factors influencing global upstream impacts of tobacco use

Based on structural decomposition analysis (SDA), the change in global upstream impacts of tobacco use is attributed to five factors (Eq. (8) in Methods): impact intensity, production structure, tobacco use per capita, prevalence rate (i.e., the proportion of tobacco users in adults aged 15 and above), and the number of adults. Here, tobacco use per capita, prevalence rate and number of adults collectively determine the final demand for tobacco use, which are consumer-driven factors.

Figure 3 shows the contribution of each influencing factor to changes in global upstream impacts associated with tobacco use from 2010 to 2020. The results indicate that impact intensity was an important factor in reducing most impact indicators. This is mainly because of the improvements in technology and efficiency, with less resource input producing more output and less undesirable output. Notably, in China, impact intensity change could contribute to environmental stressors reductions and economic and employment growth simultaneously (Supplementary Fig. 13). In addition, production structure change also caused prominent reductions in the upstream impacts, except for agricultural water. This finding also applies to the US, but it is a different story in India where production structure change contributed substantially to increasing the upstream impacts (Supplementary Fig. 14 and Fig. 15). In terms of consumer-driven factors, tobacco use per capita and number of adults are important factors contributing to increasing global upstream impacts. In contrast, the decline in the prevalence rate of tobacco use among adults is the major factor driving down global upstream impacts, which is also observed in the cases of the US, India, and China. The results highlight the importance of reducing tobacco use per capita, alongside reducing the prevalence of adult tobacco use. Our decomposition analysis discloses the underlying mechanisms behind the changes in global upstream impacts associated with tobacco use and identifies their key drivers.

Simulation of tobacco control and associated impacts

The prevalence rate is a crucial factor on the demand side, which contributes substantially to environmental and health improvement, despite its impact on employment and economic reduction (Fig. 3). Tobacco control and associated impacts, such as climate change mitigation, natural resource conservation, and socioeconomic development, are all global sustainability challenges facing the global community. To simulate the global environmental, social, and economic impacts of tobacco control, we design five scenarios following SDG 3.a regarding strengthening tobacco control in all countries, as appropriate. All scenarios are conducted for 2027 on a global scale, using the projected and appropriately adjusted prevalence rates. S0 serves as a baseline scenario that uses the WHO projected prevalence rates of tobacco use in 2027. Scenario S1 is based on a 30% reduction target for the prevalence rate between 2012 and 2027. S2-S4 are enhanced scenarios of S0.

Figure 4 presents the relative changes in the upstream impacts associated with tobacco control in regions under different scenarios between 2020 and 2027. We find a marked increase (58-101%) in global value-added under scenarios S0–S4. Meanwhile, employment could decrease by 9–21% by 2027 relative to 2020 levels under scenarios S1–S4, with little change in S0. GHG emissions, agriculture water consumption, and disease burden show a decline under scenarios S1–S4, although the current trend in tobacco control policies (S0) could lead to a slight increase in these indicators. In addition, the results show that tobacco control can also lead to notable reductions in global cropland use (12–31%) and non-agriculture consumption (1.5–22%). Under the current policy trends (S0), we observe an increase in global value-added and employment, while GHG emissions, agriculture water consumption, and disease burden may also increase, mainly resulting from East Asia and the Pacific. If all countries and regions were able to achieve the 30% reduction target (S1), the disease burden and environmental pressure, especially cropland use, could be substantially mitigated, while maintaining economic gains (Supplementary Data 9). However, East Asia and the Pacific, as well as South Asia, may result in substantial unemployment challenges due to tobacco control policies compared to other regions.

This analysis further looks into the effects of strengthening tobacco control measures in enhanced scenarios S2-S4, based on WHO prevalence projections. We also observe substantial reductions in disease burden, blue water consumption (agriculture and non-agriculture), cropland use (annual and permanent), and GHG emissions, accompanied by an increase in global value-added and a decrease in employment. Regionally, East Asia and Pacific, South Asia, and Europe and Central Asia are expected to contribute largely to sustainable tobacco development through strengthening tobacco control, as appropriate.

The year 2030 is a crucial agenda milestone for achieving global sustainable development. We could face heightened resource demands, increased environmental pressure, and greater health losses under WHO’s prevalence projection if the current trends in tobacco control policy continue by 2030 (Supplementary Data 10). The ambitious target of 5% adult tobacco prevalence has been committed by many countries, such as the Healthy People 2030 goal in the US36 and the “smoke-free” 2030 target in the UK37. Here, we examine the impact of achieving a tobacco-free world by 2030, that is, adult tobacco use prevalence falls to 5% or less in 2030 in all countries (Supplementary Fig. 16). Our analysis suggests that lowering the prevalence rate to 5% or less across all countries by 2030 could experience non-negligible employment and economic losses, despite potential health and environmental benefits (Supplementary Data 12). Despite low prevalence in all countries by 2030, the upstream impacts of tobacco use especially in East Asia and Pacific and Europe and Central Asia are still striking in 2030 (Supplementary Data 13). Advancing tobacco-free ambitions in East Asia and Pacific, South Asia, and Europe and Central Asia, such as China, India, Indonesia, Belgium and Turkey, could account for the majority of these reductions. They currently have a high prevalence of tobacco use among adults.

Discussion

Our results contribute to the advancement of understanding the global upstream impacts of tobacco use in different regions, facilitating the transition to a healthy, smoke-free world and supporting sustainable development. Tobacco use could impose widespread impacts within and between regions through global supply chains, due to unprecedented interconnections of world economy38. This study unveils the impact profiles of tobacco use for 164 countries and regions and the underlying dynamic change mechanism, offering important implications for sustainable production, product supply chains, and final consumption of tobacco. Furthermore, we show that the decrease in prevalence rate could reduce future tobacco use and related impacts, which may be partly offset by global population growth. Taking into account population growth and reduction in the prevalence of tobacco use, scenario analysis quantifies both short-term and long-term changes in upstream impacts owing to future tobacco control in all countries.

Accounting for the disparities of the upstream impacts driven by the two tobacco sectors can help optimize resource management and improve the sustainability of the tobacco industry. The estimated GHG emissions in this study (178 million tons) are noticeably larger than those in previous research2 through life cycle assessments (LCA) (84 million tons), indicating that the impact of tobacco use on climate change has been underestimated. The top ten downstream countries, such as China, India, Indonesia, and the US, account for over 50% of global upstream impacts of tobacco use. This highlights the potential for these countries to contribute substantially to tobacco control and sustainable development. Noticeably, high-income countries, despite generally lower prevalence of smoking among adults, tend to have a larger per capita impact compared with low-income countries. This calls for proactive tobacco control initiatives to reduce individual tobacco use towards a tobacco-free society in these regions. Compared to the growing tobacco sector, the tobacco products sector takes the major responsibility for reducing GHG emissions, non-agriculture blue water consumption, and permanent cropland use. It is necessary to improve production efficiency and optimize the process in upstream sectors. Nevertheless, the growing tobacco and tobacco products sectors both play a crucial role in mitigating agriculture blue water consumption and annual cropland use, as well as disease burden. This informs policy implications for sustainable farming practices and pollution prevention measures, emphasizing the protection of the environment and health of persons related to tobacco cultivation and manufacture, as mentioned in the WHO FCTC Article 18.

Our decomposition analysis highlights the importance of effective tobacco control by reducing the prevalence rate among adults from the consumption perspective, in addition to key production-side factors (production structure and impact intensity). Alongside reducing the prevalence of tobacco use, reducing tobacco use per capita is also important for achieving sustainability benefits. This calls for proactive actions to encourage people to change tobacco use habits, such as raising cigarette prices and promoting alternative products such as nicotine patches, nicotine gum, and strictly regulated e-cigarettes, especially in high-income countries. In addition, we find that the elasticities of global upstream impacts on tobacco consumption vary considerably across regions (Supplementary elasticity analysis). The global upstream impacts are most sensitive to tobacco consumption change in some countries, such as South Korea, Germany, and Denmark, highlighting more targeted and effective policies for tobacco consumption reduction in these regions. Notably, for countries like South Korea, it is more effective to act on reducing consumption than on lowering the prevalence rate.

Although the world has reached a consensus on the need to take action to reduce the prevalence of tobacco use, as outlined in the WHO FCTC, the achievement of established tobacco control targets and their potential impacts remain unknown. Under the existing policy trends, 106 countries and regions cannot achieve the 30% reduction target for the prevalence rate by 2025 (Supplementary Data 14), despite substantial economic and environmental benefits observed in this situation (Supplementary Data 15). In addition, in 2025 the global climate for support for tobacco control is particularly uncertain due to related cuts to funding in the US that may jeopardize the WHO and global tobacco control efforts. At this juncture, the world needs to strengthen the implementation of tobacco control practices, such as tobacco taxes, health warnings, advertising bans and smoking bans, to further decrease tobacco prevalence39. Furthermore, we demonstrate that achieving the 30% reduction target and appropriately enhanced targets by 2027 could lead to substantial environmental, health and economic benefits. However, this may also trigger noteworthy global unemployment challenges. Regionally, East Asia and Pacific, South Asia, and Europe and Central Asia take the major responsibility for tobacco control and account for most of the short- and long-term changes in the resulting impacts, which shows potential synergies between tobacco control targets and sustainable development in these regions.

It should be noted that both short-term and long-term policies should address the potential challenge of unemployment, as our results highlight the marked reduction in job opportunities owing to tobacco control. In addition, given selective deglobalization and more regionalized configurations of global supply chains recently, we develop complementary scenarios that model the structural transformation in the final demand of the targeted regions in 2027 (Supplementary scenario analysis). Our case study reveals that while selective deglobalization may yield net global gains in economic, environmental, and health outcomes, targeted policies are needed to address job losses. Policymakers should pay attention to those job losses especially in agriculture and manufacturing associated with the tobacco industry. Any job losses resulting from falling tobacco consumption can be offset by the creation of new employment opportunities23. To ensure a just transition, it is a priority to provide training, technical assistance, and financial support to tobacco farmers to facilitate their transition to non-tobacco alternatives such as high-value horticultural crops, bioenergy crops, and solar farm operation, as specifically required by the WHO FCTC Article 1740. Furthermore, when people spend less on tobacco, they tend to spend more on other products of some labor-intensive sectors41, such as food, healthcare and education. This may create even more employment in these sectors.

Despite some economic gains of tobacco use observed in our research, the economic costs of tobacco use are also well-documented34,42,43, including increased healthcare expenditures and productivity losses due to premature death and disability. The total economic cost of tobacco use was estimated at US$1436 billion in 2012, equivalent to 1.8% of the global GDP33. This is much higher than the estimated economic gains throughout the global tobacco supply chain in our research. Additionally, the negative environmental externalities associated with disposing of poorly degradable tobacco product waste44, such as cigarette butts and toxic substances, and healthcare waste from treating smoking-related diseases (e.g., plastics, pharmaceuticals) require urgent attention. Policy interventions should hold the global tobacco industry accountable for the economic costs of externalized waste management associated with tobacco use.

However, there is evidence that tobacco companies interfere with tobacco control policymaking, leveraging greenwashing strategies45,46 and the economic dependence on tobacco in tobacco-reliant economies47. This phenomenon is particularly prevalent in low- and middle-income countries3. To counter interference, governments should enforce transparency laws combating greenwashing, require mandatory disclosure of corporate environmental and social impacts, such as EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, implement a strict ‘polluter pays’ principle, expose lobbying, and ban undue corporate sponsorship of policymakers to hold tobacco firms accountable and protect public health policies.

Realizing the projected tobacco control scenarios, especially in tobacco-dependent economies, requires a coherent, multi-phased policy framework. This framework should systematically integrate three critical pillars: first, foundational economic diversification through subsidies for alternative crops and retraining programs for displaced workers to create viable livelihoods; second, phased tobacco control via stepped excise taxes and smoking bans, paired with cessation support to ensure public health gains without exacerbating socioeconomic hardship; and finally, international partnerships for alternative agriculture technology transfer and the provision of transition aid to de-risk the process in the most affected economies. Critically, the synergy between these pillars is essential: successful tobacco control measures liberate household expenditures and public resources that economic diversification redirects toward productive alternatives, while international cooperation accelerates and de-risks this entire process. This integrated framework not only improves public health but also enhances macroeconomic performance by redirecting scarce resources from harmful consumption toward investments in productivity and sustainable income growth. By intensifying tobacco control efforts, it is possible to generate sustainability co-benefits across multiple dimensions and advance the goal of a healthy and tobacco-free world.

Methods

EEMRIO analysis

Economic activity is pulled by primary inputs and driven by final demands simultaneously48. The EEMRIO approach has been extensively utilized to examine environmental impacts associated with economic activities. The consumption-based MRIO analysis perceives the economy as demand-driven using the Leontief input-output model49. The Leontief model traces the total (direct and indirect) upstream impacts driven by the downstream final demand through product supply chains. The model allows us to attribute upstream production activities and embodied environmental, social, and economic changes to final demand, i.e., tobacco use in our study, and further understand the connections between tobacco control and global sustainable development.

Global final demands from the downstream tobacco industries drive global upstream impacts of tobacco use along product supply chains. However, most of the existing input-output literature from the final demand perspective primarily concentrates on quantifying total consumption-based environmental responsibility50, without discussing tobacco use issues. Previous studies mainly focus on the direct effects of tobacco combustion. The upstream impacts driven by tobacco use have not been well discussed in previous studies. Few studies adopt life cycle assessment (LCA) to analyze the environmental impacts of tobacco2,26–29, however, they only consider limited information on the supply chain, and the research scope is limited to individual enterprises and plants, lacking a comprehensive system analysis within the global economic scope.

Scenario analysis under different tobacco control targets

The SDG target 3.a is intended to strengthen the implementation of the WHO FCTC in all countries, as appropriate. The monitoring framework for noncommunicable diseases specifies reducing the prevalence rate of tobacco use in adults aged 15 years and older by 30% in all countries by 2025, compared to the baseline levels in 20108. However, under the existing policies, the world cannot achieve the voluntary target of 30% reduction by the imminent 202551, and only 56 countries can reach this goal57. Therefore, we extend the 30% reduction target to 2027, relative to the 2012 baseline. To explore the potential impacts of tobacco control, we conduct the scenario analysis of lowering the prevalence of tobacco use among adults aged 15 years and older for the world by 2027, following the UN SDG 3.a. Apart from the baseline scenario that uses the projected prevalence rate, other scenarios reduce the prevalence rate either proportionally or to a predetermined lower threshold. That means the variations observed in each scenario, compared to the benchmark level, can be attributed to tobacco control.

First, our scenario analysis focuses on the national voluntary target for 2027. Following this agenda, we design a scenario in which all countries could achieve the 30% reduction target. In addition, we design four scenarios: a baseline scenario featuring the WHO projected prevalence rate for all countries in 2027, and three enhanced scenarios derived from the baseline, each with unique predetermined thresholds that all countries must adhere to.

Furthermore, we explore the potential impacts of building a tobacco-free world by 2030. Some countries, such as the UK and the US, have proposed national targets of a smoke-free society by 2030, in which the prevalence rate of tobacco use in adults aged 15 and above in all countries is no larger than 5%. Therefore, we further conduct a scenario analysis using 5% as the benchmark prevalence rate for all countries in 2030. In detail, based on the WHO estimates for prevalence rate in adults in 2030, the prevalence rate is lowered to 5% for countries with a prevalence rate higher than 5%.

Our scenario analysis utilizes the forecast of MRIO tables from the GLORIA database, incorporating population and prevalence rate projections. The global impacts in different scenarios can be quantified based on Eq. (6) in SDA decomposition. More specifically, the number of adult tobacco users in the target year is calculated by multiplying the projected adult population size and targeted prevalence rate under tobacco control scenarios. Of note, in our scenario analysis, tobacco use per capita in 2025, 2027, and 2030 is calculated by dividing final demand by the estimated number of adult tobacco users, which is determined using the projected prevalence rate from the WHO and the projected adult population size from the UN. More detailed descriptions of scenario settings can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Limitations

In modeling the impacts of tobacco use, we included eight important indicators across environmental, social and economic dimensions that are closely related to tobacco use. An in-depth assessment of all relevant indicators covering the three dimensions simultaneously is inherently difficult and limited by the availability and matching of data. Furthermore, there are certain inconsistencies in the GLORIA database, particularly regarding value-added data in some low- and middle-income countries. We provide validity tests for these value-added data and show that the main conclusions remain unchanged (Supplementary uncertainty assessment). Moreover, a few countries lack data on tobacco prevalence; we supplement this by using values from representative countries (detailed imputation process is provided in the Supplementary Information), which may introduce some uncertainties to the results. Therefore, we have assessed the sensitivity of the model to these imputations. We adjust the imputed tobacco prevalence rates by ±5% and ±10% changes for the countries with missing data. Our primary findings remain unchanged and robust to the data imputation (Supplementary uncertainty assessment). In addition, tobacco may be consumed by children, which is omitted in this study because the data on tobacco use among children are scant in many regions51. This could introduce some uncertainties in our decomposition results. Moreover, per capita tobacco use and the outcomes of tobacco control scenarios could be overestimated, especially in countries with a substantial number of tobacco users under the age of 15.

This research supposes a future reduction in prevalence rate, according to the specific settings of the scenarios. To ensure a clear analysis and highlight the future impacts of tobacco control, our scenario analysis does not include the specific measures or policies required to achieve the tobacco control target, as indicated by the reduction in prevalence rate. Some uncertainties arise from the assumption that the future prevalence rate of tobacco use could be reduced to 5% or less by 2030, as it faces strong resistance from various stakeholders. Furthermore, in our scenario analysis of a tobacco-free world by 2030, we use the latest GLORIA MRIO tables for 2028 and assume that the production structure, consumption per capita, and impact intensity in 2030 are the same as those in 2028, due to limited data availability in 2030. This could be a reasonable assumption for short-term input-output studies38, as it is difficult for consumption behavior to change and for new technologies to emerge and be adopted in the short term. In addition, our analysis presents an analytical framework to investigate how selective deglobalization transmits downstream consumption effects to upstream sectors, while recognizing potential case-by-case divergence in outcomes. Future regionalization scenario research could systematically investigate more complex structural transformations. This includes examining sectoral linkage reconfigurations, such as the shift of intermediate product production via friend-shoring and nearshoring, and their effects across interconnected industries. It could also analyze how spatially heterogeneous trade policies like carbon border adjustments and selective protectionism reshape global trade patterns.

We do not examine the instant emissions and health impacts of direct tobacco combustion during the final use of tobacco, which involves determining country-specific physical quantities, prices, and impact intensities of tobacco consumption. This study focuses on the upstream emissions-related disease burden driven by downstream tobacco use along global supply chains, where tobacco use is measured in monetary terms rather than physical quantities. The lack of country-specific tobacco prices makes it difficult to determine the physical quantities of tobacco ultimately consumed by each country or region. Furthermore, the diversity of tobacco products also adds to the complexity of evaluating the health effects.

Data sources

In this study, our analysis is based on the global MRIO tables from the GLORIA database30,31. The MRIO dataset contains 164 countries and regions, including 4 aggregated regions (namely, the rest of Americas, the rest of Europe, the rest of Africa, and the rest of Asia-Pacific), which cover most of the middle- and low-income countries with a high prevalence rate of tobacco. Each country or region’s economy is comprised of 120 sectors. Therefore, the global MRIO table captures the interactions among 19680 sectors across countries and regions, as illustrated through a 19680×19680 square matrix of intermediate product transactions. Besides extensive coverage of individual countries, the GLORIA dataset also contains detailed sectoral divisions, including tobacco products and growing tobacco sectors. In addition, the GLORIA MRIO tables give detailed satellite account data on the country- and sector-specific stressor indicators, enabling EEMRIO analysis of the sustainability impacts related to tobacco use in different countries.

The population aged 15 and above in 2010, 2020, and the projections for 2025 and 2030 are collected from UN World Population Prospects 2022. The estimated prevalence of tobacco use among adults in 2010 and 2020, and the projected prevalence in 2025 and 2030 are derived from World Health Organization. Especially the prevalence rate in 2027 is estimated through the geometric interpolation method. Given the aggregation of regions, we bridge and match the GLORIA regions with the country classifications in WHO and UN. In SDA decomposition, the monetary variables need to be deflated to exclude the impacts of price fluctuations. Specifically, we utilize the double deflation methods to convert the MRIO tables at current prices into constant 2015 prices, with the implicit price indices from the national accounts of the UN Statistics Division. The implicit price indices of seven sectoral classifications are mapped to 120 sectors of the GLORIA MRIO tables (Supplementary Data 1).
