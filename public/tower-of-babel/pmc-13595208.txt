The orientation of cholesterol’s hydroxy group affects its membrane dynamics and intracellular transport
Authors: Line Lauritsen, Amanda Therese Pauli, Miklas PW Larsen, Peter Reinholdt, Holger A Scheidt, Yuanjian Xu, Douglas F Covey, Laura Depta, Hogan P Bryce-Rogers, Luca Laraia, Jacob Kongsted, Daniel Wüstner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe brain, though less than 10% of body mass, contains about 25% of total cholesterol (CHL), emphasizing CHL’s key role in neuronal function. Many actions of CHL are stereospecific, as shown by differences from its 3α-hydroxy epimer, epicholesterol (epiCHL). How this minor structural change alters membrane properties and sterol transport remains unclear. Here, we compare fluorescent analogs of CHL (cholestatrienol [CTL]) and epiCHL (epicholestatrienol [epiCTL]), which closely mimic their natural counterparts. Biophysical membrane properties, such as flip-flop, acyl-chain ordering, and interbilayer transfer, depend on the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group. Similarly, transport by sterol transport proteins (STPs) and intracellular trafficking of the sterols in human astrocytes are stereospecific. Treatment with 25-hydroxycholesterol increases the uptake of both epimers, but only CTL shows enhanced esterification and lipid droplet storage. These findings demonstrate that subtle CHL structural changes affect cellular homeostasis and establish epiCTL as a useful probe of sterol stereospecificity and trafficking.Keywords: cholesterol, epicholesterol, fluorescence, membrane, intracellular transport, ACAT

Introduction

Cholesterol (CHL) is the most abundant lipid species in the brain, where it is essential for isolating neurons with myelin sheaths and for regulating synaptic activity.1 CHL also controls the activity of membrane receptors and ion channels, and thereby directly impacts cognitive function.2,3,4 CHL is also the precursor of steroid hormones and oxysterols, which play important roles in a variety of processes, including recovery from injuries and stroke, preventing development of neurodegenerative diseases, and maintenance of sterol homeostasis and efflux from the brain.5,6,7,8,9

Many of the effects of CHL on receptor activity are dependent on specific interactions, which are reduced upon structural changes in the sterol molecule. For example, the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group is important (Figure 1A), as CHL’s epimer with a 3-hydroxy group in the α-orientation, epicholesterol (epiCHL), often cannot replace CHL in regulating protein activity.10,11,12 Complicating the matter, CHL also indirectly affects membrane proteins by regulating the biophysical properties of the surrounding lipid bilayer.13,14,15 This includes CHL’s well-known condensing effect, which results in straightening of fatty acyl chains and thereby tighter packing of phospholipids in the fluid phase. As a consequence, the mechanical properties of the bilayer change, including an alteration of the lateral pressure profile, which in principle can change the conformational dynamics of receptors and thereby their activity.14,15,16 Interestingly, not only the interaction with receptors but also to some extent the impact on bulk lipid properties differ between CHL and epiCHL. This has, for example, been shown for the ability to condense membranes, and this was assessed by the membrane dipole potential, which is lower for epiCHL compared to CHL, likely due to differences in hydrogen bonding at the bilayer/water interface.17,18 Similarly, epiCHL mixes less effectively with saturated phosphatidylcholine in model membranes and therefore has a slightly lower ability to induce the biologically relevant liquid-ordered phase, particularly in vesicles with asymmetric lipid composition between the two leaflets.19,20

To our understanding, very little is known about the differing effects of CHL and epiCHL on protein and lipid conformations in membranes, and the role of CHL’s stereochemistry on its trafficking in cells. Why has nature preferred the natural stereochemistry of the 3-hydroxy group of CHL? We argue that, besides the control of membrane biophysical properties and stereospecific interactions with membrane receptors, the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group could impact sterol trafficking and metabolism. Profiling of epiCHL could help uncover which sterol transport proteins (STPs) and sterol-mediated processes that are actually tolerant to the epimerization, with implications for e.g., drug design. For comparison, it has been established that the biophysical properties of CHL compared to its more polar derivatives, such as oxysterols, have a large impact on sterol trafficking and metabolism in cells. For example, CHL, but not oxysterols, such as 27- or 25-hydroxycholesterol (25HC), resides preferentially in the plasma membrane (PM), while both types of sterols can regulate synthesis and esterification of CHL in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER).21,22,23,24,25 Side-chain oxidized CHL derivatives like 27- and 25HC are in general much more potent than CHL in inducing these effects, likely because they interact more specifically with receptors26 and due to their differing affinity for STPs.27 Additionally, they could be more accessible to STPs due to higher partitioning into the aqueous phase.28,29 The latter also results in much more rapid intracellular trafficking of these oxysterols compared to CHL.30,31 The lack of comparable insight into subcellular transport of epiCHL is largely due to a lack of suitable probes to study the trafficking of CHL epimers. Fluorescent analogs of CHL often contain attached dyes, which change the probe’s properties, thereby potentially masking small structural differences. Here, we present an intrinsically fluorescent analog of epiCHL based on the well-known cholestatrienol (CTL), which only contains two additional double bonds in the sterol ring system (Figure 1A). While CTL has the 3-hydroxy group in β-orientation like CHL, the fluorescent epimer, epicholestatrienol (epiCTL), has the 3-hydroxy group in α-orientation, like epiCHL. We show that CTL and epiCTL exhibit similar membrane properties as CHL and epiCHL, that STPs can differentiate between the two epimers, and that the intracellular trafficking of CTL and epiCTL is stereospecific.

Results

The orientation of the 3-hydroxy group of CHL affects its membrane properties

To investigate how the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group of CHL and epiCHL and their fluorescent analogs CTL and epiCTL (Figure 1A) affects the membrane properties, we first examined the impact of these sterols on a 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) bilayer. 2H-NMR experiments, in which deuterium atoms were substituted along the palmitoyl chain of POPC, were used to determine the order parameter in vesicles composed of pure POPC-d31 or POPC-d31 containing 30 mol% of CHL, CTL, epiCHL, or epiCTL (Figure 1B). All four sterols increased the order parameter of the sn-1 chain relative to pure POPC-d31. CHL produced the strongest ordering effect, whereas epiCHL resulted in a slightly reduced order parameter, particularly in the central region of the bilayer. Incorporation of the fluorescent analogs CTL and epiCTL further decreased the order parameter, following a trend similar to CHL and epiCHL, with CTL inducing higher order than epiCTL.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations supported the NMR findings (Figures 1C and 1D). The calculated order parameters for the sn-1 (palmitic acid [PA]) and sn-2 (oleic acid) chains again showed that CHL induced the highest order, followed by epiCHL, with CTL and epiCTL only marginally lower. Analysis of hydrogen bonding revealed that all sterols formed a comparable number of hydrogen bonds (H-bonds) to the POPC lipids (Figures 1E–1H). However, CHL and CTL established significantly more H-bonds to water compared to their epimers, resulting in an overall reduction in the total number of H-bonds for epiCHL and epiCTL to nearly half that of CHL and CTL.

Next, we analyzed the sterol position within the bilayer over a 1,000-ns-long MD simulation. On this timescale, CHL and CTL remained stable in one side of the bilayer without signs of flip-flop (Figure 1I). In contrast, both epiCHL and epiCTL exhibited flip-flop events, which are visible as abrupt changes in their z-position (Figure 1I). To be clear, we note that flip-flop events only occur in some of the shown traces (and indeed, most sterols do not show any events within a 1,000 ns time frame). We define a change in z-position as a flip-flop event, if the sterol has been moved from z > 10 Å to z < −10 Å across the bilayer.

As shown in Figures S2–S6, these flip-flop events can occur through different mechanisms. In some cases, a sterol transfers from one leaflet to the other, keeping the original orientation (orange boxes Figures S4–S6) and then moving back to the original leaflet. For some traces, after a delay, the sterol rotates to the typical orientation with the hydroxyl group facing out (Figure S6 epiCTL 18). In other cases, we find an initial rotation event, which is then followed by a later transfer between leaflets (Figure S6 epiCTL number 47; Figure S4 epiCHL number 54). Occasionally, we even find cases where a sterol may rotate to have the hydroxyl group facing inward toward the membrane center for an extended period of time (black boxes Figure S4) without transferring leaflets. During the simulated timescale (1,000 ns) for three replicas, we have only observed flip-flop events for epiCHL and epiCTL, but not for CHL (Figure S3) and CTL (Figure S5), which remain more stable in z-position and orientation throughout the simulated trajectories.

The failure to observe any flip-flop events for CHL is reasonably consistent with earlier simulations.32 Although the authors of that study were able to observe CHL flip-flop events, this was only when using unbiased coarse-grained simulations (i.e., not all-atom simulations), and primarily in more fluid membrane systems (such as in 1,2-diarachidoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine [DAPC] membranes). More condensed systems, such as dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC)/20% CHL, were estimated to have a flip-flop rate of kflip ≈ 103 Hz. Our unbiased all-atom MD simulations of POPC/30% CHL found no flip-flop events for any of the 60 simulated CHL molecules across three replicas (1 μs each), suggesting kflip < 5 × 103 Hz. Thus, the observation of multiple flip-flop events for epiCHL and epiCTL in unbiased all-atom MD simulations is noteworthy, as it indicates that epimerization of the 3-hydroxy group of CHL substantially lowers the barrier to transbilayer migration even in relatively ordered membrane environments. Part of this effect likely arises from the weaker membrane-ordering effect of the epimers relative to CHL.

A summary of flip-flop frequencies is shown in Figure 1J, which reveals that neither CHL nor CTL migrates to the opposite leaflet during the MD simulations. In contrast, both epiCHL and epiCTL underwent flip-flop events in 7.7% ± 2.1% of the traces, and epiCHL in 6.6% ± 1.4% of the traces. These are calculated based on 3 individual simulation runs of 60 traces. The increased flexibility of epiCHL and epiCTL is likely due to the reduced number of H-bonds formed in the membrane, resulting in a less constrained bilayer positioning. From the hydrogen-bonding analysis, we find that, for CHL and CTL, the most prevalent mode of binding is between the sterol hydroxyl group and water, accounting for around 85% of the H-bonds. The remaining H-bonds are formed between the sterol and the POPC lipids. For the epimers, the total number of H-bonds formed is significantly lower (by about a factor of 1.8), primarily caused by a lower occurrence of sterol-water H-bonds. We also note that epiCHL and epiCTL, unlike CHL and CTL, are able to form a small amount of sterol-sterol H-bonds, although this mode of hydrogen bonding is, in absolute terms, comparably rare (Figures 1E–1H).

Passive and protein-mediated sterol transfer between vesicles is stereospecific

To examine how the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group influences protein-mediated sterol transfer, we employed an in vitro fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay. Donor liposomes (LD) containing CTL or epiCTL served as FRET donors, while acceptor liposomes (LA) were loaded with Dansyl-phosphatidylethanolamine (PE). Mixing LD and LA liposomes allowed monitoring of the fluorescence ratio I/I0 over time. At t = 0, a sterol binding domain of an STP was added (Figure 2A). An increase in I/I0 indicated transfer of the fluorescent sterol from LD to LA, whereas a constant signal reflected no transfer.

Two classes of STPs were investigated.33 The first class comprised the Aster proteins, which facilitate CHL transport from the PM to the ER.34 CTL was transferred most efficiently by Aster-A, less efficiently by Aster-B, and only partially by Aster-C (Figure 2B). In contrast, epiCTL was transported most efficiently by Aster-A and Aster-C, with Aster-B showing moderate activity (Figure 2C). The preference of Aster-C for epiCTL is notable, as it is in line with its preference for the enantiomer of its sterol-inspired inhibitor astercin-2.35

The second class of STPs tested was the steroidogenic acute regulatory protein-related lipid transfer (START) family, which is known to mediate intracellular CHL transport.36 CTL was transferred efficiently by STARD1 and STARD5, less efficiently by STARD4, and not at all by STARD3 (Figure 2D). STARD5 has been reported to bind CHL or cholic acid, with recent data suggesting that cholic acid derivatives may be the endogenous ligands. However, these results support the notion that both classes of lipids could be substrates.27,37 EpiCTL was transported efficiently by STARD5, moderately by STARD4, and was not detectable by STARD1 and STARD3 (Figure 2E). Transport efficiencies of CTL and epiCTL by the eight tested proteins are summarized in the supplemental information and in Table S2, where they are compared to those of dehydroergosterol (DHE) (Figure S17). Interestingly, transport of DHE (for structure see Figure S17A) by the investigated STPs is also different from what is observed for CTL and epiCTL. Here, Aster-B proves to be the most efficient, followed by Aster-A, and last by Aster-C (Figure S17B). For the STARD proteins, STARTD1 and STARD4 prove to be very efficient for DHE (Figure S17C), whereas STARD5 almost does not transfer DHE but is very efficient for both CTL and epiCTL (Figures 2D and 2E).

Interestingly, control experiments performed without the addition of STPs (dotted line [D + A] in Figures 2B–2E) showed stable I/I0 values when the LD contained CTL (Figures 2B and 2D). In contrast, LD containing epiCTL displayed a consistent increase in I/I0 (Figures 2C and 2E). This important result suggests that epiCTL has a higher rate of spontaneous sterol exchange between liposomes compared to CTL. Supporting that notion are earlier findings of enhanced interbilayer transfer of epiCHL compared to CHL.38 Like the higher flip-flop rate observed for epiCTL (Figure 1), increased passive intermembrane exchange is likely a result of less stable membrane interactions of epiCTL compared to CTL. Alternatively, epiCTL could facilitate vesicle fusion, resulting in an apparent increase of sterol transfer between the liposomes.

Stereospecific distribution of CTL and epiCTL in IM-NHA

Given the stereospecificity observed in protein-mediated sterol transfer, we next examined whether CTL and epiCTL distribute differently within cells. Immortalized human astrocytes (IM-NHA) cells were loaded with CTL/BSA or epiCTL/BSA for 24 h, followed by either direct imaging (uptake) or a subsequent 24 h-efflux period (Figure 3A, first row). Cells were co-stained for lipid droplets (LDs, Bodipy) (Figure 3A, second row), late endosomes/lysosomes (LEs/LYs, RhDex) (Figure 3A, third row), or the endocytic recycling compartment (ERC, transferrin-CF640R) (Figure S12). Validation of CTL distribution in the PM can be found in Figure S10, and validation that the sterol loading protocol does not affect the overall sterol distribution was carried out using filipin staining, as shown in Figure S11. CTL fluorescence intensity in cells was consistently higher than that of epiCTL (Figure 3A, first row). Quantification of mean fluorescence intensity per cell revealed a nearly 5-fold higher median intensity per cell for CTL compared to epiCTL (Figures 3B and 3C). Analysis of 24 h of sterol efflux revealed that CTL-treated cells retained ∼72% of their initial fluorescence, compared to only ∼32% for epiCTL-treated cells (Figures 3B and 3C).

To ensure that the integrated fluorescence intensity of CTL and epiCTL is similar, and that the intensity thereby can be used as an indicator of the amount of sterol present in the cells, we recorded fluorescence emission spectra of CTL and epiCTL at equal concentrations (Figures S8A and S8B). The “start” condition is the spectra of CTL and epiCTL loaded on BSA in lipoprotein-deficient serum (LPDS) medium. Here, the integrated intensity of the spectra is shown in Figure S8C, where both CTL and epiCTL (blue bars) have approximately the same integrated intensity, with epiCTL exhibiting slightly higher values. The “uptake” condition is the emission spectra measured in media taken from the dish with cells after 24 h of uptake. The decrease in intensity from start to uptake, in both spectra (Figures S8A and S8B) and in integrated intensity (Figure S8C), should reflect the amount of fluorescent sterol taken up by the cells during the 24 h. This shows an approximately 70% uptake for both CTL and epiCTL (Figure S8D). Comparing spectral results to microscopy results shown in Figure 3, this suggests that an equal amount of CTL and epiCTL is taken up by the cells; however, since the intensity of epiCTL in the cells is 5-fold lower than CTL, this result suggests that epiCTL is converted to something nonfluorescent inside the cells. To determine sterol efflux over a 24-h period, the media containing CTL/BSA or epiCTL/BSA after uptake were exchanged with fresh LPDS media, and the emission spectra were acquired from this media after 24 h of efflux. Here, both the integrated intensity (Figure S8, yellow bar) and the percentage effluxed (Figure S8D) suggests that the cells are effluxing more CTL than epiCTL. Again, comparing to the microscopy results in Figure 3, there is an inconsistency, since they suggest a higher efflux of epiCTL.

This discrepancy suggests that epiCTL may undergo intracellular conversion to a nonfluorescent derivative. Such a process would lead to an underestimation of epiCTL uptake by imaging and could explain the apparent higher efflux measured in cells. To further investigate, this uptake and efflux time series were measured (Figures S13 and S14). Uptake was measured from 1 to 24 h, showing a linear increase in CTL intensity over time, whereas epiCTL enters and distributes faster in the cells, but then plateaus after 8 h (Figures S13B and S13C). The plateau of epiCTL can be a combination of two things: (1) the membranes are filled to capacity with epiCTL, and (2) epiCTL is converted to a nonfluorescent byproduct. Efflux dynamics of both CTL and epiCTL were measured from 1 to 50 h (Figure S14). Here, it is evident that a higher amount of epiCTL is disappearing from the cells over time compared to CTL. In an independent experiment using Triton X-100 to solubilize cells, we found no evidence for self-quenching of either CTL or epiCTL fluorescence in cells (Figure S9). Together with the lower amount of epiCTL found in the efflux media (Figures S8C and S8D), this would indicate that the epiCTL is metabolized to something nonfluorescent by the cells. To test whether epiCTL or eventual metabolites derived from epiCTL can affect cellular physiology, intracellular calcium levels were monitored using the calcium-sensitive dye, Cal520, as an indicator. Cells treated with epiCTL/BSA displayed a significantly elevated calcium concentration compared to cells treated with CTL/BSA and CHL/BSA (Figure S15). Cells treated with epiCHL/BSA also showed a significant increase in calcium levels compared to treatments with CTL/BSA and CHL/BSA; however, not to the same extent as the cells treated with epiCTL/BSA (Figure S15). These results indicate that epiCTL or its metabolites trigger a specific cellular response.

During uptake, a larger fraction of CTL is found in LDs compared to epiCTL (Figure 3D). Upon efflux, CTL partitioning in LDs decreased by ∼35%, whereas epiCTL decreased only ∼11% (Figure 3D). In contrast, partitioning into LEs/LYs was similar for both sterols and remained largely unchanged between uptake and efflux (∼4% variation in the mean for CTL and ∼6% for epiCTL, Figure 3E). The experimental setup is illustrated in Figure 3F, showing uptake from BSA, intracellular distribution, and efflux.

25HC selectively promotes esterification and LD accumulation of CTL, but not epiCTL

To probe mechanistic differences in intracellular trafficking, IM-NHA cells were treated with 25HC, which is known, among others, to stimulate CHL esterification.25,39,40 Treatment with 25HC increased uptake of both CTL and epiCTL (Figures 4A and 4B). In contrast, efflux was reduced by ∼10% for CTL, whereas epiCTL efflux remained unchanged (Figures 4A, 4B, and S16A). Consistent with increased esterification, CTL showed higher accumulation in LDs in cells treated with the oxysterol (Figure 4C). 25HC increased the fractional distribution of CTL, but not epiCTL, in LDs (Figure 4C). Here, 25HC can in principle compete with the fluorescent sterols for esterification, membrane binding sites, and intracellular transport. Since we observed both, a clear increase in uptake of CTL and epiCTL and a pronounced redistribution of CTL in LDs, we hypothesize that 25HC affects sterol uptake and esterification.

To test this hypothesis, cells were treated with avasimibe, an inhibitor of acyl-coenzyme A-acyl transferase (ACAT).41 Avasimibe did not alter the increased uptake of CTL and epiCTL (Figure S16B), indicating that the sterol uptake is ACAT-independent. However, avasimibe reduced the enrichment of CTL in LDs, likely because it blocks the esterification (Figure 4C). It has been shown using purified enzyme that ACAT is a stereospecific enzyme that is highly selective for the orientation of the 3-hydroxy group.42,43,44 Our results support such previous findings obtained from in vitro experiments by showing that the ACAT inhibitor avasimibe selectively reduces the accumulation of the CHL analog CTL in LDs of living cells, while avasimibe does not affect the epimer epiCTL (Figure 4C).

Figure 4D shows a schematic summary of our results obtained in cells. Using intrinsically fluorescent analogs of CHL and epiCHL, we find that 25HC stimulates overall sterol uptake, with no apparent impact on sterol efflux from astrocytes. Previously, it has been shown that 25HC enhances the trafficking of CHL from the PM to the ER,45 and here we show that this effect is not stereospecific. However, the subsequent sterol esterification by ACAT is stereospecific. The overall efflux after 24 h is also stereospecific; here, CTL on its own is released from the LDs to a greater extent than epiCTL, seen as the decrease in upregulation in the LD from uptake to efflux (Figure 4C), whereas there is no change for epiCTL. When adding 25HC, the upregulation of CTL in LDs is also kept under efflux conditions, indicating that the CTL is not released from the LDs. When adding avasimibe, the intracellular distribution of CTL and epiCTL becomes comparable, and no difference is found in droplet-associated fluorescence for either of the stereoisomers when going from uptake to efflux (Figure 4C). These results suggest that CHL efflux is at least partly ACAT-dependent.

Discussion

In this study, we present epiCTL (scheme for synthesis found in Figure 5), a fluorescent analog of epiCHL. Using NMR and MD simulations, we compared its effect on membrane properties not only with that of CTL, a well-established analog of CHL, but also with those of the natural sterols, CHL and epiCHL. CHL and CTL increased bilayer order parameters and acyl chain order more strongly than their epimers (Figures 1B–1D). In contrast, epiCHL and epiCTL exhibited higher membrane mobility, reflected in increased flip-flop rates (Figures 1I and 1J). We attribute this to the reduced number of H-bonds formed by the epimers, epiCHL and epiCTL, within the bilayer (Figures 1E–1H). This agrees with earlier findings by Róg et al.,46 who demonstrated that a reduced amount of H-bonds at the membrane/water interface correlated with an increase in flip-flop rates when comparing CHL and keto-CHL.

Scheme for the synthesis of epiCTL

(1) 7-dehydrocholesterol, (2) cholesta-5,7-dien-3-one, (3) cholesta-5,7-dien-3α-ol, (4) 3α-acetoxycholesta-5,7-diene, and (5) 3α-acetoxycholesta-5,7,9(11)-triene and epiCTL cholesta-5,7,9(11)-trien-3α-ol.

Due to the small and compact size of CHL, the addition of fluorophores often alters its biophysical properties47,48 and intracellular localization.49,50 Fluorophore placement and orientation can strongly affect the recognition of CHL by STPs.27 We also demonstrate that CTL and epiCTL mimic the bilayer positioning of CHL and epiCHL (Figures 1 and S7). The orientation of the 3-hydroxy group alone dictates the binding, and transport by certain STPs. Strikingly, we found that epiCTL has a much higher passive intermembrane transfer than CTL (Figure 2), likely due to the less efficient anchoring of epiCTL in the bilayer. These findings are substantiated by Figure S13, showing a higher initial uptake and intracellular distribution of epiCTL. Together with the results obtained in membrane simulations, these kinetic experiments also suggest that stereospecific hydrogen bonding of CHL is an important parameter in determining sterol trafficking dynamics in cells. Not only the membrane properties but also the recognition and transfer of sterols by STPs are stereospecific. Indeed, Aster-C, STARD1, and STARD4 displayed the highest stereospecificity of the tested STPs (Table S2), emphasizing that CTL and epiCTL are powerful probes for resolving subtle stereospecific differences in sterol trafficking.

We also observed stereospecific behavior of CTL and epiCTL at the cellular level. Although CTL and epiCTL showed a comparable uptake from media (Figure S8), CTL fluorescence was approximately 5-fold higher per cell (Figures 3A–3C). In parallel, epiCTL, but not CTL, triggered elevated cytosolic calcium levels (Figure S15). These findings suggest that epiCTL triggers specific cellular responses that are not activated by either CTL or CHL, and to a lesser extent epiCHL. Also, we cannot rule out that epiCTL is chemically converted in the cells, for example, into a nonfluorescent sterol species with particular activity. Subcellular localization of the two probes was largely comparable, with only modest differences in LD partitioning (Figure 3D). During treatment with epiCTL and less so with epiCHL, we observe an increase in intracellular calcium levels (Figure S15). This could be due to a stereospecific activation of either a channel51 or a receptor11 in the PM or the ER. It could also be a consequence of higher ion permeability in membranes containing epiCHL and epiCTL compared to CHL and CTL, respectively. In fact, it has been reported that epiCHL, compared to CHL, does increase the permeability of Rb+ ions in liposomes prepared from lecithin52 due to higher mobility of the sterols in the bilayer. Further supporting this notion is a recent study showing a strong decrease in calcium permeation across lipid membranes in the presence of CHL, and we speculate that epiCHL and epiCTL are less efficient in preventing passive calcium transport across the membrane, which could increase calcium leak flux from the ER.53,54 In our experiments, epiCHL affects the calcium levels of the cells less than epiCTL (Figure S15), and epiCTL is also found to be slightly more mobile in the bilayer from our MD simulations (Figure 1). Together with the strongly reduced hydrogen bonding capacity, this could result in more pronounced ion permeation across the membrane in the presence of epiCTL. Finally, while being less likely, it cannot be ruled out that epiCTL causes photosensitization of calcium channels, as recently shown for 7-dehydrocholesterol and TRPA1 and TRPV1 channels.55

Our data further suggest that sterol esterification is stereospecific. Treatment with 25HC, a known agonist for ACAT-mediated esterification,25,39,40 enhanced uptake of both CTL and epiCTL, but only promoted LD accumulation of CTL (Figures 4A and 4B). This effect was blocked by the ACAT inhibitor, avasimibe, confirming that esterification of CTL, but not epiCTL, is ACAT-dependent. Interestingly, 25HC did not increase efflux of either sterol under our conditions (Figure 4B), in contrast to its reported role in enhancing CHL efflux in fibroblasts and macrophages via liver X receptor (LXR) α-driven ATP-binding cassette transporter (ABC) A1 upregulation.56 Future studies should be directed toward exploring stereospecific interactions of sterols with LXRs, ABC transporters, and ion channels in living cells, and epiCTL will be an important tool for such experiments.

Given that many membrane proteins contain CHL-binding pockets, our fluorescent analog of epiCHL could be highly useful for studying stereospecific protein-sterol ligand interactions and sterol-mediated signaling. For example, the hedgehog pathway component smoothened,12 the acetylcholine57 and serotonin11 receptors, but also the sterol sensor SCAP bind CHL, but not epiCHL,58 according to some in vitro studies, but the relevance of this stereospecificity for cellular signaling is not understood. Here, epiCTL could be an important tool for both detailed binding studies and live-cell imaging of receptor activation.

Our study reveals that a single structural change of CHL affects its membrane dynamics (number of H-bonds to water and flip flop rate), protein-mediated transport, and intracellular transport. We were able to prove that CTL and epiCTL, our intrinsically fluorescent analogs of CHL and epiCHL, were able to mimic the membrane properties of CHL and epiCHL. This allowed us to resolve differences in protein-mediated transport between model membranes, revealing that STPs can discriminate between the two epimers. The finding that Aster-C and STARD4 preferentially transport epiCTL over CTL, suggests that even a single inversion at the 3-hydroxy position can reshape sterol flux. This stereochemical sensitivity offers opportunities for designing tailored steroidal ligands that selectively modulate CHL transport and metabolism.

Fluorescence microscopy of live astrocytes showed differences in the metabolism of the two epimers. CTL was able to be esterified, and this effect was amplified by treatment with 25HC, and diminished by the addition of avasimibe. Whereas epiCTL was metabolized through another pathway, converting it to something nonfluorescent that triggered an amplified calcium signal. These findings highlight that stereospecificity extends beyond receptor specificity to core aspects of intracellular trafficking and handling of CHL. Beyond providing fundamental insights, epiCTL proved to be a powerful tool to investigate sterol stereospecificity in living cells.

Limitations of the study

Our study has several limitations. First, although epiCTL is a close mimic of epiCHL, as we demonstrate here, its visualization in cells requires UV-optimized microscopy, which is not available in all laboratories. Second, epiCTL, like CTL and other triene sterols, bleaches quite rapidly, making long-term observations impossible. Third, its metabolism in cells might lead to unknown but bioactive compounds, whose identification should be the focus of follow-up work.

NMR spectroscopy

1-palmitoyl-d31-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC-d31)and CHL were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids, Inc. (Alabaster, Al). The desired molar ratio of the respective molecules was first mixed in ethanol. For overnight lyophilization the solvent was evaporated, and the samples were redissolved in cyclohexane. The obtained fluffy powder was hydrated with 50 wt% H2O-buffer (10 mM HEPES, 100 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) and equilibrated by ten freeze-thaw cycles. The static 2H NMR spectra were acquired on a Bruker (Bruker Biospin, Rheinstetten, Germany) DRX300 NMR spectrometer using the quadrupole echo pulse sequence64 on a high-power probe with a 5 mm solenoid sample coil. The relaxation delay was 1 s, the delays between the 90°pulses (of ∼3.2 μs) were 50 μs. Using the depaked spectra65 (see also Figure S1), smoothed order parameter profiles were calculated66 following the procedure described in.67 The measurements were carried out at a temperature of 25°C.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Membrane bilayers containing in each leaflet 70 POPC lipids and 30 sterol molecules were assembled using packmol. Membranes were assembled for CHL, epiCHL, and their fluorescent analogs. The systems also included 10,000 water molecules, 22 K+, and 22 Cl- ions, corresponding to a concentration of about 120 mM KCl. The water molecules were described by the TIP3P force field,68 while the POPC lipids and CHL were described by the amber lipid14 force field.69 Force field parameters for CHL, CTL, epiCHL, and epiCTL were derived using a force matching procedure. An initial parameter set was assigned from GAFF,70 with charges assigned using ESP-fitting, based on r2scan-3c calculations with Orca,62 version 5.0.3. Force-field parameters were then derived using the ForceBalance71 program, based on quantum-mechanical energies and gradients evaluated using r2scan-3c72 calculations using Orca. All bonded parameters were optimized, and symmetry-equivalent parameters were assigned equal parameters. We employed an iterative procedure, where the current iteration of the force field was used to run a 10 ns gas-phase stochastic dynamics trajectory with Gromacs,63 from which 100 structures were extracted in each iteration. Quantum-mechanical energies and gradients were evaluated on these structures, and the force-field parameters were then updated to reproduce the complete set of data as well as possible. We repeated this procedure for 10 iterations with a target temperature of 300K in the stochastic dynamics sampling, followed by an additional 5 iterations at an elevated temperature of 1000K, to obtain a better coverage of the parameter space.

All molecular dynamics simulations were carried out using the Gromacs program,63 version 2023.1. The membranes were equilibrated in a three-stage procedure. First, the structures were minimized for 5000 steps with a steepest descent minimizer to remove any bad contacts. Next, we carried out NVT equilibration (200 ps), and NPT equilibration (2 ns) was carried out. A time-step of 2 fs was used. Long-range electrostatics were treated with the particle mesh Ewald method73 with a cutoff distance of 12 Å. Bonds involving hydrogens were constrained using LINCS.74 The temperature was controlled by a Nose-Hoover thermostat75,76 (towards 298.15K), while the pressure (in the NPT simulations only) was controlled by a semi-isotopic Berendsen barostat77 (towards 1 bar). After equilibration, production molecular dynamics runs (1000 ns) were carried out. In the production simulations, we switched to a Parrinello-Rahman barostat.78 The resulting trajectories were analyzed using Gromacs (for deuterium order parameters), and in-house Python scripts relying on the MDAnalysis library79 for tilt angle distributions, hydrogen bonding, and autocorrelations of the tilting angle.

Sterol transfer assay

Preparation of vesicles

A 2 mM stock solution of DHE (Avanti Polar Lipids), CTL, or epiCTL (for the synthesis of epiCTL see supplemental information) in absolute ethanol was prepared. Dansyl-PE (1 mL, 1 mg/mL) in chloroform was obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids. A stock solution of DOPC in chloroform (10 mg/mL) had previously been prepared from a 25 mg/mL solution (Avanti Polar Lipids). The following steps all took place in glass-vials covered in aluminium foil, to keep the light-sensitive lipids in the dark as much as possible: The stock solutions were mixed in a molar ratio of 90/10 DOPC/ligand for the donor vesicles and 97.5/2.5 Dansyl-PE for the acceptor vesicles to a final volume of 1 mL in chloroform. Evaporation of the solvent under a stream of nitrogen, followed by drying under vacuum overnight, afforded the dried lipid films. The lipid films were hydrated in a buffer of 20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, and 2 mM DTT to a final concentration of 260 μM. The solutions of the lipid films were vortexed extensively until full hydration was observed and sonicated for five min in a 40°C water bath, followed by five freeze-thaw cycles (-196°C to 40°C ). Homogeneous unilamellar vesicles were obtained by extrusion 13 times through a polycarbonate membrane (0.1 μm pore size, Avanti Polar Lipids) at 40°C. Solutions were kept on ice and used on the same day as preparation.

Microplate-based cholesterol transfer assay

In a non-binding clear-bottom 96-well plate (Greiner Bio-one), wells were prepared as follows:

Preparation for run

A master mix of donor and acceptor liposomes was made in buffer (20 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 2 mM DTT) affording a final assay concentration for both donor and acceptor as 12.5 μM (total liposome concentration 25 μM) and a final assay volume of 100 μL. For compound containing runs, protein and compound were incubated at a concentration 20 times the desired assay concentration for 15 min.

The run

95 μL of liposome mixture was then added to each well (maximally 6 wells per run). Fluorescence intensity measurements were performed in a Tecan Spark Cyto plate reader at 25°C, measuring from the bottom at 10 sec intervals. The excitation filter was set at 340 ± 20 nm, and the emission filter was set to 535 ± 20 nm. After approximately 2 minutes, the measurement was paused, the plate was ejected, and 5 μL protein (or protein + compound pre-mix) was added as quickly as possible and mixed with a pipette to a desired final concentration. The measurement was continued, and the total measuring time was 12 min. Data was normalised to I0 of the donor + acceptor (before adding protein). Where spurious fluorescence quenching was observed upon protein addition, data was normalized to the first time point after addition (t = 0.25 min). All data was plotted in GraphPad Prism 5.

Labeling cells with CTL and epiCTL

IM-NHA cells were plated in 35 mm microscope dishes (MatTek,) for sterol trafficking measurements the day prior to loading with the fluorescent sterols. CTL and epiCTL were loaded onto fatty-acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich). In short, this was done by adding 2 ⋅ 10−7 mol of the sterol to a clean glass vial, evaporating the solvent before resuspending it in 10 μL of absolute ethanol and then adding 990 μL of 50 mg/mL BSA in M1-media, resulting in a final concentration of 200 μM of the sterol. Then the solution was vortexed for 5 min, before it was allowed to equilibrate for 0.5-1 hour at room temperature in the dark. The final concentration of ethanol was under 1% (v/v).

For uptake trafficking measurements, IM-NHA were incubated with 20 μM of the sterol/BSA complex and ∼25 μg/mL Rhodamine Dextran (RhDex, Invitrogen™) in LPDS media, DMEM high glucose (Sigma-Aldrich) containing 10% lipoprotein-deficient serum (LPDS, Merck) and 68.8 nM Na-oleate (Sigma-Aldrich) for either 1, 3, 6, 8, or 24 h. For treatment with avasimibe (Bionordika) and/or 25HC (Sigma-Aldrich) they were both added in a concentration of 20 μM together with the addition of sterol/BSA.

The cells prepared for efflux measurements were loaded with 20 μM sterol/BSA in LPDS media one day after plating. 24 h after loading the media was exchanged to fresh LPDS media, and ∼25 μg/mL RhDex was used for stainng LE/LYs, and treatment with avasimibe and/or 25HC was continued. The cells were left to efflux for either 1, 3, 5, 8, 24, or 50 hrs.

Sterol live-cell imaging

Before imaging, the cells were washed carefully with M1-medium, and the lipid droplets (LDs) were labeled by incubating the cells for 10 min at 37°C with 1 μM Bodipy (Invitrogen™), and then they were washed 3×with M1-media.

When imaging the endocytic recycling compartment (ERC) Transferrin-CF640R (biotium) was used. Here, the cells were incubated at 37°C in M1-media containing 20 μg/mL Transferrin-CF640R. Bodipy (1 μM) was added after 10 min of incubation and maintained for the final 10 min, followed by 3× flushing with M1-media.

Abberior STAR ORANGE membrane (abberior GmbH, Cat.no. STORANGE-0206-5NMOL) was used to label the PM to verify CTL distribution in the PM. When adherent to the glass slide IM-NHA cells are spread in xy and rather flat in z. Due to this shallow shape, the PM rarely stands out completely clear. PM labeling was carried out in IM-NHA cells loaded 24 h with CTL/BSA in LPDS media. Here, a 40 nM labeling solution was made from the 10 μM DMSO stock of abberior STAR ORANGE membrane in M1 media, and the cells were incubated with this for 20 min at 37°C. The cells were washed 3x with M1 media before imaging. STAR ORANGE membrane is consisting of a cholesterol membrane anchor linked to the dye via a PEG anchor ensuring the dye is kept in the outer leaflet of the PM. Co-localization between CTL and STAR ORANGE membrane can be seen in Figure S10, highlighting an area of the cell where the CTL distribution is showing a similar pattern as the PM stain, and some signal from intracellular vesicles in the upper right corner.

The cells were imaged at room temperature in M1-media at a UV-optimized widefield epifluorescence Leica DMIRBE microscope using a 63 × 1.3 NA oil immersion objective (Leica Lasertechnik GmbH) containing a 10×extra magnification lens in the emission light path, resulting in a final pixel size of 193 nm. For illumination control, a Lambda SC smart shutter (Sutter Instrument Company) was used. For capturing the images, an Andor Ixon blue EMCCD camera operated at -75°C was driven by the Andor Solis: X-5771 (Solis version: 4.16.30003.0). Transferrin-CF640R signal was captured using the Y5 Leica filter cube with a bandpass filter 620/60 nm as excitation filter, a 660 nm dichromatic mirror, and a 700/75 nm bandpass emission filter. Rh-Dex and STAR ORANGE membrane using the Rhod Et Leica filter cube bandpass filter 530/30 nm as excitation filter, a 560 nm dichromatic mirror, and a 550 nm longpass emission filter. Bodipy was imaged using an L5 Leica filter cube with a bandpass filter 480/40 nm as excitation filter, a 505 nm dichromatic mirror, and a 527/30 nm bandpass emission filter. The sterols CTL and epiCTL were imaged using a custom filter cube from Chroma Technology with a bandpass 335/20 nm excitation filter (Chroma), a 365 nm dichromatic mirror (Chroma), and a bandpass 405/40 nm emission filter (Chroma). When imaging the sterols, a 100-frame bleaching stack with an exposure of 0.4 s/frame was recorded, which was used for background and autofluorescence correction, as described below.

Image analysis of sterol trafficking

All data analysis were carried out using custom-written scripts in MATLAB2023b (The MathWorks, Inc). All UV images of CTL and epiCTL were corrected for autofluorescence by subtracting the last frame in the bleaching stack from the first frame,80,81 resulting in only the true signal from the probe. To obtain single-cell data, the Bodipy signal of the cells was segmented using the built-in cytoplasm 2.0 model (‘cyto2’) in Cellpose,82 which can be run as a function in MATLAB. Here, the model parameters were updated so that they gave the best possible segmentation with a cell diameter of 150 pixels, a flow error threshold of 2.5, and a cell threshold of −5. To exclude cell debris, abnormally small cells, and segmentation artifacts, a size threshold was applied such that objects occupying less than 1% of the image were discarded. When staining the cells with organelle-specific markers, there will be some background staining of the dyes in the cytosol. Since the cells have a varying amount of dye in the cytosol and thickness, it is favorable to do a local background subtraction to enhance the structures of the organelles. Here, this is done by morphological operations, first an erosion and then a dilation (carried out by the MATLAB function ‘imopen’) with a disk size larger than the organelle, which results in an image of the local background. The local background can then be subtracted from the original image, leaving the structures of the wanted organelles. To detect these in an automated manner, the mean signal of the image is calculated, and the mask is defined as the signal above that mean multiplied by a threshold factor. Since the organelles vary a bit in size and brightness, a disk size of ∼1 μm and a threshold of 5 for LDs, a disk size of ∼2.9 μm and a threshold of 2 for RhDex, and a disk size of ∼5.8 μm and a threshold of 2 for the ERC were used. The masks of the single cells were used to calculate the mean UV intensity per cell. The fraction UV for each organelle was calculated by applying the organelle mask within the single cell, and calculating the mean intensity found in the organelle mask divided by the mean of the remaining UV intensity in the cell. Here, a value >1 indicates a higher level of sterol in that organelle, and a value < 1 indicates less sterol in that organelle.

Efflux kinetics of CTL and epiCTL

To compare differences between the efflux kinetics of CTL and epiCTL, time series of the efflux kinetics are acquired, here 24 h loading of either CTL/BSA or epiCTL/BSA are carried out in 12 individual dishes, and then one dish is used pr. time point (Figure S14A). Mean UV intensity over time can then be assessed (Figure S14B). To compare the efflux kinetics of CTL and epiCTL, the median of each time point for each replicate was normalized to the median of the first measurement. All the data combined was fitted to a single exponential with a baseline (Equation 1). Each experiment was also fitted and the standard deviation of between the 3 fits are depicted as the standard deviation shown for each fit in Figure S14C, where each individual dot is the median value of the normalized UV intensity for the experiment at that time point.

The parameters for the overall fit is given in Table S1, where the errors are the 95% confidence bounds associated with the fit. From the curves and the fits it can be seen that CTL and epiCTL has a similar time constant (1/b), being ≈ 2.2 hours for CTL and ≈ 3 hours for epiCTL, however what differs between the two samples is the amplitude which is almost 40% higher for epiCTL than for CTL and the offset, which is almost 30% lower for epiCTL than for CTL. Meaning more CTL is contained in the cells than epiCTL. Comparing the efflux of CTL and epiCTL from the LE/LYs, the fractional distribution does not change over time (Figure S14D), however, the fractional distribution in the LDs changes over time for CTL, whereas it is remaining the same for epiCTL. For CTL, higher fractions are found in the LDs at the earlier time points, 1, 3, and 5 hrs, compared to the later time points, 27 and 50 hrs (Figure S14E).

Filipin staining

To ensure that our cholesterol loading protocol is not interfering with the overall distribution of cholesterol, filipin was used to characterize the free cholesterol distribution in IM-NHA cells grown in full media (Figures S11A–S11C) and IM-NHA cells grown in LPDS media supplemented with 20 μM CHL/BSA (Figures S11D–S11F). IM-NHA were grown in MATTEK chambers under uptake conditions here one chamber was grown for 24 h in LPDS media supplemented with 20 μM CHL/BSA, and one chamber was grown in full media. Cells were washed once with 1X PBS and then fixed for 12 min using ROTI®Histofix (Carl ROTH), then the cells were washed 3x with PBS, and 50 μg/mL filipin in 1X PBS was added to the cells, and they were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, then they were washed 3x with M1 media before imaging. The imaging was carried out on our Leica DMIRBE microscope using the DAPI filter cube (A4 Leica filter cube, band-pass excitation filter: 360/40 nm, dichromatic mirror: 400 nm, band-pass emission filter: 470/40 nm).

Determination of apparent differences in calcium signaling

Cell preparation

IM-NHA were plated in 8-well Ibidi chambers (Ibidi GMBH) for calcium imaging the day prior to loading with the sterols. The cells were loaded with 20μM sterol/BSA 24 h prior to imaging. CHL/BSA and epiCHL/BSA were prepared similar to CTL/BSA and epiCTL/BSA. Labelling with the calcium sensor Cal520-AM (ATT Bioquest) was carried out right before imaging. Here, the cells were stained with 0.5 μM Cal520-AM in M1 media for 60 min at 37°C, and then washed 3 times before imaging.

Imaging of Cal520

Images of our calcium sensor, Cal520, were recorded on a Ti2-Nikon widefield microscope using 488 nm excitation from a LED-illumination source (CoolLED pE-300) through a FITC filter cube (excitation filter: 467-498 nm, dichroic mirror: 506 nm, emission filter: 513-556 nm). Images were captured using a Plan Apo λ20X objective with a NA of 0.75 and 80 ms of exposure on an Andor Zyla VSC-00580 camera resulting in a pixel size of 323.6 nm.

Calcium analysi

Analysis of the Cal520 intensities was carried out in MATLAB, where the cells were segmented using the pretrained model cyto2 in Cellpose, with model parameters being a cell diameter of 100, flow error threshold of 1, cell threshold of -2, and normalized images. From this mask the median Cal520 signal per cell could be calculated and apparent calcium levels for the four different conditions can be visualized in Figure S15E.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with a minimum of three independent replicates unless otherwise stated in the figure legend. When data are presented as cell distributions, statistical significance was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Statistical significance is indicated as follows: p-values>0.05 is non-significant (n.s.), p-values ≤ 0.05 is assigned ∗, p-values ≤ 0.01 is assigned ∗∗, p-values ≤ 0.001 is assigned ∗∗∗, and p-values ≤ 0.0001 is assigned ∗∗∗∗. When comparing two means of three independent replicates, a two sample t-test was used. All statistical analyses were performed in MATLAB2023b.
