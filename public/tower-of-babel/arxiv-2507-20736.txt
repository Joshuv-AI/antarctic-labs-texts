Thermodynamic Constraints on the Emergence of Intersubjectivity in Quantum Systems
Alessandro Candeloro, Tiago Debarba, Felix C. Binder

Abstract
Ideal quantum measurement requires divergent thermodynamic resources. This is a consequence of the third law of thermodynamics, which prohibits the preparation of the measurement pointer in a fully erased, pure state required for the acquisition of perfect, noiseless measurement information. In this work, we investigate the consequences of finite resources in the emergence of intersubjectivity as a model for measurement processes with multiple observers. Here, intersubjectivity refers to a condition in which observers agree on the observed outcome (agreement), and their local random variables exactly reproduce the original random variable for the system observable (probability reproducibility). While agreement and reproducibility are mutually implied in the case of ideal measurement, finite thermodynamic resources constrain each of them. Starting from the third law of thermodynamics, we derive how the achievability of ideal intersubjectivity is affected by restricted thermodynamic resources. Specifically, we establish a no-go theorem concerning perfect intersubjectivity and present a deviation metric to account for the influence of limited resources. We further present attainable bounds for the agreement and bias that are exclusively dependent on the initial state of the environment. In addition, we show that either by cooling or coarse-graining, we can approximate ideal intersubjectivity even with finite resources. This work bridges quantum thermodynamics and the emergence of classicality in the form of intersubjectivity.



I
Introduction

In quantum mechanics, measurement of any system has to be understood not only as a dynamical mechanism but also as a fundamental principle: the associated probabilities are dictated by the Born Rule, and textbook projective measurement is postulated to result in a pure quantum state. This scenario has recently attracted attention due to the discrepancy between idealised projective measurements and the third law of thermodynamics

;
Debarba et al. 2024
;
Mohammady and Miyadera 2023
;

Taranto et al. 2023
. Thermodynamics is primarily concerned with macroscopic systems consisting of multiple constituent parts and their interactions. For this reason, thermodynamic considerations are essential for modelling measurement and the transition from quantum to classical.

The first foundational efforts to explore such a quantum-to-classical transition involved the study of decoherence

;

;
Zurek 2022
. These concepts have recently found a more structured framework in operational objectivity and Spectrum Broadcast Structures (SBS)
Horodecki et al. 2015
;

Nonetheless, the framework developed thus far faces some limitations. First, it does not describe measurement as a process, but just as a target state, if not solely in entropic terms. Furthermore, the discussion is usually limited to projective measurement, implicitly omitting destructive measurement as a possibility. For this reason, in this article, we adopt a broader notion of objectivity known as intersubjectivity

This can be understood as the minimal scenario for information broadcasting with multipartite agreement: multiple environments access local subsystems to extract information about the system’s initial state. In this framework, additional properties, such as non-disturbance of the system or the environment, are not generally required. Only the broadcasting of information is essential

Moreover, thermodynamics limits the ability to create the redundancies necessary to broadcast information encoded in a quantum system

We investigate the constraints imposed by the third law of thermodynamics, and quantify the extent to which information can be broadcast and how consensus about observed outcomes can be achieved when available thermodynamic resources are finite. Our results thereby address the thermodynamic cost of building the type of correlations that result in intersubjectivity, adding to the broader understanding of the thermodynamic value of correlations

We report the following central results: first, we quantify agreement between observers and limitations on it. When the system loses its original information, we observe that interactions aimed at maximising agreement generally result in a proportional bias in the broadcasted information. Second, we discuss methods to overcome these limitations by coarse-graining environments into macrofractions. We show that the amount of achievable agreement between observers exponentially converges to unity with the size of the coarse-grained local pointers. Lastly, we compare the theoretical bound with a standard model for the emergence of objectivity: a star-spin model that results in pure dephasing.

II
Ideal intersubjectivity

Quantum objectivity was first formally articulated within the framework of Quantum Darwinism
Zurek 2003
, developed to encapsulate the essential features of the quantum-to-classical transition. More recently, the notion of objectivity has been refined into stricter formulations, such as Strong Quantum Darwinism
Le and Olaya-Castro 2019
and Spectrum Broadcast Structure
Horodecki et al. 2015
, within an operational framework grounded in information-theoretic principles
Korbicz 2021
. However, these definitions of objectivity face two principal limitations: First, Quantum Darwinism and Strong Quantum Darwinism exclude the physically relevant case of destructive measurement, thereby imposing overly stringent conditions. Second, objectivity is characterised solely by the properties of the final output state, whereas quantum measurement should be regarded as a dynamical physical process. In this work, we address both limitations by introducing a more general, first-principles definition of objectivity, which we term
ideal intersubjectivity
.

We consider the system of interest
S
in an initial state
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
, and a collection of environments
\bm{P}
which are initially uncorrelated as
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}
. Informally, after the joint evolution of system and environments, intersubjectivity is achieved when many observers can independently access information about the initial state of the system
S
, and all observers agree on the observed outcome. In particular, the redundant encoding of the diagonal information of
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
to each environmental partition
P_{i}
can be considered as the broadcasting of the random variable
X
, on the system
S
, described by the probability distribution
\{p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
. The evolution is described by a unitary operator
U
that yields a final state
\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}=U\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}
. More formally, we require that the following three properties be satisfied for ideal intersubjectivity.

Locality
: There exists a joint probability distribution among the environmental subsystems (observers) in terms of
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
local measurements
\bm{\Pi}^{(i)}=\{\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
, with
i=1,...,N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(x_{1},...,x_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}})=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{x}}\right\},

(1)

where the measurement operators
\bm{\Pi}_{\bm{x}}=\otimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\Pi^{(i)}_{x_{i}}
describe the joint measurement. The marginal probabilities

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\neq i}\sum_{x_{j}}\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(x_{1},...,x_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}})=

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}\Pi^{(i)}_{x_{i}}\right\}\,,

(2)

correspond to local measurements by each observer.

Probability reproducibility
: Each observer’s independent measurement obeys the same probability distribution:

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}}(x)=p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)\,,

(3)

for all
x=1,...,d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
and
i,j=1,...,N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
. This property encapsulates the joint unbiasedness of information broadcasting (to local degrees of freedom).

Agreement
: The probability of observing distinct outcomes across the different environments vanishes, i.e.,

\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(x_{1},...,x_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}})\neq 0\iff x_{1}=...=x_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}

(4)

Consequently, the final state of the environment
\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}
is only supported on the agreement subspace
\mathcal{A}=\text{span}\{\otimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
. That is,

\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\otimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\right\}=1

(5)

This condition thus ensures perfect correlations between the different environments.

Ideal intersubjectivity is defined by the simultaneous fulfilment of all three properties. Note that the second and third properties are theory-independent and can be formulated without reference to quantum mechanics. We furthermore make no assumptions regarding the dimension of the Hilbert space, and all the following results hold even in the infinite-dimensional case. The only assumption concerns the number of measurement outcomes, which must be finite, a condition justified by the finite resolution inherent to any realistic measurement procedure.

It is worth noting that the three properties so defined are not independent. Indeed, Ozawa’s Theorem states that if a collection of measurements
\bm{\Pi}^{(i)}
,
i=1,...,N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
satisfies locality and probability reproducibility for a projective measurement on the system
S
, then it also satisfies the agreement condition
Ozawa 2019
.
A general proof of the theorem, which extends Ozawa’s original to mixed states as required here, is provided in App.
A
. The theorem implies that if information about a projective measurement on
S
is perfectly broadcast to multiple environments, then anyone locally accessing these systems will always agree on the observation. In other words, ideal broadcasting implies agreement and, thus, intersubjectivity. However, broadcasting redundant information cannot be achieved with constrained thermodynamic resources
Debarba et al. 2024
, indicating the necessity to align with a thermodynamically consistent framework.

In this scenario, what prerequisites must be fulfilled for observers to reach
agreement
, considering the possible discrepancy between the copies and the original information? Does striving for maximum agreement inherently involve reducing local bias? We address these questions in the following sections.

III
Third law of thermodynamics and intersubjectivity

Ideal intersubjectivity, together with Ozawa’s Theorem introduced in the previous section, provides a framework for understanding the redundant broadcasting of information from a central quantum system to multiple observers. It ensures that all observers have access to consistent information about the system and that they reach agreement on the outcome of any independent measurement run.

On the other hand, the third law of thermodynamics implies that pure states can only be prepared at the cost of infinite resources such as time, complexity, and energy
Taranto et al. 2023
. The same holds for any state with pure subspaces – that is, any state that is not full-rank. In ideal intersubjectivity, pure states are implicitly assumed, but, as we will argue shortly, they are necessary to satisfy the agreement condition given in Eq. (
5
).

The agreement condition in Eq. (
5
) can only be satisfied if the final state is of sufficiently reduced rank, and such a rank-deficient state can only be unitarily reached from an initial state with that same rank. Hence, achieving ideal intersubjectivity requires starting from a severely rank-deficient initial state, a scenario that is physically unrealistic unless infinite resources are expended to cool the environments. For these reasons, thermodynamic constraints suggest that ideal intersubjective agreement cannot be exactly achieved in practice.

We note here that, within thermodynamics, unitary operations describe closed systems. They represent the most natural and optimal choice among those operations that preserve rank, and thus satisfy the third law of thermodynamics. While one might consider more general evolutions, such as completely positive trace-preserving (CPTP) maps that reduce rank, such operations are inconsistent with thermodynamic principles, as they explicitly violate the third law, any unitary dilation in turn requiring rank-deficient input states. Consequently, we restrict our attention to evolutions that do not decrease rank, with unitary dynamics being the most efficient within this class. Nevertheless, our approach is also consistent with more general non-unitary thermal operations
Janzing et al. 2000
;
Brandão et al. 2013
, as auxiliary systems can be coupled to each pointer and subsequently discarded during the readout of the statistics.

The impossibility of achieving agreement under full-rank state preparation, as dictated by the third law, has deeper implications for the framework of intersubjectivity. Ozawa’s Theorem asserts that locality and probability reproducibility together imply agreement. Thus, the logical negation of this statement indicates that if agreement cannot be achieved, then either locality or probability reproducibility must be abandoned. Since locality is essential for intersubjectivity, allowing multiple observers to access information locally from their respective environments, we are forced to abandon or at least weaken the requirement of probability reproducibility, and, by extension, ideal classical broadcasting. We summarise these results in the following observation

Observation 1

(Impossibility of thermodynamically-consistent ideal intersubjectivity)
.

Agreement (Eq. (
5
)) and probability reproducibility (Eq. (
3
)) cannot be satisfied simultaneously, and ideal intersubjectivity cannot be attained without the use of diverging thermodynamic resources.

This result raises an important question: given a finite amount of resources, how closely can we approximate ideal intersubjectivity? In the next section, we address this question in depth, describing the fundamental limits of finite resources to agreement and probability reproducibility.

IV
Finite resource-consistent intersubjectivity

As ideal intersubjectivity is in conflict with the third law of thermodynamics, we now turn to investigating how closely it can be approximated. Recalling the definitions of local measurement probabilities in Eqs. (
1
) and (
2
), we start by introducing a more permissive version of intersubjectivity, which we refer to as
biased joint information broadcasting
:

Definition 1

(Biased joint information broadcasting (BJIB))
.

A process leads to biased joint information broadcasting if many observers can access the same information locally, reproducing a noisy version of information about the initial state of the system
S
:

\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x)=\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}}(x)\neq p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)\,,

(6)

for all
x=0,...,d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1
and for all
i,j=1,...,N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
.

This definition agrees with semi-classical information broadcasting
Debarba et al. 2024
, where the information accessed locally is imperfect with respect to the one originally intended to be copied. Indeed, Eq. (
6
) can be understood as a more permissive and biased version of probability reproducibility in Eq. (
3
).

As stated in Observation
1
, the ability to reproduce redundant information is naturally constrained by the thermodynamic resources in the environments. Here, a finite-resource quantum thermodynamic process is characterised by the unitary interaction of the system state
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
(on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
) with a thermal environment prepared as

\tau_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z_{\beta}}\,,

(7)

with inverse temperature
\beta
, Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{i}E_{i}|E_{i}\rangle\langle E_{i}|
, collective environmental energy levels
E_{i}
, and partition function
Z_{\beta}=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{e^{-\beta H}\right\}
. For non-interacting environmental subsystems, the set
\{\left|\right.\!{E_{i}}\!\left.\right\rangle\}
includes all possible combinations of local environmental energy eigenstates. At non-zero temperature, the state
\tau_{\beta}
is full-rank.

As the information about the system needs to be encoded in subspaces of the environment, we must decompose each local Hilbert space into
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
subspaces
\{|E_{i}\rangle\langle E_{i}|\}_{i\in D_{x}}
, where
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
is the number of outcomes,
D_{x}
is a possibly infinite collection of indices that specify the orthognal subspace corresponding to the
x
th outcome. In this way, we can rewrite our initial environment state as

\tau_{\beta}=\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}A_{x}\,,

(8)

with each orthogonal component
A_{x}
defined as

\displaystyle A_{x}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in D_{x}}\frac{e^{-\beta E_{i}}}{Z_{\beta}}|E_{i}\rangle\langle E_{i}|\,.

(9)

We denote the trace of these orthogonal components as
a(x)=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A_{x}\right\}
, and we also define
\bm{a}=\{a(0),a(1),\ldots,a(d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1)\}
. This decomposition is, in principle, arbitrary, but in the following, we will show how to select and identify the one that allows for optimal intersubjectivity with finite resources. We stress that we have not assumed that the environment has a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, only that the number of outcomes is finite.

We proceed to quantify how close BJIB can get to ideal intersubjectivity. To do so, we define two quantities that capture the deviation from ideal intersubjectivity of a unitary process
U
. First, for the final state
\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}=U\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}
, with the composed pointer prepared as
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}=\tau_{\beta}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
, the
agreement
is quantified by

\displaystyle\text{Agr}\left(U\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}\mathbb{I}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\right\}\,.

(10)

This quantity measures the support of the final state on the agreement subspace
\mathcal{A}
. The value of
\text{Agr}\left(U\right)
reflects the probability on average of the observers to agree on their measurement output. Conversely,
disagreement
is naturally given by
\text{Dis}\left(U\right)=1-\text{Agr}\left(U\right)\,
.

We now have all the ingredients for introducing our main result:

Theorem 1

(Maximum agreement)
.

For biased joint information broadcasting, as defined in
1
, the maximal agreement is

\max_{U}\text{Agr}\left(U\right):=\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}=\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}a_{x}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,.

(11)

and there exists an evolution
U^{\text{opt}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$BJIB$}}}
attains this maximum.

Any other unitary
U
that satisfies BJIB will have a smaller agreement, i.e.,
\text{Agr}\left(U^{\text{}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$BJIB$}}}\right)\leq\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
. The maximally attainable value is in turn definitionally upper-bounded by unity:
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}\leq 1
.

The formal proof of Thm.
1
is provided in App.
B
, with an explicit construction of the optimal unitary which makes Eq. (
11
) achievable. We sketch its essence here: this relies on the decomposition of the initial state of the environment between the agreement subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}}
and its orthogonal part, the disagreement subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
. In this way, the initial state becomes

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}=\rho^{\text{agr}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}+\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\,.

(12)

Now, to have a maximal agreement, we need to focus on how to realise an unbiased information broadcast of the probability
\{p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)\}_{x}
with respect to the agreement subspace. This constraint the unitary evolution on
\mathcal{H}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{A}}
. To fix the unitary also on the disagreement subspace, we enforce the biased joint information broadcast condition in Eq. (
6
). This additionally forces the unitary evolution to be a specific form, eventually fixing the overall unitary evolution on the total Hilbert space.

Theorem
1
establishes an achievable upper bound on the maximum agreement given a fixed amount of resources, as captured by the quantity
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
. The maximal agreement depends only on this function, which is independent of the initial state of the system
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
while being only a function of the initial state of the environments
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}
and on the number of environments
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
that must agree. One can note that
\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}a(x)\equiv\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\right\}=1
, therefore it will only be equal to one, satisfying the ideal intersubjectivity if each local pointer’s initial state has a single support subspace with dimension smaller than
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}/d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
, in other words
\text{rank}(\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})<d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}/d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
. This implies that each individual environment does not need to cool down its local pointers to a rank-
1
state to obtain perfect intersubjective broadcasting. However, it must be cold enough to have a specific support subspace that fits all the system information. Furthermore, one can define the minimum observable disagreement as
\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}=1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
.

A comment on the dynamics that yield the maximum agreement is also necessary. Theorem
1
above provides a class of unitaries that realise the maximum agreement. An underlying Hamiltonian description can be obtained by taking the logarithm of the unitary operator. Rather than further relating such a procedure to basic, physical properties, we here give a simple circuit-inspired implementation of the required unitary itself: this can be achieved by applying a generalised SWAP gate between the system and one environment and then applying a sequence of generalised CNOT gate on the environments.

According to Observation
1
, the information locally encoded in the environments will be biased, as the information cannot be completely copied to each pointer. The local bias can be quantified by the statistical distance between the probability distribution obtained by a local observer
\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}}
and the probability distribution
p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}
on the initial state of
S
that we originally aimed to broadcast,

\displaystyle\text{Bias}_{j}\left(U\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{T}}(\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}},p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}})

(13)

where
D_{\mathrm{T}}(p,q)=\sum_{i}|p_{i}-q_{i}|/2
refers to the
l_{1}
-distance between
\{p(x)\}
and
\{q(x)\}
. We note that for systems satisfying BJIB, the bias will not depend on
j
, so we drop it from our notation in the remainder of the paper and simply use
\text{Bias}\left(U\right)
.

In Theorem
1
, we have only discussed the maximum agreement. We now address the bias of such optimal unitary that we have found, and summarise the result in the following theorem:

Theorem 2

(Bias for optimal BJIB)
.

For the optimal unitary
U^{\text{opt}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$BJIB$}}}
, the local probabilities can be written as a convex combination of the original probability and a noisy contribution

\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x)=\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)+(1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}})\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(x)\,,

(14)

where

\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(x)=\frac{a(x)(1-a(x)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1})}{1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}}\,.

(15)

As a result, the bias is

\text{Bias}\left(U^{\text{\text{opt}}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$BJIB$}}}\right)=\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}D_{\mathrm{T}}(p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x),\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(x)):=\beta_{\bm{a}}^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}\,.

(16)

The detailed proof is provided in App.
C
. Here, one can observe that the bias of the optimal unitary depends on the probability distribution to be copied
\{p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)\}_{x}
. On the other hand, it is proportional to the minimum disagreement, defined as
\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}=1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
. Consequently, as this quantity approaches zero, both perfect agreement and vanishing bias are attained, thereby realising ideal intersubjectivity.

Furthermore, we note that minimum disagreement
\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
can also be written as

\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}=(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1)S_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}(\bm{a})\,,

(17)

where

S_{q}(\bm{p})=\frac{1-\sum_{i}p_{i}^{q}}{q-1}\,,

(18)

is the Tsallis entropy
Tsallis 1988
;
Tsallis 2011
, which has applications in systems where energy and entropy are non-extensive
Krisut and Yoo-Kong 2024
. We thus conclude that the disagreement is proportional to
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
and the Tsallis entropy of
\bm{a}=\{a(0),a(1),\ldots,a(d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1)\}
. Note that for a pure state, the agreement converges to one, and the minimal bias diminishes to zero, as expected.

As the number of environments
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
increases, the upper bound
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
correspondingly decreases, reflecting the increased difficulty of establishing perfect correlations among a larger number of systems under fixed thermodynamic resource constraints. We also note that as
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
gets closer to
1
, bias goes to zero, achieving ideal intersubjectivity. Conversely, for all non-optimal unitaries in Eq. (
6
), the agreement is smaller.

As previously noted, enhancing agreement and reducing bias requires increasing the value of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
as close to unity as possible. This can be achieved by appropriately selecting the dimensions and components of the subspaces
D_{x}
, as discussed in Sec.
IV
. The optimal configuration can be constructed by the following procedure: first, assign the eigenvectors
|E_{i}\rangle\langle E_{i}|
associated with the largest eigenvalues
E_{i}
to the largest subspace
D_{x}
, where “largest” refers to the subspace with the greatest dimension
d_{x}
. Next, assign the eigenvectors corresponding to the second-largest eigenvalues to the second-largest subspace, and so on. By sequentially filling all subspaces in this manner, we maximise
a_{0}
, then
a_{1}
(given the maximal
a_{0}
), and so on, ultimately yielding the highest possible value for
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
. Notably, the grouping
{D_{x}}
is independent of
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
. This optimal arrangement can be realised when there is sufficient freedom to design the local measurement
\{\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
. However, in the presence of physical constraints, one may be limited to performing a fixed measurement
\{\Pi_{x}\}_{x}
. As a result, such restrictions also limit the ability to optimise over the choice of
{D_{x}}
, thereby imposing a corresponding constraint on the attainable value of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
.

Following this initial optimisation step, the most natural strategy to further increase
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
is to increase the purity of the initial environmental states
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}
. However, this approach may be unfeasible if the environments are insufficiently isolated or if the energy required is prohibitively high. For these reasons, in the next section, we introduce an alternative method to improve
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
without increasing the amount of initial resources.

V
coarse-graining

The coarse-graining of the environments has been used to approach ideal objectivity, for instance, in Refs.
Zurek 2003
;
Korbicz 2021
. Here, coarse-graining refers to the grouping of subsystems into a larger composite system, commonly referred to as macrofractions
Blume-Kohout and Zurek 2006
;
Mironowicz et al. 2017
. We show that with this method, we can approximate ideal intersubjectivity even with a finite amount of thermodynamic resources. This is a feasible method for increasing
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
once the initial states and purities of the environments are fixed, thereby establishing coarse-graining as a necessary condition for approaching ideal intersubjectivity. Our result generalises previous model-dependent findings
Mironowicz et al. 2018
;
Lee and Korbicz 2024a
;
Lee and Korbicz 2024b
;
Schwarzhans et al. 2023
, highlighting the essential role of coarse-graining in attaining ideal intersubjectivity within a model-independent framework.

We identify two different methods of coarse-graining. In the first case, we coarse-grain the system by simply incorporating subsystems together and keeping the total number of subsystems
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
constant. This method simply increases the size of the environments and, as we will show, makes
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
larger. This approach is particularly well-suited for infinite-dimensional systems, such as bosonic systems, where analysis is restricted to a finite subset of modes. We adopt a similar strategy in the second case but maintain a fixed total Hilbert space dimension. In doing so, the grouping of subsystems into macrofractions effectively reduces the total number of environments, i.e.,
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}\to N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}^{\mathrm{cg}}
. See Fig.
2
for a pictorial representation.

Whether we use the first or second coarse-graining method, coarse-graining corresponds to a mapping of the original environments
\{P_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
into a new set of coarse-grained environments
\{P_{i}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}\}_{i}
. The total number of environments after coarse-graining depends on the specific method used. However, regardless of the method, in our notation, each coarse-grained environment consists of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
original environments grouped together. Following this redefinition, the initial environmental state is replaced by
\tau_{\beta}^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
, and consequently, the decomposition introduced in Eq. (
9
) must also be updated accordingly. This results in the coarse-grained versions
A_{x}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}
and
a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})
. Formally, when we coarse-grain, the initial coarse-grained environment state is

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}=\bigotimes_{j\in D^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}}=\sum_{x_{1},...,x_{d}}A_{x_{1}}\otimes...\otimes A_{x_{d}}\,,

(19)

where
D^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}
is a collection of indices corresponding to systems constituting the
i
th macrofraction. The dimension of the macrofraction is
{l_{\text{cg}}}=|D^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}|
, which we assume to be independent of
i
, since we consider all new
P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}
to have the same dimension.

We now want to decompose this state as

\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}}=\sum_{x}A^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{x}

(20)

in such a way that
a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{x}\right\}
increases the coarse-grained optimal agreement
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
, as we increase
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. Regardless of the coarse-graining method applied, the problem simply consists in understanding how to construct
A^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{x}
(or
a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})
), from combinations of
A_{x_{1}}\otimes..\otimes A_{x_{d}}
(or
a(x_{1})...a(x_{d})
). After this reconstruction, the original bound
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
gets remapped to
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}:=\sum_{x}a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
, along with
\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
and
\beta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
.

In general, there is no unique way to employ such mapping from original environments to coarse-grained ones. However, for
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}=2
, the following scaling holds

Theorem 3

(Approaching ideal intersubjectivity with coarse-graining)
.

For
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}=2
outcomes, there exists a coarse-graining for which the function
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
scales as

\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}\simeq 1-N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}\frac{e^{-D(\bm{a})({l_{\text{cg}}}-1)}}{\sqrt{{l_{\text{cg}}}}}F(\bm{a})+O(a({l_{\text{cg}}}|1)^{2})\,,

(21)

for
{l_{\text{cg}}}
large. Here,
D(\bm{a})
and
F(\bm{a})
are functions that depend solely on
\bm{a}=\{a(0),a(1),\ldots,a(d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1)\}
and not on the size of the coarse-graining
{l_{\text{cg}}}
.

This theorem is demonstrated in App.
D
, where we also provide a precise definition of the coarse-grained projectors
\{A^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{x}\}_{x}
. Notably,
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
converges exponentially to
1
as a function of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
, indicating that ideal intersubjectivity can, in general, be recovered even under finite resources, provided that subsystems are grouped into sufficiently large macrofractions.

For
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}>2
, deriving an analytical formula becomes particularly challenging. Therefore, to determine whether
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
approaches
1
exponentially fast as
{l_{\text{cg}}}
increases, we numerically explore the scaling of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
. More precisely, we investigate the behaviour of
a({l_{\text{cg}}}|0)
, that is, how the largest probability scales. In fact, to guarantee that
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
increases with
{l_{\text{cg}}}
and approaches
1
, it is sufficient for
a({l_{\text{cg}}}|0)
to also increase with
{l_{\text{cg}}}
and to converge toward
1
.

Inspired by the case
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}=2
, we devise a similar strategy to group products of
a(x_{i})
. We refer the reader to App.
D.2
for details. In Fig.
3
, we show
1-a({l_{\text{cg}}}|0)
as a function of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. A fit of the form
c_{0}e^{c_{1}{l_{\text{cg}}}}
is also provided, along with the fit parameters listed in Table

. We observe that the model provides a good fit, and we argue that
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
approaches 1 exponentially fast with respect to
{l_{\text{cg}}}
, even for
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}>2
. For completeness, we also include the case
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}=2
in the plot. Additionally, we note that the rate of the exponential decay remains nearly constant across the cases
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}>2
, indicating that the functional dependence of
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
does not vary significantly.

We conclude this section with a remark regarding our result. In this section, we showed that coarse-graining, if properly performed as described in App.
D
, can yield ideal intersubjectivity even with finite resources in a model-independent fashion. No other assumptions were made in that regard, apart from unitary evolution. We show in the next section that intersubjectivity can be achieved even without ideal unitary evolution. We conclude that coarse-graining proves to be a sufficient condition to attain ideal intersubjectivity. On the other hand, there are no other strategies that we can devise other than grouping environments together to increase
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}
. It thus becomes a necessary condition as well, without requiring full cooling of all pointers and the consequent use of infinite thermodynamic resources.

Most of the results in Quantum Darwinism and objectivity are Hamiltonian-dependent. In this paper, however, we have established some general results using an approach that does not rely on a specific model and holds for arbitrary unitaries. In this section, we aim to connect our theoretical findings to a standard model of objectivity in order to show how agreement and bias in a specific model relate to our bounds.

We consider a central qubit system
S
coupled to
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
qubits, whose Hamiltonian can be written as

H_{\star}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\sigma_{z}^{({\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}})}\otimes\sigma_{z}^{(i)}\,,

(22)

with
\sigma_{z}^{({\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}})}
the Pauli operator acting on the system, while
\sigma_{z}^{(i)}
acting on
P_{i}
only. This model had been extensively studied in the literature
Zwolak et al. 2009
;
Zwolak et al. 2010
;
Mironowicz et al. 2017
, proving to result in quantum Darwinism and SBS structure in the coarse-grained limit.

The initial state of the system is given by
|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{p_{0}}|0\rangle+\sqrt{1-p_{0}}|1\rangle
, while each environment is initialised in a thermal state diagonal in the
\sigma_{x}^{({\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}})}
basis. The joint evolution under the unitary
U_{\star}(t)
, generated by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
22
), leads to the final state
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}^{t}
. To extract the information that has been broadcast to the environments, we employ a local measurement
\{\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\}_{x=0,1}
on each pointer, optimised to distinguish between the evolved branches of the system state. An analogous procedure is used in the coarse-grained scenario, where the measurement is now defined on the macrofractions, i.e.,
\{\Pi^{(i,{l_{\text{cg}}})}_{x}\}_{x=0,1}
.

To simulate the evolution with a very large number of environmental spins
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
and large macrofractions, we employ a method developed in Ref.
Zwolak et al. 2010
. This approach takes advantage of the fact that dynamics generated by Eq. (
22
) can be efficiently described in terms of spin states, significantly reducing the numerical memory required for the simulation. Further details are provided in App.
E
, while here, we only provide a sketch of the method used.

Given the structure of the Hamiltonian, the unitary evolution can be written in terms of a conditional unitary

U_{\star}(t)=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes\left[V(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\right]+|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes\left[V(-t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\right]\,

(23)

which results in an environmental state at time
t
that reads as

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(t)=p_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}+p_{1}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,,

(24)

where
\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)=V(t)\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}V(t)^{\dagger}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)=V(-t)\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}V(-t)^{\dagger}
are the local environments for the respective branch. The decomposition of the state into spin states allows us to represent the system in a block diagonal form. Further, for a macrofraction of size
{l_{\text{cg}}}
we have that

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}_{{l_{\text{cg}}}}$}}}(t)=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}\left[p_{0}N^{(0)}_{j}(t)+p_{1}N^{(1)}_{j}(t)\right]^{\oplus B_{j}}\,,

(25)

see App.
E
for details and definitions of
N_{j}^{(x)}
and
B_{j}
. Here
N_{j}^{x}
,
x=0,1
are matrices whose dimension is up to
(2{l_{\text{cg}}}+1)\times(2{l_{\text{cg}}}+1)
, which means that we need to evaluate matrices with dimensions that scale linearly with
{l_{\text{cg}}}
rather than exponentially.

Using this structure, we can efficiently simulate the evolution of the total environment and evaluate the optimal measurement for discriminating between the two branches based on optimal discrimination theory
Bae and Kwek 2015
;
Bergou 2010
. Notably, the optimal discrimination measurement can also be expressed in a block-diagonal form, further enhancing computational efficiency.

We simulate a pure-dephasing model with up to
1024
environmental spins and evaluate agreement and bias, respectively, defined in Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
13
), and compare it with respect to our bounds. More specifically, we compute the coarse-grained version of agreement and bias as a function of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
and evaluate their minimum on a typical timescale of the Hamiltonian evolution. We compare these values to the coarse-grained version of our bounds, namely
\gamma^{({l_{\text{cg}}})}_{\bm{a},N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
and
\beta_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}
. We report Disagreement and Bias in Fig.
4
: we observe that, as the coarse-graining size
{l_{\text{cg}}}
increases, not only does
\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
approach zero – consistent with the results of the previous section – but both the minimum disagreement for the pure-dephasing model and the bias also exhibit an exponential decay toward zero, albeit at a slower rate.

We also observe that there is always a gap between our bound and the agreement and bias evaluated for the pure dephasing Hamiltonian. This discrepancy is mainly due to the fact that the pure dephasing evolution described by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
22
) corresponds to a local interaction. In contrast, the optimal unitary evolution might not be local and could require additional fine-tuning. However, the values ultimately approach
0
as the size of the coarse-graining increases. This indicates that locality plays a role in determining the rate at which we approach ideal intersubjectivity, but not whether ideality is ultimately attained by coarse-graining. This further suggests that coarse-graining methods are crucial for universal objectivity, regardless of the model used.

As a final comment on the central spin model, we emphasise that the coarse-graining method also influences the measurement time, i.e., the minimum time required to reach an
\epsilon
-close approximation to the ideal values of bias and agreement. In App.
E
, we plot the agreement and bias as functions of time for various values of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. While the maximum (minimum) agreement (bias) stabilises beyond a certain coarse-graining size, increasing
{l_{\text{cg}}}
still has a significant effect on the time required to reach these extremal values (see Fig.
5
). Specifically, larger coarse-graining sizes lead to a reduction in the minimum time needed to achieve the desired level of precision. This suggests that coarse-graining impacts not only the asymptotic values of agreement and bias but also the dynamics of convergence, potentially accelerating the measurement process – a direction we leave open for future investigation.

VII
Discussions and Conclusions

The interconnection between measurement, thermodynamics, and the emergence of classicality has been of central research interest in quantum thermodynamics and the foundations of quantum theory. Particularly the clash between textbook projective measurement and the third law of thermodynamics has received attention in recent years. We address this question by appealing to quantum intersubjectivity – a more permissive form of objectivity that does not require the system to be preserved when observers gather information about it. Here, ideal intersubjectivity is defined by the requirements of locality, probability, reproducibility, and agreement (between different observers). Ozawa’s Theorem
Ozawa 2019
revisited in Sec.
II
and in App.
A
established agreement as a necessary result of the other two properties.

We first showed that ideal intersubjectivity cannot be unitarily produced between observers that are initially in local thermal equilibrium (Observation 1). This first observation can be directly intuited from the fact that agreement implies a rank-reduced final state compared to the generally full-rank initial state. It naturally leads to the questions of how close we can get to ideal intersubjectivity, if it can be approximated, and at what cost.

Following this observation, our first main result (Theorem
1
) gives a saturable upper bound for the maximal amount of agreement that can be achieved with an initially thermal environment. While the optimal unitary operation that does so is not unique, an explicit construction is provided in App.
B
. The second main result was then to show the unavoidable trade-off incurred when optimising for agreement: a bias is introduced vis-à-vis the original statistics encoded in the system. Thm.
2
quantifies this bias for the case of a maximal-agreement unitary. As the last of our three main results, we showed that coarse-graining (i.e., combining parts of the environment into larger macro-environments) lets us recover perfect agreement arbitrarily closely. This is stated precisely in Thm
3
. Therefore, ideal intersubjectivity is recovered not only in the limiting case of cooling the environment to absolute zero but also at finite temperatures, provided the environment undergoes appropriate coarse-graining. This finding reconciles Ozawa’s conclusions with the conventional macroscopic limit, here formalised as a coarse-graining procedure essential for a consistent quantum-to-classical transition. It parallels a similar recent use of coarse-graining for obtaining objectivity under equilibration of a closed quantum system
Schwarzhans et al. 2023
. We finished by illustrating our results in the relevant limits of the paradigmatic central-spin system in Sec.
VI
.

Extending this work, it would be interesting to investigate how the emergence of intersubjectivity is further constrained by considering a smaller set of processes, such as the ones due to microscopic energy-conservation and thermal operations
Janzing et al. 2000
;
Brandão et al. 2013
;
Ng and Woods 2019
;
Lostaglio 2019
.

Finally, we observed that the speed of measurement, defined as the time scale at which we approach ideal intersubjectivity, strongly depends on the level of coarse-graining applied to the environments. Quantifying this dependence constitutes a tantalising avenue for future research.

Appendix A
Generalisation of Ozawa’s theorem

We present a generalised proof of Ozawa’s Theorem
Ozawa 2019
, extending the original result from pure states to arbitrary density matrices. For clarity, we first consider the minimal case of two environmental subsystems (
N_{P}=2
). The extension to
N_{P}>2
follows naturally through identical reasoning. This generalisation preserves the theorem’s core insight while accommodating the mixed-state scenarios inherent in thermodynamic contexts.

We recall that Ozawa’s theorem states that if locality and probability reproducibility are satisfied, then the agreement condition is automatically fulfilled. In this context, locality implies the existence of local measurements
\{\Pi^{(i)}_{x}\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
and local probability distributions
\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x_{i})
, while probability reproducibility means that each observer’s measurement obeys the same probability distribution originally derived from the system
\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(x)=\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{j}$}}}(x)=p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)
. We can write the probabilities in the Heisenberg picture as

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{1}$}}}(x)=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{S}\otimes\Pi^{(1)}_{x}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{P_{2}}\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:P_{1},P_{2}$}}}\right\}=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(1)}_{x}(t)\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{1}$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{2}$}}}\right\}\,,

(26)

where
\Pi^{(1)}_{x}(t)=U^{\dagger}(\mathbb{I}_{S}\otimes\Pi^{(1)}_{x}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{P_{2}})U
is the operator in the Heisenberg picture. Similarly, this must hold also for
\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{2}$}}}(x)
and
\Pi^{(2)}_{x}(t)
. Assuming now probability reproducibility, we have that

\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(i)}_{x}(t)\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{1}$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{2}$}}}\right\}=\left\langle\right.\!{x}\!\left.\right|\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\left|\right.\!{x}\!\left.\right\rangle\,,

(27)

for
i=1,2
. The last equation must hold for all initial factorised states, and in particular, for any arbitrary pure state
\left|\right.\!{\psi^{0}}\!\left.\right\rangle=\left|\right.\!{\psi^{0}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}}\!\left.\right\rangle\left|\right.\!{\psi^{0}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{1}$}}}}\!\left.\right\rangle\left|\right.\!{\psi^{0}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{2}$}}}}\!\left.\right\rangle
. Given that
A^{(S)}_{x}(0)=|{x}\rangle\!\langle{x}|
, it follows that
\Pi^{(1)}_{x}(t)
and
\Pi^{(2)}_{x}
are all orthogonal projectors and we have that Eq. (
27
) can be written as

A^{(S)}_{x}(0)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\Pi^{(1)}_{x}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle=\Pi^{(2)}_{x}(t)|\psi_{0}\rangle\,.

(28)

This means that, by linearity, it also holds for any separable density matrix
\rho_{0}=\sum_{j}q_{j}|\psi_{0}^{(j)}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}^{(j)}|
(and thus also for any factorised state), implying

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(1)}_{x}(t)\Pi^{(2)}_{y}(t)\rho_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=\delta_{xy}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A^{(S)}_{x}(0)\rho_{0}\right\}=

(29)

\displaystyle=\delta_{xy}\langle x|\rho_{S}|x\rangle\,,

(30)

which is nothing but the agreement condition for two environments. We have thus proved that locality and probability reproducibility imply agreement even in the case of a mixed initial state, extending Ozawa’s result to this scenario.

\displaystyle\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\sum_{y_{i}=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}A_{y_{i}}-\sum_{y=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}A_{y}=\sum_{\{y_{1},...y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}A_{y_{i}}\,,

(60)

where we have introduced the notation
\sum_{\{y_{1},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\}}
to denote the sum over the indices
\{y_{1},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\}
, excluding the case where
y_{1}=y_{2}=...=y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
(that would correspond to the agreement subspace).

Applying the constraint of BJIB given in Eq. (
6
), we need a symmetric broadcast of information across all environments. Given the form of the initial state on
\mathcal{H}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
, this is not possible. Thus, in order to satisfy the BJIB, we must avoid acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}
in order to prevent an asymmetric distribution of the information. We thus conclude that

W=\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}}\,.

(61)

In this way, the global evolution that maximises agreement and satisfies BJIB is

U=V+W=\sum_{x,y=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}|y\rangle\langle x|\otimes T_{x,y}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}+\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{D}}}\,.

(62)

We can now evaluate the bias, completing the proof of Theorem
2
. The action of the whole unitary can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}

\displaystyle=U(\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{agr}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}+\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}+\rho^{\text{off}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}})U^{\dagger}=

(63)

\displaystyle=U\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{agr}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}+U\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}+U\rho^{\text{off}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}=

(64)

\displaystyle=V\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{agr}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}V^{\dagger}+W\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}W^{\dagger}+U\rho^{\text{off}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}U^{\dagger}=

(65)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}^{\text{agr}}+\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}+\tilde{\rho}^{\text{off}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}\,,

(66)

where we have used the fact that
V
acts only on
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{agr}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}
while
W
acts only on
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}

\tilde{\rho}^{\text{off}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}

(67)

The first term yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\Pi_{z}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}\tilde{\rho}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S:\bm{P}$}}}^{\text{agr}}\right\}

(70)

\displaystyle=p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(z)\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}\,,

(71)

while the second yields

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\Pi_{z}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\right\}

(72)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi_{z}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}\left(\sum_{y_{1},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}A_{y_{i}}-\sum_{y}A_{y}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\right)\right\}=

(73)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi_{z}A_{y_{1}}\right\}\prod_{i=2}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A_{y_{i}}\right\}-\sum_{y}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi_{z}A_{y}\right\}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A_{y}\right\}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}

(74)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y_{1}}\delta_{z,y_{1}}a(y_{1})\sum_{y_{2},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\prod_{i=2}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}a(y_{i})-\sum_{y}\delta_{z,y}a(y)a(y)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}

(75)

\displaystyle=a(z)\sum_{y_{2},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\prod_{i=2}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}a(y_{i})-\sum_{y}\delta_{z,y}a(y)a(y)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}\,.

(76)

\sum_{y_{2},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\prod_{i=2}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}a(y_{i})=\sum_{y_{2}}a(y_{2})\sum_{y_{3},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\prod_{i=3}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}a(y_{i})=\sum_{y_{4},...,y_{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}}\prod_{i=3}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}a(y_{i})=...=1\,,

(77)

we have that

\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes\Pi_{z}\otimes\mathbb{I}^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}-1}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}^{\text{dia}}\otimes\rho^{\text{dis}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}\right\}=a(z)-a(z)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,,

(78)

which eventually leads to

\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(z)=p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(z)\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}+a(z)-a(z)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,,

(79)

which can be rewritten as a mixture of probabilities

\tilde{p}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}(z)=\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(z)+(1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}})\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(z)\,,

(80)

where

\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(z)=\frac{a(z)-a(z)^{N}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}{1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}}\,.

(81)

We can eventually evaluate the bias

\displaystyle\text{Bias}\left(U^{\text{\text{opt}}}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$BJIB$}}}\right)

\displaystyle=D_{\mathrm{T}}(p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x),\tilde{p}(x))

(82)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x}|p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x)-(1-\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}})\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(x)|

(83)

\displaystyle=\delta^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}D_{\mathrm{T}}(p_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}(x),\eta_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}(x)).

(84)

This completes the proof of Theorem
2
.

Appendix D
Exponential scaling of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\textbf{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}

Our aim is to demonstrate the exponential approach of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
to
1
in terms of the dimension of the coarse-graining
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. We recall the definition of
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}

\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a}}=\sum_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}a(x)^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,.

(85)

The coarse-grained version of the bound
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
means that we have redefined
\{a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})\}_{x=0}^{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}
in terms of products of
a(x_{i})
. In order to increase
\gamma^{(N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}})}_{\bm{a},{l_{\text{cg}}}}
, we need that
a(0|{l_{\text{cg}}})
grows to
1
when
{l_{\text{cg}}}
increases, while
a(1|{l_{\text{cg}}}),...,a(d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1|{l_{\text{cg}}})
must decrease with
{l_{\text{cg}}}
, in order to concentrate the largest eigenvalues in the
A_{0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}
partition. We can understand this strategy as the one that mimics the closest thing to a pure state.

The
a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})
must be defined as the sum over
a(x_{1})^{l_{1}}...a(x_{r})^{l_{r}}
with
l_{1}+..+l_{r}={l_{\text{cg}}}
, given that our initial state is given simply as in Eq. (
19
), that we report here again

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}=\rho{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},..x_{l_{\text{cg}}}}A_{x_{1}}\otimes...\otimes A_{x_{l_{\text{cg}}}}\,.

(86)

The available eigenvalues to be collected in this

are given from the following expansion

\displaystyle\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\rho{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{l_{\text{cg}}}}\prod_{i=1}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{A_{x_{i}}\right\}=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{l_{\text{cg}}}}\prod_{i=1}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}a(x_{i})=

(87)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k_{0}+...+k_{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}={l_{\text{cg}}}}\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{k_{0},...,k_{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}}a(0)^{k_{0}}...a(d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1)^{k_{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}}\,,

(88)

where in the last step we have used the multinomial theorem, and where

\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{k_{0},...,k_{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}}=\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}!}{k_{0}!...k_{d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}-1}!}

(89)

is the multinomial coefficient. We need to use the elements in the sum in Eq. (
88
) to define the coarse-grained
a(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})
. As one can imagine, dealing with such an expression is not easy. We thus analytically study the simplest scenario with
d_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}=2
in App.
D.1

in App.
D.2

D.1
The case of

In this case, the expression for the expansion simplifies to

(90)

90

(91)

Now, we can expand this as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{k}a(0)^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-k}a(1)^{k}=1-\sum_{k=\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}+1}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{k}a(0)^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-k}a(1)^{k}\,.

Making the change of variable

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{k}a(0)^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-k}a(1)^{k}

(95)

\displaystyle=\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}!}{\left(k^{\prime}+\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}\right)!\left(\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}-k^{\prime}\right)!}=

(97)

(98)

which leads to

Considering that the hypergeometric function can be written as

In our case, we identify

(102)

We eventually get

\displaystyle\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}\left(1,\frac{1-{l_{\text{cg}}}}{2};\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+3}{2};-\frac{a(1)}{a(0)}\right)

(104)

from which we have that

a(0|{l_{\text{cg}}})=1-a(0)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}a(1)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}}\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}}\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}\left(1,\frac{1-{l_{\text{cg}}}}{2};\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+3}{2};-\frac{a(1)}{a(0)}\right)\,.

(105)

Since we are interested in large

\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}}\simeq\frac{2^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}\,.

(106)

The hypergeometric function defined above has the following asymptotic behaviour
Temme 2003
;
Paris 2013
;
Cvitković et al. 2017

\operatorname{{\vphantom{\mathnormal{F}}}{}_{2}\kern-0.5pt\mathnormal{F}_{1}}(b,a+\epsilon\lambda;c+\lambda;z)\sim\frac{1}{(1-\epsilon z)^{b}},\quad|\lambda|\to+\infty\,.

In our case we identify

(108)

As a last step, we consider

a(0)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}a(1)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}}\frac{2^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}=a(1)\left(a(0)a(1)\right)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}2^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}=a(1)\left(2\sqrt{a(0)a(1)}\right)^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}\,.

This quantity decreases as
{l_{\text{cg}}}
increase if

. Without loosing in generality, we can write

In this way

We have equality iff

a(0)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1}{2}}a(1)^{\frac{{l_{\text{cg}}}+1}{2}}\frac{2^{{l_{\text{cg}}}-1/2}}{\sqrt{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}=a(1)e^{-D(\bm{a})({l_{\text{cg}}}-1)}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi{l_{\text{cg}}}}}\,.

At the same time, we have that

Appendix E
Details on pure dephasing Hamiltonian

In this section, we provide the methods for the simulation of a star-shaped quantum system specified by the Hamiltonian

H_{\star}=\frac{g}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\sigma_{z}^{(S)}\otimes\sigma_{z}^{(i)}\,.

(123)

To efficiently simulate this system for large
N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}
, we adopt the approach developed in Ref.
Zwolak et al. 2010
, which utilizes the symmetry of the interaction. In addition to their derivation, here we provide the method we used to efficiently evaluate the optimal discrimination measurement between the two branches, exploiting the block diagonal form of the density matrix.

Considering an initial product state
\rho(0)=\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$S$}}}\otimes_{i}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P_{i}$}}}
, the evolution is given by
U(t)=\exp(-iH_{\star}t)
, which can be expanded as a conditioned unitary

U(t)=|0\rangle\langle 0|\otimes[V(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}]+|1\rangle\langle 1|\otimes[V(-t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}]\,,

(124)

where
V(t)
is a unitary matrix given as
V(t)=\exp(-itg\sigma_{z}/2)
. We are mainly interested in the evolution of the environments, which can be easily written as

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(t)=p_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}+p_{1}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}\,,

(125)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=V(t)\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}V(t)^{\dagger}

(126)

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=V(-t)\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}V(-t)^{\dagger}

(127)

is the evolution of a single environment state, while
\rho_{{}_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}
corresponds to its initial state. Further, the state of a coarse-grained macrofraction has the same structure, i.e., for any coarse-graining of size
{l_{\text{cg}}}
we have that

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}(t)=p_{0}\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}+p_{1}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}\,.

(128)

The strategy for evaluating such an evolution involves rewriting the two branch states,
\tilde{\rho}_{x}(t)^{\otimes N}
,
x=0,1
, as direct sums of total spin states. This reformulation allows the matrix to be expressed in a block-diagonal form, significantly reducing the memory cost of the computation. Specifically, the largest block to handle has dimensions
(2N+1)\times(2N+1)
, compared to the full density matrix of the same fraction, which scales as
2^{N}\times 2^{N}
. This simplification is crucial in our analysis, as it enables us to efficiently determine eigenvalues and the optimal discrimination measurement, and thus investigate how agreement and bias scale with the increasing size of the macrofraction.

The first step to write
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}_{{l_{\text{cg}}}}$}}}(t)
in block diagonal form consists of diagonalising the local density matrix
\tilde{\rho}_{x}(t)
. The diagonalisation can be understood as a rotation of the two-level system, to change the representation from
\sigma_{z}
to
\sigma_{\vec{n}}
, where
\vec{n}
is the vector in the Bloch sphere along which the density matrix is diagonal. The rotation can be written as

R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)=e^{-i\alpha J_{z}}e^{-i\beta J_{y}}e^{-i\gamma J_{z}}\,,

(129)

where
J_{y}
and
J_{z}
are the components of the angular momentum in the fundamental representation, i.e., proportional to the Pauli matrices. Therefore, we have that

R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)R(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)^{\dagger}=\textup{Diag}[\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}]\,,

(130)

and similarly for the other branch

R(-\alpha,-\beta,-\gamma)\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)R(-\alpha,-\beta,-\gamma)^{\dagger}=\textup{Diag}[\lambda_{-},\lambda_{+}]\,.

(131)

Following
Zwolak et al. 2010
, the rotation angles can be written as

\displaystyle\alpha=\gamma

\displaystyle=t

(132)

\displaystyle\sin(\beta/2)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\rho_{00}-\lambda_{+}}{\sqrt{|\rho_{01}|^{2}+(\rho_{00}-\lambda_{+})^{2}}}

(133)

\displaystyle\cos(\beta/2)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{01}}{\sqrt{|\rho_{01}|^{2}+(\rho_{00}-\lambda_{+})^{2}}}\,,

(134)

where
\rho_{00}=\langle 0|\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}|0\rangle
and
\rho_{01}=\langle 0|\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}|1\rangle
. We note that
\beta
is time-independent, as well as
\lambda_{\pm}
, and thus can be computed at the start of the simulation.

At this point, we can write
\textup{Diag}[\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}]^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
as the direct sum of total spin states using the Clebsch-Gordan coefficients

\textup{Diag}[\lambda_{+},\lambda_{-}]^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}[M_{j}]^{\oplus B_{j}}\,,

(135)

where we have defined

M_{j}^{(0)}=\textup{Diag}\left[\lambda_{+}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2+j}\lambda_{-}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2-j},...,\lambda_{+}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2-j}\lambda_{-}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2+j}\right]\,,

(136)

and

B_{j}=\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2-j}-\binom{{l_{\text{cg}}}}{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2-j-1}\,.

(137)

The very same step can be performed for the second branch, just by swapping
\lambda_{+}
and
\lambda_{-}
, obtaining
M_{j}^{(1)}
. In this representation, the basis of the density matrix
\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)
corresponds to
\{|j,m\rangle_{\vec{n}}\}
, while for the
\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)
corresponds to
\{|j,m\rangle_{\vec{n}^{\prime}}\}
. In order to have a block diagonal representation of
\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}_{{l_{\text{cg}}}}$}}}(t)
, we need to rotate back the two branch states in the same basis
\{|j,m\rangle_{z}\}
. To do so, we rotate each block with the rotation
R^{j}(-\gamma,\beta,\alpha)
, where
j
corresponds to the representation of the corresponding block. In other words

R^{j}(-\gamma,-\beta,-\alpha)=e^{i\gamma J_{z}}e^{i\beta J_{y}}e^{i\alpha J_{z}}\,,

(138)

where now
J_{y}
and
J_{z}
are the spin in the
j
th representation. In this way, we get into the new representation

\tilde{\rho}_{x}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}[N^{(x)}_{j}(t)]^{\oplus B_{j}}\,,

(139)

with
x=0,1
and where

N_{j}^{(0)}(t)=e^{i\gamma J_{z}}e^{i\beta J_{y}}e^{i\alpha J_{z}}M_{j}^{(0)}e^{-i\gamma J_{z}}e^{-i\beta J_{y}}e^{-i\alpha J_{z}}\,,

(140)

and similarly for
N_{j}^{(1)}(t)
. In this way, both branch states are written on the same basis and the total state can be written as

\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}(t)=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}\left[p_{0}N^{(0)}_{j}(t)+p_{1}N^{(1)}_{j}(t)\right]^{\oplus B_{j}}\,.

(141)

Given that we want to optimally discrimiante between the two branches, we need to find a measurement
\{\Pi_{0}(t),\Pi_{1}(t)\}
that optimally discriminate between
\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
. To do so, we use optimal discrimination theory
Bergou 2010
and we identify the optimal measurement for discrimination as the one to evaluate bias and agreement as per the definition in Eq. (
13
) and Eq. (
10
) respectively.

For binary discrimination of two equiprobable hypotheses
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, the strategy to find the optimal measurement is based on the operator

\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho_{0}-\rho_{1}\right)\,.

(142)

The optimal measurement can be found by diagonalising this operator
\Lambda
. If we denote
\{\lambda_{k},|\phi_{k}\rangle\}_{k}
the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, we can number them in such a way that

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}<0

\displaystyle\text{ for }1\leq k<k_{0}

(143)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}>0

\displaystyle\text{ for }k_{0}\leq k\leq D

(144)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}=0

\displaystyle\text{ for }D<k\leq D_{\Lambda}\,,

(145)

where
D_{\Lambda}
is the dimension of
\Lambda
. Then the optimal measurement for the discrimination is given as

\displaystyle\Pi_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{k_{0}-1}|\phi_{k}\rangle\langle\phi_{k}|

(146)

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{D_{\Lambda}}|\phi_{k}\rangle\langle\phi_{k}|\,.

(147)

This means that in the one-shot scenario, if we perform measurement
\{\Pi_{1},\Pi_{0}\}
and we observe outcome
x=0,1
, we can infer that the state is
\rho_{x}
with some probability of error given as
P_{\textup{e}}=1/2(1-\textnormal{Tr}\left\{|\Lambda|\right\})
.

In our case, the two hypotheses corresponds to
\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
and
\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}
, and to compute the optimal measurement to discriminate them, we need to evaluate
\Lambda
and its eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Thanks to the block diagonal matrix representation, this can be computed efficiently as follows. Given the representation in the spin basis
\{|j,m\rangle\}
is given as Eq. (
139
), we have that

\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}\left[N_{j}^{(0)}(t)-N_{j}^{(1)}(t)\right]^{\oplus B_{j}}\,.

(148)

We clearly see here that we do not need to diagonalise the full
\Lambda
, but only each block
N_{j}^{(0)}(t)-N_{j}^{(1)}(t)
, whose maximum dimension scales as
2{l_{\text{cg}}}+1\times 2{l_{\text{cg}}}+1
, i.e., linearly with
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. To each block corresponds the pair of eigenvalues and eigenvectors
\{\lambda_{k}^{(j)},|\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\rangle\}
, and if
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}<0
then the eigenvector will correspond to outcome
x=1
, and to otucome
x=0
if
\lambda_{k}^{(j)}\geq 0
. By denoting
D^{0}_{j}
the set of
k_{j}
for which
\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}\geq 0
and
D^{1}_{j}
the set of
k_{j}
for which
\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}<0
, we have that on a specific block
\Pi^{(0)}_{j}=\sum_{k_{j}\in D^{0}_{j}}|\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}\rangle\langle\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}|
and
\Pi^{(1)}_{j}=\sum_{k_{j}\in D^{1}_{j}}|\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}\rangle\langle\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}|
. We eventually get the optimal discrimination measurement as

\Pi^{(x)}=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}\left[\Pi^{(x)}_{j}\right]^{\oplus B_{j}},\quad x=0,1\,.

(149)

This means that if we perform this pair of measurements, we would obtain

\displaystyle p_{t}(x|{l_{\text{cg}}})=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(x)}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P^{{l_{\text{cg}}}}_{i}$}}}(t)\right\}

\displaystyle=p_{0}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(x)}\tilde{\rho}_{0}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}\right\}+p_{1}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(x)}\tilde{\rho}_{1}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}\right\}=

(150)

\displaystyle=p_{0}p_{t}(x|\rho_{0}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}})+p_{1}p_{t}(x|\rho_{1}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}})\,,

(151)

where

\displaystyle p_{t}(x|\rho_{y}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}})

\displaystyle=\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(x)}\tilde{\rho}_{y}(t)^{\otimes{l_{\text{cg}}}}\right\}=

(152)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}B_{j}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\Pi^{(x)}_{j}N_{j}^{(y)}(t)\right\}=

(153)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{{l_{\text{cg}}}/2}B_{j}\sum_{k_{j}\in D^{x}_{j}}\langle\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}|N_{j}^{(y)}(t)|\lambda_{k_{j}}^{(j)}\rangle\,.

(154)

Using the latter form, we can thus evaluate efficiently the probabilities for the bias and agreement by just diagonalising the blocks
N_{j}^{(0)}(t)-N_{j}^{(1)}(t)
and computing the latter formula. The bias thus becomes

\text{Bias}\left(U_{\star}(t)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|p_{t}(0|{l_{\text{cg}}})-p_{0}\right|+\left|p_{t}(1|{l_{\text{cg}}})-p_{1}\right|\right)\,,

(155)

while the agreement is given as

\text{Agr}\left(U_{\star}(t)\right)=\sum_{x=0}^{1}\textnormal{Tr}\left\{\left[\Pi^{(x)}\right]^{\otimes\frac{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}{{l_{\text{cg}}}}}\rho_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$\bm{P}$}}}(t)\right\}=p_{0}p_{t}(0|{l_{\text{cg}}})^{\frac{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}{{l_{\text{cg}}}}}+p_{1}p_{t}(1|{l_{\text{cg}}})^{\frac{N_{\hskip-0.5pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\tiny{$P$}}}}{{l_{\text{cg}}}}}\,.

(156)

We report these two quantities as a function of time
t
in Fig.

.

Coarse-graining and time of measurement in the star spin model
H_{\star}

In this subsection, we comment on the time evolution of agreement and bias for the
H_{\star}
model, as reported in Fig.
5
.

As we see from this plot, coarse-graining significantly influences the evolution of the two quantities in a similar manner. As
{l_{\text{cg}}}
increases, both the maximum value and the duration for which we stay close to it increase. After some threshold value of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
, there is no visible difference in the maximum or minimum values. That difference becomes evident and can only be appreciated in the logarithmic plot shown in Fig.
4
.

However, even after reaching such a threshold, increasing
{l_{\text{cg}}}
continues to have some effect, particularly on the threshold time at which the plateau is reached. Specifically, larger coarse-graining enables the two quantities to attain their ideal value more quickly and maintain it for a longer time, forming a stable plateau. This suggests that coarse-graining also impacts the speed of measurement, and we conjecture that there exists a threshold value of
{l_{\text{cg}}}
after which the minimum time required to reach the stable plateau does not decrease as we increase
{l_{\text{cg}}}
. Investigating this behaviour in detail is left for future work.
