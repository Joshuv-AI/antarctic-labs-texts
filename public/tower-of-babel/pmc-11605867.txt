Perception of undergraduate medical students and examiners towards grand objective structured clinical examination
Authors: Dawan J. Hawezy, Saman Taher Barzinjy, Govand Saadadin, Gasheen Abdilwahid Hawezy, Waleed Baqer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe assessment of undergraduate medical students consists of a written component and an advanced clinical competency test that evaluates the students’ skills. The Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) were only implemented in most medical schools in the last ten years, similar in many developing countries. It was first used with other clinical assessment methods. This study was designed to investigate how medical students in the fourth and sixth grades and examiners perceived the unique format, features, and quality of the Grand Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (GOSCE), a novel method for assessing students. The aim was to understand how this innovative method, distinct from traditional assessment methods, prepares students for real-world scenarios where they encounter different specialty cases. Additionally, the study aimed to understand the significant role and challenges faced by the Faculty of General Medicine at Koya University in implementing this type of OSCE and to identify potential improvements to enhance the quality of the assessment process.MethodsFollowing the administration of two distinct GOSCE examinations for the fourth and sixth stages, feedback forms were created using Google Forms and distributed to students and examiners. The feedback forms covered all aspects of the examination process. The responses were then rigorously analyzed using the scientific tool SPSS, allowing for a comparison between students from both stages and the faculty and external examiners.ResultsIn the survey of 104 undergraduate students (78% of attendants), the majority (84n,80%) rated the fairness of the assessment as good, which is the primary goal of GOSCE. A large percentage (89n,85.6%) agreed that the examination covered a wide range of clinical skills. Eighty students (76.85%) believed the patients cooperated and found the findings precise; on the other hand, The obtained responses from 24 out of 33 faculty staff members (75%) and 29 out of 35 external examiners (82%) strongly agreed that the examination adequately covered a wide variety of clinical abilities. Only two of 68 examiners (2.9%) believed the examination needed more to provide a learning opportunity.ConclusionWith the insightful suggestions for improvement provided by the participants, GOSCE has a promising potential to evolve into a fair, objective clinical tool for assessing medical students, instilling a sense of optimism for its future development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06388-w.

Introduction

Traditional techniques for evaluating medical students’ clinical competency (short and long cases) have several drawbacks, including a lack of uniformity and organization and biased reviews with low reliability and validity [1]. To address numerous shortcomings and enhance the clinical performance of medical students, the objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) was implemented in the 1970s [2, 3]. It is an evaluation technique created by Harden and his associates to measure the clinical performance of the applicants precisely. Based on Miller’s competency pyramid, competence is evaluated at the “show how” performance level [4, 5]. Since its launch, the OSCE has become a popular tool for assessing clinical competency [6].

The OSCE has played a significant role in evaluating clinical competency in health professions education worldwide. Its use is essential in experiential training, where students’ performance in practice situations must be effectively assessed. The competencies examined at different OSCE stations may range significantly in complexity, making it a versatile tool for evaluating a wide range of clinical skills [7].

Acceptability is a crucial part of an evaluation considering several factors, including feasibility, time restrictions, cultural acceptability, and cost efficiency. The OSCE’s global acceptance, involving educators, stakeholders, administrators, patients, and, most importantly, students, is a testament to its success and potential for further improvement. This widespread acceptance not only reassures us of the OSCE’s effectiveness but also its potential for further refinement.

In Iraq, the assessment of senior medical students consists of a written component and an advanced clinical competency test that evaluates the students’ skills. The Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE) was only implemented in most medical schools in the last ten years, similar in many developing countries. It was first used with other clinical assessment methods; it was done over several days, with various specialties participating (for example, separate OSCEs for pediatrics, obstetrics, gynecology, and medicine). Different formats have been tried in the Kurdistan region, Iraq, first introduced in 2007 at Hawler Medical University. These include incorporating a global rating system into the checklist, utilizing a blueprint to ensure content validity, and creating a comprehensive clinical exam that evaluates clinical competence in all specialties daily [8–10].

Above all, OSCE is resource-dependent and fraught with pitfalls, mainly occurring concurrently at five sites. As a result, cautious planning and structuring are necessary to preserve equality and consistency in several areas, including testing supplies, setup, and apparatus [11].

The OSCE format was successfully introduced by the University of Koya’s Faculty of General Medicine in 2020 for the clinical assessment of sixth-year students pursuing a Bachelor of Medicine and Surgery (MBChB). Moreover, the OSCE format was introduced for fourth-year students in the same year that it was, and third-year students began taking the same kind of evaluation in 2022. This successful implementation is a testament to the institution’s progress. Until the integrated medical curriculum was implemented in 2023, the conventional test format was maintained for the remaining phases. Preclinical phase 1 (1–3 years) and clinical phase 2 (4–6 years) comprise the new curriculum’s two phases. In years two through six of the training programs, an OSCE will be given in addition to a summative exam.

This OSCE arrangement supplants the conventional OSCE format, with the distinction that the Grand OSCE (GOSCE) encompasses all subspecialties collectively rather than through separate examinations. Additionally, certain stations will feature integrated specialties, enhancing students’ preparedness for future professionalism.

The OSCE can be very important when assessing students’ knowledge and abilities, reviewing the curriculum, and evaluating instruction. In light of the revised curriculum, this study was designed to investigate how medical students in the fourth and sixth grades and examiners perceived the format, features, and quality of the GOSCE, a new method for assessing students. The aim was to understand how this method prepares students for real-world scenarios where they encounter different specialty cases. Additionally, the study aimed to understand the challenges faced by the Faculty of General Medicine at Koya University in implementing this type of OSCE and to identify potential improvements to enhance the quality of the assessment process.

Materials and methods

Study design

This qualitative study was based on an online open-ended questionnaire survey.

Setting and time. This study was carried out in the Faculty of Medicine, Koya University, Koya, Erbil, Kurdistan Region of Iraq, immediately after finishing the 2nd round examinations at the end of the 2023–2024 academic year.

Participants

Target population

The study population comprises students from the fourth and sixth stages of Koya medical faculty who took part in GOSCE examinations on two different days. Additionally, faculty examiners and external examiners involved in all stations of both examinations were included in the research.

Sampling method

Convenience sampling was used to select 132 students from the 4th and 6th years. The participants were invited electronically to fill out a Google form about their experiences and perceptions of the GOSCE in the faculty of Medicine. A revised questionnaire was prepared using the Google form and sent via the students’ representatives to all the students of the mentioned stages (132 students).

Another form was given to the faculty staff and external examiners, who participated in both examinations to obtain their feedback about the process.

After the questionnaire was completed, it was distributed to five specialists in medical education. These specialists took notes on the questionnaire, and then all of the problems associated with the alterations to the questions were resolved.

A pilot study (to compute R-value) In the pilot study, ten students were chosen randomly from the same group of people who were the study’s target. These students were then requested to answer the questions, and Cronbach’s alpha was calculated according to the reliability, which was found to be 0.78. This outcome was then disregarded for the final analysis.

Data collection

The questionnaire was taken from research done for the assessment of GOSCE in medical schools for Malaysian pharmacology students, Basra medical students, and Sulaymaniyah medical students [7, 10, 11] and comprised three Sect. (29 questions); first section was composed of demographic data from the respondents, second section consisting of 26 closed-ended questions in which the Likert scale has been used for them in the following format: strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree, strongly disagree, very bad, bad, neutral, good, very good, necessary and unnecessary, the closed-ended questions were related to the student’s opinions and feedbacks regarding the GOSCE, the last section of the questionnaire form composed of one open-ended question regarding any suggestions, determining solid and weak points, time adequacy for task performance and recommendations for improvement and better arrangements of the exam.

The form was sent to all students and examiners participating in the GOSCE examination; students and examiners who did not fill out the form or did not respond were excluded from the study.

Data analysis

Version 23.0 of the SPSS software was used to analyze the questions on the forms. The interpretable answers were compiled and arranged into groups. The authors examined the comments and identified their major themes to conduct a thematic analysis of the responses using standard coding procedures. This was part of the qualitative data analysis. These concepts were used to create an organized code classification. The data were electronically coded utilizing a sequence of iterative stages, and the coding structure was examined and adjusted several times as new understandings and connections between the themes found in the comments were discovered. Every open item underwent independent analysis and reporting. When required, multiple responses were programmed for every subject. Only one duplicated response was recorded. Missing data was assigned to illegible, blank, or off-topic replies.

Regarding sociodemographic factors, 59 students (56.7%) identified as female, 45 students lived in dormitories, 29 were from the city, and the remaining came from outside the town on exam day. Discussion

To address the issue of fairness among students, which is a significant concern in terms of patients’ conditions and the selection of teachers in most OSCE examinations, particularly in our faculty located in a small town with a limited number of patients and a majority of external examiners as teachers, we have decided to conduct all examinations on a single day instead of having separate OSCE assessments.

Compared to Sediq et al. and al-Khateeb et al. [9, 12], which are done in Sulaymaniyah and Hawler colleges, respectively, our students’ satisfaction with all aspects of arrangement and the scientific base is better, although all examinations are done together, the difference may be due to the small number of students in comparing with them. Out of our region, we compared our results with Ansari T et al. and Alghamdi KS et al. [13, 14]. Students were less satisfied with the fairness of the examination and other aspects of osce arrangement and scientific bases.

In other universities, the OSCE examination was conducted separately. We implemented a new and challenging method of performing the OSCE for all branches together. We made sure to gather feedback from our students regarding their preference for either separate or Grand OSCEs. Of the 84 respondents, 80.7% strongly agreed that the Grand OSCE format is superior to separate OSCEs. Another objective of doing all examinations on the same day is to ensure that when younger doctors graduate, they are capable of simultaneously managing various specialties in the casualty room, opposite of a study done by al-Khateeb et al. [9] at Hawler Medical University, which regarded uncooperative patients as another problem raised by the students as a weakness of their OSCE examination. Most students in both stages rated patient cooperation as good. In contrast, only 5% rated it as bad; this is considered a strength of our faculty that may be attributed to a hospital and faculty in a small town.

The allotted time for each station in the sixth stage was five minutes, while for the fourth stage, it was three minutes. Most faculty and external examiners concurred that this duration was sufficient, with no significant difference in the P value among the examiners. On the flip side, students from both the sixth and fourth stages accepted the time constraints without notable differences, suggesting that the skills and procedures required were appropriately aligned with the allotted time, which is nearly identical to Ansari T et al. [14] and opposite to Sediq et al. and Alghamdi KS et al. [12, 13]

One criticism we have encountered is the limited time to study all courses before the examination. Therefore, we have asked our participants for their feedback on this matter; there is a statistically significant difference in the P value between the results of the fourth and sixth stages; approximately 80% of fourth-stage students agree or strongly agree that the time required to prepare for the GOSCE exam was satisfactory for studying before the examination. In comparison, only four students (4%) in the sixth year agree. This difference can be attributed to the difference in technical preparation time, with the sixth-year students having five days after theory examinations compared to 8 days for the fourth-stage students.

Regarding all other technical preparation and scientific basis of examination, there was no significant difference between the fourth and sixth stages, While two-thirds of students rated the examination in general as good; in contrast, less than half of Sediq et al. and only 36.9% of Alghamdi KS et al. [12, 13] rated their examination as the same, It is a satisfactory outcome, and we can consider using this sort of assessment in the coming years, taking into account all the facts and notes.

Teachers are crucial in achieving scientific outcomes, especially when faculty councils decide on new assessment models. We take this into account by considering examiners’ feedback. The results are reliable because most respondents are external examiners with extensive experience in teaching and examination.

In the present study, the examiners exhibited considerable satisfaction with GOSCE attributes, quality, and organization following international papers [15, 16]. The GOSCE examiners involved in the current study were from multi-specialties, and most of them had previous experience in GOSCE, using the checklist and global assessment. Most examiners accepted that the GOSCE assessment was fair and that the tasks were interesting and educative. They believe the test covered multiple clinical skills, minimized the chance of failing, and delivered enough time for task demonstration, and as more than half of responders were external examiners from other local medical colleges with a long history of student assessment with no statistically significant difference between local and external examiners that also give strength to our evaluation as most of our external examiners were with a long history of process of medical education and examination, comparing with local faculties, our faculty examiners agreement and strongly agreement about fairness, coverage of a wide range of clinical skills, minimizing the chance of failing to have opportunity to learn was 80.4%, 66.6% 50.1% and 86.7% respectively while in Sulaymaniyah university was 82.8, 79.7%, 73.5 and 85.9% respectively [12].

The strength of the current study is that it was the first study in our university to be conducted, aiming to assess students’ and examiners’ perceptions of a new style of OSCE. In addition, self-administered questionnaires filled out after the exam and before the release of the results could minimize interviewer bias and self-report-related issues. The sample size was comparatively large, and both groups had a high response rate. Our study had a few limitations as it was a questionnaire-based observational study without interviewing the participants, and our evaluation was a multi-specialty examination; lack of control groups and sample size are two other limitations of the study.

We need to go into more detail about each station and each specialty.

Conclusions

The opinions of the 4th and 6th-year medical students and their examiners regarding GOSCE, organization, and performance quality were evaluated, we can schedule this type of GOSCE for the upcoming academic years, as most participants agree on the organization and specifics of this examination format. However, it will require additional preparation for future exams, and every aspect of feedback should be considered, even if it comes from a small number of students. This includes paying particular attention to the duration of time allotted for studying.

This type of examination can be applied in other medical colleges to benefit students in the future and provide better arrangements.
