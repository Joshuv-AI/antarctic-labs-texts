Formal Verification of Markov Processes with Learned Parameters
Muhammad Maaz, Timothy C. Y. Chan

Abstract
We introduce the problem of formally verifying properties of Markov processes where the parameters are given by the output of machine learning models. For a broad class of machine learning models, including linear models, tree-based models, and neural networks, verifying properties of Markov chains like reachability, hitting time, and total reward can be formulated as a bilinear program. We develop a decomposition and bound propagation scheme for solving the bilinear program and show through computational experiments that our method solves the problem to global optimality up to 100x faster than state-of-the-art solvers. To demonstrate the practical utility of our approach, we apply it to a real-world healthcare case study. Along with the paper, we release markovml , an open-source tool for building Markov processes, integrating pretrained machine learning models, and verifying their properties, available at https://github.com/mmaaz-git/markovml .

Introduction

Markov processes are fundamental mathematical models used across computer science, operations research, engineering, and healthcare
[
1
]
. In computer science, they capture the behavior of probabilistic systems such as hardware, communication protocols, or autonomous agents
[
2
]
. In engineering, they are used to analyze degradation and failure in complex machinery
[
3
]
. In healthcare, they model patient transitions between clinical states and underpin cost-effectiveness analyses of medical interventions
[
4
]
.

As machine learning (ML) becomes increasingly integrated into real-world systems, Markov models are evolving to incorporate heterogeneous, data-driven parameters. For instance, ML models can estimate failure rates from sensor data or patient-specific transition probabilities based on clinical features
[
5
]
. This gives rise to a new class of Markov processes where parameters are not fixed but are instead
learned functions
.

Despite this growing trend, there is limited work on rigorously analyzing such systems. In healthcare, most studies rely on Monte Carlo simulation
[
6
]
, which supports subgroup analyses (e.g., patients over 60) but cannot provide formal guarantees – an important limitation in high-stakes domains like medicine or safety-critical infrastructure.

In this paper, we present a new framework for the formal verification of Markov processes with ML-based parameters. Our key insight is that properties of such systems can be encoded as
bilinear programs
, allowing us to obtain exact results with formal guarantees. While our primary motivation is healthcare, the approach generalizes to any application where learned models feed into Markov processes. This enables us to rigorously answer questions such as: Given a bound on the input, what is the worst-case probability of reaching a failure state? Is the failure rate for machines with certain properties guaranteed to remain below 0.01%? For a clinical subgroup, is the expected treatment cost within a government threshold?

To summarize, our main contributions are:

1.

We introduce a general framework for formally verifying properties of Markov processes whose parameters are given by ML models.

2.

We show that for a broad class of models, including linear models, tree ensembles, and ReLU-based neural networks, verification problems can be expressed as (mixed-integer) bilinear programs.

3.

We develop a novel decomposition and bound propagation method that significantly accelerates global solution times, outperforming existing solvers by orders of magnitude.

Problem Formulation

Notation

Vectors are lowercase bold, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
, with
i
-th entry
x_{i}
, and matrices by uppercase bold letters, e.g.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
with
(i,j)
-th entry
M_{ij}
. The identity matrix is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}
, the vector of all ones by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
, with dimensions inferred from context. The set of integers from
1
to
n
is denoted by
[n]
.

Preliminaries

A (discrete-time, finite-state)
Markov chain
with
n
states, indexed by
[n]
, is defined by a row-stochastic transition matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, where
P_{ij}
is the probability of transitioning from state
i
to state
j
, and a stochastic initial distribution vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\pi_{i}
is the probability of starting in state
i
. Furthermore, if we assign rewards to each state, we call this a
Markov reward process
. A Markov reward process has a reward vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
r_{i}
is the reward for being in state
i
for one period. A state is
absorbing
if it cannot transition to any other state, and
transient
otherwise.

Below, we recount three of the key properties commonly computed in practice
[
50
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Reachability)
.

The probability of eventually reaching a set of states
S\subseteq[n]
, from a set
T\subseteq[n]
of transient states equal to the complement of
S
, assuming that the chain will reach
S
from
T
with probability 1, is given by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is the transition matrix restricted to
T
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
is the transition matrix from
T
to
S
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}
is the initial distribution over
T
.

Definition 3.2

(Expected hitting time)
.

The expected number of steps to eventually reach a set of states
S\subseteq[n]
, from a set
T\subseteq[n]
of transient states from a set
T\subseteq[n]
of transient states equal to the complement of
S
, assuming that the chain will reach
S
from
T
with probability 1, is given by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is the transition matrix restricted to
T
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}
is the initial distribution over
T
.

Definition 3.3

(Total infinite-horizon discounted reward)
.

The total infinite-horizon discounted reward, with a discount factor
\lambda\in(0,1)
is given by
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}^{t}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
.

For each of the above quantities, we can restrict the analysis to a single state, e.g., for reachability starting from a state
i
, we simply set
\tilde{\pi}_{i}=1
and for
j\neq i
, set
\tilde{\pi}_{j}=0
.

These three quantities enable rich analysis of Markov processes and the systems they model. Reachability can be used to verify the probability of failure in a system. Expected hitting time can be used to compute the expected time to failure, or life expectancy of a person. Total reward can be used to compute the resource consumption of a system or total cost of a drug.

We wish to
verify
these properties, namely finding their maximum or minimum. If the Markov process’ parameters are fixed, as is common in model checking, these quantities can be computed exactly by solving a linear system. However, in our case, the parameters are given by ML models, and so we will formulate an optimization problem to derive bounds on these quantities. As the three quantities have similar formulations, we will proceed in the rest of the paper by studying the total reward. It will be easy to modify results for the other two quantities.

3.1
Embedding ML Models

We now consider
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
as functions of a
feature vector

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m
is the number of features. We encode the relationship from
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
via a set of functions,
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k_{f}}
, where
k_{f}
is the number of functions. Each function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\ell_{i}}
for
i=1,2,\ldots,k_{f}
takes a feature vector and outputs a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta_{i}}}
(e.g., a classifier may output class probabilities for three classes). We concatenate these vectors to form the output vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
, where
\ell=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{f}}\ell_{i}
.

Our key assumption on the functions will be that they are
mixed-integer linear representable
(MILP-representable), meaning that the relationship between the inputs and outputs can be expressed using linear constraints and binary variables. This is a broad class of functions that includes, e.g., piecewise linear functions (including simple “if-then” rules), linear and logistic regression, tree-based models, and neural networks with ReLU activations

It is important to note that in the case of logistic regression and neural network classifiers, there is a non-linearity introduced by the softmax or logistic function. Typically in the formal verification literature, it suffices to check the logits, not the actual probabilities. However, in our problem, we need the probabilities as they are inputs into our Markov process. Practically, we handle this issue in this paper and in our software using Gurobi’s built-in
spatial branch-and-bound techniques
for nonlinear functions – essentially dynamic piecewise linear approximations.

Next, the output vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
is linked to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
through affine equalities:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\pi}}}
,
\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{P}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{P}}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{r}}}
, for fixed
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\pi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times\ell},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times\ell}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\pi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note the sizes of the matrices mean that every element of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
must be bound with an affine equality to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
. In other words, if we fix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
, we can compute
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
exactly. The
\vect
operator vectorizes a matrix, i.e., by concatenating rows into one column vector, so that
\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}}
.

The affine equalities allow for common formulations like one of the parameters equaling an element of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
, e.g.,
\pi_{1}=\theta_{1}
. Another common situation is that we have a function that outputs the probability of transitioning to a specific state (say, a death state in a healthcare model), and the remaining probability mass is, e.g., distributed uniformly over the remaining states:
\pi_{n}=\theta_{1}
and
\pi_{i}=(1-\sum_{j\neq i}\theta_{j})/(n-1)
for
i\neq n
. Of course, the affine equalities also allow for parameters to be fixed as constants.

Next, we may have linear inequalities on the parameters:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\pi}}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{P}}}\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{P}}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{r}}}
, for fixed
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\pi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}}\times n},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}}\times n^{2}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\pi}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{P}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{r}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}}
, where
k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}},k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}},k_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}}
are the number of linear inequalities on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, respectively.

Linear inequalities are an appropriately general assumption as they arise naturally from fixing a monotonic ordering on the rewards or the probabilities. In reliability engineering and healthcare, a common assumption is that of
increasing failure rate
, which implies a certain stochastic ordering on the transition probabilities
[
51
,
52
]
. Effectively, this introduces a series of linear inequalities on the transition probabilities. See Figure
1
for a diagram of the full pipeline.

Lastly, we assume that the feature vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
lies in a set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
. We assume that the set
\mathcal{X}
is
mixed-integer-linear representable
(MILP-representable), i.e., it can be written as a finite union of polyhedra
[
53
]
. This is satisfied by a wide range of sets, including box constraints, polytopes, and discrete sets. In practice, this may look like, e.g., that we restrict
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
to represent the set of patients that are either under 18 or over 65, as this is a union of two intervals.

Figure 1:
Example of our pipeline. A feature vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
is passed through different functions, here a linear regression and a neural network, to obtain the output vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
, which then determines the parameters of the Markov process through affine equalities.

With these assumptions, we can now formulate the following optimization problem for the total infinite-horizon discounted reward:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{min}/\text{max}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k_{f}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{i}=f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall i\in[k_{f}],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\pi}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{P}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{P}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{r}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\pi}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{P}}}\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{P}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{r}}},

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}=1\quad\forall i\in[n],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}=1,

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq P_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i,j\in[n]\times[n],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq\pi_{i}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[n],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}.

Above,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
is a new vector we have introduced, which in Markov process theory would be referred to as the
value function
, and often arises in reinforcement learning, which equals
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
. We can easily convert this into a feasibility problem, to find a feasible
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
, by replacing the objective function with an inequality,
W_{\min}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\leq W_{\max}
. With slight modifications, we can also formulate reachability and hitting time: the full formulations are in Appendix
A
.

4
Solving the Optimization Problem

Under the assumptions that the functions
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{k_{f}}
are MILP-representable and
\mathcal{X}
is MILP-representable, the optimization problem above is a
mixed-integer bilinear program
, as we have a bilinear objective and a bilinear constraint. We will leverage the observation that strong bounds on variables are crucial for solving bilinear programs, and will derive such bounds by decomposing our problem and using various results from Markov theory.

Our strategy is as follows: obtain bounds on the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
by solving a series of smaller MILPs, propagate these bounds to the parameters via the affine equalities, obtain bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
using linear algebra arguments, and then tighten the
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
bounds using interval matrix analysis. All proofs are in Appendix
B
.

Bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}

For each
\theta_{i}
, we minimize and maximize it by solving two smaller MILPs, over the set
\mathcal{X}
. Namely, for each
i\in[k_{f}]
, for each
j\in[\ell_{i}]
, solve for
\mathop{\text{min}/\text{max}}_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\theta_{i,j}\;\text{s.t.}\;\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta_{i}}}=[\theta_{i,1},\ldots,\theta_{i,\ell_{i}}]=f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})
, which are MILPs as
\mathcal{X}
and
f_{1},\ldots f_{k_{f}}
are MILP-representable.

Bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}

We now propagate the bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
to the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
via the affine equalities.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
be a vector where each component satisfies the bounds
\theta_{i}^{\min}\leq\theta_{i}\leq\theta_{i}^{\max}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,\ell
. Consider an affine transformation defined by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times\ell}
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. Then, each component
y_{i}
of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}
satisfies
b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}A_{ij}\cdot\left(\theta_{j}^{\min}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{ij}\geq 0}+\theta_{j}^{\max}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{ij}<0}\right)\leq y_{i}\leq b_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}A_{ij}\cdot\left(\theta_{j}^{\max}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{ij}\geq 0}+\theta_{j}^{\min}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{ij}<0}\right)
.

Bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}

Next, we derive bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
using well-established facts from Markov theory. First, as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, we analyze bounds on
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
be row-stochastic and
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Then, each element in
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
is in the interval
[0,1/(1-\lambda)]
and the row sums are all equal to
1/(1-\lambda)
.

Now we can propagate the above bounds to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
r_{i}^{\min}\leq r_{i}\leq r_{i}^{\max}
. Then, each element in the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
satisfies
\gamma\min_{j}r_{j}^{\min}\leq v_{i}\leq\gamma\max_{j}r_{j}^{\max}
, where
\gamma=\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
.

Lemma
4.3
provides valid bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
. However, these bounds are not tight, as Lemma
4.2
applies to
any
row-stochastic matrix, and does not take into account the specific element-wise bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
that we derived from Lemma
4.1
. In order to derive tighter bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
, we need to incorporate the specific element-wise bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
. This is difficult because of the matrix inverse. In the following section, we show how to solve this problem with interval matrix analysis and hence derive tighter bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
.

4.1
Tightening bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}

Interval matrix analysis studies a generalization of matrices (or vectors), where each element is an
interval
instead of a real number (see
[
54
]
for an overview). Equivalently, it can be viewed as studying the set of all matrices that lie within given intervals. There are easy generalizations of arithmetic of real numbers to intervals, which can be used to generalize matrix arithmetic to interval matrices. However, inverting an interval matrix is difficult due to the inherent nonlinearity of the inverse operation. Consider the equivalent problem of solving a linear system with interval matrices. The solution set is not rectangular, so cannot be described as an interval matrix. Hence, we wish to find the interval matrix of smallest radius that contains the solution set, known as the
hull

[
54
]
.

Due to the interval extensions of the basic arithmetic operations, it is possible to generalize the Gauss-Seidel method, a common iterative procedures for solving linear systems, to interval matrices. Beginning with an initial enclosure of the solution set, it iteratively refines the intervals and returns a smaller enclosure of the solution set. We provide a full description of the Gauss-Seidel method in Appendix
C
. The interval Gauss-Seidel method is optimal in the sense that it provides the smallest enclosure of the solution set out of a broad class of algorithms
[
54
]
.

Hence, we can apply the Gauss-Seidel method to tighten the bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
, as
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
is a linear system. We use the bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
from Lemma
4.1
to form the interval matrices, and for our initial guess, we can use the bounds derived from Lemma
4.3
.

Still, Gauss-Seidel is not guaranteed to obtain the hull itself. A well-known sufficient condition for Gauss-Seidel to obtain the hull is that the matrix in the linear system is an
interval M-matrix
, a generalization of the notion of an M-matrix
[
55
]
to interval matrices. Suppose a matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
is element-wise bounded by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\max}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\min}}}
. It is an
interval M-matrix
if and only if
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\min}}}
is a (non-singular) real M-matrix in the usual sense and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\max}}}
has no positive off-diagonal elements
[
54
]
. Hence we can prove a necessary and sufficient condition for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
to be an interval M-matrix.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
be a row-stochastic matrix bounded element-wise by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\min}}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\max}}}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
is an interval M-matrix if and only if
\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}^{\max})\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}
, where
\rho
is the spectral radius.

Notably, we only need check the spectral radius of the matrix formed by the upper bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
. There are various conditions that can be used to obtain the necessary bound on the spectral radius. Since
\lambda<1
, we have
1/\lambda>1
, so if we can show that the spectral radius of the upper bound matrix is less than
1
, then this automatically implies the condition in Theorem
4.4
, and hence that the Gauss-Seidel method will obtain the hull, i.e., the tightest possible rectangular bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
. This may occur if, for example, the row sums of the upper bounds are
\leq 1
.

Example 4.5
.

Let
\lambda=0.97
, and suppose we have the following bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}:\begin{bmatrix}[0.5,0.6]&[0.2,0.5]\\
[0.1,0.4]&[0.5,0.6]\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}:\begin{bmatrix}[0,100]\\
[0,100]\end{bmatrix}

The bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
are:

7
Case Study

As a short case study, we re-analyze the healthcare cost-benefit analysis of
Maaz et al. [59]
, which models patients after an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). Patients are discharged with a
modified Rankin scale (mRS)
, a measure of post-arrest morbidity (0: perfect health, 6: death), which determines yearly mortality and quality of life. The Markov chain in their model has 20 states in total: discharged with mRS 0-6, first-year post-discharge with mRS 0-5, later-year post-discharge with mRS 0-5, and death. The only allowable transitions are, for each mRS, discharge to first-year, first-year to later-year, later-year to later-year; and, any state to death. A patient’s discharge mRS, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}
, is given by an ML model that takes as input the patient’s age and sex, features of the OHCA, and the time-to-defibrillation.
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, representing the
net monetary benefit
(NMB), a common health economic metric, are fixed and taken from literature.

Maaz et al. [59]
perform their analysis on data from 22,017 real OHCAs in Canada. We obtained the data they used, trained a decision tree, and reconstructed their Markov process in our software package
markovml
. By setting appropriate constraints to build
\mathcal{X}
, we can now perform subgroup analyses. For example, we found that the best-case (or, maximum) NMB for men is about 50% higher than for women. This reflects lower mortality among men, i.e., lower probability of being discharged with mRS 6 (death). Indeed, this is corroborated by the healthcare literature which finds that women have at least 50% higher mortality than men after an OHCA
[
60
]
. Notably, doing this analysis as an optimization problem allowed us to solve it in an automated way, without requiring the underlying patient data nor simulating representative patients. We refer the reader to the code attached with our paper, which contains the ML model (due to data privacy, we cannot share the patient data), as well as further details on the implementation.

8
Discussion and Conclusion

We have introduced a novel framework for the formal verification of Markov processes with learned parameters, enabling exact reasoning about systems where transition probabilities and rewards are specified by machine learning models. Our approach formulates the verification task as a bilinear program and introduces a decomposition and bound propagation scheme that significantly accelerates computation—often by orders of magnitude, while maintaining global optimality guarantees. This work contributes to the broader goal of safe and transparent deployment of ML in high-stakes settings. Our healthcare case study demonstrates how the method can support robust decision making in critical domains. We have implemented this in the software package
markovml
, which provides a flexible language for defining Markov processes, embedding a range of ML models, and optimizing key metrics.

Limitations

Our experiments show that the method scales well across problem dimensions including the number of states, models, and model complexity. However, a key limitation is scalability for neural networks: our method worked well on small networks but degrades with increasing depth or width. This is consistent with known computational hardness results for neural network verification. Future work could improve performance by integrating state-of-the-art techniques from the neural network verification literature, such as
\alpha,\beta
-CROWN.

Future directions

Our software package,
markovml
, is open-source and designed for extensibility. We envision future extensions that support richer ML models and eventually integrates with other neural network verifiers. We hope this work encourages further research at the intersection of probabilistic model checking and formal verification of ML.

Appendix A
Formulations of feasibility, reachability, and hitting time

A.1
Feasibility of the total infinite-horizon reward

The total infinite-horizon reward feasibility problem can be written as:

\displaystyle\exists\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad W_{\min}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\leq W_{\max}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k_{f}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{i}=f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall i\in[k_{f}],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\pi}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{P}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{P}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{r}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\pi}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{P}}}\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{P}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{r}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{r}}},

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}P_{ij}=1\quad\forall i\in[n],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi_{i}=1,

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq P_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i,j\in[n]\times[n],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq\pi_{i}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[n],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}.

where
W_{\min}
and
W_{\max}
are bounds on the total reward, which may be
\pm\infty
.

A.2
Reachability

To reformulate the total reward formulation for reachability to a target state
S\subseteq[n]
from a set of transient states
T\subseteq[n],T\cap S=\emptyset
, observe that we can simply make the following substitutions:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}
gets replaced with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}
, which is the initial probability over
T
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
gets replaced with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
, which is
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
restricted to
T
, and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
gets replaced with
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
is the transition matrix from
T
to

. Lastly, there is no more discount rate
\lambda
. We also require
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
to be strictly substochastic so that it remains invertible (i.e., the row sums are strictly less than
1
), and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}
are now substochastic (i.e., the row sums are less than or equal to
1
). The optimization problem is then:

\displaystyle\mathop{\text{min}/\text{max}}\limits_{\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k_{f}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{i}=f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall i\in[k_{f}],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\pi}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\pi}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{Q}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{Q}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{R}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{R}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\tilde{\pi}}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\tilde{\pi}}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{QR}}}[\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}})]\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{QR}}},

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{|T|}Q_{ij}<1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}R_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{|T|}(\tilde{\pi})_{i}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq Q_{ij}<1\quad\forall i,j\in[|T|]\times[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq R_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i,j\in[|T|]\times[|S|],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq(\tilde{\pi})_{i}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}.

With a slight abuse of notation, we still maintain the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
, but is instead now defined as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
. As well, note that the stochastic constraints have been replaced with substochastic constraints (strict substochastic for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
).

The feasibility problem is:

\displaystyle\exists\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\quad W_{\min}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}\leq W_{\max}

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{1},\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{2},\cdots,\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{k_{f}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}_{i}=f_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}})\quad\forall i\in[k_{f}],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{\tilde{\pi}}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{\tilde{\pi}}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{Q}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{Q}}},

\displaystyle\quad\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}})=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A_{R}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b_{R}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{\tilde{\pi}}}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\tilde{\pi}}}\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{\tilde{\pi}}}},

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{C_{QR}}}[\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})\vect(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}})]\leq\boldsymbol{\mathbf{d_{QR}}},

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{|T|}Q_{ij}<1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{j=1}^{|S|}R_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{i=1}^{|T|}\tilde{\pi}_{i}\leq 1,

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq Q_{ij}<1\quad\forall i,j\in[|T|]\times[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq R_{ij}\leq 1\quad\forall i,j\in[|T|]\times[|S|],

\displaystyle\quad 0\leq\tilde{\pi}_{i}\leq 1\quad\forall i\in[|T|],

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{X}.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

For a given component
y_{i}
, we can write the transformation as:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}A_{ij}\theta_{j}+b_{i}

for all
i=1,2,\ldots,k
.

Each term in the summation depends linearly on
\theta_{j}
. If
A_{ij}\geq 0
, then
A_{ij}\theta_{j}
is maximized at
\theta_{j}^{\max}
and minimized at
\theta_{j}^{\min}
. Vice versa, if
A_{ij}<0
, then
A_{ij}\theta_{j}
is maximized at
\theta_{j}^{\min}
and minimized at
\theta_{j}^{\max}
.

Applying this to all terms in the summation, we get the required bounds.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Note that the matrix
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})
is a (non-singular) M-matrix. By Theorem 2.3 in Chapter 6 of
[
55
]
, each element of the matrix is non-negative, so is
\geq 0
.

Next, consider the row sum of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
for a row
i
:
\sum_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}_{ij}
, and then expand into the Neumann series:
\sum_{j}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}^{k})_{ij}
. We can exchange the order of summation as all terms are non-negative, and then we have our sum is
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}\sum_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}^{k})_{ij}
. Every positive integer exponent of a row-stochastic matrix is also row-stochastic, so
\sum_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}^{k})_{ij}=1
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, this simplifes to the geometric series
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{k}
, which equals
\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
. As each row sum is
\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
, and each element is non-negative, each element must also be at most
\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Recall that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, so
v_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}_{ij}r_{j}
. Thus, we can see
v_{i}
is a positive linear combination of the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, where the nonnegative weights are from
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}})^{-1}
and sum to
1/(1-\lambda)
. To maximize this combination, we assign all the weight to the maximum upper bound of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
, and to minimize it, we assign all the weight to the minimum lower bound of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{r}}
. Hence, we have the required bounds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
. Clearly,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M}}
is bounded element-wise by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\max}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\min}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\min}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\max}}}
. The upper bound
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\max}}}
has no positive off-diagonal elements, as all of the elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\max}}}
are between
0
and
1
. So, the only thing we need to check is that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\min}}}
is an M-matrix.

From the definition of an M-matrix
[
55
]
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{M^{\min}}}
is an M-matrix if and only if
1\geq\rho(\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\max}}})=\lambda\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P^{\max}}})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Appendix C
Gauss-Seidel Method for Interval Matrices

Our exposition here follows section 5.7 of
Horáček [61]
. Suppose we have a linear system
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{A}}
is an interval matrix and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{b}}
is an interval vector. The set of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}
that satisfies this system is referred to as the solution set. It is in general complicated, so we hope to find a rectangular enclosure of the solution set. The smallest such enclosure is referred to as the hull of the solution set.

Suppose we have an initial enclosure of the solution set,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(0)}
. In general this takes some effort to obtain, but we obtained this for our specific problems. Then, the interval Gauss-Seidel method proceeds as follows:

1.

For each variable
x_{i}
, compute a new enclosure of the solution set as:

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(k+1)}=\frac{1}{A_{ii}}\left(b_{i}-\sum_{j<i}A_{ij}x_{j}^{(k+1)}-\sum_{j>i}A_{ij}x_{j}^{(k)}\right)

2.

Update the enclosure of the solution set:
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(k+1)}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{x}}^{(k)}\cap\boldsymbol{\mathbf{y}}^{(k+1)}
.

3.

Repeat steps 1 and 2 until stopping criterion is met (either a maximum number of iterations or the difference in subsequent enclosures is less than some tolerance).

All operations above are interval arithmetic operations. For real intervals, we have:
[a,b]+[c,d]=[a+c,b+d]
,
[a,b]-[c,d]=[a-d,b-c]
,
[a,b]\cdot[c,d]=[\min(ac,ad,bc,bd),\max(ac,ad,bc,bd)]
. Division is defined as multiplication by the reciprocal, i.e.,
[a,b]/[c,d]=[a,b]\cdot[1/d,1/c]=[\min(a/c,a/d,b/c,b/d),\max(a/c,a/d,b/c,b/d)]
, if
0\notin[c,d]
.

Note that the zero division issue is not a concern for us, as the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
, in the total reward setting, have lower bounds always strictly positive, due to the discount factor
\lambda\in(0,1)
. In the reachability and hitting time settings, the diagonal elements of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
have lower bounds always strictly positive, due to the strict substochastic property of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
. In practice, we use a small numerical offset to handle this.

Appendix D
Solving other formulations

D.1
Reachability and hitting time

The reachability and hitting time formulations are in Appendix
A
. The key difference that affects our theoretical results and algorithms is that the matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
, which is a submatrix of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
restricted to the transient states, is strictly substochastic, and we don’t have a discount factor
\lambda
anymore. This introduces some technical difficulties in guaranteeing invertibility of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
. However, all our theoretical results all follow through with minor modifications.

Lemma D.1
.

For a strictly row-substochastic matrix
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
with all elements in
[0,1]
:

1.

The spectral radius of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is strictly less than
1
.

2.

The matrix
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})
is a (non-singular) M-matrix.

3.

The matrix
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
has all non-negative elements.

4.

The row sums of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
are at most
1/(1-\alpha)
, where
\alpha=\max_{i}\sum_{j}Q_{ij}
is the maximum row sum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
.

5.

Each element of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
is in the interval
[0,1/(1-\alpha)]
.

Proof.

The row sum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is strictly less than
1
, so, by a well-known result in linear algebra, the spectral radius of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is strictly less than
1
. It follows that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is a (non-singular) M-matrix by the definition of an M-matrix
[
55
]
, and hence that the inverse has all non-negative elements by Theorem 2.3 in Chapter 6 of
Berman and Plemmons [55]
.
∎

Note that the maximum row sum
\alpha
is strictly less than
1
, so the formula is well-defined. We use this result to obtain the appropriate initial bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
for the reachability and hitting time formulations.

Corollary D.2
.

Given a Markov reachability problem with element-wise bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
, as defined in Appendix
A
, the initial bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
are:

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\max_{j}\sum_{k}R_{jk}^{\max}

where
\alpha=\max_{i}\sum_{j}Q_{ij}^{\max}
is the maximum row sum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
.

Proof.

First, note that the vector
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
has elements which are the row sums of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
. So, for each
i
,
\sum_{j}R_{ij}^{\min}\leq(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}})_{i}\leq\sum_{j}R_{ij}^{\max}
. Next, noting that the row sums of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
are at most
1/(1-\alpha)
, we have, by a similar argument as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, that the upper bound on
v_{i}
is:

\displaystyle v_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\max_{j}\sum_{k}R_{jk}^{\max}

For the lower bound, unlike the argument in Lemma
4.3
, we don’t know what the row sums of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
actually are, so we use the smallest possible row sum, which is
0
. So, we have:

\displaystyle v_{i}\geq 0

∎

Corollary D.3
.

Given a Markov hitting time problem with element-wise bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
, as defined in Appendix
A
, the initial bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
are:

\displaystyle 0\leq v_{i}\leq\frac{1}{1-\alpha}

where
\alpha=\max_{i}\sum_{j}Q_{ij}^{\max}
is the maximum row sum of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
.

Proof.

The bound simply follows from the fact that
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
is the row sums of
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})^{-1}
.
∎

Note, however, that we
do not
know what the row sums of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
are, because it is not fixed. However, in practice, in order to assert the strict row-substochastic property, we use a small numerical offset, which is what we use in our implementation. Specifically, we set the row sums of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
to be at most
1-\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is by default set to
10^{-6}
in our software implementation. Based on this, we can set
\alpha
to be
1-\epsilon
.

Next, we can tighten the bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
by forming the appropiate linear systems:
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
for reachability and
(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{I}}-\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}})\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathbf{1}}
for hitting time. In both cases, though
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
is a submatrix of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
, it is square, so we can analyze the similar interval M-matrix condition for the Gauss-Seidel method to achieve the hull of the solution set.

Corollary D.4
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
be a row-substochastic matrix bounded element-wise by
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q^{\min}}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q^{\max}}}
. Then, it is an interval M-matrix if and only if
\rho(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q^{\max}}})\leq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is identical to that of Theorem
4.4
, but without the discount factor.
∎

Based on the above extensions of our theoretical results, we now have similar algorithms for reachability and hitting time. As usual, we first optimize for bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\theta}}
, then we propagate to
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{Q}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{R}}
(if applicable), obtain initial bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
as above, and then apply the Gauss-Seidel method to tighten the bounds on
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{v}}
.

D.2
Feasibility

Our method easily extends to the feasibility version of all three problems. We still obtain all the necessary bounds as in the optimization version. At the end, when we would optimize for the quantity of interest, we simply check if the overall problem is feasible, given the bounds on the quantity of interest.

Appendix G
Numerical experiments setup

For all experiments, we fix the number of features
m=5
, the feature set
\mathcal{X}=[-1,1]^{5}
, and the discount factor
\lambda=0.97
. We train ML models on randomly generated data (

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{10000\times 5}
,
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{5}
, and then generate data points as
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}}+\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{10000}
. For classification models, we use the same linear form but draw data points from a Bernoulli distribution with probabilities
\sigma(\boldsymbol{\mathbf{X}}\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\beta}})
, where
\sigma(\cdot)
is the logistic function.

For rewards, we train a regression model whose output is set to
r_{1}
. For
i=2,\ldots,n
, we set
r_{i}
to be output divided by
i
. For probabilities, we train binary classifiers for
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}
and rows of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}
. For
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{\pi}}
, we set
\pi_{1}
equal to the classifier output, the remaining probability is assigned to
\pi_{2}
, and the remaining are set to zero. For rows of
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}

0
. We make the last state absorbing, i.e., transitions to itself with probability
1
. The Markov process is essentially a discrete-time birth process, as one can only transition to the next state, or stay in the same state, with the final state being absorbing.

For each configuration, we run 10 instances, each with random data, train the models on the random data, and solve the optimization problem directly with an out-of-the-box solver and with our method.

\boldsymbol{\mathbf{P}}

Experiment statistical significance

Guidelines:

•

The authors should answer "Yes" if the results are accompanied by error bars, confidence intervals, or statistical significance tests, at least for the experiments that support the main claims of the paper.

•

The factors of variability that the error bars are capturing should be clearly stated (for example, train/test split, initialization, random drawing of some parameter, or overall run with given experimental conditions).

•

The method for calculating the error bars should be explained (closed form formula, call to a library function, bootstrap, etc.)

•

The assumptions made should be given (e.g., Normally distributed errors).

•

It should be clear whether the error bar is the standard deviation or the standard error of the mean.

It is OK to report 1-sigma error bars, but one should state it. The authors should preferably report a 2-sigma error bar than state that they have a 96% CI, if the hypothesis of Normality of errors is not verified.

•

For asymmetric distributions, the authors should be careful not to show in tables or figures symmetric error bars that would yield results that are out of range (e.g. negative error rates).

•

If error bars are reported in tables or plots, The authors should explain in the text how they were calculated and reference the corresponding figures or tables in the text.

Broader impacts

Answer:

Guidelines:

•

The answer NA means that there is no societal impact of the work performed.

•

If the authors answer NA or No, they should explain why their work has no societal impact or why the paper does not address societal impact.

•

Examples of negative societal impacts include potential malicious or unintended uses (e.g., disinformation, generating fake profiles, surveillance), fairness considerations (e.g., deployment of technologies that could make decisions that unfairly impact specific groups), privacy considerations, and security considerations.

•

The conference expects that many papers will be foundational research and not tied to particular applications, let alone deployments. However, if there is a direct path to any negative applications, the authors should point it out. For example, it is legitimate to point out that an improvement in the quality of generative models could be used to generate deepfakes for disinformation. On the other hand, it is not needed to point out that a generic algorithm for optimizing neural networks could enable people to train models that generate Deepfakes faster.

•

The authors should consider possible harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended and functioning correctly, harms that could arise when the technology is being used as intended but gives incorrect results, and harms following from (intentional or unintentional) misuse of the technology.

•

If there are negative societal impacts, the authors could also discuss possible mitigation strategies (e.g., gated release of models, providing defenses in addition to attacks, mechanisms for monitoring misuse, mechanisms to monitor how a system learns from feedback over time, improving the efficiency and accessibility of ML).
