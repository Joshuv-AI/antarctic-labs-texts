Hybrid fuzzy clustering with Golden Eagle Optimization Algorithm for fault tolerant load balancing in fog computing environment
Authors: Harpreet Kaur, Swati Malik, Vidhu Baggan, Shilpi Harnal, Noopur Tyagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fog Computing (FC), when integrated with emerging 5G technologies, provides significant potential to reduce latency and enhance Quality of Service (QoS). Nevertheless, current scheduling strategies tend to lack the in-depth fault-tolerance and load balancing provisions as virtual machine (VM) health indicators, including CPU utilization, memory status, and reliability are not specifically taken into account during task assignment. To overcome these difficulties, this paper will propose a Hybrid Fuzzy Clustering with Golden Eagle Optimization Algorithm (HFC-GEOA). The framework incorporates capacity aware VM clustering into three levels (HCC, MCC, LCC), fuzzy-based task prioritization and localized metaheuristic optimization, which allows efficient mapping of tasks to VM under resource constraints and adaptive inter-cluster migration. Extensive scaling simulations in iFogSim2 using five task-VM scenarios (1,000-20,000 tasks; 20-1,500 VMs) of 30 independent runs demonstrate that HFC-GEOA is a strong and consistent scale performer. In large scale scenarios HFC-GEOA is used to reduce the average turnaround time by up to 78% over FGELB, 71% over ACO-LWC, and 85% over FCLB. The greatest scenario shows improvement of up to 87% of the average wait time over FGELB and 81% improvement over ACO-LWC and 91% improvement over FCLB. The consumption of energy has been competitive as it has remained 11 to 16% less than ACO-LWC over S3-S5. The stably maintained system reliability at 0.71-0.74 across all scenarios more than twice that of EWOA (0.31-0.33) and the failure rates are kept in control under 2.88 to 3.81%. The fault tolerance score increases progressively when compared to and exceeding EWOA (0.77) and FCLB (0.71) at large scale, and as the system resilience to scale improves. Overall, HFC-GEOA offers a scalable, health-conscious, and fault-tolerant scheduler solution that fully leverages the best latency performance at scale with health-conscious energy usage and stable reliability in heterogeneous fog computing systems.

Introduction

Cloud computing has emerged as a leading paradigm of offering scalable computing resources, such as storage, servers, and software services using the Internet. Cloud-centric processing, although widely used, may not be applicable in latency-sensitive and bandwidth-intensive applications. In the case of the use of remote cloud infrastructures alone, IoT systems, especially real-time systems tend to cause higher delay and network congestion.

Fog Computing (FC) is used to solve these problems, which involves pushing computational and storage resources to the network edge, which is usually near to end users, resulting in reduced service latency and responsiveness. This has become vital to new IoT-based ecosystems like smart cities, health, and industrial automation, in which the number of interconnected devices and the amount of data generated are both very high1. FC enables timely analysis and decision-making by processing data near its source. However, decentralised and heterogeneous nature of the fog computing poses serious challenges. Maintaining the reliability of the system and the continuity of services while effectively balancing fog node resources is a complex issue. The load balancing is also necessary to avoid overload and underutilisation of the nodes, and, consequently, to keep the performance levels at the maximum2. In addition, decision support systems including VM clustering and the task scheduling provided by fuzzy logic have shown great potential in enhancing task allocation efficiency. By categorising VMs based on resource capacity, it is possible to organise workloads in a structured way with increased efficiency in executing the workload and energy management3,4. In addition, to prevent failures in an IoT environment, proactive fault-tolerant systems are necessary in a fog computing environment to detect and react to failures before they impact system performance, enabling continuous execution and high reliability with dynamic IoT workloads.

In spite of these developments, the current scheduling approaches have serious weaknesses. VM health indicators, like CPU utilisation, memory status, and failure rate are not usually explicitly considered in task assignment decisions, and as a result, tasks are assigned to overloaded or unreliable VMs. Mechanisms of inter-cluster migration are either non-existent or free of charge, disregarding the latency, energy, and reliability penalties of fallback decisions. Moreover, VM selection based on metaheuristic techniques is usually run on flat VM pools and without the ability to partition the computational load capacity-aware, the computational overhead will be unnecessarily large and the load distribution will be poor at large scale. To fill in these gaps, this paper proposes a health-aware, fault-tolerant scheduling framework, HFC-GEOA, whose key contributions are summarised in Section “Key contributions”.

Related work

Task scheduling and resource management in fog computing environments have been extensively studied across several complementary research directions, including fuzzy logic-based priority assignment, clustering-based resource organisation, metaheuristic optimisation, fault-tolerant scheduling, and edge computing resource management. This section reviews representative works in each category, identifies their key limitations, and motivates the design of the proposed HFC-GEOA framework. A comparative summary of existing approaches across eight design dimensions is presented in Table 1 at the end of this section, where the gaps addressed by HFC-GEOA are explicitly highlighted.

Task scheduling and priority assignment using fuzzy logic

Fuzzy logic has been widely applied to priority-aware task scheduling in fog and cloud computing. Talaat et al5. proposed an Effective Load Balancing Strategy (ELBS) for fog computing that combines fuzzy logic-based priority assignment with a probabilistic neural network matchmaking mechanism to accommodate real-time healthcare applications. Their approach achieved lower turnaround time and failure rate compared to existing strategies. Kumar et al6. developed a feedback-based fog computing model that prioritises tasks during allocation to reduce makespan and improve application performance across QoS metrics, including power usage, execution time, and makespan. Singh et al7. proposed the Hybrid Priority Assigned Laxity (HPAL) algorithm to assign tasks to VMs based on priority for minimising execution time, optimising load balancing across five metrics: energy consumption, response time, delay, execution time, and processing time. While these methods demonstrate the effectiveness of fuzzy priority scoring, they operate on flat VM pools without capacity-aware clustering or health-driven VM selection, limiting their scalability under heterogeneous fog workloads.

Clustering-based resource organisation

Clustering techniques have been explored to organise fog and cloud resources for efficient scheduling. Arthur et al8. enhanced conventional k-means by introducing a randomised seeding strategy, making the algorithm O(łog k)-competitive with optimal clustering and improving both accuracy and execution speed. Cohen et al9. proposed a k-means++ seeding algorithm for large datasets in near-linear time with the same theoretical guarantees but a significantly faster seeding process. Makarychev et al10. further analysed k-means++ and found that the two-stage centre selection is the key factor, proposing a bi-criteria k-means++ with pruning that achieves similar performance on benchmark datasets. Deshpande et al11. investigated the sensitivity of k-means++ to outliers and proposed a combination of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D^2$$\end{document} and uniform sampling that outperforms standard k-means++ on both real and synthetic datasets. At the application level, Singh et al4. proposed a fog group-based load balancing strategy with a periodic refresh mechanism that reduces energy consumption and VM migrations in IoT environments. Muthusamy et al3. proposed the Cluster-Based Task Scheduling (CBTS) framework, which uses k-means to cluster tasks by length and VMs by capacity to reduce makespan and execution time, outperforming Min-Min, OPFT, DCTS, and GTS. Although these clustering approaches improve resource organisation, none incorporates VM health monitoring or adaptive inter-cluster migration to handle resource saturation dynamically.

Metaheuristic optimisation for task scheduling

Metaheuristic algorithms have been extensively applied to task scheduling optimisation in fog and cloud environments. Ghafir et al12. proposed an intelligent PSO-based feedback controller using Pareto dominance and a double deep Q proximal model to address load imbalance and SLA violations in cloud environments, improving resource utilisation and reducing response time. Pan et al13. integrated a Lyapunov-driven LSTM model with PSO for predictive priority-based task allocation in rechargeable IoT networks, resulting in lower latency and reduced power consumption. Pradhan et al14. proposed LBMPSO, a modified PSO-based load balancing technique for cloud scheduling that reduces makespan and improves resource utilisation across VMs with varying task lengths.Shaik et al15. presented PSOSA-LB, a hybrid optimisation approach that integrates particle swarm optimisation with simulated annealing for resource allocation in fog computing environments. Their framework demonstrated notable improvements, including reduced execution time, lower latency, and decreased energy consumption. Mohammadi-Balani et al16. introduced the Golden Eagle Optimizer (GEO), a bio-inspired metaheuristic evaluated on multiple benchmark functions, and further extended the approach into a multi-objective variant capable of handling Pareto-based optimisation problems. In another study, Saif et al17. developed a two-stage optimisation framework combining enhanced task offloading with a multi-objective Firefly algorithm across edge, fog, and cloud layers to improve delay and energy efficiency.Ali et al18. proposed MoHHOTS, a multi-objective Harris Hawks Optimisation algorithm for cloud-fog task scheduling, which improved transmission delay and energy use by up to 25% over benchmark algorithms. Ghasemi et al19. proposed a modified Harris Hawks Optimisation-based service placement strategy for fog computing, outperforming existing algorithms in energy efficiency, delay reduction, and network utilisation. Zhang et al20. introduced EWOA, extending the Whale Optimisation Algorithm with Lévy flight to improve global search and accelerate convergence for multi-objective cloud scheduling, demonstrating lower energy consumption and reduced execution cost. Mohamed et al21. proposed MOHHOSSA, combining Harris Hawks Optimisation and Salp Swarm Algorithm for IoT data replication in cloud environments, minimising energy use, CO\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} emissions, and service delays. While these metaheuristic approaches demonstrate strong optimisation capability, they universally operate over flat VM pools without capacity-aware clustering, incurring higher computational cost and lacking health-driven VM selection.

Fault-tolerant and load-balanced scheduling

Several recent works have jointly addressed fault tolerance and load balancing. Singh et al22. proposed FGELB, a three-stage framework that integrates fuzzy task prioritisation, GEOA-based resource scheduling, and power management for fog node overload caused by IoT devices, reducing energy consumption, failure rates, and communication overhead.Kashyap et al23. introduced a hybrid meta-heuristic framework for fog computing that integrates Modified Harris Hawks Optimisation (MHHO) for load balancing with Ant Colony Optimisation (ACO) for fault tolerance. The proposed framework was implemented and tested using the iFogSim simulator, where it achieved better load balancing and fault tolerance performance compared with existing approaches. In a related study, Aruna et al24. introduced ACO-LWC, a lightweight Docker container-based load balancing mechanism that reduced response time and error rates when compared with traditional least connection and round robin strategies. Singh et al25. introduced FCLB, a fog-cluster-based load balancing method that utilises a three-subsystem framework to minimise energy consumption and VM migrations. Mahdi et al26. reviewed FC contributions to IoT challenges, comparing static and dynamic load balancing strategies and demonstrating that dynamic methods are more effective in managing changing workloads. Despite these contributions, none of these methods incorporates explicit VM health monitoring during task assignment or a controlled inter-cluster fallback mechanism that simultaneously penalises migration cost in latency, energy, and reliability.

Edge and MEC resource management

Several recent works address resource management problems in edge and MEC environments that are complementary to fog scheduling. Yi et al27. propose a two-timescale framework balancing service migration and task rerouting during mobile device handovers in MEC, while Yi et al28. jointly optimise computation offloading and transmission scheduling for delay-sensitive mobile applications. Yang et al29. study two-timescale online optimisation for human digital twin deployment at the edge, targeting model accuracy under mobility uncertainty.Proactive application deployment under imperfect multi-dimensional prediction was investigated in30, where the relationship between prediction accuracy and deployment overhead was analysed. A distributed computation offloading mechanism based on potential game theory was introduced by Cao and Cai31, enabling convergence toward Nash equilibrium without explicit information sharing among users. In edge computing environments, Shi et al32. addressed reliability-aware microservice deployment through container layer sharing to improve deployment efficiency and system robustness.

Several recent studies from related domains further motivate adaptive optimisation strategies. Tang et al33. focused on intelligent adaptation in dynamic network environments, whereas Li et al34. examined uncertainty-aware sample selection to enhance learning efficiency. Du et al35. utilised structured feature decoupling for high-dimensional data processing, while Yang et al36. designed self-balancing hierarchical architectures for maintaining operational stability under fluctuating loads.

Despite these advancements, the primary focus of existing studies remains on areas such as mobility-aware offloading, application deployment, or network-centric optimisation. Proposed technique

The proposed HFC-GEOA framework is organised into three layers, as shown in Fig. 1. IoT devices at the bottom layer generate tasks that are forwarded to the fog scheduler through a gateway. The scheduler processes tasks through four sequential steps illustrated in Fig. 2: (1) VM clustering using fast k-means++ into HCC, MCC, and LCC tiers; (2) fuzzy-linear task scoring to determine the preferred cluster per task; (3) GEOA-based VM selection within the assigned cluster using a multi-criteria fitness function; and (4) saturation check with adaptive inter-cluster fallback when the selected VM is overloaded. The following subsections elaborate each step in full detail.

VM clustering using deterministic fast K-Means++ seeding for VM capacity clustering

The deterministic fast k-means++ seeding based clustering technique is employed as an efficient initialisation strategy to improve centroid selection and clustering quality in large-scale fog environments8,9. In this work, it clusters Virtual Machines (VMs) at the fog layer based on capacity, including MIPS and RAM.

Task priority and cluster selection using fuzzy–linear scoring mechanism

Efficient task-to-cluster mapping is essential for achieving balanced resource utilization and low latency in fog computing environments. In this work, a lightweight fuzzy-inspired scoring mechanism is employed to assign each incoming task dynamically to one of three VM clusters – High-Capacity Cluster (HCC), Medium-Capacity Cluster (MCC), or Low-Capacity Cluster (LCC) – based on its priority and computational demand, as described in Algorithm 2.

Membership value computation

Each task \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_i$$\end{document} is characterized by two attributes: priority \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} and computational demand \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_s$$\end{document}, measured in million instructions (MI). Both attributes are normalized using Min–Max scaling to eliminate range bias:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mu _{\text {priority}} = \frac{p_i - p_{\min }}{p_{\max } - p_{\min }}, \qquad \mu _{\text {burst}} = 1 - \frac{t_s - t_{\min }}{t_{\max } - t_{\min }} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The inversion of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _{\text {burst}}$$\end{document} ensures that tasks with shorter computational demand receive higher preference, reflecting the scheduling bias toward lightweight, time-sensitive workloads.

Normalized priority score

The normalized priority score \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p \in [0,1]$$\end{document} is computed as a weighted linear combination:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} n_p = \psi _1 \cdot \mu _{\text {priority}} + \psi _2 \cdot \mu _{\text {burst}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi _1 = 0.65$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi _2 = 0.35$$\end{document} are fixed weighting coefficients satisfying \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\psi _1 + \psi _2 = 1$$\end{document}. The higher weight assigned to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _{\text {priority}}$$\end{document} reflects the emphasis on scheduling urgency over task size.

Cluster assignment

Based on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p$$\end{document}, each task is assigned a preferred cluster using fixed threshold-based rules:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Cluster}(t_i) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} \text {HCC}, & \text {if } n_p \ge 0.75, \\ \text {MCC}, & \text {if } 0.45 \le n_p< 0.75, \\ \text {LCC}, & \text {if } n_p < 0.45. \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This preferred cluster label is stored per task and used to guide the subsequent GEOA-based VM assignment stage. Tasks with high priority and short burst size are directed to HCC, while less critical or computationally intensive tasks are routed to MCC or LCC.

Priority-aware ordering and batch scheduling

To ensure high-priority tasks are processed first while maintaining temporal fairness, each task is assigned a scheduling score:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S(t_i) = p_i - \lambda \cdot a_i, \quad \lambda = 0.1 \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_i$$\end{document} is task priority and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_i$$\end{document} is arrival time. The factor λ = 0.1 penalizes late-arriving tasks mildly, preventing priority inversion without starving delayed high-priority tasks.

To improve scalability, tasks are processed in temporal batches. All tasks are first sorted by arrival time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$a_i$$\end{document}, and those arriving within a 3-second window are grouped into a single batch \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {B}_w$$\end{document}. Within each batch, tasks are re-sorted in descending order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(t_i)$$\end{document}, enforcing priority-aware scheduling at the sub-batch level. This two-level scheme arrival-based grouping followed by intra-batch priority ordering reduces scheduling decision latency while preserving urgency-aware dispatch.

Hybrid Fuzzy Clustering with Golden Eagle Optimization Algorithm (HFC-GEOA)

The Golden Eagle Optimization Algorithm (GEOA) is inspired by the predatory behaviour of golden eagles, which first survey the terrain in wide spirals (cruising) and then dive directly toward a selected target (attacking). This adaptive transition from broad exploration to focused exploitation is exploited here to solve the task-to-VM scheduling problem. In the proposed framework each eagle represents a task and each prey candidate corresponds to a virtual machine (VM), as illustrated in Fig. 3. To support efficient execution in fog environments, HFC-GEOA first applies a deterministic k-means++ clustering mechanism to partition VMs into three capacity tiers based on resource similarity and geographical proximity. Once the appropriate cluster \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} is selected for a given task, GEOA is executed within that cluster to identify the optimal task-to-VM mapping. This coordinated design achieves adaptive and balanced scheduling by minimising performance variation under dynamic workload and mobility conditions.Fig. 3Main steps of GEOA to deal with clusters: (a) attack vector is calculated, (b) cruise hyperplane is constructed, (c) a random cruise vector is generated inside the cruise hyperplane, (d) a step vector is constructed from the attack and cruise vectors (intra-cluster), and (e) if the task fails to find the optimal VM within the cluster, a fallback procedure performs inter-cluster migration.

GEOA search within cluster

Before GEOA execution, candidate VMs in the target cluster are pre-sorted in descending order of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FC_j$$\end{document} so that agents are initialised near high-quality solutions, accelerating convergence without additional fitness evaluations.

A task \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_i$$\end{document} is assigned to a VM cluster based on its fuzzy task score. Within the cluster, the optimal VM is identified using the attack and cruise vectors of GEOA.

Attack vector. The attack vector drives exploitation toward the current best VM:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \overrightarrow{A_i} \;=\; \overrightarrow{X^{*}} - \overrightarrow{X_n} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^{*}$$\end{document} is the best-fit VM position and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X_n$$\end{document} is the current agent position. It quantifies the direction and magnitude of movement toward the most promising candidate and is used for exploitation (attacking the prey).

Cruise hyperplane. The cruise vector supports adaptive exploration around the current best solution. The tangent hyperplane centred on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^{*}$$\end{document} is defined by23\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^{n} m_j k_j \;=\; D \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overrightarrow{m} = [e_1, e_2, \dots , e_n]$$\end{document} is the normal vector (i.e. the attack vector) and D is a scalar constant. This hyperplane supports cruise exploration by confining the search to a locally structured neighbourhood around the best VM rather than moving blindly.

The cruise hyperplane equation is expressed as24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^{n} e_j k_j \;=\; \sum _{j=1}^{n} e_j^{\textsf{T}} k^{*}_j \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overrightarrow{A_i} = [e_1, e_2, \dots , e_n]$$\end{document} is the attack vector, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K = [k_1, k_2, \dots , k_n]$$\end{document} represents the decision variables (VM positions in the cluster), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K^{*} = [k^{*}_1, k^{*}_2, \dots , k^{*}_n]$$\end{document} is the location of the best VM found within the cluster. This constraint ensures that cruise movement occurs in a tangent plane near the best solution, balancing local and global search.

Random point on cruise plane. A new exploration point is sampled on the cruise hyperplane:25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_s \;=\; \frac{D - \displaystyle \sum _{j \ne s,\; j \in C_j} e_j}{e_s} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_s$$\end{document} is the s-th element of point T, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} is the current cluster, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_j$$\end{document} is the j-th component of the attack vector. Only VMs inside the same cluster \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_j$$\end{document} are considered, ensuring task assignments remain within cluster boundaries.

The full target point on the hyperplane is26\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} T_i \;=\; \left( T_1 = \textrm{rand},\; T_2 = \textrm{rand},\;\dots ,\; T_s = \frac{D - \sum _{j \ne s,\; j \in C_j} e_j}{e_s},\; \dots ,\; T_n = \textrm{rand} \right) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where random values are drawn uniformly from [0, 1]. The cruise vector attracts agents toward a locally valid exploration point rather than a stored memory location.

Step vector. The step vector combines attack and cruise contributions to produce the guided movement toward the optimal VM:27\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Delta S_i \;=\; u_1 f_a \frac{\overrightarrow{A_i}}{\Vert \overrightarrow{A_i}\Vert } + u_2 f_c \frac{\overrightarrow{T_i}}{\Vert \overrightarrow{T_i}\Vert } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_a$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_c$$\end{document} are the adaptive attack and cruise coefficients, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_1, u_2 \sim \mathcal {U}(0,1)$$\end{document}. The position of each search agent at iteration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_c + 1$$\end{document} is updated by adding the step vector to the current position:30\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} S^{t_c+1} \;=\; S^{t_c} + \Delta S_n^{t_c} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S^{t_c}$$\end{document} is the agent position at iteration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_c$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta S_n^{t_c}$$\end{document} is the step vector computed at that iteration. Agent positions are clamped to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[0,\,|C_j|-1]$$\end{document} so that every index refers to a valid VM.

Adaptive attack/cruise transition. The attack and cruise coefficients are updated over iterations to promote a gradual shift from exploration to exploitation:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} F_a&= F_a^{0} + \frac{t_c}{t_m} \left| F_a^{t_m} - F_a^{0} \right| \\ F_c&= F_c^{0} - \frac{t_c}{t_m} \left| F_c^{0} - F_c^{t_m} \right| \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_c$$\end{document} is the current iteration and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_m = 50$$\end{document} is the maximum number of inner iterations. Diversity and early stopping. To maintain population diversity, each agent undergoes an intra-iteration random mutation with probability 0.05, replacing its current index with a uniformly sampled VM. Fallback procedure based on VM fitness and cluster capacity

The system employs a hierarchical fallback mechanism guided by cluster capacity and VM suitability37, as illustrated in Fig. 2. When a preferred cluster is saturated, tasks are adaptively redirected to the next available tier, preventing allocation failure and maintaining system throughput under dynamic workloads. Clusters are labelled as HCC, MCC, or LCC based on their average capacity score (Eq. 5).

Each task \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$H_i$$\end{document} initiates allocation within its preferred cluster tier, following the ordered fallback chain:32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \textrm{Fallback}(H_i) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll} {[}\text {HCC}\rightarrow \text {MCC}\rightarrow \text {LCC}], & N_i \in \text {HCC} \\ {[}\text {MCC}\rightarrow \text {HCC}\rightarrow \text {LCC}], & N_i \in \text {MCC} \\ {[}\text {LCC} \rightarrow \text {MCC} \rightarrow \text {HCC}], & N_i \in \text {LCC} \end{array}\right. } \end{aligned}$$\end{document}The chain prioritises the adjacent capacity tier before escalating further, minimising unnecessary resource mismatch and migration overhead. Within each candidate cluster, GEOA evaluates VMs using the full fitness function (Eq. 10).

VM selection. The optimal VM is selected by minimising the fitness cost within the preferred cluster:33\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} v^{*} = \underset{v \in C_{\text {preferred}}}{\arg \min }\; FC_j(v) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Saturation check and fallback condition. To ensure QoS compliance, the selected VM is rejected and fallback is triggered if any of the following saturation conditions hold:34\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} U_{\text {CPU},i}> 0.85 \;\text {OR}\; Q_i> 15 \;\text {OR}\; \bigl (FC_j(v^{*}) < \theta _{\text {fitness}} \;\text {AND}\; U_{\text {CPU},i}> 0.70\bigr ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{\text {fitness}} = 0.60$$\end{document} (Table 3). The composite condition prevents assignment to near-overloaded VMs even when their raw fitness nominally satisfies the threshold, providing a more robust saturation guard than single-metric checks.

Migration overhead. When fallback occurs, migration latency is explicitly modelled to ensure realistic cost accounting:35\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} c^{\text {mig}} = \frac{s_{\text {MB}}}{B} + d_{\text {proc}} + \varepsilon \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where B = 100 Mbps is the inter-cluster link bandwidth, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{\text {proc}} = 0.002$$\end{document} s is the processing delay, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon \sim \mathcal {U}(0,\,0.02)$$\end{document} s is a stochastic network jitter term. The associated migration energy \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$e_{\text {mig}} = s_{\text {MB}} \times 0.5$$\end{document} J is included in the total energy (Eq. 42), ensuring fallback decisions carry realistic overhead in both latency and energy metrics.

Fallback ratio. The efficiency of primary cluster selection is quantified by:36\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} FR = \frac{N_{\text {fb}}}{N_{\text {tot}}} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {fb}}$$\end{document} is the number of inter-cluster task migrations and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_{\text {tot}}$$\end{document} is the total number of submitted tasks. A lower FR indicates that the fuzzy scoring mechanism is routing tasks accurately to their preferred clusters, reducing migration overhead and improving overall QoS.

Reliability and fault-tolerance modelling

Failure rate model. The per-VM failure rate is modelled as load-dependent, reflecting the well-established relationship between resource utilisation and execution failure probability:37\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \lambda _v = \lambda _{\text {base}} (1 + \kappa \, U_{\text {CPU},v}) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{\text {base}} = 0.010$$\end{document} and κ = 3.0. A VM at full utilisation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{\text {CPU}} = 1.0$$\end{document}) therefore carries a failure rate of 0.040 – four times the base rate. For migrated tasks, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _v^{\text {mig}} = 1.20 \cdot \lambda _v$$\end{document}, reflecting the additional transfer risk of inter-cluster state migration.

Task failure probability. Three independent failure sources are combined to produce a realistic per-task failure probability:38\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} P_{\text {fail}}(v_i) = \min \!\bigl (0.20,\; 1-(1-p_1)(1-p_2)(1-p_3)\bigr ) \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_1 = \min (0.12,\,\lambda _v \cdot \tau \cdot k_f)$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_f=3.5$$\end{document}) captures VM-load execution failure, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_2 = 0.015 + 0.0075 \cdot \textbf{1}_{\text {mig}}$$\end{document} models scheduling and migration instability, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_3 = 0.005\,U_{\text {Mem},i}$$\end{document} captures memory-pressure failures. The overall probability is clamped at 0.20 to reflect realistic fog environment bounds.

Fault-tolerance and operational stability are evaluated using the mean exponential reliability model, which avoids numerical underflow for large VM populations:39\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} R = \frac{1}{|V|} \sum _{v \in V} e^{-\lambda _v \cdot T} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where T = 30 s is the scheduling horizon. Fault-tolerance index. Performance evaluation

The proposed HFC-GEOA algorithm is implemented in iFogSim2 and compared with ACO-LWC24, EWOA20, FCLB25 and FGELB22.

The simulations are performed on an Intel x64 2.6 GHz dual-core machine with 6 GB RAM under Windows 10 (64-bit). The experimental setup is representative of a typical edge/fog simulation environment, where the focus is on algorithmic efficiency and not hardware scaling.

The fog environment includes a cloud layer, a set of clustered fog nodes and heterogeneous IoT-generated tasks. Experiments vary task volumes (1,000–20,000) and VM configurations (20–1,500 VMs) across five scenarios (S1–S5) to evaluate the effectiveness of dynamic load balancing and task scheduling under heterogeneous conditions.

Performance is evaluated using energy consumption, average turnaround time, average waiting time, failure rate, system reliability, fallback ratio, and fault tolerance. All results are reported as mean ± std over 30 independent runs using seeds 1–30, as detailed in Tables 1 and 2.

The fallback mechanism is analysed in terms of inter-cluster task migration when local resources become overloaded or unsuitable. Fault tolerance

Fault tolerance is a unified resilience score integrating execution success, scheduling stability, and system reliability:58\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} FT = \omega _1 \cdot (1 - F_r) + \omega _2 \cdot (1 - FR_{fb}) + \omega _3 \cdot R \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _1 = 0.50$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _2 = 0.30$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega _3 = 0.20$$\end{document} are weighting coefficients reflecting the relative importance of task success, fallback stability, and system reliability. A higher FT score indicates a more resilient scheduling strategy.

Ablation study and component analysis

A controlled ablation study was performed for the 5k–150 scenario over 30 independent runs, to quantify the incremental contribution of each architectural component. Three degraded variants were tested: NO_CLUSTER disables VM clustering and assigns all VMs to a flat pool, NO_FUZZY disables fuzzy scoring and uses VM-proportional random cluster assignment, and NO_GEOA disables GEOA and uses greedy FIFO selection within the same cluster pool.

As shown in Table 5, removing clustering increases energy by 61.6% and AWT by 53.0%, confirming that capacity-aware VM grouping is the dominant contributor to both energy efficiency and latency reduction. Removing fuzzy scoring increases AWT by 8.8% and energy by 1.6%, while FT remains comparable, indicating that fuzzy routing provides a moderate but consistent benefit to scheduling quality. Removing GEOA degrades AWT by 12.8% and FT by 9.6% (Wilcoxon p < 0.001), demonstrating that fitness-guided search is essential for reliability-aware assignment. The full HFC-GEOA achieves the best balanced trade-off across all metrics, confirming all three components are complementary and collectively necessary.

Table 6 further confirms that GEOA-guided assignment improves AWT by 11–52% and FT by 11–16% over greedy FIFO across all scenarios, with improvements becoming more pronounced at larger scale.

Results and discussions

The experimental results demonstrate that HFC-GEOA consistently outperforms all baseline approaches across multiple task–VM configurations, achieving balanced improvements in latency, energy consumption, and system reliability. These gains are primarily attributed to the integration of cluster-aware filtering, fuzzy-driven prioritisation, and localised metaheuristic optimisation, which collectively enable efficient workload distribution and reduced queuing delays.

The selected baselines represent three comparison categories: (1) fault-tolerant metaheuristic approaches (EWOA), against which HFC-GEOA’s fault-tolerance mechanism is directly evaluated; (2) energy-aware load balancing methods without explicit fault recovery (FGELB, ACO-LWC), which assess scheduling efficiency independent of fault-tolerance contributions; and (3) the cluster-state-based greedy method FCLB, which serves as a non-metaheuristic baseline to quantify the benefit of optimisation-driven assignment. This multi-category design allows each architectural stage of HFC-GEOA to be evaluated against the most relevant competing strategy.

Mechanism-level analysis of performance advantages

The performance advantages of HFC-GEOA over baseline algorithms are directly attributable to its structural design. The fuzzy admission mechanism routes tasks to capacity-matched clusters before VM selection, ensuring that high-priority tasks (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p \ge 0.75$$\end{document}) are structurally directed to HCC where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\text {exec}} = MI/\text {MIPS}$$\end{document} is minimised. This contrasts with FCLB’s arrival-order greedy selection which assigns tasks without priority awareness, producing a fallback rate of 70–74% (Table 13) vs HFC-GEOA’s controlled 34–58% at S3–S5 – each additional fallback incurring migration delay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$W_{\text {mig}}$$\end{document} (Eq. 35) that accumulates into the observed 91% AWT gap at S4 (Table 9).

GEOA’s adaptive attack/cruise schedule (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_a$$\end{document}: 0.50→2.00; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_c$$\end{document}: 1.00→0.50, Eq. 31) mathematically guarantees a transition from broad exploration to focused exploitation, preventing the premature convergence to locally loaded VMs that afflicts FCLB’s pure-exploitation greedy strategy and ACO-LWC’s pheromone-reinforced historical bias under dynamic fog workloads. The 5% intra-iteration mutation and cubic fitness amplification \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FC_j = \max (0, 1-\text {cost})^3$$\end{document} (Eq. 10) further sharpen inter-VM discrimination by expanding the effective fitness range from [0.62, 0.93] to [0.24, 0.80]. Conclusion and future scope

This study presented HFC-GEOA, a hybrid fuzzy clustering and Golden Eagle Optimization-based framework for task-to-VM allocation in fog computing environments. The observed performance improvements are directly attributable to the joint effect of three design choices that address distinct scheduling bottlenecks.

The fast k-means++ clustering mechanism reduces the per-task VM search space from |V| to |V|/k, achieving a factor-of-k reduction in GEOA computational cost compared to flat-pool metaheuristics such as FGELB, while ensuring that capacity-matched VMs are grouped together before any assignment decision is made. The fuzzy-linear membership scoring routes high-priority tasks (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_p \ge 0.75$$\end{document}) directly to HCC where execution time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t_{\text {exec}} = MI/\text {MIPS}$$\end{document} is minimised by design, which is the primary driver of the 63–266% AWT improvement observed when fuzzy scoring is removed in the ablation study. The GEOA fitness function, with its adaptive attack/cruise coefficient schedule (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_a$$\end{document}: 0.50→2.00; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_c$$\end{document}: 1.00→0.50) and cubic amplification \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$FC_j = \max (0,1-\mathcal {C})^3$$\end{document}, prevents premature convergence to locally loaded VMs – a limitation of both FCLB’s greedy strategy and ACO-LWC’s pheromone-reinforced historical bias – producing 13–14% higher fault tolerance scores than the NO_GEOA variant. Together these three mechanisms produce the observed reduction in average waiting time of up to 87% over FGELB, 91% over FCLB, and a fault tolerance score of 0.83 at S5, without relying on idealized fault assumptions.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. The framework assumes static VM configurations with fixed MIPS, RAM, and geographic coordinates, which may not reflect real fog deployments where VMs are dynamically provisioned. No user mobility model is incorporated, limiting applicability to scenarios such as vehicular fog computing. The fallback chain may exhaust all clusters under extreme resource scarcity, as evidenced by S1 where the 50:1 task-to-VM ratio produces a fallback rate of 97.5% and AWT of 118.72 s. Fitness weights \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_1$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_4$$\end{document} were selected via grid search and may not generalise to all workload types.

Future work will address these limitations through four specific directions. First, dynamic VM re-clustering triggered by workload drift detection will extend HFC-GEOA to provisioning-aware fog environments. Second, integration with two-timescale MEC frameworks will enable mobility-aware task routing across handover boundaries. Third, reinforcement learning-based adaptive weight tuning for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_1$$\end{document}–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_4$$\end{document} will replace the static grid search with an online policy that adapts to changing workload distributions. Fourth, physical testbed validation on Raspberry Pi-based fog nodes will quantify the gap between iFogSim2 simulation and real deployment conditions, providing deployment-grade overhead estimates for the HFC-GEOA scheduling pipeline.
