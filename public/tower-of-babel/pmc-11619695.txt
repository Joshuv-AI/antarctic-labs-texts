Facilitating comprehensive child health monitoring within REDCap - an open-source code for real-time Z-score assessments
Authors: Priyanka Mendon, Michael Witsch, Marianne Becker, Aurélie Adamski, Michel Vaillant
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonitoring of somatic development through the assessment of anthropometric variables such as weight, height, and BMI is vital for evaluating the physical development and nutritional status of children. This approach aids in the early identification of somatic developmental disorders, enabling timely medical interventions. It traditionally relies on Z-scores, which compare anthropometric variables with reference standards. In addition to somatic development monitoring, the early detection and management of pediatric and adolescent hypertension are crucial due to potential long-term health risks. However, manual calculations of Z-scores are time-consuming and error-prone, impeding timely interventions for at-risk children. This article introduces an innovative open-code solution for real-time Z-score assessments directly within the electronic data capture platform, Research Electronic Data Capture, (REDCap™), aiming to streamline the monitoring of somatic development in children.MethodsLeveraging the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) references, our approach integrates Z-score computations directly into REDCap, providing a secure and user-friendly environment for healthcare professionals and researchers. We employed Bland-Altman analyses to compare our method with established calculators (Knirps and Growth XP™) using synthetic data values for all variables.ResultsBland-Altman plots demonstrated strong agreement between our REDCap calculations and the Knirps and Growth XP systems. Z-scores for height, BMI, and blood pressure consistently aligned, affirming the accuracy of our approach across the measurement range.ConclusionThe integration with REDCap streamlines data collection and analysis, eliminating the need for separate software and data exports. Moreover, our solution uses the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards and National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) references. This not only ensures calculation accuracy but also enhances its suitability for diverse research contexts. The Bland-Altman analyses establish the reliability of our method, contributing to a more effective approach to child growth and blood pressure monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12874-024-02405-0.

Background

Pediatric somatic development monitoring

Monitoring somatic development in children plays a critical role in assessing their physical development and nutritional status of children. It involves comparing weight, height, and BMI measurements with standard values to detect early signs of developmental disorders and enable timely medical interventions, if indicated. Traditional methods employ Z-score and percentile calculations to assess a child’s growth, weight, and BMI status relative to a reference population, to identify stunted growth, obesity and accelerated growth [1, 2].

In addition to the described somatic monitoring, the early detection and management of pediatric and adolescent hypertension are vital due to the potential long-term health risks of unrecognized and hence untreated hypertension [3].

About REDCap

Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [9] is a secure web platform created in 2004 at Vanderbilt University. Designed for researchers to design, develop, and manage online databases and surveys, REDCap offers a streamlined process for rapid project creation. It provides a vast array of tools that can be tailored to virtually any data collection strategy.

REDCap provides automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to Excel and common statistical packages (SPSS, SAS, Stata, R), a built-in project calendar, a scheduling module, ad hoc reporting tools, and advanced features such as branching logic, file uploading, and calculated fields.

It is widely used in academic research institutions. The REDCap Consortium has 6,634 active partners in 154 countries. Notably, it is free to non-profit organizations that join the REDCap Consortium [9].

Need for developing a clinical data collection interface for real-time Z-score calculations

An assessment of the existing tools and software reveals a lack of freely available options with real-time calculations specifically tailored to child somatic development monitoring and blood pressure studies. It is also observed that these tools are non-integrated into a secure web-based platform that facilitates structured data collection and analysis in clinical settings.

The REDCap Shared Library [10] offers a repository of data collection instruments and forms that are available for download and use by researchers at REDCap partner institutions. However, currently there is no instrument available in the Shared Library that provides automated Z-score computations, which are required for a project for screening of Familial Hypercholesterolemia in School Children in Luxembourg [11].

This study aimed to assess the prevalence of familial hypercholesterolemia in primary school children in Luxembourg City between 7 and 12 years old, invited for their mandatory medical school examinations between 2021 and 2023. This study serves as a relevant reference for the context and importance of real-time Z-score calculations, providing valuable insights into the application of growth monitoring and blood pressure assessments in a large-scale research setting.

To address this need gap, we designed a data collection instrument to collect variables and incorporate backend calculations, to allow real-time Z-Score computations by leveraging the ease of use of REDCap. This instrument was then validated by adding synthetic participant values in REDCap and two comparator software tools to verify the accuracy of the resulting values.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive overview of our methodology, the implementation of real-time Z-score calculations within the REDCap platform, and validation by comparison with two existing software tools. By sharing this open code solution, we aim to promote standardized growth and blood pressure assessments, facilitate timely interventions, and enhance evidence-based pediatric care. The objective is to foster collaboration and avoid duplicative efforts in similar studies. Enabling researchers to access the code would promote sustainability for future research on child growth and hypertension by providing a robust tool for real-time Z-score computations.

Methods

The Methods section describes the rationale of selecting REDCap, the growth standards used for computations, and how the data collection instrument was designed with back end calculations.

Rationale of selecting REDCap

The user-friendly interface requires no background knowledge or technical expertise. REDCap’s web-based nature is compliant with 21CFR part 11 (Part 11 of Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations), FISMA (Federal Information Security Modernization Act), HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act) and GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation). This software is available at no cost for REDCap Consortium partners. Thus, REDCap makes an ideal choice for data collection instruments due to its broad accessibility.

For this project, REDCap version 13.1.29 was used.

Description of the growth standards used

The algorithm utilizes the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards (5 to 19 years) developed in 2007, providing a single growth reference for school-aged children and adolescents [14–16]. The WHO standards are the first globally representative growth standards [17].

To enable accurate blood pressure assessments, we employ the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) LMS tables for the calculation of Height Z-scores, along with age and sex data referring to the guidelines proposed in “The Fourth Report on the Diagnosis, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure in Children and Adolescents” [18] .

NHANES provides data that are instrumental in establishing growth and health standards. Blood pressure standards based on sex, age, and height, provide a more precise classification of blood pressure according to body size. In clinical settings, the height percentile is determined by using the CDC Growth Charts, employing the pre-calculated LMS values for height-for-age (HA) and sex assessment.

The LMS method uses three parameters: the median (M), the generalized coefficient of variation (S), and the power in the Box-Cox transformation (L). The LMS method adjusts for skewness and kurtosis in the distribution of anthropometric data, providing a more accurate reflection of the underlying growth patterns. This is crucial for variables like height and BMI, which may not follow a normal distribution.

The height Z-score is then used in the computation of the systolic and diastolic blood pressure Z-score.

Synthetic participant data with specific values for each of the data entry fields was entered in both REDCap and the comparator software tools to ensure same results are obtained.

To validate the blood pressure Z-scores of REDCap with the Knirps calculator, the REDCap Blood pressure Z-scores values were compared against the Knirps Blood Pressure percentile values using the Z-score to percentile table (Supplementary Table).

Bland-Altman plots for demonstrating the agreement in methods of REDCap, Knirps and GrowthXP

Bland-Altman plots, also known as Tukey Mean-Difference plots, are used to compare measurement calculations performed using two distinct tools: our custom calculations in REDCap and the Knirps calculator. These plots allow us to visually assess the agreement between the two measurement methods by examining the distribution of the differences between them. They provide a clear overview of how closely the REDCap calculations align with those produced by the Knirps calculator [26].

As can be deciphered from Fig. 7, Bland-Altman plots demonstrate strong agreement between the proposed REDCap solution, the Knirps calculator, and the Growth XP system. Discussion

Our open-source code solution seamlessly integrates Z-score calculations into the REDCap platform, facilitating real-time assessments and offering customization options.

As mentioned before, current tools and software offering Z-score and Percentile Calculations based on WHO 2007 growth charts, including Epi Info 7 offering data entry forms for these calculations.

However, there is a scarcity of other freely available options with real-time calculations specifically tailored to child growth and blood pressure monitoring. To address this identified need gap, this proposed open-code solution emerges as a specialized approach within the REDCap platform.

By incorporating Z-score calculations directly within REDCap, this solution streamlines the entire data collection and analysis process for child growth monitoring and hypertension studies, allowing researchers to perform all tasks within a unified platform. This eliminates the need for external software or data exports and empowers timely interventions for children at risk.

The open-code approach fosters collaboration, creating a community of users committed to continuous improvement. This collaborative potential ensures the tool’s sustainability, maintaining its relevance in the dynamic field of child health research.

However, our newly developed product has limitations. The “Calculated Field” module, intended for simple calculations and conditional logic, restricts us to basic mathematical operations. Despite the notification of calculation validity in the Logic Editor text box, instances of a blank calculated data entry field have occurred due to inaccuracies in the formula.

To facilitate a better user experience for entering logic, the logic editor is displayed in a modal dialogue box. The logic editor also prompts the user with a “valid” notification at the bottom left of the dialogue box to gauge the correctness of the entered logic as seen in Fig. 8.Fig. 8Screen capture of REDCap logic editor module highlighting the validity of the entered logic

Screen capture of REDCap logic editor module highlighting the validity of the entered logic

Calculated fields rely on values from other fields, and modifying these source values may prompt the recalculation of dependent fields, potentially causing unforeseen results, if not managed carefully. However, there is a corrective feature in the Data Quality module called as “Rule H: Incorrect values for calculated fields”, which re-runs the calculations and “fixes” these errors. However, after this fix, the data entry forms that have been impacted must be re-saved for the updated calculation to reflect in the data field audit trail [12].

Complex calculations within the “Calculated Field” module could potentially influence the performance of data entry forms, leading to slower page loading times, especially when dealing with large datasets or intricate logic.

Debugging errors in calculated fields, especially complex or nested calculations, could be challenging perhaps because of the lack of comprehensive debugging tools.

Differences in REDCap versions across projects or institutions, may lead to compatibility issues for calculated fields, particularly if users with different versions access the same project.

Despite these limitations, we used our open-source code solution within REDCap successfully during the EARLIE study without major issues in the calculations. Statistical analyses are currently ongoing to produce the final results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper introduces an open-code solution integrated within the REDCap platform for real-time Z-score calculations, which is valuable for pediatric somatic growth monitoring and hypertension studies using the WHO and NHANES reference standards. The methodology addresses the existing need gap associated with manual and non-integrated methods of Z-score calculations. With this approach, data collection would be streamlined and efficient. Lastly, the solution demonstrates broad applicability in pediatric research settings. The article is summarized in Fig. 9.
