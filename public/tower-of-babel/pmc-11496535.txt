Analysis of body composition, functionality and muscle-specific strength of older women with obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Leonardo Augusto da Costa Teixeira, Luana Aparecida Soares, Sueli Ferreira da Fonseca, Gabriele Teixeira Gonçalves, Jousielle Márcia dos Santos, Ângela Alves Viegas, Adriana Netto Parentoni, Pedro Henrique Scheidt Figueiredo, Vanessa Amaral Mendonça, Ana Cristina Rodrigues Lacerda
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
During aging, changes in body composition can result in sarcopenic obesity, which is a condition in which obesity occurs accompanied by the loss of muscle mass and strength caused by sarcopenia. Although the effects of obesity and sarcopenia on body composition are known, the muscle-specific strength in older women with sarcopenic obesity remains under-researched. The objective of this study was to evaluate community-dwelling older women for the absence or presence of obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity and compare them in terms of body composition, functional physical performance and muscle-specific strength. One hundred and fifty-six older women (± 74 years) were evaluated for body composition using Dual X-ray Absorptiometry, handgrip strength with a Jamar dynamometer and functional performance using gait speed and timed up and go tests. The presence of obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity was found in 32.7%, 15.4% and 25% of the sample, respectively. Comparing groups, community-dwelling older women with sarcopenic obesity exhibited poorer functional physical performance (TUG ± 14 s), and lower muscle-specific strength (± 1.18). Sarcopenic obesity was associated with muscle-specific strength (95% IC 0.016–0.241), and TUG (95% CI 1.001–1.137). These findings indicate that the combination of obesity and sarcopenia has a negative impact on skeletal muscle, reducing muscle-specific strength and physical performance in older women with more declines than either disease alone. Therefore, this comprehensive assessment gives useful information for incorporating muscle-specific strength into the diagnosis of sarcopenic obesity in the older people.

Introduction

During aging, changes in body composition combined with changes in lifestyle habits contribute to the development of obesity, sarcopenia and these conditions concomitantly, known as sarcopenic obesity1. The hypertrophy and increase in the number of adipocytes characteristic of obesity, combined with the loss of lean muscle mass resulting from sarcopenia, contribute to the infiltration of fat between muscle fibers, further reducing their contractile capacity, performance and muscle-specific strength2,3. These events expose older people to a greater probability of adverse health outcomes, such as loss of mobility, loss of functional independence, hospitalization, and mortality4–6.

Sarcopenic obesity is an emerging individual and public health problem as older adults face the combined physical and pathophysiological consequences of two conditions at the same time4,6. Inflammation is a component of aging, functional performance in chronic conditions, obesity, sarcopenia, and sarcopenic obesity7–9. In addition to the particular metabolic consequences of obesity, the chronic low-grade inflammation that occurs during aging can add to the inflammation of sarcopenia, resulting in greater muscle catabolism, loss of the muscle-specific strength and the functionality in the older7,10,11.

The term “muscle-specific strength” has already been studied as a method to qualify muscle strength per unit of appendicular skeletal muscle mass12–14. It is a measure obtained from the relativization of muscle strength measurements by lean muscle mass, which is expressed by the muscle-specific strength, also known as muscle quality index, as it serves dual roles: not only does it assess the capacity to generate force, but it also has significant predictive value for mortality across various clinical populations15, and has been suggested as a promising alternative for the assessment of sarcopenia in the older12,16. Along these lines, studies suggest that different muscle-specific strength indices are stronger predictors of muscular performance than body mass index (BMI), measures of lean muscle mass or muscular strength alone17,18.

Although numerous studies have examined anthropometric characteristics, body composition, functional physical performance, and muscle-specific strength in sarcopenia19–23, few have investigated these aspects in the context of sarcopenic obesity24. Therefore, the objective of the present study was to compare the compartmentalized body composition, physical performance and muscle-specific strength of older people with obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity. In addition, this study aims to investigate the relationship between body composition parameters and muscle-specific strength in older women to provide a useful understanding for clinical and research purposes, specifically for screening, diagnosing, and monitoring sarcopenic obesity.

Methods

This cross-sectional and exploratory study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Vales do Jequitinhonha and Mucuri (UFVJM), with protocol: 1,461,306. The research was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and was edited following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Sample

Community-dwelling older women were recruited from their records at the Basic Health Units (UBS) of Diamantina, Minas Gerais, Brazil. All participants were visited in their homes and answered a questionnaire with information about medical history, lifestyle habits and comorbidities. All participants presented written informed consent. The inclusion criteria were women over 65 years of age; be functionally independent in the community; and capable of carrying out the proposed assessment procedures.

Exclusion criteria were cognitive dysfunction assessed by the Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE) with a cut-off point depending on the scholarity level25; older women unable to walk independently in the community; who have been hospitalized or have suffered fractures in the last three months; presence of neoplasia in the last five years; in palliative care; subjects who practiced physical activity regularly (more than three times a week); with severe visual and hearing impairment; with neurologic sequelae; and those with inflammatory, endocrine, metabolic, respiratory or cardiovascular conditions in acute phase.

Procedures

The evaluations occurred between June 2016 and June 2017 in the exercise physiology laboratory (LAFIEX) and in inflammation and metabolism laboratory (LIM) at UFVJM. The procedures included two previously planned sessions. On the first day, they received visits to their homes where they answered the health questionnaire, and the inclusion and exclusion criteria were checked. On the second day, participants underwent body composition exams and physical functional tests to assess sarcopenia, obesity and sarcopenic obesity.

Anthropometric and body composition assessment

The volunteers’ body mass was measured using a digital scale (Welmy, model 110, precision of 0.1 kg and resolution of 0.1 kg) with an attached stadiometer (accuracy of 0.5 cm) to assess weight and height. The older women were evaluated without wearing shoes and wearing light clothing. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing body mass by height squared (kg/m2), and the classification according to WHO was verified. To assess compartmentalized body composition, Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA - Lunar Type DPX, with Encore 2005 software) was used26, and the fat percentage (%), total fat mass (kg), visceral fat (g), and appendicular lean muscle mass—ALM (kg) were extracted from DXA reports. The same evaluator performed all body composition assessments in the morning with the participants fasting of food and liquid. Afterwards, there was a 15-min break for rest and a standardized snack, and then the participants began the handgrip strength and the physical performance tests.

Assessment of muscle strength and physical performance

Muscle strength was assessed by handgrip strength (HGS) using the Jamar® dynamometer1,13. Participants were instructed to remain in a seated position with their hand in a neutral angle, their elbow flexed (90º) and their shoulder in a neutral angle, an isometric contraction of the dominant hand was requested 3 times applied to the dynamometer handles, which was expressed in kg. The results of the average of three measurements were used for the analyses1,13.

The “gait speed test” (GS) and the “Timed up and Go” (TUG) were applied to evaluate the physical capacity of older women considering agility, balance, gait speed and lower limb muscle strength27. The TUG is a test that consists of documenting the time taken by the individual to get up from a chair, walk three meters, go around an obstacle, return and sit down again. The longer the time taken to perform the test, the worse the functional performance will be. The GS consists of documenting the time taken to cover a space of 6 m, the longer the time spent, the worse the performance in the test27.

Assessment of muscle-specific strength

Muscle-specific strength was assessed relativizing the values obtained in measurements of muscle strength (HGS expressed in kg) by the measured values of appendicular lean muscle mass (ALM expressed in kg), applied in the formula: Muscle-specific strength = HGS/ALM16.

Sample classification

Obesity was diagnosed when % of fat was equal to or greater than 40%28,29, and sarcopenia was detected when HGS ≤ 20 kg23 and ALM < 15 kg13,30,31. The classification of the sample followed ESPEN recommendations1. Sarcopenic obesity was identified when obesity and sarcopenia were present concomitantly, i.e., % of fat ≥ 40, HGS ≤ 20 kg and ALM < 15 kg1.

Statistical analyzes

Sample size was carried out using the Openepi software considering a population of 2522 older women (> 65 years old) from the city of Diamantina, Minas Gerais, registered with the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE—ibge.gov.br). Thus, considering a prevalence of sarcopenic obesity of 11%32, effect size of 0.80, significance level of 5% and confidence interval of 80%, a sample size of 156 older women was stipulated.

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 22.0 (SPSS Statistics; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to perform statistical analyses. Data normality was verified using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and continuous variables were expressed as mean, and minimum and maximum values (min–max). The Kruskal–Wallys ANOVA test and/or one-way independent ANOVA, as appropriate, were used to compare the mean differences of continuous variables between older women without and with obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity.

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to verify the association between sarcopenic obesity and muscle-specific strength. Linear regression analysis was performed to verify the association between total fat percentage and muscle-specific strength. Four assumptions were used in the linear regression analysis: linearity, residual distribution, homoscedasticity and absence of multicollinearity. Scatterplots were used to evaluate the behavior of independent variables and residuals, and a histogram was used to observe the distribution of residuals. Variance inflation factor (VIF) values below 10.0 were used to define the absence of multicollinearity. Additionally, the autocorrelation of the variables was verified using the Durbin-Watson test and values between 1.5 and 2.5 showed that there was no autocorrelation in the data. Data behavior analyzes were also carried out through the analysis of estimation curves. Statistical significance was set at 5%.

Results

After the initial survey, 411 community-dwelling older women were identified in the records of all basic health units in Diamantina, Minas Gerais, Brazil. After checking addresses and visiting homes, 270 older women were interviewed in their homes to apply the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, 156 older women were eligible and performed all laboratory evaluation procedures, as showing in Fig. 1.

In this sample, the average age was 74 years (65–96), weight 61 kg (36–97), height 1.50 m (1.39–1.70), BMI 27 kg/m² (16–40), HGS 20 kg (4–40), ALM 14.7 kg (8.6–22.8), fat percentage = 40% (22–56), muscle-specific strength 1.37 (0.34–2.22), GS 0.98 m/s (0.26–1.71) and time spent in TUG = 12.5 s (6.2–38.8). When classified according to %fat, the sample showed 57% (n = 90) of obese individuals. In contrast, adopting the classification WHO recommendations, the sample showed 4% (n = 6) of subjects with low BMI, 48% (n = 75) adequate BMI and 48% (n = 75) with obesity.

Regarding muscle mass and strength, 50.6% (n = 79) presented low handgrip strength and 66.7% (n = 104) low ALM. After proposed classification, 32.7% (n = 51) of participants had obesity without sarcopenia, 15.4% (n = 24) presented sarcopenia, and 25% (n = 39) had sarcopenic obesity. The age was significantly higher in the sarcopenia group (± 79 years), which presented lower values of body weight (± 47 kg), BMI (± 21 kg/m2), total fat mass (± 15 kg), visceral fat (± 530 g) and ALM (± 12 kg). Discussion

In this study, we find that muscle-specific strength was associated with age, % fat, functional physical performance and with sarcopenic obesity in community-dwelling older women. These results reinforce that sarcopenic obesity may be worse than sarcopenia and obesity alone24,33, especially in terms of muscle quality. They also support the findings which demonstrates that body fat accumulation can directly affect skeletal muscle by reducing the muscle quality and strength of the older people. Therefore, the analyzes suggest that the specific muscle strength should be incorporated into the clinical evaluation of the older people15, contributing to health care strategies aimed at precise diagnosis and monitoring of sarcopenic obesity.

Although the older women in the group with obesity presented higher mean values of muscular strength, the group with sarcopenic obesity presented the lowest values of handgrip strength and muscle-specific strength, suggesting that body fat can affect the specific muscle unit of muscle tissue, reducing its contractile capacity and resulting in worse muscle-specific strength and functional physical performance in the older18,24. Although it was not possible to specifically measure fat infiltration in muscle mass, our data suggest that this can be a key factor that can potentially affect the specific force of the muscle, reinforcing the findings of Pereira et al., 2024, in a study in which muscle strength was normalized for muscle radiodensity, a marker of muscle fat infiltration15. Reinforcing that body fat accumulation can by intramuscular fat infiltration mechanisms, affect muscle quality and physical performance of older people. Studies involving the measurement of muscle radiodensity in sarcopenic obesity are needed.

The pathophysiology of sarcopenic obesity involves the infiltration and storage of intra- and intermuscular fat, increased inflammation and muscle catabolism10,34. The deposition of lipids and their metabolites in muscle tissue24,35 can induce a reduction in GLUT4 expression, compromising insulin signaling, resulting in reduced glucose uptake, increased oxidation of fatty acids in mitochondria, greater oxidative stress, inflammation and sarcopenia11,18,36. Furthermore, cytokines produced by fat infiltration into muscle tissue can cause muscle atrophy by inducing the degradation of muscle filament proteins and cell apoptosis35,37–39. Therefore, since our results showed higher total body fat percentages in older women with sarcopenic obesity compared to those with obesity but without sarcopenia, it seems plausible to suggest that fat accumulation affects the skeletal muscle quality may lead to worse physical-functional outcomes. Furthermore, this allows us to speculate that body fat can affect muscle function and quality in community-dwelling older women.

In both sarcopenia and obesity, there are gaps in the literature regarding diagnosis and classification1,22,40–42. The World Health Organization published recommendations for an integrated strategy for older care (ICOPE), informing clinicians and researchers about the intrinsic capacity of older population, suggesting the assessment of five domains: vitality, locomotor, auditory function, visual function, cognitive function and psychological43. These recommendations encourage an integrated look at the various physical and physiological characteristics, as well as their changes throughout aging44. Our results are in line with such recommendations, providing information that can be useful for integrated assessment of the older women, especially in the context of obesity, sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity. This indicates that older adults with a higher percentage of body fat and lower functional physical performance also have worse muscle-specific strength, suggesting a condition of sarcopenic obesity. Furthermore, when sarcopenia occurs together with obesity, older women present lower physical performance and specific muscle strength, indicators of greater metabolic complications, worse functional independence and mortality in older population3,11,16.

In our results BMI diagnosed obesity has identified 48% while the % fat measured by DXA showing 57% of older with obesity, this difference can potentially contribute to underreporting of the disease and worsening clinical symptoms. There are a several controversies in the literature regarding the diagnosis of obesity using BMI45–48. There is evidence that this measure has limitations, as it is not capable of discriminating the percentage of lean muscle mass or bone mineral density of patients, resulting in underreporting and/or incorrect diagnosis of obesity, especially in aging, which is marked by changes in body composition45,48. However, advances in research are needed to understand more accurate methods for determining obesity, especially in aging marked by changes in body tissues.

Finally, we demonstrated the use of a possible strategy that could be useful for future studies that investigate the use of muscle-specific strength as a diagnostic strategy for sarcopenia12 and sarcopenic obesity15 in older people. New studies are necessary to test and adapt these values to nationally representative samples, and to propose appropriate cutoff points for different population in the diagnosis of sarcopenic obesity.

Similar to other studies in sarcopenic obesity, results should be considered initial, and limitations should be reported. The cross-sectional design of the study does not allow establishing a cause-and-effect relationship between the variables. The research environment is older women from the community and the results do not apply to men or institutionalized or hospitalized people. New research that also investigates sociodemographic characteristics and participants’ lifestyles are needed. Additionally, the all-female sample can be seen as both a weakness and a strength, given that changes in body composition differ between the sexes, especially with aging. Another important point to highlight is that the gold standard instruments recommended to evaluate the specific muscle unit are the isokinetic dynamometer and computed tomography, however we adopted the recommended strategies for evaluating sarcopenia. Advances in research are needed to confirm these findings using the gold standard assessment of the specific muscle unit.

Final considerations

These findings demonstrate that the coexistence of obesity and sarcopenia exerts a compounded detrimental effect on skeletal muscle, leading to greater reductions in muscle-specific strength and physical performance in older women compared to the presence of either condition alone. Consequently, this comprehensive evaluation highlights the importance of incorporating muscle-specific strength as an important component in the diagnosis of sarcopenic obesity in older adults.
