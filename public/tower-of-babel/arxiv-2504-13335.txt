Abstract
. Harmonic generation plays a crucial role in contrast-enhanced ultrasound, both for imaging and therapeutic applications. However, accurately capturing these nonlinear effects is computationally demanding when using traditional time-domain approaches. To address this issue, we develop algorithms based on a time discretization that uses a multiharmonic Ansatz applied to a model that couples the Westervelt equation for acoustic pressure with a volume-based approximation of the Rayleigh–Plesset equation for the dynamics of microbubble contrast agents. We first rigorously establish the existence of time-periodic solutions for this Westervelt-ODE system. We then derive a multiharmonic representation of the system under time-periodic excitation and develop iterative algorithms that rely on the successive computation of higher harmonics assuming either real-valued or complex-valued solution fields. In the real-valued setting, we characterize the approximation error in terms of the number of harmonics and a contribution arising from the fixed-point iteration. Finally, we investigate these algorithms numerically and illustrate how the number of harmonics and the presence of microbubbles influence the propagation of acoustic waves.

1.
Introduction

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound has become an important tool in biomedical applications, with gas-filled microbubbles being used to improve both diagnostic and therapeutic procedures.
While ultrasound propagation can be described by linear acoustic models in the small-amplitude regime, nonlinear effects arise at higher acoustic pressures, leading to the generation of higher harmonics in the frequency domain.
These nonlinearities are further amplified by microbubble contrast agents, which exhibit strongly nonlinear oscillatory behavior when exposed to ultrasound waves. This delicate back-and-forth interaction is beneficial for improving resolution in imaging but also for enhancing therapeutic treatments, such as targeted drug delivery; see, for example,
[
18
,
36
,
12
]
for details. With the rise in the number of applications of contrast-enhanced ultrasound, accurate modeling and efficient simulation in this context have also become prominent research topics; see, e.g.,
[
39
,
6
,
10
,
28
]
, and the references given therein.

The present work builds upon
[
32
]
, where time-domain mathematical models for ultrasound contrast imaging with microbubbles based on a nonlinear acoustic wave equation coupled to a Rayleigh–Plesset-type ODE
in a continuum (effective-medium) description of the bubbly mixture
have been derived and investigated in terms of local well-posedness and numerical simulations. As noted in
[
32
]
, a major computational challenge when simulating such systems stems from different time scales on which the wave equation and ODE (nonlinearly) evolve. As a result, straightforward numerical approaches for solving such systems demand using prohibitively small time steps. In this work, we approach this issue by developing
multiharmonic
algorithms for time-periodic solution fields, which offer a potentially more efficient modeling alternative by making use of harmonic expansions; see, e.g.,
[
3
,
4
]
, where multiharmonic ideas have been explored for problems arising in electromagnetism and, e.g.,
[
33
,
21
,
22
,
15
]
, where they have been developed for single-physics acoustic models. Instead of resolving every oscillation in the space-time domain, these methods decompose the field into a sum of harmonics, which can be obtained as solutions of suitable (in our case) Helmholtz problems and algebraic equations.
These methods are especially promising for simulating real-time ultrasound applications in which the number of harmonics used can be relatively low.

As the starting time-domain model of contrast-enhanced ultrasound we employ the following coupled nonlinear wave-ODE system:

(2)

\boxed{\begin{aligned} &\ \ p_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=\eta(p^{2})_{tt}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}(x)v_{tt}+h(x,t)\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&\ \ v_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}v_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}v=\zeta v^{2}+\xi(2vv_{tt}+v_{t}^{2})-\mu p\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\end{aligned}}

consisting of the damped Westervelt equation
[
40
]
for the acoustic pressure
p=p(x,t)
(that is, fluctuations in the background pressure) and an ODE pointwise a.e. in space for the volume variation
v=v(x,t)
of microbubbles. The system in (
2
) models the interaction of acoustic pressure waves with oscillations of
microbubbles
.

1.1.
Modeling background

In the Westervelt equation,
c>0
denotes the
(constant)
speed of sound in the medium,
b>0
the diffusivity of sound, so that the term
-b\Delta p_{t}
introduces strong damping, and
\rho_{0}>0
the mass density of the mixture at equilibrium. The nonlinearity coefficient is given by
\eta=\frac{\beta_{a}}{\rho_{0}c^{2}}
, where
\beta_{a}
is the nonlinearity parameter in the medium. The source of the pressure waves is provided in part through a contribution due to bubble oscillations, modeled by the term
c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{tt}
, where
n_{0}=n_{0}(x)
is the bubble number density at equilibrium, and by the source function
h=h(x,t)
. We allow
n_{0}
to vary in space (that is, we assume that
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
), as this setting is relevant for imaging applications; see, e.g.,
[
25
]
. Moreover,
n_{0}
and
h
regulate the strength of the acoustic source and will be assumed to be small enough in the well-posedness analysis; see Theorem
2.3
and the discussion in Section
2.3
on the physical meaning of the smallness assumption.

The ODE in (
2
) captures nonlinear harmonic oscillations driven by the acoustic pressure via the term
-\mu p
. The total microbubble volume is given by
V=v_{0}+v
, where
v_{0}
is the equilibrium volume. The coefficient
\delta=\frac{4\nu}{\omega_{0}R_{0}^{2}}
is the viscous damping coefficient and
\omega_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{3\kappa P_{0}}{\rho_{0}R_{0}^{2}}}
the natural frequency, where
R_{0}
is the bubble radius at equilibrium volume
v_{0}
(that is,
v_{0}=\frac{4\pi}{3}R_{0}^{3}
),
\kappa
the adiabatic exponent and
P_{0}
the ambient pressure in the mixture (so that the total pressure is
P_{0}+p
).
The coefficients appearing on the right-hand side of the ODE are given in terms of equilibrium values by
\mu=\frac{4\pi R_{0}}{\rho_{0}}
,
\zeta=\frac{(\kappa+1)\omega_{0}^{2}}{2v_{0}}
, and
\xi=\frac{1}{6v_{0}}
. As discussed in
[
17
, Ch. 5]
, the ODE in (
2
) can be seen as a
volume-based
approximation of the following Rayleigh–Plesset equation:

(3)

\rho_{0}\left[RR_{tt}+\tfrac{3}{2}R_{t}^{2}\right]=p_{b}-4\nu\frac{R_{t}}{R}-p,

where
\nu
is the kinematic viscosity and
p_{b}
a constant pressure contribution, which can be derived using the relation
V=\frac{4\pi}{3}R^{3}
and the adiabatic gas law
\frac{p_{b}}{P_{0}}=\left(\frac{v_{0}}{V}\right)^{\kappa}
.
Introducing the volume variable and expanding the resulting expression about the equilibrium radius
R_{0}
, while neglecting higher-order terms, yields a simplified ODE for the volume perturbation
v
. A detailed derivation of this approximation is provided in
[
34
, Sec. 2.3]
.
Reformulating the dynamics in terms of
v
instead of
R
removes singular terms and results in a more manageable nonlinear structure. Since pressure perturbations couple more directly to volume oscillations, the volume-based formulation is also better suited for harmonic expansions. In contrast, a harmonic expansion in terms of
R
is expected to lead to higher-order interactions that complicate the isolation of harmonic components due to the cubic dependence of volume on radius.

We equip (
2
) with absorbing-type boundary conditions of the following form:

(4)

\beta p_{t}+\gamma p+\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\quad\beta,\,\gamma>0,

and we are interested in time-periodic solutions that satisfy

(5)

\displaystyle p(0)=p(T),\qquad p_{t}(0)=p_{t}(T)\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle v(0)=v(T),\qquad v_{t}(0)=v_{t}(T)\qquad\text{ in }\Omega,

which corresponds to steady-state oscillatory (stable cavitation) regimes rather than transient cavitation.
Although in the existence analysis we do not require the source
h
to be time periodic, for developing multiharmonic algorithms, we assume that
h
represents the second time derivative of a
T
-periodic function
g
; see Section
3
for details.

1.2.
Main contributions

The overall purpose of the present work is to establish multiharmonic approximation approaches for time-domain systems in the form of (
2
), that is, systems that incorporate both nonlinear acoustic propagation and nonlinear microbubble oscillations. To approximate the problem we employ a cut-off Fourier series for the pressure and volume fields:

(6)

\displaystyle p\approx p^{N}=\real\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\exp(\imath m\omega t)p_{m}^{N}(x)\right\},\quad v\approx v^{N}=\real\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\exp(\imath m\omega t)v_{m}^{N}(x)\right\},

with
\omega=\dfrac{2\pi}{T}
. In addition to rigorously considering the setting of real-valued pressure-volume fields, we also discuss multiharmonic algorithms derived from complex-valued fields (that is, from dropping the operator in (
6
)). This approach is sometimes adopted in electromagnetism (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
41
]
) and it has been discussed in
[
21
]
for the de-coupled Westervelt equation. Here, it results in simplified algorithms, which are, however, only formally investigated; see Section
5
for details. Toward reaching the overall goal of the work, our main contributions pertain to

•

rigorously establishing the existence of time-periodic solutions for the Westervelt-ODE system in (
2
);

•

deriving cutoff multiharmonic approximations of the system under time-periodic excitation for computing
(p^{N}_{m},v^{N}_{m})
;

•

developing and analyzing
linearized
multiharmonic cut-off algorithms for computing
(p^{N}_{m},v^{N}_{m})
.

In particular, we characterize the error of linearized multiharmonic algorithms for real-valued fields in terms of the number of harmonics and a contribution due to the fixed-point iteration; see Theorem
4.1
. For convenience, an overview of algorithms investigated in this work is provided in Figure
1
.

1.3.
Novelty and related work

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work to rigorously develop multiharmonic approaches for coupled nonlinear wave-ODE systems of this type, providing both well-posedness theory and numerical analysis. An approach to second harmonic generation for a linearization of (
2
) with
\eta=0
and
b=0
in the wave equation has been formally set up in
[
17
, Ch. 5]
without convergence guarantees.

Rigorous multiharmonic studies have been performed on single-physics equations. Existence of periodic solutions of nonlinear acoustic models, including the Westervelt equation, has been investigated rigorously in
[
21
,
33
,
22
,
23
,
7
,
8
]
. An iterative multiharmonic algorithm for the Westervelt equation has been proposed and rigorously studied in
[
33
]
. A numerical algorithm based on using the complex Fourier Ansatz for the de-coupled Westervelt equation and a boundary element approach for the resulting Helmholtz problems has been developed and investigated in
[
15
]
. In the context of electromagnetism, a multiharmonic treatment of the quasi-stationary Maxwell problem has been developed and rigorously analyzed in
[
3
]
; numerical simulation aspects are discussed in
[
4
]
. These results, however, do not address the coupling to microbubble/ODE dynamics.

Periodic solutions of Rayleigh–Plesset-type equations that do not incorporate modeling of the acoustic propagation have been rigorously investigated in the literature; we refer to, for example,
[
42
,
16
]
and
[
37
, Ch. 9]
, and the references provided therein. We mention in passing that the mathematical literature on non-periodic models in nonlinear acoustics of non-bubbly media is quite rich; see, e.g.,
[
24
,
20
,
1
,
11
]
and the references provided therein.

1.4.
Organization of the paper

The remainder of the exposition is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we determine sufficient conditions for the well-posedness of the time-periodic boundary value problem for the Westervelt-ODE system (
2
). In Section
3
, we derive a multiharmonic cut-off representation of the system and propose a linearization which results in a simplified setting for computing Fourier coefficients in (
6
). In Section
4
, we characterize the error and prove the convergence of the proposed linearized multiharmonic scheme to the solution of the Westervelt-ODE system as the number of harmonics
N
tends to
\infty
. The main theoretical result of the section is contained in Theorem
4.1
. In Section
5
, we discuss multiharmonic algorithms resulting from complex solutions fields. Finally, in Section
6
, we investigate and compare the introduced algorithms.

2.
Existence of time-periodic solutions

In this section, we establish the basis for the numerical analysis by investigating the existence and uniqueness of periodic solutions for the coupled PDE-ODE system. More precisely, given
T>0
and a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{2,3\}
, we study the problem:

(7)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &p_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=\eta(p^{2})_{tt}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}(x)v_{tt}+h\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\beta p_{t}+\gamma p+\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&p(0)=p(T),\ p_{t}(0)=p_{t}(T)&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&v_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}v_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}v=\zeta v^{2}+\xi(2vv_{tt}+v_{t}^{2})-\mu p\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&v(0)=v(T),\ v_{t}(0)=v_{t}(T)&&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

The analysis will be based on successive approximations of the system, for which knowledge of linear time-periodic ODE and wave problems will be very helpful. We thus discuss those results next.

Notation
. Below we use
\textup{lhs}\lesssim\textup{rhs}
to denote
\textup{lhs}\leq C\cdot\textup{rhs}
, where
C>0
is a generic constant. When writing norms in Bochner spaces, we omit the temporal domain
(0,T)
. For example,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}(L^{q}(\Omega))}
denotes the norm in
L^{p}(0,T;L^{q}(\Omega))
.

2.1.
Auxiliary existence results for linear time-periodic problems

To set up the local well-posedness analysis of (
7
), we first state two separate results on the well-posedness of an ODE (describing damped oscillations with forcing) and a linear time-periodic wave problem.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T>0
and
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
. Furthermore, let
\delta
,
\omega_{0}>0
. Then, the periodic ODE problem

(8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}v_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}v=f&&\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&v(0)=v(T),\ v_{t}(0)=v_{t}(T)&&\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

has a unique solution

(9)

\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{X}_{v}=\{v\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega)):\,v(0)=v(T),\,v_{t}(0)=v_{t}(T)\ \text{a.e.}\}

that satisfies

(10)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\coloneqq\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|v_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|v_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}\lesssim\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}.

Proof.

The existence and uniqueness can be established using Floquet theory (see, for example
[
35
]
and
[
14
, Ch. 3]
) as the only solution of the homogeneous problem is zero due to the fact that
\delta\omega_{0}>0
. The details are provided in Appendix
A
for completeness.
∎

Proposition 2.2

(see
[
33
, Theorem 2.1]
)
.

Let
T>0
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
d\in\{2,3\}
, be a bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary, and
\beta,\,\gamma>0
,
c
,
b>0
. Let
h\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
. Then there exists a unique (weak) solution

(11)

\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{X}_{p}=\,\begin{multlined}\Bigl\{p\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{3/2}(\Omega))\cap L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega)):\\
||\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}||_{H^{1}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}<\infty,\ \|\Delta p_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}<\infty,\,p(0)=p(T),\ p_{t}(0)=p_{t}(T)\text{ a.e.}\Bigr\}\end{multlined}

of the time-periodic boundary-value problem

(12)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &p_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p-b\Delta p_{t}=h\quad&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\beta p_{t}+\gamma p+\nabla p\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }{\partial\Omega\times(0,T)},\\
&p(0)=p(T),\ p_{t}(0)=p_{t}(T)&&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

The solution satisfies

(13)

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}||p||_{L^{2}(H^{2}(\Omega))}+||p_{t}||_{L^{2}(H^{3/2}(\Omega))}+\|p_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\Delta p_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla p\cdot{\bf n}\|_{H^{1}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\displaystyle C(b,\gamma,c,\beta,T,\Omega)||h||_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}},

for some constant
C=C(b,\gamma,c,\beta,T,\Omega)>0
, independent of
h
.

We note that having
\gamma>0
in (
12
) is crucial for uniqueness. Furthermore, having strong damping (that is,
-b\Delta p_{t}
with
b>0
) in the wave equation contributes to its parabolic-like character, and is heavily exploited in the proof of the above statement. We also note that the functions
f
and
h
do not have to be time periodic for the well-posedness results in this section, although we will make that assumption on the acoustic source in Section
3
to develop (iterative) multiharmonic algorithms.

2.2.
Analysis of the Westervelt-ODE system

Equipped with the previous results on linear problems, we are now ready to prove the well-posedness of the coupled pressure-volume problem. To state it, we introduce

(14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}=\left\{(p,v)\in\mathcal{X}_{p}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}:\,\|p\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\leq r_{p},\ \|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\leq r_{v}\right\},

where
r_{p}>0
and
r_{v}>0
will be set as small enough in the course of the proof below. The proof will have constructive nature using successive approximations.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
T>0
,
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\{2,3\}
, be open, bounded, connected, with
C^{1,1}
boundary,
\beta
,
\gamma
,
c
,
b
,
\delta
,
\omega_{0}>0
and
\eta
,
\mu
,
\zeta
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
,
n_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
,
h\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
.
Then there exist
\delta_{p}>0
and
\delta_{v}>0
, such that if

(15)

||h||_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq\delta_{p}\quad\text{and}\quad\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}n_{0}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq\delta_{v},

then there exist radii
r_{p}>0
and
r_{v}>0
, such that problem (
7
) has a unique solution
(p,v)\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}
.

Proof.

As announced, the proof follows by constructing the solutions using successive approximations. Let
r_{p}
,
r_{v}>0
. We take
(p^{(0)},v^{(0)})\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}
and set up successive approximations using the system given by

(16a)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &p^{(N)}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta p^{(N)}-b\Delta p^{(N)}_{t}=\eta((p^{(N-1)})^{2})_{tt}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{(N)}_{tt}+h\,&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\beta p^{(N)}_{t}+\gamma p^{(N)}+\nabla p^{(N)}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\hskip-7.96674pt\\
&p^{(N)}(0)=p^{(N)}(T),\ p^{(N)}_{t}(0)=p^{(N)}_{t}(T)&&\text{in }\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

and

(16b)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &v^{(N)}_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}v^{(N)}_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}v^{(N)}&&\\
=&\,-\mu p^{(N-1)}+\zeta(v^{(N-1)})^{2}-\xi\left(2v^{(N-1)}v^{(N-1)}_{tt}+(v^{(N-1)}_{t})^{2}\right)&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&v^{(N)}(0)=v^{(N)}(T),\ v^{(N)}_{t}(0)=v^{(N)}_{t}(T)&&\text{in }\Omega.\end{aligned}\right.

We proceed through several steps.

(i) Let
N=1
. Note that we can first solve (
16b
) and use its solution as the input for solving (
16a
). By Lemma
2.1
, we have a unique
v^{(1)}\in\mathcal{X}_{v}
that solves (
16b
), such that

(17)

\displaystyle\|v^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\lesssim\|p^{(0)}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|v^{(0)}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}.

From here, we find that

(18)

\displaystyle\|v^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\lesssim r_{p}+r_{v}^{2},

and we can conclude that
\|v^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\leq r_{v}
provided
r_{p}
and
r_{v}
are small enough
. By Proposition
2.2
, the linear problem in (
16a
) for
N=1
has a unique solution
p^{(1)}\in\mathcal{X}_{p}
, such that

(19)

\displaystyle\|p^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\|-\eta((p^{(0)})^{2})_{tt}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}{v^{(1)}_{tt}}+h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|p^{(0)}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|v^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}+\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}.

By using the fact that
\|v^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\leq r_{v}
, we obtain

(20)

\displaystyle\|p^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\lesssim r_{p}^{2}+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}r_{v}+\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}.

From here, we can conclude that
\|p^{(1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\leq r_{p}
for small enough
\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}
by reducing
r_{p}
and
\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.

(ii) Let now
(p^{(N-1)},v^{(N-1)})\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}
. By Lemma
2.1
, we have

(21)

\displaystyle\|v^{(N)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\lesssim\|p^{(N-1)}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty}(\Omega))}+\|v^{(N-1)}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}

and by Proposition
2.2
, the linear problem in (
16a
) with periodic time conditions has a unique solution
p^{(N)}\in\mathcal{X}_{p}
, such that

(22)

\displaystyle\|p^{(N)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\leq

\displaystyle C\|-\eta((p^{(N-1)})^{2})_{tt}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{(N)}_{tt}+h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\|p^{(N-1)}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|v^{(N)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}+\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}.

By then proceeding analogously to the case
N=1
, we obtain

(23)

\displaystyle\|p^{(N)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\leq r_{p},\quad\|v^{(N)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\leq r_{v},

provided
\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
and
\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}
are small enough.
We thus conclude that for small enough
\|h\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}
and
\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
, there exist
r_{p}>0
and
r_{v}>0
, such that if
(p^{(0)},v^{(0)})\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}
, then for each
N\geq 1
, problem (
16
) has a unique solution
(p^{(N)},v^{(N)})\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}}
.

(iii) We next wish to show that
\{(p^{(N)},v^{(N)})\}_{N\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{X}_{p}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
.
We note that for
N_{1}
,
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, the difference
(\overline{p},\overline{v})=(p^{(N_{1})}-p^{(N_{2})},v^{(N_{1})}-v^{(N_{2})})
solves

(24a)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &\overline{p}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{p}-b\Delta\overline{p}_{t}&&\\
=&\,-\eta((p^{(N_{1}-1)}-p^{(N_{2}-1)})(p^{(N_{1}-1)}+p^{(N_{2}-1)}))_{tt}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}(v^{(N_{1})}_{tt}-v^{(N_{2})}_{tt})\,&&\text{in }\Omega,\\
&\beta\overline{p}_{t}+\gamma\overline{p}+\nabla\overline{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\hskip-7.96674pt\\
\end{aligned}\right.

and

(24b)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\overline{v}_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}\overline{v}_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}\overline{v}\\
=&\,\begin{multlined}-\mu(p^{(N_{1}-1)}-p^{(N_{2}-1)})+\zeta(v^{(N_{1}-1)}-v^{(N_{2}-1)})(v^{(N_{1}-1)}+v^{(N_{2}-1)})\\

+v^{(N_{2}-1)}(v^{(N_{1}-1)}_{tt}-v^{(N_{2}-1)}_{tt})+(v^{(N_{1}-1)}_{t}-v^{(N_{2}-1)}_{t})(v^{(N_{1}-1)}_{t}+v^{(N_{2}-1)}_{t})\bigr)\quad\text{in }\Omega.\end{multlined}\end{aligned}\right.

From here, similarly to before, using Proposition
2.2
and Lemma

, we obtain

(25)

\displaystyle\|p^{(N_{1})}-p^{(N_{2})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\lesssim r_{p}\|p^{(N_{1}-1)}-p^{(N_{2}-1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|v^{(N_{1})}-v^{(N_{2})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}

and

(26)

\displaystyle\|v^{(N_{1})}-v^{(N_{2})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|p^{(N_{1}-1)}-p^{(N_{2}-1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+r_{v}\|v^{(N_{1}-1)}-v^{(N_{2}-1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}.\end{multlined}

By adding
\eqref{10}+\lambda\cdot\eqref{1a}
, with
\lambda>0
, we obtain

(27)

\displaystyle\|p^{(N_{1})}-p^{(N_{2})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+(\lambda-C\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})\|v^{(N_{1})}-v^{(N_{2})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\lambda+r_{p})\|p^{(N_{1}-1)}-p^{(N_{2}-1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+C\lambda r_{v}\|v^{(N_{1}-1)}-v^{(N_{2}-1)}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}

for some
C>0
.
We then impose
C\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\lambda
,
and choose
r_{p}
,
\lambda
, and
r_{v}
sufficiently small to conclude that
\{(p^{(N)},v^{(N)})\}_{N\geq 1}
is a Cauchy sequence. Thus, there exists
(p,v)
such that
\{(p^{(N)},v^{(N)})\}_{N\geq 1}
converges to it in
\mathcal{X}_{p}\times\mathcal{X}_{v}
. Passing to the limit in (
16
) proves that
(p,v)
solves the pressure-volume problem.

(iv) Finally, to show that such a constructed solution is unique, we take

(28)

(p^{<1>},v^{<1>}),\,(p^{<2>},v^{<2>})\in\mathbb{B}_{r_{p},r_{v}},

and note that the difference
(\overline{p},\overline{v})=(p^{<1>}-p^{<2>},v^{<1>}-v^{<2>})
solves

(29a)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &\overline{p}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{p}-b\Delta\overline{p}_{t}=-\eta(\overline{p}(p^{<1>}+p^{<2>}))_{tt}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}\overline{v}_{tt}\,&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\
&\beta\overline{p}_{t}+\gamma\overline{p}+\nabla\overline{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\hskip-7.96674pt\\
\end{aligned}\right.

and

(29b)

\displaystyle\overline{v}_{tt}+\delta\omega_{0}\overline{v}_{t}+\omega_{0}^{2}\overline{v}=-\mu\overline{p}+\zeta\overline{v}\left(v^{<1>}+v^{<2>}\right)-\xi\left(2(\overline{v}v^{<1>}_{tt}+v^{<2>}\overline{v}_{tt})+\overline{v}_{t}(v^{<1>}_{t}+v^{<2>}_{t})\right).

We obtain analogously to before

(30)

\displaystyle\|\overline{p}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\lesssim r_{p}\|\overline{p}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\overline{v}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}

and

(31)

\displaystyle\|\overline{v}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\lesssim\|\overline{p}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}+r_{v}\|\overline{v}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}.

For
r_{v}
,
r_{p}
, and
\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
small enough, these inequalities allow us to conclude that
\|\overline{p}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}=\|\overline{v}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}=0
. This step completes the proof. ∎∎

The small-solution setting of Theorem
2.3
effectively forces the nonlinear ODE to retain the damped harmonic oscillator structure with
T
-periodic coefficients. Indeed, under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, for
r_{v}
small enough, so that

(32)

\max\,\left\{2|\xi|,\frac{1}{\delta\omega_{0}}|\xi|,\frac{1}{\omega_{0}^{2}}|\zeta|\right\}\cdot r_{v}<1,

we can rewrite the ODE in the following form:

(33)

\displaystyle v_{tt}+\frac{\delta\omega_{0}-\xi v_{t}}{1-2\xi v}v_{t}+\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}-\zeta v}{1-2\xi v}v=-\frac{\mu}{1-2\xi v}p.

Setting
\ell=\dfrac{\delta\omega_{0}-\xi v_{t}}{1-2\xi v}>0
,
q=\dfrac{\omega_{0}^{2}-\zeta v}{1-2\xi v}>0
, and
f=-\dfrac{\mu}{1-2\xi v}p
, we see that the volume fluctuation

can be represented as the solution of a second-order ODE (pointwise a.e. in space) with positive

-periodic coefficients and a
T
-periodic right-hand side, namely

(34)

\displaystyle v_{tt}+\ell(t)v_{t}+q(t)v=f(t),

where
\ell(0)=\ell(T)
,
q(0)=q(T)
,
f(0)=f(T)
.

2.3.
On the smallness assumption

The assumption (
15
) on the smallness of the microbubble number density
n_{0}
in Theorem
2.3
regulates the strength of the source of acoustic waves, along with the smallness of the external source

. To assess how meaningful this assumption is, we consider a nondimensional system obtained by introducing the scaling

(35)

\displaystyle x=L\tilde{x},\qquad t=\frac{L}{c}\tilde{t},\quad p=p^{\textup{ref}}\,\tilde{p},\qquad v=v^{\textup{ref}}\,\tilde{v},

where
L
is a characteristic length scale,
p^{\textup{ref}}
is a reference pressure amplitude, and
v^{\textup{ref}}
a reference volume. After the change of variables and division by
\frac{c^{2}p^{\textup{ref}}}{L^{2}}
, we obtain the dimensionless pressure equation:

(36)

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{tt}-\Delta\tilde{p}-\tilde{b}\Delta\tilde{p}_{t}=\tilde{\eta}(\tilde{p}^{2})_{tt}+\tilde{\kappa}{n}_{0}\tilde{v}_{tt}+\tilde{\alpha}h\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),

where the relevant transformed coefficient is given by

(37)

\tilde{\kappa}{n}_{0}=\frac{\rho_{0}c^{2}v^{\textup{ref}}}{p^{\textup{ref}}}{n}_{0}.

Since
v^{\textup{ref}}n_{0}
can be understood as the gas volume fraction (see
[
32
, Sec. 2]
), the smallness assumption corresponds to requiring moderate concentrations of microbubbles. For typical parameters in medical imaging,
c\sim 1500
m
/
s,
\rho\sim 1000\,
kg
/
m
3
,
p^{\textup{ref}}\in[10^{5},10^{6}]\,
Pa, and
R_{0}\sim 10^{-6}\,
m,
v^{\textup{ref}}=\frac{4}{3}R_{0}^{3}\pi
, we obtain
\tilde{\kappa}n_{0}\ll 1
for microbubble densities in the range
n_{0}\in[10^{9},10^{12}]\,
bubbles
/m^{3}
.

3.
Time discretization via a multiharmonic Ansatz for real fields

In this section, we discuss the time discretization of the system by using a multiharmonic Ansatz, in the general spirit of
[
21
]
. We focus on a special case of having
T
-periodic acoustic excitation. That is, we assume that the acoustic source term has the form
h=g_{tt}
, where the function
g
is given by

(38)

g(x,t)=\real\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{M}\exp(\imath m\omega t)h_{m}^{M}(x)\right\},\quad\omega=\dfrac{2\pi}{T},\quad h_{m}^{M}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}).

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
, a unique time-periodic solution
(p,v)
of the system given in (
7
) exists.
For numerical approximations, we thus employ the following multiharmonic Ansatz:

(39)

\displaystyle u^{N}(x,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left(\exp(\imath m\omega t)u_{m}^{N}(x)+\exp(-\imath m\omega t)\overline{u^{N}_{m}(x)}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\real\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\exp(\imath m\omega t)u_{m}^{N}(x)\right\}.

Alternatively, the multiharmonic Ansatz can be written in a more standard Fourier series form:

(40)

u^{N}(x,t)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left[u^{c}_{m}(x)\cos(m\omega t)+u^{s}_{m}(x)\sin(m\omega t)\right]

with
u^{N}_{m}(x)=u_{m}^{c}(x)-\imath u_{m}^{s}(x)
. We then look for the approximate pressure-volume field
(p^{N},v^{N})\in X_{N}\times X_{N}
, with

(41)

X_{N}=\left\{\real\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\exp(\imath m\omega t)u_{m}^{N}(x)\right\}\,:\,u_{m}^{N}\in H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})\right\},

where the coefficients
(p^{N}_{m},v^{N}_{m})
in the expansion are determined from the following system:

(42)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \ &p_{tt}^{N}-c^{2}\Delta p^{N}-b\Delta p_{t}^{N}&&\\
=&\,\eta\,\textup{Proj}_{X_{N}}\left[((p^{N})^{2})_{tt}\right]+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v^{N}+\textup{Proj}_{X_{N}}h&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&\beta p_{t}^{N}+\gamma p^{N}+\nabla p^{N}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0&&\ \text{on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\\[2.84526pt]
&v_{tt}^{N}+\delta\omega_{0}v_{t}^{N}+\omega_{0}^{2}v^{N}&&\\
=&\,-\mu p^{N}+\,\textup{Proj}_{X_{N}}\left[\zeta(v^{N})^{2}+\xi\left(2v^{N}v_{tt}^{N}+(v_{t}^{N})^{2}\right)\right]&&\text{in }\Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\right.

We next show that (
42
) can be equivalently rewritten as a system of Helmholtz problems and algebraic equations for computing
(p^{N}_{m},v^{N}_{m})
.

3.1.
A multiharmonic cut-off algorithm

Below we skip writing the dependencies of Fourier coefficients on

Proposition 3.1
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem

Additionally, each of the individual functions

Proof.

We begin by considering the derivation of the multiharmonic algorithm for the Westervelt’s equation and the ODE separately. For the PDE, one can follow the steps outlined in
[

(47)

\displaystyle 0=

(51)

3.2.

Next, we propose a linearized multilevel method. That is, for

) reveals more clearly the influence of microbubbles on the wave propagation, through the dissipation signaled by the
i\mathfrak{b}p^{N}_{m}
term and modification of the wave number via the
-(k^{2}+\mathfrak{a})p^{N}_{m}
term. ∎

4.
Convergence of the linearized multiharmonic algorithm for real fields

We next wish to establish convergence of the iterative scheme in (
54
) as
N\rightarrow\infty
in a suitable norm. We can mimic the proof of Theorem
2.3
to show by induction that for each
N\geq 1
, problem (
54
) has a unique solution
(p^{N},v^{N})\in X_{N}\times X_{N}
.
For fixed
N\geq 1
, the existence and uniqueness of
p^{N}
follow by noting that, thanks to Proposition
3.2
, harmonics
p^{N}_{m}
are obtained as solutions of the Helmholtz problems in (
60
).
Furthermore, an analogous testing procedure to the one in Theorem
2.3
applied on (
54
) yields

(64)

\|p^{N}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{p}}\leq r_{p},\quad\|v^{N}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}\leq r_{v},

provided
r_{p}
,
r_{v}
, and
\|\mu\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
are sufficiently small. We omit these details here and focus on establishing convergence.
The convergence will be shown in the norms of the spaces
Y^{0}_{p}
and
Y^{0}_{v}
, where

(65)

\displaystyle Y_{p}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle H^{\ell+1}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\cap H^{\ell+2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap H^{\ell+2}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega)),

\displaystyle Y_{v}^{\ell}=

\displaystyle H^{\ell+2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))

for
\ell\geq 0
. To make writing more compact, we introduce the short-hand norm notation

(66)

|\!|\!|(p,v)|\!|\!|_{Y^{\ell}_{p}\times Y^{\ell}_{v}}\coloneqq\|p\|_{Y^{\ell}_{p}}+\|v\|_{Y^{\ell}_{v}}

for
(p,v)\in Y^{\ell}_{p}\times Y^{\ell}_{v}
, and we also adopt the operator notation

(67)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{L}_{1}p^{N},\phi^{N}>\coloneqq\begin{multlined}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(p_{tt}^{N}\phi^{N}+c^{2}\nabla p^{N}\cdot\nabla\phi^{N}+b\nabla p_{t}^{N}\cdot\nabla\phi^{N}\right)\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s\\
+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}(c^{2}(\beta p_{t}^{N}+\gamma p^{N})+b(\beta p_{tt}^{N}+\gamma p_{t}^{N}))\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\textup{d}s\end{multlined}

and

(68)

<\mathcal{L}_{2}v^{N},\phi^{N}>\coloneqq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(v_{tt}^{N}+\delta\omega_{0}v_{t}^{N}+\omega_{0}^{2}v^{N}\right)\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s,\quad\phi^{N}\in X_{N}.

Then the algorithm can be restated as follows. Given the previous iterate
(p^{N-1},v^{N-1})\in X_{N}\times X_{N}
, we compute
(p^{N},v^{N})
for
N\geq 1
as the solution of

(69a)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{L}_{1}p^{N},\phi^{N}>=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left({\eta}((p^{N-1})^{2})_{tt}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{tt}^{N}+h\right)\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s,

and

(69b)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{L}_{2}v^{N},\phi^{N}>=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl\{-\mu p^{N}+\zeta(v^{N-1})^{2}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+\xi\left(2v^{N-1}v_{tt}^{N-1}+(v_{t}^{N-1})^{2}\right)\Bigr\}\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s,\end{multlined}

for all
\phi^{N}\in X_{N}
.

In the convergence proof, we will involve the truncated Fourier series of a continuous-in-time function
u
given by

(70)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{N}(x,t)=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\left[u^{c}_{m}(x)\cos(m\omega t)+u_{m}^{s}(x)\sin(m\omega t)\right]\in X_{N},\quad N\geq 0,

with the coefficients

(71)

\displaystyle u_{m}^{c}(x)=\frac{2}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(x,t)\cos(m\omega t)\,\textup{d}t,\quad u_{m}^{s}(x)=\frac{2}{T}\int_{0}^{T}u(x,t)\sin(m\omega t)\,\textup{d}t.

By integrating the time-periodic Westervelt equation and acoustic boundary conditions in (
7
) over
(0,T)
, we can conclude that
\int_{0}^{T}p(x,t)\,\textup{d}t=0
in
\Omega
. Thus
\tilde{p}^{0}=0
, which is reflected by having
p^{N}_{0}=0
in the multiharmonic algorithms.

It can be shown analogously to
[
3
, Lemma 12]
that the following error bound holds for
\ell\geq 1
:

(72)

\displaystyle|\!|\!|(p-\tilde{p}^{N},v-\tilde{v}^{N})|\!|\!|_{(Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega)))\times Y^{0}_{v}}\leq

\displaystyle CN^{-\ell}(\|p\|_{Y_{p}^{\ell}\cap H^{\ell+2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|v\|_{Y_{v}^{\ell}}),

which we can exploit to characterize the error of the scheme in the next statement. We include the proof of (
72
) in Appendix
B
for completeness.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
2.3
hold with the acoustic source term
h=g_{tt}
, where

is given in (

(73)

\displaystyle(p,v)\in\left(\mathcal{X}_{p}\cap Y_{p}^{\ell}\cap H^{\ell+2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))\right)\times\left(\mathcal{X}_{v}\cap Y_{v}^{\ell}\right),\quad\ell\geq 1.

Let

and assume that
(p^{N},v^{N})
are computed using (

. Then, provided

,
r_{v}
, and

(74)

\displaystyle|\!|\!|(p-p^{N},v-v^{N})|\!|\!|_{Y^{0}_{p}\times Y^{0}_{v}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}q|\!|\!|(p-p^{N-1},v-v^{N-1})|\!|\!|_{Y^{0}_{p}\times Y^{0}_{v}}+CN^{-\ell}(\|p\|_{Y^{\ell}_{p}\cap H^{\ell+2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}+\|v\|_{Y^{\ell}_{v}}),\end{multlined}

where

Proof.

The proof follows by splitting the error as follows:

(75)

\displaystyle p-p^{N}=

\displaystyle(p-\tilde{p}^{N})-(p^{N}-\tilde{p}^{N})\eqqcolon\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})-e^{p,N},

\displaystyle v-v^{N}=

\displaystyle(v-\tilde{v}^{N})-(v^{N}-\tilde{v}^{N})\eqqcolon\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N})-e^{v,N},

and then representing
(e^{p,N},e^{v,N})

as the solution of a suitable semi-discrete wave-volume system. The discrete error
(e^{p,N},e^{v,N})
can be seen as the solution of

(76a)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{p,N},\phi^{N}>=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left({\eta}(p^{2}-(p^{N-1})^{2})_{tt}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}(v_{tt}-v_{tt}^{N})\right)\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s\\
+<\mathcal{L}_{1}\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}),\phi^{N}>\end{multlined}

and

(76b)

\displaystyle<\mathcal{L}_{2}e^{v,N},\phi^{N}>

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Bigl(-\mu(p-p^{N})+\zeta(v^{2}-(v^{N-1})^{2})\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+\xi(2(vv_{tt}-v^{N-1}v_{tt}^{N-1})+(v_{t}^{2}-(v_{t}^{N-1})^{2}))\Bigr)\phi^{N}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s\\
+<\mathcal{L}_{2}\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N}),\phi^{N}>\end{multlined}

for all
\phi^{N}\in X_{N}
. Note that in the testing procedure for this problem we can exploit the fact that
\int_{0}^{T}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}(\cdot)\,\textup{d}t=0
for time-periodic functions. By testing (
76a
) with
e^{p,N}
,
e_{t}^{p,N}
, and
e_{tt}^{p,N}
, employing Young’s and Hölder’s inequalities, and combining the resulting estimates, we can derive the following bound:

(77)

\displaystyle\|e^{p,N}\|^{2}_{Y^{0}_{p}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|{-\eta}(p^{2}-(p^{N-1})^{2})_{tt}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}(v_{tt}-v_{tt}^{N})\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|^{2}_{Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))},\end{multlined}

where we have also exploited the equivalence of the norms
\|w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
and
\|\nabla w\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|w\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
(see
[
31
, Theorem 1.9]
). The derivation of (

C

Then by using the rewriting

(78)

\displaystyle((p^{N-1})^{2})_{tt}-(p^{2})_{tt}=\,2(p^{N-1}_{t}-p_{t})(p^{N-1}_{t}+p_{t})+2(p^{N-1}-p)p^{N-1}_{tt}+2p(p^{N-1}_{tt}-p_{tt}),

and the fact that

for all

(79)

\displaystyle\|e^{p,N}\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}r_{p}\|p^{N-1}-p\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}+{\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}\|v_{tt}^{N}-v_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|_{Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}.\end{multlined}

From here via

and the triangle inequality, we arrive at

(80)

\displaystyle\|p-p^{N}\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}r_{p}\|p^{N-1}-p\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}+{\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}}\|v_{tt}^{N}-v_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|_{Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}.\end{multlined}

Similarly, testing (
76b
) with
e^{v,N}
,
e_{t}^{v,N}
, and

\displaystyle\|e^{v,N}\|^{2}_{Y^{0}_{v}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|p-p^{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|v^{2}-(v^{N-1})^{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
\|-\xi(2(vv_{tt}-v^{N-1}v_{tt}^{N-1})+(v_{t}^{2}-(v_{t}^{N-1})^{2}))\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N})\|^{2}_{Y^{0}_{v}}.\end{multlined}

By using the fact that
\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{v}}
,

for all

, and the triangle inequality, from (

(82)

\displaystyle\|v-v^{N}\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|p^{N}-p\|_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+r_{v}\|v^{N-1}-v\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N})\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}.\end{multlined}

Adding
\lambda\cdot
(

(83)

\displaystyle\|p-p^{N}\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}+\lambda\|v-v^{N}\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}r_{p}\|p^{N-1}-p\|_{Y^{0}_{p}}+\lambda r_{v}\|v^{N-1}-v\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|_{Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}+\lambda\|\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N})\|_{Y^{0}_{v}}.\end{multlined}

(84)

\displaystyle|\!|\!|(p-p^{N},v-v^{N})|\!|\!|_{Y^{0}_{p}\times Y^{0}_{v}}\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}q(r_{p},r_{v})|\!|\!|(p-p^{N-1},v-v^{N-1})|\!|\!|_{Y^{0}_{p}\times Y^{0}_{v}}\\
+(1+\|n_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)})C_{0}|\!|\!|(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}),\textup{err}(\tilde{v}^{N}))|\!|\!|_{\left(Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))\right)\times Y^{0}_{v}}\end{multlined}

for some

Convergence of the scheme then follows in a straightforward manner from (

4.1.
Setting the zeroth microbubble harmonic to zero

Unlike with the Westervelt equation, from the equation for
v
, we cannot in general conclude by integrating from
0
to
T
that
\int_{0}^{T}v(t)\,\textup{d}t=0
. Nevertheless, in numerical methods for solving multiharmonic problems it is often assumed that the zeroth harmonic does not contribute significantly to the dynamics; this assumption is also made in the formal two-harmonic generation study for the linearized wave-ODE model with
b=\eta=0
in
[
17
, Ch. 5.3.2]
.

With the assumption
p_{0}^{N}=v_{0}^{N}=0
for all
N\geq 0
, the system derived in Proposition
3.2
further simplifies to

(86)

\displaystyle m=1:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \text{(i)}&\hskip 9.24994pt-p_{1}^{N}-\frac{c^{2}+\imath b\omega}{\omega^{2}}\Delta p_{1}^{N}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{1}^{N}=-h_{1}^{N}-\sum_{k=3:2}^{2N-3}\eta\overline{p_{\frac{k-1}{2}}^{N-1}}p_{\frac{k+1}{2}}^{N-1}\\[2.77501pt]
\text{(ii)}&\hskip 9.24994ptv_{1}^{N}=\alpha_{1}\left(-\mu p_{1}^{N}+\sum_{k=3:2}^{2N-3}\left(\zeta-\xi\omega^{2}\frac{k^{2}+3}{4}\right)\overline{v_{\frac{k-1}{2}}^{N-1}}v_{\frac{k+1}{2}}^{N-1}\right)\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(87)

\displaystyle m=\left\{2,\ldots,N\right\}:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \text{(i)}&\hskip 9.24994pt-p_{m}^{N}-\frac{\left(c^{2}+\imath mb\omega\right)}{\omega^{2}m^{2}}\Delta p_{m}^{N}+c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{m}^{N}\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt=-h_{m}^{N}-\frac{\eta}{2}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}p_{l}^{N-1}p_{m-l}^{N-1}-2\sum_{k=m+2:2}^{2(N-1)-m}\overline{p_{\frac{k-m}{2}}^{N-1}}p_{\frac{k+m}{2}}^{N-1}\right)\\[2.77501pt]
\text{(ii)}&\hskip 9.24994ptv_{m}^{N}=\alpha_{m}\left(-\mu p_{m}^{N}+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}\left(\frac{\zeta}{2}-\frac{\xi\omega^{2}}{2}(m-l)(2m-l)\right)v_{l}^{N-1}v_{m-l}^{N-1}\right.\\
&\hskip 9.24994pt\left.+\sum_{k=m+2:2}^{2(N-1)-m}\left(\zeta{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\frac{\xi\omega^{2}(k^{2}+3m^{2})}{4}\right)\overline{v_{\frac{k-m}{2}}^{N-1}}v_{\frac{k+m}{2}}^{N-1}\right)\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

where
\alpha_{m}=\left(-m^{2}\omega^{2}+\imath m\delta\omega_{0}\omega+\omega_{0}^{2}\right)^{-1}
for

.
Now
v_{m}^{N}
can be eliminated explicitly by substituting the second equation into the first, leading to a system of inhomogeneous Helmholtz equations.

5.
Time discretization via a multiharmonic Ansatz for complex fields

In this section, we formally investigate multiharmonic algorithms based on neglecting the fact that the excitation and solution fields should be real-valued. We look for approximate solutions in

(88)

\tilde{X}_{N}:=\left\{\sum_{m=0}^{N}\exp(\imath m\omega t)u_{m}^{N}(x)\,:\,u_{m}^{N}\in H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})\right\}.

This approach, adopted also in the investigation of the (de-coupled) Westervelt equation in

\textup{Proj}_{X_{N}}(\cdot)

In this complex setting, for

Proposition 5.1
.

Using linear independence of the functions

\text{(ii)}&\quad\frac{1}{\alpha_{m}}v_{m}^{N}+\mu p_{m}^{N}=\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}\left(\zeta-\xi\omega^{2}(m-l)(2m-l)\right)v_{l}^{N-1}v^{N-1}_{m-l},\end{aligned}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

where
\alpha_{m}=\left(-m^{2}\omega^{2}+\imath m\delta\omega_{0}\omega+\omega_{0}^{2}\right)^{-1}
for
m\in\left\{1,\ldots N\right\}
. Additionally, each of the individual functions
p_{m}^{N}
for
m\in\left\{0,\ldots,N\right\}
is supposed to satisfy boundary conditions (

).

Remark 5.3

(On the assumption of complex fields)
.

In this section, the approximations
(p^{N},v^{N})
are complex-valued, so convergence could only be studied in the complex counterpart of
X_{N}\times X_{N}
. This does not by itself imply that
\Re(p^{N},v^{N})\to(p,v)
as
N\to\infty
; such a conclusion would require enforcing the reality constraint (symmetric
\pm m
spectrum with
\hat{u}_{-m}=\overline{\hat{u}_{m}}
). Following
[
3
]
, one may formulate the multiharmonic Ansatz with complex Fourier coefficients (omitting the explicit
\Re
in the time reconstruction), but the resulting nonlinear maps are not complex-differentiable (holomorphic). Hence a Newton scheme in the sense of complex analysis is not applicable; in practice one uses the real formulation (or a split into real/imaginary parts) despite the algebraic brevity of the complex notation.

For clarity, eq. (
86
) (real basis) contains both sum- and difference-frequency couplings generated by the quadratic terms, visible in the two convolution sums
\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}p_{l}\,p_{m-l}
and
\sum_{k=m+2:2}^{2(N-1)-m}p_{(k-m)/2}\,p_{(k+m)/2}
.
In contrast, the complex projection (
100
) is posed on the half-spectrum
\{e^{\mathrm{i}m\omega_{0}t}\}_{m=1}^{N}
without imposing
\hat{u}_{-m}=\overline{\hat{u}_{m}}
; only the sum-frequency interactions remain and the block system becomes lower-triangular (solvable by substitution). If one augments the complex space to include
\pm m
and enforces the reality constraint, the complex and real formulations are algebraically equivalent. In our parameter regime, the neglected difference-frequency contributions are numerically negligible, which explains the agreement observed in Fig.
3
in Section
6
.

5.3.
A simple two-harmonic approximation scheme

In certain imaging applications, the fundamental frequency and the second harmonic are considered the most significant; see, for example,
[
38
]
. We thus compare the multiharmonic iterative scheme with a simplified two-harmonic scheme to evaluate the extent of information loss.

For two harmonics, that is, for
N=2
with
p_{0}^{(2)}=0
and
v_{0}^{(2)}=0
, the approximations for the pressure
p
and the volume
v
are given by

(101)

\displaystyle p(x,t)\approx p^{(2)}(x,t)

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{(2)}(x)\exp(\imath\omega t)+p_{2}^{(2)}(x)\exp(2\imath\omega t),

\displaystyle v(x,t)\approx v^{(2)}(x,t)

\displaystyle=v_{1}^{(2)}(x)\exp(\imath\omega t)+v_{2}^{(2)}(x)\exp(2\imath\omega t).

The corresponding scheme for computing
p_{1,2}^{(2)}
and
v_{1,2}^{(2)}
can be found by setting
N=2
in Proposition
5.1
. This results in the system

(102)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m=1:&\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \text{(i)}&\quad p_{1}^{(2)}+\frac{c^{2}+\imath\omega b}{\omega^{2}}\Delta p^{(2)}_{1}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{1}^{(2)}=h_{1}^{(2)},\\[3.00003pt]
\text{(ii)}&\quad\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}v_{1}^{(2)}+\mu p_{1}^{(2)}=0,\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}\\[5.0pt]
m=2:&\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \text{(i)}&\quad p_{2}^{(2)}+\frac{c^{2}+2\imath\omega b}{4\omega^{2}}\Delta p_{2}^{(2)}-c^{2}\rho_{0}n_{0}v_{2}^{(2)}=h_{2}^{(2)}+\frac{\beta_{a}}{\rho_{0}c^{2}}(p_{1}^{(2)})^{2},\\[3.00003pt]
\text{(ii)}&\quad\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}}v_{2}^{(2)}+\mu p_{2}^{(2)}=\left(\zeta-3\xi\omega^{2}\right)(v_{1}^{(2)})^{2},\end{aligned}\end{cases}\end{aligned}

where, as before,

(103)

\alpha_{m}=\frac{1}{-m^{2}\omega^{2}+\imath m\delta\omega_{0}\omega+\omega_{0}^{2}}=\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2})^{2}+(m\delta\omega_{0}\omega)^{2}}-\imath\frac{m\delta\omega_{0}\omega}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2})^{2}+(m\delta\omega_{0}\omega)^{2}}

for
m\in\{1,2\}
.
Above, we have rewritten
\eta
as
\eta=\dfrac{\beta_{a}}{\rho_{0}c^{2}}
to make the influence of the nonlinearity parameter in the medium
\beta_{a}
on the system explicit. In this two-harmonic setting, we can express
v_{1}^{(2)}
in terms of
p_{1}^{(2)}
and
v_{2}^{(2)}
in terms of
p_{2}^{(2)}
:

(104)

\displaystyle v_{1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{1}\mu\,p_{1}^{(2)},

\displaystyle v_{2}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{2}\left[-\mu p_{2}^{(2)}+(\zeta-3\xi\omega^{2}){\alpha}_{1}^{2}\mu^{2}\left(p_{1}^{(2)}\right)^{2}\right].

We can then use these expressions to eliminate
v_{1}^{(2)}
and
v_{2}^{(2)}
for the first and third equations in (
102
). Multiplying the resulting equations by
m^{2}\omega^{2}/c^{2}
yields the following system for the two pressure coefficients:

(105)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} m=1:\qquad&\frac{\omega^{2}}{\tilde{c}_{1}^{2}}p_{1}^{(2)}+\left(1+\imath\frac{b\omega}{c^{2}}\right)\Delta p_{1}^{(2)}=\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}h_{1}^{(2)},\\
m=2:\qquad&\frac{4\omega^{2}}{\tilde{c}_{2}^{2}}p_{2}^{(2)}+\left(1+\imath\frac{2b\omega}{c^{2}}\right)\Delta p_{2}^{(2)}=\frac{4\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}h_{2}^{(2)}+\frac{4\omega^{2}}{\rho_{0}c^{4}}\left(\beta_{a}+\tilde{\beta}_{a}\right)\left(p_{1}^{(2)}\right)^{2},\end{aligned}

where

(106)

\frac{1}{\tilde{c}_{m}^{2}}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\rho_{0}n_{0}\mu\alpha_{m},\qquad\tilde{\beta}_{a}=c^{4}\rho_{0}^{2}n_{0}(\zeta-3\xi\omega^{2})\mu^{2}\alpha_{1}^{2}\alpha_{2}.

The above system clearly displays the influence of microbubbles on wave propagation, particularly through their impact on the effective wave speed and the nonlinearity parameter.

•

From equations (
105
), we see that the effective wave number in the presence of microbubbles in a two-harmonic setting is

(107)

\tilde{k}_{m}=\frac{m\omega}{\tilde{c}_{m}},\quad\text{with }\tilde{c}_{m}\ \text{ given in }\eqref{def cm}.

Note that
\tilde{k}_{m}
is in general complex since
\alpha_{m}
is complex, and thus the attenuation in the wave propagation is due both to the acoustic dissipation via the
\imath\frac{mb\omega}{c^{2}}\Delta p_{m}^{(2)}
terms as well as the presence of microbubbles via the
\imath\Im(\tilde{k}^{2}_{m})p_{m}^{(2)}
terms.

•

In the second equation in (
105
), we see the influence of microbubbles on the nonlinearity parameter as their presence leads to the additional
\tilde{\beta}_{a}
term on the right-hand side, thus effectively enlarging the nonlinearity parameter
\beta_{a}
. By setting
\tilde{\beta}=0
and replacing
\tilde{c}^{2}_{m}
with
c^{2}
, the system reduces to the two-harmonic expansion of Westervelt’s equation for a strongly damped medium without bubbles.

6.
Numerical experiments

In this section, we investigate numerically the algorithms set up in Sections
3
and
5
. To discretize the resulting Helmholtz problems, we use a conforming finite element method. We note that algorithmic frameworks of the multiharmonic-FEM type used in this section are known as the Harmonic Balance Finite Element Method (HBFEM) in the context of electromagnetism; see, e.g.,
[
27
,
3
,
4
]
.

The discretization of the classical Helmholtz equation
-\Delta u-k^{2}u=f
with
k>0
, and the issues related to the so-called pollution effect and sign-indefiniteness have been extensively studied in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
2
,
19
,
29
,
30
]
, and the references contained therein. Recall that the Helmholtz problems appearing in linearized problems in (
86
) and (
100
) have the general form

(108)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &-\left(1+\imath\omega\frac{mb}{c^{2}}\right)\Delta u-(k^{2}+\mathfrak{a})u+\imath\mathfrak{b}u=f_{\omega}\quad\text{ in }\,\Omega,\quad\text{with }k=\frac{m\omega}{c},\\
&(\imath\omega m\beta+\gamma)u+\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{ on }\,\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\right.

for a known right-hand side
f_{\omega}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{C})
(that depends on
\omega
), and with

(109)

\mathfrak{a}=\mu\rho_{0}n_{0}\frac{m^{2}\omega^{2}(\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2})}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2})^{2}+(m\delta\omega_{0}\omega)^{2}},\quad\mathfrak{b}=\mu\rho_{0}n_{0}\frac{m^{3}\omega^{3}\delta\omega_{0}}{(\omega_{0}^{2}-m^{2}\omega^{2})^{2}+(m\delta\omega_{0}\omega)^{2}}.

The variational form of (
108
) is given by

(110)

a(u,\phi)=l(\phi)\quad\text{for all }\phi\in H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{C}),

where the sesquilinear form
a:H^{1}(\Omega)\times H^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined as

(111)

\displaystyle a(u,\phi)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\int_{\Omega}\left(1+\imath\omega\frac{mb}{c^{2}}\right)(\nabla u\cdot\overline{\nabla\phi})\,\textup{d}x-(k^{2}+\mathfrak{a})\int_{\Omega}u\overline{\phi}\,\textup{d}x+\imath\mathfrak{b}\int_{\Omega}u\overline{\phi}\,\textup{d}x\\
+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(1+\imath\omega\frac{mb}{c^{2}}\right)(\imath\omega m\beta+\gamma)u\overline{\phi}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\end{multlined}

and
l(\phi)\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}f\overline{\phi}\,\textup{d}x
. We then have

(112)

\displaystyle\real\left(a(u,u)\right)=\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(k^{2}+\mathfrak{a})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\gamma-\frac{b\beta(m\omega)^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

and

(113)

\displaystyle\Im\left(a(u,u)\right)=\frac{m\omega b}{c^{2}}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\mathfrak{b}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\gamma\frac{b}{c^{2}}+\beta\right)\omega m\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

Therefore, we can conclude that

(114)

\displaystyle\sqrt{2}|a(u,u)|\geq

\displaystyle\real\displaystyle\left(a(u,u)\right)+\Im\left(a(u,u)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\left(1+\frac{m\omega b}{c^{2}}\right)\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(\mathfrak{b}-k^{2}-\mathfrak{a})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\\
+\left(\gamma\left(1+\omega m\frac{b}{c^{2}}\right)+\beta\left(1-\frac{bm\omega}{c^{2}}\right)m\omega\right)\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)},\end{multlined}

which showcases how the coercivity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is influenced by the relative behavior of the various medium and frequency-dependent parameters. Note that from the complex part of (
110
), we obtain the bound

(115)

\frac{m\omega b}{c^{2}}\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\frac{\mathfrak{b}}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\gamma\frac{b}{c^{2}}+\beta\right)\omega m\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{b}}\|f_{\omega}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which carries over to the finite element setting.

6.6.
Conclusion

The numerical results demonstrate that microbubbles introduce significant attenuation and phase shifts to the wave propagation, particularly at greater distances from the source. The impact increases with microbubble density and source amplitude, and is further influenced by the driving frequency.
Regarding the number of harmonics
N
, in the considered numerical settings using three to five harmonics within the simplified multiharmonic framework obtained from complex fields provides a good compromise between computational efficiency and accuracy. This relatively low number of harmonics makes the approach promising for use in practical applications.

Outlook

In practice, nonlinear acoustic interactions between microbubbles and ultrasound waves generate not only harmonics, which are frequency components at integer multiples of the driving frequency, but also subharmonics, which appear at fractional multiples of the driving frequency, such as
\frac{\omega}{2}
,
\frac{\omega}{3},\,\ldots
; see, e.g.,
[
26
]
. Subharmonics primarily appear due to non-spherical deformations and multibubble interactions. While these effects are not included in our current model, extending the framework to incorporate them would be an interesting direction for future research.

Expanding the present theoretical and numerical framework to explore other phenomena relevant to applications of focused ultrasound waves is also of practical interest. For instance, localized heating, cavitation, and nonlocal attenuation play an important role in therapeutic ultrasound applications such as targeted drug delivery, and it would be worthwhile to investigate the potential role of multiharmonic expansions in these modeling contexts.

Furthermore, investigating inverse problems related to reconstructing spatially varying parameters, such as the bubble number density
n_{0}=n_{0}(x)
or the nonlinearity parameter
\beta_{a}=\beta_{a}(x)
from measured acoustic signals is an important task in the context of contrast-enhanced ultrasound imaging, as it could help improve diagnostic accuracy in the long run.

+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}(c^{2}(\beta(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{t}+\gamma\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))+b(\beta(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{tt}+\gamma(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{t}))e^{p,N}_{tt}\,\textup{d}\Gamma\textup{d}s.\end{multlined}

We integrate by parts in time in the
c^{2}
and
b
terms on the left-hand side:

(169)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(c^{2}\nabla\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\cdot\nabla e^{p,N}_{tt}+b\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{t}\cdot\nabla e^{p,N}_{tt}\right)\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\left(c^{2}\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{t}\cdot\nabla e^{p,N}_{t}+b\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{tt}\cdot\nabla e^{p,N}_{t}\right)\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s

and then estimate

(170)

\displaystyle\left|<\mathcal{L}_{1}\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}),e^{p,N}_{tt}>\right|\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\varepsilon\|e^{p,N}_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\varepsilon\|\nabla e^{p,N}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\varepsilon\|e^{p,N}_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}\\
+\|(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}\\
+\|\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.\end{multlined}

The presence of the term
\|\nabla(\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N}))_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}
above is the reason for assuming
p\in H^{\ell+2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
(in addition to
p\in\mathcal{X}_{p}\cap Y^{\ell}_{p}
). We also have

(171)

\left|-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}f^{N}e^{p,N}_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s\right|\lesssim\|f^{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\varepsilon\|e^{p,N}_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}.

Thus, the outcome of testing in Step III for small enough
\varepsilon
is

(172)

\displaystyle\|e^{p,N}_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|e^{p,N}_{tt}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\varepsilon\|\nabla e^{p,N}_{t}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|f^{N}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\textup{err}(\tilde{p}^{N})\|^{2}_{Y^{0}_{p}\cap H^{2}(H^{1}(\Omega))}.\end{multlined}

Combining the three obtained estimates leads to (
77
), provided
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. ∎
