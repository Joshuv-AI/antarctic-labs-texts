Association of race and area of deprivation index with prostate cancer incidence and lethality: results from a contemporary North American cohort
Authors: Marco Finati, Alex Stephens, Giuseppe Ottone Cirulli, Giuseppe Chiarelli, Shane Tinsley, Chase Morrison, Akshay Sood, Nicolò Buffi, Giovanni Lughezzani, Andrea Salonia, Alberto Briganti, Francesco Montorsi, Gian Maria Busetto, Craig Rogers, Giuseppe Carrieri, Firas Abdollah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocioeconomic and demographic factors contribute to disparity in prostate cancer (PCa) outcomes. We examined the impact of Area of Deprivation Index (ADI) and race on PCa incidence and lethality in a North American cohort.MethodsOur cohort included men who received at least 1 prostate-Specifig Antigen (pSA) test within our Health System (1995-2022). An ADI score was assigned to each patient based on their residential census block, ranked as a percentile of deprivation relative to the national level. Individuals were further categorized into quartiles, where the fourth one (ADI 75-100) represented those living in the most deprived areas. We investigated PCa incidence and lethality, using cumulative incidence estimates and competing-risk regression. An ADI × Race interaction term examined whether the relationship between ADI and outcomes varied based on race.ResultsWe included 134 366 patients, 25% of whom were non-Hispanic Black (NHB). Median (IQR) follow-up was 8.8 (5-17) years. At multivariate analysis, individuals from the third quartile (ADI 50-74, 95% CI = 0.83 to 0.95) and the fourth quartile (ADI ≥75, 95% CI = 0.75 to 0.86) showed significant reduced hazard ratios for PCa incidence, when compared with the first quartile (ADI <25, all P < .001). In contrast to the overall cohort, PCa incidence increased with ADI in NHB men, who were persistently at higher hazard for both PCa incidence and lethality than non-Hispanic White (NHW), across all ADI strata (all P < .001).ConclusionsLiving in more deprived areas was associated with lower PCa incidence and higher lethal disease rate. Conversely, PCa incidence increased with ADI for NHB, who consistently showed worse outcomes than NHW individuals, regardless of ADI.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains the most common noncutaneous cancer and the second most common oncological cause of mortality in US men.1 Notably, in the United States non-Hispanic Black (NHB) men face a higher risk both of incidence and lethality from PCa.2 This difference is particularly pronounced in PCa mortality, with NHB individuals having more than double the risk compared with non-Hispanic White (NHW) Americans.3 The underlying cause of this disparity is complex and involves a multitude of biological, behavioral, and access to care factors. However, recent literature seems to highlight socioeconomic factors and geographical location as the main culprits of this inequity.4,5 Nonetheless, demographic and socioeconomic variables often interplay, which makes it difficult to comprehensively evaluate their impact on PCa outcomes. Moreover, a more recent expanded view of social determinants of health should include not only individual or family income but also the impact of living in more disadvantaged neighborhoods.6 In this perspective, the Area of Deprivation Index (ADI) might serve as a predictor of a more global perspective of cancer care.7-12 The ADI is a numeric indicator created by publicly available data of income, education, employment, and housing quality, determined at the census block level. Rankings of neighborhoods are created by comparing the ADI of a neighborhood to both the nation and the state the neighborhood is located within. This index is higher the more the area has a socioeconomic disadvantage.13,14 To date, very few studies have evaluated the association of living in a certain neighborhood with PCa outcomes,15-19 especially when considering differences between NHB and NHW. However, these preliminary findings showed a positive association between ADI level and poor oncological outcomes, even when comparing US-born individuals with immigrants of the same racial group.20 To address this void, we examined the validity of ADI as a predictor of PCa incidence and lethality, using a large contemporary North American cohort. We hypothesized that living in more deprived areas might lead to reduced access to care and screening, thus increasing PCa mortality. Additionally, we aimed to examine the interaction between ADI and race with cancer control outcomes.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We used our institutional database, which included electronic medical records for all men receiving care in Henry Ford Health (HFH), between 1995 and 2022. Our health system covers more than 50 medical centers and hospitals within the Detroit metropolitan area. Our population is very socially and ethnically diverse, making it an ideal setting to study the impact of sociocultural, economic, environmental, and biological variables on the prognosis of oncological diseases. We included only patients aged 40-70 years who were residents of Michigan and received at least one PSA test at HFH between 1995 and 2022 (200 066 patients). Patients were excluded if they had been previously diagnosed with PCa (2960 excluded) or if there were missing geographical/residential data necessary to match them with their respective census blocks (10 013 excluded). Patients were also excluded if no follow-up was available after their first PSA test (39 964). We focused exclusively on NHB and NHW patients (15 723 excluded). Our selection criteria yielded to a final cohort of 134 366 eligible patients (see the Consort Diagram in Figure S1).

Variables

To account for differences in the examined cohort, we extracted the following variables for each patient: first PSA, age at first PSA, race (NHB, NHW), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [0, 1, ≥2], ZIP code, and ADI at study entrance. ADI is a numeric variable calculated using different indicators of income, educational level, home value, and quality of life. This numeric index was calculated for each census block and ranked as a percentile relative to the national level.14 The 5-digit ZIP codes extracted from patient addresses were paired with their respective plus-4 codes using the US Postal Service’s ZIP code database. These 9-digit ZIP codes were used to match patients within their census block.21 Based on previous methodology,15,19,22,23 patients were further categorized into ADI quartile, where the fourth quartile (Q4: ADI 75-100) represents individuals living in the neighborhoods with the most disadvantageous socioeconomic status. The following data on any PCa diagnoses were also recorded: date of diagnosis, pSA level at diagnosis (when available), International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) PCa Grade Group, and presence of distant metastasis (cM+) or any development of distant metastasis during follow-up.

Endpoints

Our endpoints were the likelihood of developing any PCa or lethal PCa, defined as death from PCa or development of metastatic disease, either at diagnosis or during follow-up. Death date and cause of death were obtained by linking our database with the Michigan Vital Records registry. To address the limitation regarding the lack of information on patients moving or dying outside Michigan, follow-up was defined as their last encounter at HFH, and patients were censored at that point. Conversely, death due to a cause other than prostate cancer was defined as a competing event.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics consisted of median and IQR for continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Our analysis consisted of multiple steps. First, we calculated cumulative incidence estimates to examine any difference in any PCa incidence and lethal PCa for each ADI quartile. Difference in cumulative estimates were compared by Gray’s test. Second, the same cumulative incidence curves were repeated separately for NHB and NHW men to compare differential associations between ADI quartiles and PCa incidence/lethality across race. Third, multivariable competing-risk regression analysis tested the impact of ADI on any PCa diagnosis and lethal PCa. This multivariable analysis included age at first PSA test, CCI at first PSA, race (NHB vs NHW), and ADI quartiles (the least deprived area [ADI 0-24] was considered as reference). A formal ADI × Race interaction term was then introduced to examine whether the relationship between ADI and the outcomes varies between NHB and NHW men. Death from causes other than PCa was considered the competing event. Time-to-event estimates and hazard ratios were calculated from the first PSA date for both incidence and lethality because we included metastasis at both PCa diagnosis and during further follow-up. Two-sided P values less than .05 indicated statistical significance. All analyses were performed using RStudio (Rstudio Team, 2023.6.1.524). Patients were retrospectively enrolled in HFH-IRB#12641-29. An institutional review board waiver for informed consent was obtained before conducting this study, in accordance with institutional regulations when dealing with deidentified previously collected data.

Results

Demographic information and clinical variables are summarized in Table 1. A total of 134 366 patients were included, 25% (33456/110910) of whom were NHB. Median (IQR) follow-up was 8.8 (5-17) years, and median (IQR) age at first PSA was 52 (46-59) years for NHB and 55 (49-62) years for NHW, respectively. Patients in the fourth quartile (ADI ≥75) exhibited more comorbidities, whereas the distribution of PSA value (both at first test and at diagnosis) and ISUP grade groups was similar across all ADI quartiles. NHB and NHW were more often in the fourth (ADI ≥75: 52.2%) and third (ADI 50-74: 34.4%) quartile, respectively. The detection rate was higher in NHB for both any PCa (9.6% vs 4.2%) and clinically significant disease (ISUP > 1: 7.4% vs 3%). Descriptive statistics stratified by race (NHB vs NHW) are reported in Table 2. The number of patients lost at follow-up (censored) at 5, 10, 15, and 20 years for each group are displayed in Table S1.

Prostate cancer incidence analysis

When patients were categorized based on their ADI quartile, the 20-years PCa incidence was 9.7% for ADI 0-24, 8.6% for ADI 25-49, 7.8% for ADI 50-74, and 8.8% for ADI ≥75 (Gray’s test P value <.001; Figure 1). For the same ADI quartile, the 20-years PCa incidence rates in NHB were 11.5%, 13.5%, 12.6%, and 12.1%, respectively (Gray’ s test P value = .009; Figure 2, A), whereas in NHW patients they were 8.8%, 7.8%, 6.8%, and 5.4% (Gray’s test P value <.001; Figure 2, B). At multivariate analysis (Table 3), patients in the third (ADI 50-74 HR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.83 to 0.95, P < .001) and fourth quartile (ADI ≥75 HR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.75 to 0.86, P < .001) had a lower hazard for any PCa diagnosis, when compared with individuals in the lowest ADI quartile (ADI <25). The interaction term between ADI quartile and race (NHB vs NHW) was significant, indicating an opposite trend in NHB compared with the overall population. Specifically, NHB individuals in the second, third, and fourth ADI quartile had a higher hazard ratio compared with those in the least deprived quartile (ADI <25, all P values <.001). Furthermore, when comparing men within the same ADI quartile, the hazard ratio for any PCa in NHB (vs NHW) increased from 1.46 (95% CI = 1.31 to 1.63) in the first ADI quartile to 2.68 (95% CI = 2.46 to 2.93) in the highest ADI quartile (all P < .001). Age, comorbidity index, and baseline PSA value were also independent predictors for PCa incidence (all P < .001).

Lethal prostate cancer analysis

When patients were categorized based on their ADI quartile, the 20-years lethal PCa rates were 1.3% for ADI <25, 0.89% for ADI 25-49, 0.98% for ADI 50-74, and 1.7% for ADI ≥75 (Gray’s test P < .001; Figure 3). For the same ADI quartile, the 20-years PCa incidence rates in NHB were 2.0%, 1.4%, 1.2%, and 2.3%, respectively (Gray’ s test P < .001; Figure 4, A), whereas in NHW patients they were 0.92%, 0.79%, 0.93%, and 1.2% (Gray’s test P = .140; Figure 4, B). At multivariate analysis (Table 4), ADI was not associated with a difference in lethal PCa. NHB men had almost a 2-fold hazard ratio for lethal PCa across all ADI strata (all P < .001), but the interaction term did not show a significant variation of race impact on lethal PCa, according to ADI level. Age, PSA value, and CCI >1 (when compared with CCI = 0) were independent predictors for lethal disease.

Discussion

PCa remains a significant public health concern in the United States, with notable disparities in incidence and mortality rates among NHB and NHW men.2,3 Although biological and genetic predisposition have been suggested to play a role in the less favorable outcomes observed in NHB patients, socioeconomic and demographic factors are well known to be the primary drivers of health-care inequities in this disease.4,5 However, all these factors contributing to disparities often interplay together and the relative contribution of each of them remains complex to be examined. ADI is a numeric measure that encompasses a wide range of indicators, including educational attainment, income levels, and social support. This index may offer a unique and comprehensive measurement of all these aforementioned factors,14 but its relationship with PCa outcomes has never been thoroughly addressed. In this perspective, we aimed to examine if living in more deprived areas might be associated with any difference in PCa incidence and mortality. Thereafter, we explored how ADI and race interact and the impact of that on PCa control outcomes.

Our findings are severalfold. First, the increase of ADI level was associated with a reduced incidence of PCa. This might stem from the fact that living in the most deprived areas decreases access to health care and screening. On the other hand, people living in less deprived neighborhoods tend to have fewer comorbidities and may be financially more resourceful, better educated, and more compliant with screening. Second, the increase of ADI was not associated with a proportional increase of lethal PCa, although patients living in the most deprived area showed the highest rate of mortality or metastasis from PCa, regardless of race. Third, ADI was not able to explain the outcomes disparity in NHB men, which were consistently at higher risk of incidence and death from PCa when compared with their NHW counterparts, within the same ADI quartile. Fourth, the interaction term between ADI and race revealed that, in contrast to the overall cohort, PCa incidence increased with ADI in NHB men. Moreover, the racial disparity in PCa incidence became more pronounced with increasing ADI. On the other hand, race did not significantly affect the relationship between ADI and PCa lethality, where findings from the overall population seem applicable with no distinction based on race. These patterns suggest that additional factors related to disparity may not be fully captured by this aggregate indicator of neighborhood deprivation. For instance, awareness and screening practices might differ in African American communities and may not be fully reflected by ADI. Similarly, diagnosis of less aggressive disease among NHW individuals in low ADI areas (where NHB are less present) might have increased the incidence of PCa in these neighborhoods.

Individual socioeconomic and residential status have consistently been reported as significant risk factors for cancer-specific survival, but large national cancer databases often lack this information. A previous report investigating the impact of ADI on survival among patients with common nonmetastatic cancers concluded that individuals in the most deprived areas have higher cancer-specific mortality, including PCa, but the magnitude of this effect varies among different cancer types.10 Overall, the existing literature on PCa and its association with ADI is scarce and relies on small sample reports. Two previous brief reports have shown an association of higher ADI with adverse pathological features (Gleason Grade Group ≥8 and/or PSA >20 ng/mL at diagnosis) and risk of metastatic disease.15,16,18 Another report of 2113 men from the North Carolina–Louisiana Cancer Project have shown how individuals in the most deprived areas have higher mortality from PCa, although no subanalysis based on racial difference was performed.19 Our cohort partially corroborated these preliminary findings, where individuals in the highest ADI quartile exhibited more advanced disease (Gleason Grade Group ≥8: 21% vs 16%-19% in the first 3 quartiles, P < .001) and higher lethal PCa rate. However, cohorts that focus on patients already diagnosed with PCa might allow conclusions limited to the impact of neighborhood deprivation on access to and quality of treatment. In this regard, we believe our analysis is the first to comprehensively evaluate the impact of ADI on screening rates, diagnosis, and subsequent mortality from PCa, starting with a cohort at the time of the first PSA test.

The association of ADI with disparity in oncological outcomes has never been fully understood. A previous report analyzing the relationship between ADI and race in survival from breast cancer in Georgia patients concluded that NHB women had higher mortality than NHW women in Georgia, but similarly to our findings, this disparity was not explained by ADI.22 The only case-control study exploring the relationship between racial disparities in PCa outcomes and neighborhood deprivation found that residing in more deprived areas was linked to higher PCa incidence and mortality, but this was specifically observed only in NHB men.20 However, they used a final set of 6 variables to create a simplified deprivation index for their socioeconomic and neighborhood deprivation evaluation. In contrast, ADI is a standardized and publicly available index, calculated for all census blocks across the United States. This means that having the census block information for each patient is sufficient to determine their ADI value. In other words, findings based on ADI are reproducible and can be validated in other populations, making it a valuable tool for enhancing the generalizability of such studies.

Nevertheless, several limitations must be addressed in this study. First, we were able to record residential data only at the time of entrance in the study. Although we accounted for patients moving or dying outside Michigan by censoring them at the last encounter with HFH, the lack of access to previous addresses remains a notable limitation. However, when restricting survival analyses to patients enrolled in our database for at least 10 years, we did not observe different findings. Second, numerous other factors influence prostate cancer incidence and subsequent lethality, including family history, exposure to additional risk factors, and comorbidities and medications affecting PSA values and diagnostic pathways (eg, obesity, 5-a reductase inhibitors). These factors, which we could not adjust for in the multivariable analysis, should be considered limitations of our study. Third, although ADI is readily available and easy to compute for any region across the United States, its validity might differ from state to state, and findings from specific regions may not be universally applicable across the country. Similarly, racial and ethnic distributions in other regions might affect the ADI impact, as demonstrated in our study. Fourth, the retrospective nature of this study may have introduced some bias in data collection, which must be taken into account.

Conclusion

Living in less deprived areas resulted in a higher incidence of PCa, whereas the increase in ADI was not associated with a proportional increase in the risk of lethal PCa, although patients living in the most deprived areas showed the highest rate of lethal disease. Conversely, incidence increased with ADI for NHB men, who were consistently at higher risk for any outcomes evaluated when compared with NHW individuals at the same ADI level. This racial disparity is more prominent in the most deprived areas, but only for incidence.
