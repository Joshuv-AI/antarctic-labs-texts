Accidental ingestion of a live fish (Pseudorinelepis genibarbis) complicated by esophageal perforation in a 7-month-old child: A case report
Authors: Diana Lucy Vásquez-Agurto, Daniel Alberto Anchante-Llosa, Katherine Yhomara Becerra-Bravo, Katherine Gliceli Diaz-Mamani, Victor Roman-Lazarte
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Foreign body ingestion is a common event in pediatric emergencies. However, the ingestion of live animals is extremely rare and can lead to severe complications. We present the case of a 7-month-old infant who accidentally ingested a live fish, resulting in respiratory distress and severe injuries with multiple lacerations in both the respiratory and digestive tracts. Therapeutic bronchoscopy and endoscopy were performed to manage the condition. This exceptional case was characterized by extensive impaction of spines and scales in the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts, requiring highly specialized management and multiple interventions without the need for open surgery. No complications were reported following the extraction. The ingestion of live animals poses a significant clinical challenge. Strengthening child supervision, particularly in rural settings, is crucial to preventing such incidents.

Introduction

Foreign body ingestion (FBI) is one of the most common reasons for pediatric emergency visits. While some cases remain asymptomatic, others can develop severe symptoms requiring complex surgical interventions. The signs and symptoms associated with FBI vary depending on the nature of the ingested object and may include respiratory or gastrointestinal obstruction, lacerations, and perforations in the respiratory and digestive tracts. The most frequently ingested foreign objects include coins, toys, and button batteries, with prevalence depending on the specific context. In general, these objects tend to be small and solid [1].

In contrast, the ingestion of live animals is an extremely rare phenomenon, more commonly observed in rural settings or among populations with greater exposure to the natural environment. This situation typically involves marine or freshwater animals during fishing activities and is more frequently reported in adults or adolescents [2,3]. However, the absence of adequate supervision in infants may increase the likelihood of unexpected ingestion events.

In this context, we present the case of a 7-month-old infant who ingested a live fish (Pseudorinelepis genibarbis) in a remote area of the Peruvian Amazon, where supervision was limited. The fish was given to the infant by an older sibling with no intent to cause harm. This case is particularly notable due to the species' hard scales and spines [4], which added complexity to its clinical management.

Case presentation

We report the case of a 7-month-old male infant from Masamari, Junín (central Peruvian Amazon), with no relevant family, pathological, or obstetric history. The mother reported that the child accidentally ingested a live fish while they were fishing by the riverbank. Shortly afterward, she noticed respiratory distress and central cyanosis, prompting an immediate visit to the local health center.

At the regional hospital, physicians attempted to remove the fish, successfully extracting only fragments. A chest X-ray revealed a right pneumothorax, leading to the placement of a chest drain. The patient was then referred to a higher-level hospital for further management.

The infant arrived at the referral hospital 24 h after ingestion. Upon admission, he was breathing spontaneously with oxygen support via a nasal cannula and had a chest drain in the right hemithorax. His vital signs included a heart rate of 147 beats per minute and a respiratory rate of 26 breaths per minute. Physical examination revealed no foreign object in the oral cavity, but intercostal and subcostal retractions, subcutaneous emphysema in the anterior cervical and thoracic regions, decreased breath sounds in the lower right hemithorax, and fine crackles at the base of the left hemithorax were observed. The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable. The patient was admitted to the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) on the same day.

Multiple therapeutic bronchoscopies and endoscopies were performed for the removal of the foreign bodies. Initially, through the previously placed 4.0 endotracheal tube, laminar spines were extracted using grasping forceps. After successful extubation and patient stabilization, additional spines were identified and removed, along with the aspiration of dense secretions to prevent atelectasis. Finally, a flexible esophagogastroduodenoscopy allowed the extraction of a fish spine embedded in the lateral left wall of the cervical esophagus.

Enteral nutrition was gradually introduced on day 13 of hospitalization. The patient was discharged from the ICU on day 19 and from the hospital on day 25, with a full diet and an unremarkable physical examination.

At the follow-up visit seven days after discharge, the patient was asymptomatic, tolerating a full diet, with no evidence of masses in the oral, cervical, or thoracic regions, and no signs of inflammation or abnormal bowel movements. Follow-up endoscopy and bronchoscopy revealed no remaining foreign bodies, and no signs of new injuries or complications. These findings are detailed in the supplementary material.

Discussion

This report describes an exceptional case of accidental ingestion of a live fish of the species Pseudorinelepis genibarbis in a 7-month-old infant, an extremely rare occurrence. The nature of the ingested foreign body is remarkable due to the anatomical characteristics of the fish, including its hard scales and thick spines, which complicated both clinical and surgical management. This case highlights the importance of timely transfer to a hospital with the capacity to provide specialized and appropriate treatment.

Compared to other cases of FBI, this case stands out due to the extensive impaction of spines and scales in both the gastrointestinal and respiratory tracts, necessitating highly specialized management and multiple diagnostic and therapeutic interventions. The management of FBI depends on the type of ingested object. In general, endoscopy is the preferred procedure for removing foreign objects, especially sharp ones, to prevent perforation before they reach the duodenum. Surgery is rarely required in such cases [5].

Regarding the ingestion of live animals, particularly fish, previous cases have been documented in adults, such as those reported by Tam et al. [6] and Fu et al. [7]. In these cases, initial management focused on securing the airway. In Fu et al.'s case, endoscopic extraction combined with antibiotic therapy prevented the need for major surgery. In contrast, Tam et al. described a case of complete airway obstruction requiring emergency cricothyroidotomy for the removal of the fish's spines and scales.

In the present case, the presence of multiple perforations and complications, such as pneumomediastinum, underscored the need for a multidisciplinary approach. Diagnostic and therapeutic bronchoscopy and endoscopy played a crucial role in the successful resolution of the case. Peters et al. [8] emphasized the use of esophagoscopy for the removal of foreign bodies in the upper third of the esophagus in pediatric patients. Similarly, Oke et al. [9] noted that flexible bronchoscopy can be an effective and safe technique, even in community or regional hospitals (such as the initial receiving hospital in this case), by reducing complications such as hemorrhage or additional injuries.

Regarding complications, Aronberg et al. [10] conducted a systematic review identifying fish bones as the most common cause of esophageal perforations. In this case, perforation was not observed, but multiple esophageal lacerations were present. These authors highlighted that computed tomography is the most sensitive auxiliary method for such situations, allowing for appropriate treatment planning. Additionally, guidelines from the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) and the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) emphasize the importance of endoscopy in the evaluation and management of foreign bodies in pediatric patients, as well as post-removal monitoring for objects in the lower digestive tract to ensure their elimination [11].

In this case, no complications were reported following the extraction of the spines and scales from the upper digestive tract, and the patient had normal bowel movements at discharge.

Patient perspectives

The patient's family initially expressed concern about the financial burden associated with transportation and transfer to the capital city for specialized medical care. However, they later reported high satisfaction with the healthcare professionals, highlighting the clear guidance and continuous support provided throughout the treatment process. Additionally, follow-up visits proceeded without complications, and the patient experienced a full recovery. The family was relieved that all medical expenses were covered by the Ministry of Health, ensuring access to the necessary care without financial strain.

Conclusions

This case highlights the complexity of managing multiple lacerations in the respiratory and digestive tracts caused by the ingestion of a live fish, a species characterized by its spines and hard scales. Successful resolution required multiple evaluations and minimally invasive interventions, including endoscopy and bronchoscopy, which prevented the need for major surgical procedures. Additionally, this case underscores the importance of caregiver education to reinforce child supervision, particularly in rural or high-exposure environments, with the aim of preventing similar incidents in the future.
