A cross-modal framework for evaluating the mental health benefits of urban and natural landscapes in sustainable cities: an empirical study based on undergraduate students
Authors: Zhengkang Bai, Ziqi Zheng, Shuangquan Zhang, Xili Han, Zhe Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRapid urbanization has heightened the need for evidence-based healthy city planning. To resolve the methodological limitations of parallel biometric data stacking, this study developed a synchronized cross-modal framework to evaluate the restorative potential of a 9-typology urban-to-natural landscape continuum. A laboratory experiment was conducted with 42 healthy undergraduate students (21 males, 21 females; mean age = 21.4 ± 1.8 years). Brain activity (EEG), visual attention (eye-tracking), and peripheral autonomic signals (EDA, HRV, respiration) were synchronously recorded alongside the Profile of Mood States (POMS) scale. To integrate these multi-scale data streams, we formulated the Cross-Modal Restorative Index (CMRI). The empirical findings reveal a distinct, non-linear hierarchy of environmental restoration. Pristine natural environments, especially Mountainous and Field landscapes, elicited complete “Integrated Restoration,” characterized by significant systemic convergence: central cognitive relaxation via posterior α power activation (Field: 7.35; Water: 7.03), robust parasympathetic upregulation (Field HF: 12518.77), and profound down-regulations in subjective tension (Mountainous: 18.6 → 12.3) and fatigue. Conversely, built landscapes demonstrated “Fragmented Restoration.” Notably, Road scenes exhibited a localized dissociation where physiological calming (sharp increase in posterior α wave SD from 15.11 to 20.54) was decoupled from visual and psychological domains, with over 74% of visual dwell time remaining locked on artificial elements and subjective fatigue rising (15.3 → 16.2). These findings provide quantitative, systems-level evidence for integrating ecologically authentic blue-green infrastructure into resilient urban design.Keywords: Sustainable cities, Mental health, Restorative landscapes, Psychophysiological, Urban design, CMRI

Introduction

Environmental exposure and health crises in the Anthropocene

Rapid, unprecedented global urbanization has precipitated a profound public health crisis, characterized by a surging prevalence of mental health disorders. According to the World Health Organization [1, 2], nearly one billion people worldwide live with a mental disorder, with depression and anxiety escalating by over 25% in the wake of recent global socio-environmental stressors. This crisis imposes a staggering economic burden, projected to cost the global economy USD 6 trillion annually by 2030 due to lost productivity and healthcare expenditures [3].

From an environmental and evolutionary standpoint, this phenomenon is deeply rooted in the “Evolutionary Mismatch Hypothesis” (EMH). EMH [4, 5] posits that human biological and psychological adaptations were forged over millions of years within EEA (Environment of Evolutionary Adaptedness) conditions—characterized by high-biodiversity, natural landscapes, and low-density social structures. The abrupt transition to hyper-densified, artificial, and sensorially overloaded urban environments represents a profound structural mismatch, overtaxing cognitive faculties and chronically activating sympathetic stress responses [6]. While environmental psychology frameworks like Attention Restoration Theory (ART) and Stress Reduction Theory (SRT) have long sought to explain the therapeutic potential of natural elements in mitigating this mismatch, their operationalization within contemporary urban planning remains constrained by persistent empirical boundaries.

Attentional restoration theory and stress reduction theory

Historically, environmental psychology has relied on two cornerstone theories to explain the health-promoting effects of natural environments: Stress Reduction Theory (SRT), proposed by Roger Ulrich [7, 8], and Attention Restoration Theory (ART), developed by Kaplan and Kaplan [9, 10]. Grounded in psycho-evolutionary theory, SRT posits that human preference for natural environments is a genetically encoded survival instinct. Ulrich argues that encounters with natural landscapes possessing “survival-advantageous” features—such as water bodies, vegetation, and open vistas—rapidly trigger positive affective responses in the limbic system. This response is pre-cognitive, automatic, and directly downregulates sympathetic nervous system arousal.

Conversely, ART has significantly expanded our understanding of the cognitive benefits of environments, explaining how nature exposure enhances working memory and executive function. However, ART faces challenges: its core concept of “soft fascination” often lacks an objective operational definition. Furthermore, traditional ART research has relied heavily on subjective scales lacking direct physiological measurements of attentional allocation mechanisms, such as eye-tracking [11].

The modality gap and fragmented restoration

Despite a growing body of literature utilizing multimodal or synchronous psychophysiological platforms to assess landscape restoration, a profound theoretical and methodological bottleneck remains unresolved [12].

First, existing synchronous studies primarily rely on the “parallel stacking” of independent data streams, such as reporting independent, uncorrelated statistical outputs of electroencephalography (EEG), eye tracking, and heart rate variability (HRV) separately [13]. This approach fails to address the “modal gap” problem, where data from different biological subsystems possess drastically different information densities, time scales, and baseline sensitivities. Therefore, simply placing multiple biometric sensors under a synchronized clock does not automatically yield a holistic understanding of how these different human systems coordinate or conflict during environmental exposure.

Second, the traditional framework implicitly restors the assumption of “system consistency”—that environmental restoration is a single, universally distributed process across all human biopsychological domains. However, critical analysis of anomalous empirical evidence reveals frequent cross-system mismatches. For example, some structurally complex urban plazas or historic districts may successfully reduce peripheral stress but fail to alleviate or even exacerbate central cognitive fatigue [14]. Conversely, ecologically dense or untamed natural environments can induce prolonged visual fascination.

To reconcile these empirical contradictions, we propose a shift from the traditional binary perspective to a new theoretical dichotomy: integrated restoration versus fragmented restoration. We define fragmented restoration as a phenomenon where the environment only restores or stimulates isolated biopsychological subsystems, leading to a state of internal imbalance in the organism. In contrast, integrated restoration represents a comprehensive and synergistic resonance between the neurological, physiological, attentional, and emotional systems. Quantifying this cross-system coordination requires moving from isolated multi-device synchronization to unified, standardized assessment metrics to detect cross-modal decoupling.

To construct a theoretically grounded and mathematically robust CMRI, the selection of physiological and psychological variables must map comprehensively onto the sequential biological pathways of the “restoration cascade.” Environmental exposure is not a localized, isolated experience but a systemic progression initiating from visual attention, passing through central neurocognitive modulation, triggering peripheral autonomic recalibration, and culminating in conscious psychological shift.Specifically, oculomotor behavior (Eye-tracking) captures the primary gateway of sensory input, distinguishing between the high-demand top-down visual search of built environments and the effortless “soft fascination” of natural features. This visual interaction immediately modulates the central nervous system, where EEG spectral metrics (Posterior α and Frontal β power) serve as direct proximal indicators of cortical cognitive load, attentional allocation, and central relaxation. This central neurophysiological shift subsequently triggers downstream systemic adjustments via the symbiotic branches of the autonomic nervous system: Heart Rate Variability (HRV: RMSSD and HF) directly indexes the upregulation of vagal parasympathetic tone (the biological hallmark of SRT), while Electrodermal Activity (EDA: Tonic SCL and Phasic SCR) monitors sympathetic arousal and stress-reactive sweat gland activity, complemented by respiration rates (ΔAVRESP) to capture somatic stabilization. Finally, the Profile of Mood States (POMS) scale is integrated to assess the conscious, subjective macro-integration of these multi-level biometrics, serving as the affective end-point of the recovery process.By synthesizing these highly coupled yet distinct dimensions—spanning from millisecond-level central cognitive processing to sustained peripheral autonomic tone and macro-scale psychological affect—the CMRI bypasses the limitations of single-modality bias. It provides an empirically justified, holistic framework capable of evaluating whether an environment elicits a synchronized mind-body recovery or causes cross-systemic fragmentation.

Contribution of this research

Our research breaks away from routine technological accumulation and simplistic environmental dichotomies, offering substantial theoretical and methodological advancements to the field of environmental neuroscience across three interconnected dimensions:

First, we advance the methodological paradigm from parallel data stacking to dynamic cross-modal biological decoupling. Traditional environmental psychology literature frequently treats multi-source biometric streams as isolated, stacked variables, relying on disconnected post-hoc statistical regressions that fail to capture the systemic co-evolution of the human body. Transcending these limitations, this study utilizes the ErgoLAB platform to establish a high-precision, millisecond-level synchronized framework that treats central, autonomic, and psychological systems as an integrated biological unit. By mapping the micro-temporal chain reactions the instant visual contact occurs, our framework transitions the scientific focus from hardware-reliant concurrent logging to the empirical evaluation of cross-systemic state coupling.

Second, we mathematically formulate the Cross-Modal Restorative Index (CMRI) as a standardized, universal diagnostic protocol rather than a customized data fusion tool. By transforming fundamentally heterogeneous, multi-scale biosignals into three non-overlapping, normalized dimensions—the Eye-tracking Restorative Index (ERT), Physiological Restorative Index (PSI), and Psychological Restorative Index (PRI)—the CMRI possesses strict, scenario-independent boundary conditions that render it highly scalable across diverse human-factor and urban simulation contexts. Maintaining the granular operational independence of these sub-indices allows the model to mathematically diagnose “cross-modal decoupling,” capturing instances where highly patterned urban geometries intensely demand visual attention while failing to downregulate, or even actively compounding, peripheral somatic stress—a critical biological anomaly that a consolidated, single-score index would inevitably cancel out. Furthermore, this modular architecture structurally respects the hierarchical sequence of the “restoration cascade,” wherein visual attention allocation (ERT) operates as the primary behavioral gateway, neuro-autonomic recalibration (PSI) acts as the continuous physiological mediator, and subjective mood shift (PRI) represents the macro-scale conscious end-point, enabling empirical validation of their interconnectedness through post-hoc correlation matrices and trajectory clustering instead of assuming a flat, monolithic recovery.

Third, we reframe the traditional landscape spectrum from a crude binary division to an empirical matrix of non-linear perceptual thresholds. Rather than merely introducing arbitrary intermediate spatial categories between the classic “nature-urban” poles, this study establishes a refined testing atlas comprising nine typical landscape typologies to explore the continuous environmental gradient. This refined spectrum allows us to empirically capture the non-linear inflection points, perceptual redundancy effects, and cognitive saturation thresholds that govern human psychophysiological systems during spatial transitions. Consequently, our conceptualization of “integrated restoration” versus “fragmented restoration” moves beyond semantic re-labeling of inconsistent multisystem responses, providing healthy city planners with a high-fidelity diagnostic toolkit to identify the precise structural boundaries where landscapes shift from providing superficial sensory stimulation to eliciting deep, holistic systemic recovery.

Materials and methods

Participants and statistical power analysis

A total of 42 healthy undergraduate and graduate students (21 males, 21 females; mean age = 21.4 ± 1.8 years) participated in the experiment [15]. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, normal hearing, and no history of neurological, cardiovascular, or psychiatric disorders. Participants were instructed to abstain from alcohol, caffeine, and strenuous physical exercise for 24 h prior to the study.

To verify the statistical adequacy of the sample size (N = 42) for a within-subject repeated-measures design featuring nine landscape conditions, an a priori and post-hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (Version 3.1.9.7). For a repeated-measures ANOVA (within-factors, 9 measurements), assuming a standard significance level (α = 0.05), a conservative non-sphericity correction (ε = 1), and a medium effect size (f = 0.25), a sample size of N = 42 yields a statistical power (1 - β) of 0.914 [16, 17]. This significantly exceeds the standard statistical benchmark of 0.80, confirming that the sample size provides sufficient statistical sensitivity to detect micro-level fluctuations in multi-systemic physiological and cognitive responses across the landscape continuum [18].

Experimental equipments

To objectively quantify the differential restorative effects of the studied landscapes, we employed a highly precise, multimodal data acquisition approach. All physiological and behavioral data streams were collected and temporally synchronized using the ErgoLAB Human-Machine-Environment Synchronous Cloud Platform (V3.0) developed by Beijing Kingfar Technology (https://www.kingfar.cn/).

The platform’s crystal oscillator clock synchronization ensured millisecond-level alignment across all sensors (Fig. 1) allowing for reliable cross-modal analysis of cognitive and emotional responses .The following data streams were simultaneously recorded: (1) Neurophysiological Data (EEG). Brain activity was measured using a 16-channel ErgoLAB EEG system. The high sampling rate (4096 Hz) ensured high temporal resolution for artifact rejection and spectral analysis. Data were analyzed using the ErgoLAB (Fig. 2) platform to determine the Power Spectral Density (PSD) within five major frequency bands: Delta (1–4 Hz), Theta (4–8 Hz), Alpha (9–14 Hz), Beta (14–30 Hz), and Gamma (30–49 Hz). These metrics served as objective indicators of cognitive load, arousal level, and attentional state induced by the visual stimuli. (2) Oculomotor Behavior (Eye-tracking): Visual attention and search strategies were continuously monitored using a Tobii Pro Fusion remote eye tracker(250 Hz), with trajectory and fixation data processed by the ErgoLAB Eyetracking Analysis Module. Key metrics, including fixation count, average fixation duration, and time to first fixation on specific Areas of Interest (AOIs), were extracted to map the spatial and temporal allocation of attention. (3) Peripheral Physiological Data (EDA, HRV, RESP): Peripheral nervous system responses were recorded using the ErgoLAB Biosensor wireless wearable physiological recorder.

Processing methods

Raw biosignals collected via the ErgoLAB synchronized platform were subjected to independent, modality-specific preprocessing pipelines to eliminate motion artifacts, ocular noise, and environmental interference before mathematical indexing:

Raw EEG signals were processed using the following algorithmic chain: (1) A zero-phase finite impulse response (FIR) bandpass filter was applied between 0.5 Hz and 40 Hz to remove low-frequency drift and high-frequency muscle artifacts. (2) A notch filter centered at 50 Hz was implemented to attenuate AC power-line interference. (3) Bad channels exhibiting sustained impedance spikes or flatlines were interpolated using spherical spline interpolation. (4) Independent Component Analysis (ICA) utilizing the Infomax algorithm was executed to isolate and mathematically eliminate ocular artifacts (eye blinks and saccades) and cardiac fields, guided by topographic distribution and spectral profiles. (5) Continuous data were epoched into 1-second segments. (6) Power Spectral Density (PSD) was computed via Welch’s method (50% overlapping Hamming windows) to extract absolute power (µV2) [19, 20].

Electrodermal activity (EDA) data were downsampled to 32 Hz and smoothed using a 1 Hz low-pass Butterworth filter to suppress rapid motion artifacts. To resolve the overlapping nature of skin conductance responses, Continuous Decomposition Analysis (CDA) was executed via the Ledalab framework implemented in ErgoLAB. This mathematical decomposition decoupled the raw EDA signal into two distinct, non-overlapping dimensions: (1) Tonic Skin Conductance Level (SCL, representing background sympathetic tone) and (2) Phasic Skin Conductance Response (SCR, capturing immediate emotional arousal events exceeding a threshold of 0.01 µS).

Psychological indicators

To ensure a comprehensive and objective quantification of the restorative effects of rural landscapes, this study complemented objective physiological data collection with the synchronous administration of the Profile of Mood States (POMS). These instruments were employed to capture participants’ subjective affect, mood states, and experiential connections to the landscapes following exposure.

Profile of Mood States (POMS): The POMS [25, 26]was utilized to assess participants’ instantaneous, current mood states immediately following experimental stimulation. The scale comprises 40 adjective items designed to evaluate multiple dimensions of mood, including Tension, Anger, Fatigue, and Vigor. Participants rated the extent to which each adjective corresponded to their actual feelings at that moment. Assessments were conducted using a 5-point Likert scale, with response options ranging from “Not at all,” “A little,” “Moderately,” “Quite a bit,” to “Extremely.” This scale provided a subjective basis for quantifying transitions in emotional states and characterizing the mood profile distribution associated with landscape stimuli (Table 1).

Experimental materials

The pictures of this experiment are from Yanjiang Village, Wufu Village and Guangming Village in Changsha City, Hunan Province - the methods are suitable for this study (Fig. 3). These villages are located in the subtropical monsoon climate zone, featuring diverse ecological environments such as vast fields, water bodies, forests and low-strength building structures. This combination provides a landscape gradient from natural to semi-architectural, which is in good agreement with the comparative framework of the study. Furthermore, their limited urbanization ensures the ecological authenticity of the natural landscape, making them an ideal choice for studying the integrated and fragmented restoration responses in landscape types.

Site selection location

The visual stimuli employed in this study consisted of a curated set of high-definition photographs representing diverse landscape typologies. To ensure high ecological validity and consistent image quality, all photographs were captured using a Nikon Z30 mirrorless camera equipped with a lens set to a 16 mm focal length. This focal length (equivalent to approx. 24 mm in 35 mm full-frame format) was selected to provide a wide field of view that approximates the human peripheral visual experience, thereby enhancing the sense of immersion—a critical component of the “Extent” dimension in Attention Restoration Theory.

To ensure the construct validity and representative categorization of the photographic stimuli, a panel of five independent experts (comprising two environmental psychologists, two landscape architects, and one urban planner) conducted a formal evaluation of the initial pool of 45 images.The valuation protocol was strictly structured around three target dimensions, with each expert responding to standardized items scored on a 5-point Likert agreement scale (1 = “Strongly Disagree”, 5 = “Strongly Agree”) “Visual Typicality”: This photograph accurately and unambiguously represents the core aesthetic and spatial characteristics of its designated landscape typology.“Structural Clarity”: The compositional structure, focal elements, and visual boundaries within this image are distinct and free of perceptual ambiguity.“Emotional Neutrality”: The sensory presentation of this scene is emotionally neutral and free of confounding atmospheric anomalies.

The inter-rater reliability among the five judges was quantified using Fleiss’ Kappa coefficient independently for each dimension to evaluate consensus stability. The dimensional reliability analysis yielded a Fleiss’ Kappa of k = 0.856 (p < 0.001) for Visual Typicality, k = 0.812 (p < 0.001) for Structural Clarity, and k = 0.858 (p < 0.001) for Emotional Neutrality (Table 2).

Experimental procedure

The experiment was conducted in a soundproof, climate-controlled environmental behavior simulation laboratory (24℃±1℃, relative humidity 60% ±5%). A within-subject repeated-measures design was employed, where each participant was exposed to all nine distinct urban-to-natural landscape typologies via high-fidelity visual-auditory stimuli. To completely systematically eliminate order bias, learning curves, and sequence fatigue, the presentation sequence of the nine landscape conditions was stringently counterbalanced using a Williams Latin Square design. Unlike standard Latin Squares, the Williams square is mathematically balanced for first-order residual effects, ensuring that every landscape typology appears in each chronological sequence slot an equal number of times, and that no two conditions immediately precede or follow each other more than once across the entire cohort.

The timeline of the experimental protocol is structured as follows:

Sensory Stabilization & Baseline Phase (3 min) — Upon sensor attachment and calibration, participants sat quietly facing a uniform gray screen while listening to neutral pink noise. This 3-minute period is physiologically required to establish a stable, resting-state autonomic homeostasis baseline, particularly for capturing reliable tonic electrodermal activity (EDA) and low-frequency components of Heart Rate Variability (HRV). At the immediate conclusion of this baseline period, participants were instructed to complete the pre-exposure Profile of Mood States (POMS-Pre) scale via a digital tablet to establish their baseline psychological affect.

Stimulus Exposure Phase (90 s per condition) — Participants viewed and listened to the assigned landscape typology. This duration is optimal for eliciting sustained neural oscillations (EEG α/β blocks) and stable phasic physiological shifts without inducing cognitive boredom or ocular fatigue (Fig. 5). The instant the 90-second visual exposure terminated, participants immediately completed the post-exposure Profile of Mood States (POMS-Post) scale to capture the immediate macro-scale psychological affect before any situational emotional dissipation.

An example of an electroencephalogram during the experiment

Washout and Recovery Phase (2 min between trials) — Following each landscape exposure and its corresponding POMS evaluation, a 2-minute washout period was implemented, during which participants were re-exposed to the resting gray screen and a central fixation crosshair (+). A 2-minute duration is strictly derived from autonomic recovery kinetics; it allows the sweat gland activity (skin conductance levels) to recede to the pre-stimulus baseline and provides a sufficient temporal window to calculate short-term RMSSD and HF bands of HRV, preventing physiological bleeding or carry-over from the preceding landscape type into the subsequent trial (Fig. 6).

Results

Empirical verification of washout efficiency and carry-over effects

Before evaluating the core hypotheses, a preliminary statistical check was executed to confirm the absence of carry-over or order effects across the repeated trials. Participants were categorized into distinct sequence blocks based on their Latin Square allocation. A mixed-design ANOVA was performed, specifying Presentation Order as the between-subject factor, Landscape Typology as the within-subject factor, and the baseline values captured immediately prior to each stimulus exposure as the dependent variables.The statistical analysis revealed no significant main effect for Presentation Order on pre-stimulus heart rate (F(8, 33) = 1.04, p = 0.428, ηp² = 0.20), skin conductance baseline (F(8, 33) = 0.89, p = 0.534, ηp² = 0.17), or EEG total power spectral density (F(8, 33) = 1.12, p = 0.375, ηp² = 0.21). Furthermore, no significant interaction effects between Presentation Order and Landscape Typology were detected (p > 0.05). These empirical checks definitively demonstrate that the 2-minute washout period was completely effective in resetting participants’ psychophysiological states to baseline levels, successfully neutralizing any confounding carry-over or sequence-dependent artifacts.

Physiological data

Electroencephalogram(EEG)

Across all conditions, natural landscapes generally elicited higher posterior alpha activity, with the highest values observed in Field (7.35) and Water (7.03) scenes. However, some artificial environments also produced relatively strong alpha responses—for example, City (7.08) exceeded several natural categories, including Forest (6.53) and Mountainous (6.59). Beta activity showed similar condition differences, with City again presenting the highest value (8.30). These patterns indicate that, although natural environments produced the overall strongest neural recovery signatures, certain artificial scenes also showed comparable effects, and not all natural scenes demonstrated uniformly high responses.

Data normalization, outlier management, and group aggregation

Cross-modal statistical coupling and justification for aggregation

Given the inherent biological variability of physiological signals across human subjects, a rigorous multi-stage outlier detection protocol was enforced. At the individual subject-trial level, data points for any physiological metric that exceeded ± 3 standard deviations (SD) from that participant’s running mean across the specific experimental block were classified as statistical outliers induced by transient sensor displacement or micro-movements. These corrupted segments (accounting for approximately 2.4% of the total data volume) were excluded from subsequent feature calculations and handled via pairwise deletion to maintain statistical integrity without inflating the type I error rate.

Outlier management and data cleaning

Prior to aggregating the individual normalized sub-indices into the collective population-level framework, a Pearson correlation analysis was executed on the normalized individual variation vectors (ΔPRI, ΔERT, and ΔPSI) across all subject-trial blocks. This step serves as a critical methodological verification to establish the construct validity and mathematical eligibility of cross-modal integration.As presented in Table 8, the statistical outcomes revealed highly significant positive correlations among all three distinct restorative dimensions. Specifically, the Eye-tracking Restorative Index (ERT) exhibited a robust positive linkage with both the Physiological Restorative Index (PSI) (r = 0.456, p < 0.01) and the Psychological Restorative Index (PRI) (r = 0.582, p < 0.001). Concurrently, a significant convergence was confirmed between PSI and PRI (r = 0.514, p < 0.01).These tight statistical couplings provide empirical proof of the “restoration cascade” hypothesis. Visual immersion in restorative landscapes (captured by ERT) does not act as an isolated cognitive event, but rather functions as a direct behavioral trigger that systematically co-varies with autonomic/central nervous system de-excitation (PSI) and subjective emotional recovery (PRI). Consequently, combining these three highly coordinated modalities into a single, unified Cross-Modal Restorative Index (CMRI) is theoretically justified and mathematically sound, avoiding the risk of arbitrarily aggregating independent or orthogonal variable.

Individual-level normalization framework

To enable cross-modal comparisons and construct interpretable restorative indices, all raw data were subjected to Min-Max normalization, mapping values to the [0, 1] interval where 1 represents the highest restorative potential. Normalization was performed at the individual level for each participant, followed by robust group-level statistical aggregation via mixed modeling to derive the definitive indices per landscape category (N = 42). This approach ensures comparability across physiological, eye-tracking, and psychological modalities, highlighting relative restorative effects. Overall, the indices reveal that natural-dominant landscapes (e.g., forests and Mountainous) consistently score higher (closer to 1) across modalities, indicating superior restorative capacity compared to built environments (e.g., cities and roads, often below 0.5), aligning with biophilia and attention restoration theories.

For physiological data, the restorative index was computed as the average of normalized values from 8 indicators. Positive indicators (higher values indicate greater restoration) included posterior alpha power, phasic EDA, RMSSD, HF power, and ΔAVRESP; negative indicators (lower values indicate greater restoration) comprised beta power, tonic EDA, and LF/HF ratio. The normalized value for the i-th indicator in the k-th landscape was defined separately for positive and negative indicators, where min and max are computed across all landscapes for that indicator per individual. The physiological restorative index is the average of these normalized values. For example, if a landscape yields high alpha power (normalized to 0.85) and low beta power (normalized to 0.92), it contributes to a high PRI, reflecting autonomic relaxation.

Discussion

Multimodal couplings and systemic synergies across the landscape continuum

Rather than functioning as isolated psychological or biological phenomena, the environmental restoration process manifests as a multi-systemic covariance across neural, autonomic, attentional, and subjective psychological domains. By scaling heterogeneous bio-signals onto a standardized [0, 1] Cross-Modal Restorative Index (CMRI) framework, this study captures the mathematical convergence or divergence among different human subsystems, avoiding the simplistic dualism of ‘nature versus city’. The robust positive correlations observed between the Eye-tracking Restorative Index (ERT), Physiological Restorative Index (PSI), and Psychological Restorative Index (PRI) (Table 8) suggest a highly coupled, cross-modal response triggered during environment exposure. However, we note that these synchronous shifts represent statistical coupling and multi-systemic convergence rather than a definitive, unidirectional causal chain, as the cross-sectional, repeated-measures design captures immediate psychophysiological adjustments rather than long-term longitudinal transformations. Natural-dominant landscapes (e.g., Mountainous, Field, and Water) consistently elicited a higher baseline of systemic alignment, characterized by a concurrent surge in parasympathetic dominance (RMSSD, HF power) and a significant downregulation of central cognitive arousal (posterior alpha power activation). This structural convergence supports the integration of Biophilia and Attention Restoration theories, suggesting that low-demand, evolutionarily adaptive visual configurations mitigate stress while simultaneously freeing directed attentional resources without overtaxing cognitive capacity.

This convergence on natural superiority can be attributed to biophilic responses, where innate human affinities for organic environments trigger autonomic nervous system recalibration toward relaxation [27–29]. Water and Field landscapes likely evoke “soft fascination” through their expansive [30], undemanding visual features—such as rippling surfaces or open vistas—that facilitate involuntary attention and reduce cognitive load, as evidenced by heightened alpha power and lowered tonic EDA (e.g., 3.80 in Field vs. baseline 7.17). The Road exception may stem from perceptual familiarity or “prospect-refuge” dynamics [31, 32], where linear paths offer a sense of navigability and safety, paradoxically inducing physiological calm akin to natural trails, despite urban connotations. This finding challenges simplistic nature-vs. -built dichotomies and suggests that design elements mimicking natural flow (e.g., curved roads) could amplify restoration.

Comparatively, prior studies like those by Ulrich using EDA and HRV in hospital settings have similarly reported natural views reducing sympathetic arousal, but often without multimodal integration [7]. Our results extend this by quantifying exceptions like Road, aligning with recent meta-analyses [33–35]that highlight context-dependent effects in semi-natural urban elements, though our higher indices for Water and Field (exceeding 0.75) surpass typical values in controlled lab simulations, likely due to our high-fidelity synchronization via ErgoLAB.

Mountainous and field landscapes emerge as emotional and cognitive restoratives

In the psychological restorative index (PsychIndex), calculated as the average normalized change scores across seven POMS dimensions (with positive Δ for negative emotions like depression and tension, and vice versa for self-esteem and energy), Mountainous (PsychIndex = 0.88) and Field (= 0.85) landscapes yielded the highest values, driven by substantial reductions in negative states and boosts in positives. For Mountainous, tension dropped markedly (18.6 to 12.3, Δ = 6.3) and energy rose (e.g., + 4.5 points), while Field showed the largest depression decrease (2.3 points) and fatigue reduction (17.1 to 14.0, Δ = 3.1), alongside anger mitigation (3.6 points). These shifts indicate enhanced emotional regulation, with average positive metric gains of 4.1 points across landscapes but peaking here.

Such outcomes may arise from the perceptual vastness and coherence in these scenes, fostering a sense of “being away” and compatibility per Kaplan’s Attention Restoration Theory [36], which in turn alleviates directed attention fatigue and promotes affective uplift. Mountainous terrains, with their layered depths, likely induce awe—a restorative emotion linked to broadened cognitio [37–39]—while Fields’ openness supports mindfulness-like states, reducing rumination as reflected in lowered panic and depression scores.

Attention Index distinctly isolates pristine natural environments

The eye-tracking restorative index (AttentionIndex), based solely on the normalized total fixation duration percentage on natural elements (NaturalTFD%), was overwhelmingly dominated by pristine natural landscapes, with Forest (AttentionIndex = 0.92, 85.911% TFD on forest), Mountainous (= 0.95, 90.832% including Mountainous and fields/forests), and Water (= 0.87, 70.219%) achieving near-maximal scores. These metrics highlight prolonged first fixation times (e.g., 1.364 s in Forest) and high fixation counts (84.996% in Forest), contrasting sharply with built scenes like City (0% natural TFD, index = 0.05) where artificial elements claimed 90.580% dwell time.

This isolation of natural dominance supports ART’s “fascination” component, where complex yet harmonious natural features captivate involuntary attention, enabling directed attention recovery without effort [40, 41]. The high TFD% in these environments likely reflects bottom-up saliency from organic textures and colors, drawing gaze away from stressors and toward restorative cues, as visualized in our AOI heatmaps via ErgoLAB.

In comparison to previous work, our findings provide precise physiological validation for ART beyond self-reports, echoing eye-tracking studies like Fei that noted increased natural fixations correlating with reduced stress [42], but with lower resolutions (e.g., < 70% TFD). Our indices, exceeding 0.85 in top performers, suggest amplified effects in immersive rural stimuli, diverging from urban greening research [43] where mixed scenes yield moderate indices (= 0.50), thus advocating for preserved pristine habitats in therapeutic design.

Empirical deconstruction of fragmented restoration via CMRI

The utility of the Cross-Modal Restorative Index (CMRI) becomes particularly salient when interpreting the multi-systemic anomalies captured in our 9-typology landscape spectrum. Traditional multimodal studies often treat anomalous data—such as the high physiological restoration index observed in artificial “Road” landscapes (PRI = 0.631)—as experimental noise or unexplainable outliers. However, viewed through our framework, the “Road” landscape presents a classic manifestation of Fragmented Restoration. While it triggered low sympathetic arousal and high posterior alpha power (driving its elevated PRI) due to the low-demand visual familiarity and predictability of linear paths (prospect-refuge dynamics), it concurrently generated remarkably low attentional (ERI = 0.121) and psychological (PSI = 0.448) restorative returns. This stark cross-modal divergence empirically proves that physiological calming does not inherently imply cognitive or emotional restoration. Conversely, pristine natural landscapes like “Forest” and “Mountainous” exhibited true Integrated Restoration, where the elevated attentional indices (ERI = 0.946 and 1.000, respectively) locked in perfect synergy with emotional uplift (PSI = 0.647 for Mountainous). By capturing these multi-systemic interactions, the CMRI demonstrates that the restorative capacity of an environment cannot be accurately captured by single or stacked modalities; it is defined by the degree of cross-system synchronization it commands. This shift from measuring “how much” an environment restores to “how cohesively” it restores constitutes the primary theoretical advancement of this study over prior multimodal literature.

Deep mechanistic probe into fragmented and anomalous restorative trajectories

A critical strength of the cross-modal framework lies in its capacity to mechanistically diagnose ‘anomalous’ findings that traditional parallel-data stacking designs often discard as experimental noise. The most striking anomaly observed was the ‘Road’ landscape, which exhibited a remarkably elevated Physiological Index (PRI = 0.631) alongside a severely depressed Eye-tracking Index (ERT = 0.121) and Psychological Index (PSI = 0.448). Under traditional SRT frameworks, the surge in posterior alpha power variance and parasympathetic tone would categorize ‘Road’ as a highly restorative habitat. However, our cross-modal deconstruction reveals this as a classic state of Fragmented Restoration. The physiological calming induced by the linear road stimulus likely stems from perceptual predictability and ‘prospect-refuge’ mechanics; the geometric clarity of a paved corridor offers high navigability, minimizing short-term spatial uncertainty and lowering immediate sympathetic arousal. Yet, because the visual field is depleted of biophilic features and dominated by low-fascination artificial elements (attracting over 90% of total dwell time to built infrastructures), it fails to trigger bottom-up attention recovery or subjective affective uplift, resulting in a severe cross-system decoupling.

Similarly, subtle psychometric anomalies emerged in pristine natural habitats, such as the unexpected slight increase in panic scores (+ 0.6 points) following ‘Forest’ exposure, and the rise in depression scores (17.1 to 18.3) within the ‘Water’ condition. Rather than invalidating the therapeutic metrics of blue-green infrastructure, these nuances point to the psychological complexity of ‘ecological wildness’. High-density, unmanaged forest canopies can induce feelings of spatial confinement or claustrophobia, where a low ‘refuge’ property paradoxically heightens subconscious hyper-vigilance. Concurrently, static or oversized water bodies under overcast sensory exposures can shift subjective affect from tranquility toward melancholy or emotional flatness, explaining the localized spike in depression scales. These empirical variations demonstrate that landscape restoration is non-linear and context-dependent, mediated tightly by the micro-structural configuration of the landscape elements rather than their absolute ecological authenticity.”

Cross-modal evidence reveals fragmented versus integrated restoration

Across modalities, our results reveal that landscapes do not generate restoration in a uniform way. Natural environments tended to elicit a coordinated restorative response, where neural activity, visual attention, physiological arousal, and emotional states all shifted in a similar direction. In contrast, urban and semi-urban environments showed partial or fragmented restoration, improving one system while failing to improve—or even worsening—others. For example, natural scenes such as Field and Water produced clear and synchronous recovery signals: posterior alpha power was high (Field = 7.35; Water = 7.03), tonic EDA dropped substantially (Field = 3.80; Water = 3.91 vs. Baseline = 7.17), HRV increased markedly (Field HF = 12518.77), and participants devoted most of their dwell time to natural elements (Forest = 85.91%; Mountainous = 73.82%). Psychological states also improved, with Mountainous showing a large reduction in tension (18.6 → 12.3) and Field showing the highest drop in depression (18.2 → 15.9). These mutually reinforcing signals indicate that natural landscapes simultaneously relax the brain, reduce physiological arousal, guide attention toward low-demand natural features, and improve mood—demonstrating a fully integrated form of restoration.

Urban environments presented a contrasting pattern, with restoration confined to specific subsystems. Road scenes are a notable example: although posterior alpha variability increased sharply (SD from 15.11 → 20.54) and tonic EDA decreased (4.43), indicating physiological relaxation, eye-tracking showed that 74% of dwell time remained focused on artificial structures. Emotional recovery was also limited, with some dimensions showing little or no improvement (e.g., Road fatigue increased from 15.3 → 16.2). A similar dissociation appeared in City scenes: beta activity was highest among all categories (8.30), reflecting heightened cognitive load, yet alpha power (7.08) was comparable to several natural scenes. Despite this neural pattern, attentional engagement remained dominated by Architecture (76.53% dwell time), and psychological responses were inconsistent or negative (e.g., a slight rise in depression: 18.3 → 18.5). These cross-modal contradictions demonstrate that urban scenes may reduce autonomic load but do not reliably promote attentional soft fascination or emotional restoration, resulting in a fragmented recovery profile.

Collectively, these findings extend classical restoration theories by demonstrating that restoration is not a single-domain outcome but a multisystem alignment process. Traditional frameworks such as Attention Restoration Theory and Stress Reduction Theory assume that recovery emerges consistently across cognitive and affective systems [44–46]; however, our cross-modal evidence shows that only natural landscapes produce this coordinated alignment, whereas urban forms activate isolated components—typically physiological downregulation—without engaging the full restorative cascade. The theoretical contribution of this study is therefore twofold: first, we introduce a cross-modal model of restoration that distinguishes integrated (natural) from partial (urban) recovery; second, we empirically demonstrate that the coherence of restoration across neural, attentional, physiological, and emotional systems—not improvements in any single domain—is the hallmark of truly restorative environments. Thus, while urban landscapes retain some restorative potential, their effects remain constrained and incomplete compared with the synchronized, multimodal recovery profile characteristic of natural environments.

Future and limitations

Future

Future research should extend the present findings by increasing ecological validity and broadening the scope of multimodal restoration assessment. (1) Moving beyond static images toward immersive VR, 360° panoramas, and in-situ physiological recordings will help determine whether the integrated restorative patterns identified here generalize to real-world multisensory environments. (2) Future work should examine more diverse populations—across age groups, cultural backgrounds, and environmental familiarity—to clarify how individual differences shape cross-modal restoration profiles. (3)Methodological advances could improve the multimodal index itself. Rather than assigning equal weights to neural, attentional, physiological, and psychological systems, future studies may employ hierarchical modeling, machine-learning fusion, or structural equation modeling to derive optimized and theoretically grounded weightings.(4) Targeted manipulations of landscape attributes—such as openness, blue–green ratios, visual coherence, and spatial depth—would help identify which environmental components most effectively trigger integrated restoration, providing actionable insights for landscape design and urban planning.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting these findings. First, although the laboratory setting ensured high measurement precision and millisecond-level synchronization across modalities, the controlled environment cannot fully replicate the sensory richness of outdoor environments, potentially constraining ecological validity. Second, the participant sample consisted primarily of young adults, limiting demographic generalizability and potentially underrepresenting groups whose restorative responses differ due to age, cultural norms, or environmental experience. Third, the equal-weighted multimodal index, while methodologically transparent, assumes comparable contributions across cognitive, affective, neural, and autonomic domains; this simplification may obscure more nuanced interdependencies between systems. Finally, while this study acknowledges that urban landscapes possess partial restorative potential, the design did not isolate specific urban elements responsible for these effects. Consequently, the nature of urban restoration—and its boundary conditions—remains only partially understood.

Implications of findings

The multimodal evidence presented in this study extends beyond regional applicability, offering critical insights for addressing the global mental health crisis precipitated by rapid urbanization in the Anthropocene. The distinction between “Integrated Restoration” in rural natural settings and “Fragmented Restoration” in built environments bears profound implications for global public health policy and urban governance:

Bridging the Global “Evolutionary Gap” for Planetary Health As the global urban population approaches 68% by 2050, the “Evolutionary Mismatch” identified in this study represents a universal public health challenge. Our findings suggest that preserving ecologically authentic rural landscapes is not merely a matter of aesthetic conservation but a critical strategy for Planetary Health. These landscapes act as essential “ecological reservoirs” that provide the necessary sensory inputs (Integrated Restoration) to recalibrate the human nervous system. Policies globally should prioritize the conservation of accessible rural “green and blue infrastructures” as a preventative medical resource to combat the rising tide of urban stress-related disorders.

Conclusions

This study establishes a multimodal framework that systematically compares the restorative effects of natural and urban landscapes across neural, physiological, attentional, and psychological domains. The key conclusions are as follows:

Rural natural landscapes serve as a critical “Ecological Antidote” to Evolutionary Mismatch. The study data confirms that rural environments—specifically those maintaining ecological authenticity like fields and mountainous terrains—effectively bridge the “evolutionary rift” precipitated by rapid urbanization. Unlike urban settings that trigger “mind-body dissociation,” rural landscapes provide the specific sensory signals (natural fractals and organic rhythms) required to re-synchronize human biological systems, proving them to be not merely aesthetic resources but essential public health infrastructures for mitigating the mental health crises of the Anthropocene.
