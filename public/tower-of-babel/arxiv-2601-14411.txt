A streamlined proof of the Kakeya set conjecture in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3}
Larry Guth and Hong Wang and Joshua Zahl

Abstract
. We present a streamlined and simplified proof of the Kakeya set conjecture in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} .



1.
Introduction to the Kakeya problem

A
Besicovitch set
is a compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that contains a unit line segment pointing in every direction. The Kakeya set conjecture asserts that every Besicovitch set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
has Minkowski and Hausdorff dimension
n
. We refer the reader to
[
4
]
and the references therein for an introduction to the Kakeya conjecture and its connections to problems in harmonic analysis.

The Kakeya set conjecture was proved in dimension two by Davies
[
1
]
, and was recently proved in dimension three by the second and third authors in a sequence of papers
[
6
,
7
,
8
]
. The purpose of this paper is to present a new, streamlined proof of the main result from the third paper
[
8
]
.

The first paper,
[
6
]
, proves an important special case of this conjecture called the sticky Kakeya conjecture. The sticky case was formulated by Katz and Tao, who also outlined an approach to proving it, cf.
[
5
]
. The paper
[
6
]
proves the sticky case of the Kakeya conjecture, approximately following the outline from
[
5
]
but dealing with significant technical issues. The second paper
[
7
]
obtains a mild generalization of the sticky Kakeya theorem, and uses it to prove that every Besicovitch set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has Assouad dimension 3; this is a weak form of the Kakeya set conjecture. The third paper,
[
8
]
, reduces the general case of the Kakeya conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to the sticky case that was proved in
[
6
,
7
]
. In this paper, we present a streamlined version of this reduction.

We begin with several definitions.
Suppose that
\mathbb{T}
is a set of tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with radius
\delta
and length 1. We write

U(\mathbb{T})=\bigcup_{T\in\mathbb{T}}T.

Our goal is to understand
|U(\mathbb{T})|
, the volume of
U(\mathbb{T})
. We write
|\mathbb{T}|
for the cardinality of
\mathbb{T}
and we write
|T|
for the volume of a tube
T\in\mathbb{T}
. We will prove that if
\mathbb{T}
satisfies suitable anti-clustering hypotheses, then
|U(\mathbb{T})|
is almost as large as
|\mathbb{T}||T|
.

In order to describe these anti-clustering hypotheses, we need some language that describes how much convex sets cluster in other convex sets. Suppose that
\mathbb{W}
is a finite set of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and suppose that
K
is another convex set. We define

(1)

\mathbb{W}[K]=\{W\in\mathbb{W}:W\subset K\},

and

(2)

\Delta(\mathbb{W},K)=\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}[K]}|W|}{|K|}.

We can think of
\Delta(\mathbb{W},K)
as a kind of density that measures how
\mathbb{W}
concentrates in
K
. We define a maximal density

(3)

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{W})=\max_{K\text{ convex}}\Delta(\mathbb{W},K).

The quantity
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{W})
measures the extent to which the sets from
\mathbb{W}
cluster into larger convex sets. Quantities of this type were first introduced by Tom Wolff in
[
9
]
.

If
\mathbb{W}
is a set of convex sets, we say that
Y
is a shading of
\mathbb{W}
if
Y(W)\subset W
for each
W\in\mathbb{W}
. We define

(4)

U(\mathbb{W},Y)=\bigcup_{W\in\mathbb{W}}Y(W).

If the shading
Y
is clear from context, we sometimes write
U(\mathbb{W})
for
U(\mathbb{W},Y)
. We also measure how full the shading
Y
is:

(5)

\lambda(\mathbb{W},Y)=\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}}|Y(W)|}{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}}|W|}.

With these definitions, we can now state the main result from
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Kakeya set conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
)
.

For every
\beta>0
, there is some
\eta>0
so that the following holds for all sufficiently small
\delta>0
.

If
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta
-tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, and
Y
is a shading of
\mathbb{T}
with
\lambda(\mathbb{T},Y)\geq\delta^{\eta}
, then

(6)

|U(\mathbb{T},Y)|\geq\delta^{\beta}|\mathbb{T}||T|.

Remark 1.2
.

The original Kakeya conjecture concerns direction-separated tubes. One version says that if
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta^{-(n-1)}

\delta
-tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\delta
-separated directions, then
|U(\mathbb{T})|\gtrapprox 1
. It is straightforward to check that if the tubes of
\mathbb{T}
are direction separated, then
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\lesssim 1
, and so Theorem
1.1
implies a similar estimate for direction separated tubes in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. The volume bound (
6
) thus implies that every Besicovitch set in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has Minkowski and Hausdorff dimension 3.

The goal of this paper is to give a detailed proof of Theorem
1.1
. A key ingredient is the sticky Kakeya theorem from
[
6
,
7
]
. We give a self-contained proof that Theorem
1.1
follows from the sticky Kakeya theorem. Our proof of Theorem
1.1
has the same main ideas as the one in
[
8
]
, but it has been reorganized to make it more concise. A couple parts of the argument in
[
8
]
, such as polynomial partitioning, are no longer needed.

The introduction of
[
8
]
gives an overview of the main ideas of the proof. The expository paper
[
2
]
gives an overview of the Kakeya problem, the sticky case, and the reduction to the sticky case. The expository paper
[
3
]
gives a detailed outline of the reduction from the general case to the sticky case. The current paper closely follows that outline.

2.
Notation and Definitions

2.1.
Convex sets and shadings

We will use blackboard bold letters to denote finite families of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and their non-bold equivalents to denote members of these sets, i.e.
\mathbb{V}
will be a family of convex sets, and
V_{1},V_{2}
or
V,V^{\prime}
will denote elements of
\mathbb{V}
. Similarly for
\mathbb{W}
and
W
, and for
\mathbb{T}
and
T
. We will often use the notation
\sum_{\mathbb{V}}f(V)
in place of
\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}}f(V)
, etc. If
\mathbb{V}
is a family of convex sets, each of which have comparable volume, then we will write
|V|
to denote the volume of a convex set from
\mathbb{V}
.

If
V
is a convex set, the “dimensions” of
V
will be the lengths of the axes of its outer John ellipsoid, listed in increasing order. We will denote this by
v_{1}\times v_{2}\times\ldots\times v_{n}
. A
\delta
-tube
is the
\delta
neighbourhood of a unit line segment in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, a
slab
is a convex set of dimensions
\theta\times 1\times 1
, for some
0<\theta\leq 1
, and a
plank
is a convex set of dimensions
a\times b\times 1
for some
0<a\leq b\leq 1
.

A
shading
of a set
V
is a measurable set
Y(V)\subset V
. We will use
(\mathbb{V},Y)
to denote a family of convex sets and their associated shadings
\{Y(V)\colon V\in\mathbb{V}\}
. For brevity we will sometimes say “let
(\mathbb{V},Y)
be a family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,” or “let
(\mathbb{T},Y)
be a set of
\delta
-tubes.”

We define the multiplicity of a set of convex sets
\mathbb{W}
with a shading
Y
as

(7)

\mu(\mathbb{W},Y)=\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}}|Y(W)|}{|U(\mathbb{W},Y)|}.

When the shading
Y
is clear from the context, we write
\mu(\mathbb{W})
instead.

Information about
\mu(\mathbb{W})
is essentially equivalent to information about
|U(\mathbb{W})|
, but it can be more intuitive to think about one than the other. For instance, in Theorem
1.1
, it would be equivalent to replace the conclusion by
\mu(\mathbb{T})\lesssim\delta^{-\beta}
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define
\mathbb{V}_{Y}(x)=\{V\in\mathbb{V}:x\in Y(V)\}
and
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)=|\mathbb{V}_{Y}(x)|.
We say that
(\mathbb{V},Y)
has constant multiplicity if
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)
is roughly the same for all
x\in U(\mathbb{V},Y)
. We say that
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is a refinement of
(\mathbb{V},Y)
if
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}
and
Y^{\prime}(V)\subset Y(V)
for each
V\in\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
. For
c>0
, we say that
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is a
c
-refinement of
(\mathbb{V},Y)
if
\sum_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}|Y^{\prime}(V)|\geq c\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|Y(V)|
. If
Y
is a shading on
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
is a subset of
\mathbb{V}
, we will often abuse notation and continue to use
Y
to refer to the restriction of the shading to
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
.

2.2.
Pigeonholing and
\lessapprox
notation

Many steps in our proof involve dyadic pigeonholing of the following types:

(i)

If
\mathbb{V}
is a family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each of which has a John ellipsoid with axes of lengths between
\delta
and
\delta^{-1}
, then we wish to pigeonhole to find numbers
v_{1}\leq\ldots\leq v_{n}
and a subset
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
consisting of convex sets whose John ellipsoids have axes of lengths roughly
v_{1}\times\ldots\times v_{n}
. We can always find a subset with
\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V^{\prime}|\gtrsim(\log 1/\delta)^{-n}\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V|
with this property.

(ii)

If
\mathbb{V}
is a family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a shading
Y
, then we wish to find a subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a number
\nu
such that
\sum_{\mathbb{V}}\chi_{Y(V)}(x)\sim\nu
on
X
. We can always find such a set so that
\nu|X|\gtrsim(\log|\mathbb{V}|)^{-1}\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|Y(V)|
.

(iii)

If
(\mathbb{V},Y)
is a family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and their associated shading, then we wish to find a subset
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
consisting of convex sets whose shadings
\{Y(V)\colon V\in\mathbb{V}\}
have roughly the same measure. If
a=\min\{|Y(V)|\colon V\in\mathbb{V},\ Y(V)\neq\emptyset\}
and
A=\max_{\mathbb{V}}|Y(V)|
, then we can always find such a set so that
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y)
is a
\log(A/a)
-refinement of
(\mathbb{V},Y)
.

(iv)

Sometimes we wish to perform the pigeonholing from several of the above items simultaneously. For example if
(\mathbb{V},Y)
is a family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each of which has a John ellipsoid with axes of lengths between
\delta
and
\delta^{-1}
, then there exists a set
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
as in Item (
i
), and a subset
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a number
\nu
as in Item (
ii
), so that
\nu|X|\gtrsim(\log|\mathbb{V}|)^{-1}(\log 1/\delta)^{-n}\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V|
.

Quantities of the form
\log(1/\delta)
or
\log|\mathbb{V}|
are harmless distractions, and in practice any shading
Y(V)
with small measure (i.e.
|Y(V)|\leq\delta^{n}
) can be replaced by
Y(V)=\emptyset
without affecting our arguments. To streamline our arguments, if
X
and
Y
are function of
\rho
, then we introduce the notation “
X\lessapprox Y
” to mean that for all
\epsilon>0
, there is a constant
C=C(\epsilon)
so that
X\leq C_{\epsilon}\rho^{-\epsilon}Y
. The quantity
\rho
will often be implicit—here is how
\lessapprox
notation is applied to the items described above.

(i
′
)

For inequalities involving a convex set, or a collection of convex sets
\mathbb{V}
, we will implicitly define
\rho
to be
\min_{\mathbb{V}}\delta(V)
, where
\delta(V)
is the ratio of the shortest to the longest axes of the John ellipsoid of
V
(up to harmless constants, we could also take
\delta(V)
to be the ratio of the diameter of the largest ball contained inside
V
to the smallest ball that contains
V
). Thus for Item (
i
) above, we could write
|\mathbb{V}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{V}|
.

(ii
′
)

For inequalities involving the cardinality of a set
\mathbb{V}
, we will implicitly define
\rho
to be
|\mathbb{V}|^{-1}
. Thus for Item (
ii
) above, we could write
|\mathbb{V}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{V}|
.

(iii
′
)

For inequalities involving a family
(\mathbb{V},Y)
of convex sets and their associated shading, we will implicitly define
\rho
to be
a/A
, as described in Item (
iii
). Thus for Item (
ii
) above,
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y)
is a
\gtrapprox 1
refinement of
(\mathbb{V},Y)
.

(iv
′
)

Items (
i
′
), (
ii
′
), and (
iii
′
) combine as described in Item (
iv
) above.

2.3.
Uniform sets of tubes

It will sometimes be useful to consider sets
\mathbb{T}
that are uniform in the following sense.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\delta>0
, let
M=\lceil\log\log 1/\delta\rceil
, and let
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We say that
\mathbb{T}
is
uniform
if for each scale of the form
\rho=\delta^{k/M}
,
k=0,\ldots,M
, there is a set of
\rho
-tubes
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
so that

(i)

\mathbb{T}=\bigcup_{\mathbb{T}_{\rho}}\mathbb{T}[T_{\rho}]
.

(ii)

The tubes
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
are essentially distinct. Therefore each
T\in\mathbb{T}
lies in
T_{\rho}
for
\sim 1
choice of
T_{\rho}\in\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
.

(iii)

|\mathbb{T}[T_{\rho}]|
is constant up to a factor
\sim 1
as
T_{\rho}
varies in
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
.

We call the numbers
N_{k}=|\mathbb{T}[T_{\rho_{k}}]|
the
branching numbers
of
\mathbb{T}
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\delta>0
, let
M=\lceil\log\log 1/\delta\rceil
, and let
(\mathbb{T},Y)
be a set of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and their associated shading. We say that
(\mathbb{T},Y)
is
uniform
if the following holds:

•

\mathbb{T}
is uniform.

•

For each point
x\in U(\mathbb{T},Y)
, the sets
\{T\in\mathbb{T}\colon x\in Y(T)\}
are uniform, and have roughly the same branching numbers.

The reason for setting
M=\log\log(1/\delta)
in our above definitions is as follows. First, since
(\log|\mathbb{T}|)^{\log\log(1/\delta)}\lessapprox 1
, by pigeonholing, every set
\mathbb{T}
of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has a subset
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
of cardinality
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{T}|
that is uniform, and every pair
(\mathbb{T},Y)
has a
\approx 1
-refinement that is uniform.

Second, if
\mathbb{T}
is uniform, then for
every
scale
\rho\in[\delta,1]
, there is a set of essentially distinct
\rho
-tubes
\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
that satisfies items (i) and (ii) from Definition
2.1
, and satisfies the following slightly weaker analogue of (iii): there is a number
N_{\rho}
so that
|\mathbb{T}[T_{\rho}]|\approx N_{\rho}
for all
T_{\rho}\in\mathbb{T}_{\rho}
. Similarly for Definition
2.2
; the second bullet point is weakened to: the branching numbers
N_{k}
are roughly the same up to a
\approx 1
factor.

3.
Partial Kakeya estimates

In this section, we consider Kakeya-type estimates for sets of tubes obeying two important non-clustering conditions. These non-clustering conditions are called Katz-Tao conditions and Frostman conditions. They are both descended by the non-concentration conditions introduced by Wolff in
[
9
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A family of convex sets
\mathbb{V}
is called
C
-convex-Katz-Tao (or
C
-Katz-Tao for short) if
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V})\leq C
. Informally, we say
\mathbb{V}
is Katz-Tao if
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V})\lessapprox 1
.

Definition 3.2
.

If
\mathbb{V}
is a set of convex sets in
K
, we define

(8)

C_{F}(\mathbb{V},K)=\frac{\sup_{K^{\prime}\subset K}\Delta(\mathbb{V},K^{\prime})}{\Delta(\mathbb{V},K)},

where the supremum is taken over all convex subsets of
K
. We say that
\mathbb{V}
is
C
-convex-Frostman (or
C
-Frostman for short) in
K
if
C_{F}(\mathbb{V},K)\leq C
. Informally, we say that
\mathbb{V}
is Frostman in
K
if
C_{F}(\mathbb{V},K)\lessapprox 1
. If
K
is the unit ball, then we abbreviate this as
C_{F}(\mathbb{V})
, and we say that
\mathbb{V}
is
C
-Frostman.

Remark 3.3
.

(A) The property of being Frostman is
inherited upwards
: Suppose
\mathbb{V}=\sqcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
is
C
-Frostman inside
K
, where each
W\in\mathbb{W}
is contained in
K
and
V\subset W
for each
V\in\mathbb{V}_{W}
. Then there is a subset
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{W}
that is
\lessapprox C
-Frostman in
K
. If in addition the quantity
\sum_{\mathbb{V}_{W}}|V|/|W|
is approximately the same for each
W\in\mathbb{W}
, then we may take
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}=\mathbb{W}
, and in fact
\mathbb{W}
is
O(C)
-Frostman in
K
.

(B) The property of being Katz-Tao is
inherited downwards
: If
\mathbb{V}
is
C
-Katz-Tao, then every subset
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}
is also
C
-Katz-Tao.

Proof.

We prove (A). Recall that
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)=\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}_{W}}|V|}{|W|}
. Define

\mathbb{W}_{D}=\{W\in\mathbb{W}:\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\sim D\}.

There are
\lessapprox 1
values of
D
. Choose
D
so that
\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}}\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}_{W}}|V|\approx\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}}|V|
.

Now consider a convex set
U\subset K
. Since
\mathbb{V}
is
C
-Frostman in
K
, we have

(9)

\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}[U]}|V|}{|U|}\leq C\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}[K]}|V|}{|K|}.

Now

\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}[U]}|V|\geq\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}[U]}\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}_{W}}|V|\sim\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}[U]}D|W|.

Plugging this equation into the last, we see that

\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}[U]}D|W|}{|U|}\lesssim C\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}[K]}|V|}{|K|}.

On the other hand, since
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}[K]
,
\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}}|V|\approx\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}}\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}_{W}}|V|\sim\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}}D|W|.
Plugging this into the right-hand of the last equation and cancelling a factor of
D
leaves

\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}[U]}|W|}{|U|}\lessapprox C\frac{\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}_{D}[K]}|W|}{|K|}.

In other words,
C_{F}(\mathbb{W}_{D},K)\lessapprox C=C_{F}(\mathbb{V},K)
. In the special case that
\mathbb{W}=\mathbb{W}_{D}
, the argument above shows that
C_{F}(\mathbb{W},K)\lesssim C=C_{F}(\mathbb{V},K)
.

To prove (B), just note that

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})=\max_{K}\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}^{\prime}[K]}|V|}{|K|}\leq\max_{K}\frac{\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}[K]}|V|}{|K|}=\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}).\qed

Definition 3.4
.

We write
K_{KT}(\beta)
for the following statement. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta=\eta(\epsilon,\beta)>0
so that the following holds for any
0<\delta\leq\delta(\epsilon,\beta)
. Let
\mathbb{T}
be a set of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the following properties.

•

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
.

•

Y
is a shading of
\mathbb{T}
with
\lambda(\mathbb{T},Y)\geq\delta^{\eta}
.

Then

(10)

\mu(\mathbb{T},Y)\leq\delta^{-\epsilon}|\mathbb{T}|^{\beta}.

Note that
K_{KT}(1)
is trivial. It is not hard to prove
K_{KT}(\beta)
for some
\beta
slightly less than 1. Our goal is to prove
K_{KT}(0)
.
One important case is when
|\mathbb{T}|\sim\delta^{-2}
. In this case,
K_{KT}(\beta)
gives
\mu(\mathbb{T})\lesssim\delta^{-2\beta-\epsilon}
, which is equivalent to
|U(\mathbb{T})|\gtrsim\delta^{2\beta+\epsilon}
.

Definition 3.5
.

We write
K_{F}(\beta)
for the following statement. For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta=\eta(\epsilon,\beta)>0
so that the following holds for any
0<\delta\leq\delta(\epsilon,\beta)
. Let
\mathbb{T}
be a set of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the following properties.

•

C_{F}(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
.

•

Y
is a shading of
\mathbb{T}
with
\lambda(Y)\geq\delta^{\eta}
.

Then

(11)

\mu(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\epsilon}\delta^{-2\beta}\left(|\mathbb{T}|\ |T|\right)^{1-\beta/2}.

Or equivalently,

|U(\mathbb{T})|\geq\delta^{\epsilon}\delta^{2\beta}\left(|\mathbb{T}|\ |T|\right)^{\beta/2}.

Let us parse the algebra in this bound. Since
C_{F}(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, we have
|\mathbb{T}|\geq\delta^{-2+\eta}
. One important case is when
|\mathbb{T}|\sim\delta^{-2}
. In this case, the bound
C_{F}(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\lesssim\delta^{-\eta}
is equivalent to the bound
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\lesssim\delta^{-\eta}
. So
\mathbb{T}
is both Frostman and Katz-Tao. In this case,
K_{KT}(\beta)
gives
|U(\mathbb{T})|\gtrsim\delta^{2\beta+\epsilon}
, and
K_{F}(\beta)
gives the same bound
|U(\mathbb{T})|\gtrsim\delta^{2\beta+\epsilon}
. When
|\mathbb{T}|\gg\delta^{-2}
, then
K_{F}(\beta)
gives a stronger bound, because the last factor
\left(|\mathbb{T}|\ |T|\right)^{\beta/2}
is
\gg 1
. The exact exponent
\beta/2
in this final factor is not crucial in our analysis, and the argument would work equally well with any positive exponent
c\beta,\ 0<c<1
. There is one step (see Remark
8.2
) where it is important that
c<1
.

Remark 3.6
.

If
K_{KT}(\beta)
holds, then we get for free the following slightly more general version of
K_{KT}(\beta)
: For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta=\eta(\epsilon,\beta)>0
so that the following holds for any
0<\delta\leq\delta(\epsilon,\beta)
. Suppose

4.
Organizing convex sets

If
\mathbb{V}
is a family of convex sets with
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V})\gg 1
, it turns out to be useful to consider the convex sets
W
so that
\Delta(\mathbb{V},W)
is near maximal. These convex sets
W
can be used to organize
\mathbb{V}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

(Maximal density factoring lemma) Let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then there is a set
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}
with
\sum_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}|V|\gtrapprox\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V|
; a family
\mathbb{W}
of convex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each of approximately the same dimensions; and a partition

(12)

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{W\in\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W},

where each set in
\mathbb{V}_{W}
is contained in
W
. This partition has the following properties.

(i)

\mathbb{W}
is Katz-Tao, i.e.
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{W})\sim 1
.

(ii)

\mathbb{V}_{W}
is Frostman in
W
, i.e.
|\mathbb{V}_{W}|\frac{|V|}{|W|}\sim\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)
for each
W\in\mathbb{W}
. Furthermore
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\sim\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})
.

Proof.

After replacing
\mathbb{V}
by a subset for which
\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V|
is preserved up to a
\approx 1
factor, we may suppose that each convex set in
\mathbb{V}
has approximately the same dimensions. We may also suppose that each set in
\mathbb{V}
is compact (if not, then we replace each set by its closure, and then undo this step at the end of the proof). We choose sets
W_{j}
one at a time by a greedy algorithm.
Choose
W_{0}
to maximize
\Delta(\mathbb{V},W_{0})
(a maximizer exists, since
\mathbb{V}
is finite and the sets in
\mathbb{V}
are compact). Then set
\mathbb{V}_{1}=\mathbb{V}\setminus\mathbb{V}[W_{0}]
. Now choose
W_{1}
to maximize
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{1},W_{1})
. Continue in this way until
\mathbb{V}_{s}
is empty. Then stop. For each
j
, we set
\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}=\mathbb{V}_{j}[W_{j}]
; the sets
\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}
are disjoint.

Next we pigeonhole
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{j},W_{j})
. For each
1\leq\log\nu\leq\log(|\mathbb{V}|/|V|)
, we set
J(\nu)=\{j:\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{j},W_{j})\sim\nu\}
. We set
\mathbb{V}_{\nu}=\bigcup_{j\in J(\nu)}\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}
. We note that
\mathbb{V}=\bigsqcup_{\nu}\mathbb{V}_{\nu}
. We choose
\nu
so that
|\mathbb{V}_{\nu}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{V}|
. Choose a subset
\mathbb{W}
of
\{W_{j}\}_{j\in J(\nu)}
of approximately the same dimensions, so that
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
satisfies
|\mathbb{V}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{V}_{\nu}|
, and replace
J(\nu)
with the set of indices
j
such that
W_{j}\in\mathbb{W}
. Observe that we have the desired bound on the size of
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
.

We have
\sum_{\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}}|V|\sim\nu|W_{j}|
for each
j\in J(\nu)
. Since
V\in\mathbb{V}
and each
W\in\mathbb{W}
has approximately the same dimensions,
|\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}|
is comparable for each
j\in J(\nu)
.

Note that
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{j},W_{j})=\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}_{j})
is non-increasing. Set
j_{1}
to be the first number in
J(\nu)
. Since
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}_{j_{1}}
and
\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}\subset\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
, we have

(13)

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})\leq\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}_{j_{1}})=\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W_{j_{1}}},W_{j_{1}})\leq\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime}).

Therefore, all the inequalities above are equalities, and thus Item (
ii
) is satisfied for
W_{j_{1}}
. But since each set
\mathbb{V}_{W_{j}}
has approximately the same cardinality, and each set
W_{j}
has approximately the same volume, we have that
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)
is approximately the same for all
W\in\mathbb{W}
, and thus Item (
ii
) is satisfied for all
W\in\mathbb{W}
.

It remains to verify Item (
i
). Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set. Then

|\mathbb{V}^{\prime}[U]|\geq\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}[U]}|\mathbb{V}_{W}|\gtrsim\sum_{W\in\mathbb{W}[U]}\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\frac{|W|}{|V|}\gtrsim|\mathbb{W}[U]|\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})\frac{|W|}{|V|}.

Re-arranging, we obtain

|\mathbb{W}[U]|\lesssim\frac{|\mathbb{V}^{\prime}[U]|\,|V|}{\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})|W|}\leq\frac{\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})|U|}{\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})|W|}=\frac{|U|}{|W|}.\qed

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{W}
be families of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and suppose there is a partition
\mathbb{V}=\bigcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
We say that
\mathbb{W}

factors

\mathbb{V}
if the following is true:

•

\mathbb{W}
is Katz-Tao, i.e.
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{W})\sim 1
.

•

\mathbb{V}_{W}
is Frostman in
W
, i.e.
|\mathbb{V}_{W}|\frac{|V|}{|W|}\sim\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)
for each
W\in\mathbb{W}
. Furthermore
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\sim\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V})
.

•

Each
W\in\mathbb{W}
has approximately the same dimensions, and each set in
\mathbb{V}_{W}
is contained in
W
.

In the above definition, we choose the implicit constants to match those coming from Lemma
4.1
. Thus Lemma
4.1
says that
\mathbb{W}
factors
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
. We call the set
\mathbb{W}
coming from Lemma
4.1
the maximal density factoring of
\mathbb{V}
, and we call Lemma
4.1
the maximal density factoring lemma.

Let us now discuss what we gain from the maximal density factoring lemma. We began with an arbitrary set of convex sets
\mathbb{V}
. The set
\mathbb{V}
does not obey any “non-clustering condition” such as
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V})\lessapprox 1
. Using the factoring lemma, we can often reduce the problem of understanding
\mathbb{V}
to the problem of understanding the Frostman sets
\mathbb{V}[W]
for
W\in\mathbb{W}
, and the problem of understanding the Katz-Tao set
\mathbb{W}
.

Our goal is to prove a bound on
\mu(\mathbb{T})
when
\mathbb{T}
is Katz-Tao. Our proof is by induction, and along the way we will see other sets of tubes and more generally other sets of convex sets. At first sight, these other sets are not Katz-Tao or Frostman, but by using the maximal density factoring we can reduce matters to Katz-Tao sets and Frostman sets, and we can study these by induction.

5.
Multiplicities, refinements, and factoring

As discussed in the previous section, if
\mathbb{V}
is a family of convex sets then we will bound
\mu(\mathbb{V})
by applying the maximal density factoring lemma and then bounding
\mu(\mathbb{V}_{W})
and
\mu(\mathbb{W})
. In this section, we explain exactly how
\mu(\mathbb{V})
is related to
\mu(\mathbb{V}_{W})
and
\mu(\mathbb{W})
.

Intuitively, it makes sense to have a bound of the form
\mu(\mathbb{V})\lessapprox\mu(\mathbb{V}_{W})\mu(\mathbb{W})
. However, our set
\mathbb{V}
has a shading
Y
and it is important to keep track of the shading. So we are looking for an inequality of the form
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)\lessapprox\mu(\mathbb{V}_{W},Y)\mu(\mathbb{W},Y_{\mathbb{W}})
. We will need to define
Y_{\mathbb{W}}
. In our applications, we are usually given a lower bound for
\lambda(\mathbb{V},Y)
, and for our product estimate to be useful, we will need to have a lower bound for
\lambda(\mathbb{W},Y_{\mathbb{W}})
. The main work in this section is to carefully choose
Y_{\mathbb{W}}
and prove a lower bound for
\lambda(\mathbb{W},Y_{\mathbb{W}})
.

One main result is the following.

Proposition 5.1

(Factoring and multiplicity)
.

Let
\mathbb{V}=\bigsqcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
, where
\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{W}
are families of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and each family
\mathbb{V}_{W}
is
C
-Frostman in
W
. Suppose that the sets in
\mathbb{V}
have approximately the same dimensions. Let
Y
be a shading on
\mathbb{V}
.

Then there is a refinement
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
and a subset
\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{W}
with a shading
Y_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}
so that the following holds. Define
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{W}=\mathbb{V}_{W}\cap\mathbb{V}^{\prime}
so that
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}=\sqcup_{W\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{W}
.

(1)

(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is a
\gtrapprox 1
refinement of
(\mathbb{V},Y)
.

(2)

\lambda(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}})\gtrapprox C^{-1}\lambda(\mathbb{V},Y)^{2}
.

(3)

(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}})
has constant multiplicity.

(4)

For each
W\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
,
(\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{W},Y^{\prime})
has constant multiplicity. This multiplicity is also the same for all
W\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
.

(5)

For all
W\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}
, we have

(14)

\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)\lessapprox\mu(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}})\mu(\mathbb{V}_{W},Y^{\prime}).

(6)

For every

,

(15)

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}_{Y^{\prime}}(x)\subset\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}W\in\mathbb{W}^{\prime}\\

(7)

|U(\mathbb{V}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cap B(x,w_{1})|

, up to a factor

.

.

is stated in

The shading

Before proving Proposition
5.1
, we first introduce some basic facts about shadings. The following lemma says that a mild refinement cannot substantially increase the multiplicity of an arrangement.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
(\mathbb{W},Y)
be a family of convex sets, and let
(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
be a
c
-refinement. Then

(16)

\mu(\mathbb{W},Y)\leq c^{-1}\mu(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime}).

In particular, if
(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is a
\gtrapprox 1
refinement of
(\mathbb{W},Y)
, then
\mu(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\lessapprox\mu(\mathbb{W},Y)
.

Proof.

We have
|U(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|\leq|U(\mathbb{W},Y)|
, and thus

\mu(\mathbb{W},Y)=\frac{\sum_{\mathbb{W}}|Y(W)|}{|U(\mathbb{W},Y)|}\leq c^{-1}\frac{\sum_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}|Y^{\prime}(W)|}{|U(\mathbb{W},Y)|}\leq c^{-1}\frac{\sum_{\mathbb{W}^{\prime}}|Y^{\prime}(W)|}{|U(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|}=c^{-1}\mu(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime}).\qed

We remark that if
(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is a
\gtrapprox 1
refinement of
Y
, then it can happen that
\mu(\mathbb{W}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\gg\mu(\mathbb{W},Y)
, but this is okay in our argument because the inequality goes in a favorable direction.

We say that
(\mathbb{V},Y)
is not empty if there is some

with

non-empty. We begin with a basic lemma about multiplicity.

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose that
(\mathbb{V},Y)
is not empty. If
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)\geq\mu_{lower}
for every

, then
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)\geq\mu_{lower}
. If
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)\leq\mu_{upper}
for every

, then
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)\leq\mu_{upper}
.

Proof.

For the lower bound, we have

\mu_{lower}|U(\mathbb{V},Y)|\leq\int_{U(\mathbb{V},Y)}\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)dx=\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}}|Y(V)|.

For the upper bound, we have

\sum_{V\in\mathbb{V}}|Y(V)|=\int_{U(\mathbb{V},Y)}\mu(\mathbb{V},Y)(x)dx\leq\mu_{upper}|U(\mathbb{V},Y)|.\qed

Proposition
5.1

\mathbb{W}^{\prime}

\mathbb{V}

Definition 5.7

Lemma 5.8
.

Let

Lemma 5.9

6.
Multiplicity estimates for slabs and planks

In Section
5
we reduced the problem of controlling
\mu(\mathbb{V},Y_{\mathbb{V}})
for an arbitrary collection of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
to the problem of controlling the multiplicity of two new families of convex sets, one of which is Katz-Tao and the other is Frostman. The estimates
K_{KT}(\beta)
and
K_{F}(\beta)
control the intersection multiplicity of sets of
tubes
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that are Frostman and Katz-Tao, respectively. In this section we will show that incidence bounds for arbitrary convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
can be systematically reduced to incidence bounds for tubes.

Katz-Tao multiplicity estimates.
Lemma
6.1
below says that the Kakeya estimate
K_{KT}(\beta)
implies an analogous estimate for planks of dimensions
a\times b\times 1
. Furthermore, a stronger estimate holds when
a<\!\!<b
, provided that the planks do not concentrate into slabs (i.e. sets of dimensions
\theta\times 1\times 1
for
\theta<\!\!<1
). To make this latter statement precise, we must define what it means for planks to concentrate into slabs.

If
\mathbb{P}
is a set of planks in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
of dimensions
a\times b\times 1
,
\theta\in[a/b,1]
, and
S
is a
\theta\times 1\times 1
slab, then we define

(28)

\mathbb{P}_{S}=\{P\in\mathbb{P}:P\subset S\textrm{ and }\angle(TP,TS)\leq\theta\}.

In the above,
\angle(TP,TS)
refers to the angle between the planes
TP,TS
, where
TP
is the plane spanned by the two longest axes of
P
, and similarly for
S
(this is defined up to accuracy
\theta
).

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose that
K_{KT}(\beta)
holds. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta,b_{0}>0
such that the following holds for any
0<a\leq b\leq b_{0}
. Let
\mathbb{P}
be a set of
a\times b\times 1
planks in
B_{1}
, and let
Y_{\mathbb{P}}
be a shading on
\mathbb{P}
with density
\lambda(\mathbb{P})\geq a^{\eta}
. Suppose that the planks do not concentrate into slabs, in the following sense: there exists
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
so that
for every
\theta\in[a/b,1]
and every
\theta\times 1\times 1
plank
S
,
|\mathbb{P}_{S}|\leq a^{-\eta}\theta^{\gamma}|\mathbb{P}|
.

If
\mathbb{P}
is Katz-Tao, then
\mu(\mathbb{P})\leq a^{-\epsilon}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{\gamma\beta}|\mathbb{P}|^{\beta}
.
More generally, we have

(29)

\mu(\mathbb{P})\leq a^{-\epsilon}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{P})^{1-\beta}\left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^{\gamma\beta}|\mathbb{P}|^{\beta}.

Remark 6.2
.

We will only apply Lemma
6.1
in the special cases
\gamma=0
(in which case the non-concentration condition is vacuous, and can be ignored), and
\gamma=1
. When
\mathbb{P}
is Katz-Tao and
\gamma=0
, the RHS of (
29
) is
\lessapprox|\mathbb{P}|^{\beta}
, which matches the estimate
K_{KT}(\beta)
for tubes.

Remark 6.3
.

The inequality automatically holds in the case when
\mu(\mathbb{P})\leq a^{-\eta}
. The right-hand side of (
29
) is always
\geq a^{-\eta}
, because the hypothesis implies that
|\mathbb{P}|\geq a^{\eta}(b/a)^{\gamma}
and
\eta
is much less than
\epsilon
.

Frostman multiplicity estimates.
Lemma
6.4
below says that the Kakeya estimate
K_{F}(\beta)
implies an analogous estimate for planks of dimensions
a\times b\times 1
, provided we avoid the following bad scenario: Let
\mathbb{P}
be a set of planks of dimensions
a\times b\times 1
, let
P\in\mathbb{P}
, let
\theta\in[a/b,1]
. We define the “thickened plank”
P_{\theta}=N_{\theta b}(P)
, i.e.
\tilde{P}
has dimensions roughly
\theta b\times b\times 1
. If
\tilde{P}
contains a very large number of planks from
\mathbb{P}
, then this can cause an unacceptably large intersection multiplicity
\mu(\mathbb{P})
. The next result says that this is the only obstruction to obtaining an analogue of
K_{F}(\beta)
for planks.

Lemma 6.4
.

Suppose that
K_{F}(\beta)
holds. Then for every
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\eta,b_{0}>0
such that the following holds for any
0<a\leq b\leq b_{0}
. Let
\mathbb{P}
be a set of
a\times b\times 1
planks in
B_{1}
, and let
Y
be a shading on
\mathbb{P}
with density
\lambda(Y)\geq a^{\eta}
. Suppose that the planks do not concentrate into thickened planks, in the following sense: there is a number
M\geq 1
so that for each
\theta\in[a/b,1]
and every
P\in\mathbb{P},
we have
|\mathbb{P}[P_{\theta}]|\leq M\theta.

Then

(30)

|U(\mathbb{P},Y)|\geq a^{\epsilon}C_{F}(\mathbb{P})^{\beta/2-1}b^{2\beta}\big(M^{-1}b^{2}|\mathbb{P}|\big)^{\beta/2},

and

(31)

\mu(\mathbb{P})\leq a^{-\epsilon}C_{F}(\mathbb{P})^{1-\beta/2}M^{\beta/2}\frac{a}{b}b^{-2\beta}\left(b^{2}|\mathbb{P}|\right)^{1-\beta/2}.

Remark 6.5
.

Both inequalities automatically hold in the case when
\mu(\mathbb{P})\leq a^{-\eta}
. To see this, note that
M\geq b/a
(by taking
\theta=a/b
) and that
C_{F}(\mathbb{P})\gtrsim\frac{1}{|\mathbb{P}|ab}
.

Tubes that factor through planks.

6.1.

Y
. Define the set of incidence triples

Tri(\mathbb{S})=\{(x,S_{1},S_{2}):x\in Y(S_{1})\cap Y(S_{2})\}.

There is a natural measure on
Tri(\mathbb{S})
coming from the volume measure for
x
and the counting measure for
S_{1},S_{2}
. We can define the angle between two slabs
S_{1},S_{2}
, which is well defined up to additive error
\delta
. Then we define

Tri_{\theta}(\mathbb{S})=\{(x,S_{1},S_{2})\in Tri(\mathbb{S}):\textrm{angle}(S_{1},S_{2})\sim\theta\}.

Definition 6.7
.

We say that
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for
\mathbb{S}
if
|Tri_{\theta}(\mathbb{S})|\approx|Tri(\mathbb{S})|
up to
\log(1/\delta)
factors. If
\mu(\mathbb{S})\geq 2
, then
\mathbb{S}
always has a typical intersection angle
\theta\in[\delta,1]
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Suppose
\mathbb{S}
is a set of
\delta\times 1\times 1
slabs contained in
\tilde{S}
, a
\theta\times 1\times 1
slab, with
\delta\leq\theta\leq 1
. Suppose the slabs of
\mathbb{S}
are shaded with
\lambda(\mathbb{S},Y)\geq\delta^{\eta}
. If
\theta
is a typical intersection angle for
\mathbb{S}
, then
|U(\mathbb{S},Y)|\gtrsim\delta^{2\eta}|\tilde{S}|
.

Proof.

We let
f=\sum_{S\in\mathbb{S}}1_{Y(S)}
. Note that

\int f^{2}=|Tri(\mathbb{S})|=\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathbb{S}}|Y(S_{1})\cap Y(S_{2})|.

By hypothesis, we have

\int f^{2}=|Tri(\mathbb{S})|\approx\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}\in\mathbb{S},\textrm{angle}(S_{1},S_{2})\sim\theta}|Y(S_{1})\cap Y(S_{2})|\leq|\mathbb{S}|^{2}\frac{\delta^{2}}{\theta}.

On the other hand, we know that
\int f\gtrsim|\mathbb{S}||S|\delta^{\eta}=|\mathbb{S}|\delta^{1+\eta}
. By Cauchy-Schwarz, since
f
is supported in
U(\mathbb{S})
,

\int f^{2}\geq\frac{(\int f)^{2}}{|U(\mathbb{S})|}\approx\frac{|\mathbb{S}|^{2}\delta^{2+2\eta}}{|U(\mathbb{S})|}.

Comparing, we get

|U(\mathbb{S})|\gtrapprox\theta\delta^{2\eta}=|\tilde{S}|\delta^{2\eta}.\qed

This
L^{2}
bound leads to sharp estimates for the analogue of Theorem
1.1
where tubes are replaced by slabs that have thickness
\delta
and length and width close to 1.

Lemma 6.9
.

Suppose
\mathbb{S}
is a set of
\delta\times b\times c
slabs in
B_{1}
shaded with
\lambda(\mathbb{S})\geq\delta^{\eta}
. If
b,c\geq\delta^{\eta}
and
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{S})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, then
\mu(S)\lesssim\delta^{-8\eta}.
If
C_{F}(\mathbb{S})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, then
|U(\mathbb{S})|\geq\delta^{8\eta}.

We will not use this lemma in the sequel, and so we leave the proof to the reader.

6.2.
Planks

Next we suppose that
\mathbb{P}
is a set of
a\times b\times 1
planks in
B_{1}
. We will study
\mu(\mathbb{P})
by relating it to incidence problems involving slabs and tubes. In a sense, an incidence problem for planks can be broken down into an incidence problem for slabs and an incidence problem for tubes. The incidence problem for slabs is solved completely using Lemma
6.8
. Assuming
K_{KT}(\beta)
or
K_{F}(\beta)
we get information about the incidence problem for tubes. In this way, we are able to prove estimates for
\mu(\mathbb{P})
based on various geometric assumptions about
\mathbb{P}
.

Let us describe this process informally first and then state a formal lemma. Given two intersecting planks,
P_{1},P_{2}
, define the angle of intersection as the angle between the planes
TP_{1},TP_{2}
, where
TP
is the plane spanned by the two longest axes of
P
. If the planks
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
have dimensions
a\times b\times 1
, then this angle is meaningfully defined up to accuracy
a/b
. Let
\theta
denote a “typical” angle of intersection for
\mathbb{P}
.

First we note that if the angle between
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
is
\gg\theta
, then their intersection will not play an important role. Therefore, we can subdivide
\mathbb{P}
into smaller collections of planks as follows. If
S
is a
\theta\times 1\times 1
slab in
B_{1}
, we define
\mathbb{P}_{S}
as in (
28
). We can now decompose
\mathbb{P}
as
\mathbb{P}=\sqcup_{S}\mathbb{P}_{S}
, where
S
varies over some essentially distinct slabs
S
. If
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection of
(\mathbb{P},Y)
, then morally the sets
U(\mathbb{P}_{S},Y)
are disjoint and we can study them separately. Fix a single
S
and we study
\mathbb{P}_{S}
.

Next let
Q
denote a
\theta b\times b\times b
box, and let
\mathbb{P}_{Q}
be the set of planks
P\in\mathbb{P}_{S}
so that
P\cap Q
has dimensions
a\times b\times b
. In other words,
\mathbb{P}_{Q}
are the planks that intersect
Q
“tangentially”, making the intersection as large as possible. Assuming that
\mu(\mathbb{P})\gg 1
and assuming that
\mathbb{P}_{Q}
is not empty, then morally Lemma
6.8
tells us that
|U(\mathbb{P}_{S},Y)\cap Q|\approx|Q|
. For each plank
P\in\mathbb{P}_{S}
, the
\theta b
-neighborhood of
P
can be covered by boxes
Q
as above, and so we see that morally most of this neighborhood is contained in
U(\mathbb{P}_{S},Y)
. This motivates defining thickened version of
\mathbb{P}_{S}
as follows.

Definition 6.10
.

For each
P\in\mathbb{P}
, define
P_{\theta}
to be the
\theta b
-neighborhood of
P
. Define
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
to be a set of essentially distinct prisms of the form
P_{\theta}
, so that each
P^{\prime}\in\mathbb{P}
is contained in a set
P_{\theta}\in\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
for which
P^{\prime}_{\theta}
is comparable to
P_{\theta}
. Note that if
P_{\theta}
and
P^{\prime}_{\theta}
are comparable, then they are contained in comparable slabs
S
and
S^{\prime}
. Thus
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
decomposes into a boundedly overlapping union
\bigcup_{S}\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S}
, where the union is taken over incomparable slabs of dimensions
\theta\times 1\times 1
.

In the previous paragraph, we saw that if
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for
\mathbb{P}
, then morally
U(\mathbb{P}_{S})
is almost the same as
U(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})
.

Next we have to study
U(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})
. There is a change of variables that takes
S
to the unit ball and takes each
P_{\theta}\in\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S}
to a tube of radius
b
. Therefore, the problem of estimating
|U(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})|
is equivalent to a problem about tubes which we can bound using
K_{KT}(\beta)
and/or
K_{F}(\beta)
.

The following two lemmas gives a precise version of the moral statements above. First we carefully define the typical angle of intersection.

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
(\mathbb{P},Y)
be a family of
a\times b\times 1
tubes and their associated shading. Let
\eta>0
and suppose that
\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\geq\delta^{-\eta}
. Then there is an
\approx 1
refinement
(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
of
(\mathbb{P},Y)
and a number
\theta\in[a/b,1]
with the following properties. In what follows, let
A=e^{\sqrt{\log a^{-1}}}

•

(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
has constant multiplicity on
U(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
up to a factor
\sim 1
.

•

\max_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime}}(x)}\angle(TP_{1},TP_{2})\sim\theta

•

If
\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)\subset\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime}}(x)
with
|\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)|\geq\frac{1}{A}|\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime}}(x)|
, then

\max_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)}\angle(TP_{1},TP_{2})\approx\theta.

Proof.

First we replace
(\mathbb{P},Y)
with an
\approx 1
refinement
(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
that has constant multiplicity. Next, fix a point
x\in U(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
.

Let
B=e^{(\log a^{-1})^{3/4}}
. The point here is just that
A^{N}\ll B
and
B^{N}\ll a^{-1}
for any real number
N
.

For a subset
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)
, we define the maximal angle of
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}
as

M(\mathbb{P}^{\prime})=\max_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}}\angle(TP_{1},TP_{2}).

We first check if there is a subset
\mathbb{P}_{1}\subset\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)
so that
|\mathbb{P}_{1}|\geq\frac{1}{A}|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|
and with
M(\mathbb{P}_{1})\leq\frac{1}{B}
.
If not, we set
\mathbb{P}_{Y_{almost}}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)
and
\theta(x)=1
.

If there is such a
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, we select such a set
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, and we check if there is a subset
\mathbb{P}_{2}\subset\mathbb{P}_{1}
so that
|\mathbb{P}_{2}|\geq\frac{1}{A}|\mathbb{P}_{1}|
and
M(\mathbb{P}_{2})\leq(1/B)M(\mathbb{P}_{1})
.
If not, we output
\mathbb{P}_{Y_{almost}}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{1}
and
\theta(x)=M(\mathbb{P}_{1})
.

Since
|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|\approx\mu(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})\gtrapprox\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\gtrapprox a^{-\eta}
and since
M(\mathbb{P}_{k})\geq a/b\geq a
for every
k
, this process must stop eventually at some
\mathbb{P}_{k}
with
k\lesssim\log_{B}a^{-1}
and
|\mathbb{P}_{k}|\geq A^{-k}|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|
.
Then we output
\mathbb{P}_{Y_{almost}}(x)=\mathbb{P}_{k}
. Since we have
|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{almost}}(x)|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|
for each
x\in U(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
, we see that
Y_{almost}
is a
\gtrapprox 1
refinement of
Y_{1}
.

At this moment, we have
\theta(x)
which may be different at different points
x
. We let
Y^{\prime}
be a
\gtrsim\frac{1}{\log a^{-1}}
refinement of
Y_{almost}
so that
\theta(x)
is roughly constant on
U(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
(up to a factor
\sim 1
) and so that
|\mathbb{P}_{Y}(x)|
is roughly constant on
U(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
(up to a factor
\sim 1
).
∎

Definition 6.12
.

We say that
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for

(\mathbb{P},Y)
if
(\mathbb{P},Y)
satisfies the conclusions of Lemma
6.11
for some
A\geq e^{(\log(a^{-1})^{1/4}}
.

In particular, if
(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
and
\theta
are the output of Lemma
6.11
, then
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for
(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
. We made the definition of “typical angle of intersection” a little bit looser than the conclusion of Lemma
6.11
so that some mild refinements of
(\mathbb{P},Y^{\prime})
will still have typical angle of intersection
\theta
.
This definition of typical angle of intersection for planks is stronger than the definition that we used for typical intersection angle of slabs, Definition
6.7
. In this definition for planks, at every point
x\in U(\mathbb{P},Y)
, the intersecting planks have angle at most
\theta
and there are many pairs of planks with angle
\sim\theta
. Furthermore, this remains true if the shading
Y
is replaced by a mild refinement.

Lemma 6.13
.

Let
\eta>0
and let
(\mathbb{P},Y)
be a set of
a\times b\times 1
planks in the unit ball with
\lambda(\mathbb{P},Y)\geq a^{\eta}
and
\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\geq a^{-\eta}
.

Then there is a refinement
(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
of
(\mathbb{P},Y)
with
|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}|\gtrapprox|\mathbb{P}|
and
\lambda(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\gtrapprox\lambda(\mathbb{P},Y)
, a number
N\geq 1
, a number
\theta\in[a/b,1]
, and a set of
\theta\times 1\times 1
slabs
\mathbb{S}
so that the following holds.
For each
S\in\mathbb{S}
, let
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}
be as defined in (
28
) and Definition
6.10
.
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{S\in\mathbb{S}}\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}
. Then each
P_{\theta}\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}
contains
\sim N
planks
P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}
. Furthermore, there is a shading
Y_{\theta,S}
on
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}
so that

(1)

If

|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cap B_{\theta b}|\gtrapprox a^{4\eta}|B_{\theta b}|

\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\lessapprox\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}}a^{-4\eta}\frac{aN}{b\theta}\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S}).

Remark 6.14
.

If
(\mathbb{P},Y)
has a typical angle of intersection
\theta
, then Lemma
6.13
holds for this value of
\theta
.

Proof.

We begin with a pair
(\mathbb{P},Y)
with
\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\geq a^{-\eta}
. Apply Lemma
6.11
to
(\mathbb{P},Y)
. This yields a refinement
(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
and a typical angle of intersection

obeying the conclusions of Lemma
6.11
:

•

Our set
(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
has roughly constant multiplicity: for each

,
|\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)|\sim m
.

•

For each
x\in U(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})
,
\max_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)}\angle(TP_{1},TP_{2})\sim\theta

•

If
\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)\subset\mathbb{P}_{Y_{1}}(x)
with
|\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)|\geq e^{-\sqrt{\log(a^{-1})}}m
, then

(34)

\max_{P_{1},P_{2}\in\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime\prime}}(x)}\angle(TP_{1},TP_{2})\approx\theta.

As per Remark
6.14
, if
(\mathbb{P},Y)
already has a typical angle of intersection, then we can skip this step.

In the rest of the proof, we will continue to refine
\mathbb{P}
and
Y_{1}
. We will consider various
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}\subset\mathbb{P}
and
\tilde{Y}
refining

. For each refinement

that we consider, we will check that

(35)

\sum_{P\in\tilde{\mathbb{P}}}|\tilde{Y}(P)|\geq(\log 1/a)^{-O(1)}\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}}|Y(P)|

We will call a refinement good if it obeys (
35
). If
\tilde{Y}
is a good refinement, we will further refine

to have roughly constant multiplicity:

(36)

\textrm{ For each }x\in U(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\tilde{Y}),|\mathbb{P}_{\tilde{Y}}(x)|\gtrsim(\log 1/a)^{-O(1)}m.

Combining (
36
) and (

), we see that
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for
(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\tilde{Y})
.
Equation (

) also implies that each shading enjoys
\lambda(\tilde{\mathbb{P}},\tilde{Y})\geq(\log 1/a)^{-O(1)}\lambda(\mathbb{P},Y_{1})\geq a^{\eta}
and
|\tilde{\mathbb{P}}|\geq(\log 1/a)^{-O(1)}|\mathbb{P}|
.

Informally, we find good refinements either by pigeonholing or by removing parts of our shaded set that are far below average in some respect. Now we turn to the details.

By pigeonholing, we may find a good refinement
(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{2})
and a number
N\geq 1
so that each set
P_{\theta}
contains about
N
prisms
P\in\mathbb{P}_{2}
.
Next we consider slabs
S\subset B_{1}
with dimensions
\theta\times 1\times 1
. For a given
\theta\times 1\times 1
slab
S
, define
(\mathbb{P}_{2})_{S}
as in (
28
). We note that
\mathbb{P}_{2}=\sqcup_{S}\mathbb{P}_{2,S},
where the union is over all essentially distinct
\theta\times 1\times 1
slabs
S\subset B_{1}
.

Because the maximal angle of intersecting planks in
(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{2})
is
\lesssim\theta
, each point
x\in U(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{2})
lies in
U(\mathbb{P}_{2,S},Y_{2,S})
for
\lesssim 1
sets

. We know that
|\mathbb{P}_{2,Y_{2}}(x)|\geq(\log 1/a)^{O(1)}m
and so there must be some

so that

. Now we can find a good refinement
(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{3})
so that for each

.

Let
\mathbb{S}
be the set of
S

\lambda(\mathbb{P}_{2,S},Y_{3,S})\gtrapprox\lambda(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{3})\gtrapprox a^{\eta}
. Now define
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}=\sqcup_{S\in\mathbb{S}}\mathbb{P}_{2,S}
and
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}=\mathbb{P}_{2,S}
. Note that
(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y_{3})
is a good refinement of
(\mathbb{P}_{2},Y_{3})
.

Now consider a box
Q
of dimensions
\theta b\times b\times b
. Define

\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}=\{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}:|P\cap Q|\approx ab^{2}\}.

If
P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}
, then

is a slab of dimensions

. We want to use our estimates about slabs to study these slabs inside of

\mathbb{S}_{Q}=\{P\cap Q:P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}\}
. (Note that the slabs in
\mathbb{S}_{Q}

The slabs of
\mathbb{S}_{Q}
come with a natural shading

, where
Y_{3,Q}(P\cap Q)=Y_{3}(P)\cap Q
.
We will apply Lemma
6.8
to estimate

. We know that
\theta
is a typical angle of intersection for
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Y_{3}}
in the sense of Definition
6.12
, and this implies that

is also a typical angle of intersection for

in the sense of Definition
6.7
. To apply our Lemma
6.8

\lambda(Y_{3,Q})\gtrapprox\lambda(Y_{3,S})\gtrapprox a^{\eta}
. If
Q

6.8
gives the key inequality:

(37)

|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y_{3,S})\cap Q|\geq|U(\mathbb{S}_{Q},Y_{3,Q})|\gtrapprox a^{3\eta}|Q|.

Notice that there is an essentially unique slab
S
of dimensions
\theta\times 1\times 1
so that
Q\subset S
and
\angle(TQ,TS)\lesssim\theta
. For this slab
S
,
\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}\subset\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}
. Moreover, we can tile
S
with a set
\mathbb{Q}_{S}
of boxes

P=\sqcup_{Q\in\mathbb{Q}_{S}}P\cap Q,

where
P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}

is non-empty.

Note that a plank
P
lies in
\mathbb{P}_{Q}
for many boxes

, and these

. Let
\mathbb{Q}_{P}=\{Q:P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}\}

Y_{4}(P)=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathbb{Q}_{P},Q\textrm{ dense}}Y_{3}(P)\cap Q.

Next we show that
Y_{4,S}
is a good refinement of

for each

. To see this, we note that

\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}}|Y_{3,S}(P)|=\lambda(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y_{3,S})\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}}|P|.

On the other hand,

\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}}|Y_{3,S}(P)\setminus Y_{4,S}(P)|=\sum_{Q\in\mathbb{Q}_{S},Q\textrm{ not dense}}\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{Q}}|Y_{3,S}(P)\cap Q|\leq

B_{\theta b}\subset Q
, we say

. We let

. Since the multiplicity of

(\mathbb{P},Y)
.

The definition of

in terms of dense balls gives item 1.

And now for each plank

, define

Y_{\theta,S}(P_{\theta})=U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime})\cap P_{\theta}.

With this definition, it follows immediately that

.

P\subset P_{\theta,S}
, then

Recall that for each

, there are
\sim N
planks

with

. Therefore, we have

(39)

\lambda(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})\gtrapprox a^{3\eta}\lambda(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime})\gtrapprox a^{4\eta}

This proves item 2.

We also have

(40)

|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y)|\geq|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y^{\prime})|\gtrsim\sum_{S\in\mathbb{S}}|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})|=\sum_{S\in\mathbb{S}}|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})|.

This proves item 3.

We can also use this setup to control

. Notice that since each point

lies in

for
\lesssim 1
slabs

\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime},Y)\lessapprox\max_{S\in\mathbb{S}}\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S}).

Next we estimate

using that
U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})=U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})
. We know that

\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})=\frac{\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}}|Y^{\prime}(P)|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})|}\lessapprox\frac{\sum_{P\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}}|P|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y_{S}^{\prime})|}=\frac{|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}||P|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})|}=\frac{|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}||P|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})|},

and

\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})=\frac{\sum_{P_{\theta}\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}}|Y_{\theta,S}(P_{\theta})|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})|}\gtrapprox a^{4\eta}\frac{\sum_{P_{\theta}\in\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}}|P_{\theta}|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})|}=a^{4\eta}\frac{|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}||P_{\theta}|}{|U(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})|}.

We have

|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S}|\approx\frac{1}{N}|\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S}|,

|P_{\theta}|=\frac{\theta b}{a}|P|.

Plugging in gives

\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\lessapprox\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{S},Y^{\prime}_{S})\lessapprox a^{-4\eta}\frac{aN}{b\theta}\mu(\mathbb{P}^{\prime}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta}).

This proves item 4.
∎

By a linear change of variables, we can convert
\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S}
from a set of

of

-tubes in

To summarize, we have reduced the original incidence problem for

6.3.
A Katz-Tao multiplicity estimate

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

We may suppose that
\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\geq a^{-\eta}
, since otherwise we are done by Remark
6.3
. Apply Lemma
6.13
. Abusing notation, we will continue to refer to the resulting refinement by
(\mathbb{P},Y)
. By Lemma
6.13
, there is a number
\theta\in[a/b,1]
, a number

\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)\lessapprox a^{-3\eta}\frac{aN}{b\theta}\mu(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S}).

Lemma

also gives a density lower bound

\lambda(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S},Y_{\theta,S})\gtrapprox a^{4\eta}.

By a linear change of variables, we can convert

to a set of
b
-tubes

in

Now apply Lemma
3.7
(noting Remark

in place of

and

are chosen sufficiently small, then

\displaystyle\mu(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})

\displaystyle=\mu(\mathbb{T})\leq a^{-\epsilon/2}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})^{1-\beta}|\mathbb{T}|^{\beta}

\displaystyle\lessapprox a^{-\epsilon/2}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{P})^{1-\beta}\left(\frac{b\theta}{aN}\right)^{1-\beta}(|\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S}|)^{\beta}

\displaystyle\lesssim a^{-\epsilon/2}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{P})^{1-\beta}\left(\frac{b\theta}{aN}\right)^{1-\beta}(N^{-1}|\mathbb{P}_{S}|)^{\beta}.

And so plugging in (

(42)

\begin{split}\mu(\mathbb{P},Y)&\lessapprox a^{-3\eta}\frac{aN}{b\theta}\mu(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})\\

&\leq a^{-\epsilon/2-3\eta}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{P})^{1-\beta}\left(\frac{a}{b\theta}\right)^{\beta}|\mathbb{P}_{S}|^{\beta}.\end{split}

We know

6.4.

Proof of Lemma

If

and

are chosen sufficiently small depending on

(noting Remark

\displaystyle|U(\mathbb{T})|

\displaystyle\gtrapprox a^{\epsilon/2}(C|\mathbb{S}||S|)^{\beta/2-1}b^{2\beta}(b^{2}|\mathbb{T}|)^{\beta/2}

\displaystyle\approx a^{\epsilon/2}C^{\beta/2-1}(|\mathbb{S}||S|)^{-1}b^{2\beta}(b^{2}|S||\mathbb{P}_{\theta}|)^{\beta/2}

\displaystyle=a^{\epsilon/2}C^{\beta/2-1}(|\mathbb{S}||S|)^{-1}b^{2\beta}(b^{2}\theta|\mathbb{P}|/N)^{\beta/2}

Therefore if

\displaystyle\approx|\mathbb{S}||U(\mathbb{P}_{\theta,S})|\approx|\mathbb{S}|\ |S|\ |U(\mathbb{T})|\geq a^{\epsilon}C^{\beta/2-1}b^{2\beta}(M^{-1}b^{2}|\mathbb{P}|)^{\beta/2}.

This gives the desired lower bound for

. To get an upper bound for

∎

Definition 7.1

Lemma 7.4

Let

\delta

\rho_{k}

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\leq C(n)^{M}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}_{\rho_{m}}[T_{\rho_{m-1}}]).

Proof.

Suppose that

is a convex set. We have to estimate
\Delta(\mathbb{T},K)=\frac{|T||\mathbb{T}[K]|}{|K|}
.

Let
K_{m}
denote the

be the corresponding thickened tube in

T_{\rho_{2}}\subset T_{\rho_{1}}\cap K_{2}
and in general

(50)

|\mathbb{T}[K]|\lessapprox\prod_{m=1}^{M}|\mathbb{T}_{\rho_{m}}[T_{\rho_{m-1}}\cap K_{m}]|\leq C(n)^{M}\prod_{m=1}^{M}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}_{\rho_{m}}[T_{\rho_{m-1}}])\frac{|K_{m}\cap T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}{|T_{\rho_{m}}|}.

Now

\frac{|K_{m}\cap T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}{|T_{\rho_{m}}|}=\frac{|K_{m}\cap T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}{|T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}\cdot\frac{|T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}{|T_{\rho_{m}}|}=\frac{|K_{m}|}{|K_{m-1}|}\cdot\frac{|T_{\rho_{m-1}}|}{|T_{\rho_{m}}|}.

Plugging this into (

7.1.
A multi-scale decomposition of
\mathbb{T}

In order to apply Theorem
7.3
(A) or Theorem

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
N\geq 1
be an integer. Define
\epsilon=1/\sqrt{N}
, and let
\eta\leq\eta_{1}\leq\eta_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\eta_{N}\leq\epsilon

be a uniform set of
\delta

(A) If

(i)

\mathbb{T}
is
\lessapprox\delta^{-5\epsilon}
Frostman at every scale.

(ii)

with
\tau\leq\delta^{\epsilon}\theta
, and an integer

so that the following holds:

•

•

, we have
C_{F}(\mathbb{T}_{\tau}[T_{\rho}])\geq(\rho/\tau)^{\eta_{j}}
for all

(B) If
\Delta_{\max}(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, then at least one of the following holds.

(i)

\mathbb{T}
is
\lessapprox\delta^{-\epsilon}
Katz-Tao at every scale.

(ii)

, and an integer

.

Proof.

We begin with part (A). To begin, we check if there is a scale

\mathbb{T}_{1}
to be

(1)

For each

is a set of

B_{1}
.

(2)

\prod_{j=1}^{J}\delta_{j}\approx\delta
.

(3)

\prod_{j=1}^{J}|\mathbb{T}_{j}|\approx|\mathbb{T}|
.

(4)

Suppose that for some
J

\mathbb{T}_{j}

•

and

•

\rho\in[\delta_{j}^{1-\epsilon},\delta_{j}^{\epsilon}]

Main Lemma 1

In this section, we sketch the proof of Main Lemma
1
. We first recall the statement.

Main Lemma
.

K_{KT}(\beta)
implies
K_{F}(\beta)
.

Let’s first digest what the lemma is saying. Suppose that
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}
with
C_{F}(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
. We have to prove the bound

(57)

\mu(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\epsilon}(\delta^{-2})^{\beta}\left(\delta^{2}|\mathbb{T}|\right)^{1-\beta/2}.

Since
C_{F}(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
, we have
|\mathbb{T}|\geq\delta^{-2+\eta}
. If
|\mathbb{T}|\approx\delta^{-2}
, then
C_{F}(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\leq\delta^{-\eta}
is equivalent to
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\lessapprox\delta^{-\eta}
, and then
K_{KT}(\beta)
gives
\mu(\mathbb{T})\leq\delta^{-\epsilon}\delta^{-2\beta}
, which is the desired bound. So the case
|\mathbb{T}|\approx\delta^{-2}
is trivial. So the content of the lemma is in the case when
|\mathbb{T}|\gg\delta^{-2}
.

If
|\mathbb{T}|\gg\delta^{-2}
, then
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\gg 1
, so we can not immediately apply
K_{KT}(\beta)
to
\mathbb{T}
. If
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}\subset\mathbb{T}
is a random subset with
|\tilde{\mathbb{T}}|\sim\delta^{-2}
, then it’s not hard to check that
\Delta_{max}(\tilde{\mathbb{T}})\lessapprox 1
. We can apply
K_{KT}(\beta)
to
\tilde{\mathbb{T}}
, and we get
\mu(\tilde{\mathbb{T}})\lessapprox\delta^{-2\beta}
which is equivalent to
|U(\tilde{\mathbb{T}})|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\beta}
. We have
|U(\mathbb{T})|\geq|U(\tilde{\mathbb{T}})|
, and this translates into

(58)

\mu(\mathbb{T})\lessapprox(\delta^{-2})^{\beta}\left(\delta^{2}|\mathbb{T}|\right)

Comparing (
58
) with our goal (
57
), we see that we need to reduce the exponent on the last factor from 1 to
1-\beta/2
. In the proof of Kakeya, any exponent strictly less than 1 will work. We can also describe the lemma in terms of
|U(\mathbb{T})|
, which may be more intuitive. Since we assume
K_{KT}(\beta)
, we already know that if
C_{F}(\mathbb{T})\lessapprox 1
and
|\mathbb{T}|\sim\delta^{-2}
, then
|U(\mathbb{T})|\gtrapprox\delta^{2\beta}
. Now we need to show that if
C_{F}(\mathbb{T})\lessapprox 1
and if
|\mathbb{T}|
is far larger than
\delta^{-2}
, then
|U(\mathbb{T})|
is a little larger than
\delta^{2\beta}
. While this may sound intuitive, it is not at all trivial to prove, and the proof will depend crucially on sticky Kakeya.

We organize the proof as a bootstrapping or self-improving argument.

Lemma 8.1
.

Suppose that
K_{KT}(\beta)
holds and that
K_{F}(\gamma)
holds for some
\gamma>\beta
. Then
K_{F}(\gamma-\nu)
holds for
\nu=\nu(\gamma,\beta)>0
. For
\beta
fixed,
\nu=\nu(\gamma,\beta)>0

9.2.
Maximal density factoring revisited

In Lemma
4.1
, we chose sets
W
to maximize
\Delta(\mathbb{V},W)
. For some families
\mathbb{V}
of convex sets (for example if
\mathbb{V}
is both Katz-Tao and Frostman), there might be many equally valid candidates for
W
. To prove Lemma
9.1
, it is important to choose carefully in the case of a tie or a near tie. When the maximum density is achieved by two convex sets with very different sizes, we choose
W
to be the convex set of smaller volume. More precisely, we adjust our maximization process as follows. We let
\eta_{\textrm{bias}}>0
be a tiny exponent to be chosen later. Then, instead of choosing
W
to maximize
\Delta(\mathbb{V},W)
, we choose
W
to maximize

|W|^{-\eta_{\textrm{bias}}}\Delta(\mathbb{V},W).

This leads to a variation of Lemma
4.1
with two small changes:

Lemma 9.2
.

(Biased maximal density factoring lemma) Let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite family of convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
contained in a convex set
U
, and suppose
\eta_{\textrm{bias}}>0
. Then there is a set
\mathbb{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{V}
with
\sum_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}|V|\gtrapprox\sum_{\mathbb{V}}|V|
; a family
\mathbb{W}
of convex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each of approximately the same dimensions; and a partition

(81)

\mathbb{V}^{\prime}=\bigsqcup_{W\in\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W},

where each set in
\mathbb{V}_{W}
is contained in
W
. This partition has the following properties:

If
K\subset W\in\mathbb{W}
,

(82)

\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},K)\lessapprox\left(\frac{|K|}{|W|}\right)^{\eta_{\textrm{bias}}}\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W).

In particular,
C_{F}(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\lesssim 1
.

For each
W\in\mathbb{W}
,
\Delta(\mathbb{V}_{W},W)\gtrsim\left(\frac{|W|}{|U|}\right)^{\eta_{\textrm{bias}}}\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{V}^{\prime})
.

(83)

\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{W})\lesssim\left(\frac{|W|}{|U|}\right)^{-\eta_{\textrm{bias}}}.

The proof of Lemma
9.2
is the same as Lemma
4.1
, so we omit the details.

We will call the decomposition
\mathbb{V}=\bigsqcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
a
maximal density factoring with bias
\eta_{\textrm{bias}}
. Observe that we can still apply Proposition
5.1
to the factoring
\bigsqcup_{\mathbb{W}}\mathbb{V}_{W}
—Proposition
5.1
requires that
\mathbb{V}_{W}
be
C
-Frostman in
W
, and this remains the case with
C\sim 1
.

Appendix A
Appendix: Probability lemmas

In this appendix, we give probability arguments to prove Lemma
3.8
and Lemma
7.6
. The proofs are based on a simple probability lemma which is a small variation on Chernoff’s inequality.

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose that
X_{1},...,X_{N}
are i.i.d. random variables. Suppose that
0\leq X_{1}\leq M
almost surely and that
m=\mathbb{E}(X_{1})
. If
Nm\geq M
, then for every
S
,

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq e^{10}e^{-\frac{S}{Nm}}.

If
Nm<M
, then for every
S
,

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq e^{10}e^{-\frac{S}{M}}.

Proof.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
be a parameter to choose later. For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, using the Markov inequality and independence, we see that

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq e^{-\lambda S}\mathbb{E}[e^{(X_{1}+...X_{N})\lambda}]=e^{-\lambda S}\mathbb{E}(\prod_{n=1}^{N}e^{\lambda X_{n}})=e^{-\lambda S}\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda X_{1}})^{N}.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, the function
x\mapsto e^{\lambda x}
is convex, and so given our distributional information about
X_{1}
we can bound

\mathbb{E}(e^{\lambda X_{1}})\leq\frac{m}{M}e^{\lambda M}+1-\frac{m}{M}.

Plugging in, we get

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq e^{-\lambda S}\left(\frac{m}{M}e^{\lambda M}+1-\frac{m}{M}\right)^{N}.

We first consider the regime
Nm\geq M
. In this regime, we choose
\lambda=\frac{1}{Nm}
. Since
Nm\geq M
, we have
0\leq\frac{M}{Nm}\leq 1
, and so
e^{\lambda M}=e^{\frac{M}{Nm}}\leq 1+10\frac{M}{Nm}
. Plugging in and simplifying, we get

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq e^{-\frac{S}{Nm}}\left(1+\frac{10}{N}\right)^{N}\leq e^{10}e^{-\frac{S}{Nm}}.

This gives the conclusion in the regime
Nm\geq M
. On the other hand, if
Nm<M
, we define
N^{\prime}
so that
N^{\prime}m=M
. Since
X_{n}\geq 0
almost surely, we can bound

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N}>S]\leq\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{N^{\prime}}>S]\leq e^{10}e^{-\frac{S}{N^{\prime}m}}=e^{10}e^{-\frac{S}{M}}.\qed

Now we consider random translations and rigid motions of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
R_{v}(x)=x+v
.
A random translation of size
\leq\rho
is a translation
R_{v}
by a vector
v\in B_{\rho}
selected randomly from the uniform measure on
B_{\rho}
. A random rigid motion is a random rotation composed with a random translation of size
\leq 1
.

Now we restate and prove Lemma
3.8
:

Lemma
3.8
.

Suppose that
\mathbb{T}
is a set of (essentially distinct)
\delta
-tubes in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
R_{1},...,R_{J}
be random rigid motions of size 1, with
J=C_{F}(\mathbb{T})
, and let
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}R_{j}(\mathbb{T})
. Then with high probability the tubes of
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
are essentially distinct up to multiplicity
\lessapprox 1
and also
C_{F}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lessapprox 1
.
After refining
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
to make the tubes essentially distinct, we have
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}|\approx C_{F}(\mathbb{T})|\mathbb{T}|
and
C_{F}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lessapprox 1
.

Proof.

By assumption, the tubes of
\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct. It could happen that two different tubes of
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
are not essentially distinct. However, we will show that with high probability, this effect is very mild. We will show that with high probability, for any
\delta
-tube
T_{0}
, there are
\lessapprox 1
tubes of
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
contained in
100T_{0}
.

We define
X_{j}=|\mathbb{R}_{j}(\mathbb{T})[100T_{0}]|
, which we think of as a random variable. Note that
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}
.

Since the tubes of
\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct, we have
X_{j}=|\mathbb{R}_{j}(\mathbb{T})[100T_{0}]|=|\mathbb{T}[R_{j}^{-1}(100T_{0})]|\lesssim 1
. We choose
M\lesssim 1
and note that
0\leq X_{j}\leq M
almost surely.

Next we want to estimate
\mathbb{E}(X_{j})
. By linearity of expectation,
\mathbb{E}(X_{j})=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{P}\left[R_{j}(T)\subset 100T_{0}\right]
.
Since
R_{j}
is a random rigid motion, we note that for each
T\in\mathbb{T}

(106)

\mathbb{P}\left[R_{j}(T)\subset 100T_{0}\right]\lesssim|T_{\delta}|^{2}.

Therefore,

m=\mathbb{E}(X_{j})\lesssim|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|^{2}.

We claim that
Jm\lesssim 1=M
. To see this, we note that
Jm\lesssim C_{F}(\mathbb{T})|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|^{2}
. Now suppose that
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})=\Delta(\mathbb{T},K)
for some convex set

C_{F}(\mathbb{T})=\frac{\Delta(\mathbb{T},K)}{\Delta(\mathbb{T},B_{1})}=\frac{|\mathbb{T}[K]|}{|\mathbb{T}||K|}

\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct, we have
|\mathbb{T}[K]|\lesssim(|K|/|T_{\delta}|)^{2}

Jm\lesssim C_{F}(\mathbb{T})|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|^{2}=\frac{|\mathbb{T}[K]||T_{\delta}|^{2}}{|K|}\lesssim|K|\lesssim 1.

Therefore, if
S\geq 1
, then Lemma
A.1
(or the standard Chernoff bound) gives

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{J}>S]\leq e^{10}e^{-S/M}\sim e^{-S}.

In other words,
\mathbb{P}[|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|>S]\lesssim e^{-S}
. The number of essentially distinct

is at most
\delta^{-O(1)}
, and so we can choose

so that with high probability
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|\leq S
for every

.

If we refine
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
to be a set of essentially distinct tubes then we still have
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}|\approx C_{F}(\mathbb{T})|\mathbb{T}|
.

Next we estimate
C_{F}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})
. Refining
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
to make the tubes essentially distinct will affect

by a factor
\lessapprox 1
, so we can ignore the refinement.

Let
K\subset B_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set of dimensions
k_{1}\times\ldots\times k_{n-1}\times 1
, with
\delta\leq k_{1}\leq\ldots\leq k_{n-1}\leq 1
. Let
X_{j}=|R_{j}(\mathbb{T})[K]|
, which we think of as a random variable. Note that
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[K]|=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}
. We will show that (for each
K
),

(107)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}>A\Delta(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},B_{1})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}\right]\lesssim e^{-A}.

Since there are only
(1/\delta)^{O(1)}
essentially different choices for the convex set
K\subset T_{\rho}
, we have that with probability at least
1-(1/\delta)^{O(1)}e^{-A}
, for every
K
we have
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[K]|\lesssim A\Delta(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},B_{1})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}
and so
C_{F}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lesssim A
. We can choose
A\lessapprox 1
so that the probability is at least
99/100
.

We will check (
107
) using Lemma
A.1
. Note that
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\sim C_{F}(\mathbb{T})\Delta(\mathbb{T},B_{1})\sim\Delta(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},B_{1})\sim|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}||T_{\delta}|
. Define

M=|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}||K|=\frac{\Delta(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},B_{1})|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}=\frac{\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}\geq|\mathbb{T}[R_{j}^{-1}(K)]=X_{j}.

Clearly
X_{j}\geq 0
, so
0\leq X_{j}\leq M
almost surely. For each
T\in\mathbb{T}
, we claim that

(108)

\mathbb{P}[R_{j}(T)\subset K]\lesssim|K|.

To see this, pick a point
x\in T
(for example
x
could be the center of
T
) and bound

\mathbb{P}(R_{j}(T)\subset K)\leq\mathbb{P}(R_{j}(x)\in K)\lesssim\frac{|K|}{|B_{1}(x)|}\sim|K|.

By linearity of expectation, (
111
) gives the bound

\mathbb{E}(X_{j})\lesssim|\mathbb{T}||K|.

Define
m=\mathbb{E}(X_{j})
. Since
J=C_{F}(\mathbb{T})
we have
Jm=C_{F}(\mathbb{T})|\mathbb{T}||K|=|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}||K|=M
. Therefore, Lemma
A.1
tells us that for every
S
,

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{J}>S]\lesssim e^{-S/M}.

We plug in
S=A\Delta(\mathbb{T}^{\prime},B_{1})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}=AM
, and we get (
107
).
∎

Finally, we restate and prove Lemma
7.6
.

Lemma
7.6
.

Let
\delta\leq\rho\leq 1
. Suppose that
\mathbb{T}
is a set of
\delta
-tubes in
T_{\rho}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\frac{|T_{\rho}|}{|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|}\sim J\geq 1
. Let
R_{1},...,R_{J}
be a set of
J
random translations of size
\leq\rho
. Let
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{J}R_{j}(\mathbb{T})
. Let
A\geq 1
. Then with probability at least
1-(\rho/\delta)^{O(1)}e^{-A}
, the tubes of
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
are essentially distinct up to a factor
\lesssim A
and we have
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lesssim A\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})
.
After refining
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
to make the tubes essentially distinct, we have with high probability
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}|\approx J|\mathbb{T}|
and
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lessapprox\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})

The proof ideas are the same as for Lemma
3.8
above.

Proof.

First we check that with high probability, for any
\delta
-tube
T_{0}\subset T_{\rho}
,
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|\lessapprox 1
. This will show that after refining
\mathbb{T}^{\prime}
to be essentially distinct, we still have
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}|\approx J|\mathbb{T}|
.

We define
X_{j}=|\mathbb{R}_{j}(\mathbb{T})[100T_{0}]|
, which we think of as a random variable. Note that
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}
.

Since the tubes of
\mathbb{T}
are essentially distinct, we have
X_{j}=|\mathbb{R}_{j}(\mathbb{T})[100T_{0}]|=|\mathbb{T}[R_{j}^{-1}(100T_{0})]|\lesssim 1
. We choose
M\lesssim 1
and note that
0\leq X_{j}\leq M
almost surely.

Next we want to estimate
\mathbb{E}(X_{j})
. By linearity of expectation,
\mathbb{E}(X_{j})=\sum_{T\in\mathbb{T}}\mathbb{P}\left[R_{j}(T)\subset 100T_{0}\right]
.
Since
R_{j}
is a random translation of size
\rho
, we note that for each
T\in\mathbb{T}
,

(109)

\mathbb{P}\left[R_{j}(T)\subset 100T_{0}\right]\lesssim\frac{|T_{\delta}|}{|T_{\rho}|}.

Therefore,

m=\mathbb{E}(X_{j})\lesssim|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|/|T_{\rho}|.

We claim that
Jm\lesssim 1=M
. To see this, we note that
Jm\lesssim J|\mathbb{T}||T_{\delta}|/|T_{\rho}|\sim 1
by our assumption about
J
.

Therefore, if
S\geq 1
, then Lemma
A.1
(or the standard Chernoff bound) gives

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{J}>S]\leq e^{10}e^{-S/M}\sim e^{-S}.

In other words,
\mathbb{P}[|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|>S]\lesssim e^{-S}
. The number of essentially distinct
T_{0}
is at most
\delta^{-O(1)}
, and so we can choose
S\lessapprox 1
so that with high probability
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[100T_{0}]|\leq S
for every
T_{0}
.

Next we estimate
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})
.

Let
K\subset 2T_{\rho}
be a convex set of dimensions
k_{1}\times\ldots\times k_{n-1}\times 1
, with
\delta\leq k_{1}\leq\ldots\leq k_{n-1}\leq\rho
. Let
X_{j}=|R_{j}(\mathbb{T})[K]|
, which we think of as a random variable. Note that
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[K]|=\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}
. We will show that (for each
K
),

(110)

\mathbb{P}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{J}X_{j}>A\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}\right]\lesssim e^{-A}.

Since there are only
(\rho/\delta)^{O(1)}
essentially different choices for the convex set
K\subset T_{\rho}
, we have that with probability at least
1-(\rho/\delta)^{O(1)}e^{-A}
, for every
K
we have
|\mathbb{T}^{\prime}[K]|\lesssim A\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}
and so
\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T}^{\prime})\lesssim A\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})
.

We will check (
110
) using Lemma
A.1
. Define
M=\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})|K|/|T_{\delta}|
. Note that

M\geq|\mathbb{T}[R_{j}^{-1}(K)]=X_{j}.

Clearly
X_{j}\geq 0
, so
0\leq X_{j}\leq M
almost surely. For each
T\in\mathbb{T}
, we claim that

(111)

\mathbb{P}[R_{j}(T)\subset K]\lesssim|K|/|T_{\rho}|.

To see this, pick a point
x\in T
(for example
x
could be the center of
T
) and bound

\mathbb{P}(R_{j}(T)\subset K)\leq\mathbb{P}(R_{j}(x)\in K)\leq\frac{|B_{\rho}(x)\cap K|}{|B_{\rho}(x)|}\leq\frac{k_{1}\cdots k_{n-1}\cdot\rho}{\rho^{n}}\sim\frac{|K|}{|T_{\rho}|}.

By linearity of expectation, (
111
) gives the bound

\mathbb{E}(X_{j})\lesssim\frac{|\mathbb{T}||K|}{|T_{\rho}|}.

Define
m=\mathbb{E}(X_{j})
. By our definition of
J
we have
Jm\sim\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}\lesssim\Delta_{max}(\mathbb{T})\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}=M
. Therefore, Lemma
A.1
tells us that

\mathbb{P}[X_{1}+...+X_{J}>S]\lesssim e^{-S/M}.

We plug in
S=A\Delta_{max}\frac{|K|}{|T_{\delta}|}=AM
, and we get (
110
). ∎
