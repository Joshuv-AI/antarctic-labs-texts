Nursing Perceptions of the Intended Use of Artificial Intelligence to Prevent Medication Errors: A Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Juan Martinez‐Puertas, Noelia Frutos‐Rodríguez, Alejandro Winderholler‐Heinzenknecht, Carmen Morales‐Plaza, Adrian Martinez‐Ortigosa, Miguel Rodriguez‐Arrastia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo explore the perceptions of nursing professionals in high‐demand healthcare services regarding the adoption of AI‐based support systems for the prevention of medication errors.DesignA qualitative descriptive study was conducted between November 2024 and March 2025.MethodsSixteen semi‐structured interviews were held with nurses from emergency and intensive care units, guided by conceptual dimensions of the Technology Acceptance Model framework. Participants were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling. ATLAS.ti v.9 software was used for an inductive thematic analysis.ResultsTwo major themes emerged: (i) professional reflections on medication safety and related risks; and (ii) integrating artificial intelligence into nursing practice to reduce such risks and prevent medication errors. While artificial intelligence was recognised as a promising resource to support clinical decision‐making and reduce cognitive load, nurses identified barriers, including limited training, inadequate technological infrastructure, unreliable data sources, and ethical concerns that could compromise its safe implementation and thereby hinder its potential to prevent medication errors.ConclusionAI‐based support systems are perceived as useful, but complex resources for addressing medication errors, which remain a critical challenge in healthcare. Its successful implementation depends not only on the availability of resources, but also on the organisational context and the ability to respond to the needs and concerns of healthcare professionals.Implications for Clinical PracticeIntegrating artificial intelligence into routine workflows to support clinical decision‐making and reduce medication errors in high‐demand settings requires more than infrastructure and technical training. Effective adoption demands participatory design, clear role delineation, and context‐sensitive training aligned with medication‐management processes. Lack of alignment may result in artificial intelligence increasing complexity instead of contributing to safer and more efficient medication administration.Reporting MethodMethods and findings are reported following SRQR recommendations.Patient or Public ContributionNo patient or public contribution.Keywords: artificial intelligence, medication errors, nursing, qualitative study

Introduction

Patient clinical safety, according to the World Health Organisation, aims to reduce any risks related to care to an acceptable level (Sheikh et al. 2017). Although initiatives to enhance patient safety existed before the late 1990s, the Institute of Medicine's report ‘To Err Is Human’ (1999) is widely acknowledged as a catalyst for global awareness and helped shape early 21‐century patient safety initiatives. Over two decades later, it remains a core dimension of healthcare quality and a continuing challenge for systems and institutions worldwide (Institute of Medicine 2000; Kirwan et al. 2019; Reason 2000). However, ensuring safety should not be understood solely as a matter of institutional accountability, as the provision of safe, high‐quality care also depends on the clinical judgement and decision‐making of healthcare professionals, who work at the frontline of patient care (Sist et al. 2024). In this regard, particularly in high‐demand care units such as emergency departments or intensive care units (Duhalde et al. 2023; Tu et al. 2023), medication administration errors continue to represent the main cause of adverse events related to healthcare interventions. These errors are frequently underreported (Afaya et al. 2021; Tariq et al. 2023), despite an estimated incidence of 37.4% among all reported adverse events (Naseralallah et al. 2023); and include omissions or delays in administration, wrong‐patient delivery, allergic reactions or drug interactions, inaccurate dosage calculations, and errors stemming from unclear or misleading labelling (Tariq et al. 2023).

Background

A key consideration in medication safety is that nurses hold primary responsibility for medication administration in high‐demand environments and often serve as the final safeguard against errors, even when other professionals are involved in the prescribing process (Deswal et al. 2023; Schroers et al. 2020). This position of frontline role places nurses at increased risk of being exposed to or committing medication errors, which highlights the importance of understanding these events not as isolated actions, but as consequences of broader systemic conditions. System‐based models of clinical error consider these mistakes as an inherent part of human performance, arising primarily from organisational and structural weaknesses rather than individual failings (Reason 2000). Consequently, safety strategies that rely solely on professional vigilance and adherence to the ‘five rights’ of medication administration are now considered insufficient (Institute for Safe Medication Practices 2023), requiring more robust interventions to develop a safety culture that facilitates the implementation of standardised protocols and new system‐level safeguards (Machen et al. 2019; McKinney et al. 2019).

The growing need to enhance access to clinical information and support safe decision‐making in high‐pressure settings has drawn attention to the development of new clinical decision support systems designed to assist nursing workflows, improve task performance and reduce workload (Cheng et al. 2024). Among these, artificial intelligence (AI) applications in nursing practice have shown potential to improve patient safety by means of early error detection, patient stratification, and optimised medication management (Holgate 2021; Martinez‐Ortigosa et al. 2023). AI refers to software capable of mimicking human cognitive processes to perform a wide range of complex tasks (European Comission 2018; Khubchandani et al. 2024), including functions such as synthesising clinical evidence and guiding real‐time decision‐making (Costa et al. 2024). This technology often relies on machine learning, natural language processing, and large‐scale data modelling, and can be classified as predictive, prescriptive, or generative AI, aimed at forecasting clinical outcomes, recommending interventions, or generating new solutions to support decision‐making, respectively (Martinez‐Ortigosa et al. 2023). The integration into nursing practice may contribute significantly to the digital transformation of healthcare systems and the reduction of medication‐related adverse events (Institute for Safe Medication Practices 2023); however, the absence of specific regulatory frameworks governing its use in clinical settings and limited involvement of frontline professionals continues to represent a significant hurdle to its design, implementation and adoption (Khubchandani et al. 2024).

Active involvement of nurses in acquiring domain‐specific digital competences and critical understanding of these technologies therefore remains essential to ensure that artificial intelligence becomes an ethically grounded and context‐sensitive support within bedside care (Seibert et al. 2021; Von Gerich et al. 2022). Recent reviews have emphasised the need to examine more deeply the barriers and facilitators perceived by healthcare professionals in relation to the adoption of this type of technology in clinical practice (Alqaraleh et al. 2025; Cheng et al. 2024; Hennrich et al. 2024). Moving beyond a merely descriptive understanding of adoption processes (Khan Rony, Numan, et al. 2024), the use of theoretical frameworks such as the ‘Technology Acceptance Model’ can provide a more structured analytical lens (Davis 1985). This model introduces two central constructs: perceived usefulness, defined as the belief that a technology improves work performance, and perceived ease of use, understood as the expectation that it reduces effort. Together, these constructs shape the intention to use a new technology and guide strategies aimed at facilitating its integration into routine clinical activities (Davis et al. 1989; Jiao and Cao 2024). This guiding framework (see Figure 1) offers a theoretical to explore nurses' acceptance and adoption of artificial intelligence and allows for an examination of how these evaluations relate to real‐world experiences with medication errors, helping to identify contextual factors that support or hinder the introduction of AI tools in high‐demand clinical settings.

The Study

Thus, the aim of this study is to explore the perceptions of nursing professionals in high‐demand healthcare services regarding the adoption of AI‐based support systems for the prevention of medication errors.

Methods

Design

A descriptive qualitative study was conducted between November 2024 and March 2025. This methodological approach facilitated an in‐depth understanding of nurses' perceptions regarding the barriers and needs with the adoption of AI‐based clinical decision support systems for preventing medication errors in high‐demand settings.

Participants

Participants were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling methods. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) registered nurses, (ii) currently working or having previous experience in high‐demand care settings such as intensive care units, reanimation units or emergency departments, (iii) with more than 6 months of professional experience in these clinical settings, (iv) willing to voluntarily participate in the study and (v) having provided informed consent prior to data collection. The minimum requirement of 6 months of experience was methodological rather than competence‐based, and was established to ensure sufficient exposure to the medication‐safety practices and organisational dynamics of high‐demand care settings, and to avoid perspectives linked to absolute novice status that could bias the contextual understanding of medication management (Ho et al. 2021; Najafi and Nasiri 2023). Initial recruitment was carried out at the University of Almeria, utilising the accessibility of this site to identify eligible participants efficiently. Following this initial phase, snowball sampling was implemented to expand the participant pool by inviting recruited participants to refer other eligible individuals. This iterative recruitment process facilitated the inclusion of participants who might otherwise have been inaccessible through conventional methods (Atkinson and Flint 2001; Hendricks and Blanken 1992). Data Collection

A semi‐structured interview protocol was developed based on the existing literature (Fareed et al. 2024; Gupta et al. 2021; Jiao and Cao 2024) and conceptual dimensions of Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), namely usefulness and ease of use (Davis 1985). These dimensions informed the construction of the core interview questions, which explored nurses' views on the potential applications, benefits, and challenges of using AI‐based systems to prevent medication administration errors (see Table 2 and Figure 1). Sixteen interviews were conducted by two researchers trained in qualitative methods research. Six interviews were carried out face‐to‐face, whilst the remaining ten were conducted online via video conferencing. All interviews were digitally recorded and lasted between 40 and 60 min. Data confidentiality was strictly maintained within the research process. The letter ‘P’ (participants) was used to ensure the anonymity of the participants in the transcription of the interviews. Participants were given the opportunity to review transcriptions from the recordings before commencing data analysis. The data collection process employed an approach informed by the concept of information power, prioritising the depth and richness of insights over the notion of reaching a predetermined point of saturation (Braun and Clarke 2019).

Data Analysis

An inductive thematic analysis was undertaken using ATLAS.ti v.9 software, as this method is well suited to qualitative descriptive studies where the aim is to capture participants' accounts with minimal interpretive transformation (Colorafi and Evans 2016). This approach enabled themes to arise directly from the data, consistent with recommendations for descriptive designs that prioritise the content of participants' narratives (Creswell and Creswell 2018). The coding process remained fully inductive; the dimensions of TAM informed only the development of the interview guide and the later interpretive phase, without determining codes or shaping the generation of themes. The analysis was carried out following the phases proposed by Braun and Clarke (2006): (i) familiarisation with the data, involving repeated readings of the transcripts and the development of initial notes to gain a general sense of the content; (ii) systematic coding of the data, categorising transcriptions with meaningful codes; (iii) theme generation, grouping codes from the previous phase into potential themes representing shared meanings; (iv) theme development and review to ensure consistency among codes grouped under themes; (v) refining the analysis, fine tuning theme definitions and names; and (vi) report writing. Rigour

Methods and findings are reported in line with Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) recommendations (O'Brien et al. 2014). The trustworthiness of this study was established by adhering to Lincoln and Guba (2006) criteria. A detailed description of the methods and data collection process was provided to enhance transferability, along with the inclusion of direct participant quotations in the findings. Credibility was strengthened by verifying participants' perspectives at the end of each interview to ensure accurate representation of their views. Confirmability was achieved following the independent analysis of transcripts conducted by both first authors, followed by collaborative discussions to compare, align and refine emerging themes. Finally, dependability was reinforced by consulting a third researcher in cases of disagreement to ensure consistency and reliability in interpreting the data. All researchers reached a unanimous agreement on the final report.

Ethical Considerations

This study proposal was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Nursing, Physiotherapy and Medicine at the University of Almería (EFM 360/24), in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and subsequent modifications. Before commencing the study, participants who chose to take part provided written informed consent. They were continually given the option to withdraw at any time, ensuring their confidentiality and anonymity.

Findings

Participant Characteristics

Sixteen female nurses were involved in the study. The mean age of participants was 36.31 years (SD = 9.583), with ages ranging from 24 to 54 years. Among the nurses interviewed, 37.5% (n = 6) worked exclusively in intensive care units, 56.25% (n = 9) in emergency departments and 6.25% (n = 1) across both services. Work experience in high‐demand care settings ranged from 10 to 300 months (equivalent to 25 years), with a mean of 104.88 months (SD = 99.253). Regarding their self‐reported use of artificial intelligence, participants rated their prior experience with a mean score of 1.75 out of 5 (SD = 1.125). This item referred to any interaction with AI tools in personal or professional contexts. No definitions or examples of AI were provided, and no list of platforms was shown, as the intention was to capture participants' own understanding of what they considered to be AI (Pyo et al. 2023; Renjith et al. 2021). Theme 1: Professional Reflections on Medication Safety and Related Risks

This theme explores nurses' experiences with medication errors, highlighting the types of errors and perceived contributing factors in high‐demand care settings. Participants identified workload, interruptions and systemic flaws as key causes. They also shared emotional and psychological reactions such as anxiety and shame. Finally, they emphasised the need for stronger institutional support and a more solid safety culture.

Subtheme 1.1: Common Medication Error Types and Professional Responses

The nurses interviewed described a range of medication errors occurring in routine clinical practice, despite acknowledging an array of institutional and individual strategies for preventing medication errors, which include dispensing systems, visual aids, double‐checking routines, as well as formal and informal information sources. These mainly comprised prescription errors (e.g., incorrect drug selection or dosage), administration errors (e.g., wrong route or timing) and omission errors (e.g., failure to administer a prescribed dose):

Sometimes you are in a rush and just assume it's the usual dose or way, but the prescription or the medication format might have changed that day. That's how mistakes happen, when you go on autopilot. (P1)

There are times when you skip a dose just because there's no clear note that it was pending, or you're assuming someone else already gave it. And even if we double‐check, if the prescription isn't updated or visible, you're prone to repeat or miss things without even realising. (P12)

Conversely, professionals reported emotional responses once an error occurred, such as insecurity, fear, shame, and concern about possible repercussions when reporting medication errors, along with anxiety over losing the trust of patients, families or physicians. These emotions were often linked not only to the potential consequences of reporting, but also to unclear reporting procedures, lack of institutional support, and a workplace culture that does not consistently encourage transparency:

[…] I definitely think there's a cultural component to it. Having to say ‘I made a mistake’ causes me insecurity and above all, fear; not just because of the professional consequences, but also because of the impact on the patient's security and the loss of trust. With so many factors that can lead to an error, I believe there's a lack of institutional support to help manage it properly. Deep down, you know that at some point, you're going to make a mistake. (P2)

Subtheme 1.2: Enablers and Constraints Influencing Medication Errors

Most participants identified several systemic and contextual shortcomings related to medication errors, such as short‐time contracts, staff shortages, time constraints, poor communication, organisational concerns and insufficient training, particularly among newly qualified nurses. Likewise, participants highlighted organisational challenges like high turnover, workload pressure and inconsistencies across units. The absence of a well‐established safety culture and the prevalence of errors in high‐pressure environments were frequently emphasised. Participants also highlighted some difficulties in managing treatment prescriptions, which in some cases were provided verbally rather than recorded in electronic medical records (EMRs), or were registered with incomplete information. These challenges in documentation and communication often generated doubts regarding dosage, infusion rate, timing, or even the prescription itself:

When you're on short‐term or part‐time contracts like mine, being here in the emergency department this morning, but possibly somewhere else in the afternoon, you realise how likely it is that you'll end up making a mistake; especially when we're short‐staffed, rushing around, or when a colleague has to waste time teaching me. For example, they might show me the verification system, but if tomorrow I'm called to work in another unit with a different system… It's always the same story (P5)

Similar packing, and not taking the time in here (intensive care units) to properly read and organise the medication labelling, are what lead to error. Even after taking the medication out, it's important to read it carefully and make sure no mistake is being made […] because, at the end of the day, it's a human being who restocks it (P10)

Theme 2: Integrating Artificial Intelligence Into Nursing Practice

This second theme explores nurses' perceptions on the use of AI in high‐pressure healthcare settings. They outlined several possible applications of AI for preventing medication errors and showed generally positive attitudes towards its integration into routine practice. They also described perceived barriers that may limit its effective implementation in clinical settings, reflecting both their intentions to use and current levels of engagement with such technologies.

Subtheme 2.1: Conceptual Understandings and Perceived Clinical Utility

Our participants showed different levels of knowledge regarding the concept of AI, with a predominance of limited understanding regarding its functioning, in spite of having used some AI‐related resources. Some reported accessing information about AI mainly via social media, news outlets, training courses, or personal interactions. Having said that, the most commonly anticipated benefits in clinical settings included increased time efficiency, stress reduction, enhanced patient safety, better evidence‐based decision‐making and overall improvements in the quality of care:

When they say artificial intelligence, I think of what I've seen on TV and social media. So, I'm not quite sure what AI really is, beyond ChatGPT. I suppose it's meant to help, and if used properly, it would help you because supposedly it gathers all the knowledge and simplifies it for you. That might allow us more time to focus on patient care, and maybe in the future, it could offer you reliable input for clinical decision‐making. […] Still, we should keep in mind that the final responsibility remains with the professional (P15)

In this vein, participants suggested multiple potential applications of AI‐based resources to reduce medication administration errors, including access to drug‐related information, dose calculation, regimen planning, automated alerts and integration of clinical data. They also proposed complementary uses, such as support for clinical decision‐making (e.g., triage, diagnosis, care planning), drafting documentation, patient monitoring, staff training and managing medical inventories. These accounts reflected the perceived capacity of AI to support early error detection and optimise medication management, although applications related to patient stratification did not explicitly emerge within the narratives provided:

It would be great if, right away, it showed you information such as: “metamizole: routes of administration, doses, causes, possible side effects, incompatibilities…” and do so quickly, even including images. Many medications are quite similar, so it would be helpful if potential risks appeared clearly as well. Perhaps there'd be a dedicated mobile or tablet for medication queries, where if a doubt arises, you simply type in the name or brand, and it provides details on dosage, dilution, complications, images, incompatibilities, and key warnings. That would be a huge time‐saving resource! (P8)

I'm not entirely sure on how it works, and I believe it may be something quite technical. However, based on what I've seen, these techs (AI‐based resources) could really help us, particularly for automating repetitive tasks. What I already checked on Instagram, it's already being used for things like drafting documentation, triage, monitoring, or inventory… Honestly, it seems very promising (P14)

Subtheme 2.2: Usability Concerns and Barriers to Effective Adoption

A large proportion of the participants expressed a positive attitude towards implementing AI‐based technologies as a support resource in their healthcare settings, with younger professionals showing greater openness. While perceptions regarding their ability to use these technologies varied, they did not show significant reluctance. Participants agreed on the importance of being involved to ensure it addresses their actual needs, as well as the need for targeted training to support the gradual adoption of AI into routine practice:

If there's something that can help save time and make our job more efficient, it should be always welcomed. We might not know how to use it properly at first, so it would be essential to plan proper training for everyone who will be using it, with guides in different formats, videos, simulation and so on (P11)

I think that, like with any new system, if we want it to stay, we need to be more involved (in its design). Not make it something generic, but something that truly responds to what we actually need. For example, something specifically focused on the administration of the most commonly used medications in our unit. It's about creating something genuinely useful in our day‐to‐day practice (P4)

Despite their acceptance towards the use of AI‐based resources in their clinical settings, participants also voiced concerns regarding the reliability of information sources, risks to patient privacy and the necessity of maintaining human oversight. Additionally, some feared a potential overreliance on technology and a diminished sense of professional fulfilment. They further agreed that successful implementation would be challenged by limited funding, inadequate infrastructure, insufficient managerial support, lack of proper training and resistance to change:

[…] I think its use can be a double‐edged sword in terms of safety. Everything we see out there shows that Gen‐AI [generative artificial intelligence] sometimes provides fake information, requires a lot of resources, lacks privacy safeguards, or could even lead to dependency. That's why I think that, if it's going to be introduced, it should come with proper training, help debunk myths, and allow us to see it for what it could be, a useful resource for us. Otherwise, it will just be perceived as yet another top‐down imposition (P16)

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the perceptions of nursing professionals in high‐demand healthcare services regarding the adoption of AI‐based support systems for the prevention of medication errors. While the most prevalent underlying roots of such errors have been widely documented (Kwiecień‐Jaguś et al. 2025; Ottosen and Bucknall 2024), our findings contribute to a more context‐sensitive and hands‐on understanding of these causes, founded in the daily clinical experience of frontline professionals within high‐demand healthcare settings. Participants viewed AI as a potentially valuable resource to reduce cognitive load, increase confidence in complex medication processes, and enhance real‐time decision‐making when facing uncertainty or high workloads. These perceived benefits align with their lived experiences of medication‐related risk, where time constraints, interruptions, and limited access to reliable information frequently challenge safe practice. These results also suggest potential and forward‐looking prevention strategies, particularly considering the integration of AI‐based support systems into organisational environments operating under structural and workload pressures, with adoption shaped by perceived usefulness perceived ease of use in relation to medication‐error prevention (Khanijahani et al. 2022; Ramadan et al. 2024). Participants identified a number of ways AI‐based systems could support medication management, including drug‐related information, calculating doses, planning regimen, receiving automated alerts and integrating clinical data (Alqaraleh et al. 2025). These findings are consistent with recent studies exploring the role of AI in mitigating clinical risks, which similarly highlight its potential to support clinicians in addressing workload‐related vulnerabilities and enhancing the reliability of medication processes (Amin et al. 2024; Graafsma et al. 2024; Mertes et al. 2024).

The implementation and integration of these AI‐based support systems into healthcare settings, however, cannot be fully considered without examining concrete real‐world clinical contexts and needs due to their own specificity and operational complexity (Martinez‐Ortigosa et al. 2023). As in previous studies, our findings identified recurring structural drivers in high‐demand settings, such as care overload, understaffing and insufficient training, as recurring contributors to errors (Naseralallah et al. 2023; Park et al. 2023). Nevertheless, participants introduced a more nuanced interpretation, as missing information was not viewed as a direct causal factor in error occurrence. This contrasts with previous studies on settings with informational scarcity (Ishak and Ibrahim 2024; Kwiecień‐Jaguś et al. 2025), suggesting that perceived risk may vary depending on the access and competence in managing clinical information, more than on its mere availability (Machen et al. 2019). Furthermore, it reinforces the well‐established notion that errors are rarely due to individual negligence alone; rather, they occur within clinical environments where interruptions, work organisation, documentation issues (e.g., incomplete or verbally communicated prescriptions) and poor communication systems act as triggers (Ishak and Ibrahim 2024; Syyrilä et al. 2021). Although EMRs did not emerge explicitly in participant contributions, their relevance is well established. In this regard, prior research has underscored the importance of EMRs in reducing medication errors and alleviating workload (Naamneh and Bodas 2024). This contextualised view aligns with systemic models such as Reason's, where multi‐causality and latent conditions carry greater weight than isolated acts (Naseralallah et al. 2023; Reason 2000). In this sense, the professional narratives move beyond merely describing errors; they also acknowledge the emotional burden and symbolic impact involved, identified as part of a second victim phenomenon (Collado‐González et al. 2024), which is frequently overlooked in institutional discourses and underscores the need for effective support mechanisms (Mahat et al. 2024). Building on this perspective, AI‐based systems offer a potential solution to mitigate these errors and associated emotional burden, as participants reported their ability to reduce stress, increase time efficiency, support evidence‐based decision‐taking, enhance patient safety and improve the overall quality of care (Alqaraleh et al. 2025). More specifically, the functions most frequently mentioned, − rapid access to drug‐related information, automated alerts, dose calculation support and real‐time integration of clinical data ‐, might directly address several of the structural vulnerabilities identified earlier, such as interruptions, incomplete documentation and high cognitive load (Brabcová et al. 2023; Ishak and Ibrahim 2024). These findings may be particularly interesting when considering the feasibility of implementing new preventive measures, considering that these emotional tolls may not only affects individual wellbeing, but may also diminish professionals' capacity to consistently engage in safety routines, such as double‐checking or participating in ongoing training. This coincides with other studies that highlight the gap between formalised safety policies and their effective implementation (Collado‐González et al. 2024; Park et al. 2022), opening a line of reflection into the sustainability and adaptability of current preventive strategies and new proposals, particularly in high pressure healthcare units with high turnover rates.

In this study, nurses offer a qualitative counterpoint to the growing body of quantitative evidence on the usability of current clinical decision support systems and artificial intelligence in preventing medication errors (Levivien et al. 2022; Mertes et al. 2024) as well as other studies that sometimes assume an easy AI implementation into clinical practice (Costa et al. 2024; Park et al. 2025). Despite valuing its capability within high‐demand contexts to reduce errors, increase clinical accuracy and alleviate cognitive burden (Graafsma et al. 2024) participants also expressed concerns related to ethics, data privacy ‐for both patients and professionals‐, and lack of transparency (Amin et al. 2024; Khan Rony, Kayesh, et al. 2024). These perceptions do not merely delay technological adoption; they also reflect a deeper epistemological tension between algorithmic rationality and experience‐based clinical reasoning (Braun et al. 2021; Morley et al. 2020). AI is not perceived as a stand‐alone solution, instead as a support resource whose effectiveness is mediated by organisational context, the quality of training and prevailing organisational culture (De Micco et al. 2025; Tursynbek et al. 2024). Resistance to change should therefore not be interpreted solely as individual reluctance or lack of adaptability, but a symptom of structural limitations that either foster or hinder innovation (Petersson et al. 2022). Accordingly, the implementation of AI‐based support systems in high‐demand clinical setting should entail a broader organisation transformation, one that considers newer models such as deep learning or federated learning to improve transparency and privacy (De Micco et al. 2025); as well as specific capabilities aligned with the needs identified by nursing professionals in high pressure healthcare units, for instance, dosage calculation, medication identification, integration of patient clinical data or others (Martinez‐Ortigosa et al. 2023). This ultimately implies a critical reassessment of care models that rely on AI‐based support systems or similar technologies centred on efficiency, so as not to displace the core focus of the therapeutic relationship (Alami et al. 2021; Hähnel et al. 2024).

Limitations and Future Lines of Research

This study presents a number of limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the results. Despite the heterogeneity in participants' backgrounds, their perceived experience with AI‐based resources was generally limited. Nonetheless, this perspective was considered relevant as the aim was to explore initial barriers and needs for potential adoption, even without prior interaction with such systems. On the other hand, the study focused exclusively on nursing professionals working in some high‐demand care units. Although this lens offers valuable insights within a critical clinical context, it may restrict the transferability of the findings to other healthcare settings. All participants were women, which may have limited the exploration of potential gender‐related differences. Furthermore, questions regarding the use of AI in other clinical areas were not included, as the study was intentionally focused on medication‐error prevention to ensure analytic depth and support the development of future nursing‐specific models. Future research should therefore broaden to other healthcare professionals involved in medication administration and clinical decision‐making, and explore a wider range of care environments where AI‐based resources may also prove useful. Lastly, addressing the limiting factors identified in this study will require the development of feasible and context‐specific AI prototypes tailored to the reality of their clinical practice; this will ensure an ethical and sustainable implementation that is aligned with their organisational culture.

Conclusion

Medication errors remain a critical challenge in high‐demand clinical settings, often rooted in structural and systemic constraints that directly affect the quality and safety of care. The results of this study support the perceived potential of artificial intelligence as a resource to address some of these risks, notably with those related to decision‐making and error prevention support. However, successful adoption depends on more than recognition of its benefits. Organisational readiness, targeted training and capabilities, infrastructure availability, and a shared understanding of its clinical role are all essential conditions. The current gap between technological promise and professional realities reflects a need for more context‐aware, ethically responsible and participatory approaches. Further research should prioritise the development of AI‐based systems that are feasible, tailored to the clinical workflows of high‐pressure environments and aligned with the values and expectations of those expected to use them. Only then can these systems contribute meaningfully to improving patient safety and supporting the demands of clinical care.

Implications for Clinical Practice

This study yields relevant insights into the practical challenges and conditions required for the effective implementation and integration of artificial intelligence in high‐demand clinical settings. Nurses recognised the potential of AI to support decision‐making and reduce medication errors, but also identified structural and organisational barriers that may limit its usefulness and ease of use in everyday practice. These concerns are not rooted in resistance to innovation, but in the tension between technological potential and everyday clinical constraints. For these reasons, any AI system must be co‐developed with end users, tailored to specific clinical workflows, transparency and embedded within a broader institutional commitment to patient safety. Participatory design, role clarification and context‐sensitive training emerge as essential components, not add‐ons, for successful adoption. Without this alignment, AI risks becoming just another layer of complexity, more than a support for safer and more effective care.
