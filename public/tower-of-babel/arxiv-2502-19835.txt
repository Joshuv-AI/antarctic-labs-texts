Introduction

Given an undirected graph

, a theorem of Gallai
[

and every integer

-paths in

\rvert\rfloor\geq k

of components of the subgraph

of

An

-path
is a non-trivial path which intersects

precisely in its two endvertices.
Gallai’s result does not apply to bidirected graphs, no matter whether components are replaced by strong or weak components (defined in Section

is not in general necessary for the existence of

pairwise disjoint

-paths. Considering weak components, the example on the right-hand side of the figure shows that the condition is not in general sufficient.

In his paper

Given a digraph

and the digraph

Concretely, Kriesell proves that for each subset

and each integer

, there exist

X

S,T\subseteq V(D)
satisfying

\left\lvert S\cap T\right\rvert+\sum\lfloor\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lvert V(C)\cap(X%
\cup S\cup T)\right\rvert\rfloor\geq k
where the sum is taken over the weak components of the subdigraph

of

The proof is done in two steps. First, Kriesell defines a certain auxiliary digraph

obtained from

by splitting the vertices outside

and shows that the existence of
k

X

. Second, he exploits Tutte’s characterization
3.2
of the existence of a matching of a specific cardinality and relates this to the desired characterization of the existence of
k
pairwise disjoint
X
-paths in

In the paper at hand, we generalize Kriesell’s theorem and proof to bidirected graphs.
These objects were first introduced and studied by Kotzig in a series of papers
[
Kotzig-1
,
Kotzig-2
,
Kotzig-3
]
and were rediscovered independently by Edmonds and Johnson
[
Edmonds-Johnson
]
.
They are defined as (undirected) graphs together with two signs for every edge, one at each endvertex. In particular, every bidirected graph has a unique underlying undirected graph. Moreover, we can regard bidirected graphs as a generalization of directed graphs inasmuch as every directed graph gives rise to a bidirected graph where any directed edge was replaced by an edge with negative sign at the initial vertex and positive sign at the terminal vertex, while bidirected graphs may additionally contain edges with the same sign at both endvertices.
We will mostly consider bidirected graphs which are allowed to have multiple edges with the same pair of signs. For the sake of clarity, we refer to them as bidirected multigraphs. However, we do not allow them to contain loops.

For the proof of our generalized Gallai theorem for bidirected multigraphs, Theorem
3.1
, we need a substitute for Kriesell’s digraph

or

or

In the process of adapting Kriesell’s proof to bidirected multigraphs, the multigraph

with vertex set

and edge set consisting of those edges

of

or

turns out to be suitable. Here,

denotes the

of

, assigning to each pair of an edge and one of its endvertices a plus or minus sign. If replacing any directed edge of a digraph

to

by an edge with sign

at

corresponds precisely to the directed subdigraph

As a consequence of Theorem
3.1
, we will prove that

-paths in bidirected multigraphs satisfy a kind of an Erdős-Pósa property, namely that there exists a function

such that for any bidirected multigraph

and any integer

,

admits

-paths or a vertex set

of cardinality at most

We will verify this for

[

.
More general, a family

of graphs is said to satisfy the

iff there is a function

2
Preliminaries: Bidirected multigraphs

We use standard graph-theoretic terminology from
[
Diestel
]
.
The following definitions for bidirected multigraphs are based on Section 2 of
[
Bowler-et-al_Mengers-Thm
]
.

A
bidirected multigraph

B
is a pair
(G,\sigma)
consisting of a multigraph
G=(V,E)
together with a
signing

\sigma\colon\thinspace\underline{E}(B)\to\{+,-\}
where
\underline{E}(B)
denotes the set of half-edges of
B
, that is,
\underline{E}(B)\coloneq\{(v,e)\in V\times E\mathbin{|}v\textup{ is an %
endvertex of }e\}
.
Given a half-edge
(v,e)
of
B
, we say that
e
has sign
\sigma(v,e)
at
v
.
Moreover, edges with signs
\alpha
and
\beta
at their endvertices are referred to as
(\alpha,\beta)
-edges.
The
vertex set

V(B)
of
B
is the vertex set
V=V(G)
of the
underlying multigraph

G
and the
edge set

E(B)
of
B
is the edge set
E=E(G)
of
G
. We call the elements of
V(B)
and
E(B)
the
vertices
and
edges
of
B
, respectively.
If
B
has no multiple edges with the same pair of signs, we refer to
B
as a
bidirected graph
.

When drawing a bidirected multigraph, we omit any negative sign while representing any positive sign as a bar perpendicular to the respective edge near the respective endvertex. For an example, see Figure
3.1
.

Let
B=(G,\sigma)
be a bidirected multigraph.
A
bidirected sub(multi)graph
of
B
is a bidirected (multi)graph
B^{\prime}=(G^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
where
G^{\prime}
is a submultigraph of
G
and
\sigma^{\prime}
is the restriction of
\sigma
to
\underline{E}(B^{\prime})\subseteq\underline{E}(B)
.
Given a vertex set
Y\subset V(B)
, we denote by
B-Y
the bidirected submultigraph of
B
whose underlying multigraph is
G-Y
, that is, the multigraph obtained from
G
by deleting all vertices in
Y
.

A
path
in
B
is a path
P=v_{0}e_{1}v_{1}\dots e_{\ell}v_{\ell}
in the underlying multigraph
G
such that for each internal vertex
v\in\{v_{1},\dots,v_{\ell-1}\}
, the two half-edges of
P
containing
v
have distinct signs, that is,
\sigma(v_{i},e_{i})\neq\sigma(v_{i+1},e_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,\ell-1\}
.
We also call
P
a
(v_{0},\sigma(v_{0},e_{1}))-(v_{\ell},\sigma(v_{\ell},e_{\ell}))
path
.
The
length
of
P
is the number
\ell
of its edges. We say that
P
is
trivial
iff it has length zero.
Given a vertex set
X\subseteq V(B)
, an
X
-path
in
B
is a non-trivial path
P=v_{0}e_{1}v_{1}\dots e_{\ell}v_{\ell}
in
B
whose endvertices
v_{0}
and
v_{\ell}
lie in
X
whereas
X
contains no other vertex of
P
.
Note that every path in
B
defines a bidirected subgraph of
B
in the obvious way, and we usually do not distinguish between
P
and the corresponding bidirected graph.

Similarly as for directed multigraphs, there are different notions of connectivity for their bidirected counterparts.
A bidirected multigraph
B
is
strongly connected
iff it is non-empty and for any two vertices
u,v\in V(B)
, there exist signs
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
B
contains both a
(u,\alpha)-(v,\beta)
path and a
(u,-\alpha)-(v,-\beta)
path.
It is said to be
weakly connected
iff its underlying multigraph is connected (and thus non-empty).
Clearly, every strongly connected bidirected multigraph is in particular weakly connected.
The
strong (weak) components
of
B
are its maximal strongly (weakly) connected bidirected submultigraphs.
Note that the weak components of
B
are precisely the components of its underlying multigraph.
For an (undirected) multigraph
H
, we denote by
\mathcal{C}(H)
the set of its components, and for a bidirected multigraph
B=(G,\sigma)
, we write
\mathcal{C}(B)\coloneq\mathcal{C}(G)
.

The Erdős-Pósa property for

-paths in bidirected multigraphs

Here is our second main theorem, saying that

Theorem 4.1
.

Then by Theorem

\scalerel*[5.5pt]{\Big{\lfloor}}{\ensurestackMath{\addstackgap[1.5pt]{\big{%
\lfloor}}}}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lvert V(C)\cap(X\cup S\cup T)\right\rvert%
