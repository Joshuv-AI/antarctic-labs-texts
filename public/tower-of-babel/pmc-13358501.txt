Global trends and inequalities in environmental risk-attributable chronic obstructive pulmonary disease mortality among adults aged 80+ years, 1990–2021: an analysis of the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Yan Wang, Shuai Jiang, Chengzeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile the aging global population faces a rising chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) mortality burden, comprehensive assessments of this burden concerning multiple environmental risks and socioeconomic inequalities among the oldest-old (aged ≥80 years) remain limited. This study aims to comprehensively assess the burden of environmental risk-attributable COPD mortality, its temporal trends, and socioeconomic inequalities among the global population aged ≥80 years.MethodsData from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021 were used to analyse COPD deaths and age-specific mortality rates attributable to eight environmental risk factors among adults aged 80 years and older from 1990 to 2021. Mortality estimates were derived from multiple GBD data sources, including household surveys, censuses, and vital registration systems. We examined mortality patterns and trends at the global, regional, and national levels. Temporal trends in age-specific mortality rates were assessed using Joinpoint regression, and Socio-demographic Index (SDI) related inequalities across 204 countries were evaluated using the slope index of inequality (SII) and relative concentration index (RCI).ResultsIn 2021, the leading environmental risk factors for age-specific COPD mortality rates were smoking {351.85 per 100,000 population [95% uncertainty interval (UI): 268.79–433.10]}, ambient particulate matter pollution [241.60 (175.22–299.69) per 100,000 population], and household air pollution from solid fuels (HAP) [157.27 (83.42–292.74) per 100,000 population]. The leading contributor to male COPD mortality was smoking [717.70 (555.94–871.14) per 100,000] and ambient particulate matter pollution [335.99 (245.85–411.01) per 100,000 population], whereas female mortality was primarily driven by ambient particulate matter pollution [181.00 (123.04–234.92) per 100,000 population] and HAP [141.30 (76.19–250.28) per 100,000 population]. From 1990 to 2021, COPD mortality rates attributable to HAP showed the largest reduction [percentage change: −72% (−83% to −53%)]. Joinpoint regression revealed temporal patterns. Smoking attributable mortality increased until 1998, then sustained a decline. Ambient particulate matter pollution attributable mortality rose until 2014 before decreasing. Ambient ozone attributable mortality followed a fluctuating pattern. High temperature was the only factor among the eight assessed with a statistically significant, overall increasing trend throughout 1990–2021 {annual percent change: 1.49% [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.98–2.00]}. In terms of health inequalities, HAP demonstrated the highest degree of socio-demographic inequality in 2021 [RCI: −0.404 (−0.474 to −0.334); SII: −545.59 (−637.30 to −453.89) per 100,000 population], with the relative inequality increasingly concentrated in lower-SDI countries. For ambient particulate matter pollution, absolute inequality shifted increasingly toward disadvantaged settings. Notably, both absolute and relative inequalities in ozone-attributable COPD mortality increased from 1990 to 2021, with mortality disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI countries.ConclusionsThese findings provide a critical evidence base for formulating integrated public health strategies aimed at mitigating burdens of environmental risk-attributable COPD mortality among adults aged 80 years and older. Such strategies entail prioritizing clean energy, clean cooking technologies, and ozone control in lower-SDI regions, along with reinforcing tobacco and climate-related actions globally.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study identified smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and household air pollution from solid fuels (HAP) as the three leading environmental risk factors of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) mortality in the population aged ≥80 years (the oldest-old).

• In 2021, HAP-attributable COPD mortality showed the highest socio-demographic inequality, while that attributable to ambient ozone pollution exhibited widened health inequalities over the past 32 years, with both mortality burdens concentrated in lower-Socio-demographic Index (SDI) countries.

What is known and what is new?

• Environmental exposures are established COPD risk factors.

• This study quantified the global burden of COPD mortality attributable to eight environmental risk factors among the oldest-old in 2021, characterized its temporal trends from 1990 to 2021, identified disparities by sex, age, region, and country, and evaluated the absolute and relative inequalities across SDI between 1990 and 2021.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings provide a critical evidence base for developing tailored policies, prioritizing clean energy and ozone control in lower-SDI regions and reinforcing tobacco and climate-related actions in the world.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, has posed a substantial challenge to global health systems. In 2021, COPD was the fourth leading cause of death globally, resulting in 3.5 million deaths, and remains a major cause of disability and death among adults who are 80 years and above (the oldest-old) (1). COPD has been incorporated into the World Health Organization’s (WHO’s) Global Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases and the United Nations’ 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development (2). The United Nations projects that the global population of the oldest-old will triple to over 400 million by 2050 compared to 2019, which will be accompanied by a rapid increase in the COPD-related mortality burden in this population (3).

COPD poses particularly severe threats to the oldest-old. With aging, reductions in respiratory reserve, diminished pulmonary elasticity, impaired mucociliary clearance, and delayed tissue repair may collectively increase the likelihood of adverse outcomes from obstructive pulmonary disease (4). Beyond these physiological declines, the oldest-old commonly present with comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and frailty syndrome, which further compromise the body’s tolerance to environmental stressors (4). Moreover, lifetime cumulative exposure to occupational dust, household air pollution, and tobacco smoke contributes to the heightened burden of COPD in later life (5). Systematically identifying COPD mortality attributable to environmental factors in this age group represents an important foundation for formulating rational healthcare planning, optimizing resource allocation, and developing age-appropriate preventive strategies. This need is especially pressing against the backdrop of intensifying global population aging and the mounting pressure on healthcare systems to care for the oldest-old.

Epidemiological evidence indicates that environmental risk factors are known to play a pivotal role in the onset and progression of COPD (6). In addition to smoking, research has identified a range of other environmental risk factors. Among these, ambient particulate matter pollution has been associated with accelerated decline in lung function and elevated COPD mortality (7). Household air pollution from solid fuel combustion, affecting nearly 2.1 billion people worldwide, is a major contributor to COPD burden (8). Occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes account for a substantial fraction of COPD cases in working-age populations, with health effects persisting into later life (9,10). More recently, research has also shown that ambient ozone pollution (11), extreme temperatures (12), and secondhand smoke (13) are associated with elevated risks of COPD incidence and mortality (14,15). Given that environmental risks for COPD are multifactorial, a comprehensive assessment covering major relevant exposure types is necessary to better understand the disease burden among the oldest-old population.

Health inequalities exist in COPD across different socioeconomic statuses (16,17). Almost 90% of COPD deaths in people under 70 years old take place in low- and middle-income countries (2). Besides, environmental factors tied to COPD are not evenly spread across different populations. For instance, household air pollution from burning solid fuels is most common in low- and middle-income countries (8), and low-income groups are more likely to be exposed to ambient air pollution (18). In many low- and middle-income countries, particularly in rural areas and among older populations, access to spirometry (the gold standard for COPD diagnosis) remains limited (19,20). This often leads to underdiagnosis and delayed treatment of COPD in these regions. Meanwhile, in these resource-constrained settings, essential COPD medications such as long-acting bronchodilators and inhaled corticosteroids are frequently unaffordable for local residents or otherwise difficult to obtain (20,21). These circumstances collectively contribute to health inequities in COPD control among populations of low socioeconomic status. Given this context, it is particularly important to elucidate the health inequities in COPD mortality attributable to environmental risk factors across groups of differing socioeconomic status.

Previous studies based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) framework have estimated the COPD burden and its attributable risks at global and regional levels, but several gaps remain. Many rely on broad age bands that blur the physiological vulnerabilities of the oldest-old, so the results are hard to translate into planning for this group (14,22-24). Additionally, although some studies have documented the evolving trends in environment-related COPD mortality, their analyses of environmental risk factors often lack breadth and depth, have not sufficiently compared the relative importance of different hazards across regions and time periods (14,23-25). Moreover, studies focusing on specific environmental exposures typically address only one or a few risk factors, precluding a comprehensive appraisal of the various environmental hazards associated with COPD within this age group (22,26,27). Finally, few studies have systematically examined socioeconomic inequalities in environmentally attributable COPD mortality among the very old, which hampers a comprehensive identification and address the most disadvantaged populations (25,28,29).

Given the rapidly aging global population and the increasing burden of COPD among the oldest-old (aged 80+ years), a comprehensive assessment that integrates age-specific, multi-risk factor, and inequality-focused estimates is urgently needed. Utilizing data from the GBD Study 2021, this study aims to comprehensively and systematically assess the mortality burden of COPD attributable to eight major environment-related risk factors among the global population aged 80 years and above from 1990 to 2021. We analyzed in detail the long-term trends of this burden, differences across sex and age subgroups, and explored in depth its geographical distribution patterns at the global, regional, and national levels. Additionally, this study evaluated health inequalities associated with socioeconomic development levels. This is of critical significance for developing targeted and equitable public health strategies and protecting the most vulnerable populations under the dual pressures of environmental change and aging population. Meanwhile, this study provides scientific evidence for improving WHO guidelines on environmental health for the oldest-old population (30) and for countries to implement Sustainable Development Goal health and climate targets (31), facilitating the operationalization of healthy aging in precision public health. We present this article in accordance with the GATHER reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0133/rc).

Methods

Overview

Data from the GBD 2021 were used to analyze the global trends of COPD from 1990 to 2021. Although more recent GBD 2023 estimates are released, their use for global-level analyses is restricted under the current GBD publication policy. Because the present study required globally comparable estimates to examine worldwide trends and inequalities in COPD mortality attributable to eight environmental risk factors among adults aged 80 years and older, GBD 2021 was the most recent and most appropriate accessible dataset for this analysis. GBD 2021 provides estimates for epidemiological patterns across 371 health conditions, 88 risk factors, and 54 categories of natural injuries within 204 countries and territories (hereafter termed countries) (32). In GBD 2021, COPD was identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes: ICD-9 codes 491–492, and 496, and ICD-10 codes J41–J44, and J47 (32). The clinical definition followed the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease classification, characterized by a post-bronchodilator ratio of forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) to forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.7 (6). The 95% uncertainty interval (UI) for all indicators represents the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the uncertainty distribution, derived from 500 draws of the posterior distribution as per standard GBD methodology (32). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study involved secondary analysis of existing database data with no participant involvement, thus not requiring ethics committee approval.

Estimates of COPD mortality have drawn mainly on vital registration records and surveillance data from the Cause of Death database (32). Verbal autopsy data were not directly included in the COPD-specific model; instead, they were incorporated into the broader chronic respiratory disease model and then redistributed proportionally (32). Notably, the availability and quality of primary input data differed greatly between countries and over time. In the early 1990s, only a limited number of countries—mostly high-income ones—had complete vital registration systems with medical certification of cause of death. In many low- and middle-income countries, the GBD study relied on alternative data sources, such as sample registration systems (e.g., China’s Disease Surveillance Points and India’s Sample Registration System), verbal autopsy studies, mortality estimates modeled using censuses, and hospital-based records (32). The completeness, diagnostic accuracy, and geographic representativeness of these alternative sources were generally lower than those of high-quality vital registration, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South and Southeast Asia during the 1990s. As a result, COPD mortality estimates for earlier years and low-income settings with sparse data tend to have broader uncertainty ranges, and interpretations of these estimates should take this context into account. The GBD study standardized data from multiple ICD revisions. It also applied quality control, outlier removal, and reclassification of nonspecific causes of death. These steps improve consistency and comparability across long time spans and different regions (32). COPD mortality was estimated with the standard Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm). Separate models were built for each sex, using covariates such as smoking exposure, air pollution, the Healthcare Access and Quality Index, and the Socio-demographic Index (SDI). COPD deaths were first combined with those from other chronic respiratory diseases to form a total mortality envelope. The CoDCorrect algorithm was then used for calibration, and deaths were redistributed proportionally to each specific cause. This process enhances the reliability of cross-period and cross-region comparisons (32).

GBD 2021 assessed 88 risk factors in total. Eight of them are environmental factors linked to COPD: ambient ozone pollution, ambient particulate matter pollution, high temperature, household air pollution from solid fuels (HAP), low temperature, occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes, secondhand smoke, and smoking. All eight were modeled within a unified comparative risk assessment framework (33). Exposure levels were estimated according to the data sources and characteristics of each pollutant. Multiple data streams were integrated, including ground monitoring, satellite remote sensing, chemical transport models, household surveys, and administrative records. Spatiotemporal methods such as Spatiotemporal Gaussian Process Regression (ST-GPR) and Bayesian spatial models were then applied to produce complete exposure distribution maps (33). Relative risks were derived with the meta-regression-Bayesian, regularised, trimmed (MR-BRT) tool, which uses Bayesian priors, regularization, and 10% trimming to limit between-study heterogeneity and remove potential outliers (33). Population attributable fractions were computed at the grid-cell level against the theoretical minimum-risk exposure level, followed by population-weighted aggregation. Detailed definitions and modeling methodologies for each risk factor can be found in Table S1.

The SDI, a composite development indicator, was computed based on lag-distributed per capita income, fertility rate among females under 25 years of age, and average educational attainment of the population aged 15 years and older. SDI values range from 0 to 1, with higher values corresponding to higher development levels. Countries were classified into five SDI quintiles (low, low-middle, middle, high-middle, high) based on their 2021 SDI values (32). The proportional contribution of each risk factor was estimated by dividing the number of COPD deaths in individuals aged ≥80 years attributable to each environmental risk factor by the total number of COPD deaths in this age group. Detailed data sources and methods have been reported in the core GBD 2021 publications (32,33). The GBD 2021 data used in this study were accessed via https://gbd2021.healthdata.org/gbd-results/.

Inequality analysis

In this study, we aimed to quantify the absolute and relative inequalities in COPD mortality linked to each risk factor. We calculated the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and the relative concentration index (RCI) across countries for 1990 and 2021 (36,37). The SII measures the absolute difference in health outcomes between populations at the lowest and highest socio-demographic status—here, represented by SDI values. By contrast, the RCI assesses the gap between the cumulative distribution of health outcomes and that of the population sorted by socioeconomic status. This indicator helps indicate whether the disease burden falls more heavily on certain socioeconomic groups (38). Together, the two indicators can help policymakers decide which high-risk populations and regions to prioritize.

The SII was estimated using weighted least squares regression. Mortality rates were regressed on the midpoints of cumulative population ranges, with countries ordered by their SDI (36). The index represents the difference in estimated values between the most advantaged (highest SDI) and the most disadvantaged (lowest SDI) populations. A positive SII value indicates that the disease burden is concentrated in more developed countries, whereas a negative value indicates a concentration in less developed countries. An SII of zero indicates no inequality. A larger absolute value of the SII corresponds to a greater degree of inequality.

The RCI is closely related to the concentration curve (similar to the Lorenz curve), which is plotted by mapping the cumulative percentage of the population, ranked by SDI, against the cumulative percentage of COPD mortality attributable to each risk factor (36). The RCI is defined as twice the area between the concentration curve and the line of perfect equality (the 45-degree diagonal). The index typically ranges from −1 to 1. If the concentration curve lies above the line of perfect equality, the RCI is negative; if it lies below, the RCI is positive. Consistent with the SII, a positive RCI indicates that the health burden is concentrated in more developed countries, while a negative RCI indicates a concentration in less developed countries. A value of zero implies perfect equality. The magnitude of the absolute value reflects the degree of the inequality.

Statistical analysis and graphical visualization were performed using R 4.3.3 software and Adobe Illustrator 2020 software.

Results

Overall mortality and temporal trends

In 2021, among the oldest-old, the top three environmental risk factors for COPD mortality were smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and HAP. The age-specific mortality rates for these factors were 351.85 (95% UI: 268.79–433.10) per 100,000, 241.60 (175.22–299.69) per 100,000, and 157.27 (83.42–292.74) per 100,000, respectively (Table 1; Figure 1). These factors also ranked highest in absolute death counts, accounting for 0.55 million deaths (0.42 million–0.68 million), 0.38 million (0.27 million–0.47 million), and 0.25 million (0.13 million–0.46 million), respectively. From 1990 to 2021, age-specific COPD mortality rates attributable to most environmental risk factors declined, while ambient ozone pollution, ambient particulate matter pollution, and high temperature showed non-significant overall changes (Table 1; Figure 2). The three risk factors with the largest decreases were HAP [−72% (−83% to −53%)], secondhand smoke [−35% (−44% to −22%)], and low temperature [−31% (−39% to −22%)].

From 1990 to 2021, joinpoint regression analysis indicated that COPD mortality attributable to all evaluated environmental risk factors, except high temperature, exhibited non-monotonic trends with identifiable turning points (Table 2). HAP-attributable mortality demonstrated a continuously declining trend that generally accelerated over time. Smoking-related mortality rose steadily from 1990 to 1998, after which it began a sustained decline. Disparities by sex

In 2021, among the oldest-old population, males had higher age-specific COPD mortality rates than females across all evaluated risk factors (Table 1; Figure 1). Furthermore, the composition of the leading risk factors varied between the two sexes. For males, the top three risk factors were smoking [717.70 (95% UI: 555.94–871.14) per 100,000], ambient particulate matter pollution [335.99 (245.85–411.01) per 100,000], and occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes [243.37 (124.27–382.79) per 100,000]. However, female COPD mortality was predominantly attributed to ambient particulate matter pollution [181.00 (123.04–234.92) per 100,000], HAP [141.30 (76.19–250.28) per 100,000] and smoking [116.99 (72.84–169.07) per 100,000]. Between 1990 and 2021, females exhibited greater relative reductions in mortality for most factors compared to males, with the exception of HAP, which showed a slightly smaller reduction in females [−71% (−82% to −50%)] than in males [−75% (−85% to −54%)].

Disparities by age group

In 2021, absolute death counts among the oldest-old population decreased progressively across age groups (85–89, 90–94, and 95+ years) (Table 1; Figure 1). Smoking-related age-specific mortality rates were highest across all three age groups: 436.84 (95% UI: 337.30–535.95) per 100,000, 505.10 (382.50–628.27) per 100,000, and 430.25 (296.38–563.70) per 100,000, respectively, peaking in the 90–94 years age group. From 1990 to 2021, for COPD caused by environmental factors other than HAP, the 95+ years age group showed the most adverse trends, with either the greatest increases or the smallest reductions in COPD mortality rates.

Mortality by SDI

In 2021, regions with low-middle SDI experienced the highest age-specific mortality rates for several risk factors within the oldest-old population (Table 1; Figure 1). Specifically, age-specific mortality rates in these regions were highest for smoking [540.39 (95% UI: 411.45–676.03) per 100,000], ambient ozone pollution [350.90 (77.67–595.66) per 100,000], occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes [252.20 (159.97–351.62) per 100,000], secondhand smoke [139.99 (54.95–229.37) per 100,000], and high temperature [33.91 (−1.08 to 82.73) per 100,000]. Ambient particulate matter pollution and low temperature peaked in middle SDI regions at 425.48 (297.93–531.58) per 100,000 and 143.01 (114.72–175.81) per 100,000, respectively. HAP reached its maximum in low SDI regions at 806.54 (632.57–968.86) per 100,000. From 1990 to 2021, ambient ozone pollution mortality increased in Low-middle and Low SDI regions, while decreasing in High-middle and Middle SDI regions. Most risk factors showed declining mortality in High SDI regions.

Regional and national mortality

The GBD super region of Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania exhibited the highest age-specific mortality rates among the oldest-old population for several risk factors: ambient particulate matter pollution [543.84 (95% UI: 390.80–682.27) per 100,000], low temperature [198.74 (159.22–242.79) per 100,000], occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes [313.74 (200.96–448.12) per 100,000], secondhand smoke [177.92 (71.17–295.53) per 100,000], and smoking [755.29 (566.61–934.08) per 100,000] (Table 1; Figure 1). The South Asia showed highest rates for ambient ozone pollution [475.58 (103.72–804.89) per 100,000], high temperature [42.06 (0–104.00) per 100,000], and HAP [775.90 (506.57–1,105.95) per 100,000]. From 1990 to 2021, age-specific mortality rates due to ambient ozone pollution increased in South Asia [98.4% (63.1–161.9%)] and Sub-Saharan Africa [65.8% (42.9–100.4%)]. Ambient particulate matter pollution mortality rates rose in South Asia [107.1% (27.6–265.3%)]. Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia showed the largest decreases for most factors.

At the national level, the highest age-specific mortality rates per 100,000 in 2021 varied by risk factor (Table S2; Figure 3). Nepal had the highest mortality rates for ambient ozone pollution [677.14 (95% UI: 138.85–1,157.88) per 100,000] and occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes [609.51 (382.52–877.20) per 100,000] and smoking [1,210.94 (834.53–1,613.09) per 100,000]. Other countries ranking highest included China for ambient particulate matter pollution [655.97 (470.78–831.66) per 100,000]; Pakistan for high temperature [74.48 (0.59–185.36) per 100,000]; Papua New Guinea for HAP [1,893.90 (1,343.64–2,508.14) per 100,000] and secondhand smoke [353.09 (134.79–581.97) per 100,000]; and Democratic People’s Republic of Korea for low temperature [292.33 (203.07–459.58) per 100,000]. From 1990 to 2021, ambient ozone pollution mortality increased in most countries, with the largest increases in Madagascar [886.6% (392.1–2,316.1%)], Kenya [582.3% (357.9–890.9%)], and Sri Lanka [503.0% (232.7–1,390.5%)]. Conversely, mortality attributable to HAP decreased in nearly all countries. Belarus, Ukraine, and Singapore reported the largest declines across multiple risk factors.

Socioeconomic health inequalities

The COPD mortality attributable to environmental risk factors among the oldest-old population was disproportionately concentrated across socioeconomic levels (Table 3; Figure 4). Inequalities in mortality linked to ambient ozone pollution were persistent and intensifying. The RCI and SII were both negative in 1990 and 2021, signifying a concentration of mortality among populations with lower socioeconomic status. Compared to 1990, the degrees of both relative and absolute inequality worsened in 2021. For mortality attributable to ambient particulate matter pollution, the degree of absolute inequality has become evident. Although the SII in 1990 was not statistically significant, and relative inequality was insignificant in both years, the SII dropped to −68.52 per 100,000 (95% CI: −108.90 to −28.13) by 2021. This shift indicates a growing absolute inequality, with the mortality burden falling more heavily on groups of lower socioeconomic status. For high temperature-related mortality, both the RCI and SII were also negative, again pointing to a higher burden among less advantaged groups. Compared with 1990, relative inequality in 2021 improved, but absolute inequality became widener. For low temperature-related COPD mortality, the RCI in 2021 was positive [0.166 (95% CI: 0.094–0.237)] and higher than in 1990. This suggests that deaths were concentrated among groups of higher socioeconomic status. In contrast, neither the relative inequality in 1990 nor the absolute inequality in either 1990 or 2021 reached statistical significance. COPD mortality attributable to HAP showed the greatest inequality, with negative RCI and SII values in both 1990 and 2021. By 2021, both measures reached the most extreme levels among all risk factors [RCI: −0.404 per 100,000 (−0.474 to −0.334); SII: −545.59 per 100,000 (−637.30 to −453.89)]. The trend demonstrated relative deterioration alongside absolute alleviation, as relative inequality worsened in 2021 compared to 1990, while absolute inequality was mitigated. Although the mortality associated with occupational particulate matter, gases, and fumes was concentrated in lower SDI regions, both absolute and relative inequalities were reduced in 2021 compared to 1990, with relative inequality no longer being significant in 2021. Secondhand smoke and smoking shared similar patterns of socioeconomic inequality. In 1990, both factors primarily showed relative inequality concentrated in economically underdeveloped regions, while absolute inequality was not significant. However, by 2021, although relative inequality diminished to non-significant levels, the degree of absolute inequality increased significantly, with the burden remaining concentrated in economically underdeveloped regions.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the distribution patterns and long-term trends of COPD mortality attributable to eight environmental risk factors among the global population aged 80 years and older from 1990 to 2021. To our knowledge, this is the first global study on environmental risk factors for COPD mortality that focuses specifically on the oldest-old population, encompasses a comprehensive range of environmental risk factors, and incorporates health inequality analysis. The findings show that although age-specific mortality rates linked to most environmental risk factors have dropped markedly, the related patterns still differ based on geographic region, demographic traits, and socioeconomic status. Within the oldest-old population, smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and HAP remain the primary drivers of COPD deaths. The greatest absolute and relative inequality in 2021 was observed for HAP-attributed COPD deaths.

As the leading risk factor, smoking contributed to the highest age-specific COPD mortality rate and number of deaths among the eight included environmental risk factors in 2021. Although smoking-attributable mortality rates declined overall from 1990 to 2021, exceptions were observed; for instance, Norway experienced the largest increase, at 66.0%. This trend may be explained by the time-lag effect of the tobacco epidemic. According to the classical four-stage model of the tobacco epidemic proposed by Lopez et al., smoking-attributable mortality typically peaks approximately 30–40 years after the peak in smoking prevalence (39,40), because the chronic diseases caused by smoking, particularly COPD, require prolonged cumulative exposure and sustained inflammatory and fibrotic processes before manifesting clinically (41). For the current oldest-old population, their peak smoking periods coincided with the mid-20th century tobacco epidemic, a period preceding major tobacco control legislation and during which smoking prevalence in many high-income countries exceeded 50% among males (42,43). By this age, the cumulative pack-years of smoking may be associated with a range of pathological changes. These include chronic airway remodeling, mucous gland hyperplasia, and progressive loss of alveolar attachments (41,44). However, studies show that quitting smoking is still worthwhile, even late in life. It may slows the decline in lung function, lowers the risk of COPD exacerbations, and extends life expectancy (45-47). From a policy standpoint, integrating age-appropriate smoking cessation support—including pharmacotherapy and behavioral counseling—has been proven effective among the oldest-old (48). Raising tobacco taxes, prohibiting tobacco advertising and promotion, and running anti-smoking media campaigns are regarded as a relatively effective set of measures to lower smoking prevalence and tobacco consumption across all age groups (49,50). Long-term implementation of these measures may help reduce current smoking rates and lifetime tobacco exposure, potentially playing a preventive role in alleviating the disease burden for the next generation of older adults.

Although ambient particulate matter pollution ranks as the second leading risk factor, COPD mortality linked to it has seen little decline worldwide over the past 32 years. South Asia recorded an increase of 107.1%, and lower-SDI regions also exhibited an upward trend. China and India, for example, reported approximately 216,000 and 90,800 COPD deaths, respectively. Large populations, rapid industrialization, and weak pollution controls likely explain this pattern (51). The trend roughly follows the environmental Kuznets curve: pollution tends to rise during the early and middle stages of economic growth, then falls as incomes climb and regulation and technology improve (52). Higher-SDI countries seem to have reached the improvement stage, while lower-SDI regions are still in a phase of rapid industrialization, urban growth, and heavy emissions. Our inequality analysis indicates that the absolute burden of COPD deaths tied to ambient particulate pollution has shifted toward vulnerable populations, though relative inequality remains low and has barely changed over time. The oldest-old are particularly susceptible: age-related declines in mucociliary clearance and antioxidant defense heighten vulnerability to particulate-induced oxidative stress and systemic inflammation (53). In lower-SDI regions, limited resources for exposure reduction and inadequate access to timely medical care compound this vulnerability (54). Addressing this upward trajectory will require targeted interventions in lower-SDI regions and rapidly industrializing areas such as South Asia, with particular attention to reducing exposure among older, high-risk populations. Priority measures include accelerating the transition to clean energy, strengthening investment in pollution control infrastructure, and integrating air quality monitoring into public health surveillance systems.

Our study revealed that, although the age-specific HAP-attributable COPD mortality rate declined by 72.2% between 1990 and 2021, it remained above 1,500 per 100,000 in low-SDI countries such as Papua New Guinea, North Korea, and Nepal. Among the eight environmental factors examined, HAP-attributable COPD mortality in 2021 exhibited the most severe absolute and relative inequalities. Over time, absolute inequality in HAP-related COPD mortality has narrowed, whereas relative inequality has widened, with the burden increasingly concentrated in low-SDI countries. This divergence reflects the asynchronous progress of the global energy transition. According to World Bank estimates, roughly 2.8 billion people worldwide still rely on traditional polluting fuels and technologies for cooking, and 4 billion lack access to clean cooking energy (55). Additionally, 66% of the population in low- and middle-income countries lives in regions where achieving the 2030 Sustainable Development Goal of universal clean energy access is unlikely (56). While higher-SDI countries have largely transitioned to cleaner fuels and stoves, many impoverished areas continue to depend on traditional solid fuels—most notably biomass (such as wood, crop residues, and dung) and coal (57). Unlike clean household energy sources like electricity and gas, the combustion of these solid fuels is often inefficient and incomplete. This process generates significantly higher levels of fine particulate matter, carbon monoxide, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and other toxic by-products (58). Similar to cigarette smoke, this combustion mixture contains multiple carcinogenic compounds and a remarkably high carbonaceous core content, and its inhalation may trigger persistent macrophage-mediated inflammation that contributes to both acute airway hyperresponsiveness and chronic airway remodeling, ultimately accelerating airflow limitation in COPD (8). For older adults in these settings, chronic exposure to these pollutants may interact with age-related frailty, poor household ventilation, and limited access to healthcare, thereby sustaining a disproportionately high COPD burden (59). Reducing HAP-related COPD burden may involve accelerating the clean energy transition in the most affected low-SDI countries. Targeted subsidies and the promotion of clean cookstoves could help expand access to cleaner household energy in lower-SDI countries. At the same time, community-led approaches and sustained investment in infrastructure may help ensure that vulnerable older adults benefit. In addition, many of the hardest-hit low-SDI countries face limited domestic resources. Advancing their clean energy transition is therefore likely to depend on stronger international financing mechanisms and cross-regional cooperation.

Although global age-specific COPD mortality linked to ambient ozone pollution showed no notable change, marked increases were observed in South Asia (98.4%) and sub-Saharan Africa (65.8%), regions that often experience high temperatures and intense solar radiation. This trend may reflect the complex interplay between climate change and atmospheric photochemistry. Ozone is a secondary pollutant formed when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react under ultraviolet light (18). As global temperatures rise, this reaction tends to proceed more rapidly. Warmer conditions can also stimulate vegetation to release more biogenic volatile organic compounds, adding to the pool of ozone precursors (60,61). Hot meteorological conditions may further promote ozone accumulation at ground level (18). Notably, inequalities in ozone-attributable COPD mortality increased from 1990 to 2021, with mortality increasingly concentrated in lower-SDI countries. Previous studies have shown that climate change may amplify the inequitable exposure to compound heatwave and ozone pollution (62). Potential contributing factors to this widening gap include the concentration of polluting industries in lower-income regions (63), limited policy prioritization and investment in pollution control in many areas where mortality is highest (64), and the unequal distribution of climate change impacts (65). Addressing the unequal distribution of climate-sensitive air pollution, through measures such as precursor emission controls and climate-adaptive public health planning, warrants policy attention (66).

Age-specific COPD mortality rates attributable to high temperature showed no significant changes during the study period, but high temperatures can affect COPD patients through multiple mechanisms including potentially inducing airway inflammation and oxidative stress, promoting pollutant formation, and increasing respiratory infection risk (67,68). One possible explanation is that adaptation measures such as wider access to cooling and improvements in healthcare may have partly mitigated the adverse effects in some settings. As the global climate continues to warm and extreme heat events become more frequent, the importance of this risk factor may become increasingly prominent (69).

Males had higher age-specific COPD mortality rates than females associated with most environmental risk factors. For males, the highest mortality was attributable to smoking and ambient particulate matter exposure—this may be tied to the fact that males have historically initiated tobacco use at an earlier age and had a higher prevalence of use, as well as gender-based differences in occupational patterns (9,39). Ambient particulate matter and hazardous air pollutants were identified as leading risk factors for COPD in women. This pattern likely stems from the gendered division of household labor and the resulting exposure to indoor air pollution (70). The 95+ years age group experienced the most adverse trends between 1990 and 2021. This observation indicates that as population aging progresses, the environmental health vulnerability of the oldest-old population is becoming increasingly prominent—a factor that deserves specific attention when formulating public health policies.

Evidence indicates that patients with COPD faced markedly higher risks throughout the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Although these acute pandemic-related factors likely caused fluctuations in the absolute number of COPD deaths between 2020 and 2021, they are unlikely to have substantially distorted our core measure—the estimated share of COPD mortality attributable to environmental exposures. This can be explained by three key reasons. First, the GBD 2021 framework included methodological adjustments to account for excess mortality and reclassifications of cause of death during the pandemic period (32,33). Second, under the Comparative Risk Assessment framework, estimates of environmentally attributable COPD mortality are calculated using population attributable fractions based on well-established long-term exposure-response relationships. Acute pandemic-driven changes, such as competing mortality risks from severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection or temporary barriers to healthcare access, are not expected to significantly alter the relative risk patterns shaped by decades of cumulative exposure to smoking, occupational hazards, and air pollution. Even if the total number of deaths shifted, the underlying proportion of COPD mortality linked to environmental factors is theoretically robust against such short-term shocks. Third, this theoretical expectation is supported by our empirical data. Both the overall temporal trends (Figure 2) and joinpoint regression analyses (Table 2) show no significant structural shifts in mortality patterns during 2020–2021, with trajectories remaining largely consistent with pre-pandemic baselines observed in 2019.

This study has several notable limitations. To begin with, our analysis is based on estimates from the GBD 2021 study. Primary data supporting estimates for the early 1990s and low-income countries with limited vital registration systems is far sparser and of poorer quality, compared with data from more recent years and high-income settings. While the GBD modelling framework compensates for this through spatiotemporal smoothing, covariate-driven imputation and ensemble modelling, estimates for these location-years remain more dependent on model outputs and carry greater uncertainty, as shown by wider UIs. For this reason, temporal trends across the full 1990–2021 period should be interpreted carefully: observed changes may partly reflect improvements in data availability and reporting quality, rather than being driven solely by epidemiological transitions. What is more, underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of COPD are likely more prevalent in low-income regions with weak healthcare infrastructure and limited diagnostic capacity, especially where pulmonary function testing is limited for people aged 80 years and above. This may lead to underestimates of actual mortality in this group and potentially bias cross-regional comparisons. Second, metrics for measuring health inequality need to be interpreted with caution. In this study, the calculation of the SII and RCI did not include the CIs of the mortality estimates themselves, which may affect the precision of statistical inferences regarding inequality trends. Third, the GBD framework does not explicitly distinguish between different clinical phenotypes of COPD and does not include all potential environmental and occupational risk factors, which limits a more granular exploration of risk mechanisms. Fourth, potential cohort effects could not be formally evaluated in this study because the available age stratification for adults aged 80 years and older was limited to three closed age groups (80–84, 85–89, and 90–94 years) and one open-ended age group (≥95 years), which limited the performance of robust Age-Period-Cohort analysis. Therefore, we could not fully disentangle age, period, and cohort effects.

Conclusions

This study of the global population aged 80 years and above reveals that although COPD mortality related to environmental risk factors has declined over the past 32 years, smoking, ambient particulate matter pollution, and HAP remain the three leading risk factors. Mortality rates were generally higher among males, populations in East and South Asia, and those in low-SDI regions, and health inequalities have persisted or intensified for several risk factors. These findings underscore the need for targeted and stratified preventive strategies tailored to the oldest-old population. Priority interventions should focus on strengthening tobacco control measures and age-appropriate smoking cessation support for high-risk groups. For low SDI regions and rapidly industrializing areas such as South Asia, accelerated transitions to clean energy, expanded implementation of clean cooking technologies, and enhanced pollution control infrastructure are likely critical to reducing exposures to ambient particulate matter and HAP. Given the increasing burden of ambient ozone related mortality in warmer, lower-income regions, environmental policies should incorporate climate-sensitive air quality management to mitigate disproportionate risks to vulnerable populations.
