Canonical Thermodynamics
Arnaldo Spalvieri

Abstract
In the paper, thermodynamics of canonical systems is derived from the multinomial distribution of the occupancy numbers of quantum eigenstates. The cathegorical distribution (i.e. the one-particle distribution) on which the multinomial distribution is based, should be derived from the maximum entropy principle, but, being the multinomial distribution intractable, the paper proposes to take instead the Boltzmann distribution as cathegorical distribution, discussing the reason why the entropy of the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution should closely approximate the entropy achieved by the multinomial distribution equipped with the entropy-maximizing cathegorical distribution. After this, the imposition of Clausius’ equation on the Shannon entropy of the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution leads to an unexpected result: in general, the Lagrange multiplier β \beta that imposes the energy constraint in constrained entropy maximization turns out to be substantially different from the inverse temperature. To support this unexpected result, the paper presents an example where, with β \beta equal to the inverse temperature, the thermodynamic entropy (i.e. the entropy at a given temperature) of the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution is greater than the Bose-Einstein thermodynamic entropy. However, the latter is derived from entropy maximization with constrained expected energy and expected number of particles, therefore if we plug β \beta equal to the inverse temperature in the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution, then the thermodynamics of the canonical system obtained in this way turns out to be non-compatible with the principle of maximum entropy. Keywords: Thermodynamics, Canonical Systems, Occupancy Numbers, Multinomial Distribution, Canonical Typicality, Maximum Entropy Principle, Gibbs Correction Factor, Sackur-Tetrode Entropy Formula.



I
Introduction

In an infinitesimally small reversible transformation at constant
density (number of particles over volume), the common temperature

T
expressed in Kelvin degrees of heat bath and system at the
equilibrium with it is defined by Clausius’ equation

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{d\mu_{\cal E}}=\frac{1}{T},

(1)

where
\mu_{\cal E}
is system’s expected internal energy and

S
, expressed in
\mbox{J}\cdot\mbox{K}^{-1}
, is the
thermodynamic entropy of the system. For this definition to be
operative we need a definition of
S
, and this definition must
hold also in the quantum regime. However, form the times of Gibbs,
there is a long debate, still today ongoing, about the definition
of
S
for systems belonging to the canonical ensemble, i.e.
closed systems (with
closed system
we mean a system with a
fixed number of particles) that exchange energy with a thermal
reservoir of virtually unlimited heat capacity (the heat bath) at
the thermal equilibrium with it.

The famous Gibbs paradox shows that the sought entropy cannot be
the Shannon-Gibbs entropy of the Boltzmann distribution of
microstates. In fact, Gibbs introduced the correction term

\log(N!)
to the entropy of microstates, where
N
is the number
of particles of the canonical system, to make it consistent, at
least in the classical case, with the thermodynamic entropy. With
Stirling’s approximation

\log(N!)\approx N(\log(N)-1),

(2)

this term is the
fundamental ingredient of the Sackur-Tetrode entropy formula.
However, the Sackur-Tetrode entropy formula holds only in the
classical regime, as it is apparent from the fact that it can
become negative in the quantum regime. Besides this limitation, it
should also be observed that the term
-\log(N!)
is a sort of

deus ex machina
whose introduction has been justified in the
past by arguments based on the difference between distinguishable
particles and indistinguishable particles, but this argument is
questionable. This author fully agrees with the following passage
from Swedensen
[
1
]
, which is of crystal-clear clarity:

…the properties of a classical ideal gas do not depend on
whether distinguishable or indistinguishable particles are used.
Consequently, a proper definition of the entropy should give the
same answer in both cases. Specifically, factor of 1/N! should be
present in both cases. This means that the results of computer
simulations with distinguishable particles should be compared
directly with the results of real experiments in the classical
regime; no correction factors are needed
(in the classical
regime, we remark).

In the end, the term
-\log(N!)
is still today object of debate
and discussion, see for instance
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.
More generally, still today the thermodynamic entropy of such a
system is object of ongoing research, see e.g. the recent paper

[
5
]
.

The picture changes radically if, in place of canonical systems,
we consider grand-canonical systems, i.e. open systems that are at
the thermal equilibrium with the heat bath and that randomly
exchanges particles with it along time. An example of
grand-canonical system is the blackbody, a system at the thermal
equilibrium with the surrounding environment that, through
radiation and absorption, exchanges photons with it. By
constrained entropy maximization, Bose shows in
[
6
]
that
the occupancy number of each quantum state of the blackbody is a
geometrically distributed random variable independent of the
occupancy numbers of the other quantum states. Due to the
randomness of the processes of radiation and absorption, each
random variable randomly changes its value along time
independently of the other random variables, and therefore also
the sum of these random variables randomly changes in time. An
intriguing analogy exists between the blackbody and queueing
theory: also the number of customers enqueued in an M/M/1 queue is
geometrically distributed, see e.g.
[
7
]
, so the number
of photons populating a quantum state of the blackbody behave
statistically as the number of customers enqueued in an M/M/1
queue. Einstein, starting from Bose’s approach, analyzes the
monoatomic ideal gas
[
8
]
, the main difference
between the two analyses being that, to deal with the density of
the gas, Einstein adds the
expected
number of system’s
particles among the constraints of constrained entropy
maximization. Einstein calls this constraint
the number of
particles
, but, as it is clear from his results, system’s number
of particles is a random variable and the constraint is actually
the mean value of system’s random number of particles. The result
of Einstein’s constrained entropy maximization is again that the
occupancy numbers of the quantum states are geometrically
distributed random variables independent between them. Finally,
Bose and Einstein show that, imposing Clausius’ equation, the
thermodynamic entropy of both the photon gas and the gas of
massive particles is obtained when the Lagrange multiplier
\beta

that imposes the energy constraint in constrained entropy
maximization is equal to the inverse of the temperature of system
and environment multiplied by Boltzmann’s constant. However, the
particles of a massive gas enclosed in a container do not join and
leave the container, therefore, although Einstein’s analysis can
lead to very good approximations also in the case of closed
systems, strictly speaking it does not apply to a gas of massive
particles confined inside a region of space bounded by walls.

To sum up, while it is universally recognized that the
thermodynamic entropy of a grand canonical system is the Shannon
entropy of his occupancy numbers multiplied by Boltzmann’s
constant, and nobody doubts of this result from more than 100
years, we must recognize that, for canonical systems, despite the
160 years of debate and research that followed the first
appearance of eqn. (
1
), scientists have not still
reached a wide agreement about the entropy
S
appearing in
(
1
). To our opinion, this basically happens because
there is not still today wide agreement about the probability
distribution of the occupancy numbers of a canonical system.
Pathria and Beale observe in 1.6 and 5.4 of their book

[
9
]
that, besides the term
-\log(N!)
, also the
occupancy numbers of the individual quantum eigenstates should
have a role in the macroscopic description of a closed system of
massive particles at the thermal equilibrium in the quantum
regime, but, as far as we know, till today the consequences of
this observation are unexplored. In this paper we fill this gap,
first introducing the probability distribution of the occupancy
numbers of a canonical system, then applying Clausius’ equation to
its Shannon entropy.

II
Multinomial distribution of the occupancy
numbers in the bosonic canonical ensemble

Consider a closed system of
N
particles of the same species, let

{\bar{c}}=(c_{1},c_{2},\cdots,c_{N})
be the random eigenstate where a
PVM leaves the system (the
microstate
) with
c_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}},\ i=1,2,\cdots,N,

{\mathbb{C}}
being the set of
quantum eigenstates allowed to a particle. In the following we
assume that particles’ eigenstates are independent and identically
distributed (i.i.d.) random variables. Independency seems to be a
reasonable assumption for systems of non-interacting particles,
while identical distribution seems to be a reasonable assumption
for systems at the equilibrium. With the i.i.d. assumption we
have that the probability
P_{\bar{c}}
of microstate
\bar{c}
is

\displaystyle P_{\bar{c}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}P_{c_{i}}=\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}P_{c}^{n_{c}},

(3)

where
P_{c_{i}}
is the probability that the PVM leaves the
i
-th
particle in eigenstate
c_{i}
, the first equality is the assumption
of independency, the second equality is the assumption of
identical distribution, that is

Pr(c_{1}=c)=Pr(c_{2}=c)=\cdots=Pr(c_{N}=c)=P_{c},

and

\displaystyle n_{c}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1,&\mbox{if}\ c_{i}=c,\\
0,&\mbox{elsewhere},\end{array}\right.

is the random
occupancy number
of
eigenstate
c
, i.e. the random number of particles that the PVM
leaves in eigenstate
c
. The i.i.d. assumption is not new at all.
For instance, quantum eigenstates are i.i.d. random variables also
in the universally accepted Boltzmann probability distribution of
microstates, which in fact is the special case of (
3
)
obtained with

\displaystyle P_{c}=Z^{-1}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}},

(6)

where
\epsilon_{c}
is the energy eigenvalue of eigenstate
c
,

\beta
is the Lagrange multiplier that imposes the energy
constraint in Jaynes’ constrained maximization of the entropy of
microstates
[
10
]
, and
Z
is the one-particle partition
function:

Z=\sum_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}}.

(7)

In what follows, we
will call the probability distribution (
6
) the
one-particle Boltzmann distribution
, independently of the meaning
that we attribute to
\beta
, that is, also for
\beta
not equal
to the inverse temperature.

The most complete description of the system at the macroscopic
level is the vector
{\bar{n}}=(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{|{\mathbb{C}}|})
of the random occupancy numbers, that we call the
occupancy macrostate
or, simply,
macrostate
. In the context
of probability theory, the random experiment that generates the
random vector of the occupancy numbers consists of

•

a fixed number
N
of trials

•

each
trial is independent of the others

•

each trial has
|{\mathbb{C}}|
mutually exclusive outcomes

•

on each trial, the outcome

c
occurs with probability
P_{c}
,
c\in{\mathbb{C}}

•

the
occupancy number
n_{c}
counts the number of trials for which the
outcome
c
occurs.

It is well known that
the random vector of the occupancy numbers generated by such a
random experiment is multinomially distributed. This can be easily
proved as follows. Since any two random vectors
\bar{c}_{i}
and

\bar{c}_{k}
are mutually exclusive, the probability
P_{\bar{n}}

of macrostate
\bar{n}
in a system of
N
particles is

\displaystyle P_{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bar{{c}}\in\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}}P_{\bar{c}},

(8)

where
\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}
is the set of distinct microstates
whose occupancy macrostate is
\bar{n}
. The number of microstates

|\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}|
belonging to
\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}
is
equal to the multinomial coefficient, that we indicate with

W_{\bar{n}}
,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}|=W_{\bar{n}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{N!}{\prod_{c\mbox{\tiny$\in$}\mathbb{C}}n_{c}!},&\mbox{if}\ \sum_{c\mbox{\tiny$\in$}\mathbb{C}}n_{c}=N,\\
0,&\mbox{elsewhere},\end{array}\right.

therefore, in view of the equiprobability of
microstates descending from our assumptions, one concludes that
the probability distribution of macrostate
\bar{n}
must be

\displaystyle P_{\bar{n}}

\displaystyle=W_{\bar{n}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}P_{c}^{n_{c}},

(11)

that is the multinomial distribution. The one-particle
distribution
\{P_{c}\}
that equips the above multinomial
distribution is called in probability theory the
cathegorical
distribution or
generalized Bernoulli

distribution, the trials being generalized (non-binary) Bernoulli
trials and the
|{\mathbb{C}}|
cathegories being the
|{\mathbb{C}}|
eigenstates. With two cathegories, the trials are Bernoulli
trials and the multinomial distribution reduces to the binomial
distribution.
Recently, starting
from the quantum state
\ket{\bar{n}}
of the bosonic system
expressed as

\displaystyle\ket{\bar{n}}=\sum_{\bar{c}\in\mathbb{C}_{\bar{n}}}\frac{\ket{\bar{c}}}{\sqrt{W_{\bar{n}}}},

(12)

see e.g.
[
11
]
and chapter 7 of
[
12
]
,
this author has shown in
[
13
]
that, both in the
empirical approach of canonical typicality of

[
14
,
15
]
, and in the Bayesian approach, the
density operator
\hat{\nu}
of the mixed state of the bosonic
system is characterized by the multinomial distribution:

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}=\sum_{\bar{n}\in\mathbb{O}}W_{\bar{n}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}P_{c}^{n_{c}}\ket{\bar{n}}\bra{\bar{n}},

where

\mathbb{O}
is the set of occupancy macrostates allowed to a
system with
N
particles. See also
[
16
,
17
]
for the
multinomial distribution in statistical mechanics. The multinomial
distribution finds wide application in data analysis, where the
measured vector
\bar{n}
generates the empirical cathegorical
distribution
\{P_{c}=n_{c}/N\}
. In this context, paper

[
18
]
demonstrates that the multinomial distribution is
the maximum entropy distribution among all the distributions of
occupancy numbers (among which, for instance, the multivariate
hypergeometric distribution) with the same the cathegorical
distribution and the same value of
N
.

The Shannon entropy of the multinomial distribution, call it

H_{m}
, which is equal to the von-Neumann entropy of
\hat{\nu}
,
is

\displaystyle H_{m}=-E\{\log(P_{\bar{n}})\}

\displaystyle=-E\{\log(W_{\bar{n}})\}-NE\{\log(P_{c})\}

(13)

\displaystyle=NH_{c}-\log(N!)+\sum_{c\in\mathbb{C}}E\{\log(n_{c}!)\},

(14)

where
H_{c}
is the Shannon entropy of the cathegorical
distribution and
E\{\cdot\}
denotes the expectation over the
probability distribution of the random variable appearing inside
the curly brackets, specifically, in (
14
)

\displaystyle\hskip-7.11317ptE\{\log(n_{c}!)\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{c}=0}^{N}P_{n_{c}}\log(n_{c}!)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{c}=0}^{N}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}N\\
n_{c}\end{array}\right)P_{c}^{n_{c}}(1-P_{c})^{N-n_{c}}\log(n_{c}!),\hskip-4.26773pt

where

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}N\\
n\end{array}\right)=\frac{N!}{(N-n)!n!}

is the binomial coefficient. Looking at (
14
), we
immediately recognize that
NH_{c}
is the Shannon-Gibbs entropy of
microstates and that
-\log(N!)
is the Gibbsian correction term.
The novelty introduced by (
14
) is the sum

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{\log(n_{c}!)\},

(17)

that, added to the first two
terms, makes
H_{m}
a legal Shannon entropy and guarantees
H_{m}\geq 0
also in the quantum regime. This sum, which is negligible
when, as it happens in the classical regime, the probability that
two or more particles occupy the same quantum state, is the answer
given by the multinomial distribution to the issue raised by
Pathria and Beale in 1.6 and 5.4 of their textbook
[
9
]

about the role that the product
\prod_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}n_{c}!

should have in system’s entropy. The computational burden of the
numerical evaluation of this term can be mitigated as suggested in

[
19
,
20
]
.

III
Maximum entropy of the multinomial distribution

When applied to our specific case, the general principle of
constrained entropy maximization asks to find the cathegorical
distribution that maximizes the Shannon entropy of the multinomial
distribution of the occupancy numbers with constraints

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}P_{c}=1,

\displaystyle N\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\epsilon_{c}P_{c}=\mu_{\cal E}.

(18)

The attempts that we made to find the sought cathegorical
distribution in closed form failed, therefore, in the conclusive
section, we will present the results of numerical constrained
maximization, which remains feasible.

The difficulty encountered by the exact entropy-maximizing
cathegorical distribution pushes us to search for tractable
approximations to it. To this aim, we observe that, in the
classical regime, the sum (
17
) can be ignored
because the multinomial coefficient becomes constant and equal to

\log(N!)
, therefore constrained maximization of the entropy of
the multinomial distribution reduces to constrained maximization
of the entropy of microstates, leading to the one-particle
Boltzmann distribution. Furthermore, the entropy-maximizing
one-particle probability distribution can be approximated to the
one-particle Boltzmann distribution whenever the zeros of the
partial derivatives of the Lagrangian remain substantially
unchanged when the sum (
17
) is added to the
entropy of microstates. This can happen in a much wider regime
than the classical one. Actually, note that, whenever

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{\log(W_{\bar{n}})\}\approx\alpha H_{c},

(19)

with

0<\alpha<1
, the one-particle distribution resulting from
constrained maximization of the entropy of the multinomial
distribution will be close to the one-particle distribution
resulting from constrained maximization of the entropy of
microstates. We discuss the above approximation starting from the
application of Strirling’s approximation (
2
) to each
term of the sum (
17
), leading to

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84544pt\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{\log(W_{\bar{n}})\}

\displaystyle\approx N(\log(N)-1)-\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{n_{c}(\log(n_{c})-1)\}

\displaystyle=N\log(N)-\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{n_{c}\log(n_{c})\}

(20)

\displaystyle=\log(N)\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{n_{c}\}-\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{n_{c}\log(n_{c})\}

\displaystyle=NE\left\{\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{n_{c}}{N}\log\left(\frac{N}{n_{c}}\right)\right\}.

(21)

In practice, we can include in the set
{\mathbb{C}}
only those
eigenstates having non-negligible probability, because only these
eigenstates will give a non-negligible contribution both to the
Shannon entropy of the cathegorical distribution and to the above
sum. The number of these eigenstates will be small in the quantum
regime, where the vast majority of particles occupy eigenstates
with energy eigenvalues close to the ground level, therefore, for
large
N
, these few eigenstates will be well-populated, enabling
the use of Stirling’s approximation.
1
1

1

Stirling’s
approximation alone is not enough to conclude that the
one-particle Boltzmann distribution is the cathegorical
distribution that maximizes the entropy of the multinomial
distribution with energy constraint. To see this, we write

\displaystyle d\left(NP_{c}\log(NP_{c})\right)-d\left(E\left\{n_{c}\log(n_{c})\right\}\right)=

\displaystyle=\log(E\left\{n_{c}\right\})E\left\{dn_{c}\right\}-E\left\{\log(n_{c})dn_{c}\right\}\neq 0,

(22)

therefore the gradient of
NH_{c}
w.r.t.
\{P_{c}\}
has not the same
zeros of the gradient of (
20
) w.r.t.
\{P_{c}\}
.

If, based on the above argument, we accept
(
21
), then (
19
) becomes

\displaystyle E\left\{\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{n_{c}}{N}\log\left(\frac{N}{n_{c}}\right)\right\}\approx\alpha H_{c},

(23)

which is strongly intuitive, because the argument of the
expectation is the Maximum Likelihood Estimator of
H_{c}
based on
the sample
{\bar{c}}
. Passing from intuition to mathematics, in
support of (
23
) we cite paper
[
21
]
,
where the author demonstrates that the difference

H_{c}-E\left\{\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{n_{c}}{N}\log\left(\frac{N}{n_{c}}\right)\right\}

is sandwiched as

\displaystyle 0\leq H_{c}-E\left\{\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{n_{c}}{N}\log\left(\frac{N}{n_{c}}\right)\right\}\leq\log\left(1+\frac{|{\mathbb{C}}|-1}{N}\right).

Coming to the
multinomial distributions, for its entropy we have from
(
21
)

\displaystyle H_{m}

\displaystyle\approx N\cdot E\left\{D\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{N}||{\bar{P}}\right)\right\},

(24)

where

\displaystyle D\left(\frac{\bar{n}}{N}||{\bar{P}}\right)=\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{n_{c}}{N}\log\left(\frac{n_{c}}{NP_{c}}\right),

(25)

is the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the empirical
cathegorical distribution and its limit for
N\rightarrow\infty
. Bounds on the tails of the probability distribution of
the random Kullback-Leibler divergence (
25
) can be found in

[
22
]
.

Based on these considerations and on the numerical results to be
shown in a coming section of this paper, we conclude that, both in
the quantum regime and in the classical regime, the one-particle
Boltzmann distribution can closely approximate the
entropy-maximizing cathegorical distribution and consider in what
follows the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution of the occupancy
numbers:

\displaystyle P_{\bar{{n}}}

\displaystyle=W_{\bar{{n}}}Z^{-N}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}n_{c}}

\displaystyle=\frac{W_{\bar{{n}}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}n_{c}}}{\sum_{{\bar{n}}\in{\mathbb{O}}}W_{\bar{{n}}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta n_{c}\epsilon_{c}}},

where in the second equality we have substituted the multinomial
expansion of system’s partition function
Z^{N}
,

\displaystyle Z^{N}=\left(\sum_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}}\right)^{N}=\sum_{{\bar{n}}\in{\mathbb{O}}}W_{\bar{{n}}}\prod_{c\in\mathbb{C}}e^{-\beta n_{c}\epsilon_{c}}.

The expected energy of the system with multinomial-Boltzmann
distribution of the occupancy numbers is equal to the expected
energy obtained from the Boltzmann distribution with the same

\beta
, that is

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mu_{\cal E}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{Z}\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\epsilon_{c}e^{-\beta\epsilon_{c}}.

(26)

The Shannon entropy of the multinomial-Boltzmann distribution is

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptH_{m,B}

\displaystyle=\beta\mu_{\cal E}+N\log(Z)-E\{\log(W_{\bar{{n}}})\},

(27)

which, in the classical regime, is approximated as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptH_{m,B}

\displaystyle\approx\beta\mu_{\cal E}+N\log(Z)-\log(N!).

(28)

IV
Thermodynamic entropy of canonical quantum systems

If we recognize
that, when casted in the form of von-Neumann entropy, the Shannon
entropy of the multinomial distribution of the occupancy numbers
multiplied by Boltzmann’s constant can represent the thermodynamic
entropy of a canonical quantum system, and if, by the arguments
discussed in the previous section, we take for the cathegorical
distribution that equips the multinomial distribution the
one-particle Boltzmann distribution, then we find the
\beta
of
the one-particle Boltzmann’s distribution by imposing Clausius’
equation
(
1
):

\displaystyle\frac{dS}{kd\mu_{\cal E}}=\frac{dH_{m,B}}{d\mu_{\cal E}}=\beta+\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{dE\{\log(n_{c}!)\}}{d\mu_{\cal E}}=\frac{1}{kT},

(29)

where

k=1.38\cdot 10^{-23}\ \mbox{J}\cdot\mbox{K}^{-1}

is Boltmann’s constant and the derivatives inside the sum are
related to
\beta
by (
II
), (
18
),
(
6
), (
7
). In practice, the above

\beta
can be found numerically by approximating the derivatives
to the ratio of finite increments:

\displaystyle\frac{dE\{\log(n_{c}!)\}}{d\mu_{\cal E}}\approx\frac{E\{[\log(n_{c}!)]_{\beta+\Delta}\}-E\{[\log(n_{c}!)]_{\beta}\}}{[\mu_{\cal E}]_{\beta+\Delta}-[\mu_{\cal E}]_{\beta}}.

In the classical regime, the probability that two or more
particles occupy the same quantum state becomes negligible,
therefore the terms that characterize the quantum regime are
approximately zero:

\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}E\{\log(n_{c}!)\}\approx 0,

\sum_{c\in{\mathbb{C}}}\frac{dE\{\log(n_{c}!)\}}{d\mu_{\cal E}}\approx 0.

These two approximations lead to the classical
\beta
,

\beta\approx\frac{1}{kT},

(30)

and, after that, to all the
thermodynamics of classical canonical systems.

Equation (
29
) is the main novelty of this paper.
It says for the first time that
\beta
, which, before the
imposition of Clausius’ equation, is a merely mathematical entity,
after the imposition of Clausius’ equation becomes a physical
entity that, here is the novelty, can be different from the
inverse temperature. It is equal to the inverse temperature for a
one-particle system both in the classical and in the quantum
regimes. It is equal to the inverse temperature in the classical
regime whichever is the number of particles. However, in the
quantum regime it depends on the number of particles, showing one
more example where thermodynamics of small systems can be
different from that of large systems, even at the equilibrium.

V
Ideal quantum gas

In the case of a non-relativistic monoatomic
particle in a D-dimensional box with all the sides of length
L
,
the energy eigenvalues with aperiodic boundary conditions are

\epsilon_{i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{D}}=\sum_{k=1}^{D}\frac{h^{2}i_{k}^{2}}{8mL^{2}},\ 1\leq D\leq 3,\ i_{k}=0,1,\cdots,\hskip-8.5359pt

(31)

where
m
is the mass of the particle and

h=6.626\cdot 10^{-34}\ \mbox{J}\cdot\mbox{s}

is
Planck’s constant.

Energy and entropy in the classical regime can be derived from our
approach simply by neglecting the sum (
17
),
without the need of the
ad hoc
introduction of the Gibbsian
correction term. Using also the standard approximation of the
Z

function to an integral, the result is

\displaystyle\mu_{\cal E}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{NDkT}{2},

(32)

\displaystyle\hskip-11.38092ptS

\displaystyle\approx k\left(\frac{ND}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{L^{2}2\pi mkT}{h^{2}}\right)+1\right)-\log(N!)\right)

(33)

\displaystyle\approx kN\left(\log\left(\frac{L^{D}}{Nh^{D}}\sqrt{(2\pi mkT)^{D}}\right)+\frac{D}{2}+1\right),

(34)

where in the second approximation we use Stirling’s approximation
(
2
). As expected, the last formula is the textbook
Sackur-Tetrode entropy formula.

In the following section, we will present numerical results
obtained by our approach and by the Bose-Einstein approach,
therefore, for completeness, we recall here also the formulas of
Einstein’s analysis of the quantum gas that we use to derive the
numerical results. While in the semi-classical Einstein’s analysis
quantum states are cells of volume
h^{D}
in phase space, here we
adopt the modern way of expressing Einstein’s results, which is
based on the energy eigenvalues (

), see e.g.

[

]
. The entropy-maximizing distribution, with system’s
energy
\mu_{\cal E}
and system’s expected number of particles

\mu_{N}
as constraints, is the product of geometric
distributions, one for each quantum eigenstate. The expected
number of particles in a quantum eigenstate with energy eigenvalue

\epsilon
is the celebrated Bose-Einstein statistics:

\mu_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta\epsilon+\gamma}-1},

where
\beta
and
\gamma
are the two Lagrange multipliers that
impose the two constraints. Given the two constraints, the two
Lagrange multipliers are found by solving the system made by the
two equations

\displaystyle\mu_{\cal E}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\epsilon\in{\cal E}}\frac{m_{\epsilon}\epsilon}{e^{\beta\epsilon+\gamma}-1},

(35)

\mu_{N}=\sum_{\epsilon\in{\cal E}}\frac{m_{\epsilon}}{e^{\beta\epsilon+\gamma}-1},

(36)

where
{\cal E}
is the set of the energy eigenvalues (
31
) and

m_{\epsilon}
is the multiplicity of the energy eigenvalue

\epsilon
, i.e. the number of
D
-tuples
(i_{1},i_{2},\cdots,i_{D})

that, substituted in (
31

\epsilon
as a
result. The Shannon entropy of the system is

\displaystyle H_{BE}=\sum_{\epsilon\in{\cal E}}m_{\epsilon}\left(\frac{\beta\epsilon+\gamma}{e^{\beta\epsilon+\gamma}-1}-\log(1-e^{-\beta\epsilon-\gamma})\right).

(37)

After the imposition of
Clausius’ equation (
1

\beta

(kT)^{-1}
(in this case, this
happens also in the quantum regime), and
-\gamma kT
is what
today we call the
chemical potential
.
Also the Bose-Einstein
approach brings by itself the Gibbsian correction term in the
entropy formula. The results for energy and entropy in the

VI
Conclusive discussion

In this section we present our conclusive comments. In doing this,
we help ourselves with numerical results obtained, for the
Bose-Einstein approach and for our approach, with particles of
mass
1.6735575\cdot 10^{-27}
kg in a 1D box of length
10^{-7}

m. The expected number of particles is always equal to 100. In our
case the number of particles can formally be seen as a random
variable with mean value equal to 100 and with delta-type
distribution, so both the approaches meet the same constraint on
the expected number of particles and we expect that, with the same
expected energy, the entropy obtained from the Bose-Einstein
approach will always be not lower than the entropy of our
approach. We compare the Bose-Einstein entropy to ours not because
we want to compare the entropy of a grand-canonical system to the
entropy of a canonical system, rather because we want to compare
the entropy of the multinomial distribution with the
entropy-maximizing distribution with the same constraints. Note
that the maximum entropy property of the multinomial distribution
previously mentioned refers to different constraints than those
imposed here.

Before the conclusion of this paper, we comment one more time a
result that, for sure, is unexpected by most readers. As it is
clear from the Gibbs paradox, from the universally recognized
success of the Bose-Einstein approach based on the occupancy
numbers of grand-canonical systems, from the quantum state
(
12
), and, in the end, from the fact that thermodynamics
deals with macroscopic properties of the system, microstates are
not a thermodynamic entity. The entire thermodynamics of systems
at the equilibrium descends from the statistical properties of the
occupancy numbers and, for this reason, there is no doubt that
thermodynamics can survive without the concept itself of
microstates and without the equality
\beta=(kT)^{-1}
deriving
from the imposition of Clausius’ equation on their entropy. If,
due to affection for the equation
\beta=(kT)^{-1}
, we want to
keep microstates and their entropy among the fundamental entities
of thermodynamics, then Fig.
4
forces us to renounce to
the coexistence between the maximum entropy principle and
thermodynamics of canonical systems. But, to our opinion, this
renounce would be much more disruptive than the renounce to the
vision of microstates as a fundamental entity of thermodynamics.
