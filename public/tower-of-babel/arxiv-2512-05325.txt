LYNX : Learning Dynamic Exits for Confidence-Controlled Reasoning
Ömer Faruk Akgül *,1 , Yusuf Hakan Kalaycı *,1 , Rajgopal Kannan 2 , Willie Neiswanger 1 , Viktor Prasanna 1 1 University of Southern California, 2 DEVCOM ARL

Abstract
Large reasoning models achieve strong performance on complex tasks by generating extended chains of thought, but they often “overthink”: continuing to reason long after they internally have enough information to answer correctly. This wastes inference-time compute and can even hurt accuracy. Existing attempts to stop early either manipulate decoding with extra sampling and heuristics, rely on auxiliary verifier models, or operate only as post-hoc analysis pipelines without formal guarantees. We introduce LYNX , an online early-exit mechanism that turns a model’s own hidden-state awareness into confidence-controlled stopping decisions. LYNX attaches exit decisions to naturally occurring reasoning cues (e.g., “hmm”, “wait”) during generation, trains a lightweight probe on hidden states at those cue tokens using supervision from forced exits , and wraps the resulting scores in split conformal prediction to obtain distribution-free control over the rate of premature exits. Crucially, we train and calibrate this probe once on a generic mathematical corpus and then reuse it unchanged across benchmarks, decoding temperatures, and even non-mathematical tasks. Across three model families spanning 1.5B to 32B parameters (DeepSeek-R1-1.5B, QwQ-32B, and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-8B), a single mathematically trained probe per base model yields strong accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs. On GSM8K , LYNX matches or improves baseline accuracy while reducing tokens by 40 40 – 65 % 65\% ; on MATH-500 it improves accuracy by up to 12 12 points with roughly 35 35 – 60 % 60\% fewer tokens; on AIME 2024 it recovers baseline accuracy with more than 50 % 50\% token savings; and on CommonsenseQA , a non-math benchmark, it transfers zero-shot with modest accuracy gains and up to 70 % 70\% fewer tokens. Compared to state-of-the-art early-exit methods, LYNX offers competitive or superior Pareto frontiers while remaining fully online, requiring no proxy models at inference, and providing explicit, user-tunable confidence guarantees. Code is available at https://github.com/farukakgul/LYNX .

Introduction

Reasoning-oriented large language models (LLMs) have recently achieved breakthrough performance on mathematical problem solving, logical puzzles, and competitive programming by generating long, explicit chains of thought
(
28
,
18
)
. Models such as OpenAI’s o1
(
18
)
, DeepSeek-R1
(
12
)
, Google DeepMind’s Gemini 2.5
(
11
)
, and Anthropic’s Claude 3.7 Sonnet
(
5
)
routinely emit hundreds or thousands of tokens per problem, exploring and revising candidate solution paths before committing to a final answer. This test-time “thinking” is a major source of their strength, but it also raises a central question:
how can we allocate this inference-time compute more intelligently, stopping early when further reasoning is unlikely to help?

Recent empirical studies reveal that large reasoning models frequently exhibit three critical failure modes during extended chain-of-thought generation
(
22
)
. First, models often arrive at correct intermediate solutions but continue reasoning and talk themselves into incorrect final answers, a form of counterproductive overthinking. Second, even when maintaining correctness, models generate extensive redundant reasoning that provides no additional value, wasting computational resources without improving accuracy. Third, some problems trigger unbounded reasoning loops that exhaust the model’s context window before producing any answer, leading to complete failure. These observations suggest that intelligent early-exit mechanisms are not merely an efficiency optimization but a fundamental requirement for reliable deployment of reasoning models.

In this paper we ask:
Can we turn a model’s internal confidence signals into an
online
, cue-triggered early-exit rule with explicit statistical guarantees?
We answer this in the affirmative with
LYNX
, a framework that enables reasoning models to leverage their own hidden-state awareness for confidence-controlled stopping decisions. Unlike existing approaches that manipulate decoding or rely on external verifier models, LYNX operates along three key axes:

1.

Cue-triggered exits.
Reasoning models naturally emit structural cues during their thinking process, tokens like “hmm”, “wait”, “alternatively”, or paragraph breaks that mark transitions between reasoning segments. Rather than suppressing these cues, we treat them as
decision points
: whenever a cue appears, we consider stopping.

2.

Self-contained supervision.
Instead of relying on a proxy LLM to extract and grade intermediate answers, we generate training labels by
forcing exits
at cue positions during data collection: we cut the chain of thought, prompt the base model to output its final answer, and compare against the ground-truth label. This yields a large set of cue-level examples where each hidden state is labeled as “safe to exit now” or “continue thinking”, using only the base model and task answers.

3.

Conformal confidence control.
A fixed probe threshold is brittle across datasets and decoding settings. We therefore wrap probe scores in split conformal prediction
(
26
,
21
,
4
)
, turning them into an adaptive stop/continue rule with a user-chosen miscoverage level. This yields a distribution-free guarantee on the rate of premature exits under mild exchangeability assumptions.

A central design goal of LYNX is
deployability
. Training a new probe and retuning thresholds for every benchmark would undermine the goal of efficient inference. Instead, for each base model, we train and calibrate LYNX
once
on a generic mathematical corpus and then freeze it. In our experiments, we instantiate LYNX on modern open-source reasoning models from three distinct families, including Qwen-based architectures (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, QwQ-32B) and Llama-based architectures (Llama-3.1-Nemotron-8B), using a moderate number of problems from a mathematical training corpus to (i) collect cue-triggered forced-exit labels and (ii) calibrate conformal thresholds. The resulting probe and thresholds are then applied
unchanged
across four math benchmarks and a non-math benchmark, demonstrating strong generalization across model scales (1.5B to 32B parameters), architectural families, task domains, and decoding temperatures.

Our experiments show that this simple, reusable early-exit head yields strong accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs. On
GSM8K
, LYNX matches or improves baseline accuracy while reducing tokens by up to
60
–
65\%
across different models; on
MATH-500
, it improves accuracy by up to
12
points with approximately
35
–
60\%
fewer tokens; on
AIME 2024
, it recovers baseline accuracy with more than half the tokens removed; and on
CommonsenseQA
it transfers zero-shot with modest accuracy gains and substantial token savings. When compared to state-of-the-art early-exit methods under matched settings, LYNX consistently provides more favorable Pareto frontiers, while remaining fully online and providing explicit confidence guarantees.

In summary, this paper makes the following contributions:

1.

We introduce
LYNX

, a novel online early-exit framework that combines cue-triggered decision points, self-contained probe training via forced exits, and conformalized confidence control to enable reasoning models to make statistically-guaranteed stopping decisions based on their own hidden states, without modifying decoding, injecting tokens, or requiring external verifiers.

2.

We demonstrate
cross-architecture generalizability
across three model families (Qwen and Llama), model scales (1.5B to 32B parameters), and task domains (mathematical and commonsense reasoning), showing that a single mathematically-trained probe per base model transfers effectively without per-task retraining.

3.

We establish
superior accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs
compared to state-of-the-art methods, with experiments showing LYNX matches or improves baseline accuracy while achieving
35
–
65\%
token reductions, and provide the first deployment-ready early-exit method with explicit, user-tunable confidence guarantees via conformal prediction.

2
Related Work

We situate LYNX within three complementary research areas: probing neural representations, inference-time optimization for reasoning, and conformal prediction for reliable decisions.

Probing internal representations for verification.

Probing uses simple classifiers trained on hidden states to extract information that models encode internally but may not explicitly output
(
2
,
14
)
. Zhang et al. demonstrate that reasoning models encode rich signals about solution correctness in their hidden states: a lightweight probe on activations at intermediate “final answer” tokens can predict answer correctness with strong calibration on mathematical benchmarks
(
31
)
. Related work on latent knowledge shows that hidden states contain information about truth and uncertainty even when not directly expressed in outputs
(
7
)
. However, these verification pipelines are fundamentally post-hoc, requiring full trace generation, answer extraction using auxiliary models, offline labeling, and post-processing. LYNX bridges this gap by using probes
online
during generation: we train on hidden states at naturally occurring cues using self-contained supervision from forced exits, enabling confidence-controlled stopping decisions without external labeling.

Inference-time optimization for efficient reasoning.

Large reasoning models frequently overthink, generating unnecessarily long chains of thought that waste compute and sometimes hurt accuracy
(
31
,
29
,
16
,
30
,
23
,
27
,
22
)
. DEER
(
29
)
detects action-transition tokens as exit points, elicits trial answers, and uses entropy-based confidence scores over token probabilities to decide whether to stop. Think-or-Not
(
30
)
frames reasoning length as mode selection, using tree search over candidate continuations to estimate confidence. However, its efficiency gains can be misleading, as it performs speculative sampling during generation but reports only visible output tokens, hiding substantial inference costs. Other approaches include FlashThink
(
16
)
, which queries external verifiers on partial reasoning; NoWait
(
27
)
, which suppresses self-reflection tokens; Dynasor-CoT
(
10
)
, which monitors internal uncertainty signals; and Zero-Step Thinking
(
23
)
, which routes between reasoning modes. While these methods achieve token savings, they typically manipulate generation through extra prompting, verifier queries, or speculative sampling; rely on hand-tuned thresholds requiring recalibration across models and temperatures; and provide no formal statistical control over incorrect early exits. By contrast, LYNX leaves decoding unchanged, operates solely on hidden states at natural cues, requires no auxiliary models at inference, and provides distribution-free guarantees via conformalized probe scores.

Conformal prediction for reliable decisions.

Conformal prediction provides finite-sample, distribution-free guarantees on prediction error, requiring only that test examples are exchangeable with a held-out calibration set
(
26
,
21
,
4
)
. Recent work applies conformal methods to language models: Quach et al. construct prediction sets over token sequences with coverage guarantees
(
19
)
; and Savcisens et al. uses conformal probes to analyze the trilemma between accuracy, informativeness, and guaranteed coverage in LLM outputs
(
20
)
. In the standard setting, given a base predictor
f
, calibration set
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and nonconformity score
S(x,y)
, one computes the empirical
(1-\delta)
-quantile
q_{1-\delta}
of calibration scores to form prediction sets
\mathcal{C}(x_{\text{test}})=\{y:S(x_{\text{test}},y)\leq q_{1-\delta}\}
that satisfy
\Pr[y_{\text{test}}\in\mathcal{C}(x_{\text{test}})]\geq 1-\delta
. LYNX applies this framework to probe scores estimating whether a partial chain of thought is sufficient to answer correctly, yielding a conformalized threshold that implements a stop/continue rule with user-chosen confidence level
c=1-\delta
and an explicit guarantee on incorrect early exits. To our knowledge, LYNX is the first early-exit method for reasoning models to provide such distribution-free statistical guarantees.

3
Method

Figure 2:
LYNX pipeline:
(Offline)
We collect forced-exit labels at naturally occurring cue tokens from mathematical problems, train a lightweight probe on hidden states at these cues, and calibrate conformal thresholds using a held-out set.
(Online)
During generation, whenever a cue appears, the probe scores its hidden state and a conformalized threshold decides whether to exit early or continue reasoning.

Having established that (i) reasoning models encode internal correctness signals in hidden states, (ii) existing early-exit methods lack statistical guarantees and require per-task tuning, and (iii) conformal prediction can provide distribution-free control over prediction errors, we now describe how LYNX combines these insights into a practical early-exit framework. The key challenge is to transform post-hoc probing pipelines into an online decision mechanism with explicit confidence guarantees that generalizes across tasks without retraining.

LYNX operates in two stages (Figure
2
). Offline, we generate chains-of-thought on a mathematical corpus, identify natural cue tokens, construct forced exits at each cue to obtain supervision, and train a probe with conformal calibration on hidden states. Online, we run the base model unchanged; whenever a cue appears, the probe scores its hidden state, and a conformalized threshold decides whether to exit or continue. We formalize each component below, and provide a consolidated pseudo-code description in Algorithm
1
in Appendix
A
.

3.1
Reasoning model and cue tokens

Let
x
be a problem and let the autoregressive LLM
f_{\theta}
generate a sequence of tokens

(x,t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{T})=f_{\theta}(x),

(1)

with an explicit “thinking” segment typically delimited by special tokens such as
<think>
and
</think>
. For each position
j
, we denote by
h_{\ell}(j)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the hidden state of layer
\ell
at token
t_{j}
. We write
L
for the total number of layers.

Reasoning models like DeepSeek-R1 often emit self-reflective tokens, “hmm”, “wait”, sentence breaks, etc., that mark transitions between reasoning segments (e.g., switching from exploration to verification, or revising a previous plan). We treat these as natural decision points and define a small set of cue token types

\mathcal{S}=\{\text{``hmm''},\ \text{``wait''},\ \text{``alternatively''}\},

(2)

and for a given trace we collect all positions

E(x)=\{e\in\{1,\dots,T\}:t_{e}\in\mathcal{S}\}.

(3)

These cue positions are the only places where LYNX considers early exit. Between cues, generation proceeds exactly as in standard CoT decoding.

3.2
Supervision via forced exits

We assume a supervised training corpus

\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}=\{(x_{i},a_{i}^{\star})\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
where
a_{i}^{\star}
is the gold answer for problem
x_{i}
.
For each
(x_{i},a_{i}^{\star})
, we perform a single long CoT rollout with the base model under a fixed decoding configuration (temperature, max length, etc.), obtaining tokens
\{t_{i,j}\}_{j=1}^{T_{i}}
and hidden states
\{h_{i,\ell}(j)\}_{\ell=1}^{L}
.
We then extract all cue positions

E_{i}=\{e\in\{1,\dots,T_{i}\}:t_{i,e}\in\mathcal{S}\}
.

For every cue
e\in E_{i}
, we construct a
counterfactual forced exit
. Let
\mathrm{pref}_{i}(e)
denote the prompt consisting of
x_{i}
and the generated tokens up to (but not including)
t_{i,e}
. We append the model’s native answer cue (e.g.,

</think> Final Answer: \boxed{
)
and let the base model generate a short answer-only continuation:

\hat{a}_{i}(e)=\mathrm{Exit}_{\theta}(x_{i},e).

We parse this continuation into a normalized answer
\mathrm{parse}(\hat{a}_{i}(e))
using the same rule-based extractor as in evaluation, and define a binary label

y_{i}(e)=\mathbf{1}\big[\mathrm{parse}(\hat{a}_{i}(e))=a_{i}^{\star}\big]\in\{0,1\}.

(4)

This procedure yields a dataset of cue-level examples

\mathcal{D}_{\text{cue}}=\big\{(x_{i},e,y_{i}(e)):1\leq i\leq N,\ e\in E_{i}\big\}
,
where each example answers: “If we had exited at this cue, would the resulting answer have been correct?” Crucially, labels are obtained using only the base model and task answers; no external LLM or human annotation is required.

We partition
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cue}}
into a probe-training subset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}}
and a calibration subset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}
, which will be used for conformal threshold estimation.

3.3
Probe representation and training

At each cue
(x_{i},e)
we turn the corresponding hidden states into a fixed-dimensional representation. Let

h_{i,\ell}(e)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the hidden state at token
t_{i,e}
in layer
\ell
.
Rather than relying on a single layer, we aggregate information from a small set of layers

\mathcal{L}_{\text{sel}}=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},L\}
(two middle layers and the final layer), and define

z_{i}(e)=\big[h_{i,\ell_{1}}(e)\,\|\,h_{i,\ell_{2}}(e)\,\|\,h_{i,L}(e)\big]\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}},

(5)

where
\|
denotes concatenation.
Recent empirical studies have shown that different layers of LLMs encode reasoning patterns at varying levels of granularity, ranging from local token-level relationships to task-specific predictions.
(
15
,
25
)
.

We then train a small multilayer perceptron (MLP) probe
g_{\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}}\to[0,1]
to estimate the probability that an early exit at this cue would be correct:

p_{i}(e)=g_{\psi}\big(z_{i}(e)\big)\approx\Pr\big(y_{i}(e)=1\,\big|\,z_{i}(e)\big).

(6)

Concretely,
g_{\psi}
is a two-layer MLP with ReLU activations and a sigmoid output:

\displaystyle u_{i}(e)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{ReLU}\big(W_{1}z_{i}(e)+b_{1}\big),

(7)

\displaystyle p_{i}(e)

\displaystyle=\sigma\big(W_{2}u_{i}(e)+b_{2}\big),

(8)

with parameters
\psi=(W_{1},b_{1},W_{2},b_{2})
.

Since cue-level labels are typically imbalanced, especially for the smaller models, (most forced exits early in reasoning are not yet safe), we use class-imbalance-aware binary cross-entropy. Let
\mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}}=\{(z_{k},y_{k})\}_{k=1}^{n}
be the probe-training set, with
n_{1}
positive and
n_{0}
negative examples. We define class weights

w_{1}=\frac{n}{2n_{1}}
,
w_{0}=\frac{n}{2n_{0}}
,
and train
g_{\psi}
by minimizing

\mathcal{L}(\psi)=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\Big(w_{1}y_{k}\log p_{k}+w_{0}(1-y_{k})\log(1-p_{k})\Big),

(9)

where
p_{k}=g_{\psi}(z_{k})
.

3.4
Conformalized early-exit rule

The probe
g_{\psi}
produces a scalar confidence score
p_{i}(e)\in[0,1]
for each cue, but how should we convert this into an exit decision? A fixed threshold (e.g., “exit if
p\geq 0.9
”) is brittle across datasets and temperatures. Instead, we apply split conformal prediction (§
2
) to obtain a data-driven threshold with explicit statistical guarantees.

User-specified confidence and threshold calibration.

The user selects a target confidence level
c\in(0,1)
, which controls the tradeoff between efficiency and reliability: higher
c
means more conservative exits (fewer tokens saved, lower error rate), while lower
c
means more aggressive exits (more tokens saved, higher error rate). We set the miscoverage level
\delta=1-c
and calibrate a threshold
q_{\delta}
as follows.

From the calibration subset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}=\{(z_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{m}
, we compute nonconformity scores for correctly answered cues (
y_{j}=1
):

s_{j}=1-g_{\psi}(z_{j})\quad\text{for all }j\text{ with }y_{j}=1.

(10)

Intuitively,
s_{j}
is small when the probe is confident that exiting at this cue is safe. We then set

q_{\delta}=\mathrm{Quantile}\big(\{s_{j}:y_{j}=1\},\,1-\delta\big),

(11)

the
(1-\delta)
-quantile of these scores.

Inference-time policy.

At test time, we generate CoT as usual. Whenever the model emits a cue token at position
e
for input
x
, we compute
z(x,e)
and
p(x,e)=g_{\psi}(z(x,e))
. We then apply the conformalized decision rule:

\text{Exit at cue }e\quad\text{iff}\quad 1-p(x,e)\leq q_{\delta}.

(12)

If the condition fails, we continue decoding until the next cue or the natural end of the CoT.

Statistical guarantee and practical considerations.

Under the standard exchangeability assumption, that test examples are drawn from the same distribution as calibration data
(
26
,
21
)
, this split-conformal construction controls the fraction of incorrect early exits at approximately
\delta
. Formally, if
(x_{\text{test}},e_{\text{test}})
is exchangeable with
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}
and we exit at
e_{\text{test}}
, then
\Pr[y(e_{\text{test}})=1]\geq 1-\delta

(
4
)
.

In practice, strict exchangeability would require calibrating on a held-out portion of each test benchmark. However, standard reasoning benchmarks (AIME, AMC etc.) do not provide separate calibration splits, and creating such splits would reduce the number of available test examples. Instead, we calibrate LYNX once on a generic mathematical corpus and reuse the resulting thresholds across all downstream benchmarks. This design choice prioritizes deployability, enabling zero-shot transfer without per-task tuning, at the potential cost of some miscalibration under domain shift. Our experiments (§
4
) show that this single mathematically-trained probe generalizes effectively across multiple math benchmarks and even to non-mathematical commonsense reasoning tasks, suggesting that the probe learns domain-general signals of reasoning sufficiency rather than task-specific patterns. For applications requiring stricter guarantees, practitioners can recalibrate thresholds on a small held-out subset of their target distribution.

For each base model and decoding temperature, we precompute thresholds for a grid of confidence levels (e.g.,
c\in\{0.97,0.95,0.90,0.80,0.70\}
) during the offline calibration stage, allowing users to select their preferred accuracy-efficiency operating point at deployment time without additional computation.

4
Experiments

Having established LYNX’s theoretical framework, we now empirically show how our framework preserves reasoning accuracy while substantially reducing inference cost, with behavior that generalizes across model families, architectures, scales, and tasks.

4.1
Experimental setup

Models and LYNX instantiation.

We evaluate LYNX on three open-source reasoning models spanning two architectural families and an order of magnitude in scale:
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B

(
9
)
,
QwQ-32B

(
24
)
(both Qwen-based), and
Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Nano-8B-v1

(
6
)
(Llama-based). All three models emit explicit
<think>
blocks and natural reasoning cues (“hmm”, “wait”, “alternatively”), making them suitable for cue-triggered early exit.

For each base model, we instantiate a single LYNX head by training a probe and calibrating conformal thresholds on Hendrycks MATH
(
13
)
at temperature
T=0.6
, following the procedure in §
3
. Critically, this same probe is then deployed unchanged at both
T=0.6
and
T=0.0
across all evaluation benchmarks, testing LYNX’s robustness to decoding temperature shifts without retraining.

Training and calibration data.

We use Hendrycks MATH
(
13
)
, a challenging mathematical reasoning dataset with seven subject categories (Algebra, Counting & Probability, Geometry, Intermediate Algebra, Number Theory, Prealgebra, Precalculus) and five difficulty levels. For the DeepSeek-1.5B model, we draw 400 training and 200 calibration problems per category (2,800 train, 1,400 calibration total); for QwQ-32B and Nemotron-8B, we use 200 train and 100 calibration per category to balance compute constraints. On each problem we generate a single long CoT rollout at
T=0.6
, extract cue positions, construct forced-exit labels as in §
3.2
, and split into probe-training (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}}
) and calibration (
\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}
) sets. Implementation details (probe architecture, optimization, hardware) are in Appendix
B
.

Evaluation benchmarks.

We evaluate on five benchmarks:

•

GSM8K

(
8
)
: Grade-school math word problems (1,319 test examples).

•

MATH-500

(
17
)
: 500-problem subset of competition mathematics.

•

AMC 2023

(
3
)
: American Mathematics Competition problems (40 problems).

•

AIME 2024

(
1

For mathematical tasks we use standard CoT prompting (“reason step by step, put the final answer in
\boxed{}
”) and parse numeric answers with task-specific normalization. For CommonsenseQA we use CoT-augmented multiple-choice prompts. We evaluate at two temperatures:
T=0.0
(deterministic, main results) and

Metrics.

We report pass@1 accuracy (math) or multiple-choice accuracy (CommonsenseQA) and average generated tokens per example, including all thinking and answer tokens. Efficiency is quantified as:

\Delta\text{Tokens}=\frac{\text{Tokens}_{\text{method}}-\text{Tokens}_{\text{baseline}}}{\text{Tokens}_{\text{baseline}}}
.
We sweep LYNX’s confidence parameter
c\in\{0.97,0.95,0.90,0.80,0.70\}
to trace accuracy–efficiency curves.

4.2
Baselines and cost accounting

We compare LYNX against DEER
(
29
)
and Think-or-Not (ToN)
(
30
)
, the current state-of-the-art early-exit methods for reasoning models, chosen for their strong reported performance and public availability. DEER uses entropy-based confidence scores over token probabilities at action-transition tokens to decide whether to stop. ToN performs tree-search over candidate continuations, using token-level entropies to select between long and short reasoning modes. Both methods require hand-tuned thresholds per model and temperature, whereas LYNX provides a single confidence knob
c
with formal coverage guarantees.

Cost accounting.

For all methods we count
total generated tokens
by the base model, including any speculative branches or trial answers that do not appear in the final output. For LYNX and DEER this coincides with visible tokens, as our policy never induces extra generations. For ToN we include all internal sampling costs. This provides a realistic proxy for inference cost. We use authors’ public implementations with matched prompts, decoding settings, and maximum CoT length (13,000 tokens). All methods are evaluated on the same hardware (Appendix
B
).

5
Results

We now demonstrate that LYNX achieves superior accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs compared to state-of-the-art baselines while providing interpretable, statistically-grounded control over early exits. Our analysis addresses three questions: (i) Does LYNX form better Pareto frontiers than DEER and ToN? (ii) Does the confidence knob
c
behave predictably across datasets, models, and temperatures? (iii) How do different model families and decoding settings affect early-exit performance?

6
Conclusion

We introduced LYNX, a cue-triggered, conformal early-exit mechanism that turns reasoning models’ internal hidden-state awareness into confidence-controlled stopping decisions. For each base model, LYNX trains a single lightweight probe using self-contained supervision from forced exits and wraps the resulting scores in split conformal prediction to provide user-tunable guarantees on incorrect early exits. Across three model families (Qwen and Llama), three scales (1.5B–32B parameters), four mathematical benchmarks, and one non-mathematical task, a single mathematically-trained probe per model yields strong accuracy–efficiency tradeoffs: matching or improving baseline accuracy while reducing tokens by 40–95% depending on the chosen confidence level.

Compared to state-of-the-art methods, LYNX offers competitive or superior Pareto frontiers while requiring no auxiliary models at inference, no decoding modifications, and providing explicit statistical guarantees. The method generalizes across temperatures without retraining and transfers zero-shot to non-mathematical domains, suggesting it captures domain-general signals of reasoning sufficiency rather than task-specific patterns.

Limitations.

Our study focuses on open-source models with explicit thinking segments and self-reflective cue tokens. Models without such structure may require alternative trigger definitions. Our conformal guarantees assume exchangeability between calibration and deployment data; under strong domain shift the effective coverage may deviate from nominal levels. We train separate heads per base model and temperature-independent probes per family, and do not optimize the cost of forced-exit dataset construction, viewing it as amortized over downstream usage.

Future work.

Immediate directions include applying LYNX to code generation, multi-modal reasoning, and tool-augmented settings; learning latent decision points beyond hand-specified cues; co-training base models and probes for improved linear readability; and combining LYNX with external verifiers or self-consistency methods for additional reliability gains.

Appendix B
Implementation Details

Hardware and compute.

All experiments run on 4× NVIDIA RTX A6000 (Blackwell, 96GB) GPUs. Maximum CoT length is set to 13,000 tokens for all models and methods.

Probe architecture.

The probe
g_{\psi}
is a two-layer MLP with hidden dimensions
[256,64]
and GELU activation, taking as input concatenated hidden states from three layers (two middle, one final) at each cue token. We optimize with Adam (lr=1e-3, weight decay=1e-4), class-weighted binary cross-entropy, and early stopping on a held-out validation split of
\mathcal{D}_{\text{probe}}
.

Training and calibration data sizes.

For DeepSeek-R1-1.5B: 400 training + 200 calibration problems per Hendrycks MATH category (2,800 train, 1,400 calibration total). For QwQ-32B and Nemotron-8B: 200 train + 100 calibration per category to balance compute (1,400 train, 700 calibration total).
