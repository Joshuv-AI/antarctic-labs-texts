A novel comprehensible non-intrusive sensitivity-driven additive Aerodynamic Shape Optimization (AASO) and its implementation using the Lattice Boltzmann Method (AASO-LBM)
Authors: F.-J. Granados-Ortiz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shape optimization is a relevant topic in many fields such as e.g. fluid energy harvesting, passive mixer design or pressure loss reduction in channels. Although the literature is rich in applications of surrogate-based, adjoint-based or topology-based optimization methods, there are no methods for easy non-parametric non-intrusive optimization. Also, some methods such as the adjoint shape optimization allow only minor shape modifications and are rare in unsteady flows. In this paper, a comprehensible sensitivity-driven Additive Aerodynamic Shape Optimization technique (AASO) is proposed, which aims to optimize iteratively the shape of an object by aggregation/removal of small to large-sized pieces of material to areas where they impact the most. This method can be applied to steady and unsteady flows without any code modifications. In this work the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM) is combined with the (AASO) technique to deal with irregular geometries. This leads to the here named Additive Aerodynamic Shape Lattice Boltzmann Method for optimization (AASO-LBM). The method has been successfully tested for the optimization of an object in a laminar channel (in both steady and unsteady regimes), which is representative of many applications such as micromixing or bladeless microturbine design.

Introduction

When it comes to hold a prominent position in a field, to optimize the available resources is a must. Engineering is one of those fields in which to optimize also the performance of the product itself can be the differential factor. In aero and hydrodynamics, one of the most relevant aspects to optimize the performance is to carry out the namely shape optimization, which consists of obtaining the best aerodynamic shape for a given objective (e.g. reduction in drag force). Plenty of examples can be found, e.g. in vehicle aerodynamics, where improving the response to lift and drag of cars and aircrafts is translated into fuel consumption savings1. Despite these applications classically illustrate the motion of turbulent flows over objects, the effect of shape is equally important in laminar flow applications, such as (autonomous) underwater vehicles (AUV)2, micro aerial vehicles (MAV)3, very specific hydrokinetic energy device applications4, microturbines5, energy harvesting in microchannels6, and development of passive micromixer devices that use vortex shedding to promote mixing7,8.

Although experimental prototyping is key, in industry and academia it is very rare to perform optimization studies directly in an experimental testing phase, and most of the burden usually lies on computational studies. This is so, thanks to the increase in computational resources and accuracy in current computational facilities and softwares. As mentioned in previous publications, the old trend of carrying out 90% of the work experimentally has been actually reversed and now the computational investigation is the workhorse in fluid-based engineering design9.

However, to perform shape optimization in CFD is very challenging. First, to make shape modifications in standard commercial CFD codes requires to recreate geometry and re-mesh for each shape modification (unless the mesh is non body-fitted or mesh deformation is possible, which also has some risks, as the mesh quality is affected), and if the shape modification is moderate, may even require to rethink the spatial discretization in common finite volume method (FVM) softwares such as ANSYS Fluent or OpenFoam. Second, each variation in shape in CFD needs relatively long simulation elapsed time, not being possible to deploy standard optimization algorithms directly. The flow regime is also an important fact, since small variations are enough to, e.g., produce a shock-free flow in transonic flows (shape variations of boundary layer thickness order)10, but laminar flows are less sensitive (e.g. pressure drop in laminar flow pipes is unrelated to roughness unless its size is very remarkable11).

There are several investigations out there that remark the importance of an appropriated shape optimization, and the selection of the optimization method is of strong importance. In Fig. 1 it is shown a summary of the most popular optimization methods in CFD, concretely applied to shape optimization. All the optimization methods in Fig. 1 have been grouped as direct methods, surrogate-based, gradient-based, and artificial intelligence-based. However, some methods could actually share groups. Another classification could be made based on parametric optimization (shape modifications controlled by well-known parameters, e.g. variations in NACA parameters) or non-parametric shape optimization (e.g. free form deformations on an existing NACA profile), but there are methods that can work with both forms of optimization, so this classification is omitted to avoid confusing readers, and the parametric discussion will take place when necessary.

The chart in Fig. 1 starts with the direct methods, which are unaffordable in most CFD problems, since they require to run one CFD simulation per sample. To avoid such computational burden, surrogate models or machine learning-aided reduced order modelling techniques can be used, in order to have functions or models inexpensive to evaluate. The direct optimization methods usually present in CFD optimization are heuristic or metaheuristic12.

The most popular method in the CFD literature is arguably the use of surrogate-based models or response surfaces, which consists of approximating a true response of a Quantity of Interest (QoI) by interpolation or regression on specific points selected through an initial design of experiment (DoE). This approach is considered a non-intrusive parametric method, which means that certain specific parameters are tightly varied in the DoE phase. An example of application can be seen in13, where the reduction in erosion in a 90-degree bend (elbow) circular pipe is investigated via response surface optimization. The use of Neural Networks as models trained with CFD simulations (surrogate models in practice) is also becoming a rising practice, especially when temporal and/or spatial variations of QoIs are desired (often known as Reduced Order models)14. In addition to this, probabilistic methods such as the Bayesian optimization method provide a more advanced option, which includes an acquisition function to control the sampling for the surrogate15. Finally, it is worth noting that surrogate-based optimization performs parametric optimization only from a specific range of values of the design space parameters, upon their DoE, and the surrogates are later sampled by optimization algorithms, so their ability to avoid local optima is very good, within the specified design parametric space.

Gradient-based methods have features that overcome surrogate-based method limitations. The most relevant feature is its ability to work with non-parametric shape optimization with the popular adjoint method. This method, which can be implemented from a discrete or continuous formulation, performs the computation of the sensitivity of a QoI to the changes in a baseline shape16. The creation of an adjoint problem from the Navier-Stokes equations allows to compute efficiently the derivatives of the objective function (in practice, our QoI) with respect to the design variables (gradient computation). This method outperfoms direct differentiation methods (e.g. finite difference17) or One-factor-at-Time (OAT or OFAT)18, in the computation and use of the gradient16. However, the adjoint method only allows to perform limited free-form modifications (mostly seen for steady flows, with very few unsteady applications in the literature)10 and its implementation is intrusive and not straightforward. Thus, the aim of the adjoint method is not to obtain new shapes “ by large modifications”, but to perform small non-parametric variations upon a baseline, being combined in FVM/FEM body-fitted mesh simulations with Free Form Deformation (FFD) methods, Radial Basis Functions (RBF) or similar methods19. For large modifications, a hybrid approach (e.g. first the deployment of a parametric method and then adjoint method with small modifications) is usually the best option, as in Granados-Ortiz et al. (2023)20. For a very complete review on the challenges faced in aerodynamic shape optimization, with great emphasis on the adjoint method, please see21. Must be pointed out that the adjoint method can be also deployed in other CFD solvers with non body-fitted mesh approach, such as LBM or even FVM/FEM with Immersed Boundary method (IBM). Popular LBM solvers such as OpenLB include the option for optimization via adjoint method but, as specified in its user’s guide22, it requires the theoretical calculation of the adjoint Lattice Boltzmann equations and case-specific feature implementation. Relevant works in the literature as those by Kusano23,24 revealed that the method can be applied to unsteady problems. If the adjoint method is deployed with the IBM method, there is no need to re-mesh for each geometry, but the immersed boundaries grid points are not necessarily located at the fluid-solid interface (perturbations in the shape cannot be directly translated into sensitivity on the numerical grid), making the adjoint derivation more complicated as ghost-cells are required25. This is an important extra layer of complexity, that likely limited its applicability and popularity, existing very few works (and all very recent) in the literature25,26.

Topology optimization (TO) is also a very relevant gradient-based method. This method consists of answering the questions of “where to add or remove material”, and originally comes from the solid mechanics field27. This method still has room for improvement in fluid dynamics, but it is a well established method in structural mechanics. The most important difference that can be spot from the literature is that topology optimization allows to add/remove areas/volumes of material, in contrast to the adjoint method, which is only used to modify the shape of the object. The method has been applied to a wide range of shape optimization problems, mostly for internal flows. Examples of application can be the very extensive work on internal flow cooling system designs in28, ducted flows with different configurations in29, heat sinks30 , or even the combination of aerodynamic loads and structural optimization of airfoils31,32. However, it is rare to see applications of topology optimization for non-internal flows33 or unsteady problems34. To the knowledge of the author, the combination of steady/unsteady aerodynamic shape topology optimization has been only explored by35 in the literature. To sum up the capabilities of topology optimization, it can be pointed out that despite the advantages of this non-parametric method, the implementation of this intrusive approach is not easy and this possibly hinders its applicability. Depending on the CFD solver, TO may exhibit different advantages/disadvantages. If body-fitted mesh solvers are used, the geometries must be re-meshed, what makes the method more cumbersome. If non body-fitted methods such as LBM (with/without combination with advanced options such as e.g. immersed boundary method [IBM]) or are employed, then to deal with complex geometries may become less problematic in principle. Nevertheless, in the discussion of the adjoint method with non body-fitted approach it was outlined that other problems arose. Such drawbacks are also present in the TO method, and will not be again discussed here. The reader interested in further details can consult the following works in TO with non body-fitted method36–40. It is also worth noting that there are some variants of gradient-based methods which rely on automatic differentiation, such as41–43. However, these methods still have parametric dependence, require to construct an adjoint formulation and have intrusive nature.

Regarding the application of Artificial Intelligence-based optimization, often referred to as data-driven optimization, this type of optimization method relies on the use of Artificial Intelligence (AI), or the Machine Learning (ML) sub-branch to obtain optimal designs. First of all, it is worth noting that the use of techniques such as gaussian processes or neural networks to obtain models to be used as response surface/surrogate models would be grouped in the surrogate-based optimization. However, there are methods that actually allow to enhance surrogate-based modelling by means of Machine Learning techniques, such as the Machine Learning-Aided Design Optimization (MLADO) method. This technique was applied in8 for parametric shape optimization of a vortex shedding-based micromixers in a multi-objective context. The method showed that the use of ML can support and accelerate the process of the creation of surrogates by discarding useless simulations (vortexless simulations in this case). Possibly, one of the most popular applications of AI/ML is to reduce the design space (also known as feature extraction), in which data-driven techniques such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) or autoencoders have been found of strong interest44. Following the same approach, there are many works out there on the use of proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) and dynamic mode decomposition (DMD) to construct Reduced Order Models (ROM). In the work developed by Gianluigi Rozza and his group one can see interesting applications of these methods in shape optimization in combination with small deformations using FFD in cruise ship shape optimization45,46. Also, investigations such as47 propose adjoint-free methods that use deep learning techniques for the high dimensional wing shape design optimization, which generate various wing shapes restricted by geometric reality and feasibility constrains. Although still in a very immature phase, AI/ML models could also be trained to predict the flow behaviour upon a geometry, what can lead to better performance from specific shapes. This could be done by training recurrent neural networks (RNNs), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) or long short-term memory models (LSTM). An example of potential application is the work by Zhang48, who combined some of the aforementioned techniques. In some way, MLADO could also fit in this category, since the ML algorithm was trained with previous data to predict the behaviour of future geometries. Another very popular method that is gaining increasing interest is the use of Reinforcement Learning, typically from control theory, which consists on training an artificial agent to learn the best behaviour within an environment in order to achieve a reward49. A recent application can be seen in50, where the shape of an airfoil is designed as a Markov decision process and Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) is applied. The agent successively modifies the 2D shape until certain metrics are optimized, similarly to the work in51. Finally, a very extensive literature review on aerodynamic shape optimization using Machine Learning methods can be consulted in52. This reference contains a very detailed explanation of each method.

The literature review presented above leads to the conclusion that surrogate-based methods are non-intrusive, usually parametric and relatively easy to implement, but their performance strongly depends on the DoE and can be time consuming. The adjoint method deals well with small shape variations in non-parametric shapes, but it is an intrusive method and without direct straightforward implementation. Strongly connected to the adjoint method there it is the topology optimization field. Despite dealing really well with performing small and large shape variations in a domain, is even harder to implement (especially in unsteady problems). Thus, from these methods a natural research gap question arises for the most pragmatic engineers: is there any quick and non-intrusive alternative for automated shape optimization? From this question, the Additive Aerodynamic Shape Optimization Method (AASO) arises.

The AASO is a simple and comprehensible local sensitivity-driven method that consists of the addition/removal of portions of material to a surface in those areas in which shape modifications benefit the most. Although this in principle has strong resemblance to the adjoint/TO methods, the AASO method is non-intrusive, does not require to compute any adjoint equations, and performs modifications of any size. Since the method needs an appropriate CFD environment, in this work the AASO is blended with the Lattice Boltzmann Method (LBM), leading to the entitled Additive Lattice Boltzmann optimization Method (AASO-LBM). The additive shapes are adapted to the LBM lattice, thus no mesh refinements (nor time discretization adjustments) are necessary. In Table 1 a comparison of the advantages/disadvantages of AASO-LBM versus the aforementioned methods is shown.

AASO-LBM is by no means intended to replace already well-established methods such as parametric methods, adjoint method or topology optimization, but it can be a good non-intrusive alternative when making changes within a code is not possible, for prototyping or as second method in a hybrid approach (e.g. to make non-parametric modifications on a parametric optimal design). In this manuscript, the AASO algorithm is applied to laminar flows, and this is the most recommended target audience for the method, since laminar flows are less sensitive to non-smooth surface variations. Nevertheless, the method could be applied to turbulent flows, with awareness of its caveats. The method has been coupled with LBM in this work, since it is of unsteady nature and deals very well with complex surfaces, in addition to being easier to code, faster, uses a robust stream-and-collide approach (useful for quick convergence after each initialization), and more computationally efficient than other methods such as FVM/FEM. Although as later explained in Section “Optimization method” the material is added according to specific “additive” shapes (e.g. square shapes in the examples presented), the method could be also applied within Bézier curves, FFD splines or Radial Basis Functions (RBF)53,54 that allows a smoother shape deformation on the go. Methodology

The AASO-LBM method consists of the use of Lattice Boltzmann simulations to perform certain shape modifications on its lattice distribution around the object by addition/removal of material on the most sensitive areas. The process is sensitivity-driven, since a sensitivity map is created around the object to decide where to add/remove material in an automated iterative approach. This optimization algorithm is non-intrusive but automated. In a nutshell, the process is as follows (for a detailed and proper explanation, please see Section “Optimization method”).

First, the lattice distribution over the domain is set for the CFD LBM simulation. This is the baseline design, which is simulated and stored for the first iteration of the AASO-LBM algorithm. The discretization must be decided also considering the size of the addition/removal of material, since these modifications must fit the lattice distribution.

Second, the lattices on the border of the object are detected (e.g. by using a Python or Matlab script). Centered on these border lattices, addition/removal of material takes place. The size, distribution (dimensionless sampling exploration), type (add or remove) and approach (symmetric or non-symmetric growth pattern) are decided by the practitioner as set-up parameters.

Third, the LBM solver is called to simulate each geometry. Since no modifications are required inside the LBM code, the process is non-intrusive. The code is just mandatorily executed to simulate the flow over all the modified geometries.

Fourth, the sensitivity indexes, which measure the sensitivity of the objective (the actual Quantity of Interest, QoI) to the shape modification, are computed to decide which shape modification works the best.

The process described here is repeated until the shape modifications do not improve the performance anymore. In this manuscript, since the objective is just to show the potential of the method, the entire work has been implemented in Matlab scripts, which included the LBM with Single Relaxation Time (SRT) and Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) collision model, and the test cases consisted of steady/unsteady laminar flows over an object in a channel at different Reynolds numbers and AASO-LBM set-up. The numerical details of the LBM method used in this work, as well as its validation with data from the literature, are given in the next section.

The selection of the scheme provides both the values of the velocity components \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{ij}$$\end{document} and the coefficients \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_i$$\end{document}.

Also, the collision term is very complex58. Although there are several options in the literature, the simplest one is the Bhatnagar-Gross-Krook (BGK) operator model, given by:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _i (\varvec{x}, t)= -\frac{f_i(\varvec{x}, t) - f^{eq}_i(\varvec{x}, t)}{\tau } \Delta t, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document} is a relaxation time. However, despite the simplification made with introducing the BGK collision operator \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i (\varvec{x}, t)$$\end{document}, Eq. (6) is still nonlinear, since the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_i^{eq}$$\end{document} is a nonlinear term. This term is obtained from the Maxwell distribution function in discrete velocities and the Taylor series expansion of the exponential term58, thus finally obtaining:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_i^{eq} (\varvec{x}, t) = E_i \rho \left( 1 + \frac{\varvec{u}\cdot \varvec{c_i}}{c_s^2} + \frac{(\varvec{u}\cdot \varvec{c_i})^2}{2c_s^4} - \frac{\varvec{u}\cdot \varvec{u}}{2c_s^2} \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_i$$\end{document} are coefficients that depend on the DdQq scheme selected57 (in this work, the D2Q9 scheme will be used), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_s$$\end{document} refers to the speed of sound, whose value is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_s=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{3}}$$\end{document} in the present work (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_s$$\end{document} depends on the scheme58). For an isothermal scenario, pressure p in the LBM is related to the speed of sound, by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p=\rho /c_s^2$$\end{document}.

The last step is to connect both the Navier-Stokes equations and LBM, which can be done by means of the Chapman-Enskog method59. With this approach, one can obtain the kinematic shear viscosity related to the relaxation time:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \nu = c_s^2 \left( \tau - \frac{\Delta t}{2} \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This viscosity is used in the Reynolds number, represented by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re=U_{c}L_{c}/\nu$$\end{document}, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{c}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{c}$$\end{document} the characteristic velocity and length, respectively. In this work, the Single Relaxation Time (SRT) method is used, with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta t =1$$\end{document}.

The Lattice Boltzmann Method with the BGK collision operator (often referred to as LBGK) represents the collision and streaming of particles,

which can be decomposed into the collision and streaming terms by10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & Collision \, (or \, relaxation): \, \, \, f^*_i (\varvec{x}, t) = f_i (\varvec{x}, t) -\frac{f_i(\varvec{x}, t) - f^{eq}_i(\varvec{x}, t)}{\tau } \Delta t, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} & Streaming \, (or \, propagation): \, \, \, f_i(\varvec{x} + \varvec{c}_i \Delta t, \,t+ \Delta t) = f^*_i (\varvec{x}, t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_i^*$$\end{document} represents the distribution after collision.

Problem specification and boundary conditions

The AASO-LBM optimization method will be applied to the laminar flow past a square cylinder in a channel as geometry. This is a very appropriate test case because: (i) it involves the aerodynamic shape optimization of the cylinder, which is representative of an arbitrary shape, (ii) there is a transition between steady and unsteady regimes well studied in the literature60, (iii) the shape is constrained by channel walls, which affect vortex shedding structures (this is not a free flow), and (iv) this configuration represents the micromixer geometry studied in previous works by the author7,8 and under improvement. On this geometry, several test cases will be deployed, at steady and unsteady regimes, and with different objectives (maximize lift-to-drag ratio, maximize drag in a very narrow design space, and maximize unsteady oscillatory local velocity at a low pressure drop). The method could be tested in other scenarios such as objects in free-flows, bended channels, heat exchangers/mixers, bladeless microturbines, etc. However, the geometry shown in this work shares enough resemblance to these problems to encourage its application with reasonable expectations.

As in any CFD method, boundary conditions must be imposed. The initial geometry in the validation and numerical experiments is shown in Fig. 2, which consists of a 2D microchannel of width H, length L and a square (cylinder) of side D (diameter D), whose center of gravity is located with coordinates (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O_x, O_y$$\end{document}) from inlet and bottom wall, respectively. The inlet in the channel is a parabolic flow (Poiseuille), given by12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} u_x = \frac{4u_{max}}{H^2} y(H - y); \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{max}$$\end{document}, the velocity at H/2, should be low enough (recommendable lower than 0.15, in lattice units) to keep a low Mach and thus perform with enough accuracy and stability. Regarding the Reynolds number, the characteristic velocity used for the computation is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{c} = u_{max}\frac{2}{3}$$\end{document}, which is the mean velocity at the inlet in lattice units, and the characteristic length is the object side/diameter. Thus, in this work:13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Re = \frac{U_{c} L_{c}}{\nu } = \frac{u_{max} \frac{2}{3} D}{\nu }. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}As a note, in the validation of the numerical code, since some validation data in the literature uses a different definition of the Reynolds number, there will be also a Reynolds based on the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{max}$$\end{document} velocity, named \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re_m$$\end{document}. At the outlet, a constant pressure is imposed, together with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_y=0$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\rho =1$$\end{document}. The boundary conditions are imposed according to the Zou-He method (also known as non-equilibrium bounce-back method), see61,62 for details in the implementation.Fig. 2Sketch of the geometries simulated by LBM.

Regarding the computation of forces and velocities on the solid boundaries, the fullway bounce-back method is implemented. This method consists on reflecting back the population of particles hitting (or in contact) with a surface, and it is modelled in the streaming step by5714\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} f_{\overline{i}}(\varvec{x_b}, \,t+ \Delta t) = f^*_{i}(\varvec{x_b}, \,t), \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{i}$$\end{document} denotes the opposite bounce directions (reflection is computed by imposing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{c}_{\overline{i}} = - \varvec{c}_i$$\end{document}), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_b$$\end{document} the boundary nodes (from which the population comes towards the solid nodes). Lift and drag forces on the object are computed by means of the momentum exchange method. This method, first suggested by Ladd63,64, consists of identifying the population that comes in and out from the fluid nodes to the solid nodes, and vice versa. In this work, it has been implemented by first identifying the solid node index, and then, if the i-th velocity leads to a solid node, the computation of forces in each direction is made accordingly65:15\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_x= & \sum _{i=1}^9 c_{ix} \left( f^*_i(x_b,\, t) + f^*_{\overline{i}}(x_b+\varvec{c_i}\Delta t,\, t) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} F_y= & \sum _{i=1}^9 c_{iy} \left( f^*_i(x_b,\, t) + f^*_{\overline{i}}(x_b+\varvec{c_i}\Delta t,\, t) \right) , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{ix}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c_{iz}$$\end{document} represent the x and y components of discrete velocities \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varvec{c}_i$$\end{document}, respectively. Thus, the computation of lift and drag coefficients is:17\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_D= & \frac{F_x}{\frac{1}{2} \rho _{char} U_{c}^2} = \frac{9F_x}{2 \rho u_{max}^2}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} C_L= & \frac{F_y}{\frac{1}{2} \rho _{char} U_{c}^2} = \frac{9F_y}{2 \rho u_{max}^2}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

Validation

In order to provide trustworthy results for the optimization study, the numerical code is first verified&validated with highly corroborated data from the literature. This validation process is developed with problems very close to the test cases in this work: 2D flow around a round cylinder and around a square cylinder, both confined in a channel. When a flow is confined in a channel, the bounce-back method is properly tested, since there is not only fluid-solid interaction in the object, but also between the oscillatory unsteady flow and the channel walls. Moreover, a parabolic inlet flow is imposed, as well as a non-slip condition at the walls. In addition, since in the additive/removal of material with AASO-LBM, (almost) any shape is expected, thus testing a curved and squared object shape provides a better insight in the validation.

Optimization method

The optimization method flow chart is introduced in Fig. 11, together with the expanded AASO-LBM loop in Fig. 12. The process obviously starts with the simulation of the baseline design (see the pseudocode in Algorithm 1), whose inputs also become further inputs to the AASO-LBM process itself. As Algorithm 1 and Fig. 11 depict, after running the baseline simulation, the Quantity of Interest (QoI) is computed and then stored as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$QoI_{prev\_shap}$$\end{document}. This is so because later, in the AASO-LBM loop, the sensitivity of the QoI to the shape modifications is to be computed as the relative difference between the QoI of the last/previous shape and the QoI from the simulation of the new shape modification.

The Algorithm 2 pseudocode shows at the beginning that a restriction box must be defined. This restriction box states a maximum size when adding material or a minimum size when removing material. That is to say, if one pursues the objective of achieving minimal drag on the obstacle, the material might be progressively removed until a nearly zero obstacle remains. To avoid this, a minimum dimension must be set. It is recommended to create an inner and outer restriction box (not necessarily a rectangular shape), so that maximum and minimum sizes are controlled. Then, the process consists of adding/removing pieces of material at each iteration following a sensitivity-driven approach, until shape modifications do not improve performance.

General aspects of the method

In a nutshell, the steps in the AASO-LBM optimization method, broken down in Algorithms 1 and 2 pseudocodes, consist of the addition/removal of pieces of material pointwise (or better said, “latticewise”) and then computing a sensitivity index to measure the impact of each shape modification upon the previous design in the iterative process.

As shown in Algorithm 1, the first step is to compute the baseline simulation. This baseline simulation could be in principle obtained from any CFD simulation, as long as the data are correctly interpolated on the same lattice discretization that is to be used in the iterative process of addition/removal of material. However, for the sake of consistency, it is strongly recommended to run the baseline geometry on the same set-up of the AASO-LBM. That is to say, same lattice discretization, same relaxation scheme, and same lattice model. This avoids errors in further computation of sensitivities in the AASO-LBM process. The examples shown in this manuscript are developed under this recommended practice.

Let suppose there is a 3D rectangular domain of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[N_x, N_y, N_z]$$\end{document} lattices and a specific baseline geometry design. Then, the dimensionless numbers are specified, which include, e.g., the Reynolds number (Re), and characteristic velocity and length (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$U_{c}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{c}$$\end{document}, respectively). This now allows to define the kinematic viscosity (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu$$\end{document}) and relaxation of the LBM (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\omega$$\end{document}). Finally, the lattice model is defined (e.g., D3Q19) and the baseline geometry is run. Once the simulation is finished, the Quantity of Interest (QoI) is stored, to be used in the computation of sensitivity in the first iteration of the AASO-LBM optimization algorithm. The connection of the baseline simulation with the full AASO-LBM process is shown in Fig. 11.

Upon the baseline simulation results, the AASO-LBM method is applied as depicted in Fig. 11 and Algorithm 2. The first step is to determine a restriction (outer and/or inner) box of generic dimensions \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[R_x, R_y, R_z]$$\end{document}, which cannot be exceeded by the addition/removal of material on the baseline (and further) designs. Also, the conditions for the addition of material are set in this initial step, which require the symmetric/non-symmetric addition (array vsymm), addition or removal of material (array vadd) and the sizes of the portion of material to add/remove (array vscaling).

Addition/removal of material and indexing of geometries

For efficiency in the mapping, sampling and storage of data, each geometry is identified by compact notation indexes (i, j, k, l) per design AASO-LBM iteration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$it_{AASO}$$\end{document}. That is to say, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$geometry(i,j,k,l)|_{itAASO}$$\end{document}. The four indexes (i, j, k, l) that characterize each candidate correspond to four parameters, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i = 1, 2, ..., n_b$$\end{document} refers to the lattice index at the border of the object (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_b$$\end{document} is the total number of lattices along the border), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j=\{1,\,0\}$$\end{document} refers to the addition(1)/removal(0) of material, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k > 0$$\end{document} stands for the scaling of the portion of material (centered in i) to add/remove from the surface, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l=\{1,2,3,4\}$$\end{document} stands for the or symmetry condition (growth pattern) for addition/removal (1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, \equiv \,$$\end{document}no symmetry, 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, \equiv \,$$\end{document}symmetry in x-axis, 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, \equiv \,$$\end{document}symmetry in y-axis, 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\, \equiv \,$$\end{document}symmetry in both x and y-axis). As an example, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$geometry(i=25,\,j=1,\,k=0.25,\,l=2)$$\end{document} means that, at the the lattice index \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$i=25$$\end{document}, addition will take place (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j=1$$\end{document}), by adding a piece of material of side \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$25\%$$\end{document} of the size of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{c}$$\end{document}, under a symmetry condition 2, which means that on the left and right sides of the object the same portion of material is to be added (mirroed with respect to the y-axis). It is obvious that when symmetry is added (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$l>1$$\end{document}), the process is faster, since the symmetric counterpart of the obstacle is not sampled.

Another trick to speed up the process is to increase the dimensionless sampling exploration step (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$expl\_step$$\end{document}), which is made dimensionless with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta x$$\end{document}. This parameter must be an integer, since it represents the sampling to be done lattice by lattice on the border of the obstacle. This parameter must be adjusted according to the size of the lattice. The most complete sampling is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$expl\_step=1$$\end{document}, since the addition/removal of material would be done centered on all the lattices on the border. However, for very fine lattice grids this is impractical, because the performance of the simulation is essentially unperturbed when sampling two consecutive lattices and the number of simulations is high. Thus, to increase notably the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$expl\_step$$\end{document} is a good solution to scalability issues. If the simulation takes a long elapsed time per run, it is recommended a larger value of the dimensionless sampling exploration step, symmetric exploration (if possible), and testing few scaling factors. In addition, it is possible to calculate in advance its value, based on the affordable computational budget, since the number of total lattices along the border \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n_b$$\end{document} is known. An example of the sampling over an object to illustrate the role of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$expl\_step$$\end{document} is shown in Fig. 10. As shown in Fig. 10b, there is some overlap of the addition/removal shape and the original object, which is added/subtracted by boolean operation. Special care must be taken when the removal overlap occurs in the inner restriction box, which must always be 1 (material exists). Similarly, when the addition of material occurs outside the outer restriction box (this represents a limit, thus its value outside has to be 0), a value of zero must be imposed. In this work, the limits are represented by a restriction box for the sake of demonstration, but these “boxes” can actually have any shape. It just depends on the application. It is worth noting that despite some of the here mentioned actions can be taken to reduce the computational burden, this method is not suitable for expensive simulations (in computational time terms), since it requires at least a moderate amount of computations. A good advantage of the AASO method deployed on LBM is that each CFD simulation is initialized with the previous optimal simulation. Since the additive changes in the domain are not large, the perturbation on the LBM simulation is relatively little (mostly, thanks to the stream-and-collide nature of the method), and each new LBM simulation converged really fast.

Selection of optimal candidates throughout the AASO-LBM loop

Specific parameters to control the performance of the AASO-LBM optimization algorithm are the sensitivity threshold (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\delta$$\end{document}), the dimensionless sampling exploration step (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$expl\_step$$\end{document}), the simulation times per sampled border lattice (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$max\_sim\_time\_sampl$$\end{document}), and the simulation time for the optimal candidate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$max\_sim\_time\_optim$$\end{document}). As can be seen in the AASO-LBM loop diagram depicted in Fig. 12, the process is sensitivity-driven, so that a sensitivity index is computed during the process two times: first for all the sampled lattices around the object (border), and second for the sensitivity of the potential optimal candidate of the current iteration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$it_{AASO}$$\end{document}. In both cases, the sensitivity is computed by (in compact notation):20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} Sensit_{i,j,k,l} = \frac{QoI_{i,j,k,l} - QoI_{prev\_shape_{i,j,k,l}}}{QoI_{prev\_shape_{i,j,k,l}}}* flag\_min, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where QoI and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$QoI_{prev\_shape}$$\end{document} stand for the Quantity of Interest for the current shape modification and previous shape (baseline if \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$it_{AASO}=1$$\end{document}), respectively. The (i, j, k, l) indexes represent the compact notation to refer to different factors in the shape modification process (explained in the previous Section “Addition/removal of material and indexing of geometries”). The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$flag\_min$$\end{document} is simply a flag, whose value is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document} for minimization , and 1 for a maximization. This guarantees the use of the same algorithm independently of a minimization or maximization problem.

Since the object border is sampled to test the impact of adding shape modifications, a simulation of elapsed time \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$max\_sim\_time\_sampl$$\end{document} is run for each modification. The greater the elapsed time, the better converged (more accurate) the LBM simulation will be, but higher simulation times. Thus, a trade-off between simulation time and accuracy must be made. Nevertheless, the selection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$max\_sim\_time\_sampl$$\end{document} only affects the selection of potential optimal candidates at each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$it_{AASO}$$\end{document} iteration (see vector vSensit in Fig. 12, which includes the sensitivities for all planned shape modifications around the border (see Fig. 9), whose best candidate is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$geom\_opt\_it$$\end{document}). In order to compare the performance of AASO-LBM with other methods, adjoint optimization studies have also been carried out. The adjoint approach has been run in ANSYS Fluent, which is arguably the most popular tool in the literature to perform adjoint optimisation. The optimization study has been conducted with a polynomial morphing mesh and under 200 adjoint iterations. Also, the design region is identical to the restriction box used in AASO-LBM, for a fair comparison. As shown in Fig. 13, the adjoint method deforms the shape of the square object up to a geometry with resemblance to the one obtained by AASO-LBM (see Fig. 14). Unsteady flow analysis

Finally, in order to also show the applicability to an unsteady problem, the same geometry but at a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Re=66.66$$\end{document} is to be optimized. For this study, the objective is to maximize the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{std(u)/U_c}{\Delta p/p_c}$$\end{document}, with u the total velocity at the representative downstream point \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P(3L/4, \, H/2)$$\end{document}, i.e. to achieve the maximum standard deviation in total velocity at the lowest channel pressure drop rate (with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p_c=\rho U_c^2$$\end{document}). A maximization of standard deviation in velocity can be viewed as a way to promote the amplitude in vortex shedding oscillations, of high importance in applications such as bladeless turbines for energy harvesting69 or design of vortex shedding-based passive micromixers7,8.

For the unsteady problem, the set-up of AASO-LBM is the same, but one has to control that the flow is conveniently solved at each iteration before applying the next shape modification. Whilst in the steady case the convergence of the simulation was very fast, in the unsteady scenario convergence is achieved around 10 lattice time units later. During the process, it was intended to capture at least 4 “clean” oscillations before applying the next change, in order to ensure that the periodic motion is conserved, see Fig. 20a. For this reason, around 30 dimensionless time units were simulated per border lattice sampled. Hence, the elapsed time of the simulation is considerably larger than in the steady case, taking \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sim$$\end{document} 1.25 days for 3 AASO-LBM iterations on a single modern CPU core (3.20 GHz processor, 16 GB RAM). It is recalled that this implementation has been done in MATLAB (an interpreted code language), and with no parallelization, so the computational times could be dramatically reduced in a parallelized version with a compiled code language. To summarize, after applying the AASO-LBM method to the steady&unsteady laminar flow past a cylinder in a channel flow, several aspects must be outlined. The comparison of the use of the adjoint optimization technique with a FVM solver has shown that the adjoint approach requires performing shape variations that are not always (computationally) feasible. Although the geometry variations per iteration kind of resemble the ones obtained by the AASO-LBM (but with less “serrated” surface), the computational mesh suffers strong distortion and cannot be solvable. Other works in the literature have applied the adjoint method to LBM in flow past cylinders, as the work by Kusano23,24 in the study of reduction of aeolian tone (aeroacoustics). The application of the adjoint method led to cylinder shapes with protrusions that suppress the aeolian tone while decreasing drag. Despite the adjoint-LBM is faster than AASO-LBM, and provided very interesting optimal designs, the work by Kusano reflects some potential concerns for practitioners with respect to the expected results of AASO-LBM (some already discussed in Table 1). First, the adjoint-LBM method is not easy to code and it is intrusive, what hinders its applicability, in contrast to the non-intrusive AASO method proposed in this paper. Second, the geometries that can be generated are single-object (just the current object shape is modified), opposite to AASO-LBM, which can generate segregated geometries thanks to the option of material removal (see, e.g. Fig. 17). Third, when the design has not enough freedom to deform (i.e. restriction box or design box very narrow), the adjoint method can fail to find a solution, as seen in Section “Steady flow analysis (Re = 20)”. For these scenarios, the option of not only deforming the shape but also remove portions of object represents an alternative. However, as mentioned in the introduction, each method has its own advantages and drawbacks, and it is decision of the practitioner to choose one method over others.

Conclusions

The investigation carried out in this manuscript has been twofold. First, an easy and comprehensible sensitivity-driven Additive Shape Optimization technique (ASO) has been introduced, which aims to optimize the shape of an object by straightforward aggregation/removal of small or medium size pieces of material in areas where they impact the most. Second, the non-intrusive implementation of the proposed optimization method with the well-known Lattice Boltzmann Method (called AASO-LBM), thanks to the ability of LBM to deal with complex geometries. The AASO-LBM method has been applied to the shape optimization of an object confined in a channel, tested in 7 different optimization designs, for objects in steady and unsteady channel flows.

In this work it has been demonstrated that the AASO method represents a non-intrusive, straightforward, local sensitivity-driven approach to further explore changes in object shapes. In contrast to the adjoint method, this method is non-intrusive, easy to implement in unsteady flows, and allows to perform medium&large shape modifications controlled by a scaling factor. When combined with the LBM method, the AASO-LBM optimization approach also adds a plus on dealing with complex geometries, which in addition to the easy non-intrusive implementation, makes this method also an interesting alternative in comparison to other advanced methods such as topology optimization. AASO-LBM optimization is stable and robust, and does not require to refine any Design of Experiment to improve a surface response, as in surrogate-based methods.

The method has been applied to shape optimization of a square cylinder in a channel for both a steady (lift to drag maximization and drag maximization) and unsteady laminar flow (maximization of pointwise standard deviation of velocity over time with respect to pressure drop) in this work. The AASO-LBM optimization technique was capable of finding non-parametric modifications on the shape that led to an improved performance. The shapes obtained for the lift to drag maximization in the steady flow had strong resemblance to shapes characteristic in fluid topology optimization. The shapes in the maximization of drag in the steady flow in a very constrained restriction box provided unexpected segregated shapes with outstanding performance too, where the adjoint method could not make any improvements, achieving a total of 45% increase in drag. The unsteady oscillatory flow problem under analysis, although it was remarkably heavier to simulate than the steady studies (the unsteady flow simulation must be converged enough to capture at least 3-4 clean oscillations for robust optimization), it provided an important improvement in velocity amplitude at a low pressure drop, of strong importance e.g. in design of passive micromixers.

The present manuscript intends to pave the way for AASO applications and improvements. As demonstrated in this work, the AASO-LBM method still has room for improvement, especially in terms of dealing with local minimum/maximum. Some future work can be directed into hybrid methods, the combination with low-fidelity data, the application of evolutionary rules, colorred to combine the method with Machine Learnign-aided design optimization (MLADO), or to apply collocation methods for a more efficient sampling exploration. Also, another disadvantage of the method is the fact that the shapes constructed by the algorithm are “sharp”. Whilst this is not very problematic with laminar flows, turbulent flows may behave differently due to wall modifications, that may affect turbulence flow performance. This could be improved by combining the AASO/AASO-LBM method with smoothing methods (e.g. Bézier curves or Radial Basis Functions) or the application of any free form deformation method. Scalability issues (application to costly CFD simulations) can be greatly alleviated by using a larger dimensionless sampling exploration step, symmetric exploration (if possible), and testing few scaling factors, as demonstrated in this work, but further investigation would be useful to boost the applicability of the method. Also, a future perspective could be to apply AASO/AASO-LBM in combination to AI methods, such as e.g. Reinforcement Learning, since multiple areas on the surface could be modified simultaneously acting independently but achieving a global reward. The AASO/AASO-LBM method can be of strong interest in real-world applications that involve laminar flows such as microfluidics (e.g. in passive vortex shedding-based fluid mixing), laminar mixing bioreactors that operate in laminar regime to avoid damage in cells, micro-energy harvesting from vortex induced vibrations by means of piezoelectric elements, or high-viscosity fluid mixing in the food industry.
