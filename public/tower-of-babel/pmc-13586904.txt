Methodological Reporting Quality of Artificial Intelligence-Based Orthopedic Studies: A Literature Analysis
Authors: Gündüz Ercan KUTLUAY, Ahmet Furkan KUMRU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveWe set out to examine how completely artificial intelligence (AI)-based orthopedic studies report their methods.MethodsA PubMed search covering 2023-2025 was run with a predefined strategy. Screening of titles, abstracts and full texts left 280 eligible papers, and 200 of these were drawn at random for scoring. Each paper was rated against a six-item framework built from the TRIPOD-AI, STARD-AI and CONSORT-AI guidelines.ResultsMean score across the 200 studies was 4.8±0.9. Twenty-eight percent reached 6/6, 37% scored 5/6 and 23% scored 4/6; the remaining 12% scored 3 or below. Model description, dataset characteristics and performance metrics appeared in every paper (100%), and training and validation procedures in 88%. External validation was far less common, reported by only 32%, while 61% included some form of clinical comparison.ConclusionsReporting in this literature is moderate to high overall, yet two weaknesses persist: external validation and clinical integration. Work in this area would benefit from closer attention to generalizability, clinical relevance and the existing reporting guidelines.

INTRODUCTION

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have moved artificial intelligence (AI) from a research curiosity to a working tool in medicine within a short period. Greater computing power, larger datasets and better algorithms lie behind this shift, and in image-based tasks the resulting systems now match and in some cases exceed expert readers.

Orthopedics leans heavily on radiography, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), so it was always likely to feel the effect early. Reported applications cover image analysis, disease detection, classification, surgical planning, rehabilitation and outcome prediction1, 2. Image analysis has drawn the most attention, with claims of better diagnostic accuracy, less interobserver variability and more uniform workflows3.

Publication counts have risen accordingly. Bibliometric work places the inflection point around 2017, after which DL-based imaging studies came to dominate the field4. The volume of this output says something about what is expected of AI in orthopedic practice.

The clinical picture is less encouraging. What a model achieves technically and what it delivers at the bedside are not the same thing, and the distance between the two remains the central problem. Development and validation are still commonly carried out on retrospective data from a single center, which says little about performance elsewhere. Data quality and the absence of external validation add to the difficulty1.

How these studies are reported is a separate concern. Without a transparent account of model development, dataset characteristics, validation strategy and performance measures, a reader can neither reproduce the work nor judge it. Poor reporting also leaves room for bias, and model performance may end up looking better than it is. Earlier appraisals of the orthopedic AI literature point the same way. Median TRIPOD completeness among ML prediction models in orthopedic surgery was 53%, with 41% of studies at high risk of bias5. For diagnostic imaging models, mean adherence to the CLAIM checklist was 63%, the abstract domain scoring worst6. In orthopedic trauma the median TRIPOD figure was 62%, and external validation had been performed for only 5 of 45 models7. Numbers of this kind have prompted calls for orthopedic-specific reporting guidance8.

TRIPOD-AI9, STARD-AI10 and CONSORT-AI11 were written to address exactly this. How closely the current orthopedic AI literature follows them, however, has not been established.

We therefore scored a sample of AI-based orthopedic studies against a framework derived from these guidelines, with the aim of showing where reporting is already adequate and where it is not.

MATERIALS and METHODS

Study Design

The work is a literature analysis of reporting quality in AI-based orthopedic studies.

Search Strategy

We searched PubMed with the following query: (orthopedic[Title/Abstract] OR orthopaedic[Title/Abstract]) AND (“machine learning”[Title/Abstract] OR “deep learning”[Title/Abstract] OR “artificial intelligence”[Title/Abstract]).

The search returned 1,111 records published between 2023 and 2025. Title, abstract and full-text screening against the predefined criteria left 280 eligible studies. From these, 200 were drawn by simple random sampling—a compromise between a workload that could be handled and a sample that still represents the pool—and carried forward to scoring.

Study Selection

Screening ran in three stages: titles, then abstracts, then full texts.

Studies meeting any of the following criteria were excluded:

• Studies in which AI was not used analytically within the methodology,

• Review articles, editorials, or commentaries,

• Studies in which AI was mentioned only in the discussion without being actively used in the analysis.

To be included, studies were required to use AI actively within the methodology for at least one of the following purposes:

• prediction,

• classification,

• segmentation,

• data-driven modeling.

Decisions were made on the full text, not on the title and abstract alone.

Data Collection and Assessment

Each included study was assessed against criteria we had defined in advance. The study-by-study assessments, together with the full list of included papers, appear in Supplementary File 1.

Methodological Quality Assessment

Our framework draws on the established reporting guidelines for AI-based clinical research—TRIPOD-AI, STARD-AI and CONSORT-AI9, 10, 11—which set out what a study must report if it is to be transparent, reproducible and clinically interpretable.

Six criteria recur across all three documents, and we treated these as the core elements of methodological rigor.

They were defined as follows:

1. AI model description: The type, architecture or methodological approach of the algorithm is stated clearly (TRIPOD-AI: model specification).

2. Dataset description: Data source, sample size and the main characteristics of the dataset are given (TRIPOD-AI/STARD-AI: data source and participants).

3. Training and validation process: Model development is described, including how the data were split into training, validation and test sets and, where relevant, the cross-validation approach (TRIPOD-AI: model development and validation).

4. External validation: Performance is tested on an independent dataset (TRIPOD-AI/CONSORT-AI: external validation).

5. Performance metrics: Performance is reported with suitable measures—accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity (STARD-AI: diagnostic accuracy measures).

6.Clinical comparison or relevance: The model is compared with a clinical reference standard such as expert assessment or a conventional method, or its output is interpreted in a clinical context (CONSORT-AI: clinical relevance and comparison).

Each item scored 1 if explicitly reported and 0 if not, giving a total of 0 to 6 per study.

We kept the framework deliberately compact rather than applying every item of the source guidelines, so that diagnostic, predictive and classification studies could be placed on the same scale.

Assessment Approach

Scoring was based on the full text. An element that was not explicitly reported was scored as absent; where information was missing or ambiguous we made no assumptions.

Two reviewers scored the studies independently and settled disagreements by discussion. We did not calculate a statistical measure of interobserver agreement.

Outcome Measure

The outcome measure was the reporting quality score, from 0 to 6, of each included study.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis was descriptive. Counts and percentages are given for categorical variables; continuous variables are given as mean ± standard deviation, with medians where these are informative. Each study’s score is the sum of its six binary items. No comparative hypothesis testing was carried out, since the aim was to describe reporting practice rather than to contrast groups. Statistical Package for the Social Sciences for Windows (version 21.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used throughout.

RESULTS

Study Selection

The PubMed search returned 1,111 records from 2023 to 2025. Screening left 280 eligible studies, from which 200 were randomly sampled and analyzed. Figure 1 summarizes the process.

General Characteristics

DL and ML approaches accounted for most of the sample. Imaging-based diagnostic classification, clinical outcome prediction and automated measurement were the commonest applications. Imaging data—radiography, CT and MRI—were used most often, followed by clinical and text-based datasets.

Methodological Quality Scores

Scores ranged from 0 to 6. The mean was 4.8±0.9 and the median 5. Table 1 shows the distribution.

Subgroup Observations

Studies with external validation were few, and most of these came from multicenter work. Clinical comparison was more often present in imaging-based diagnostic applications.

It was reported less often in outcome-prediction and text-based studies.

Overall Assessment

Taken together, reporting quality sits at a moderate-to-high level, with external validation and clinical integration as the clear weak points.

DISCUSSION

We scored 200 AI-based orthopedic studies against a framework taken from TRIPOD-AI, STARD-AI and CONSORT-AI. Reporting proved uneven, notwithstanding how quickly the field has grown.

Model characteristics, dataset information and performance metrics were nearly always described, so the technical layer of these papers is in reasonable shape. The gaps lie further along. External validation, reported by only 32% of studies, is the finding we consider most important: without evidence from an independent dataset there is little basis for expecting a model to work in another population, and clinical applicability suffers accordingly9.

Similar patterns have been described elsewhere. Wynants et al.12 found most prediction models to carry a high risk of bias and to be of limited use in practice; Liu et al.13 reported that AI models are largely assessed under controlled conditions with little external validation. Whether the performance figures in this literature would hold in routine care is therefore an open question.

Clinical comparison appeared in 61% of studies, which suggests that a fair proportion of this work does try to anchor model output to a clinical benchmark. The comparisons themselves vary so much in form that they are hard to read side by side. CONSORT-AI makes the point that an AI system has to be judged in a clinical context and against patient-relevant outcomes, not on technical performance alone11.

Moving a model into practice is not simply a matter of accuracy. Dataset shift, restricted generalizability and algorithmic bias can all erode performance once a system leaves its development setting14. A high reported accuracy, on its own, does not make a useful clinical tool.

The field, then, has reached a degree of methodological maturity without closing the gap to clinical use. Multicenter data, proper external validation and outcomes that matter to patients are where the effort should go next.

PROBAST offers a structured way to assess bias in prediction model studies, and DECIDE-AI and CONSORT-AI cover the evaluation of AI interventions in clinical settings11, 15, 16. Wider use of these documents would help on both counts.

Study Limitations

Several limitations apply. We searched PubMed only. Sampling 200 of the 280 eligible studies may have affected how representative the sample is. Our six-item score allows studies to be compared but does not capture every aspect of reporting quality, and the largely retrospective nature of the included work limits what can be said about real-world impact. Interobserver agreement was also not quantified with a kappa statistic.

CONCLUSION

Reporting in AI-based orthopedic research has improved, but external validation and clinical integration remain the weak links. For many technically successful models it is still unclear whether they work in practice, and that uncertainty is the main gap in the current literature.

Algorithmic performance alone is not enough; generalizability, clinical relevance and patient-centered outcomes deserve at least equal attention in future work.
