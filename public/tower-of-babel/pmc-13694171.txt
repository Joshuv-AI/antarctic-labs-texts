Changes in Burnout and Other Types of Emotional Distress Among KUSM Medical Students Between 2019 and 2025
Authors: Samuel Ofei-Dodoo, Rosey Zackula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBurnout among medical students is associated with depression, fatigue, suicidal ideation, and impaired academic performance. Authors evaluated changes in burnout and well-being among medical students at the University of Kansas School of Medicine (KUSM) between 2019 and 2025 and explored students’ perspectives on promoting wellness.MethodsA repeated cross-sectional, anonymous online survey was administered to all KUSM medical students in 2025. Measures included burnout, depression, fatigue, quality of life, general health, suicidal ideation, and open-ended wellness questions. Quantitative findings were compared with a similar 2019 survey. Bivariate analyses, multivariable logistic regression, and thematic analysis were performed.ResultsOf 808 eligible students, 295 participated (36.5%) and 288 completed the survey (response rate, 35.6%). Overall burnout prevalence remained similar to 2019 (43.4% vs. 48.0%), although depersonalization decreased significantly (16.3% vs. 26.5%; p = 0.002). Depression remained common (40.4%), while fatigue worsened significantly (p = 0.018). Overall, 7.6% of respondents reported suicidal ideation during the previous 12 months. Burnout was significantly associated with depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation (all p <0.001). Third-year students had the highest rates of psychological distress and more than twice the odds of screening positive for depression than first-year students (aOR, 2.13; 95% CI, 1.04–4.36). Qualitative analysis identified 10 themes, with students attributing burnout primarily to insufficient time, limited flexibility, excessive workload, and reduced autonomy.ConclusionsBurnout remains highly prevalent among medical students and is closely associated with depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation. Students emphasized the need for system-level interventions that increase flexibility, protected time, and autonomy to improve learner well-being.Keywords: professional burnout, medical students, depression, fatigue, mental health

INTRODUCTION

Medical students in the United States face intense academic demands, prolonged study hours, and high-stakes evaluations, all of which contribute to substantial stress and emotional strain.1–3 A large body of literature has consistently documented high rates of burnout, depression, anxiety, and other forms of psychological distress in this population.4–6 For example, a 2019 study of the University of Kansas School of Medicine (KUSM) students found that 48% reported manifestations of burnout, 46% screened positive for depression, and 44.6% experienced high levels of fatigue within the past week.4 Burnout, characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment,7 has been reported in more than half of U.S. medical students in some national surveys.8,9 These findings underscore that psychological distress is not an isolated issue but a pervasive and persistent concern across medical education.

Importantly, the consequences of emotional distress extend beyond individual well-being. Burnout and related conditions have been associated with impaired academic performance, reduced empathy, increased risk of professional misconduct, and lower long-term career satisfaction, all of which may ultimately compromise patient care and the sustainability of the physician workforce.10–12 Thus, addressing medical student wellness is not only an educational priority but also a critical component of maintaining a high-quality health care system.

In response to these concerns, the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME) now requires medical schools to implement structured wellness and support programs.13 In alignment with this mandate, KUSM has continued to support student well-being through a multifaceted approach that includes access to counseling and Student Health services, academic and personal coaching, small-group learning and peer support, wellness and recreational activities, and social and community-building opportunities. These initiatives reflect an institutional commitment to addressing students’ emotional and physical well-being. Most of these initiatives have been in place prior to the 2025 data collection. However, despite widespread implementation of such programs, there remains limited evidence regarding their effectiveness in improving student well-being over time within individual institutions. Therefore, this follow-up study was conducted to evaluate the changes in student wellness associated with these wellness initiatives at KUSM by comparing 2025 survey findings with baseline data from the 2019 study.4

METHODS

Study Design, Setting, and Participants

This repeated cross-sectional survey study included 808 medical students across the three campuses of KUSM. The 2025 survey used methods similar to those employed in the study published in 2019,4 with the addition of two questions: (1) an item assessing suicidal thoughts and (2) an open-ended question asking students to share resources and/or activities they believe would help promote wellness among medical students.

A sample size of 250 participants was determined to provide adequate statistical power (>0.95) to detect significant relationships among variables, assuming 2 degrees of freedom, an effect size of 0.30, and a significance level of p <0.05.14 The study was approved by the University of Kansas Medical Center Institutional Review Board (IRB).

The KUSM has three campuses: Kansas City, Salina, and Wichita. The Kansas City campus is a large, urban academic medical center, with class sizes of 175 students in each of the first two years and 125 in the third and fourth years. The Wichita campus, located in an urban/suburban setting, is community-based, with 28 students in each of the first two years and 75 in the third and fourth years (this includes 45 students who complete their first two years in Kansas City and then transfer to Wichita for their clinical training). The Salina campus, situated in a town of 46,889, enrolls eight students per class. Each campus offers distinct clinical experiences and educational opportunities. While the curricular goals, objectives, and assessment methods are standardized across all campuses, the student experience differs.4,15

Study Measures

The survey included items on demographic information (age, gender, year in training, and campus location), burnout, symptoms of depression, suicidal ideation, fatigue, quality of life, and self-reported general health.

Burnout

We measured burnout using two validated single-item measures of emotional exhaustion and depersonalization adapted from the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI-22).16 The emotional exhaustion item (“I feel burned out from my work/education”) and depersonalization item (“I’ve become more callous toward people since I became a medical student”) have demonstrated strong correlations with their respective MBI-22 domains and have been used in previous studies of medical students.4,8,9,16

Students rated each item on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (Never) to 6 (Every day). Scores were categorized as low, moderate, or high using established cutoffs.8,9 Higher scores indicate greater burnout. Consistent with prior studies, students with high scores in emotional exhaustion and/or depersonalization were classified as having at least one manifestation of burnout.8,9

Symptoms of Depression

Symptoms of depression were assessed using the 2-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-2), a validated screening tool for depression with reported sensitivity ranging from 72% to 91% and specificity ranging from 67% to 85%, depending on the cutoff score used.17,18 The PHQ-2 asks respondents how often, during the past 2 weeks, they have experienced (1) feeling down, depressed, or hopeless and (2) little interest or pleasure in doing usual activities.

Suicidal Ideation

Suicidal ideation was assessed using a single-item screening question: “During the past 12 months, have you had thoughts of taking your own life?” Single-item measures commonly are used in large survey-based studies to screen for suicidal ideation and have demonstrated utility for population-level surveillance, although they are intended for screening purposes and not diagnostic evaluation.19

Fatigue

Fatigue was assessed using a standardized linear analog scale ranging from 0 (as bad as it can be) to 10 (as good as it can be), consistent with the approach described by West, et al.20 Students rated their level of fatigue during the past week, and scores of 5 or lower were classified as indicating high fatigue.9

Quality of Life and General Health

Quality of Life and General Health were assessed using two single-item measures. Overall quality of life was measured using a global item from the World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL)-BREF instrument, which has been validated for use among medical students.21 Participants rated their overall quality of life on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very poor) to 5 (Very good).

General health was assessed using a single item adapted from the WHOQOL-BREF: “How satisfied are you with your health?”21 Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Very dissatisfied) to 5 (Very satisfied).

Data collection

Study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap®) tools hosted at the University of Kansas Medical Center.22,23 Each student received an email invitation containing a link to the 13-item survey. Participation was voluntary, and responses were anonymous. To prevent multiple submissions from the same individual, REDCap’s participant list feature was used to distribute a unique survey link to each student. Once a participant accessed the survey through the link, the link became invalid, ensuring that each participant could submit only one response. Data were collected from October 15, 2025, through December 31, 2025.

Statistical Analysis

Standard descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population and estimate the prevalence of burnout among the respondents. Differences between groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, chi-square tests for categorical variables, and correlation analyses as appropriate. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with burnout, symptoms of depression, and fatigue. Covariates included year in training, age, gender, and campus location. All analyses were two-sided with a significance level of α = 0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM® SPSS® Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). No adjustment was made for multiple comparisons; secondary analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

We used immersion-crystallization approach24 to analyze the content of the open-ended responses regarding resources and/or activities the respondents believed would help promote wellness among medical students.

The study followed STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology)25 and CHERRIES (The Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys)26 reporting guidelines.

RESULTS

Demographic Information

Among the 808 KUSM medical students invited to participate in the 2025 survey, 295 completed at least part of the questionnaire, yielding a participation rate of 36.5%. Of these, 288 completed the entire survey, resulting in an overall response rate of 35.6% and a survey completion rate of 97.6%. The 2025 sample had a margin of error of 4.6% at the 95% confidence level, indicating acceptable precision for estimating characteristics of the overall KUSM medical student population.27

Among the 288 respondents who completed the survey, 171 (59.8%) were female, 239 (86.0%) were younger than 30 years, and 83 (29.0%) were first-year medical students (Table 1). Compared with respondents to the 2019 survey, the 2025 cohort differed significantly with respect to age group (χ2[4] = 18.03, p = 0.001) and campus (χ2[2] = 15.17, p <0.001). No significant differences were observed between survey cohorts in gender or year in medical school.

Overall, 7.6% (n = 22) of respondents reported having thoughts of taking their own life during the previous 12 months. By campus, suicidal ideation was reported by 7.5% (n = 12) of respondents from the Kansas City campus, 7.2% (n = 8) from the Wichita campus, and 14.3% (n = 2) from the Salina campus. Rates by year in medical school were 7.3%, 8.2%, 9.9%, and 5.7% among first-, second-, third-, and fourth-year students, respectively. Although these differences were not statistically significant, third-year students consistently exhibited the numerically highest rates of burnout, depression, suicidal ideation, and fatigue. Despite these findings, students across all years generally rated their quality of life and overall health favorably.

Open-ended Results

A total of 127 respondents provided open-ended feedback on resources and activities they believed would promote wellness among medical students. Thematic analysis identified 10 major themes, which are presented in Table 4 in descending order based on the frequency with which each theme was identified in respondents’ comments. Across these themes, students consistently emphasized that burnout stems less from a lack of wellness resources than from insufficient time, flexibility, and autonomy. The most frequently identified recommendations included protected time off, more flexible absence policies, improved access to mental health services, reduced administrative burden, and practical support to enable students to prioritize their well-being.

DISCUSSION

This study provides an updated assessment of burnout and well-being among medical students at KUSM six years after the institution’s previous wellness survey. Although the overall prevalence of burnout remained largely unchanged between 2019 and 2025 (48.0% vs. 43.4%), several important findings emerged. First, depersonalization declined significantly, suggesting modest improvement in one dimension of burnout. Second, fatigue worsened despite relatively stable rates of burnout and depression. Third, students experiencing burnout were substantially more likely to report depressive symptoms, fatigue, and suicidal ideation. Finally, the qualitative findings demonstrated that students perceive burnout to be driven less by a lack of wellness resources than by limited time, flexibility, and autonomy. Together, these findings suggest that institutional approaches focused primarily on wellness programming may be insufficient without concurrent efforts to address structural and contextual contributors to student well-being.

The prevalence of burnout observed in this study is consistent with reports from medical schools nationally and internationally. A systematic review by Frajerman, et al.28 estimated that approximately 44% of medical students experience burnout, while other meta-analyses have reported prevalence estimates ranging from 37% to 52%, depending on the instruments used and study populations.29 Our findings therefore suggest that burnout remains a persistent challenge despite increasing institutional attention to learner well-being. Although depersonalization declined significantly compared with the 2019 survey, the persistence of emotional exhaustion and overall burnout indicates that meaningful improvements in student well-being remain difficult to achieve.

One notable finding was the significant increase in fatigue despite relatively stable rates of burnout and depression. Fatigue has increasingly been recognized as an important indicator of learner well-being that may precede or accompany burnout. The worsening fatigue observed in this study may reflect the cumulative effects of excessive workload, chronic sleep deprivation, and competing academic and clinical responsibilities, all of which have been associated with fatigue, burnout, and psychological distress among medical students and residents.30–33 These demands often intensify during the transition to clinical training, when students must simultaneously balance patient care responsibilities, licensing examination preparation, frequent evaluations, and uncertainty regarding residency applications.9,30,32 Our qualitative findings support this interpretation, as students overwhelmingly attributed burnout to insufficient time, limited flexibility, and competing academic demands rather than a lack of wellness resources.

Although differences by class year were not statistically significant, a consistent pattern emerged in which burnout, depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation peaked during the third year of medical school before improving during the fourth year. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies demonstrating that the transition into clinical clerkships represents one of the most stressful periods of undergraduate medical education.30,34 Third-year students must adapt to new clinical environments, frequent schedule changes, professional evaluations, and uncertainty regarding residency competitiveness, while also preparing for licensing examinations. Our multivariable analysis further supports this observation, with third-year students having more than twice the odds of screening positive for depression compared with first-year students. These findings suggest that wellness and mental health interventions may be particularly valuable during the transition into clinical training.

The finding that male students had lower odds of reporting high fatigue than female students also warrants consideration. Although gender remained significantly associated with fatigue in the adjusted model, the survey did not assess several personal and family factors that may contribute to differences in fatigue, including marital or partnership status, parenting, caregiving responsibilities, and other competing demands outside of medical school. These factors may represent important unmeasured confounders or contextual influences on student well-being. Importantly, the open-ended responses highlighted the challenges students face in balancing medical education with family and personal responsibilities, suggesting that well-being cannot be fully understood by examining academic demands in isolation. These competing priorities may become increasingly relevant as students progress through medical school and their personal circumstances evolve. In particular, the third year may represent a period when increasing academic and clinical demands coincide with changes in students’ personal and family responsibilities. Students may marry, divorce, become parents, assume caregiving responsibilities, or take on other personal commitments that affect available time, stress, and overall well-being. Future studies should therefore collect more comprehensive information about students’ personal and family circumstances to better understand how these factors interact with medical education demands, fatigue, and burnout. Such information also would help clarify whether observed gender differences in fatigue reflect differences in the educational experience, differences in competing responsibilities, or a combination of these factors.

Consistent with previous literature, students experiencing burnout were significantly more likely to report depressive symptoms, fatigue, and suicidal ideation. These findings reinforce the well-established relationship between burnout and adverse mental health outcomes among medical students. Dyrbye and colleagues have repeatedly demonstrated that burnout is associated with depression, suicidal ideation, decreased empathy, and reduced professionalism among medical trainees.30,35,36 Although this study cannot establish causality because of its cross-sectional design, the strong associations observed underscore the importance of routinely assessing multiple dimensions of psychological distress rather than burnout alone.

Perhaps the most important contribution of this study comes from the qualitative findings. Students reported that the institution already offers numerous wellness resources, including counseling services, coaching, peer support, and wellness activities, but emphasized that they often lack sufficient time, flexibility, and autonomy to use them. Rather than requesting additional wellness lectures or programming, students consistently advocated for protected time off, flexible absence policies, fewer administrative requirements, predictable schedules, and reduced curricular burden. These findings align with growing evidence that organizational and educational factors contribute substantially to burnout and that interventions focused solely on individual resilience may be insufficient.37,38 The open-ended responses further suggest that the competing demands of medical education and students’ personal lives should be considered when developing institutional approaches to well-being.

The qualitative findings also can be interpreted through the lens of Self-Determination Theory (SDT), which proposes that psychological well-being depends on satisfaction of three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.39,40 Students repeatedly described barriers that directly reflect frustration of these needs. Requests for greater schedule flexibility, protected wellness time, and fewer mandatory activities reflect a desire for greater autonomy over learning and personal life. Calls for clearer expectations regarding residency preparation, remediation, and academic requirements suggest a need to strengthen students’ sense of competence. Meanwhile, recommendations emphasizing mentoring, small-group learning communities, peer support, counseling, and opportunities to spend time with family and friends illustrate the importance of relatedness. The latter is particularly relevant given students’ descriptions of competing family and personal responsibilities, which may influence their ability to maintain meaningful relationships and sources of support. According to SDT, environments that support these three needs foster intrinsic motivation, psychological well-being, and resilience, whereas environments that undermine them may contribute to emotional exhaustion, disengagement, and burnout.38,40

Our findings therefore support a shift from primarily individual-focused wellness initiatives toward more system-level interventions. National organizations, including the National Academy of Medicine and the Association of American Medical Colleges, have increasingly emphasized that burnout largely is an organizational phenomenon requiring organizational solutions.41,42 Counseling services and wellness activities remain important components of student support, but these resources are unlikely to fully address burnout if students continue to experience excessive workload, inflexible scheduling, competing demands, and limited autonomy. Structural interventions such as protected wellness time, streamlined curricula, transparent communication, flexible leave policies, predictable scheduling, and enhanced support during clinical transitions may better address the underlying contributors identified by students. In addition, future institutional wellness assessments should consider students’ personal and family circumstances so that interventions can account for the broader context in which students manage the demands of medical education.

Interestingly, despite relatively high rates of burnout, depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation, most respondents continued to report good quality of life and satisfaction with their overall health. Similar findings have been observed elsewhere and may reflect the multidimensional nature of well-being.43 Students may perceive their overall quality of life positively because of supportive peer relationships, strong professional identity formation, meaningful patient interactions, or optimism regarding future careers despite experiencing substantial psychological distress.44 This apparent paradox further emphasizes that a single global measure of quality of life may not adequately capture the complexity of learner well-being. A comprehensive assessment should instead consider multiple dimensions, including burnout, fatigue, depressive symptoms, social and family demands, and students’ perceptions of autonomy, support, and overall quality of life.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. It represents one of the few repeated institutional surveys directly comparing burnout and wellness outcomes over time using similar methodology. The inclusion of quantitative and qualitative data provides a more comprehensive understanding of student well-being than either approach alone. Additionally, incorporating suicidal ideation into the 2025 survey offers important contemporary data regarding mental health among medical students.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and self-reported measures may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Although the response rate was comparable with many contemporary surveys of medical students, nonresponse bias remains possible. The study was conducted at a single medical school, which may limit generalizability to other institutions. The relatively small number of respondents from the Salina campus limited statistical power for campus-specific comparisons. Finally, the timing of data collection may have influenced the prevalence estimates. Data were collected between October 15 and December 31, 2025, during a period of varying academic and clinical demands. Because levels of burnout, depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation may fluctuate throughout the academic year, the findings may not fully represent students’ experiences at other time points. Longitudinal studies are needed to better characterize temporal changes in medical student well-being.

CONCLUSIONS

Burnout remains highly prevalent among KUSM medical students and continues to be strongly associated with depression, fatigue, and suicidal ideation. Although prevalence of depersonalization was lower than observed in 2019, overall burnout remained common, and fatigue worsened. Third-year students consistently exhibited the numerically highest burden, highlighting an important opportunity for targeted intervention during the transition to clinical training. Importantly, students identified insufficient time, flexibility, and autonomy, not a lack of wellness resources, as the primary drivers of burnout. Viewed through the framework of SDT, these findings suggest that institutional efforts should move beyond expanding wellness programming toward creating learning environments that better support students’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Such organizational approaches may provide a more sustainable strategy for improving medical student well-being.
