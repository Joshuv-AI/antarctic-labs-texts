Topology and Network Dynamics of the Lightning Network: A Comprehensive Analysis Journal: PREPRINT
Danila Valko, Jorge Marx Gómez

Abstract
Leveraging a validated set of reconstructed Lightning Network topology snapshots spanning five years (2019–2023), we computed 47 computationally intensive metrics and network attributes, enabling a comprehensive analysis of the network’s structure and temporal dynamics. Our results corroborate prior topology studies while offering deeper insight into the network’s structural evolution. In particular, we quantify the network’s topological stability over time, yielding implications for the design of heuristic-based pathfinding and routing protocols. More broadly, this work provides a detailed characterization of publicly available Lightning Network snapshots, supporting future research in Payment Channel Network analysis and network science.



1
Introduction

Payment Channel Networks (PCNs) are off-chain scaling solutions that enable users to transact through pre-funded, bidirectional channels without broadcasting each transaction to the underlying blockchain. By settling only the final results on-chain, PCNs significantly reduce network congestion and transaction fees, thus enabling high-throughput and low-cost micro payments. The Lightning Network (LN) is the most prominent implementation of a PCN, built atop Bitcoin. Other notable examples include the
Raiden Network
[
31
]
for Ethereum and the multi-chain-compatible
Celer Network
[
7
]
.

LN holds significant promise as a scalable and efficient layer for decentralized digital payments. However, realizing its full potential requires a deeper understanding of its network topology and systemic behavior. Ongoing development efforts are focused on improving routing efficiency, enhancing privacy, and expanding liquidity through techniques such as channel splicing and multi-path payments. Despite this progress, challenges persist – particularly in the areas of infrastructure utilization, channel management, and interoperability.

The topology of the LN was first systematically analyzed by Seres et al.
[
36
]
. Their research, studied two network snapshots from January 2019, introduced early insights into degree distribution and explored the network’s robustness against random node failures. A more detailed investigation was conducted by Rohrer et al.
[
33
]
, who also assessed robustness and introduced key topological metrics such as network diameter and average distance. Their analysis, based on two observation periods (October-November 2018 and January-February 2019), emphasized structural vulnerabilities associated with node dominance. Subsequently, Martinazzi and Flori
[
24
]
presented an early study covering one year of the LN’s evolution from January 2, 2018, to January 12, 2019. They focused on centralization trends, efficiency metrics, and the network’s resilience against attacks exploiting the influence of highly connected nodes.

Expanding the analytical scope, Zabka et al.
[
44
,
42
]
conducted a large-scale empirical study, classifying LN nodes by software client and geographic location using seven snapshots between March 2018 and January 2020. Their analysis revealed that node proximity played a minor role, so that nodes tend to connect to large hubs, even if there is a large geographical distance between them. Their accurate client classification contributed valuable insights into the LN’s implementation diversity, which is relevant for economical analysis and assessing security risks. Together with Mizrahi and Zohar
[
25
]
, these works laid the foundation for accessing basic LN statistics and topology characteristics, although their analyses were limited in temporal scope.

The most comprehensive and up-to-date study is also done by Zabka et al. (2024)
[
43
]
, who analyzed over seven million network messages across ten reconstructed network snapshots from 2019 to 2021. Their work revealed that the top 5% of nodes handle the majority of transaction paths, and that centrality inequality (measured by the
Gini
index) increased by more than 15 points during the observed period. These findings suggest growing structural centralization, raising critical concerns about the network’s resilience, fairness, and user privacy. Complementing this empirical approach, Guasoni et al.
[
14
]
explored LN topologies from a theoretical standpoint. They demonstrated that minimizing routing costs can inherently lead to centralized structures. Their work showed that finding the globally optimal topology is NP-complete and that intuitive configurations, such as star topologies, are not always cost-optimal.

Despite covering broader time windows and introducing new topology-related metrics, current research still provides only a partial view of what full-scale network science analysis could offer. A more holistic and temporally consistent approach is needed to fully capture the dynamic structural properties of the LN.

From a research standpoint, access to validated and well-documented network data is critical for accurate modeling, benchmarking, and simulation of real-world LN behavior. Notably, there exists a single major public data source that captures LN gossip messages over a long time period, now 2019-2023
[
10
]
. These messages can be used to reconstruct historical snapshots of the LN topology at different points in time. While this data has supported research in areas such as routing optimization and liquidity management, only a limited number of studies have deeply examined the architectural, topological, and temporal features of the LN itself (e.g.
[
36
,
44
,
42
,
43
]
). To date, there has been no comprehensive analysis utilizing this data source, nor any work thoroughly characterizing the network’s structural evolution and stability using a wide set of network science metrics.

This gap is significant, particularly for applications relying on heuristic-based pathfinding, routing optimization, or pattern mining – each of which assumes some degree of topological consistency and stability in the underlying network. Discovering the temporal stability of LN’s topology is thus essential.

To this end, we previously developed and validated a dataset containing 336 Lightning Network topology snapshots
[
41
,
40
]
spanning the years 2019–2023, which we use here to conduct a comprehensive analysis. Accordingly, this work aims to:

1.

Leverage the historical LN topology dataset to perform an extensive topological and temporal analysis using a diverse set of computationally intensive network-science metrics, and to share the resulting computations in machine-readable form with the broader research community.

2.

Define and evaluate metrics of topological stability to quantify the extent to which the LN’s payment structure remains consistent over time.

3
Results

3.1
Extended network analysis

3.1.1
Network sparsity

The LN is widely recognized as a
sparse
network, meaning that only a small fraction of all possible connections between nodes are actually present. This sparsity arises from its design as a
peer-to-peer payment channel network
, where users open channels selectively, typically driven by considerations of performance, cost, or trust. Unlike fully connected or
dense
networks, most LN nodes maintain relatively few connections, while a small subset of
hub nodes
manage a disproportionately large number of channels.

The key structural properties reflecting this sparsity include: (1)
Low average degree
. The majority of nodes have only a few connections. (2)
Low edge density
. For
n
nodes, a fully connected graph contains
n(n-1)/2
edges. In contrast, the LN is expected to exhibit a relatively much lower edge count. Early studies already identified this low density and the resulting centralization around key nodes
[
33
,
36
]
, an extended view on the current dynamics is on the
Figure
2
. It shows that, although the total number of nodes and channels grows over time, the average node degree and overall density steadily decline indicating that network expansion is not matched by proportional connectivity growth.

Figure 2:
The average node degree and the network density over time

This sparsity has several important implications. First, routing efficiency relies heavily on a small number of central nodes, which can make the network more vulnerable to targeted attacks
[
25
]
. Second, the network benefits in terms of scalability and cost-efficiency, as fewer connections reduce overhead and allow for more flexible, on-demand channel creation. Finally, sparsity can improve privacy by limiting the visibility of transactions across the network, although actual privacy levels also depend on the specific routing algorithms used
[
27
]
.

Having established that the LN is structurally sparse, we next examine whether this sparsity follows a particular pattern. Specifically, whether the LN’s degree distribution exhibits the heavy-tailed behavior characteristic of scale-free networks.

3.1.2
Scale-free network properties

A scale-free network is characterized by a degree distribution that follows a power law, at least asymptotically. Mathematically, the probability
P(k)
that a node has degree
k
follows
P(k)\sim k^{-\alpha}
, where
\alpha
is typically between 2 and 3
[
8
]
. This distribution implies that while most nodes have few connections and a small number of nodes, that are known as
hubs
, are highly connected and play a critical role in maintaining the structure and function of the network. Typically, the LN is expected to exhibit high clustering with localized connectivity. Recent longitudinal studies have confirmed both its persistent sparsity and the dominance of a small number of high-degree nodes in its topology
[
44
,
43
]
.

The key characteristics of a scale-free network include: (1) Most nodes have a low degree, and the network exhibits a heavy-tailed degree distribution. (2) A small number of nodes have a very high degree, making the presence of
hubs
a defining feature. (3) There is no characteristic scale for node connectivity, which gives rise to the term "scale-free". (4) In comparison to random graphs, scale-free networks are generally robust to random failures, as the chance of a critical amount of high-degree nodes failing concurrently is very small
[
33
]
.

In this work, we aimed to numerically confirm the scale-free nature and power-law degree distribution of the LN over the available time frame. To this end, we adopted the methodology presented in
[
33
]
, namely, fitting a power-law curve to the empirical degree distributions of our LN snapshots.

Figure
3
presents a graphical representation of the fitted curves. The average estimate for the power-law exponent
|\alpha|
is 2.19±0.09

While preferential attachment explains how new links are formed, it does not reveal how robust the resulting structure is. Therefore, we next analyze the LN’s connectivity and resilience, focusing on how well the network withstands link removals or node failures.

3.1.3
Connectivity and resilience

The foundational metric for analyzing network connectivity is
the number of bridges
(or cut-edges) – edges whose removal increases the number of connected components within the graph. A higher number of bridges typically indicates a more fragile network structure, characterized by numerous single points of failure. As illustrated in
Figure
5
, the number of bridges rises noticeably after 2020, suggesting that the LN is becoming increasingly vulnerable to targeted node or channel failures despite its overall growth.

A related concept is
the minimal edge cover
, defined as a set of edges such that every node is incident to at least one edge. A smaller edge cover suggests a sparse yet efficient configuration that guarantees basic node reachability. In the context of the LN, the size of the minimal edge cover increases over time (see
Figure
5
), indicating growing connectivity demands, which align with the observed increase in the number of bridges.

Transitivity, or the global clustering coefficient, captures the probability that two neighbors of a node are themselves connected. Higher transitivity signifies greater local redundancy, which contributes positively to fault tolerance. Conversely, lower transitivity is associated with loosely connected subgraphs, reducing robustness. The latter pattern in the LN is evident in
Figure
5
.

Similarly, the average clustering coefficient, which represents the mean of local clustering coefficients across all nodes, offers insight into the network’s local structure. In the case of the LN, declining average clustering values suggest growing sparsity and less resilient local connections.

Overall, this analysis indicates that while the LN exhibits increasing connectivity in terms of edge coverage, it simultaneously develops structural vulnerabilities. The growing number of bridges points to a fragile topology with several critical links, while low clustering metrics suggest insufficient local redundancy. These factors combined highlight the need for strategic improvements in the network’s design to enhance both global robustness and local fault tolerance.

Beyond structural robustness, it is also essential to understand how the LN’s topology affects information flow and operational efficiency. The following subsection examines these dynamic aspects of network function.

3.1.4
Function and dynamics

To analyze the function and dynamics of the LN, it is important to understand how efficiently information flows through the network, which nodes are critical to that flow, and how robust or vulnerable the structure is to changes. For this analysis, we calculated nine metrics, of which four are particularly essential:
global efficiency
,
information centrality
,
effective size
, and
Burt’s effective size
.

As shown above, both the average degree and network density decline over time, indicating that nodes are forming fewer connections and the overall connectivity of the network is weakening. As the network becomes sparser, the focal node exhibits improved local efficiency, reflected in an increase in Burt’s effective size (see
Figure
6
). This suggests that while the number of connections may have decreased, the remaining ties are less redundant and more structurally valuable. However, the simultaneous decline in effective size, information centrality, and global efficiency reveals a more nuanced picture. Although the node retains a strategic position within its immediate neighborhood, its overall access to the broader network is diminishing. The drop in information centrality indicates a reduced role in facilitating communication between other nodes, while the decline in global efficiency suggests that the network as a whole is becoming less effective at transmitting information quickly and broadly.

Together, these trends point to a shift toward a more fragmented and less cohesive network – one that is increasingly unable to support efficient information flow at scale. In this context, a node may maintain local structural importance but lose broader influence and reach. Thus, in line with the connectivity and resilience analysis above, while a node’s remaining ties may be non-redundant and structurally valuable, its overall influence and access may be shrinking as the LN becomes more isolated.

3.1.5
Emergent patterns

This analysis provides a more detailed understanding of how the network patterns evolve over time. While it was previously shown that the network is growing in size, it is not becoming more cohesive. In fact, metrics such as the
resource allocation index
and the
Jaccard coefficient
suggest that new connections are more likely to be cross-cutting or bridging previously isolated regions, rather than reinforcing existing clusters (see
Figure
7
).

The observed decline in both the resource allocation index and the Jaccard coefficient, combined with an increase in the number of detected communities (as estimated using the fast label propagation algorithm
[
37
]
and the asynchronous label propagation algorithm
[
30
]
), points to a network that is becoming increasingly fragmented and structurally dispersed, see
Figure
7
. A lower resource allocation index indicates that nodes are sharing fewer common neighbors, which reduces the potential for efficient information or resource flow based on local network structure. Similarly, the declining Jaccard coefficient reflects a decrease in neighborhood overlap between connected nodes, suggesting that ties are becoming less cohesive and more diverse. Meanwhile, the growing number of communities implies that the network is splitting into a larger number of smaller, tightly-knit groups that are internally cohesive but more isolated from one another.

Together, these trends indicate a loss of global integration and weakening overall cohesion, as the network shifts from a more interconnected structure to one characterized by fragmented clusters with limited overlap and shared connectivity.

To assess whether this structural fragmentation affects the network’s stability and routing behavior, we next turn to a detailed analysis of topological persistence and payment hop distribution over time.

3.2
Payment hop distribution and topological stability analysis

In this section, we analyze the
stability
of the LN topology from two distinct perspectives. The first is an abstract, network-theoretic view, focusing on the statistical properties and stability of the network’s structural features. The second is a domain-specific view, evaluating stability in the context of payment functionality within the network.

For the first perspective, we examine the network’s degree distribution, a fundamental topological metric, and perform statistical analyses to assess its temporal stability. Specifically, we apply the
Kolmogorov–Smirnov
(KS) test, which evaluates whether two samples are drawn from the same distribution. The null hypothesis of the KS test assumes that the two samples share the same distribution, and a p-value below 0.05 indicates that this hypothesis should be rejected.

We conducted the KS test between successive network snapshots taken at times
t
and
t+1
to evaluate how the degree distribution evolves over time. The right panel of Figure
8
illustrates the temporal distribution of p-values resulting from KS tests. As shown, very few (3.9%) transitions between snapshots are statistically significant (i.e., p < 0.05). These significant changes typically occur near large data gaps, where snapshots differ due to technical limitations.

The left panel of Figure
8
shows the
Wasserstein
distance
[
32
]
between corresponding degree distributions of successive snapshots. This distance metric quantifies the minimum cost of transforming one distribution into another; smaller values indicate greater similarity. As observed, the trend of Wasserstein distances closely mirrors the KS p-value dynamics, with notable deviations primarily occurring also around data gaps. On average, the absolute distance remains low, approximately 0.17, suggesting minimal change between most successive snapshots.

Figure 8:
Degree distribution stability tests

For the second perspective, we assess topological stability from payment pathfinding and operational point of view. We defined a metric called the
node intersection rate
, which measures the fraction of overlapping nodes (based on unique node
ID
s) between two consecutive snapshots. Formally, for two snapshots
S_{t}
and
S_{t+1}
, the node intersection rate
I_{node}
is defined as:

I_{node}(\scalebox{0.8}{\mbox{$\displaystyle t,t+1$}})=\frac{|V_{t}\cap V_{t+1}|}{|V_{t}|},

(1)

where
V_{t}
and
V_{t+1}
represent the sets of nodes in snapshots
t
and
t+1
, respectively.

For channel-level stability, we introduced the
channel intersection rate
approximation, which considers whether a direct channel or an equivalent route of equal or shorter length exists between the same pair of nodes in both snapshots. This accounts for potential reconfigurations in the network while preserving the routing capability. The channel intersection rate
I_{channel}
is defined as:

I_{channel}(\scalebox{0.8}{\mbox{$\displaystyle t,t+1$}})=\frac{|E_{t}^{\sim}\cap E_{t+1}^{\sim}|}{|E_{t}^{\sim}|},

(2)

where
E^{\sim}
is the set of effective channels or equivalent paths for the node intersection
V_{t}\cap V_{t+1}
in snapshots at a time
t
and
t+1
, respectively.

Figure
9
demonstrates this stability quantitatively: the node intersection rate (left) remains close to 1.0 across nearly the entire period, meaning that the vast majority of nodes persist between consecutive snapshots. Similarly, the channel intersection rate (right) stays consistently high, indicating that most payment paths remain intact over time. Together, these results highlight the network’s remarkable structural persistence.

Since the network is organized into active node hubs and neighborhoods, we sought to estimate the intersection metrics by taking their existence into account. Specifically, we employed the advanced
ForestFire
[
34
]
sampling technique to randomly select 100 naturally connected subgraphs, each consisting of 100 nodes, uniformly covering the entire network graph in each snapshot for intersection rate calculations. This sampling method is well-regarded for effectively capturing the topological features and statistical characteristics of large-scale graphs
[
18
]
.

Figure 9:
Node and channel intersection over time

Notably, the sampled parts of the network (see solid line in the
Figure
9
) are even more stable, on average, than the full network. For nodes, the average intersection rate for the samples is about 0.996, compared to 0.988 in the full network. For channels, the sampled average is 0.993, compared to 0.984 in the full network. This suggests that the neighborhoods and most important parts of the network stay active and well-connected over time. Even the direct intersection between the earliest snapshot (20-01-2019) and the latest one (16-07-2023) shows a notable overlap: 31% of nodes and 73% of channels are retained. When considering the sampled version, the node overlap increases to 43%, having 72% for channels.

To evaluate payment hop stability, we analyze the proportion of payment hops that repeatedly traverse the same nodes across snapshots. This reflects the persistence of routing importance among key nodes over time and complements the structural view with a dynamic perspective on usage patterns.

To obtain data on payment routing, we generated a set of 5,000 uniformly distributed transactions across each network snapshot. We then simulated the pathfinding process using a Dijkstra-based shortest path algorithm, incorporating the approximated cost functions of widely used LN software clients, namely,
LND
[
21
]
,
Eclair
(
ECL
)
[
12
]
, and
Core Lightning
(
CLN
)
[
9
]
. Finally, the total number of hops made by each of the three native algorithms for every unique node
ID
was calculated.

The

Despite temporal variations, the distribution remains consistently skewed, with a small set of nodes handling the majority of hops. This indicates persistent dynamic centralization in routing, highlighting the dominance of key nodes over time. The average trend (dashed line) reinforces the network’s stable yet uneven hop distribution, despite growth and changes in the network.

Finally, to quantify the decentralization and inequality within the LN snapshots, we employ the
Gini
index, following the approach of Zabka et al.
[

]
, particularly in the context of multi-path payments.

The
Lorenz

A lower
Gini
index indicates a more equitable and decentralized network, whereas a higher value reflects growing centralization and increased vulnerability – particularly if the most frequently used nodes become unresponsive. Obviously, a topology dominated by a small number of highly influential nodes, through which the majority of payments are routed, may compromise the overall robustness of the network
[
43
]
. However, from a network stability perspective, it is evident that these central nodes tend to remain online for extended periods or are seamlessly replaced, along with their associated payment channels, thereby contributing to pathfinding stability.

These results together provide strong empirical evidence that the LN has become increasingly centralized over time, reaching
Gini
values exceeding 0.95, but also topologically stable. These findings align with our above results obtained from evaluating actual LN’s routing algorithms (
LND
,
ECL
, and
CLN
) and payment hops distribution, as illustrated in the
Figure

.

Overall, this analysis supports the conclusion that the general LN topology remains largely stable, despite gradually evolving over time.

4
Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive longitudinal analysis of the LN, highlighting several key structural and functional trends over time. In summary, the LN demonstrates strong topological stability but is simultaneously evolving toward increased fragmentation and centralization.

First, the connectivity and resilience analysis reveals that: although the LN shows increasing connectivity (e.g., a larger minimal edge cover), it also displays greater fragility, reflected in the rising number of bridges and declining clustering coefficients. This indicates a network that is expanding but structurally vulnerable, with weak local fault tolerance.

Second, from a functional and dynamic perspective, the LN is becoming less efficient in terms of global communication, as evidenced by declining global efficiency and information centrality. However, nodes are increasingly positioned in strategic local configurations, reflected in the rise of Burt’s effective size. This duality highlights the network’s move toward localized optimization at the expense of global cohesion.

Third, the emergent structural patterns analysis point to increasing fragmentation. Declining values in the resource allocation index and Jaccard coefficient, combined with a rising number of communities, suggest that new links are bridging disparate parts of the network rather than reinforcing existing structures. As a result, the LN is developing into a set of loosely connected clusters rather than a tightly integrated mesh.

Fourth, the topological stability analysis confirms that the LN core is highly stable over time, with node and channel intersection rates consistently exceeding 0.95. Even sampled sub-networks show exceptionally high continuity, indicating persistent structural core regions within the network. Additionally, payment hop distribution and Gini coefficient analyses underscore a persistent and growing centralization in routing, where a small number of nodes consistently handle the majority of traffic.

These findings offer both alignment and contrast with existing literature, while also offering actionable insights for network development and directions for future research. For instance, these results partially align with previous studies
[
43
,
2
]
, who observed high centrality and highlighted the risks of further routing centralization. Similarly, earlier works have acknowledged the LN’s inherent trade-off between decentralization and performance. However, this study extends prior findings by offering a multi-faceted temporal analysis, revealing that while centralization persists and the whole network became fragmented, the routing structure remains remarkably stable – suggesting that central nodes are not only dominant but also reliably present.

The findings highlight the need for strategic measures to strengthen the LN’s resilience at both local and global levels. One important step is to encourage redundancy through topology-aware design, by promoting routing policies or incentives that support the creation of redundant, non-bridge links and increase clustering, thereby reducing single points of failure. Additionally, implementing decentralization incentives, such as adaptive fee structures or localized routing algorithms, could help distribute traffic more evenly across the network and lessen the dependence on a small set of central nodes. Thus, while the LN demonstrates commendable structural stability, its increasing fragmentation and centralization raise concerns about future scalability and robustness. Addressing these challenges will require both protocol-level innovations and deeper behavioral insights into the evolving dynamics of LN participants.

5
Limitations

While this study provides a comprehensive longitudinal assessment of the LN topology and dynamics, several limitations should be acknowledged to contextualize the findings.

First, data completeness and coverage present inherent constraints. The dataset is derived from publicly available collections of gossip messages, which may not capture the exact LN representation at all times. Missing or delayed propagation of gossip data, combined with network churn, can result in temporary under-representation of nodes or channels. Although temporal validation using external datasets confirmed high consistency, residual data gaps could affect fine-grained temporal comparisons.

Additionally, the reconstruction methodology necessarily involves simplifications. Reconstructing snapshots provide robust temporal resolution but may not fully capture intra-period events such as short-lived channels or transient routing behaviors.

Second, metric interpretation is bounded by the chosen analytical framework. While the study computed an extensive set of network science metrics, these measures capture structural characteristics rather than economic or behavioral dimensions, such as liquidity distribution, fee dynamics, or channel capacities. Consequently, some functional aspects of LN routing performance and user behavior remain outside the present analysis. Moreover, simulation assumptions in the payment hop analysis, particularly those using Dijkstra-based pathfinding and approximated cost functions, represent simplified models of real LN routing algorithms and circumstances. Actual routing may incorporate dynamic fee updates, liquidity constraints, or probabilistic heuristics not reflected here in full.

Despite these limitations, the adopted methodology, data and its validation checks, and multi-metric design provide a strong empirical basis for understanding the LN’s topological evolution. Future research could extend this framework by integrating directional and capacity-weighted analyses, improving data collection coverage, and linking network topology to transaction-level or user-level behavior.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we provided a detailed longitudinal analysis of the LN’s topology from 2019 to 2023 by utilizing reconstructed topology snapshots derived from publicly available
gossip
messages data.
Notably, we quantified topological stability over time, a critical factor for routing protocols and economic modeling. By making our methods and metrics transparent and openly available, we aim to support ongoing research into the LN’s architecture, resilience, and long-term scalability.
