Dimensional crossover of class D real-space topological invariants
Martin Rodriguez-Vega, Terry A. Loring, Alexander Cerjan

Abstract
The topological properties of a material depend on its symmetries, parameters, and spatial dimension. Changes in these properties due to parameter and symmetry variations can be understood by computing the corresponding topological invariant. Since topological invariants are typically defined for a fixed spatial dimension, there is no existing framework to understand the effects of changing spatial dimensions via invariants. Here, we introduce a framework to study topological phase transitions as a system’s dimensionality is altered using real-space topological markers. Specifically, we consider Shiba lattices, which are class D materials formed by magnetic atoms on the surface of a conventional superconductor, and characterize the evolution of their topology when an initial circular island is deformed into a chain. We also provide a measure of the corresponding protection against disorder. Our framework is generalizable to any symmetry class and spatial dimension, potentially guiding the design of materials by identifying, for example, the minimum thickness of a slab required to maintain three-dimensional topological properties.



I
Introduction

The possible topological properties of non-interacting Hamiltonians are dictated by their spatial dimension, and discrete and crystal symmetries
Chiu et al. 2016
. In particular, changing the dimension of a system while keeping its discrete symmetries unaltered generally yields a topological phase transition. Examples in condensed matter systems include transitions from a trivial insulator to a quantum spin Hall (QSH) phase in HgTe quantum wells
Bernevig et al. 2006
;
König et al. 2007
, and transitions from three-dimensional topological insulators with two-dimensional surface states to QSH states with one-dimensional helical states
Zhang et al. 2010
;
Park et al. 2015
;
Wang et al. 2019
;
Salehi et al. 2016
.

Such topological transitions due to dimensional shifts have previously been characterized theoretically by analyzing edge states in finite-size systems, the topological invariants based on band representation
Liu et al. 2010
;
Scheurer et al. 2015
, and transport properties
Kobayashi et al. 2015
. However, a real-space framework capable of illuminating how a system’s topology changes during a dimensional shift has traditionally been missing due to the dearth of local topological markers needed to probe the topology of systems lacking translational symmetry. Such a theory could be useful to guide the design of materials and next-generation devices, for example, to identify how thin a slab can be while maintaining its three-dimensional topological properties, or how thick a nanowire can be while maintaining its edge Majorana modes.

\mathcal{C}

We introduce two local topological markers derived from the spectral localizer, a Chern number, and a
\mathcal{Z}_{2}
invariant that characterizes two and one-dimensional Shiba lattices embedded in two-dimensional superconductors. As these markers do not rely on projecting to an occupied subspace, they are endowed with a measure of the corresponding local topological protection related to the local spectral gap. The local nature of the markers permits us to track their changes and corresponding local protection as the dimension of the Shiba lattice changes from two to one dimension. The real-space topology in 2D Shiba lattices has been characterized using a global position-space invariant
Mascot et al. 2019a
;
Mascot et al. 2019b

We will next introduce the specific model we consider, define the local topological markers, and present our results. We conclude this paper with a discussion of possible extensions of our work to other physical systems and other symmetry classes.

III
Spectral localizer

In this section, we introduce the local topological markers for 2D and 1D class D Hamiltonians. For a detailed tutorial on the spectral localizer and Clifford pseudospectrum (in the context of photonic systems), see Ref.
Cerjan and Loring 2024a
. Broadly, the spectral localizer framework seeks to classify material topology by first translating a system’s description into a single Hermitian matrix and then diagnose its topology via matrix homotopy, i.e., the study of which types of invertible matrices can be path-connected such that every element in the path shares the same type (e.g., Hermiticity) and remains invertible. As such, the spectral localizer framework does not rely on vector bundles, though it is provably equivalent in some cases
Loring and Schulz-Baldes 2017
.

For a d-dimensional system with open boundaries described by the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
defined on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space, the spectral localizer is defined as
Loring 2015

\displaystyle L_{\bm{\lambda}}

\displaystyle(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\kappa(X_{j}-x_{j}\mathcal{I})\otimes\Gamma_{j}+\left(\mathcal{H}-E\mathcal{I}\right)\otimes\Gamma_{d+1},

(3)

where the matrices
\Gamma_{i}
generate a Clifford representation,
\mathcal{I}
is the identity matrix,
X_{j}
are the position operators, and
\kappa>0
is a scaling parameter with units of energy divided by length. Intuitively,
\kappa
is chosen to balance the position and energy contributions to the spectrum of
L_{\bm{\lambda}}
. For bounded Hamiltonians, such as those describing tight-binding models, a proven range of validity exists
Loring and Schulz-Baldes 2020
. Generally, for systems with gapped bulk spectra, this scaling parameter can be set by the bulk gap width
E_{\textrm{gap}}
and the smallest length of the finite system
L
as
\kappa\sim E_{\textrm{gap}}/L
. In comparison with other theories of local topological markers
Kitaev 2006
;
Bianco and Resta 2011
, setting
\kappa
is similar to the choice of integration radius used by those markers.
Finally,
\bm{\lambda}=(\bm{x},E)
is the point in position-energy space at which we are probing the local topological properties.

The spectral localizer
L_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})
encodes both the local topological invariant and a measure of its local protection. In particular, as this framework’s local markers are all rooted in matrix homotopy arguments, the spectral localizer locally quantifies topological protection by how close a system’s
L_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})
is to being non-invertible at a chosen
\bm{\lambda}
. In other words, the local gap

\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})=\text{min}\left\{\left|\Sigma\left(L_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})\right)\right|\right\},

(4)

where
\Sigma\left(L_{\bm{\lambda}}(\bm{X},\mathcal{H})\right)
corresponds to the spectrum of the spectral localizer, gives the minimum distance an eigenvalue must move for
L_{\bm{\lambda}}
to become non-invertible. As the spectral localizer is Hermitian (for Hermitian
\mathcal{H}
), any perturbation
\delta L
to
L_{\bm{\lambda}}
, either through changing the choice of
\bm{\lambda}
or perturbing the system
\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}+\delta\mathcal{H}
cannot yield a non-invertible
L_{\bm{\lambda}}+\delta L
so long as
\|\delta L\|<\mu_{\bm{\lambda}}
. Here,
\|\cdot\|
is the
L_{2}
matrix norm, i.e., its largest singular value.

In the following sections, we specialize the spectral localizer for the 2D and 1D cases, and define in each case the property that characterizes whether the Hamiltonian and position operators of a class D system can be continued to an atomic limit, i.e., a set of commuting matrices, or not, without breaking parity and while maintaining a non-zero local gap.

III.2
1D invariant

The 1D spectral localizer can also be constructed using the Pauli matrices as the choice of Clifford representation as

L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}=(x,E)}(X,\mathcal{H})=\left(\mathcal{H}-E\mathcal{I}\right)\otimes\sigma_{y}+\kappa(X-x\mathcal{I})\otimes\sigma_{x},

(7)

where
\mathcal{H}
is also given by Eq. (
1
). Note that the choice of
\mathcal{H}
need not change bewteen using
L^{(2D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
and
L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
. Instead, the dimensional reduction is performed through the omission of the second position operator
Y
in the definition of
L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
. As such,
L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
can be equally applied to 1D chains as well as effective 1D chains embedded in 2D materials (or any material of arbitrarily higher dimension). Similarly, one can also define
L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
in terms of other position operators such as
Y
or
X+Y
. However, for concreteness, we arrange our Shiba chains along the x-direction in the 2D superconductor.

The 1D local topological invariant in class D is defined by first transforming
\mathcal{H}
to be in a basis in which it is purely imaginary, so that
\mathcal{H}^{\mathsf{T}}=-\mathcal{H}
. Such a basis must exist due to the system’s particle-hole symmetry. In this basis for
\mathcal{H}
, and noting the choice of Pauli matrices used in Eq. (
7
), the 1D spectral localizer is both block off-diagonal and real symmetric,
(L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}})^{\dagger}=(L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}})^{\mathsf{T}}=L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}

L^{(1D)}_{\bm{\lambda}}
is invertible, its matrix homotopy can be classified using only one of its off-diagonal blocks (which removes this spectral pairing) as

z_{2,\bm{\lambda}}=\text{sign}\left[\text{det}\left\{\kappa(X-x\mathcal{I})-i(H-E\mathcal{I})\right\}\right],

(8)

see Theorem D1 in Ref.
Cerjan and Loring 2024b
for an accessible explanation.

IV
Results

Leveraging the local topological invariants for Class D systems, we explore the Shiba lattice’s parameter space for 1D and 2D geometries and consider values previously used in the literature for this model

;
Mascot et al. 2019a
;

and

Using the same parameters for both geometries, we find regions where the Shiba island and chain are both topological, regions where the Shiba island is topological and the chain is trivial, regions where the island is trivial and the chain is topological, and regions where both geometries are trivial. IV.2
Effects of disorder

To test the robustness of our result, we consider disorder in the local chemical potential. The additional term we include in the Hamiltonian is given by

\mathcal{H}_{dis}=-\sum_{\mathbf{r},\sigma}\delta\mu(\mathbf{r})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{r}\sigma}c_{\mathbf{r}\sigma},

(9)

where
\delta\mu(\mathbf{r})
is taken to be an uncorrelated, uniformly distributed random variable,
\delta\mu(\mathbf{r})\in[-W,W]

\pm 1
standard deviations. For this disorder strength, the 2D local gap is closed along the position

V
Conclusions and outlook

We have established a framework based on the spectral localizer, a real-space numeric K-theory approach, to characterize topological transitions due to spatial dimension changes. In particular, we introduced local topological markers for systems in class D, with particle-hole symmetry and broken time-reversal and chiral symmetry, and applied them to Shiba lattices as experimentally-relevant examples. We considered a 2D circular Shiba island and deformed it into a 1D chain by reducing its size along one of its dimensions. By computing the local markers across the dimension change, we found that the transition from 2D topology to 1D topology occurs via a reduction in the number of sites with Chern number
|C_{\bm{\lambda}}^{\textrm{L}}|>0
together with a decrease in the localizer gap protecting it. At a critical thickness, we found the emergence of nontrivial 1D topology, marked by
z_{2,\bm{\lambda}}=-1
, protected by a nonzero 1D localizer gap whose size increases for the 1D chain geometry. We tested the robustness of our results against disorder in the chemical potential. We found that the 1D localizer gap, and thus the 1D topology, is more robust than its 2D counterpart.

The framework introduced can be readily generalized to other symmetry classes. For example, the spectral localizer, Eq. (
3
), can be defined for a 3D topological insulator and a 2D quantum spin Hall system. The corresponding topological invariants would be a Chern number, analogous to Eq. (
6
), and the spin Chern number. More generally, the framework could help guide the design of topological materials for device applications. One of the strengths of the spectral localizer framework is its numerical efficiency, which enables it to be applied directly to experimental systems without developing a low-energy approximation
Spataru et al. 2025
. As such, our results may be useful for the design and analysis of topological superconducting nanowires
Vaitiekėnas et al. 2020
.
