Mechanical isolation of stromal vascular fraction using a spiral-based device: cellular outcomes and point-of-care workflow considerations
Authors: Marcos Sforza, Olga Ivanenko, Nazanin Biabani, Lara Sforza, Deepak M. Kalaskar, Zahra Mohri, Afshin Mosahebi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStromal vascular fraction (SVF) from adipose tissue represents a promising source of regenerative cells. A major trend in SVF isolation is the increasing use of dedicated devices and systems to enhance standardization and reproducibility. Real-world implementation of new technologies requires consideration of workflow-related factors alongside biological results. This study primarily aimed to characterize the novel BMC Uniq® SVF device, describe a mechanical isolation approach (MI-SVF) with this device, and evaluate the cellular outcomes of MI-SVF. Enzymatic isolation (EI-SVF) was included as a reference method for comparison of selected cellular parameters.ResultsThe BMC Uniq® SVF device is a spiral-based, closed system composed of two housings and three filters, designed as a sterile disposable unit. Lipoaspirate samples from 10 healthy female donors were equally divided and handled using MI-SVF (decantation, device processing, centrifugation) or EI-SVF (adding collagenase and post-digestion steps to the MI-SVF protocol). Total cell yield was significantly greater in the EI-SVF group (mean ± standard error (SE) 94.9 × 104 ± 6.4 × 104 versus 37.5 × 104 ± 2.7 × 104 for MI-SVF; p = 0.002). The flow cytometry demonstrated significantly higher relative proportions of CD90+ (10.3 ± 1.3% versus 5.4 ± 0.9%; p = 0.014) and CD73+ cells (10.7 ± 0.7% versus 6.6 ± 0.7%; p = 0.010) in MI-SVF compared with EI-SVF, with no significant differences in CD105+ percentage. There were no differences in cell viability (MI-SVF: mean 87.0%; EI-SVF: 83.9%). MI-SVF samples showed a CFU-F frequency of 5.0 ± 0.3% and consistently demonstrated adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic differentiation potential.ConclusionsThe BMC Uniq® SVF device is a simple closed single-use system for mechanical SVF extraction in point-of-care applications. Fresh MI-SVF samples derived in such a way had one of the highest reported cell yields and demonstrated viability, immunophenotype, clonogenic capacity, and multilineage differentiation potential that were comparable to, and in some cases exceeded, those of previously described non-enzymatic protocols. Relative to EI-SVF, the mechanical approach showed a lower total cell count but a higher relative percentage of CD73+ and CD90+ cells. Background

One of the main focuses in regenerative medicine is employing cell populations that have a promising therapeutic potential and at the same time are easily available in routine clinical settings. In this context, stromal vascular fraction (SVF) is a subject of great interest because adipose tissue is abundant and easily harvested, providing a valuable source of regenerative cells. As a heterogeneous cell population, SVF includes mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs), endothelial cells, pericytes, macrophages, and other cells contributing to various regenerative processes [1, 2]. Importantly, MSCs consist of non-clonal populations comprising true stem cells with varying multipotency, lineage-committed progenitors, and differentiated cells [3], and MSCs from adipose tissue are commonly referred to as adipose-derived stromal cells (ADSCs) [1]. Although some studies were focused on in vitro expanded ADSCs [4, 5], use of fresh non-cultured SVF suggests potential benefits across a range of applications, such as wound healing [6], aesthetic fat grafting [7], osteoarthritis [8], and many others [2].

SVF extraction protocols could be classified into two main categories: purely mechanical approaches and methods that also involve enzymatic digestion [9, 10]. Enzymatic protocols typically achieve higher cell yield [9, 11] but are time-consuming and costly. Additionally, enzymatically processed tissues are subject to increased regulatory oversight due to classification as more than minimal tissue manipulation (for instance, by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, FDA [12]). In contrast, mechanical methods rely on physical dissociation of lipoaspirate, providing faster, simpler, and more cost-effective processing with a lower regulatory burden [11, 13]. Although mechanical isolation may yield fewer cells, multiple studies have shown that other cellular outcomes (viability, immunophenotype, differentiation potential, etc.) could be comparable to those from enzymatic protocols [11, 14, 15]. Taken together, these features and the demonstrated performance of mechanical SVF extraction have underpinned the growing interest and further development of non-enzymatic approaches.

A prominent trend in SVF isolation methods is the emerging use of various devices and systems for more controlled tissue processing [11, 16]. By reducing open handling steps, standardized systems improve reproducibility and decrease inter-operator variability. For instance, even relatively simple mechanical manipulations, such as tissue mincing or emulsification, may be applied in substantially different ways depending on operator technique and choice of instruments, whereas device-based approaches impose defined actions. Compared with protocols relying on multiple separate tools, systems provide a more uniform environment for tissue dissociation [16, 17]. Devices could be especially valuable for the intraoperative setting, where lipoaspirate can be processed with minimal additional complexity to the surgical workflow.

Beyond biological metrics, it is crucial to consider how readily the SVF isolation protocol could be integrated into real clinical workflows, especially in the point-of-care setting. Regulatory compliance, processing time, sterility, reproducibility, and procedural costs directly determine protocol feasibility [18] in the operating theatre, where prolonged workflows and laboratory-only steps are less practical. Technically simple, single-use devices operating as closed systems and requiring minimal training may therefore offer practical advantages, even when cellular outcomes are not the maximal possible. This perspective shifts evaluation criteria from only biological endpoints toward practical deployability, highlighting the importance of comprehensive estimation of the proposed SVF extraction methods [16, 18].

While SVF isolation protocols and cellular outcomes were explored in previous studies (for example [1, 9, 11]), the performance of individual devices may differ due to variations in mechanical processing principles, device design (e.g., filter pore size), and overall workflow. Therefore, device-specific evaluation remains necessary to determine biological outcomes and practical applicability. The BMC Uniq® SVF system represents a novel device that has not yet been characterized, featuring a relatively uncommon spiral-based design.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the BMC Uniq® SVF device as a point-of-care mechanical isolation system, to describe a mechanical extraction protocol (MI-SVF) with this device, and to characterize the MI-SVF cellular outcomes. Enzymatic isolation (EI-SVF) was included as a reference method for comparison of selected cellular parameters such as cell yield, viability, and immunophenotype. Functional in vitro assays were performed for MI-SVF samples to confirm the presence of stromal progenitor populations.

Methods

SVF extraction device

The device consists of upper and lower housings, 3 filters (hole size ranges 1.45–2.2 mm, 0.6–1.8 mm, and 0.45–1.35 mm for the first, second, and third filters, respectively), and a spiral. Inlet and outlet ports of the device can be connected with standard syringes via the luer lock. When lipoaspirate is manually passed through the device, the spiral induces a flow that directs cells toward the filters along spiral trajectories. Such a design is intended to reduce direct frontal cell–filter collisions and promote more gradual cell–filter surface interactions, potentially lowering localized mechanical stress during filtration [20]. The device is a sterile single-use unit made from medical-grade polycarbonate to ensure contamination-free processing.

Donors

The present study involved 10 consecutive human donors (females only; age mean ± standard deviation (SD): 34.1 ± 6.8 years; body mass index: 24.8 ± 1.2) with no known metabolic disorders or other considerable health issues. Subjects underwent liposuction for aesthetic reasons; around 120 ml of lipoaspirate was collected from the lower abdomen area of each subject. Sample size was determined primarily by number of available donors during the study period. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national guidelines, and all the participants provided informed consent. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Leon, Spain (OEBA-ULE-019–2021). Donor material collection and experimental procedures were carried out at the facilities of the University of Leon.

SVF isolation

To extract SVF from fresh lipoaspirate, each of the samples (n = 10) was divided into two equal 60-mL portions, assigned to the MI-SVF and EI-SVF protocols, respectively. Processing steps were adapted from previously published protocols commonly used for SVF isolation [9, 21, 22] with specific modifications as detailed below.

Throughout the study, only freshly isolated SVF samples were used; whenever a workflow category (MI-SVF or EI-SVF) was included in analyses, all available samples from that category (n = 10) were evaluated.

SVF cell yield and viability

After isolation, MI-SVF and EI-SVF samples were analyzed by the NC-200 device (ChemoMetec, DK). Cell yield (number of nucleated cells obtained per ml of adipose tissue) and viability were assessed. Measurements were made in triplicate.

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was performed on MI-SVF and EI-SVF in order to determine the relative proportion of cells expressing clusters of differentiation (CD) markers commonly associated with ADSC phenotypes [23]. Surface marker profiling was carried out using the MSC Phenotyping Cocktail Kit, anti-human, REAfinity™ (Miltenyi Biotec), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Only viable (APC-Vio770A−, Miltenyi Biotec) nucleated cells were included in further analysis. The panel consisted of antibodies against CD90 (RTC-A), CD73 (APC-A), and CD105 (VioBlue-A). In turn, HLA-DR (VioGreen-A) was employed to identify immune and activated hematopoietic cells. The PE-A channel was associated with MSC-negative markers CD14, CD19, CD34, and CD45. For each stained sample, a minimum of 5 × 104 events were acquired using the MACSQuant™ Analyzer 16 Flow Cytometer (Miltenyi Biotec). Gating parameters for surface marker expression were established using matched isotype controls. Data was analyzed utilizing MACSQuantify™ Software, and all metrics were expressed as percentages of total viable nucleated cells.

Colony forming unit-fibroblast (CFU-F) assay

From this stage onward, only MI-SVF samples (n = 10) were included in further analyses. To estimate the clonogenic capacity of MI-SVF-derived cells, the CFU-F assay was carried out; key protocol steps were adapted from [24]. SVF cells were seeded in triplicate (1,000 viable cells per 9.4 cm2 well; α-Minimum Essential Medium with 10% FBS and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin; Gibco, unless otherwise stated). After 14 days of cultivation, cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) twice, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Fisher), washed again twice with PBS, stained with 0.5% Crystal Violet (Chemodex), and washed twice with distilled water. Colony counting was performed under a microscope by 2 independent evaluators, and their scores were averaged. Only colonies with a diameter greater than 1 mm were included in the analysis. CFU-F values were calculated according to the following formula: CFU-F = (number of colonies/number of seeded cells) ×100%.

Differentiation potential

To evaluate the multipotency of ADSCs from MI-SVF samples, a trilineage differentiation potential assessment was conducted as per Minimal criteria for defining multipotent MSCs [23]. Briefly, cells (0.5 to 1 million cells) were seeded in T-175 flasks and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence in basal medium (DMEM supplemented with 15% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin). Next, cells were trypsinized and seeded into wells (25,000–50,000 cells per well; 1.88 cm2) in triplicate. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the basal medium was replaced with the lineage-specific differentiation media (StemPro Adipogenesis, Osteogenesis, or Chondrogenesis Differentiation Kit). Parallel wells maintained in basal medium served as controls.

Adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation was assessed after 14 days, whereas osteogenic differentiation was evaluated after 21 days. The following staining was applied: Oil Red O (Acros Organics) for adipogenic differentiation, Alizarin Red S (Alfa Aesar) for osteogenic differentiation, and Alcian Blue (Alfa Aesar) for chondrogenic differentiation. Images of stained histological sections were assessed by the semi-quantitative scoring scale: 4 – strong positive (high intensity of staining), 3 – sub-strong positive, 2 – moderate positive, 1 – mild positive, 0 – negative [25].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out by the SciPy v1.11.4 Python library [26]. Differences between MI-SVF and EI-SVF sample groups were assessed via a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test [27] followed by Bonferroni multiple comparison correction [28]. The level of significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Results were visualized via the Matplotlib v3.10.0 library [29].

Results

SVF cell yield and viability

Paired fresh EI-SVF and MF-SVF sample groups were compared through a series of analyses. The enzymatic-based approach yielded a significantly higher total number of SVF cells (mean ± standard error (SE): MI-SVF 37.5 × 104 ± 2.7 × 104, EI-SVF 94.9 × 104 ± 6.4 × 104, unadjusted p = 0.002, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.010, effect size η2 = 0.757). At the same time, the percentage of viable cells was comparable between the two groups: MI-SVF 87.0 ± 1.8%, EI-SVF 83.9 ± 2.5%, unadjusted p = 0.232, η2 = 0.150. Flow cytometry

Both EI-SVF and MI-SVF sample groups demonstrated the presence of cells expressing ADSC-related markers CD90, CD73, and CD105 (Fig. 2c). MI-SVF had a significantly greater relative proportion of CD90+ and CD73+ cells with a quite large effect size η2. Specifically, MI-SVF demonstrated mean ± SE 10.3 ± 1.3% of CD90+ cells, while EI-SVF had 5.4 ± 0.9%, unadjusted p = 0.014, η2 = 0.550. Similarly, the relative proportion of CD73+ cells in MI-SVF and EI-SVF accounted for 10.7 ± 0.7% and 6.6 ± 0.7%, respectively; unadjusted p = 0.010, Bonferroni-adjusted p = 0.049, η2 = 0.598. At the same time, there were no significant between-group differences for CD105+ cells (p > 0.05). Please refer to Supplementary Table S1 for detailed statistics.

Discussion

Overall, the proposed BMC Uniq® SVF device is a closed single-use system with a spiral as a key element. While some other devices and systems achieve SVF isolation through, for instance, filtration with direct flow [30], emulsification by marbles [31], or cutting by blades [14], spiral-based conceptions have been relatively less represented in the field [32]. From a physical standpoint, the spiral induces limited shear exposure and indirect flow patterns, promoting more gentle mechanical dissociation of the lipoaspirate [20]. In contrast to more complex multi-part systems (for example, The Celution System by Cytori Therapeutics [33]), the present device is visually transparent and technically straightforward, which allows fast device preparation and relatively short operator training. Importantly, the proposed device is a sterile disposable unit, reducing the risk of contamination and contributing to procedural safety.

In general, the cellular outcomes of our MI-SVF protocol were consistent with ranges reported in the literature for mechanical isolation methods. Based on the investigated metrics, we did not find indications that the present device induces increased mechanical stress that affected cellular outcomes. As expected, enzymatic processing resulted in a significantly higher total cell yield; however, the total cell count per ml of adipose tissue achieved with MI-SVF (mean 37.5 × 104 cells) was among the higher values reported for non-enzymatic approaches. For instance, Bowen et al. [34] provided values of 33.6 × 104 cells/ml, Solodeev et al. (used the rotating blades device [14]) obtained 20.1 × 104 cells/ml, and the meta-analysis [11] estimated a pooled mean SVF cell yield of 11.96 × 104 cells/ml for human samples. At the same time, cell viability of MI-SVF (mean 87.0%) was comparable to that described in previous studies (for example, study [11] reported a range from 45.5 to 98.0%) and values that were observed in our EI-SVF samples. Likewise, relative proportions of MSC-related CD markers were consistent with published data for mechanically extracted fresh SVF (for instance, with pooled expression levels of CD105 and CD73 as 4.1 and 11.6%, respectively [11]), indicating preservation of stromal cell populations. Comparable trends were observed for CFU-F frequency (for example, François et al. [35] showed median values around 4–5.0% as well). Besides, ADSCs from MI-SVF samples retained multilineage differentiation capacity, demonstrating adipogenic, chondrogenic, and osteogenic potential similar to or superior to previous studies [36, 37]. A detailed review of other mechanical SVF extraction protocols and their outcomes is presented in the previously published paper [11].

We observed variability both between donors and between SVF extraction protocols (Fig. 3). In previous studies (for example [38, 39]), donor-related variability has been associated with age, sex, body mass index, and other factors. Notably, such variability affects not only quantitative parameters but also functional properties, for instance, immunomodulatory capacity [40]. Between extraction methods, we observed differences in total cell yield as well as in CD marker expression profiles, including non-uniform relative proportions of individual markers. These findings are consistent with the concept that enzymatic digestion and mechanical processing differ in their ability to disrupt the adipose tissue matrix and release specific cell populations [9]. Similar variability, including significant differences in ADSC marker expression profiles, was reported in previous split-sample analyses comparing various protocols [14, 15]. Taken together, these observations support the need for more personalized approaches towards SVF- and ADSC-based therapies.

Due to restrictions regarding enzymatically processed tissues (for instance, FDA regulations [12]), the primary focus of this study was the MI-SVF protocol and resulting outcomes. Similarly, the present device and protocol were developed primarily for point-of-care use, taking into account processing time and technical simplicity. Although MI-SVF approaches typically yield a lower total cell number compared with EI-SVF [9, 14, 36], this does not necessarily mean reduced therapeutic potential. According to previously published studies (for example [30, 41]), MI-SVF potentially may show favorable outcomes; this direction requires further investigations, as historically the main attention was directed towards EI-SVF [9, 42]. Notably, a universally accepted therapeutic dose of SVF-derived cells has not yet been established [42]. While some articles [43] suggested potential advantages of higher cellular doses, SVF injections may be prepared to reach a predefined target cell number, for example, by adjusting the dilution of the SVF suspension in the carrier [43]. Ultimately, the choice of isolation method may involve balancing cellular yield against the practical advantages of MI-SVF, including regulatory considerations, shorter processing time, and lower cost.

In addition to regulatory considerations, another argument supporting the development of point-of-care approaches lies in potential unfavorable effects that may arise during MSC ex vivo expansion. For instance, in addition to between-donor variability, the review [44] highlighted specific problems such as cellular senescence, altered immunogenicity, and negative impact of cryopreservation. In particular, cellular senescence may induce fundamental changes in the cellular secretome and thereby affect paracrine signaling, potentially influencing immune cell migration and tissue homeostasis; senescence has even been suggested as one of the factors contributing to the failure of certain clinical trials [40]. Further studies are needed to better understand the impact of MSC in vitro cultivation. In this context, approaches utilizing fresh SVF may help minimize culture-associated alterations of cellular phenotype and function.

There are a few potential technical issues and their solutions that should be taken into account. As the BMC Uniq® SVF device is compact and flow-driven, system blockage and flow resistance may occur as the result of increased lipoaspirate viscosity, which, in turn, may originate from low temperature, high fat density, or blood contamination [45, 46]. Washing of the lipoaspirate to remove blood components and debris can substantially reduce viscosity and prevent obstruction. Maintaining a consistent processing temperature and avoiding overloading the device with large single volumes further supports stable flow conditions. In the case of passing difficulties, flow should be controlled through slow, uniform advancement rather than high pressure, with preference for multiple short processing cycles instead of one prolonged pass. Pre-filtration of large tissue fragments may additionally limit progressive device filter saturation. Regarding organizational aspects, the proposed mechanical protocol typically would require approximately 15–20 min; however, the device requires one operator only, so parallelizing SVF extraction with other preparatory or treatment-related actions may allow integration into the workflow without a substantial extension of the overall procedure time in the surgery room.

Several limitations of the present in vitro study should be acknowledged. Firstly, only healthy females within a relatively narrow age range (26–48 years) were included, and the sample size n = 10 was relatively limited. The range and dispersion of outcome metrics may differ in patients with other demographic profiles or underlying conditions. Secondly, the protocol steps and findings are limited to human-derived samples; application to animal models may require adaptation of the protocols. Next, all assays were conducted using freshly isolated SVF as the input material, and the potential effects of cryopreservation or additional handling steps were not examined. Additionally, cellular outcomes were evaluated using a limited set of commonly applied metrics, including a three-marker CD panel and differentiation assays for the three mesenchymal lineages. All the SVF extraction stages, including manually passing lipoaspirate through the device, were performed by a single operator, and inter-operator variability was not assessed. Finally, the proposed protocols were based on established practices in our lab, so potential differences in other steps (such as alternative centrifugation settings) may influence the results.

Future studies may focus on further standardization of SVF isolation protocols and a more comprehensive evaluation that integrates both biological outcomes and workflow-related parameters. It would be important to explore systematically on large samples donor- and condition-related variability, such as age, site of lipoaspiration, comorbidities, etc. Incorporating broader immunophenotyping panels (for instance, CD34, CD45, and CD31) and additional functional assays (such as interferon-gamma-induced upregulation of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase as a marker of immunosuppressive activity) may provide a more comprehensive characterization of the heterogeneous SVF population. From a translational perspective, strategies to minimize operator-dependent variability should be addressed to improve robustness in routine use. Finally, assessment and optimization of cost–performance trade-offs may help position mechanical SVF isolation approaches within real-world point-of-care settings.

Conclusions

In this study, we presented a novel device for mechanical extraction of SVF from adipose tissue. The BMC Uniq® SVF device is a technically straightforward single-use closed system, compatible with widely used methods and equipment, and suitable for point-of-care applications. While enzymatic isolation yielded significantly higher total SVF cell counts, fresh SVF obtained via the mechanical approach had a greater relative proportion of cells expressing MSC-related markers CD73 and CD90. Relative to other non-enzymatic approaches, our protocol demonstrated one of the biggest reported SVF cell yields and comparable or, in some cases, superior other cellular characteristics, such as viability, immunophenotype, CFU-F metrics, and trilineage differentiation potential. Overall, this work contributes to a methodological framework of evaluating mechanical SVF isolation protocols beyond cellular and clinical outcomes alone, combining biological performance with practical workflow considerations.
