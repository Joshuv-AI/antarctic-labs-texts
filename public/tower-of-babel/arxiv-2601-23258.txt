Agnostic Language Identification and Generation
Mikael Møller Høgsgaard, Chirag Pabbaraju

Abstract
Recent works on language identification and generation have established tight statistical rates at which these tasks can be achieved. These works typically operate under a strong realizability assumption: that the input data is drawn from an unknown distribution necessarily supported on some language in a given collection. In this work, we relax this assumption of realizability entirely, and impose no restrictions on the distribution of the input data. We propose objectives to study both language identification and generation in this more general “agnostic” setup. Across both problems, we obtain novel interesting characterizations and nearly tight rates.



1
Introduction

Learning a language from a finite amount of data, and in particular from finitely many positive examples from the language, is a fundamental problem, both for humans as well as computers. Two natural language learning problems are those of
language identification
and
language generation
. In language identification, the objective is to output an exact representation of the language that is presumed to be generating the examples being seen, whereas the objective in language generation is to simply be able to generate new valid examples from the unknown language, despite potentially not learning an exact representation of it. In this regard, the latter task is an easier task than the former.

An extremely bare-bones framing of this problem, devoid of any structural assumptions on language, is as follows. A language may simply be thought of as an abstract set
L
of valid sentences or strings. If the alphabet that defines these sentences is finite (e.g., the finite English alphabet {a,b,…,z}), and sentences themselves are finite, then a language may further be thought of as a countable subset of a countable universe
U
of all possible strings. Stated thus, the language identification task may be framed as follows: Given a finite dataset
S\subseteq L
of positive examples from
L
, output (possibly the representation of) a set
L^{\prime}
such that
L^{\prime}=L
. On the other hand, the language generation task becomes the following: Given a finite dataset
S\subseteq L
of positive examples from
L
, output a new unseen example
z
from
L
. In order to make these problems tractable, one typically assumes
realizability
: namely, that the unknown language
L
is one from a known collection
{\mathcal{C}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots\}
of languages. Furthermore, the way that the dataset
S\subseteq L
is obtained is up to further modeling: it may be generated as an online sequence by an adversary, or it may be generated as an i.i.d. sequence from a distribution supported on
L
.

The setup where the data may be generated in an online, adversarial fashion has been extensively studied in the literature. Several classical results have been established for language identification
(
5
;
1
)
, and more recently, several results have been established for language generation
(
7
;
10
;
4
)
. As it turns out, the online language identification problem is in general quite hard, and intractable for many natural formal language collections even with the realizability assumption. In stark contrast, the online language generation problem is tractable for every countable language collection. Coming to the statistical setting, where the examples are generated i.i.d. from a distribution over the unknown target language, recent work by
6
studies algorithms that minimize the probability of identifying an incorrect language, or generating a string outside the underlying target language, as a function of the number
n
of i.i.d. examples seen from the language. The results of
6
characterize precise rates at which these tasks may be achieved. Importantly, all these results operate under the realizability assumption—that the data is generated entirely from some language from the reference collection.

Since the online identification problem is largely intractable even with this realizability assumption, whereas the generation problem is much more tractable, recent works
(
9
;
8
;
2
)
have also studied the online generation problem beyond the realizable setting, where the input data stream may be corrupted to have noisy examples outside the target language (but without any signal as to which examples are noisy). This setting, which may be termed as the “agnostic” setting, is arguably more representative of real-world datasets. The results in these works obtain precise conditions on the nature of noisy examples in the input stream that allow for successful generation in the online setting. However, the study of a similar agnostic setup in the statistical setting where the data is drawn from a distribution has so far been lacking in the literature.

In this work, we aim to bridge this gap, by considering the agnostic setting for language identification and generation, where the data is drawn from an arbitrary distribution over the universe, with support not necessarily equal to any language from the reference collection. We propose reasonable objectives to study these problems in this more general agnostic setting, and derive statistical rates at which the objectives may be achieved.

1.1
Agnostic Identification

The precise statistical objective studied by
6
for language identification in the realizable setting is the following. Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be an unknown distribution over the universe
U
, and let
\supp({\mathcal{D}})
denote its support (i.e., elements of
U
that have positive mass under
{\mathcal{D}}
). Given as input an i.i.d. dataset
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfies
\supp({\mathcal{D}})=L^{\star}
for some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the task of an identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, possibly using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, is to identify an index
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})
so as to minimize:

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\neq L^{\star}\}\right].

(1)

6
shows that for any collection that can be identified in the (realizable) online setting, there exists an algorithm for which the quantity above goes down exponentially with the sample size
n
.

In the agnostic setting, given that the support of the underlying distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
may not exactly coincide with any language in the reference collection, the natural objective would be to instead identify the language in the collection that is
most representative
of the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
, in that it is most likely to contain strings drawn from
{\mathcal{D}}
. Towards this, we define the following objective for agnostic identification:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n):=\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\right]-\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]\right].

(2)

To get a sense of this objective, consider the special case where realizability holds, i.e.,
\supp({\mathcal{D}})=L^{\star}
for some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
. In this case,
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]=0
, so the objective above simply equals
\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]
, which is always at most
\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\neq L^{\star}\}\right]
. Hence, (
2
) would appear to be a
weaker
objective than the objective (
1
) studied in the realizable case. However, as established in
6
, it is impossible to obtain any vanishing rate for the objective in (
2
) for any collection
{\mathcal{C}}
that is not identifiable in the online setting. As alluded to earlier, collections that are identifiable in the online setting need to satisfy a very restrictive condition
(
5
;
1
)
, making the task intractable for most interesting language collections.

Coming back to the agnostic setup, the above implies that,
even
if we restrict ourselves to special cases of distributions
{\mathcal{D}}
for which some language
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L^{\star}\right]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
, it would still be hopeless to obtain a vanishing rate on an objective of the form (
1
) in general. This further motivates considering our slightly weaker objective in (
2
) which allows us to gracefully handle both, distributions
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfying
\supp({\mathcal{D}})\neq L^{\star}
for any
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, as well as collections
{\mathcal{C}}
that are not identifiable in the online setting. Indeed, our first result shows that we can make the objective in (
2
) go down at an (almost)
exponential
rate, for any countable collection
{\mathcal{C}}
and distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfying that there exists some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
which attains
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
. Note again that our result guarantees such a rate even when the collection may not be identifiable in the online setting, and the support of the distribution is not equal to any language in the collection.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal, see
Theorems

2.1
and
2.2
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any countable collection and
{\mathcal{D}}
be any distribution. If there exists
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
, then for any function
g(n)=o(n)
, there exists an identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that satisfies

{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\lesssim e^{-g(n)}.

Moreover, under this assumption, the best that any algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
can do is
{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\gtrsim e^{-n}.

The informal statement above is made precise in
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
. In fact, our algorithm has the stronger property that the language it identifies
attains

\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
with (almost) exponentially high probability.

Our identification algorithm requires that the infimum
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
be attained by some language in the collection. Remarkably, our next result shows that this assumption is necessary in a very strong sense: if the infimum is not required to be attained within the collection, there exist distributions that necessitate arbitrarily slow rates!

Theorem 1.2

(Informal, see
Theorem

2.3
)
.

There exists a countable collection
{\mathcal{C}}
, such that for any identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
and rate function
R(n)=o(1)
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
for which no
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
attains
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}]
, and furthermore,

{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\gtrsim R(n)

for infinitely many
n
.

Theorems

1.1
and
1.2
together establish that agnostic language identification, at least in the sense of the objective in (
2
), is
tightly characterized
by a rather surprising criterion: that the quantity
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
across languages in the collection be infimized within the collection. If this criterion holds, then one can minimize (
2
) at an optimal, exponentially fast rate; if it doesn’t hold, then a priori one can only guarantee arbitrarily slow rates. The qualitative takeaway is as follows: if all that the language collection has are languages that get arbitrarily close to the infimum, but never quite attain it, then no matter how slowly an algorithm chases the infimum, it can’t afford to chase it any faster.

1.2
Agnostic Generation

We now turn towards studying language generation in the agnostic setting. We start again by revisiting the generation objective studied by
6
in the realizable setting: Given as input an i.i.d. dataset
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfies
\supp({\mathcal{D}})=L^{\star}
for some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, the objective of a generation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, possibly using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, is to output a string
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})
so as to minimize:

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\notin L^{\star}\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right].

(3)

6
show that for
every
countable language collection, there exists a generation algorithm that achieves an exponential rate on this objective. Note that it is necessary to exclude
{\mathbf{S}}
from
L^{\star}
in the objective; otherwise the algorithm can trivially output an example from
{\mathbf{S}}
itself.

In the agnostic setting, where the support of the distribution may be arbitrary, a natural thing to do is to simply replace
L^{\star}
with
\supp({\mathcal{D}})
. This gives rise to the following objective for agnostic generation:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n):=\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right].

(4)

When
{\mathcal{D}}
is allowed to be arbitrary, the right-hand side above is entirely independent of the reference collection. If an algorithm has no reference to base the support of the unknown distribution, how is it supposed to generate new strings from its support? Indeed, our first result formally shows that this objective is intractable to achieve in general.

Theorem 1.3

(Informal, see
Theorem

3.1
)
.

Fix any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For any generation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
such that

{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\geq 1-\varepsilon

for infinitely many
n
.

The negative result above either necessitates imposing structural assumptions tying the distribution to the collection, or introducing a comparative term with reference to the collection into the objective (like how we had the term
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
to compare to for identification).
However, while there was a natural way to measure the “identification error” of a language
L
in the collection as
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
, it is not clear what the “generation error” of a language
L
ought to be, and how one might compare it to the term in (
4
). Instead of outputting a single string, one might consider
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})
to be a
distribution
output by
{\mathcal{A}}
; in this case, it is tempting to consider the reference collection
{\mathcal{C}}
to comprise of distributions, instead of languages. Unfortunately, the requirement to not output anything from the input sample introduces idiosyncrasies in this model. For example, the benchmark distribution to compare with no longer gets fixed by the input distribution alone, but may depend on the input sample or its size. This is discussed further in
Appendix

A
.

Thus, we turn towards studying minimal structural assumptions we can impose on the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
which allow us to obtain meaningful rates for generation. As a reference point, recall that stipulating
\supp({\mathcal{D}})=L^{\star}
for some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, which brings us back to the realizable setting, allows obtaining exponential rates for (
4
). Perhaps surprisingly, we find that for finite collections, it suffices to assume a weaker condition, namely
\supp({\mathcal{D}})\supseteq L
for some
L\in{\mathcal{C}}
, to achieve exponential rates. Note that this setting allows infinitely many strings outside any language from the collection to be seen in the input, and hence considerably extends beyond the realizable setting.

Theorem 1.4

(Informal, see
Theorems

3.2
and
3.4
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any finite collection of languages and
{\mathcal{D}}
be any distribution. If
\supp({\mathcal{D}})\supseteq L
for some
L\in{\mathcal{C}}
, then there exists a generation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that satisfies

{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\lesssim e^{-n}.

Moreover, under this assumption, the best that any algorithm can do is
{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\gtrsim e^{-n}
.

We remark that our generation algorithm works more generally for any countable collection, under the assumption that the distribution satisfies an intuitive condition stated in
Section

3.2
. In fact, our algorithm is qualitatively different from previous algorithms, several of which crucially depend on the input being consistent with some language in the collection.

With this, we proceed to establish all our results in detail.

2
Agnostic Language Identification

In this section, we describe all our results about agnostic language identification. Recall the objective in agnostic identification: Given as input a sample
{\mathbf{S}}
of
n
examples drawn i.i.d. from an unknown distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
, an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, predicts an index
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})
in reference to a countable collection
{\mathcal{C}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots\}
, so as to minimize the objective:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n):=\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\right]-\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]\right].

For notational convenience, we will denote the quantity
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L^{\prime}\right]-\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
to be the
excess error
of language
L^{\prime}
. Furthermore, given a sample
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
, we will use
{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}
to denote drawing
{\mathbf{x}}
from the uniform distribution over
{\mathbf{S}}
. In this case, the quantity
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
denotes the empirical error of language
L
.

2.1
Almost Exponential Rate When
\inf
Attained

To begin, let us assume that the collection
{\mathcal{C}}
and the underlying distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
satisfy that
\exists L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
. In this case, we will give an algorithm that achieves an (almost) exponentially decaying excess error rate.

Arguably, the first natural algorithm to consider here is
Empirical Risk Minimization
. Namely, consider an enumeration of the collection as
{\mathcal{C}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots\}
. Then, consider the algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
that returns
L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})}
, where
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})\in\arg\min_{i\in\{1,2,\dots,f(n)\}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{i}]
, for any function
f(n)
that increases to infinity. Namely,
{\mathcal{A}}
returns a language that minimizes the empirical error incurred on the sample from within a finite window of the collection, whose size
f(n)
increases with the sample size
n
. Unfortunately, standard analyses only guarantee a polynomial rate for this algorithm.

To see this, observe that Hoeffding’s inequality, together with the union bound, guarantees that the empirical errors
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{i}]
computed by
{\mathcal{A}}
estimate
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{i}]
to within a precision of
\varepsilon
simultaneously for every
L_{i}
in the window
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{f(n)}\}
, with probability at least
1-2f(n)\cdot\exp(-2\varepsilon^{2}n)
. Since we assumed the existence of some
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
that attains
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
, this
L^{\star}
is guaranteed to belong to the interval
\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots,L_{f(n)}\}
for sufficiently large
n
. Then, the empirical minimizer returned by the algorithm is guaranteed to have excess error at most
2\varepsilon
. This is because the empirical error of
L^{\star}
will be at most
\varepsilon
off its true error, and the empirical error of any language whose excess error is at least
2\varepsilon
will also be at most
\varepsilon
off its true error; so, any such language will not minimize the empirical error. We thus have that
{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\lesssim 2\varepsilon+2f(n)\cdot\exp(-2\varepsilon^{2}n)
, which, upon minimizing
\varepsilon
, yields
{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\log(f(n))+\log(n)}{n}}
. This is drastically slower than the near-exponential rate that we are targetting.

A sample
{\mathbf{S}}=({\mathbf{x}}_{1},\dots,{\mathbf{x}}_{n})
, a function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)=\infty
, a countable language collection
{\mathcal{C}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots\}

2:

Let
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})
be the largest index
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,f{\left(n\right)}\right\}
such that for all
j<i
, it holds that

\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i}\right]>\frac{2}{f{\left(n\right)}}

Towards obtaining this near-exponential rate, we propose
Algorithm

for agnostic identification. Instead of returning the minimizer of the empirical error from the window
\{L_{1},\dots,L_{f(n)}\}
, the algorithm returns the largest-indexed language that beats every language before it in empirical error by a margin of at least
2/f(n)
. This simple yet crucial modification allows us to restrict our attention solely to the smallest-indexed language in the collection that attains
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
; since there are only constantly many languages before this language in the collection, we can guarantee a near-exponential rate for large enough
n
.

Theorem 2.1

(Agnostic Identification Almost Exponential Rate)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}=\{L_{1},L_{2},\dots\}
be a countable collection of languages and
{\mathcal{D}}
be any distribution over a universe
U
. Suppose there exists
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L^{\star}\right]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
. Let
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be any function satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)=\infty
. Then, for large enough
n
(depending only on
{\mathcal{C}}
,
{\mathcal{D}}
and
f
), with probability
1-2f(n)\exp{\left(-\frac{n}{2f(n)^{2}}\right)}
over
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
,
Algorithm

1
returns
L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})}
satisfying
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})}\right]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
. Hence,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\leq 2f(n)\exp{\left(-\frac{n}{2f(n)^{2}}\right)}.

Proof.

Let
L_{i_{\star}}
be the language in
{\mathcal{C}}
with the smallest index
i_{\star}
such that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
. Since
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(n)=\infty
, we know that for large enough
n
,
f(n)\geq i_{\star}
, implying that the algorithm will consider
L_{i_{\star}}
as a candidate. Now, since
i_{\star}
is the smallest index such that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L\right]
, we know that for all
j<i_{\star}
, it holds that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]>\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]
. Since there are only finitely many
j<i
, there exists some constant
c_{{\mathrm{gap}}}
such that for all
j<i_{\star}
it holds that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\geq c_{{\mathrm{gap}}}
.

Now, by Hoeffding’s inequality, for any
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,f(n)\right\}
,

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}}\left[\left|\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]-\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\right)\right|\geq\frac{1}{f(n)}\right]\leq 2\exp{\left(-\frac{n}{2f(n)^{2}}\right)}.

Thus, by the union bound, we have that with probability at least
1-2f(n)\exp{\left(-\frac{n}{2f(n)^{2}}\right)}
, it holds for all
j\in\left\{1,\ldots,f(n)\right\}
that

\displaystyle\left|\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]-\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{f(n)}.

Now, on this event, we have that for all
j<i_{\star}
,

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]\geq\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\right)-\frac{1}{f(n)}\geq c_{{\mathrm{gap}}}-\frac{1}{f(n)}.

But notice also that for
n
large enough, we will have that
c_{{\mathrm{gap}}}>\frac{3}{f(n)}
, implying that for all
j<i_{\star}
,

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]>\frac{2}{f(n)}.

Thus,
Algorithm

1
will consider selecting
i_{\star}
as
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})
. Now for
j>i_{\star}
, we have by definition of
i_{\star}
that

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\geq 0.

Thus, for all
j>i_{\star}
it holds that

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]\geq-\frac{1}{f(n)}+\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}]\right)\geq-\frac{1}{f(n)}

\displaystyle\implies\qquad

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{i_{\star}}\right]-\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathbf{S}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\not\in L_{j}\right]\leq\frac{1}{f(n)},

implying that the algorithm will not select
j
as
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})
for any
j>i_{\star}
. Thus, we have that the algorithm will return
L_{i_{\star}}
, with probability at least
1-2f(n)\exp{\left(-\frac{n}{2f(n)^{2}}\right)}
, for large enough
n
. The law of total expectation then yields the bound on
{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)
claimed in the theorem.
∎

2.2
Exponential Rate Optimal When
\inf
Attained

We now show that, under the assumption that
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
is attained within the collection, an exponential rate is the optimal rate that can be achieved by any algorithm.

Theorem 2.2

(Agnostic Identification Exponential Rate Optimal)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any collection of languages over a universe
U
satisfying that there exist
L,L^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with both
L\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{L}\neq L}\tilde{L}\right)\neq\emptyset
and
L^{\prime}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{L}\neq L^{\prime}}\tilde{L}\right)\neq\emptyset
. Then, for any identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over
U
such that there exists
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}\left[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}\right]=\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]
, and it further holds that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\geq\exp(-5n)

for infinitely many
n
.

The lower bound instance holds for any collection
{\mathcal{C}}
having two languages
L
and
L^{\prime}
that contain “signature” strings
s_{0}
and
s_{1}
respectively, such that these strings do not belong to any other language in the collection. We can then construct two distributions
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
supported only on these two signature strings, such that
L
attains
\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]
, while
L^{\prime}
attains
\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]
. Furthermore, both these distributions produce a common input of
n
examples with at least an exponential probability. Conditioned on this input, the language output by an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
must not equal at least one of
L
or
L^{\prime}
: if it doesn’t equal
L
, then the algorithm suffers constant excess error under
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
, and vice versa for
L^{\prime}
. Randomizing over
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
then gives
{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\gtrsim e^{-n}
for infinitely many
n
, either for
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{D}}_{0}
or
{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{D}}_{1}
. The formal proof is given below.

Proof.

Fix
s_{0}\in L\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{L}\neq L}\tilde{L}\right)
and
s_{1}\in L^{\prime}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}},\tilde{L}\neq L^{\prime}}\tilde{L}\right)
. Define distributions
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
as

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}=s_{0}]=\frac{3}{4},\quad\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}=s_{1}]=\frac{1}{4};

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}=s_{0}]=\frac{1}{4},\quad\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}=s_{1}]=\frac{3}{4}.

Then, since
s_{0}
is not in any language other than
L
, we have that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\geq\frac{3}{4}
for every
\tilde{L}\neq L
. Similarly, we also have that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\geq\frac{3}{4}
for every
\tilde{L}\neq L^{\prime}
. Furthermore, we can also observe that

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]=\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]=\frac{1}{4},

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]=\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\prime}]=\frac{1}{4}.

Now, consider any identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, which, upon taking as input a sample
{\mathbf{S}}
of size
n
, outputs some language
L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\in{\mathcal{C}}
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the realization of the input sample
S=(s_{0},\ldots,s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{1})
, where the first
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
examples are equal to
s_{0}
and the rest of the
\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil
examples are equal to
s_{1}
. Now, for any realization of the randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, the language
L_{{\mathcal{A}}(s,{\mathbf{r}})}
is not equal to at least one of
L
or
L^{\prime}
. Combined with the observations above, this implies that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]+\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]\geq\frac{3}{4}
.
Using this, we have that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right]+\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\cdot\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\left.\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\cdot\left(\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right)\right]\right)

(Law of total expectation and independence of
{\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}}
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]+\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right]

(since
\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S],\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\geq(\frac{1}{4})^{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}(\frac{3}{4})^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}\cdot\left(\frac{3}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n+2}\geq\exp(-5n).

(since
\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]=\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]=\frac{1}{4}
and
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]+\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})}]\geq\frac{3}{4}
)

This implies the existence of some
b\in\{0,1\}
, such that for infinitely many
n
,

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{\tilde{L}\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin\tilde{L}]\right]\geq\exp(-5n).

∎

2.3
Arbitrarily Slow Rate When
\inf
Not Attained

Our next result shows that the assumption about
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
being attained within
{\mathcal{C}}
is necessary not only for obtaining near-exponential rates, but in fact for preventing arbitrarily slow rates a priori.

Theorem 2.3

(Arbitrarily Slow Rate When
\inf
Not Attained)
.

There exists a countable collection
{\mathcal{C}}
over a universe
U
such that for any identification algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, and for any rate function
R:{\mathbb{N}}\to(0,1)
satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}R(n)=0
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
for which no
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}]=\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]
, and furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{IdErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\geq\frac{R(n)}{8}

for infinitely many
n
.

We first sketch the proof of the lower bound, before stating all the technical details. Consider a universe of strings consisting of pairs
(w,y)
, where
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
y\in\{-1,0,1\}
. We first specify a common base distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
over
{\mathbb{N}}
to draw the first component of a pair. Then, for any given bit string
z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we consider the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
, which first samples
w
according to
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
, and then outputs the pair
(w,z_{w})
. Thus,
z
can be thought of as specifying the unique
0/1
labels associated with every
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
; a sample from
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
is generated by first drawing
w
from the marginal distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
, and then “labeling” it by the
w^{\text{th}}
bit of
z
.

Our language collection will have a language
L_{I}
for every finite bit string
I\in\{0,1\}^{\geq 1}
. The language
L_{I}
will contain all pairs
(w,I_{w})
for
1\leq w\leq|I|
, and will then be padded with pairs
(w,-1)
for all
w>|I|
. Namely, each
L_{I}
has “label information” for only a finite prefix of
{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, notice that every
L_{I}
necessarily misses out on the label information about an infinite tail of
{\mathbb{N}}
; furthermore, as the prefixes
I
get longer, the mass that any valid distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
assigns to these tails shrinks to 0. Together, we get that
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{I}]>\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]=0
for every
L_{I}
.

We will now consider choosing
{\mathbf{z}}
as a uniformly random bit string in
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
. In this case, any finite sample of size
n
reveals label information about only finitely many
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and the labels at all other values of
w
are uniformly random bits. Thus, any algorithm that guesses some language
L_{I}
from the collection incurs constant error at all these unseen values of
w
. All that remains is to appropriately specify the base distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
: if for the given rate function
R(n)
, we can specify
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
to be such that it allocates roughly
R(n)
mass to blocks of unseen
w
values, our job would be done. Indeed, the seminal work of
3
on universal rates in learning gives a construction of such a probability distribution in terms of a pre-specified rate function. Adopting their construction into our analysis, together with an instantiation of Fatou’s lemma, suffices to complete the argument.

We now give the formal proof, for which we will require the following technical lemma from
3
:

Lemma 2.4

(Lemma 5.12 in
3
)
.

Let
R:{\mathbb{N}}\to(0,1)
be any rate function satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}R(n)=0
. There exists a probability distribution
p
over
{\mathbb{N}}
, two increasing sequences of natural numbers
(n_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(k_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, and a constant
\frac{1}{2}\leq C\leq 1
such that the following hold for all
i\geq 1
:

1.

\sum_{k>k_{i}}p_{k}\leq\frac{1}{n_{i}}
.

2.

n_{i}p_{k_{i}}\leq k_{i}
.

3.

p_{k_{i}}=C\cdot R(n_{i})>0
.

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

2.3
.

Proof of
Theorem

2.3
.

The universe
U
for our collection
{\mathcal{C}}
will be
U={\mathbb{N}}\times\{-1,0,1\}
. For any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
and any
I\in\{0,1\}^{i}
, consider the language

\displaystyle L_{I}=\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}\left\{(j,I_{j})\right\}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{j>i}\left\{(j,-1)\right\}\right).

Now define the collection
{\mathcal{C}}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathbb{N}}\left(\bigcup_{I\in\{0,1\}^{i}}L_{I}\right).

We first observe that the collection
{\mathcal{C}}
is countable, as it is a countable union of finitely many languages.

Now, given
R
, consider the probability distribution
p
and sequences
(n_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and
(k_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
guaranteed by
Lemma

2.4
. We will now define a base distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
over
{\mathbb{N}}
. For this, define
\sigma_{0}=0
, and for
i\geq 1
, define
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}k_{j}
. Now define
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
as follows, where for every
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}}[{\mathbf{w}}=w]=\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}},\quad\text{ where $i$ is such that }2\sigma_{i-1}+1\leq w\leq 2\sigma_{i}.

Note that
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
has positive mass on every
w\in{\mathbb{N}}
, since
1=2\sigma_{0}+1<2\sigma_{1}<2\sigma_{2}<\dots
. Now, for any sequence
z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
over the universe
U
as

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}=(w,y)]=\begin{cases}\p_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}[{\mathbf{w}}=w]&\text{ if }y=z_{w},\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

In other words, to generate a sample
{\mathbf{x}}
from
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
, we first draw
{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
, and then output the string
{\mathbf{x}}=({\mathbf{w}},z_{{\mathbf{w}}})
. This alternative view of sampling from
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
will be useful going forward. In particular, a sample
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}^{n}
can instead be viewed as the draw
{\mathbf{S}}=({\mathbf{W}},z_{\mathbf{W}})
, where
{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
and
z_{\mathbf{W}}
corresponds to the bits in
z
at indices in
{\mathbf{W}}
.

Now, we observe that for any
z
,
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]=0
, but no
L^{\star}\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfies
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L^{\star}]=0
. To see this, fix any
z
. For any
L_{I}\in{\mathcal{C}}
, note that
L_{I}
only contains strings
(j,-1)
for
j>|I|
, and so,
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{I}]\geq\sum_{j>|I|}\p_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}}[{\mathbf{w}}=j]>0
. But now, for any
i
, consider
L_{I}
, where
I=z_{1:2\sigma_{i}}
. Then,
\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{I}]=\sum_{j>i}p_{k_{j}}\leq\sum_{k>k_{i}}p_{k}\leq 1/n_{i}
. Thus,
\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]=0
, but the infinimum is not achieved by any language in the collection.

Now, let
{\mathcal{A}}
be any (possibly randomized) identification algorithm using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, which, upon taking as input a sample
{\mathbf{S}}
of size
n
outputs some language
L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}\in{\mathcal{C}}
. Consider drawing a uniformly random sequence
{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
; it then holds that

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n)}\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]\right]\geq\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\e_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}[({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbf{w}}})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]\right]

(alternative view of sampling)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\e_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}[({\mathbf{w}},{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbf{w}}})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]\right]

(
{\mathbf{w}}
,
{\mathbf{r}}
,
{\mathbf{W}}
all independent)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\cdot\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}[(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\cdot\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}[(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]

(Reverse Fatou’s Lemma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\e_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{z}}}[(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]

(since
p_{k_{i}}=C\cdot R(n_{i})
, and
{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{r}}
independent)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n_{i}}}[w\notin{\mathbf{W}}]\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{z}}}[(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ w\notin{\mathbf{W}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n_{i}}}[w\notin{\mathbf{W}}]\cdot\frac{1}{2}\right]

(
\star
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\left[\left(1-\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\right)^{n_{i}}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\right]=\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{4k_{i}}\cdot 2k_{i}\cdot\left(1-\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\right)^{n_{i}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{i\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{n_{i}p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\right)

(Bernoulli’s inequality)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}.

(since
n_{i}p_{k_{i}}\leq k_{i}
)

In the above, we could apply Reverse Fatou’s Lemma since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C\cdot R(n_{i})}\cdot\frac{p_{k_{i}}}{2k_{i}}\sum_{w=2\sigma_{i-1}+1}^{2\sigma_{i}}\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}^{n_{i}},{\mathbf{r}}}[(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\leq\frac{1}{2k_{i}}\cdot 2k_{i}\leq 1.

(using
p_{k_{i}}=C\cdot R(n_{i})
)

Furthermore, at step
(\star)
, we used that, once
{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{W}}
have been realized,
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}}
is independent of
{\mathbf{z}}_{w}
, conditioned on
w\notin{\mathbf{W}}
, and hence
(w,{\mathbf{z}}_{w})
is not in
L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}})}
with probability at least
1/2
.

The above analysis implies that there exists
z\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that for infinitely many
n
,

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}^{n}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]\right]=\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}^{n}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L_{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})}]-\inf_{L\in{\mathcal{C}}}\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}\notin L]\right]\geq\frac{C\cdot R(n)}{4}\geq\frac{R(n)}{8}.

(since
C\geq 1/2
)

∎

3
Agnostic Language Generation

We will now study agnostic generation; that is, given a sample
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
for some distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over the universe
U
, we will aim to construct an algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
, possibly using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, that generates a string
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\in U
from the unseen support of
{\mathcal{D}}
with high probability. Namely, the algorithm aims to minimize:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n):=\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right]=\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}].

Note that for the objective above to remain achievable as
n
gets large, one must assume that
\supp({\mathcal{D}})=\infty
.

3.1
General Lower Bound

We begin with a result that has a “no free lunch” flavor; namely, it shows that the objective in (
4
) is intractable in general without further assumptions on the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Theorem 3.1

(Agnostic Generation Lower Bound)
.

Fix any
0<\varepsilon<1
. There exists a countable universe
U
, such that for any generation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over
U
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Before proceeding to the proof of the theorem, we note that the lower bound above applies to
every
algorithm, regardless of any collection of languages that the algorithm may have access to. In particular, we may assume that the algorithm always has access to the trivial singleton collection containing the language
L=U
. In this case, the algorithm, in principle, always has access to a language
L
satisfying
|L\cap\supp({\mathcal{D}})|=\infty
, but this is not helpful in any meaningful way. This highlights further the issues in introducing a reference language collection
{\mathcal{C}}
into the objective (
4
), as also referenced in the discussion in
section

1.2
. Regardless, as we shall see later, it
is
possible for an algorithm to exploit access to a collection
{\mathcal{C}}
in order to succeed at the objective in (
4
) with an
exponential
rate, when the underlying distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
is suitably “well-behaved” with respect to the given collection
{\mathcal{C}}
.

Proof.

Define the universe
U
to be the set
{\mathbb{N}}\times\{1,2,\dots,\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil\}
. For any sequence
z
in
\{1,\ldots,\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, define a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
over the universe as

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{x}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}}[{\mathbf{x}}=(w,y)]=\begin{cases}2^{-w}&\text{if }y=z_{w},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We may alternatively view the process of sampling from
{\mathcal{D}}_{z}
as first drawing
{\mathbf{w}}
from a base distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}
over
\mathbb{N}
defined as
\p_{{\mathbf{w}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}}[{\mathbf{w}}=w]=2^{-w}
, and then outputting the string
({\mathbf{w}},z_{{\mathbf{w}}})
.
Viewed this way, a sample
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}^{n}
can instead be written as
{\mathbf{S}}=({\mathbf{W}},z_{\mathbf{W}})
, where
{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n}
and
z_{\mathbf{W}}
corresponds to the bits in
z
at indices in
{\mathbf{W}}
.

Now, let
{\mathcal{A}}
be any generation algorithm using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, which, upon taking as input a sample
{\mathbf{S}}
of size
n
outputs a string
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\in U
. For a uniformly random
{\mathbf{z}}\in\{1,2,\dots,\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\limsup_{n}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\right]\geq\limsup_{n}\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\right]

(Reverse Fatou’s Lemma)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\e_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}}))\right]

(alternative sampling view)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\e_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{z}}}\left[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}})\right]\right]

(
{\mathbf{r}}
independent of input and
{\mathbf{z}}
)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\e_{{\mathbf{W}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{N}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\e_{{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbf{W}}}}\left[\p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}}}\left[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}})\right]\right]\right].

In the first step above, Reverse Fatou’s Lemma applies since each
\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]
is at most 1. In the last line, we separated the sampling of
{\mathbf{z}}
into two parts:
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}}
(which corresponds to sampling
{\mathbf{z}}
at indices in
{\mathbf{W}}
) and
{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}}
(which corresponds to sampling
{\mathbf{z}}
at all remaining indices); both of these parts are independent of each other. Now note that once
{\mathbf{W}},{\mathbf{r}}
and
{\mathbf{z}}_{\mathbf{W}}
are realized,
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})
is fixed to be some
(w^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in U
. If
w^{\prime}\in{\mathbf{W}}
, then
\p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}})]=1
. Otherwise,
w^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}
; in this case,
\p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}})]=1-1/\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil\geq 1-\varepsilon
, since this is the chance that the value of
{\mathbf{z}}
at index
w^{\prime}
is realized to not be
y^{\prime}
. Thus, we have that
\p_{{\mathbf{z}}_{{\mathbb{N}}\setminus{\mathbf{W}}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathbf{z}}})\setminus({\mathbf{W}},z_{{\mathbf{W}}})]\geq 1-\varepsilon
regardless, which implies that the last quantity in the display above is at least
1-\varepsilon
. But this implies the existence of a
z\in\{1,2,\dots,\lceil 1/\varepsilon\rceil\}^{\mathbb{N}}
for which

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{z}^{n}\hskip-1.59442pt,\hskip 0.79727pt{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{z})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\geq 1-\varepsilon,

which completes the proof.
∎

3.2
Exponential Rate for Well-Behaved Distributions

With the general negative result above in mind, we now introduce a condition on the collection
{\mathcal{C}}
and the distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
that will allow us to construct a generation algorithm. At a high level, this condition requires for there to be a bounded interval within the universe where every language in the collection not contained within the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
has a
witness
string.
1
1

1

We assume that every language in the collection is infinite.
For the purposes of designing an algorithm, this property allows us to eventually rule every such language, since its witness within the bounded interval will never show up in the input.

More formally, recall that we assume the universe of strings
U
to be a countable set; that is, we can enumerate all the strings in
U
as
U=\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots\}
. Then, for any distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over
U
and any countable collection of languages
{\mathcal{C}}=\left\{L_{1},L_{2}\ldots\right\}
, where each
L_{i}\subseteq U
, we define the quantity
i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})
as

\displaystyle i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\penalty\ \middle|\penalty\ \forall L\in{\mathcal{C}}\text{ s.t. }L\nsubseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}}):\exists k\leq i\text{ s.t. }u_{k}\in L\text{ but }u_{k}\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}})\right\}.

(5)

Here, we define the
\min
over an empty set to be
\infty
. In words,
i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})
is the smallest index in the enumeration of
U
such that for any language not fully contained in the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
, there exists a string in the language with index at most
i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})
in the enumeration of
U
that is
not
in the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
.
For instance, notice that such a finite index always exists for any finite collection of languages
{\mathcal{C}}
.

We now show that if
i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})
is finite, and there exists at least one language in the collection that is fully contained in the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
, then one can construct a generation algorithm, which with high probability, generates a string from the unseen support of
{\mathcal{D}}
.

Theorem 3.2

(Agnostic Generation Exponential Rate)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be a countable collection and
{\mathcal{D}}
be a distribution over the universe
U
. If
i({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{D}})<\infty
, and there exists a language
L\in{\mathcal{C}}
such that
L\subseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}})
, then with probability at least
1-c\cdot\exp(-C\cdot n)
over
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
(where
c,C
are constants depending only on
U,{\mathcal{D}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
, the string
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})
returned by
Algorithm

2
satisfies
{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}})\in\supp({\mathcal{D}})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}
. Namely,

{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\leq c\cdot\exp(-C\cdot n).

Proof.

Consider the execution of
Algorithm

2
. We first notice that any language

that satisfies

must have

satisfying

is not in the support of
{\mathcal{D}}
(by definition of

over
{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}^{n}
(where

3.3
Tightness of Exponential Rate

Our final result shows that a better-than-exponential rate is impossible for agnostic generation under the assumption that there exists
L\in{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying
L\subseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}})
.

Theorem 3.4

(Agnostic Generation Exponential Rate Lower Bound)
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}
be any collection over a universe
U
such that there exist languages
L,L^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
|L\cap L^{\prime}|<\infty
and
U\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\not=\emptyset
. For any generation algorithm
{\mathcal{A}}
using randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, there exists a distribution
{\mathcal{D}}
over
U
such that
\exists L\in{\mathcal{C}}
with
L\subseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}})
, and furthermore

\displaystyle{\mathrm{GenErr}}({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{D}},{\mathcal{C}},n)\geq\exp(-2n)/4

for infinitely many
n
.

To get some intuition for the lower bound, consider the special case where the collection has two languages
L,L^{\prime}
satisfying
L\cap L^{\prime}=\emptyset
, and
U\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. In this case, let
s
be any string that belongs to neither of
L
or
L^{\prime}
. Consider two distributions
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
that each put mass
1/2
on
s
, and spread the rest of their mass on
L
and
L^{\prime}
respectively. Under both
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
, with probability
2^{-n}
, the input comprises solely of
n
copies of
s
. Conditioned on this input, the output of the algorithm must necessarily not belong to one of
L
or
L^{\prime}
, since these are disjoint languages. But if the output does not belong to
L
, then the algorithm errs if the true distribution was
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
, and vice versa. Randomizing over
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
yields the lower bound. We now give the details of the entire argument.

Proof.

Let
m=|L\cap L^{\prime}|
. We first consider the case that
m=0
. In this case, let
s
be a string which is in neither of
L
and
L^{\prime}
; such an
s
exists by assumption that
U\setminus(L\cup L^{\prime})\not=\emptyset
. Now, let
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
be a distribution that has mass
1/2
on
s
, and spreads the rest of its mass arbitrarily on the strings in
L
, ensuring positive mass on every string. Similarly, let
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
be a distribution that has mass
1/2
on
s
, and spreads the rest of its mass arbitrarily on the strings in
L^{\prime}
, again ensuring that every string gets positive mass. Note that
L\subseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})
and
L^{\prime}\subseteq\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})
. Now consider realizing the sample
{\mathbf{S}}=s^{n}
, which gets realized with the same probability in both
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
; in this case, observe that
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus s^{n}=L
and
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus s^{n}=L^{\prime}
. Then, for any realization
{\mathbf{r}}
of
{\mathcal{A}}
’s randomness, by the disjointedness of
L
and
L^{\prime}
, it must hold that either
{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus s^{n}
or
{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus s^{n}
. Namely, we have that
\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus s^{n}\}+\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus s^{n}\}\geq 1
. Therefore, for any
n
,

\displaystyle\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{b})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]+\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right]+\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=s^{n}]\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus s^{n}\}\right]+\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=s^{n}]\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus s^{n}\}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\left(\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus s^{n}\}+\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(s^{n},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus s^{n}\}\right]\right)\geq\frac{1}{2^{n+1}}\geq\exp(-2n).

The above implies that
\exists b\in\{0,1\}
such that
\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{b})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\geq\exp(-2n)
for infinitely many
n
.

Next, consider the case that
m>0
. Fix
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{16m}
. Let
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
be a distribution that has mass
(1-\varepsilon)/m
on every string in
L\cap L^{\prime}
, and spreads the rest of the
\varepsilon
mass arbitrarily on
L\setminus L^{\prime}
, ensuring that every string gets positive mass. Similarly, let
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
be a distribution that has mass
(1-\varepsilon)/m
on every string in
L\cap L^{\prime}
, and spreads the rest of the
\varepsilon
mass arbitrarily on
L^{\prime}\setminus L
, again ensuring that every string gets positive mass. Note that
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})=L
and
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})=L^{\prime}
. Let

be any realization of the sample
{\mathbf{S}}
such that the set of distinct strings in
S

L\cap L^{\prime}
, i.e.,
\{s:s\in S\}=L\cap L^{\prime}
. We observe that the probability of realizing such an

is the same under both
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
and
{\mathcal{D}}_{1}
by definition of the distributions. Furthermore, we have that
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus S=L\setminus L^{\prime}
and
\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus S=L^{\prime}\setminus L
. This means that for any realization of the randomness
{\mathbf{r}}
, it must hold that either
{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus S
or
{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus S
, implying that
\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus S\}+\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\notin\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus S\}\geq 1
. Therefore, for any

\displaystyle\max_{b\in\{0,1\}}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{b}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{b})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]+\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n},{\mathbf{r}}}[{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}]\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right]+\e_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus{\mathbf{S}}\}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in(L\cap L^{\prime})^{n}}}{{\{s:s\in S\}=L\cap L^{\prime}}}}\left(\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus S\}\right]+\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{1}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\cdot\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus S\}\right]\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in(L\cap L^{\prime})^{n}}}{{\{s:s\in S\}=L\cap L^{\prime}}}}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]\cdot\left(\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{0})\setminus S\}\right]+\e_{{\mathbf{r}}}\left[\mathbbm{1}\{{\mathcal{A}}(S,{\mathbf{r}})\not\in\supp({\mathcal{D}}_{1})\setminus S\}\right]\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle S\in(L\cap L^{\prime})^{n}}}{{\{s:s\in S\}=L\cap L^{\prime}}}}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[{\mathbf{S}}=S]=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}\left[\{s\in{\mathbf{S}}\}=L\cap L^{\prime}\right].

We will now show that
\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}\left[\{s\in{\mathbf{S}}\}=L\cap L^{\prime}\right]
is at least
\exp(-2n)/2
for all large enough

n
, completing the proof.

So now, notice that

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\exists s\in L\cap L^{\prime}:s\notin{\mathbf{S}}]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in L\cap L^{\prime}}\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[s\notin{\mathbf{S}}]=m\left(1-\frac{1-\varepsilon}{m}\right)^{n}\leq m\exp(-n(1-\varepsilon)/m)

\displaystyle\leq m\exp(-n/2m)=m\exp(-n/4m)\exp(-n/4m)\leq\frac{4m^{2}}{n}\cdot\exp(-n/4m),

where in the third-to-last inequality, we used that
1+x\leq e^{x}
for all real
x
, in the second-to-last inequality, we used that
\varepsilon\leq 1/2
, and in the last inequality, we used that
e^{-x}\leq 1/x
for all

Similarly, we have that

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\exists s\in{\mathbf{S}}:s\notin L\cap L^{\prime}]=1-\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\forall s\in{\mathbf{S}}:s\in L\cap L^{\prime}]=1-(1-\varepsilon)^{n}\leq 1-\exp(-2n\varepsilon)=1-\exp(-n/8m),

where in the inequality above, we used that

for

Combining the above, we obtain that

\displaystyle\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\{s\in{\mathbf{S}}\}=L\cap L^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=1-\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}\left[\exists s\in L\cap L^{\prime}:s\notin{\mathbf{S}}\text{ or }\exists s\in{\mathbf{S}}:s\notin L\cap L^{\prime}\right]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\exists s\in L\cap L^{\prime}:s\notin{\mathbf{S}}]-\p_{{\mathbf{S}}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{0}^{n}}[\exists s\in{\mathbf{S}}:s\notin L\cap L^{\prime}]

(union bound)

\displaystyle\geq\exp(-n/8m)-\frac{4m^{2}}{n}\cdot\exp(-n/4m)=\exp(-n/8m)\left(1-\frac{4m^{2}}{n}\cdot\exp(-n/8m)\right).

For

, which, since

\exp(-2n)/2
.

4
Conclusion

In this paper, we initiated a study of agnostic language identification and generation, where the data distribution may be arbitrary, and not necessarily supported on any language from the reference collection. We proposed natural objectives to study for both the problems. For language identification, we derived a precise characterization for when exponential excess error rates may be obtained, and for language generation, we proved a general lower bound, and also identified a sufficient condition for finite collections that allows for exponential error rates. A natural open question here is to determine whether this sufficient condition allows an exponential rate for agnostic generation more generally for all countable collections.

More broadly, the setting of agnostic generation is rather nuanced, and as alluded to earlier, several natural objectives run into pathologies. While the objective we studied seems natural, our general lower bound suggests that it may be too strong. As future work, it would be interesting to study if there are other meaningful objectives that capture the difficulty of agnostic language generation more faithfully.
