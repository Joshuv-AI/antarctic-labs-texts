Catheter‑related bloodstream infections and risk factors in infants hospitalized in neonatal intensive care units at Mogadishu, Somalia: two years of experience
Authors: Tigad Abdisad Ali, Ahmet Doğan, Ali Kutta Çelik, Suad Abdikarim Isse, Faduma Nur Adan, Fardowsa Hassan Ahmed, Mohamed Dahir Omar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCatheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) are among the most common healthcare-associated infections in neonates and contribute substantially to morbidity and mortality. This study aimed to determine the incidence of CRBSIs and to examine the risk factors, clinical characteristics, and microbial profiles of CRBSIs, with emphasis on catheter type and birth weight classification.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted at the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) of the Mogadishu-Somalia-Turkey Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital. Medical records of neonates admitted between January 2022 and December 2024 were reviewed. Of 1,781 neonates admitted, 300 met the inclusion criteria according to the 2009 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines for CRBSI diagnosis, which include paired blood cultures, catheter tip cultures, or differential time to positivity. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23, including Spearman’s correlation and multivariable logistic regression to identify independent risk factors.ResultsDuring the study period, 1,781 neonates were admitted to the NICU, of whom 300 developed CRBSIs, corresponding to an incidence of 16.8%. Of these, 60% were male and 40% female. The majority (53%) were born between 36 and 42 weeks of gestation, while 29.3% had birth weights > 2,500 g. Significant associations were observed between prognosis and parameters including birth weight, gestational age, catheter type, C-reactive protein (CRP) levels, and culture sample type (p < 0.005).Analysis of culture results showed that Klebsiella spp. was the most frequently isolated pathogen, followed by E. coli and Candida spp. A significant correlation was identified between cathetertype and isolate type (p = 0.008). Furthermore, the highest frequency of Gram-negative isolates was observed among preterm gestational age groups (p = 0.002).Regarding antimicrobial resistance, Gram-negative isolates exhibited high resistance to ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, ampicillin-sulbactam, amoxicillin-clavulanate, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Gram-positive isolates, including MRSA and MRCNS, showed substantial resistance to clindamycin, erythromycin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole.ConclusionAmong neonates in Mogadishu, low birth weight, preterm gestational age, catheter type, and elevated CRP levels were significantly associated with CRBSI prognosis. The high prevalence of antibiotic resistance among both Gram-negative and Gram-positive pathogens underscores the urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and infection control measures in neonatal intensive care units.

Introduction

Venous catheters are essential medical devices widely used in intensive care settings, particularly among hospitalized neonates who are at increased risk of healthcare-associated infections due to prematurity, immature immune systems, fragile skin barriers, and prolonged hospitalization [1]. In neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), the most commonly used central venous catheters (CVCs) include umbilical venous catheters (UVCs), peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs), and tunneled CVCs [2].Catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in this vulnerable population [3]. These infections are associated with extended hospital stays, delayed management of underlying conditions, adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, increased healthcare costs, and elevated mortality rates [4]. Recognizing their clinical significance, the United States Department of Health and Human Services has prioritized the prevention and surveillance of catheter-associated infections [5, 6]. Although numerous studies have examined CRBSIs in neonates, most have focused on individual risk factors such as birth weight or gestational age [7, 8]. However, limited literature has compared the clinical and microbiological characteristics of CRBSIs across different catheter types and birth weight classifications.Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate the risk factors, microbiological profiles, and antibiotic resistance patterns associated with CRBSIs in neonates, with particular emphasis on catheter type and birth weight classification. We hypothesized that both catheter type and neonatal birth weight are significantly associated with the risk of CRBSIs and the distribution of causative organisms.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of Mogadishu-Somalia-Turkey Recep Tayyip Erdoğan Training and Research Hospital, a tertiary referral and teaching hospital and one of the largest referral centers in Somalia. The NICU admits both inborn neonates (delivered within the hospital) and outborn neonates (referred from other healthcare facilities or following home deliveries), ensuring a diverse patient population representative of both primary admissions and referrals.

Study population

The target population included all neonates admitted to the NICU who underwent catheter placement. Inclusion criteria were: documented catheter insertion and availability of complete clinical and microbiological data. Neonates with incomplete records or missing blood culture results, or those discharged within 24 h of catheter insertion, were excluded. A total of 1,781 neonates were admitted during the study period, and 300 cases met the inclusion criteria.

Data collection and variables

Medical records were reviewed to collect demographic variables (gender, gestational age, birth weight), clinical parameters (body temperature, blood pressure, episodes of apnea, bradycardia), catheter type, laboratory results (CRP), and microbiological culture findings. The primary outcome was the occurrence of catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) according to 2009 IDSA guidelines. Secondary outcomes included type of isolated microorganism and antibiotic resistance patterns.

Catheter definitions

For the purpose of this study, catheters were categorized as follows:

Central Venous Catheter (CVC): Any catheter inserted into a large central vein for intravenous therapy or monitoring.

UVC (Umbilical Venous Catheter): Inserted via the umbilical vein shortly after birth.

PICC (Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter): Inserted through a peripheral vein (arm or leg) and advanced until the tip resides in a central vein (superior or inferior vena cava).

SVC (Standard Central Venous Catheter): Inserted directly into major central veins such as the femoral vein, internal jugular vein (IJV), or subclavian vein.

PVC (Peripheral Venous Catheter): Also referred to as a peripheral intravenous line (PIVC). Inserted into a superficial peripheral vein (hand, foot, or scalp). PVCs do not reach central circulation and are generally used for short-term therapy.

Diagnosis of catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI)

The diagnosis of CRBSI in this study was established according to the Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Management of Intravascular Catheter-Associated Infections (Infectious Diseases Society of America [IDSA], 2009 Update). A definitive diagnosis required fulfillment of at least one of the following criteria:Catheter tip culture concordance: Isolation of the same microorganism from at least one percutaneous blood culture and from the catheter tip culture.Quantitative paired cultures: Isolation of the same organism from two blood samples—one obtained from the catheter hub and the other from a peripheral vein—that meet quantitative culture criteria.Differential time to positivity (DTP): A blood culture drawn from the catheter becoming positive at least 2 h earlier than a peripheral blood culture.Quantitative intraluminal comparison: A blood sample obtained from one catheter lumen yielding a colony count at least three times higher than that obtained from another lumen of the same catheter [9].

Microbiological methods

Microbiological Testing Blood and catheter tip cultures were performed according to the hospital’s standard microbiology protocols throughout the study period (January 2022 – December 2024), ensuring consistency in testing methods. Both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, as well as Candida spp., were routinely analyzed. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing for bacterial isolates was performed according to EUCAST (European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing) guidelines. Antifungal susceptibility testing was not routinely conducted.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and histogram visualization. Normally distributed variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables are summarized as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between categorical groups were performed using the chi-square (χ²) test, with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons to reduce the risk of Type I error.To identify independent risk factors associated with catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), a two-step logistic regression approach was used. Variables with p-values < 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for all variables in the multivariable model. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Mogadishu Somali-Turkey Recep Tayyip Erdogan Training and Research Hospital (MSTH: 17625). All methods were conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. This study involves infants who are unable to provide consent themselves. For these cases, consent was obtained from their parents, guardians, or next of kin."

Results

Nearly one-third (29.3%) had a birth weight greater than 2,500 g. Most neonates had normal vital signs, with 74.3% maintaining a normal body temperature and 57.7% having normal blood pressure. Discussion

This study provides critical insights into neonatal outcomes and antimicrobial resistance trends in a tertiary care setting in Mogadishu, Somalia. The incidence of neonatal catheter-related infections was associated with a high rate of adverse outcomes, including mortality. Our findings underscore the substantial burden of healthcare-associated infections in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) within resource-limited settings. A statistically significant association was observed between neonatal mortality and key clinical factors such as low birth weight, preterm gestational age, catheter type, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. These results are consistent with evidence from similar low-resource contexts. For example, a study in Tanzania emphasized the impact of low birth weight and prematurity on neonatal outcomes, especially in settings with limited access to advanced neonatal care [10]. Likewise, studies conducted in Ethiopia and Nigeria have documented similar challenges, highlighting the heightened vulnerability of preterm and low birth weight neonates [11, 12].

The elevated risk of mortality observed in neonates with certain catheter types and raised CRP levels suggests the critical role of early identification and management of infection. Elevated CRP, a marker of systemic inflammation, has been widely recognized as an early indicator of neonatal sepsis and poor outcomes. In our study, neonates with elevated CRP were more likely to have poor prognoses, reinforcing its value as a diagnostic tool in infection surveillance. Furthermore, the association between catheter type and adverse outcomes suggests that invasive devices, while necessary for care, may contribute to infection risk if proper insertion and maintenance protocols are not rigorously followed. These findings highlight the importance of strict adherence to catheter care guidelines and continuous staff training to minimize infection-related complications. Our findings reveal a high prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly among Gram-negative pathogens such as Klebsiella spp. and Escherichia coli. These organisms exhibited significant resistance to commonly used antibiotics, including third-generation cephalosporins. These findings are in line with several studies across sub-Saharan Africa, which report widespread resistance to standard empirical therapies. For instance, a systematic review on neonatal sepsis in Africa demonstrated alarmingly high resistance rates to ceftriaxone and other commonly used antibiotics, significantly limiting therapeutic options for neonates [13, 14]. A study from Kenya similarly identified Klebsiella spp. and E. coli as the predominant pathogens, showing resistance to ceftriaxone and gentamicin—key components of empirical neonatal sepsis treatment protocols [15].In the Somali context, the threat is particularly severe. Previous reports have identified Somalia as one of the countries with the highest mortality rates attributable to AMR globally [16]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for comprehensive national strategies to combat antimicrobial resistance, especially in neonatal care units. In addition to Gram-negative organisms, our study also identified high levels of resistance among Gram-positive pathogens. Notably, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and methicillin-resistant coagulase-negative Staphylococci (MRCNS) were prevalent, with high resistance rates to frontline antibiotics such as clindamycin and erythromycin. Similar resistance patterns have been reported in neonatal studies from Ethiopia and Nigeria [17–20], further underlining the pressing need for robust antimicrobial stewardship interventions in NICUs. Antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) have proven effective in curbing inappropriate antibiotic use and minimizing the spread of resistant pathogens in both high- and low-income countries. The implementation of such programs in Somali healthcare settings would represent a crucial step toward addressing the escalating threat of AMR in neonatal populations.Finally, our findings highlight the critical importance of implementing effective infection prevention and control (IPC) strategies. In settings like Somalia, where healthcare infrastructure remains underdeveloped and access to diagnostic microbiology is limited, IPC protocols play a central role in preventing the transmission of multidrug-resistant organisms. Strengthening hand hygiene compliance, ensuring proper catheter insertion and maintenance techniques, and providing ongoing staff education are essential components of IPC programsEvidence from other low- and middle-income countries demonstrates that targeted IPC interventions can lead to substantial reductions in both neonatal infection rates and overall mortality [21]. Investing in these strategies is particularly urgent in Somalia, where vulnerable neonatal populations face heightened risks due to limited healthcare resources and a high burden of antimicrobial resistance.

While our microbiological analysis focused on bacterial pathogens, it is important to note that antifungal susceptibility testing was not routinely performed during the study period. As a result, potential fungal pathogens, such as Candida species, may have been underrepresented in our findings. Given the known risk of catheter-associated fungal infections in neonatal populations, future studies should incorporate fungal culture and susceptibility data to provide a more comprehensive understanding of bloodstream infections.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary care hospital in Mogadishu, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings within Somalia or similar resource-limited regions. The retrospective design limited the ability to collect additional important variables, such as long-term neonatal outcomes and maternal health factors, which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of neonatal mortality and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Moreover, the absence of molecular diagnostic tools hindered the identification of specific resistance mechanisms and genetic determinants of AMR. Although the sample size was relatively substantial, it may not fully reflect the variability in neonatal care practices and AMR patterns across different healthcare facilities or regions. Future multicenter, prospective studies incorporating molecular analyses are warranted to address these gaps and strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusions

This study provides critical insights into the burden of catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs) among neonates in Mogadishu, Somalia. Our findings highlight significant associations between neonatal mortality and factors such as low birth weight, preterm birth, catheter type, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) levels. Gram-negative bacteria, particularly Klebsiella spp. and E. coli, were the most commonly isolated pathogens, exhibiting alarmingly high resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, and ampicillin-sulbactam. Similarly, Gram-positive isolates, including MRSA and MRCNS, demonstrated high resistance to clindamycin, erythromycin, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. These findings underscore the urgent need for robust infection prevention and control strategies in neonatal intensive care units, especially in resource-limited settings. Establishing comprehensive antimicrobial stewardship programs and revisiting catheter care protocols will be crucial to reducing the incidence of CRBSIs and improving neonatal outcomes. Furthermore, our results provide a foundational reference for public health efforts targeting antimicrobial resistance in Somalia and similar sub-Saharan African contexts.Future research should focus on multicenter, prospective studies incorporating molecular diagnostic techniques to better understand resistance mechanisms and guide targeted interventions. Collaborative efforts among clinicians, microbiologists, policymakers, and global health partners are essential to mitigate the impact of CRBSIs and combat the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance in neonatal care.
