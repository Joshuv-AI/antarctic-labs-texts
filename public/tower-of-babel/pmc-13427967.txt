Real-World Comparative Effectiveness and Safety of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors in Advanced Renal Cell Carcinoma: A Systematic Review
Authors: Chin Hang Yiu, Gwyneth Yuet Yin Leung, Isabelle C. Lee, Christine Y. Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundImmune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)-based regimens are the standard first-line therapy for advanced renal cell carcinoma (RCC). However, their comparative effectiveness and safety in routine clinical practice remain incompletely characterised.ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to systematically synthesise comparative real-world evidence on the efficacy and safety of ICI-based regimens in patients with advanced RCC.MethodsA systematic search of MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus was conducted from database inception to 21 January 2026, in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Observational cohort studies evaluating ICI-based regimens in advanced RCC with an active comparator were included. Primary efficacy outcomes were overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and objective response rate (ORR). Safety outcomes included treatment-related adverse events. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, with efficacy and safety evaluated separately. Owing to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity, findings were synthesised narratively.ResultsOverall, 58 retrospective cohort studies comprising 35,215 patients were included. Most studies evaluated first-line therapy and involved populations with mixed histology and heterogeneous prognostic risk profiles. Overall, ICI-tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) combinations were generally associated with more favourable survival outcomes compared with dual ICI therapy or TKI monotherapy, with the most consistent benefit observed for PFS. Evidence comparing dual ICI therapy with TKI monotherapy was mixed, with most studies reporting no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes. Comparative safety data were limited, with the majority of studies relying on unadjusted descriptive analyses, limiting robust comparative interpretation of safety outcomes.ConclusionsReal-world evidence suggests that ICI–TKI combinations are associated with more favourable survival outcomes compared with other treatment options in advanced RCC, while the comparative effectiveness of dual ICI therapy versus TKI monotherapy remains inconclusive. Substantial heterogeneity and methodological limitations, particularly in safety reporting, limit definitive interpretation. High-quality real-world studies incorporating robust confounding adjustment and clinically relevant subgroup analyses are needed to better inform treatment selection in routine practice.Trial RegistryInternational Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) registration number: CRD420251149614.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11523-026-01227-y.

Key Points

In real-world clinical practice, immune checkpoint inhibitor–tyrosine kinase inhibitor (ICI–TKI) combinations were generally associated with more favourable survival outcomes compared with dual ICI therapy or TKI monotherapy.Comparative safety evidence for ICI-based regimens remains limited, as most real-world studies relied on unadjusted descriptive analyses, limiting robust assessment of relative toxicity.Substantial population heterogeneity and residual confounding across real-world studies highlight the need for methodologically rigorous comparative analyses to better inform treatment selection in routine clinical practice.

Introduction

Kidney cancer is the 14th most frequently diagnosed cancer globally, with an estimated 430,000 new cases and 155,000 deaths annually [1]. Prognosis remains poor for advanced disease, with 5-year relative survival rates of approximately 76% for regional disease and only 19% for distant metastases [2]. For more than a decade, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), including sunitinib and sorafenib, represented the standard first-line treatment for advanced renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [3].

The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has substantially transformed the treatment landscape of advanced RCC [4]. Dual ICI therapy with nivolumab plus ipilimumab was the first ICI-based regimen approved for first-line treatment of metastatic clear cell RCC, based on the landmark CheckMate-214 trial, which demonstrated superior overall survival compared with sunitinib [5]. Subsequently, ICI–TKI combinations were approved following pivotal phase III trials, including KEYNOTE-581, which showed improved survival outcomes with pembrolizumab–lenvatinib compared with sunitinib monotherapy [6]. As a result, current clinical guidelines recommend ICI-based regimens as standard first-line therapy for patients with advanced RCC across all International Metastatic RCC Database Consortium (IMDC) risk categories, particularly for those with clear cell histology [7, 8].

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) remain the gold standard for establishing comparative efficacy, but may have limited generalisability to routine clinical practice owing to eligibility criteria and controlled settings [9]. Real-world data (RWD) have therefore become increasingly important for evaluating treatment effectiveness in broader, more heterogeneous patient populations [10]. Emerging evidence suggests that up to 35% of patients with metastatic RCC treated in routine practice would be ineligible for participation in pivotal clinical trials and may experience poorer outcomes [11, 12]. In addition, several observational studies have reported shorter survival with ICI-based therapies in real-world settings than that observed in clinical trials [13–15].

To date, systematic reviews in advanced RCC have largely focused on synthesising evidence from RCTs and indirect treatment comparisons, including network meta-analyses, which primarily estimate comparative efficacy under controlled trial conditions [16–18]. Indirect comparisons from clinical trials also suggest potential heterogeneity in treatment effects across different ICI-based regimens [19]. However, there remains limited synthesis of comparative real-world evidence across ICI-based regimens in advanced RCC. Such evidence may better reflect treatment effectiveness in routine clinical practice, where patient populations are more heterogeneous than those included in clinical trials. Therefore, the aim of this systematic review was to synthesise comparative real-world evidence on the efficacy and safety of ICI-based regimens in patients with advanced RCC. These findings complement existing trial-based evidence and may inform interpretation of treatment effectiveness in routine clinical practice.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [20]. The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD 420251149614). Any protocol amendments and corresponding justifications are documented in Supplementary Table S1.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in MEDLINE (via Ovid), Embase, and Scopus from database inception to 21 January 2026. The search strategy combined three key concept domains using Boolean operators: (1) renal cell carcinoma; (2) immune checkpoint inhibitors (including specific agent names); and (3) real-world evidence and observational study designs (including terms such as registry, healthcare claims, and medical record database). The full search strategy for each database is detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies were observational cohort studies evaluating outcomes associated with ICI use in patients with advanced RCC, with no restrictions on line of therapy or prior treatment exposure. The inclusion of studies across all treatment lines was intended to capture the breadth of available real-world evidence. Studies were required to include a comparator group to enable evaluation of the comparative efficacy and/or safety of ICI-based versus non-ICI-based standard treatments (e.g. TKI-based regimens). Studies directly comparing different ICI-based regimens (e.g. dual ICIs versus ICI–TKI combinations) were also included. Included studies were required to report at least one efficacy outcome (e.g. survival endpoints) and/or one safety outcome (e.g. adverse events).

Studies were excluded if the immunotherapy cohort did not clearly specify the ICI agents used, to avoid inclusion of non-ICI immunotherapies (e.g. interferon or interleukin-based regimens). Additional exclusion criteria included RCTs, case–control studies, commentaries, editorials, narrative or systematic reviews, non-English publications, and conference abstracts or posters without full-text availability. The English-language restriction was applied as previous empirical research has shown that language-restricted systematic reviews do not differ meaningfully from language-inclusive reviews in effect estimates in biomedical fields where English is the dominant language of publication [21].

Outcomes

Consistent with a previous systematic review [22], primary efficacy outcomes were overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), and objective response rate (ORR). Additional efficacy outcomes included, but were not limited to, time on treatment (ToT) and time to next treatment (TTNT). Primary safety outcomes comprised adverse events (AEs), including treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) and immune-related adverse events (irAEs), without restriction on severity. Additional safety outcomes included treatment discontinuation due to AEs and healthcare resource utilisation measures.

Study Selection

All records identified through the database searches were imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) [23], and duplicates were removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by at least two reviewers (C.H.Y., G.Y.Y.L., I.C.L.). Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were assessed independently by the same reviewers to determine final inclusion. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a senior investigator (C.Y.L.) to achieve consensus.

Data Extraction

Data extraction of each included study was independently performed by at least two reviewers (C.H.Y., G.Y.Y.L., I.C.L.) using a prespecified data extraction template. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and, if required, consultation with a senior investigator (C.Y.L.). Extracted data included study characteristics (author, year of publication, country and data source), sample size (overall and by study cohort), population characteristics (line of treatment, histology and IMDC risk classification), and reported efficacy and/or safety outcomes.

Quality Assessment

Methodological quality of the included non-randomised, non-interventional cohort studies was independently assessed by two investigators (C.H.Y. and G.Y.Y.L.) using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [24]. The NOS tool has been applied in prior systematic reviews of real-world comparative effectiveness and safety of ICIs in colorectal cancer and small cell lung cancer [22, 25]. Studies were evaluated across three domains: selection, comparability and outcome assessment. Overall study quality was classified as good, fair or poor according to Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) criteria. In line with a previous systematic review of real-world evidence [26], efficacy and safety outcomes were evaluated separately, reflecting differences in analytical approaches and outcome ascertainment across studies. This approach is consistent with the methodological principle that risk of bias may vary across outcomes within the same study depending on confounding control and outcome measurement methods [27].

Data Synthesis

Given substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Heterogeneity was evident across three domains: clinical heterogeneity (including histological subtypes, IMDC risk group, treatment line and comparator type); methodological heterogeneity (including unadjusted analyses, multivariable regression, propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting); and reporting heterogeneity (including inconsistent availability and format of effect estimates). Many studies did not report effect estimates in a standardised format suitable for quantitative pooling. Under these conditions, meta-analysis would risk generating summary estimates that combine fundamentally different patient populations, interventions and analytical frameworks. This approach is consistent with Cochrane guidance, which advises against meta-analysis when clinical and methodological heterogeneity is present [28]. Accordingly, findings were synthesised narratively.

Results

The database search identified 3589 records. After removal of duplicates, 2303 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 120 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in 58 studies being included in this systematic review (Fig. 1) [29–86]. Two studies that otherwise met the inclusion criteria were excluded because it was unclear whether the immunotherapy cohorts consisted exclusively of ICI agents [87, 88]. Notably, a high-quality study by Chakiryan et al. employing 1:1:1 propensity score matching reported that patients in the immunotherapy cohorts may have received non-ICI immunotherapies (e.g. interferon-α or high-dose interleukin-2) [87], precluding definitive attribution of observed outcomes to ICI treatment.Fig. 1PRISMA flow diagram of the study selection process [20]. Study Characteristics

Table 1 summarises the key characteristics of the included studies [29–86]. All studies were retrospective cohort designs and collectively included 35,215 patients with advanced or metastatic RCC, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 25 to 6297 (median 221) patients. Most studies were conducted in Japan (n = 18) [34, 37–39, 42, 60, 62, 64, 65, 71, 73, 77, 78, 80, 81, 84], followed by international studies (n = 13) [30, 35, 36, 41, 43, 47, 53, 55, 66, 69, 74, 76, 82], and the USA (n = 11) [32, 40, 48, 54, 56, 58, 61, 67, 72, 83, 85]. Only two studies used healthcare administrative or insurance claims data [40, 50], while other studies primarily used clinical data from electronic health records or cancer registries [29–39, 41–49, 51–86]. Baseline characteristics of all included studies (n = 58)

EHR electronic health record, IMDC International Metastatic Renal Cell Carcinoma Database Consortium, RCC renal cell carcinoma

With respect to treatment line, 50 studies evaluated first-line therapy [29–47, 49–51, 53, 55, 56, 58–63, 65–68, 70–73, 75–78, 80–86], 5 evaluated second-line therapy [48, 52, 64, 69, 74], 1 included any treatment line [57], and 2 did not specify treatment line [54, 79]. Regarding histology, most studies (n = 34) included mixed histology, with patient populations predominantly composed of clear-cell RCC rather than papillary RCC [33–35, 37, 38, 41–43, 45, 49, 51, 53, 55, 57, 61–66, 68–70, 75, 76, 78, 80–82, 84–86]. In terms of IMDC risk classification, the majority of studies (n = 36) included patients across all risk groups (favourable, intermediate and poor) [29, 31, 33, 35, 37, 42, 44, 47, 48, 52, 53, 57–65, 67, 68, 70, 71, 74–76, 78, 80–86], with a higher proportion of intermediate- and poor-risk patients. This was followed by studies restricted to intermediate- and/or poor-risk patients only (n = 14) [30, 32, 34, 36, 38, 39, 43, 45, 49, 51, 55, 66, 73, 77]. The most frequently evaluated treatment cohorts were ICI plus TKI combinations (ICI–TKI), dual ICI (ICI–ICI), and TKI monotherapy. Specific agents investigated within these regimens were generally consistent with regulatory approvals and clinical practice guidelines, such as pembrolizumab–lenvatinib and nivolumab–cabozantinib (ICI–TKI), nivolumab–ipilimumab (the only ICI–ICI regimen investigated), and sunitinib monotherapy (TKI), supporting the real-world relevance of the included evidence. Quality Assessment

The quality assessment for efficacy outcomes is summarised in Supplementary Table S3. Of the 57 studies reporting efficacy outcomes [29–53, 55–86], 27 were assessed as good quality. These studies demonstrated sufficient comparability between treatment cohorts, with potential confounders appropriately addressed through multivariable Cox regression analyses, propensity score matching, or inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). The remaining 30 studies were rated as poor quality, primarily due to inadequate adjustment for confounding. For safety outcomes [29, 31, 33, 34, 39, 40, 42, 44, 46, 52, 54, 60, 64, 66, 70–73, 77, 80, 81, 83, 84], 5 were assessed as good quality, while the remaining 19 were rated as poor quality. Most safety analyses were descriptive and lacked adjustment for treatment differences or potential confounders, limiting the validity of comparative safety conclusions (Supplementary Table S4).

ICI-Based Versus Non-ICI Based Regimens

ICI–TKI Versus TKI Monotherapy

The most common comparison was ICI–TKI versus TKI monotherapy (n = 15) [29, 31, 36, 41, 42, 45, 48, 52, 56, 61, 68, 75, 79, 80, 86], of which seven studies rated as good quality in terms of efficacy outcomes [29, 45, 48, 52, 61, 80, 86]. Among good-quality studies, ICI–TKI was associated with improved PFS compared with TKI monotherapy, while OS findings were less consistent across studies [45, 52, 80, 86]. Using stabilised IPTW, Goebell et al. reported that TKI monotherapy was associated with a significantly shorter median PFS compared with ICI–TKI (8.5 versus 12.3 months; hazard ratio [HR] 1.51, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.19–1.91), whereas no statistically significant difference in OS was observed (median 31.7 versus 29.0 months; HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.77–1.41) [86]. Similarly, in the second-line setting, Huang et al. used propensity score matching and found a longer median PFS with ICI–TKI compared with TKI monotherapy (9.2 versus 5.4 months; p = 0.002) [52]. Findings from poor-quality studies generally showed similar directional trends, although interpretation is limited by lack of confounding adjustment.

Regarding safety, outcomes were generally comparable between ICI–TKI and TKI monotherapy [29, 31, 52, 80]. One study reported a higher incidence of grade ≥ 3 AEs with ICI–TKI compared with TKI monotherapy (66.1% versus 39.7%, p = 0.004) [42]. However, all these studies were considered poor quality, as safety outcomes were reported descriptively without adjustment for treatment differences or potential confounders.

ICI–ICI Versus TKI Monotherapy

A total of 13 studies compared dual ICI with TKI monotherapy [29, 31, 36, 45, 56, 61, 63, 68, 73, 75, 77, 80, 86]. Overall, evidence from good-quality studies did not demonstrate a consistent survival advantage of dual ICI over TKI monotherapy. Among the seven good-quality studies [29, 45, 61, 73, 77, 80, 86], results were largely neutral, with only Kido et al. reporting significant improvements in OS (HR 0.51, 95% CI 0.27–0.95), PFS (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37–0.97) and ORR (38.5 versus 22.9%, p = 0.018) in favour of dual ICI using IPTW-adjusted analyses [73]. Within this study, OS and PFS benefits were limited to the IMDC poor-risk subgroup and were not observed in the intermediate-risk subgroup. Two additional good-quality studies reported higher ORR with dual ICI but without corresponding survival benefits [29, 77], while the remaining good-quality studies showed no significant differences in survival endpoints. Findings from poor-quality studies were consistent with this overall pattern, with all reporting no significant survival differences between the two regimens [31, 36, 56, 63, 68, 75].

Regarding safety, all five studies reporting comparative safety data were rated as poor quality [29, 31, 73, 77, 80]. No clinically meaningful differences were reported between dual ICI and TKI monotherapy [29, 31, 73, 77, 80]. In Kido et al., no significant differences were observed in any or grade ≥ 3 AEs; however, AE-related treatment discontinuation was higher with dual ICI (69% versus 17%) [73]. Importantly, although IPTW was applied to efficacy analyses, safety outcomes were unadjusted, limiting comparative interpretation. Overall, safety data were descriptive and lacked confounding adjustment.

ICI Monotherapy Versus TKI Monotherapy

Across five studies comparing ICI monotherapy with TKI monotherapy [50, 59, 64, 69, 74], all were considered poor quality, primarily due to lack of adequate confounding adjustment. Across these studies, no consistent differences in survival outcomes were observed. Santoni et al. and Stukalin et al. both conducted studies in the second-line setting and reported no statistically significant differences in survival outcomes between nivolumab and cabozantinib [69, 74]. In addition, Santoni et al. stratified patients by IMDC risk category (favourable, intermediate and poor) and found no significant differences in survival outcomes within any risk subgroup. However, given the lack of confounding control, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Other

Three good-quality studies compared ICI-based regimens with targeted therapy, which predominantly consisted of TKIs but also included other targeted agents such as mTOR inhibitors (e.g. temsirolimus) [57, 71, 78]. Across these studies, ICI-based regimens were associated with improved efficacy outcomes compared with targeted therapy. Additional studies compared ICI-based regimens with TKI monotherapy and reported similar directional findings favouring ICI-based regimens [47, 51, 53, 59, 65]; however, interpretation is limited by heterogeneity in study quality and confounding adjustment. Notably, Sawaya et al. reported improved median OS and ORR with ICI-based regimens compared with TKI monotherapy in patients with sarcomatoid features (48 versus 18 months; 49.5% versus 13.8%) as well as those without sarcomatoid differentiation (72 versus 48 months; 42.9% versus 39.2%) [51]. In the only study specifically evaluating bone-predominant metastatic RCC, Meng et al. reported worse outcomes with ICI-based regimens compared with TKI monotherapy [85]; however, these findings were limited by poor baseline comparability and small sample size, and should be interpreted cautiously.

Different ICI-Based Regimens

ICI–TKI Versus ICI–ICI

A total of 34 studies compared outcomes between ICI in combination with TKI and dual ICI [30–34, 36–40, 43–45, 47, 49, 51, 53–56, 58, 60, 61, 66–68, 70, 75, 76, 80–84, 86]. Across good-quality studies, ICI–TKI was generally associated with improved efficacy outcomes compared with dual ICI therapy. Several high-quality studies employing propensity score matching or IPTW demonstrated improved outcomes across multiple efficacy endpoints, including OS, PFS, ORR, TTNT and time to treatment failure (TTF) [32, 34, 38, 49, 51, 67, 86]. Notably, Zarrabi et al. reported significantly worse OS (HR 3.28, 95% CI 1.04–10.3) and PFS (HR 2.30, 95% CI 1.20–4.43) with ICI–ICI compared with ICI–TKI in the IMDC favourable-risk subgroup, while no significant differences were observed in the intermediate- or poor-risk subgroups [67]. Findings from lower-quality studies were broadly consistent, although interpretation is limited by lack of confounding adjustment.

Regarding safety, 15 studies reported comparative safety data [31, 33, 34, 39, 40, 44, 49, 54, 60, 66, 70, 80, 81, 83, 84]. Of these, five studies were rated as high quality owing to good baseline comparability between treatment groups [34, 39, 40, 49, 83]. Notably, these were the only studies across the entire systematic review to achieve good comparability ratings in safety outcomes. Three studies reported a higher incidence of any adverse events in the ICI–TKI cohort [34, 39, 49]. Shah et al. examined healthcare resource utilisation using multivariable-adjusted analyses and found no significant differences between ICI–TKI and ICI–ICI in inpatient stays involving intensive care unit admission (adjusted incidence rate ratio 0.70, 95% CI 0.43–1.14) [40]. The remaining studies were predominantly unadjusted and generally reported no significant safety differences between regimens; however, as with other treatment comparisons in this review, the reliance on unadjusted analyses and lack of confounding control limit the robustness of comparative safety conclusions.

Different ICI–TKI Regimens

Comparisons between different ICI–TKI regimens showed heterogeneous efficacy outcomes, with no single regimen demonstrating consistent superiority across studies [30, 35, 46, 62, 82]. Direct head-to-head comparisons between specific ICI–TKI regimens were limited. Santoni et al. compared nivolumab–cabozantinib with pembrolizumab–axitinib and reported no significant difference in OS (not reached versus 55.7 months, adjusted HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.58–1.31), while median PFS favoured nivolumab–cabozantinib (27.6 versus 16.2 months, adjusted HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.37–0.74) [35]. Similarly, Liu et al. reported improved OS and disease control with nivolumab–cabozantinib compared with nivolumab–sunitinib [46]. Overall, evidence comparing different ICI–TKI regimens was limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneity in study design and inconsistent adjustment for confounders, precluding definitive conclusions.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first systematic review to synthesise comparative real-world observational evidence on the efficacy and safety of ICI-based regimens in advanced RCC. Overall, the available real-world evidence suggests that ICI-based regimens are associated with improved outcomes compared with non-ICI-based regimens. In particular, ICI–TKI combinations are associated with improved survival outcomes compared with TKI monotherapy, findings that are consistent with results from pivotal trials such as KEYNOTE-581 [6]. ICI–TKI regimens also appeared to provide more consistent benefits than dual ICI therapy. Network meta-analyses of clinical trial data have similarly suggested superior efficacy of ICI–TKI regimens relative to other first-line options [17, 89], and the findings from our review are broadly consistent with these trial-based findings. Although recent trial evidence from COSMIC-313 suggests PFS benefits from adding a TKI to dual ICI therapy (nivolumab–ipilimumab plus cabozantinib versus nivolumab–ipilimumab) [90], this strategy was not evaluated in the included real-world studies. Moreover, while indirect trial evidence indicates potential heterogeneity in effectiveness across individual ICI–TKI regimens [19], the available real-world data were limited and insufficient for robust comparative assessment. Taken together, these real-world findings are consistent with trial-derived evidence suggesting improved survival outcomes with ICI–TKI combinations relative to alternative regimens. However, given the retrospective and observational nature of the included studies, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal and as supportive of existing trial evidence rather than as a basis for independent treatment recommendations. Treatment selection between ICI–TKI and dual ICI therapy should continue to be individualised on the basis of trial evidence, patient risk profile, comorbidities and therapeutic goals. Further high-quality real-world studies evaluating contemporary ICI–TKI regimens are therefore needed to inform optimal regimen selection in routine clinical practice.

In contrast to ICI–TKI combinations, dual ICI therapy with nivolumab–ipilimumab was not consistently associated with improved survival or treatment response outcomes compared with TKI monotherapy in real-world settings. Most studies reported no statistically significant differences [31, 36, 45, 56, 61, 63, 68, 75, 86], highlighting a potential divergence between trial efficacy and real-world effectiveness. These inconsistencies may reflect substantial population heterogeneity, as most studies enrolled patients with mixed histology and heterogeneous IMDC risk profiles, as well as residual confounding inherent to observational designs [91]. Differences in outcome definitions and assessment frequency between real-world studies and clinical trials, particularly for PFS and ORR, may further contribute to discrepancies in observed effectiveness and should be considered when interpreting results. Future real-world studies with more robust confounding adjustment, longer follow-up and clinically meaningful subgroup analyses—including granular stratification by histology, IMDC risk, disease burden and patient characteristics—are warranted to better delineate the comparative efficacy of dual ICI therapy in routine clinical practice. Until such data are available, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, and treatment decisions should continue to be individualised on the basis of trial evidence, patient risk profile and comorbidities.

This review also highlights the limited availability of high-quality real-world evidence comparing the safety of ICI-based regimens. Only five studies in this systematic review were assessed as good quality, all evaluating ICI–TKI versus ICI–ICI comparisons [34, 39, 40, 49, 83]. Most safety analyses relied solely on descriptive statistics without adjustment for treatment differences, limiting the ability to draw robust comparative safety conclusions. AEs may also be under-captured in routine clinical datasets compared with clinical trials, further limiting interpretation of these findings. These findings highlight the need for future real-world studies employing rigorous analytical methods, such as propensity score matching or IPTW, to better account for confounding and reduce bias in safety assessments. For instance, IPTW can reduce confounding by balancing measured baseline characteristics between treatment groups, thereby approximating a randomised setting and improving the reliability of comparative estimates [92].

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of this systematic review is its comprehensive search strategy, which captured relevant real-world studies evaluating ICI-based regimens in advanced RCC. The included studies offer high external validity by capturing treatment outcomes in patient populations that are more representative of routine clinical practice than those typically enrolled in clinical trials. Minimal restrictions on patient characteristics, treatment line, and ICI-based regimens allowed inclusion of a wide spectrum of real-world clinical scenarios. Importantly, the included studies largely reflect therapies routinely used in clinical practice or approved by regulatory agencies, enhancing the clinical relevance and generalisability of the findings [93]. A broad range of efficacy and safety outcomes was evaluated, and inclusion of studies from diverse geographic regions—including international real-world datasets such as ARON-1—further strengthens the generalisability of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Substantial heterogeneity across studies in terms of patient characteristics, sample sizes, treatment regimens, follow-up duration and analytical approaches precluded quantitative synthesis and robust subgroup or meta-analytic comparisons. Subgroup observations in this review, including apparent differences by IMDC risk group and histological subtype, should be interpreted as exploratory as these analyses were typically conducted in small subsets. Publication bias is also possible, as small single-centre studies reporting null or unfavourable results may be less likely to be published, potentially leading to an over-representation of positive findings in literature. A formal assessment of publication bias (e.g. funnel plots or Egger’s test) was not feasible given the narrative synthesis approach.

Furthermore, the observational nature of the included studies makes them susceptible to residual confounding and bias, and therefore reduced internal validity [91]. Unadjusted analyses are susceptible to confounding by indication, whereby patients with more favourable baseline characteristics may be preferentially assigned to certain treatments, potentially biasing comparative effectiveness estimates. Propensity score-based methods and IPTW are also limited to measured variables and cannot address unmeasured confounders such as physician preference, patient frailty or reasons for treatment selection that are not routinely captured in medical records. Accordingly, even adjusted estimates from the included studies should be interpreted cautiously, as residual confounding cannot be excluded in any observational comparison. These findings should therefore be interpreted in the context of existing randomised trial evidence. Real-world data can help contextualise whether treatment effects observed in trials are reproduced in broader populations but do not provide the same level of causal inference as well-conducted RCTs.

Conclusions

Real-world evidence from this systematic review suggests that ICI–TKI combinations are associated with favourable survival outcomes compared with other first-line options in advanced RCC, whereas the comparative effectiveness of dual ICI therapy versus TKI remains inconsistent. Substantial heterogeneity and methodological limitations across studies, particularly regarding comparative safety, limit definitive conclusions. Clinicians should interpret current real-world evidence cautiously and continue to individualise treatment decisions according to patient risk stratification, comorbidities and therapeutic goals. High-quality real-world studies with robust confounding adjustment and clinically meaningful subgroup analyses are needed to guide optimal treatment selection in routine clinical practice.
