A comparison of methods for assessing root canal curvature using periapical radiographs: an intrarater/interrater reliability and agreement study
Authors: Zahra Tabari-Khomeiran, Farzaneh Ostovarrad, Nargess Simdar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the level of agreement among four different methods used to measure root canal curvature in first and second molars, based on periapical (PA) radiographs from patients.MethodThis study investigated intra and interrater reliability and agreement, involving 150 root canals from the first and second molars of 103 patients. Radiographs were obtained from two dental imaging centers through convenience sampling, and each radiograph was assigned two unique identifiers to ensure blinding in measurements. An oral and maxillofacial radiologist and three dental students, who received training from the oral and maxillofacial radiologist, independently measured the curvature angles of all the canals using four different measurement techniques. In the initial round, each observer completed 600 measurements. After a ten-day period, the students repeated their measurements independently to evaluate consistency. The collected data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 and MedCalc version 23.2.7.ResultsThe average curvature angles measured by the Schneider, Hankins & ElDeeb, Weine, and Luiten methods were approximately 21.70° (± 12.62°), 21.81° (± 13.77°), 27.51° (± 18.07°), and 30.63° (± 18.95°), respectively, with each method showing different standard deviations. The Luiten method demonstrated the highest overall interrater agreement at 0.969, followed by Weine at 0.949, Hankins & ElDeeb at 0.935, and Schneider at 0.921. In terms of intrarater reliability, Weine (0.967) and Luiten (0.966) showed greater consistency than Schneider (0.939) and Hankins & ElDeeb (0.938). Pairwise comparisons revealed the strongest interrater agreement between Schneider and Weine (0.909), and between Weine and Luiten (0.963), whereas the weakest was between Schneider and Hankins & ElDeeb (0.575). Good agreement was also noted between Schneider and Luiten (0.876), and between Weine and Hankins & ElDeeb (0.721).ConclusionsAll four measurement techniques demonstrated excellent agreement levels, indicating their reliability in evaluating root canal curvature. However, given the highest interrater agreement observed with the Luiten method, it may be considered the most consistent choice for clinical practice, especially in situations requiring precise and reproducible measurements. Clinicians are advised to consider adopting the Luiten method or selecting a technique that best aligns with their specific clinical context to enhance treatment planning accuracy and outcomes.Keywords: Root canal curvature, Periapical radiographs, Agreement coefficient, Schneider, Weine, Luiten

Introduction

The shape of the root canal significantly influences the success of endodontic treatment. Increasing the curvature of the root canal from less than 5 degrees to more than 20 degrees raises the risk of lodgement from 12.5% to 52.3% [1]. The British Endodontic Society considers a canal angle over 45 degrees as a key factor in classifying endodontic cases as Level 3 complexity for treatment [2]. An accurate evaluation of root canal curvature is crucial for effective treatment planning and instrumentation, as it helps minimize procedural errors and post-treatment complications. Several methods have been developed to measure canal curvature on radiographs, each offering various benefits and limitations [3–5]. Micro-CT and AI-assisted approaches offered a detailed examination of canal configurations, including measurements of length, curvature, and the position of calcified segments [6–14]. However, among these, PA radiography remains one of the most accessible, practical, and commonly employed tools for assessing root canal anatomy [15].

PA radiographs are frequently used in clinical practice to assess root canal anatomy because they are readily available and provide detailed images of the root structures [16]. Various techniques have been introduced to measure canal curvature from these radiographs, such as the Schneider, Weine, Hankins & ElDeeb, and Luiten methods [4]. Although these approaches are commonly used, there is limited research comparing how consistently they agree with each other, especially regarding intra and interrater reliability, which are essential for determining their usefulness in clinical practice.

Understanding the level of agreement among these measurement methods can guide clinicians in selecting the most appropriate technique tailored to their specific needs. The findings also contribute to the existing literature by clarifying the reliability of commonly used radiographic assessment methods. Ultimately, this understanding enhances the accuracy of root canal curvature assessments, thereby supporting improved clinical outcomes in endodontic practice.

Although laboratory studies have examined the consistency of root canal measurement methods in the literature (e.g., Faraj, [17]; Faraj, [18]; Günday, [19]; Hartmann et al. [20]), to our best knowledge, previous studies on this topic have primarily been conducted using in-vitro approaches. As a result, the level of agreement of these methods in clinical settings remains unclear and has not been definitively addressed.

As understanding these agreements is crucial for ensuring diagnostic consistency, treatment precision, and patient safety, it is essential to perform relevant research to enhance the current knowledge base by generating data that are relevant to real-world clinical environments. This is particularly important because the results, for the first time, offer practical and robust evidence for dental professionals, helping them to adopt precise techniques for measuring root canal angles during routine clinical practice. This study aims to evaluate the agreement among four radiographic measurement techniques—Schneider [21], Hankins & ElDeeb [22], Weine [23], and Luiten [24] as more popular methods—when used to assess root canal curvature in first and second molars.

Methods

The GRRAS (Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies) checklist is utilized to document this research. It serves as a tool to ensure that studies assessing the reliability and agreement of measurements are thoroughly and transparently reported. The GRRAS emphasizes the importance of providing detailed information about the methods, participants, and statistical analyses employed, facilitating accurate evaluation and interpretation of the findings [25]. The study protocol received approval from the Research Ethics Committees of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1404.022) and was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design, setting and sampling

This intrarater/interrater reliability and agreement study was conducted in Guilan University of Medical Sciences from January 2025 to June 2025. The PA radiographs needed for this study were sourced from two maxillofacial radiology centers, which offer both general and specialized services to the public. The minimum sample size for this study was calculated to be 150 root canals from first and second molars for each method, based on the study objectives and parameters, using the sample size determination formula for the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient developed by Zou [26].

Canal curvature measurements

The images used in this study were captured with a Planmeca X-ray unit (Helsinki, Finland) employing the parallel technique. Curvature measurements were conducted following the methodologies outlined by Schneider [21], Weine [23], Luiten et al. [24], and Hankins & ElDeeb [22]. To ensure blinding during the two measurement sessions, each root canal was assigned two identifiers—one for the first round of measurement and another for the second. Four observers (a maxillofacial radiologist and three sixth-year dental students) independently measured the curvature angle of each root canal using all four methods (Schneider, Weine, Luiten, and Hankins & ElDeeb). All students serving as observers in this study had successfully completed a minimum of 11 credits in endodontics— comprising two in theoretical coursework and five in clinical training— as well as five credits in oral and maxillofacial radiology— including two in theoretical and three in practical courses— in accordance with the dental education curriculum. Furthermore, these observers received training from the oral and maxillofacial radiologist co-investigator, which included a comprehensive explanation of each method, supplemented by relevant images and videos for reference. To confirm their understanding of the techniques, all observers assessed the curvature values on ten randomly selected PA radiographs. The agreement among the measurements was 0.7. After calibration, the first round of measurements was carried out independently by four evaluators: OB1, OB2, OB3, all dental students, and OB4, a maxillofacial radiology specialist (FO). The students performed a second round of measurements separately for the entire sample, with approximately a 10-day interval between repetitions of the same method. To reduce observer fatigue and ensure data accuracy and consistency, they were advised to consider two strategies. First, conduct measurements within designated times with planned breaks to minimize mental and physical exhaustion. It was strongly recommended to take breaks whenever they felt fatigued, even during these scheduled measurement periods. Second, rotate roles regularly to prevent prolonged focus on the same task. The consistency of measurements within and between examiners was assessed using the ICC with a two-way mixed effects model, focusing on consistency.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 26 and MedCalc 23.2.7. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the degree of canal curvature and observer variability. The interrater and intrarater agreement for each measurement technique were assessed using the two-way mixed effects, consistency, multiple raters/measurements ICC [27, 28] with a 95% confidence interval. ICC values were interpreted as follows: (a) Poor agreement: ICC < 0.5, (b) Moderate agreement: ICC between 0.5 and 0.75, (c) Good agreement: ICC between 0.75 and 0.9, and (d) Excellent agreement: ICC >0.9 [29]. As this study data doesn’t follow a normal distribution, Freidman test, as appropriate distribution-free test was used to compare curvature angles measured by the four methods. The Friedman test is a non-parametric method designed for analyzing related samples when the assumption of normality is not satisfied. Using the Friedman test provides more precise and reliable statistical conclusions in such situations [30]. Pairwise comparisons between root canal curvature methods were performed by Bland-Altman as the method of choice for method comparison [31]. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A 10-degree threshold provides a practical benchmark for distinguishing between minor fluctuations and substantial changes that could have clinical implications. The pairwise analysis shows that the agreement between methods varies. The highest ICC (0.909) were observed between Weine and Luiten, indicating excellent agreement. Comparisons involving Luiten and Hankins & ElDeeb showed moderate agreement and larger mean differences, with some methods differing by over 50°.

Discussion

This study offers a comprehensive comparative evaluation of the methods of root canal curvature assessment proposed by Schneider [21], Weine [23], Luiten et al. [24], and Hankins & ElDeeb [22], with particular emphasis on their reproducibility. The findings showed outstanding consistency (correlation coefficients greater than 0.90) among observers for all methods. This is consistent with the results reported by Hartmann et al. [20] and Zhu et al. [32].

Although the Schneider method demonstrated the lowest level of agreement among the four methods tested, its straightforward approach, as suggested by Zhang [33] and Balani et al. [1], likely contributed to its overall reliability. Importantly, the Schneider method also displayed the most advantageous confidence interval compared to the other evaluated techniques.

Assessment of interrater consistency for the Weine method also revealed excellent agreement. Previous studies have suggested that the Weine method yields more consistent results than other techniques, particularly when image quality is less than ideal. Its straightforwardness, efficiency, and high reliability make it a convenient option for both clinical practice and research [32].

Assessing the agreement of the Luiten method—this method demonstrated the highest level of interrater agreement (ICC = 0.969). This high reliability may be due to its well-defined reference points and segmentation of the canal path into two measurable parts, which reduce interpretation errors in gradually curved canals. Among all methods, Luiten’s had the lowest standard deviation and narrowest confidence interval (0.960–0.976), indicating high consistency across raters and repetitions. Thus, it appears to be the most stable and repeatable method in the present study, making it highly suitable for clinicians daily practice and studies requiring minimized observer bias and enhanced consistency.

Although the Hankins & ElDeeb method demonstrated a high level of agreement (ICC = 0.935), it ranked second in agreement following the Schneider method. Difficulties in accurately delineating the tooth’s long axis with this technique, especially for less experienced examiners, may explain its slightly lower repeatability. Additionally, since this method only assesses the apical portion of the canal and does not consider the curvature in the coronal segment, this limitation could affect the overall agreement results.

In comparison of agreement among the four methods, all methods demonstrated good to excellent interrater agreement. These findings are in line with those of Hartmann et al. [20], who reported excellent agreement across six root curvature measurement methods in an in vitro setting. Similarly, Faraj & Botsioukis [18] reported good agreement among Schneider, Weine, and Pruett methods.

The highest agreement between pairs of methods was found between Weine and Luiten (0.963), followed by Schneider and Weine (0.909). The lowest agreement was observed between Schneider and Hankins & ElDeeb (0.575). The agreement between Schneider and Luiten (0.876), and Weine and Hankins & ElDeeb (0.721), was considered good.

When comparing these results with previous studies, the findings are consistent with those of Hartmann et al. [20] and Nagmood et al. [34], which also reported excellent reliability among quantitative root curvature measurement methods. Notably, both previous studies were laboratory-based and performed on extracted teeth, while the present study was conducted clinically using radiographic images. Strict inclusion criteria, such as clear visibility of the canal from orifice to apical foramen, ensured high-quality imaging in our study, thereby reducing error in identifying reference points and drawing lines, ultimately improving reproducibility and agreement.

In the study by Gunday et al. [19], a strong correlation (r = 0.83) was reported between Schneider and Weine methods. In the present study, agreement between these two methods was 0.909—slightly higher and consistent with their findings. However, Gunday’s study was limited to 100 mandibular molars, which reduced the anatomical variability of the sample [19]. In contrast, the current study included both maxillary and mandibular molars (150 cases), offering a broader range of curvature patterns. This anatomical diversity can be considered a strength of the present research in validating the reliability of quantitative root curvature measurement methods.

In the study by Zhu et al. [32], the reproducibility of Schneider and Weine methods was tested on premolars. Although positive agreement and repeatability were reported, interpretation of their findings should consider methodological limitations. Compared to molars, premolars have simpler anatomy and wider canals, which may reduce the central positioning accuracy of endodontic files in radiographs. Thus, the favourable results observed in their study might not fully reflect the challenges of measuring curvature in more complex molar canals, as addressed in the present research.

In addition to the above-mentioned points, as demonstrated in the study by Faraj & Boutsionis [18], visual estimation of root canal curvature—still commonly employed by many clinicians—has been shown to be an unreliable approach for curvature assessment. Despite the variability in agreement coefficients among quantitative methods such as those proposed by Schneider, Weine, Luiten, and Hankins & ElDeeb, these techniques provide a far more objective and reliable alternative to visual approximation. In other words, the findings of several studies not only underscore the relatively high reliability of quantitative methods, but also highlight the fact that employing measurable indices can minimize discrepancies arising from subjective interpretation in curvature measurement. From both clinical and research perspectives, the use of such quantitative methods is therefore recommended as a more standardized and dependable substitute for visual assessments.

According to our findings, the Luiten method yielded the highest inter-examiner and intra-examiner agreement among the methods tested, suggesting a high level of reproducibility. At first glance, this might be interpreted as an indication of superior accuracy. However, a more detailed analysis reveals that this method presents certain clinical limitations in cases with specific anatomical configurations. For example, in teeth where the canal extends from the orifice in a relatively straight path but deviates markedly in a mesial or distal direction from the tooth’s longitudinal axis, the Luiten method—due to its lack of a mechanism to detect angular shifts between its defined reference points (from the orifice to 2 mm apically and from the apical foramen to 1 mm coronally)—tends to underestimate the true curvature and may report a nearly zero-degree angle. Since the method focuses only on abrupt angular changes within short segments, rather than evaluating the canal’s overall directional deviation, it fails to accurately reflect the full extent of curvature in cases where the canal follows a continuous curved path. Clinically, this limitation is significant. If sole reliance is placed on the Luiten method, such canals may be mistakenly classified as “straight,” leading to an increased risk of procedural errors, such as furcal perforation, particularly during initial root canal instrumentation, due to improper alignment between the file and the actual canal trajectory.

In contrast, the Hankins & ElDeeb method—which estimates curvature based on the angle between the tooth’s longitudinal axis and a line parallel to the apical third of the canal—demonstrated a lower agreement coefficient compared to Luiten but performed better in detecting overall canal deviation in such cases. This may be due to the fact that the Hankins & ElDeeb method evaluates the angular discrepancy between the global orientation of the tooth and the final trajectory of the canal, rather than focusing solely on abrupt directional changes. As a result, even in the absence of a distinct curvature (i.e., when the canal exhibits a uniform angular deviation), this method still captures a significant angle, thus providing a more realistic reflection of the canal’s true morphology.

Taken together, these findings suggest that although the Luiten method offers advantages in terms of repeatability and ease of application in conventional radiographic imaging, it falls short in detecting clinically relevant deviations such as dilacerations or gradual curvatures. This highlights the importance of using multiple complementary methods [35] or performing comparative assessments—especially in complex clinical scenarios.

In this regard, it is also proposed that a more comprehensive classification of root curvature patterns be developed, based on a combination of curvature location, number of deflection points, and overall angulation relative to the tooth’s long axis. Once standardized, such a classification could serve as a reference for selecting the most appropriate measurement technique for each anatomical type, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment prognosis.

Clinical relevance

In cases of dilacerations, the Hankins & ElDeeb method appears to be the most appropriate due to its ability to effectively capture abrupt changes in the canal’s direction. For curvatures located in the coronal third, both the Luiten and Weine methods demonstrate better suitability, as they incorporate measurements within the coronal segment of the canal. When the curvature is concentrated in the apical third, the Hankins & ElDeeb method again proves to be more effective owing to its sensitivity to deviations in the apical region. However, for canals exhibiting multiple curvatures, none of the evaluated methods is sufficiently capable of accurately representing the entire canal anatomy. Clinicians, particularly general dentists, should be vigilant about this matter, as overlooking it can lead to complications such as perforation risk resulting from misclassification.

In spite of all these recommendations, combining or comparing multiple measurement techniques early—perhaps even during the initial assessment—may improve accuracy and consistency in clinical practice. Early integration of different methods could enable practitioners to cross-validate results, select the most appropriate technique based on initial findings, and enhance overall treatment planning.

Policy-level implications

Given the high reliability and agreement demonstrated by the various measurement methods, it is advisable to integrate standardized root canal curvature measurement techniques, such as the Luiten method, into dental curricula and postgraduate training programs. This integration would ensure that future practitioners are equipped with validated, reliable methods for assessing root canal curvature, ultimately improving diagnostic accuracy and treatment outcomes. Additionally, continuing education courses for practicing endodontists could emphasize the importance of selecting measurement techniques with demonstrated high agreement, fostering consistency across clinical settings. Establishing guidelines that endorse specific, evidence-based measurement methods may also promote standardization and reduce variability in endodontic care.

Limitations

This research has some limitations. One of the primary limitations of this study pertains to the type of radiographs employed. Due to the inherent two-dimensional nature of PA imaging, it was not possible to visualize the buccolingual curvature of root canals. Another limitation was the dependency of measured values on observer proficiency; although repeated measurements helped improve data reliability, observer fatigue due to the large number of samples potentially influenced the outcomes. Furthermore, the inability to compare the results with gold-standard imaging methods such as CBCT constitutes another notable limitation. The use of convenience sampling and retrospective selection may introduce selection bias. Furthermore, considering the numerous inclusion criteria in the study, the generalizability of the results may have been limited.

Given that all the evaluated methods in this study require visualization of the apical third of the root canal, extra caution should be taken when applying them to maxillary molars. In the present study, this limitation contributed to the unequal number of samples between maxillary and mandibular molars (123 mandibular molars vs. 27 maxillary molars).

Since the data distribution in this study did not follow a normal distribution, and the assumption of equal variances was confirmed at the 0.01 significance level, the results should be approached with caution.

Recommendation

We suggest conducting subgroup analyses by observer type in future research to better understand how different levels of experience or training influence measurement reliability and agreement.

For future studies, it is advisable to use images where the root canals are clearly visible, with sufficient contrast. Measurements should be taken in increments of twenty to reduce observer fatigue. Additionally, it is preferable to analyze images with a minimum resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels in a controlled, dimly lit environment.

Conclusion

The four methods evaluated for assessing root canal curvature in first and second molars demonstrated excellent inter and intrarater agreement in this study. Pairwise comparisons showed excellent agreement between Weine and Luiten, Schneider and Weine, and good agreement between Schneider and Luiten as well as between Weine and Hankins & ElDeeb. Conversely, the greatest discrepancies were observed when comparing Schneider versus Hankins & ElDeeb and Luiten versus Hankins & ElDeeb.

Overall, based on the literature review and the results of the present study, it can be concluded that quantitative methods for measuring root canal curvature angles are significantly superior to visual estimation. However, due to the differences among the quantitative methods themselves and their inherent limitations in precisely estimating root canal curvature, a multifaceted clinical approach is essential. This should involve integrating data obtained from multiple measurement techniques [35] along with three-dimensional imaging assessments and direct clinical observations.

Furthermore, it is recommended that appropriate guidelines and training programs be developed and implemented to promote and educate general dentists and dental students on the use of quantitative measurement methods.
