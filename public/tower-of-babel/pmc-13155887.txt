Female genital mutilation or cutting
Authors: Chia-Yuan Chang, Jacquelyn J Cragg
Published: 2026

Female genital mutilation or cutting (FGM/C) affects more than 230 million females of all ages worldwide, including an estimated 58 000 in Canada1

The World Health Organization defines FGM/C as “all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or injury to the female genital organs for nonmedical reasons.”2 This practice is common in more than 30 countries, particularly Egypt and Sudan, and in sub-Saharan Africa.

There are several distinct types of FGM/C, with differing health implications

Clinicians should screen for FGM/C using culturally sensitive language (e.g., “Have you ever had a procedure done to your genital area?”).2,3 Understanding the type (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.251645/tab-related-content) informs anticipatory guidance, obstetric planning, and potential need for surgical intervention such as deinfibulation.2 Type I and II are most common (80% to 85%), whereas type III accounts for approximately 10%.2 Documentation should use “FGM/C,” including type, and the patient’s preferred terminology (e.g., “circumcision,” “cut,” or “closed”).

Female genital mutilation or cutting is illegal in Canada, and cases involving minors should be reported

The Criminal Code of Canada prohibits performing or aiding FGM/C and removing a child from the country to be cut.3 Reporting to child protection services is mandatory when a person younger than 18 years has been recently subjected to FGM/C, or faces imminent risk.3 Acute pain, hemorrhage, infection, or urinary retention may indicate recent procedures.4 Mentions of special ceremonies or preparing a child for marriage during extended travel raises reasonable concern for planned FGM/C and should warrant gentle, open-ended inquiry.5

These procedures are associated with urinary, gynecologic, and psychological complications

Clinicians should inquire about signs and symptoms of complications, as 10% to 30% of people with FGM/C report dysmenorrhea, 10% to 20% dyspareunia, and roughly 29% have urinary symptoms.3 Information should be given on sexual and obstetric consequences, posttraumatic stress disorder counselling, infectious disease testing, and gynecologic subspecialty referral when appropriate.4

Patients who have had FGI/M are at increased risk of obstetric complications

Patients may have a narrowed introitus and recurrent urinary infections, which may complicate pregnancy and make vaginal examination difficult.3 Those with infibulation are at higher risk of prolonged labour, perineal tears, postpartum hemorrhage, and cesarean delivery.6 Antenatal care should include birth planning, counselling on deinfibulation, and referral to experienced obstetric providers.6
