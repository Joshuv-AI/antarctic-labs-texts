QFT on Fuzzy AdS Spaces: Classical Limit and Boundary Correlation Functions
Bojana Brkić, Ilija Burić, Maja Burić, Dušan - D ord¯ević, and Duško Latas

Abstract
Quantum field theory on two- and three-dimensional fuzzy anti-de Sitter spaces is introduced and studied. We find a complete set of solutions to the fuzzy Klein-Gordon equation and identify the commutative limit in which they reduce to classical scalar field modes. After introducing the noncommutative boundary via semi-classical states, the fuzzy modes are used to obtain the boundary two-point function. In both two and three dimensions, this gives an interesting two-parameter deformation of the conformal two-point function. For the fuzzy AdS 2 , the two-point function is given explicitly in terms of the Appell function F 1 F_{1} .

Introduction

Many attempts to quantise gravity have emerged from theoretical considerations over the past decades. While it is impossible to single out or adopt one approach due to the lack of experimental or observational data, general relativity hints at certain properties that the full quantum theory of gravity should have. In particular, based on the area law of black hole thermodynamics, it has been conjectured that gravity is holographic, meaning that the degrees of freedom related to a spacetime region are encoded at its boundary. The most concrete realisation of this principle through the AdS/CFT correspondence,
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, has led to numerous investigations of classical and quantum fields on anti-de Sitter space.

The original AdS/CFT correspondence related string theory on AdS
{}_{5}\times S^{5}
to
\,\mathcal{N}
=4 super Yang-Mills theory in four dimensions, but it has since been extended to other models of gauge/gravity duality, not necessarily involving string theory. Moreover, even without a concrete model of quantum gravity in mind, one can study asymptotically AdS spacetimes with additional matter content and use the well-developed holographic dictionary to extract different properties of the boundary field-theory duals. This procedure, called the
bottom up
approach, has led to a successful holographic description of several systems in condensed matter physics,
[
4
]
. For example, Einstein’s gravity with a negative cosmological constant, a
U(1)
gauge field and a complex scalar in the bulk was shown to lead to superconductor behaviour in the dual field theory, thus realising a holographic superconductor in
(2+1)
dimensions,
[
5
]
.

Another aspect of quantum gravity that is common to most approaches, is that at sufficiently small length scales the notion of points becomes meaningless, and the spacetime coordinates come to be described by non-commuting operators. The microscopic origin of noncommutativity varies across different models and will not concern us in this work. Instead, our starting point will be a fixed noncommutative (or
quantum
) spacetime, singled out by symmetry and consistency requirements. From there, one can proceed to define and study quantum field theory on this background, hoping that the difference compared to its commutative counterpart captures the leading quantum-gravitational effects.

Apart from its relation to gravity and models of condensed matter physics, anti-de Sitter space can potentially be used as a regulator for ordinary QFT in flat space. This idea, first proposed in
[
6
]
, was recently combined with insights from conformal bootstrap,
[
7
]
, to give a modern take on the S-matrix bootstrap, with promising initial results,
[
8
]
. Similar questions have also been explored in the noncommutative context. In particular,
[
9
]
studied the potential of the fuzzy sphere,
[
10
]
, to serve as a regulator for quantum field theory on the Moyal-Weyl plane.

With the above various roles of AdS spaces in mind, in this work we will initiate the study of quantum field theory on the
fuzzy
anti-de Sitter space. For simplicity, we restrict our attention to two and three dimensions. Among various approaches to noncommutative geometry, fuzzy AdS spaces that we use are constructed within the frame formalism of
[
11
]
. The basic idea of the formalism, in short, is the quantisation rule that replaces coordinates
and
elements of a moving frame of some classical manifold by operators on a Hilbert space. This procedure is to respect the classical frame relations, which are reproduced by quantum commutators. Along with a number of quantum spaces that have been analysed within this approach, starting with the fuzzy sphere, the formalism has recently been used to formulate noncommutative versions of de Sitter and anti-de Sitter spaces in various dimensions,
[
12

13
,
14

One of the benefits of working in the frame formalism is that it allows for a natural definition of classical and quantum field theory, giving a mathematical framework to study field quantisation and renormalisation on curved noncommutative spaces. The main objectives of this work are, 1) to determine the complete set of free field modes on fuzzy AdS
2
and AdS
3

The relation between NC geometry and AdS/CFT is not by itself a new one. There have been several different proposals for noncommutative versions of (A)dS, and within some, dynamics of quantum fields in the bulk and/or on the boundary was studied. In

Perhaps the main difference of ours compared to the above approaches lies in the use of semi-classical states, which provide us with notions of classical geometry such as points, regions and the spacetime boundary. In addition, spacetime coordinates, scalar fields etc. are operators on a Hilbert space rather than elements of an abstract algebra. The boundary-two point function for the fuzzy AdS

Summary of results

Let us now describe the results of the present work in more details. We begin in Section
2
by defining fuzzy AdS
d+1
spaces, with the focus on two and three dimensions (
d=1,2
). Our approach uses several well established constructions, however put together in a very particular way. As already mentioned, the spacetime coordinates are defined as operators acting in a unitary irreducible representation of the symmetry group
SO(d,2)
. They are accompanied by momenta, another set of operators, so as to form a noncommutative frame in the sense of
[
11
]
. The commutation relations in the algebra are used to define differential and tensor calculi, which in turn suffice for the definition of classical field theory. While one of the advantages of the frame formalism is a natural treatment of gauge fields, we will focus on scalar field theory. In the two-dimensional case, the above part of our construction mostly coincides with the
h
-deformed Lobachevsky plane,
[
18
,
11
]
, upon Wick-rotating to Lorentzian signature. However, in contrast to
[
18
,
11
]
, we will insist that coordinates and momenta are not elements of an abstract algebra, but operators in a certain discrete series representation of
SO(d,2)
, labelled by a negative integer
l
. Therefore, the fuzzy space will be specified by two parameters,
l
and the constant of noncommutativity
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
. An immediate further observation is that the fuzzy AdS
3
comes with more degrees of freedom than its commutative counterpart, which organise into an
internal
space
S^{1}
. The consistency of this fact with the existence of a commutative limit is discussed in detail in later sections.

The second principal ingredient that we make use of are the so-called semi-classical states. These are defined similarly to generalised coherent states in the sense of Perelomov,
[
26
]
, i.e. as translates of a certain reference state by group elements. In our case, the reference state is the lowest-weight vector of the discrete series representation and translations are performed by means of (exponentials of) momenta. Semi-classical states are used to define the notion of local measurements and therefore make contact with ordinary, commutative geometry.

In contrast to some approaches, we do not use the semi-classical states to define a quantisation map.

Sections
3
and
4
are dedicated to the analysis of solutions to the Klein-Gordon equation on the fuzzy AdS
2
and AdS
3
, respectively. We use the method advocated in
[
25
]
, that can be applied to any noncommutative space of Lie algebra type and reduces the eigenvalue problem of the fuzzy Laplacian, originally an operator equation, to a partial differential equation in ordinary, commutative variables. For AdS spaces, the resulting differential equation is completely integrable and it is straightforward to write down a complete set of eigenfunctions, that is, eigenoperators. In order to understand properties of these solutions, we will study the expectation values of the eigenoperators (i.e., field modes) in semi-classical states. These can be computed exactly, see equations (
57
) and (
77
) for AdS
2
and AdS
3
, respectively. Remarkably, in the limit
|l|\gg{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{-1}\gg 1
, these expressions reduce precisely to the commutative field modes. This fact will motivate us to identify the latter as the classical limit. In three dimensions, the fuzzy modes carry three quantum numbers, one more than the commutative ones. However, in semi-classical states, the dependence on the additional quantum number trivialises and one-parameter families of noncommutative solutions behave as the same classical solution.

The complete set of field modes paves the way towards the definition and analysis of quantum field theory. In Section
5
, we consider the simplest such theory, namely the free massive scalar. We shall follow the standard rules of quantisation and demand that creation and annihilation operators corresponding to field modes satisfy canonical commutation relations, thus generating the usual Fock space. After defining correlation functions and establishing the Wick theorem, we proceed to the main subject of the section – the computation of the boundary two-point function. The latter is computed using the modes of Sections
3
and
4
, and we again write its expectation values in semi-classical states. In two dimensions, the result can be written exactly in terms of standard special functions,

G_{\partial\partial}=\frac{2^{-1-2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\Gamma({{\mathbb{\Delta}}}-2l)^{2}}{\Gamma(-2l)^{2}\Gamma\left({{\mathbb{\Delta}}}+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\left(\frac{1}{-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\right)^{2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\,F_{1}\left(2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}},{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}-2l,{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}-2l,2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}+1;\frac{i\langle\hat{t}_{1}\rangle}{l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}},\frac{-i\langle\hat{t}_{2}\rangle}{l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\right)\ .

(1)

Here,
{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}
is the conformal dimension of the fundamental field
\phi
, related to its mass in the usual way,
\langle\hat{t}_{i}\rangle
are expectation values of the time coordinate in aforementioned semi-classical states and
F_{1}
denotes the Appell function. We show that in the limit
|l|\gg{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{-1}\gg 1

1
) reduces to the usual conformal two-point function. In three dimensions, we do not have a closed form expression for the two-point function such as (

We believe that the above results are a promising start and warrant further investigations of quantum field theory on fuzzy AdS spaces: some of the future directions are discussed in the concluding section. Among these is the analysis of symmetries. Namely, while our construction of fuzzy AdS spaces is heavily based on representation theory of the group
SO(d,2)
, the latter is not a symmetry of these spaces. This is also reflected in the boundary correlator (

Other topics discussed in the last section include extended families of localised states and their relation to internal degrees of freedom, the inclusion of interactions and extensions to higher dimensions and/or spaces of positive curvature.

Fuzzy AdS bulk in two and three dimensions

In this section, we review the material necessary for the construction of scalar field theories on the fuzzy AdS
2
and AdS
3

2.1
Scalar fields on AdS

We will mostly study the AdS
d+1
space using Poincaré coordinates
({\tt z},{\tt x}^{\mu})
in which the metric reads

\mathrm{d}s^{2}=\ell_{\text{\tiny{AdS}}}^{2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}{\tt z}^{2}+\eta_{\mu\nu}\mathrm{d}{\tt x}^{\mu}\mathrm{d}{\tt x}^{\nu}}{{\tt z}^{2}}\,,\qquad\mu,\nu=0,\dots,d-1\ .

(2)

Throughout this work, we shall set the AdS radius to
\ell_{\text{\tiny{AdS}}}=1
. The Minkowski boundary sits at
\{{\tt z}=0\}
and we will write coordinates on it as
({\tt x}^{\mu})=({\tt t},{\tt x}^{i})
. The metric
\eta_{\mu\nu}
is of mostly-positive signature.

Our main object of study is quantum field theory of a real scalar on the fixed AdS background. The field, denoted
\phi
, is expanded in modes of the free theory. The latter satisfy the Klein-Gordon equation

\nabla^{2}\phi=M^{2}\phi\equiv\mathbb{\Delta}(\mathbb{\Delta}-d)\,\phi\,,\qquad\mathbb{\Delta}=\frac{d}{2}+\sqrt{M^{2}+\frac{d^{2}}{4}}\equiv\frac{d}{2}+\nu\ .

(3)

We have denoted the mass of the field by
M
and the conformal dimension of the corresponding field on the boundary by
{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}
. For future convenience, we have also defined the quantity
\nu
in the second equation. In Poincaré coordinates, the Laplacian takes the form

\nabla^{2}={\tt z}^{2}\left(\partial_{{\tt z}}^{2}-\partial_{{\tt t}}^{2}+\partial_{{\tt x}^{i}}\partial_{{\tt x}_{i}}\right)-(d-1)\,{\tt z}\partial_{{\tt z}}\ .

(4)

It commutes with translations in
{\tt t}
and
{\tt x}^{i}
, leading to eigenfunctions

f({\tt z},{\tt t},{\tt x}^{i})=e^{-i(\omega{\tt t}-k_{i}{\tt x}^{i})}{\tt z}^{\frac{d}{2}}\left(c_{1}J_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{\omega^{2}-k^{i}k_{i}}\,{\tt z}\right)+c_{2}Y_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{\omega^{2}-k^{i}k_{i}}\,{\tt z}\right)\right)\ .

(5)

The two linearly independent solutions behave near the boundary as
{\tt z}^{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}
and
{\tt z}^{d-{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}
, respectively. For most of this work, we will use the first solution, and denote it by
\phi_{\omega,k}
. The normalisation constant
c_{1}
in two and three dimensions is given in Appendix
A
.

The propagator on AdS
d+1
reads, see e.g.
[
27
]
,

G_{bb}^{\text{comm}}({\tt z}_{1},{\tt x}_{1};{\tt z}_{2},{\tt x}_{2})=\frac{C_{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}{\zeta^{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\ _{2}F_{1}\left({{\mathbb{\Delta}}},{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}-\frac{d-1}{2};2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}-d+1;-\frac{4}{\zeta}\right)\,,

(6)

where
\zeta
is the chordal distance between the pair of points
({\tt z}_{1},{\tt x}_{1})
and
({\tt z}_{2},{\tt x}_{2})
. In Poincaré coordinates,

\zeta=\frac{\eta_{\mu\nu}({\tt x}_{1}-{\tt x}_{2})^{\mu}({\tt x}_{1}-{\tt x}_{2})^{\nu}+({\tt z}_{1}-{\tt z}_{2})^{2}}{{\tt z}_{1}{\tt z}_{2}}\ ,

(7)

and the normalisation constant
C_{\mathbb{\Delta}}
is given by

C_{\mathbb{\Delta}}=\frac{\Gamma(\mathbb{\Delta})}{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\mathbb{\Delta}-\frac{d}{2}+1\right)}\ .

(8)

In Appendix
A
we review how the propagator (
6
) arises as an integral over the field modes (
5
). In this work, we will be particularly interested in correlation functions on the boundary. In the boundary limit the propagator reduces to the (prefactor times the) conformal two-point function

G_{bb}^{\text{comm}}({\tt z}_{1},{\tt x}_{1};{\tt z}_{2},{\tt x}_{2})\to({\tt z}_{1}{\tt z}_{2})^{{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\,\frac{C_{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}{({\tt x}_{1}-{\tt x}_{2})^{2{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}}\equiv({\tt z}_{1}{\tt z}_{2})^{{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}\,G^{\text{comm}}_{\partial\partial}({\tt x}_{1},{\tt x}_{2})\ .

(9)

The last equality defines the two-point function
G^{\text{comm}}_{\partial\partial}({\tt x}_{1},{\tt x}_{2})
, which, notice, we take
not
to be unit-normalised. This way of obtaining correlation functions of the boundary theory is in line with the extrapolate dictionary,
[
28
]
. In the commutative case, it gives the same form of the two-point function of operators dual to the scalar field as the standard Gubser-Klebanov-Polyakov-Witten dictionary,
[
2
,
3
]
, but it does not require computations using the (Euclidean) action.

2.2
Fuzzy AdS
2

Classically, gravity can be described as geometry of a Riemann or a Riemann-Cartan space. In the tetrad formulation, which is for many purposes in physics more fundamental, the basic field is the
moving frame

{\tt e}_{\alpha}={\tt e}_{\alpha}^{\mu}({\tt x})\partial_{\mu}
, where
\{{\tt x}^{\mu}\}
denote coordinates,
\alpha=0,\dots,
are frame indices and
\mu=0,\dots,d
are coordinate indices. The metric is locally flat, that is in frame indices
\,{\tt g}_{\alpha\beta}=\eta_{\alpha\beta}
. In coordinate indices,
\ {\tt g}_{\mu\nu}({\tt x})={\tt e}^{\alpha}_{\mu}({\tt x})\,{\tt e}^{\beta}_{\nu}({\tt x})\,\eta_{\alpha\beta}\,
, where
{\tt e}^{\alpha}_{\mu}({\tt x})
is the matrix inverse of the frame
{\tt e}_{\alpha}^{\mu}({\tt x})
. Other characteristics of a Riemann-Cartan space, like connection and torsion can be introduced.

The basic assumption of the NC frame formalism is that quantisation of a curved space is done in a locally flat frame, that is, that vector fields
{\tt e}_{\alpha}
are quantised as noncommutative derivations
\,e_{\alpha}=[{\hat{p}}_{\alpha},\ \ ]
. This corresponds to the following quantisation prescription

{\tt e}_{\alpha}^{\mu}({\tt x})={\tt e}_{\alpha}{\tt x}^{\mu}\ \mapsto\ e_{\alpha}^{\mu}({\hat{x}})=[{\hat{p}}_{\alpha},{\hat{x}}^{\mu}]\ ,

(10)

and introduces momenta
{\hat{p}}_{\alpha}
as a noncommutative version of the frame. Relations in the momentum algebra mostly determine differential geometry of a specific NC space: for example, if momenta form a Lie algebra, the corresponding noncommutative space is of constant curvature (assuming, in addition, a torsionless connection),
[
11
]
.

We will not discuss further properties of the NC frame formalism, nor shall we explain the details of the construction of fuzzy spaces that we use: the fuzzy AdS
3
was introduced in
[
14
]
, while fuzzy AdS
2
is defined in complete analogy with fuzzy dS
2
,
[
25
]
.

The simplest way to quantise AdS
2
space is in Poincaré coordinates. We use the following quantisation of coordinates and momenta:

\displaystyle\hat{z}=i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}E_{+}\,,\qquad{\hat{p}}_{z}=H\,,

(11)

\displaystyle\hat{t}=-i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}H\,,\qquad{\hat{p}}_{t}=E_{+}\ ,

(12)

where
H,E_{\pm}
are generators of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, acting in a certain discrete series representation
T_{l}^{-}
, with
l
a negative integer or half-integer. Our conventions regarding the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and its representations are collected in Appendix
B
. Using the realisation of the discrete series written there, the coordinates and momenta assume the form of differential operators

\displaystyle\hat{z}={\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\xi\,,\hskip 113.81102pt{\hat{p}}_{z}=\xi\partial_{\xi}+l+1\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\hat{t}=-i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\left(\xi\partial_{\xi}+l+1\right)\,,\hskip 45.23978pt{\hat{p}}_{t}=-i\xi\,,

(14)

that act on functions
f(\xi)
of a positive real variable
\xi\,
. Finally,
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
is a constant, referred to as the constant of noncommutativity.

Differential and pseudo-Riemannian geometry of the fuzzy AdS
2
is encoded in coordinate-momentum and momentum-momentum commutation relations. The former, i.e. the frame relations

[{\hat{p}}_{z},\hat{z}]=\hat{z}\,,\qquad[\hat{p}_{t},\hat{t}]=\hat{z}\,,

(15)

mean that the momenta
\{{\hat{p}}_{z},{\hat{p}}_{t}\}
are to be thought of as noncommutative analogues of the classical vector fields

{\tt e}_{z}={\tt z}\partial_{{\tt z}}\,,\qquad{\tt e}_{t}={\tt z}\partial_{{\tt t}}\ .

(16)

In the following, we will study eigenfunctions of the scalar Laplace-Beltrami operator, which reads

\Delta\phi=[{\hat{p}}_{z},[{\hat{p}}_{z},\phi]]-[{\hat{p}}_{z},\phi]-[{\hat{p}}_{t},[{\hat{p}}_{t},\phi]]\ .

(17)

The Laplacian (
17
) is uniquely specified by the rules of frame formalism. Let us, however, mention that some other versions of the Laplace operator have also been considered in the literature. A common choice that was studied in related models,
[
22
,
29
]
, is to drop the linear term
[{\hat{p}}_{z},\phi]
from (
17
). We will call the resulting operator the
d’Alembertian
and denote it by
\Box\,
. A comparison of the Laplacian and the d’Alembertian in the case of the fuzzy de Sitter spaces was done in
[
25
]
, where it was found that their eigenfunctions are related by multiplication by a simple factor. Also,
\Delta
differs from the quadratic Casimir of
\mathfrak{so}(2,1)
that was used e.g. in
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
– in fact, its centraliser in
SO(2,1)
is trivial. For reasons to be explained in subsequent sections, we shall use the Laplacian (
17
) for the remainder of this work.

Note that our definition of the fuzzy AdS
2
involves two parameters,
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
and
l
. The latter characterises the discrete series representation
T_{l}^{-}
. Looking ahead, the classical AdS space arises upon taking
|l|
to be large and
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
to be small. This will be elaborated in great detail in Sections

and

.

The notion of local measurements on the fuzzy AdS
2
is established through
semi-classical states
. The latter are states
|{\tt x}\rangle
in the Hilbert space, labelled by points of the classical spacetime, such that for a local observable
\mathcal{O}
with the noncommutative analogue
\mathcal{\hat{O}}
, the value
\mathcal{O}({\tt x})
is approximated by
\langle{\tt x}|\mathcal{\hat{O}}|{\tt x}\rangle
. In the case at hand, the semi-classical states are generated by acting with (exponentials of) momenta on the lowest-weight vector
|\Psi_{0}\rangle\,
in the Hilbert space, see Appendix
B
,

|\lambda,c\rangle\equiv\lambda^{-\hat{p}_{z}}\,e^{-c\hat{p}_{t}}\,|\Psi_{0}\rangle\ .

(18)

The lowest-weight vector is the solution to equations
\tilde{E}_{-}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=0
,
\tilde{H}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-l|\Psi_{0}\rangle
. In our realisation of the discrete series, it is given by the function

\Psi_{0}(\xi)=\frac{\xi^{-2l-1}e^{-\xi}}{2^{2l+1/2}\sqrt{\pi\Gamma(-2l)}}\equiv N\xi^{-2l-1}e^{-\xi}\ .

(19)

Expectation values of coordinates in the lowest-weight state are

\langle\Psi_{0}|{\hat{z}}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\,,\qquad\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{t}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=0\ ,

(20)

and therefore this state corresponds to the classical point
({\tt z}_{0},{\tt t}_{0})=(-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk},0)
. Expectation values of coordinates in other semi-classical states follow by the frame relations (
15
) and the Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff formula and read

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,c\,|\hat{z}|\lambda,c\rangle=\lambda\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\lambda\,,

(21)

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,c\,|\hat{t}|\lambda,c\rangle=\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{t}+c\hat{z}|\Psi_{0}\rangle=-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}c\ .

(22)

These expectation values are nothing else but the coordinates of the classical point obtained from
({\tt z}_{0},{\tt t}_{0})
by a flow along the integral curves of
{\tt e}_{{\tt z}}
and
{\tt e}_{{\tt t}}
,

\lambda^{{\tt e}_{z}}\,e^{c{\tt e}_{t}}\cdot({\tt z}_{0},{\tt t}_{0})=(\lambda{\tt z}_{0},{\tt t}_{0}+c{\tt z}_{0})=(-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\lambda,-l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}c)\ .

(23)

In our realisation of the discrete series, the semi-classical states are

\langle\xi|\lambda,c\rangle=N\,\lambda^{l}\,\xi^{-2l-1}\,e^{-\frac{1-ic}{\lambda}\,\xi}\,,\qquad N=\left(2\pi\,2^{4l}\,\Gamma(-2l)\right)^{-1/2}\ .

(24)

From here, one can also see that the semi-classical states satisfy the completeness relation

\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}c\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\lambda}\,|\lambda,c\rangle\langle\lambda,c|\equiv P=-\frac{4\pi}{2l+1}\,I\ ,

(25)

where
I
denotes the identity operator. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\langle\xi|P|\xi^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=N^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lambda^{2l-1}(\xi\xi^{\prime})^{-2l-1}e^{-\frac{\xi+\xi^{\prime}}{\lambda}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}c\,e^{\frac{i(\xi-\xi^{\prime})}{\lambda}c}

(26)

\displaystyle=2\pi N^{2}\xi^{-4l-2}\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lambda^{2l}e^{-\frac{2\xi}{\lambda}}=-\frac{2^{-2l+1}}{2l+1}\xi^{-2l-1}\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\ .

The prefactor multiplying the delta function arises due to the fact that states
|\xi\rangle
are not normalised to
\delta
-function, see Appendix
B
. Comparing the last equation with the inner product (

) in the discrete series gives the resolution of the identity (
25
). The overlaps of the semi-classical states are

\langle\lambda,c\,|\lambda^{\prime},c^{\prime}\rangle=\left(\frac{\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+i(c\lambda^{\prime}-c^{\prime}\lambda)}{2\sqrt{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}}\right)^{2l}\ .

(27)

These are all the properties of the semi-classical states that we will need in the following sections. For a general discussion similar generalised coherent states in the context of group theory, see
[
26
]
, and
[
30
]
more specifically in noncommutative geometry.

2.3
Fuzzy AdS
3

A model of the fuzzy AdS
3
which satisfies the axioms of the frame formalism was proposed in
[
14
]
. As in two dimensions, coordinates and momenta are constructed using generators of the classical isometry group acting in some irreducible representation. The isometry group of AdS
3
is locally isomorphic to
SO(2,2)\sim SL(2,\mathbb{R})\times SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and we denote its generators by
\{H,E_{\pm}\}
and
\{\bar{H},\bar{E}_{\pm}\}
. The bared generators commute with unbared ones and both sets satisfy the usual
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
brackets. Following
[
14
]
3
3

3

More precisely, our coordinate generators have the opposite sign compared to
[
14
]
.
, we put

\displaystyle{\hat{z}}=-2i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{E_{+}\bar{E}_{+}}\,,\hskip 165.02606pt{\hat{p}}_{z}=H+\bar{H}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle{\hat{x}}=i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{\bar{E}_{+}}{E_{+}}}\,\left(H-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)+\sqrt{\dfrac{E_{+}}{\bar{E}_{+}}}\,\left(\bar{H}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\right)\,,\qquad{\hat{p}}_{x}=E_{+}+\bar{E}_{+}\,,

(29)

\displaystyle{\hat{t}}=i{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{\bar{E}_{+}}{E_{+}}}\,\left(H-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)-\sqrt{\dfrac{E_{+}}{\bar{E}_{+}}}\,\left(\bar{H}-\dfrac{1}{4}\right)\right)\,,\qquad{\hat{p}}_{t}=E_{+}-\bar{E}_{+}\ .

(30)

The generators act as operators in the representation
T_{l}^{-}\otimes\bar{T}_{l}^{-}
of
\mathfrak{so}(2,2)
. In particular, properties of the discrete series representations ensure that
E_{+},\bar{E}_{+}
are positive operators and thus their positive square roots may be defined. Explicitly, using the realisation of the discrete series detailed in Appendix
B
, we substitute in the above expressions

H=\xi\partial_{\xi}+l+1\,,\quad E_{+}=-i\xi\,,\qquad\bar{H}=\bar{\xi}\partial_{\bar{\xi}}+l+1\,,\quad\bar{E}_{+}=-i\bar{\xi}\,,

(31)

to turn coordinates and momenta into differential operators that act on functions
f(\xi,\bar{\xi})
. More generally, we could have allowed for different parameters
l
and
\bar{l}
for the discrete series representations of the two
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
factors. In the following we will stick to the simplest choice
l=\bar{l}
, made above.

The discussion of differential geometry and semi-classical states from the previous subsection carries to the present case in the obvious way. The action of the Laplacian on scalars reads

\Delta\phi=[{\hat{p}}_{z},[{\hat{p}}_{z},\phi]]-2[{\hat{p}}_{z},\phi]-[{\hat{p}}_{t},[{\hat{p}}_{t},\phi]]+[{\hat{p}}_{x},[{\hat{p}}_{x},\phi]]\ .

(32)

As in two dimensions, the Laplacian breaks AdS symmetry – the centraliser of
\Delta
in
SO(2,2)
is the one-dimensional subgroup generated by
H-\bar{H}
. The semi-classical states are given by

|\lambda,b,c\rangle=\lambda^{-\hat{p}_{z}}e^{-b\hat{p}_{x}}e^{-c\hat{p}_{t}}|\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}\rangle\,,

(33)

where
|\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}\rangle
is the lowest-weight vector in the representation space. Explicitly, as functions of
\xi
and
\bar{\xi}
, these states are

\langle\xi,\bar{\xi}|\lambda,b,c\rangle=N^{2}\lambda^{2l}\left(\xi\bar{\xi}\right)^{-2l-1}e^{\frac{i}{\lambda}\,\left(\xi(b+c+i)+\bar{\xi}(b-c+i)\right)}\,,

(34)

where the normalisation constant
N
was introduced in (

The overlaps are

\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\lambda^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\rangle=\left(\frac{\left(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+i\,((b-c)\lambda^{\prime}-(b^{\prime}-c^{\prime})\lambda)\right)\left(\lambda+\lambda^{\prime}+i\,((b+c)\lambda^{\prime}-(b^{\prime}+c^{\prime})\lambda)\right)}{4\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\right)^{2l}\ .

Expectation values of coordinates in semi-classical states are determined as in two dimensions. In the lowest-weight state, we find

\langle\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}|{\hat{z}}|\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}\rangle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-2l\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(-2l)^{2}}\,{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\,,\quad\langle\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{t}|\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}\rangle=0=\langle\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}|\hat{x}|\Psi_{0}\otimes\bar{\Psi}_{0}\rangle\ .

From here, the frame relations lead to expectation values in general states

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{z}|\lambda,b,c\rangle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-2l\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(-2l)^{2}}\,{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\lambda\,,

(35)

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{t}|\lambda,b,c\rangle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-2l\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(-2l)^{2}}\,{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}c\,,

(36)

\displaystyle\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{x}|\lambda,b,c\rangle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-2l\right)^{2}}{\Gamma(-2l)^{2}}\,{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}b\ .

(37)

We will simply write
\langle\hat{z}\rangle
,
\langle\hat{t}\rangle
,
\langle\hat{x}\rangle
, if the semi-classical state in question is understood. In the next sections, we will use the asymptotic form of these expressions for large
|l|
,

\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{z}|\lambda,b,c\rangle\sim-2l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\lambda\,,\quad\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{t}|\lambda,b,c\rangle\sim-2l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}c\,,\quad\langle\lambda,b,c\,|\hat{x}|\lambda,b,c\rangle\sim-2l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}b\ .

(38)

The main difference between two- and three-dimensional fuzzy AdS spaces is that the latter contains
internal
degrees of freedom not present in the commutative case. A quick, non-rigorous, way to appreciate this fact is by counting variables that are used to characterise fuzzy functions. For the fuzzy AdS
2
, the Hilbert space of the discrete series representation
T_{l}^{-}
is made up of single-variable functions
f(\xi)
. Consequently, the operators
\mathcal{\hat{O}}
on this space can be thought of as functions of two variables, the operator’s matrix elements
\langle\xi|\mathcal{\hat{O}}|\xi^{\prime}\rangle
. This matches the dimension of the commutative space. In the case of the fuzzy AdS
3
, the Hilbert space consists of two-variable functions
f(\xi,\bar{\xi})
and thus the operators are represented by functions of four variables
\langle\xi,\bar{\xi}|\mathcal{\hat{O}}|\xi^{\prime},\bar{\xi}^{\prime}\rangle
, one more than the classical dimension. In Sections
4
and
5
, it is exhibited how these additional degrees of freedom are consistent with the existence of a commutative limit.

3
Scalar field theory on the fuzzy AdS
2

In this section, we will solve the Klein-Gordon equation on the fuzzy AdS
2
and analyse properties of the solutions. In the first subsection, a complete set of free field modes is determined. In the second, we find expectation values of these modes in semi-classical states. It is shown that in the
limit
l\to-\infty
the properly normalised modes reduce to their commutative counterparts.

3.1
Solutions to the noncommutative Klein-Gordon equation

The aim of this subsection is to determine a complete set of eigenfunctions of the fuzzy Laplacian (
17
). This requires solving an equation for operator-valued functions, for which no general solution methods are available. In order to proceed, one may try to choose noncommutative coordinates in which the equation simplifies – such an approach was used for the fuzzy dS
2
and dS
4
in
[
25
]
to reduce the fuzzy Klein-Gordon equation to its commutative analogue. In this work, we will follow a different path, also introduced in
[
25
]
, which allows to write, for any noncommutative space of Lie algebra type, the fuzzy Klein-Gordon equation as a partial differential equation in ordinary variables. In addition, for AdS spaces the Laplace operator admits sufficiently many first integrals, allowing for the complete solution theory.

Recall that the algebra of functions on the fuzzy AdS
2
is given by

\mathcal{A}=\text{End}(\mathcal{H}_{l})\cong T_{l}^{-}\otimes(T_{l}^{-})^{\ast}\cong T_{l}^{-}\otimes(T_{l}^{-})^{B}\ .

(44)

In the last step, we used the fact that taking the dual of a discrete series representation is equivalent to applying the Bargmann automorphism, see Appendix
B
. We shall realise the first factor in (
44
) according to Appendix
B
, by functions of the variable
\xi\equiv\xi_{L}
; for the second factor, the variable is denoted by
\xi_{R}
. Putting everything together and using properties (
125
) of the Bargmann automorphism, we can think of
\mathcal{A}
as consisting of two-variable functions
f(\xi_{L},\xi_{R})
and write the momenta as differential operators

\text{ad}_{{\hat{p}}_{z}}=\xi_{L}\partial_{\xi_{L}}+\xi_{R}\partial_{\xi_{R}}+2l+2\,,\qquad\text{ad}_{{\hat{p}}_{t}}=-i\left(\xi_{L}-\xi_{R}\right)\ .

(45)

The expression for the Laplacian (
17
) as a differential operator in variables
\,(\xi_{L},\xi_{R})
directly follows. To find its eigenfunctions, we introduce polar coordinates
(\rho,\varphi)
through

\xi_{L}=\rho\sin\varphi\,,\quad\xi_{R}=\rho\cos\varphi\,,\qquad\rho\in(0,\infty)\,,\ \ \varphi\in\left(0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\ .

(46)

The range for the angle
\varphi
comes form the fact that both
\xi_{L}
and
\xi_{R}
are positive. This fact will come to play an important role in the following. Let us look for eigenfunctions of
\Delta
of the form
\phi(\rho,\varphi)=\rho^{-2(l+1)}g(\rho,\varphi)
. In terms of
g(\rho,\varphi)
, the Klein-Gordon equation reads

\rho^{2}\left(\partial_{\rho}^{2}+2\cos\left(\varphi+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)^{2}\right)g=M^{2}g\ .

(47)

Since the equation does not involve derivatives with respect to
\,\varphi
, we may consider solutions of the form
g(\rho,\varphi)=f(\rho)\,\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{0})
. Defining

\beta=\sqrt{2}\cos\left(\varphi_{0}+\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\,,\qquad\beta\in(-1,1)\,,

(48)

we obtain the equation for
f(\rho)

\rho^{2}\left(\partial_{\rho}^{2}+\beta^{2}\right)f=M^{2}f\ .

(49)

The solution that is regular at
\rho=0
is

f(\rho)=c_{\beta}\,\sqrt{\rho}\,J_{\nu}\left(\beta\rho\right)\,,

(50)

where
c_{\beta}
is a normalisation constant. We shall denote the corresponding field mode by
\phi_{\beta}
. Plugging back in the prefactors, we get

\phi_{\beta}(\rho,\varphi)=c_{\beta}\,\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{0})\,\rho^{-2l-\frac{3}{2}}\,J_{\nu}\left(\beta\rho\right)\ .

(51)

The coefficient
c_{\beta}
will be fixed in the next subsection by considering the commutative limit. The modes
\{\phi_{\beta}\}
form a complete set of solutions to the fuzzy Klein-Gordon equation.

3.2
Classical limit and boundary behaviour

The physical and geometric properties of modes
\phi_{\beta}
that were found in the previous subsection are obscured by the choice of variables
(\rho,\varphi)
. Had we used another realisation for the discrete series representations, the field modes would have looked very different. The physically relevant properties of
\phi_{\beta}
are revealed by computing their matrix elements between semi-classical states. This is carried out in the remainder of the section.

We denote the expectation values of modes in semi-classical states by

\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle=\left(2^{2l+1}\pi\right)^{2}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}(\xi_{L}\xi_{R})^{2l+1}\mathrm{d}\xi_{L}\,\mathrm{d}\xi_{R}\ \phi_{\beta}(\xi_{L},\xi_{R})\langle\xi_{L}|\lambda,c\rangle\langle\xi_{R}|\lambda,c\rangle^{\ast}\ .

(52)

By plugging in the solutions (
51
) and the explicit form of semi-classical states (
24
), and integrating over the delta function in
\varphi
, the last expression is reduced to

\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle=\frac{2\pi\,\lambda^{2l}c_{\beta}}{\Gamma(-2l)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\rho\,\rho^{-2l-\frac{1}{2}}\,J_{\nu}(\beta\rho)\ e^{-\frac{\rho}{\lambda}\,(\sin\varphi_{0}+\cos\varphi_{0})+\frac{ic\rho}{\lambda}\,(\sin\varphi_{0}-\cos\varphi_{0})}\ .

(53)

To evaluate the integral, we use the formula given in
[
31
]
, section 6.621,

\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}x\,x^{\mu-1}e^{-\alpha x}J_{\nu}(\beta x)=\frac{\left(\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}\right)^{\nu}\Gamma(\nu+\mu)}{\alpha^{\mu}\Gamma(\nu+1)}\ _{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{\nu+\mu}{2},\frac{\nu+\mu+1}{2};\nu+1;-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\right),

(54)

which is valid for

\text{Re}(\alpha+i\beta)>0\,,\qquad\text{Re}(\alpha-i\beta)>0\,,\qquad\text{Re}(\mu+\nu)>0\ .

(55)

Parameters
\beta
and
\nu
have the same meaning in (
54
) and in our setup, while the other are related by

\mu=-2l+\frac{1}{2}\,,\qquad\alpha=\frac{\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}+ic\beta}{\lambda}\ .

(56)

Thus, the conditions (
55
) are satisfied and we arrive at the expression for the modes

\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle=\frac{2\pi\,\lambda^{2l}\,c_{\beta}}{\Gamma(-2l)}\,\frac{\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)^{\nu}\Gamma(\nu-2l+\frac{1}{2})}{\alpha^{\nu-2l+1/2}\,\Gamma(\nu+1)}\ _{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{\nu-2l+\frac{1}{2}}{2}\,,\frac{\nu-2l+\frac{3}{2}}{2}\,;\nu+1;-\frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\right)\ .

(57)

This is one of the main results of the present section. Recall that
\lambda
and
c
are proportional to expectation values of coordinates
\langle\hat{z}\rangle
and
\langle\hat{t}\rangle
, see equations (
21
)-(
22
). Upon substituting these expressions in (
57
), the mode is turned into a function of coordinates. We go on to exhibit some properties of this function. Firstly, for small values of
\lambda\sim\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle
, one obtains
\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle\sim\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle^{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}=\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle^{{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}
, which agrees with the behaviour of commutative modes near the boundary, as explained in Section
2.1
. This observation will be important later when we compute the boundary two-point function of operators dual to the scalar field on the fuzzy AdS. Had we used noncommutative modes proportional to
Y_{\nu}(\beta\rho)
in (
51
), we would have obtained
\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle\sim\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle^{d-{{\mathbb{\Delta}}}}
, with
d=1
, which again coincides with the commutative behaviour of the other linearly independent solution.

More importantly, the modes (
57
) reduce to the ones on the ordinary AdS
2
, (
5
), by taking the limit
l\to-\infty
. This result will be at the root of our claim that the latter gives the commutative limit of the fuzzy AdS
2
. To establish it, we begin from the limit formula relating hypergeometric and Bessel functions,
[
32
]
, section 5.7,

J_{\nu}(\tilde{z})=\lim_{a,b\to\infty}\,\frac{\left(\frac{\tilde{z}}{2}\right)^{\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu+1)}\ _{2}F_{1}\left(a,b;\nu+1;-\frac{\tilde{z}^{2}}{4ab}\right)\ .

(58)

Here,
\tilde{z},\nu
can be arbitrary complex numbers and
a,b
tend to infinity through complex values. We put

a=-l+\frac{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}{2}\,,\qquad b=-l+\frac{\nu+\frac{3}{2}}{2}\,,

(59)

which implies

\tilde{z}^{2}=4\left(-l+\frac{\nu+\frac{1}{2}}{2}\right)\left(-l+\frac{\nu+\frac{3}{2}}{2}\right)\frac{\beta^{2}}{\left(\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}+ic\beta\right)^{2}}\ \frac{\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle^{2}}{l^{2}{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{2}}\ .

(60)

Therefore, in the
l\to-\infty\,
limit
a
and
b
go to infinity, while
\tilde{z}
remains finite,

\tilde{z}\sim\frac{2\beta\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\left(\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}-i\frac{\langle\hat{t}\rangle}{l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\beta\right)}\sim\frac{2\beta\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}}\ .

(61)

Applying (
58
) to the expression (
57
) for the modes and simplifying, we get

\displaystyle\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi c_{\beta}\sqrt{\frac{2\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}}\left(\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}-i\frac{\beta\langle\hat{t}\rangle}{l{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\right)^{2l-1/2}J_{\nu}\left(\frac{2\beta\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim 2\pi c_{\beta}\left(2-\beta^{2}\right)^{l-\frac{1}{4}}e^{-i\,\frac{2\beta\langle\hat{t}\rangle}{\ {\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}}\ J_{\nu}\left(\frac{2\beta\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}}\right)\ .

(62)

To get to the second line, we used the familiar limit giving the exponential function,
e^{x}=\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{x}{n}\right)^{n}
. We shall introduce the suggestive notation

\omega=\frac{2\beta}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\sqrt{2-\beta^{2}}}\,,\qquad\omega\in\left(\frac{-2}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}},\frac{2}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\right)\ .

(63)

Then the limiting form of the modes is written as

\langle\phi_{\beta}\rangle\sim\frac{4\pi c_{\beta}}{\sqrt{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}}\left(\frac{8}{4+{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right)^{l-\frac{1}{4}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\ e^{-i\omega\langle\hat{t}\rangle}\sqrt{\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}\ J_{\nu}\left(\omega\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle\right)\ .

(64)

In terms of the dependence on
\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle
and
\langle\hat{t}\rangle
, these are precisely the classical modes of Section
2.1
. Had we used the d’Alembertian modes instead of the Laplacian ones, the result would have lacked the
\sqrt{\langle{\hat{z}}\rangle}
factor in front of the Bessel function, and the order of the Bessel function would have been different from
\nu
. This further motivates our choice of Laplacian over d’Alembertian. Matching the overall normalisations instructs us to put

c_{\beta}=\frac{\sqrt{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{8}{4+{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{2}\omega^{2}}\right)^{-l+\frac{1}{4}}\ .

(65)

The coefficients
c_{\beta}
are not fully determined by the above argument because (
64
) is valid only in the
l\to-\infty
limit and therefore the right hand side of (
65
) may be multiplied by an arbitrary function of
l
that tends to one in this limit. Equations (
57
) and (
64
) are the main results of the present section.

4
Scalar field on the fuzzy AdS
3

This section is dedicated to the Klein-Gordon equation on the fuzzy AdS
3
and its solutions. In the first subsection, which is largely similar to Section
3.1
, a complete set of eigenfunctions of the Laplacian is found. The main difference compared to AdS
2
is that now noncommutative modes carry one more quantum number than their commutative analogues. Their expectation values in semi-classical states are determined in the second subsection. Since the calculation follows the same pattern as in two dimensions, most of it is relegated to Appendix
C
. At the end of the second subsection, we show how taking the
l\to-\infty\,
limit gives rise to classical modes, the additional quantum number entering only through overall normalisation. The normalisation can be fixed by considering the two-point function, which is done in the next section.

4.1
Solutions to the Klein-Gordon equation

We follow the same strategy as in two dimensions and, for this reason, we will be brief. Fuzzy functions, i.e. the elements of the operator algebra

\mathcal{A}=\text{End}\left(\mathcal{H}_{l}\otimes\mathcal{\bar{H}}_{l}\right)\,,

(66)

are represented by integral kernels
\phi(\xi_{L},\xi_{R},\bar{\xi}_{L},\bar{\xi}_{R})
, with
\,\xi_{L},\,\xi_{R},\,\bar{\xi}_{L},\,\bar{\xi}_{R}\in(0,\infty)
. On them, the momenta act as

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{{\hat{p}}_{z}}=\xi_{L}\partial_{\xi_{L}}+\xi_{R}\partial_{\xi_{R}}+\bar{\xi}_{L}\partial_{\bar{\xi}_{L}}+\bar{\xi}_{R}\partial_{\bar{\xi}_{R}}+4l+4\,,

(67)

\displaystyle\text{ad}_{{\hat{p}}_{t}}=-i\left(\xi_{L}-\xi_{R}-\bar{\xi}_{L}+\bar{\xi}_{R}\right)\,,\qquad\text{ad}_{{\hat{p}}_{x}}=-i\left(\xi_{L}-\xi_{R}+\bar{\xi}_{L}-\bar{\xi}_{R}\right)\ .

(68)

From these expressions, the Laplacian (
32
) is readily computed. The operator greatly simplifies by passing to variables

\xi_{L}=\chi_{L}\,e^{\zeta_{L}},\quad\bar{\xi}_{L}=\chi_{L}\,e^{-\zeta_{L}},\qquad\xi_{R}=\chi_{R}\,e^{\zeta_{R}},\quad\bar{\xi}_{R}=\chi_{R}\,e^{-\zeta_{R}}\,,

(69)

where
\chi_{L},\chi_{R}\in(0,\infty)
and
\,\zeta_{L},\zeta_{R}\in(-\infty,\infty)
, and further introducing

\rho=\sqrt{\chi_{L}^{2}+\chi_{R}^{2}}\,,\qquad\tan{\varphi}=\frac{\chi_{R}}{\chi_{L}}\,,\qquad\zeta_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}\,(\zeta_{L}\pm\zeta_{R})\,,

(70)

with
\rho\in(0,\infty)
,
\,\varphi\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})\,
,
\,\zeta_{\pm}\in(-\infty,\infty)
. Acting on functions of the form
\phi=\rho^{-\frac{8l+7}{2}}\,g(\rho,\varphi,\zeta_{+},\zeta_{-})
, the Klein-Gordon equation reduces to the following equation for the function
\,g
,

\left(\rho^{2}\partial_{\rho}^{2}-4\rho^{2}\big(1-\cosh 2\zeta_{-}\sin 2\varphi\big)-\frac{3}{4}\right)g=M^{2}g\ .

(71)

Similarly as in two dimensions, the Laplacian contains derivatives with respect to the variable
\rho
only. We may therefore look for solutions of the form
f(\rho)\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{0})h(\zeta_{+})\delta(\zeta_{-}-\zeta_{-0})
, with an arbitrary function
h(\zeta_{+})
. We further simplify our ansatz by assuming solutions of the form

g(\rho,\varphi,\zeta_{+},\zeta_{-})=f(\rho)\,\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{0})\,\delta(\zeta_{+}-\zeta_{+0})\,\delta(\zeta_{-}-\zeta_{-0})\,,

(72)

and denote

\gamma^{2}=\cosh 2\zeta_{-0}\,\sin 2\varphi_{0}-1\ .

(73)

The general solution for the function
f(\rho)
is then given in terms of Bessel functions. Picking the solution regular at
\rho=0
and plugging it back into the mode, we arrive at

\phi_{\gamma}=c_{\gamma}\,\rho^{-4l-3}\,J_{\nu}(2\gamma\rho)\,\delta(\varphi-\varphi_{0})\,\delta(\zeta_{+}-\zeta_{+0})\,\delta(\zeta_{-}-\zeta_{-0})\,,

(74)

where, recall,
\nu=\sqrt{1+M^{2}}
. We shall in the following assume that
\gamma^{2}>0
: this additional condition ensures that the argument of the Bessel function
J_{\nu}\,
is real, i.e. that the solution (
74
) does not increase exponentially for

5
Two-point function and holography

5.1
Quantum field theory

One of features of the noncommutative frame formalism is that the quantisation of the field theory follows a natural path, closely analogous to the commutative case. For finite matrix geometries, such as the fuzzy sphere, the most common approach to quantisation is via the path integral. Indeed, on these geometries the path integral reduces to an ordinary Lebesgue integral and provides a regularisation for the quantum field theory. The path integral approach is less straightforward to apply for spaces defined via infinite dimensional representations. In this work, we will instead use canonical methods.

Our main object of interest are
n
-point functions of the fundamental scalar field. We will write
\Phi
to signify the quantum field, in contrast to the classical noncommutative field
\phi
. The vacuum expectation value of any operator
\mathcal{O}
will be denoted by
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle\equiv\langle 0|\mathcal{O}|0\rangle
. In particular, we wish to compute expectation values of
n
-point correlators in semi-classical states

G_{n}({\tt x}_{1},\dots,{\tt x}_{n})=\langle{\tt x}_{1},\dots,{\tt x}_{n}|\langle\Phi\otimes\dots\otimes\Phi\rangle|{\tt x}_{1},\dots,{\tt x}_{n}\rangle\ .

(80)

Here,
{\tt x}_{i}
are points of the classical manifold,
|{\tt x}_{i}\rangle
the corresponding semi-classical states and
|{\tt x}_{1},\dots,{\tt x}_{n}\rangle
their
n
-fold tensor product. The quantity (
80
) is expected to receive corrections compared to the correlation function of the commutative theory, controlled by the scale of noncommutativity.

To define the middle factor in (
80
), we begin with the mode expansion of the classical noncommutative field
\phi
,

\phi=\iiint\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\left(\phi_{\omega,k,s}a_{\omega,k,s}+\phi_{\omega,k,s}^{\ast}a_{\omega,k,s}^{\dagger}\right)\ .

(81)

We have written the expansion for the fuzzy AdS
3
, the more complicated of the two cases we are considering. Moreover, we denote the internal label (previously
\varphi_{0}
) by
s
in order to indicate how the construction generalises to other backgrounds. The coefficients
a,a^{\dagger}
are promoted to operators satisfying the canonical commutation relations,

[a_{\omega,k,s},a^{\dagger}_{\omega^{\prime},k^{\prime},s^{\prime}}]=\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\,\delta(k-k^{\prime})\,\delta(s-s^{\prime})\,,\qquad[a,a]=[a^{\dagger},a^{\dagger}]=0\ .

(82)

These relations are the same as in the commutative theory, except that creation and annihilation operators carry the additional internal index
s
. The Hilbert space of the fuzzy QFT is defined as the Fock space generated by
\{a^{\dagger}_{\omega,k,s}\}
from the vacuum state
|0\rangle
obeying

a_{\omega,k,s}|0\rangle=0\ .

(83)

In expressions (
81
)-(
83
),
\omega
ranges over positive values that it can assume and
k
over the interval
(-\omega,\omega)
– these are direct generalisations of the ranges in the commutative theory. The additional quantum number
s
goes over all its values.

The simplest correlators (
80
) are one-point functions, which are however seen to vanish identically. Our main focus in this section are two-point functions. As in ordinary quantum field theory, they may be expressed as integrals over modes. Denoting all the quantum numbers collectively by
\Lambda=(\omega,k,s)
, we have

\displaystyle G_{2}=\langle\Phi\otimes\Phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\Lambda\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda^{\prime}\,\langle 0|(\phi_{\Lambda}a_{\Lambda}+\phi^{\ast}_{\Lambda}a^{\dagger}_{\Lambda})\otimes(\phi_{\Lambda^{\prime}}a_{\Lambda^{\prime}}+\phi^{\ast}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{\Lambda^{\prime}})|0\rangle

(84)

\displaystyle=\iint\mathrm{d}\Lambda\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda^{\prime}\,\langle 0|\phi_{\Lambda}a_{\Lambda}\otimes\phi^{\ast}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}a^{\dagger}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}|0\rangle=\iint\mathrm{d}\Lambda\,\mathrm{d}\Lambda^{\prime}\,\phi_{\Lambda}\otimes\phi^{\ast}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\,\langle 0|[a_{\Lambda},a^{\dagger}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}]|0\rangle\ .

Due to the canonical commutation relations (
82
), we arrive at

\langle\Phi\otimes\Phi\rangle=\iiint\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\langle\phi_{\omega,k,s}\otimes\phi^{\ast}_{\omega,k,s}\rangle\ .

(85)

The resulting two-point function is End
(\mathcal{H})
-valued and may be further evaluated between any pair of states. Taking these to be (tensor products of) semi-classical states defines the correlator (
80
),

G_{2}({\tt x}_{1},{\tt x}_{2})=\iiint\mathrm{d}\omega\,\mathrm{d}k\,\mathrm{d}s\,\langle{\tt x}_{1}|\phi_{\omega,k,s}|{\tt x}_{1}\rangle\langle{\tt x}_{2}|\phi_{\omega,k,s}|{\tt x}_{2}\rangle^{\ast}\ .

(86)

Continuing along these lines, we see that the free theory on the fuzzy AdS space satisfies Wick’s theorem. Therefore, all
n
-point functions (
80
) are simply obtained from the two-point function.

Summary and perspectives

In this work we initiated the study of quantum field theory on fuzzy AdS spaces. Focusing on two and three dimensions, we determined a complete set of modes for the (noncommutative) free scalar field and computed the boundary two-point function. By analysing expectation values of modes inside
semi-classical
states, we identified the commutative limit

|l|\gg{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{-1}\gg 1\,,

(105)

in which the fuzzy two-point function reduces to the commutative one. In three dimensions, the derivation exhibits an interesting mechanism by which the additional degrees of freedom present on the noncommutative space are made consistent with the classical limit.

The way how the classical limit arises, even in two dimensions, is technically quite intricate. Let us recap the main points. As mentioned, the classical regime involves taking two limits: the representation label is sent to infinity,
l\to-\infty
, and the noncommutativity parameter to zero,
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\to 0
(we take from the start that
\ell_{\text{\tiny{AdS}}}=1
). Both these types of limits are familiar in other examples of noncommutative spaces. Usually either only
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
is present, and treated as a formal parameter, or both labels occur but can be related to one another by a Casimir-type relation. Our case is different from either of the above and there is a curious interplay between the noncommutativity parameter
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
and the choice of the discrete series representation of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
. As was explained, the fact that discrete series are realised by functions of a
positive
variable
\xi
led to the restriction on the angle
\varphi\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
and consequently the UV frequency cut-off,
|\omega|<\frac{2}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}
. Had we used the principal series representation, the cut-off would not have existed. In principle, we are still free to impose a relation between
l
and
{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}
, as long as it is consistent with (
105
). It will be interesting to see if such a relation is implied by consistency/classical limit of some other observables not studied in this work.

While these initial results are very promising, a number of open questions about the status of quantum field theory on fuzzy AdS spaces remain. The main future direction that we have in mind is to go beyond the free theory and study interactions. To this end, the frame formalism provides natural analogues for most concepts of ordinary curved space QFT, with a few omissions. For instance, it appears that our definition of the Laplacian implies that one should not use the trace as the noncommutative analogue of the integral, but modify it by a certain factor.

A simplistic approach, at least to boundary correlators of weakly coupled theories, is to write the latter in terms of Witten diagrams as in the commutative case, however with all propagators being fuzzy. E.g. for the

G_{3}({\tt x}_{1},{\tt x}_{2},{\tt x}_{3})=\lambda\int\limits_{\text{AdS}}\mathrm{d}{\tt z}\,\mathrm{d}^{d}{\tt x}\,\sqrt{|g|}\ G_{b\partial}({\tt z},{\tt x};{\tt x}_{1})\,G_{b\partial}({\tt z},{\tt x};{\tt x}_{2})\,G_{b\partial}({\tt z},{\tt x};{\tt x}_{3})+O(\lambda^{2})\,,

It remains to be seen, though, if such expansions in modified Witten diagrams as above are the appropriate way to compute perturbative correlators on fuzzy AdS spaces, i.e. whether the Feynman-Witten rules follow from a consistent QFT based on an action. As a different approach to the above questions, one may proceed by a further analysis of boundary correlation functions and try to identify them either as correlators of some

An interesting further comparison that we will make in the future concerns the ‘deep quantum’ regime of the two-point function. Namely, in the present work we computed expectation values of the two-point function inside pairs of semi-classical states, as opposed to more general matrix elements where the left and right pairs are not the same. In the terminology of
[

, this is the ‘semi-classical’ regime. By contrast, in the ‘deep quantum’ regime, the left and right pairs of states differ from each other, but for each pair the two semi-classical states are close to one another. For quantum spaces that originate from matrix models, it is expected on general grounds that the semi-classical and deep-quantum regimes are the only ones in which the two-point functions does not approximately vanish.

While these (existing or potential) connections are intriguing, their significance remains unclear. Nevertheless, it is tempting to try and realise our spaces as saddle points of some modified versions of IKKT-type matrix models.

A related set of questions, which is specific to three dimensions, regards the nature of internal degrees of freedom, such as their transformation properties. We saw that, when working in semi-classical states (
33
), the effect of the internal space was minimal, entering only through normalisation factors of modes. An approach to exploring the additional degrees of freedom would be through sets of suitably localised states which extend (

, is briefly discussed in Appendix

[

The flat space limit and cosmology

We conclude by mentioning two extensions of the above constructions that may have interesting applications. In this work, we restricted ourselves to two and three dimensions, as well as a negative cosmological constant. The most relevant physical applications will require to drop either one or both of these restrictions. A particularly interesting case is that of AdS
4
, for which most of the technology is already in place. Both the differential-geometric and representation-theoretic aspects of our construction are straightforwardly generalised to four dimensions. On the one hand, the coordinates can be defined as components of the Pauli-Lubanski vector of
\mathfrak{so}(3,2)
, akin to
[
13
]
, while momenta are given by translation and dilation generators. Furthermore, unitary representations of
\mathfrak{so}(3,2)
are well-understood and many of them admit lowest-weight vectors. Therefore, the construction of semi-classical states would proceed in essentially the same way as in this work. Of course, it remains to be seen if the commutative limit with properties as above continues to exist in four dimensions. The AdS
4
is of particular interest in view of taking the flat space limit,
\ell_{\text{AdS}}\to\infty
. It is natural to speculate that this would lead to a quantum field theory on some noncommutative deformation of
\mathbb{R}^{1,3}
. In fact, the flat space limit and its properties present an interesting open question already for models studied in this work.

On the other hand, the case of positive cosmological constant, in particular the fuzzy dS
4
, may be relevant for the early universe cosmology, where quantum gravitational effects play and important role. To this end, the main task that remains to be done is the definition of semi-classical states. It is still to be seen whether the principal or the discrete series representations of
SO(4,1)
provide the more appropriate version of the fuzzy dS
4
. In the case of the latter, one may speculate that semi-classical states can be defined as in this work, but this is to be further analysed. There are also some ideas for the appropriate notion of semi-classical states for principal series representations. Ultimately, the appropriateness of the set of states depends on having a good commutative limit. Assuming all the challenges are overcome, the appropriate analogue of Figure
4
would be compared to inhomogeneities in the CMB.

Appendix B
Representations of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})

In this appendix, we collect some properties of the group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
and its representations that are used in the main text. The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
is spanned by generators
\{H,E_{\pm}\}
that obey the brackets

[H,E_{+}]=E_{+}\,,\quad[H,E_{-}]=-E_{-}\,,\quad[E_{+},E_{-}]=2H\ .

(122)

We often make use of the linear combinations of these generators,

\tilde{H}=\frac{i}{2}(E_{+}-E_{-})\,,\qquad\tilde{E}_{+}=\frac{1}{2}(E_{+}+E_{-}+2iH)\,,\qquad\tilde{E}_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(E_{+}+E_{-}-2iH)\,,

(123)

which satisfy the same brackets as
\{H,E_{\pm}\}
. These linear combinations are convenient because
\tilde{H}
generates the maximal compact subgroup of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
, and thus has a discrete spectrum in any unitary irreducible representation.

The group
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
has two families of discrete series representations, denoted
T_{l}^{-}
and
T_{l}^{+}
,
[
39
]
. Here,
l
is an integer or a half-integer and for the first family
l\leq-1
, while
l\geq 1
for the second family. The carrier space of
T_{l}^{-}
will be denoted by
\mathcal{H}_{l}
. The two types of representations are related by

\left(T_{l}^{-}\right)^{\ast}=T_{-l}^{+}\,,

(124)

where
\pi^{\ast}
stands for the dual (contragredient) representation to
\pi
. Another way to relate the positive and negative discrete series is by means of the
Bargmann automorphism
,
B
, i.e.
\left(T_{l}^{-}\right)^{B}=T_{-l}^{+}
. The Bargmann automorphism is an outer automorphism of
SL(2,\mathbb{R})
that acts on the generators by

B(H)=H\,,\qquad B(E_{+})=-E_{+}\,,\qquad B(E_{-})=-E_{-}\ .

(125)

The negative discrete series representations are of lowest-weight type. The lowest-weight vector
\Psi_{0}
satisfies

\tilde{E}_{-}\Psi_{0}=0\,,\qquad\tilde{H}\Psi_{0}=-l\Psi_{0}\,,

(126)

and the representation space is spanned by vectors
\Psi_{n}=\tilde{E}_{+}^{n}\Psi_{0}
.

We shall use the realisation of
T_{l}^{-}
on the space of functions
f(\xi)
, with
\xi>0
, square-integrable with respect to the inner product,

(f_{1},f_{2})=2^{2l+1}\pi\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\xi\,\xi^{2l+1}f_{1}(\xi)^{\ast}f_{2}(\xi)\ .

(127)

The action of generators on these functions reads

H=\xi\partial_{\xi}+l+1\,,\qquad E_{+}=-i\xi\,,\qquad E_{-}=-i\left(\xi\partial_{\xi}^{2}+2(l+1)\partial_{\xi}\right)\ .

(128)

We use the notation
f(\xi)=\langle\xi|f\rangle
; due to the nontrivial measure in the scalar product in (
127
), it is then consistent to write

\langle\xi|\xi^{\prime}\rangle=(\pi\,2^{2l+1}\xi^{2l+1})^{-1}\,\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})\,,

(129)

where
\delta(\xi-\xi^{\prime})
is the standard
\delta
-function. The ‘ket’
|\xi\rangle
is proportional to the eigenvector of
{\hat{z}}\,
,

\langle\xi|z\rangle=({\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}\pi\,2^{2l+1}\xi^{2l+1})^{-1/2}\,\delta\left(\xi-\frac{z}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}\right)\,,

(130)

while the eigenfunctions of the time
\hat{t}
are

\langle\xi|t\rangle=({\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}^{1/2}\pi\,2^{l+1})^{-1}\,\xi^{-l-1+i\,\frac{t}{{\mathchar 22\mkern-9.0muk}}}\ .

(131)

The lowest-weight vector is given by the function

Appendix D
Appell functions

Appell functions are certain generalisations of the hypergeometric function to functions of two variables. There are four of them,
F_{1},\dots,F_{4}
, and they are well integrated in
Mathematica
. We briefly summarise the definition and some properties of
F_{1}
, the function that appears in the main text. For a comprehensive account, see
[
33
]
.

The Appell function
F_{1}
may be defined through its double series expansion

F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime};\gamma;x,y)=\sum_{m,n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\alpha)_{m+n}(\beta)_{m}(\beta^{\prime})_{n}}{(\gamma)_{m+n}m!\,n!}\ x^{m}y^{n}\,,\qquad|x|,|y|<1\ ,

(151)

where
(\alpha)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol. It is clearly symmetric under the simultaneous exchange
\beta\leftrightarrow\beta^{\prime}
,
x\leftrightarrow y
. The function may also be characterised through a pair of differential equations that it satisfies. One of these equations reads

x(1-x)\,\frac{{\partial}^{2}F_{1}}{{\partial}x^{2}}+y(1-x)\,\frac{{\partial}^{2}F_{1}}{{\partial}x\,{\partial}y}+\big(\gamma-(\alpha+\beta+1)x\big)\,\frac{{\partial}F_{1}}{{\partial}x}-\beta y\,\frac{{\partial}F_{1}}{{\partial}y}-\alpha\beta\,F_{1}=0\,,

(152)

and the second is obtained by
\beta\leftrightarrow\beta^{\prime}
,
x\leftrightarrow y
. As the hypergeometric function,
F_{1}
obeys a number of relations; for particular values of variables or parameters it reduces to the hypergeometric function. The two properties used in this work are integral representation

F_{1}(\alpha,\beta,\beta^{\prime};\gamma;x,y)=\frac{\Gamma(\gamma)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\gamma-\alpha)}\int\limits_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}t\ t^{\alpha-1}(1-t)^{\gamma-\alpha-1}(1-xt)^{-\beta}(1-yt)^{-\beta^{\prime}}\,,

(153)

valid for
\text{Re}(\gamma)>\text{Re}(\alpha)>0
, and the reduction formula

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{1}\left(\alpha,\frac{1}{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{\varepsilon};\gamma;\,\varepsilon x,\,\varepsilon y\right)=\ _{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\gamma,x+y)\ .

(154)

Here,
{}_{1}F_{1}(\alpha,\gamma,z)
is Kummer’s confluent hypergeometric function.

Appendix E
Generalised coherent states in three dimensions

In the main text, we focused on semi-classical states (
18
), (
33
), in which we computed the expectation values of field modes and the propagator. These states are defined by means of momenta, and hence by the noncommutative frame. In two dimensions, they coincide with generalised coherent states in the sense of Perelomov,
[
26

Following the general theory of
