Prevalence and determinants of sedentary behavior among university students in Yemen: a national multicenter cross-sectional study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSedentary behavior is increasingly recognized as an independent risk factor for adverse physical and mental health outcomes. University students are particularly vulnerable due to prolonged sitting associated with academic demands. However, data on SB among university students in Yemen remain limited. This study aimed to assess the prevalence, patterns, and determinants of sedentary behavior among Yemeni university students.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among 8,312 university students aged 18–30 years from 28 public and private universities across 10 Yemeni cities. Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling approach via online platforms, which may introduce selection bias. Data were collected using a web-based, self-administered questionnaire including sociodemographic characteristics and the validated Arabic version of the Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire (SBQ). Average daily sedentary time was calculated, and prolonged sedentary behavior was defined as ≥ 7 h/day. Descriptive statistics and logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with prolonged sedentary behavior.ResultsThe mean daily sedentary time was 326.3 ± 211.4 min, and 28.6% of students reported prolonged sedentary behavior. Watching television was the most common sedentary activity, followed by academic work and phone use. Sedentary time was higher among females and younger students in descriptive analyses. In multivariable analysis, enrollment in private universities (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.68–2.08), studying in scientific/engineering/technical (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.30–1.68) or administrative/economic disciplines (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.12–1.53), smoking (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.12–1.47), and lack of access to university physical activity facilities (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.16–1.47) were independently associated with higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior. Older age was associated with lower odds (21–24 years: OR = 0.82; ≥25 years: OR = 0.64).ConclusionSedentary behavior is common among Yemeni university students, with certain groups at higher risk. These findings suggest that university-based strategies, such as improving access to physical activity facilities and promoting active lifestyles, may help reduce sedentary time and support student health.

Background

Rapid technological advancements, urbanization, and lifestyle changes have contributed to a substantial increase in sedentary behavior (SB), which has become a major public health concern [1]. SB is defined as any waking behavior characterized by energy expenditure ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs) while in a sitting, reclining, or lying position, such as watching television, reading, commuting, or office-based work [2]. SB is often confused with physical inactivity, which refers to not meeting recommended physical activity (PA) guidelines [3]. Importantly, individuals may achieve recommended levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) while still spending large portions of their day in sedentary behaviors.

Adults typically spend between 5 and 11.5 h per day in sedentary activities, and higher sitting time has been associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiometabolic diseases [4–7]. In addition, prolonged sedentary behavior has been linked to adverse mental health outcomes, including anxiety and depression, particularly when involving passive activities [8]. Collectively, this evidence supports SB as an independent health risk factor associated with a range of negative health outcomes.

In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) incorporated recommendations on sedentary behavior into its global physical activity guidelines, emphasizing the importance of reducing sedentary time and increasing physical activity levels to mitigate associated health risks [9] Similarly, the American Diabetes Association recommends interrupting prolonged sitting with brief periods of light activity [10].

Young adulthood represents a critical life stage during which long-term health behaviors are established [11]. University students are particularly vulnerable to high levels of sedentary behavior due to academic demands, including prolonged sitting during lectures, studying, and screen-based activities [12]. Evidence suggests that sedentary time among university students may equal or even exceed that observed in office workers [13]. These patterns raise concerns regarding the long-term health implications of sedentary lifestyles adopted during this transitional period [14].

Over the past decade, several studies in the Arab region have examined sedentary behavior among university students [15–17]. However, evidence from Yemen remains extremely limited. While some studies have explored physical activity patterns in the Yemeni population [18, 19], research specifically addressing sedentary behavior is lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the prevalence, patterns, and determinants of sedentary behavior among university students across Yemen using a validated Arabic multi-domain questionnaire.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted among university students across Yemen between January and July 2025. The study involved 28 public and private universities across 10 Yemeni cities, with the largest proportion of participants recruited from Sana’a. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [20].

Study population and eligibility criteria

The target population consisted of male and female undergraduate students aged 18–30 years who were currently enrolled at the participating universities. Students were eligible if they were enrolled in any academic faculty and provided informed consent to participate. Students outside the specified age range or not currently enrolled at the participating universities were excluded. Students with medical or physical conditions that could substantially limit mobility or predispose them to prolonged sitting (e.g., severe physical disabilities) were not specifically excluded, as this information was not collected.

Sample size calculation

The minimum required sample size was calculated using Epitools, an online epidemiological calculator [21]. Owing to the absence of national data on sedentary behavior among Yemeni university students, prevalence estimates were adopted from a previous study conducted among Saudi university students. Assuming an expected prevalence of sedentary behavior of 58%, a margin of error of 5%, a 95% confidence level, and an infinite population, the calculated minimum sample size was 375 participants. To enhance statistical power, allow subgroup analyses, and improve geographic and institutional representation across multiple universities and cities, a substantially larger sample was recruited, yielding a final sample of 8,312 students.

Study tools

Data were collected using a structured, self-administered questionnaire administered in Arabic. The questionnaire comprised four main sections. The first section collected sociodemographic and academic information, including age, sex, university type (public or private), academic year, college type, grade point average (GPA), employment status, and smoking status. Self-reported weight and height were obtained to calculate body mass index (BMI), which was categorized according to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria [22].

The second and third sections included the Arabic version of the Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire (SBQ), a validated tool designed to assess time spent in sedentary activities [23]. The SBQ assesses sitting time across nine domains, including television viewing, computer or video game use, listening to music while sitting, phone use, paperwork or office work, reading, playing a musical instrument, arts and crafts, and sitting during transportation. Participants reported the time spent in each activity separately for weekdays and weekends using categorical response options ranging from “none” to “6 hours or more”. The Arabic version of the SBQ has demonstrated excellent content validity (S-CVI = 0.99) and high interrater reliability (κ = 0.95), supporting its use in Arabic-speaking populations [23].

Average daily sedentary time (minutes/day) was calculated using the formula: [(weekday sedentary time × 5) + (weekend sedentary time × 2)] ÷ 7. Prolonged sedentary behavior was defined as sitting for ≥ 7 h per day, consistent with epidemiological evidence demonstrating increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiometabolic outcomes beyond this threshold [4, 6]. Responses indicating implausible sedentary durations (> 18 h/day) were considered invalid and excluded from analysis.

The final section included seven additional items assessing sitting habits, frequency of breaks during prolonged sitting, awareness of health risks related to sedentary behavior, access to and use of university physical activity facilities, presence of back or neck pain, and self-reported efforts to reduce sedentary time and increase physical activity. In this context, “sitting habits” referred to behavioral patterns such as frequency of breaks, posture-related practices, and strategies used to interrupt prolonged sitting.

Sampling strategy

Participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling strategy. The online survey link was disseminated through official and informal university student communication channels, including social media platforms such as WhatsApp and Telegram. Students from both public and private universities across different academic disciplines were invited to participate. To improve coverage and diversity, the survey was distributed across multiple universities, faculties, and academic levels using student networks and institutional channels. However, equal representation across institutions could not be ensured due to the non-probability nature of the sampling approach. Due to the open online distribution method, the total number of students who received the survey link could not be determined, and therefore, a formal response rate was not calculated. This limitation may introduce selection bias, as participation depended on students’ access to digital platforms and willingness to respond. Participation was voluntary and anonymous, and no identifying personal information was collected. All questionnaire items were set as mandatory to prevent incomplete responses.

Data quality control

To ensure data quality, the questionnaire was pilot tested before the main data collection. Built-in validation rules were incorporated into the online survey to minimize data entry errors and missing responses. A total of responses with invalid or implausible values (e.g., sedentary time > 18 h/day) were excluded during data cleaning; the number of excluded responses was recorded and reported in the Results section. All submitted questionnaires were screened for completeness and plausibility, and responses containing invalid or extreme values were excluded before statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). Given the large sample size, parametric statistical tests were applied.

Independent samples t-tests were used to compare the mean sedentary time between male and female students. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to examine associations between prolonged sedentary behavior (≥ 7 h/day) and potential predictors (including age, sex, BMI, type of university, type of college, academic year, smoking status, employment status, awareness of health risks, and access to physical activity facilities). BMI data were missing for 536 participants and were excluded from analyses involving BMI. Variables with p-values < 0.20 in the univariable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent factors associated with prolonged sedentary behavior while adjusting for potential confounders. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF), and model goodness-of-fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

During data cleaning, responses containing invalid or implausible values (e.g., sedentary time > 18 h/day) were excluded. A total of 480 responses were removed prior to analysis, resulting in a final analytical sample of 8,312 university students from 28 universities across 10 Yemeni cities. Among participants, 58.6% were male and 41.4% were female. The majority were aged 21–24 years (56.8%), enrolled in public universities (67.6%), and studying in medical and health colleges (57.7%). Most students were non-smokers (84.0%) and not engaged in employment alongside their studies (62.0%).

Overall sedentary behavior

The mean daily sedentary time among all participants was 326.31 ± 211.36 min per day. Sedentary time was similar between weekdays (325.68 ± 222.88 min/day) and weekends (327.89 ± 231.52 min/day). Overall, 28.6% of students reported prolonged sedentary behavior, defined as sitting for seven hours or more per day. Sedentary behavior across sociodemographic characteristics

Females reported a slightly higher mean daily sedentary time than males (331.07 ± 216.42 vs. 322.94 ± 207.67 min/day). Sedentary time was highest among students aged ≤ 20 years and decreased with increasing age. Underweight students had the highest mean sedentary time, followed by those with normal weight and overweight, while students with obesity reported the lowest sedentary time.

Students enrolled in private universities demonstrated substantially higher sedentary time compared with those in public universities (382.23 ± 226.50 vs. 299.53 ± 198.23 min/day). When stratified by type of college, the highest sedentary time was observed among students in scientific, engineering, and technical colleges, followed by administrative and economic colleges, medical and health colleges, humanities and social sciences, and other colleges.

First-year students reported the highest sedentary time, while lower values were generally observed in higher academic years. In terms of academic performance, students with a GPA below 70% showed the highest sedentary time compared with other GPA categories.

Sedentary behavior across lifestyle and environmental factors

Smokers reported substantially higher sedentary time than non-smokers (362.72 ± 219.35 vs. 319.38 ± 209.11 min/day). Students engaged in full-time work alongside their studies had higher sedentary time compared with part-time or non-working students. Students who reported taking breaks every 30 min had the lowest sedentary time, whereas those who took breaks every two hours or rarely took breaks accumulated significantly more sedentary time.

Students who regularly used these facilities reported the lowest sedentary time, while those reporting no access accumulated the highest sedentary time. Notably, more than half of the participants indicated that physical activity facilities were unavailable at their universities (52.3%), and 22.8% were unaware of their existence (Fig. 1).

Students who reported using a comfortable and supportive chair when sitting for long periods had slightly higher sedentary time than those who did not (333.57 ± 216.75 vs. 323.19 ± 208.94 min/day). Students experiencing frequent back or neck pain reported higher sedentary time compared with those reporting no pain. Students who were aware of the health risks of prolonged sitting reported lower sedentary time than those who were not aware (323.02 ± 208.24 vs. 332.94 ± 217.41 min/day). Furthermore, students who actively applied strategies to reduce sitting time reported lower sedentary time (313.09 ± 213.98 min/day) compared with those who found it difficult to implement such changes (330.91 ± 210.63 min/day) or those not considering behavioral modification (332.59 ± 208.96 min/day).

Barriers to reducing sedentary behavior

Academic stress and university workload were identified as the most common barriers to reducing sedentary behavior and increasing physical activity, reported by 69.39% of participants. Other reported barriers included an unsuitable environment for physical activity (40.83%), lack of personal motivation or incentives (27.71%), limited awareness of the importance of physical activity (14.67%), and health problems preventing exercise (6.24%) (Fig. 2).

Domain-specific sedentary activities and sex differences

Across both weekdays and weekends, watching television was the most time-consuming sedentary activity, followed by office or paperwork-related activities and phone use. On weekdays, females spent significantly more time watching television, talking on the phone, engaging in arts-and-craft activities, and playing musical instruments, whereas males spent more time using computers or playing video games and sitting during transportation. On weekends, total sedentary time was significantly higher among females compared with males. Females continued to report greater time spent watching television, talking on the phone, listening to music, and engaging in artistic activities, while males spent more time on computer use, office-related tasks, and transportation-related sitting (Tables 2 and 3).

Factors associated with prolonged sedentary behavior

In the univariable logistic regression analyses, age ≥ 25 years, being in the fifth academic year, studying in humanities and social sciences colleges, and awareness of the health risks of prolonged sitting were associated with lower odds of prolonged sedentary behavior. In contrast, enrollment in private universities, studying in scientific/engineering/technical or administrative/economic colleges, being underweight, smoking, and lack of access to university facilities that support physical activity were associated with higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior.

After multivariable adjustment, students aged 21–24 years (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.71–0.95, p = 0.008) and ≥ 25 years (OR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.52–0.78, p < 0.001) had significantly lower odds of prolonged sedentary behavior compared with those aged ≤ 20 years. Enrollment in private universities remained strongly associated with higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior (OR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.68–2.08, p < 0.001). Similarly, students studying in scientific, engineering, and technical colleges (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.30–1.68, p < 0.001) and administrative and economic colleges (OR = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.12–1.53, p = 0.001) had higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior compared with those in medical and health colleges.

Smoking was also independently associated with higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.12–1.47, p < 0.001). Furthermore, students who reported no availability of university facilities supporting physical activity (OR = 1.30, 95% CI: 1.16–1.47, p < 0.001) or who had access but did not use these facilities (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47, p = 0.004) showed significantly higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior.

Sex, BMI categories, type of college classified as humanities/social sciences or others, academic year, and awareness of health risks related to prolonged sitting were not independently associated with prolonged sedentary behavior after adjustment (Table 4). No evidence of multicollinearity was observed (all VIF values < 3.0), and the model demonstrated adequate goodness-of-fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow p = 0.32).

Discussion

This large multicenter cross-sectional study represents the first national investigation of sedentary behavior among university students in Yemen and provides important insights into the magnitude, patterns, and determinants of prolonged sitting in this understudied population. The findings demonstrate that sedentary behavior is present in a substantial proportion of students, with 28.6% of participants reporting prolonged sedentary behavior (≥ 7 h/day). These results underscore sedentary behavior as a growing public health concern among Yemeni university students and place Yemen within the global context of rising sedentary lifestyles among young adults [4, 24, 25].

The mean daily sedentary time was 326.3 ± 211.4 min, which is comparable to estimates reported among university students in high-, middle-, and low-income countries, where daily sitting commonly ranges between five and eight hours [13, 14]. This similarity suggests that despite cultural, economic, and infrastructural differences, university students worldwide share common behavioral patterns driven by academic demands, digital learning, and screen-based leisure activities [11, 26]. The comparable sedentary time reported on weekdays and weekends further indicates that prolonged sitting among students is not restricted to formal academic schedules but persists during leisure time, reinforcing concerns about habitual sedentary lifestyles. Watching television emerged as the most prevalent sedentary activity, followed by office or academic work and phone use. This pattern mirrors findings from studies in the Middle East, Europe, and Asia, where screen-based behaviors dominate sedentary time among university students [13, 17, 27].

In the present study, female students reported slightly higher sedentary time than males, particularly during weekends. Domain-specific analysis revealed that females engaged more frequently in passive and socially oriented sedentary activities such as television viewing, phone use, listening to music, and arts and crafts, whereas males accumulated more sedentary time through computer use, gaming, and transportation-related sitting. These findings are consistent with prior studies conducted in Arab and Middle Eastern countries, which have reported higher sedentary time among female students [27, 28]. While cultural and social norms have been suggested as potential explanations in previous literature, these interpretations should be made cautiously, as such factors were not directly assessed in the present study [29]. However, sex was not independently associated with prolonged sedentary behavior after multivariable adjustment, suggesting that environmental, academic, and institutional factors play a more prominent role in shaping sedentary behavior than sex alone. This finding aligns with recent evidence emphasizing the importance of contextual determinants over individual demographic characteristics [11, 13].

Academic-related factors emerged as important factors associated with sedentary behavior in this study. Students enrolled in private universities demonstrated substantially higher sedentary time and higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior compared with those in public universities. These differences may be related to variations in academic structure, teaching modalities, and reliance on technology; however, such explanations remain speculative and were not directly measured in this study [17, 30]. Students in scientific, engineering, and technical colleges exhibited the highest sedentary time, followed by those in administrative and economic disciplines. These programs typically involve extended hours of lectures, laboratory work, and computer-based assignments, which may contribute to prolonged sitting. Similar patterns have been reported in studies from Asia and Europe [29, 30].

Furthermore, first-year students demonstrated higher sedentary time than students in later academic years in the descriptive analysis. This pattern may be related to curricular structure, as early academic years are often characterized by dense lecture schedules, whereas senior students may engage more in internships or field-based activities [31]. However, academic year was not independently associated with prolonged sedentary behavior in the multivariable analysis, suggesting that other contextual or environmental factors may play a more important role.

Similarly, students with lower academic performance showed higher sedentary time in descriptive analyses, although this variable was not included in the multivariable model. The relationship between academic performance and sedentary behavior may reflect a complex and potentially bidirectional association, whereby excessive sitting may be linked to reduced academic engagement, while lower academic performance may also be associated with more passive study behaviors [30, 32].

Smoking emerged as an independent predictor of prolonged sedentary behavior in the multivariable analysis, consistent with evidence demonstrating the clustering of unhealthy behaviors among young adults [33, 34]. Sedentary behavior, smoking, poor diet, and inadequate physical activity often coexist, collectively increasing the risk of non-communicable diseases later in life [5, 6]. These findings highlight the need for integrated health promotion strategies that address multiple risk behaviors simultaneously.

Students who reported frequent back or neck pain also exhibited higher sedentary time in the descriptive analysis, supporting previous research linking prolonged sitting with musculoskeletal discomfort [35]. However, given the cross-sectional design, the direction of this relationship cannot be determined, and it is likely to be bidirectional, as prolonged sitting may contribute to musculoskeletal symptoms, while existing pain may also reduce physical activity and increase sedentary behavior.

Poor posture, inadequate ergonomic conditions, and prolonged screen exposure may be associated with musculoskeletal symptoms. However, given the cross-sectional design of this study, the direction of this relationship cannot be determined. It is likely to be bidirectional, as prolonged sedentary behavior may contribute to musculoskeletal symptoms, while existing pain may also reduce physical activity and increase sedentary time. Promoting ergonomic awareness and active breaks may therefore have potential benefits for both reducing sedentary time and improving musculoskeletal health [36].

Access to physical activity facilities emerged as a critical factor associated with sedentary behavior. Students who reported no availability of university physical activity facilities or reported having access but not using these facilities were more likely to report prolonged sedentary behavior. This finding reinforces the ecological model of health behavior [37]. Despite awareness of the health risks associated with prolonged sitting, knowledge alone was not independently associated with prolonged sedentary behavior after multivariable adjustment. This observation suggests that educational interventions alone may be insufficient and should be complemented by structural and environmental changes [9, 11].

Academic stress and university workload were identified as the most commonly reported barriers to reducing sedentary behavior and increasing physical activity. Similar barriers have been reported globally [30, 38]. In Yemen, these challenges may be compounded by broader contextual factors, including limited infrastructure, economic constraints, and ongoing sociopolitical instability [18, 19]. Lack of motivation and unsuitable environments for physical activity were also frequently reported, highlighting the need for interventions addressing both psychological and environmental barriers. Time-efficient physical activity programs, integration of movement into academic settings, and peer-supported initiatives may be particularly effective in this context [39].

Public health and policy implications

Universities represent a critical setting for early intervention, as behaviors established during young adulthood often persist into later life and influence long-term health outcomes [12, 40]. The findings suggest that university-based strategies to reduce sedentary behavior may be beneficial, including promoting active learning environments, incorporating movement breaks during lectures, improving access to recreational facilities, and integrating health promotion into university policies and curricula [37, 41]. Targeted interventions focusing on groups with higher odds of prolonged sedentary behavior identified in this study may yield public health benefits.

Future studies should use longitudinal designs to clarify temporal relationships between sedentary behavior and academic, physical, and mental health outcomes. The use of objective measures such as accelerometers [4, 13] and intervention studies evaluating university-level policies are needed to inform evidence-based strategies for reducing sedentary behavior among Yemeni university students.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several important strengths. First, it represents one of the largest multicenter studies of sedentary behavior among university students in the Middle East and the first conducted at a national level in Yemen, including participants from 28 public and private universities across 10 cities. The large sample size enhanced the statistical power and robustness of the findings. Second, the use of the validated Arabic version of the SBQ allowed for a comprehensive assessment of multiple sedentary domains. Additionally, the inclusion of several sociodemographic, academic, and lifestyle variables enabled the identification of factors associated with prolonged sedentary behavior using multivariable regression analysis.

However, several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any inference of causal relationships between sedentary behavior and the associated factors identified; thus, all observed relationships should be interpreted as associations only, and the possibility of bidirectional relationships (e.g., between sedentary behavior and musculoskeletal symptoms) cannot be excluded. Second, participants were recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling strategy through online platforms, which introduces potential selection bias. Students who are more digitally connected, more health-aware, or more motivated may have been more likely to participate, potentially affecting the representativeness of the sample. In addition, due to the open online distribution of the survey, the response rate could not be determined, limiting the ability to assess non-response bias. Although the study included a large and geographically diverse sample from multiple universities and cities, the findings may not be fully generalizable to all university students in Yemen.

Third, sedentary behavior was assessed using a self-reported questionnaire, which is subject to recall bias, social desirability bias, and potential inaccuracies in estimating time spent in sedentary activities. Although the Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire is a validated tool, objective measures such as accelerometers may provide more precise estimates of sedentary time. Fourth, although multiple sociodemographic, academic, and lifestyle variables were included in the analysis, some potentially important confounding factors were not assessed. These include the distinction between academic and leisure-related screen time, commuting duration, and participation in structured physical activity, all of which may influence sedentary behavior. Finally, despite efforts to ensure data quality through validation procedures, the reliance on self-reported anthropometric data (height and weight) may have introduced measurement error in body mass index classification.

Conclusion

Sedentary behavior is common among university students in Yemen, with a substantial proportion reporting prolonged sitting of ≥ 7 h per day. Prolonged sedentary behavior was independently associated with enrollment in private universities, studying in scientific, engineering, or technical disciplines, smoking, and limited access to university physical activity facilities, whereas older age was associated with lower odds. These findings suggest that university-based strategies aimed at reducing sedentary time may be beneficial, including improving access to physical activity facilities, incorporating active breaks into academic settings, and promoting healthier lifestyle behaviors among students. Addressing sedentary behavior in this population may support long-term health promotion efforts in Yemen.
