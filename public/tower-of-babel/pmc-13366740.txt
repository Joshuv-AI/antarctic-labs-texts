Association between postcesarean delivery inpatient opioid consumption & post-discharge opioid usage: a prospective observational study
Authors: Ashley Veade, Alison Kosmacki, Sandy Hoang, Paige Keasler, Owen Hamilton, Jeannie Kelly, Ruizhi Huang, Fan Zhang, Alex Hincker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe United States continues to experience increasing morbidity and mortality from opioid misuse with prescription opioids. Prescription opioids account for 32% of opioid related deaths. Cesarean delivery is the most common surgery among females. This makes this patient population directly at risk for opioid misuse as 75% of opioid prescriptions are filled after a cesarean delivery. Creating a plan for optimizing postpartum opioid pain control while limiting narcotic usage is a must. Primary objective was to identify an association of inpatient opioid use and post-discharge opioid consumption after cesarean delivery (CD).MethodsThis prospective, observational study at a single academic tertiary hospital included patients undergoing CD between February to October of 2021. 142 patients met inclusion criteria with 110 (77%) patients completing the follow up phone call. Post-CD inpatient opioid consumption, discharge opioid prescription amount, and sociodemographics were recorded from the electronic medical record. Women with opioid use disorder, wound complication, peripartum hysterectomy, general anesthesia for delivery, or readmission for infection or surgery were excluded. Patients were then contacted by phone approximately two weeks post-discharge to assess the status of their opioid tablets and their subjective pain control. Opioid amounts were recorded as Oxycodone 5 milligram (mg) tablets. Primary outcome was the association between inpatient opioid use and post-discharge opioid consumption. Secondary outcomes included disposition of tablets at two weeks post-discharge. Outcomes were analyzed using multivariable logistic regression between quartiles of inpatient and post-discharge opioid consumption.ResultsAfter CD, inpatient opioid use predicted outpatient opioid use (r2 = 0.25; p < 0.001): patients in the highest quartile of inpatient use (≥ 13 tablets) consumed a mean of 16.6 tablets as inpatients and 13.1 tablets as outpatients. The lowest quartile of inpatient opioid users (≤ 5 tablets) consumed a mean of 1.0 tablet as inpatients and 1.7 tablets as outpatients (t = 18.1, p < 0.001 and t = 7.8, p < 0.001, respectively). 80/110 (73%) of patients had leftover opioids, with only 5 patients (6%) disposing of their tablets.ConclusionThe amount of tablets consumed post-CD as an inpatient is predictive of outpatient opioid consumption with minimal disposal of unused opioid tablets at two weeks post-discharge.Keywords: Opioid analgesics, Postpartum period, Cesarean section, Pain management, Acute pain

Background

In 2018, 46,802 Americans died from opioid-related deaths, with 32% due to prescription opioids [1]. In 2020, American obstetricians performed 3.6 million deliveries with 31.8% being cesarean sections [2]. Opioid prescriptions are filled by 27% of patients following vaginal delivery and 75% following cesarean delivery [3]. As such, postpartum outpatient opioids are one of the most common sources of narcotics [4]. One case-control study noted an incidence of 5.4 maternal deaths from opioid overdose per 100,000 with this being even higher among opioid use disorder patients: 118 per 100,000 [5]. Although this data is from those with public insurance, this continues to highlight the impact of opioids during the postpartum period. The CDC recommends 3 days or less treatment for acute pain with an opioid, with a maximum duration of 7 days [6]. This suggests that most opioids should be consumed while inpatient for postpartum patients. However, there is little data on utilizing inpatient postpartum opioid usage to guide discharge opioid prescribing. Unfortunately, best practice guidelines for outpatient post-delivery opioid prescribing are limited. The data available suggests that providers overprescribe discharge opioid prescriptions relative to inpatient use, which risks tablet surpluses and diversion [7]. For example, a retrospective study at our institution demonstrated that the median post-cesarean patient consumed 35 mg oxycodone as an inpatient but was prescribed 100 mg at discharge [8]. However, this review did not examine post-discharge opioid consumption or patient pain scores. Fully understanding how postpartum patients evaluate and manage their pain after discharge is critical to reduce excess opioids through accurate prescribing.

The primary objective of the current study is to identify the association between post-delivery inpatient and outpatient opioid consumption. Secondary aims include evaluating the number of tablets prescribed, consumed, and disposed of in two weeks post-discharge after cesarean delivery and quantifying patient perceived pain control in the first two week post-discharge.

Methods

This prospective, observational study took place at a single tertiary, academic hospital and included patients who were admitted postpartum following cesarean delivery from February thru October 2021. Women were eligible for inclusion in the study if they were greater than 18 years of age, underwent a live birth, were English speaking, and planned to receive postpartum care within the same hospital system to allow for consistent follow up care. Women were excluded if they had a medical contraindication to oxycodone and morphine, were taken to the operating room during their admission for any indication other than cesarean delivery or postpartum tubal ligation, underwent a cesarean hysterectomy, underwent general anesthesia for their cesarean delivery, were re-admitted to the hospital within 14 days after discharge for infection or surgical procedure, had pre-existing opioid use prior to delivery, or had a post-operative wound complication within 14 days after delivery. Opioid usage prior to delivery was defined as outpatient usage of opioids within 30 days of delivery or the filling of an opioid as documented within the Illinois or St. Louis County Prescription Drug Monitoring Program (PDMP) databases in that same interval, or a history of established opioid use disorder. Wound complications were defined as wound infections, seromas, or hematomas requiring surgical or medical intervention. The Washington University in St. Louis Human Research Protection Office approved this study (IRB # 202012045). Statistics were run utilizing R and SAS 9.4 statistical softwathereres for windows.

Eligible women were approached for enrollment in this study either during their antepartum stay on the labor and delivery floor or immediately postpartum. The records of those who were enrolled prior to delivery were reviewed postpartum to confirm they still met eligibility criteria. 18 patients were excluded after postpartum chart review. Enrolled patients consented to receive a phone call from the study team 14 days after delivery to assess the number of opioid tablets they had been prescribed, consumed, and disposed. Additionally, their perceived pain scores were assessed through a questionnaire. The medical records of all enrolled patients were also reviewed at time of delivery and 14 days post-discharge, and participant sociodemographic and clinical data were extracted. Sociodemographic and baseline clinical data included age, race, body mass index (BMI), parity, insurance payer, mode of delivery, length of hospital stay in days, and the discharge opioid prescriber (intern, resident or attending). The amount of inpatient opioids consumed postpartum was recorded, including both total amount and the amount consumed in the final 24 hours before discharge. As it was by far the most commonly administered opioid, consumption was quantified as 5-mg oxycodone tablets. Alternative forms of opioids prescribed were converted to morphine milliequivalents (MME) and then to 5-mg oxycodone tablets to allow for comparison. Opioids are available to patients as soon as the neuraxial anesthesia wears off, as soon as a few hours from delivery.

All post operative cesarean delivery patients also received non-narcotic adjuncts. At our major academic hospital, all cesarean delivery patients received 24 hours immediate post operative scheduled 30 milligrams (mg) intravenous (IV) ketorolac and scheduled two acetominophen 500 mg tablets every six hours as managed by Obstetrical Anesthesia. After 24 hours, they are transitioned from IV ketorolac to oral ibuprofen 600 mg tablets every six hours in addition to the scheduled acetaminophen. Cesarean delivery patients also received neuraxial anesthesia with long acting morphine (100mcg intrathecal or 3 mg epidural). This is our standard post operative non-narcotic pain control protocol. Gabapentin is not a routinely scheduled post cesarean delivery medication, but ordered when a patient is in need of additional pain medication.

Perceived pain scores at the time of the 14-day phone call were recorded in categories of “very poorly controlled,” “poorly controlled,” “somewhat controlled,” “well controlled,” and “very well controlled.” The final reported status of prescribed opioids were recorded as either consumed, saved, disposed of, or diverted.

Participant data was stratified into quartiles of either highest or lowest post-discharge opioid consumption. Sociodemographic and baseline clinical data were compared between the highest and lowest quartile post-discharge opioid consumers using chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression was utilized to analyze post-discharge opioid consumption by quartile of inpatient opioid consumption. Multivariable logistic regression was utilized to analyze mean amounts of opioid prescribed, consumed, and disposed of oxycodone tablets and perceived pain control at 2 weeks post-discharge, stratified by highest and lowest quartile of post-discharge opioid consumption. Two-sided p values of < = 0.05 were considered significant. Scatterplots were created and linear regression was performed to compare total inpatient opioid use to outpatient use, inpatient opioid use in the final 24 hours to outpatient use, and finally outpatient opioids prescribed to outpatient opioid use.

Results

In total, 195 patients were screened for eligibility for participation. After excluding screen-failed patients, 160 were approached for this post-discharge opioid study. 142 patients ultimately met inclusion criteria after cesarean delivery and were included in data analysis, with 110 (77%) patients completing the follow up post-discharge phone call assessment; enrollment is detailed in Fig. 1. Patient sociodemographics of all included patients were recorded by highest and lowest post-discharge opioid consumption in Table 1. There was no significant difference in patient characteristics between the highest and lowest post-discharge opioid consumption groups. Notably, post-cesarean delivery discharge prescribing was primarily performed by interns (68.4% of the time). Primary or repeat cesarean delivery and length of post-operative stay were not associated with post-discharge opioid usage.

Our primary aim was evaluating an association between post-delivery inpatient opioid consumption and outpatient opioid consumption. Results are demonstrated in Table 2 of the 110 patients who answered postpartum phone calls. We found a significant association between inpatient opioid usage and outpatient opioid consumption (p < 0.001). Among our highest quartile inpatient users, defined by consuming ≥ 13 tablets during inpatient post-operative stay (N = 30), more than 50% consumed 15 or more 5 mg oxycodone tablets in the post-discharge setting. This is compared to 79% of lowest quartile inpatient consumers, defined by consuming less than or equal to 5 tablets during inpatient stay (N = 34), consuming less than 5 tablets in the 2 week post-discharge setting. This association is also visually demonstrated in Fig. 2 which shows a significant correlation between total inpatient opioid consumption and total outpatient opioid consumption (r2 = 0.25).

Our secondary aims were to evaluate how many tablets were prescribed at discharge, consumed or diverted in the 2 weeks post-discharge, and saved or not disposed of at time of our call to the patient. These results are stratified by post-discharge opioid consumption quartiles demonstrated in Table 3. Overall, 136/142 (95.8%) of patients received an outpatient opioid prescription, and 131/136 (96.3%) of those filled the prescription. We found the mean number of 5 mg oxycodone tablets prescribed for the entire cohort was 19.49 (SD 4.99). Despite being prescribed a mean of 19.49 tablets, the mean number of tablets consumed by the cohort was 9.69 (SD 8.19), almost half of what was prescribed at time of discharge. Only 26/110 (23%) of patients consumed all opioids prescribed with no patients requiring an additional refill. Additionally, 16/21 (76%) of patients who did not consume any opioids as inpatients post-delivery as an inpatient received a discharge opioid prescription, with a mean prescription of 16 tablets; 13/16 (81%) of those filled this prescription. There was also notable interpatient variability between the highest and lowest quartile of outpatient opioid consumers regarding the amount outpatient opioid consumption. The highest quartile outpatient users (> 16 tablets) were prescribed an average of 22.7 (SD 4.46) tabs and consumed 20.5 (SD 3.90) tabs. In contrast, the lowest quartile outpatient users (< 2 tablets) were prescribed 17.9 (SD 6.56) tabs and consumed only 0.5 (SD 0.80) tablets.

The association between inpatient consumption and outpatient prescription is shown in Fig. 4. Inpatient opioid consumption was statistically significantly correlated with outpatient opioid prescription (r2 = 0.04, p 0.014). However, the amount to which prescriptions were titrated based on inpatient use was minimal. This is demonstrated by the lowest quartile of inpatient opioid users consuming an average of 1.8 tablets (SD 2.01) as inpatients and were prescribed an average of 15.3 (SD 7.89) tablets at discharge; Compared to the highest quartile of inpatient opioid users consumed an average of 17.8 tablets as inpatients (SD 5.73), and were prescribed an average of 22.8 tablets (SD 11.97) at time of discharge.

Inpatient opioid consumption by outpatient opioid prescription of 5 mg oxycodone tablets

The rate of opioid disposal was minimal among patients prescribed opioids. As demonstrated again in Table 3, at the time of our phone call 2 weeks post-discharge, the mean number of tablets for the entire cohort leftover and not disposed of was 7.9 (SD 7.71) 5 mg tablets of oxycodone. Of the tablets reported to be not consumed, the mean number of reported proper disposal of tablets for the cohort was 0.59 (SD 3.42). Overall, 80/110 (73%) of patients had leftover opioids, and only 5 patients (6%) disposed of their tablets.

Perceived pain control at 2 weeks post-discharge was found to be overall controlled for both highest quartile and lowest quartile of post-discharge opioid consumers, with few patients reporting “poorly” or “very poorly” controlled pain as seen in Table 4. However, perceived pain control was better in the lowest-quartile post-discharge opioid consumption patients than in the highest-quartile patients, (p = 0.001). It is notable that 50% of the highest quartile post-discharge opioid users felt their pain was only “somewhat controlled”.

Discussion

In this prospective study, inpatient opioid consumption after cesarean delivery was associated with post-discharge opioid use. Although discharge prescriptions were modestly higher among patients who used more opioids in the hospital, prescribing remained poorly calibrated to actual post-discharge need, particularly among low inpatient users. Most patients had leftover tablets at 2 weeks, and disposal was uncommon. Notably, few patients reported poorly controlled pain, suggesting that more individualized prescribing may reduce excess tablets.

Our findings are consistent with prior literature demonstrating opioid overprescribing after delivery, but they extend this work by prospectively examining opioid use 2 weeks after discharge in relation to inpatient consumption [7, 8, 10]. Excess prescribing in our cohort frequently resulted in leftover tablets with minimal disposal, highlighting the potential for unused opioids to remain in the community. Together, these findings support using inpatient opioid consumption to guide more precise, patient-centered prescribing after cesarean delivery.

Guidance for individualized opioid prescribing after cesarean delivery remains limited. Our findings suggest that stratifying discharge prescriptions by inpatient opioid consumption could reduce excess tablets while maintaining adequate pain control. In our cohort, this approach would have reduced prescribed tablets by 41.9%, decreasing the number of unused tablets available for diversion.

Additionally, multimodal analgesia remains an important component of post-cesarean pain management and may reduce opioid requirements. At our institution, patients receive scheduled acetaminophen and NSAIDs, with opioids reserved for breakthrough pain. Future studies should evaluate whether combining individualized discharge prescribing with structured multimodal counseling further reduces opioid exposure while maintaining adequate pain control.

Strengths of this study include the prospective nature, location of a major academic institution with a diverse patient population, and higher follow up phone call rate at 77%. There are limitations of this study to mention. First, our study focuses solely on English only speaking patients. However, one could argue current data demonstrates white, Non-hispanic patients have received the brunt of the opioid epidemic with prescription opioid misuse highest in this population from 1999 to 2018 [11]. Second, there was a lack of knowledge on why patients did not dispose of medication after discharge and limitation. Recall bias based on the nature of patient reporting opioid consumption at time of post partum telephone call at 2 weeks was a notable limitation. Attempts to limit this included having the patient have the bottle in hand and count the left over pills over the phone with us. Ultimately, the evaluation of ‘subjective pain’ can be affected by measurement bias as it is a subjective assessment and patients may vary on how they self assess their pain.

Conclusion

Inpatient opioid consumption after cesarean delivery was associated with post-discharge opioid use, yet discharge prescribing frequently exceeded patient need. Most patients had leftover tablets, and disposal was rare. These findings support using inpatient opioid use to individualize discharge prescriptions and reduce excess tablets available for diversion. Improved counseling on multimodal analgesia, when to seek reassessment for uncontrolled pain, and safe medication disposal may further optimize postpartum pain management and opioid stewardship.
