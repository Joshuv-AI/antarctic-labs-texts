Genetic diversity and trait associations in biofortified short-duration sweet potato (Ipomoea Batatas L.) genotypes
Authors: Moon Modak, Mst. Masuma Akter, Mahmuda Akhter Hasi, Sanzida Akter Trisha, Alvi Al Muhimine, Ebna Habib Md Shofiur Rahaman, Arif Hasan Khan Robin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSweet potato (Ipomoea batatas L.) is a nutrient-dense crop rich in carbohydrates, vitamins A and C, and dietary fiber, with broad adaptability. Biofortified cultivars containing high beta-carotene (> 8 mg/100 g) and anthocyanins (> 25 mg/100 g) are limited. This study aimed to evaluate genetic variability, trait associations, and yield performance among twenty biofortified short-duration sweet potato genotypes.MethodsGenotypes were evaluated for morphological, physiological, nutritional, and yield traits in a randomized complete block design with three replications. Data were analyzed using ANOVA, correlation, PCA, cluster analysis, and genetic parameter estimation.ResultsSignificant genotypic differences (p < 0.001) were observed for the most traits, including number and mass of total and economic tuberous roots. The greatest yield was recorded in genotype BAU Sweetpotato-5 (44.8 t ha⁻¹), which was followed by G-54 (27.1 t ha⁻¹) and Amtoli-3 (17.3 t ha⁻¹). Yield was positively correlated with root number and mass. A total of 79% of variation was described by the first five components, according to principal component analysis (PCA), with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 47%. The principal component (PC) scores of genotypes were separated largely by higher, negative coefficients of number and mass of both total and economic root traits, nutritional traits — % of glucose, fructose, sucrose and lower, negative coefficients of two physiological traits —leaf vapor pressure (LVP), vapor pressure deficit (VPD); and number of leaves per vine. Cluster analysis grouped genotypes into four distinct clusters with cluster II representing high-yielding genotypes and cluster I representing moderately carotene-enriched genotypes. High heritability and genetic advance of mean for yield (89% and 130%), economic root mass, (87% and 135.6%) leaf area (97.6% and75%, respectively) indicate their strong potential for selection.ConclusionThe study revealed substantial genetic variability among the evaluated sweetpotato genotypes, providing valuable information for future breeding, genetic studies, and conservation efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07754-6.

Background

Sweet potato, Ipomoea batatas (L.) Lam, is a significant tuberous root crop that is highly valued for its culinary and industrial use worldwide [1, 2]. It belongs to the Convolvulaceae family and is the only species in the Ipomoea genus that produces edible tuberous roots, which are utilized as a raw material in multiple sectors and as a staple food [1, 2]. Globally, it is regarded as one of the most health-promoting and economically viable food crop. Beyond its agronomic advantages, sweet potato is highly valued for its nutritional qualities, including a low glycemic index (< 41) and elevated levels of beta-carotene, vitamin C, flavonoids, and phenolic acids, which collectively contribute to its significant health benefits [3, 4] Worldwide, it is used in bread, breakfast dishes, French fries, syrup, starch, drinks, and as a significant ingredient in animal feed [5]. It is the seventh most significant food crop in the world, behind rice, wheat, potatoes, maize, barley, and cassava [6, 7]. The production of sweet potatoes worldwide varied from 89 to 92 million tons between 2018 and 2021, with Asian nations accounting for 61.5% to 66.6% and African nations for 28.6% to 33.7% of the total production [8]. According to a 2017 assessment by the International Potato Center, sweet potatoes rank third among essential food crops in seven countries in central and eastern Africa, fourth among priority crops in six South African countries, and eighth among four West African countries [9].

In Sub-Saharan African countries such as Malawi, Rwanda, and Uganda, sweet potato, being a staple crop, provides the highest caloric yield per hectare per day compared to other crops [10]. The nations that include Asia and Africa, as well as the United States, are among the top producers of sweet potatoes in [11].

With a harvest of almost 51 million tons of tuberous roots, China (mainland) emerged as the greatest producer of Sweetpotatoes in 2019, according to agricultural data released by the Food and Agriculture Organization [11]. At present, sweet potatoes are cultivated in tropical regions as well as in temperate zones with warmer climates [12]. Asia stands as the foremost producer globally, yielding over 125 million tons of fresh roots annually [13].

Sweet potato, locally known as “Misti Alu,” is widely cultivated by resource-limited communities in the riverine regions of Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, India, and the Maldives [5, 14]. The production of sweet potatoes in Bangladesh has been rising gradually [15, 16]. It is the fourth most significant source of carbohydrates in Bangladesh. In 2022, 304 thousand tons of sweet potatoes were produced, an increase of 8.55% from the year before [15, 16]. Between 2018 and 19 and 2020–21, the agricultural area expanded by 13.2%, from 23 to 27 thousand hectares, and total production increased by 15.7%, reaching 279.8 thousand metric tons [17]. Under ideal circumstances—such as optimal soil fertility, adequate irrigation, balanced nutrient management, and effective pest and disease control—up to 40 tons per hectare yield can be achieved, although the national average yield remains below 10.5 tons per hectare in Bangladesh [18]. Yield in the farmers’ fields remain low, primarily due to reliance on local, unimproved genotypes [19]. By contrast, yields could be greatly increased by adopting new clones or superior varieties [20].

In an effort to alleviate water constraint and lessen reliance on rice farming, the Bangladeshi government is encouraging the production of alternative crops, such sweet potatoes, on unused land [21]. The growing demand for nutrient-dense food has driven efforts to cultivate sweet potatoes on underutilized land in Bangladesh [22] Genetic variability studies are essential in a breeding program because they facilitate the selection of parents with desirable traits such as improved nutritional quality (e.g., higher sugar content, starch composition, or protein content), enhanced insect and disease resistance, and increased yield potential [22]. For breeding to be successful, the best parents for hybridization must have genetically varied genotypes [23]. According to recent studies, genotype selection and environmental factors are crucial for the development of sweet potatoes [24, 25]. The importance of choosing genetically distant parents when breeding different crops has also been highlighted by genetic diversity studies, which look at both qualitative and quantitative traits [26, 27].

Orange- and purple-fleshed sweet potato varieties are still scarce in Bangladesh, in part because they take longer to mature—typically 120 to 150 days [9]. A sweet potato variety called BARI Mistialu-17 was developed by the Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute (BARI) which is purple flesh and high in anthocyanins. However, a study by Alam et al. [25] found that BARI Mistialu-17 takes 110–120 days for tuberous root growth, However, farmers want crops that are high-yielding, short-duration, and climate-adaptable, and a variety that fits in between two rice growing seasons - aman and boro [28, 29].

To support the development of superior sweet potato cultivars through effective and focused breeding strategies, this study aims to identify genotypes that combine rapid crop maturity with enhanced nutritional content and robust agronomic traits. The desired outcomes include the development of climate-resilient, nutrient-dense cultivars that promote sustainable agriculture and improve food and nutritional security in Bangladesh. Accordingly, the objectives of the study are to identify the genetic variability among biofortified short-duration sweetpotato genotypes based on morpho-physiological and yield traits, determine the trait associations contributing to yield and related components, and evaluate the nutritional contents of the studied genotypes.

Materials and methods

Experimental site

The research was conducted during the growing season from September to November 2024 at the Char land (Riverine Island) of the Old Brahmaputra River. The experimental site is located at approximately 24.7240° N latitude and 90.4374° E longitude, with an elevation of about 14 m above sea level. The Char land features sandy-loam soil with excellent drainage, natural fertility from river deposits, and abundant sunlight, providing optimal conditions for healthy root development and high yields.

During the experimental period, the average temperature ranged from approximately 26.15 °C in November to 30.6 °C in September. Relative humidity varied from 66.6% to 80.5%, while monthly rainfall decreased steadily from 114.6 mm to 17.3 mm (Table S1). These characteristic and representative climatic conditions helped ensure uniform growth of the individual genotypes within the trial.

Experimental materials

This study examined morphological, physiological, nutritional, and yield-contributing characteristics of 20 sweet potato genotypes. Three check varieties—BARI Sweetpotato-15, BARI Sweetpotato-17, and BARI Sweetpotato-18 were collected from the Bangladesh Agricultural Research Institute (BARI) [29] and one check variety BAU Sweetpotato-5 was collected from Bangladesh Agricultural University (BAU). One Japanese sweet potato genotype and three locally collected varieties—Amtoli-1, Amtoli-2, and Amtoli-3—were also included. The study assessed twelve CIP-bred sweet potato genotypes (Appendix S1). A comprehensive overview of the sweet potato genotypes used in this investigation is presented in Table 1 and illustrated in Figure S1.

Experimental design and techniques

The experiment was carried out using a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications, comprising a total of 60 plots (Table S2). Each plot measured 2.5 × 2 m. Vines were planted at a spacing of 40 cm between plants and 60 cm between plots to ensure proper growth and management. Every plot contained 24 vines, each about 30 cm in length with 3 to 4 nodes. Standard agronomic practices were followed, including irrigation on the 10th, 30th, and 60th days after planting. We thank the reviewer for this observation. Weeding was carried out three times, on the 20th, 30th, and 45th days after planting, to maintain weed-free conditions, and earthing-up was performed on the 30th and 45th and to support the vine development and tuberous root formation. The crop was harvested on the 90th day, following the maturity timeline of the early-maturing genotypes under study.

Data collection

The sweet potato genotypes were evaluated for twenty quantitative characters representing physiological, morphological, yield-contributing, and nutritional traits (Table 2). Chlorophyll content (as determined by a SPAD meter), stomatal conductance, leaf vapor pressure, vapor pressure deficit, and electron transfer rate were among the physiological characteristics that were observed using LI-600 [30]. porometer. Morphological and yield-related traits were recorded following the CIP/IBPGR Descriptors for Sweetpotato developed by the International Potato Center (CIP) and the International Board for Plant Genetic Resources (IBPGR). The morphological characteristics including vine length, number of leaves per vine, leaf length, leaf width, leaf area, and total mass of shoots per plot were measured (Table 2). In accordance with marketability requirements, yield-related information, such as yield (t ha− 1) and number of total and economic roots and root mass per plot, were recorded at harvest. The content of glucose, fructose, and sucrose in fresh tuber samples were determined using three separate digital refractometers HI96803, HI96804 and HI96801 [33] respectively (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Prior to conducting variance component analysis and other statistical procedures, data were screened for outliers using Grubbs’ test in Minitab 20 [34] and further screened whether any of those identified outliers have any impact on significance of a particular trait. The normality of each trait was then assessed using the Anderson–Darling test, and all traits were found to follow a normal distribution (p >0.05). These steps ensured that the data met the assumptions of normality and were suitable for subsequent statistical analyses. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using Minitab 20 statistical software [34] Pearson correlation analysis was performed in RStudio using the Hmisc package [35]. Cluster analysis were carried by ward method of clustering [36] and principal component analysis (PCA) were carried out using the cluster [37] and factoextra [38] packages, respectively. Genetic parameter estimation was done with the variability package [39]. Data visualization was achieved using ggplot2 [40], reshape [41], dplyr [42], and RColorBrewer [43] packages in RStudio. All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 4.4.1) [44].

Results

Analysis of variance revealed highly (p < 0.001) significant genotypic differences for all morphological traits, including chlorophyll content, nodes per vine, vine length, number of leaves per vine, leaf length, leaf width and shoot mass (Table 3). The yield-contributing traits such as number of total roots, number of economic roots, mass of total roots, mass of economic roots and yield (t ha− 1) also exhibited highly (p < 0.001) significant variation (Table 3). Among the nutritional traits, glucose and sucrose showed significant genotypic differences (Table 3).

Genotypic differences and trait variability

The analysis revealed significant genotypic variation (p < 0.05) for chlorophyll content, number of vines per plant, vine length, number of leaves per vine, leaf length, shoot mass, glucose, sucrose, number of total roots, total root mass, total number of economic roots, economic root mass and yield ton per hectare (Table S3, Figure S2, Figure S3, and Fig. 1). Chlorophyll content ranged from 41.50 (G-138) to 65.63 (BAU Sweetpotato-5) (p < 0.05, Figure S2), while electron transport rate varied from 89.25 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (G-47) to 225.71 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (G-287) (Table S3). Stomatal conductance, leaf vapor pressure, and vapor pressure deficit ranged between 0.38 and 0.73 mol m⁻² s⁻¹, 3.23–3.86 kPa and 1.01–1.63 kPa, respectively (Table S3). Glucose, fructose, and sucrose contents ranged from 2.20 to 7.90%, 2.17–6.63%, and 2.17–8.23%, respectively (Table S3, Figure S2). Among morphological traits, the number of vines per plant, vine length, and number of leaves per vine significantly varied from 5.00 to 8.50 m, 2.09–4.45 m, and 15.00–25.33 m, respectively (p < 0.05, Table S3, Figure S2). Leaf area ranged widely, from 113.36 m² (G-276) to 527.86 m² (BAU Sweetpotato-5) (Fig. 1), while shoot mass spanned from 29.0 kg (JAPAN) to 195.7 kg (BARI sweetpotato 17) (P < 0.05, Table S3, Figure S2). Significant difference was observed for total root yield plot− 1, economic root yield plot− 1, and estimated yield ha− 1. The number of total roots per plot varied markedly, with BARI sweetpotato 17 producing the highest 196 roots, followed by G-193 producing 140 roots and BAU Sweetpotato-5 producing 136 roots per plot. By contrast, Japan and G-310 produced only 29 and 32 roots per plot respectively (p < 0.05, Table S3, Fig. 1). Similarly, total root mass per plot differed significantly, with BAU Sweetpotato-5 weighing 22 kg and G-54 weighing 14 kg also for economic root traits, BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-54 produced the highest numbers of marketable roots 116 and 94 roots per plot respectively, whereas Japan and Amtoli-2 produced lower number of roots 11 and 13 per plot, respectively (p < 0.05, Table S3, Fig. 1). The maximum economic root mass was also recorded in BAU Sweetpotato-5 weighing 19 kg, followed by G-54, G-193, and G-276, while G-310, BARI sweetpotato 18, Amtoli-1, Amtoli-2, and Japan weighing 1.0 kg (p < 0.05, Table S3, Fig. 1). Significant variation was also observed in total yield per hectare (p < 0.05), with BAU Sweetpotato-5 (45 t ha⁻¹) achieving the highest yield, followed by G-54 (27 t ha⁻¹) and Amtoli-3 (17 t ha⁻¹), whereas Amtoli-2 and Japan (2 t ha⁻¹) exhibited the lowest (p < 0.05, Table S3, Figure S3).

The mean values ranged from 0.5 mol m⁻² s⁻¹ for stomatal conductance to 250 cm² for leaf area, highlighting the broad variation across the evaluated genotypes (Table 4). The minimum and maximum values also indicated substantial diversity for yield (1.4–49 t ha⁻¹), number of economic roots (8–130 roots plant⁻¹), total number of roots (19–252 roots plant⁻¹), leaf area (113–527.5 m²), and electron transport rate (42.9–296 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) (Table 4). Traits such as glucose content, sucrose content, number of economic roots, mass of economic roots, and yield exhibited high coefficients of variation (CV) (Table 4). For example, glucose content (Glu, CV = 55.5%) and sucrose content (Sru, CV = 43.4%) showed considerable variation, while mass of economic roots (CV = 84.6%) and yield (CV = 79.5%) displayed extreme variability. Trait association

Pearson’s correlation analysis confirmed that the yield-related traits — total number of roots, total root mass, number of economic roots, and mass of economic roots per plot — were positively and significantly correlated with each other (Fig. 2). The morphological parameter leaf area, shoot mass significantly positively correlated with total and economic root mass and yield ton per hectare also physiological parameter chlorophyll content significantly positively correlated with these yield contributing traits (Fig. 2). Leaf area significantly and positively correlated with number of vines per plant (r = 0.54) (Fig. 2). Vapor pressure deficit and electron transport rate tends to rise accordingly as they positively correlated (Fig. 2). Similarly, nutritional traits such as glucose, fructose, sucrose exhibited strong positive correlations among themselves (Fig. 2). Stomatal conductance showed a significant negative correlation with leaf vapor pressure (r= −0.54), vapor pressure deficit (r= −0.71) and electron transport rate (r= −0.58) (Fig. 2).

In contrast, traits such as vapor pressure deficit (4.4%), chlorophyll content (9.6%), and leaf vapor pressure (2.2%) had low GCV, pointing to greater environmental influence (Table 7).

Heritability estimates varied widely across traits. Leaf area (98%), leaf length (92%), leaf width (88%), mass of total roots (90%), yield per hectare (90%), mass of economic root (86.8%) and chlorophyll content (80%) exhibited high heritability (Table 7). Moderate heritability was accounted for number of economic roots (70%), shoot mass (68%), and number of total root (60%) (Table 7). By contrast, low heritability was found for vapor pressure deficit (4.7%), electron transport rate (8.7%), leaf vapor pressure (9.3%), stomatal conductance (17%), fructose (11%) (Table 7).

Genetic advance as a percentage of the mean (GAM) reinforced the above trends. The highest GAM values were recorded for mass of total roots (135.6%), highlighting their potential for rapid genetic improvement (Table 7). High GAM was also observed for yield per hectare and mass of total root (130.1%), number of economic roots (74.6%), leaf area (75.1%), and total number of roots (52%) (Table 7). On the other hand, traits such as leaf vapor pressure (1.4%), vapor pressure deficit (2%), fructose (8.0%), and electron transport rate (8.1%) showed very low GAM, indicating limited scope of selection (Table 7).

Discussion

Phenotypic variation and trait-based selection: identifying high-yielding genotypes

A comprehensive phenotypic assessment of 20 sweetpotato genotypes revealed considerable variation across morphological, physiological, nutritional, and yield-related traits (Table 3; Table S3; Figure S2 and S3; Fig. 1). This genotypic diversity indicates the presence of a broad genetic base, which is advantageous either for selection or using in a breeding program aimed at improving key traits. Traits such as total root yield, shoot mass, number of roots, chlorophyll content, and percent glucose showed significant variation among genotypes, highlighting their importance in selecting superior sweetpotato genotypes [45, 46]. Laurie et al. [47], suggested that traits like shoot mass and chlorophyll content reflect plant vigor and stress tolerance, making them valuable indicators for selecting resilient genotypes.

BAU Sweetpotato-5 emerged as a standout performer compared to all other genotypes (Fig. 1), as shown by its distinct position in both the PCA biplot (Fig. 5) and grouping in Cluster II (Fig. 3 and Figure S3). Thus, the multivariate analyses confirmed a strong association of BAU Sweetpotato-5 with its higher total root mass, number of roots, and yield per hectare (Fig. 5, Table S3). The clear separation of BAU Sweetpotato-5 at the negative axis of PC1 in the PCA biplot further support its association with yield-contributing traits such as total root mass, number of economic roots, economic root mass and yield per hectare (Fig. 5). The heatmap-based clustering (Fig. 4) indicated that BAU Sweetpotato-5 has a distinct grouping pattern, which corresponds with its higher mean values for chlorophyll content, vine length, and shoot biomass (Table S3; Figure S2). These results indicated that the higher vine length and chlorophyll content of BAU Sweetpotato-5 increases the photosynthetic area and possibly also efficiency that in turn enhances yield-contributing traits such as root number and mass. These traits contribute to robust source-sink dynamics and improved photosynthetic efficiency, resulting in superior yield potential [47–50].

Trait interrelationships with yield, stress tolerance, and nutritional value in sweet potato

The correlation network among key physiological, morphological, and nutritional traits provided insight into how specific trait interacted to drive yield gain (Fig. 2). Positive correlations between yield, total root mass, and number of roots confirm these traits as primary determinants of economic output, supporting the data presented in Table 4. Conversely, genotypes producing a high number of small, non-marketable tubers (< 30 g) contributed minimally to total yield, emphasizing the importance of selecting for root size in breeding programs [51, 52].

From a physiological perspective, the correlation (r = 0.96) between vapor pressure deficit and leaf vapor pressure suggests effective stomatal regulation (Fig. 2)—a trait linked to enhanced photosynthetic stability under moderate stress [53, 54]. The out-performer genotype BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-54 in terms of yield also maintained high stomatal conductance and chlorophyll content (Table S3, Figure S2). Similar physiological advantages have been reported in high-yielding genotypes by Ling et al. [55], reinforcing the importance of these traits in breeding for resilience.

Cluster analysis revealed distinct groups with specific trait profiles (Fig. 4, Table S4). Cluster III and cluster II combined high sugar containing genotypes with higher number of storage roots, indicating dual potential for yield and nutritional improvement. Selection of genotypes that express both high yield and superior nutritional quality offers a twofold advantage—producers benefit from higher marketable output, while consumers gain access to more nutritious food. This dual benefit aligns with the priorities of modern breeding programs that aim to enhance food security and health outcomes simultaneously [47, 56]. The positive correlation among glucose, fructose, and sucrose shows that these sugars work together to maintain stable sweetness in sweetpotato plants (Fig. 2). Lewthwaite et al. [57] reported that a balanced glucose-to-sucrose ratio supports osmotic adjustment under stress might be plausible for the genotypes under Cluster III. By explicitly exploring interrelationships among all morpho-physiological and nutritional traits in PCA and cluster patterns (Figs. 2 and 4), this study thus identified promising genotypes and traits.

Multivariate analyses reveal selection priorities and genetic advancement

The consistent results obtained from the cluster analysis, PCA, and post-hoc analysis for mean performance (Fig. 1 Tables S3, Figure S2, Figure S3) confirms that genotypic divergence observed through multivariate analysis is biologically meaningful. These insights guide the selection of genetically diverse, high-performing parents to accelerate genetic advancement in sweetpotato breeding. Hierarchical clustering differentiated five distinct genotype groups, revealing substantial genetic divergence between Clusters II and IV (Fig. 4, Table S4). Cluster II was distinguished by elevated nutritional traits and high root number, whereas the genotypes of Cluster IV (containing the genotype G-54) accounted for higher leaves per vine, shoot mass, and vine number while the cluster II separated high yielding genotypes BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-54 (Figs. 1 and 4, Figure S3). This wide divergence supports potential for heterosis breeding through strategic hybridization, as proposed in earlier root crop studies [58, 59]. Trait-based clustering closely corresponded with the PCA grouping, confirming consistency between distance- and variance-based approaches. Principal Component 1 (PC1; 27.94% variance) was primarily influenced by yield-contributing attributes, while Principal Component 2 (PC2; 19%) was governed by morphology- and physiology-related traits (Fig. 5). The congruence between PCA and clustering validates the robustness of the multivariate approach in distinguishing genotypes with distinct physiological and agronomic profiles. This trend reinforces their value as high-yielding selections with superior assimilate partitioning efficiency. Conversely, genotypes with higher PC2 scores showed vigorous vegetative growth with longer vines, larger leaf area, and higher chlorophyll content those traits generally enhance photosynthetic efficiency and help the plants adapt to moderate abiotic stresses [47, 60].

Such canopy-related attributes can indirectly sustain yield stability by maintaining higher source–sink balance during stress episodes, supporting the integration of physiological traits in breeding strategies for resilience. These insights guide the selection of genetically diverse, high-performing parents to accelerate genetic advancement in future sweetpotato breeding.

Genetic diversity through multivariate approaches for breeding

Substantial variability observed across yield-contributing and nutritional traits demonstrated rich genetic diversity among the evaluated sweetpotato genotypes. The consistent pattern of phenotypic coefficient of variation (PCV) exceeding genotypic coefficient of variation (GCV) indicated the influence of environmental factors on trait expression. However, the relatively narrow PCV–GCV differences for traits such as total root mass, economic root mass, and yield ton per hectare (Table 7) suggest a strong genetic basis and reduced environmental interference, signifying their reliability for direct phenotypic selection. Similar findings in cassava, taro, and yam show that traits with small PCV–GCV differences are largely heritable and easily improved through selection [61–63].

High GCV and PCV estimates for traits related to root number and mass further confirm the presence of exploitable genetic variation, implying their value as primary targets for yield improvement in sweetpotato. In contrast, moderate variability for sucrose, glucose, and leaf area indicates that selection may yield gradual but consistent improvement, particularly under optimized management and multi-environment evaluation. The high heritability values estimated for yield, chlorophyll content, and leaf area (Table 7) emphasize a predominant genetic influence on these traits [64]. Johnson et al. [65]emphasized that heritability alone does not guarantee genetic gain. Therefore, the concurrent observation of high heritability coupled with high genetic advance as a percentage of the mean (GAM) for total root mass, yield ton per hectare, number of total roots, number of economic roots, and leaves indicates the predominance of additive gene action (Table 7). Such traits are expected to respond effectively to selection, making them prime candidates for improving both productivity and physiological efficiency in sweet potato breeding programs.

Balancing genetic potential and environmental effects in selection of physiological traits

Chlorophyll content exhibited high heritability and moderate GAM (Table 7). Chlorophyll content is a key indicator of photosynthetic capacity and overall plant vigor, which directly influences crop productivity [66]. Chlorophyll levels may also be influenced by environmental factors such as light intensity, temperature, and nutrient availability, which may cause variation in chlorophyll content independent of genetic factors. Therefore, while genetic improvement for chlorophyll content is feasible, environmental conditions must also be carefully managed to optimize its expression and maximize yield potential. Stomatal conductance showed moderate heritability and genetic advance (Table 7), indicating partial environmental influence but still amenable to improve through selection. Given its role in gas exchange and water-use regulation, Stomatal conductance is a valuable trait for breeding under stress-prone conditions [9]. In contrast, leaf vapor pressure and vapor pressure deficit exhibited low heritability and genetic advance along with large PCV–GCV gaps, highlighting their high environmental sensitivity and limited breeding value [47, 60]. Electron transport rate showed moderate variability and heritability, suggesting its selection potential, although environmental effects remain influential [67]. Overall, chlorophyll and stomatal conductance emerge as promising physiological indicators for enhancing photosynthetic performance and stress resilience, whereas leaf vapor pressure and vapor pressure deficit appear unreliable as selection criteria due to their strong environmental dependency.

Conclusions

This study revealed substantial genotypic variability among sweetpotato genotypes across morphological, nutritional, and yield-related traits, underscoring the genetic richness within the evaluated materials. The consistent superior performance of BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-54 confirms the existence of distinctly high-performing genotypes with desirable yield and quality attributes. The high heritability and genetic advance observed for leaf area, total root mass, and number of economic roots emphasize the strong genetic control and potential for improvement of these traits. Trait association analysis revealed positive correlation among key yield and nutritional traits, indicating these traits combinedly contribute to superior performance. Cluster analysis further distinguished genotypes based on overall trait performance, with BAU Sweetpotato-5 and G-54 (Cluster II) exhibiting the highest leaf area, leaf length, leaf width, yield per hectare, number and mass of total roots, number and mass of economic roots, number of vines per plant, and chlorophyll content. Integrating these analyses enabled identifying promising genotypes with desirable combinations of yield, quality, and nutritional attributes. Collectively, these findings advance current understanding of genetic diversity and trait dynamics in sweet potato, providing a valuable foundation for future genetic improvement and varietal development efforts.
