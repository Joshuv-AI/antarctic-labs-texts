A note on intrinsic topologies of groups Thanks: The research of the first named author was funded in whole by the Austrian Science Fund FWF [10.55776/ESP399].
S. Bardyla, L. Elliott, J. D. Mitchell, and Y. Péresse

Abstract
. We investigate topologies on groups which arise naturally from their algebraic structure, including the Frechét-Markov, Hausdorff-Markov, and various kinds of Zariski topologies. Answering a question by Dikranjan and Toller, we show that there exists a countable abelian group in which no bounded version of the Zariski topology coincides with the full Zariski topology. Complementing a recent result by Goffer and Greenfeld, we show that on any group with no algebraicity the semigroup Zariski topology is hyperconnected and hence, in many cases, is distinct from the group Zariski topology. Finally, we show that on the symmetric groups, the semigroup Hausdorff-Markov topology coincides with the topology of pointwise convergence.

1.
Introduction

Recall that
a group topology
for a group
G
is any topology
\tau
on
G
under which multiplication and inversion are continuous in
(G,\tau)
. The symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
on a set
X
has a natural group topology, called the
topology of pointwise convergence
, which it inherits as a subspace of the Cartesian product
X^{X}
under the usual Tychonoff product topology, where every copy of
X
is given the discrete topology. The topology of pointwise convergence is defined in terms of the underlying set
X
, but it coincides with the group Zariski topology for
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
which is defined below purely in terms of abstract algebraic properties. In this sense, the topology of pointwise convergence is ‘intrinsic’ to
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
as an abstract group.
In this paper, we consider topologies, such as Zariski topologies, which arise from the algebraic properties of any abstract group. In the rest of this section, we state our main results including the necessary definitions and relevant context. Sections
2,3
and
4
contain the proofs of our results.

We compose function from left to right and adopt the convention that
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
.
Let
G
be a group. A function
f:G\rightarrow G
is called a
group polynomial of degree

n
if
(x)f=a_{0}x^{\varepsilon_{1}}a_{1}\dots x^{\varepsilon_{n}}a_{n}
, where
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\in G
and
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
for all
i\leq n
. We consider constant functions to be group polynomials of degree
0
.

Definition 1.1
.

For any group
G
the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
on
G
is generated by the sets
\{x\in G:(x)f\neq 1_{G}\}
, where
f
is a group polynomial.

The group Zariski topology was introduced by Markov
[
24
]
in order to describe countable nontopologizable groups. Recall that a group
G
is called
nontopologizable
if the only Hausdorff topology on
G
which makes the group operations continuous is discrete. Markov showed that a countable group
G
is nontopologizable if and only if the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is discrete. A topology
\tau
on a semigroup
S
is called
shift-continuous
, if for every
a\in S
the shifts
l_{a}:S\rightarrow S
,
s\mapsto as
and
r_{a}:S\rightarrow S
,
s\mapsto sa
are continuous.
Since for every group
G
the topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is shift-continuous (see e.g.
[
14
]
), we have that
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is discrete if and only if there exist group polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
such that
\{1_{G}\}=\bigcap_{i\leq n}\{x\in G:(x)f_{i}\neq 1_{G}\}
. It follows that the discreteness of the group Zariski topology on
G
is witnessed by the group polynomials of some bounded degree. This perhaps inspired Banakh and Protasov
[
2
]
to introduce the following weaker versions of the group Zariski topology.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
G
be a group. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)
be the topology on
G

generated by the sets
\{x\in G:(x)f\neq 1_{G}\}
, where
f
is a group polynomial of degree at most
n
.

It is clear that for each group
G
,
\mathfrak{Z}_{0}^{\pm}(G)
is the anti-discrete topology,
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{Z}_{m}^{\pm}(G)
whenever
n\leq m
, and
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)
forms a basis for
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
.
The arguments above allow us to reformulate Markov’s theorem as follows: a countable group
G
is nontopologizable if and only if
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)
is discrete for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, for a nontopologizable group
G
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
. This observation serves as a motivation for the following question posed in
[
8
, Question 2.4]
.

Question 1.3

(Dikranjan, Toller)
.

Is it true that for every group
G
there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{n}(G)
?

The group Zariski topology has its natural counterpart for semigroups.
Let
S
be a semigroup. A function
f:S\rightarrow S
is called a
semigroup polynomial of degree

n
if
(x)f=a_{0}xa_{1}\dots xa_{n}
for some
a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\in S^{1}
, where
S^{1}=S
if
S
contains a unit or
S^{1}=S\cup\{1\}
otherwise.
Just as in groups, constant functions are semigroup polynomials of degree
0
.

Definition 1.4
.

For any semigroup
S
the semigroup Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}(S)
on
S
is generated by the sets
\{x\in S:(x)f\neq(x)g\}
, where
f
and
g
are semigroup polynomials.

.

The semigroup Zariski topology admits a similar stratification as the group Zariski topology.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
S
be a semigroup. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(S)
be the topology on
S

generated by the sets
\{x\in S:(x)f\neq(x)g\}
, where
f
and
g
are semigroup polynomials of degree at most
n
.

It is easy to check that for each group
G
we have
\mathfrak{Z}(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
. Let
G
be a commutative group. Observe that group polynomials on
G
have the form
(x)f=ax^{n}
, where
a\in G
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Since for each negative integer
n
,
\{x\in G:ax^{n}\neq 1_{G}\}=\{x\in G:a^{-1}x^{|n|}\neq 1_{G}\}
, it follows that
\mathfrak{Z}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
. Fix any semigroup polynomials
(x)f=ax^{n}
and
(x)g=bx^{m}
on
G
, where
a,b\in G
and
n,m\leq k
. Put
(x)h=ab^{-1}x^{n-m}
and observe that
h
is a group polynomial of degree
\leq k
. Note that

\{x\in G:(x)f\neq(x)g\}=\{x\in G:(x)h\neq 1_{G}\}
.
At this point it is easy to see that
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{n}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this paper we obtain the following answer to Question
1.3
.

Theorem 1.6
.

There exists a countable commutative group
G
such that
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G)
is properly contained in
\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G)
for every non-negative integers
n<m
. In particular,
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\mathfrak{Z}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)\neq\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{n}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G).

The following question appeared in
[
14
, Question 2.8]
.

Question 1.7

(Elliott, Jonušas, Mesyan, Mitchell, Morayne, Péresse)
.

Does for every group
G
the semigroup Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}(G)
coincide with the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
?

Recently, Goffer and Greenfeld
[
18
]
answered Question
1.7
in the negative by constructing a countable group
G
such that
\mathfrak{Z}(G)\neq\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
. Although their example is quite technical, as its construction required small cancellation theory.

In this paper, we unveil a whole class of examples of groups with distinct group Zariski and semigroup Zariski topologies. By
\operatorname{Sym}_{\omega}(X)
we denote the subgroup of the symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
which consists of elements with finite support.

Theorem 1.8
.

Let
X
be an infinite set and
G
be a group such that
\operatorname{Sym}_{\omega}(X)\subseteq G\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Then
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{4}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)\neq\mathfrak{Z}(G)
.

Theorem
1.8
is based on the following two key theorems. The first one appeared in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 1.9

(Banakh, Guran, Protasov)
.

Let
X
be an infinite set and
G
be a group such that
\operatorname{Sym}_{\omega}(X)\subseteq G\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Then
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{4}(G)=\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is the topology of pointwise convergence. In particular,
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is Tychonoff and totally disconnected.

The formulating of the second key theorem requires two more definitions.

Definition 1.10
.

A topological space
X
is called
hyperconnected
if the intersection of any two open nonempty subsets of
X
is nonempty.

Note that no infinite hyperconnected space is Hausdorff.

Definition 1.11
.

For an infinite set
X
a subgroup
G
of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is said to have
no algebraicity
if for all finite
S\subseteq X
and
q\in X\setminus S
, the set

\{(q)g:g\in G\hbox{ such that }(x)g=x\hbox{ for all }x\in S\}
is infinite.

It is easy to check that the class of groups with no algebraicity includes all dense subgroups of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
with respect to the topology of pointwise convergence and, as such, all subgroups of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
that contain
\operatorname{Sym}_{\omega}(X)
; the automorphism group of the following relational structures: the random graph,
(\mathbb{Q},\leq)
, or the random poset
[
27
]
; any group with a Rubin action on an infinite space
[
4
]
, in particular the Thompson groups
F,T,V
, Grigorchuk’s group, and the homeomorphism groups of the following spaces: the Cantor set, the Hilbert cube, or any nonempty manifold of dimension at least
1
(see
[
7
,
19
]
for background). The second key theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.12
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
with no algebraicity. Then
\mathfrak{Z}(G)
is hyperconnected.

It is easy to see that if a topology
\tau_{1}
is contained in a topology
\tau_{2}
and
\tau_{2}
is hyperconnected, then so is
\tau_{1}
. Also, note that
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{3}(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{Z}(G)
for each group
G
. Thus, Theorem
1.12
implies the following.

Corollary 1.13
.

Let
G
be a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
with no algebraicity. Then
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}_{3}(G)
is hyperconnected and thus non-discrete.

Corollary
1.13
implies a partial solution of the following question posed in
[
8
, Question 2.2]
.

Question 1.14

(Dikranjan, Toller)
.

Let
G
be an infinite group. Is
\mathfrak{Z}_{3}^{\pm}(G)
always non-discrete?

Also, Theorem
1.12
and Corollary
1.13
establish the sharpness of Theorem
1.9
.

Corollary 1.15
.

Let
X
be an infinite set and
G
be a group such that
\operatorname{Sym}_{\omega}(X)\leq G\leq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Then the following dichotomy holds:

(1)

if
n\geq 4
, then
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)
is the topology of pointwise convergence and thus is totally disconnected;

(2)

if
n\leq 3
, then
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}^{\pm}(G)
is hyperconnected.

Note that all the Zariski topologies introduced above depend exclusively on the algebraic structure of a given (semi)group. There are other topologies that reflect mainly algebraic properties of (semi)groups. Namely, the Hausdorff-Markov topology on groups and its semigroup counterparts.

Definition 1.16
.

Let
G
be a group. The
group Hausdorff-Markov
topology on
G
is the intersection of all Hausdorff group topologies on
G
.

A topology
\tau
on a semigroup
S
is called a
semigroup topology
if the semigroup operation is continuous in
(S,\tau)
.

Definition 1.17
.

Let
S
be a semigroup. The
semigroup Hausdorff-Markov
topology
\mathcal{HM}(S)
is the intersection of all Hausdorff semigroup topologies on
S
. The
semigroup Fréchet-Markov
topology
\mathcal{FM}(S)
is the intersection of all
T_{1}
semigroup topologies on
S
.

By the celebrated result of Markov
[
24
]
, for each countable group
G
, the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
coincides with the group Hausdorff-Markov topology on
G
. For each semigroup
S
we have
\mathfrak{Z}(S)\subseteq\mathcal{HM}(S)
, see
[
14
]
. The following question was posed in
[
14
, Question 2.7]
.

Question 1.18

(Elliott, Jonušas, Mesyan, Mitchell, Morayne, Péresse)
.

Is the semigroup Fréchet-Markov topology on a semigroup
S
always contained in the semigroup Zariski topology on
S
?

Goffer and Greenfeld
[
18
]
observed that the Shelah group
G
constructed under CH in
[
30
]
is an example of a group of cardinality
\aleph_{1}
such that
\mathcal{FM}(G)
is discrete, but
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is not discrete. In particular, the Shelah group gives a consistent negative answer to Question
1.18
, as well as it shows that the aforementioned results of Markov cannot be generalized over uncountable groups in ZFC. Recently, Poór and Rinot
[
25
]
constructed a Shelah-like group
G
of size
\aleph_{1}
in ZFC. They showed that
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is not discrete, but every
T_{1}
topology that turns
G
into a topological group is discrete. Their proof however works also for any
T_{1}
semigroup topology. Thus we have the following result that settles Question
1.18
in ZFC.

Theorem 1.19
.

There exists a group
G
of size
\aleph_{1}
such that
\mathcal{FM}(G)
is discrete, but
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is not discrete.

Gaughan
[
16
]
showed that the topology of pointwise convergence coincides with the Hausdorff-Markov topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Banakh, Guran and Protasov
[
1
]
showed that the topology of pointwise convergence coincides with the intersection of all Hausdorff shift-continuous topologies on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
such that for each
y\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
the function
\psi_{y}:\operatorname{Sym}(X)\rightarrow\operatorname{Sym}(X)
,
x\mapsto xyx^{-1}
is continuous.
In this paper, we complement the aforementioned results of Gaughan, and Banakh, Guran and Protasov.

Theorem 1.20
.

For every set
X
the semigroup Hausdorff-Markov topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is the topology of pointwise convergence.

2.
Separating Zariski topologies on groups

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.6
and
1.19
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

Let
F
be the free commutative group over a set
\{x_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
.
For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
let
N_{k}
be the subgroup of
F
generated by the set
\{x^{k}_{2n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Let
G_{k}
be the quotient group
F/N_{k}
. Finally, let
G
be the algebraic sum

\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}G_{k}=\big\{f:\mathbb{N}\to\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}G_{k}:(k)f\in G_{k}\hbox{ and }|\{k\in\mathbb{N}:(k)f\neq 1\}|<\aleph_{0}\big\}.

It is clear that
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G)\subseteq\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G)
for all
n\leq m
. It is also clear that the topology
\mathfrak{Z}_{0}(G)
is anti-discrete and
\mathfrak{Z}_{1}(G)
is the cofinite topology. In particular,
\mathfrak{Z}_{0}(G)\neq\mathfrak{Z}_{1}(G)
.
Hence it suffices to show that the topologies
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G)
and

\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G)
are distinct, whenever
n<m
are positive integers. Consider the following subset of
G
:

T_{m}=\big\{\phi\in G:(k)\phi=1\hbox{ if }k\neq m,\hbox{ and }(m)\phi\in\{x_{n}N_{m}:n\in\mathbb{N}\}\big\}.

Claim 2.1
.

The subspace topology on
T_{m}
inherited from
(G,\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G))
is the cofinite topology.

Proof.

Fix a semigroup polynomial
(x)f=ax^{p}
, where
a\in G
and
p\leq n
. Assume that there exists
x\in T_{m}
such that
(x)f=1
. Note that to prove the claim it suffices to show that the set
\{x\in T_{m}:(x)f=1\}
is finite. By the choice of
x
, we have
a=x^{-p}
. Then, by the definition of
T_{m}
,
(k)a=((k)x)^{-p}=1
whenever
k\neq m
.
Hence we can restrict our attention to the equation
(m)ax^{p}=1
within
G_{m}
where
(m)a=hN_{m}
for some word
h\in F
.
Since for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(m)ax_{n}^{p}=hN_{m}x_{n}^{p}=hx_{n}^{p}N_{m}
(multiplication is taken within
F
), we have that
(m)ax_{n}^{p}=N_{m}
if and only if
hx_{n}^{p}\in N_{m}
.
For convenience, we identify the group
G_{m}
with the subgroup
\{\phi\in G:(k)\phi=1\hbox{ for all }k\neq m\}
of
G
. Then the element
x_{n}N_{m}\in T_{m}\subseteq G_{m}
is a solution to
(x)f=1
if and only if
hx_{n}^{p}\in N_{m}
.

Suppose that
x_{n}N_{m}\in T_{m}
is such that
hx_{n}^{p}\in N_{m}
. If the integer
n
is even, then
h
should contain either the letter
x_{n}
or the word
x_{n}^{-p}
, because the number of
x_{n}
’s in the word
hx_{n}^{p}
should be a multiple of
m
and
p\leq n<m
. If
n
is odd, then
h
must contain the word
x_{n}^{-p}
, by the definition of
N_{m}
.
Since
h
contains only finitely many letters, viewing
T_{m}
as a subset of
G_{m}
, we get

\big|\left\{x_{n}N_{m}\in T_{m}\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$hx_{n}^{p}\in N_{m}$\end{tabular}\right\}\big|\leq\big|\left\{x_{n}\;\middle|\;\begin{tabular}[]{@{}l@{}}$x_{n}$ or $x_{n}^{-1}$ is a letter of $h$\end{tabular}\right\}\big|<\aleph_{0}.

Thus the equation
ax^{p}=1
has finitely many solutions in
T_{m}
, as required.
∎

On the other hand,

\{x\in G:x^{m}=1\}\cap T_{m}=\{f\in T_{m}:(m)f=x_{2n}N_{m},n\in\mathbb{N}\},

witnessing that the set
\{f\in T_{m}:(m)f=x_{2n}N_{m},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is closed in
T_{m}
with respect to the subspace topology inherited from
(G,\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G))
. In particular, it follows that the subspace topology on
T_{m}
inherited from
(G,\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G))
is not cofinite.
Hence the topologies
\mathfrak{Z}_{n}(G)
and
\mathfrak{Z}_{m}(G)
are distinct, as required.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the group
G_{k}
from the proof of Theorem
1.6
is isomorphic to the direct sum of countably infinitely many copies of
\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}_{k}
. Thus,
the group
G
from Theorem
1.6
is isomorphic to the direct sum of countably infinitely many copies of all cyclic groups.

Recall that for a positive integer
n
a group
G
is called
n-Shelah
if for each
A\subseteq G
such that
|A|=|G|
we have
A^{n}=G
.
Recently, Poór and Rinot
[
25
]
for every regular cardinal
\kappa
constructed a 10120-Shelah group of size
\kappa^{+}
in ZFC.
Let
G
be a 10120-Shelah group of size
\aleph_{1}
constructed in
[
25
]
. By
[
25
, Lemma 5.11]
,
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is not discrete, but every
T_{1}
topology that turns
G
into a topological group is discrete. In the following we explain why their proof works also for any
T_{1}
semigroup topology.

Proof of Theorem
1.19
.

Let
G
be the 10120-Shelah group of size
\aleph_{1}
constructed by Poór and Rinot in
[
25
]
. As we mentioned above, the group Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}^{\pm}(G)
is not discrete. To show that the semigroup Fréchet-Markov topology
\mathcal{FM}(G)
is discrete, consider any
T_{1}
semigroup topology
\tau
on
G
. If
1_{G}
has a countable open neighborhood
U
in
(G,\tau)
, then define
H
to be a (countable) subgroup of
G
generated by
U
. By the construction of
G
(see
[
25
]
), there exists
g\in G
such that
H\cap gHg^{-1}=\{1\}
. Since the topology
\tau
is shift-continuous, the conjugation map
\psi_{g}:G\rightarrow G
,
x\mapsto gxg^{-1}
is a homeomorphism of
G
. It follows that
gUg^{-1}
is an open neighborhood of
1_{G}
in
(G,\tau)
. But then
\{1_{G}\}=U\cap gUg^{-1}\in\tau
, witnessing that
\tau
is the discrete topology.

Assume that every open neighborhood of
1_{G}
in
(G,\tau)
is uncountable and thus of cardinality
\aleph_{1}
. Fix any
g\in G\setminus\{1_{G}\}
. Since the space
(G,\tau)
is
T_{1}
the set
U=G\setminus\{g\}
is an open neighborhood of
1_{G}
. The continuity of the multiplication in
G
yields the existence of an open neighborhood
V
of
1_{G}
such that
V^{10120}\subseteq U
. Since
G
is a 10120-Shelah group and
|V|=|G|
, we have
g\in G=V^{10120}\subseteq U
, which contradicts the choice of
U
. The obtained contradiction implies that the semigroup Fréchet-Markov topology on
G
is discrete.
∎

3.
The semigroup Zariski topology on groups with no algebraicity

In this section, we are going to prove Theorem
1.12
which together with Theorem
1.9
establishes Theorem
1.8
. Let
G
be a group.
Recall that every basic open set
U\in\mathfrak{Z}(G)
is specified by two finite tuples
(p_{0},\dots,p_{n-1})
and
(q_{0},\dots,q_{n-1})
of semigroup polynomials over
G
where

U=\{x\in G:(x)p_{i}\neq(x)q_{i}\text{ for }0\leq i\leq n-1\}.

For the purposes of a concise and convenient notation, we will specify such open sets
U
via pairs of “ragged matrices” which hold the coefficients of the corresponding semigroup polynomials.

Definition 3.1
.

An
n
-rowed ragged matrix

R
over a semigroup
S
is a
n
-tuple
(r_{0},\dots,r_{n-1})
in which every
r_{i}
is a
(d_{R,i}+1)
-tuple of elements of
S
, i.e.,
r_{i}=(r_{i,0},r_{i,1}\ldots r_{i,d_{R,i}})
. We refer to

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X
be an infinite set. Let
\tau
be a semigroup topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
and

. Then

U_{x,x}\in\tau
if and only if
\operatorname{Sym}(X)\setminus U_{x,x}\in\tau
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow):
Note that
U_{x,x}
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Every open subgroup
G
of a semitopological group is closed as the complement of
G
is the union of cosets of

and hence open.

(\Leftarrow):
Assume that
U_{x,x}
is closed. Let
y\in X\setminus\{x\}
be arbitrary and
g\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be such that
(x)g=y
.
Then the right coset
{U_{x,x}}g
is
U_{x,y}
. Since the right shift by
g
is a permutation of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
, it follows that

V:=(\operatorname{Sym}(X)\setminus U_{x,x})g=\operatorname{Sym}(X)\setminus U_{x,y}=\{f\in\operatorname{Sym}(X):(x)f\neq y\}

is open and contains the identity permutation, which we denote by
1_{X}
. Since
\tau
is a semigroup topology, there exists an open neighbourhood
W
of
1_{X}
such that
WW\subseteq V
. For every
z\in X
and
A\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}(X)
let

(z)A=\{(z)\rho:\rho\in A\}.

Observe that for all
\rho,\sigma\in W
we have
(x)\rho\sigma\neq y
and thus
(x)\rho\neq(y)\sigma^{-1}
. In other words,

(x)W\cap(y)W^{-1}=\emptyset
. Thus
(X\setminus(x)W)\cup(X\setminus(y)W^{-1})=X
. Two cases are possible:

(1)

|X\setminus(x)W|=|X|
;

(2)

|X\setminus(y)W^{-1}|=|X|
.

(1) Note that
x\in(x)W
as
1_{X}\in W
. Since
|X\setminus(x)W|=|X|
, it is easy to find
\sigma_{x}\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
such that
(x)\sigma_{x}=x
and
(x)W\cap((x)W)\sigma_{x}=\{x\}
. We have the following:

\{x\}=(x)W\cap(x)W\sigma_{x}=(x)W\cap(x\sigma_{x}^{-1})W\sigma_{x}\supseteq(x)(W\cap\sigma_{x}^{-1}W\sigma_{x}).

It follows that

. Since
\tau
is a semigroup topology,

. Thus

is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
which has a nonempty interior with respect to
\tau
. Hence

is open as required.

(2) Note that

as

. Since
|X\setminus(y)W^{-1}|=|X|

\sigma_{y}\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
such that
(y)W^{-1}\cap((y)W^{-1})\sigma_{y}=\{y\}
and
(y)\sigma_{y}=y
. We have the following:

\{y\}=(y)W^{-1}\cap(y)W^{-1}\sigma_{y}=(y)W^{-1}\cap(y\sigma_{y}^{-1})W^{-1}\sigma_{y}\supseteq(y)(W^{-1}\cap\sigma_{y}^{-1}W^{-1}\sigma_{y}).

It follows that

and, as such,

1_{X}=1_{X}^{-1}\in(W^{-1}\cap\sigma_{y}^{-1}W^{-1}\sigma_{y})^{-1}\subseteq U_{y,y}^{-1}.

Since
U_{y,y}^{-1}=U_{y,y}
and
(\sigma_{y}^{-1}W^{-1}\sigma_{y})^{-1}=\sigma_{y}^{-1}W\sigma_{y}
, we obtain the following:

1_{X}\in W\cap\sigma_{y}^{-1}W\sigma_{y}\subseteq U_{y,y}.

Hence, being a group with nonempty interior with respect to
\tau

is open as required.

Proof.

x,y\in X
let
\phi_{x,y},\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be such that
(x)\phi_{x,y}=y
,
(y)\phi_{x,y}=x
, and

We say that a subsemigroup
T
of a semigroup
S
is
maximal
if for each
x\in S\setminus T
,
S
is generated by
T\cup\{x\}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
X
be an infinite set.
U_{x,x}
is a maximal subsemigroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
for all
x\in X
.

Proof.

Let
f,g\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)\setminus U_{x,x}
. It suffices to show that
g
belongs to the subsemigroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
generated by
\{f\}\cup U_{x,x}
. It is easy to see that there exists
h\in U_{x,x}
such that
((x)g)h=(x)f
. Then
(x)ghf^{-1}=x
and thus
\phi=ghf^{-1}\in U_{x,x}
. So

g=\phi fh^{-1}\in U_{x,x}fU_{x,x}

as required.
∎

We are in a position to prove Theorem
1.20
. We need to show that for each set
X
the semigroup Hausdorff-Markov topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is the topology of pointwise convergence.

Proof of Theorem
1.20
.

First let us assume that
X
is finite.
Then
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is finite as well and, as such, every Hausdorff topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is discrete. Note that the topology of pointwise convergence on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
is also discrete. So in this case we are done.

Assume that the set
X
is infinite.
Since the topology of pointwise convergence itself is a Hausdorff semigroup topology, it suffices to show that every Hausdorff semigroup topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
contains the topology of pointwise convergence.
Let
\tau
be a Hausdorff semigroup topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Recall that the sets
U_{x,x}=\{g\in\operatorname{Sym}(X):(x)g=x\}
,
x\in X
form a neighbourhood subbasis of the identity in the topology of pointwise convergence.
Suppose for a contradiction that
\tau
does not contain the topology of pointwise convergence on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Then there exists
x\in X
such that
U_{x,x}
is not open in
\tau
.
It follows from Lemma
4.1
that
U_{x,x}
is not closed in
\tau
.
Then the closure
\overline{U_{x,x}}
of
U_{x,x}
with respect to
\tau
is a subsemigroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
that properly contains
U_{x,x}
.
By Lemma
4.3
,
U_{x,x}
is a maximal subsemigroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
and so
\overline{U_{x,x}}=\operatorname{Sym}(X)
.

Let
y\in X\setminus\{x\}
. By Lemma
4.2
, the set
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}
is closed in the semigroup Zariski topology on
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. Since the Hausdorff semigroup topology
\tau
contains the semigroup Zariski topology
\mathfrak{Z}(\operatorname{Sym}(X))
, the set
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}
is closed in
\tau
.
Observe that

U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y}=\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}\cap U_{x,x}
. It follows that
U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y}

is a closed subset of
U_{x,x}
, where the latter is endowed with the subspace topology inherited from
(\operatorname{Sym}(X),\tau)
.
Note that
U_{x,x}
is isomorphic to
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
. So we apply Lemma
4.1
to
U_{x,x}
to conclude that
U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y}
is open in
U_{x,x}
.
Therefore there exists
W\in\tau
such that

U_{x,x}\cap W=U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y}.

Then
V=W\setminus\overline{(U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y})}\in\tau
and
V\cap U_{x,x}=\emptyset
.
So by the density of
U_{x,x}
in
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
, we have
V=\emptyset
. Taking into account that the set
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}
is a closed in
(\operatorname{Sym}(X),\tau)
, we get

W\subseteq\overline{(U_{x,x}\cap U_{y,y})}\subseteq\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}.

As
W
is a nonempty open set and
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}
is a subgroup of
\operatorname{Sym}(X)
, it follows that
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}
is open. Let
z\in X\setminus\{x,y\}
. Let
\phi\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)
be such that
(x)\phi=x
,
(y)\phi=z
and
\phi^{2}=\operatorname{id}_{X}
, then

\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,z\}}=\{\phi f\phi:f\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}\}=\phi\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}\phi

is also open, as shifts are homeomorphisms in
(\operatorname{Sym}(X),\tau)
.
It follows that
\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}\cap\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,z\}}
is open. As

1_{X}\in\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,y\}}\cap\operatorname{Sym}(X)_{\{x,z\}}\subseteq U_{x,x}

and
U_{x,x}
is a group, it follows that
U_{x,x}
is open, which is a contradiction.
∎
