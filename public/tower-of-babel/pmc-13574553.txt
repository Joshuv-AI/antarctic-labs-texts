Current Practice in the Diagnosis and Management of Mycoplasma pneumoniae Infections: A Survey of Korean Physicians in 2024
Authors: Young June Choe, Hyeon-Jong Yang, Joon Kee Lee, Hye-Kyung Cho, Heungsup Sung, Gwang Cheon Jang, In Young Yoo, Byung-Wook Eun, Soo-Han Choi, Byung Ok Kwak, Hwan Soo Kim, Eun Lee, Kyunghoon Kim, Eun-Ae Yang, Seo Hee Yoon, Bongjin Lee, Jeong-Yong Lee, Yae-Jean Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMacrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MRMP) is a growing concern in pediatric pneumonia. This study investigated current diagnostic and treatment practices for M. pneumoniae infections among Korean pediatricians, focusing on variations and challenges related to MRMP management.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted among Korean pediatricians, including general pediatricians and subspecialists in September 2024. The survey assessed diagnostic methods, antibiotic choices, corticosteroid use, and factors influencing treatment decisions.ResultsA total of 452 pediatricians participated in the survey. Polymerase chain reaction was the preferred diagnostic method (92.0%), although serologic tests were also commonly used (37.4%). Macrolides were the dominant first-line treatment (89.0%), with clarithromycin and roxithromycin being equally favored. Doxycycline was the most frequent second-line choice (67.0%). Corticosteroid use varied among specialties, with up to 94% of respondents using them occasionally or almost always. Treatment guidelines were the primary factor influencing antibiotic choices (76.5% for first-line, 59.7% for second-line).ConclusionThis study reveals diverse practices and a need for standardized guidelines in managing M. pneumoniae infections, particularly in the context of rising MRMP prevalence. Clearer recommendations on diagnostic testing, antibiotic selection, and corticosteroid use are crucial to optimize patient care and mitigate the spread of resistance.

INTRODUCTION

The increasing prevalence of macrolide-resistant Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MRMP) has emerged as a significant global health concern, particularly in East Asia, where resistance rates have reached greater than 80%.1 The primary mechanism of macrolide resistance in M. pneumoniae is associated with point mutations in the 23S rRNA gene, specifically the A2063G transition.2 The clinical implications of macrolide resistance are substantial, as it can lead to treatment failure, prolonged illness duration, and increased complications.3 The rise of MRMP poses a challenge for clinicians, especially in pediatric populations where alternative treatment options like tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones are often contraindicated due to potential adverse effects.45

Despite the growing threat of MRMP, there remains a lack of standardized treatment protocols and clear guidelines for managing these infections, particularly in regions with high prevalence rates.67 The optimal choice of alternative antibiotics, duration of therapy, and the role of adjunctive treatments like corticosteroids remain areas of debate and uncertainty.89 The absence of consensus on these critical aspects of MRMP management can lead to variability in clinical practice, potentially impacting patient outcomes and contributing to the further spread of resistance.

The current landscape underscores the urgent need for evidence-based guidelines and standardized treatment protocols for MRMP infections. To address this knowledge gap and contribute to the development of effective management strategies, we conducted a comprehensive survey among healthcare professionals in Korea, a region with a high prevalence of MRMP up to 90%.10 The survey aimed to gather insights into the current practices, challenges, and perspectives of clinicians involved in the diagnosis and treatment of M. pneumoniae pneumonia, with a particular focus on macrolide resistance. By understanding the current landscape and identifying areas of consensus and disagreement, this study seeks to inform the development of evidence-based guidelines and improve the management of MRMP infections in Korea and beyond.

METHODS

The study employed a cross-sectional survey design to gather data from healthcare professionals involved in the management of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) in Korea. The survey was developed in collaboration with experts in pediatric infectious diseases, pediatric allergy and respiratory medicine, pediatric emergency medicine, pediatric critical care medicine, and laboratory medicine between July and August 2024 (Supplementary Data 1). The survey instrument consisted of 32 questions, encompassing various aspects of MPP diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, and the management of MRMP infections. The questions were designed in both open-ended and closed-ended formats to capture a comprehensive range of perspectives and facilitate quantitative analysis. The survey underwent content validation by an expert panel to ensure its relevance and comprehensiveness.

The survey was disseminated online through a dedicated survey platform from September 10th to 30th, 2024. Participation was actively solicited from members of the Korean Pediatric Society, the Korean Society of Pediatric Infectious Diseases, the Korean Academy of Pediatric Allergy and Respiratory Disease, the Korean Society of Pediatric Emergency Medicine, and the Korean Society of Critical Care Medicine. In total, the survey invitation reached approximately 6,400 pediatricians across Korea, of whom 452 responded, yielding an approximate response rate of 7%. Participation in the survey was voluntary and anonymous, ensuring the confidentiality of respondents’ personal information. The collected data were subsequently analyzed using statistical software, employing descriptive statistics, frequency analysis, and cross-tabulation to identify trends, patterns, and areas of consensus or disagreement among the participants.

Ethics statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Korea University Anam Hospital on September 4, 2024 (IRB No. 2024AN0416). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable national and institutional ethical guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study.

RESULTS

Of 452 respondents, 250 (55.3%) were general pediatricians, followed by 70 (15.5%) pediatric infectious disease specialists, and 67 (14.8%) pediatric allergy and pulmonology specialists (Table 1). Those who practiced in Incheon/Gyeonggi province were the largest proportion (139, 30.8%), followed by those in Seoul (132, 29.2%). High-volume clinicians who treated more than 20 patients with MPP per week accounted for 25.7% of the respondents, and 11–20 patients with MPP per week accounted for 28.3% of the respondents. The majority of the respondents (369, 81.6%) answered that the most common age group among their patients with MPP was 6–12 years.

Of 452 respondents, 234 (51.8%) reported that they occasionally use corticosteroids, while 41.8% reported always using corticosteroids and 36.9% reported administering corticosteroids from the start of antibiotic therapy (Table 6). More specifically, the most common triggers for steroid administration were persistent fever after antibiotic initiation (201, 44.5%) and sustained high fever (217, 48.0%). Frequency and timing of steroid use differed by clinician specialty, clinic volume, and patient severity. Persistent high fever despite antibiotic use (217, 48.0%) was the most common clinical scenario for corticosteroid use. However, 41.8% of respondents answered that they were using corticosteroid always and 36.9% of respondents used steroid from the beginning with the first antibiotics.

DISCUSSION

The survey results highlight the diverse practices and perspectives among healthcare professionals in Korea regarding the diagnosis and management of MPP, particularly in the context of macrolide resistance. The variability in diagnostic testing preferences, antibiotic choices, and adjunctive treatment strategies underscores the need for standardized guidelines and evidence-based recommendations to optimize patient care and mitigate the impact of macrolide resistance. The lack of consensus on key aspects of MPP management, such as the interpretation of diagnostic test results and the selection of alternative antibiotics for MRMP, emphasizes the importance of ongoing research and education to bridge these knowledge gaps. This variability and lack of consensus in the management of pediatric pneumonia, especially in the face of rising antibiotic resistance, is not unique to Korea. Similar challenges have been observed in other countries, including the United States, where studies have highlighted inconsistencies in outcome measures and treatment approaches for pediatric community-acquired pneumonia.11 The absence of standardized guidelines and the reliance on non-specific or infrequent outcomes further complicate the evaluation and comparison of treatment efficacy across different studies and clinical settings.12

While a high proportion of respondents (73.9%) acknowledged the need for additional testing to confirm MRMP, only 19.5% reported routinely performing such tests. Rather than indicating that clinicians are simply underestimating MRMP, these numbers more likely reflect practical barriers that limit resistance testing in routine clinical care. Common problems include the absence of widely approved, the predominance of laboratory-developed tests that lack standardization, long turnaround times for send-out or sequencing-based methods, and instances where clinicians may receive only provisional or qualitative information that is of limited use for immediate treatment decisions. Because additional resistance testing often does not return timely actionable results during the clinical course, it frequently fails to assist real-time therapeutic choices. As noted in recent national guidance, a variety of molecular approaches have been used to detect macrolide resistance—traditional PCR and 23S rRNA gene sequencing, restriction fragment length polymorphism, real-time PCR with high-resolution melting analysis, loop-mediated isothermal amplification, and single-nucleotide-polymorphism PCR—but most of these remain laboratory-developed assays rather than standardized commercial kits. These methods therefore suffer from lack of standardization and relatively long assay times, underscoring the need for development of rapid, kit-based assays capable of reliably detecting macrolide-resistance genotypes. Importantly, standard PCR assays typically detect only the pathogen; some specialized assays or kits can simultaneously detect 23S rRNA mutations associated with macrolide resistance, so clinicians and laboratories must confirm whether a requested test includes resistance detection when submitting specimens. The variability in approaches to treatment failure, with some clinicians favoring antibiotic changes and others opting for additional testing, further underscores the need for standardized protocols to guide clinical decision-making in these challenging scenarios.13 The judicious use of diagnostic tests, or diagnostic stewardship, is crucial in ensuring accurate identification of MRMP, facilitating timely and appropriate treatment decisions, and ultimately curbing the propagation of antibiotic resistance.14

A notable finding of our survey is the very high frequency of corticosteroid use. Although one item showed that 51.8% of respondents use steroids “occasionally,” separate items indicated that 41.8% “always” use steroids and 36.9% administer steroids from the start of antibiotic therapy; combining these responses suggests that about 90% of clinicians use corticosteroids at least intermittently for M. pneumoniae infections. This implies that corticosteroid therapy is widely adopted in clinical practice, and a substantial proportion of clinicians initiate steroids early in the treatment course. The observed differences in practice patterns among different specialties highlight the importance of interdisciplinary collaboration in developing comprehensive guidelines for MPP management. A meta-analysis evaluating the efficacy of glucocorticoids as an adjunctive therapy for MRMP in children demonstrated a significant reduction in both the length of hospital stay and fever duration, compared to conventional macrolide treatment alone.15 Yet, the varying perspectives on steroid use for appropriate duration, for instance, suggest the need for evidence-based recommendations that balance the potential benefits of inflammation reduction with the potential risks of immunosuppression.1617 Similarly, the diverse approaches to hospitalization and patient education underscore the importance of tailoring management strategies to individual patient needs and clinical contexts.

Our findings reveal several gaps between real-world practice and the existing recommendations in Korea for MPP management. For instance, current Korean Pediatric Guidelines (2019) advise that in children who do not respond to initial macrolide therapy, clinicians should consider either switching to a second-line antibiotic such as a tetracycline for age-appropriate patients or a fluoroquinolone in adolescents, or adding a corticosteroid as adjunct therapy.18 In our survey, however, only a minority of pediatricians indicated that they would switch to a second-line antibiotic in a macrolide-unresponsive case, whereas the majority reported they would continue macrolide treatment and manage the patient by adding corticosteroids. This suggests a discrepancy between recommended practice and actual clinical behavior: despite the high prevalence of MRMP, physicians remain hesitant to use second-line antibiotics, potentially due to concerns about age restrictions or side effects, and instead rely on immunomodulation with steroids. These examples highlight areas where Korean pediatricians’ practices diverge from prevailing guidelines, underscoring the need to reconcile these differences through updated clinical guidance or targeted education.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional survey design provides a snapshot of current practices but does not capture temporal trends or changes in practice over time. Therefore, we cannot determine causality or draw definitive conclusions about the long-term impact of different management strategies. Second, the voluntary nature of participation may have introduced selection bias, as clinicians with a particular interest in MPP or strong opinions on its management were more likely to take part. This means the respondents might not fully represent the average practices of all Korean pediatricians. Third, our survey relied on self-reported data, which are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. In other words, participants may tend to over-report adherence to recommended guidelines or describe their ideal intended practices rather than their actual day-to-day clinical behavior. These potential biases should be kept in mind when interpreting the findings. Finally, the survey focused on the practices and perspectives of Korean pediatricians, and the findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare professionals or other countries. Future studies should consider including a wider range of participants and exploring practice variations across different healthcare settings and cultural contexts.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths and adds significant value to the field of MPP management. It provides a comprehensive overview of current diagnostic and treatment practices for MPP among a diverse group of Korean pediatricians. This information is crucial for understanding the current landscape of MPP management and identifying areas where standardized guidelines and education are needed. Furthermore, the study highlights important areas of consensus and disagreement among clinicians regarding key aspects of MPP management, such as diagnostic testing, antibiotic selection, and corticosteroid use. This information can inform the development of evidence-based guidelines and educational initiatives to improve the quality of care for children with MPP.

To translate these findings into improved care, we recommend some strategies. First, there should be an emphasis on continuing medical education and guideline dissemination to ensure that pediatricians are aware of and confident in following evidence-based recommendations for MPP. Educational initiatives could clarify when and how to appropriately employ alternatives like doxycycline or fluoroquinolones in children with MPP. Second, updated clinical guidelines could provide clear algorithms or decision aids for managing suspected macrolide-resistant cases. Third, our findings on widespread early corticosteroid use indicate a need for recommendations on optimal steroid use. We suggest that future guidelines and expert consensus statements address the role of corticosteroids in MPP more explicitly, including criteria for initiation and appropriate dosing and duration, so that steroids are used judiciously.

In conclusion, this survey provides valuable insights into the current landscape of MPP management in Korea, highlighting the challenges posed by macrolide resistance and the need for standardized guidelines and evidence-based recommendations. The findings underscore the importance of ongoing research, education, and interdisciplinary collaboration to optimize the diagnosis, treatment, and management of MPP in the era of macrolide resistance.19 The development and implementation of clear guidelines based on the best available evidence will be crucial in improving patient outcomes, reducing the burden of disease, and mitigating the spread of antibiotic resistance.
