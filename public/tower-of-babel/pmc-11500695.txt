Impact of Varying Pre-exposure Prophylaxis Programs on HIV and Neisseria gonorrhoeae Transmission Among MSM in the Netherlands: A Modeling Study
Authors: Maarten Reitsema, Jacco Wallinga, Ard I. van Sighem, Daniela Bezemer, Marc van der Valk, Fleur van Aar, Janneke Cornelia Maria Heijne, Elske Hoornenborg, Ganna Rozhnova, Birgit van Benthem, Maria Xiridou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

INTRODUCTION

The HIV epidemic in the Netherlands is mostly concentrated in key populations who are at higher risk of acquiring HIV. Men who have sex with men (MSM) accounted for 54% of the new HIV diagnoses in the Netherlands in 2022, women accounted for 18%; transgender individuals accounted for 3%.1 HIV diagnoses have been decreasing in the past decade because of improved HIV care and prevention programs leading to an increased uptake of HIV treatment,2,3 a decreased time between HIV infection and diagnosis, and rollout of pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP).2,4 Overall, the total number of annual new diagnoses in the Netherlands has been less than 500 since 2020; 86% of the total estimated population with HIV and 92% of those diagnosed and linked to care had a suppressed viral load below 200 copies/mL.1

The probability of acquiring HIV can be reduced by pre-exposure prophylaxis5–7 taken either daily or event-driven.6 PrEP usage may also result in reduced HIV anxiety,8 which is also a barrier to HIV testing.9,10 In 2019, a 5-year PrEP program was started in the Netherlands, in which up to 8,500 participants can get PrEP in combination with 3-monthly consultations that include HIV and STI testing. The PrEP program was planned to run for 5 years, followed by an evaluation to determine the best course of action for after 2024.

We used an agent-based model to estimate the effect of the 5-year PrEP program starting in 2019 on the transmission of HIV and Neisseria gonorrhea (NG) among MSM, and to assess the impact of a hypothetical discontinuation of PrEP provision on HIV and NG transmission. In addition, we explored the effect of PrEP programs varying in capacity and consultation frequencies after the 5-year program.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Transmission Model

We updated an earlier developed agent-based transmission model11–13 to simulate HIV and anogenital NG transmission within the sexual network of MSM in the Netherlands. NG was chosen as secondary STI, because it is the most prevalent STI among MSM in the Netherlands.14 In brief, the model simulated 20,000 MSM aged 15–64 years having casual and steady sexual relationships with other MSM. Results were extrapolated to an estimated MSM population of 200,000. Partnership formation rates depend on age, (known) HIV status, and level of sexual activity. HIV and NG can be transmitted through condomless anal intercourse. NG infections can be symptomatic or asymptomatic. Asymptomatic infections are cleared after 6 months. MSM with symptomatic infections seek testing when symptoms appear and receive immediate treatment. Therefore, we assumed no change in their sexual behavior. HIV infection starts with a short acute phase, followed by a long chronic phase with lower transmission probability than during the acute phase. Without diagnosis, we assume MSM enter the AIDS phase and do not engage in sexual contacts. HIV/STI testing behavior depends on known HIV status and sexual activity. Once diagnosed with HIV, MSM immediately enter HIV care, and eventually become virally suppressed. The model was calibrated to annual HIV diagnoses in 2016–2021 and anogenital gonorrhea positivity rates in 2016–2019 using Approximate Bayesian computation.15,16 The model and calibration are described in detail in the Supplement Material, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/QAI/C342.

PrEP in the Model

MSM were eligible for PrEP if they had either in the past 6 months: (1) a gonorrhea diagnosis, (2) condomless anal intercourse with a casual partner, or currently (3) a steady partner with a detectable viral load and diagnosed HIV status. These criteria were chosen to closely resemble Dutch guidelines.17

We used data from the PrEP program (from the national STI surveillance database14) to determine characteristics of the PrEP population in the model. Specifically, we used the consultations of MSM at enrollment from 2019 to 2022 to calculate the median number of partners and median age of MSM when they start PrEP (Table 1). The number of MSM on PrEP in each month in the model was determined from the data. In the national STI surveillance database, PrEP discontinuation was registered. From these data, a "time-on-PrEP" distribution was estimated using maximum likelihood estimation. From this distribution, a time-on-PrEP duration was sampled for each MSM when starting PrEP.

Eligible MSM were recruited to start PrEP if the modeled PrEP population size was smaller than the reported program size for each month. We assumed no informal PrEP use outside of the program. The MSM starting PrEP were sampled from the eligible MSM: 50% had 7 or more sexual partners in the past 6 months, and 50% were 33 years or older, to match age and partner distributions to the PrEP program data. MSM would leave the PrEP program if they were diagnosed with HIV, turned 65 years of age, or reached the end of their sampled time-on-PrEP duration. MSM could re-enter the PrEP program in the model if eligible.

MSM on PrEP had consultations consisting of an HIV and gonorrhea test at regular intervals. PrEP effectiveness was expressed as per sex act reduction of HIV transmission probability of 86%, capturing both adherence and efficacy.5,6

Current PrEP Program

We assessed the effect of the current PrEP program starting mid-2019 and ending in either 2024, 2029, or continued to 2034. These 3 scenarios were simulated up to the end of 2034, and each was compared with a hypothetical scenario without PrEP. When a PrEP program ends, we assumed participants to immediately stop their PrEP use.

Prospective PrEP Programs

We assessed the effect of adaptations of the PrEP program after 2024. We varied the PrEP program after mid-2024 by changing the capacity, and/or by changing the consultation frequency. The capacity was set to either 8,500, 12,000, or 16,000 participants. Consultation frequencies were varied from 3-monthly for all participants, 6-monthly for all participants, 70% of participants testing every 3 months and 30% testing every 6 months (mixed consultation frequency). This distribution was based on the distribution of PrEP dosing regimens: According to data from the national STI surveillance database from 2019 to 2022,14 approximately 30% of PrEP users used event-driven PrEP and 70% used daily PrEP. We assumed that those using event-driven PrEP tested less frequently compared with those on daily PrEP14. MSM were randomly assigned to 3-monthly or 6-monthly consultations. To reach capacity after program expansion, new PrEP users were immediately recruited from the eligible population. These 9 scenarios were compared with a scenario where the PrEP program was stopped in mid-2024. All scenarios were run to the end of 2034.

Outcome Measures

For the effect of the PrEP program, we calculated the percentage of infections of both HIV and NG that could be averted because of PrEP as:% averted infections= (infections without PrEP) − (infections with PrEP)(infections without PrEP)100%.

This was done per year and cumulatively for the entire period 2019–2034.

For prospective PrEP programs, we calculated the cumulative number of acquisitions averted for both HIV and NG, and the cumulative number of additional PrEP consultations from 2024 to 2034 by comparing each of the different PrEP scenarios with the scenario with the PrEP program ending mid-2024. Results are presented as the median with the 95% credible interval (95% CrI), that is, the interval in which 95% of model output falls.

Sensitivity Analysis

We varied 3 parameters pertaining to PrEP separately: the time on PrEP, PrEP efficacy, and percentage of MSM with 7 or more partners when starting PrEP. The time-on-PrEP durations were doubled; the PrEP efficacy was at 96% (higher efficacy), 76% (lower efficacy), and 0% (zero efficacy, still with HIV/STI testing and PrEP consultations for those in the program). The percentage of men with 7 or more partners was increased from 50% to 75% that was evenly distributed over both age categories. The sensitivity analysis scenarios are presented next to a baseline scenario (capacity of 8500 participants and 3-monthly consultations) by comparing each with the counter factual scenario with no PrEP program.

RESULTS

Current PrEP Program

We calculated that the current 5-year PrEP program could result in 2.4% (95% CrI: −0.6% - 4.7%) of HIV infections averted when looking only at the years 2019–2024. Continuing the PrEP program until 2034 could lead to considerable reductions in new HIV and NG infections. However, in the scenarios where the program was discontinued in 2024 or 2029, the percentage of infections averted diminished over time (Fig. 1). If the program was continued until 2034, the annual percentage of infections averted was the largest in 2034: 8.3% (95% CrI: 2.6%–15.2%) and 13.7% (95% CrI: 7.6%–24.2%) for HIV and NG, respectively; cumulatively from 2019 to the end of 2034, 5.2% (95% CrI: 3.2%–7.2%) of HIV infections and 5.9% (95% CrI: 3.6%–15.0%) of NG infections could be averted. This translates to a total of 3500 HIV infections averted cumulatively. With a 10-year PrEP program, from 2019 to 2029, the cumulative percentages of averted infections over the period 2019–2034 would be lower: 3.3% (95% CrI: 1.4%–5.2%) and 4.4% (95% CrI: 2.2%–11.5%) for HIV and NG, respectively. In this scenario, the median percentage of averted infections annually was the highest in 2028 for HIV (6.8%, 95% CrI: 1.5%–11%) and in 2029 for NG (8.5%, CrI 95%: 3.4%–19.1%), after which the annual percentage averted infections decreased. If the current 5-year PrEP program stops in 2024, then 0.5% (95% CrI: −1.5% to 2.4%) and 0.0% (95%CrI: −2.6% to 5.0%) of HIV and NG infections, respectively, can be averted cumulatively over 2019–2034.

Prospective PrEP Programs

A lower consultation frequency from 2024 onward had little effect on the cumulative number of averted infections for both HIV and NG, (Fig. 2 and Table 2). When the consultation frequency was reduced from 3-monthly to 6-monthly for all participants, the cumulative number of averted HIV infections reduced from 3,140 (95% CrI: 1,780–4,780) to 2,980 (95% CrI: 1,390–4,750) if the program capacity was kept at 8,500 individuals. For NG, the same reduction in consultation frequency could result in a change in the cumulative number of averted infections from 27,930 (95% CrI: 14,560–46,280) to 26,670 (95% CrI: 12,090–49,420). Reducing consultation frequencies from 3-monthly to 6-monthly with a maximum program capacity of 16,000 persons also resulted in small changes in the cumulative number of averted infections for both HIV and NG. In contrast, the cumulative number of consultations decreased substantially: from 589,330 (95% CrI: 586,240–591,160) to 272,590 (95% CrI: 271,770–273,290).

A larger capacity could lead to more averted infections over the period 2024–2034, but also more consultations. If the program capacity increased to 16,000 participants while keeping consultation frequency at 3-monthly, the cumulative number of averted HIV infections could increase from 3,140 (95% CrI: 1,780–4,780) to 3,940 (95% CrI: 2,420–5,460); the number of consultations would increase from 316,050 (95% CrI: 314,120–317,580) to 589,330 (95% CrI: 586,240–591,160).

In the scenarios examined here, we found similar levels of NG positivity rate, but quite considerable variation in the number of averted NG infections (Table 2). The positivity rate did not decline proportionately with the increase in averted infections, and had also not changed considerably compared with the rate at the start of the PrEP program in 2019. The scenario with the largest cumulative number of averted NG infections (capacity of 16,000; mixed consultation frequency) had a gonorrhea positivity rate in 2034 of 9.3%. The scenario with the smallest cumulative number of averted NG infections (maximum capacity of 8,500; 3-monthly consultation frequency) had a gonorrhea positivity rate in 2034 of 10.6%.

Sensitivity Analysis

The efficacy of PrEP had the most influence on the number of HIV infections averted in our sensitivity analyses (Table 3). A lower PrEP efficacy could lead to a lower number of HIV infections averted. However, the PrEP program could still have a positive effect on HIV transmission even when PrEP efficacy was set to 0% because of the 3-monthly consultations. The various scenarios did not result in different outcomes for NG.

DISCUSSION

Our study shows that PrEP programs could have a substantial effect on averting new HIV and NG infections by reducing transmission. However, this effect may diminish in time, if PrEP is interrupted at some point. Discontinuing PrEP programs could rapidly lead to a reduced annual percentage of averted infections of both HIV and NG to preprogram levels. As such, the highest percentage of cumulatively averted infections was found when the PrEP program continued the entire simulated period, while the prolonged effect of a discontinued 5-year program was not discernible from the counterfactual scenario without PrEP after 10 years.

Increasing the program capacity could considerably enhance the impact of PrEP and result in lower number of HIV and NG infections, compared with the program with the current participant capacity. However, the number of consultations would be substantially increased. This could be mitigated by changing the consultation frequency to 6-monthly for all participants without undoing the gained effects on transmission.

A strength of our study is the inclusion of a second STI, namely NG. The PrEP program is affected by the prevalence of other STIs, because a diagnosis such as gonorrhea is an indication to start PrEP. On top of that, PrEP may affect the transmission of STIs, because PrEP consultations include gonorrhea tests. Our results indicated that a PrEP program could result in a substantial reduction in the number of NG infections, which was also found in other modeling work.11,19 However, our model showed only a small reduction in NG positivity rate in 2034 compared with 2019 and only small differences in NG positivity in 2034 between the scenarios we examined. This indicates that a positive effect on transmission might not be reflected in surveillance data, because these depend on testing frequencies. Frequent screening could have a low probability to reduce prevalence and incidence, because of the density of the sexual network.20

Another strength is that we used data from the PrEP program as implemented in the Netherlands. Because we used an agent-based model, we could accurately match the modeled population of PrEP users to the Dutch population of PrEP users, by translating guidelines for PrEP eligibility and matching the characteristics of PrEP users (number of partners and age) as in our PrEP program.

A study evaluating the impact of PrEP in Montreal found around 20% of HIV infections averted over 2015–2021, but in those years their coverage (3.1%–15.9%) was higher than ours (see Table 6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/QAI/C342), and earlier years with lower coverage were excluded.21 A United Kingdom modeling study found that a PrEP program with a coverage of around 3% after 10 years could cumulatively avert 11%, with a wide uncertainty range, of HIV infections. Our current results showed a smaller impact on HIV transmission than our earlier work.11 That can be a result of several factors that were different between the 2 studies. First, the number of PrEP users in this study was smaller than in11 and we have shown that with higher number of PrEP users, the impact of PrEP on HIV transmission could be higher. Second, in this study, half of participants had less than 7 partners in the previous 6 months when starting PrEP to match the program data. Third, MSM starting PrEP are sampled from all eligible MSM in this study, whereas in,11 MSM had to have had an HIV/STI test in the previous 6 months. MSM with more sexual partners are more likely to get consistently tested for HIV/STIs in our model, which could have led to a more sexually active population on PrEP.

We did not account for possible effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, such as changes in sexual behavior or changes in HIV/STI care. A reduction in sexual activity could have led to a reduction in transmission and thus a smaller impact due to PrEP. During the first lockdown (March–May 2020), STI incidence was lower than in the same period in 2019, but during June–Dec 2020, STI incidence was higher than in 2019.22 Compliance to sexual distancing (abstaining from sexual contacts with people outside of one's household) decreased after the first lockdown, even with new lockdowns imposed.23 At the first months of the pandemic, PrEP enrollment was slowed down, which was reflected in our model. PrEP prescription refills and HIV/STI testing in case of symptoms or when notified by a partner were not scaled down.22 Care at HIV treatment centers was reorganized such that consultations were done online or by telephone, and blood was drawn at other locations2: Time between the first positive HIV test and ART initiation did not seem to increase during 2020–2022.1

Furthermore, we did not account for changes in the behavior of MSM because of the PrEP program. When MSM are on PrEP, they may engage more frequently in CAI24,25 or have more sexual partners.26 The effect of a PrEP program on NG transmission may be lower if condom usage decreases, while MSM on PrEP would still be protected against acquiring HIV. Similarly we made a simplifying assumption that condoms provide complete protection, although studies have found less than 100% efficacy.27,28 However, within a modeled partnership, we used the lower condom use propensity of the MSM. We expect that with lower condom efficacy, the impact of PrEP in reducing HIV transmission would be higher. Furthermore, we assumed an immediate increase in the number of PrEP users as the program expanded in 2024. This contrasts with the slow scale up of the program in 2019. However, in 2019–2021, the capacity of the program was 6,500; enrollment was scaled down in the first months of the pandemic; and personnel/facilities were not immediately available at the start of the program, thus slowing down PrEP expansion. Furthermore, currently, acceptability and willingness to use PrEP is high among MSM in the Netherlands and there is a waiting list of more than 3,000 MSM. Therefore, it is expected that PrEP capacity could be expanded in 2024 much faster than in 2019. With a slower PrEP uptake, fewer HIV infections would probably be averted.

We assumed a clearance time of 6 months for asymptomatic gonorrhea. Estimating the natural duration of gonorrhea is methodologically and ethically complicated. The ExGen study on the natural history of rectal NG infections found a median duration of 9 weeks, based on 20 infections of which 14 were censored because of treatment.29 Gonorrhea in modeling studies is often a composite of infections in different anatomical locations, and durations may vary from 30 days,30 to 228 days.31 Yet, the impact of these discrepancies on prevalence estimates is mitigated when the model is calibrated to observed prevalence, because similar values of prevalence can be achieved with shorter infection durations by, for example, increasing transmission rates. However, if the natural duration of gonorrhea is considerably shorter than the time between consultations, the effect on NG transmission is probably overestimated here. Finally, we used32 to adjust HIV transmission probability with viral load and when the person living with HIV has an NG coinfection. More recent studies suggest that the reverse, an increased HIV transmission probability when the HIV-negative partner has gonorrhea, might also be true.33

Our work suggests that increasing the capacity of the PrEP program is an effective way to reduce HIV transmission, and that consultation frequency could be reduced to 6-monthly without reducing the number of averted infections for both HIV and NG, when capacity is increased. Reducing the number of consultations could alleviate the cost and burden of PrEP consultations, though the cost of a PrEP program does not correspond directly to the number of consultations performed. A cost-effectiveness analysis could provide more insights. Similarly, the health benefits in a cost-effectiveness analysis are also not directly expressed as the number of infections averted, and benefits are also affected by other factors such as time to diagnosis for HIV.

We looked at the effects of changing the capacity of and consultation frequency for PrEP programs. These changes could lead to effects not captured in our transmission model. Counseling can encourage healthy sexual behaviors, so reduced PrEP consultations might lead to changes in adherence or condom use.34 Counseling for correct use of PrEP can be needed for younger users for whom PrEP might be their first daily medication.35 Extending time between consultations from monthly to quarterly could decrease adherence in young PrEP users.36 Adherence and PrEP retention might be improved with long-acting injectable PrEP that has also been shown to be effective in preventing HIV acquisition.37 Interest and intention to use injectable PrEP among current oral PrEP users is high among MSM; however, it is not yet available in the Netherlands.38

CONCLUSIONS

A continued PrEP program for MSM in the Netherlands could result in substantial reductions in HIV and NG transmission, but when stopped, transmission could return to preprogram levels within years. More infections could be averted if the capacity is expanded. At the same time, reducing consultation frequency could reduce the number of consultations required for a larger capacity without reducing the number of averted infections.
