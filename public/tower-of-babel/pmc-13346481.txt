Pre-blast prediction of ground vibration in surface mining using rock acoustic impedance and wave attenuation calibrated with in-situ measurements
Authors: Rajeev Verma, Bhanwar Singh Choudhary, Geleta Warkisa Deressa, Dimitrii Gromyka, Alexander Shikhov, Khushboo Kaushal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting blast-induced ground vibration (BIGV) in surface mining is crucial for ensuring environmental safety. Conventional empirical models often require risky and costly site-specific trial blasts. This study intends to develop a pre-blast assessment framework by correlating laboratory-derived acoustic properties with field-monitored vibration data from a limestone mine. The methodology integrates comprehensive geomechanical and non-destructive Ultrasonic Pulse Velocity (UPV) testing to characterise the dynamic response of the rock mass. Peak Particle Velocity (PPV) and frequency are monitored for multiple production blasts. The laboratory UPV tests provided key insights into the material’s dynamic response, which were validated by fundamental wave-mechanical relationships. Results confirmed a strong linear correlation (R2 = 0.82) between specimen length and particle displacement, supporting the theoretical strain-kinematic relationship, while wave propagation behaviour was analysed using power-law relationships to describe geometric spreading and material attenuation. The derived attenuation coefficient of 0.675 is lower than the empirical decay exponent of 1.46 (derived from the USBM model), indicating a relatively conservative representation of vibration attenuation. Model performance was evaluated using statistical metrics, showing comparable predictive capability to the USBM model in terms of R², RMSE, and MAE, with improved representation of material-specific attenuation behaviour. The proposed framework integrates geomechanical principles with mathematical modelling to develop a physically informed alternative to purely empirical approaches. This hybrid BIGV prediction method can be applied at the pre-blast planning stage, thereby improving prediction reliability and supporting safer, more efficient mining operations.

Introduction

Background and importance of blasting

Rock blasting remains the most widely used and efficient method for rock excavation in surface mining and civil engineering projects. In large surface mines, bench blasting is routinely employed to fragment rock masses for excavation1–5. Despite advances in mechanical excavation technologies, controlled blasting remains the most cost-effective technique for breaking hard rock formations and achieving the required fragmentation for downstream mining operations6–9. According to the conclusion of various studies, the method offers high productivity and cost efficiency compared with most mechanical excavation techniques, particularly in hard rock conditions10–17. Thus, after decades of research and planning, the bench blasting process has evolved and been refined18–21. A schematic of bench blasting configuration in a surface mine and its related terminologies is shown in Fig. 1.

During blasting, the detonation of explosives releases a large amount of energy in the form of stress waves that propagate through the surrounding rock mass and the atmosphere22–25. While only a portion of the explosive energy contributes to useful rock breakage, the remaining energy manifests as environmental side effects, including ground vibration, air overpressure, flyrock, dust, and noise26–31. Among these, blast-induced ground vibration (BIGV) is one of the most critical environmental concerns because of its potential to affect nearby structures, slopes, underground openings, and sensitive installations1,2,7,23,32–34.

Effect of blast-induced ground vibrations (BIGV)

The blasting process has adverse impacts on the flora and fauna in the vicinity of the operation35–38. Blasting has been experiencing serious operational issues due to noise and ground vibrations generated during the event. Despite its low cost and high economic viability for the mining and civil excavation industries, as per various studies10,12,14–16,39. It faces significant social pressure and downtime, forcing engineers to either switch to mechanisation or develop blasts with more controlled outputs40–47.

Most impactful among these environmental disturbances are Blast-Induced Ground Vibration (BIGV) and Air Overpressure (AOP), which have a significant impact in the area, both on the ground and in the air48–52. These impacts are monitored and mitigated using advanced instrumentation and controlled blasting technology. Directorate General of Mines Safety DGMS-India and global industry safety bureaus53 have issued dedicated guidelines for performing blasting operations, as shown in Table 1. Variations are observed among country-specific norms due to differences in the strata present geographically and the habitat constraints of the working area.

Ground vibrations generated during blasting propagate through the rock mass in the form of elastic waves54,5557. The critical design parameters that influence blasting efficiency and overall ground stability include bench height, bench width, face angle, blasthole diameter, blast design pattern, firing sequence, free face, and the mechanical properties of the rock mass through which the waves propagate18,20,56,58–6163. Excessive vibration levels can lead to structural damage, instability in mine slopes, discomfort to nearby communities, and restrictions on blasting activities21,64,65. Thus, a major parameter that the industry has used to set the standards is the frequency and the amplitude of the waves generated during the blast operation66-68. This has become a milestone for initiating research worldwide. The properties of waves are key to unlocking the BIGV.

Prediction of blast-induced ground vibrations

The propagation of blast-induced waves in the ground is a dynamic process involving multiple reflections, refractions, and mode conversions as waves encounter discontinuities or variations in the bedding structure21,55,69–7375. As depicted in the schematic in Fig. 2, body waves travel through the subsurface layers, while surface waves propagate along layer boundaries to reach the ground surface; both are recorded by seismographs. Differences in the arrival times, amplitudes, and frequencies of these waves, depending on the geological characteristics of each layer, are observed by instruments. The figure highlights the fundamental wave-propagation behaviour followed in numerous wave elasticity and frequency related studies and the monitoring method developed to assess how the vertical, radial, and transverse components of ground vibration are monitored to evaluate wave attenuation and energy transmission through varying rock strata76,7779.

Limitations of current approaches and identified research gap

The effectiveness of the existing blast-monitoring techniques in the literature suggests limited predictive reliability. Although widely applied, these methods employ seismographs and rely on empirical peak particle velocity equations, as summarised in Table 2, where the primary parameters are the scaled distance and the explosive charge, calculated from site monitoring. While numerical investigations can simulate blast dynamics, they often neglect the complex geometries of blast designs and lithological stratification, leading to oversimplified, less accurate results.

To address these limitations, the present study aims to develop a pre-blast prediction framework for blast-induced ground vibrations through laboratory experiments and calibrate it using field investigations. From a fundamental perspective, blast-induced vibrations result from stress waves generated during explosive detonation and their subsequent propagation through the surrounding rock mass. The attenuation behaviour of these waves is influenced by several intrinsic material properties, including density, elastic moduli, and wave propagation velocities. Among these parameters, compressional wave velocity (VP) is particularly significant because it reflects the rock mass’s elastic properties and can be conveniently measured through laboratory ultrasonic pulse-velocity tests. Despite the physical relevance of these parameters, relatively few vibration prediction approaches explicitly incorporate laboratory-measured rock properties into the modelling framework.

Methodological framework for predicting blast-induced ground vibration

In the initial stages of mine planning, exploration drilling yields rock core logs that provide the first and most direct representation of the subsurface features. These core logs can be systematically analysed to evaluate wave-propagation characteristics in the rockmass using non-destructive testing methods, allowing material behaviour to be assessed without damaging the samples. The methodology employed in this study adopts a multidisciplinary physics-based approach to establish a reliable attenuation model for predicting BIGV, based on the physical and mechanical properties of the rock samples.

Although the core samples in this experimental study were prepared from freshly exposed rock blocks at the active working site, they represent samples similar to those obtained from core logs. These cores form the foundation for subsequent analysis. Geomechanical experiments were required to be performed for determining the physical and mechanical properties of the rock specimens, including Uniaxial Compressive Strength (UCS), Tensile Strength (TS), Young’s Modulus, cohesion, and friction angle. Furthermore, non-destructive ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) testing was performed to determine wave propagation characteristics, including P- and S-wave velocities, dynamic elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio. These parameters provide insights into the material response and wave transmission behaviour through the rock matrix. A key consideration in the proposed framework is the scale and frequency difference between laboratory measurements and field-scale blast vibrations. Ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) testing operates at significantly higher frequencies (24–63 kHz) than blast-induced seismic waves (5–80 Hz), which may influence attenuation behaviour due to frequency-dependent material response. While both wave types follow similar fundamental principles of propagation, geometric spreading, and material damping, the laboratory-derived attenuation parameter (βₗ) is interpreted as a relative indicator of intrinsic material behaviour rather than a direct representation of field-scale attenuation. This distinction is acknowledged in the present study and is taken into account in the interpretation of the developed prediction model.

Subsequently, using a comprehensive literature review, the established wave-propagation principles were integrated with experimental findings to derive empirical relationships governing seismic and acoustic wave attenuation in rock media. In the blast monitoring phase of the field-based recording, the blast design parameters, including burden, spacing, charge per delay, and explosive characteristics, were recorded. The BIGV and air-overpressure (AOP) were monitored at different radial distances using a seismograph, while in-situ geological observations were documented to relate site conditions with measured vibration responses.

The collected data were analysed statistically through regression-based modelling of PPV with scaled distance parameters, primarily following the USBM empirical model. This validates the relationship between charge weight, distance, and vibration response through coefficient optimisation.

Finally, a site-specific vibration attenuation function was developed for the studied rock type by integrating laboratory-derived rock properties with field vibration observations. The core step of this methodology introduces a material-specific parameter—the lab-derived rock attenuation factor βl—for transitioning the prediction framework from a purely field-based empirical model to a hybrid physics-informed formulation. The developed attenuation coefficient α and the geometric damping factor γ, collectively forming βl, can be used as complementry method to the site-specific rock constant βs in the final peak particle velocity prediction model. Hypothesis formulation using empirical modelling

This section builds on the methodological structure by establishing the theoretical relationships and assumptions underlying model development. While the previous section outlines the experimental and data-acquisition approach, this section focuses on formulating the underlying wave-mechanics-based relationships and empirical expressions that underpin the proposed predictive model.

The majority of the BIGV is caused by the body waves, and only a part is reflected as surface waves. This occurs due to wave attenuation and geometric spreading of wave energy, causing seismic waves to lose energy over distance and resulting in a decrease in particle displacement and velocity with increasing distance from the source. These phenomena are highly dependent on the internal composition, structural heterogeneity, and elastic properties of the rock mass, as well as on their behaviour under seismic or acoustic agitation.

To quantify these wave propagation characteristics, namely pressure amplitude, displacement amplitude, and particle velocity, empirical relationships given by Aki and Richards111, Cooper and Kurowski112, and Hustrulid5 were identified. These formulations relate the wave properties to materials’ geomechanical, structural, and dynamic elastic characteristics, ultimately enabling the calculation of particle vibrational velocity in the rock being studied.

A key parameter governing stress-wave transmission in rock media is acoustic impedance (Z), which represents the material’s resistance to wave propagation and governs how explosive energy is distributed, reflected, and transmitted throughout the rock mass113,114. The displacement amplitude (ξ) represents the maximum displacement of particles in the medium due to a seismic wave. The calculation of ξ depends on the wave properties, the energy of the wave, and the medium through which the wave is travelling73115,. For a harmonic wave, the particle displacement ξ(t) can be written as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\xi\:\left(t\right)=\xi\:\mathrm{c}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{s}(\omega\:t+\phi\:)$$\end{document}

where, ξ is the displacement amplitude, ω is the angular frequency (ω = 2πf), ϕ is the phase angle, and t is the time. The displacement amplitude is also related to the pressure amplitude of the wave, the acoustic impedance of the rock and the angular frequency of wave propagation. This approach introduces a laboratory-derived material parameter that characterises the intrinsic energy dissipation behaviour of the rock during wave propagation. The coefficients α and γ correspond to the attenuation coefficient and geometric damping factor, respectively, which together define the attenuation exponent βl.

The central hypothesis is that this intrinsic βl can serve as a substitutional predictor of the site-specific βs in the final Peak Particle Velocity (PPV) prediction model. If validated, this parameter can improve predictive reliability and reduce dependence on extensive field trials, thereby enabling vibration prediction during early stages of mine design. where kc is a new calibrated constant incorporating energy source factors, and βl is the lab-derived decay exponent.

Fieldwork investigation and laboratory analysis

Geological and geographical details of the study area

The mine site is located in the central region of India, where ample limestone is mined out for cement production. The geographical location is shown in Fig. 5. The area forms part of the Rewa plateau, belonging to the Bhander series of the Upper Vindhyan. This group of rocks is virtually unfossiliferous except for the occurrence of stromatolites. The formations are horizontal, with a slight local dip of 0–50 degrees in some areas. The altitude ranges from 290 to 300 m. The region’s topography is mostly flat with a general dip of 2° to 6° towards the south between S10°W and S5°E. It lacks any notable relief. The region lacks woodland, and its topography ranges from 312 m above mean sea level (MSL) in the north to 300 m above MSL in the south. A shaley limestone divides the two horizontal bands that make up the mine’s limestone deposit. Lower calcareous and upper arenaceous facies are the two broad categories into which the Vindhyan formations fall. The Bhander limestone is a calcareous horizon within the upper arenaceous rocks, which range in thickness from 5 to 15 m. The predominant rock type in the region is the Bhander limestone deposit, which is overlain by Sirbu shale, ranging in thickness from 0 to 2 m. It has two major sets of joints across and along the strike, making it an extensively jointed structure.

Rock sample collection

To study the wave motion in this type of rock strata, multiple blocks of limestone rock were collected, each belonging to a different limestone bench, for laboratory testing to evaluate their mechanical and dynamic properties. Representative block samples collected from benches are labelled as 3BOB (3rd bench overburden), 4BSL (4th bench shaley limestone), 5BHML (5th bench high-magnesium limestone), and 7BLML (7th bench low-magnesium limestone). The mine’s litho-log presented in Fig. 7; Table 5 shows the lithological sequence of the limestone quarry, illustrating the stratified arrangement of rock formations from the surface to the submerged benches.

In-situ rock mass characterisation

The mine’s geology and other notable features were recorded during the study. Majorly, the mine contained limestone in two horizontal bands, separated by shaley limestone, ranging from 0 to 2 m. The limestone belt varied in thickness from approximately 5 to 15 m and lies as a calcareous horizon within the upper arenaceous rocks. It is widely jointed with two major joint sets along and across the strike and two other joint sets roughly at an inclination of 15 and 105 degrees from the horizontal, as shown in Fig. 8. The geomechanical properties of rock present at various benches were also measured using the Schmidt hammer surface rebound hardness test for in-situ impact testing, following the ISRM guidelines119. The rebound number values ranged from 25 to 55, clearly stating that 3BOB: R = 32–45 → moderate strength (≈ 40 MPa), 4BSL: R = 25–30 → relatively weaker, weathered or shaley zones with UCS roughly in the weak band (≈ 25 MPa), 5BHML: R = 45–55 → indicates the strongest, most competent bench with UCS in the band ≈ 50 + MPa, and 7BLML: R ≈ 48 → strong limestone, UCS ≈ 45 MPa.

The point load strength index (Is) test was conducted; the results show that diametral loading produced values of 2.08–2.36 MPa, while axial loading yielded slightly lower values of 1.81–2.23 MPa, with an average Is50 of 2.22 MPa across all tests. From point load index (PLI) testing, the UCS of moderate strength limestone usually falls in this range (based on ISRM119, Deere & Miller122, and published case studies123,124): weak limestones: ~20–40 MPa, moderate strength limestones: ~40–80 MPa, strong limestones: ~80–120 MPa, and very strong limestones: 120 MPa and above.

Overall, the test results indicate that the rock in the study area falls within the moderate-strength category and exhibits largely consistent strength and mechanical behaviour, with minor variations likely arising from subtle inhomogeneities or microstructural non-conformities within the single rock sample.

Dynamic elastic properties of rock using ultrasonic test results.

The bulk density of the rocks is consistently high, ranging from 2.62 to 2.73 t/cbm (2620 to 2730 kg/m3), confirming a compact and low-porosity carbonate lithology. Seismic wave measurements reveal a wide variation in P-wave velocity (VP = 4.67–6.50 km/s) and S-wave velocity (VS = 1.01–2.04 km/s), yielding VP/VS ratios mostly in the range of 2.7–6.2, indicating heterogeneous elastic behaviour likely controlled by microfractures, grain packing, and anisotropy.

Dynamic elastic properties derived from ultrasonic wave velocities exhibit a moderate but systematic variation across the tested samples. Young’s modulus E ranges from approximately 8 to 32 GPa, while the shear modulus G varies between 2.7 and 11.3 GPa. The Lamé parameter λ and bulk modulus K fall within ~ 51–108 GPa and ~ 54–110 GPa, respectively, after ensuring consistent unit representation.

Poisson’s ratio is observed in a relatively narrow range of 0.42 to 0.49, with most values clustering between 0.45 and 0.48. The upper end of this range reflects the dynamic (small-strain) ultrasonic measurements, which typically yield higher Poisson’s ratios than static laboratory tests125,126. Consequently, the elevated Poisson’s ratio contributes to higher calculated bulk and Lamé moduli. Values approaching 0.49 suggest near-incompressible behaviour; however, they should be interpreted cautiously, as they may also reflect measurement sensitivity, wave-mode assumptions, or minor uncertainties in coupling between rock and transducers.

A clear inverse relationship is observed between VP/VS ratio and elastic stiffness, where lower VP/VS values (~ 2.7–3.2) correspond to higher stiffness (E ≈ 25–32 GPa), and higher VP/VS values (> 5) correspond to lower stiffness (E ≈ 8–12 GPa). This trend is consistent with wave propagation theory, which predicts that increasing VP/VS ratios indicate relatively lower shear resistance127.

Despite samples originating from the same lithological unit, a noticeable spread in VP (implicitly reflected through VP/VS ≈ 2.7–6.2) and elastic moduli is evident, highlighting the influence of microstructural variability, mineral composition, and local heterogeneity within the rock mass. All elastic parameters were computed using standard isotropic elastic relationships and verified for unit consistency. Such trends have been widely reported in dynamic elastic characterization of rocks, where ultrasonic measurements tend to overestimate Poisson’s ratio due to measurement sensitivity and wave-mode assumptions111.

The static mechanical properties obtained from UCS testing show Young’s modulus values ranging from approximately 6 to 9 GPa, with Poisson’s ratio between 0.22 and 0.36, which are consistent with typical values reported for limestone under static loading conditions. In comparison, the dynamic elastic moduli derived from ultrasonic pulse velocity measurements are significantly higher, with Young’s modulus ranging from 8 to 32 GPa and Poisson’s ratio from 0.42 to 0.49. This difference reflects the contrast between small-strain behaviour, where microcrack closure and elastic response dominate, and static testing, where larger strains are involved; in dynamic testing, progressive microcrack closure dominates, whereas progressive damage governs static loading. Such discrepancies between static and dynamic elastic properties have been widely reported for rock materials and are primarily attributed to strain amplitude effects and microstructural features128,129.

The observed ratio between static and dynamic moduli in rocks is typically reported to range between 1 and 3, depending on lithology and internal structure130. The present study shows a slightly higher variation in certain samples, which may be attributed to inherent heterogeneity, microcrack distribution, and sensitivity of ultrasonic measurements to wave propagation characteristics.

Recent studies on carbonate and sedimentary rocks confirm that dynamic elastic parameters derived from wave velocities exhibit strong but non-linear correlations with static properties, with variability influenced by porosity, mineral composition, and structural discontinuities. Overall, the static and dynamic datasets exhibit consistent mechanical trends and provide complementary insights into the deformation and wave propagation behaviour of the studied rock mass131. The difference between static and dynamic elastic properties observed in this study is consistent with widely reported behaviour in rock mechanics literature.

Experimental results show that dynamic testing effectively captures microstructural variability by evaluating correlations between longitudinal and shear wave velocities and static parameters. Some experiments indicate micro-features that are reasonably engaged under slow static loading conditions do not alter high-frequency waves due to the disparity between static and dynamic modulus characteristics132. Grain size, cementation quality, and microfracture orientation influence wave velocity and dynamic elastic constants, contributing to the observed scatter. These variations reflect inherent geological heterogeneity within a single lithology and support the use of ultrasonic wave behaviour as a proxy for understanding larger-scale wave propagation phenomena, such as those associated with blasting.

The close alignment between observed and modelled PPV values from Fig. 20B (R2 = 0.84) shows that the BIGV dataset effectively captures the site’s vibration response characteristics. The model’s prediction aligns with results reported by Singh et al.134 and Khandelwal and Singh135 for similar rock mass conditions in Indian mines. The scatter plot (Fig. 20A) further confirms that most data points lie close to the regression line, with only a few deviations attributable to field uncertainties in blasting, indicating natural variability due to local geological heterogeneity, and minor manual errors such as variations in charge confinement and stemming efficiency.

Despite covering a wide range of blast events, the monitored dataset exhibits several inconsistencies and limitations that restrict the reliability of empirical vibration prediction. The recorded PPV values show slight irregular attenuation patterns for comparable scaled distances, indicating the influence of uncontrolled field parameters. Additionally, differences in sensor positioning and coupling, along with environmental noise and signal interference, introduce uncertainty in vibration and frequency measurements. The dataset also includes a few anomalously high or low PPV readings that do not correspond proportionally to the maximum charge per delay or radial distance, suggesting that the conventional scaled distance law alone fails to capture the complex interactions between explosive energy distribution, geological heterogeneity, and wave propagation mechanisms. These discrepancies highlight the shortcomings of traditional empirical methods, such as the prediction equations described in Table 2, which rely on curve fitting of limited data and assume uniform propagation conditions.

A novel UPV-based vibration prediction model development

The approach presented in this work aims to convert the microstructural characteristics measured by ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) measurements at the laboratory scale into a predictive model for large-scale PPV resulting from blasting events. This is accomplished by empirically decomposing the overall wave-decay exponent into two physically distinct components: material attenuation and geometric damping (also known as the scale effect).

Power law modelling and parameter derivation

Wave decay in rock mechanics is conventionally modelled using a power-law function, which describes how an amplitude parameter (Y) decays or scales with distance (X) or length: Y = A Xb. The actual reality of wave propagation necessitates this functional form; for example, geometric spreading causes wave amplitude to decline proportionately to the inverse of distance. The relationship is simplified by a logarithmic transformation (ln Y = ln A + b ln X), which allows the exponent b to be estimated by simple linear regression. This non-linear relationship is also crucial for modelling scale effects in rock properties (velocity vs. length, VP vs. L). As the sample size (L) increases, measured wave velocity (VP) asymptotically approaches the true rock mass velocity (VPtrue) by averaging out microcracks and heterogeneities. A linear model is not used for modelling, as a linear equation for velocity scaling (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{P}=E+FL$$\end{document}) is physically impossible, as it erroneously predicts an infinite velocity increase. Similarly, for attenuation modelling (L vs. u), a linear decay model (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:u=C-DL$$\end{document}) is fundamentally unphysical, as it implies a constant decay rate irrespective of current amplitude and can predict impossible negative amplitudes at large distances. Consequently, the power-law form (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{P}\propto\:{L}^{\gamma\:}$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:u\:\propto\:\:{L}^{-\alpha\:}$$\end{document}) is employed because it aligns with wave physics fundamentals and correctly captures the asymptotic approach to true rock mass values at large scales.

Derivation of geometric damping factor (γ)

The positive trend between length (L) and P-wave velocity (VP) suggests a scale effect, in which measured wave velocity increases with sample size, likely due to reduced microcrack influence, more effective elastic properties, and better representation of intact rock properties in longer samples. The scale effect was modelled as:19\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{V}_{P}={A}_{v}{L}^{\gamma\:}$$\end{document}

where γ is defined as the Geometric Damping Factor. This parameter reflects the change in effective rock quality with increasing sample size, which, in turn, influences the overall wave decay. Linear regression was performed on ln(VP) versus ln(L) for each sample category. The exponent from the VP-L relationship quantifies how wave-propagation efficiency changes with scale. This can be interpreted as a factor related to material heterogeneity and the transition from sample-scale to rock-mass-scale behaviour.

Derivation of material attenuation coefficient (α)

The decay of particle velocity (u) with length (L) represents the true Attenuation Coefficient due to material damping. The overall wave decay due to energy dissipation within the rock’s microstructure was analysed by modelling the relationship between particle velocity (u) and sample length (L):20\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:u={A}_{u}{L}^{-\alpha\:}$$\end{document}

where α is the composite decay exponent. This coefficient is a quantitative measure of the intrinsic energy absorption due to internal friction, microcracks, and grain boundaries.

Discussion of model applicability limitations

The proposed attenuation framework introduces a laboratory-derived parameter (βl) to characterise the intrinsic energy dissipation behaviour of the rock mass and to complement conventional field-based empirical models. While the results indicate a reasonable agreement between predicted and measured PPV trends, the applicability of the model must be interpreted within the scope and assumptions of this study.

A key consideration is the scale transition between laboratory measurements and field conditions. The attenuation parameter βl is derived from ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) testing, which operates at significantly higher frequencies compared to blast-induced seismic waves. Although the wave characteristics differ, both follow similar fundamental principles of wave propagation, geometric spreading, and material damping. Therefore, βl is interpreted as a relative indicator of attenuation behaviour rather than a direct representation of field-scale response.

Another important aspect is the distinction between dynamic and static material properties. The elastic modulus derived from UPV measurements represents dynamic or small-strain conditions and is typically higher than that obtained from static mechanical tests such as uniaxial compressive strength (UCS). This difference arises from strain-amplitude effects under low-strain dynamic loading. Accordingly, the dynamic properties are used to characterise wave propagation behaviour, while recognising their limitations under field-scale conditions.

The model is validated using data from a single limestone mine, and its generalisation to other geological formations should be approached with caution. Rock heterogeneity, discontinuities, weathering, and in-situ stress conditions can significantly influence attenuation behaviour. Thus, should be interpreted as formation-specific and requires further validation across different rock types.

The predictive framework retains dependence on the calibrated constant , which incorporates source-related factors such as explosive characteristics and blast geometry. While the introduction of βl reduces reliance on purely empirical site constants, it does not eliminate the need for field calibration. Instead, the framework should be viewed as a hybrid approach that enhances the physical basis of conventional prediction methods.

Although regression-based modelling is adopted in this study, consistent with approaches such as the USBM model, it offers clear interpretability and a direct link between material properties and vibration response. Future work may explore integration with data-driven or hybrid modelling techniques.

Finally, the uncertainty associated with field measurements, including variability in blast execution, instrumentation limitations, and environmental noise, has not been explicitly quantified. Incorporating statistical error analysis, confidence intervals, and cross-validation would further strengthen model robustness.

Conclusion

In a heterogeneous limestone mine site, this study investigated the feasibility of utilising laboratory-derived acoustic characteristics from non-destructive ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) testing as a predictive tool for field-scale blast-induced ground vibration (BIGV). The integration of field monitoring data from 13 production blasts with laboratory testing enabled the development of a site-specific attenuation framework.

The intrinsic data scatter, however, validated the shortcomings of conventional empirical models in accurately representing intricate geological variation. Limitations and future work

While the proposed framework provides useful insights into the relationship between rock properties and vibration attenuation, several limitations remain. First, the laboratory experiments were conducted on intact rock specimens, which primarily reflect intrinsic material behaviour. In actual mining conditions, wave propagation is strongly influenced by large-scale features such as joints, fractures, bedding planes, and weathered zones, which can significantly alter attenuation characteristics. Second, the use of UPV testing introduces a frequency-scale limitation, as ultrasonic waves operate at much higher frequencies than blast-induced vibrations. As a result, the derived attenuation behaviour may not fully capture frequency-dependent mechanisms occurring at the field scale. Third, the study is based on a specific limestone geological setting, and variations in mineral composition, porosity, and structural features in other rock types may require recalibration of the proposed parameter.

Future research should extend the framework by incorporating rock mass classification parameters, discontinuity characteristics, and frequency-dependent attenuation models. In addition, advanced experimental techniques—such as material damping characterisation—along with hybrid approaches combining physics-based and machine-learning models, may further improve prediction accuracy and field applicability. These efforts will be essential to address the current limitations and to validate the proposed framework across a broader range of geological and operational conditions.
