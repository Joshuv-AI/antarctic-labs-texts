Complete chloroplast genome sequence of the medicinal plant Oxyceros horridus (Rubiaceae) and phylogenetic analysis
Authors: Thu Thao Thi Huynh, Minh Trong Quang, Hoang Danh Nguyen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOxyceros horridus Lour. 1790, valued for ornamental and medicinal properties, has been extensively utilized in traditional medicinal in Vietnam. This study assembled and characterized the first chloroplast of Oxyceros horridus, consisted of 155,333 bp with a GC content of 37.5%. The genome included 130 annotated genes (including 85 protein-coding genes, 37 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes), exhibited a typical quadripartite structure consistent with those found in other members of the Rubiaceae family. Phylogenetic analysis placed O. horridus in Ixoroideae subfamily, closely related to Fosergia shweliensis and Gardenia tenophylla. This study enriches cp genome data and phylogenetic insights within Rubiaceae.Keywords: Oxyceros horridus, Găng gai cong, genome assembly, plastome, Rubiaceae

Introduction

The Rubiaceae Juss family, recognized as one of the largest flowering plant families, though only a few are well-known and widely used, such as Coffea and the fragrant ornamental Ixora and Gardenia species (POWO 2024). This family mainly consists of climbing flowering plants that thrive in warm climates and tropical regions (Chamchumroon and Puff 2003; Wong et al. 2018). One member of the Rubiaceae family, Oxyceros horridus is distinguished for its delightful fragrance, ornamental purposes, and medicinal properties. Oxyceros horridus, known by its heterotypic synonym Randia siamensis (Miq.) Craib 1911, is indigenous to the rainforest of Indochina, thriving primarily in wet tropical biomes (Ho 1999; Chamchumroon and Puff 2003). This plant is known by various local names in Vietnam, including ‘Găng gai cong’, ‘Mo tró’, and ‘Ắc ó’ (Ho 1999). The species features appealing flowers, shiny leaves, and a vine-like growth habit, making it well-suited for use in gardens and landscaping projects (Persson 1995; Ho 1999; Chamchumroon and Puff 2003). All parts of O. horridus plant have been widely utilized in traditional medicine in Thailand for different purposes, such as serving as an emmenagogue or hematinic (fruits, stems), herbal drink for regulating blood pressure (leaves), and as medicine having antipyretic and anti-scurvy properties (roots) (Pongboonrod 1979). Khwanchuea et al. (2007) have demonstrated that an extract of O. horridus has both a hypertensive and a positive chronotropic effect on the cardiovascular system (Khwanchuea et al. 2007). Recently, a study conducted by Sutopo et al. (2024) revealed the remarkable anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and moisturizing properties of an ethanol extract derived from O. horridus stems, suggesting its potential as a valuable ingredient in future skincare formulations (Sutopo et al. 2024).

Most research on O. horridus has primarily concentrated on its morphological traits, while investigations at the molecular level have been relatively infrequent. Moreover, analyses of the chloroplast (cp) genome yielded valuable insights into the evolutionary history and genetic diversity (Dobrogojski et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2022; Huynh et al. 2024; Nguyen et al. 2024). Consequently, this study aimed to characterize the complete cp genome of O. horridus by utilizing next-generation sequencing technology. This approach aimed to (1) delineate the structural features of its cp genome and (2) furnish preliminary insights into its phylogenetic relationship within the Rubiaceae family.

Materials and methods

Specimens of O. horridus were collected from Tay Ninh Province, Vietnam (11°24’23.0"N, 106°14’44.6"E) (Figure 1). These specimens are preserved at the Department of Microbiology and Parasitology, Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City under voucher number FGRG-07.2024-Ganggaicong (contact: Minh Trong Quang, email: qtminh@ump.edu.vn). The specimens underwent dehydration by treatment with silica gel packets and were subsequently maintained at ambient temperature. Genomic DNA was extracted employing the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (#69104, Qiagen). The extracted DNA was prepared for sequencing using the Miseq sequencer (Illumina, USA) with 150 bp paired-end mode. The following sequence purification step was performed with Trimmomatic version 0.32, with approximately 5.03 Gb of high-quality reads were obtained as the result (Bolger et al. 2014). The complete cp genome of O. horridus was de novo assembled using GetOrganelle v1.7.7.1 (Jin et al. 2020). Genome annotation was executed with the CpGAVAS2 tool (Shi et al. 2019). Then, the cp genome of O. horridus was submitted to GenBank under accession number PQ287262. The representation of the circular cp genome was generated using OGDraw version 1.3.1 (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html) (Greiner et al. 2019). The gene maps for both cis-splicing and trans-splicing were constructed utilizing the CPGview website (http://www.1kmpg.cn/cpgview/) (Liu et al. 2023).

To clarify the phylogenetic relationships, we obtained 26 cp genomes from the GenBank database, including that of O. horridus, 24 related species from the Rubiaceae family, and an outgroup (Lonicera szechuanica Batalin 1895 from the Caprifoliaceae family). The protein-coding sequences of all selected species were extracted and aligned using MAFFT version 7 (Katoh and Standley 2013) with default settings, integrated within Geneious Prime version 2024.0.5. Following this, sequences with gaps and poor alignments were manually removed in Geneious Prime. Maximum likelihood tree reconstruction was performed using the TVM+I + G evolutionary model as predicted by jModeltest version 2.1.7 (Posada 2008). The phylogenetic tree was reconstructed via IQ-TREE webserver (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) with 1,000 bootstrap replicates (Nguyen et al. 2015). The finalized tree was edited and visualized using the iTOL version 5 webserver (Letunic and Bork 2021).

Results

The cp genome of O. horridus was de novo assembled from 407,821 pair-end reads, yielding an average coverage depth of 792.9×, with coverage ranging from a minimum of 327× to a maximum of 1089× (Figure S1). The O. horridus cp genome is 155,333 bp in length and exhibits the typical quadripartite structure, which includes a large single-copy (LSC) region of 85,576 bp, a small single-copy (SSC) region of 18,141 bp, and a pair of inverted repeat (IR) regions, each 25,808 bp in length (Figure 2). The overall GC content of the genome is 37.5%, with the IR regions displaying the highest GC content at 43.2%. The LSC region has a GC content of 35.4%, while the SSC region registers the lowest GC content at 31.3%.

The cp genome encodes a total of 130 genes, including 85 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 37 tRNA genes, and 8 rRNA genes. Within the IR regions, there are duplications of 7 PCGs (rps19, rpl2, rpl23, ycf2, ndhB, rps7, and rps12), 4 rRNA genes (rrn5, rrn4.5, rrn23, and rrn16), and 7 tRNA genes (trnA-UGC, trnI-CAU, trnI-GAU, trnL-CAA, trnN-GUU, trnR-ACG, and trnV-GAC). Additionally, nine cis-splicing (atpF, clpP, petB, petD, rpoC1, rpl2, rpl16, rps16, and ycf3) and one trans-splicing (rps12 with three exons) genes were identified (Figures S2 and S3).

The phylogenetic analysis using cp PCGs from related Rubiaceae species confirmed that the Oxyceros genus is closely related to the clade that includes the genera Fosbergia and Gardenia, with strong support indicated by a bootstrap value of 100 (Figure 3).

Discussion and conclusion

At present, twelve species belonging to the Oxyceros genus are recognized (POWO 2024), and this study has successfully assembled the inaugural complete cp genome for this genus. The O. horridus cp genome measures 155,333 bp, which falls within the size spectrum typically observed in the Ixoroideae subfamily, ranging from 154,717 bp in Fosbergia shweliensis (J. Anthony) Tirveng & Sastre 1997 to 155,379 bp in Emmenopterys henryi Oliv. 1889 (Tesfaye et al. 2007; Duan et al. 2017; Geng et al. 2020; Deng et al. 2024) (Table 1). To reveal the phylogenetic placement of O. horridus, complete cp sequences from 25 species across three subfamilies within the Rubiaceae family were analyzed (Figure 3). Our phylogenetic analysis offered insights into the relationship placement of the Oxyceros genus within the Rubiacae family with robust bootstrap values, which is based on molecular data from the cp genome. Furthermore, this taxonomic classification of O. horridus is aligned with prior studies concerning Rubiaceae phylogeny, consisting of a consensus phylogeny based on the resulting phylogenetic trees of 130 studies (Razafimandimbison and Rydin 2024), molecular phylogeny from five different cp sequences (rbcL, trnT-trnF, rps16, atpB-rbcL, and ndhF) (Bremer and Eriksson 2009; Mouly et al. 2014), morphological assessments (Andreasen and Bremer 2000; Vinckier et al. 2000), and integrated molecular phylogenetic studies integrated nuclear DNA (ITS) and cp DNA (rbcL) (Andreasen and Bremer 2000). Nevertheless, due to the rarity of the cp genome reports in Oxyceros species, there exists a pronounced necessity for obtaining additional cp genome sequences from this genus to further clarify its phylogenetic relationship. In conclusion, this study has augmented the cp genome resources for the family Rubiaceae and the Oxyceros genus. As the explosive growth of sequencing technologies, the availability of genomic resources is unveiled to furnish a deep understanding about phylogenetic relationships within the Rubiaceae family.
