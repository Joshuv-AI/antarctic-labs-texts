Effectiveness of an Affordable Toa Uzito Offloading Device for Healing Plantar Diabetic Foot Ulcer: A Matched Case–Control Clinical Study
Authors: Zulfiqarali G Abbas, Roozbeh Naemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are difficult to manage in resource‐limited settings, where access to standard offloading devices is limited for healing of plantar diabetic foot ulcers. This is due to the cost, limited availability, and the need for specialised expertise. Toa Uzito is a low‐cost offloading device developed to address this gap. We evaluated the effectiveness of a low‐cost offloading device (Toa Uzito) in improving healing outcomes in patients with plantar ulcers. A matched case–control clinical study was conducted at a specialised diabetic foot centre in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. Patients with a single plantar DFU were enrolled. Those treated with Toa Uzito were compared with matched controls who received standard care without offloading. Controls were matched based on age, body mass index, and diabetes duration. The primary outcome was time to complete ulcer healing. Healing were analysed using Kaplan Meier survival analysis and Cox regression. A total of 621 patients were included (128 intervention, 493 controls), with a mean follow‐up of 133.8 days. Ulcer healing was significantly better in the intervention group: 44.9% healed (versus 34.9% in controls) by 12 weeks, and increasing to 85% (versus 75.2% in controls) by 24 weeks. All patients in the intervention group healed, with a shorter mean healing time (125.7 vs. 137.5 days). Toa Uzito also demonstrated significantly superior healing efficacy compared with standard care after stratification by SINBAD classification (χ2 = 71.2, p < 0.01) . The low‐cost Toa Uzito significantly improves healing rates and reduces time to healing. Its affordability and simplicity makes it an effective offloading strategy for promoting DFU healing in resource‐limited settings.Keywords: diabetic foot, low cost offloading, offloading devices, plantar ulcer, ulcer classification

Introduction

Eighty percent of the people with diabetes come from low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. The global population from Asia (4.8 billion—59%) and Africa (1.5 billion—18%) covers 77% of the world population, and the majority of them are in LMIC [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. It is forecasted that the greatest potential increases in diabetes prevalence rates will occur in LMICs and will have a huge burden in the next 20 years [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Diabetes remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality across the globe and specifically in LMICs [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Among the numerous complications of diabetes, the most severe are diabetes‐related foot complications (DRFC), which are linked to the highest rates of morbidity and mortality [1, 2, 3, 4, 5].

Across the world, data suggest that 40%–60% of all lower limb non‐traumatic amputations are related to diabetes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9]. Approximately 25% of those with diabetes will develop a foot ulcer during their lifetime [10, 11]. Five‐year recurrence rates of ulcer are 70% and up to 85% of amputations in people with diabetes are preceded by a foot ulcer [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9]. People with diabetes with one lower limb amputation have a 50% risk of developing a serious lesion in the second limb within 2 years. These patients have also a 70% mortality rate in the 5 years, and for those patients receiving renal treatment the mortality rate is 74% at 2 years following the initial amputation [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12]. The Hazard Ratio (for mortality) at 10 years for people with diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) is twice as compared to those with no diabetes [13]. Foot complications, especially the septic limb, can be serious and costly [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16]. Most published reports confirm that foot lesions in Sub Saharan Africa, people with diabetes are associated with diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), commonly have an infectious aetiology, and can be critical and costly [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16].

We previously estimated the expenses for treating foot ulcers in five countries with widely varying health care practices, reimbursement policies, and gross domestic products [14, 15, 16]. It was estimated that in Tanzania the cost to the patient and relatives about $ 3060 (International $: $Int), which is 1.6% of the $Int 188 645 in the United States to treat the same DFU [14, 15, 16]. In LMICs, medical insurance or reimbursement of medical expenses are not widely available, and therefore the management of a foot ulcer can cost 2 years of average income for the patient as seen in the case of Tanzania. Recently similar study was done but for all the seven regions globally and we found in fact the cost have actually gone high from 24 month of salary to 30 month of salary from Sub‐Saharan Africa [16]. It is very common in the LMICs that the patient is the income generator in a culturally extended family: thus, a foot ulcer episode is devastating and catastrophic for a patient and their family [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16].

Calluses that are commonly associated with DPN cause an increase in the plantar pressure, hence making the foot susceptible to DFU [4]. Generally, because excess pressure inhibits DFU healing, hence offloading the foot has been considered a useful measure to facilitate DFU healing. In a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis, offloading has been established as a requirement to promote effective healing of DFUs [17].

However, access to non‐removable offloading devices, such as total contact casts (TCC) and removable devices, can be costly and difficult to maintain in LMICs [14, 15, 16]. It is a well‐known fact that TCC is the gold standard treatment for offloading DFU treatment, but it is also a well‐known fact that it is not widely used. Due to a variety of reasons, the total contact cast has not been used routinely in clinics, especially in LMICs. These include: the inability to maintain the cast due poor environmental conditions, climate condition like high temperature and humidity (leads to maceration of skin and followed by new ulcer and infection), to maintain the cast in the sense of hygiene in people with low social economic conditions, the cost, inability to directly access and care for ulcers, as well as the impaired mobility due to non‐removable nature [1, 5].

In addition there are other factors such as: potential for new ulcers to develop, increased risk of infection if not properly managed, discomfort from prolonged wear, potential complications in patients with poor circulation (peripheral arterial disease), active infections in the foot, and the need for specialised staff to apply and monitor the cast.

The lack of access to more sophisticated offloading devices can contribute to imposing a heavy socio‐economic burden in LMICs [14, 15, 16]. To decrease the associated burden of DRFC, a cost‐effective offloading intervention to facilitate wound healing following DFU was necessary. However, making a typical conventional offloading device, such as removable and non‐removable contact casts and footwear, would incur extra costs that are not usually feasible in LMICs. Excessive costs and the scarcity of skilled personnel to implement and manage off‐loading devices often impede the implementation of this therapeutic option.

To achieve a low‐cost solution, we developed an offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) that requires no special training or costly set up, and which can be implemented easily even at grass root level means even in villages. This device has an affordable cost (costing less than 9 cents of USD), can be changed every day if needed along with dressing, needs no special trained person, and can be applied by the person who dresses the wound. We named this low‐cost offloading device Toa Uzito, meaning offloading in the local Swahili language. After implementation in clinical practice, we carried out this study to investigate the effectiveness of this low‐cost intervention in DFU healing.

Therefore, this matched case–control clinical study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of the low‐cost Toa Uzito offloading device in promoting healing of plantar diabetic foot ulcers compared with matched controls receiving standard care. We hypothesised that patients treated with the Toa Uzito device would demonstrate improved healing outcomes and shorter healing times compared to patients managed without structured offloading.

This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of using a low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) in improving the healing efficiency of plantar DFU.

Methods

Study Design and Settings

The study was matched case control clinical study with patients who had DFU and attended a specialist diabetes service in Dar‐es‐Salaam, Tanzania. The matched case control clinical study involved two groups: intervention and controls. Cases were patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and attended a diabetes clinic in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, between December 2020 and December 2021, where their management included a low‐cost off‐loading intervention (Toa Uzito). Controls were patients who also met the inclusion and exclusion criteria but attended the same clinic between January 2013 and December 2020, and their management did not include the low‐cost off‐loading intervention (Toa Uzito).

Standard care was provided for all the patients at the centre in accordance with the international protocol of ulcer treatment and guidelines by International Working Group of the Diabetic Foot [8]. All patients (case and controls) gave informed consent to participate in the study according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients

The inclusion criteria were patients with diabetes presenting with a single plantar DFU on one foot at baseline. DFU was defined as a break in the skin of the foot of a person with diabetes penetrating at least through the epidermis and into the dermis according to International Working Group of Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) consensus guidelines [8]. Exclusion criteria were the presence of major lower limb amputation on either ipsilateral or contralateral foot at baseline. All the patients included in the study were consecutive patients who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria as mentioned above.

Matching Procedure

Intervention group was treated with the Toa Uzito offloading devices and matched with historical controls according to age, body mass index (BMI), and duration of diabetes. Matching was undertaken to minimise confounding and improve comparability between the intervention and control groups. This was done by matching each patient in the intervention group with 3–4 patients in the control group who had similar age, BMI, and duration of diabetes with up to 10% variation.

Data Collection

Categorical Measures

The general categorical measures were gender, marital status, diabetes type, smoking status, presence of retinopathy and history of ulceration according to IWGDF protocols [18]. The foot‐specific categorical measures included DPN (10‐g monofilament assessed at 10 sites across both feet, including Hallux, 3rd Toe, and 5th Toe, 1st Meta Tarsal Head (MTH), 3rd MTH, 5th MTH, lateral midfoot, medial midfoot, centre of the hindfoot, and dorsum of the foot) as proposed in the literature [19, 20]. All registered patients at the centre also undergo extensive evaluation of diabetic peripheral neuropathy which includes biothesiometer test and hot and cold evaluation. The presence of peripheral arterial disease (PAD) was also assessed by taking a relevant history for PAD, examining for signs of ischaemia and palpating of the peripheral foot pulses (dorsalis pedis and posterior tibialis pulses) in accordance with IWGFD guidelines [8]. In addition, ulcers were assessed in detail and were also classified according to SINBAD classifications [8].

The SINBAD classification only requires clinical examination, and is feasible to employ in restricted resource settings. The score is sum of scores across 6 categories as follows: Site, Ischaemia, Neuropathy, Bacterial infection, Area, and Depth where the scores under each are added together and presented as SINBAD score. The scores are given as: site (forefoot 0 points versus midfoot or hindfoot 1 point), ischaemia (at least one palpable pulse 0 points) versus reduced pedal blood flow (1 point), Neuropathy (0 points for no loss of sensation versus lost 1 point), bacterial infection (defined using IWGDF criteria) as absent (0 points) versus present (1 point), area for less than 1 cm2 (0 points) versus equal to greater than 1 cm2 (1 point), and depth as either superficial skin and subcutaneous tissue (0 points) versus deep including muscle or tendon (1 point) [8]. Treatment (Intervention vs. Control) and Follow‐Up

The Toa Uzito was made from a rolled sponge that is used under the retainer bandage, either proximal or distal to the ulcer depending on the ulcer location as decided by the lead physician, to facilitate offloading (Figures 1 and 2). The ulcers were given standard care in the control group, and the offloading intervention was used only in the intervention group. Patients in the control group were managed similarly but without the low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito). The offloading device (Toa Uzito) was changed by the nurses whenever the patients come for their wound dressing appointment that could be daily or every other day.

Material Characteristics of Sponge

The Toa Uzito device was constructed using an open‐cell polyurethane foam sponge commonly available for domestic use. The sponge measured approximately 20 × 10 × 5 cm and exhibited high compressibility with recovery following compression. Its low cost, lightweight structure, ease of shaping, and conforming properties make it suitable as an offloading material in low‐resource settings. For the Toa Uzito device, this sponge appears suitable because it: conforms well to the plantar contour, provides cushioning, is inexpensive and widely available, can easily be cut into customised shapes, and is lightweight and comfortable for patients.

All patients were treated initially with intravenous antibiotics followed by oral antibiotics. Dressing, cleaning, and debridement of ulcers were done on a regular basis. All ulcers on first presentation were clinically infected and therefore required systemic antibiotic therapy according to standard diabetic foot infection management principles. In many LMICs, advanced wound care products are often unavailable or unaffordable, making standard wound care combined with appropriate antibiotic therapy the practical approach. All patients received standard diabetic foot care including wound cleansing, dressing, regular sharp debridement where indicated, infection control, and offloading of plantar ulcers. The Toa Uzito device represented the offloading intervention evaluated in this matched case–control clinical study. Identical wound care was received by both groups.

In order to determine if the ulcer was completely healed or censored (not healed) during the follow‐up until they get healed or for at least 17 weeks, patients were extensively monitored.

Patients in both the control and intervention groups received crucial guidance. The diabetic foot nurse emphasised the importance of maintaining their regular foot care routines during checkups. Additionally, strict management of diabetes and high blood pressure was enforced through the concurrent use of oral hypoglycaemics and antihypertensive medications, and anti‐platelet medications were administered when necessary. Moreover, patients benefited from quarterly educational sessions conducted by the diabetic foot care nurse. Consistent glycaemic control was monitored by obtaining fasting or random blood glucose readings at each follow‐up appointment.

The rolled sponge is used under the non‐elastic bandage proximal or distal to the ulcer to facilitate offloading.

The statistical analysis was planned to account for the matched study design and the time‐to‐event nature of the primary outcome. All statistical tests were performed using IBM SPSSv.25. Kaplan–Meier Survival Analyses were used to investigate the differences in wound healing efficiency between the two groups.

In addition, Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the differences in wound healing efficiency between the two groups (considered as strata) considering the effect of all confounding variables (considered as covariates).

Results

A total of 621 patients (364 male, 257 female) with single plantar DFU in the study, with age (55.8 ± 10.2 years); BMI (26.8 ± 5.2 kg/m2), and duration of diabetes (125.2 ± 89.4 months). All patients had type 2 diabetes. During a follow‐up mean period of 133.8 ± 110.9 (Mean ± SD) days, a total of 128 (M/F: 73/55) patients in the intervention group were managed with the Toa Uzito and 493 (M/F: 291/202) were in the control group (Figure 3).

Following matching, the intervention and control groups were generally comparable with respect to demographic and clinical characteristics. No clinically important differences were observed in the matching variables.

Fifteen patients—1 from the intervention group and 14 from the control group—were lost to follow‐up, leaving 606 patients who completed the follow up. In the intervention group there were 57/127 (44.9%) and 108/127 (85%) patients that were healed by 12 and 24 weeks treatment durations respectively (Figure 4). These figures in the control group were 167/479 (34.9%) and 360/479 (75.2%) patients respectively (Figure 4). During treatment, 14 participants had amputation, 4 (1 major) in Intervention and 10 (4 major) in the Control group.

Overall Healing Efficiency Comparison

The Kaplan–Meier test indicated that the healing efficiency in low‐cost offloading was superior (Mean [95% CI]) compared to control: As all 127/127 patients who stayed in the trial healed during 125.7 [102.8–148.7] days in the intervention, whereas 472/479 patients healed during 137.5 [127.8–147.2] days in the control group, as presented in Figure 4 (χ 2 = 6.012, p = 0.014). When Cox Regression Analysis was conducted to control for all confounding variables including sex, ulcer history, previous amputation, SINBAD scores, and all individual component scores, and ulcer side, and all the continuous measures that were significantly different between the two groups (Table 1 and 2), the difference is survival between the intervention and control was significant (χ 2 = 71.2, p < 0.01).

When these results were adjusted for the differences in SINBAD wound classification the difference in healing efficiency was significant (χ 2 = 5.651, p = 0.017) (Table 3).

Discussion

Overall Healing Efficiency Comparison

The results of this matched case–control clinical study indicate the superior healing efficiency in the cases versus controls that is in line with offloading being regarded as improving the healing efficacy of DFU based on recent systematic review [17].

Patients treated with the intervention achieved higher healing rates, shorter healing times, and more favourable time‐to‐healing outcomes. These findings suggest that effective pressure redistribution can be achieved using an affordable offloading strategy, offering a practical alternative for diabetic foot care in resource‐limited settings.

The beneficial effect of the Toa Uzito device is plausible and consistent with the fundamental principle of offloading in diabetic foot management. Repetitive plantar pressure and mechanical stress impair wound healing by disrupting tissue repair and perpetuating local inflammation. By redistributing pressure away from the ulcer site, offloading creates a more favourable environment for tissue regeneration. The improved healing observed in this study therefore supports the established concept that reducing plantar pressure is central to successful management of neuropathic plantar diabetic foot ulcers.

Despite this, the results of the current study cannot be directly compared against the conventional offloading devices such as Total Contact Cast (TCC) as the gold standard for treating DFU. Total contact casts have shown improved healing rates when compared to other off‐loading devices in the treatment of plantar neuropathic DFU, with healing rates ranging from 89% to 92%, [21, 22] and in some cases over a significantly shorter period [23, 24]. Our low‐cost offloading device (Toa Uzito) has shown a healing rate of almost 100% of the plantar ulcers and a significant reduction in ulcer healing time.

This is relevant as Sinacore [25] reported that 45% of clinics offered no off‐loading in the management with TCC of DFU. More recently, studies have shown that TCC is not regularly used in the management of plantar DFU, and footwear modification is the preferred method of offloading despite its ineffectiveness in reducing plantar pressure and shearing forces [26, 27, 28]. This is despite TCC being recognised as the gold standard treatment for plantar DFU by every consensus committee [27].

The application of a TCC requires skill, is labour intensive and takes time [29]. While TCC is still in use in LMICs, it is hardly used as treatment for plantar DFU [1, 2, 3, 4]. The result of our study can be compared with the previous study in which alternative off‐loading methods, including an accommodative dressing, a healing shoe, and a walking splint, were compared to the gold standard total contact cast [30]. In that study, it was found that the healing rate of forefoot ulcers using alternative off‐loading methods was comparable to TCC when the location of the ulcer, participant age, and duration of ulceration were considered [30]. In addition, a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis based on the studies [17, 30, 31, 32], stipulates that ‘the felted foam with removable ankle‐high devices versus devices alone may cause little‐to‐no difference in proportions of ulcers healed’. However, the evidence is reported to be very uncertain for the studies categorised under ‘other offloading techniques’ [17]. Despite this, these devices are low cost and not labour intensive; even a marginal gain in healing efficacy would be advantageous.

Differences in Healing Efficacy Across the Ulcer Classifications

It is interesting to observe that the efficacy of the investigated low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) in healing ulcers varied across the three ulcer classifications. In brief only ulcers with SINBAD classifications 4 and 6 were found to have significantly better healing efficacy using the Toa Uzito. While the outcome was not significant for the SINBAD 5 category wounds, when the outcome was considered in conjunction with SINBAD ulcer classification the results were significant (χ 2 = 5.651, p = 0.017). Specifically, 127/127 patients healed during an average of 125.7 days in the low‐cost offloading device whereas only 472/479 patients healed during an average of 137.5 days in the control group. This indicates that not only was the percentage of full healing 1.5% more for offloading intervention versus in control (100% vs. 98.5% respectively), the duration of healing was also shorter by 11.8 days (8.6%) on average in Toa Uzito compared to the control group.

Strength and Limitations

Firstly, an important strength of this study is clinical relevance to resource‐limited healthcare systems. The intervention was developed using readily available materials and integrated into routine clinical practice without requiring expensive equipment or specialised infrastructure. As diabetes prevalence continues to increase across low‐ and middle‐income countries, affordable and accessible offloading strategies may contribute to improving diabetic foot outcomes in settings where conventional technologies cannot be implemented consistently.

Secondly, an important strength of the present study is the matched case–control design. Patients were matched for age, body mass index, and duration of diabetes. This approach improved comparability between groups and reduced the likelihood that the observed differences in healing were explained by baseline differences in patient characteristics. Furthermore, the relatively large sample size and the use of time‐to‐event analyses strengthened the robustness of the findings.

Thirdly, the strength of this study is the long follow‐up of patients until completion of healing or until they are censored. Despite the relatively big number of patients in this study, the number of patients in the intervention group could be further increased. However, this study is generally in line with the most studies conducted on offloading interventions so far as indicated in the recent systematic review where low‐to moderate certainty of evidence supporting their outcomes is indicated [17].

Fourthly, our proposed intervention is low‐cost, affordable, simple, clinically practical, and effective, which makes it ideal for resource‐limited settings.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a non‐randomised study that was not blinded. Second, the study was conducted at a single specialist diabetic foot centre, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other healthcare settings.

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The results of this study indicated that the use of a low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) can improve the healing efficacy of DFU by decreasing the healing time and increasing the number of DFUs that heal. The cost was less than 10 cents of a US dollar, which means we obtained 12 devices at a cost of only ~1 US dollar. The cost for bandages is estimated to be an average of 5 cents of a US dollar which makes the whole intervention very much low cost compared to other offloading interventions. It can be applied by any nurse globally. Considering the low cost of the device (Toa Uzito) and ease of adaptation and simplicity of application, and the fact that it is not labour intensive, this can have huge benefits to the healthcare systems, specifically in low resource settings. Considering that diabetes imposes a heavy burden on the health services across most African nations, the findings of this study have implications by decreasing the expenses for treating DFU to both the patient and society.

The principal implication of this study extends beyond evaluation of a single device. The findings reinforce the well‐established principle that effective pressure redistribution is central to healing plantar diabetic foot ulcers. Although non‐removable knee‐high offloading devices remain the recommended first‐line intervention in international guidelines, their limited availability and affordability in many low‐ and middle‐income countries restrict implementation. The Toa Uzito device was developed as an affordable, locally available alternative that may increase access to offloading therapy where conventional devices are inaccessible. Our findings therefore support innovation in context‐appropriate offloading solutions while maintaining adherence to the fundamental principle that some effective offloading is preferable to no offloading.

Effective pressure redistribution is essential for ulcer healing and patient adherence is a major determinant of success [33]. Any device that meaningfully reduces plantar pressure is likely to improve healing compared with standard footwear [33]. However, current evidence still favours non‐removable knee‐high devices (particularly Total Contact Casts and non‐removable walkers) as the benchmark against which new offloading devices should be evaluated [17].

Our findings are also further supported by a recent study by Gauna et al. [33] that offers a strong evidence base for the introduction of the low‐cost Toa Uzito device. The Toa Uzito provides adequate plantar pressure resdistribution, promotes good adherence, and achieves healing outcomes that are superior to existing practice in LMICs settings.

The outcome of this matched case–control clinical study can also have direct implications for health care practices in resource‐limited settings.

Conclusions

This matched case–control clinical study has indicated the superior efficacy of low‐cost offloading device (Toa Uzito) in improving healing time for DFU. The patients treated with this low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) all showed total healing of ulcers and had shorter time to heal. This can positively affect the healing and reduction of amputation rates. The low‐cost offloading intervention (Toa Uzito) is simple to implement, affordable, and is easily adaptable for use at the grass root level anywhere in the world.
