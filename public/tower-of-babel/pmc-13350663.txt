Persistent Hypoxia After Tricuspid Valve Replacement
Authors: John Nguyen, Thomas Merino, Vijay Kandula, Leticia Castillo Kontak, Raul Mercado Garcia, Angel Caldera, Aditi Shankar, Rafael Gonzalez, Soumya Neravetla, Jose F Condado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) is a novel therapy for symptomatic severe tricuspid regurgitation in high-risk surgical patients. History of Presentation

A 76-year-old woman with severe symptomatic tricuspid regurgitation (TR), deemed at high risk for surgical therapy by the multidisciplinary heart team and not amenable for edge-to-edge repair given a large coaptation gap, underwent transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR), with reduction of TR to trivial. After the procedure, she was hemodynamically stable but developed persistent hypoxia that worsened in the upright position, requiring high-flow oxygen by nasal cannula. The patient did not require any supplemental oxygen when seated or lying down.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, patent foramen ovale (PFO), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, severe TR, and worsening exertional dyspnea.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnoses considered in her case included pneumonia, lung injury, pulmonary embolism, congestive heart failure, and right-to-left shunt.

Discussion

TTVR is a treatment option for patients with severe symptomatic TR, poor candidacy for surgery, and complex anatomy unsuitable tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair.1 These complexities include enlarged tricuspid annulus or coaptation gaps, excessive leaflet tethering, and pacemaker/defibrillator lead impingement.1 TTVR has been shown to reduce TR severity and improve quality of life up to 2 years of follow-up.1 Hemodynamic and echocardiographic improvements have also been shown, attributed to reverse RA dilation and reverse RV remodeling over time. However, periprocedural and immediate postprocedural complications can include atrioventricular conduction disease requiring permanent pacemaker implantation, excessive bleeding, and right heart dysfunction.1 To date, POS has not been described as a common adverse effect of TTVR.

For this patient, the mechanism of disease likely involved acute post-TTVR RA anatomic distortion and new or unmasked RV dysfunction leading to transient increase of right-sided pressures, and thus, POS via a known PFO. There is at least 1 published case of POS from PFO after transcatheter aortic valve replacement, where computed tomography showed horizontal aorta compressing the RA.2

Immediately after TTVR, right-sided anatomical changes may promote increased right-to-left shunting in the setting of a pre-existing PFO.3 The prosthetic TTVR frame deployed in the tricuspid annulus may effectively elevate the annular plane. The reduction of regurgitant volume can also reduce RA and inferior vena cava stretching. Although RA reverse remodeling and dilation reduction can occur over time, postprocedural acute reduction in regurgitant volume and RA pressure may alter the RA shape and configuration of the atrial septal structures such as an existing PFO.3 In an upright posture, the inferior vena cava jet may become more coaxial to an existing PFO orifice, leading to greater right-to-left shunting. In our case, artifact created by the TTVR precluded direct comparison of the preprocedural and postprocedural echocardiographic images.

The reduction of severe TR immediately after TTVR improves forward flow that subsequently can increase RV afterload. This can cause an increase in RV pressure that overwhelms an RV that has been remodeled by longstanding severe TR, leading to RV dysfunction and elevated RA pressure in the absence of pulmonary hypertension. With a concomitant PFO, the elevated RA pressure can surpass left atrial pressure, promoting flow from the RA into the left atrium. Acute post-TTVR RV dysfunction combined with the acute post-TTVR anatomical changes of the RA can lead to dynamic right-to-left shunting through an existing PFO, resulting in POS.4,5 Further quantification with repeating right heart catheterization may have helped to fully objectify this theory. However, our patient improved and was weaned off oxygen therapy after PFO closure. Interestingly, this patient had a known history of PFO but had no prior embolic event, and thus, did not have an indication for closure before TTVR.6

Undifferentiated hypoxia after TTVR should warrant thorough work-up to assess a wide differential diagnosis, including decompensated heart failure, pulmonary embolism, and pneumonia. Medical management should take place to address any periprocedural complications. With any platypnea-orthodeoxia, work-up to confirm PFO including TTE and TEE should be performed before PFO closure. PFO closure has been shown to effectively address POS as a stand-alone indication.5,7 This case describes a rare complication of POS in a novel TTVR treatment option.

Conclusions

TTVR is a treatment option in select patients with symptomatic severe TR and poor candidacy for surgery and transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. Acute post-TTVR anatomical and hemodynamic changes in the RA and RV may unmask or worsen any underlying intracardiac shunting. In the setting of an existing PFO, RA configurational changes and RV dysfunction may work in tandem to increase right-sided pressures without pulmonary hypertension, and worsen right-to-left shunting through a PFO, leading to platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome. PFO should be highly considered on the differential diagnosis for any post-TTVR patient with persistent hypoxia, particularly if the hypoxia follows a paradoxical pattern with postural changes.
