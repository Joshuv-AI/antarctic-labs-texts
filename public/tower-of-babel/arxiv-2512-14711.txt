Promoting Fairness in Information Access within Social Networks
Changan Liu, Ahad N. Zehmakan, Xiaotian Zhou, Zhongzhi Zhang

Abstract
The advent of online social networks has facilitated fast and wide spread of information. However, some users, especially members of minority groups, may be less likely to receive information spreading on the network, due to their disadvantaged network position. We study the optimization problem of adding new connections to a network to enhance fairness in information access among different demographic groups. We provide a concrete formulation of this problem where information access is measured in terms of resistance distance, offering a new perspective that emphasizes global network structure and multi-path connectivity. The problem is shown to be NP-hard. We propose a simple greedy algorithm which turns out to output accurate solutions, but its run time is cubic, which makes it undesirable for large networks. As our main technical contribution, we reduce its time complexity to linear, leveraging several novel approximation techniques. In addition to our theoretical findings, we also conduct an extensive set of experiments using both real-world and synthetic datasets. We demonstrate that our linear-time algorithm can produce accurate solutions for networks with millions of nodes.

Introduction

Social networks have become vital channels for communication, allowing users to share information quickly and connect with others globally. However, they can harbor biases and inequities for different demographic groups
[
1
,
2
]
. It has been consistently observed that disadvantaged and minority groups, such as non-male workers in a male-dominated industry, have less access to life-altering information, such as job advertisements

Consequently, there has been a growing interest in introducing countermeasures to amplify the information access for disadvantaged groups. Roughly speaking, the

is the likelihood of being exposed to a piece of information circulating in the network. This is usually measured by social capital and centrality indicators such as degree, closeness, and information centrality
[
3
,
9
,
10
,
11

. Then, the goal is to close the gap in information access level between advantaged and disadvantaged groups in a network. It is worth emphasizing that here “fairness” usually refers to “equality”. Since this a new and emerging line of research, more complex notion of fairness still to be formulated and studied.

Existing attempts towards fair information access typically involve two steps: firstly, quantifying the degree of unfairness in information access

,

,

,
4
,

,

,

; and secondly, devising proactive countermeasures

. One well-studied approach is to engineer the seed users for the information spread to achieve fairness
[

,
4
]
. However, these strategies have two fundamental shortcomings: they are temporary solutions (i.e., they need to be applied actively) and are not very practical since we cannot control the seed users in many scenarios. An alternative, that doesn’t suffer from these shortcomings, is modifying the underlying network structure to resolve the inequality by enhancing the structural importance of the disadvantaged group, cf.
[
12
,
2
,
14
]
. However, one of the main challenges in this setting is the complexity bottlenecks, particularly given the massive size of many real-world networks.

Various graph parameters have been introduced to measure the centrality, social capital, and information access of a node (user) in a network, such as degree, closeness, PageRank
[

,
17
]
. One measure which has gained substantial popularity is average resistance distance

,

]
, due to its ability to capture complex local and global network structures beyond other measures. Consequently, we use this measure to define the notion of fairness.

Some common graph operations, used to reach various objectives such as fairness, include edge/node deletion, addition, and rewiring

,

,

We study the problem where the input is an
n
-node graph
G
(representing a social network), a budget
k
, a disadvantaged group
T

, and we aim to minimize the gap between their information access (measured in terms of average resistance distance) by adding

Our contribution can be summarized as follows:

•

We introduce a new perspective on fairness of information access by formulating it through effective resistance. We show that this problem is NP-hard and its objective function is not supermodular.

•

We propose a greedy approach which performs well, but its run time is cubic, making it impractical for large networks.

•

Leveraging several novel approximation techniques, such as fast Laplacian solver and the high-dimensional convex hull approximation, we introduce a linear time algorithm.

•

We conduct experiments on a large set of datasets. The experiments demonstrate that our proposed linear time algorithm not only produces accurate solutions, but also can manage networks with millions of nodes.

II
Related Work

In this section, we briefly review the literature related to our work.

Information Dissemination.
Social networks play a key role in spreading information, sharing news, and promoting lesser-known businesses. The efficiency of this process is influenced by the network’s topology, which can be characterized by structural properties like spectral radius, algebraic connectivity, and mixing time
[
25
,
26
,
11
]
. One important measure is the Kirchhoff index (the total pairwise resistance distance, see Definition
III.5
)
[
27
]
, which reflects network connectivity and has wide applications. A lower Kirchhoff index suggests better connectivity and is related to other metrics like commute and cover time
[
28
]
.

A wide spectrum of research studies has focused on enhancing the overall information access by reducing the Kirchhoff index. For instance, in
[
29
]
, the authors investigated the minimization of Kirchhoff index by assigning edge weights, while in
[
30
,
31
]
, the authors examined minimizing the graph resistance distance by adding edges. However, these works optimized a single metric of global connectivity while neglected the
fairness
aspect, which refers to whether the intervention would improve the information access of various groups in a fair manner. In this paper, we develop fast algorithms that simultaneously optimize the Kirchhoff index and fairness in information access.

Algorithmic Fairness.

Algorithmic fairness has been an active research topic in social network analysis, with systematic developments on the input, algorithm, and output
[
32
,
33
]
. Despite the prevalence of graph-structured data in many areas
[
34
]
, existing works usually overlooked them, focusing on independent and identically distributed data such as spatial or text data
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
. In recent years, novel methods have emerged to extend traditional fairness notation and algorithms to graph data, including group-based fairness for centrality measures
[
15
,
14
]
, embeddings
[
39
]
, influence maximization
[
4
,
12
,
13
]
, and clustering
[
40
]
, where groups are often divided by attributes such as gender, religion or race
[
14
]
. There are also individual fairness approaches based on the premise that similar nodes should be treated similarly
[
41
,
42
]
. Our work focuses on the group-based fairness of the graph algorithm input, namely the graph structure itself.

Furthermore, during recent years, fair access to information has emerged as a pressing concern. Information access, which is closely related to the notion of social capital
[
43
,
3
]
, pertains to the acquisition and utilization of information from social networks and was initially introduced in
[
3
]
. Prior research on fair information access mainly relied on the influence maximization framework, which aims to maximize the influence of seed nodes while also satisfying certain fairness constraints through fair seeding
[
4
,
7
,
3
]
or edge recommendation
[
12
]
. Another line of research has considered the network structure solely, such as decreasing information unfairness by drawing help of the access matrix
[

]
or access signatures
[

. However, their methods can not handle large networks due to the lack of fast algorithms. Our work differs from most of these works in two ways: We not only provide a novel and intuitive formulation of the problem building on the popular resistance distance, but also devise an efficient and accurate algorithm which can handle networks with several million nodes.

III-C

Resistance Distance

For an undirected connected network
\mathcal{G}
, we could construct a corresponding electrical network, with each edge being replaced by a resistance of one ohm. Next, we introduce three quantities of resistance distance based on this electrical network.

Definition III.3
.

(Pairwise Resistance Distance
[
45
]
). For a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, let
u,v\in V
be two distinct nodes and
\bm{\mathit{b}}_{uv}=\bm{\mathit{e}}_{u}-\bm{\mathit{e}}_{v}
, the pairwise
resistance distance between
u
and
v
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{uv}=\bm{\mathit{b}}_{uv}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{uv}.

Definition III.4
.

(Node Resistance Distance
[
46
]
). For graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, the resistance distance
\mathcal{R}_{v}
of node
v
is defined as the pairwise resistance distances between node
v
and all nodes in
V
, i.e.,

\mathcal{R}_{v}=\sum_{u\in V}\mathcal{R}_{uv}=n\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}_{vv}+\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\big).

Definition III.5
.

(Kirchhoff index
[
27
]
). The Kirchhoff index
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{G})
of a graph
\mathcal{G}
is defined as the sum of resistance distances over all node pairs, which is a measure of the overall connectedness of a network; namely,

\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{G})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u,v\in V\\
u<v\end{subarray}}\mathcal{R}_{uv}=n\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{{\dagger}}\big).

Intuition.
Lower resistance distance between nodes implies easier communication and exchange of information. This measure has particularly gained significant popularity since it is capable of encoding complex local and global structure which impact information spread. Two complementary views make this concrete.

Electrical view:
injecting one unit of current at
u
and removing it at
v
yields a voltage drop equal to
\mathcal{R}_{uv}
.

Random-walk view:
the commute time satisfies
\mathrm{CommuteTime}(u,v)=2m\,\mathcal{R}_{uv}
, so smaller resistance means easier mutual reachability and faster diffusion. Please refer to
[
47
,
48
,
49
]
for more details.

IV
PROBLEM FORMULATION

IV-A

Definitions

In social networks, different groups are often found to have unequal access to information due to a range of factors such as socio-economic status, demographics, or other factors that shape their position within a network. This issue has been explained and highlighted in prior work
[
2
,
9
]
. As the first step to address this issue, we formulate the information access of a group in Definition
IV.1
.

Definition IV.1
.

(
I
, Group Information Access). For any non-empty group
S\subseteq V
in
\mathcal{G}
, we measure its capability of accessing information from the network using the average resistance distance, which is the sum of resistance distances of the nodes in
S
divided by its size, that is

\displaystyle I_{S}=\frac{\sum_{v\in S}\mathcal{R}_{v}}{\left|S\right|}=\frac{n}{\left|S\right|}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}_{S}+\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\big).

The parameter
I_{S}
measures the information access capability of a group
S
. The smaller the value of
I_{S}
, the better access the group has to the information disseminated in the network. The rationale is that resistance distance reflects the “effective proximity” of a node to the rest of the graph by aggregating contributions from all paths. Averaging these scores over a group yields a stable and interpretable notion of how easily the group can exchange information with the network. We call a group advantaged if it has a lower
I
and disadvantaged if it has a higher
I
. To simplify our analysis, we focus on the two-category case in this paper, where nodes are categorized as either advantaged or disadvantaged. Nevertheless, our algorithms can easily be extended to cover cases where nodes belong to more than two categories. It is noteworthy that in the real-world social networks, categorization into two groups based on factors such as gender is common.

Definition IV.2
.

(
U
, Information Access Unfairness). For an advantaged group
S
and a disadvantaged group
T
in
\mathcal{G}
, the extent of unfairness in information access is defined as

\displaystyle U_{\mathcal{G}}=I_{T}-I_{S}.

The intuition behind the definition of
U
is to measure to what extent the network’s structure enables equitable access to information among different groups. A value closer to 0 indicates a fairer network, whereas a larger value indicates more significant disparity between groups. Specifically, when
U_{\mathcal{G}}=0
, the network satisfies group equality
[
14
]
.

Definition IV.3
.

(
R
, Graph Resistance).
We define the resistance of a graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
to be

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{G}}=\frac{I_{V}}{2}=\frac{\sum_{v\in V}\mathcal{R}_{v}}{2n}=\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\big).

The graph resistance
R
is closely related to the Kirchhoff index in Definition
III.5
.

Remark.
As we mentioned, graph resistance and efficient information dissemination have inverse relation. Thus, we interchangeably use the terminologies of
minimizing graph resistance
and
maximizing information access efficiency
.

IV-B

Fair Information Access: Problem Statement

We write
I_{S}(E_{a})
and
I_{T}(E_{a})
to denote the resulting average resistance distance of group
S
and
T
respectively after adding the edges in
E_{a}
, and
R_{\mathcal{G}}(E_{a})
to denote the resulting overall graph resistance.
We study the following objective function:

\displaystyle F(E_{a})=(1-\lambda)R_{\mathcal{G}}(E_{a})+\lambda\big(I_{S}(E_{a})^{2}+I_{T}(E_{a})^{2}\big)

(1)

where the hyperparameter
\lambda
controls the trade-off between the minimization of graph resistance and unfairness.

Rationale of the objective function.
Eq. (
1
) couples a network–efficiency term with a group–aware term.
Minimizing the effective resistance
R_{\mathcal{G}}
alone is group–agnostic and may perpetuate existing advantages, whereas selecting edges by group access alone can myopically inflate local access without meaningfully shortening network-wide paths, yielding diminishing
global returns
.
We therefore adopt a standard bi–criteria scalarization that (i) reduces global impedance (
R_{\mathcal{G}}
) and (ii) steers access in a group–aware manner.

For the fairness part, we optimize the sum of squared group access scores,
I_{S}^{2}+I_{T}^{2}
, rather than the raw disparity
U:=I_{T}-I_{S}
in Def. IV.2.
Directly optimizing
U
is ill–behaved (it can be negative and is non–monotone), while
|U|
is non–smooth and hinders large–scale optimization.
In contrast,
I_{S}^{2}+I_{T}^{2}
is nonnegative, smooth, and—critically—decomposes cleanly into edge–wise contributions, enabling scalable marginal estimation.
This surrogate is also linked to disparity (Cauchy–Schwarz):
for
I_{S},I_{T}\geq 0
,

|U|=|I_{T}-I_{S}|\ \leq\ \sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{I_{S}^{2}+I_{T}^{2}},

so lowering the squared sum tightens a direct bound on
|U|
.
Intuitively, the first–order change

\mathrm{d}(I_{S}^{2}+I_{T}^{2})=2I_{S}\,\mathrm{d}I_{S}+2I_{T}\,\mathrm{d}I_{T}

weights improvements by the current group levels, which biases selections toward reducing disparity; in our experiments this aligns with edges that benefit the disadvantaged group more.
The above design is consistent with common practice in fairness optimization
[
13
,
54
]
.
(When a third group
O
exists, we extend the second term by adding
I_{O}^{2}
.)

For a connected undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, if we add a set
E_{a}
of nonexistent edges to
\mathcal{G}
forming a new graph
\mathcal{G}(E_{a})=(V,E\cup E_{a})
, the objective function
F
will decrease. We next prove this property.

Lemma IV.4
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
be a connected graph and
e\notin E
be a potential edge to be added. Let
S,T\subseteq V
be two demographic groups. Define
\Delta(e)=F(\emptyset)-F(\{e\})
as the decrease of
F
upon the addition of edge
e
. Then
\Delta(e)>0
.

Proof.
Note that
F
is defined as a non-negative linear combination of resistance distance. By Rayleigh’s monotonicity law
[
55
]
, adding edges can only decrease resistance distance. Therefore, we conclude that
\Delta(e)>0
.

\Box

Lemma
IV.4
indicates that the addition of any non-existing edge will lead to a decrease of the objective function
F
. Then, a question naturally arises: How can we find the optimal edge set
E_{a}
with
k
edges such that
F
is minimized? More precisely, the fair information access maximization problem can be stated as follows.

Problem 1
.

(
F
air
I
nformation
A
ccess
M
aximization,
FIAM
). Given a connected undirected graph
\mathcal{G}=(V,E)
, two demographic groups
S,T\subseteq V
, a fixed integer

, we aim to select the edge set
E_{a}\subseteq\bar{E}
with
\left|E_{a}\right|=k
, and add these chosen edges to graph
\mathcal{G}
forming a new graph
\mathcal{G}(E_{a})=(V,E\cup E_{a})
, so that
F
is minimized. In the other words:

E_{a}=\argmin_{H\subseteq\bar{E},|H|=k}F(H).

(2)

IV-C

Hardness and Non-Supermodularity

In this section, we argue that Problem
1
is NP-hard. In
[
56
]
, the authors proved that the problem of minimizing the Kirchhoff index of a graph by adding
k
edges is NP-hard. This proof relies on a reduction from the
3
-colorability problem
[
57
]
. Our problem is at least as hard as theirs.
Note that by setting
\lambda=0
in Eq. (
1
), the quantity that we optimize differs from the Kirchhoff index only by a multiplicative factor, according to Definitions
III.5
and
IV.3
. Therefore, we can conclude that the
FIAM
problem is NP-hard. This implies that we cannot hope for a polynomial time algorithm. Furthermore, the objective function in Problem
1
is not supermodular, as stated below.

Fig. 1:
A
8
-node toy network to show the non-supermodularity of the objective function.

Theorem IV.5
.

Function
F(\cdot)
is not supermodular.

Proof.
To illustrate the non-supermodularity, consider the network with
8
nodes in Fig.
1
, where the solid lines represent the existing edges and the dashed lines represent the edges to be added. Define
B=\emptyset
,
C=\{(1,2)\}
and
e=(2,6)
. Then, we have
F(B)=64.58,F(B\cup\{e\})=56.45,F(C)=56.88
, and
F(C\cup\{e\})=48.70
. Thus,

F(B)-F(B\cup\{e\})=8.13\leq F(C)-F(C\cup\{e\})=8.18.

This concludes the proof.

\Box

V
Simple Greedy Algorithm

The
FIAM
problem is combinatorial, and its optimal solution can be found using a brute-force approach. This involves computing
F(E_{a})
for each set
E_{a}
of the
{\bar{m}\choose k}
candidates on the augmented graph
\mathcal{G}(E_{a})
, requiring
\Theta(n^{3})

VI-C

Farthest
: Diameter Search on an Approximate Hull

To overcome the prohibitive cost of a brute-force search for the furthest node pair, we propose the
Farthest
algorithm. The core strategy is to tackle the geometric equivalent of the problem—finding the diameter of the point set—through a series of carefully chosen approximations centered around the efficient estimation of the convex hull.

A key insight from geometry is that the diameter of a point set is determined by a pair of points on its convex hull. However, computing the exact convex hull has a prohibitive time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(n^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor})
in high dimensions, making it impractical. To circumvent this, we leverage the
ApproxCH
algorithm
[
63
,
64
]
. Instead of finding the exact hull, this algorithm efficiently outputs a small subset of points,
\hat{P}
, that approximates the true convex hull. This step is crucial because it dramatically reduces the search space. We formalize the effectiveness of this approach in the following lemma, which guarantees that the diameter of this small approximate set is a faithful approximation of the true diameter.

Lemma VI.2
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
P
be the input point set,
\hat{P}=\textsc{ApproxCH}(P,\epsilon/25)
, and
c=|\hat{P}|
. Define
D_{P}=\max_{i,j\in P}\|\bm{\mathit{v}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{v}}_{j}\|
and
D_{\hat{P}}=\max_{i,j\in\hat{P}}\|\bm{\mathit{v}}_{i}-\bm{\mathit{v}}_{j}\|
as the diameters of
P
and
\hat{P}
respectively. Then,
D_{P}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon/5}}{{\approx}}D_{\hat{P}}^{2}
.

Remark.
The parameter
c=|\hat{P}|
depends on the network structure and the membership of groups
S
and
T
. While it could be as large as
n
, experiments in Section
VII
show it is usually quite small for real-world social networks.

While this significantly narrows down the candidate pairs, two computational challenges remain.
First, the coordinate vectors exist in a very high-dimensional space (up to
d=2n
), which makes subsequent geometric computations like finding the approximate convex hull prohibitively expensive. To address this, we employ the Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) Lemma
[
65
]
. This powerful result guarantees that a set of high-dimensional vectors can be projected into a much lower-dimensional space using a random matrix, while preserving all pairwise Euclidean distances up to a small relative error
(1\pm\epsilon)
. Specifically, it allows us to reduce the dimension from
d
to a much more manageable
q=\mathcal{O}(\log n/\epsilon^{2})
, which depends only logarithmically on the number of nodes. This step is critical for making the overall algorithm scalable.

Second, the initial computation of the coordinate vectors is itself a major bottleneck, as it requires solving linear systems with the graph Laplacian
\bm{\mathit{L}}
and calculating specific entries from its pseudoinverse
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
—operations that are prohibitive if performed exactly. We accelerate this significantly by using two specialized techniques: a nearly linear-time solver for SDDM systems
[
66
,
67
,
68
]
to efficiently approximate solutions to the linear equations, and the
AppDiag
routine
[
18
]
to rapidly approximate the diagonal entries of
\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}
needed for the distance metric’s scaling coefficients. These tools together bypass slow operations like direct matrix inversion and enable the fast construction of our coordinate vectors.

Theoretical Analysis

The
Farthest
algorithm (Algorithm
3
) serves as the core subroutine for finding the most influential non-existent edge in each step. Its performance is summarized as follows.

Lemma VI.5
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let
\bar{\Delta}^{\max}=\max_{e\in\bar{E}}\bar{\Delta}(e)
and let
\tilde{\tilde{\Delta}}^{\max}
be the output of the
Farthest
algorithm. The time complexity of
Farthest
(Algorithm
3
) is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(m+nc\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right)
, and its output satisfies
\tilde{\tilde{\Delta}}^{\max}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon}}{{\approx}}\bar{\Delta}^{\max}.

Proof.
For the time complexity, it takes
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(m\epsilon^{-2})
time to determine the node coordinates and
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(nc\epsilon^{-2})
time to approximate the convex hull. Therefore, the total time complexity of
Farthest
is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(m+nc\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right)
.
For the approximation guarantee, let
\tilde{\Delta}^{\max}=\max_{e\in\bar{E}}\tilde{\Delta}(e)
. From the results in Lemma
VI.2
, we have that the diameter found on the approximate hull is a good approximation of the diameter of the full set of approximate points, i.e.,
\tilde{\tilde{\Delta}}^{\max}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon/5}}{{\approx}}\tilde{\Delta}^{\max}
. Based on Lemma
VI.4
,
\tilde{\Delta}^{\max}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\epsilon/5}}{{\approx}}\bar{\Delta}^{\max}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\bar{\Delta}^{max}\leq

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon/5)^{2}\bar{\Delta}^{max}\leq\tilde{\tilde{\Delta}}^{max}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon/5)^{2}\bar{\Delta}^{max}\leq(1+\epsilon)\bar{\Delta}^{max},

which concludes the proof.

\Box

Our main algorithm,
Fast
(Algorithm
2
), iteratively calls
Farthest
to select
k
non-existent edges to add to the graph. The overall performance of
Fast
is detailed as follows.

Theorem VI.6
.

For any integer
0<k\leq\bar{m}
and any error parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
Fast
runs in time
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(k(m+nc)\epsilon^{-2})
and outputs a set of
k
edges. In each of the
k
iterations, the algorithm selects a non-existing edge that is an
\epsilon
-approximation of the optimal choice at that step.

Proof.
The approximation guarantee for each iteration is established by Lemma
VI.5
. The
Fast
algorithm performs
k
rounds of this selection process (Lines 2-5 of Algorithm
2
). In each round, it calls
Farthest
, taking
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\left(m+nc\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right)
time. Consequently, the total time complexity of
Fast
is
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(k\left(m+nc\right)\epsilon^{-2}\right)
. As we will observe in Section
VII
,
c
is usually very small in real-world networks and

, which results in a nearly linear time complexity.

Remark
.
It is important to clarify the scope of guarantees offered by our surrogate-based approximation.
The classical
(1-1/e)
guarantees for cardinality-constrained submodular maximization do not apply as
F
is monotone non-increasing but not submodular.
This limitation is intrinsic to the problem structure rather than to our algorithm.
Importantly, approximation error does not accumulate across iterations: each edge still decreases
F

Moreover, when
F
exhibits weak submodularity, a constant-factor greedy bound remains valid and degrades gracefully with estimation error.

VII-A

Experimental Setup

Machine Configuration.
All algorithms are implemented in Julia and run on a Linux box with 4.2 GHz Intel i7-7700 CPU and 32GB of main memory. All experiments are executed using a single thread.

Baselines.
We compare our proposed algorithms against the following algorithms, which are based on classical centrality measures or methods for minimizing the Kirchhoff index
[
30
]
.

•

Random
: randomly choose
k
unconnected node pairs from two groups
S
and
T
.

•

Optimum
:
the optimal strategy has two variants,
Optimum-R
and
Optimum-U
for choosing
k
non-adjacent node pairs whose addition maximally decreases the graph resistance and unfairness respectively using a brute-force searching method.

•

DP
,
BP
(DS, BS): choose
k
non-adjacent node pairs with the maximum product (summation) of degrees, betweennesses, for two nodes from distinct groups.

•

Fiedler
: choose
k
non-adjacent node pairs with the maximum difference of the Fiedler value from distinct groups.

•

ER
: choose
k
non-adjacent node pairs with the maximum resistance distance from distinct groups.

Note that the idea of the last six baselines comes from
[
30
]
but with proper adjustment to our problem setup. We have evaluated similar strategies to
DP
and
DS
for closeness and PageRank centrality. However, the performance is very poor, and therefore we do not report their results in our plots.

Performance Measures.

We focus on three measures: 1) the unfairness between different groups (i.e.,
U
), where a smaller absolute value is preferred; 2) the graph resistance
R
, where a lower value is desired; 3) the running time of the algorithms.

Datasets.
To evaluate our proposed strategies, we conduct experiments on a corpus of real-world networks (see Table
I
) sourced from SNAP
[
69
]
and Network Repository
[
70
]
, as well as synthetic networks generated by the Barabasi-Albert-homophily (BAh) model
[
71
]
which has been frequently used in graph fairness related works
[
13
,
72
,
73
]
. Note that nodes in the real-world networks come with inherent attribute information, which determines the groups
S
and
T
.

Network Generation Model.

Different random graph models have been proposed to mimic real-world social networks, cf.
[
74
]
. Such models are usually tailored to possess fundamental properties consistently observed in real-world networks, such as small diameter and power-law degree distribution. (One advantage of such synthetic graph structures over the real-world networks is that they give us the freedom to generate graphs of various sizes.) We have adopted a variant of the well-known Barabasi-Albert model called the Barabasi-Albert-homophily (BAh) model
[
71
]
. This variant has been frequently used in graph fairness related works
[
13
,
72
,
73
]
. It incorporates a binary group attribute for each node, distinguishing between majority and minority groups. The probability of a newly added node belonging to the minority group is denoted by
f_{a}
(for some
f_{a}<0.5
) and the probability of attachment
\Pi_{ij}
between new and existing nodes depends on both degree (as in the original BA model) and a homophily parameter
h
, as follows

\displaystyle\Pi_{ij}\propto\begin{cases}h\bm{\mathit{d}}_{j}&if\quad Group(i)=Group(j)\\
(1-h)\bm{\mathit{d}}_{j}&if\quad Group(i)\neq Group(j)\end{cases}

(6)

The parameter
h
controls the level of homophily in the network, with
h>0.5
generating homophilic networks and
h<0.5
generating heterophilic networks. Choosing
h=0.5
results in the standard BA model and
h=1
results in the network fragmenting into two components corresponding to the two groups. We set
f_{a}=0.3
and
h=0.7
in all our experiments. We use BAhx to represent the BAh network with x nodes.

VIII
Conclusion and Future Work

We fill a crucial gap in the field of network fairness by leveraging the concept of resistance distance as a measure of information access. We showed that it can be utilized to identify disadvantaged groups concerning information access. We then proposed effective algorithms, particularly a fast edge recommendation algorithm, for optimizing efficiency and mitigating bias in information access. We demonstrated through extensive experiments that our algorithm effectively optimizes fair information dissemination in large-scale social networks.

A potential avenue for future research is to characterize the approximability of FIAM. Additionally, further research is required to gain more insight into other measures of information access besides resistance distance and the trade-offs between different fairness criteria when mitigating bias in information access. Since recommendations may be accepted probabilistically, we plan to adapt the framework by recommending more than

k
edges to ensure about
k
are realized.

Many networks emerging in real world are directed and weighted. A prospective research direction would be to shed some light on up to what extent our algorithmic machinery could be exploited to capture these larger classes of networks and what challenged are encountered that require development of novel approaches and techniques.

\displaystyle n\mathrm{Tr}\left((\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\bm{\mathit{\widetilde{Z}}})\bm{\mathit{L}}(\bm{\mathit{Z}}-\bm{\mathit{\widetilde{Z}}})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\sum_{i=1}^{q}\left\|\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}-\tilde{\bm{\mathit{z}}}_{i}\right\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{2}\leq n\delta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\left\|\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}\right\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{2}.

Considering
\mathrm{Tr}\big(\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\big)\leq n(n^{2}-1)/6
(cf.
[
75
]
), we obtain

\displaystyle n\delta^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{q}\|\bm{\mathit{z}}_{i}\|_{\bm{\mathit{L}}}^{2}=n\delta^{2}\|\bar{Q}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}=n\delta^{2}\sum_{(a,b)\in E}\|\bar{Q}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{ab}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle n\delta^{2}\left(1+\frac{3\epsilon}{125}\right)\sum_{(a,b)\in E}\|\bm{\mathit{E}}^{X}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{ab}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\delta^{2}\left(1+\frac{3\epsilon}{125}\right)\|\bm{\mathit{E}}^{X}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\delta^{2}\left(1+\frac{3\epsilon}{125}\right)\mathrm{Tr}\left(\bm{\mathit{E}}^{X}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{B}}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{E}}^{X}\right)\leq\delta^{2}\frac{(1+3\epsilon/125)n^{4}}{6}.

On the other hand, we give a lower bound on
\left\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\right\|^{2}
:

\begin{split}\left\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\right\|^{2}&\geq\left(1-{3\epsilon}/{125}\right)\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}^{\top}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{E}}^{X}\bm{\mathit{L}}^{\dagger}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\\
&\geq\frac{1-3\epsilon/125}{\lambda_{n-1}^{2}}\geq\frac{1-3\epsilon/125}{n}.\end{split}

Combining the above two equations, we have

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\big|\left\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\right\|-\|\bm{\mathit{\widetilde{Z}}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\|\big|^{2}}{\left\|\bm{\mathit{Z}}\bm{\mathit{b}}_{e}\right\|^{2}}\leq\delta^{2}\frac{1+3\epsilon/125}{6(1-3\epsilon/125)}n^{5}\leq\left(\frac{\epsilon}{125}\right)^{2},

together with the initial condition, this ends the proof.∎
