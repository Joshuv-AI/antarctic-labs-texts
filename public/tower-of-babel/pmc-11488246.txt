Comprehensive analysis of PLATZ family genes and their responses to abiotic stresses in Barley
Authors: Xue Feng, Gehao Zhu, Quan Meng, Jianbin Zeng, Xiaoyan He, Wenxing Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlant A/T-rich protein and zinc-binding protein (PLATZ) transcription factors are pivotal regulators in various aspects of plant biology, including growth, development, and responses to environmental stresses. While PLATZ genes have been extensively studied and functionally characterized in various plants, limited information is available for these genes in barley.ResultsHere, we discovered a total of 11 PLATZ genes distributed across seven chromosomes in barley. Based on phylogenetic and conserved motif analysis, we classified PLATZ into five subfamilies, comprising 3, 1, 2, 1 and 4 genes, respectively. Analysis of gene structure demonstrated that these 11 HvPLATZ genes typically possessed two to four exons. Most HvPLATZ genes were found to possess at least one ABRE cis-element in their promoter regions, and a few of them also contained LTR, CAT-box, MRE, and DRE cis-elements. Then, we conducted an exploration of the expression patterns of HvPLATZs, which displayed notable differences across various tissues and in response to abiotic stresses. Functional analysis of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 in yeast cells showed that they may be involved in drought tolerance. Additionally, we constructed a regulatory network including miRNA-targeted gene predictions and identified two miRNAs targeting two HvPLATZs, such as hvu-miR5053 and hvu-miR6184 targeting HvPLATZ2, hvu-miR6184 targeting HvPLATZ10.ConclusionIn summary, these findings provide valuable insights for future functional verification of HvPLATZs and contribute to a deeper understanding of the role of HvPLATZs in response to stress conditions in barley.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05690-5.

Introduction

Plants, especially crops, are essential sources of biological energy for human survival. Abiotic stresses, such as salt, drought, high temperature, cold stress, have severe adverse effects on plant growth, development, and productivity, making them significant factors contributing to global agricultural yield reduction [1]. It is estimated that 50% of the annual yield losses in major crops worldwide are related to abiotic stress [2]. Fortunately, through long-term evolution, plants have developed a series of physiological, metabolic, and defense systems to maintain their life and sustained growth.

Transcription factors (TFs) are crucial regulatory factors in plant signal transduction processes, participating in various physiological and biochemical processes by modulating the expression of downstream genes [3, 4]. Currently, numerous transcription factors have been identified to participate in plant growth, development, and responses to abiotic stresses, including NAC [5, 6], MYB [7, 8], WRKY [9, 10] and TCP [10, 11]. Zinc finger transcription factors represent one of the most expansive and diverse families within the plant kingdom [12]. Hanas et al. [13] first discovered a zinc finger-containing transcription factor, IIIA, in Xenopus laevis oocyte, marking the beginning of widespread interest in zinc finger proteins. Increasing evidence shows that zinc finger proteins can influence plant growth and development processes and enhance resistance to abiotic stresses [14–16].

Plant A/T-rich zinc binding protein PLATZ is a novel family found in plants, initially identified in pea (Pisum sativum), named PsPLATZ1 [17]. This transcription factor binds non-specifically to AT-rich sequences to suppress gene transcription [18]. Members of the PLATZ family share two distant conserved motifs: one located at the N-terminus, C-X2-H-X11-C-X2-C-X(4–5)-C-X2-C-X(3–7)-H-X2-H motif, and another centrally located, C-X2-C-X(10–11)-C-X3-C motif, both essential for PLATZ function [18]. PLATZ has been identified in multiple species including Arabidopsis thaliana [16], Triticum aestivum [12, 19], Oryza sativa [20, 21], Zea mays [22], Glycine max [23], Fagopyrum tataricum [24] and so on. In terms of growth and development, ZmPLATZ12 shows specific expression in maize endosperm starch cells, encoding the PLATZ protein FL3. FL3 interacts with RPC53 and TFC1 to influence endosperm development and consequently maize grain size [25]. TaPGS1 could bind to E-box motifs in the promoters of TaFL3 and OsFL3, regulating their expression in wheat and rice, thereby impacting grain development [26]. Rice SG2, encoding the PLATZ protein, exhibits high expression in the spikelet, and regulates rice grain size by modulating cell division [20]. PLATZ is also notably involved in how plants react to abiotic stressors. For instance, AtPLATZ2 binds to the promoters of Ca2+ sensor protein family members CBL4/SOS3 and CBL10/SCaBP8, negatively regulating Arabidopsis resistance to salt stress [27]. AtPLATZ7, regulates root ROS accumulation through the RGF1 signaling pathway, thereby influencing meristem development [28]. GmPLATZ17 interacts with GmDREB5, suppressing the expression of downstream drought-related genes to affect plant drought resistance [29]. In Phyllostachys edulis, PhePLATZ1 positively regulates plant drought resistance by influencing the ABA signaling pathway [30].

Barley is the fourth largest cereal crop in area after wheat, maize and rice. Its production faces significant threats from abiotic stresses [31]. Consequently, the identification of genes associated with abiotic stress tolerance and the development of genetically modified plant varieties with improved stress resilience are essential objectives for plant breeders. However, researches concerning the role of PLATZ genes are still limited in barley. Here, we identified 11 potential HvPLATZ genes through bioinformatic analysis of the barley genome. Subsequently, we conducted analyses on evolutionary relationships, conserved motifs, gene structures, chromosomal distribution, and their expression profiles in different tissues and under diverse abiotic stress conditions. Furthermore, we anticipated miRNA-targeted HvPLATZ. This finding offer valuable insights for understanding the role of HvPLATZs in plant growth and their responses to environmental stressors.

Materials and methods

Identification and general characterization of HvPLATZ proteins

To identify and characterize HvPLATZ proteins, we conducted a BLASTP using AtPLATZs and OsPLATZs [22]. We retrieved gene and protein sequences from the Ensemble Plants database (http://plants.ensembl.org) in Hordeum vulgare (MorexV3_pseudomolecules_assembly) [32, 33]. From each gene ID, we selected the longest transcript sequence while excluding incomplete sequences lacking start or termination codons. To validate the sequences, we cross-referenced them with the SMART database [34]. The putative HvPLATZ genes were designated as HvPLATZ1 to HvPLATZ11 based on their chromosomal localization. Furthermore, we employed the ExPASy tool (http://www.expasy.ch/tools/pi_tool.html) to compute the number of amino acid, isoelectric points (pI) and molecular weights (MW) [35].

Phylogenetic analysis and classification of HvPLATZs

We conducted a phylogenetic analysis to classify HvPLATZs by aligning 11 unique HvPLATZ amino acid sequences with PLATZs from Arabidopsis, rice, wheat and maize. Alignment was conducted using ClustalW with default settings [36]. Subsequently, we utilized MEGA 7.0 to construct a phylogenetic tree via the neighbor-joining (NJ) method, with the following parameters: poisson model, pairwise deletion and 1000 bootstrap replications [37]. To enhance the visualization of the phylogenetic relationships, we utilized iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) [38].

Chromosome distribution and synteny block of HvPLATZ

To determine the chromosome distribution of HvPLATZ genes in barley, we employed the Circos tool [39]. Furthermore, The classification of PLATZ duplication patterns, utilizing default settings, was performed using MCScanX [40]. We used the ParaAT tool to compute the nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka) and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) for HvPLATZs. Subsequently, we calculated the Ka: Ks ratio using Calculator 2.0 software [41]. Additionally, to estimate gene duplication time, we used Ks/2λ, where λ = 1.5 × 10− 8 [41].

Gene structure and motif analysis of HvPLATZs

We analyzed the exon and intron organization of HvPLATZ genes using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) [42]. Additionally, conserved motif sequences within HvPLATZs were identified as described Bailey et al. [43] by using MEME online program (http://meme.sdsc.edu/meme/itro.html). The motifs were further annotated using InterProScan [44]. To visualize both the gene structures and the distribution of conserved motifs across HvPLATZs, we utilized TBtools [45].

Analysis of Cis-elements in HvPLATZ gene promoters

We investigated the promoter regions of HvPLATZ genes by extracting sequences extending 1500 base pairs upstream from the transcription start site in the barley genome. Using PlantCARE software (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) [46], we identified and analyzed cis-elements within these promoter regions. The graphical representations of these cis-elements were annotated and visualized using TBtools.

Predicting 3D structures of HvPLATZs

We employed Swiss-Model to predict the 3D structures of HvPLATZs protein [47], and structure was evaluated using the SAVES server.

Prediction of miRNA-HvPLATZ regulatory networks

To predict miRNAs that potentially regulate HvPLATZ gene expression, we utilized the psRNATarget server under default parameters [48]. Subsequently, we employed Cytoscape V3.8.2 software to visualize the interaction networks.

Gene expression analysis

We analyzed HvPLATZ gene expression across barley tissues and under abiotic stress conditions from IPK (https://apex.ipk-gatersleben.de/apex). Expression levels were quantified using transcripts per Kilobase of exon per million reads mapped (TPM). Differential expression was determined by identifying genes with an absolute log2 ratio ≥ 1, indicating differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To visualize these expression patterns, we created a heatmap using TBtools. The heatmap displays expression levels in different tissues (log2 (TPM+1)) and their responses to various abiotic stresses (log2 fold change).

Functional analysis of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 in yeast cells

The coding sequences of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 were separately inserted into pYES2 vectors, resulting in the creation of recombinant plasmids named pYES2-HvPLATZ6 and pYES2-HvPLATZ8. The vector was digested by BamHI and EcoRI. The CDS was amplified by KOD FX Neo (TOYOBO, Japan). DNA was ligated by ClonExpress- Entry One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme, China). These plasmids, along with an empty vector control (pYES2), were introduced into the Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain INVSc1 using the PEG/LiAc method according to pYES2 (INVSc1) Yeast Expression kit instruction manual (Coolaber, China). Positive transformants were subsequently selected by growth on synthetic medium lacking uracil (SD-Ura). Prepare a series of liquid media containing 0 mM, 30 mM, 60 mM, 90 mM, 120 mM, and 135 mM PEG 3350 in SG-U (galactose substituted for glucose) medium. Take experimental and blank control strains, shake cultures for 24 h to confirm growth, dilute the cultures and plate them onto SG-Ura plates.

Plant growth and treatment

The hydroponic experiment was conducted in a greenhouse at Qingdao Agricultural University, Qingdao, China. Barley cultivar “Golden Promise” were used in this study. All the testing barley seeds were harvested in the summer of 2022 at the Jiaozhou Experimental Station, Qingdao Agricultural University, Jiaozhou, Shandong, China. The seeds were first treated with a 3% H2O2 solution for 10 min, followed by seven rinses with distilled water. Afterward, the seeds were planted on moist filter papers at an environment of 18/22℃. Once germination occurred, uniform seedlings were transplanted to 2-liter pots filled with 1.5 L of basic nutrient solution (BNS). On the seventh day post-transplantation, polyethylene glycol (PEG) was added into the containers to create three treatment groups: BNS as a control, BNS supplemented with 10% PEG, and BNS combined with 100mM NaCl. The composition of BNS is detailed in the study by Feng et al. [49]. Plant root samples for RNA isolation were collected six hours after the treatments began. All samples were stored at -80℃ for subsequent analyses.

Quantitative RT‑PCR validation

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was executed using the QuantStudio3 PCR system from Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, following the protocol described in [49]. Total RNA was isolated with the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The synthesis of first-strand cDNA was carried out utilizing the PrimeScipt™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan), followed by qRT-PCR using the SYBR Green Supermix (Takara, Japan) with HvActin as a reference gene [50]. The total volume of the PCR reaction was maintained at 10 µl. The cycling conditions included initial denaturation at 94℃ for 30 s, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94℃ for 5 s, annealing at 58℃ for 15 s, and elongation at 72℃ for 10 s. The experiments were replicated three times using the 2−ΔΔCt relative quantification method. Gene-specific primers were designed through Primer-BLAST (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/tools/primer-blast/index.cgi?LINK_LOC=BlastHome). The primer details are listed in Table S6. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and the data presented are mean ± SE values.

Statistical analysis

DPS Data Processing System software was used to perform statistical analysis. Significance analysis was conducted with ANOVA followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test and significance was accepted at P < 0.05.

Results

Here, we have identified 11 HvPLATZ genes. In Table S1, we summarized their characteristics, including names, IDs, chromosomal locations, amino acid lengths ranging from 212 to 272, molecular weights from 22.7 to 29.2 kDa, and isoelectric points (pI). Detailed coding and protein sequences can be found in Table S1. Additionally, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the Neighbor-Joining method to show the evolutionary relationships among PLATZs, comparing 12 Arabidopsis [21], 15 rice [21], and 11 barley PLATZs. Five Subfamilies were identified. Subfamily 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 contained 3, 1, 2, 1 and 4 HvPLATZ, respectively (Fig. 1).

Analysis of HvPLATZ gene structure and protein motifs

To identify protein motifs, we utilized MEME and chose the 10 conserved motifs in HvPLATZ proteins, labeled as motifs 1–10 (Fig. 2A). Further details about these conserved motifs can be found in Table S2. Importantly, all of HvPLATZs contained motif 1–5. The conserved motifs 1, 2, 3 and 5 overlap with the PLATZ domain. The nuclear localization signal usually consists of four to eight amino acids. These amino acid sequences are rich in basic amino acids such as R (arginine) and K (lysine) and often contain P (proline). Motif 4 is KRRKGIPHRAPFG, and it may be a nuclear localization signal. Then, we predicted NLS using cNLS Mapper (https://nls-mapper.iab.keio.ac.jp/cgi-bin/NLS_Mapper_form.cgi). The results was consistent with our predication. Four of 11 HvPLATZs contain motif 6, 7 and 9. Two proteins contain motif 8 and 10. We employed the GSDS web server to examine the gene structures of HvPLATZs. The results revealed that HvPLATZs exhibited an uneven distribution of two to four exons (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, we predicted the 3D structures of HvPLATZ5, HvPLATZ7, HvPLATZ4, HvPLATZ10 and HvPLATZ2 proteins, which belong to subfamily1, subfamily 2, subfamily 3, subfamily 4 and subfamily 5, respectively. This analysis revealed that different HvPLATZs share similar 3D structures in the N-terminal region but exhibit differences in the C-terminal region (Fig. 3).

Location and duplication of HvPLATZs

We conducted an analysis of the chromosomal distribution of HvPLATZs. Notably, all 11 HvPLATZs were successfully mapped to the barley genome, with the exception of chromosome 4 and 5 (Fig. 4). There were one HvPLATZ gene located on chromosome 2 and 3. Two HvPLATZs could be found on chromosomes 1. Three HvPLATZs were found on chromosome 7, while four HvPLATZs were situated on chromosome 6. Using the BLAST and MCScanX methods, we identified two duplication events within the HvPLATZ genes, including HvPLATZ3/HvPLATZ8 and HvPLATZ7/HvPLATZ10 (Fig. 5). To shed light on the evolutionary constraints acting, we calculated the Ks value, Ka value, Ka: Ks ratio, and divergence time for paralogous PLATZ genes. All of the Ka: Ks ratios in segmental duplicated HvPLATZ gene pairs were below one. These duplication events occurred between 0.17 and 0.85 million years ago (Table S3).

Cis-elements analysis in the promoters of TaPLATZs

Totally, the promoters of the 11 HvPLATZ genes were analyzed for various cis-elements, including ABRE (ABA-responsive element), CAT-box, DRE (dehydration-responsive element), GARE-motif (gibberellin-responsive element), GC-motif, GT1-motif, LTR (low temperature-responsive element), MBS (MYB binding site), MRE (meristem expression), O2-site (anaerobic inducibility), P-box (gibberellin-responsive element), RY-element (seed-specific regulation), and others involved in light responsiveness and zein metabolism. These elements play roles in regulating gene expression under conditions such as ABA response, dehydration stress, gibberellin signaling, anaerobic conditions, light availability, and low temperatures (Fig. 7). Generally, nine HvPLATZs genes (80%) carried ABRE cis-elements, seven HvPLATZ genes (63%) owned LTR cis-elements, five HvPLATZ genes owned GC-motif, four HvPLATZ genes owned CAT-box, MBS and GARE motif, three HvPLATZ genes had GT1-motif and O2-site, two HvPLATZ genes had TCA-element and P-box, and one HvPLATZ gene carried RY-element and TATC-box.

MiRNA-mRNA regulatory network of HvPLATZs

We constructed a regulatory network involving 71 known miRNAs in barley and the 11 HvPLATZ genes. Within this network, we pinpointed two miRNAs with the potential to target and regulate two HvPLATZs, such as hvu-miR5053 and hvu-miR6184 targeting HvPLATZ2, hvu-miR6184 targeting HvPLATZ10 (Table S5).

Tissue-specific expression profiles of HvPLATZs

Transcript analysis revealed distinct expression patterns of HvPLATZ gene family members across various developmental stages and plant tissues. These 11 HvPLATZ genes were classified into three groups based on their expression profiles (Fig. 8). Group 1 includes HvPLATZ7, which showed minimal to no expression across all tissues examined. Group 2 comprises five genes (HvPLATZ4/5/6/8/11) with specific tissue-specific expression patterns; for example, HvPLATZ5 was exclusively expressed in grains. Group 3 consists of five genes that exhibit varying expression across analyzed tissues, further subdivided into three subgroups. Subgroup 1 includes HvPLATZ2 and HvPLATZ3, which displayed high expression levels (log2TPM + 1>1) across all tissues. Subgroup 2 includes HvPLATZ1, which showed low expression (log2TPM + 1<0.5) across all tissues. The remaining two genes were categorized into subgroup 3.

Expression patterns of HvPLATZs under abiotic stress

We examined the expression of HvPLATZ genes in response to various abiotic stresses, such as drought, heat and salt. The collective analysis revealed significant alterations in HvPLATZ gene expression levels (as depicted in Fig. 9). In detail, after exposure to drought stress, HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 exhibited up-regulation with two fold. Under heat stress, HvPLATZ2 demonstrated decreased expression patterns with 1.3 fold. HvPLATZ6 showed up-regulation in response to salt stress with two fold. Conversely, some HvPLATZs remained unresponsive to the abiotic stresses examined in the current study. For example, HvPLATZ4 and HvPLATZ7 displayed no changes in expression across all stress conditions, it is consistent with the results that HvPLATZ4 and HvPLATZ4 were not expressed in leaves and roots. In addition, all of HvPLATZ genes were chosen for qRT-PCR to verify the reliability of transcriptome data, and the results were uniform to the sequencing data (Figure S1).

The effect of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 on drought tolerance of yeast cells

Due to shared cellular characteristics like cell walls and vacuoles between yeast and plants, yeast has become a valuable model for studying gene functions of plant genes. In this study, the impact of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 on drought tolerance was assessed using yeast cells. Growth of a yeast INVSc1 strain containing HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 genes was compared to that of a strain containing an empty pYES2 vector under PEG treatment conditions. Control group can grow under conditions of 0 mM, 30 mM, 60 mM, and 90 mM, but cannot grow under 120 mM. Experimental group shows inconsistent growth compared to the control group, suggesting that HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 increased the tolerance of yeast to drought (Fig. 10).

Discussion

Transcription factors could regulate gene expression by binding to specific DNA sequences, known as cis-acting elements, in the promoters of target genes [51]. Among these, C2H2-type zinc finger proteins are acknowledged as one of the largest families of transcription factors in plants, pivotal for governing various biological processes through precise gene regulation mechanisms. In Arabidopsis, there are 176 members [52, 53], and 33 are also found in other eukaryotes, while the remaining 143 are specific to plants [53]. Zinc finger proteins play a crucial role in plant responses to abiotic stressors. They can function independently in transcriptional regulation and also collaborate with other proteins to exert their effects [54, 55]. PLATZ transcription factors represent a novel class of plant-specific zinc finger DNA-binding proteins that can nonspecifically target A/T-rich sequences to suppress transcription of target genes [17]. Recent studies have shown that PLATZ transcription factors play essential roles across multiple facets of plant growth and development [15, 25, 28], seed dehydration tolerance [56], seed germination under salt stress [57], response to drought during seedling stage [16], and regulation of leaf senescence [58]. Although numerous studies have underscored the critical role of PLATZ TFs in responding to abiotic stresses, our understanding of PLATZs remains limited. In this study, we identified 11 HvPLATZ genes, denoted as HvPLATZ1 to HvPLATZ11 in accordance with their chromosomal localization in barley. These HvPLATZs, categorized into five subfamilies, exhibited distinct genetic structures and expression patterns (as depicted in Figs. 1, 8 and 9). Gene duplications are considered to be among the primary driving forces in the evolution of genomes and genetic systems. Duplicate genes provide raw materials for the evolution of mechanism novelties, in turn, facilitate the generation of new functions [59]. In this study, two duplicated events were found in barley genome, such as HvPLATZ3 and HvPLATZ8, HvPLATZ7 and HvPLATZ10 (Fig. 5; Table S3). However, phylogenetic tree showed that HvPLATZ7 and HvPLATZ10 could not be classified in same subfamily (Fig. 1), indicating the different gene function between duplicated genes during the evolution. Indeed, HvPLATZ10 were highly expressed in seedlings and also be involved in abiotic stress, but HvPLATZ10 were almost no expressed (Figs. 8 and 9). Meanwhile, 32 PLATZ gene pair in both barley and wheat were identified through synteny analysis. Fu et al. [19] reported that TaPLATZ26, TaPLATZ30, TaPLATZ31 and TaPLATZ32 were highly expressed in grain, and TaPLATZ7 showed high expression in root. In our study, HvPLATZ5 and HvPLATZ6 were also highly expressed in grain, and HvPLATZ3 showed high expression in root. Furthermore, HvPLATZ3, HvPLATZ5 and HvPLATZ6 had ABRE cis-element, and this is a element that most TaPLATZ genes had. It indicated that HvPLATZ6/HvPLATZ26, HvPLATZ3/TaPLATZ7 and HvPLATZ5/HvPLATZ30 may have same function in plant development. This research supplies valuable insights for future investigations aimed at characterizing the functions of PLATZ genes, ultimately enhancing adaptive capabilities in plants when confronted with abiotic stressors.

Drought constitutes a significant abiotic stress factor and ranks among the primary culprits for reduced crop yields [60]. ABA-responsive transcription factors exhibit robust responses to drought stress and hold pivotal roles in plant defense mechanisms against drought [4, 61, 62]. Zhang et al. [30] highlighted that PhePLATZ1, a PLATZ transcription factor in Phyllostachys edulis, enhances drought resistance. Similarly, AtPLATZ1 improves seed dehydration tolerance in maize [56]. The GmPLATZ17 gene in soybean, as reported by Zhao et al. [29], suppresses GmDREB5 expression, thereby reducing drought tolerance. Additionally, Liu et al. [18] demonstrated that PLATZ4 was highly expressed in leaf and positively influences drought tolerance in Arabidopsis by regulating the expression of PIP2;8. In the current study, HvPLATZ6 and PLATZ8 (homologous AtPLATZ4) were expressed in epidermis and exhibited up-regulation under drought stress (Fig. 9). Meanwhile, the promoter of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 have ABRE element (Fig. 7). The function analysis of HvPLATZ6 and HvPLATZ8 showed that PYES2-PLATZ6 and PYES2-PLATZ8 could grow on SG-Ura medium containing 135mM PEG (Fig. 10), indicating it is interesting targets for future studies about the drought response In ABA-dependent pathway in plants.

High Temperature stress has profound implications for plant geographical distribution and diminishes overall plant productivity, posing a threat to food security. For instance, with each 1℃ rise in global average temperature, wheat, rice and maize, global yields are projected to decrease by an average of 6.0%, 3.2% and 7.4%, respectively [63]. Kumar et al. [64] reported that miR5053 targeting PeCHS gene involved in flavonoid biosynthetic. Several study have reported that flavonoid were responsible for heat and drought stress [65, 66]. In the current study, HvPLATZ2 exhibited down-regulation under heat stress (Fig. 9), while hvu-miR5053 were capable of targeting and inducing the degradation of HvPLATZ2 (Table S5), this provides a target for future study in high temperature stress.

Soil salinization stands as a significant abiotic environmental stress that hinders plant growth. Elevated soil salt levels lead to osmotic stress, ion toxicity (particularly Na+), and ancillary pressures like oxidative stress within plant cells [67]. The ABA-dependent pathway for plant salt tolerance, steered by PLATZ transcription factors, primarily functions by regulating stomatal conductance, boosting the efficiency of reactive oxygen metabolism, and activating ion transport pathways [27]. Rehman et al. [68] found that GhiPLATZ17 and GhiPLATZ22 contribute to enhanced salt resistance in upland cotton. Similarly, Zhang et al. [57] showed that over-expressed GhPLATZ1 in plant exhibit strong osmotic and salt resistance. In contrast, AtPLATZ2 was found to directly suppress the expression of CBL4 and CBL10, thereby compromising salt resistance in Arabidopsis [27]. In this study, only HvPLATZ6 exhibited significant t induction under salt stress (Fig. 9), suggesting HvPLATZ6 may be involved in salt tolerance in barley.

Overall, we carried out a thorough analysis of PLATZ family genes in barley. These findings lay the groundwork for manipulating PLATZ genes and aiding marker-assisted breeding in barley. However, it is imperative to conduct functional identification in future research to unveil the precise functional traits of HvPLATZ genes.
