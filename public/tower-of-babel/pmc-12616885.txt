Impact of Air Pollution in Modifying the Relationship Between Climatic Variables and Hand, Foot and Mouth Disease: A Multi‐City Time Series Study in Jiangsu Province, China
Authors: Xin Liu, Ziying Chen, Huan Fan, Ruiyun Li, Zhe Lou, Hong Ji, Jianli Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious research has primarily focused on the impact of climatic variables and air pollution on Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease (HFMD). However, there remains limited understanding of how air pollution levels modify these relationships across different regions and populations. This study employed a two‐stage, multi‐city time‐series analysis using data from 13 cities in Jiangsu Province (2015–2023) to explore these effects. A multistage analytical approach, including the distributed lag non‐linear model, multivariate meta‐regression, and attributable risk calculation, was used to quantify the association between climatic variables, air pollutants, and HFMD. Findings indicated that HFMD incidence is closely associated with meteorological conditions, with peak risk at 24.8°C for average temperature and 89.2% for average relative humidity. Low average wind speed and short sunshine hours (SH) also contributed to increased risk. Air pollutants, such as PM2.5, SO2, and O3, significantly modified these associations. For example, PM2.5 and SO2 increased HFMD risk at higher temperatures, while O3 reduced risk. Under low humidity, some pollutants exhibited protective effects, though risk increased with high humidity. NO2 had the strongest influence in reducing variability, while high PM2.5 and SO2 concentrations weakened the protective effects of SH. These findings emphasize the non‐linear influence of climatic variables on HFMD risk and suggest that air pollution's modification of these relationships varies by gender, age, and location. This provides important insights for developing targeted, timely public health warnings.

Introduction

Preschool‐aged children are the primary victims of hand‐foot‐mouth disease (HFMD), an acute infectious condition caused by enteroviruses, including Enterovirus 71 (EV71) and Coxsackievirus A16 (CV‐A16) (A. Bouman et al., 2005). Over the last few decades, HFMD has imposed a serious burden on the social, economic, and sanitary systems of several nations in the Asia‐Pacific area (L. G. Chan et al., 2000; Y. Chen et al., 2022). On 2 May 2008, HFMD was designated as a class C infectious illness that required notification in China (M. Zeng et al., 2012). Despite the implementation of EV‐71 targeted vaccination against HFMD in China since 2016, the incidence of HFMD remains high. In fact, there were approximately 1,673,481 reported cases in China in 2023 alone, making it the second most reported infectious disease in China (National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China, 2023).

There is a well‐established body of literature exploring the influence of meteorological factors on the incidence of HFMD. Studies suggest that climatic conditions can affect the viability of enteroviruses in the environment and influence the transmission dynamics of the disease (H. Qi et al., 2018; X. Xiao et al., 2017). For instance, increases in temperature and relative humidity have been shown to enhance the survival and transmission potential of enteroviruses. Gou et al. reported that a 1°C increase in temperature and a 1% rise in relative humidity were associated with a 2.8% and 2.47% increase in the weekly incidence of HFMD, respectively, in Lanzhou (F. Gou et al., 2018). Similarly, Hao et al. observed an “M” shaped relationship between HFMD incidence and temperature (J. Hao et al., 2020), while studies in Chengdu, Guilin, Guangdong, and a multi‐city analysis in China revealed an inverted “V” shaped relationship between temperature and HFMD incidence (Z. Du et al., 2019; X. Xiao et al., 2017; F. Yin et al., 2016; G. Yu et al., 2019). Further analysis by Luo and Yu indicated that the effects of climate variables on HFMD were not consistent across different regions of China. This heterogeneity may be influenced by variations in healthcare infrastructure, economic conditions, and population density, which modulate the contribution of environmental factors to HFMD risk (C. Luo et al., 2022; G. Yu et al., 2018). Thus, it is crucial to integrate both meteorological and socio‐economic factors when analyzing regional discrepancies in HFMD incidence.

The role of air pollution in influencing infectious disease transmission has garnered increasing attention in recent years, particularly within the context of rapidly urbanizing regions such as China. While previous studies frequently categorized air pollution as a confounding variable, emerging research is now beginning to recognize its potential to modify the relationship between climate variables and the incidence of diseases like HFMD. Notably, investigations into the interplay between air pollution and meteorological variables in the context of HFMD remain limited. Research by Cao suggests that elevated concentrations of PM2.5 and SO2 are associated with an increased risk of HFMD (H. Cao et al., 2024). Allouche's study also suggests that air pollution may weaken the immune system, thereby rendering individuals more vulnerable to infections (J. Allouche et al., 2022). Furthermore, Qian et al. identified that elevated levels of NO2 were linked to an increased risk of HFMD, indicating that air pollution can exacerbate conditions conducive to virus transmission (J. Qian et al., 2023). However, the current literature often treats air pollution as a secondary factor, primarily exploring exposure‐lag‐disease correlations rather than a more integrated approach to understanding its role as a modifier of disease dynamics (S. Gu et al., 2020). Additionally, air contaminants are often regarded as confounders rather than as modifiers (F. Yin et al., 2019). In addition, previous studies have focused on HFMD in specific geographic contexts, such as single cities, coastal versus inland areas (C. Zhang et al., 2024), or in highland, mountainous, and basin regions (C. Luo et al., 2022). However, sub‐regional analyses focusing on plain cities within a particular province have rarely been conducted. This limitation may restrict both the geographic scope of the studies and the interpretation of heterogeneity.

Considering its high prevalence of HFMD, dense population, and location in the middle and lower Yangtze Plain, Jiangsu Province was selected as the research region and stratified into three subregions—Southern, Central, and Northern Jiangsu—based on geographic location, socioeconomic status, and air pollution characteristics. With the glaring disparities in climate, it is essential to investigate how air pollution affects the link between climatic parameters and HFMD in various geographic locations. In order to estimate the physical impact of air pollutants and climatic variables on the occurrence of HFMD, we utilized data collected from 13 cities in Jiangsu Province spanning the years 2015–2023. We employ a considerably larger data set of HFMD patients than other research to ascertain how varying degrees of air pollution influence the correlation between air pollution exposure, climatic conditions, and HFMD incidence. This sophisticated methodology, including innovative analytical techniques, offers fresh perspectives on the physical impacts of air contaminants.

Materials and Methods

Study Area

Jiangsu Province is 107,200 square kilometers in size and is situated on the eastern coast of mainland China. It extends from 116°18′ to 121°57′E and 30°45' to 35°20′N. The 13 cities are divided into three regions: the southern region encompassing Nanjing, Wuxi, Changzhou, Suzhou, and Zhenjiang; the central region including Nantong, Yangzhou, and Taizhou; and the northern region comprising Xuzhou, Yancheng, Suqian, Lianyungang, and Huaian. Data Collection

The data on HFMD cases from 2015 to 2023 were extracted from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention (CISDCP). We retrieved daily meteorological monitoring data, including average temperature (AT, °C), average relative humidity (ARH, %), average wind speed (AWS, m/s), sunshine hours (SH, h). The daily meteorological data were obtained from the fifth‐generation reanalysis (ERA5) provided by the European Center for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). The daily surveillance data for air pollution were derived from the China National Environmental Monitoring Centre, including SO2(μg/m3), CO(mg/m3), NO2(μg/m3), PM10(μg/m3), PM2.5(μg/m3), and O3(μg/m3). The map of China's provincial administrative divisions was from National Platform for Common Geospatial Information Services. Administrative map of Jiangsu Province was from the National Earth System Science Data Center and the National Science and Technology Basic Conditions Platform. We calculated the arithmetic mean for each air pollution variables across prefecture‐level cities, considering them as the unit of analysis for the whole research duration. Subsequently, the arithmetic average of the same variable for each city was defined as a new substitutive variable, representing the variations in air quality among cities. City‐specific factors were included as possible modifiers in the second‐stage meta‐regression model to assess the modifying effects of prolonged air pollution levels (A. Gasparrini et al., 2012).

Subgroup Analyses

According to earlier research, environmental variables have different impacts on HFMD depending on age and gender (W. Cai et al., 2023; S. Yan et al., 2019; C. Zhang et al., 2024). Consequently, gender and age subgroup analyses were performed (age < 3, 3 ≤ age < 6 and age ≥ 6) (Y. Ding et al., 2024). In the meanwhile, we conducted a subgroup study covering three different regions (southern, central, and northern Jiangsu) based on the provincial topographical and climatic variables.

Statistical Analysis

First‐Stage Analysis

The aggregate exposure‐reaction relationship between each climatic element and the amount of HFMD occurrences in every city may be quantified using the distributed lag nonlinear model (DLNM) with a “cross‐basis” function grounded in a quasi‐Poisson distribution. We constructed the model as follows (C. Zhang et al., 2024): Yt∼Quasi−Possionμt logμt=α+∑cb(climaticvariables,lag) +ns(times,df)+ns(additionalclimaticvariables,df)+βholidayt+γDowt Yt denotes the daily amount of HFMD occurrences at time t , cb(climatic variables) signifies the cross‐basis function for each climatic variable, articulating the exposure‐lag‐reaction correlation. Three knots were established for meteorological variables, along with insolation in the exposure dimension, utilizing 4 degrees of freedom (dfs) for lagged reaction. 0–16 days was selected as the lag range to examine the lag impact, 3–5 days was taken into account latent duration and the roughly 2‐week infectious period associated with HFMD. To address prolonged and cyclical patterns in HFMD, a natural cubic spline function was applied, analyzing data spanning 7 years on an annual basis (Y. Chen et al., 2022; C. Luo et al., 2022; L. Yang et al., 2023). Additional climatic variables contain AT, ARH, AWS and SH, and we implemented the ns algorithm with 3 df. holiday t regulations for the impact of national vacation days. We established the middle value of every climatic variable as a reference point, acting as a benchmark for determining relative risk (RR). Dowt was utilized to regulate the day of the week.

Second‐Stage Analysis

The cumulative exposure‐reaction association for each city was established by aggregating all lag effects, enabling parametric dimensionality reduction for the subsequent analytical phase. A multivariate meta‐regression model that included only intercepts was formulated to clarify the overall pooled exposure‐reaction association in Jiangsu Province and to explore the variability among city‐specific associations. Subsequently, air pollutant levels were incorporated as separate meta‐predictors to construct meta‐regression models with a singular meta‐predictor, and their impacts were evaluated using maximum likelihood (ML) estimation (A. Erman et al., 2019; G. Li et al., 2018). Furthermore, the Wald test was employed to assess the efficacy of predictors in elucidating heterogeneity across 13 cities, while the I 2 statistic (indicating statistically significant residual heterogeneity when P < 0.05) was calculated to analyze the moderating impacts of air pollution levels (A. Gasparrini et al., 2012).

An analysis of subgroups was based on three defined parts: southern, central, and northern Jiangsu. We aggregated the impact evaluations throughout every municipality by area utilizing a multivariate meta‐regression model and subsequently illustrated total pooled exposure‐reaction trendlines for three parts.

Sensitivity Analysis

The ultimate model parameters were established by a synthesis of existing information and a methodical sensitivity analysis. This process utilizes the aggregate of the quasi‐AIC serves as the criterion for parameter determination and modeling components, which includes adjusting the maximum lags and df for meteorological variables, df for the natural cubic spline function to remove prolonged and cyclical patterns, and the number of knots for the exposure‐response spline (Figures S5–S12 in Supporting Information S1).

Using R software version 4.2.3, statistical analyses and modeling were executed. The ‘dlnm’ package facilitated the fitting of DLNM, meanwhile the mvmeta package for performing all multivariate meta‐regression models.

Comprehensive Aggregated Estimations of the Correlations Between Climatic Variables and HFMD

Figure 3 presents comprehensive aggregated estimates of the associations between climatic variables and HFMD. The associations between HFMD and AT as well as ARH exhibited an inverted V‐shape patterns. The cumulative risk ratio (RR) of HFMD increased with rising AT, peaked at 24.8°C (RR = 1.19, 95%CI: 1.09–1.29), and subsequently declined significantly. Similarly, the RR showed a steadily increasing linear trend with rising ARH, reaching a peak at 89.2% (RR = 1.39, 95%CI: 1.23–1.54). Our finding suggested that AWS below 2 m/s constitute a risk factor (RR > 1) for HFMD. Additionally, SH increased the prevalence of HFMD, exhibiting a risk effect within the duration of 0.2–5.9 hr (RR > 1).

Overall relationships and city exposure‐response curves for the relationship between climatic variables and HFMD in Jiangsu Province (The red line represents the pooled cumulative exposure‐response relationships, while the gray area indicates its 95% confidence intervals for exposure to climatic variables and HFMD for each subgroup, respectively).

The RR was similar for males and females, with only a slight elevation observed in the overall cumulative curve for females. For children younger than 3 years, the RR at equivalent levels of AT and ARH was higher compared to other age groups. With increasing AT, the RR for the ≥6‐year age group peaked earlier compared to other age groups, indicating a more pronounced impact of SH on HFMD cases in this age category. Furthermore, AWS significantly influenced HFMD across all age groups as a risk factor only when below 2 m/s.

Across the three regions, the cumulative effect curves between HFMD and AT were similar. However, the peak effect for AT was higher in the central region compared to the southern and northern region, whereas the peak effect of ARH was higher in the southern region compared to the central and northern regions. When ARH exceeded thresholds of 75.6%, 77.2% and 75.3% in the southern, central, and northern regions respectively, it transitioned from a protective factor to a risk factor for HFMD. AWS within the range of 2–4 m/s acted as a protective factor against HFMD in the southern and central regions, while AWS below 2 m/s was identified as a risk factor. However, no significant effect of AWS on HFMD observed in the northern region. For SH, durations of 0.6–5.1 hr, 0.4–5.9 hr, and 0.l–5.3 hr were identified as risk factors in the southern, central and northern region, respectively.

Impact of Air Pollution Levels on Modifications

Our detailed results are presented in Tables S2–S6 in Supporting Information S1. The influence of O3, SO2, and PM2.5 substantially modified the association between AT and HFMD, with PM2.5 demonstrating the most pronounced effect in diminishing heterogeneity (ΔI 2 = 1.949%). With the exception of O3, all examined air pollutants significantly influenced the relationship between ARH and HFMD, with NO2 having the strongest impact in decreasing heterogeneity (ΔI 2 = 4.323%). The relationship between AWS and HFMD was also notably affected by PM2.5, NO2, and O3, among which NO2 showed the greatest reduction in heterogeneity (ΔI 2 = 7.632%). Additionally, significant modifying effects of PM2.5, SO2 and NO2 were observed on the association between HFMD and SH, with NO2 exhibiting the most substantial reduction heterogeneity (ΔI 2 = 5.492%) (Table 2).

The age group demonstrating impacts from air pollutant adjustments similar to those observed in the overall group was the 3–6 years age category. PM2.5 substantially modified the association between AT and HFMD across all age categories, except among those aged over 6 years. Meanwhile, O3 significantly moderated this association in all groups except children younger than 3 years. Across all three age categories, NO2 substantially modified the association between ARH and HFMD, with the most pronounced effect in reducing heterogeneity observed among the age group of 6 years and older (ΔI 2 = 12.292%). The impact of air pollutants was found to be more significant in females compared to males, particularly in heterogeneity analyses of the associations between AT, ARH and HFMD. In this analysis, SO2 was identified as a significant moderating factor among females but not among males.

In the northern region of Jiangsu, the relationship between HFMD cases and AT, ARH and SH were modified by air pollutants. However, no specific modifying effects were observed between AWS and HFMD. In the central region, NO2 exhibited a distinct modifying effect, significantly contributing to the reduction in heterogeneity (ΔI 2 = 7.994%). Additionally, air pollutants modified the relationship between ARH and HFMD across all three regions, with NO2 consistently serving as an important effect modifier, substantially reducing heterogeneity.

After visualizing the modification effects of significant meta‐predictors, the results showed that elevated PM2.5 and SO2 concentrations at the 90th percent increased the RR associated with AT on HFMD. In contrast, higher O3 concentration exhibited relatively strong protective effects, particularly under high AT conditions. Furthermore, lower concentrations of PM2.5, SO2 and CO at the 10th percentile exhibited significant protective effects under conditions of low ARH, whereas high concentrations of PM2.5 and SO2 heightened the risk. When SH exceeded 6 hr, high SO2 and PM2.5 concentrations at the 90th percentile weakened the protective effect of SH against HFMD. However, the impact of NO2 initially enhanced protection but this protective effect diminished after SH exceeded 10.7 hr (Figure 4).

Notable modifiers influencing the association between HFMD and climatic variables across diverse populations and regions are illustrated in Figures S2–S4 in Supporting Information S1. Comparisons between gender subgroups revealed no significant differences in curve patterns between males and females. Additionally, our findings indicated that among children aged 3–6 years, air pollutants exerted a stronger influence on the association between climatic variables and HFMD compared to children aged under 3 years. The number of air pollutants affecting the correlation between climatic variables and HFMD was lowest among those aged 6 years and older.

Regional studies examining the impact of air pollutants on AT, it was found that, in the northern region, low levels PM2.5, SO2, NO2, and CO marginally reduced the risk of HFMD. However, these pollutants also attenuated the protective effect against HFMD when AT dropped below 16.3°C. A similar impact of PM2.5 on AT was observed in the southern region as well. Notably, elevated AT was associated with an increasing risk of HFMD in both southern and northern regions, with the risk being greater in the northern region compared to the southern region. Concerning ARH, the 10th percentile of PM2.5, SO2, and CO in the southern region slightly increased HFMD incidence risk, whereas NO2 exhibited the opposite effect. In central region, the scenario differed as low O3 concentrations reinforced the protective effect of ARH, thereby positively contributing to HFMD prevention. Conversely, in the northern region, low concentrations of PM2.5, SO2, CO, and NO2 enhanced the protective effect of ARH against HFMD, whereas O3 reduced this protective effect. Furthermore, when examining SH, low levels PM2.5, NO2, and CO in northern region slightly elevated the risk of HFMD. However, when SH exceeded 6 hr, these pollutants, along with SO2, significantly enhanced the protective effect against HFMD. In contrast, the role of NO2 related to SH in the central region was distinctly different from its role in the northern region.

Discussion

Our analysis indicates that higher AWS throughout 2021 may be due to strong atmospheric convection and typhoons activity (J. P. C. Center, 2021). The notable decline in HFMD cases between 2020 and 2023 may be associated with factors related to the COVID‐19 pandemic (M. A. Crane et al., 2021). Specifically, the reallocation of healthcare resources toward COVID‐19 control possibly reduced surveillance and reporting accuracy for HFMD. Additionally, decreased healthcare‐seeking behaviors during the pandemic could have led to further underreporting (F. Contarino et al., 2024). In addition, non‐pharmaceutical interventions such as mask usage, improved hygiene, social distancing, and temporary closures of educational facilities likely contributed to an actual decrease in HFMD transmission (W. Ma et al., 2024). Therefore, the reduction in reported HFMD cases during this period may reflect both genuine decreases in disease incidence and increased underreporting.

We applied a two‐stage analysis to characterize the associations of multiple meteorological indicators and air pollutants with HFMD cases in 13 cities of Jiangsu Province. Our primary objective was to determine whether and how air pollution levels to modify the associations of exposure to climatic variables and the number of HFMD cases across various communities and regions.

Our results indicated that high AT and ARH significantly influence HFMD incidence, demonstrating an inverted V‐shaped exposure‐response relationship. This observation is consistent with prior studies (A. Gasparrini et al., 2012; C. Zhang et al., 2024). Comfortable AT and ARH conditions are likely to enhance viral viability while encouraging increased outdoor activities among children, thereby increasing exposure risks (A. Hagiwara et al., 1983; A. Kramer et al., 2006; X. Xiao et al., 2017). Conversely, extremely high AT may reduce viral persistence, thus reducing transmission likelihood (X. Xiao et al., 2017), Additionally, high AT can limit sweating and decrease children's metabolic rate, making them more susceptible to HFMD (Y. Yang et al., 2018).

Subgroup analysis indicated gender differences, with females exhibiting a steeper exposure‐response trend compared to males. This may be attributed to physiological, immunological, or behavioral distinctions between sexes (K. L. Flanagan et al., 2013; G. Yu et al., 2019). Age‐specific analysis further underscored heightened susceptibility among infants under 2 years and older children (≥6 years), reflecting immature immunity and greater environmental exposure, respectively (J. Liu et al., 2019; X. Wei et al., 2021). Regarding climatic variables, prolonged SH exceeding 6 hr consistently exhibited protective effects across all subgroups, potentially through virus inactivation via ultraviolet radiation (J. F. Sánchez‐Pérez et al., 2019; X. Wu et al., 2014). In contrast, low AWS (<2 m/s) increased HFMD risk, likely due to diminished dispersion of airborne viruses and increased interpersonal contact outdoors (H. Cao et al., 2024; Q. Zhang et al., 2019).

Significant observations were made concerning air pollutants, indicating that elevated concentrations of PM2.5 and SO2 exacerbate the adverse effects of HFMD under high AT condition, which is consistent with previous findings (D. Li et al., 2024; H. Peng et al., 2022). Possible explanations for these observations include PM2.5 promoting the attachment of enteroviruses (T. M. Cruz‐Sanchez et al., 2013), reducing the body's antioxidant capacity, and mediating inflammatory reactions, thereby increasing the susceptibility to infectious diseases (M. He et al., 2017; Z. Qing‐qing et al., 2022). Additionally, SO2 adversely affected human health by irritating the respiratory tract, promoting oxidative damage and impairing immunity function (P. Orellano et al., 2021; Y. Yu et al., 2018).

Furthermore, we observed that elevated level of PM2.5 and SO2 might reduce the hazard impacts of HFMD under high ARH conditions, whereas higher concentrations of CO and NO2 increased this risk. In high‐humidity environments, elevated concentrations of PM2.5 and SO2 can cause noticeable discomfort, prompting reduced outdoor activities and increased adoption of targeted protective measures. Additionally, due to the high water solubility, SO2 is more readily absorbed under higher humidity conditions, reducing the likelihood of entering upper respiratory tract (C. Luo et al., 2022). Exposure to CO has been documented to cause several adverse health effects, including inflammation and oxidative damage (C. A. Piantadosi, 2008). Although direct laboratory evidence regarding the effects of NO2 on enteroviruses is lacking, NO2 exposure may induce systemic inflammation and immune activation, thus weakening the body's resistance to viral infection (D. C. Carslaw et al., 2016). These results of this study are consistent with those reported in a study conducted in Ningbo regarding NO2 exposure (S. Gu et al., 2020).

We found that O3 complexly modified the relationship between climate variables and HFMD. According to regional subgroup analysis, elevated O3 concentrations in northern Jiangsu appeared to mitigate the risk of HFMD under high AT conditions. Observations from Shandong and Guilin support this result (G. Yu et al., 2019; C. Zhang et al., 2024). Under elevated AT conditions, increased production of ground‐level O3 occurs due to accelerated reactions. Exposure to O3 has been shown to significantly induce cytokine production, which may inhibit EV71 replication and viral transmission (Y. C. Lin and S. C. Wu, 2006; Y. C. Lin et al., 2007). This mechanism could also explain why elevated ground‐level O3 reduced HFMD risks under high AT conditions.

Compared to the southern and central regions of Jiangsu, a significantly higher number of air pollutants were found to significantly modify the associations between climatic variables and HFMD incidence in the northern region. This regional disparity may be attributed to several interacting factors. First, northern Jiangsu tends to experience higher baseline levels of air pollutants due to the concentration of heavy industries and relatively lax environmental regulations, which may enhance the observable interaction effects between pollutants and climatic factors. Second, the geographical and meteorological characteristics of the region—such as lower humidity, more frequent temperature fluctuations, and weaker atmospheric dispersion capacity—could prolong pollutant residence time and enhance environmental viral stability, thereby amplifying the interactions among pollutants, climate, and disease. Furthermore, the urban morphology and ventilation conditions in northern cities, characterized by high building density and less favorable wind conditions, may lead to pollutant accumulation and reduced dilution, increasing population exposure (H.M. Cheng et al., 2021; M. Pantusheva et al., 2022). In contrast, southern Jiangsu benefits from stronger coastal ventilation and more consistent urban planning, potentially weakening the modifying role of air pollutants.

It should be noted that our results were exclusively presented for those pollutants identified as significant modifiers of the associations between climatic variables and HFMD incidence (as determined by Wald test, P < 0.05). Similarly, previous studies (C. Li et al., 2022; C. Luo et al., 2022; C. Zhang et al., 2024) have also employed statistical tests with a significance threshold of “P” value to identify air pollutants that significantly modify these associations. The referenced studies cover Sichuan Province, Shandong Province, and Jiangsu Province—the region examined in our study—which vary in terms of HFMD incidence rates, economic and industrial structures, as well as geographic and climatic characteristics. These variations may account for discrepancies in the types of air pollutants identified as significant modifiers in different areas. In addition, regional disparities in the 10th and 90th percentile concentrations of air pollutants may partly explain the inconsistent findings observed in different studies. For instance, Luo et al. reported that elevated concentrations of SO2 (19.6 μg/m3) in Sichuan Province attenuated the risk of HFMD under high ambient temperature conditions (C. Luo et al., 2022). In contrast, our study identified an increased risk associated with similarly high concentrations of SO2 (13.98 μg/m3) under the same conditions. Likewise, research conducted in Shandong Province indicated that lower concentrations of PM2.5 (42.62 μg/m3) enhanced the morbidity risk of HFMD (C. Zhang et al., 2024), which is consistent with our finding that an even lower PM2.5 concentration (37.32 μg/m3) was associated with increased HFMD risk.

These discrepancies underscore that the modifying effects of air pollutants on the relationship between climatic variables and HFMD incidence are primarily determined by their absolute concentration levels, rather than categorical designations such as “high” or “low.” Given that such thresholds for pollutant concentrations are region‐specific and context‐dependent, it is essential to exercise caution when interpreting and comparing results across regions with significant variations in pollutant distributions. Consequently, direct inter‐regional comparisons may possess limited interpretative value unless the local pollution context is taken into consideration.

In subgroup analysis for gender and age, we found that the associations between climatic variables and HFMD was substantially influenced by air pollution, particularly among females and children younger than 2 years. This heightened vulnerability in younger children may be attributed to their relatively immature immune systems (A. Bouman et al., 2005; N.Goksugur and S. Goksugur, 2010), making them more susceptible to enterovirus infections driven by the combined influences of air pollutants and climatic variables. Our findings align with previous studies indicating that physiological and immunological differences between males and females (J. E. Clougherty, 2010; H. Guo et al., 2014), potentially rendering females more susceptible to the adverse effects of air pollutants. However, our observations regarding gender differences contrast with results from prior research, underscoring the necessity for further investigations into potential sex‐specific disparities in the effects of air pollutants and climatic variables on HFMD.

This study revealed the vulnerable population for HFMD in Jiangsu Province through subgroup analyses based on age and gender, leading to conclusions that differ from previous research findings. Additionally, we investigated how atmospheric environmental factors influence the occurrence of HFMD cases across different locations, a crucial step in developing comprehensive public health strategies that address both direct and indirect environmental impacts. Nevertheless, our exploration has certain drawbacks. Although we observed macroscopic connections in our ecological study, this technique unavoidably restricts the inference of causality. Furthermore, a portion of the heterogeneity remained unexplained by air pollutant levels alone. Other elements which could possibly serve as modifiers include population density, cultural customs, individual self‐defense strategies, and public health initiatives; however, because of the scarcity of data, these factors were excluded from this analysis.

Conclusions

To summarize, in Jiangsu Province, the study confirmed that high AT and ARH increase the risk of infection, while extremely high AT may reduce the risk. The susceptibility of the associations between climatic factors and HFMD to the influence of air pollution was more pronounced among children under the age of 3 years and females. The impacts of AT, ARH, and AWS on HFMD differed according to various levels of air pollution. Furthermore, the correlation between meteorological factors and HFMD could be significantly impacted by air pollution, and this impact might exhibit considerable variation across different geographic areas. Our results have substantial significance for health authorities in enhancing public health policies and establishing an early warning system aimed at managing HFMD reinfection outbreaks affected by external factors.
