Quality of life and functional outcomes after rectal sparing approaches following neoadjuvant therapy for rectal cancer: a prospective cohort multicentre study
Authors: Teresa Gavaruzzi, Paola Del Bianco, Laura Gavagna, Quoc Riccardo Bao, Daniela Rega, Angelo Restivo, Simona Deidda, Claudio Belluco, Andrea Lauretta, Carlo Aschele, Antonino Spinelli, Carmela Cervone, Lucia Perna, Francesco Bianco, Giacomo Sarzo, Claudio Coco, Giuditta Chiloiro, Andrea Muratore, Alfredo Mellano, Germana Chiaulon, Lorella Lotto, Salvatore Pucciarelli, Maria Antonietta Gambacorta, Paolo Delrio, Gaya Spolverato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:This prospective observational multicenter cohort study aimed to establish the functional outcomes and quality of life (QoL) of patients with rectal cancer who showed a major (mCR) or complete clinical response (cCR) after neoadjuvant therapy and underwent a rectum-sparing approach (watch and wait, WW, or local excision, LE).Materials and Methods:QoL and functional function were investigated using the EORTC QLQ-30, EORTC QLQ-CR29, the MSKCC bowel function instrument, and the FIQL index. Patients filled these questionnaires at the time of enrollment (T1), and 6 (T2) and 12 months (T3) thereafter. Variation over time for each group and comparisons between cCR patients who underwent WW or LE were performed.Results:Of 161 patients who were enrolled, 112 had a cCR (LE n = 50, WW n = 62) and 49 a mCR (all LE). LE patients showed changes after surgery, WW patients showed more gradual changes. LE patients showed reduced anxiety and depression and improved emotional functioning but also an increased fecal incontinence and reduced sexual function in men after surgery. WW patients showed a significant improvement over time in role functioning and anxiety, while their diarrhea and stool frequency progressively reduced. Among patients with a cCR, LE patients showed a significantly higher incontinence compared to WW patients. However, the two groups did not differ in terms of the impact of incontinence on QoL.Conclusion:Functional outcomes and QoL of patients who underwent WW or LE approach generally improve over time, but LE patients also showed some impact of surgery.

Introduction

The gold standard approach for locally advanced rectal cancer is neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME)[1]. The nCRT plays an important role in local disease control and tumor regression by significantly reducing the tumor size, the depth of tumor penetration and the risk of nodal metastases[2]. About 20–30% of patients achieve a pathological complete response (pCR)[3,4]. However, urinary continence and sexual functioning are negatively affected in at least 30% of patients who undergo TME[5–7]. Up to 50% of patients have a multidimensional bowel dysfunction syndrome, consisting of fecal incontinence, urgency, frequent bowel movements, collectively referred to as the low anterior resection syndrome (LARS), which often impacts their quality of life (QoL)[8,9].

In order to avoid the morbidities and negative impact on functional outcomes associated with neoadjuvant therapy followed by TME, an increasing interest has arisen towards rectal sparing approaches that avoid these sequelae while, and, at the same time, not impacting the oncological outcomes[10–13]. Rectum-sparing approaches include the simple observation obtained with a strict follow up (watch-and-wait, also referred to as wait-and-see or watchful waiting, WW)[4], which is indicated for patients with clinical complete response (cCR), and the full thickness transanal local excision (LE) of the scar or tumor remnant, indicated for patients with cCR or major clinical response (mCR) after neoadjuvant therapy[14]. The impact of these techniques on oncological outcomes has been summarized by 3 recent reviews and meta-analyses, 2 focusing on the comparison between WW and LE[15,16] and including 8 and 9 studies respectively, and 1 encompassing also the comparison with radical surgery and including 18 studies[17]. These summaries of available evidence have shown that LE and WW approaches yield no differences in terms of rectum preservation rate, local control and distant recurrences[15,16], as well as no differences in overall survival, 5-year disease-free survival, distant metastasis, and mortality[17].

Nonetheless, patients and clinicians need to be aware of the impact that these options have on functional outcomes and QoL. Indeed, more in general, the importance of considering health-related QoL after cancer treatment has increased exponentially[18,19], as it has become clear that the impact on the well-being and QoL of patients has to be considered together with the clinical outcomes of the treatment options. This is especially essential as the involvement of patients in their healthcare and patient-centeredness have increased in the past years, yielding to an increase of shared decision making in clinical care[20,21].

Few studies have assessed the impact of both rectum-sparing approaches on QoL and bowel functions[12,14,22–27]. While most of these studies have assessed these outcomes in patients treated with LE, they were often compared with patients treated with TME[12,14,25], less frequently with patients undergoing WW[24,26,27]. Moreover, these studies were often retrospective and characterized by small sample sizes.

The present article provides data on functional outcomes and QoL of patients recruited in the ReSARCh (Rectal Sparing Approach after preoperative Radio- and/or Chemo-therapy in patients with rectal cancer) trial, an observational, prospective, multicenter study conducted in Italy[13,28,29]. The aim of this ancillary study is to evaluate the QoL and functional outcomes after LE or WW approach in rectal cancer patients who showed a mCR or cCR after neoadjuvant therapy, assessing for each approach the trend over time and, among patients who achieved cCR, comparing the outcomes of the two approaches.

Written informed consent to participate in the study was provided by all patients. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of the coordinating center (University Hospital of Padova, ref. 0074522, 21/12/2015) and by each participating institution. The trial is registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02710812), and the protocol has been published[28].

Patients’ selection and clinical characteristics

Inclusion criteria and study protocol were previously described[13,28,29]. From April 2016 to June 2020, in each of the 16 centers participating, patients with the following selection criteria were enrolled in the study: patients with adenocarcinoma of the rectum, ≥18 years old, tumor not above 12 cm from the anal verge at proctoscopy, candidates to receive neoadjuvant treatment and able to undergo a TME surgery. Then, recruitment in the study depended on the clinical response after neoadjuvant treatment, which needed to be major or complete. Clinical and pathological TNM staging were reported according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer, eighth edition. Clinical staging has been performed at diagnosis and after 7–8 weeks from the end of neoadjuvant therapy. If there was evidence of mCR or cCR at endoscopy, and absence of metastatic lymph nodes at MRI, the patient was restaged after 11–12 weeks from completion of nCRT, and then eligible for rectum-sparing treatment.

The clinical response was definite as cCR if there were no palpable masses at digital rectal exam, no mucosal abnormality at endoscopy, and no metastatic nodes at magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). mCR was defined as the absence of a pathological regional lymph node on MRI, with the presence at proctoscopy of a residual scar or a superficial ulcer less than 2 cm.

All patients were fully informed about the standard of care (TME) and were described the rectum-sparing approaches possible in their case. In particular, patients with a mCR were offered to undergo LE, patients with cCR were offered to undergo LE or WW at the physician’s and patient’s discretion. For LE patients, any surgical transanal technique was accepted provided that the following oncological principles were respected: (1) a macroscopic free margin of at least 5 mm around the residual tumor/scar; and (2) a full-thickness excision performed, including mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria and the perirectal fat.

Patients after LE or WW approaches were seen every 3 months for the first 2 years, and then every 6 months for the following 3 years. At each follow-up, patients underwent: digital rectal examination, proctoscopy, carcinoembryonic antigen, and blood chemistry routine. In addition, patients performed: MRI every 6 months for 5 years, yearly chest-abdominal computed tomography scan, colonoscopy 1 year after treatment and if negative after 3 years and then every 5 years.

Quality of life, bowel function, and fecal continence

After the neoadjuvant therapy, at the time of enrollment (T1), and at 6 (T2) and 12 months (T3) thereafter, each patient was asked to fill four questionnaires assessing QoL, bowel function and fecal continence. Specifically, they filled the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire – Core 30 (EORTC QLQ-C30)[31], its colorectal module (EORTC QLQ-CR29)[32], the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Centre (MSKCC) bowel function instrument[33,34], and the Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life (FIQL) scale[35].

The EORTC QLQ-C30 contains 30 items and includes a scale measuring the global health status, 5 functioning scales (physical, role, emotional, cognitive, and social), 3 symptom scales (fatigue, pain, nausea, and vomiting), and 6 single symptom items (dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, diarrhea, and financial difficulties)[31].

EORTC QLQ-CR29 consists of 29 items, including items in 4 scales (urinary frequency, blood/mucus in stool, stool frequency, body image) and 19 single items (urinary incontinence, dysuria, abdominal pain, buttock pain, bloating, dry mouth, hair loss, taste, anxiety, weight, flatulence, fecal incontinence, sore skin, embarrassment, stoma care problems, sexual interest for men, sexual interest for woman, impotence, dyspareunia)[32]. There are also 11 items allocated for specific subpopulations, including males, females, and stoma patients. The EORTC questionnaires were scored using the recommended EORTC procedures[36]. Resulting scores range from 0 to 100, and higher scores indicate better functioning for functional scales and higher levels of symptoms for symptoms scales.

The MSKCC Bowel Function questionnaire[33,34] includes 18 items grouped in three subscales (frequency, dietary, and urgency/soilage), 4 individual items of clinical significance (incomplete emptying after a bowel movement, another bowel movement within 15 min, knowing the difference between gas and a bowel movement, inability to control the passage of flatus), and one total score that is obtained by summing all 18 items. The questionnaire was scored according to the validation article. The score range is 18–90 for the total score, 1–5 for each individual item, 6–30 for each frequency subscale, and 4–20 for the dietary and urgency/soilage subscales. Higher scores indicate better bowel function.

The FIQL scale assesses the specific QoL issues that can be impacted in patients with fecal incontinence. It includes 29 items gauging the impact of fecal incontinence within 4 domains: lifestyle issue (10 items, e.g., “It is important to plan my schedule (daily activities) around my bowel pattern”), coping/behavior (9 items, e.g., “Whenever I am away from home, I try to stay near a restroom as much as possible”), depression/self-perception (7 items, e.g., “During the past month, have you felt so sad, discouraged, hopeless, or had so many problems that you wondered if anything was worthwhile”), and embarrassment (3 items, e.g., “I worry about others smelling stool on me”). Item can be scored from 1 to 4, with a fifth answer option labelled “not a problem”[35].

The questionnaire was scored according to the validation article. The score range is 10–40 for the lifestyle domain, 9–36 for the coping/ behavior domain, 7–28 for the depression/self-perception domain, and 3–12 for the embarrassment domain. Higher scores indicate better QoL.

Patients’ self-reported socio-demographic information, including patients’ age and sex, education level, marital status, and employment status, were collected. Clinical data that were considered were: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA), Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG), Body Max Index, and response to neoadjuvant treatment.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. For each outcome variable, a mixed effects model was fitted over time in order to estimate the change in QoL scores from baseline and subsequent assessments. This model addresses missing outcome data under the missing at random assumption using full information maximum likelihood, which analyzes all available observed data without imputing missing values. This approach produces parameter estimates and standard errors that account for data structure and missingness uncertainty, assuming missingness depends only on observed data. Kenward–Roger degrees-of-freedom approximation was used to model all available data from each patient. Random effects were modeled for intercept and slope (unstructured covariance matrix), and fixed effects included the treatment choice, the time of assessment as a categorical variable, and their interaction, while adjusting for potential confounders. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P < 0.01 was considered statistically significant. Clinical significance was determined using the framework of minimally important differences, determined by small and medium distribution-based cut-offs (0.3 and 0.5 SD, respectively)[37], calculated on the values at the baseline assessment (T1). A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the impact of missing data on the study results and validate the robustness of the treatment effect estimates. Missing values in the dataset were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE), implemented with the predictive mean matching method. Specifically, 50 imputed datasets were generated with 5 iterations each, using a specified predictor matrix and a fixed seed to ensure reproducibility. To thoroughly validate robustness, two distinct analyses based on patient inclusion were performed. The primary analysis (MICE-a) involved a full sample imputation, where the dependent longitudinal variable was imputed across all patients, incorporating those with missing covariates into the imputation model. For a sensitive check, a second analysis re-ran the imputation of the dependent longitudinal variable, excluding patients with missing covariates (MICE-b). This two-pronged approach allows for a comprehensive evaluation of how both the missing data mechanism and the imputation method potentially influence the final conclusions. Data were analyzed using RStudio (RStudio: Integrated Development for R. RStudio Inc., Boston, Massachusetts, USA).

Results

A total of 178 patients were enrolled in 16 centers between April 2016 and June 2020[29]. Of these, 12 participating centers joined this ancillary study, accounting for a total of 161 patients. Overall, 133 (82.6%) filled out the questionnaires at the baseline (T1), 120 (74.5%) after 6 months (T2), and 101 (62.7%) after 12 months (T3). Overall, 75 (46.6%) patients filled all the 3 questionnaires, 43 (26.7%) 2 of them, and 43 (26.7%) only 1. The pattern was similar for LE and WW patients.

Patients’ characteristics are described in Table 1. The median (IQR) age was 64 (58–72) years, and 113 (70.2%) were men. The majority of patients reported living with a family member (82.8%) and was not currently employed (58.8%, more frequently in the WW group than the LE group). Most patients had an ASA score less than 3 (80.6%) and an ECOG of 0 (88.5%). Significant baseline differences in employment status and ASA scores were observed between the LE and WW groups. These two covariates were identified as potential confounders and included as fixed effects to isolate the treatment impact on QoL outcomes. To preserve the sample size, a “missing” category was created for cases with missing data. Considering the standard of treatment at the time of enrollment, most of the patients (n = 131, 85.7%) underwent long-course radiotherapy, whereas only 15 (9.3%) patients underwent short-course radiotherapy and 3 (2.3%) total neoadjuvant therapy. After the neoadjuvant treatment, 112 (69.6%) patients had a cCR and 49 (30.4%) patients achieved a mCR. Of those with a cCR, 50 (44.6%) underwent LE, and 62 (55.4%) underwent WW. Local excision and watch-and-wait trend over time

The pattern of responses over time is shown in Figures 1–2 (see also Supplemental Digital Content Tables S1–S4, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347). Patients who underwent LE showed a series of significant changes that happened after surgery. Specifically, relative to T1, at T2 they showed a statistically and clinically significant improvement in their emotional functioning (from 78.3 to 84.4, P = 0.005), and a statistically and clinically significant reduction in their anxiety (from 61.6 to 70.6, P = 0.007), that were both maintained at T3 (emotional functioning: 84.3, P = 0.006; anxiety: 71.2, P = 0.005). Similarly, they showed a clinically significant improvement in their depression and self-perception from T1 to T2 that was maintained at T3. Moreover, both blood and mucus in stool, and buttock pain showed a clinically significant improvement from T1 to T3. However, following surgery, LE patients reported a statistically and clinically significant increase in fecal incontinence (from 2.4 to 12.6, P = 0.0001) that remained clinically significant at T3 (8.8). The answers to the FIQL questionnaire also highlighted an increased frequency of fecal incontinence, especially at T3. Indeed, as shown in Table 2, the number of patients indicating that they did not experience fecal incontinence decreased from 32–35% in T1 and T2 to 23–25% at T3, indicating that an additional 10% of patients reported fecal incontinence. Finally, in the subgroup of male patients, a clinically significant worsening in male sexual functioning was observed after surgery that was maintained at T3. When assessing the trend over time for patients who underwent LE with a traditional technique (TAE) separately from those for whom a micro-invasive technique was used (no-TAE) on selected outcomes (see also Supplemental Digital Content Table S2-b, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347), the pattern of increased fecal incontinence after surgery was confirmed in both subgroups, with a clinical and statistical difference for the TAE subgroup after surgery (from 1.4 to 13.4, P = 0.0008) and a clinical difference for the no-TAE subgroup (from 4 to 12.7) and the difference remained clinically significant after 1 year relative to the baseline for both subgroups (TAE: 7, no-TAE: 11.7). For blood and mucus in stools, the pattern of clinical improvement over time found for the LE group was confirmed only in the TAE subgroup (T1: 14, T3: 7.7). Patients who followed the WW approach showed, generally, more gradual changes over time. Specifically, in the year following the end of neoadjuvant therapy, a clinically significant improvement was observed in their role functioning and insomnia, while their anxiety showed a statistically and clinically significant decrease (from 55.9 to 75.3, overall P = 0.0001). At the same time, a statistically and clinically significant progressive reduction was found for diarrhea (from 14.9 to 2.6, overall P = 0.001) as well as pain (from 15.6 to 7.8, overall P = 0.0075), dysuria (from 4.8 to 0, overall P = 0.0020), sore skin (from 18.8 to 7.2, overall P = 0.0034), buttock pain (from 16.9 to 2.6, overall P = 0.003), fatigue (from 22.3 to 11.1, overall P = 0.0003), and dry mouth scores (from 15.6 to 4.2, overall P = 0.0002). Additionally, a clinically significant improvement was reported over time in the stool frequency (from 10.4 to 3.6), fecal incontinence (from 4.7 to 0), urinary frequency (from 20.1 in T1 to 12.2 in T2), abdominal pain (from 8.5 to 1.7), nausea and vomiting (from 3.7 to 1), as well as in sexual functioning for both men (from 54 to 62.2) and women (from 78.8 to 89.3) and a reduction in impotence (from 28.9 in T2 to 18.1 in T3) and in dyspareunia (from 23.4 to 2.5). Additionally, the MSKCC questionnaire showed a clinically significant improvement over time in having another bowel movement within 15 min (from 3.5 to 4.0), the control of passage of gas (from 3.4 to 3.8), in frequency (from 21.8 to 23.8) and dietary (from 16.5 to 17.8), while the FIQL scores showed a clinically significant improvement in the coping behavior (from 27.2 to 29.9) and a clinically significant reduction followed by an improvement in the embarrassment subscale (from 10 to 8.7 to 9.9).

LE patients reported a worse blood and mucus level in stools over all time assessments (overall LE: 10.3, WW: 5.3, P = 0.0047, also clinically relevant). This difference was clinically significant at all time points, and also statistically significant at T1. The overall difference was statistically and clinically significant also for the fecal incontinence (overall LE: 8.9, WW: 1.3, P = .0.0093) but this effect was driven by the surgery, as there was no difference at T1, whereas LE patients showed a statistically and clinically significantly higher incontinence at T2 and T3 compared to WW patients (T2: 13.1 vs. 1.3, P = 0.0022; T3: 10.9 vs. 0, P = 0.0036). Of the clinically significant differences, LE patients showed higher levels of anxiety at T2 relative to WW patients (LE: 77.3, WW: 64.6), higher levels of urinary incontinence at both T2 (LE:6, WW: 1.8) and T3 (LE: 5.1, WW: 1.5), higher sore skin at T2 (LE: 15.4, WW: 8.8), higher stool frequency at T3 (LE: 8.5, WW: 3.0), higher flatulence at T3 (LE: 14.8, WW:8.6), higher hair loss at T3 (LE: 3.4, WW: 0), but also lower urinary frequency at T3 (LE: 9.1, WW: 16.8). The clinically significant differences in the MSKCC questionnaire confirmed worse bowel function for the LE group after surgery, with difference in the frequency and urgency at both T2 and T3, and at T3 worse scores for: another bowel movement in 15 min, knowing the difference between gas and bowel movement, controlling the passage of gas, and dietary subscales. On the other hand, the clinically significant differences in the FIQL questionnaire showed better QoL for LE patients after surgery on the coping/behavior, depression/self-perception and embarrassment subscales. To note that there were other clinically significant differences at T1, with LE patients reporting better weight and male and female sexual functioning, while also reporting lower dysuria, abdominal pain, and embarrassment due to bowel movement (both in the QLQ-CR29 questionnaire and in the FIQL questionnaire), as well as better knowledge of the difference between gas and bowel movement. The analysis of the subgroups undergoing LE with TAE or no-TAE techniques on selected outcomes showed similar patterns of results between the two subgroups, without yielding to significant differences (see Supplemental Digital Content Table S6-b, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347).

Sensitivity analysis

The results of the sensitivity analysis to evaluate robustness to missingness are reported in Supplemental Digital Content Tables S1-MI–S8-MI, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347. In brief, the pattern of results of the analysis over time (Supplemental Digital Content Tables S1-MI–S4-MI, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347), confirmed the findings for WW patients for diarrhea and anxiety at the 1% level, and for fatigue and dry mouth at the 5% level, whereas findings concerning pain, dysuria, buttock pain and sore skin were not confirmed. For LE patients, findings are confirmed for emotional functioning, anxiety and fecal incontinence, all at the 5% level. For the comparison between groups in complete responders (Supplemental Digital Content Tables S5-MI–S8-MI, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347), the analysis confirmed the difference in blood and mucus in stools at the 1% level, and the difference in fecal incontinence at the 5% level, as well as clinical difference.

The two multiple imputation (MI) methods produced highly similar results to each other and to the original dataset across almost all scales (Supplemental Digital Content Figures S1–S8, available at: http://links.lww.com/JS9/G347). For almost all scales and all time points, the estimated mean scores for the original, MICE-a, and MICE-b datasets are very close to each other. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) also show significant overlap across the three datasets for all scales and time points. Where differences exist, such as the slight shifts in means or the change in CI width, the clinical interpretation remained the same.

Discussion

This ancillary study of the ReSARCh trial represents the first QoL, bowel function, and fecal continence analysis study including both rectal sparing approaches. This study examined and compared the impact of LE and WW on functional and QoL outcomes of patients achieving a mCR or cCR after neoadjuvant treatment.

Both groups showed signs of the known effects of neoadjuvant treatment at baseline that tended to improve over time, especially for the WW group, whereas the LE group showed a clear impact of the surgical treatment on individual outcomes.

Patients who underwent LE showed significant post-surgical improvement in their emotional functioning, reduction in their anxiety, and improvement in their depression and self-perception. The LE group showed a clear improvement after surgery, whereas the WW group showed a more gradual pattern of improvement over time. The surgical removal is likely to be associated with a greater reassurance as it is known in the literature that often people show an “action bias,” a preference for action over inaction, even when they have the same effects, especially when potential regret is stressed[38,39]. This would also be in line with findings from previous studies comparing LE and TME showing no significant differences regarding the emotional functioning, anxiety, depression and self-perception[12].

However, LE led to a marked impairment in their fecal continence that remained clinically significant after 12 months. This finding confirms the results of previous studies analyzing the continence after radiotherapy and LE, which reported a fecal continence impairment after LE that remained moderate after 36 months[40]. This is likely due to the use of devices with a diameter of 3–4 cm for the LE, which can cause infringements of the internal anal sphincter. A recent meta-analysis[41] comparing the TransAnal Minimally Invasive Surgery (TAMIS) platform with rigid platforms (Transanal Endoscopic Microsurgery (TEM), Transanal Endoscopic Operation (TEO)) found similar operative and specimen outcomes. However, rigid platforms were associated with higher rates of readmission and complication, particularly urinary retention and rectal bleeding. When performing TAMIS using Single Incision Laparoscopic Surgery (SILS) port or a GelPOINT Path, the transanal device is smaller (30 vs. 40 mm) and shorter (30–37 vs. 12–20 cm) compared to devices used in TEM or TEO. Despite these technical differences, no studies compared long-term impact of these approaches on QoL and bowel function. Moreover, patients undergoing LE after neoadjuvant treatment will still have a slower and less reactive muscle improvement due to the radiotherapy. Furthermore, during the LE, a full-thickness excision from the mucosa to the perirectal fat was performed, and it is sometimes not possible to close the surgical incision. Studies comparing closure versus non-closure of the defect after LE have shown that leaving the defect open is generally safe. However, closure may help reduce the risk of early post-operative complications such as bleeding. Although some studies[42] have reported that non-closure can cause temporary symptoms, these do not appear to significantly affect long-term functional outcomes. To note, from the surgical wound, there will be the production of serum and mucus, as shown by the finding that the score on the blood and mucus in stool subscale was higher in the LE group than the WW group. However, patients could also interpret this as a sort of incontinence. On the other hand, when surgical wounds are closed, this can give the sensation of tenesmus, and patients can interpret this sensation as fecal incontinence. Nonetheless, as previously reported, when compared to TME, the rate of fecal incontinence is statistically reduced in patients treated with LE. It is important to note that LE can lead to good functional outcomes and, in our opinion, it is the only rectal sparing option in case of mCR, since there is no evidence that the WW approach is oncologically safe for patients with mCR.

When analyzing patients in the WW group, in the year following the end of neoadjuvant therapy, their role functioning and insomnia gradually improved, and their anxiety gradually decreased. These findings confirm data from the Dutch WW registry, where emotional, social functioning, and anxiety improved during the time, and at an extended follow-up of 24 months. Even if there is a prolonged analysis of QoL, in this study, LE was used as salvage surgery in case of local regrowth, and a real comparison between different rectal sparing approaches could not be considered.

When comparing LE and WW approaches in patients showing a complete response, the impairment in fecal continence was significantly worse in patients treated with LE, both at T2 and T3. LE patients also reported a worse blood and mucus level in stools over all time assessments. This is not surprising considering the abovementioned impact of surgery on fecal continence, even if fecal incontinence was also reported for patients in the WW group. Data from the Dutch WW registry showed that the rate of patients with fecal incontinence after 24 months almost doubled compared to the rate at 3 months. Even in the absence of a surgical procedure, LARS can also develop in patients followed with a WW approach, due to the effect of preoperative radiotherapy, including nerve damage, impairment of the anal sphincter and radiation-induced fibrosis.

When locally advanced rectal cancer showed a complete or major response after neoadjuvant treatment, rectum-sparing approaches have been shown to yield to comparable oncological outcomes[15–17], while avoiding the highly likely sequelae of more invasive surgical approaches (i.e., TME), that are likely to impact on patients’ QoL and functional outcomes, especially on their fecal continence[14,22–27]. In this setting, clinicians should be aware that, even if LE has the advantage of histological proof of complete response, LE has more impact on fecal continence of the patients compared to WW. However, the impact of fecal incontinence on QoL was nonetheless similar, as shown by the FIQL scores, which did not differ between LE and WW patients, suggesting that the difference between the groups is in the frequency of fecal incontinence, not in the severity of the impact on QoL. Patients and clinicians need to be aware of the impact that these options have on the outcomes for the patients to inform their decision making.

This study has several limitations, the main one being that it was observational and not randomized, potentially incurring in selection bias. However, in order to be able to sustain the organization and long multicenter data collection, resources are needed, and in this study, they were not available. Nonetheless, our study is one of the first to collect a large number of patients and to analyze their functional and QoL data up to 1 year. Other studies have been planned to collect partially randomized data[43]. Secondly, as is common in this type of research, especially when resources to collect data are limited, missing data were common, with only 46.6% of patients completing all three questionnaires. In order to examine a potential selection bias, we used MI to conduct a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of missingness on the results. Most of the main results were confirmed, although some comparisons showed a reduced level of statistical significance after MI. This reduction is attributable to the increase in standard errors and wider CIs following MI, which reflect the additional uncertainty introduced by imputing missing values through incorporating both within- and between-imputation variability. Such results indicate that the inference is more conservative and realistic.

Another limitation is that the decision making process that yielded the choice of WW or LE was not examined in the current study. While it is ascertained that all patients were fully informed, it would have been interesting to explore whether their involvement and participation in the decision making process may further benefit patients’ outcomes, especially emotional outcomes, as shown in other contexts[44–46]. As in similar studies, the findings on sexual functioning should be considered cautiously as they derive from subgroup analyses and have often wide interval ranges. Finally, the data were collected until 12 months, thus limiting the possibility to assess long-term functional outcomes beyond that date.

Conclusion

This study shows that the patients who undergo rectal sparing approaches after neoadjuvant therapy for rectal cancer report a gradual improvement over time in almost all the scales of bowel function and QoL questionnaires, confirming that the rectum may be preserved without impairing health-related QoL. For mCR, LE represents a rectal sparing approach that leads to satisfactory functional outcomes. For cCR patients, LE was found to increase fecal incontinence, although the impact of fecal incontinence on QoL seems to be similar to WW. Therefore, these findings can be very useful both for patients and clinicians in a decision making process after neoadjuvant treatment for rectal cancer.
