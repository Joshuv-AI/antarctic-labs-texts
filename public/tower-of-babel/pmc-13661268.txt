DFCP1-containing ER microdomains mediate lysosomal membrane repair
Authors: Maja Radulovic, Sascha Pust, Athanasios Kournoutis, Di Chen, M Isabel Giner, Qijian Liang, Santosh Phuyal, Thomas J Böddeker, Katy McCarron, Eric Herrmann, Kevin Rose, Sebastian W Schultz, Andreas Brech, James H Hurley, Claudio Bussi, Luis Bonet-Ponce, Maximiliano G Gutierrez, Camilla Raiborg, Harald Stenmark
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLysosomal membrane integrity is essential for preserving cellular homeostasis in response to different stressors. Upon lysosomal membrane permeabilization, cells activate several mechanisms for lysosomal membrane repair, including ESCRT proteins, phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P)-dependent lipid transfer from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and conjugation of ATG8 family proteins to single membranes (CASM). The interplay between these pathways and the regulation of the lipid transfer machinery remain incompletely understood. Here we show that phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P)-containing ER domains play a major role in lysosomal membrane repair. PI3P is formed on lysosome-proximal ER domains by the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase PIK3C3/VPS34 in response to membrane damage, and inhibition or depletion of PIK3C3 inhibits lysosome repair. Mechanistically, the ATPase DFCP1/ZFYVE1 accumulates on lysosome-proximal ER domains by its PI3P binding, triggered by Ca2+ efflux from lysosomes and requiring the ULK1 kinase complex and ER proteins of the VAP family. Downstream of CASM, PI4P, ESCRTs and PI3P, DFCP1 promotes focal accumulation of the lipid channel VPS13C on ER domains proximal to damaged lysosomes to promote their repair. The function and dynamics of DFCP1 depend on its ability to bind and hydrolyse ATP, and absence of DFCP1 compromises cellular resistance to vacuolar damage induced by Listeria monocytogenes. We conclude that DFCP1 mediates concentration of the ER-associated lipid transport machinery at damaged lysosomes to promote their sealing in response to Ca2+ flux and PIK3C3 activation.Subject terms: Lysosomes, Macroautophagy

Main

Lysosomes are the cell’s main stations for degradation of macromolecules, particles and pathogens, and they also serve as hubs for metabolic regulation1,2. As lysosomes contain lipases and other molecules that can potentially cause membrane damage, and as they accumulate endocytosed molecules, particles and pathogens, their membrane is prone to damage. Such damage can activate cell death and inflammatory signalling by several alternative pathways, and protective cellular mechanisms have therefore evolved to limit the harmful consequences of lysosome destabilization, namely lysosomal membrane repair and degradation of damaged lysosomes by lysophagy3,4.

Although discovered relatively recently5,6, lysosomal membrane repair has gained considerable attention as a notable mechanism in cellular homeostasis with relevance to infections, ageing, neurodegenerative diseases and cancer3,4. To date, several pathways of lysosome repair have been identified5–11, including the endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT)5,6, conjugation of ATG8 proteins to single membranes (CASM)10–13, and phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P) triggered phospholipid and cholesterol transport from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)14,15. Phospholipid and cholesterol transport is mediated by ORP family proteins that form ER–lysosome contact sites through binding to VAP proteins in the ER membrane and PI4P in the lysosome membrane14,15. The lipid channel proteins ATG2A/B, VPS13C and LYVAC are also recruited to mediate high-capacity phospholipid transport from ER to damaged or osmotically stressed lysosomes14,16,17.

While ESCRT recruitment, CASM and the involvement of PI4P and ER–lysosome contact sites in lysosomal repair have been well documented, their interrelationships have been incompletely characterized, and the mechanisms that control lipid transport are not known. In the present study we show that phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P), produced by the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase PIK3C3, is formed on characteristic ER domains after lysosome damage, triggered by Ca2+ release. ER-associated PI3P causes recruitment of the double FYVE domain containing protein DFCP1/ZFYVE1, which has previously been implicated in autophagosome biogenesis18. This facilitates lysosome repair after both chemically and pathogen-induced damage by promoting focal accumulation of the ER-associated lipid transport machinery at damaged lysosomes.

Results

PI3P is formed on ER domains in response to lysosome damage

Because phosphoinositides are important regulators of both cellular membrane dynamics19 and formation of membrane contact sites20 (MCSs), we hypothesized that phosphoinositides at the ER might play a role in the lysosomal damage response. PI4P is formed rapidly on lysosome membranes by PI4K2A in response to their damage14,15. We addressed the possibility that PI3P production on the ER could also play a role in the lysosomal damage response although no increase in PI3P has been detected with the standard PI3P probe 2xFYVEHrs (refs. 15,21) (Extended Data Fig. 1a,b and Supplementary Video 1). As this probe requires a certain density of PI3P molecules in the membrane to detect PI3P, it will not detect low PI3P levels. Previous studies have shown that very low levels of phosphoinositides, including PI3P, can be detected by probes that contain an additional membrane targeting domain that promotes coincidence detection18,22. To investigate whether PI3P is formed on ER membranes after lysosome damage, we therefore used an ER-targeted PI3P probe, GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1, which has previously been used to detect PI3P formed on ER domains that promote autophagosome biogenesis18.

Live-cell imaging of untreated U2OS cells expressing this probe showed faint labelling in reticular ER-like structures as well as on vesicle-like structures, probably reflecting some recruitment to PI3P on PI3P-rich endosomes. Of note, however, when a low concentration (250 µM) of LLOMe (a compound that causes rapid and specific damage of lysosome membranes23,24) was added to the cells, tubular and ring-shaped membrane domains were strongly detected with the PI3P probe within minutes after lysosome damage (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Video 2). To clarify the origin of these domains, we performed triple-labelling live-fluorescence microscopy with U2OS cells stably expressing GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1, and coexpressing the ER marker mCherry–VAPB, and the lysosome marker LysoTracker. This imaging showed that the PI3P probe emerged on VAP-B-containing ER domains shortly after addition of LLOMe to the cells, and in multiple events, these domains seemed to wrap around lysosomes (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Video 3).

To analyse in detail the relationship between membrane damage, PI3P formation and ER mobilization, we monitored single lysosomes by live microscopy and correlated loss of LysoTracker fluorescence (a sign of membrane damage) with acquisition of GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1 and mCherry–VAPB. Loss of LysoTracker fluorescence was accompanied by acute accumulation of GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1 and mCherry–VAPB (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Video 4). These results demonstrate a tight temporal coupling between lysosome damage, PI3P formation and ER mobilization.

PI3P formed by PIK3C3 is required for repair of damaged lysosomes

Although PI3P can be formed on cellular membranes by several mechanisms, the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase PIK3C3/VPS34 is responsible for production of most of this phosphoinositide19,25. Under basal conditions, formation of PI3P on ER domains was completely abolished by a specific inhibitor of PIK3C3, SAR405 (ref. 26) (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d and Supplementary Video 5). More notably, SAR405 strongly reduced the LLOMe-induced formation of PI3P on ER domains (Fig. 1d,e and Supplementary Video 6). These findings indicate that PI3P is rapidly generated by PIK3C3 at ER sites in response to lysosomal damage.

The accumulation of PI3P on lysosome-contacting ER domains in response to lysosome damage raised the question whether PI3P is required for lysosome repair. To address this, we used the well-established LysoTracker recovery assay, which measures regain of LysoTracker fluorescence in lysosomes following recovery after LLOMe-induced lysosome damage5,6. As expected, lysosomes in HeLa cells rapidly lost lysosomal LysoTracker fluorescence after addition of LLOMe, which was rapidly recovered as a sign of lysosome repair. Of note, however, when cells were treated with LLOMe and the PIK3C3 inhibitor SAR405, LysoTracker recovery was strongly inhibited (Fig. 1f, Extended Data Fig. 1e and Supplementary Video 7a–c). A similar result was obtained when PIK3C3 was depleted by siRNA (Fig. 1g,h and Extended Data Fig. 1f). This indicates that PIK3C3 is required for lysosomal membrane repair.

PIK3C3 is activated by Ca2+ and ULK1 in response to lysosome damage

The above results raised the question of how PIK3C3 is activated upon lysosome damage. Previous studies have shown that Ca2+ leakage from damaged lysosomes is a major trigger of the lysosomal damage response5,7,14, and we therefore hypothesized that Ca2+ released from damaged lysosomes might trigger PIK3C3 activation. To address this, we treated cells expressing the GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1 probe with the Ca2+ chelator BAPTA-AM and monitored its effect after LLOMe treatment. As shown in Fig. 1i,j, GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1 recruitment in response to LLOMe was strongly inhibited by BAPTA-AM, indicating that Ca2+ is indeed a trigger of PIK3C3 activation upon lysosome damage. During autophagy induction, PIK3C3 is activated by the ULK1 kinase complex downstream of Ca2+ release from the ER27–29, and we therefore asked whether ULK1 is involved in the lysosomal damage response. Notably, the specific ULK1 inhibitor MRT68921 strongly inhibited GFP-ER-FYVEWDFY1 recruitment upon LLOMe treatment (Fig. 1k,l), and we therefore propose that Ca2+ release from damaged lysosomes triggers ULK1 to activate PIK3C3 on ER membranes.

DFCP1-containing ER domains respond to lysosomal membrane damage

A protein that has been previously shown to control the dynamics of ER domains is DFCP1, an ATPase that accumulates in characteristic ER domains, omegasomes, in response to amino acid starvation18,30. DFCP1 contains an ER-targeting domain and two non-canonical FYVE domains, which bind weakly but specifically to PI3P31. To probe whether DFCP1 responds to lysosome damage, we treated U2OS cells with LLOMe and used live-fluorescence microscopy to monitor the dynamics of monomeric NeonGreen (mNG)-tagged DFCP1 (ref. 30). At a low (250 µM) LLOMe concentration, we observed a noteworthy redistribution of mNG-DFCP1 from discrete puncta into larger structures that displayed highly dynamic behaviour, including rapid morphological rearrangements and recurrent fusion and fission events captured by fast live-cell imaging (Fig. 2a,b, Supplementary Video 8 and Extended Data Fig. 2a).

The recruitment dynamics were compared with those of the ESCRT protein CHMP4B and found to be within a similar time scale of a few minutes after LLOMe addition, indicating that DFCP1-containing ER domains respond rapidly to lysosome damage (Fig. 2c,d). However, depletion of DFCP1 did not affect CHMP4B recruitment at either 5 or 15 min following lysosomal damage, suggesting that DFCP1 does not play a major role in the initial membrane resilience response after damage (Fig. 2e,f and Extended Data Fig. 2b). The DFCP1 response occurred at LLOMe concentrations as low as 100 µM (Extended Data Fig. 2c) and was also observed with other compounds that cause lysosome damage, including the lysosomotropic agent glycyl-L-phenylalanine 2-naphthylamide (GPN; Extended Data Fig. 2d), the amphiphilic antihistamine ebastine (Extended Data Fig. 2e) and the antipsychotic drug chlorpromazine (Extended Data Fig. 2f). This indicates that DFCP1-containing ER domains respond dynamically to several types of stressors. In addition, DFCP1 response to lysosomal damage coincided spatially with the VAPB-positive ER domains and occurred in regions where LysoTracker fluorescence was lost, confirming that DFCP1 accumulation is tightly coupled to sites of lysosomal damage (Fig. 2g).

The colocalization of mNG-DFCP1 with ER and lysosomes was analysed by measuring the average intensity profile of DFCP1, ER and lysosomes as a function of distance from the interface of DFCP1 puncta (Extended Data Fig. 2g,h). Intensities were normalized to reveal variations from locally expected intensity, taking the cell geometry and distance from the nucleus into account (Methods and Extended Data Fig. 2i). Registered DFCP1 punctum together with the corresponding intensity profile across its interface, as well as the surrounding ER and lysosomes with their respective intensity profile relative to the DFCP1 punctum interface, are shown in Extended Data Fig. 2j. Quantitative analysis revealed weak colocalization of DFCP1 with both the ER and lysosomes in untreated cells. Following LLOMe treatment, colocalization between DFCP1 and the ER increased to approximately twofold relative to control cells. Notably, colocalization with lysosomes increased more prominently, reaching approximately threefold enrichment at the DFCP1 puncta interface and up to 4.6-fold enrichment at the centre of larger DFCP1-positive structures (Fig. 2h). Together, these analyses confirm persistent association of DFCP1 with ER membranes under all conditions while demonstrating enhanced accumulation of ER-associated DFCP1 at damaged lysosomes following LLOMe treatment.

By lattice structured illumination live-cell microscopy we monitored the distribution of DFCP1-positive ER microdomains relative to lysosomes in LLOMe-treated cells, using LGALS3 as a marker of damaged lysosomes. This analysis showed that the DFCP1-positive ER microdomains did not wrap around entire lysosomes. Instead, they encircled parts of the damaged lysosomes in a highly dynamic manner (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Video 9). In addition, preventing LLOMe-induced lysosomal membrane damage by inhibiting its cathepsin C-dependent activation with E64D24 completely abolished DFCP1 accumulation, demonstrating that DFCP1 specifically responds to lysosomal damage (Extended Data Fig. 2k).

ER binding and VAP family proteins are required for DFCP1 response to lysosome damage

Cryo-electron tomography demonstrated that LLOMe-treated lysosomes localize in close proximity to the ER with a distance between the organelles of 20–25 nm, closely matching the length of the ATG2 and VPS13C lipid channels involved in ER-mediated lysosome repair (Extended Data Fig. 3a–d and Supplementary Table 1). Quantitative analyses of tomograms showed that ER–lysosome contacts increased after LLOMe treatment15,24. Upon induction of lysosomal damage, DFCP1 rapidly accumulated around LAMP1-positive structures, indicating that its response coincides with lysosome disruption (Fig. 3a, Extended Data Fig. 3e and Supplementary Video 10). Of note, the LLOMe-induced DFCP1-positive structures were also tightly associated with the ER (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Video 11), suggesting that DFCP1 accumulation during damage occurs at ER–lysosome MCSs (Fig. 3c). MCSs between lysosomes and ER contain ER-bound VAP proteins as an essential constituent15. To investigate whether VAP proteins are required for DFCP1 mobilization, we monitored mNG-DFCP1 recruitment after LLOMe treatment in HeLa cells lacking VAP-A and VAP-B32. Recruitment of mEGFP-DFCP1 was strongly reduced in the VAP-A-VAP-B double KO cells (Fig. 3d,e), and was also reduced by siRNA-mediated depletion of a third VAP family member, MOSPD2 (ref. 33) (Fig. 3f,g and Extended Data Fig. 3f). This indicates that VAP family proteins are required for DFCP1 mobilization in response to lysosomal damage. A DFCP1 construct lacking the ER-binding region, mEGFP-DFCP1ΔER, failed to respond after LLOMe treatment even in the presence of VAP family proteins (Fig. 3h,i and Supplementary Video 12), confirming that ER binding is required for the relocalization of DFCP1 after lysosome damage.

Ca2+, ULK1 and PI3P trigger the DFCP1 response to lysosome damage

Previous studies of autophagy have shown that DFCP1 responds to cytosolic Ca2+ (ref. 34), and we found that PI3P formation on ER membranes upon lysosome damage is activated by Ca2+ release. We therefore monitored whether chelation of cytosolic Ca2+ with BAPTA-AM5 would affect DFCP1 redistribution to LAMP1-containing lysosomes after LLOMe treatment. Indeed, treatment with BAPTA-AM completely abolished DFCP1 redistribution after LLOMe-induced lysosome damage (Fig. 4a–c). Of note, the ESCRT-III protein CHMP4B was recruited to damaged lysosomes in response to LLOMe even in the presence of BAPTA-AM, indicating that ESCRT recruitment is less sensitive to Ca2+ than DFCP1. To exclude the possibility that the effect of BAPTA-AM resulted from a general inhibition of autophagy35, we confirmed that BAPTA-AM treatment did not affect autophagic activity under these experimental conditions, indicating that its effect on DFCP1 redistribution is specific to lysosomal damage (Extended Data Fig. 3g,h).

The lysosomal Ca2+ channel TRPML1/MCOLN1 has been shown to respond to lysosome damage5,36, and we therefore monitored whether an inhibitor of this channel, ML-SI3 (ref. 37), would affect DFCP1 redistribution after LLOMe treatment. This was found to be the case (Fig. 4d,e and Supplementary Video 13), indicating that the Ca2+ flux that triggers DFCP1 recruitment after lysosome damage at least partially originates from TRPML1 channels.

DFCP1 has been found to form multiple puncta after incubation of cells with Thapsigargin, a compound that causes accumulation of cytosolic Ca2+ due to inhibition of the sarcoplasmic reticulum/ER Ca2+ ATPase38,39. Following lysosomal damage, cells co-treated with Thapsigargin and LLOMe exhibited increased lysosomal resilience compared with cells treated with LLOMe alone, as indicated by LysoTracker staining (Extended Data Fig. 3i), suggesting that elevated intracellular Ca2+ promotes lysosomal membrane resilience. Interestingly, even in the presence of Thapsigargin, LLOMe treatment caused a strong increase in DFCP1 profiles (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Video 14), supporting the notion that Ca2+ release from lysosomes triggers DFCP1 redistribution. A DFCP1 mutant lacking the ER-binding domain did not show any foci neither in response to Thapsigargin nor LLOMe (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Video 15). This shows that the DFCP1 foci triggered by Ca2+ and lysosome damage represent ER-bound DFCP1.

Because localization of DFCP1 to ER membranes during autophagy induction has been shown to require its binding to PI3P18, we also investigated LLOMe-induced DFCP1 redistribution in the absence and presence of the PIK3C3 inhibitor SAR405. Fluorescence microscopy showed that DFCP1 puncta were completely abolished in the presence of SAR405 (Fig. 4h,i and Supplementary Video 16). Likewise, DFCP1 constructs harbouring mutations in the FYVE domains that prevent PI3P binding18 or that completely lack both FYVE domains failed to respond to lysosome damage (Fig. 4j,k). Furthermore, transient depletion of PIK3C3 significantly reduced DFCP1 recruitment following LLOMe-induced damage (Extended Data Fig. 3j). These results indicate that PIK3C3-mediated formation of PI3P on ER domains causes DFCP1 recruitment in response to lysosome damage.

As we found that PIK3C3-mediated PI3P formation depended on ULK1, we addressed whether ULK1 is also required for DFCP1 mobilization after lysosome damage. Both the ULK1 inhibitor MRT68921 and siRNA against the ULK1 complex component FIP200 strongly inhibited DFCP1 mobilization after LLOMe treatment (Fig. 4l–n and Extended Data Fig. 3k,l). We conclude that the DFCP1 responds to lysosome is activated by PIK3C3 in response to Ca2+ efflux and ULK1 activation.

DFCP1 mediates lysosomal membrane repair in an ATP-dependent manner

The rapid mobilization of DFCP1-containing ER microdomains close to lysosomes in response to their damage suggested the possibility that DFCP1 could be involved in membrane repair. To measure the importance of DFCP1 for lysosomal membrane repair, we used the LysoTracker recovery assay5,6. These analyses showed that LysoTracker recovery was impaired in DFCP1 knockout (KO) cells30 compared with wild-type (WT) cells (Fig. 5a,b and Supplementary Video 17). To investigate whether ER binding of DFCP1 is required for its function in lysosomal membrane repair, we stably expressed either WT DFCP1 or DFCP1ΔER in DFCP1-KO cells and assessed lysosomal repair following LLOMe-induced damage using the LysoTracker recovery assay. While cells expressing WT DFCP1 efficiently restored lysosomal integrity after LLOMe treatment, lysosomal repair was strongly impaired in cells expressing DFCP1ΔER (Fig. 5c, Extended Data Fig. 4a and Supplementary Video 18). These results demonstrate that ER binding of DFCP1 is essential for its role in mediating lysosomal membrane repair and support a functional requirement for ER association in the DFCP1-dependent repair process. As an additional assay for membrane repair we also stained WT and DFCP1-KO cells with antibodies against the galectin LGALS3/GAL3, which is known to be recruited to damaged lysosome membranes6,40. In agreement with the LysoTracker recovery data, we detected much stronger LGALS3 staining in lysosomes of DFCP1-KO cells than in WT cells after recovery periods of one or two hours after LLOMe-mediated lysosome damage (Fig. 5d,e).

Our previous work has shown that the NPC1 inhibitor U18666A, which causes accumulation of cholesterol in the lysosome membrane, makes the lysosomes partially refractory to lysosome damage15. We now found that U18666A treatment before LLOMe inhibited mobilization of DFCP1 around lysosomes (Fig. 5f,g). Collectively, these analyses demonstrate that DFCP1 is required for efficient lysosomal membrane repair.

To understand whether the ability of DFCP1 to bind and hydrolyse ATP is important for its function in lysosome repair, we took advantage of previously described mutants that affect its ATP-binding and ATPase activity30. Upon damage, these mutants showed increased persistence at damage sites compared with WT DFCP1 (Fig. 5h), without affecting CHMP4B recruitment or overall LAMP1 (Fig. 5i and Extended Data Fig. 4b). While DFCP1-KO cells reconstituted with WT DFCP1 showed normal lysosomal LysoTracker recovery after LLOMe treatment, cells reconstituted with the ATP-binding defective K193A mutant or the ATPase-defective T189A mutant failed to rescue LysoTracker recovery (Fig. 5j and Extended Data Fig. 4c). Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP) analysis further showed that both ATPase mutants displayed reduced fluorescence recovery relative to WT DFCP1 15 min after LLOMe treatment, indicating reduced dynamics at damage sites (Fig. 5k and Extended Data Fig. 4d). This demonstrates that the ability of DFCP1 to bind and hydrolyse ATP is central to its function in lysosomal membrane repair.

DFCP1 promotes focal accumulation of VPS13C at damaged lysosomes

Lipid channel proteins mediate high-capacity lipid transfer between membranes41, and the lipid channels ATG2 and VPS13C have been found to be required for lysosomal membrane repair14,16. To monitor whether DFCP1 regulates recruitment or organization of these proteins, we used cells expressing Halo-ATG2 (ref. 42) or VPS13C-Halo16 and monitored their response to LLOMe by live spinning-disk fluorescence microscopy. Consistent with previous reports, both Halo-ATG2A and VPS13C-Halo were recruited in response to LLOMe-induced lysosomal damage. Strikingly, VPS13C-Halo recruitment and DFCP1 mobilization peaked at the same time whereas Halo-ATG2A was recruited several minutes later (Fig. 6a). The strong correlation between VPS13C recruitment and DFCP1 mobilization prompted us to monitor the colocalization of these proteins after lysosome damage. Live-cell microscopy showed that VPS13C-Halo accumulated uniformly in response to LLOMe, and this was followed by proximal recruitment of DFCP1 as sharply defined puncta that extended to form ring- or cup-shaped microdomains. VPS13C then accumulated strongly into these microdomains and remained there for a few minutes until both DFCP1 and VPS13C became more diffuse (Fig. 6b,c, Extended Data Fig. 5a and Supplementary Video 19). To address whether VPS13C accumulation into microdomains depends on DFCP1, we studied VPS13C-Halo localization in LLOMe-treated WT and DFCP1-KO U2OS cells. Notably, in cells lacking DFCP1, VPS13C-Halo failed to cluster into microdomains in response to LLOMe and instead was recruited diffusely around lysosomes (Fig. 6d–f and Supplementary Video 20). This indicates that DFCP1 mediates focal accumulation of VPS13C lipid channels at ER microdomains proximal to damaged lysosomes.

Relationship of DFCP1 to other components of the lysosomal damage response

The DFCP1 redistribution after lysosome damage raised the question whether it is linked to ESCRT- or PI4P-mediated lysosome repair5,6,14,15. To monitor whether ESCRT recruitment on lysosomes depends on DFCP1, we monitored whether the ESCRT scaffold ALIX is recruited to LLOMe-treated lysosomes in the absence of DFCP1. We detected no effect on ALIX recruitment in DFCP1-KO cells30 when compared with WT cells, indicating that ESCRT recruitment is independent of DFCP1 (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). Likewise, we observed no effect on PI4P formation on damaged lysosomes, monitored with SidM as a probe for PI4P43 (Extended Data Fig. 5d). To investigate whether the DFCP1 response to lysosome damage requires ESCRT proteins or PI4P, we monitored DFCP1 dynamics in cells treated with siRNAs against the ESCRT proteins TSG101 and ALIX5,6, or in the knockout of PI4K2A, the phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase responsible for PI4P formation on damaged lysosomes14,15. Neither the knockdown of ESCRT nor the knockout of PI4K2A resulted in any effect on DFCP1 mobilization after LLOMe treatment (Extended Data Fig. 5e–h). These results indicate that the response of DFCP1 to lysosomal damage is independent of ESCRTs and PI4P.

To investigate the hypothesis that DFCP1 is part of the ER-mediated lipid transport response to lysosome damage, we performed epistasis-type experiments, which involved siRNA-mediated knockdown of DFCP1 in HeLa cells with KO of VAP-A/B or PI4K2A. Using the LysoTracker recovery assay we found that lysosome repair was inhibited in PI4K2A cells as shown previously14,15 and in VAP-A/B KO cells. However, there was no additive effect of knocking down DFCP1 in cells lacking PI4K2A or VAP-A/B (Fig. 6g,h and Extended Data Fig. 6a–d). This supports the idea that DFCP1 operates in the same pathway as PI4K2A and VAP-A/B (ER-mediated lipid transport).

To understand the sequence of events in the lysosomal damage response, we used spinning-disk microscopy of U2OS cells expressing fluorescently tagged components of the repair machinery. With frame rates as frequent as 10 s, the numbers of fluorescent puncta after LLOMe addition were measured and quantified (Fig. 6i). Of note, the ATG8 family protein GABARAP showed the fastest response, with a detectable increase already 1 min after LLOMe addition. The next fastest response was observed with the PI4P probe SidM, which increased 2 min after LLOMe, and this was followed by the ESCRT protein ALIX after 4 min and DFCP1 after 6 min. Notably, the ATG8 family protein LC3B showed the slowest response with a detectable increase 8 min after LLOMe addition. These results are consistent with the idea that CASM with GABARAP triggers ESCRT recruitment and that PI4P-dependent ER–lysosome MCSs precede DFCP1 recruitment10.

DFCP1 depletion causes increased lysophagy

Previous work from our laboratory has shown that KO of DFCP1 reduces the ability of cells to perform several types of selective autophagy, including mitophagy and aggrephagy30. We therefore asked whether DFCP1 would also be involved in lysophagy. To probe the relationship between DFCP1 puncta during autophagy induction and lysosome damage, we treated cells with the mTORC1 inhibitor Torin1, which is known to induce bulk autophagy44, and measured the number of DFCP1 puncta. As expected, Torin1 increased the number of DFCP1 puncta at the start of imaging (time point 0). Upon subsequent addition of LLOMe, DFCP1 puncta continued to rise over time (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Video 21). The magnitude of the DFCP1 increase after LLOMe in Torin1-treated cells was similar to the response observed with LLOMe alone. This indicates that despite the elevated baseline of DFCP1 puncta under Torin1 treatment, cells still mount an additional DFCP1 response to lysosomal damage, suggesting the existence of a DFCP1 pool that operates independently of its canonical role.

Loss of DFCP1 exacerbates vacuole damage induced by Listeriamonocytogenes

Several internalized pathogens escape phagolysosomal degradation by entering into cytosol from bacterial vacuoles46. One of these is the gram-positive bacterium Listeria monocytogenes, which produces a toxin that causes acute damage of the bacterial vacuole membrane47. We investigated if DFCP1 protects against L. monocytogenes-induced vacuolar damage. To monitor if DFCP1 is recruited to L. monocytogenes vacuoles, mNG-DFCP1-expressing U2OS cells were infected with L. monocytogenes, and eventual colocalization between the internalized bacteria and mNG-DFCP1 was studied by confocal fluorescence microscopy. The microscopy showed a strong accumulation of mNG-DFCP1 to the bacteria-containing vacuoles, and this did not occur with a L. monocytogenes mutant unable to damage vacuole membranes, Δhly (Fig. 8a–c). This demonstrates that DFCP1-containing ER microdomains respond to L. monocytogenes infection.

Discussion

While previous work has shown that PI4K2A and its product PI4P promote lysosomal membrane repair via recruitment of lipid transfer proteins14,15, this study has revealed that PIK3C3 and PI3P also play an important role. PI3P formed on ER domains functions to recruit DFCP1, which promotes lysosome repair in response to both chemical damage and microbial insults. DFCP1 accumulates on microdomains of the ER, in response to lysosome damage, whose dynamics are regulated by DFCP1’s ability to bind and hydrolyse ATP. The lipid channel VPS13C, which is initially recruited uniformly at damaged lysosomes, accumulates into these microdomains, and we propose that DFCP1 mediates lysosome repair by concentrating the lipid transport machinery at ER–lysosome contact sites where high lipid transport is required (Fig. 8f).

Sensing of lysosomal membrane damage seems to be tightly linked to leakage of Ca2+ from the lysosome lumen to the cytosol, as reported for activation of repair mechanisms involving ESCRT proteins5, ER-mediated lipid transfer14, sphingomyelinase activation7 and annexin-mediated repair8. We discovered that DFCP1 response to lysosome damage is highly Ca2+ dependent and is much more sensitive than ESCRT recruitment to Ca2+ chelation. The sensitivity of DFCP1 mobilization to inhibition of the lysosomal Ca2+ channel TRPML1 indicates that local Ca2+ flux from the damaged lysosome, promoted by TRPML1 activation, is a major trigger of the DFCP1 response. This could explain the local recruitment of DFCP1 at ER microdomains proximal to damaged lysosomes. We found that elevated Ca2+ levels after lysosome damage activate PIK3C3 via the protein kinase ULK1, similar to what has been reported for autophagy induction where Ca2+ flux from the ER triggers PIK3C3 via ULK1 (refs. 27–29).

DFCP1-containing ER domains have previously been shown to be associated with phagophores and lipid droplets in a PI3P-dependent manner18,30,49–51. In autophagy, DFCP1-driven omegasomes have been proposed to provide lipids to the growing phagophore30,52. In lipid droplet homeostasis, DFCP1-positive ER membranes provide platforms for fusion of small lipid droplets into larger ones to promote lipid droplet expansion53, and DFCP1 also contributes to lipid droplet growth by inhibiting the activity of a lipid droplet-metabolizing triglyceride lipase54. With the additional function for DFCP1 in lysosome repair, a pattern emerges that DFCP1 controls focal ER domains that can associate with other cellular structures to control their biogenesis, size or integrity. Characterizing the structure of membrane-bound DFCP1 and the function of its ATPase domain will be essential for understanding the general function for DFCP1 in ER-associated processes.

In addition to PI3P and Ca2+, VAP family proteins are also required for the DFCP1 response to lysosome damage. We found that the DFCP1 response was sensitive to double knockout of VAP-A and VAP-B but also to knockdown of a third VAP family member, MOSPD2. The involvement of VAP family proteins is interesting because these proteins have already been shown to mediate MCS formation between the ER and damaged lysosomes for lipid transfer15. They have also been found to interact with ULK1 and FIP200 (ref. 55), raising the possibility that, after lysosome damage and subsequent formation of lysosome–ER contacts, VAP-mediated recruitment of the ULK1 complex might create a local environment favourable for PIK3C3 activation and thereby for DFCP1 recruitment. In future studies it will be pertinent to investigate the relationships between MCS formation and DFCP1 function in membrane repair.

The involvement of DFCP1 in selective autophagy of mitochondria and protein aggregates30 made us initially hypothesize that its depletion would inhibit lysophagy. Instead, we detected a profound increase in lysophagy in DFCP1-KO cells in response to LLOMe. This can probably be explained as a response to faulty lysosomal membrane repair in the absence of DFCP1. Consistent with this notion, DFCP1, although facilitating mitophagy and aggrephagy, is not essential for neither bulk nor selective autophagy18,30, suggesting that additional ER proteins have overlapping functions in ER remodelling during autophagy.

Our detailed live microscopy revealed notable temporal differences between the various components of the lysosomal damage response, with the CASM component GABARAP responding first, followed by PI4P, ESCRT and DFCP1. We also found that VAPB responds earlier to lysosome damage than DFCP1, which is consistent with the finding that VPS13C, which is a binding partner of VAP proteins56, is also recruited before DFCP1. Two proteins that responded later than DFCP1 were LC3B and ATG2. This is noteworthy as both these proteins are important for lysophagy40,57. Further studies will be needed to clarify whether differential CASM and lipid transport machineries mediate lysosome repair and lysophagy.

Lysosomal membrane damage plays important roles in transmission of neurotoxic protein aggregates58,59 and in the cytosolic entry of intracellular pathogens9,48,60. In agreement with this we found that DFCP1-containing ER microdomains are recruited to the vacuoles of pathogenic L. monocytogenes and that KO of DFCP1 reduces bacterial escape from vacuoles. It will now be interesting to investigate the importance of DFCP1-mediated lysosomal membrane repair in neurodegenerative diseases and infections.

FRAP microscopy

For FRAP experiments, U2OS-DFCP1-KO cells stably expressing mNG-DFCP1, mNG-DFCP1T189A and mNG-DFCP1K193A cells were seeded on 35-mm MatTek dishes (MatTek corporation, #P35G-1.5-20-C) and incubated overnight. Cells were then treated with DMSO or LLOMe for indicated time point. The experiment was performed on a Zeiss LSM880 Airyscan FAST confocal microscope equipped with a ×63 oil-immersion objective (Plan-Apochromat ×63/1.40 NA) at 37 °C/ 5% CO2. Focus was maintained using the definite focus 2 system and images were acquired using Zeiss Zen software at one image per second. Three pre-bleach images were acquired before bleaching individual mNG-DFCP1-positive structures at 100% laser intensity (30 iterations).

FRAP analysis was carried out following the procedure described previously65 using Fiji66. For each acquisition, mean fluorescence intensity was measured within three regions of interest (ROIs): the bleached region, a non-bleached reference vesicle within the same cell and a cell-free background region. After subtraction of background intensity from both bleached and non-bleached ROI, a ratio of bleached to non-bleached intensity was calculated for each time point to correct for any fluorescence loss occurred during image acquisition. The resulting value was normalized to the mean pre-bleach intensity, such that the pre-bleach baseline was set to 1 across all conditions.

Pulse-chasable reporter processing assay for lysosomal autophagic flux (Lysoflux assay) and global autophagic flux

Monitoring of lysosomal autophagic flux was performed using a pulse-chasable reporter processing assay as previously described45. In brief, the LGALS3 gene was fused to an EGFP–HaloTag transgene in a Gateway-compatible pENTR vector using standard molecular biology methods. The resulting lentiviral construct, pLenti-PGK-Blast-EGFP-HaloTag-LGALS3, was generated by Gateway LR recombination and subsequently used to establish stable cell lines, as detailed earlier.

For the assay, cells stably expressing EGFP-HaloTag-LGALS3 were incubated in DMEM (10% FBS, 100 U ml−1 penicillin and 100 μg ml−1 streptomycin) containing 100 nM TMR-conjugated Halo ligand (Promega, G8251) for 30 min. Cells were then treated with DMEM supplemented with 250 μM LLOMe for 0 or 15 min, or 2 h, followed by washing and incubation with fresh DMEM for 4 h. Afterward, cells were washed twice with PBS, scraped and centrifuged at 2,000g for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris, pH 7.5, 137 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM CaCl2 and 1% NP-40) supplemented with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentration was quantified using a BCA assay.

Global autophagic flux was assessed using the same pulse–chase strategy in U2OS WT and DFCP1-KO cells stably expressing HaloTag7-LC3B.

Band detection was also performed using the ChemiDoc XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Listeria monocytogenes infection of U2OS cells

U2OS cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well onto 18-well chamber slides (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, without antibiotics) and incubated overnight at 37 °C in 5% CO2. L. monocytogenes (WT and Δhly mutant) was cultured overnight at 37 °C with shaking (220 rpm) in brain heart infusion (BHI) broth. Bacteria were collected by centrifugation at 300g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in antibiotic-free DMEM. The optical density (OD) of the bacterial suspension was measured at 600 nm, with an OD600 of 0.1 corresponding to approximately 1 × 107 colony-forming units per ml. Infections were performed at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 for WT L. monocytogenes, and MOI 10 for the Δhly mutant, which replicates more slowly. To facilitate bacterial contact and uptake, cells were centrifuged at 800g for 5 min at room temperature, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were then washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular bacteria and subsequently incubated in fresh DMEM for an additional 4 h before downstream analysis.

Environmental control was provided by a heated stage and an objective heater (20-20 Technologies). Images were deconvolved using softWoRx software and processed in ImageJ/Fiji. Additional fast live-cell imaging was performed on a Zeiss Elyra 7 Lattice-SIM microscope equipped with a Plan-Apochromat ×40/1.4 NA Oil objective and a ×1.6 tube lens, using a Hamamatsu ORCA-Fusion BT sCMOS camera. mNeonGreen and mAzamiGreen were excited at 488 nm (3% laser power); emission was collected in the 495–550 nm band (FS LBF 405/488/561/642 beam splitter) at 100 ms exposure per frame (frame interval 531.40 ms; lateral pixel size, 0.099 µm). Time-lapse acquisitions comprised 80 frames (~215 s total) with z-stacks of five optical sections (z-step, 0.360 µm; total z-range, 1.44 µm). All live-cell imaging was performed at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Structured illumination images were processed using the SIM2 algorithm with weak auto-sharpness in Zen Blue v.3.12 (Carl Zeiss).

DFCP1 puncta quantification and correlation analysis

DFCP1 puncta and their colocalization with the ER and lysosomes were analysed using confocal z-stacks of U2OS-DFCP1-KO cells stably expressing mNG-DFCP1. Cells were immunostained with anti-LAMP1 (lysosome marker) and anti-Reticulon-4 (ER marker) under control conditions (no treatment) or after 15 min of LLOMe treatment. Nuclei were counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). For normalization steps, we first detected the cell outline via a low intensity threshold of the maximum projection in z of the ER staining. Further, we detected nuclei within the image via thresholding of a maximum projection in z of the DAPI signal. Combining these data, we assigned pixels within the mask of the cells (based on the ER stain) to individual cells based on distance to the closest nucleus. This analysis yielded a mask of each cell and a distance map indicating each pixel’s minimal distance to the cell nucleus.

For each segmented cell, we applied the cell mask to each slice of the confocal stack of the DFCP1, ER and lysosome channels and further analysed each cell individually. DFCP1 puncta were identified via thresholding of the confocal stack as those connected pixel that exceeded the intensity of the mean of the maximum projection of DFCP1 within the cell plus 3 × s.d. thereof. Puncta with a volume below 60 pixels were discarded. The cellular organization of ER and lysosomes is not homogeneous but shows typically brighter signal closer to the nucleus. To account for such systematic cellular occurring between DFCP1 puncta closer or further from the nucleus, we normalized all data by an intensity map representing the average intensity of a given channel at a given distance from the nucleus per z-slice. Utilizing the distance map of all pixels relative to the cell nucleus, we calculated the average intensity for each distance from the nucleus in a given channel and z-slice. These intensities are then transferred to the pixels with the respective distance to generate a three-dimensional normalization map capturing the average intensity distribution in the cell. Normalizing each voxel in the confocal stack by this intensity map, we arrived at an intensity scale where a value of 1 indicates that the local intensity is equal to the average intensity of all pixels in the same cell, in the same z-slice, and at the same distance from the nucleus. Values < 1 indicate lower-than-expected intensity, and values > 1 indicate higher-than-expected intensity. With this normalization at hand, we then extracted an image around the centre plane of each DFCP1 punctum in the normalized DFCP1, ER and lysosome channel. Next, we calculated the average intensity as a function of distance from the detected DFCP1 interface, where a distance of zero indicates the outline of the respective DFCP1 punctum, with negative values inside, positive values outside. The same was carried out for the corresponding ER and lysosome images to interrogate colocalization or lack thereof. The individual intensity profiles for each puncta from all cells within one experiment were then aligned and the median intensity across all puncta calculated.

EM grid seeding and cryo-FIB milling

Gold Quantifoil R2/2 200-mesh EM grids (EMS Q250-AR2) were glow-discharged (30 s, 25 mA) and coated with 0.01% poly-L-lysine (Sigma A-005-M). U2OS cells were trypsinized (0.25%, Thermo Fisher 25-200-056) and seeded at 20,000 cells per grid in an eight-chamber slide (Sigma PEZGS0816). After overnight recovery, cells were treated with 250 µM LLOMe for 20 min. Grids were washed in PBS, blotted with blotting paper and a Teflon sheet (force 10, 8 s) in a Vitrobot Mark IV (37 °C, 90% humidity) and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane. Grids were clipped into notched bases for cryo-focused ion beam (FIB) milling (Thermo Fisher).

Notched grids were loaded into an Aquilos 2 with integrated fluorescence (iFLM). After scanning electron microscopy screening, grids were sputter-coated (30 mA, 10 Pa, 15 s) and GIS-coated (1 min). Auto-transmission electron microscopy milling produced ~200 nm lamellae using stepwise currents: 0.3–0.5 nA to 3 µm, 100 pA to 1 µm, 50 pA to 500 nm and 30 pA to final thickness.

Cryo-electron tomography data acquisition and processing

Lamellae were transferred from the Aquilos to a 300 kV Titan G2 equipped with a Gatan Quantum K3 detector. Images were acquired in energy-filtered transmission electron microscopy mode (20 eV slit). Autogrids were loaded so FIB pre-tilt was perpendicular to the microscope tilt axis. Whole-grid and medium-magnification montages guided targeting. Tilt series were acquired using a dose-symmetric scheme starting from the 10–15° lamella pre-tilt with 3° increments in groups of two tilts67. Nominal defocus varied from –2 to –6 µm (0.25-µm steps). Total dose per series was ~120 e⁻ per Å (ref. 68). The conditions for data collection and processing are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Scipion was used to facilitate all downstream data processing68. In brief, MotionCor3 was used to motion correct tilt series and binning in Fourier space to the physical pixel resolution was applied during correction69. CTFfind5 was used for CTF estimation and AreTomo2 or Etomo was used for automatic or manual tilt series alignment and tomogram reconstruction70–72. Tomograms were denoised using Isonet2 (ref. 73) and a short 3DEM68 run to enhance visual contrast. Membranes were segmented by using MemBrain (v.2)72. Ribosomes were template matched with PyTOM74 using EMD-2938 (ref. 75) and back projected to the segmentation with ArtiaX76.

Image analysis and post-processing

Images were analysed in Fiji/ImageJ using custom Python scripts. In brief, images were filtered to remove noise, foci of different proteins were segmented by manual thresholding, and objects were then identified and scored by the ‘Analyse Particles’ function of ImageJ15. For live-cell imaging experiments, the value at the time of LLOMe or drug addition (time 0 min) was set to 1, and all subsequent measurements were normalized to this value. Manual thresholds were chosen to detect prominent LLOMe-induced structures; therefore, only spots above a certain intensity were scored. For live-cell videos, foci counts were normalized to the cell number or area (Figs. 1b,d, 3e,i,g, 4b,e–g,i, 5b,c,g and 7a and Extended Data Figs. 1d and 5c,h).

For LysoTracker recovery analysis fully automated analysis using NisElements software, detecting LysoTracker-positive bright puncta per time point, from live-cell imaging videos was applied. Intensity-based thresholding was used for segmentation of cell area. Identical settings for all conditions within one experiment (Figs. 1f,h, 5j and 6g,h and Extended Data Fig. 3i). The analysis of lysosome damage response of various markers based on immunofluorescence confocal microscopy images was fully automated by using NisElements software, detecting bright puncta of indicated marker. Intensity-based thresholding was used for segmentation of cell area and nuclei. Identical settings for all conditions within one experiment (Figs. 2f and 4n and Extended Data Figs. 3k, 4b and 5f). For the analysis of recruitment dynamics of various markers, the semi-automated analysis using NisElements software was applied, detecting bright puncta of indicated markers per cell (transient transfection) or per video consisting of several cells (stable cell lines). For transiently transfected cells, the mean intensity of the marker was measured in the whole cell to indicate the expression level, and the cells were then assigned to a dedicated analysis, set for low, medium or high expression. Identical selection criteria were used for all conditions. Intensity-based thresholding was used for segmentation of cell area or cell numbers were counted manually (Figs. 1j,l, 4k,m, 5h and 6a,e,i and Extended Data Fig. 5d). For the intensity measurement of ER-FYVE and HRS-2XFYVE probes associated with LAMP1, automated analysis using NisElements software was used. Mean fluorescence intensities of probes, LysoTracker or LAMP1 were measured over time in LAMP1-positive areas selected by threshold-based segmentation. Identical settings for all conditions within one experiment (Fig. 1e and Extended Data Fig. 1b). Individual tracking of lysosomes was performed as described previously77 (Figs. 1c,d and 6f).

Statistics and reproducibility

The number of individual experiments and the number of cells analysed are indicated in the figure legends. We tested our datasets for normal distribution and chose an appropriate test accordingly using GraphPad Prism v.10. No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. The statistical tests performed for each experiment are specified in the figure legends. P values are indicated for each experiment and P values <0.05 were regarded as significant. Data collection and analysis were not performed blind to the conditions of the experiments and no randomization was applied. Data were excluded only for technical reasons, including loss of focus, excessive photobleaching or phototoxicity during live-cell imaging experiments. To ensure the reproducibility of the data presented, experiments were performed by different researchers and different groups.
