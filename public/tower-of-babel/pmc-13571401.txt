Is infraocclusion always caused by ankylosis? Current perspectives on primary molar ankylosis: A systematic review
Authors: Masatoshi Otsugu, Hamada Masakazu, Fumikazu Tojo, Kazuhiko Nakano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ankylosed primary molars are relatively common during the mixed dentition period; however, ankylosis does not always coincide with infraocclusion. This systematic review assessed the clinical evidence regarding ankylosis of primary molars, including its etiology, diagnosis, and management, and discussed whether infraocclusion is primarily caused by ankylosis or instead reflects a broader disturbance of tooth eruption. A literature search was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines across three databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) on 25 April 2025 and updated on 3 July 2026. The risk of bias and methodological quality of the included studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Fourteen studies met the inclusion criteria. The person-level prevalence of infraoccluded primary molars, used as a surrogate for ankylosis, ranged from 1.3% to 24.8%. Histopathological and clinical studies indicated that not all infraoccluded primary molars exhibited evidence of ankylosis. Treatment strategies varied according to the severity of infraocclusion and the presence or absence of permanent successors. The available evidence remains limited and insufficient to determine the temporal and causal relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion. Further epidemiological, histopathological, and treatment-related studies are needed.

Introduction

Dental ankylosis is defined as an eruptive abnormality characterized by the direct fusion of cementum to alveolar bone [1], [2]. As root resorption progresses, the cementum may be resorbed and replaced by alveolar bone, resulting in direct continuity between bone and dentin, a characteristic feature of replacement resorption [1], [2]. It prevents normal development and eruption of the involved tooth and, consequently, the supporting alveolar bone growth [3]. An ankylosed tooth remains static despite continued eruption of the adjacent teeth, resulting in infraocclusion [4]. Although permanent teeth are rarely affected by ankylosis, ankylosed primary molars are relatively common during the mixed dentition period [3], [5].

Infraocclusion of primary molars is a dental anomaly characterized by the affected tooth being positioned below the occlusal plane of the adjacent teeth [5]. Historically, infraocclusion has been regarded as a clinical manifestation primarily caused by ankylosis [6]. However, ankylosis is not always identified histopathologically following the extraction of infraoccluded teeth, and conversely, histologically confirmed ankylosis does not invariably lead to infraocclusion [7]. Moreover, once complete ankylosis is established, spontaneous correction of infraocclusion is unlikely, and the severity and progression of infraocclusion may also be influenced by multiple factors, including craniofacial growth [8], hypodontia [2], and genetic predisposition [5]. Therefore, the conventional concept equating infraocclusion with ankylosis may require reconsideration, and a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying pathophysiology is warranted.

Comprehensive research on primary molar ankylosis remains limited [9]. Although case reports and small observational studies have described its diagnosis, clinical presentation, and natural course, systematic evaluations of clinical outcomes and management strategies remain scarce. Most previous studies have focused on epidemiology, patterns of infraocclusion, and effects on adjacent teeth and permanent successors, whereas evidence regarding treatment approaches remains limited.

Therefore, this systematic review aimed to synthesize the available literature on ankylosis of primary molars, with particular emphasis on clinical evidence related to its etiology, diagnosis, and management. It also addresses the key question of whether infraocclusion is primarily caused by ankylosis or instead reflects a broader disturbance of tooth eruption.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [10]. The review protocol was established by the authors before the literature search but was not prospectively registered or publicly available. A literature search was conducted across three electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) on 25 April 2025 using the following search terms: “ankylosis” AND “primary tooth” and “ankylosis” AND “deciduous tooth”. The results from both search strategies were combined before duplicate removal. The literature search was updated on 3 July 2026 before resubmission using the same search strategy. This systematic review was not prospectively registered, and no prespecified protocol was publicly available. During manuscript revision, the eligibility criteria were clarified to better reflect the review question, and all potentially eligible studies were reassessed using the clarified criteria.

Review question

This systematic review aimed to synthesize the current evidence regarding primary molar ankylosis. To comprehensively address this objective, the review was structured into three predefined domains: (1) epidemiology, including the prevalence and diagnostic characteristics of primary molar ankylosis; (2) histopathology, including histopathological findings and the etiology of ankylosis; and (3) treatment and prognosis, including the natural history, clinical consequences, and effectiveness of treatment strategies. Based on these predefined domains, the literature search strategy and eligibility criteria were developed. Because the review question focused on primary molar ankylosis and its relationship with infraocclusion, the electronic search strategy was intentionally designed using ankylosis-related terminology rather than all infraocclusion-related terms. This approach was intended to identify evidence addressing ankylosis while allowing evaluation of its relationship with infraocclusion.

Eligibility criteria

The following inclusion criteria were used to select publications: studies published in English, studies involving ankylosis of primary molars, and studies that provided epidemiological, histological, or clinical follow-up data related to ankylosis of primary molars. The review was limited to English-language publications because English was the working language of the review team, allowing consistent screening and interpretation of the included studies.

The following exclusion criteria were applied: reviews, comments, editorials, and case reports; studies for which references were not available; studies written in languages other than English; and studies not involving ankylosis of primary teeth.

Observational studies, including cohort, cross-sectional, and case-series studies, were eligible. Case reports were excluded because the objective of the review was to synthesize evidence from observational studies with more representative populations. Case reports were considered only for hypothesis generation in the Discussion and were not included in the systematic synthesis.

Titles, abstracts, and publication types were screened simultaneously before full-text assessment. Studies were eligible regardless of the participants’ age, provided that they investigated ankylosed primary molars. Histological studies involving retained primary teeth in adults were included because such studies provided unique histopathological evidence that could not be obtained from exfoliated primary teeth during childhood. The presence or absence of permanent successors was extracted when reported and considered during the interpretation of treatment outcomes. Studies involving children with craniofacial anomalies (e.g., cleft lip and/or palate) or syndromic conditions were excluded. When multiple publications were based on the same study population, the most comprehensive study relevant to the review question was included to avoid duplication of data. Reports for which the full text could not be obtained were excluded.

Study selection

One of the authors (M.H.) collected records from PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science and removed duplicates using EndNote 21 software (Clarivate Analytics, Toronto, Canada). A manual review was subsequently performed to remove additional duplicates not initially identified by the software. Two reviewers (M.O. and F.T.) then independently screened the titles and abstracts of the collected studies for relevance. The same reviewers (M.O. and F.T.) subsequently performed full-text screening of the studies included from the previous step. Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion.

Data extraction and evidence synthesis

Data extraction was independently performed by one reviewer (M.O.) using a standardized data extraction form developed for this review. The extracted data were subsequently checked by a second reviewer (F.T.), and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The following items were extracted from all included studies: author and year of publication, country, study design, participants’ age, sample size, study objective, analysis methods, presence or absence of follow-up, and intervention type (if applicable).

In addition, the following items were extracted from each predefined domain:

・Epidemiology domain: diagnostic criteria, prevalence, age at which infraocclusion was most frequently detected, common site, severity, number of affected teeth, and other findings.

・Histopathology domain: diagnostic criteria, prevalence of premolar agenesis, histological findings, and clinical findings.

・Treatment and prognosis domain: diagnostic criteria, duration of follow-up, intervention and prognosis, and additional findings.

The extracted data were synthesized descriptively and organized according to the three predefined review domains (epidemiology, histopathology, and treatment/prognosis). Because of the substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, including differences in study design, diagnostic criteria, study objectives, and reported outcomes, a formal meta-analysis was considered inappropriate. Therefore, the findings were synthesized narratively according to the three predefined review domains.

Risk of bias and methodological quality

The risk of bias and methodological quality of the included studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools [11]. The appropriate JBI critical appraisal checklist (for prevalence studies, cross-sectional studies, case series, or cohort studies) was selected based on each included study’s design and primary objective before quality assessment. Each item was rated as “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” In accordance with JBI guidance [12], no overall quality score was calculated because the checklists are intended to support critical appraisal rather than generate summary scores. Methodological quality was independently assessed by two reviewers (M.O. and F.T.). Any disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Results

The study selection process is summarized in Fig. 1. In total, 1147 records were identified through database searching, including 513 from PubMed, 463 from Scopus, and 171 from Web of Science. Of these, 682 records (308 from PubMed, 248 from Scopus, and 126 from Web of Science) were retrieved using the search term “ankylosis” AND “primary tooth,” whereas 465 records (205 from PubMed, 215 from Scopus, and 45 from Web of Science) were retrieved using “ankylosis” AND “deciduous tooth.” After duplicate removal, 411 records were screened by title, abstract, and article type, and 391 were excluded. Subsequently, 20 reports were assessed for eligibility through full-text review. Three reports could not be retrieved. Following full-text assessment, three reports were excluded for the following reasons: duplicate population (n = 1), craniofacial anomalies or syndromic population (n = 1), and non-English publication (n = 1). Epidemiology including diagnostic criteria, prevalence, and clinical characteristics of primary molar ankylosis

Table 2 summarizes the studies included in the epidemiology domain, including diagnostic criteria, prevalence, and clinical characteristics [3], [5], [13], [17], [18], [19], [20]. Among the included epidemiological studies, most diagnosed infraocclusion rather than confirmed ankylosis. Therefore, the reported prevalence primarily reflects infraocclusion used as a surrogate for ankylosis. Diagnostic criteria varied among studies and were primarily based on the degree of infraocclusion relative to the occlusal plane or adjacent teeth, typically defined as 0.5–1.0 mm or more below the occlusal level [3], [5], [13], [20]. The reported prevalence ranged from 1.3% to 24.8% at the person level and from 2.7% to 8.8% at the tooth level [3], [5], [13], [17], [18], [19], [20]. The highest reported prevalence was generally observed between 6 and 10 years of age [3], [5], [13], [17], [18], [19], [20]. Ankylosis occurred more frequently in mandibular primary molars, with the mandibular first primary molar generally more commonly involved than the mandibular second primary molar [3], [5], [13], [18], [20]. Most cases were classified as mild infraocclusion, with fewer moderate and severe cases reported [3], [13], [17], [19], [20]. Etiological and histological findings of ankylosed primary molars

Table 3 summarizes the studies included in the histopathology domain, including proposed etiological factors and histological findings [6], [7]. Histopathological studies provided direct confirmation of ankylosis through microscopic examination of extracted teeth [6], [7]. Premolar agenesis was reported in 19.4%–22.6% of infraoccluded primary molars in these studies [6], [7]. Histopathological examination of infraoccluded primary molars demonstrated direct continuity between alveolar bone and dentin following root resorption, together with areas of dentin resorption accompanied by subsequent deposition of bone or bone-like tissue [6], [7]. These changes frequently involved the inner root surface and, in some cases, were limited to the apical third of the root [6], [7]. However, not all infraoccluded primary molars necessarily exhibited ankylosis [6], [7]. Clinically, affected teeth typically produced a solid sound upon percussion, lacked mobility, and were associated with increased vertical positional loss [6], [7]. Treatment and prognosis of ankylosed or infraoccluded primary molars

The management strategies and prognostic outcomes of ankylosed primary molars are summarized in Table 4a, Table 4b [2], [8], [14], [15], [16]. Most treatment studies diagnosed ankylosis clinically based on infraocclusion together with percussion, mobility, and radiographic findings, whereas histological confirmation was generally unavailable. Mildly infraoccluded teeth, especially mandibular first primary molars, usually exfoliated on schedule with minimal residual root fragments [2]. A conservative approach for mildly affected cases was often recommended when permanent successors were present [14], [15]. In non-extracted cases, progression of infraocclusion was observed in a proportion of teeth, but all exfoliated within the normal timeframe [14], [15]. Extraction was generally performed in more severely affected cases [14], [15]. Findings regarding treatment outcomes after extraction varied across studies. One study reported gradual space loss following extraction [14], whereas others found no consistent increase in residual root fragments or alveolar bone loss compared with non-extraction [2], [15]. In cases with severe infraocclusion or significant space loss, combined treatment involving space regaining with removable or fixed appliances, surgical extraction of infraoccluded primary molars, and immediate space maintenance was associated with spontaneous eruption of impacted premolars in approximately 95% of reported cases [8], [16]. Severe infraocclusion occurred more frequently in lower second and upper primary molars and was sometimes associated with adjacent tooth tipping, anterior mandibular rotation, and alveolar bone remodeling [2], [8], [16]. Agenesis of permanent teeth and other dental anomalies were reported in a subset of patients with deep infraocclusion [8], [16].

Discussion

In this systematic review, 14 studies fulfilled all inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included in the narrative synthesis. These studies were categorized as epidemiological, etiological, or prognostic. This systematic review integrates evidence across these three research domains to provide a comprehensive interpretation of the relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion in primary molars and summarizes the currently available diagnostic criteria (Table 1–4). Although the primary focus of this review was primary molar ankylosis, the relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion was a prespecified component of the review question. Accordingly, the findings regarding infraocclusion should be interpreted in the context of evidence identified through this ankylosis-focused review.

Prevalence and diagnosis of “ankylosed” or “infraoccluded” primary molars

In the seven epidemiological articles included in this systematic review, the diagnostic criteria for primary molar ankylosis were mainly based on the tooth being more than 0.5 mm or 1.0 mm below the occlusal plane, as assessed by study models, oral findings, and radiographs [3], [5], [13], [20]. The Brearley and McKibben classification is a long-established standard, and its definition of “mild” closely corresponds to the diagnostic criteria described above [21]. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the prevalence studies primarily evaluated infraocclusion rather than histologically confirmed ankylosis. In addition, the sample sizes of these studies varied widely, ranging from 150 to 8452 participants [3], [5], [13], [17], [18], [19], [20]. In the Israeli study, the reported prevalence was very high (24.8%), with significant differences observed among ethnic groups [13]. By contrast, in the study in Turkey, the reported prevalence was very low (1.3%), based on multiple strict criteria [20]. However, the other studies reported prevalence rates of approximately 5%–15% at the person level. Therefore, based on the diagnostic criteria used in these seven studies, the reported prevalence likely reflected the prevalence of infraocclusion in primary molars rather than the true prevalence of ankylosis and was comparable to the prevalence reported in recent systematic reviews of infraoccluded primary molars [22].

Association between ankylosis and infraocclusion based on pathological evidence

There has been very little new histological evidence regarding ankylosis over the past few decades. Darling and Levers reported that 98% of infraoccluded primary molars exhibited areas of dentinal resorption followed by the deposition of bone or bone-like tissue in direct continuity with the dentin on histological examination [7]. These histopathological findings are consistent with replacement resorption associated with ankylosis [7]. In addition, Kurol and Magnusson reported that more than 80% of infraoccluded primary molars were diagnosed as ankylosed based on histological findings [6]. However, the presence of infraocclusion does not necessarily indicate ankylosis [6], [7].

The following discussion incorporates additional mechanistic and developmental evidence that was not included in the systematic review. This evidence is presented solely to provide biological context and generate hypotheses for future research. Ankylosis has been described as a transient phenomenon occurring at specific stages of root resorption, with ankylosed areas subsequently undergoing resorption and resolution [23]. By contrast, this interpretation does not adequately explain cases in which ankylosed areas are observed in the absence of root resorption. Such findings suggest that infraocclusion may also arise from factors other than ankylosis. Previous studies have suggested that infraocclusion may represent a clinical manifestation of increased occlusal force following eruption of the first molar [24], mandibular growth characteristics [8], [25], and eruption disturbances, including eruption timing mismatch and weak eruptive force [6], [26], [27]. These multiple developmental and biomechanical factors may also help explain the age at which infraocclusion was most frequently detected (8–10 years) and the predominance in the mandible observed in the epidemiological studies included in this systematic review. Furthermore, teeth that become infraoccluded due to non-ankylotic factors may develop increasing discrepancies in occlusal and alveolar relationships over time [6], potentially leading to secondary ankylosis through altered mechanical and biological environments. However, ankylosis may occur focally, and histological sectioning may fail to detect small ankylosed areas. Moreover, because the available histopathological evidence was derived from selected extracted teeth, the reported histological proportions should be interpreted as observations from these samples rather than estimates applicable to all infraoccluded primary molars. Therefore, the absence of histologically identified ankylosis should not be interpreted as definitive evidence that ankylosis was completely absent. Consequently, the relationship between infraocclusion and ankylosis should be interpreted with caution, and further well-designed histopathological studies are needed to clarify this association.

On the other hand, Darling and Levers reported that ankylosis can be present even in clinically normal primary molars in approximately 9% of cases [7]. Recent transcriptomic analyses have suggested that infraocclusion may represent a molecular phenotype associated with ankylosis, characterized by inflammatory pathways and epithelial cell dysregulation, whereas non-infraocclusion may represent a temporary absence of ankylosis [28]. However, this interpretation may not fully account for the clinical heterogeneity of infraocclusion, which is likely influenced by multiple developmental and biomechanical factors. Therefore, the presence of ankylosis in clinically normal primary molars suggests that ankylosis per se is not synonymous with infraocclusion, but rather a latent condition that may or may not become clinically apparent depending on growth-related factors.

In the clinical diagnosis of ankylosis, in addition to findings of infraocclusion, the percussion sound test is considered one of the simplest methods for diagnosing ankylosed primary molars and provides relatively reliable diagnostic results [6], [29]. However, percussion changes reportedly occur only when at least 20% of the root surface is ankylosed [30]. In addition, mobility evaluation is also helpful because loss of mobility in infraoccluded teeth occurs when more than 10% of the root becomes ankylosed [30]. On the other hand, limited diagnostic accuracy has been reported not only for two-dimensional dental imaging [29], [31] but also for three-dimensional cone-beam computed tomography [32]. Therefore, the most useful clinical approach for diagnosing ankylosis is likely the combined assessment of percussion sound and tooth mobility together with longitudinal radiographic observation, regardless of the presence or absence of infraocclusion.

Nevertheless, the histopathological evidence included in this systematic review was derived from only two relatively small studies of extracted teeth published several decades ago, with limited reporting identified by the JBI critical appraisal. Because extraction is generally performed in more severely affected teeth, selection bias cannot be excluded, and these findings may not be generalizable to all infraoccluded primary molars.

Clinical course and management of infraoccluded primary molars

Management of infraoccluded primary molars has long been debated, particularly regarding the indication for extraction [2], [14], [15], [21], [33]. The available evidence does not demonstrate a clear superiority of either extraction or conservative management in teeth with permanent successors because treatment outcomes have been inconsistent across predominantly observational studies. Based on the currently available evidence, mild infraocclusion is generally considered suitable for observation, whereas severe infraocclusion associated with progressive space loss, tipping of adjacent teeth, or occlusal disturbance may warrant extraction with appropriate space maintenance [2], [14], [15], [21], [33]. This is broadly consistent with the Brearley and McKibben classification [21].

Brearley and McKibben proposed a severity-based classification of infraocclusion that links the degree of infraocclusion with clinical management [21]. Mild infraocclusion is generally managed by observation, whereas severe infraocclusion associated with progressive space loss, tipping of adjacent teeth, or occlusal disturbance may require extraction with appropriate space maintenance [21]. Although recent studies have questioned whether infraocclusion is always attributable to ankylosis, the findings of the present review are broadly consistent with this severity-based management approach when determining treatment strategies for infraoccluded primary molars with permanent successors. Consistent with this classification, conservative management remains a viable option for mild to moderate infraocclusion, and a clear superiority of extraction has not been demonstrated in a recent systematic review [34].

On the other hand, several studies have reported a relatively high frequency of missing permanent successors in infraoccluded primary molars [6], [7], [16], [18], [19]. For example, successor agenesis has been observed in approximately 30% of infraoccluded primary molars, compared with only 0.66%–3.62% in the general population [35]. In the present review, the proportion of infraoccluded primary molars without permanent successors ranged from approximately 10%–25%, which is considerably higher than the reported prevalence of premolar agenesis in the general population [6], [7], [19]. These findings suggest a possible association between infraocclusion and developmental anomalies such as agenesis. In cases of infraoccluded primary molars without permanent successors, treatment planning should extend beyond management of the infraoccluded tooth itself and adopt a comprehensive, long-term perspective, including orthodontic space closure, prosthetic rehabilitation, or implant placement [4], [33], [36]. When significant malocclusion is absent, preservation of the infraoccluded primary molar may be considered whenever possible. In the absence of ankylosis, continued monitoring is generally appropriate; by contrast, when ankylosis is present, management should be tailored according to the severity of infraocclusion and the number of affected teeth, including options such as periodic monitoring, occlusal build-up to maintain function, orthodontic space closure following extraction, or implant-based rehabilitation [4], [33], [36]. Implant-based rehabilitation should generally be considered only after completion of craniofacial growth. Preservation of infraoccluded primary molars may also contribute to maintenance of alveolar bone volume, which could be advantageous for future implant placement [37]. Regardless of the selected approach, regular follow-up is essential to monitor the progression of ankylosis and associated occlusal or alveolar changes.

Nevertheless, the treatment recommendations discussed above should be regarded as conditional because they were derived predominantly from observational studies with low-certainty evidence, many of which provided limited information regarding potential confounding factors identified by the JBI critical appraisal.

Hypothetical conceptual framework possibly related to ankylosis and infraocclusion

Based on the findings of this systematic review, together with additional mechanistic literature, we propose a hypothetical conceptual framework illustrating possible relationships between ankylosis and infraocclusion in primary molars (Fig. 3). Infraocclusion has traditionally been attributed primarily to ankylosis [5], [7], [21]. However, accumulating evidence suggests that additional factors, including occlusal forces, eruption disturbances, and craniofacial growth characteristics, may also contribute to its clinical manifestation. As shown in Table 4, Messer and Cline reported that mandibular second primary molars exhibited more severe infraocclusion than first primary molars [2]. The mandibular second primary molar plays a central role in masticatory function and is exposed to substantial occlusal forces, which may occasionally exceed the adaptive capacity of the periodontal ligament [38]. Such mechanical loading has been suggested to contribute to periodontal ligament breakdown and may be involved in the development of ankylosis [24]. In addition, Shalish et al. reported that agenesis of permanent successors, particularly premolars, was more frequently observed in cases with severe infraocclusion [16]. Given that second premolars show a relatively high prevalence of agenesis in the general population [35], the absence of a permanent successor may reduce eruptive and resorptive stimuli acting on the primary molar, thereby delaying exfoliation and increasing susceptibility to infraocclusion [39]. Regarding mandibular growth patterns, the limited available evidence presents inconsistent findings [8], [25]. Some studies, including those by Leonardi et al., have suggested that anterior (counterclockwise) growth rotation is associated with more stable occlusal relationships and reduced vertical discrepancies, possibly because of increased alveolar bone turnover and remodeling activity [8]. These characteristics may influence occlusal contact patterns and eruption dynamics of successor teeth [6], [26], [27]. By contrast, Lanteri et al. reported that posterior (clockwise) mandibular rotation is associated with increased vertical dimensions and reduced occlusal contact, which may predispose individuals to infraocclusion [25]. However, the causal relationship between growth pattern and infraocclusion remains unclear, and the association observed in previous studies may reflect shared developmental factors rather than direct causation. Indeed, familial aggregation of ankylosis and infraocclusion has been reported [5], [16], suggesting a possible genetic contribution, potentially mediated through craniofacial growth characteristics. Taken together, these findings suggest that ankylosis and infraocclusion should not necessarily be regarded as synonymous conditions. Instead, multiple mechanical, developmental, and genetic factors may contribute to their complex relationship, the temporal and causal direction of which remains uncertain. The hypothetical framework presented in Fig. 3 is intended to summarize these possible relationships and generate hypotheses for future research rather than to depict established causal pathways.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations. Most included studies were case series or observational in design, which limits the ability to draw causal inferences. In cross-sectional and cohort studies, confounders and strategies used to address them were seldom reported. In addition, because the electronic search was intentionally focused on ankylosis-related terminology, studies describing infraocclusion without explicit reference to ankylosis may not have been comprehensively identified, although manual reference-list screening was performed to minimize this risk. Furthermore, diagnostic criteria for ankylosis and infraocclusion were not standardized across studies, which may have contributed to heterogeneity in the findings. Future studies incorporating standardized diagnostic criteria, histopathological validation of ankylosis, and large-scale epidemiological designs are warranted.

Another important limitation relates to the stage at which ankylosis is identified. Most included studies focused on ankylosed or infraoccluded primary molars after complete eruption. Although ankylosis is generally considered to occur after eruption, it may also develop during the early stages of eruption, thereby mimicking a primary eruption disturbance in some cases [2], [21], [40]. In addition, teeth affected by eruption disturbances, such as primary failure of eruption or odontogenic lesions (e.g., odontomas or dentigerous cysts), are initially non-ankylosed; however, prolonged absence of eruption and functional loading may predispose these teeth to secondary ankylosis over time [41]. These observations suggest the possibility that ankylosis may not always represent the initiating factor, but occur as a secondary event in the progression of eruption disturbances. Moreover, cases of ankylosis in unerupted primary molars have been reported [42], indicating that ankylosis may arise earlier than traditionally assumed. Taken together, these findings suggest that the relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion may be more complex than the conventional unidirectional model and may involve multiple contributing factors, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Further investigation into the temporal and causal relationships between ankylosis, infraocclusion, and eruption disturbances is required. Scoping reviews incorporating case reports of impacted primary teeth, in addition to infraocclusion, may provide valuable insights into the developmental sequence of these conditions.

Conclusion

The available evidence regarding ankylosis of primary molars remains limited, heterogeneous, and derived predominantly from observational studies. Consequently, the temporal and causal relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion cannot yet be established. Nevertheless, our systematic review led to the following findings:

・Epidemiological studies on ankylosed primary molars have mainly reported the frequency of infraoccluded primary molars used as a surrogate for ankylosis, with an overall prevalence of 1.3–24.8% at the person level and 2.7–8.8% at the tooth level, although reported prevalence varied substantially among populations.

・To diagnose infraocclusion, oral findings, radiographs, and model analysis are used alone or in combination. A position more than 0.5 or 1.0 mm below the occlusal plane or Brearley and McKibben classification is commonly used as the diagnostic criterion for infraocclusion.

・Although most infraoccluded primary molars exhibited ankylosis, the presence of infraocclusion does not necessarily indicate ankylosis on histological examination. In addition, ankylosis can be present even in clinically normal primary molars.

・Based on the currently available evidence, conservative management may be considered for mild to moderate infraocclusion, whereas extraction with appropriate space management may be warranted in selected severe cases. In cases with successor agenesis, treatment planning should adopt a comprehensive, long-term perspective, including orthodontic space closure, prosthetic rehabilitation, or implant placement after completion of craniofacial growth.

・The available findings suggest that multiple developmental and biomechanical factors may contribute to the complex relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion. Whether ankylosis represents a primary event or develops secondarily under different developmental conditions remains uncertain.

This review synthesizes the currently available evidence across epidemiology, histopathology, and clinical management while highlighting the uncertain relationship between ankylosis and infraocclusion. By distinguishing established observations from hypothesis-generating concepts, it may facilitate future mechanistic and longitudinal research.
