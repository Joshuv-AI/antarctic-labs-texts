A qualitative exploration of perceived risks, motivations, and risk management strategies among brothel-based female sex workers in Ibadan, Nigeria
Authors: Olusola Monsurat Ibraheem, Oluwadamilola Adeola Oyadeyi, Esther Oluwatosin Lawal, Olubukola Omobowale, Oluwatoyin Elizabeth Olalemi, Ayodele Samuel Jegede, Adeyinka Abideen Aderinto, Isaac Folorunso Adewole, Suzanna C Francis, Deborah Watson-Jones, Imran Oludare Morhason-Bello
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFemale sex workers (FSWs) experience a higher burden of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), and other sexually transmitted infections (STIs) compared to the general population due to the nature of their work. In Nigeria, challenges such as poverty, stigma, and poor access to healthcare continue to heighten their vulnerability, even though prevention programmes are in place. However, limited qualitative evidence exists on how FSWs perceive sexual risks, the motivations driving unsafe practices, and the strategies used to mitigate them. This study draws from the constructs of the Health Belief Model (HBM) to examine the experiences of brothel-based FSWs on vaginal, oral, and Anogenital sexual activity.MethodsThis qualitative study was conducted in brothels located in Ibadan, Oyo State, Nigeria, as part of the SHINI Project. Two focus group. discussions (FGDs) with 19 participants and five in-depth interviews (IDIs) were held with purposively selected FSWs aged 18–45 years. Data were collected between August and October 2016, audio-recorded, transcribed, translated into English, and coded using NVivo 11. Thematic analysis was applied to identify patterns related to perceived risks, motivations, and coping strategies.ResultsFSWs perceived oral and Anogenital sexual activity as high-risk practices, linking them to infections such as syphilis, gonorrhoea, herpes, and human papillomavirus (HPV), as well as long-term complications including mouth cancer, faecal incontinence, and rectal trauma. Motivations for engaging in unsafe sexual behaviours included financial incentives, fear of losing clients, peer influence, and curiosity. While some participants demonstrated agency by refusing unsafe sex or negotiating safer alternatives, economic hardship, client coercion, and stigma often limited their ability to act. Coping strategies included outright refusal, condom use, negotiation for alternative styles, and frequent medical check-ups, though stigma and discriminatory health services reduced care-seeking.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the complex balance between economic survival and health risk awareness among brothel-based FSWs. To reduce these risks, this study recommends the implementation of targeted interventions targeted interventions are needed, including comprehensive sexual health education, stigma-free healthcare services, improved access to condoms and lubricants, and economic empowerment initiatives. Collaboration between government agencies, NGOs, healthcare providers, and community organisations is essential to reduce vulnerabilities and improve the health outcomes of this key population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25625-5.Keywords: Female sex workers, Sexual risk behaviour, Perceived risks, Orogenital sexual activity, Anogenital sexual activity

Introduction

Female sex workers (FSWs) represent a key population in the global response to HIV and other STIs. Due to the nature of their work, FSWs are particularly vulnerable to infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, hepatitis, and human papillomavirus (HPV) [1–3]. A study conducted in China revealed that many FSWs have asymptomatic chlamydia and syphilis [4], while a global systematic review and meta-analysis found that more than four out of ten FSWs have HPV infections [2]. The World Health Organization report showed that FSWs are more likely than women in general population to have HIV and other STIs [5]. These health risks are made worse in places where sexual health services are hard to reach or not available [6]– [7]. Recent global estimates confirm the substantial burden of HIV among key populations, with FSWs and their clients contributing significantly to new HIV infections, particularly in regions with weak health systems and high levels of stigma [8]– [9].

In Nigeria, HIV has posed a burden on public health. In 2019, the Federal Ministry of Health (FMoH) revealed that in Nigeria approximately 107,112 new HIV infections, representing about 38% of all new infections in West and Central Africa [10]. As of 2020, an estimated 1.9 million people were living with HIV in Nigeria, making it the second-highest HIV burden globally [11]. The national adult prevalence rate was 1.4% among those aged 15–49 years, with notable disparities across 36 states and Federal Capital Territory, and subpopulations. Key populations, including FSWs, people who inject drugs, and men who have sex with men, are disproportionately affected due to structural barriers such as poverty, stigma, and limited access to quality healthcare [6, 12].

Although Nigeria recorded a 21% reduction in new HIV infections between 2010 and 2019, the country still faces challenges in achieving the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets, which aim for 95% of people living with HIV to know their status, 95% of those diagnosed to receive treatment, and 95% of those on treatment to achieve viral suppression [11]. Despite constituting only, a small proportion of the adult population, FSWs and other key populations account for nearly 40% of all new HIV infections annually in Nigeria, indicating the need for intensified prevention and support efforts [7].

Recent surveillance data reveal geographical differences in the prevalence of HIV prevalence among FSWs in Nigeria. The 2020–2021 National data from the Integrated Biological and Behavioural Surveillance Survey (IBBSS) reported a prevalence rate of 15.5% among FSWs in Nigeria [13], a lower prevalence was reported in another study in Kano among FSWs to be 8.5% [7]. This persistent disparity is linked to behavioural and structural factors such as high partner turnover, inconsistent condom use, early sexual debut, substance use, and poor access to sexual health services [12]. The high prevalence of HIV and other STIs might be due to the indirect risks such as gender-based violence, financial motivations and other lifestyle risks and the poor ability to negotiate for safer sex [14]– [15]. Mobility adds another layer of risk, as women who travel for sex work are more likely to face severe forms of violence and encounter difficulties in accessing health services [16]. When combined, these behavioural, structural, and indirect risks create situations where FSWs are exposed to greater chances of infection and have fewer opportunities to protect their health [15]. Furthermore, broader socio-economic pressures, including poverty and limited negotiating power, continue to intensify their sexual vulnerability [17–19]. These risks are often compounded by stigma, legal discrimination, and criminalisation of sex work, which collectively discourage FSWs from seeking healthcare services such as HIV testing, treatment, pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), and self-testing [6, 20]. Brothel-based FSWs report a higher prevalence of STI-related symptoms compared to other subgroups, suggesting a pressing need for targeted interventions [7].

The sexual practices of FSWs, including vaginal, oral, and Anogenital sexual activity with both paying and regular partners, are influenced by overlapping socio-economic factors. These include financial pressure, fear of losing clients, limited power to negotiate condom use, substance use, and fear of client violence [6, 12]. Similar dynamics have been observed among young women in Southern Africa, where early sexual debut and risky behaviours are often driven by poverty, peer pressure, and alcohol use [21]– [22]. This implies that social and economic factors also influence the decisions individuals make about sexual behavioural practices.

In the Nigerian context, societal stigma and legal restrictions exacerbate these risks. Many FSWs are compelled to operate in informal or unregulated spaces where their exposure to health risks is heightened and access to healthcare is severely constrained [6]. This marginalisation is sustained by cultural norms and religious beliefs that label sex work as immoral, thus fuelling discrimination and exclusion [20]. Stigma also operates as a structural barrier that reduces access to HIV services and broader social protections [23]– [24]. Fear of being judged or mistreated often leads FSWs to avoid healthcare facilities, thereby limiting opportunities for early diagnosis and treatment [6].

Also compounding these challenges is Nigeria’s legal framework, which does not explicitly criminalise sex work but penalises activities related to it, such as brothel ownership and procuring [25]. This ambiguity enables selective enforcement and can result in violence, arrest, and abuse of sex workers. The fear of legal repercussions deters many FSWs from seeking health services, thereby increasing their vulnerability to HIV and STIs [6, 25].

While recent studies have contributed valuable quantitative insights into HIV risk and service uptake among FSWs in Nigeria, the lived experiences and coping mechanisms of this population remain inadequately explored. Existing literature has largely concentrated on statistical trends and measurable outcomes [7, 12, 26–28], with a limited qualitative inquiry into how FSWs perceive, interpret, and respond to health risks they encountered. To bridge this gap in existing literature, this study adopts the HBM to examine the behavioural choices of FSWs in relation to perceived sexual risks. The HBM provides a structured lens for understanding how individuals evaluate health threats and engage with preventive behaviours [29]– [30].

According to the HBM, actions are shaped by an individual’s perceived susceptibility to a health condition, perceived severity of the consequences, and perceived benefits of adopting protective measures [29]– [30]. Additionally, HBM accounts for perceived barriers that may hinder action, cues to action that trigger behavioural responses, and self-efficacy, which reflects the individual’s confidence in their capacity to take the necessary steps [29–31]. Anchored within this framework, this study explored the perceived risks, underlying motivations for engaging in high-risk sexual practices, and the coping strategies employed by brothel-based FSWs in Nigeria.

Methods

Study design and population

This study was conducted as part of the Sexual Behaviour and HPV Infection in Nigerians in Ibadan (SHINI) Project, focusing on brothel-based FSWs in Ibadan Southwest and Ibadan Southeast Local Government Areas (LGAs), Oyo State, Nigeria. These urban LGAs were purposively chosen due to their high density of brothels, clubs, bars, and massage centres, making them strategic locations for exploring sexual behaviours. The study utilized a qualitative approach, employing focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs) to gain insights into FSWs’ knowledge, practices, and motivations for engaging in sexual risk behaviours. FGDs were used to capture shared experiences, group norms, and collective perceptions within the brothel setting, while IDIs provided the opportunity to explore sensitive and personal issues in greater depth that participants might not feel comfortable disclosing in a group. In total, two FGDs were conducted, one in each LGA, involving ten participants in Ibadan Southwest and nine in Ibadan Southeast. In addition, five IDIs were carried out—two in Ibadan Southwest and three in Ibadan Southeast. This design ensured that each brothel sampled contributed one FGD and at least two IDIs. Specifically, the study examined their experiences with vaginal, oral, and Anogenital sexual activity, as well as their risk perceptions, motivations, and preventive strategies. For this study, “high-risk sexual practices” were defined as unprotected vaginal, oral, or Anogenital sexual activity and other sexual acts involving the exchange of bodily fluids without barrier protection, which are associated with increased vulnerability to HIV and other STIs.

Study sites

Brothels in the two selected LGAs were purposively chosen from a verified list sourced from an international organization with extensive experience working with FSWs in Nigeria. This list was further validated by a volunteer gatekeeper and an official from the Oyo State Ministry of Health. In total, two brothels each were selected in Ibadan Southwest and Ibadan Southeast, reflecting the high density of sex work venues in these urban LGAs.

Recruitment

FSWs aged 18–45 years who were living and working in the selected brothels were eligible for participation. A female research assistant introduced the study to potential participants, provided information leaflets, and enrolled those who consented. Two members of the research team initially visited the brothels to meet with managers and “chairladies” (FSW leaders) to explain the study’s objectives and secure approval. Afterwards, trained female research assistants approached FSWs individually to invite their participation. IDI participants were recruited after the FGDs, with the moderator announcing that individuals willing to share personal experiences in more detail were welcome to participate in follow-up interviews.

Research instrument

The study utilised structured topic guides developed for both FGDs and IDIs. These instruments were informed by an extensive review of the literature on sexual risk behaviours among FSWs and consultations with experts in sexual and reproductive health. The study was conducted between August and October 2016, and the topic guides were used consistently throughout this period to ensure uniformity of data collection. The topic guides used were initially developed for this research and has also been published as supplementary material in a related study [32]. These guides were designed to explore participants’ knowledge and perceptions of sexual risk, the nature of sexual practices with clients and non-commercial partners, motivations for engaging in unprotected sex, and the strategies employed to minimise risk. Each guide was semi-structured, built around open-ended questions to allow for in-depth responses, and complemented with follow-up probes to encourage elaboration on sensitive topics and to elicit personal narratives that might not arise through structured questioning alone. One additional IDI was conducted in Ibadan Southeast to explore an individual’s personal experiences. Emerging themes during fieldwork informed this decision and suggested the need for deeper insight into specific risk-related behaviours and coping mechanisms.

Data collection procedures

With assistance from brothel managers, one FGD was conducted in each of the two selected local government areas. Each session comprised at least nine participants and took place in a designated private space within the brothels to ensure confidentiality and comfort for open discussion. Before data collection, a week-long training was conducted for research assistants, covering qualitative research techniques, interactive discussions, and role-playing exercises using the topic guides. The guides were piloted through one FGD and two IDIs conducted in another LGA with participants who shared similar characteristics to those in the main study, in order to refine their clarity and effectiveness. Each session was facilitated by a trained female moderator and a note taker. Informed consent was obtained from all participants (FSWs and brothel owners) before the commencement of the IDIs and FGDs. For literate participants, the consent process was explained in English, and they signed a written consent form. For illiterate participants, the purpose of the study information was explained in Yoruba and pidgin English to ensure they understood, and consent was obtained orally with a thumbprint, witnessed by another person. The process was voluntary, with no pressure from researchers or brothel owners. After obtaining informed consent, participants were assigned identification numbers to protect their privacy and maintain the anonymity of their responses. At the end of each FGD, participants were asked to complete a brief demographic profile form that captured basic background information such as age, marital status, and educational level. This information was used to provide context to the qualitative findings rather than for statistical analysis. IDIs were conducted in participants’ personal rooms within the brothels. Each participant was identified by their age and marital status rather than by name. Both FGDs and IDIs were conducted in English, “pidgin” English, and Yoruba and were audio-recorded, with each session lasting between 40 and 65 min. Data collection took place between August and October 2016. Although the data were collected nine years ago, the findings remain relevant as they provide unique insights into the structural, behavioural, and socio-economic vulnerabilities of brothel-based FSWs in Nigeria. These challenges persist in contemporary contexts due to the continuing realities of limited access to health services, stigma, and gender-based inequalities. As such, the study contributes valuable evidence that remains applicable to ongoing policy and intervention discussions.

Data management and analysis

All audio recordings were transcribed verbatim and supplemented with field notes. Since the interviews were conducted in Yoruba and Pidgin English, the transcripts were subsequently translated into English to ensure consistency for analysis. Data quality checks were performed by reviewing transcripts before they were imported into NVivo 11.0 Pro software for coding. In the initial coding stage, two researchers developed parent codes, which were subsequently reviewed and refined by two experienced team members. Further reading of transcripts led to the generation of child codes. Thematic content analysis was employed to identify patterns in the data, using an iterative and inductive approach [33]. Memos and reflective diaries were used to engage further with the data and refine emerging themes. Relationships between themes were examined to provide contextual meaning, and key messages were highlighted using direct quotes from participants. Data saturation was considered reached when no new themes or insights were emerging from the FGDs and IDIs, as repeated patterns and experiences were observed across participants during coding and analysis. Basic descriptive statistics were applied only to summarise participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, while the data analysis followed a qualitative approach using thematic analysis.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approvals were obtained from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, the University of Ibadan/University College Hospital, the Oyo State Research Ethics Review Committee and University of Ibadan/University College Hospital Institutional Review Board. The study adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, which promotes respect for individuals, informed decision-making, and protecting personal information. The research protocol received approval from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine, London; the University of Ibadan/the University College Hospital, Ibadan; and the Oyo State Government in Nigeria, with reference numbers LSHTM 9736-3; UI/EC/16/005; AD13/479/712. Given the sensitive nature of this study, particular precautions were taken to protect participants. All participants were fully informed about the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks, and benefits, and they were clearly assured of their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained individually before participation, with researchers taking time to read and explain the consent form to participants with lower literacy levels.

To safeguard confidentiality and anonymity, no names were recorded during interviews; instead, unique codes were assigned. Audio recordings were stored on password-protected with encrypted backup drives, accessible only to the research team. Data were anonymised during transcription, and sensitive details were carefully handled to prevent indirect identification. Interview settings were chosen to ensure privacy and comfort, thereby reducing risks of exposure or stigma.

Recognising the potential emotional impact of discussing sexual behaviours and experiences, participants were offered the option to pause or stop interviews at any time, and trained facilitators created a supportive, non-judgemental atmosphere during data collection. Participants were also provided with information on available health services for further support if needed. As an incentive, participants received toiletries, condoms, toothbrushes, toothpaste, and light refreshments. Although the interviews were conducted almost nine years ago, the files were retained in accordance with institutional ethics and data protection protocols, which require long-term secure storage.

Results

Twenty-four women participated across Ibadan South-West and Ibadan South-East LGAs, taking part in FGDs (n = 19) and IDIs (n = 5). Ages ranged from 20 to 35 years, with most in their twenties (n = 17). The majority were single (n = 15), while others were divorced (n = 4), married (n = 3) or separated (n = 2). Educational attainment was predominantly senior secondary (n = 17), with smaller numbers reporting primary (n = 4), junior secondary (n = 2) or college (n = 1) education. Site participation comprised Ibadan South-West (FGD n = 10; IDI n = 2) and Ibadan South-East (FGD n = 9; IDI n = 3). Disease concerns among fsws

Infection and disease as a major concern

From their lived experiences, FSWs identified oral and Anogenital sexual activity as practices that exposed them to significant health risks. Some participants expressed that the vagina could harbour infections, which could be transmitted through licking or sucking. Additionally, Anogenital sexual activity was linked to faecal leakage and potential long-term damage to the anal canal. One participant explained the dangers associated with these practices:

Unprotected sex whether anal or orogenital sexual activity can be risky. Anogenital sexual activity. When the genital organs whether vagina or penis is been licked or sucked is orogenital sexual activity…mouth-to-genital contact. Orogenital sexual activity. The lady to be sucked may be having rashes in and around her pussy (vagina), and you wouldn’t know if she has an infection too. Infections could be a blood type and physically seen… When a person has sexual contact with someone who is infected, there is a chance of contracting the disease. With Anogenital sexual activity, the anus can eventually become damaged, leading to leakage, leaving the area loose and releasing fluid. These are some of the disadvantages associated with it. Anogenital sexual activity [FGD_SE_R5].

Anogenital sexual activity was also widely perceived by FSWs as a dangerous practice. They described the anal canal as a “filthy place”. They feared that faecal matter on the penis could lead to cross-infection, particularly if the penis were later used for vaginal penetration. Some participants even believed that washing the penis and taking antibiotics afterwards could reduce risk, though these practices did not eliminate their concerns. One participant elaborated:

Everyone knows that waste comes out through the anus. I am certain that if someone inserts their finger there, it will touch faeces. When a penis enters the anus during sex, it can come into contact with many infections inside. If care is not taken, the same person might then have vaginal sex with you using the same penis, and this could create serious health problems because the infection may already be in their body and transmitted to you. When my partner and I engaged in anogenital sexual activity, he removed his penis immediately, and we stopped having sex. Afterwards, we both took a shower and took some medication. [IDI_SE_27yr]

Some FSWs also perceived orogenital sexual activity as carrying risks beyond STIs. They expressed concerns that men who performed orogenital sexual activity on women were at risk of developing cancer, while women could contract dangerous infections from the penile shaft. A participant explained how FSWs often mock clients who request orogenital sexual activity, associating it with illness:

We were told that oral–genital practices like licking the vagina can expose someone to illnesses, and cancer was given as an example. Among ourselves, we joke about customers who request ‘wash-wash’ or clean-up services (oral–genital sexual activity) in order to mock them.” [FGD_SW_R8].

Risk–benefit dilemma of orogenital sexual activity

Other FSWs described a risk–benefit dilemma, weighing the financial gain against the potential health consequences. One participant reflected on how the promise of money could tempt women to accept orogenital sexual activity but warned that the long-term health costs might outweigh the immediate reward:

I dislike engaging in oral–genital sexual activity because you cannot be sure of the infections a man might have on his penis, yet you are expected to do it for money. In the end, you may contract an illness that the payment received may not be enough to treat, leaving you at a disadvantage. [IDI_SW_35yr]

Some FSWs identified the forceful actions of clients during orogenital sexual activity as a significant source of risk. They explained that clients sometimes inserted their penis deeply into their throat, making the experience both unpleasant and dangerous. This not only caused discomfort but also exposed them to the unintentional ingestion of semen, which they believed increased their vulnerability to infection. One participant explained:

I think there is a risk, because when performing orogenital sex with some men, small amounts of semen can enter the mouth. Some men push their penis into the mouth forcefully to stop you from speaking, and if you are holding and sucking it, you might still swallow some semen even if you try to spit it out. In some cases, they may push it deep enough for the semen to go straight into the throat. You also cannot tell what infections or diseases a client might bring from outside into the brothel. When having penetrative sex, a condom can be used so the semen remains contained and does not touch your body, but during oral sex, semen can gradually enter the mouth little by little. [IDI_SE_27yr]

While many participants viewed orogenital sexual activity as high risk, a few expressed a different perception, suggesting that it carried less risk compared to other practices. They reasoned that HIV transmission through orogenital sexual activity was unlikely and felt that condom failure during vaginal sex or exposure to sharp objects posed greater dangers. As one participant explained:

Orogenital sex does not transmit HIV. Sometimes condoms can break, and if a sexual partner is HIV positive, it may not be obvious, especially with the medications available today. Therefore, avoiding poor-quality condoms, sharp objects, and other risky behaviours helps protect us from HIV. There are no additional routes of infection beyond these. [FGD_SE_R4]

Physical injury and risks associated with anogenital sexual activity

Long-term anal damage

The physical consequences of engaging in Anogenital sexual activity emerged as a significant concern among FSWs. Many participants expressed fears about potential damage to the anal sphincters, which control bowel movements. A commonly cited risk was anal incontinence, where individuals experience an inability to control faecal discharge due to weakened or damaged sphincter muscles. One participant shared insight from a counselling session with her doctor, who explicitly warned her against engaging in Anogenital sexual activity. She recounted how her doctor explained the delicate nature of the anal sphincter, emphasizing the irreversible consequences of its rupture:

I remember a friend who engaged in anogenital sexual activity and she often experienced leakage when she sat down. A doctor once explained to me that the anus has a small muscle (the sphincter), and if it becomes damaged, it can cause leakage when sitting. My doctor was aware of the nature of my work, so whenever I visited the clinic, he treated me according to the symptoms I reported. [FGD_SW_R4]

Participants also described Anogenital sexual activity was as one of the most painful experiences some participants had encountered. Many participants expressed strong aversion to Anogenital sexual activity due to the intense pain involved, with some equating it to childbirth pain. One participant shared a particularly traumatic first experience, stating that

“Anogenital sex is extremely painful. I tried it once and screamed because of the pain, and I even passed faeces during the attempt. That experience made me dislike it. It is very painful, and I cannot engage in it again.” [IDI_SE_23yr].

This vivid account emphasises how painful encounters with Anogenital sexual activity can influence FSWs’ personal boundaries and future sexual choices.

This perceived lack of medical intervention contributed to heightened anxieties about engaging in Anogenital sexual activity, particularly with clients who experience delayed ejaculation. One participant described her concerns about prolonged thrusting, explaining that the longer the penetration lasts, the greater the likelihood of anal canal damage. She also explained the difficulty of defecation as a long-term consequence of repeated anal penetration:

My dear, it’s fearful! You can imagine a penis being inserted in the anus. It will damage the anus. Sometimes, you find it difficult to defecate, how much more inserting a penis. Some men don’t normally ejaculate on time. The man will continue thrusting until he ejaculates. In the process, the anus will be damaged, and it will be difficult to defecate normally again. [IDI_SW_35yr]

The fear of permanent anal damage was further expressed by another participant, who regarded Anogenital sexual activity as a high-risk sexual act. She expressed that:

That means you are trying to put my life at risk. Foreign countries have medical treatment for anogenital sexual activity, but Nigeria does not. I do not want to end up with a damaged anus, and I am not prepared to start using diapers. [IDI_SW_32yr]

expressing a strong aversion to the possibility of faecal incontinence and the subsequent use of adult diapers. This response illustrates the fear of permanent anal damage and emphasises the role of perceived health system limitations in shaping attitudes towards certain sexual practices.

Stigma and social perception

The fear of stigmatization emerged as a significant concern among FSWs and their clients who engaged in oral and Anogenital sexual activity. According to some participants these practices were not acceptable within their communities, making it difficult to discuss or admit involvement. One participant emphasized that oral and Anogenital sexual activity are considered taboo, and individuals who engage in them often deny their involvement in public due to the fear of societal judgment and potential violence:

They do not approve of engaging in anogenital sexual activities openly. I do not practise it, and many people also believe it is unacceptable. They question the reason behind participating in oral or anal intercourse. However, it is quite common here for individuals to perform oral stimulation on the penis or vagina. Still, no one would openly admit to doing so because it attracts stigma. You cannot comfortably discuss such practices with family members, as relatives or friends would not be willing to accept that type of sexual behaviour. Even when people engage in these acts privately, they strongly deny it in public, insisting that it is religiously forbidden to perform oral stimulation on a man or woman. [FGD_SW_R4]

Some participants also noted that FSWs who engage in oral and Anogenital sexual activity also risk verbal attacks and public humiliation. This negative societal perception reinforces the decision to remain silent about their experiences, restricting discussions to trusted confidants. One participant described how she selectively shares her experiences, ensuring that her conversations remain private to avoid public disgrace:

I only discussed my experience with my closest friend, not with everyone. In this type of work, it is important to have at least one person you can trust. When you share personal issues with many people, they may use it to mock or insult you. Some workers engage in anogenital sexual activities, but they would never admit it openly. Because of this, I can only speak for myself and not for others. [IDI_SW_32yr]

Physical pain and discomfort in sex work

Vaginal pain and hard sex

The physical consequences of sex work, particularly generalized body pain and anal pain, were prominent concerns among Female Sex Workers (FSWs). Many participants linked these bodily pains to the aggressive sexual behaviours of clients, often exacerbated by the desire for maximum sexual gratification. Clients, in many cases, engaged in forceful thrusting during penile-vaginal sex, a practice frequently described as “hard sex”. One participant recounted her early experiences in sex work, explaining how she endured persistent body pains due to the intensity of such sexual encounters:

When a man has sex with you from the back, he hits it so hard which may affect your womb. When a man has sex with me from the rear and I feel pains, immediately I will start to have the feelings from the womb. You know that there is a difference between a man that pays for sex and your own man. The one who pays will do anything for you because of the money he will be paying. They always come so hard on you. I could remember that when I newly joined, I was always complaining about pains then. [FGD_SW_R4]

Participants also associated aggressive penile-vaginal sex with alcohol consumption, noting that intoxicated clients sometimes demand for “hard sex.” In such situations, FSWs often found themselves powerless to refuse, as refusal could escalate into misunderstandings or even violence. One participant explained how alcohol-fuelled clients insisted on intense sexual activity, regardless of her discomfort:

Well, for me, I either have waist pains or headaches because of excessive ‘hard sex’ by customers. For example, some ‘asewo men’ (customers) would have been drunk and they must release their sperms before they leave you. You already know that they are under the influence of alcohol, and they will have sex with you very hard until they release (ejaculate). Anything short of that will result in a misunderstanding between the two of you. I normally caution them not to get me injured. It usually results in waist/womb pain. [FGD_SW_R1]

Lack of empathy from clients

The lack of emotional concern from clients was another issue frequently mentioned. Many FSWs compared their experiences with clients to those with their husbands or intimate partners. Whereas husbands displayed concern and pity when their wives expressed pain during sex, commercial clients showed little to no empathy, sometimes even mocking the FSWs for complaining. One participant explained this clear difference by emphasizing on how some clients excused their rough sexual behaviour by saying they had paid for the service.

Another point is that, if your husband had vaginal sex from the back and I complain of pain, he will show concern by saying ‘I’m sorry,’ but a customer will never tell you that because of the money he will pay at the end of the sexual act. A customer will even go to the extent of telling you that you shouldn’t have been in this kind of business since you will not be able to cope with such pains. After all, I paid for the service you rendered to me. Hardly will you find about two customers out of ten that will say sorry in such a case. [FGD_SW_R8].

Motivation for sexual risk behaviour

Participants described a range of factors that shaped their decisions, including the pursuit of financial benefits, the fear of losing clients in a competitive brothel environment, and personal curiosity or desire for experimentation. These motivations often created a tension between economic survival, professional expectations, and individual well-being, leading many FSWs to accept sexual practices such as unprotected vaginal sex, orogenital sexual activity, and Anogenital sexual activity, even when accompanied by discomfort, pain, or heightened risk of infection, as illustrated in (Fig. 2).

Financial incentives as a driving force

Monetary gain versus health risks

The financial benefit derived from engaging in sex work was a major motivation for many Female Sex Workers (FSWs). The need to meet personal and family financial obligations influenced their decision-making, particularly regarding negotiating sexual practices with clients. Many participants reported that higher financial offers from clients often encouraged them to accept unprotected vaginal sex and other discomforting sexual acts, such as oral and Anogenital sexual activity. During an FGD session, participants deliberated on the dilemma between financial gain and the risk of STIs. Some participants expressed that monetary compensation would determine their decision, whereas others outrightly rejected offers that could increase their risk of infection. One participant described a typical scenario where financial desperation could tempt an FSW to accept unprotected sex:

A customer may come and tell you to give him back style, and it depends on you to say yes or no. Some customers will come and ask you that they want to have sex without a condom. He is ready to offer 10,000 or 20,000 Naira (28 or 55 USD). It depends on your response as you wish. Some of us will think of the monetary aspect of it, and some will think of the disease aspect like HIV. Those in dire need of money will accept the offer, while some will accept such an offer because they are no more bearing children. [FGD_SW_R6]

Another FSW shared a personal experience in which she succumbed to a client’s financial offer and engaged in orogenital sexual activity despite her initial hesitation. While she later experienced feelings of guilt, she was consoled by her friends, who emphasized the financial gain as a justification for her actions:

My first encounter happened when a client asked for oral–genital activity. I refused at first, explaining that I had never tried it. He offered a large amount of money if I agreed, and he ended up showing me what to do. Later, I spoke with my friends about the different styles he introduced, as well as the enjoyment and fear I felt. They told me not to worry, reminding me that the work is mainly about earning money. [IDI_SE_25yr]

Differential pricing of sexual acts

The differential pricing of sexual acts was another significant theme that emerged from the discussions. Participants indicated that oral and Anogenital sexual activity commanded significantly higher financial compensation than vaginal sex. One participant emphasized that anal and orogenital sexual activity were priced about ten times higher than vaginal sex, demonstrating a clear economic incentive for engaging in these practices:

When a customer asks me for oral or anal sex (anogenital sexual activity), I usually tell him the price is 10,000 Naira (28 USD). If he refuses, I then offer normal vaginal sex from the front for 500–1,000 Naira (1.4–2.8 USD). I have heard that people generally pay between 10,000 and 15,000 Naira (28–41.7 USD) for anal sex (anogenital sexual activity). [FGD_SE_R7]

Client expectation and fear of losing patronage

Competition for clients

The competition for clientele among Female Sex Workers (FSWs) often compels them to engage in sexual risk behaviours to maintain their client base. The fear of losing patrons to competitors within brothels can pressure FSWs into accepting sexual requests they may otherwise refuse. One participant explained this competitive dynamic, explaining that clients have multiple options and will seek out sex workers willing to meet their specific demands:

clients sometimes request specific sexual styles, and if you are unable to provide what they want, they simply move on to someone else who can offer that service. [FGD_SE_R5].

This constant pressure to satisfy clients can lead FSWs to engage in sexual acts that do not provide them with personal satisfaction, yet they comply for the sake of ensuring customer retention.

Compliance despite discomfort

One participant recounted how she was willing to perform orogenital sexual activity on a client despite deriving no personal pleasure from the act. Instead, she viewed it as a transactional service that enhances her patronage:

Left for me, orogenital sexual activity doesn’t have any effect on me. If a guy wants me aroused, all you need to do is to suck my breast. If you suck my private part, you are just disturbing yourself; it doesn’t move me at all. [IDI_SE_27years]

Asides from orogenital sexual activity, some FSWs also comply with client requests for Anogenital sexual activity, even when experiencing significant discomfort or pain. The expectation to prioritize client satisfaction over personal well-being was reflected in one participant’s experience, where she endured severe pain during Anogenital sexual activity solely to meet her client’s demands:

For me, Anogenital sexual activity is very painful, but men enjoy it most. That is what I can explain about that. [IDI_SE_25years]

Curiosity and personal exploration

Experimentation and regret

Some Female Sex Workers (FSWs) engaged in oral and Anogenital sexual activity out of personal curiosity and a sense of adventure. Many viewed these sexual practices as emerging trends among their colleagues and sought to experiment with them. However, this initial excitement sometimes turned into regret, particularly for those who found Anogenital sexual activity too painful to endure. One participant shared her personal experience, detailing how her enthusiasm for trying Anogenital sexual activity quickly diminished due to intense discomfort:

Hmm, we were okay… (laughs…) when we were fucking (sex) each other, and my guy requested Anogenital sexual activity. I wanted it too because I also wanted to be practicing it, but along the way, I couldn’t hold the pain because it was just too painful for me. [IDI_SE_27years]

Similarly, curiosity played a significant role in some FSWs’ first-time engagement in orogenital sexual activity. Rather than being motivated by pleasure or client pressure, some women saw it as part of the profession and a service to be provided without emotional connection or personal gratification. One participant described her first experience with orogenital sexual activity, stating that she engaged in it purely for business purposes rather than for any personal desire:

I don’t have any reason; I just want to do it for business’s sake. Because the customer you are doing it with, you don’t have any feeling like when you are doing it with your guy. [IDI_SE_23years]

Preference for anogenital sexual activity

Interestingly, while many participants described Anogenital sexual activity as painful or transactional, others expressed a genuine preference for it. Some FSWs even stated that they enjoyed Anogenital sexual activity more than vaginal sex. One participant recounted a discussion she had with her friends, in which they all agreed on their preference for Anogenital sexual activity over vaginal sex:

I told them that I love Anogenital sexual activity more than vaginal sex. So, my friends also replied that they love it too more than vaginal sex. That is what my friends told me. [IDI_SE_25years]

She further elaborated on why she preferred Anogenital sexual activity, emphasizing a key reason related to male sexual pleasure and performance. She noted that men reach orgasm and ejaculate faster during Anogenital sexual activity compared to vaginal sex, making it a preferred option for both parties:

Anal will make men come easily (reach orgasm and ejaculate). [IDI_SE_25years]

Coping strategies and risk prevention measures adopted by female sex workers

Female Sex Workers (FSWs) employ various strategies to mitigate risks associated with client sexual requests. These strategies include outright refusal, offering safer sexual alternatives, consistent use of barrier methods, and frequent medical check-ups at health facilities. These measures help them navigate the challenges of client demands while prioritising their health and safety, as illustrated in (Fig. 3).

Outright refusal

Some FSWs displayed resistance to client demands they considered risky or detrimental to their well-being. These FSWs prioritized self-protection over financial gain, choosing to reject requests that could expose them to harm. One participant emphasized her strict stance against engaging in orogenital sexual activity, highlighting her willingness to forgo patronage rather than compromise her personal boundaries:

Some customers that patronized me do request I suck them and vice-versa. I have never allowed that for anybody; and they go elsewhere to get that. [FGD_SE_R2]

This emphasises the fact that not all FSWs succumb to client pressure. Some draw boundaries, demonstrating that self-agency and risk perception play crucial roles in decision-making. Although refusing a client could mean loss of income, certain FSWs prioritize their safety over financial incentives.

Negotiation for alternative sexual styles

Some FSWs employ negotiation strategies to steer clients towards alternative sexual styles that they perceive as safer or more comfortable. This approach allows them to retain clients while minimizing personal discomfort or risk. A participant illustrated this strategy by describing how she tactfully convinces clients to opt for a different sexual style that she prefers:

Personally, I prefer to treat my customers gently and keep them satisfied because I do not want them going to someone else. I normally tell them that I will satisfy them with the style that surpasses orogenital sexual activity or sucking. I may say I will give them the standing type of sex that will make them forget themselves so long as they are ready to cooperate. [FGD_SE_R2]

This negotiation tactic highlights a dual objective ensuring client satisfaction while maintaining agency over sexual preferences. The use of persuasion allows the FSW to subtly redirect the client’s demand toward an act that aligns with her boundaries and comfort level.

Use of barrier methods

The use of barrier methods, particularly condoms, was emphasized by FSWs as a critical strategy for protecting against STIs and preventing unplanned pregnancy conception. Interestingly, while condoms were widely discussed in the context of penile-vaginal and Anogenital sexual activity, no mention was made of barrier methods for orogenital sexual activity. This reveals a potential gap in protective practices among FSWs. Participants acknowledged the multiple benefits of condom use, including preventing infections such as gonorrhoea and HIV, avoiding unwanted pregnancies, and mitigating the consequences of unsafe abortions. One participant succinctly summarized these advantages, while also expressing appreciation for government initiatives providing free condoms to sex workers:

I can only talk about my own experience, as I am not sure about others. I understand that condoms help to prevent unwanted pregnancies. If you keep on aborting pregnancy, it will affect your womb and may lead to not having children. Abortion can kill, and that is one of the reasons for using condoms. Using condoms averts untimely death for us ‘prostitutes,’ unlike in the olden days of prostitution. We thank God that the government provides condoms for us. In fact, it protects us from contracting diseases like gonorrhoea and HIV. It is not only HIV that kills, gonorrhoea kills too. Its early stage is milky, then later turns bloody and causes constant headaches and may even lead to madness. It can cause permanent infertility. It is only God that can help us… Some people will not mind the amount of money they are offered because some people will come and tell you that they want it ‘raw’ (unprotected sex), and if you agree, you may contract something you never bargained for, which 10,000 naira (28USD) may never cure. [FGD_SW_R8]

This participant linked condom use not only to STI prevention but also to broader health risks associated with unsafe sexual practices, such as the long-term effects of gonorrhoea, the dangers of repeated abortions, and the financial burden of treating STIs. The excerpt also revealed an ongoing struggle between financial incentives and health-conscious decision-making, where FSWs sometimes must choose between economic survival and personal safety. The importance of safer sex education and training programs was highlighted by another participant. She described how FSWs had been taught about condom use in a previous awareness program, reinforcing the importance of education in promoting safer sex practices:

We have been taught in a condom programme before. It is like they said it is common among women. It is like they said it is curable but will still be in the body forever. [FGD_SE_R4]

This excerpt suggests that while FSWs are aware of safer sex practices, some misconceptions may persist, particularly concerning the long-term effects of STIs. The idea that some infections are “curable but will still be in the body forever” indicates a partial understanding of STI treatment and management, which may impact how FSWs perceive risk and adhere to condom use.

Frequent medical check-ups as a preventive strategy

One of the preventive strategies adopted by FSWs to manage the risks associated with unprotected sex is undergoing frequent medical check-ups. Many participants acknowledged the importance of medical screening following a potential sexual risk event, such as condom failure or exposure to infections. Some FSWs mentioned that they were educated about STIs and were advised to seek medical screening when necessary. One participant, for instance, highlighted the routine practice of HIV screening after experiencing a condom burst and the need to monitor for early signs of Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection:

I think the one they taught us about HPV was that it looks like a ‘boil’ (warts) in the vagina. At times, the ‘boil’ (warts) may be many and not curable. We were told that we should go to the hospital whenever we notice any simple signs. Also, if a condom bursts, we should go to the hospital for treatment, and we should do an HIV test every three months. [FGD_SW_R8].

Despite recognizing the importance of regular medical check-ups, some FSWs expressed concerns that fear of stigma and shame discouraged many of their colleagues from visiting health facilities. While some FSWs sought medical care freely and openly, others avoided hospitals due to the negative social perceptions attached to their profession. A participant explained this dual reality, stating that although she personally sought medical care, many others felt too ashamed to do the same:

Some of us go to the hospital while some don’t go. I do go and see the doctor whenever I’m sick. I tell him everything about myself, but some of us don’t go because of the shame and stigmas that are attached to this our job. I even go to UCH (tertiary hospital) and tell my doctor everything that pertains to my health conditions. [FGD_SW_R3]

Discussion

Perceived risks of sexual behaviours (oral and anogenital sexual activity)

This study examined the perceived risks and motivations for engaging in oral and Anogenital sexual activity among FSWs, focusing on health concerns, financial incentives, and societal stigma. The findings indicate that FSWs perceive these sexual practices as high-risk due to the potential transmission of infections, physical harm, and social discrimination. Participants frequently associated orogenital sexual activity with STIs such as syphilis, herpes, gonorrhoea, and HPV, as well as concerns about long-term conditions like mouth cancer [32]. Similarly, Anogenital sexual activity was linked to risks such as faecal contamination, rectal trauma, anal incontinence, and an increased risk of infection, particularly when transitioning between anal and vaginal penetration [32, 34]. These perceptions align with perceived susceptibility and severity within the HBM, where individuals recognize their risk of acquiring serious infections and the possible consequences on their health and well-being [35]. Although some FSWs acknowledged the health risks involved, financial benefits and client preferences often outweighed their concerns, reflecting a complex balance between perceived benefits and perceived barriers [30, 36]. These findings mirror evidence from Bangladesh and Iran, where FSWs also underestimated the risks of Anogenital sexual activity and held persistent misconceptions about STI transmission [37]– [38]. In South Africa, researchers reported that stigma, criminalisation, and poor access to accurate information continued to heighten risk among sex workers [39].

While some participants demonstrated awareness of HIV risks associated with condom failure and the use of shared sharp objects, many did not explicitly connect unprotected oral and Anogenital sexual activity with HIV exposure. This knowledge gap aligns with findings from a qualitative study in Ethiopia, where FSWs engaged in unprotected Anogenital sexual activity due to uncertainty about its role in increasing HIV and STI risk [40]. Within the context of the HBM, this reflects limited perceived susceptibility, where individuals may not fully believe they are at risk of infection from specific behaviours unless the threat is personally understood or directly experienced [30, 33]. However, some participants in the present study recognized that unprotected orogenital sexual activity could lead to infections and mouth cancer, particularly due to the swallowing of seminal fluids, contact with infected genital areas, and injuries caused by rough client behaviour. These findings are consistent with existing literature, which has established orogenital sexual activity as a risk factor for HPV-related mouth and throat cancers [41]– [42]. Studies in Canada and the United States also indicate that FSWs’ understanding of these risks is influenced not only by direct experience but also by structural barriers, including violence, stigma, and inadequate healthcare support [43]– [44]. This awareness illustrates a growing sense of perceived severity, central to motivating health-protective actions [36].

Similarly, concerns about rectal trauma and anal incontinence align with prior research highlighting the long-term complications of repeated anal penetration [32, 45]. Also, a significant concern raised by participants was the risk of cross-infection when engaging in both vaginal and Anogenital sexual activity within the same encounter. This practice was perceived as increasing the likelihood of bacterial transmission from the anal canal to the vagina, further elevating the risk of infection. This aligns with the notion of cues to action, where awareness of physical symptoms or harmful experiences prompts safer behaviour [30]. Similar findings emerged in Dhaka, Bangladesh, where researchers found that inconsistent condom use, and cross-penetration practices raised STI prevalence among FSWs [38]. These findings emphasize the importance of strengthening perceived susceptibility and severity through health education while also addressing barriers such as misinformation, stigma, and client coercion, which can limit the uptake of safer sex practices among FSWs [30, 35]. Global research also supports this, stressing the need for multi-level interventions that combine health education, stigma reduction, and accessible services to improve sexual health outcomes for FSWs [46].

Motivation for sexual risk behaviour (financial benefits, patronage/competition, curiosity)

Another key finding is that financial considerations played a crucial role in influencing FSWs’ willingness to engage in oral and Anogenital sexual activity. Many participants used a cost-benefit analysis to weigh financial compensation against potential health risks. According to HBM, this behaviour can be understood through the constructs of perceived benefits and perceived barriers. Some indicated that the monetary incentives offered for these acts were insufficient to justify the potential harm. This reflects their awareness of perceived severity and susceptibility to health risks [30, 36].

On the other hand, others felt pressured to comply due to economic desperation. This suggests that perceived barriers such as poverty and limited employment options often diminished their capacity to refuse client demands, even when health risks were recognized [30]. Similar patterns have been documented across Africa and Asia, where economic insecurity has consistently been linked with the acceptance of high-risk sexual practices [38]– [39]. Evidence from South Africa, for example, shows that FSWs experiencing poverty and stigma were more likely to accept unsafe sex due to limited alternatives, despite knowledge of potential harm [47]. Other studies on transactional sex, have also revealed that financial hardship drives individuals to accept high-risk sexual practices despite being aware of the dangers [48]– [49]. This aligns with findings that individuals are less likely to act in protective ways when barriers such as financial insecurity or social pressure outweigh their perceived control or belief in the effectiveness of preventive actions [30, 36]. These insights highlight the need to consider both structural and psychological determinants when addressing sexual health behaviours among economically vulnerable populations.

Additionally, coercion from clients and the fear of losing patronage further contributed to the willingness of some FSWs to engage in unprotected sex. This behaviour reflects the influence of perceived barriers, a key construct of the HBM. It refers to individuals evaluating obstacles that may prevent them from taking preventive health actions [30]. In this case, the economic consequences of refusing unprotected sex, such as losing income or regular clients, were seen as more immediate and pressing than the potential long-term health risks. The competitive nature of the sex work industry was highlighted as a significant factor, as some clients preferred FSWs who did not impose restrictions on sexual practices. This finding aligns with studies in Nigeria and South Africa, where economic necessity and male dominance strongly shape FSWs’ sexual behaviours [48, 50].

Peer influence also emerged as a contributing factor. Some FSWs were introduced to risky sexual behaviours through interactions with colleagues who normalized these practices as a means of increasing financial gain. This reflects broader research on peer influence in sexual decision-making, where social networks play a crucial role in shaping behavioural norms [51, 52]. This finding resonates with research in Iran and Bangladesh, where peer networks and social norms significantly influence women’s entry into and practices within sex work [37]– [38]. This can be interpreted through the lens of cues to action, which in the HBM includes external influences such as peer behaviours or social pressures that prompt individuals to act in particular ways [30, 36]. Moreover, the willingness of FSWs to adopt these behaviours despite potential harm suggests a gap in perceived susceptibility and severity, where the immediate financial benefit outweighs the perceived health threat.

Coping strategies and risk prevention measures (refusal, negotiation, barrier methods, health-seeking)

Despite the pressures associated with financial survival and client retention, FSWs employed various strategies to mitigate risks and protect their health. These included outright refusal of unsafe sexual practices, negotiation for alternative sexual styles, consistent use of barrier methods, and frequent medical check-ups. These behaviours suggested a heightened perception of susceptibility to HIV and other STIs, as well as an understanding of the serious health consequences these infections could have, which aligns with the HBM concepts of perceived susceptibility and severity [30, 35]. The willingness to refuse high-risk sexual activities demonstrated a strong sense of self-efficacy among some FSWs, who prioritized their health over financial gain. However, this decision often resulted in economic losses, as clients seeking unprotected sex chose to patronize other sex workers [48, 53]. Similar findings have been reported in South Africa and Bangladesh, where women who exercised agency in refusing unsafe sex often faced reduced income or loss of clients, showing how structural and economic pressures intersect with personal health choices [38]– [39]. This reflects the influence of perceived barriers such as financial insecurity and client expectations [30, 36]. These findings illustrate how FSWs weigh health risks against economic realities, a behavioural process that the HBM effectively explains by linking risk perception, benefits of preventive actions, and internal confidence with the likelihood of adopting protective strategies.

Negotiation for alternative sexual styles emerged as a harm-reduction strategy, allowing FSWs to maintain client satisfaction while avoiding acts they perceived as unsafe. This behaviour reflects an awareness of personal risk, which aligns with the concept of perceived susceptibility in the HBM. FSWs recognized that certain sexual acts, particularly unprotected oral and Anogenital sexual activity, carried greater risks of STIs. In line with perceived severity, participants understood the possible consequences of engaging in such acts, including health complications and social and economic disruptions [30, 35]. This aligns with the findings of another study where it was found FSWs strategically redirect client preferences to safer practices to balance risk and financial stability [48]. Comparable negotiation strategies have been noted in Iran and the United States, where women sought to balance client satisfaction with their own health by offering substitutes for riskier sexual practices [37, 44].

The use of barrier methods, particularly condoms, was widely recognized as an essential risk prevention measure. FSWs acknowledged the effectiveness of condoms in preventing STIs, unwanted pregnancies, and complications from unsafe abortions. However, gaps remained in the consistent use of protective measures, particularly for orogenital sexual activity, indicating a need for enhanced education and intervention. These challenges emphasize the need for targeted interventions that address structural and interpersonal challenges. A systematic review of interventions in high-income countries found that multi-component strategies, particularly those designed and delivered by peers, significantly improved condom use and other safer sex practices [46]. Additionally, some FSWs displayed a sense of control over their sexual health choices, which reflects self-efficacy, the confidence in one’s ability to adopt protective behaviours even under challenging circumstances [30, 36]. Nevertheless, inconsistent condom use during orogenital sexual activity suggests the need for stronger cues to action, such as education, peer influence, or supportive messaging that reinforces the importance of protection in all sexual encounters.

Frequent medical check-ups were another key preventive strategy adopted by many FSWs. Regular screenings for STIs were particularly common following condom failures or instances of unprotected sex. From the HBM perspective, this behaviour reflects the participants’ perceived susceptibility to infection and their belief in the benefits of early detection and treatment. However, despite recognizing the seriousness of STIs (perceived severity), many FSWs encountered significant barriers to accessing care. These included stigma, discriminatory attitudes from healthcare providers, and fear of being judged, which discouraged some women from seeking formal health services. Such experiences align with perceived barriers within the HBM, which can prevent individuals from engaging in protective behaviours even when they acknowledge the risks [30, 36]. Evidence from Canada and South Africa confirms that stigma, criminalisation, and structural exclusion significantly undermine access to healthcare for sex workers, even when services are available [39, 43]. In this context, cues to action such as previous episodes of illness or recommendations from peers played a role in prompting some FSWs to seek testing.

In contrast, others avoided health facilities due to past negative encounters. The variation in responses also reflects differing levels of self-efficacy, as those with greater confidence in their ability to manage healthcare interactions were more likely to attend clinics [30, 35]. Studies from Bangladesh and Iran highlight similar patterns, where stigma and fear of punishment discourage sex workers from accessing care, reinforcing the need for stigma-free and accessible health systems [37]– [38]. These findings are consistent with another study that reported stigma to significantly affects health-seeking behaviour among sex workers and recommended more inclusive and non-judgemental healthcare environments [54]. Thus, reducing stigma in healthcare settings can improve FSWs’ willingness to seek timely medical care and improve their overall health outcomes.

Implications for practice and policy (HBM-informed interventions)

This study highlights several practical and policy directions that can help improve the health and well-being of FSWs. A crucial first step is to enhance awareness of risk. Many participants in this study recognised some of the dangers of oral and Anogenital sexual activity, but others did not fully connect these acts with infections such as HIV and other long-term complications. Similar gaps in knowledge and misconceptions about STI transmission have been reported among FSWs in Bangladesh and Iran, where low awareness of sexual health risks continues to heighten vulnerability [37]– [38]. Education programmes that explain these risks in simple and accessible ways are therefore needed. These programmes should also provide opportunities for discussion, so that FSWs can ask questions and relate the information to their own experiences.

In addition to risk awareness, it is essential to highlight the benefits of protection. Condoms and lubricants are often seen only as tools for vaginal sex, yet they are also effective and practical for oral and Anogenital sexual activity. Demonstrating how to use these methods comfortably and providing them consistently, at no cost, can help increase their adoption. Evidence from interventions in high-income settings shows that condom promotion is most effective when combined with empowerment and peer-led delivery, which helps normalise their use and reduces resistance from clients [46]. Showing positive examples of protection can shift attitudes from seeing condoms as a barrier to pleasure to seeing them as tools for safety and empowerment.

Reducing barriers is another key priority. Many participants described how stigma, cost, and poor access to services prevented them from protecting themselves. This is consistent with findings from South Africa and Canada, where structural barriers such as criminalisation, discrimination, and fear of mistreatment by healthcare workers discourage service uptake [39, 43]. Policies should therefore ensure that services are friendly to sex workers, located in places where they can easily be reached, and free or affordable. Health workers should also be trained to treat FSWs with respect and without judgment, as this will encourage more women to seek care and use preventive services. Also, building self-confidence is essential. Even when FSWs know the risks and have access to protection, they may lack the confidence to negotiate safer sex with clients. Training programmes can help build negotiation and refusal skills, giving FSWs the tools to say no to unsafe practices without losing their sense of dignity. Research indicates that economic dependence and gendered power imbalances hinder FSWs’ negotiating ability, whereas peer support networks and collective empowerment enhance their capacity to insist on safe practices [38, 44]. Support from peer champions, role models, and simple reminders—such as posters or messages—can also encourage safer choices and make protective behaviours part of everyday practice.

Study strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies is its focus on brothel-based FSWs, a group that is often left out of research even though they face high risks of HIV and other STIs. The study used FGD and IDI to collect data from participants to ensure diversity of opinions. The FGDs captured shared views within groups, while the IDIs created private spaces where women could speak more freely about personal experiences. Using the HBM as a guide further strengthened the study because it linked women’s perceptions, motivations, and coping strategies to their health behaviours. Despite these strengths, the study has some potential limitations. Using FGDs to collect information could have introduced social desirability effect among participants that may be shy to openly discuss sensitive issues related to this study. To address this, the research team ensured confidentiality and included IDIs; however, the influence of peer presence cannot be eliminated. We limited recruitment of FSWs to those in the brothel and four LGAs which might not fully capture all views among sex workers operating in Ibadan and in Nigeria. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights. It shows how brothel-based FSWs in Nigeria understand risks, what motivates them to take certain decisions, and the strategies they use to cope. These findings provide new evidence that can support programmes and policies aimed at improving the sexual health and safety of FSWs.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, this study shows that many FSWs are aware of the dangers of unprotected oral and Anogenital sexual activity, persistent financial pressures, client demands, and limited negotiating power often push them towards risky practices. Misconceptions about STIs and the stigma surrounding sex work further increase their vulnerability by discouraging open health-seeking behaviours. At the same time, the coping strategies described by FSWs—such as refusing unsafe practices, negotiating alternative sexual styles, consistent condom use, and seeking medical check-ups—reflect both agency and the daily trade-offs they must make between protecting their health and sustaining their livelihood.

From the context of the HBM, these findings contribute to existing knowledge by demonstrating how perceived risks, benefits, barriers, and self-efficacy interact in shaping the behaviours of FSWs within the brothel. This highlights the need for tailored interventions that not only address individual knowledge and practices but also tackle structural barriers, such as poverty, stigma, and limited access to healthcare.

Based on these findings, we recommend multi-level interventions. First, we advocate inclusion of sexual health education to FSWs irrespective of settings to promote accurate risk perception and correct misconceptions about risk of STIs and associated complications. Second, there should be aggressive promotion and distribution of condoms/other barriers and lubricants to prevent unprotected sex and associated health risks. Third, a stigma-free, accessible, and confidential healthcare services specifically designed to meet the needs of FSWs should be promoted and institutionalized in the country. Lastly, governments and other stakeholders such as NGOs and donors should promote economic empowerment initiatives that could reduce the financial vulnerability driving unsafe sexual practices. It is equally imperative to engage wide range of stakeholders including NGOs, healthcare providers, community-based organisations, law enforcement, and international development partners to collaborate and engage in promoting context specific interventions to reduce health risks associated with their sexual behaviours.

All participants included in the study were aged between 18 and 45 years, and the legal age of adulthood in Nigeria is 18, meaning all individuals were legally able to provide informed consent (Federal Republic of Nigeria, 1999). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection. For individuals with lower literacy levels, the consent process was verbally explained before signatures or thumbprints were obtained. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any stage without consequence, and confidentiality was ensured through the use of anonymised transcripts and secure data storage. No participants under the age of 16 were recruited into this study.
