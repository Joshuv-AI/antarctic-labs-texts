A Weaker Notion of Atomicity in Integral Domains
Mohamed Benelmekki, Brahim Boulayat

Abstract
. In classical factorization theory, an integral domain is called atomic if every nonzero nonunit element can be written as a finite product of irreducible elements. Here, we introduce and study a weaker notion of atomicity, which relaxes the requirement that all elements admit a factorization into irreducibles. Namely, we say that an integral domain is completely atomic if every nonunit divisor of an atomic element is also atomic. We further consider several factorization properties associated with this notion. Then, we investigate the basic properties of such domains, provide examples, and explore the behavior of the completely atomic property under standard constructions such as localization, polynomial rings, and D + M D+M constructions. Our results highlight the independence of the completely atomic property from other classical factorization properties and introduce an important class of integral domains that lies between atomic and non-atomic domains.

1.
Introduction

The classical theory of factorization in integral domains originates in the nineteenth-century work of Kummer, Dedekind, and Kronecker, whose efforts to restore unique factorization in algebraic number rings led to the creation of ideals and the foundational tools of modern commutative algebra. As the subject developed, attention naturally expanded from the uniqueness of factorization to
the more basic question of the existence of factorizations. By the mid-twentieth
century, the concepts of irreducible and prime elements had become standard, and
the notion of an
atomic domain
—a domain in which every nonunit factors
into irreducibles—emerged as the fundamental baseline in factorization theory. The notion of atomic domain was introduced by Cohn in
[
10
]
.

However, it soon became clear that atomicity is far too restrictive for many
natural classes of non-Noetherian domains. Valuation domains with dense value
groups, integer-valued polynomial rings, and an array of pullback constructions
often contain elements that admit no factorization at all. Throughout the
1970s–1990s, work by Anderson, Anderson, Zafrullah, and others systematically
demonstrated that large families of integral domains lie strictly below atomicity,
yet still exhibit meaningful factorization-like behavior (see
[
1
,
2
]
). This recognition led to the introduction of several weaker notions of atomicity, designed to measure how far a domain deviates from the classical atomic condition while still retaining
some structural control via irreducibles.

Among these weaker notions, three concepts have become central:
near-atomic
,
almost-atomic
, and
quasi-atomic
domains. These properties were introduced to address increasingly pathological examples, and they now form a rich area of research in factorization theory. The earliest of these notions is the
almost-atomic domain
, introduced by
Boynton and Coykendall. A domain
D
is almost atomic if every nonzero
nonunit can be made atomic by multiplication with finitely many irreducible
elements
[
4
]
.
The notion of a
near-atomic
domain was introduced by Lebowitz-Lockard in
[
22
]
. A domain is near-atomic if every nonunit divides an element that becomes atomic after multiplying by irreducibles. In this way, atomic behavior does not have to occur at the element itself, but appears one step above it in the divisibility poset. Near-atomicity provides
a useful bridge between atomic and almost-atomic. The weakest of the three notions, and in some sense the most conceptually
important, is the
quasi-atomic domain
, developed in work of Boynton and Coykendall. A domain is quasi-atomic if every nonunit divides
some atomic element
[
4
]
. These notions form the following strictly descending chain:

This condition does not require that any specific element factor into irreducibles, nor that such factorizations be unique; it only requires that the divisibility poset of the domain contain enough atomic elements so that every nonunit lies below at least one of them. However, aside from trivial cases, all these domains (including atomic domains) require the existence of at least one atom (irreducible element). In this paper, we propose a weaker notion of atomicity that does not require the existence of atoms, yet the atomic behavior can still appear in the divisibility structure, even when full atomicity is absent.

Definition 1.1
.

We say that an integral domain
D
is a
completely atomic domain
if every nonunit divisor of an atomic element of
D
is also atomic in
D
.

This definition provides a natural generalization of atomic domains, defining a class of integral domains that lies between atomic and non-atomic domains. For instance, an antimatter domain (that is, a domain without atoms) is obviously a completely atomic domain. Thus, unlike classical atomic domains, these domains do not require every nonunit to factor into irreducibles, which enables the study of factorization behavior in a broader context. At the same time, they allow the transfer of atomic behavior from elements to their divisors. On the other hand, the usual definitions of factorization properties in integral domains (such as UFD, HFD, FFD, and BFD) always assume that the domain is atomic
[
1
]
. A natural question then arises: what happens if this assumption is removed or replaced by a weaker condition such as completely atomic? In this context, Coykendall and Zafrullah introduced the notion of an unrestricted unique factorization domain (U-UFD), referring to domains in which every atomic element admits a unique factorization
[
13
]
. Every UFD is therefore a U-UFD, but the converse does not hold. An AP-domain (i.e., a domain in which every atom is prime) is also a U-UFD. More recently, in 2025, Du and Gotti introduced a related concept that does not require the domain to be atomic. They defined a domain to be an unrestricted finite factorization domain (U-FFD) if every atomic element admits only finitely many nonassociate factorizations
[
14
]
.

3.
Completely atomic RIDF-domains

In this section, we study the completely atomic and RIDF properties. We begin by outlining the motivation behind their introduction and then clarify how they interface with the U-FF property.

Recall that an integral domain
D
is called a completely atomic monoid if every nonunit divisor of an atomic element of
D
is atomic in
D
. It is immediate that every atomic domain (resp., monoid) is completely atomic. However, an antimatter domain is by definition completely atomic, yet it is certainly not atomic. For other examples of completely atomic domains that fail to be atomic, see Examples
3.3
,
3.15
,
4.2
, and
4.4
. Another subclass of completely atomic domains is the class of AP-domains, since every AP-domain is a U-UFD by
[
13
, Lemma 2.2]
. On the other hand, a completely atomic domain need not be a U-FFD. For instance, it is enough to consider an atomic domain that is not IDF.

Example 3.1
.

Consider the additive submonoid of
\mathbb{Q}_{+}

M=\{\,q\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}:q\geq 1\,\}\ \cup\ \{0\}.

Clearly, the monoid
M
is a BFM, hence it is atomic. In particular,
M
is a completely atomic monoid. However,
M
is not an IDF-monoid since
1+r
divides
3
in
M
for every
r\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
with
0\leq r<1
. Thus,
M
is not a U-FFM.

Now let
K
be a field and consider the Puiseux algebra
D:=K[M]
. Then
D
is an atomic domain which is not IDF, see
[
1
, Examples 2.7(a) and 4.1]
. Hence
D
is a completely atomic domain but not a U-FFD.

Definition 3.2
.

We say that a monoid
M
is a
restricted-irreducible-divisor-finite monoid
(RIDF-monoid) if every atomic element of
M
has only a finite number of nonassociate irreducible divisors. We say that an integral domain is a
restricted-irreducible-divisor-finite domain
(RIDF-domain) if its multiplicative monoid is a RIDF-monoid.

An IDF-domain is obviously a RIDF-domain. However, the converse is not true in general as the following example shows.

Example 3.3
.

Consider a GCD-domain
D
which is not an IDF-domain. For instance, fix a prime number
p
and let
G
be the subgroup of the additive group of rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
generated by the following set
\{\frac{1}{p^{k}}\;|\;k\in\mathbb{N}\}
. Then the group algebra
D:=\mathbb{Q}[G]
is not an IDF-domain by
[
7
, Example 3.8]
.

Now, let us agree that
D
is a RIDF-domain. Let
f
be an atomic element of
D
, say
f=u\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}^{e_{i}}
where
u\in U(D)
,
e_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, and the
f_{i}
’s are nonassocite irreducible elements of
D
. Since
D
is a GCD-domain, each

is prime in

. Therefore, the elements
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
are the only nonassociate irreducible (prime) divisors of

in

, and hence

satisfies the RIDF-property. Consequently,
D
is a RIDF-domain which is not an IDF-domain. Moreover, note that
D
is a completely atomic domain since every atomic element has a prime factorization in

, and consequently its divisors also admit such factorizations. Also, note that
D
is not atomic since
X-1
is not atomic in

.

In general, the RIDF-property doesn’t implies the completely atomic (and hence the U-FF) property as the following example shows (see also Example
4.2
).

Example 3.4
.

Let
p
and

two prime numbers such that
p\neq q
. Consider the following Puiseux monoid

M:=\bigg\langle\frac{1}{q},\frac{1}{p^{k}}\ \Big|\ k\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\rangle.

One can easily check that
\mathscr{A}(M)=\big\{\frac{1}{q}\big\}
. Therefore
M
is an IDF-monoid, and hence it is an RIDF-monoid. But
M
is not a completely atomic monoid. To see this, observe that
1=q\times\frac{1}{q}
, and then
1
has an atomic factorization in
M
. On the other hand, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have
1=p^{k}\times\frac{1}{p^{k}}
, and so
\frac{1}{p^{k}}
is a divisor of
1
in
M
. However,
\frac{1}{p^{k}}
has no irreducible divisor in

, for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. In particular,
\frac{1}{p^{k}}
is not atomic in
M
, for every

. Therefore,
M
is not a completely atomic monoid.

Note that there exist integral domains that fail to be completely atomic and are not RIDF either, showing that these two properties are independent and neither implies the other. The following example illustrates this observation.

Example 3.5

Let

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
D
be an integral domain. Then
D
is a U-FFD if and only if
D
is a completely atomic RIDF-domain.

Proof.

It is clear that an U-FFD is a RIDF-domain. Let
x
be an atomic element of
D
, and let
y\in D\setminus U(D)
and
z\in D
be such that
x=yz
. Since
D
has the U-FF property,
x
has only finitely many nonassociate factorizations (resp., divisors) in
D
,
and hence
y
does as well. In particular,
y
is also atomic in
D
. Thus,
D
is a completely atomic domain.

For the converse implication, assume that
D
is a completely atomic RIDF-domain. Then the atomic submonoid
A(D)
of
D
is a FFM since
D
is a RIDF-domain. Let
x\in D
be an atomic element. Then there exists a nonempty finite of nonassociate irreducible divisors of
x
in
D
, say
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that every factorization of
x
in
D
has the form
x=ua_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots a_{n}^{e_{n}}
where
u\in U(D)
and
e_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. Indeed, let
x=y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{m}
, where
0\neq y_{j}\in D
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
, be a factorization of
x
in
D
. For
j=1,\ldots,m
, if
y_{j}\notin U(D)
then
y_{j}
is atomic in
D
since
D
is completely atomic. But every irreducible divisor of
y_{j}
is also an irreducible divisor of
x
in
D
. Thus,
y_{j}=u_{j}a_{1}^{e_{1,j}}\cdots a_{n}^{e_{n,j}}
where
u_{j}\in U(D)
and
e_{i,j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
for every
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,m
. Consequently,

y_{1}y_{2}\cdots y_{m}=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{\sum_{j=1}^{m}e_{i,j}}.

Therefore, every factorization (divisor) of
x
, in
D
, lies in

. Since
A(D)
is a FFM,

must have a finite number of nonassociate factorisations in

.
∎

Definition 3.7
.

Let
D
be an integral domain and let
0\neq a\in D
. We say that

is a
Furstenberg element
of

if

has at least an irreducible divisor in

is a

domain if every nonunit divisor of a Furstenberg element of

is also Furstenberg.

A Furstenberg domain is obviously a completely Furstenberg domain. However, the converse is not true in general (for example, consider an antimatter domain). On the other hand, it is clear that an atomic (resp., a completely atomic) domain is a completely Furstenberg domain. However, there are completely Furstenberg domains that are not atomic domains.

Example 3.8
.

Let

4.
Ascent on PSP-domains and GL-domains

This section is devoted to investigating the ascent of the completely atomic, RIDF, and U-FF properties to polynomial rings. As an application of our results, we provide some examples showing that the reverse implications in Diagram
1
do not hold in general.

It is well-known that certain factorization properties, such as atomicity or the IDF-property, may or may not be preserved when passing from a domain
D
to the polynomial ring
D[X]
. Understanding the behavior of the completely atomic (resp., RIDF, U-FF) property under such extensions is therefore a natural problem to consider. We begin this section with the following result, which states that the completely atomic property ascends in the class of GL-domains when the domain
D
contains no atoms. Before, we recall that a PSP-domain is a GL-domain, and a GL-domain is an AP-domain. Then, by
[
13
, Lemma 2.2]
, an AP-domain (resp., a PSP-domain, a GL-domain) is a U-UFD. In particular, a GL-domain is a completely atomic (resp, a RIDF-domain, a U-FFD).

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
D
be an antimatter domain. The following conditions are equivalent.

(1)

D
is a GL-domain.

(2)

D[X]
is a completely atomic domain.

Proof.

First note that, since
D
is antimatter, no atomic element of

can lie in

.

(1)
\Rightarrow
(2) Assume that
D
is a GL-domain. Let

be an atomic element of

. Then

is a finite product of primitive (irreducible) element of

, and hence it is primitive since

is a GL-domain. Hence, it follows that every nonunit divisor of

in

is also primitive. Thus, by the degree argument, every nonunit divisor of

is atomic in

. Therefore,

is a completely atomic domain.

(2)
\Rightarrow
(1) Assume that

is a completely atomic domain. Let

and

be two primitive polynomials in

. We may assume that

and

are non-constants. Since

(resp.,

) is primitive, it has an atomic factorization in

. Thus,

that the polynomial ring
D:=R[X]
is a completely atomic domain which is not antimatter. Note that
D
is an IDF-domain, and hence a RIDF-domain. However, it is clear that
D
is not an atomic domain.

If
D
is not an antimatter domain the implication (2)
\Rightarrow
(1) in Proposition
4.1
may fails.

Example 4.3
.

Let
K
be a field and let
X
and
Y
be algebraically independent variables over
K
. Consider the numerical semigroup algebra
D:=K[Y^{2},Y^{3}]\subset K[Y].
We claim that
D[X]
is a completely atomic domain, but
D
is not a GL-domain.

To see that
D[X]
is completely atomic, observe that
D
is a finitely generated
K
-algebra, hence Noetherian. Therefore,
D[X]
is also Noetherian, and hence atomic. Consequently,
D[X]
is a completely atomic domain. On the other hand,
f:=(Y^{2}X-Y^{3})(Y^{2}X+Y^{3})\in D[X]
, which is a product of two primitive polynomials in
D[X]
, is not primitive since
f=Y^{4}(X^{2}-Y^{2})
. Then, it follows that
D
is not a GL-domain.

It is well-known that the properties of being antimatter and being MCD-finite are independent. In particular, Eftekhari and Khorsandi proved the existence of integral domains that are antimatter yet fail to be MCD-finite. More precisely, in
[
15
, Theorem 2.5]
, employing a modified variant of a powerful construction originally due to Roitman
[
23
]
, the authors show that for any integral domain
D
and any unit-closed subset
S\subseteq D
, one can construct a domain
R
that is not MCD-finite and whose set of atoms is precisely
S
. By taking
S=\emptyset
, one obtains a domain without atoms, i.e., an antimatter domain. Moreover, by
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
, the polynomial ring
R[X]
is not an IDF-domain.

We now present an example demonstrating that the properties of being a GL-domain, being an antimatter domain, and being non-MCD-finite can all occur simultaneously in a single integral domain.
Thus, we then apply Proposition
4.1
to get a completely atomic domain which is not an IDF-domain.

Example 4.4
.

[
18
, Section 4]

Let
\mathbb{F}_{2}
denote the finite field with two elements. Let

\mathscr{X}=\{X,Y,Z,T_{1},T_{2},T_{3},\dots\}
be an infinite set of algebraically independent indeterminates over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Consider the integral domain

R_{0}:=\mathbb{F}_{2}\bigg[\big\{X^{\alpha},Y^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\big\}\cup\Big\{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}T_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}:\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\ \forall\,i\in\mathbb{N}\Big\}\bigg].

For the set of monomials

J:=\bigg\{\prod_{i=1}^{\infty}T_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}:\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}_{0}^{+}\ \forall\,i\in\mathbb{N}\bigg\},

consider the following subring of
R_{0}
:

R:=\mathbb{F}_{2}\big[\big\{X^{\alpha},Y^{\alpha},Z^{\alpha}:\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}_{\geq 0}\big\}\cup\big\{X^{\alpha}T,Y^{\alpha}T,Z^{\alpha}T:(\alpha,T)\in\mathbb{Q}_{>0}\times J\big\}\big].

It is clear that
R
is an antimatter domain, and by
[
18
]
it is a GL-domain which is not MCD-finite. Moreover,
[
15
, Theorem 2.1]
implies that
D:=R[x]
, the polynomial ring over
R
in the indeterminate
x
, is not an IDF-domain. Also, observe that
D
is not atomic. On the other hand, it follows from Proposition
4.1
that the polynomial ring
D
is a completely atomic domain.

Recall that a GL-domain is a completely atomic domain (resp, a RIDF-domain, a U-FFD). In
[
14
]
, the authors show that the U-FF property does not
ascend to polynomial extensions. Next we prove that the completely atomic (resp., RIDF, U-FF) property ascends to polynomial rings in the class of PSP-domains.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
D
be an integral domain. Then the following statement hold.

(1)

If
D
is a GL-domain, then
D[X]
is a completely atomic domain.

(2)

If
D
is a PSP-domain, then
D[X]
is a RIDF-domain (resp., U-FFD).

Proof.

Assume that
D
is a GL-domain. Let
f\in D[X]\setminus D
be an atomic element of
D[X]
. First, let us argue the two following claims.

Claim 1.
The set of coefficients of
f
(resp., any atomic polynomial in
D[X]
) has a unique MCD element
c
up to associates (i.e., a GCD). Moreover,
c
has a prime factorization in
D
.

Proof of Claim 1.

If
f
is primitive then we are done. Otherwise, since
f
is atomic in
D[X]
, there exists
n,t\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
A:=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}\subseteq\mathscr{A}(D)
, and
F:=\{f_{1}\ldots,f_{t}\}\subseteq\mathscr{A}(D[X])\setminus D
such that

f=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}\right).

Since
f
is not a constant,
F\neq\emptyset
. On the other hand, since each
f_{i}
is primitive (irreducible) in
D[X]
and
D
is a GL-domain, the product
h:=\prod_{i=1}^{t}f_{i}
is primitive in
D[X]
. Then, the fact that
f
is not primitive implies that
A\neq\emptyset
. Set
c:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
. Therefore,
c
is an MCD of the coefficients of
f
because
f=ch
and
h
is primitive. It remains to show that
c
is unique up to associates. Let
c^{\prime}
be an MCD of the coefficients of
f
. Then, there exists a primitive polynomial
h^{\prime}\in D[X]
such that
f=c^{\prime}h^{\prime}
. Since
D
is a GL-domain, it is an AP-domain. Thus,
a_{i}
is prime in
D
, and hence in
D[X]
, for every
i=1,\ldots,n
. Then the equality
ch=c^{\prime}h^{\prime}
together with the fact that
h^{\prime}
is primitive implies that the prime element
a_{i}
divides
c^{\prime}
in
D
, for every
i=1,\ldots,n
. Hence,
c
divides
c^{\prime}
in
D
, which implies that
c
and
c^{\prime}
are associates in
D
. Therefore, the claim is proven.

Claim 2.
Every divisor
d\in D\setminus U(D)
of
f
has a prime factorization in
D[X]
.

Proof of Claim 2.
Let
d\in D\setminus U(D)
such that
f=df^{\prime}
for some
f^{\prime}\in D[X]
. Then
d
is a common divisor of the set of coefficients of
f
in
D
. Hence, by Claim 1,
d
divides
c
in
D
. Since
c
has a (unique) prime factorization in
D
,
d
has also a prime factorization in
D
, and hence in
D[X]
. The claim is then established.

At this point, we have the tools needed to prove our statements.

(1)
Assume that
D
is a GL-domain. Let
f
be an atomic element of
D[X]
. Since
D
is a completely atomic domain and a divisor-closed subring of
D[X]
, we may assume that
f\in D[X]\setminus D
. Now, let us prove that every nonunit divisor of
f
is atomic in
D[X]
. Let
g_{1}\in D[X]\setminus U(D)
such that
f=g_{1}g_{2}
for some
g_{2}\in D[X]
. If
g_{1}\in D
, the result follows from Claim 2. If
g_{1}
is primitive, by the degree argument, then
g_{1}
has an atomic factorization in
D[X]
. Otherwise,
g_{1}=bg_{0}
for some
b\in D\setminus U(D)
and
g_{0}\in D[X]
. Then, by Claim 1,
b
can be chosen so that
g_{0}
is a primitive polynomial because
b
is a divisor of
c
in
D
. In particular,
b
and
g_{0}
are both atomic in
D[X]
. This implies that
g_{1}
is atomic in
D[X]
as desired. Therefore,
D[X]
is a completely atomic domain.

(2)
Assume that
D
is a PSP-domain. Let
f
be an atomic element of
D[X]
. Since
D
is a RIDF-domain and a divisor-closed subring of
D[X]
, we may assume that
f\in D[X]\setminus D
. Note that
D
is a GL-domain since it is a PSP-domain. We need to show that
f
has only a finite number of nonassociate irreducible divisors in
D[X]
. Since
D
is a PSP-domain,
f
has only a finite number of nonassociate irreducible divisors in
D[X]
which are non-constants. Indeed, each non-constant irreducible divisor of
f
is primitive, and hence super-primitive, and then
[
19
, Lemma 3.1(3)]
implies that
f
has at most finitely many such divisors. Let
a_{0}\in D
an irreducible (prime) divisor of
f
in
D[X]
. As previously seen,
a_{0}
divides
c
in
D
, and hence
a_{0}
is associate to some prime divisor
a_{i}
, where
i=1,\ldots,n
, of
c
in
D
. Therefore,
f
has only finitely many nonassociate constant irreducible divisors in
D[X]
. Thus,
f
has the RIDF-property in
D[X]
. Consequently,
D[X]
is a RIDF-domain. To conclude the proof, we note that Proposition
3.6
, together with
(1)
and
(2)
, proves that
D[X]
is a U-FFD.
∎

Next, we apply Theorem
4.5
to construct a class of RIDF-domains that are not IDF-domains.
This construction further illustrates that the RIDF-property is strictly weaker than the IDF-property.

Example 4.6
.

Let
R
be a PSP-domain which is not IDF. For instance, consider the Puiseux algebra
R:=K[\mathbb{Q}_{+}]
, where
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[2^{n}]{q}:n\in\mathbb{N})
with
q>2
a prime number. Note that
R
is a GCD-domain, and so it is a PSP-domain. On the other hand,
D
is not an IDF-domain by
[
5
, Example 3.8]
.

Now, consider the polynomial ring
D:=R[X]
. Then, by Theorem
4.5
,
D
is a RIDF-domain. However,
D
is not an IDF-domain since
R
is not. Furthermore, observe that
D
is not atomic, as
R
contains elements (e.g., monomials and cyclotomic polynomials) that have no irreducible divisors; see
[
5
]
.

It is well known that every PSP-domain is a GL-domain. Also, a PSP-domain is an
MCD-finite domain
[
8
, Lemma 3.4]
. This naturally raises the question of whether the RIDF-property (resp., U-FF property) ascends within these classes of domains.

Question 4.7
.

Does the RIDF-property (resp., U-FF property) ascends in the classes of the GL-domains and MCD-finite domains?
