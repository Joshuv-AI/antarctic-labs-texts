Framing effects of health education materials on rural public intention for dog vaccination against rabies in China

Background

Rabies is a zoonotic infectious disease caused by the rabies virus, resulting in approximately 59,000 human deaths globally each year [1]. China is one of the countries most severely affected by rabies, where it consistently ranked among the top three deadliest infectious diseases from 2004 to 2014 [2]. The most recent peak of rabies outbreaks occurred in 2007, with a death toll of 3,300 cases [3]. Since then, the number of human rabies deaths has steadily declined each year, dropping to 170 cases in China by 2024 (Fig. 1.) [4], representing a 94.8% reduction from the peak. Rabies cases were predominantly concentrated in rural areas, accounting for 70% of the deaths, particularly in the southern provinces such as Guangxi, Guangdong, Hunan, and Guizhou (Fig. 2.) [3].

Although rabies has a mortality rate of 100%, it can be prevented and controlled through scientific and effective measures. Vaccination of both humans and dogs is crucial. The World Health Organization highlights large-scale dog vaccination as the only and most cost-effective solution for controlling and eliminating rabies [5]. China’s “National Zoonotic Infectious Disease Prevention and Control Plan (2022–2030)” proposes to gradually eliminate dog-transmitted rabies by 2030 [6]. However, the low vaccination rate of dogs in rural areas is a major obstacle to achieving this strategic goal. A recent survey in Guangxi, a rabies-endemic province, showed that only 19.1% of rural residents participated in canine immunization [7], far below the 70% immunization rate required to eliminate rabies [8]. Studies have shown that the public’s lack of awareness about the necessity of rabies vaccination for dogs is a significant hindrance to canine immunization [9]. Therefore, implementing more efficient canine rabies immunization education in rural areas is crucial for rabies prevention and control in China.

The effectiveness of public education is significantly influenced by the discourse used. Studies have found that information recipients exhibit differences in cognitive judgment and decision-making when faced with equivalent information framed differently, known as the “framing effects“ [10]. Many studies have explored the influence of different discourse on individual decision-making, particularly in areas such as risk decision-making [11], health [12], and pro-environmental behavior [13]. Given the paucity of studies examining how different discourse promote intentions to vaccinate dogs against rabies, this study, based on the goal framing and social norm theories, explores the persuasive effectiveness of different goal frames and social norms on rural dog owners’ willingness to vaccinate their dogs and the implication for the practice of health education towards dog immunization against rabies in rural areas.

Methods

Experiment design and grouping

Based on the goal framing and social norm theories, this study used a 2 × 2 factorial experiment design. The framing effect theory emphasizes that different ways of presenting information can influence individuals’ risk preferences and decision-making [14]. The framing effect includes risk framing, attribute framing, and goal framing [15]. In the field of health education, literature primarily focuses on goal framing. Studies have explored whether goal framing impacts individual health behaviors, including disease screenings (e.g., for breast cancer [16] and colorectal cancer [17]) and preventive measures (e.g., using sunscreen [18] or dental floss [19], engaging in regular exercise [20], and quitting smoking [21]). Goal framing examines how benefits and losses of a behavior influence individuals’ behavioral intentions. It includes two types: the gain frame and the loss frame. The “gain frame” emphasizes the positive consequences of performing a specific behavior, while the “loss frame” highlights the negative outcomes of not performing it.

The goal framing information often points to the individual’s own cognition or behavior. However, the social learning research paradigm suggests that the expectations or actions of others also influence individual’s behaviors. Guided by this paradigm, the social norm theory has been developed. A social norm is a rule or standard that guides or restricts action understood by group members [22]. Research on social norms mainly used a theory proposed by Cialdini and others, which identifies two types of norms: descriptive social norm(DSN) and injunctive social norm(ISN) [23]. An ISN refers to the prescriptive social expectations perceived by an individual, or the approval or disapproval of a certain behavior by the majority of the social group. An ISN guides people’s behaviors by emphasizing the moral judgment of whether a behavior is good or bad. On the other hand, a DSN refers to the typicality and universality of the behavior in question-that is, what people actually do. When an individual perceives that most people are doing something, he or she is also likely to carry out this behavior [23].

The social norm concept has been used to explain behaviors in many fields, ranging from consumption to health promotion. In the context of vaccine inoculation, many studies report solid evidence that social norms influence people’s vaccination intentions and behaviors. Several surveys indicate that social norms are significant factors affecting people’s willingness to receive the recombinant herpes zoster vaccine [24] and COVID-19 vaccine [25–27].

In addition, Cialdini found in health interventions, utilizing DSN is usually effective for a common healthy behavior in society. However, when the public is unfamiliar with the health-promoting behavior, providing information on DSN may be less effective, as most people have not been engaged in the desired behaviors yet [22]. Therefore, Sparkman and Walton [28] suggest that the concept of dynamic descriptive norm(DDSN) needs to be utilized. They believe that DDSNs are behavior standards formed by the behaviors shown by group members over time, such as “In the past year, 75% of hotel customers will reuse towels, and this proportion is continuing to increase”. DDSN highlight changes in behavior over time and prevalence of behaviors. They create positive associations, prompt people to predict the extent of behaviors, and emphasize the appropriateness of the mainstream trend, thereby influencing behavior change. Currently, the public’s lack of awareness about the necessity of rabies vaccination for dogs in rural areas of China [9] reflects strong negative DSN. It is reasonable to adopt the concept of DDSN in this study.

Based on the above analysis, the study used goal framing (gain frame/loss frame) information and social norm information (DDSN/ISN) as independent variables, with the willingness to immunize dogs against rabies as the dependent variable. Therefore, a 2 × 2 factorial experimental design was adopted, and participants were randomly assigned to four groups [i.e., gain frame-DDSN group (Group1), gain frame-ISN group (Group 2), loss frame- DDSN group (Group 3), and loss frame-ISN group (Group 4)].

Participant and experiment procedure

According to the sample size estimation formula for continuous variables, the minimum sample size was calculated using G*power 3.1.9.7 software. Based on Cohen’s analysis [29] and prior literature [30], with a medium effect size (Effect size f = 0.25, power = 80%) set for the 2 × 2 interaction effect, a total of 128 participants were required for the experiment. Considering factors such as the attrition rate, the final total number of participants was determined to be 220, with 55 participants in each group. The study mainly targeted members of dog-owning households in rural areas. Based on prior literature [31, 32] and regulations on residence in China [33], the participants were required to meet the following conditions: 1.The household registration location is rural, or the individual has been living in rural areas for ≥ 6 months at the time of the survey. 2. Informed consent and voluntary participation in the experiment. 3.The household keeps one or more cats or dogs. 4. The individual is mentally sound, able to independently answer questionnaire information, and over 18 years old. 5. No more than one person per household.

Participants were randomly assigned to different experimental groups. Before the intervention, participants were informed about the purpose of the experiment, the procedure, research ethics, etc., and they signed an informed consent form. Subsequently, four different health education videos were distributed to the four groups, with each participant watching only one of them. After watching, they filled out a questionnaire. The experiment ended after participants submitted the questionnaire. The entire experiment lasted approximately 10 min.

The study performed a four steps sampling. At the first stage, based on prior literature, all provinces were classified into two group according their rabies rates, high rate area and low-medium rate area. Then two provinces were randomly selected in each area. At the second and third stage, two counties in each province and two villages in each county were randomly selected. The sample size in these three stages was determined by the study’s budget. At the fourth stage, local health professionals selected three or four participants in each village based on the filtering criteria. The first household was selected according to their proximity to the village administration office. And subsequent household was visited by going to the next closest front door until the target number of respondents was interviewed. Guided by the local health professionals, the participants watched the videos and filled out the questionnaire on the platform of Wenjuanxin(https://www.wjx.cn/ ) during the visiting.

Questionnaire and variable measurement

The questionnaire included three types of questions: The first type was about the demographic characteristics of the participants, including gender, age, income, and education level; the second type was manipulation check items for the health education materials; the third type was questions about the willingness to immunize dogs against rabies, awareness about rabies, and immunization experiences.

The manipulation check items for the health education materials asked participants to make two judgement: the content of the materials emphasized the dangers of not vaccinating or the benefits of vaccinating, focused on that more and more people are vaccinating their dogs against rabies or vaccinating dogs against rabies is a commendable behavior.

A scale used by Nan et al. [34] was adapted to measure the experience of dog immunization: “Has your dog been vaccinated against rabies?“, “Do you often take your dog to get rabies vaccinations?“, “Do you take your dog for rabies vaccinations every year?” The answer options were “yes or no,” valued at 1 and 0. The scores of the participants on the three questions will be added and formed a composite index for their measurement.

Similarly, a scale adapted from Nan et al. [34] measured the intention to immunize dogs against rabies: “I am willing to collect and learn more information about dog immunization,” “I am willing to take my dog to get vaccinated against rabies soon,” “I plan to take my dog to get vaccinated against rabies.” The answers were on a five-point Likert scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” valued from 1 to 5. The scores of the participants on the three questions will be added and formed a composite index for their measurement. The questions about awareness of rabies included: “I understand the relevant knowledge about rabies,” “I understand the knowledge about rabies immunization.” The answers were on a five-point Likert scale from “completely do not understand” to “fully understand” valued from 1 to 5. The scores of the participants on the two questions will be added and formed a composite index for their measurement.

Health education materials

The experiment provided participants with four different videos for rabies vaccination for dogs. All four videos included an overview of health information about rabies and rabies vaccines for animals. The design of the health information materials for the goal framing modeled after the research by Nan et al. [35]. The gain frame focused on the positive outcomes of taking action, while the loss frame highlighted the negative consequences of not taking action. The design of the social norm materials followed the example of the research by Katherine et al. [36]. The dynamic descriptive frame emphasized the common behaviors or future trends of the majority in society, while the injunctive frame emphasized the social expectations stipulated by legal provisions. Additionally, the information on rabies and rabies vaccination for animals in the materials mainly came from the “China Animal Epidemic Prevention Law” and the “WHO Expert Consultation on Rabies“ [8]. Considering the characteristics of information reception by the rural public, all the materials were made into short videos. To avoid interference from the amount of information and presentation style on the participants, the scripts for the four different types were kept consistent in length, each was around 200 words. The animation characters, scene design, font type, font size, color, and layout were roughly the same across the four groups, and the video duration was controlled to be around 1 min and 10 s (see supplementary materials for details).

Control variables considering

Research has found that demographic variables [37, 38], dog immunization experiences [7], the emotional bond between owners and dogs [39], knowledge of location of veterinary office and dog breed [37], and awareness of rabies [40] may influence the willingness to vaccinate dogs against rabies. This study considers demographic variables, awareness about rabies, and dog immunization experiences as control variables. Moreover, the price of dog immunization services could be an important factor affecting the public’s decision to vaccinate their dogs. However, China is currently vigorously encouraging dog immunization, often providing free vaccine against dog rabies. Therefore, the price of dog immunization services does not pose a significant barrier to the vaccination decision for most rural Chinese residents. The emotional bond between owners and dogs is not included because the main purpose of keeping dogs in rural areas of China is for guarding homes, not as pets. The knowledge of location of veterinary office is not included because it could mainly be reflected by rabies awareness [37]. The dog breed is not included because it is not applicable in China, where most of dog owners in rural area are breeding only one breed, the canis lupus familiaris.

Data analysis

For randomization checks, chi-square analysis was used to test the differences in demographic variables across groups. And analysis of variance was conducted to access differences in awareness of rabies and dog immunization experiences across groups. These two analyses were used to identify possible control variables too. To analyze the significance of differences in responses among different types of health education materials, the initial groups were regrouped into goal framing groups and social norm groups, and then chi-square tests were used for analysis.

Multifactorial analysis of variance or generalized linear model analysis was used to analyze the main effects and interaction effects of the experimental manipulations. The willingness to vaccinate dogs was the dependent variable, and experimental conditions and control variables were the independent variables. Before analysis, the grouping variables was regrouped into goal framing groups and social norm groups. For the above tests, the significance criterion is set at P < 0.05. All analysis was conducted using SPSS.

Results

Descriptive statistics of participants

The experiment obtained 203 valid questionnaires. Among them, there were 51 participants in Group 1, 50 in Group 2, 52 in Group 3 and 50 in Group 4. Overall, the sample had a higher proportion of females (67%) than males; the age of respondents was mainly between 36 and 45 years old ༈42%༉; the education level was concentrated in high school, technical secondary school, or vocational school, accounting for 44% of the sample; the highest proportion of households had an annual income of 20,000 to 60,000 yuan (41%) (Table 1). The average score of participants’ willingness to immunize dogs was 13.55, with a standard deviation of 1.978; the average score of dog immunization experiences was 1.57, with a standard deviation of 1.34; the average score of awareness about rabies was 6.36, with a standard deviation of 2.37.

Reliability and validity of measurements

The reliability of the three items measuring the willingness to vaccinate was tested, and the reliability coefficient value was 0.909, indicating good internal consistency and stability. Then, factor analysis was used to test the validity of the willingness to vaccinate, and the results showed that the KMO value of the willingness to vaccinate was 0.745, indicating that data can be effectively extracted. The chi-square value of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001). The cumulative variance explained after rotation was 84.6%, so the variable has high discriminant validity.

The reliability of the three items measuring dog immunization experiences was tested, and the reliability coefficient value was 0.890, indicating good internal consistency and stability, and can be used for further analysis. At the same time, factor analysis was used to test the validity of dog immunization experiences, and the results showed that the KMO value of dog immunization experiences was 0.72, indicating that data can be effectively extracted. The chi-square value of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001). The cumulative variance explained after rotation was 82%, so the variable has high discriminant validity.

The reliability of the two items measuring knowledge about rabies was tested, and the reliability coefficient value was 0.91, indicating good internal consistency and stability, and can be used for further analysis. At the same time, factor analysis was used to test the validity of knowledge about rabies, and the results showed that the KMO value of knowledge about rabies was 0.65, indicating that data can be effectively extracted. The chi-square value of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001). The cumulative variance explained after rotation was 91.5%, so the variable has high discriminant validity.

Only one factor was extracted in each above analysis.

Randomization check and control variable screening

The results of the chi-square analysis (Table 1) show that there are no significant differences in the gender, age, education level, and household income of the participants among the four groups. The results of the analysis of variance (Table 1.) show that there are significant differences in dog immunization experiences among the groups (F = 5.038, p = 0.002), while there are no significant differences in awareness of rabies among the groups (F = 2.096, p = 0.102). It can be said that the randomization of the participants was successful. Dog immunization experiences were included as a control variable in the subsequent analysis.

Manipulation check of educational materials

The manipulation check of the health education materials for goal framing and social norms shows that out of 101 participants who watched the video using the gain frame, 99 believed that the frame emphasized the many benefits of vaccinating dogs against rabies; out of 102 participants who watched the video using the loss frame, 84 agreed that the information emphasized the dangers of not vaccinating. The differences in responses between the two framing videos were significant (Pearson chi-square value = 134.261, P < 0.001). When the video described DDSNs, 94 out of 103 participants believed that the frame emphasized that more and more people are vaccinating their dogs against rabies; When the video material used the ISNs frame, 93 out of 100 participants agreed that vaccinating dogs against rabies is a commendable behavior. The differences in responses between the videos containing the two different types of norms were significant (Pearson chi-square value = 144.104, P < 0.001). Therefore, the manipulation of the educational materials was validated.

Results of intervention

Running the two-factor ANOVA, the results showed that Levene’s Test for the Equality of Error Variances was significant [F (17, 185) = 2.871, p = 0.000]. Therefore, this study chose the generalized linear model for data analysis. The results showed that the Omnibus test was significant, likelihood ratio chi-square = 14.849, degrees of freedom = 3, p = 0.002, indicating that the model is significant. The model effect test showed that the interaction between goal framing and social norms was significantly associated with the willingness to immunize dogs against rabies (Wald chi-square = 5.062, p = 0.024). Further simple effect analysis showed (Table 2) that under the DDSN, the willingness to vaccinate dogs against rabies induced by the gain frame was significantly higher than that of the loss frame (Wald chi-square = 9.937, p = 0.002, mean difference = 1.18). However, under the ISN, there was no significant difference in the willingness of rural residents to vaccinate their dogs between different goal framing health intervention materials (p = 0.958). At the same time, as shown in Table 3, under the gain frame, the persuasiveness of DDSN was stronger compared to ISN (Wald chi-square = 10.573, p = 0.001, mean difference = 1.23). However, under the loss frame, there was no significant difference in the impact of different social norm levels (p = 0.938). Fig. 3. visually plots the means to illustrate the interaction effects.

Discussion and recommendations

This study is the first to examine the impact of goal-framing information and social norm information interventions on the willingness of rural dog owners to vaccinate their dogs against rabies. The results show that after controlling for prior dog immunization experiences, the combination of DDSN with a gain frame is more effective in promoting the willingness to vaccinate dogs against rabies compared to combining the dynamic descriptive norm with a loss frame or combining the gain frame with injunctive norm information.

According to the Prospect Theory [41], individuals tend to prefer maintaining the status quo and avoiding risks when information is framed as “gains”. Conversely, a loss frame encourage people to take risks to achieve potential goals [41]. Rothman et al. extended this with the prevention-detection framework [42].They suggest that the loss frame is more effective for detection behaviors such as disease screenings, where the focus is on avoiding the negative consequences. However, for proactive health behaviors, such as disease prevention, the gain frame is more persuasive because it emphasizes the positive outcomes of action. Therefore, vaccination information should be more persuasive when the gain frame is used to highlight the benefits of vaccination [43]. However, some argued that because vaccination can be associated with potential side effects and risks, the loss frame could be more effective [44, 45]. Marcela et al. conducted a systematic review of empirical studies on goal framing in the field of vaccination found that the relative effects of “gain” and “loss” frames vary in the literature, depending not only on perceived risk but also on the characteristics of the information recipients and situational factors [43].

The results of this study are consistent with the findings of Marcela et al. and partially support the “prevention-detection framework”. Now, most people in rural areas lack awareness of rabies vaccines for dogs [9] and are less concerned about side effects.

In the field of social norm research, many scholars proposed, from the perspective of the cognitive process, that DSN and ISN influence people’s behaviors through different pathways [23]. They suggest that DSN play a role in the decision-making process of people by triggering heuristic thinking by providing implicit “social evidence”, while ISN by motivating people’s attempt to establish and maintain meaningful relationships with others [46].

Multiple studies report that unfamiliar, uncertain, and ambiguous situations can enhance the influence of DSN [47, 48]. The results of this study provide new evidence supporting this theory. Currently, most of the public lacks a good understanding of canine rabies vaccines [9]. Additionally, the results of this study further expand this theory by indicating that its effect is conditional because under the loss frame, there was no significant difference in the impact between DDSN and ISN.

This study is the first to introduce the concept of DDSN into the field of health research, and the experimental results validate the applicability of this concept. The potential role of this concept in unfamiliar or new health interventions for the public warrants further attention in future research.

The decline in rabies-related deaths in China is due to the active prevention practice. The government document “The Situation of Rabies Prevention and Control in China” [49] reported the following practice: A multi government departmental prevention and control coordination mechanism for rabies was established. Special law legislation, vaccination campaigns, awareness programs, reporting systems were performed continuously to strengthen the management of dog ownership behavior, the measures for the prevention and control of human and animal rabies, the quality supervision of human rabies vaccines and passive immunization preparations.

Our findings shade lights on promoting health education for dog rabies immunization. In promoting dog rabies immunization, using either the gain frame or loss frame alone, or relying solely on DDSN or ISN alone, does not necessarily produce different effects. However, combining the gain frame with DDSN information can significantly enhance dog owners’ willingness to vaccinate their dogs. Specifically, while emphasizing the positive outcomes of regularly or repeatedly vaccinating dogs, such as improving animal immunity, protecting the health of oneself, dogs, and family members, and helping to eliminate rabies, it is also beneficial to share stories of the advantages gained from vaccinating dogs. These vivid narratives can tap into individuals’ motivations, thus enhancing the persuasiveness of health education on dog rabies vaccination. Additionally, sharing information about the prevalence of dog rabies vaccination or future vaccination trends can subtly shift individuals’ perceptions and attitudes towards vaccination.

This study has some limitations. First, the study sample has a high proportion of educated and female participants from rural areas, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to improve the representativeness to verify the replicability and applicability of the results. Second, this study did not set up a baseline survey or control group, meaning participants’ pre-existing willingness to immunize dogs against rabies and other factors such as dog gender [37], confinement level and distance from vaccination center [40] may confound the conclusions. Third, the reliance on self-reported intention rather than actual vaccination behavior and the brief duration of the intervention videos may affect the strength and generalizability of conclusions. Future research is suggested to further investigate the long-term effects of such materials.

Conclusions

This study explores the persuasive effectiveness of different goal frames and social norms on rural dog owners’ willingness to vaccinate their dogs. The results show that using either the gain frame or loss frame alone, or relying solely on DDSN or ISN alone, does not necessarily produce different effects. However, combining the gain frame with DDSN information can significantly enhance dog owners’ willingness to vaccinate their dogs.
