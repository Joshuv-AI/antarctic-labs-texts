RCN1 helps to alleviate the damage of drought stress to rice young spike differentiation
Authors: Xinyi Feng, Xintong Zhong, Jiamin Wen, Yu Liu, Yan Wang, Manrong Zha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought is a major constraint to rice (Oryza sativa L.) yield. The young spike differentiation stage is crucial for yield determination, exhibiting heightened sensitivity to soil moisture deficits. RICE CENTRORADIALIS 1 (RCN1), a TERMINAL FLOWER-like gene, is a key regulator of young spike differentiation and may mediate drought-induced delays in floral transition in rice. This study examines the effect of drought stress during the young spike differentiation stage on rice spike structure and investigates the role of RCN1 in this process.ResultsDrought stress markedly suppresses branch differentiation, especially secondary branches. Compared to the wild type (WT), the spike structure of rcn1 mutants was more severely compromised under drought conditions, the ABA-mediated protection of spike structure was also reduced in the rcn1 mutants. Key flowering regulators, Ehd1 and Hd1, were implicated in the regulation of young spike differentiation under drought stress, with the drought response of Ehd1 negatively modulated by RCN1. Furthermore, RCN1 influenced the expression of branch-promoting genes, including LAX1, RCN2 and RCN4 under drought. RCN1 displayed high sensitivity in rice roots, the increase rate of new roots in rcn1 mutants was lower than in WT under 10% PEG-6000 treatment. Under 15% PEG-6000 treatment, rcn1 mutants showed lower survival rate and ABA content compared to WT.ConclusionsRCN1 contributes to the maintenance of spike structure under drought stress by promoting second branch formation and affecting the drought response of some spike differentiation-related genes (Ehd1, LAX1, RCN2 and RCN4). Additionally, RCN1 enhances drought tolerance of rice by promoting abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and facilitating new root germination, thereby indirectly reducing yield losses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08716-2.Keywords: Rice, Drought stress, RCN1, Spike differentiation, Yield

Introduction

Drought is a major environmental constraint limiting crop production worldwide [1]. Even in rainy regions, uneven rainfall leads to seasonal water shortages [2, 3]. This spatial and temporal variability in water availability poses a significant threat to rain-fed agriculture. Rice is the main grain crop in the world. Rice (Oryza sativa L.), a staple food for a large part of the global population, is particularly vulnerable to drought due to its high-water requirement and sensitivity to soil water deficit [4]. In South Asia, drought can cause yield losses of up to 40%, with losses exceeding 80% in approximately 22% of rain-fed rice-growing areas [5]. Although drought stress adversely affects rice at all growth stages, yield losses are most pronounced when it occurs during the reproductive phase [6, 9]. This phase encompasses a series of high water-sensitive developmental processes, and drought during this period can reduce grain yield by up to 60% [7, 10, 11]. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which reproductive-stage drought impacts rice yield is crucial for developing strategies to mitigate these losses.

Rice grain yield is determined by three main components: effective panicle number, grain number per panicle, and 1000-grain weight [12, 14]. Drought stress limits the signal transduction, stomatal activity, and photosynthetic performance, thereby affecting the plant development and yield [15, 17]. Drought stress during the early reproductive stage can induce premature leaf senescence, curtailing photosynthetic productivity later and shortening the grain-filling period [18, 20]. When drought occurs later, during flowering and grain-filling, it reduces spikelet fertility and assimilate accumulation, leading to fewer and lighter grains [21, 25]. Drought stress during the early reproductive stage (young spike differentiation stage), can impede the development of branches and spikelets, resulting in a reduced grain number per panicle [26, 29]. Even mild drought stress that does not visibly affect overall biomass can severely inhibit secondary branch formation (up to 70% reduction), ultimately decreasing spikelet number by 45% [7, 27].

Spike architecture, which encompasses primary and secondary branching, is a crucial agronomic trait that significantly contributes to rice grain yield. The young spike differentiation stage typically begins about a month before heading and is completed in about 2 weeks. Following the floral transition, the inflorescence meristem gives rise to branches and spikelets [29, 30], and the morphogenesis of the inflorescence largely determines the final grain output. TERMINAL FLOWER 1 (TFL1)/CENTRORADIALIS (CEN)-like genes, belong to the phosphatidylethanolamine-binding proteins (PEBPs) family, play important roles in determining inflorescence architecture, the tfl1 mutant exhibits earlier flowering and produces fewer flowers [31]. RICE CENTRORADIALIS 1 (RCN1) is a TFL1 -homologous gene in rice, which plays a dual role in regulating both floral transition and spike differentiation of rice. Overexpression of RCN1 significantly increases the number of secondary branches and spikelets per spike, whereas rcn1 mutants display smaller panicles and earlier heading date [32, 33]. Beyond its role in development, RCN1 has been implicated in stress responses; a previous study reported that RCN1 affects rice resistance to salt stress [34]. Our own prior research suggests that loss of RCN1 function alters the effect of drought on rice heading date [35]. In this study, we aimed to further elucidate the role of RCN1 in the response of rice spike differentiation to drought stress. We found that drought stress during the young spike differentiation stage severely impairs yield, primarily by inhibiting secondary branch formation. RCN1 mitigates this drought-induced damage, in part by affecting the drought response of EARLY HEADING DATE 1 (Ehd1) and some branch-promoting genes. RCN1 also improves rice drought tolerance by promoting ABA synthesis and new root germination, which could indirectly reduce yield losses under drought stress.

Results

The effect of drought on spike structure is stronger in rcn1 mutants than in WT plants

To observe more precisely the effects of RCN1 on spike morphology, RCN1-OE plants and rcn1 mutants were used. Compared to the wild type (WT), the RCN1-OE plants exhibited a denser spike morphology, whereas rcn1 mutants exhibited a smaller spike morphology (Figure S1a). Quantitative analysis of spike structure revealed that both spike length and the number of primary branches were significantly decreased in rcn1 mutants, while no significant differences were observed between RCN1-OE and WT (Figure S1b, c). In contrast with WT, the number of secondary branches was significantly increased in RCN1-OE plants and substantially decreased in rcn1 mutants (Figure S1d). Total spikelet number per panicle followed a similar trend as branch number in both genotypes (Figure S1e, f). These results indicated that RCN1 plays distinct roles in promoting branch formation, loss of RCN1 protein function significantly suppresses rachis elongation and primary branch initiation, whereas elevated RCN1 expression exerts only a limited effect on these two processes, despite strongly promoting secondary branching and spikelet formation.

To investigate whether RCN1 influences rice spike architecture under drought stress, we subjected WT, two rcn1 mutants, and RCN1-OE transgenic plants to different degrees of drought treatments. Given that drought can alter floral transition, affecting vegetative growth duration and indirectly influencing inflorescence development, we initiated drought treatments after the onset of young spike differentiation to minimize confounding effects from vegetative growth. Specifically, plants were subjected to moderate (50%-70% soil relative water content, SRWC) or severe (30%-50% SRWC) drought stress starting from stem elongation (Figure S2).

The impact of drought on spike structure was quantified as the percentage reduction in branch and spikelet numbers relative to normal water controls. The methodology for calculating the decline percentage is as follows: (number of branches/spikelets in normal water - number of branches/spikelets in drought stress)/ number of branches/spikelets in normal water × 100%. Drought-stressed plants consistently produced smaller panicles than their normal water counterparts (Fig. 1a, Table S1). The spike length of WT was shortened by 3.2% and 5.7% under moderate drought and severe drought stress, respectively (Fig. 1b, c). The number of primary and secondary branches in WT were 6% and 13% lower under moderate drought stress than under normal water conditions, and there were 18.7% and 68% decreases under severe drought stress. Notably, rcn1 mutants were more severely affected than WT across all parameters. The shortening of spike length in the rcn1 mutants was nearly twice that of the WT plants. Under moderate drought, the primary branches of two rcn1 mutant plants were reduced by 13.3% and 13.9%, respectively, and the number of secondary branches were decreased by 29% and 32%, which was more than twice the decline of the WT plants. Under severe drought stress, the number of branches on rcn1 mutant plants decreased by 1.5 times compared with WT plants. The primary branches of rcn1 mutants were 37.3% and 30.6% lower, and the secondary branch number were 92.6% and 92% lower (Fig. 1b, c). The number of spikelets showed the same trendline of change with branch number under different water treatments. These results indicate that drought has a detrimental effect on rice spike differentiation, with greater harm observed on secondary branches compared to primary branches. However, the RCN1-OE transgenic plants did not show a reduced decline percentage of branches under drought conditions compared to the WT plants (Figure S3, Table S2), suggesting that basal RCN1 expression in WT may be sufficient for its protective role during spike differentiation.

RCN1 knockout impairs ABA-mediated protection of spike structure during drought stress

To evaluate the role of ABA in young spike differentiation under drought stress, we sprayed water (control) or 20 µM ABA solution on the leaf surface of WT and two rcn1 mutants subjected to drought. Exogenous ABA treatment significantly upregulated ABA content in leaves (Figure S4). In WT plants, exogenous ABA application alleviated the drought-induced reduction in primary and secondary branches from 10% to 2.9% and from 20% to 6.7%, respectively (Fig. 2, Table S3). In rcn1 mutants (rcn1-4、rcn1-11), primary branch reductions under drought were 13.9% and 15.7% without ABA, and improved to 9.6% and 7.1% with ABA treatment. Secondary branch reductions in rcn1-4 and rcn1-11 were 50% and 46.7% under drought alone and improved to 25% and 28.9% with exogenous ABA (Fig. 2, Table S3). These results indicate that exogenous ABA can mitigate drought-induced damage to branching, but the protective effect is attenuated in the rcn1 mutants compared to the WT. This suggests that the role of ABA in protecting spike differentiation under drought stress is partially dependent on functional RCN1 protein.

RCN1 affecting the drought responses of Ehd1, LAX1, RCN2 and RCN4

To elucidate the effect of drought stress on the expression of spike differentiation-related genes, we measured their expression levels by qPCR (Fig. 3, Figure S5). We focused on Ehd1, Hd1, and Hd3a, which are florigen genes that participate in the process of drought stress inhibiting floral transition [36]. However, these genes have also been reported to regulate spike differentiation independently of floral transition [37]. The qPCR result showed that Hd3a transcript levels remained throughout the spike differentiation stage (9–10 W), and were not significantly altered by drought (Fig. 3a). In contrast, Ehd1 expression was markedly affected by both drought and RCN1 genotype. Under normal water conditions, Ehd1 transcript levels were comparable between WT and rcn1 mutants. However, under drought stress, Ehd1 expression in rcn1 mutants peaked at 10 weeks, reaching levels approximately three-fold higher than in WT (Fig. 3b). Hd1 expression increased significantly between 8 W and 10 W. Under drought conditions, about 3-fold higher than under normal water conditions, but no significant differences were observed between WT and rcn1 mutants (Fig. 3c). These results suggest that the florigen genes Hd1 and Ehd1 could respond to drought during the spike differentiation stage, may be involved in the spike structure regulation, and RCN1 can inhibit the induction of drought to Ehd1.

LAX SPIKE 1 (LAX1), RICE CENTRORADIALIS 2 (RCN2), and RICE CENTRORADIALIS 4 (RCN4) play similar functions in promoting rice branching [29]. Results showed that drought can inhibit the expression of these three genes (Fig. 3d, e, f). Under drought stress, the expression of LAX1, RCN2, and RCN4 in the rcn1 mutants was significantly lower than that in WT, but not distinctly different under normal water (Fig. 3d, e, f). These results indicate that RCN1 influences the drought response of LAX1, RCN2, and RCN4.

RCN1 expression in roots is highly responsive to drought

Our previous study revealed that RCN1 is most abundantly expressed in rice roots, suggesting potential root-specific functions [35]. In this study, we further investigated the dynamics of RCN1 expression in roots and leaves under drought stress. To collect roots more easily and test the RCN1 response to drought stress, rice plants are grown hydroponically, and water stress is induced by 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG-6000). The result of qRT-PCR indicates that RCN1 transcript levels in roots increased markedly from 8 W to 11 W, throughout the whole young spike differentiation period (Fig. 4a), while RCN1 expression in leaves was transiently upregulated in 8 W (Fig. 4b). Moreover, a chronic ABA treatment was used in the spike differentiation period to explore the difference in ABA response of RCN1 in roots and leaves. Quantitative RT-PCR results show that ABA significantly induced RCN1 expression in roots but had only a marginal effect in leaves (Fig. 4c, d). These results indicate that RCN1 in roots is highly sensitive to both drought and ABA signals.

Given the drought-responsive expression of RCN1 in roots, we hypothesized that RCN1 may regulate root growth under drought stress. To test this, we compared root traits between WT and rcn1 mutants using the hydroponic system with 10% PEG-6000 treatment during young spike differentiation. The results showed that drought stress promoted the new root formation in both WT and rcn1 mutants. However, the rate of new root germination in rcn1 mutants was lower than in WT under drought conditions (Fig. 5a, b). Starting from 9 days after PEG treatment, WT plants consistently produced more new roots than the rcn1 mutants. Exogenous ABA application markedly accelerated new root germination in rcn1 mutants but had no significant effect on WT (Fig. 5c, d). Fluridone (ABA synthesis inhibitor) suppressed new root formation in both genotypes, with rcn1 mutants exhibiting greater sensitivity to the inhibitor than WT (Fig. 5e, f). Collectively, these results suggest that RCN1 promotes root germination, thereby improving rice’s water-absorptive capacity under drought.

RCN1 knockout decreases drought stress tolerance

The genome-wide association study showed that RCN1 is associated with drought resistance during the vegetative stage [38]. To functionally validate this association, the WT and rcn1 mutants were treated with a 15% PEG-6000 solution. Phenotypic differences between rcn1 mutants and WT were evident under PEG-6000 treatment. rcn1 mutants were dying at 14d after PEG-6000 treatment, whereas most of the WT plants survived (Fig. 6a). Relative electrolyte leakage (EL) of the rcn1 mutants was about 1.5 times higher than that of the WT at 7 days post-treatment (Fig. 6b). Relative water content in leaves was significantly lower in rcn1 mutants than WT (Fig. 6c), likely attributable to their relatively high stomatal conductance under PEG-6000 treatment (Fig. 6d).

We further compared growth parameters after 7 days of drought treatment. PEG-6000 suppressed plant height and root length in both WT and rcn1 mutants (Figure S6a, b), and significantly reduced fresh and dry weights(Figure S6c, d). Notably, under normal water conditions, rcn1 mutants accumulated greater aboveground biomass than WT. However, this advantage was reversed under drought, as the reduction in aboveground fresh and dry weight was more pronounced in rcn1 mutants during leaf water potential decline (Figure S6c, d). The root-shoot ratio of rcn1 mutants under normal water was remarkably smaller than that of WT (Figure S6e, f). This may be one reason for the decrease in drought resistance of rcn1 mutants.

RCN1 regulates rice ABA synthesis and metabolism

To elucidate the physiological basis of RCN1 mediated drought resistance, we measured the endogenous ABA content in rcn1 mutants and WT plants. Under drought stress, leaf ABA content increased by more than 58% in WT plants, but only by approximately 20% in rcn1 mutants (Fig. 7a). In roots, ABA levels rose by 28% in WT under stress, while rcn1 mutants showed no significant change (Fig. 7b). We further investigated the expression levels of ABA-related genes. Under drought stress, the expression of ABA synthesis-related genes 9-cis-epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED) 3, NCED4, and NCED5 was significantly increased in the WT (Fig. 7c, d, e), but no significant changes were observed in the rcn1 mutants. ABA catabolic gene ABA 8′-Hydroxylase (OsABA8ox3) was decreased markedly in both rcn1 mutants and WT (Fig. 7f). Exogenous ABA application can rescue the drought resistance of rcn1 mutants (Figure S7). During the spike differentiation stage, rcn1 mutants under drought stress also exhibit lower ABA levels and slightly higher stomatal conductance than the WT (Figure S8). These results suggest RCN1 may regulate ABA synthesis and influence the plant’s resistance to drought stress.

Discussion

RCN1 alleviated drought-induced damage to spike structure

The young spike differentiation stage determines the number of branches and spikelets, and it is the most sensitive period to water deficiency in rice development [39]. One study found that the LWT-treated rice showed a notable decrease in primary branches [40]. Kato’s research showed that drought stress seriously damaged the secondary branch differentiation [27]. Consistently, our results demonstrate that drought stress markedly decreases both primary and secondary branche numbers, with secondary branches being more severely affected, suggesting that secondary branch differentiation is more susceptible to drought stress.

RCN1 is a key regulator of spike differentiation, known to promote high-order branches by stimulating the activity of secondary meristems in the uppermost phytomers [32, 33, 41]. 35 S::RCN1::NOS transgenic rice plants show more secondary branches than WT, and even differentiation of third branches [33]. In this research, we extend these findings by demonstrating that RCN1 also functions to mitigate the damage of drought stress to spike differentiation. Our study shows that the primary and secondary branch numbers of the rcn1 mutants were dramatically decreased under drought-stress conditions, the reduction of secondary branches was more severe, and almost no secondary branches were visible under severe drought stress. Exogenous ABA could promote spike differentiation under drought conditions, but the loss of RCN1 protein function diminishes the promotive effect of ABA. Our study suggests that RCN1 is necessary to maintain branch differentiation under drought stress, especially the second branch.

Interestingly, RCN1-overexpressing (RCN1-OE) lines did not exhibit enhanced protection of spike differentiation under drought stress compared to wild-type (WT) plants. Furthermore, RCN1 transcript levels in leaves were not upregulated during spike differentiation under drought conditions. One possible explanation is that the basal expression level of RCN1 in WT is already sufficient to fulfill its function in mitigating drought-induced damage to spike differentiation. Given that the mode of action of TFL1-like proteins likely requires the formation of a functional complex with FD [42, 44], simply increasing RCN1 expression alone may not be sufficient to confer additional protection against drought stress during panicle differentiation.

Studies demonstrated that RCN1 regulates floral transition by antagonizing florigen proteins, without affecting florigen gene transcription [41]. In this study, we observed that drought stress induced the expression of two florigen genes, Ehd1 and Hd1, and that their transcript levels remained elevated during spike differentiation. RCN1 inhibits the response of Ehd1 to drought. This implies that these two florigen genes may participate in spikelet differentiation under drought stress, and RCN1 affects Ehd1 transcription during this process. Endo Higashi (2011) reported that Hd1 and Ehd1 can suppress primary branch formation [37]. Our previous research also implicated Ehd1 and Hd1 in the regulation of spikelet differentiation under shade stress conditions [45]. These findings suggest that the flowering regulators, Ehd1 and Hd1, also function to inhibit spike differentiation under drought conditions, and RCN1 protects branch initiation by inhibiting the drought response of Ehd1. Furthermore, we find that RCN1 mitigates the suppressive effect of drought on the expression of LAX1, RCN2, and RCN4, thereby alleviating the adverse effects of drought on young spike differentiation.

Both Suzuki’s study and our published study have shown that RCN1 presented the high basal expression in rice roots [35, 41], implying that RCN1 might have functional roles within roots. Here, we demonstrate that RCN1 expression in roots is upregulated throughout the entire spike differentiation period under drought stress. As the first organ to perceive drought stress, roots are well-positioned to initiate stress responses. RCN1 protein has been identified as a mobile signal that can be transported through vascular bundles [41]. These results raise the possibility that RCN1 might respond to drought stress in roots and generate mobile signals that are transported to the shoot apical meristem or leaves to modulate spike structure. However, this inference needs to be confirmed by further research.

RCN1 improves rice drought resistance by promoting ABA synthesis

The function of RCN1 in improving rice drought resistance was validated in our water stress experiments conducted during the nutritional growth stage. The loss of RCN1 protein function significantly reduced drought tolerance of rice. RCN1 has been previously linked to altered ABA sensitivity and endogenous ABA levels under salt stress [34], our findings point to a similar regulatory mechanism under drought stress. Previous study has proved that TFL1 engages in competitive interactions with group A bZIP proteins involved in ABA signaling [46]. Furthermore, the ABA biosynthesis gene ABA1 and signaling componentsABI5, ABF4, and APF2 have been identified as direct targets of the TFL1-FD complex [47], and TFL1 stabilizes the ABI5 protein during seed development [48]. In rice, a transcriptional module comprising GF14h, OREB1, and MOTHER OF FT AND TFL2 regulates ABA-responsive genes [49]. Our study shows that loss of RCN1 protein function results in reduced stomatal closure. Kinoshita reports that an ABA-independent module involving FT, 14-3-3 proteins, and H+-ATPases regulates stomatal movement [50]. Notably, the expression of FD, TFL1, and several floral target genes of the FT/FD complex has been detected in guard cells [50, 52], suggesting a possible local role for these regulators in stomatal function. In addition, rcn1 mutants exhibited greater aboveground biomass and a lower root-shoot ratio than wild-type plants, likely contributing to increased water loss. RCN1 appeared to promote the emergence of new roots under drought stress, and rcn1 mutants displayed heightened ABA sensitivity during this process. Taken together, these results suggest that RCN1 modulates rice adaptation to drought primarily by reducing water loss (via stomatal regulation) and enhancing soil water uptake (via root system adjustments), with indirect effects on spike differentiation.

RCN1 influences the expression of NCED3/4/5 and spike differentiation-related genes under drought stress. However, it remains unclear whether these genes are direct targets of RCN1 or are indirectly regulated. Group A basic leucine zipper (bZIP) transcription factors are central to abscisic acid (ABA) signaling and plant development [53]. In Arabidopsis, yeast two-hybrid assays have demonstrated that all group A bZIP transcription factors can potentially interact with FT and TFL1 [54]. In rice, the 14-3-3 protein mediates the interaction between Hd3a and the group A bZIP transcription factor OsFD1, forming a ternary complex known as the florigen activation complex (FAC), which activates floral gene transcription [55]. Notably, RCN1 protein competes with Hd3a for 14-3-3 binding, thereby inhibiting flowering [41]. GF14f, a 14-3-3 homolog, directly interacts with the bZIP transcription factor OsFD2 to negatively regulate grain size, and both in vivo and in vitro experiments have confirmed a physical interaction between RCN1 and GF14f [56]. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that RCN1 may indirectly influence NCED and spike differentiation-related genes transcription through its known interaction with 14-3-3 proteins and A bZIP transcription factors, which have been implicated in ABA signaling and flower differentiation. This hypothesis warrants further investigation through transcriptomic co-expression network analysis and detailed molecular interaction studies.

In summary, RCN1 helps maintain spike structure under drought stress by promoting branch differentiation and enhancing drought resistance of rice (Fig. 8).

Materials and methods

Vector construction

The genetic background of RCN1-OE transgenic rice plants was O. sativa japonica cultivar (cv.) Nipponbare. The coding region of RCN1 was amplified by PCR from cDNA of rice (primer: 5’- TAACCCGGGACAGCTCACCGAAGTGC-3’, 5’-AATGGTACCGCGCCTCCTGGCTGCAG-3’). The amplified fragment was then inserted into pCAMIBA1300 vector between the CaMV 35 S promoter and the NOS terminator. The rcn1 knockout mutants were generated using the CRISPR-Cas9 system, two distinct single-guide RNA constructs targeting the first and second exons of RCN1, and introduced into Nipponbare rice via transformation. Two independent mutant lines, rcn1-4 (carrying an “A” insertion in the coding region) and rcn1-11 (carrying a “T” insertion in the coding region) were used in this study; the details were described in our previous report [35].

Plant growth conditions and drought stress treatments

WT (wild-type, cv. Nipponbare), RCN1-OE, and rcn1 mutant (rcn1-4 and rcn1-11) plants were cultivated during the growing season. At the three-leaf stage, seedlings were transplanted into rectangular plastic boxes (57 × 39 × 35 cm), with 8 plants/box (2 plants of each genotype). A total of 24 boxes were used. Fertilizer was applied at transplanting and during the tillering stage, containing 5.7 g urea (2.6 g N) and 6.1 g KH2PO4 (1.7 g K and 1.4 g P) per box. A 5 cm deep-water was maintained from transplanting until the peak tillering stage.

Internode elongation in rice begins almost simultaneously with the transition from vegetative to reproductive growth [57, 59]. Drought stress treatment was initiated at the onset of stem elongation, which closely coincides with the entry into reproductive development. 2/3 of the plants were subject to drought stress treatment, while the remaining 1/3 were maintained under normal water conditions as control. which is very close to the entry of the reproductive growth. The soil water content (SWC) ranges were determined by calculating the difference between wet weight (full water) and dry weight. The soil relative water content (SRWC) was calculated as: (current wet weight - dry weight) / SWC × 100. Preliminary experiments showed that initial leaf curling occurred at approximately 50% SRWC. When more than half of the rice leaves curled, SRWC was approximately 40%. Based on these observations, moderate drought was defined as 50%-70% SRWC, and severe drought was defined as 30%-50% SRWC. Potted plants were weighed daily, and clear water was added to maintain the target SRWC levels. After 14 days of treatments, the normal water supply was resumed for all the treatments until seed harvest. For transcript and physiological analyses, the top three leaves were collected every 7 days at 9:00 AM. Three biological replicates were performed per treatment per genotype, with leaves from two plants pooled for each replicate. After heading, 15 panicles per treatment per genotype were harvested from five individual plants for spike structure analysis.

To investigate the function of ABA in spike differentiation under drought stress, half of the wild-type and rcn1 mutant plants subjected to severe drought conditions were foliar-sprayed with 20 µM ABA solution. The remaining plants were sprayed with water as controls. Panicle morphology was quantified at maturity using the same sampling method described above. The ABA concentration was selected based on previous reports [60, 62].

Plant culture and treatments

Rice seedlings (20 days after sowing) were transplanted onto foam polystyrene boards with evenly spaced holes and floated on rectangular plastic boxes (57 × 39 × 35 cm) containing 7 L of Kimura B nutrient solution (NSP1050). The nutrient solution was renewed every five days.

To investigate the effect of water stress on RCN1 expression in roots and leaves, Nipponbare plants were subjected to drought stress simulated by adding 10% polyethylene glycol (PEG-6000) to the nutrient solution. Stress treatment was carried out after jointing (approximately 8 weeks after sowing). Rice plants grown in normal hydroponic solution served as controls. Roots and leaves were sampled weekly for transcript-level analysis. The sampling method is as described before.

To examine the response of RCN1 to ABA, Nipponbare plants were treated with 10 µΜ ABA during the young spike differentiation stage (approximately 8–10 weeks after sowing). ABA was applied directly to the hydroponic solution to allow root uptake, while control plants were maintained in normal hydroponic solution. Roots and leaves were collected at 9 weeks after seeding to detect the expression of RCN1. The sampling method is as described before.

To assess the drought tolerance of WT and rcn1 mutants, plants were grown in climate chambers (14 h light/28°C, 10 h dark/24°C) with 70% relative humidity. One month after transplanting, water stress was simulated by adding 15% PEG-6000 to the nutrient solution. After 5 days of treatment, the entire root system and the top three leaves were sampled, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for ABA content and gene expression analyses. After 7 days of treatment, the penultimate leaves were collected to measure relative electrolyte leakage and relative water content. Stomatal conductance was also measured on the penultimate leaves. Three biological replicates were performed per treatment per genotype. Tested the growth status (including root-shoot ratio, plant height, fresh weight, dry weight, root length, leaf water content, stomatal conductance, and relative conductivity) after 7 days of treatment. For each treatment per genotype, data were obtained from 10 individual plants.

To investigate the effects of drought, ABA, and ABA inhibition on new root formation, WT and rcn1 mutant plants were subjected to the following treatments during the vegetative stage: (1) 10% PEG-6000, (2) 10% PEG-6000 + 10 µM ABA, (3) 10% PEG-6000 + 5 µM fluridone (an ABA biosynthesis inhibitor), (4) 10 µM ABA alone, and (5) 5 µM fluridone alone. Plants grown in normal hydroponic solution were taken as control. The number of newly germinated roots was counted on days 6, 9, 12, and 15 after treatment initiation. Criteria for judging new roots: white roots that have not turned yellow and have no root hair, length<1 cm. For each treatment per genotype, data were obtained from 15 individual plants.

ABA content detection

Rice leaves and roots were ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen. Endogenous ABA content was determined using a 96T Plant abscisic acid (ABA) ELISA Kit (MEIMIAN, Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s method.

Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted from leaf and root samples using the Column Plant Total RNA Extraction and Purification Kit (Sangon, Shanghai, China), which includes a genomic DNA removal step. RNA concentration and purity were qualified, reverse transcription was carried out according to the instructions of Primescript™ RT regent Kit (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). After the reverse transcription, the fluorescent quantitative PCR was carried out by using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen, Beijing, China) and QuantStudio1 real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR instrument, and the operation was carried out according to the instructions of the kit. The primers used in the qRT-PCR are shown in Table S2. The Actin gene is used as the reference gene, and the PCR primer sequences of each gene are shown in the table below. The values presented are the mean of three biological replicates, each of which had three technical replicates.

Statistical analyses

Each value represents the mean ± SD of biological replicates. Significant differences were determined using a one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test (P < 0.05).
