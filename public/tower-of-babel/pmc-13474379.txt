Familial erythrocytosis associated with an EGLN1 (PHD2) variant of uncertain significance with emerging evidence of pathogenicity
Authors: Anil Ananthaneni, Kirsten Maddox, Riya Sam, Poornima Ramadas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFamilial erythrocytosis is a rare disorder most often linked to germline alterations affecting erythropoiesis and oxygen-sensing pathways. The EGLN1 gene encodes prolyl hydroxylase domain-containing protein 2 (PHD2), a central regulator of the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway that modulates erythropoietin (EPO) production. This report describes a mother and son with longstanding erythrocytosis, both harbouring a heterozygous EGLN1 variant of uncertain significance (c.806T>C, p.Ile269Thr), in whom secondary causes and JAK2-mutated myeloproliferative neoplasms were excluded. Both individuals required serial phlebotomy for haematocrit control and biochemical as well as molecular testing supported congenital erythrocytosis. The EGLN1 variant has been reported rarely and is supported by in-silico and functional data suggesting deleterious effects on PHD2 protein stability and function. Based on the aggregate evidence derived from a European cohort, this variant has previously been proposed to meet the criteria for pathogenic classification under the ACMG guidelines. This case adds clinical and functional evidence supporting ACMG/AMP-based classification of EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) as likely pathogenic in familial erythrocytosis.

Background

Familial erythrocytosis is an uncommon hereditary disorder characterised by a persistent increase in red blood cell mass, typically in the absence of an acquired myeloproliferative neoplasm and often with normal or near-normal leucocyte and platelet counts. It may result from high-affinity haemoglobin variants, alterations in the EPO receptor or pathogenic variants in genes involved in oxygen sensing and HIF regulation. One such gene is egl-9 homologue 1 (EGLN1), which encodes PHD2, a key oxygen-sensing enzyme within the EPO regulatory pathway and one of at least nine genes implicated in familial erythrocytosis.1–7

Under normoxic conditions, PHD2 hydroxylates conserved proline residues in the α-subunit of HIF (HIF-α), marking it for ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation via the von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) complex. In hypoxia, reduced PHD2 activity permits HIF-α stabilisation, dimerisation with HIF-β and transcriptional activation of target genes, including EPO, thereby enhancing erythropoiesis. As a negative regulator of HIF-α, loss-of-function or hypomorphic mutations in PHD2 can result in inappropriate HIF activation and dysregulated EPO production, leading to familial erythrocytosis.

Serum EPO levels can help orient the diagnostic algorithm: low or inappropriately normal EPO suggests EPOR mutations, whereas elevated EPO levels are more typical of high-affinity haemoglobin variants or defects in oxygen-sensing pathways. The Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) classification divides familial erythrocytosis into types based on the gene affected, including EPOR (type 1), VHL (type 2), EGLN1 (type 3) and EPAS1 (type 4). Germline EGLN1 mutations underlie familial erythrocytosis type 3 (ECYT3; OMIM 609820), typically in an autosomal dominant pattern. At least 10 different heterozygous EGLN1 variants have been associated with erythrocytosis, generally accompanied by inappropriately normal or elevated EPO levels.

Despite growing knowledge, the diagnostic workup of erythrocytosis remains heterogeneous in practice.8–10 In a recent single-centre study, extended investigations beyond JAK2 testing were not pursued in 58% of patients with erythrocytosis. Another study showed that a substantial proportion of patients with secondary erythrocytosis underwent invasive bone marrow biopsies, similar to those with polycythaemia vera, highlighting under-recognition of hereditary and molecular causes. This report describes a mother and son with familial erythrocytosis harbouring an EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) variant of uncertain significance, adding further evidence to the expanding genetic landscape of this condition.

Case presentation

This case involves a woman in her 40s and her adolescent son, both referred to the haematology clinic for evaluation and ongoing management of polycythaemia of unclear aetiology. The mother’s medical history included hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, morbid obesity (body mass index 48.12 kg/m²) and obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA). Her OSA was suboptimally managed because of poor tolerance and non-adherence to continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy due to claustrophobia. She reported a history of erythrocytosis dating back to age 16, which did not initially require active intervention because of ongoing menstrual blood loss.

At age 29, the mother underwent hysterectomy, after which progressive erythrocytosis necessitated serial therapeutic phlebotomies. She had a prior history of cigarette smoking but quit at age 29 and currently used e-cigarettes. She reported no evidence of chronic pulmonary disease. A family history revealed that her own mother had ‘polycythemia’, although further details were unavailable. The adolescent son’s past medical history was notable only for polycythaemia. Both mother and son were receiving therapeutic phlebotomies every 2–4 weeks in the months preceding their initial visit to our clinic.

Investigations

Initial workup was performed at an outside facility. In the mother, morbid obesity and OSA raised suspicion for secondary erythrocytosis due to obesity hypoventilation syndrome; however, daytime arterial blood gas analysis showed a normal partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), arguing against chronic hypercapnia as the driver. Carboxyhaemoglobin was 3% (reference <5% in smokers), making chronic carbon monoxide exposure from tobacco smoking an unlikely cause. Her C-reactive protein (CRP) was mildly elevated at 2.5 mg/L (reference <1.0 mg/L), but there was no evidence of active infection or systemic inflammatory disease.

In the son, exogenous androgen exposure was excluded, with a total testosterone level of 463 ng/dL (reference 300–1000 ng/dL) appropriate for age. In both individuals, peripheral blood smears showed erythrocytosis with otherwise unremarkable morphology, and there were no leukoerythroblastic features or dysplasia. Flow cytometry and BCR-ABL testing were negative, supporting the exclusion of chronic myeloid leukaemia and other clonal myeloproliferative neoplasms.

Pre-treatment serum EPO levels were 29 mU/mL in the mother and 11.2 mU/mL in the son (reference 4–26 mU/mL), interpreted as inappropriately elevated/normal in the context of significant erythrocytosis. To evaluate for haemoglobin variants, both patients underwent haemoglobin electrophoresis, which showed normal distributions (mother: HbA 97.6%, HbA2 2.4%; son: HbA 97.7%, HbA2 2.3%), ruling out common high-affinity haemoglobin variants as a cause of secondary erythrocytosis.

Molecular testing for myeloproliferative neoplasms demonstrated negative results for JAK2 V617F and JAK2 exon 12/13/14 mutations in both patients. Given persistent erythrocytosis, family history, and absence of acquired causes, targeted familial erythrocytosis gene panel testing was pursued. No clearly pathogenic variants were identified in EGLN1 (PHD2), EPAS1 (HIF2A), or EPOR. However, both mother and son were found to harbour a heterozygous EGLN1 variant of uncertain significance: Chr1(GRCh37): g.231556829A>G, c.806T>C, p.(Ile269Thr). Additional analysis excluded VHL-related erythrocytosis and revealed a normal BPGM gene, ruling out 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate mutase deficiency.

The laboratory reported an inability to fully sequence EPAS1 (HIF2A) in this case. The incomplete coverage raised the possibility of an insertion/deletion affecting primer binding, or more rarely, a polymorphism at the primer site resulting in allelic dropout; however, no confirmatory evidence for such an event was available. While custom Sanger sequencing could be considered to achieve complete coverage of the refractory HIF2A regions, it was unavailable at our facility. Consequently, the incomplete coverage of HIF2A remains a recognised diagnostic limitation of this workup, and the theoretical presence of a concurrent, undetected HIF2A alteration cannot be completely excluded.

Differential diagnosis

The initial differential diagnosis in the mother focused on secondary erythrocytosis due to chronic hypoxia from OSA and obesity hypoventilation syndrome. Normal daytime pCO2 and the absence of significant chronic lung disease made this less likely, although intermittent nocturnal hypoxia could not be completely excluded. The son’s erythrocytosis, in the absence of comorbidities or exogenous androgens, raised suspicion for a familial or hereditary aetiology.

Other key considerations included polycythaemia vera and other JAK2-mutated myeloproliferative neoplasms, which were excluded by negative JAK2 testing and the absence of other myeloproliferative features. High-affinity haemoglobin variants were also considered but were excluded by normal haemoglobin electrophoresis and the overall clinical context. In addition, EPO-producing tumours and chronic hypoxic states (such as cyanotic heart disease or chronic lung disease) were evaluated but were not supported by the clinical history, physical examination, or ancillary testing.

The presence of erythrocytosis across two generations, absence of secondary causes, and the shared heterozygous EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) variant supported a diagnosis of familial erythrocytosis, likely related to an underlying oxygen-sensing pathway defect.

Treatment

Both patients underwent serial therapeutic phlebotomy to mitigate hyperviscosity and thrombotic risk. For the mother, phlebotomies had been performed for approximately a decade prior to presentation, with a target haematocrit of <45%, consistent with conventional management strategies used in non-clonal erythrocytosis and extrapolated from polycythaemia vera data.8–10 The son had initiated regular phlebotomy more recently, with a target haematocrit of approximately 42% given his younger age and lower absolute cardiovascular risk. This variance in target thresholds was clinically driven by risk-stratification; the mother’s extensive metabolic comorbidities (morbid obesity, severe hypertension, hyperlipidaemia) significantly escalated her baseline cardiovascular and thrombotic risk profile relative to her young, otherwise healthy adolescent son. These strict targets are empirical extrapolations from clonal polycythaemia vera guidelines, as randomised, disease-specific target data for congenital erythrocytosis remain unestablished. No cytoreductive therapy was used in either case, and management remained focused on phlebotomy and risk factor optimisation.

Outcome and follow-up

The mother and son continue to be followed in the haematology clinic at regular intervals. Both have tolerated haematocrit based serial phlebotomies well, without significant treatment-related complications such as anaemia, iron deficiency symptoms such as fatigue, pica and restless legs, vasovagal reactions or thrombotic events to date. Ongoing management focuses on maintaining haematocrit within target ranges, addressing modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, and reinforcing adherence to OSA management in the mother. Further genetic counselling for the family has been arranged to discuss the implications of the EGLN1 variant, the limitations of current evidence and potential testing of additional relatives.

Discussion

The identification of an EGLN1 variant of uncertain significance in a mother–son pair with familial erythrocytosis expands the emerging body of evidence implicating PHD2 alterations in congenital erythrocytosis.2–4 6 7 11 As noted, EGLN1 encodes PHD2, a pivotal oxygen-sensing enzyme that negatively regulates HIF-α and thereby modulates EPO expression and erythropoiesis.2–4 12 Loss-of-function variants in PHD2 can impair HIF-α hydroxylation, preventing recognition by the VHL complex and leading to inappropriate stabilisation of HIF-α and upregulation of downstream target genes, including those promoting erythrocyte production.2–4 12

Beyond erythrocytosis, EGLN1 (PHD2) variants have also been reported in association with paragangliomas and pheochromocytomas, suggesting a potential tumour suppressor role.13–16 These observations highlight the pleiotropic effects of dysregulated HIF signalling and support the rationale for vigilance regarding possible tumour predisposition in some carriers, although the absolute risk remains uncertain. Because formalised consensus guidelines for tumour surveillance in asymptomatic EGLN1 carriers are lacking, we employ a non-invasive, cost-effective hybrid strategy:

This approach optimises early detection while avoiding the radiation risks, financial costs, and cumulative anxiety associated with serial unindicated imaging.

The heterozygous EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) variant identified in this family has been previously reported but remains broadly classified as a variant of uncertain significance. In an earlier report, this variant was described in an asymptomatic individual managed with phlebotomy, whose presentation was also associated with a neuroendocrine tumour. The ClinVar database documents this variant in at least two additional individuals, with reference laboratories uniformly classifying it as a germline variant of uncertain significance (Variation ID: 1761876). Notably, because the entries for this variant within the ClinVar database completely omit any phenotypic descriptions or mentions of an erythrocytosis phenotype, these laboratory submissions are highly likely the result of incidental detection during unrelated clinical sequencing. This lack of phenotypic tracking in public repositories underscores the profound importance of publishing detailed familial case series to actively build the genotype-phenotype correlations necessary for variant reclassification. A different missense variant affecting the same residue, p.Ile269Asn, has been reported in a family with elevated haemoglobin, with and without a concurrent HFE alteration, suggesting that this position is critical for PHD2 function.

A collaborative European study further characterised EGLN1 variants in a cohort of patients with erythrocytosis, identifying the p.Ile269Thr variant in five individuals, two of whom had a positive family history of polycythaemia. At least one carrier demonstrated haemoglobin and haematocrit values above the 99th percentile, supporting a potential deleterious effect.17 Based on the totality of evidence from this cohort, Delamare et al previously proposed that this variant could be classified as pathogenic under ACMG guidelines. By integrating the clinical and genetic findings of the mother-son pair described in this report, we can now explicitly apply the standardised ACMG/AMP variant interpretation framework to independently refine this assessment. Taken together, PP1-supporting co-segregation, PM2-supporting rarity in population databases, PP3 with computational/functional evidence, and the additional PS4 and PS3 moderate criteria provide two moderate and three supporting ACMG/AMP lines of evidence, which are sufficient to classify EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) as likely pathogenic in the context of familial erythrocytosis. While some clinical laboratories and public databases may still annotate this allele as a variant of uncertain significance pending formal update, our familial case and the cited cohort collectively support ACMG/AMP-based reclassification to likely pathogenic and contribute further clinical and functional data to underpin this designation.

In this family, the co-occurrence of the EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) variant in two affected individuals across generations, combined with the exclusion of other common genetic and acquired causes of erythrocytosis, lends additional support to a likely pathogenic role. Although incomplete HIF2A sequencing in this case leaves open the theoretical possibility of a second undetected lesion, the weight of prior functional data and the familial segregation pattern suggests that the EGLN1 variant substantially contributes to the phenotype.

This case underscores several important points for clinical practice. First, the diagnostic evaluation of erythrocytosis should extend beyond JAK2 testing when initial investigations are unrevealing, especially in younger patients or those with a strong family history.8–10 Reliance solely on exclusion of JAK2 mutations risks misclassification of hereditary erythrocytosis as idiopathic and may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures such as bone marrow biopsy.8–10

Second, the case highlights the growing role of multigene germline panels in elucidating hereditary erythrocytosis. Interpretation of variants of uncertain significance remains challenging, but integrating biochemical data (EPO levels), family history, population frequency, functional studies, and emerging literature can refine the assessment of pathogenicity over time. Close collaboration among haematologists, clinical geneticists, and molecular laboratories is crucial to contextualise such findings and guide appropriate counselling and family screening.

Third, from a management standpoint, therapeutic strategies remain largely empirical and extrapolated from polycythaemia vera, focusing on haematocrit control with phlebotomy and cardiovascular risk reduction.8–10 As understanding of the HIF pathway deepens, targeted interventions that modulate HIF signalling may emerge as more precise therapies for EGLN1-associated erythrocytosis. Future studies should aim to clarify long-term thrombotic risk, optimal haematocrit targets, and potential cancer risks in carriers of specific EGLN1 variants.2 6 7 13–18

This report contributes an additional familial instance of EGLN1 c.806T>C (p.Ile269Thr) in the setting of erythrocytosis and supports the accumulating evidence that this variant may be functionally important. Continued reporting of such cases, coupled with systematic functional studies, will be essential to reclassify variants and refine diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in hereditary erythrocytosis.
