Data fusion of EnMAP and sentinel-2 for high-resolution soil fertility assessment in wheat cultivation of central Khuzestan plain
Authors: Zeinab Zaheri Abdehvand, Kazem Rangzan, Danya Karimi, Seyed Roohollah Mousavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to develop a Soil Fertility Index (SFI) map for agricultural lands in central Khuzestan Province, specifically focusing on irrigated wheat cultivation. Three scenarios were evaluated using Random Forest (RF) models based on hyperspectral EnMAP and multispectral Sentinel-2 satellite data. The SFI was calculated by integrating eight key soil fertility parameters: Soil Organic Matter (SOM), Total Nitrogen (TN), available phosphorus (Pav), exchangeable potassium (Kex), Electrical Conductivity (EC), Iron (Fe), calcium carbonate equivalent (CCE), and soil texture classes. In Scenario 1, the RF model using EnMAP data demonstrated excellent prediction accuracy (R2 = 0.84, NRMSE = 0.09). Scenario 2, based on Sentinel-2 data, also performed well but with slightly lower accuracy (R2 = 0.72, NRMSE = 0.11). The highest model accuracy was achieved in Scenario 3, which fused EnMAP and Sentinel-2 data, improving spatial resolution to 10 m (R2 = 0.90, NRMSE = 0.07). This scenario provided the most accurate prediction of soil fertility distribution. The results indicated that 60.6% of the study area fell into the moderate fertility class. Key limitations in soil fertility include high CCE content and low levels of SOM, TN, and Pav. The findings underscore the importance of using integrated remote sensing data for precision agriculture and sustainable soil management, offering valuable insights for policymakers and agricultural managers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54632-8.

Introduction

Soil, as a diverse medium, exhibits significant spatial variability in its properties and fertility. Recognizing these differences is essential for effective management and improved crop yields. Typically, soil fertility (SF) assessment involves evaluating the available nutrients and the quantity of amendments applied to the soil1. Optimal SF conditions can significantly enhance crop performance and nutrient uptake, ultimately contributing to sustainable agriculture2,3. Soil nutrients, as key indicators of fertility, play a vital role in enhancing agricultural productivity4. However, challenges such as nutrient deficiencies or toxicities can limit plant growth, and identifying these issues through soil analysis often requires considerable time and precision5.

The Soil Fertility Index (SFI) is one of the most widely used indicators for optimizing crop production, minimizing environmental impacts, and managing the variability of agricultural lands6,7. It also plays a crucial role in maintaining essential nutrients for plants at optimal levels8. The SFI has been applied in various settings, including agricultural lands, gardens, and forests9,10.

Currently, advanced technologies such as remote sensing (RS) and digital soil mapping (DSM) are extensively used to examine soil properties11–13. These methods facilitate the creation of detailed soil maps, which can identify areas requiring management and improvement14,15.

RS is an effective tool for monitoring spatial and temporal variations in the physical and morphological conditions of crops, playing a crucial role in advancing precision agriculture methods16. Utilizing RS data to predict soil properties provides valuable opportunities for analyzing soil and plant properties, thanks to the availability of images with varying resolutions1. For instance, Rangzan et al.17 identified optimal areas for wheat cultivation in northwestern Khuzestan province using satellite imagery from Sentinel-2, Sentinel-3, and Landsat-9. Sentinel-2 bands and band ratios were selected due to their higher correlations, and accurate maps of the parameters influencing wheat cultivation were created. Additionally, Zhang et al.1 assessed SFI in agricultural lands by integrating Sentinel-2 images with Landsat-8 data. The results showed that combining Landsat-8 and Sentinel-2 data using the Gram-Schmidt algorithm produced a higher correlation with SFI compared to using these data separately.

Hyperspectral and spectral images are both powerful tools for agricultural studies and soil assessment18. Hyperspectral images provide extensive and precise spectral information, allowing for deeper analysis of soil properties, including both chemical and biological aspects. With their high-resolution data across numerous spectral bands, these images facilitate accurate identification of soil properties. When integrated with additional data, they can yield more precise estimates of soil status19.

Hyperspectral imaging, compared to multispectral imaging, is a more advanced technology capable of capturing finer spectral details of target features. However, due to limited accessibility outside the scientific community, hyperspectral imagery has not yet been widely adopted in precision agriculture19.

In recent years, small and affordable airborne hyperspectral sensors, such as the Headwall Micro-Hyperspec and the Cubert UHD 185-Firefly, have been developed. Advanced space-based sensors have either recently been launched or are about to be launched (e.g., PRecursore IperSpettrale della Missione Applicativa (PRISMA), Environmental Mapping and Analysis Program (EnMAP), and Hyperspectral Infrared Imager (HyspIRI). According to these advancements, hyperspectral imaging is gradually becoming more accessible for agricultural applications20. Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of these new sensors in various environmental and agricultural applications. For instance, Golkar Amoli et al.21 evaluated the capabilities of the PRISMA hyperspectral satellite to improve the accuracy of soil organic carbon (SOC) estimation. Similarly, Delogu et al.22 used PRISMA imagery for land use mapping using Random Forest (RF) and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) models. Christina et al.23 explored the use of EnMAP hyperspectral imagery for land use and land cover (LULC) mapping using machine learning (ML) models, while19 focused on monitoring soil properties, such as SOC, clay content, and carbonate, using EnMAP imagery. In addition, recent advances in hyperspectral data fusion and analysis have been highlighted by24 and25, who demonstrated that the integration of hyperspectral and multispectral data substantially improves the accuracy and reliability of soil property mapping.

The RF model is one of the most widely used ML models, known for its numerous advantages. These include its robustness to noise and model complexity, good performance even with small datasets, ease of use and interpretation, and its ability to produce reliable and accurate maps26. Numerous studies have shown that RF is highly successful and outperforms in predicting various soil properties, such as soil texture27, soil carbon stocks28–31, soil chemical properties32, soil Quality33, soil salinity34, cation exchange capacity (CEC)35, and soil types36,37. However, the application of RF in evaluating the SFI has been limited. Calculating the SFI using ML models can provide a powerful approach for analyzing complex soil data and assessing SF. This method combines soil data with ML models to predict or classify the SFI.

The innovation of this research lies in several key aspects. The first aspect is the lack of comprehensive previous studies on SF status and mapping in different areas of the Khuzestan plain. This makes the present study a pioneering work that provides accurate scientific data for the region. Second, wheat, a strategic crop in this region, plays a central role; yet, few studies have comprehensively examined the factors influencing soil fertility for this crop. Third, unlike previous studies that relied on single-variable modeling of soil fertility-related features, this research employs a more comprehensive, multivariate approach to modeling, making it more effective and practical for management decisions aimed at protecting and improving soil conditions.

The hypothesis is that integrating hyperspectral EnMAP imagery with multispectral Sentinel-2 imagery, utilizing advanced data fusion and ML approaches, will significantly enhance the accuracy and spatial resolution of SFI mapping for irrigated wheat fields in the Khuzestan Plain compared to using single datasets or single-variable models.

In contrast to previous studies that mainly focused on single data sources or specific methods, our research framework is based on a hypothesis. The integration of hyperspectral images from EnMAP with multispectral images from Sentinel-2 will enhance the accuracy of SFI predictions. To evaluate this hypothesis, three primary objectives have been outlined: i) to predict the spatial distribution of SFI for irrigated wheat cultivation utilizing both hyperspectral (EnMAP) and multispectral (Sentinel-2) imagery through a RF model; ii) to implement the Gram-Schmidt (G-S) algorithm to evaluate the efficacy and accuracy of merging hyperspectral data from EnMAP with multispectral data from Sentinel-2 in the assessment of SFI for this specific application; and iii) to identify the most significant RS products contributing to the prediction of SFI maps within the Khuzestan region.

It is located in a portion of agricultural lands in the northwest of the Karun River and the southwest of Ahvaz, in central Khuzestan Province, southwestern Iran (Fig. 1a, b, c). This region holds significant economic, agricultural, and industrial importance. Overall research framework

(iii) employing the RF model to create a spatial prediction map of SFI, considering three spatial modeling scenarios: band ratios of EnMAP, Sentinel-2, and band fusion of Sentinel-2 and EnMAP. Remote sensing products

EnMAP and sentinel-2 data

In this section of research, two satellite products, the multispectral Sentinel-2 and the hyperspectral EnMAP, were utilized to identify the relationships between spectral band ratios and the SFI. Sentinel-2 and EnMAP satellite imagery, concurrent with the soil sampling time, were downloaded for the desired area from the online spatial databases https://dataspace.copernicus.eu and https://www.enmap.org, respectively.

The EnMAP mission is a spaceborne hyperspectral satellite initiative led by a German consortium and managed by the German Aerospace Center (DLR). It is equipped with a pushbroom sensor that captures data in 224 spectral bands, ranging from 420 to 2450 nm, with a 30-m spatial resolution and a 30-km-wide field of view. EnMAP is designed to fill gaps in space-based imaging spectrometer data, providing high-quality hyperspectral information for various environmental purposes. Its advanced technology enables precise land cover mapping and supports diverse scientific applications. In this study, an L2A EnMAP product, which includes geometric and atmospheric pre-processing, was downloaded. Ultimately, 206 suitable bands were selected for processing and analysis after the removal of bad bands using the EnMAP-Box module in QGIS 3.28 software.

Sentinel-2 has 13 spectral bands with spatial resolutions varying between 10 and 60 m53. Among these, bands 2, 3, 4, and 8, with a 10-m spatial resolution, cover the visible spectrum (Blue, Green, Red) and Near-infrared (NIR). Additionally, bands 5, 6, 7, 8A, 11, and 12, with a 20-m resolution, cover NIR and short-wave infrared (SWIR1 and SWIR2) regions. Before applying the band ratio, the 20-m resolution bands were resampled using the bilinear method in ArcGIS 10.8 software to ensure consistent spatial resolution.

Sentinel-2 and EnMAP images used in this study were acquired during the pre-sowing period of irrigated wheat in late October 2022, coinciding with the soil sampling campaign. This timing was intentionally chosen to ensure that the measured soil properties and spectral responses were not affected by wheat phenological stages. Therefore, the correlation analyses between spectral band ratios and the SFI reflect inherent soil conditions under bare-soil conditions rather than variations associated with crop growth.

To determine the appropriate bands and optimal band ratios, the correlation between the bands and band ratios of Sentinel-2 and EnMAP images with the SFI was calculated. The optimal band ratios for Sentinel-2 are: B2/B11, B3/B5, B3/B11, B8/B11, and B8A/B11. For EnMAP, the optimal band ratios include: B4/B14, B4/B16, B4/B17, B5/B10, B5/B15, B5/B16, and B5/B17.

Fusion of EnMAP and sentinel-2 data

To improve the spatial accuracy of the EnMAP hyperspectral image, Sentinel-2 data and the G-S algorithm were utilized. The G-S algorithm is a scientifically based image fusion method, derived from the Gram–Schmidt transform (GST), that orthogonalizes multispectral bands treated as high-dimensional vectors54. This algorithm, one of the most important methods for satellite image fusion, preserves spectral information while merging multiple data sources and enhances low-spatial-resolution pixels to higher spatial resolution1,55. Another advantage of this method is its ability to more accurately represent spectral reflectance variations, aiding in the identification of complexities56.

To avoid errors, a similar band (Sentinel-2 Band 2), with a spectral range matching the selected EnMAP band ratios, was used. This process converted the spatial resolution of the selected EnMAP image bands to 10 m (Table 4).

For all spectral bands and band ratios, Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) and their statistical significance levels (p-values) were calculated. Only statistically significant relationships (p < 0.05) were retained as optimal band ratios. Band-ratio–based approaches have been successfully applied in previous studies for soil property estimation using multispectral Sentinel-2 data57 and hyperspectral imagery58.

Digital mapping of SFI

Auxiliary covariate

DSM models utilize auxiliary covariates to identify soil properties and assess their geographic distribution and variability within a specified study area, considering the spatial scale of the analysis11,59. These covariates include primary soil-forming factors and their proxies60.

In this study, two satellite datasets, Sentinel-2 (multispectral) and EnMAP (hyperspectral), were used to explore the relationships between band ratios and the SFI. To identify the most suitable bands and optimal band ratios, correlations were calculated between the bands and band ratios from Sentinel-2 and EnMAP images and the SFI. Band ratios exhibiting the highest correlations from both datasets were then incorporated as auxiliary variables in the RF model. To further improve the spatial accuracy of the hyperspectral EnMAP imagery, Sentinel-2 data and the G-S algorithm were applied.

The modeling was assessed under three distinct scenarios: using the best-correlated band ratios from EnMAP (scenario #1 = S1), using the best-correlated band ratios from Sentinel-2 (scenario #2 = S2), and using combined band ratios from both datasets (scenario #3 = S3). In addition, the selected band ratios, supported by previous studies24,61, were used as proxies for key soil-forming factors and their anticipated effects on soil biogeochemistry, thereby providing an effective representation of the spatial variability of SFI within the study area.

Random forest (RF)

RF method is a non-parametric technique that consists of multiple individual decision tree models trained on bootstrap samples of data62. The predictions from these individual trees are aggregated to produce a final prediction. RF can also be used to evaluate the relative importance (RI) of predictor variables63.

Key parameters in RF include the number of trees (ntree) and the number of variables considered for each split (mtry)64. RF ranks the importance of covariates by assessing the average reduction in node impurity, quantified using metrics such as the increase in standard error when auxiliary covariates are used to partition the trees within the forest. Covariates that substantially reduce variance are considered more important.

The RF model assigns a quantitative measure to the influence of auxiliary covariates on the model’s predictive accuracy. By leveraging this functionality, RF prioritizes auxiliary covariates based on their importance, providing valuable insights into the key factors affecting the modeled outcomes. In this study, the RF model was implemented in the software R (v4.3.1) using the “randomForest” package65. The hyperparameters, including the number of trees (ntree) and the number of variables randomly selected at each split (mtry), were tuned by a grid search procedure combined with tenfold cross-validation. The final model was trained with ntree = 500 and mtry = 6, which resulted in the lowest out-of-bag error. This configuration was chosen to optimize the performance of the model and ensure reproducibility.

SFI prediction performance

To validate the performance of the RF algorithm, the dataset was randomly divided, with 70% used for calibration and 30% reserved for validation purposes. Four common statistical indicators were employed to assess model performance: the coefficient of determination (R2), Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), root mean squared error (RMSE), and normalized root mean squared error (NRMSE). These indicators were calculated by comparing the measured SFI values with the predicted SFI values. (Eqs. (5, 6, 7, and 8).

The following classification scheme was used to interpret NRMSE values and assess model performance as recommended by66 and67.

NRMSE values are classified as follows to assess model performance66: NRMSE < 10%: Excellent—the model performs very well with minimal prediction error, 10% ≤ NRMSE < 20%: Good—the model performs well, but there is some room for improvement, 20% ≤ NRMSE < 30%: Moderate—the model’s performance is average, indicating the need for significant improvements, NRMSE ≥ 30%: Poor—the model’s performance is poor, with substantial errors, requiring considerable improvement66.

Additionally, a Taylor Diagram was used to evaluate the accuracy of the three model scenarios: S1, based on Sentinel-2 band ratios; S2, based on EnMAP band ratios; and S3, using a fusion of both datasets. Spatial modeling

In this study, spatial modeling of the SFI was conducted using the RF model across three scenarios.

Scenario 1: Best correlation of EnMAP band ratios:

The analysis of band ratio correlations between hyperspectral imagery (EnMAP) and the SFI revealed that the highest correlations were observed in the band ratios B4/B14, B4/B16, B4/B17, B5/B10, B5/B15, B5/B16, and B5/B17, within the spectral range of 0.434–0.496 µm. These spectral ranges fall within the visible portion of the electromagnetic spectrum and are sensitive to soil moisture and plant health. These bands effectively detect variations in soil moisture content and plant conditions, which are critical for assessing SF82,83. Therefore, these band ratios were incorporated as auxiliary variables in the RF model. To further interpret these findings, it is important to note that the concentration of strong correlations within the 0.434–0.496 µm region aligns with the known spectral behavior of hyperspectral sensors. Unlike the broader Sentinel-2 bands, EnMAP’s narrow visible–blue channels capture subtle reflectance variations related to soil color, organic matter, iron oxides, and surface moisture – properties directly linked to SF under bare-soil conditions. Previous studies have reported strong soil property sensitivity in the blue region of hyperspectral data, supporting the observed patterns in our study84,85. Therefore, these band ratios are fully justified as auxiliary variables in the RF model, enhancing its sensitivity to fine-scale soil features that cannot be captured using multispectral imagery.

Scenario 2: Best correlation of Sentinel-2 bands ratios:

The correlation analysis between the band ratios of Sentinel-2 imagery and the SFI revealed that the highest correlations were found in the following band ratios: B2/B11, B3/B5, B3/B11, B8/B11, and B8A/B11. The strong correlations between Sentinel-2 band ratios and SFI result from the ability of certain bands to capture key variations in soil and vegetation properties. Band ratios such as B2/B11, B3/B5, B3/B11, B8/B11, and B8A/B11 are particularly effective due to their sensitivity to differences in soil moisture, organic matter, and vegetation health. The visible bands (B2, B3), Vegetation red edge bands (B5, B8A), NIR band (B8), and SWIR (B11) enhance the detection of these variations, with B8A further improving sensitivity due to its narrow spectral width. This allows for more precise identification of subtle changes in vegetation and soil properties. Additionally, different soil types and fertility levels exhibit distinct spectral signatures, and these band ratios, especially those involving B8A, help highlight these differences, resulting in stronger correlations with SFI.

Generally, the combination of these spectral bands proves highly effective in assessing SF by capturing both soil and vegetation properties86,87.

Therefore, these band ratios were included as auxiliary variables in the RF model. The modeling results are presented in Table 8 and Fig. 6.

Scenario 3: Band ratio fusion of EnMAP and Sentinel-2

To improve the spatial accuracy of the EnMAP hyperspectral image, Sentinel-2 data and the G-S algorithm were utilized. In this scenario, the selected bands with a spatial resolution of 30 m were enhanced to a spatial resolution of 10 m using Sentinel-2 data and the G-S algorithm. The band ratios were recalculated and incorporated as auxiliary variables into the RF model (Table 8 and Fig. 6).

The results in Table 8 and Fig. 6 show that the RF model performances varied across the three scenarios:

In Scenario 1, using the EnMAP data, the RF model achieved excellent results, with an R2 of 0.84, explaining 84% of the variability in the dependent data. CCC was 0.92, reflecting a very high agreement between predicted and observed values. An NRMSE of 0.09 signifies a very low prediction error, categorizing the model’s performance as ‘Excellent’ with minimal prediction error. This result demonstrates high-quality and precise predictions using EnMAP data.

In Scenario 2, which used Sentinel-2 data, the RF model’s performance was somewhat lower compared to Scenario 1. The R2 value of 0.72 indicates that the model was able to explain 72% of the variability in the data, which is lower than Scenario 1. The CCC value of 0.85 reflects good agreement between predicted and observed values, but with less precision. The NRMSE of 0.11 is higher than in Scenario 1, reflecting more prediction error. According to the NRMSE classification, this result falls into the ‘Good’ category, meaning the model performs well but leaves room for improvement in predictive accuracy.

In Scenario 3, which applied the fusion of EnMAP and Sentinel-2 data, the RF model exhibited the highest performance across all metrics. With an R2 of 0.90, the model explained 90% of the variability in the observed data, making it the best-fitting model. The CCC was 0.95, indicating a very strong agreement between predicted and observed values. The NRMSE was 0.07, the lowest among the three scenarios, indicating minimal prediction error. According to the NRMSE classification, this model falls into the ‘Very good’ or ‘Excellent’ category, showing a substantial enhancement in both prediction accuracy and overall model performance with the combined use of EnMAP and Sentinel-2 data.

Conclusion

This study aimed to develop a soil fertility index (SFI) map for agricultural lands in the northwest of Karun and the southwest of Ahvaz in central Khuzestan Province, focusing on irrigated wheat cultivation. The effectiveness of hyperspectral and multispectral satellite images (EnMAP and Sentinel-2) was evaluated across three scenarios for mapping the SFI. Initially, the SFI was calculated by integrating eight key soil fertility parameters, namely Soil Organic Matter (SOM), Total Nitrogen (TN), available phosphorus (Pav), exchangeable potassium (Kex), Electrical Conductivity (EC), Iron (Fe), calcium carbonate equivalent (CCE), and soil texture classes. Subsequently, the optimal band and band ratio correlations between these images and the SFI were determined. Analysis of hyperspectral images revealed the highest correlation within the spectral range of 0.434 to 0.496 µm. For Sentinel-2 images, the best correlations were observed in the VNIR, Vegetation Red Edge, and SWIR bands. In the third scenario, the spatial resolution of hyperspectral images was enhanced from 30 to 10 m using the Gram-Schmidt algorithm. Performance evaluation of the models, using statistical indices such as R2, NRMSE, CCC, and Taylor diagrams, demonstrated that all three models performed well in mapping the SFI. However, the spatial distribution of SFI, achieved by combining EnMAP and Sentinel-2 data, demonstrated that this scenario provides highly accurate predictions for SFI in agricultural lands. The prediction indicated that most of the land in the study area falls into the moderate soil fertility (SF) class. The limitations in SF for this area include high CCE content and low levels of SOM, TN, and Pav. The declining trends in critical SFI attributes raise serious concerns about ongoing soil degradation, which could lead to lower crop productivity and potentially result in the exclusion of agricultural lands from production. To optimize wheat yield in the study region, it is recommended to increase the application of available phosphorus (Pav), total nitrogen (TN), and soil organic matter (SOM). Given the low organic matter content and the calcareous nature of the soils, foliar application of micronutrient-enriched fertilizers is advised to maximize nutrient uptake efficiency. A comprehensive wheat nutrition program should integrate a balanced fertilizer regimen, applied at recommended rates and intervals. Continuous research and monitoring are imperative to sustain soil fertility and ensure food security, contributing to broader national planning objectives. Utilizing the combination of EnMAP and Sentinel-2 data can effectively determine the exact spatial distribution of soil fertility (SF), providing valuable insights for policymakers and agricultural managers. This method facilitates a deeper understanding of SF conditions and helps identify the factors that are limiting SF in the area. However, the modeling framework—based on integrating hyperspectral and multispectral data with machine learning approaches—is flexible and can be extended to other regions with similar climatic, parent materials, and topographic conditions. For application in such regions, local calibration and validation would be necessary. Thus, while the current results provide robust insights for the Khuzestan plain, the framework offers a generalizable approach that could be applied to similar agro-environmental contexts in future studies.

For future research, it is recommended to include crop yield data, particularly wheat yield, to further evaluate the accuracy of SF predictions and establish a direct link between soil properties and agricultural productivity. Additionally, extending the analysis to rainfed agricultural areas is suggested to assess the robustness and applicability of hyperspectral- and multispectral-based SF modeling under different moisture regimes and management practices.
