Drip fertigation improves cumin productivity, essential oil content, water use efficiency and profitability in arid zones of Rajasthan
Authors: Ravindra Singh, C B Harisha, Sharda Choudhary, Y Ravi, N K Meena, Ram Swaroop Meena, Suresh Kumar, A K Verma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Rajasthan’s arid tracts, sandy soils and high evaporative demand limit border irrigation, making drip irrigation a sustainable alternative to enhance crop productivity by “feeding the plant, not the soil.” To understand the efficacy of drip fertigation, an experimental trial was conducted to assess its influence on growth, yield, water use efficiency (WUE) and economics of cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) for three consecutive years (2016–2019). The experiment comprised of ten treatments viz. control (1.0 IW/CPE ratio of surface irrigation with 100% recommended dose of fertilizer), and nine treatments were the combination of 3 levels of irrigation water depth (0.4, 0.6, 0.8 IW/CPE ratio) along with three levels (60, 80 and 100% RDF) of recommended dose of fertilizers (40:30:30, N: P:K). The analysis of the pooled dataset revealed that drip irrigation at 0.8 Irrigation water (IW)/Cumulative Pan Evaporation (CPE) ratio with 100% recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF) produced statistically superior plant height (31.37 cm), number of primary branches (PB)/plant (8.72) and secondary branches (SB)/plant (21.00), umbels/plant (UPP 34.90), umbellets umbel− 1 (UPU 5.82), seed umbel− 1 (30.73), test weight (TW 4.03 gm), seed yield (SY 830.83 kg ha− 1), essential oil (EO 4.54%), gross returns (GR 1,41,229 ₹ ha− 1), net returns (89,559 ₹ ha− 1) and benefit cost (B: C) ratio (1.73) above the control treatment. Maximum WUE (2.46 and 2.40 kg ha− 1 mm− 1) and water saving (19.4 and 58.2%) was noted under drip irrigation maintained at a 0.8 and 0.4 IW/CPE ratio with the application of 100% RDF followed by drip irrigation at 0.6 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF (2.36 kg ha− 1 mm− 1 and 38.8%, respectively). Drip fertigation at 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 IW/CPE ratios reduced seasonal water use by 58.2, 38.8 and 19.7%, respectively, compared to surface irrigation. The highest WUE (4.20 kg ha− 1 mm− 1 ) was observed under 0.4 IW/CPE + 100% RDF in a year (2016–17), while economic water productivity peaked under 0.8 IW/CPE + 100% RDF in wetter years, confirming strong year × treatment interaction effects. Essential oil is an indicator of spices and it was increased with drip fertigation. Drip fertigation is a more viable and acceptable option under harsh environmental conditions of arid and semi-arid zones.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-31467-3.Subject terms: Plant sciences, Climate sciences

Introduction

Cumin (Cuminum cyminum L.) is a key agricultural spice, native to regions including East India and the eastern Mediterranean. It is the most popular seed spice in the world after black pepper1, mostly confined to India, Bulgaria, Chile, Cyprus, Egypt, Iran, Mexico, Morocco, Syria, Tajikistan, Turkey and Uzbekistan2. India dominates the global cumin seed market in terms of cultivation, domestic use, and international trade. There is significant demand for value-added products like, seeds, perfumes, powder, oleoresin, and essential oil etc. in both domestic and international market3. In India, cumin seeds are part of the human plate due to its unique aroma. Cumin seeds are rich in nutritional values and 100 g of cumin seed contain 44.24 g of carbohydrates, 370 kcal of energy, 22.27 g of protein, 10.5 g of dietary fiber along with many vitamins and minerals4 required for human growth and development. Cumin seeds contain 2.5 −4.0% cuminol, a volatile oil that imparts their distinctive aroma and is widely used in perfumery as well as in flavoring liquors and cordials5. It is also used to remediate respiratory conditions such as asthma and bronchitis, also offers various medicinal benefits, including immune function modulation, relieving pain, exhibiting antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, lowering blood pressure, anti-cancer effects, and liver support6. In India, cultivation of cumin is confined in arid and semi-arid regions of the western part. Rajasthan and Gujarat contribute more than 95% of the cumin production. In Rajasthan, it is mostly cultivated in Barmer, Jodhpur, Jalore, Pali, Sirohi, and Nagaur districts. These districts together contribute to 61% of the state cumin cultivation area7. Cumin grows and yields best in cool and dry climate. Unfavorable post-flowering conditions such as cloudy skies, excessive humidity, unseasonal rainfall, and dew accumulation can be detrimental to crop development. High humidity and cloudy weather during the blooming and fruiting, promotes the incidence of diseases (blight and powdery mildew) and insect pests, which significantly reduces the crop yields8,9. Under arid and semi-arid regions of the western areas of India, low annual rainfall, heat waves and strong winds make irrigation water a major limitation for the cultivation of crop plants. The limited availability of water for irrigation makes the situation worse, therefore, precise water application through micro irrigation may enhance the water productivity of cumin in drought-prone regions. Drip fertigation method improved WUE by minimizing losses through evaporation, percolation and runoff compared to other open irrigation methods10. Results showed that irrigation via drip improved the water-use efficiency by increasing water saving and enhancing crop yields11. When the nutrients applied via broadcast or band placement methods, fertilizer use efficiency often remains low due to significant nutrient losses in various forms. Application of nutrient solutions along with irrigation water provides good results and saves precious fertilizer inputs12. Drip fertigation offers several advantages in many crops by improving water and fertilizer use efficiency, reducing water and nitrogen losses through leaching, supplying nutrients directly to root zone, and by reducing the application cost. Thus, drip fertigation is essential for increasing the yield of most of the crops13,14. Drip fertigation prevents runoff, delays the occurrence of pests or diseases, prevents foliage burn, and lessens ground water pollution with chemical fertilizers15. Government initiatives such as “More Crop per Drop,” promotion of on-farm water harvesting through farm ponds, and the adoption of micro-irrigation systems (drip and sprinkler) have significantly contributed to improving water-use efficiency and agricultural sustainability in India’s arid and semi-arid regions. In crops like cumin, where irrigation water availability is a key limiting factor, these technologies play a pivotal role in enhancing productivity and profitability. Drip irrigation, in particular, allows for precise and uniform water application directly to the root zone, minimizing losses due to evaporation, seepage, and deep percolation. This results in higher water productivity, improved nutrient use efficiency, and enhanced crop yields, as evidenced in cumin under arid field conditions. However, the large-scale adoption of drip irrigation faces several constraints. High initial installation costs, especially for smallholders, pose financial barriers. In arid regions, the quality of irrigation water often high in salts can cause emitter clogging and system inefficiencies. Additionally, the shorter lifespan of components, combined with the need for regular technical maintenance and system calibration, limits sustained use. Addressing these challenges through policy incentives, capacity building, and localized technological adaptations is essential to scaling drip irrigation in resource-constrained agroecosystem, where every drop of water is critical for ensuring long-term agricultural resilience.

Considering the numerous advantages of drip fertigation in most cultivated crops under favorable ecologies, research was conducted to assess the effect of drip fertigation on cumin crop in fragile environments where irrigation water is a scarce resource. The study was conducted with the hypothesis that drip fertigation would not only save irrigation water but also enhance the productivity, quality and monetary profitability of the cumin crop in dry belts of Rajasthan.

Umbels per plant

The overall number of umbels per plant was significantly influenced with varied regimes of irrigation and fertilizers. The application of drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE along with 100% RDF recorded highest number of umbels per plant (34.90) and the percentage increase in umbels per plant was 44.81% over the control (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF).

Umbellate per umbel

The number of umbellate per umbel was observed to be highest (5.65) with the application of drip irrigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF and resulting in a percentage increase of 12.35% over the control (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF).

Seed yield (kg/ha)

Different levels of drip irrigation combined with fertigation had a significant effect on seed yield (CD = 197.19, p < 0.05; Table 1). The pooled data indicated that drip irrigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF recorded significantly the highest seed yield of 830.83 kg ha− 1 which was significantly superior to the control (400.80 kg ha− 1). However, the seed yield remained statistically similar with drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPW ratio with 80% RDF (Supp. Table 3). The magnitude of increase in seed yield under drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF was 107.29% over the control (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF).

Economics analysis

The gross returns (GR, ₹/ha), net returns (NR, ₹/ha) and B: C ratio of cumin were significantly enhanced with the application of drip irrigation at various IW/CPE regimes and fertigation levels over surface irrigation (CD = 33,266 for GR, CD = 33,266 for NR, p < 0.05; Table 1). Drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF recorded the highest gross returns (₹1,37,113 ha− 1, ₹1,29,229 ha− 1 and ₹1,57,334 ha− 1), net returns (₹ 85,443 ha− 1, ₹ 77,559 ha− 1 and ₹1,05,674 ha− 1) and benefit-cost ratio (1.65, 1.5 and 2.05) and it was superior to control/surface irrigation (₹34,475 ha− 1 NR; B:C = 1.027) during all the three years (Supp. Table 4).

Oil content of cumin seed

The highest essential oil content in cumin seed was recorded with drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF during all the study years (2016–2019). There was no significant difference among the treatments. In all three consecutive years, cumin seed oil content was 4.58%, 4.17% and 4.87% under the application of drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF and the percentage increase in oil content was 24%, 25% and 1.45% over the control (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF), respectively (Supp. Table 3).

Water studies and interaction effects of year and irrigation levels

A significant interaction between year and irrigation × fertigation treatments was observed for water used, water use efficiency (WUE), and water productivity (p < 0.05), as presented in Supplementary Table 5. These interactions reflect the sensitivity of treatment performance to seasonal climatic variation, particularly rainfall variability.

Across all three years, the lowest total water use was consistently recorded in treatments with 0.4 IW/CPE, ranging from 149.5 mm in 2016–17 to 210.3 mm in 2018–19. In contrast, the standard check (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE with 100% RDF) had the highest water input, ranging from 337.3 mm to 515.1 mm. Based on the three-year average, drip fertigation reduced irrigation water use by 58.2%, 38.8%, and 19.7% at 0.4, 0.6, and 0.8 IW/CPE ratios, respectively, compared to the conventional surface irrigation method.

The highest WUE was recorded in the 0.4 IW/CPE with 100% RDF treatment in 2016–17 (4.20 kg ha− 1-mm− 1), though it declined in subsequent years with higher rainfall, indicating potential over-irrigation effects. In contrast, the standard check had the lowest and most variable WUE, dropping to 0.43 kg ha− 1 mm− 1 in 2017–18.

Water productivity (economic return per unit water used) followed a similar trend. Treatments with 0.6–0.8 IW/CPE and higher RDF levels showed stable performance across years, while those with 0.4 IW/CPE were more efficient in drier years but showed reduced stability under wetter conditions.

These findings confirm the significant Year × Treatment interaction (CD at 5% = 0.44 for water use, 0.28 for WUE, and ₹46.9/ha-mm for water productivity), emphasizing the importance of rainfall-adaptive irrigation scheduling. Optimizing drip fertigation regimes must therefore account for seasonal rainfall dynamics to sustain yield, WUE, and profitability in arid agroecosystem. (Figures 1 and 2).

Discussion

Cumin is a crop well adapted to arid and semi-arid regions such as Rajasthan and Gujarat in India, where it serves as a valuable cash crop for smallholders. Despite its adaptability, cumin productivity is highly sensitive to water and nutrient scarcity. Under favorable conditions, it can generate three to five times more profit for farmers, highlighting the importance of efficient resource management strategies such as drip fertigation.

Growth and yield attributes

The pooled results from three years (Table 1) revealed that drip fertigation significantly enhanced various growth attributes, including plant height (PH), number of primary branches per plant (PB), number of secondary branches per plant (SB), number of umbels per plant (UPP) and number of umbellates per umbel (UPU), across all experimental years. The maximum plant height was recorded with drip irrigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio using 100% RDF followed by 80% RDF. Our results showed that drip irrigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio layered with 100% RDF increased plant height by 20.79% compared to the control (surface irrigation at a 0.80 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF). The application of water at a 0.4 IW/CPE ratio was found to be insufficient to fulfil the water requirement, hence almost all growth and yield attributes were lower compared to surface irrigation and other irrigation levels. The synergy of adequate water (0.8 IW/CPE) and sufficient nutrients (100% RDF) delivered efficiently through drip irrigation ensure that the crop doesn’t experience water or nutrient stress. This promotes cell division and elongation, leading to taller, healthier plants. The results are in close conformity with the work reported by Godara et al.26 in fennel, Harisha et al.27 in coriander and Kunapara28 in cumin. They reported the highest plant height at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF and 0.8 ET with 75% RDF, respectively under the same ecologies. A study conducted by Ebrahimian29 investigated the effects of water stress on oilseed crops including sunflower, safflower, and sesame, reporting yield reductions of 16.3%, 9.4%, and 12.8%, respectively. The study also highlighted a significant decline in oil content under limited irrigation, underscoring the critical role of adequate water supply in both yield quantity and quality. Moreover, the benefits of drip fertigation in enhancing growth and productivity have been demonstrated across various field crops. For instance, Badr et al.30 reported substantial improvements in potato growth and tuber yield under drip fertigation. At the same time Ocwa et al.31 observed marked yield increases in maize under similar management conditions. Drip fertigation consistently enhanced vegetative development by significantly increasing the number of primary and secondary branches per plant, attributable to the continuous and precise delivery of water and nutrients, which supports optimal physiological processes and robust canopy development. These results suggest that drip fertigation at 0.8 IW/CPE with full RDF can substantially enhance cumin productivity even under water-limited conditions, making it a reliable and farmer-friendly technology.

Drip fertigation ensured the maintenance of optimal soil moisture levels throughout the crop growth cycle, thereby facilitating efficient water uptake and enhancing nutrient availability to the root zone. This continuous and balanced supply of water and nutrients resulted in improved physiological functioning, particularly in sustaining higher turgor pressure within meristematic cells. Elevated turgor pressure is a key driver of active cell division and elongation, ultimately contributing to increased plant height and overall vegetative vigor. Consequently, the integration of drip fertigation significantly improved crop growth dynamics and yield performance compared to conventional surface irrigation methods32. Beyond increasing output, this strategy fosters sustainable cultivation practices.

In the present investigation, the highest cumin seed yield (830.83 kg ha− 1) was achieved with drip irrigation applied at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio in conjunction with 100% of the recommended dose of fertilizers (RDF), followed by a yield of 755.63 kg ha− 1under the same irrigation level with 80% RDF, consistently across all study years. This treatment resulted in yield improvements of 9.05%, 27.07%, 28.04%, and 38.39% over the preceding combinations of 0.8 IW/CPE with 80% and 60% RDF, as well as 0.6 IW/CPE with 100% and 80% RDF, respectively. Furthermore, a remarkable yield enhancement of 51.75% was observed when compared to the control treatment (surface irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE with 100% RDF). The significant increase in cumin seed yield (830.83 kg ha− 1) with drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio + 100% RDF is the result of optimized water and nutrient availability, two critical factors in determining crop productivity. Drip irrigation significantly enhanced cumin seed yield and water productivity compared to surface and micro sprinkler irrigation methods33. Specifically, drip irrigation resulted in a 40.71% increase in yield and a 41.3% improvement in water productivity compared to surface irrigation34. The superior performance of drip irrigation can be attributed to the precise and localized application of water at a low flow rate and frequent intervals, directly within the crop root zone. This method, delivered through a low-pressure system, enhances water and nutrient availability by minimizing percolation and nutrient leaching losses. Consequently, the continuous maintenance of optimum soil moisture and nutrient concentration near the rhizosphere promotes efficient uptake, leading to improved plant growth and higher yield performance.

The observed year × treatment interactions for water use, WUE, and water productivity underscore the importance of rainfall-responsive irrigation in arid regions. Drip fertigation at 0.4 IW/CPE achieved the highest WUE in drier years (4.20 kg ha− 1 mm− 1 in 2016–17) but declined under wetter conditions, indicating reduced crop water demand. Conversely, the standard check basin treatment used the highest water (up to 515.1 mm) with the lowest and most variable WUE, suggesting inefficient water use. Agronomically, drip fertigation enhanced water and nutrient delivery near the root zone, improving uptake efficiency and supporting higher yield stability at moderate IW/CPE ratios (0.6–0.8). Physiologically, optimal soil moisture maintained by drip irrigation sustained turgor pressure, enhancing photosynthesis, assimilate transport, and the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites such as essential oils. These results emphasize that drip fertigation not only conserves water but also supports key physiological processes essential for growth and quality. Seasonal rainfall must be considered when scheduling irrigation to optimize yield, WUE, and economic return in arid agroecosystems. In contrast, the application of surface irrigation (control treatment) increased ambient humidity in the crop microclimate, creating a conducive environment for the proliferation of diseases, particularly cumin blight. The elevated disease incidence had a negatively impact on plant health and significantly reduced seed yield.

Similarly, Gohil et al.35 reported the highest values for key yield components of cumin including the number of umbels per plant, the number of umbellates per umbel, the number of seeds per umbellate, and 1000-seed weight under drip irrigation applied at a 0.8 PEF (Pan Evaporation Fraction). The findings of the present study are in close agreement with those of Meena et al.36, who observed similar enhancements in fennel under comparable irrigation regimes. In practice, farmers can optimize both yield and WUE by targeting moderate irrigation levels (0.6–0.8 IW/CPE) and adjusting based on in-season rainfall. Furthermore, Singh et al.34 demonstrated that the use of drip or micro-sprinkler irrigation systems in cumin cultivation significantly outperformed conventional surface irrigation in terms of seed yield, highlighting the superior efficiency and productivity potential of micro-irrigation methods in arid and semi-arid agro ecosystems.

Economics

The results of the present study conclusively indicate that drip irrigation applied at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio in conjunction with 100% of the recommended dose of fertilizers (RDF) significantly enhances the seed yield and economic returns of cumin. This integrated treatment consistently outperformed other irrigation and nutrient combinations across three years, resulting in a notable increase in seed yield (830.83 kg ha− 1) and net returns (₹ 55,084/ha), along with a higher benefit-cost ratio (0.706), compared to conventional surface irrigation practices. Enhanced profitability can be attributed to improved water and nutrient use efficiency under drip irrigation, which ensures a more favorable soil moisture regime and optimal nutrient availability throughout the crop growth period. These findings are in close agreement with the reports of Gohil et al.35, who observed the highest economic returns with drip irrigation at 0.8 PEF and 125% RDF in cumin, and Giana et al.37 who reported similar benefits in fennel. Therefore, it is evident that the adoption of drip irrigation at 0.8 IW/CPE ratio combined with 100% RDF can serve as a highly effective strategy for enhancing yield and economic sustainability in cumin cultivation, particularly in water-scarce arid and semi-arid regions (Supp. Table 4).

Water saving

The higher WUE at lower irrigation levels can be attributed to the efficient utilization of water for plant processes. On the other hand, higher surface irrigation led to greater water losses through percolation, leaching, evaporation, or retention in the soil, which exceeds the plant’s water requirements. In the present study, we observe that lower irrigation levels are more efficient in terms of Water Use Efficiency (WUE) compared to higher irrigation levels, aligning with findings from Quezada et al.38 in carrots and Lal et al.32 in fenugreek, both of whom reported lower WUE with excess water. These findings are similar to the results reported by Bhunia et al.39 in fenugreek and Harisha et al.27 in coriander further emphasized the role of evaporation and percolation losses, which increase as irrigation exceeds the crop’s needs. Therefore, managing irrigation to meet the specific water requirements of crops is crucial for improving productivity, optimizing water use, and ensuring sustainable agricultural practices, particularly in regions with limited water resources.

Oil content and quality

In the present study, drip fertigation was found to significantly enhance both the oil content and volatile oil yield in cumin (Table 1). These results are consistent with the findings of Harisha et al.27,40 Shivran et al.41 who reported analogous improvements in coriander, chia and fennel under elevated irrigation levels. The controlled and consistent moisture supply, coupled with precise nutrient delivery through drip fertigation, facilitates optimal metabolic processes within the plant, thereby fostering the biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, including essential oils, which ultimately leads to enhanced oil yields. Specifically, steady water availability under drip fertigation helps maintain turgor pressure in meristematic tissues, promoting active cell division and expansion. Enhanced nutrient uptake particularly nitrogen and potassium supports the biosynthesis of essential oil precursors such as terpenoids and phenylpropanoids via the mevalonate and MEP pathways, which are sensitive to water and nutrient status42,43. Optimal soil moisture also improves photosynthetic efficiency and carbon allocation, directing resources toward essential oil synthesis during key growth stages. These physiological responses collectively foster the biosynthesis and accumulation of essential oils, resulting in higher EO content and oil yield in cumin. For cumin growers, this has direct market relevance, as higher essential oil concentration not only enhances product quality and export potential but also contributes to greater profitability. The economic viability of drip fertigation has been further demonstrated through its cost-effectiveness, with studies in coriander by Unlukara et al.44; Hassan and Ali45 who reported significant improvements in yield and returns under efficient irrigation and fertigation regimes. These findings collectively highlight the broader applicability of drip fertigation for improving both productivity and profitability across seed spices and other crops, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions.

Conclusions

Cumin, a major seed spice extensively cultivated across arid and semi-arid regions of India, faces persistent productivity challenges due to acute water scarcity during its critical growth stages. With nearly one million hectares under cumin cultivation, the need for efficient water and nutrient management strategies is both urgent and essential for ensuring sustainable production. The present study clearly demonstrates that integrating drip irrigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% of the recommended dose of fertilizers (RDF) significantly enhanced plant growth attributes, seed yield, and essential oil content compared to conventional surface irrigation. The highest seed yield of 830.83 kg ha− 1 was recorded under this treatment, representing a remarkable 51.75% increase over the control. Drip fertigation also improved oil content and delivered the highest economic returns, with net returns of ₹55,084 ha− 1 and a benefit–cost ratio of 0.71, underscoring its superiority as a water- and nutrient-efficient technology.

Importantly, drip fertigation enabled comparable or higher yields with nearly 40–50% less irrigation water, thereby increasing water productivity by 41.3% and offering farmers the option to double the cultivated area with the same water resources. In addition, drip fertigation enhanced oil quality, reduced disease incidence by avoiding excess canopy humidity, and supported long-term groundwater sustainability. Even at 80% RDF, fertigation produced competitive results, suggesting potential savings in fertilizer use without compromising yield.

Overall, drip fertigation at a 0.8 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF represents a transformative, climate-resilient strategy for cumin cultivation. By ensuring higher productivity, better quality, greater profitability, and sustainable use of scarce resources, this technology holds immense promise for strengthening farmer livelihoods and securing the future of seed spice production in India’s water-limited regions. Future research should explore integrating drip fertigation with precision nutrient management, digital irrigation scheduling, and climate-smart practices to further enhance resilience, resource efficiency, and scalability in seed spice-based farming systems.

Materials and methods

Experiment site characteristics

A field experiment was conducted during three consecutive years i.e. 2016–2017, 2017–2018 and 2018–2019 in the Rabi season at the Research farm of ICAR- National Research Centre on Seed Spices, Tabiji, Ajmer, Rajasthan to evaluate the effect of drip fertigation on growth, yield, economics and quality of cumin (Fig. 5). The soil was loamy sand in texture46, low in organic carbon (0.21%), available N (180.2 kg/ha), available P (13.7 kg/ha) and medium in available K (203.1 kg/ha) with an alkaline pH (8.1). The soil contain 4.28 mg kg− 1 DTPA extractable iron, 8.69 mg kg− 1 DTPA extractable manganese, 0.74 mg kg− 1 DTPA extractable copper, 0.93 mg kg− 1 DTPA extractable zinc and 0.41 mg kg− 1 hot water extractable boron. The soil had a bulk density of 1.42 Mg/m3, a particle density of 2.56 Mg/m3, a moisture content at field capacity of 13.60% and a moisture content at the permanent wilting point of 4.11% at the beginning of the experiment (Table 3). The ten treatments consisted of control (surface irrigation at a 1.0 IW/CPE ratio with 100% RDF), and the remaining nine treatments were the combination of three levels of irrigation water depth (0.4, 0.6, 0.8 IW/CPE ratio) along with three levels (60, 80 and 100% RDF) of recommended dose of fertilizers (40:30:30, N: P:K) under drip fertigation system (Table 2). The experiment was evaluated in randomized block design (RBD) with 4 replications, conducted in a plot size of 10.0 × 3.0 m2 with a row spacing of 30 cm. The crop was sown during the 2nd −3rd week of November and harvested during the 2nd week of March with a crop duration of 110–120 days during the years under consideration. The crop variety was GC-4. The average total ET during the growing period of cumin was 501.5 mm. The drip irrigation in was scheduled every 4th day after multiplying CPE by a pan factor of 0.85. In contrast in surface irrigation, water was delivered when CPE reached to 50 mm depending on the daily PE after multiplying it by a pan factor of 0.862. During the crop period, 426.3 mm of irrigation water was used under the surface irrigation method. However, 343.6, 260.9 and 178.2 mm of irrigation water were applied at 0.8, 0.6 and 0.4 IW/CPE ratios using the drip irrigation method. Water soluble fertilizers NPK (18:18:18), urea (46%), urea phosphate (12:61) and K2SO4 (50%) were applied according to the treatment with varied RDF levels in surface as well as drip irrigation.

Meteorological observation

Weekly average weather data were obtained from the meteorological observatory at ICAR–National Research Centre on Seed Spices, Ajmer, India (Fig. 6), situated at 26°21′57.83″ N latitude, 74°35′36.80″ E longitude, and an elevation of 440 m above mean sea level (MSL). The observatory recorded daily and monthly averages of key climatic parameters including maximum and minimum temperatures, relative humidity, and rainfall. Cumulative pan evaporation was calculated daily using the open pan evaporimeter method. The maximum temperatures during the crop growing periods of 2016-17, 2017-18 and 2018-19 of the crops growing periods were 30.5, 33.8 and 32.4◦C respectively; however, corresponding values for minimum temperatures were 3.1, 3.1 and 1.5 ◦C. The weekly rainfall at the experimental site during the crop growing period was 24.3 mm in 2016, 7.2 mm in 2017 and 7.1 mm in 2018. The relative humidity ranged from 41.7 to 69.9% at 2.40 pm and 91.3 to 92.3% at 7.40 am in 2016–2017, from 39.6 to 61.1% at 2.40 pm and 76.6 to 80.3% at 7.40 am in 2017–2018, similarly it varied from 24.1 to 67.4% at 2.40 pm and 63.7 to 68.6% at 7.40 am in 2018–2019. The total pan evaporation (ET0) during the crop growing period of 2016-17, 2017-18 and 2018-19 was 460.3, 582.1, 703.0 mm. In contrast ETc was 396.8, 501.8 mm and 606.0 mm, respectively with the average seasonal ETc recorded as 501.5 mm during the crop growth period (Table 4).

Soil analysis

The available nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the soil were measured using the standard methods described by Subbiah and Asija22, Olsen21, and the flame photometer technique by Jackson23, respectively. Additionally, soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC) and organic carbon content were analyzed before crop sowing following the established protocols of Jackson23, Walkley and Black47. The experimental soil contained 148 kg ha− 1 of available nitrogen, 16.1 kg ha− 1 of available phosphorus, 325.5 kg ha− 1 of available potassium. The soil pH, EC and organic carbon were 8.1, 0.33 dSm− 1 and 0.3%, respectively (Table 3).

Input (water and fertilizer) application

In the drip irrigation system, lateral lines were laid out at 60 cm spacing, equipped with inline emitters spaced at 30 cm intervals, each having a discharge rate of 2.1 L h− 1. The system operated at a pressure of 2.5 kg cm⁻² to ensure uniform discharge across all emitters. Based on field calibration, the actual emitter discharge was observed at 2.1 L h− 1, and the overall system performance accounting for drip system efficiency and emission uniformity ranged between 90 and 95%, respectively. Soil moisture content was monitored using the gravimetric method at regular intervals to track soil water dynamics under different irrigation treatments.

Water saving estimation

Water saving was calculated based on the total seasonal water input, which included both the irrigation water applied and the effective rainfall received during the crop growth period. For each treatment, cumulative irrigation water use was recorded across all scheduled irrigation events, determined by the prescribed IW/CPE ratio. Effective rainfall was estimated using the USDA Soil Conservation Service method, accounting only for rainfall events exceeding 2.5 mm and adjusted for soil infiltration characteristics.

Irrigation was applied once every four days and a total of seven fertigation schedules were given during the entire cropping period starting fifteen days after germination of crop at 8–15 days intervals as per crop growth stage. In this trial, three irrigation regimes (0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 IW/CPE ratio) and three fertigation levels F1 − 60% nutrients (24:18.4:18.4 kg N, P2O5 and K2O per hectare), F2 − 80% nutrients (32:24:24 kg N, P2O5 and K2O per hectare) and F3 − 100% nutrients (40:30:30 kg N, P2O5 and K2O per hectare) their combination was implemented along with control. Fertigation was applied through water soluble fertilizers (WSF) such as NPK (18:18:18), urea (46%), urea phosphate (12:61) and K2SO4 (50%) in seven equal splits at 8–15 days intervals as per the treatment (Table 4).

Analysis of water studies

Water use efficiency (WUE) values as kg ha− 1 mm− 1 of irrigation water applied were calculated for each treatment after harvest using Eq. (3) according to Jensen50.

To complement WUE, economic water productivity (₹ m⁻³) was calculated as the ratio of gross income to total water used. This provides a holistic view of both biophysical and economic efficiency of irrigation, crucial for water-scarce regions.

Growth and yield attributes

The observations on plant height (PH), umbels per plant (UPP), umbellets per umbel (UPU), seed per umbel (SPU) were manually recorded on five randomly selected representative plants from each plot in every replication. Randomly selected five plants from each treatment and replication were tagged in the field for recording observations like plant height, umbels per plant, and seed yield. Seed yield per plant was recorded and converted into seed yield per hectare. Harvesting was done in the second week of March every year.
