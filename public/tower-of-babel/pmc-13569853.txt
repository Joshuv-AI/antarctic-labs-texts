Three-dimensional reconstruction of glacier-derived freshwater in East Antarctica using an end-member-independent hydrographic parameterization
Authors: Yutaka W Watanabe, Daisuke Hirano, Yoshihiko Ohashi, Miyabi Sugita, Yoshiyuki Nakano, Ryosuke Makabe, Kohei Mizobata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe melting of the Antarctic Ice Sheet contributes to global sea-level rise and widespread ocean freshening. A major driver of this process is the intrusion of warm deep water onto the continental shelf, which enhances basal melting of ice shelves and weakens their buttressing of inland ice. However, the three-dimensional distribution of glacier-derived freshwater remains poorly constrained in East Antarctica. Here we present a hybrid multiple linear regression–neural network (MLR–NN) framework that reconstructs glacier-derived freshwater from dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) residuals without prescribing fixed freshwater end-member compositions. Applying this framework to hydrographic observations around the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves, we estimate freshwater discharge rates of 70 ± 7 and 53 ± 9 Gt yr⁻¹, respectively. The reconstructed freshwater signal extends across the continental shelf to approximately 64°S. We also identify differences in freshwater discharge between observations collected during the 2010s and the 2020 s, although sparse temporal sampling prevents these differences from being interpreted as a robust long-term trend. Our results provide a three-dimensional observational reconstruction of glacier-derived freshwater in this sector of East Antarctica and demonstrate the potential of an end-member-independent hydrographic parameterization for investigating glacier–ocean interactions in data-limited polar environments.Subject terms: Physical oceanography, Environmental impact

Introduction

The Antarctic Ice Sheet contains approximately 70% of Earth’s freshwater, equivalent to about 58 m of global mean sea-level rise if fully melted1. Under ongoing global warming, the Antarctic Ice Sheet has been losing mass at an accelerating rate2. Recent studies have documented substantial mass loss, particularly in West Antarctica and the Antarctic Peninsula, driven largely by the thinning and retreat of outlet glaciers and ice shelves in response to oceanic and atmospheric warming2–4. Consequently, Antarctica’s contribution to global sea-level rise has increased markedly since the 2010s, highlighting its growing importance in future sea-level projections3,5–7.

A key focus of recent research has been the direct interaction between the Antarctic Ice Sheet and the surrounding ocean, particularly the intrusion of warm Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) onto the continental shelf and its role in enhancing basal melting beneath ice shelves8–13 (Fig. 1). Ocean-driven ice-shelf thinning can reduce buttressing and promote grounding-line retreat, thereby accelerating ice discharge from the ice sheet. Although recent advances in high-resolution, physics-based ice-sheet models coupled with climate models have improved the representation of observed Antarctic ice-sheet changes, substantial uncertainties remain in projections beyond the mid-twenty-first century14.

Conceptual schematic illustrating the framework used to estimate glacier-derived freshwater from dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC).

In the Southern Ocean (south of 30°S), the region south of 60°S with a seafloor depth shallower than 1500 m is defined as the coastal Southern Ocean (SOcoastal), whereas the remaining area is defined as the open Southern Ocean (SOopen). In the absence of glacier-derived freshwater input, seawater in SOcoastal is assumed to originate from SOopen, such that DICint = DICopen. Warm modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW) flows southward from SOopen into ice-shelf cavities within SOcoastal, where it induces basal melting and releases glacier-derived freshwater. The resulting freshwater-enriched mCDW becomes more buoyant and rises toward the ocean surface, while freshwater derived from glacier surface melting is simultaneously incorporated into the water column (DICfw; light-blue arrows). Mixing between glacier-derived freshwater and the initial seawater modifies the DIC concentration to DICcoastal, which represents the mixture of DICint and DICfw. Following Pan et al.7, DIC variations within the subsurface mCDW layer are assumed to be unaffected by sea-ice formation and melting. In the surface layer, where sea-ice formation/melting (SI) and evaporation/precipitation (EP) occur, their net effects are assumed to be equivalent between SOopen and SOcoastal and therefore cancel when estimating glacier-derived freshwater.

A major unresolved issue is the uncertainty in the mass balance of East Antarctica. East Antarctica, which contains most of the Antarctic ice, exhibits substantial regional variability in ice-sheet change, and uncertainties remain in estimates of its overall mass balance15,16. Sparse observations, uncertainties in subglacial bedrock topography, and complex glaciological processes hinder accurate quantification, particularly because incomplete mapping beneath ice shelves and limited in situ ocean observations constrain model forcing and validation7,17–20.

Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves are major marine-based outlet glaciers draining the Aurora Subglacial Basin. Together, they contain ice equivalent to approximately 5.1 m of global mean sea-level rise and have experienced substantial ice-shelf thinning and enhanced basal melting associated with the intrusion of warm ocean waters21–26. Their grounding lines are located well below sea level, making them susceptible to ocean-driven basal melting associated with intrusions of warm modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW; neutral density range γn = 28.00–28.2727,28)11,24,29,30. Satellite remote sensing and oceanographic observations have revealed thinning of the Totten Ice Shelf, grounding-line retreat, and enhanced basal melting associated with mCDW inflow15,24,25. Numerical models have also advanced our understanding of ice-sheet–ocean interactions in this region, although uncertainties in basal topography and ocean heat transport remain substantial20.

Quantifying glacier-derived ocean freshening remains challenging because of severe weather conditions in the Southern Ocean (SO; south of 30°S), limited observational coverage, and the lack of a systematic method for resolving the three-dimensional distribution of freshwater derived from glacier melting. Existing approaches have important limitations. Satellite observations are widely used to monitor ice-sheet mass balance and sea-level contribution5,15,31, and basal melt rates can be inferred by subtracting surface ice discharge from total ice-sheet mass change32. However, satellite observations cannot directly resolve the spatial distribution of glacier-derived freshwater in the ocean. Numerical simulations of coupled ocean–sea ice–ice shelf systems provide valuable insight into ice–ocean interactions20,29,33, but their ability to reconstruct ocean freshening is limited by uncertainties in physical and chemical observations, bathymetry, and model forcing11. In situ observations using tracers such as salinity and oxygen isotopes (δ¹⁸O) can detect freshwater signals16,34,35, and endmember approaches are commonly used to separate freshwater sources13,29,36. However, water-mass endmembers vary in space and time, making them difficult to constrain and introducing substantial uncertainty. Repeated in situ observations are also logistically difficult in Antarctic environments and generally provide limited spatial and temporal coverage.

To address these limitations, recent studies have employed parameterization techniques that reconstruct biogeochemical properties from routinely observed hydrographic variables7,37,38. In this framework, a target chemical property is statistically predicted from hydrographic parameters such as temperature (T), salinity (S), dissolved oxygen (DO), and pressure (Pr), which collectively reflect water-mass transport, mixing, and biogeochemical processes. Because these hydrographic variables capture dominant internal ocean processes, the parameterized concentration represents the component of the chemical property that can be explained by internal ocean dynamics. Any systematic difference between observed and parameterized concentrations can therefore be interpreted as the influence of external processes that are not explicitly represented by the hydrographic relationships.

In the Southern Ocean coastal region, glacier-derived freshwater input modifies seawater properties and carbon concentrations but is not directly represented by hydrographic parameterization. Consequently, by quantifying the residual between observed and parameterized dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC), the contribution of glacier-derived freshwater can be estimated without defining water-mass endmembers or imposing spatial constraints.

In this study, glacier-derived freshwater input is treated as the dominant external process contributing to DICex in subsurface and deep waters influenced by mCDW. In surface waters, additional effects from sea-ice formation/melting and evaporation/precipitation are considered separately. The estimated DICex is subsequently used to derive the glacier-derived freshwater fraction (Fg) through Eqs. (2)–(4).

In the present study, DIC is parameterized from hydrographic variables using a hybrid multiple linear regression–neural network (MLR–NN) framework. DIC was selected because it exhibits large spatial variability, high analytical precision, and strong sensitivity to freshwater inputs, allowing external perturbations to be detected from deviations between observed and parameterized concentrations. We applied this framework to hydrographic and biogeochemical observations from GLODAPv2.2023 and the Japan Antarctic Research Expedition (JARE) around the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves. Using this approach, we reconstructed the three-dimensional distribution of glacier-derived freshwater, estimated meltwater discharge rates, and evaluated recent changes in freshwater input to this sector of East Antarctica. Unlike conventional end-member mixing analyses, this framework estimates glacier-derived freshwater from DIC residuals without prescribing fixed freshwater end-member compositions or their spatial and temporal variability. This enables three-dimensional reconstruction of glacier-derived freshwater over broad regions of the Southern Ocean using routinely observed hydrographic variables.

In SOcoastal at the fronts of the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves within the Sabrina Depression, south of approximately 66°S, Fg generally exceeded 10‰ in surface waters shallower than approximately 100 dbar (Fig. 2; representative stations TN9, TN5, and TN26). This layer is characterized by low salinity and low temperature, reflecting freshwater input from sea-ice melt and icebergs36, and is consistent with previous estimates of glacier-derived meltwater mixed with sea-ice-derived freshwater based on δ¹⁸O endmember calculations39.

Between approximately 100 and 500 dbar, Fg was generally maintained at approximately 2‰, comparable to previous tracer-based estimates of glacier-derived meltwater in SOcoastal34,39 (Fig. 3). In Vincennes Bay (~107°E, ~66°S), located west of the Sabrina Depression, vertical profiles of Fg below the surface layer were close to zero, differing from those observed near the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelf fronts (Fig. 3, S5, S6, and Supplementary Data 1). In SOopen north of approximately 65°S, Fg showed only slightly positive values in surface waters shallower than 100 dbar and remained nearly zero below 100 dbar (Figs. 2, 3, and S5). The mean deep-water anomaly from zero in this region was 0.3‰ (Table S2), and the negligible Fg signals in regions and depth ranges with little direct glacier influence provide independent support for the validity of the parameterization-based Fg estimates.

At mooring station TM1 (116.51°E, 66.60°S; bottom depth 1020 m), located near the front of the Totten Ice Shelf, mooring systems were deployed at 500, 880, and 1000 dbar from February 2022 to March 2024 to collect T, S, DO, and Pr data (Fig. 4). This mooring station is close to sampling stations TN9 and TN5 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1). Applying the hybrid MLR–NN DIC parameterizations to the TM1 mooring data yielded time series of Fg at the Totten Ice Shelf front (Fig. 4). Annual mean Fg values were 2.1 ± 0.4‰ at 1,000 dbar, 2.8 ± 0.4‰ at 880 dbar, and 2.0 ± 0.6‰ at 500 dbar, where uncertainties represent one standard deviation. Taken together, the hydrographic and mooring observations indicate that Fg increases toward shallower depths. These results are consistent with a pathway in which warm mCDW enters the cavity beneath the Totten Ice Shelf, induces basal melting, and produces meltwater-enriched seawater that becomes more buoyant and rises toward the surface through mixing11,24,29 (Fig. 1, S7, and S8).

A caveat is that the hydrographic observations used here represent late-summer conditions, with offshore stations sampled in January and coastal stations sampled from late February to early March. These periods correspond to the end of the sea-ice melt season offshore and the period immediately before new sea-ice formation begins near the coast. Thus, shallow freshwater signals may include a peak seasonal contribution from sea-ice melt, particularly in low-salinity, low-temperature waters shallower than 100 dbar36 (Fig. 2).

In this study, the initial estimate of Fg assumes that the net effects of sea-ice formation/melting (SI) and evaporation/precipitation (EP) are the same between SOopen and SOcoastal and therefore cancel in Eq. (4)7. However, if SI is larger in SOcoastal than in SOopen, Fg in the surface layer may be overestimated. Satellite-based estimates from the 2000s indicate that the annual ratio of SI between SOopen and SOcoastal is approximately 1:2, with longitudinal variability across East Antarctica reaching ~50%40. A comparable ratio was independently derived within the Sabrina Depression from salinity deficits associated with sea-ice melt using approximately 25,000 spring hydrographic profiles collected between the mid-2000s and the 2020s, covering the same period as the observations analyzed in this study41. Based on this annual SOopen-to-SOcoastal SI ratio of approximately 1:2, we corrected Fg in surface waters shallower than the layer of maximum temperature within the upper 100 dbar by multiplying it by 0.5. The corrected freshwater fraction, Fg(c), is therefore defined as

where SIopen/SIcoastal = 0.5. The corrected value, Fg(c), is interpreted as a conservative estimate of the glacier-derived freshwater fraction in the surface layer (Figs. 2, 3a–c, and S5; see Methods).

After this correction, Fg(c) in the surface layer of SOcoastal decreased to approximately half of Fg, reaching ~5‰ in waters characterized by low salinity and low temperature above ~100 dbar. The ratio of water-column-averaged Fg(c) to water-column-averaged Fg over 0–1000 dbar [Fg(c) ave/Fg ave] was 0.89 ± 0.11 in SOcoastal. In contrast, Fg(c) differed little from Fg in SOopen because no pronounced low-salinity, low-temperature layer was observed in the upper 100 dbar; the corresponding Fg(c) ave/Fg ave ratio was 0.97 ± 0.07 (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Data 1).

Discussion

Changes in freshwater input from the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves in East Antarctica

Water-column-averaged, sea ice-corrected Fg [Fg(c) ave] exceeded 2‰ at the fronts of the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelves, decreased gradually offshore, and approached 0‰ near 64°S (Fig. 3k). This offshore decrease likely reflects dilution by mixing with surrounding seawater and/or the diminishing influence of meltwater and icebergs (Fig. 3a–g).

Observation-constrained numerical simulations29, validated against hydrographic observations, satellite-derived circulation, and independent numerical models20,30,42,43, indicate that the clockwise on-shelf circulation associated with the westward-flowing Antarctic Slope Current has a climatological transport of 0.8–1.2 Sv during 1991–2020. This circulation supplies warm modified Circumpolar Deep Water (mCDW) to the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelf fronts. We therefore adopted the median value (1.0 Sv) as the representative climatological transport of this circulation for estimating glacier-derived freshwater discharge rates in the present study. Using the median transport rate of 1.0 Sv and multiplying it by mean Fg(c) ave values of 2.23 ± 0.21‰ in the Totten Ice Shelf region and 1.67 ± 0.28‰ in the Moscow University Ice Shelf region (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Data 1), we estimated freshwater formation rates from glacier melting, here defined as basal meltwater flux Rm, for the two ice-shelf front regions (Tables 1, 2).

The resulting Rm estimates are 70 ± 7 Gt yr⁻¹ for the Totten Ice Shelf front and 53 ± 9 Gt yr⁻¹ for the Moscow University Ice Shelf front. When the ±20% range in the climatological mean transport rate of the westward-flowing current is included (1.0 ± 0.2 Sv)20,29,30,42, the corresponding uncertainties increase to 70 ± 16 Gt yr⁻¹ and 53 ± 14 Gt yr⁻¹, respectively. Within these uncertainties, our estimates are broadly consistent with previous satellite-based estimates of basal melt rates5,15,31,44–46 (Table 1). This agreement provides independent observational support for the applicability of our parameterization-based approach for estimating glacier-derived freshwater. Importantly, our method also resolves the spatial distribution of meltwater-derived ocean freshening, which extends offshore to around 64°S in East Antarctica (Fig. 3), a feature that is difficult to constrain directly using satellite observations or conventional tracer-based approaches.

We also compared temporal changes in Rm at the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelf fronts (Fig. 5 and Table 2). Between the 2010s and the 2020s, Rm was estimated to be 130 ± 26% higher in the Totten region and 218 ± 46% higher in the Moscow University region. These differences may indicate enhanced ice-shelf melting associated with increased mCDW inflow into these regions. This interpretation is further supported by differences in the spatial distribution of Fg between the 2010s and the 2020 s, which suggest increased freshwater release from ice-shelf melting in the vicinity of the ice-shelf fronts during the later period (Figs. S9 and S10). However, these estimated differences are sensitive to the treatment of a limited number of observations and should therefore be interpreted with caution (see Table 2 footnotes). Alternative grouping and filtering of the available datasets yield different estimates of the magnitude of change, highlighting the uncertainty associated with sparse temporal sampling. The currently available observations are insufficient to determine whether these differences represent a persistent long-term trend or reflect natural temporal variability in mCDW transport to the ice-shelf fronts29.

Our results are also insufficient to resolve detailed spatiotemporal variability because repeated CTD observations and mooring measurements remain limited in the coastal Southern Ocean. In addition, our Rm estimates depend on the transport rate of the westward-flowing Antarctic Slope Current reaching the Totten and Moscow University Ice Shelf fronts, for which direct current-meter observations remain sparse in both space and time. Future improvements will require application of this framework to the expanding Southern Ocean Argo observing system, which provides autonomous vertical measurements of hydrographic properties such as T, S, DO, and Pr. Additional long-term current-meter observations across the coastal Southern Ocean will also be needed to better constrain ocean transport and its temporal variability. Combined with these expanding observational capabilities, the proposed end-member-independent hydrographic parameterization framework provides a practical approach for sustained monitoring of glacier-derived freshwater and its long-term variability across East Antarctica.

Methods

Overview of the parameterization framework

In this study, hydrographic variables (temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, and pressure) are first used to parameterize dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) using a hybrid multiple linear regression–neural network (MLR–NN) framework. The residual DIC component associated with glacier-derived freshwater is then used to estimate the glacier-derived freshwater fraction (Fg) through Eqs. (2)–(4). Finally, the estimated Fg is combined with the climatological transport of the clockwise on-shelf circulation associated with the westward-flowing Antarctic Slope Current to estimate glacier-derived freshwater discharge rates. Unlike conventional end-member mixing analyses, the proposed framework estimates glacier-derived freshwater from DIC residuals without prescribing fixed freshwater end-member compositions, thereby avoiding the need to define spatially and temporally varying end-member properties. This enables robust estimation of glacier-derived freshwater over broad spatial and temporal scales (see also Supplementary Fig. S1).

Data used in this study

The observational hydrographic data used to develop the DIC parameterizations, including DIC, T, S, DO, Pr, and related variables, were obtained from the Global Ocean Data Analysis Project version 2 (GLODAPv2.2023)47–51 for the Southern Ocean south of 30°S over the period 2000–2021. The quality of chemical measurements, including carbonate-system variables, has been maintained using certified reference materials since 200052. We therefore used data collected from 2000 onward. Data quality was evaluated using World Ocean Circulation Experiment (WOCE) quality flags (https://learning.cchdo.io/submitting_data/quick_guide). Only observations with a WOCE flag of 2, indicating acceptable data quality, were used to construct the DICopen and DICcoastal parameterizations.

To estimate glacier-derived freshwater fractions in the Southern Ocean, we used hydrographic observations from the Japan Antarctic Research Expedition (JARE) 59-65 (https://ads.nipr.ac.jp/), together with GLODAPv2.2023 data47–51. In the JARE dataset, hydrographic observations north of 66°S were mainly conducted aboard T/S Umitaka-Maru, operated by Tokyo University of Marine Science and Technology, using twenty-four 8-L X-Niskin bottles from the surface to 10 m above the seafloor. These data have quality comparable to WOCE flag 2. Hydrographic observations south of 66°S were conducted aboard the icebreaker R/V Shirase using twelve 4-L X-Niskin bottles from the surface to 1000 dbar, with quality also comparable to WOCE flag 2 (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Data 1). In addition, mooring systems were deployed near the front of the Totten Ice Shelf at Mooring Station TM1 (116.51°E, 66.60°S; bottom depth 1020 m) at depths of 500, 880, and 1000 dbar from February 2022 to March 2024 to collect T, S, DO, and Pr data (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Data 1). Cruise information is summarized in Supplementary Data 1.

Development and validation of hybrid MLR-NN parameterizations for DIC

Following the MLR framework of Pan et al.7, we developed hybrid MLR–NN parameterizations of DIC using the GLODAPv2.2023 dataset. The explanatory variables identified by the MLR analysis were then used to optimize nonlinear relationships with a neural network containing three hidden nodes. Model performance was evaluated using independent testing data and five-fold cross-validation.

To identify suitable explanatory variables for DIC parameterization in SOopen and SOcoastal, multiple linear regression analyses were performed using CTD-bottle data from 2000 onward in GLODAPv2.202347–51 (Fig. S2). Variables were considered significant when their F-value, a statistical index used to evaluate the significance of regression parameters, exceeded 2.453. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF < 10 indicating the absence of substantial multicollinearity7,37,38. For both SOopen and SOcoastal DIC, T, S, AOU, and Pr satisfied these criteria and were selected as explanatory variables (Table S1). T and S primarily represent physical water-mass properties, Pr is related to pressure-dependent thermodynamic effects including CaCO₃ dissolution, and AOU reflects biological processes such as productivity and remineralization54.

These variables were then used to develop NN-based DIC parameterizations for SOopen and SOcoastal using GLODAPv2.2023 CTD-bottle data collected from 2000 onward47–51 (Figs. S1 and S2). Model performance was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation55. In each fold, 80% of the observations were randomly used for training and the remaining 20% were used for testing (Fig. S2). The resulting hybrid MLR–NN DIC parameterizations had MAD values of approximately 4 µmol kg⁻¹ for both DICopen and DICcoastal (Eqs. 2 and 3), representing an improvement of nearly 40% relative to previous MLR-only parameterizations7. These results demonstrate that the hybrid MLR–NN framework substantially improves the predictive accuracy of DIC parameterization relative to the previous MLR-only approach, providing a more robust basis for estimating glacier-derived freshwater.

All observational data used in this study are publicly available. The DIC parameterizations were developed using GLODAPv2.2023, and the hydrographic observations used to estimate glacier-derived freshwater are archived in the Arctic Data archive System (ADS). Details and direct links to the datasets are provided in the Data Availability section.

Caveat for correcting Fg in the surface layer

In surface waters shallower than approximately 100 dbar, the freshwater budget is also influenced by sea-ice formation/melting (SI) and evaporation/precipitation (EP). In the initial Fg estimate, we assumed that the net effects of SI and EP are the same between SOopen and SOcoastal and therefore cancel in Eq. (4). This assumption allows glacier-derived freshwater fractions to be estimated using available hydrographic data. However, any spatial or temporal differences in SI or EP between the two regions would be implicitly included in the residual term. Therefore, Fg in the surface layer should be interpreted as a combined signal of glacier-derived freshwater and any unaccounted differences in SI and EP. Surface-layer Fg may therefore have larger uncertainty when SI or EP differs substantially between SOopen and SOcoastal7 (Fig. 1). To reduce this uncertainty, we introduced a sea ice-corrected freshwater fraction, Fg(c), for the surface layer (Figs. 2 and 3).
