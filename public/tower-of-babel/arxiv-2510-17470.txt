Precise large deviations in geometric last passage percolation
Sung-Soo Byun, Christophe Charlier, Philippe Moreillon, Nick Simm

Abstract
. We study the last passage time in geometric last passage percolation (LPP). As the system size increases, we derive precise large deviation probabilities—up to and including the constant terms—for both the lower and upper tails. A key step in proving these results is to establish a duality formula that reformulates the LPP problem in terms of the largest eigenvalue in the Jacobi unitary ensemble (JUE). In addition, we establish a second duality formula, which relates the LPP problem to the truncated unitary ensemble (TUE). Using this, we also derive asymptotics for the moments of the absolute value of characteristic polynomials of the TUE, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction and main results

Consider the positive quadrant of the integer lattice
\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{2}
and on each site put independent and identically distributed geometric random variables
\omega_{i,j}
with parameter
q^{2}\in(0,1)
, i.e. the probability mass function of each
\omega_{i,j}
is

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}(\omega_{i,j}=k)=(1-q^{2})q^{2k},\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots

Now consider the collection
\Pi
of all up-right paths
\pi
that connect the site
(1,1)
to
(n,m)
. The last passage time is the random variable

(1.2)

G_{n,m}=\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\sum_{(i,j)\in\pi}\omega_{i,j}.

The geometric last passage percolation (LPP) model is defined by the random path that attains the maximum total weight among all up-right paths, as illustrated in Figure
1
.

In this work, we study the asymptotic behaviour of the probability
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
in regimes where
n,m,\ell
are large. The analysis of such probabilities has played a central role in the development of the Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) universality class and the early advances in integrable probability theory.

A law of large numbers type result for (

[

by Johansson, where it is shown that for
\gamma\geq 1

(1.3)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[G_{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor,N}]=\omega(\gamma,q):=\frac{(1+q\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}}{1-q^{2}}-1.

Here,

It is also known from

that the fluctuations of
G_{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor,N}
around the typical value
\omega(\gamma,q)
are described by the Tracy–Widom distribution: more precisely,

(1.4)

\mathbb{P}(G_{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor,N}\leq x)=F_{\mathrm{TW}}\Big(\frac{x-N\omega(\gamma,q)}{\sigma(\gamma,q)N^{1/3}}\Big)(1+o(1)),

as
N\to\infty,
where

\displaystyle\sigma(\gamma,q)=\frac{q^{1/3}\gamma^{-1/6}}{1-q^{2}}(\sqrt{\gamma}+q)^{2/3}(1+q\sqrt{\gamma})^{2/3},

and
F_{\rm TW}(s)
is the Tracy-Widom distribution, which we recall is given by

F_{\rm TW}(s)=\exp\bigg(-\int_{s}^{+\infty}(x-s)u(x)^{2}dx\bigg),

with
u
the Hastings-McLeod solution of the Painlevé II equation, see also Figure

. We note that the left-hand side of (
1.4

[
22
]
, see also
[

where this formula is used to derive deviation probabilities, and
[

19
]
for Fredholm determinant representations associated with variants of the LPP models.

Beyond fluctuations around the typical values, a natural next question concerns the probabilities of rare events. These can be divided into two cases:

•

the
lower tail probability
, where
G_{m,n}
is significantly smaller than its typical value, and

•

the
upper tail probability
, where
G_{m,n}
is significantly larger,

see again Figure

. Due to the typical fluctuation described in (
1.4
) and the well-known tail behaviour of the Tracy–Widom distribution, one can expect that the lower tail probability is significantly smaller—and much harder to analyze—than the upper tail probability.
This expectation is confirmed by
[
77
, Theorem 1.1]
, which shows that there are functions
\ell,i:(0,+\infty)\to(0,+\infty)
such that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[G_{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor,N}\leq N(\omega(\gamma,q)-\epsilon)\Big]=-\ell(\epsilon),

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[G_{\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor,N}\geq N(\omega(\gamma,q)+\epsilon)\Big]=-i(\epsilon).

An explicit formula for the upper tail rate function
i(x)
is known and given in
[
77
, Theorem 2.2 and (2.21)]
and
[
104
, Theorem 4.4]
, see also
[
12
]

[
77
, p.451]
that

“We will not discuss the explicit form of the lower tail rate function.”

In fact, even though Johansson’s work
[
77
]
is 25 years old, an explicit formula for

still appears to be lacking in the literature. In the recent paper
[
55
, p.29]
, the authors write

“The only impediment is that the Johansson result appears in a variational form.”
(In addition, we note that the variational characterisation of

in

Theorem 1.1

(
Lower tail probability in an almost-square lattice
)
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
,
0<\delta<\omega(1,q)
and
\mathsf{n}\in\mathbb{N}:=\{0,1,\ldots\}
be fixed. Then, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.7)

\log\mathbb{P}\Big[G_{N+\mathsf{n},N}\leq\delta N\Big]=\mathsf{L}_{1}N^{2}+\mathsf{L}_{2}N+\mathsf{L}_{3}\log N+\mathsf{L}_{4}+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big),

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{L}_{1}&=\log\big(\tfrac{1-q}{2}\big)+(1+\delta)^{2}\log\big(\tfrac{1+q}{2}\big)-\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\log q-(1+\delta)^{2}\log(1+\delta)+\frac{\delta^{2}}{2}\log\delta+\frac{(2+\delta)^{2}}{2}\log(2+\delta),\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{L}_{2}&=\mathsf{n}\bigg[\log\big(\tfrac{1-q^{2}}{4}\big)-\delta\log\big(\tfrac{2}{1+q}\big)-(1+\delta)\log(1+\delta)+(2+\delta)\log(2+\delta)\bigg],\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{L}_{3}&=-\frac{1}{12},\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathsf{L}_{4}&=\zeta^{\prime}(-1)+\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\tfrac{2(1+\delta)^{2}}{\delta(2+\delta)}\Big)-\frac{1}{8}\log\Big(\tfrac{(2q+\delta q-\delta)(2+\delta-\delta q)}{4q}\Big)+\frac{\mathsf{n}^{2}}{2}\log\Big(\tfrac{(2+\delta-\delta q)(2+\delta)}{4(1+\delta)}\Big).\end{split}

Here,
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function.

To state our results in a general rectangular lattice, it is convenient to introduce the parameters

(1.8)

a=\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma(\gamma+\delta)}-\sqrt{\delta+1}}{\gamma+\delta+1}\bigg)^{2},\qquad b=\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{\gamma(\gamma+\delta)}+\sqrt{\delta+1}}{\gamma+\delta+1}\bigg)^{2}

for
\gamma\geq 1
and
0<\delta<\omega(\gamma,q)
.
We note that these values correspond to the edges of the support of the Wachter distribution (see (
2.25
) below), once the parameters are properly chosen.
Furthermore, let
\mathfrak{m}\in(0,a)
be the unique solution to the equation

(1.9)

\displaystyle 2=(\gamma-1)\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}}-1\bigg)+\delta\bigg(\frac{q}{\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{m}}}-1\bigg).

Below, the parameter
\mathfrak{m}
serves as the left edge of the
constrained
Wachter distribution in Proposition
4.1
. See also Remark
2
for another expression of
\mathfrak{m}
.

Theorem 1.2

(
Lower tail probability in a rectangular lattice
)
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
,
\gamma>1
and
0<\delta<\omega(\gamma,q)
be fixed. Let
\mathsf{n}=\mathsf{n}_{N}
be in a fixed compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, chosen so that
\gamma N+\mathsf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
Then, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.10)

\log\mathbb{P}\Big[G_{\gamma N+\mathsf{n},N}\leq\delta N\Big]=L_{1}N^{2}+L_{2}N+L_{3}\log N+L_{4}+O\Big(\frac{\log N}{N}\Big),

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{1}&=\log\big(\tfrac{1-q^{2}-\mathfrak{m}}{4}\big)+(1+\gamma+\delta)\bigg[(\gamma-1)\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}+\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{2}\Big)+\delta\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{m}}+q}{2}\Big)\bigg]\\
&\quad-(\gamma-1)\delta\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}q^{2}}+\sqrt{(1-q^{2})(1-\mathfrak{m})}}{2}\Big)-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\log\gamma-\frac{(1+\delta)^{2}}{2}\log(1+\delta)-\frac{(\gamma+\delta)^{2}}{2}\log(\gamma+\delta)\\
&\quad+\frac{(\gamma-1)^{2}}{4}\log\Big(\tfrac{(\gamma-1)^{2}}{\mathfrak{m}(1-q^{2})}\Big)+\frac{\delta^{2}}{4}\log\Big(\tfrac{\delta^{2}}{(1-\mathfrak{m})q^{2}}\Big)+\frac{(1+\gamma+\delta)^{2}}{2}\log(1+\gamma+\delta),\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{2}&=\mathsf{n}\bigg[(1+\gamma+\delta)\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}+\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{2}\Big)+(\gamma-1)\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}+\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{2\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}(1-q^{2})}}\Big)-\delta\log\Big(\tfrac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}q^{2}}+\sqrt{(1-q^{2})(1-\mathfrak{m})}}{\sqrt{1-\mathfrak{m}}+q}\Big)\\
&\qquad-\gamma\log\gamma-(\gamma+\delta)\log(\gamma+\delta)+(2+\delta)\log(1+\gamma+\delta)+(\gamma-1)\log\big((\gamma-1)(1+\gamma+\delta)\big)\bigg],\end{split}

\displaystyle L_{3}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{12},

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{4}&=\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\frac{1}{6}\log\big(\tfrac{1-q^{2}-\mathfrak{m}}{4}\big)-\frac{1}{8}\log\Big(\tfrac{\gamma-1}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}(1-q^{2})}}-\tfrac{\delta}{\sqrt{(1-\mathfrak{m})q^{2}}}\Big)-\frac{1}{24}\log\Big((\gamma-1)\tfrac{\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}{\mathfrak{m}^{3/2}}-\delta\tfrac{q}{(1-\mathfrak{m})^{3/2}}\Big)\\
&\quad-\frac{1}{12}\log\Big(\tfrac{(\gamma-1)\delta(1+\gamma+\delta)}{\gamma(1+\delta)(\gamma+\delta)}\Big)+\frac{\mathsf{n}^{2}}{2}\log\Big(\tfrac{(\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}+\sqrt{1-q^{2}})^{2}}{4\sqrt{1-q^{2}}}\tfrac{\gamma-1}{\sqrt{\mathfrak{m}}}\tfrac{1+\gamma+\delta}{\gamma(\gamma+\delta)}\Big).\end{split}

Here,
\zeta
is the Riemann zeta function.

Next, we discuss the asymptotic expansion for the upper tail large deviation probability. In order to describe it, for
q\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\geq 1

(1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}(x)&:=-\log\bigg(\frac{2x-a-b+2\sqrt{(x-b)(x-a)}}{b-a}\bigg)+(\gamma-1)\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{ab}+x-\sqrt{(x-b)(x-a)}}{(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})\sqrt{x}}\bigg)\\
&\quad+\delta\log\bigg(\frac{\sqrt{(1-a)(1-b)}-x+1+\sqrt{(x-b)(x-a)}}{(\sqrt{1-a}+\sqrt{1-b})\sqrt{1-x}}\bigg),\end{split}

where
a
and
b
are given by (
1.8
).

Theorem 1.3

(
Upper tail probability
)
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
,
\gamma\geq 1
and
\delta>\omega(\gamma,q)
be fixed. Let
\mathsf{n}=\mathsf{n}_{N}
be in a fixed compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
, chosen so that
\gamma N+\mathsf{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
.
Then, as
N\to\infty
,

(1.14)

\log\mathbb{P}\Big[G_{\gamma N+\mathsf{n},N}\geq\delta N\Big]=U_{1}N+U_{2}\log N+U_{3}+o(1),

where

(1.15)

U_{1}=-2\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}(1-q^{2}),\qquad U_{2}=-1

and

(1.16)

U_{3}=\log\bigg(\frac{b-a}{8\pi}\,\frac{1}{(1-q^{2}-a)(1-q^{2}-b)}\frac{1}{2\Phi^{\prime}_{\gamma,\delta}(1-q^{2})}\bigg)-2\mathsf{n}\frac{d}{d\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}(1-q^{2}).

Here,
\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}
is given by (
1.13
) and
\frac{d}{d\gamma}\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}
denotes the total derivative of
\Phi_{\gamma,\delta}
with respect to
\gamma
(taking into account that
a
and
b
depend on
\gamma
).

Remark 3

(Comparison with Johansson’s formula)
.

In
[
77
, Eq. (2.21)]
, it was shown that the upper tail rate function in (
1.6
) can be written as
i(\epsilon)=2J(\mathsf{b}+\epsilon)
where

(1.17)

J(t)=\frac{\mathsf{b}-\mathsf{a}}{8q\sqrt{\gamma}}\int_{1}^{x}(x-y)\Big(\frac{\gamma-q^{2}}{y+B}+\frac{1-q^{2}\gamma}{y+D}\Big)\frac{dy}{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}}.

Here,

\mathsf{a}=\frac{(1-q\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}}{1-q^{2}}
,
\mathsf{b}=\frac{(1+q\sqrt{\gamma})^{2}}{1-q^{2}}=\omega+1
,
x=\frac{2(t-\mathsf{a})}{\mathsf{b}-\mathsf{a}}-1
,
B=\frac{\gamma+q^{2}}{2q\sqrt{\gamma}}
and
D=\frac{1+q^{2}\gamma}{2q\sqrt{\gamma}}
, where
\omega
is given in (
1.3
). It turns out that the integral above can be computed explicitly: for
x\geq 1
and
|B|\geq 1
, we have

(1.18)

\int_{1}^{x}\frac{x-y}{\sqrt{y^{2}-1}(y+B)}\,dy=\frac{B+x}{\sqrt{B^{2}-1}}\log\bigg(\frac{1+Bx+\sqrt{(B^{2}-1)(x^{2}-1)}}{B+x}\bigg)+\frac{1}{2}\log\bigg(\frac{x-\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}{x+\sqrt{x^{2}-1}}\bigg).

With
I(x,B)
denoting the above integral, we have

J(t)=\frac{\mathsf{b}-\mathsf{a}}{8q\sqrt{\gamma}}\Big((\gamma-q^{2})I(x,B)+(1-q^{2}\gamma)I(x,D)\Big).

It is now direct to check that the rate function (
1.13
) is in agreement with
J(t)
above for the appropriate choice of parameters, namely

(1.19)

J(t)=\Phi_{\gamma,t-1}(1-q^{2}).

This formula confirms the coefficient
U_{1}
in Theorem
1.3
.

The last passage percolation model is closely connected to numerous other integrable probability models. Here, we provide a brief and non-exhaustive overview of the literature on large deviation probabilities—both lower and upper tails—for such models, with an emphasis on recent developments.

One of the foundational contributions is due to Logan and Shepp
[
88
]
, who computed the equilibrium measure and rate function for Young tableaux under constraints. This corresponds to lower tail large deviations for the length of the longest increasing subsequence in a uniform random permutation. Subsequent works
[
62
,
105
]
established large deviation principles for the longest increasing subsequence and for the height function in the polynuclear growth model. Johansson
[
77
]
then used the Meixner ensemble to establish a large deviation principle for the integrated current in the Totally Asymmetric Simple Exclusion Process (TASEP).

More recently, Corwin and Ghosal
[
47
]
derived upper and lower bounds on the lower tail of the KPZ equation, capturing the qualitative behaviour of the rate function, though without sharp estimates. The exact lower tail large deviation was subsequently derived by Tsai
[
109
]
. Further refinements appeared in
[
37
,
43
]
, where the Riemann–Hilbert method was employed to obtain asymptotic expansions of the lower tail. A variational approach was developed in
[
52
,
53
]
to establish lower tail large deviations for discrete growth models, including the
q
-polynuclear growth model and the stochastic six-vertex model. In
[
16
]
, a speed-
N^{2}
upper tail large deviation principle was proved for first passage percolation—corresponding to the lower tail regime in LPP—where the model is not exactly solvable and the rate function is characterised via a variational formula.

At the process level, the scaling limit of passage times was constructed in
[
56
]
. In addition, the large deviation behaviour of midpoint transversal fluctuations in general LPP models was analysed in
[
1
,
2
]
.

Turning to the upper tail, exact large deviation principles for the one-point distribution of the KPZ equation and the ASEP with narrow wedge initial condition were established in
[
54
,
55
]
. A process-level upper tail large deviation principle for TASEP with general initial data was derived in
[
99
]
, while the directed landscape—viewed as the full scaling limit of LPP—was analysed at the metric level in
[
50
]
, yielding upper tail large deviations. In the setting of exponential LPP,
[
15
]
obtained optimal bounds for the one-point distribution in both upper and lower tail regimes, capturing the correct leading-order behaviour within the typical fluctuation scale. We also refer the reader to
[
85
,
38
,
75
]
and references therein for further literature.

2.
Dualities and further results

The underlying algebraic structures in integrable probability models sometimes lead to surprising connections between seemingly different models. These connections are exact identities which we will refer to as
duality formulas
. In this work, we will find two new duality formulas relating the probability
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
with two random matrix ensembles (see (
2.6
) and (
2.24
)).

The approach in
[
77
]
to deriving (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) is based on a duality relation between
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
and the distribution of the largest particle in the
Meixner ensemble
.
This ensemble is defined by a discrete joint probability mass function on tuples
(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
proportional to

(2.1)

\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(h_{i}-h_{j})^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{m}\binom{h_{i}+m-n}{h_{i}}q^{2h_{i}}.

Let
h^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(n,q):=\max\{h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}\}
, where
h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}
are drawn with respect to (
2.1
). It then follows from
[
77
, Proposition 1.3]
that for any
\ell\geq 0
,

(2.2)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=\mathbb{P}(h^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(n,q)\leq\ell+m-1).

In this paper we establish two additional duality formulas. We begin with the one relating
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
with the TUE.

2.1.
Truncated unitary ensemble: moments of the characteristic polynomial

Let
U
be drawn from the
Circular Unitary Ensemble
(CUE)
[
65
, Chapter 2]
, i.e.
U
is an
M\times M
unitary matrix sampled with respect to the Haar measure on the unitary group. Let
\mathsf{T}_{N,M}
be the top-left
N\times N
submatrix of
U
. We then say that
\mathsf{T}_{N,M}
is drawn from the
Truncated Unitary Ensemble
(TUE) (see
[
115
]
or
[
27
, Section 2.6]
for more background).
The joint probability density of the eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of
\mathsf{T}_{N,M}
is given by

(2.3)

\frac{1}{Z_{N,M}^{\rm trunc}}\prod_{1\leq j<k\leq N}|\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{k}|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{N}(1-|\lambda_{j}|^{2})^{M-N-1}\,dA(\lambda_{j}),\qquad|\lambda_{j}|\leq 1.

Here,
dA(z)=d^{2}z/\pi
is the area measure, and the normalizing constant is given explicitly as

(2.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{N,M}^{\rm trunc}=N!\prod_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{k!\,\Gamma(M-N)}{\Gamma(M-N+k+1)}=N!\,\Gamma(M-N)^{N}\,\frac{G(N+1)G(M-N+1)}{G(M+1)},\end{split}

where
G
is the Barnes
G
-function defined by

(2.5)

G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)G(z),\qquad G(1)=1,

see e.g.
[
97
, Section 5.17]
.
Then we have the following connection.

Theorem 2.1

(
Duality between LPP and TUE
)
.

Consider the last passage time
G_{n,m}
in a geometric environment with parameter
q^{2}
and assume without loss of generality that
n\geq m
. Then, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=c_{\ell,n,m}(1-q^{2})^{nm}\mathbb{E}(|\det(\mathsf{T}_{\ell,\ell+n-m}-q)|^{2m}),

where

(2.7)

c_{\ell,n,m}:=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(\ell+n-m+j-1)!(j-1)!}{(n-m+j-1)!(\ell+j-1)!}=\frac{G(\ell+1)G(m+1)G(\ell+n+1)G(n-m+1)}{G(\ell+m+1)G(\ell+n-m+1)G(n+1)}.

As a consequence of Theorem
2.1
, we can reformulate the result in the context of the asymptotic behaviour of the TUE. In studying TUE, one needs to distinguish between the following two regimes.

•

Weak non-unitarity:
This regime corresponds to
N\to+\infty
while
M-N
remains bounded. We will use the notation

(2.8)

M-N=\mathsf{s},\qquad\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}.

In this regime, most of the eigenvalues are in a
O(1/N)
neighbourhood of the unit circle with high probability, see Figure
3
(A).

•

Strong non-unitarity:
This regime corresponds to
N\to+\infty
with
M-N
being proportional to
N
, and we therefore use the notation

(2.9)

M=(\rho+1)N+\mathsf{t},\qquad\rho>0,\quad\mathsf{t}\in\mathbb{R},\quad M\in\mathbb{N}.

In this regime, the eigenvalues accumulate on the disc centred at
0
and of radius
1/\sqrt{1+\rho}
, see Figure
3
(B). It is therefore convenient to rescale the eigenvalues as

(2.10)

z_{j}=\sqrt{1+\rho}\,\lambda_{j}.

The droplet in the
z_{j}
-plane is the unit disc, and the associated equilibrium measure is given by

(2.11)

\frac{\rho(1+\rho)}{(1+\rho-|z|^{2})^{2}}\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|<1\}}\,dA.

Note that as
\rho\to\infty
, the measure (
2.11
) tends to
\mathbbm{1}_{\{|z|<1\}}\,dA
, which is the circular law.

Theorem 2.2

(
Moments of the characteristic polynomial of TUE matrices at weak non-unitarity
)
.

Suppose
M-N=:\mathsf{s}\in\mathbb{N}
is fixed.

(a)

(Post-critical regime)
Suppose

0<|z|<\frac{1}{2c+1}.
Then as
N\to+\infty
, we have

(2.12)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|\det(\mathsf{T}_{N,M}-z)|^{2cN}=N^{\frac{\mathsf{s}^{2}}{2}}\mathsf{G}^{\rm post}\exp\Big(\mathsf{H}_{0}^{\rm post}N^{2}+\mathsf{H}_{1}^{\rm post}N\Big)\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}}{N^{k}}+O(e^{-\epsilon N})\Big),\end{split}

for some
\epsilon>0
, where

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}^{\rm post}=\Big(\frac{c}{c+1}\Big)^{\frac{\mathsf{s}^{2}}{2}}(2\pi)^{\frac{\mathsf{s}}{2}}\frac{1}{G(\mathsf{s}+1)},

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{0}^{\rm post}=-c^{2}\log(1-|z|^{2}),\qquad\mathsf{H}_{1}^{\rm post}=\mathsf{s}\log\Big(\frac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{c}}\frac{c^{c}}{(c+1)^{c+1}}\Big).

Here,
G
is Barnes’
G
-function and the
d_{k}
’s are
N
-independent constants.

(b)

(Pre-critical regime)
Suppose

\frac{1}{2c+1}<|z|<1.
Then as
N\to+\infty
, we have

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}|\det(\mathsf{T}_{N,M}-z)|^{2cN}=N^{-\frac{1}{12}+\frac{\mathsf{s}^{2}}{2}}e^{\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}\mathsf{G}^{\rm pre}\exp\Big(\mathsf{H}_{0}^{\rm pre}N^{2}+\mathsf{H}_{1}^{\rm pre}N\Big)\Big(1+O(\frac{1}{N})\Big),\end{split}

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{G}^{\rm pre}=\mathsf{G}^{\rm post}\Big(\frac{(1+2c-1/|z|)(1+2c-|z|)}{4c^{2}}\Big)^{-\frac{1}{8}}\Big(\frac{(1+2c-|z|)(1+2c)}{4c(1+c)}\Big)^{\frac{\mathsf{s}^{2}}{2}}\Big(\frac{2(1+c)^{2}}{c(1+2c)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{12}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{H}_{0}^{\rm pre}=-c^{2}\log(1-|z|^{2})+c^{2}\,\mathsf{L}_{1},\qquad\mathsf{H}_{1}^{\rm pre}=\mathsf{s}\log\Big(\frac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{c}}\frac{c^{c}}{(c+1)^{c+1}}\Big)+c\,\mathsf{L}_{2}.

Here,
\mathsf{L}_{1}
and
\mathsf{L}_{2}
are as in Theorem
1.1
with
\mathsf{n}=\mathsf{s}
,
\delta=1/c
and
q=|z|
.

Remark 5
.

(Consistency check between Corollary

[

In

Theorem 2.4

(
Moments of the characteristic polynomial of TUE matrices at strong non-unitarity
)
.

Let
\rho>0
be fixed, and let
\mathsf{t}=\mathsf{t}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
be bounded and such that

.

(a)

(Post-critical regime)
Suppose

\displaystyle 0<\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{1+\rho}}<\dfrac{\sqrt{(\rho+c+1)(c+1)}-\sqrt{(\rho+c)c}}{\rho+2c+1}.

(2.18)

\mathbb{E}|\det(\sqrt{1+\rho}\,\mathsf{T}_{N,M}-z)|^{2cN}=N^{\frac{1}{12}}e^{-\zeta^{\prime}(-1)}\mathcal{G}^{\rm post}\exp\Big(\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rm post}N^{2}+\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\rm post}N\Big)\Big(1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{b_{k}}{N^{k}}+O(e^{-\epsilon N})\Big),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=-c(\rho+c)\log\Big(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{1+\rho}\Big)+c\log(1+\rho)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{1}^{\rm post}

\displaystyle=-\mathsf{t}\bigg(c\log\Big(1-\frac{|z|^{2}}{1+\rho}\Big)+\log\Big(\frac{\rho^{\rho}}{(\rho+1)^{\rho+1}}\Big)-\log\Big(\frac{(\rho+c)^{\rho+c}}{(\rho+c+1)^{\rho+c+1}}\Big)\bigg).

Here, the

(b)

(Pre-critical regime)
Suppose

\displaystyle\dfrac{\sqrt{(\rho+c+1)(c+1)}-\sqrt{(\rho+c)c}}{\rho+2c+1}<\frac{|z|}{\sqrt{1+\rho}}<1.

Then as

(2.19)

where

2.2.
Jacobi unitary ensemble: large deviation probabilities of the largest eigenvalues

We now present our second duality formula, which relates
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
with the JUE.
To formulate this, let
p
and
n
be two integers such that
p\geq n
and let
X
be an
n\times p
matrix whose entries are independent complex normal random variables
X_{ij}\sim N_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
. The matrix
W=XX^{*}
is called a complex Wishart matrix with
p
degrees of freedom. (In fact, it is well known that the largest eigenvalue of
W
is closely related to the LPP model, but in the case where the weights in (
1.1
) are exponential rather than geometric
[
77
, Proposition 1.4]
.) For the model with geometric weights studied here, we consider the random matrix

(2.21)

J:=W_{1}(W_{1}+W_{2})^{-1},

where
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are independent Wishart matrices with
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
degrees of freedom, respectively. The
n
eigenvalues
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
of
J
all lie in the interval
[0,1]
and have the joint probability density function

(2.22)

P(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\frac{1}{Z_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})}\,\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(x_{j}-x_{i})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\lambda_{1}}(1-x_{j})^{\lambda_{2}},

where
\lambda_{1}=p_{1}-n
and
\lambda_{2}=p_{2}-n
(see e.g.
[
65
, Chapter 3]
), and
Z_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is a Selberg integral (see e.g.
[
69
, Eq. (1.1)]
) that can be written as

(2.23)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})&=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{(j+1)!\,\Gamma(\lambda_{1}+1+j)\Gamma(\lambda_{2}+1+j)}{\Gamma(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+1+n+j)}\\
&=G(n+2)\frac{G(\lambda_{1}+n+1)}{G(\lambda_{1}+1)}\frac{G(\lambda_{2}+n+1)}{G(\lambda_{2}+1)}\frac{G(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+n+1)}{G(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+2n+1)},\end{split}

where
G
is the Barnes
G
-function (
2.5
).
The probability density (
2.22
) is known as the
Jacobi Unitary Ensemble
(JUE) with
n
particles and parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
.
Let

x^{(n)}_{\mathrm{max}}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=\mathrm{max}\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are drawn with respect to (
2.22
). The following is our second duality formula.

Theorem 2.5

(
Duality between the LPP and JUE
)
.

Consider the last passage time
G_{n,m}
in a geometric environment with parameter
q^{2}
and assume without loss of generality that
n\geq m
. Then, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
,

(2.24)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=\mathbb{P}(x^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(n-m,\ell)\leq 1-q^{2}).

The advantage of formula (
2.24
) is that, as previously mentioned, the right-hand side can be evaluated asymptotically using results from
[
45
,
66
]
.

We mention that large deviation probabilities in Hermitian random matrix ensembles (such as the JUE) have been extensively studied using several approaches, including the Coulomb gas method
[
58
]
, loop equations and
\frac{1}{N}
expansions
[
23
,

]
and orthogonal polynomials
[

. Other such results, for one-dimensional log-gases, can be found in e.g.

For the JUE, the leading order term of the lower tail probability (of order

[

.

For the upper tail probability, the leading order term (of order

]
. The results in

]

2.3.3.
Moments of the characteristic polynomial of induced spherical ensembles

In addition to the TUE model, there exists an equivalent formulation within non-Hermitian random matrix theory known as the
induced spherical ensemble

[

. From a plasma perspective, the TUE can be viewed as an extension of the Ginibre ensemble to a space of constant negative curvature called the pseudosphere, see

In

The associated equilibrium measure and leading-order energy—equivalent to the dominant term in Theorem

[

3.
Duality relations

In this section we prove Theorems
2.1
and
2.5
. We start from the expression (
3.3
) below that arises directly after applying the Robinson–Schensted–Knuth (RSK) correspondence to the LPP problem. This is the expression Johansson used in
[
77
]
to write the distribution of the last passage time in terms of the Meixner ensemble, as we shall discuss below. Here we show how manipulating this expression in an alternative way leads to the JUE and to the TUE.

3.1.
Duality formulas relating LPP, TUE and JUE

We begin with an introduction to partitions and Schur functions, see
[
89
]
for a standard reference. A partition
\lambda
is a weakly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{m})
. The number of non-zero terms in such a sequence is called the length of the partition and is denoted as
l(\lambda)
. The weight of the partition is
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}
. The Schur functions
s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})
in
m
variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}
are symmetric polynomials defined as

(3.1)

s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{\Delta(\bm{x})}\det\Big\{x_{i}^{\lambda_{j}+m-j}\Big\}_{i,j=1}^{m},\qquad\Delta(\bm{x})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(x_{i}-x_{j}).

Note that
\Delta(\bm{x})
differs from the classical definition of a Vandermonde determinant by a factor
(-1)^{\frac{m(m-1)}{2}}
.
If a partition
\lambda
has length
l(\lambda)>m
then
s_{\lambda}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})=0
. If
l(\lambda)<m
then (
3.1
) continues to be a valid definition by appending an appropriate number of zeros to the end of the partition. The Schur functions are homogeneous in the sense that if
q
is a scalar, then
s_{\lambda}(q\bm{x})=q^{|\lambda|}s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})
. Partitions are represented by their Young diagram consisting of
m
rows of boxes, with each row starting on the left with
\lambda_{j}
boxes in row
j
, for
j=1,\ldots,m
. The total number of boxes in the diagram is the weight
|\lambda|
. The conjugate of
\lambda
, denoted
\lambda^{\prime}
, is the partition obtained by transposing the Young diagram, that is row
j
of
\lambda^{\prime}
is built from column
j
of
\lambda
and vice versa. For vectors
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{\ell}
, the dual Cauchy identity states that (see e.g.
[
89
, Chapter 1, Eq. (4.3
′
)]
or
[
65
, Exercise 10.1.4]
)

(3.2)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{m}\prod_{i=1}^{\ell}(1+x_{j}y_{i})=\sum_{\lambda,l(\lambda)\leq m,\lambda_{1}\leq\ell}s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\bm{y}).

Note the appearance of the conjugate partition
\lambda^{\prime}
on the right-hand side which has length at most
\ell
and first coordinate at most
m
. We use the notation
s_{\lambda}(1_{m})=s_{\lambda}(1,\ldots,1)
, i.e.
1_{m}
has all
m
coordinates equal to
1
. The following theorem presents the known expressions for the probability distribution of
G_{n,m}
due to
[
77
,
11
,
72
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
n\geq m
and that
n,m,\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
q\in(0,1)
, and let
G_{n,m}
be the last passage time in geometric environment with parameter
q^{2}
. The following formulas express
\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)
in different ways:

•

A Schur function expansion
[
77
, Eqs. (2.2)–(2.5)]

(3.3)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=(1-q^{2})^{nm}\sum_{\lambda,l(\lambda)\leq m,\lambda_{1}\leq\ell}s_{\lambda}(1_{n})s_{\lambda}(1_{m})q^{2|\lambda|}.

•

The largest particle distribution in the Meixner ensemble
[
77
, Proof of Proposition 1.3]

(3.4)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=\mathbb{P}(h^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(n,q)\leq\ell+m-1).

•

An average over Haar distributed unitary matrices
U
of size
\ell\times\ell

[
72
, Eq.(5.53)]
(see also
[
11
]
)

(3.5)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=(1-q^{2})^{nm}\mathbb{E}(\det(1+U)^{n}\det(1+q^{2}U^{\dagger})^{m}).

We do not give the full proof of the above theorem here but we give some remarks. The Schur function expansion in (
3.3
) is a consequence of applying the RSK correspondence to geometric LPP
[
77
, Eq. (2.4)]
. To derive (
3.4
), Johansson rewrites (
3.3
) using the identity

(3.6)

s_{\lambda}(1_{n})=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i}{j-i}

and introduces new variables
h_{j}=\lambda_{j}+m-j
for
j=1,\ldots,m
. Assume that
n\geq m
(the other case is analogous by symmetry).
This leads to

(3.7)

\begin{split}s_{\lambda}(1_{m})s_{\lambda}(1_{n})&=s_{\lambda}(1_{m})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(\lambda_{j}+n-j)!}{(\lambda_{j}+m-j)!}\,\frac{(j-1)!}{(n-m+j-1)!},\qquad\mbox{if }l(\lambda)\leq m,\\
&=\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(h_{j}-h_{i})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{(h_{j}+n-m)!}{h_{j}!}\,\frac{1}{(j-1)!(n-m+j-1)!}.\end{split}

The sum over partitions becomes a sum over the set of
h_{j}
’s and this establishes (
3.4
).

Formula (
3.5
) can be proven quickly in the following way. Consider a general correlator of characteristic polynomials

(3.8)

\mathbb{E}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\det(1+Uz_{j})\det(1+U^{\dagger}w_{j})\bigg),

where
U
is an
\ell\times\ell
Haar distributed unitary matrix. By the dual Cauchy identity (
3.2
), we can expand (
3.8
) as

(3.9)

\sum_{\lambda,l(\lambda)\leq n,\lambda_{1}\leq\ell}\sum_{\mu,l(\mu)\leq n,\mu_{1}\leq\ell}s_{\lambda}(\bm{z})s_{\mu}(\bm{w})\mathbb{E}(s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(U)s_{\mu^{\prime}}(U^{\dagger}))=\sum_{\lambda,l(\lambda)\leq n,\lambda_{1}\leq\ell}s_{\lambda}(\bm{z})s_{\lambda}(\bm{w})

where we used orthogonality of Schur functions
\mathbb{E}(s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(U)s_{\mu^{\prime}}(U^{\dagger}))=\delta_{\lambda\mu}
(see e.g.
[
65
, Eq. (10.21)]
). Now choose

for

. This restricts the length of the partitions in the sum to
m
. We also choose
w_{j}=q^{2}
for

and
z_{j}=1
for

. Then

(3.10)

\mathbb{E}(\det(1+U)^{n}\det(1+q^{2}U^{\dagger})^{m})=\sum_{\lambda,l(\lambda)\leq m,\lambda_{1}\leq\ell}s_{\lambda}(1_{n})s_{\lambda}(1_{m})q^{2|\lambda|}

and we arrived again at the Schur function expansion in (

). This shows (
3.5
). The relation between averages of unitary matrices and last passage percolation was extensively discussed in
[

, Eq.(5.53)]
.

While Theorem
3.1
contained the known exact relations between the last passage time and random matrix theory, below we present two further relations we believe to be new. To present them, recall that the Jacobi ensemble
\mathrm{JUE}_{m}(\alpha,\beta)
is the joint probability density function of

on

(3.11)

\frac{1}{Z_{m}(\alpha,\beta)}\prod_{j=1}^{m}s_{j}^{\alpha}(1-s_{j})^{\beta}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2},

where

and the normalization constant is

(3.12)

Z_{m}(\alpha,\beta)=\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\alpha}(1-s_{j})^{\beta}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}.

We denote the largest point
x^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(\alpha,\beta)=\mathrm{max}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{m})
. By definition we have

(3.13)

\mathbb{P}(x^{(m)}_{\mathrm{max}}(\alpha,\beta)\leq x)=\frac{1}{Z_{m}(\alpha,\beta)}\int_{[0,x]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\alpha}(1-s_{j})^{\beta}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}.

We now prove Theorems
2.1
and
2.5
.

Proof of Theorems
2.1
and
2.5
.

We first show (

).
We will use (
3.3
) and an identity obtained in
[

, Eq. (28)]

(3.14)

s_{\lambda}(1_{m})s_{\lambda}(1_{n})=\frac{s_{\lambda^{\prime}}(1_{\ell})}{k_{n,m}^{\ell}}\int_{[0,\infty)^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}dx_{j}\,\frac{x_{j}^{n-m}}{(1+x_{j})^{\ell+n+m}}s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})\Delta(\bm{x})^{2},

where
\lambda^{\prime}
is the conjugate partition, and where (see
[

, Eqs. (87)–(88)]
)

\displaystyle k_{n,m}^{\ell}=\int_{[0,\infty)^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}dx_{j}\,\frac{x_{j}^{n-m}}{(1+x_{j})^{\ell+n+m}}\Delta(\bm{x})^{2}=Z^{(m)}(n-m,\ell).

Substituting (

) in (
3.3
), and then simplifying using the dual Cauchy identity (

(3.15)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=\frac{(1-q^{2})^{nm}}{k_{n,m}^{\ell}}\int_{[0,\infty)^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}dx_{j}\,(1+q^{2}x_{j})^{\ell}\frac{x_{j}^{n-m}}{(1+x_{j})^{\ell+n+m}}\Delta(\bm{x})^{2}.

Now for each

so that

and

(3.16)

\Delta(\bm{x})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}dx_{j}=\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{ds_{j}}{(1-s_{j})^{2m}}.

Therefore

(3.17)

\mathbb{P}(G_{n,m}\leq\ell)=\frac{(1-q^{2})^{nm}}{k_{n,m}^{\ell}}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{n-m}(1-s_{j}(1-q^{2}))^{\ell}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}.

3.2.
Dualities of JUE and CUE averages

There are several duality relations between JUE and CUE averages that are extensively discussed and used in the series of works
[
70
,
71
,
72
]
. We provide some of them for consistency checking with Theorem

.
For a partition
\lambda
we introduce the hypergeometric coefficient

(3.20)

[u]_{\lambda}=\prod_{j=1}^{l(\lambda)}\frac{\Gamma(\lambda_{j}+u-j+1)}{\Gamma(u-j+1)}.

These coefficients obey a transposition property (see e.g.
[
65
, Exercise 12.4.2 with
\alpha=1
]
)

(3.21)

[-u]_{\lambda}=(-1)^{|\lambda|}[u]_{\lambda^{\prime}}.

It will be useful to recall a particular case of Selberg integral (see e.g.
[
65
, Chapter 4]
and
[
69
]
)

(3.22)

\begin{split}S_{N}(\alpha,\beta)&:=\int_{[0,1]^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\alpha}(1-s_{j})^{\beta}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}=\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+j+1)\Gamma(\beta+j+1)\Gamma(j+2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+N+j+1)},\end{split}

valid for
\operatorname{Re}\alpha>-1,\operatorname{Re}\beta>-1
. We also recall a trigonometric version of this integral which is often referred to as the Morris integral (see e.g.
[
69
, Eqs. (1.17)–(1.18) and page 496]
):

(3.23)

\begin{split}M_{N}(\alpha,\beta)&:=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dz_{j}}{iz_{j}}\,z_{j}^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}}|1+z_{j}|^{\alpha+\beta}|\Delta(\bm{z})|^{2}=(2\pi)^{N}\prod_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+j+1)\Gamma(j+2)}{\Gamma(\alpha+j+1)\Gamma(\beta+j+1)},\end{split}

valid for
\mathrm{Re}(\alpha+\beta)>-1
, with
\mathcal{C}
denoting the unit circle oriented positively. The works
[
78
,
79
]
independently obtained a generalization of (
3.22
) where the integrand is weighted by a Schur function
s_{\lambda}(\bm{s})
. The trigonometric or Morris version of this Schur function average was obtained by Forrester
[
64
]
. Finally, a spherical version of the Schur polynomial averages was obtained in
[
74
]
and generalized further in
[
63
]
. We summarise these results in the following.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a partition.

•

Morris type
[
69
, Eq. above (2.46)]
: for any
\alpha,\beta
such that
\mathrm{Re}(\alpha+\beta)>-1
, we have

(3.24)

\int_{\mathcal{C}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dz_{j}}{iz_{j}}\,z_{j}^{\frac{\alpha-\beta}{2}}|1+z_{j}|^{\alpha+\beta}s_{\lambda}(\bm{z})|\Delta(\bm{z})|^{2}=M_{N}(\alpha,\beta)s_{\lambda}(1_{N})\frac{[-\beta]_{\lambda}}{[\alpha+N]_{\lambda}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}.

•

Spherical type
[
74
, Eq. (28)]
and
[
63
, Lemma 2.4]
: for any
\alpha,\beta,\lambda
such that
\operatorname{Re}\alpha>-1
,
\operatorname{Re}\beta>-1
,

,

,

(3.25)

\int_{[0,\infty)^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}dx_{j}\,\frac{x_{j}^{\alpha}}{(1+x_{j})^{\alpha+\beta+2m}}s_{\lambda}(\bm{x})\Delta(\bm{x})^{2}=S_{m}(\alpha,\beta)s_{\lambda}(1_{m})\frac{[\alpha+m]_{\lambda}}{[-\beta]_{\lambda}}(-1)^{|\lambda|}.

•

Jacobi type
[
69
, Eq. (2.46)]
: for any
\alpha,\beta
such that
\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha>-1
and
\mathrm{Re}\,\beta>-1
, we have

(3.26)

\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\alpha}(1-s_{j})^{\beta}s_{\lambda}(\bm{s})\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}=S_{m}(\alpha,\beta)s_{\lambda}(1_{m})\frac{[\alpha+m]_{\lambda}}{[\alpha+\beta+2m]_{\lambda}}.

From these integration formulas, we establish the following.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in\mathbb{C}
,
\operatorname{Re}\eta_{2}>m-1
and
\operatorname{Re}\eta_{1}>-1
. Then

(3.27)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\quad\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\eta_{2}-m}(1-s_{j})^{\eta_{1}}(1-(1-t)s_{j})^{N}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}\\
&=\frac{S_{m}(\eta_{2}-m,\eta_{1})}{M_{m}(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2},-\eta_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\frac{dz_{j}}{iz_{j}}\,z_{j}^{\frac{\eta_{1}+2\eta_{2}}{2}}|1+z_{j}|^{\eta_{1}}(1+(1-t)z_{j})^{N}|\Delta(\bm{z})|^{2}.\end{split}

Proof.

We expand both powers of
N
in (
3.27
) using the dual Cauchy identity (
3.2
). Integrating term by term and evaluating the resulting Schur function averages using (
3.26
) and (

) results in identical expressions and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Assume that
m,N\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in\mathbb{C}
,
\operatorname{Re}\eta_{2}>m-1
and
\operatorname{Re}\eta_{1}>-1
. Then

(3.28)

\begin{split}&\quad\frac{1}{M_{N}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})}\int_{\mathcal{C}^{N}}\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{dz_{j}}{iz_{j}}\,z_{j}^{\frac{\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}}{2}}|1+z_{j}|^{\eta_{1}+\eta_{2}}(1+tz_{j})^{m}|\Delta(\bm{z})|^{2}\\
&=\frac{1}{S_{m}(\eta_{2}-m,\eta_{1}+N)}\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\eta_{2}-m}(1-s_{j})^{\eta_{1}}(1-(1-t)s_{j})^{N}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}.\end{split}

Proof.

To establish this duality, we start from the integral on the right-hand side of (

) and make the change of variables
s_{j}=\frac{x_{j}}{1+x_{j}}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
, leading to

\begin{split}&\quad\int_{[0,1]^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}ds_{j}\,s_{j}^{\eta_{2}-m}(1-s_{j})^{\eta_{1}}(1-(1-t)s_{j})^{N}\Delta(\bm{s})^{2}\\

4.2.

In this subsection, we recall results from

Here, we consider general potentials

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L_{n}(V,W)&:=\det\bigg(\int_{\mathcal{I}}x^{j+k}e^{-nV(x)}e^{W(x)}\,dx\bigg)_{j,k=0,\ldots,n-1}\\

Let

be the equilibrium measure associated with the potential

\displaystyle 2\int\log|x-y|\,d\mu(y)=V(x)-\ell,\qquad\mbox{for }x\in\mathcal{S},

\displaystyle 2\int\log|x-y|\,d\mu(y)\leq V(x)-\ell,\qquad\mbox{for }x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathcal{S},

In the statement of the following result from

, and that the density

of

is strictly positive on
[a,b]
and admits an analytic continuation in a neighbourhood of
[a,b]
.

The precise asymptotic behaviour of the Hankel determinant

depends on the nature of the associated equilibrium measure, particularly on the number of hard edges. Theorem
4.3
treats the case with one hard edge and one soft edge, while Theorem

addresses the case with two hard edges.

The following result can be seen as a special case of

For the reader’s convenience, we provide the details of this procedure in Appendix

In what follows,
W

Assumption 4.2

The function

•

Theorem 4.3

.

Let

W:\mathcal{I}\to\mathbb{R}
be analytic in a neighbourhood of
[-1,1]
, locally Hölder continuous on
\mathcal{I}
and such that
W(x)=O(V(x))
as
x\to-\infty
. As
n\to\infty
, we have

L_{n}(V)=\exp\bigg(C_{1}n^{2}+C_{2}n+C_{3}\log n+C_{4}+O\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)\bigg),

where

\displaystyle C_{1}=-\log 2-\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}(V(x)-2(1-x))\Big(\frac{1}{\pi}+\psi(x)\Big)\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{1-x}}\,dx,

\displaystyle C_{2}=\log(2\pi)+\int_{-1}^{1}W(x)\psi(x)\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{1-x}}\,dx,

\displaystyle C_{3}=-\frac{1}{6},

\displaystyle C_{4}=2\zeta^{\prime}(-1)-\frac{1}{8}\log(\pi\psi(1))-\frac{1}{24}\log(\pi\psi(-1))+\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{W(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}\bigg(\mathchoice{{\vbox{\hbox{$\textstyle-$ }}\kern-7.8469pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptstyle-$ }}\kern-6.1589pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.54228pt}}{{\vbox{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle-$ }}\kern-5.36816pt}}\!\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{W^{\prime}(y)\sqrt{1-y^{2}}}{x-y}\,dy\bigg)\,dx.

Following
[
40
, Remark 1.4]
, we extend the above result to handle equilibrium measures supported on
[a,b]
with a soft edge at
a
and a hard edge at
b
.

Suppose
w(x)=e^{-nV(x)}e^{W(x)}
is such that the associated equilibrium measure is supported on
[a,b]
. Then, the change of variables
y_{j}=(x_{j}-\frac{a+b}{2})/(\frac{b-a}{2})
,
j=1,...,n
in (
4.6
) shows that

(A.1)

L_{n}(V,W)|_{\mathcal{I}=(-\infty,b]}=\Big(\frac{b-a}{2}\Big)^{n^{2}}L_{n}(\widetilde{V},\widetilde{W})|_{\mathcal{I}=(-\infty,1]},

where

\widetilde{V}(x)=V(\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,x),\qquad\widetilde{W}(x)=W(\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,x).

Also, if
\ell
and
\psi(x)\sqrt{\frac{x-a}{b-x}}
are respectively the Euler-Lagrange constant and the density of the equilibrium measure of
V
, the change of variables
s=(y-\frac{a+b}{2})/(\frac{b-a}{2})
in (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) implies that the equilibrium measure of
\widetilde{V}
is supported on
[-1,1]
and can be written as
\widetilde{\psi}(x)\sqrt{\frac{1+x}{1-x}}
. Furthermore,
\widetilde{\psi}
and the Euler-Lagrange constant
\widetilde{\ell}
of
\widetilde{V}
are given by

(A.2)

\widetilde{\ell}=\ell+2\log(\tfrac{b-a}{2}),\qquad\widetilde{\psi}(x)=\frac{b-a}{2}\psi(\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\tfrac{b-a}{2}\,x).

The assumptions on
W
in Theorem
A.2
can in fact be relaxed; it suffices to assume that
W
satisfies Assumption
4.2
. This can be shown straightforwardly following the argument in
[
44
, Section 1.3]
.
Substituting the above relations in Theorem
A.2
and simplifying, we obtain Theorem
4.3
.

Similarly, if
\ell
and
\frac{\psi(x)}{\sqrt{(b-x)(x-a)}}
are respectively the Euler-Lagrange constant and the density of the equilibrium measure of

\frac{\widetilde{\psi}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
. Furthermore,

are given by

(A.3)

\widetilde{\ell}=\ell+2\log(\tfrac{b-a}{2}),\qquad\widetilde{\psi}(x)=\psi(\tfrac{a+b}{2}+\tfrac{b-a}{2}x).

Using the above relations in
[

4.4
.
