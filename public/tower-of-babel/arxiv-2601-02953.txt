Spectral and Phase Structure of a QCD-Inspired Unitary Matrix Model with Fisher-Hartwig Singularities
Anuj Malik, Anees Ahmed

Abstract
We investigate the large N N limit of a complex action unitary matrix model with Fisher-Hartwig singularities, motivated by QCD-inspired models with complexified potentials. We show that the model exhibits multiple ungapped phases and a single gapped phase. The phases are characterized by Fisher-Hartwig singularities in the complex plane. We show that the phase transitions are third order, with transitions between ungapped phases forbidden. We also briefly discuss the implications for the QCD phase diagram at the end.

Introduction

The study of spectral and critical properties of random matrix models has become a central interdisciplinary topic, as these models naturally arise in quantum field theory, condensed matter physics, number theory, and statistics
27
;
23
;
5
;
18
. Unitary matrix models appear in a wide variety of physical systems, such as gauge theories
17
;
2
;
12
;
19
, two-dimensional Quantum Gravity
8
;
11
;
16
, the Ising model and disordered mesoscopic conductors
26
;
29
;
10
, as well as topological field and string theories
37
. They are also connected to the Painlevé equations and their
\tau
-functions
21
;
36
. Simpler/limiting versions of unitary matrix models have been investigated for decades by both mathematicians and physicists; see Refs.
24
;
33
;
34
;
35
and the references therein. We also refer to the excellent literature reviews Refs.
32
and
25
.

Depending on the system, the matrix model description may be non-perturbative, which has its own obvious advantages. In fact, in certain cases the non-perturbative structure can be determined in complete detail
3
;
4
, and in such cases matrix models serve as a wonderful test lab to explore resurgent asymptotics. Additionally, matrix models have attracted attention in recent years due to their relevance to systems that exhibit the
sign problem
, which obstructs the use of standard techniques such as Monte-Carlo in lattice gauge theory
20
. Recent progress in resolving the sign problem has involved methods such as complex Langevin dynamics
1
;
6
;
22
and the Lefschetz-thimble approach
14
;
9
;
30
.

SU(
N
) matrix models are a subset of unitary matrix models, where the integral which defines the partition function is restricted to SU(
N
) matrices. We write

Z=\int_{\text{SU}(N)}dU\,e^{-\mathcal{S}(U)}

(1)

where
\mathcal{S}
is the action. Expectation values in the model are defined by

\langle f(U)\rangle=\int_{\text{SU}(N)}dU\,f(U)\,e^{-\mathcal{S}(U)}.

(2)

In this work we focus on the action
\mathcal{S}=N\,\text{Tr}\,V(U)
with the potential

V(z)=-\sigma\left(\ln(1+\alpha z)+\ln\left(1+\frac{1}{\gamma z}\right)\right).

(3)

This potential is complex valued. We note here that a large number of unitary models studied in the literature have a real potential. As the potential (
3
) is complex and the model (
1
) is an SU(
N
) model, the relation
\langle U\rangle=\langle U^{-1}\rangle
may not necessarily hold for our model, whereas in the case of U(
N
) models with real potentials it always holds. The branch points of the logarithm in (
3
) are Fisher-Hartwig singularities (see Ref.
13
and the references therein), and their existence implies a rich phase structure.

The
\gamma\to\infty
limit of this model has been studied in Ref.
19
, where it describes the statistics of a two-level QCD (Quantum Chromodynamics) system restricted to a small sphere. In the triple-scaling limit
\sigma\to 0,\alpha\to 0,\gamma\to\infty
while keeping
\alpha\sigma
and
\sigma/\gamma
equal and constant, the model reduces to the SU(
N
) version of the well-known Gross-Witten-Wadia model
17
. Thus our model can be viewed as a generalization of several existing models.

This work is organized as follows. In Sec.
2
, we summarize the standard approach used to study unitary matrix models, and introduce our notation. In Sec.
3
, we discuss the ungapped phase and identify four distinct ungapped phases, each characterized by the location of poles of the potential. In Sec.
4
, we examine the gapped phase. In Sec.
5
we briefly discuss the transitions between these phases. Finally, in Sec.
6
, we summarize our main results and outline possible extensions of this work. We restrict our work to the large
N
limit, as there are no phase transitions at finite
N
. We also restrict the coupling
\sigma
and the parameters
\alpha,\gamma
to positive values.

2
Formalism

The partition function (
1
) is an integral over SU(
N
) matrices; that is, the integral is constrained to those unitary matrices
U
which satisfy
\det U=1
. This constraint can also be written as
\text{Tr}\,\ln U=0
. We can convert the constrained SU(
N
) integral to an unconstrained U(
N
) integral by introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\mathscr{N}
. In the large
N
limit, this amounts to modifying the potential as

V(z)\to V(z)+\mathscr{N}\ln z,\qquad\mathscr{N}\in\mathbb{R}.

(4)

The standard procedure now is to introduce the eigenvalues
e^{i\theta_{i}}
(
i=1,\ldots,N
) of
U
, and rewrite the U(
N
) integral as an integration over the angular variables
\theta_{i}\in[-\pi,\pi)
as

Z=\int\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{N}d\theta_{i}\Big)\,e^{-\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}},

(5)

where
\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}
is the effective action given by

\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}=N\sum_{i=1}^{N}V(e^{i\theta_{i}})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\ln{\sin^{2}{\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}}{2}\right)}}+iN\mathscr{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}.

(6)

The second sum in the above expression is the Vandermonde contribution.
In this form, an approximation to the integral can be found by using the saddle point method. However, since we are working in the exact large
N
limit, this approximation is actually exact. The definition of saddle points,
\partial S_{\text{eff}}/\partial\theta_{i}=0
for every
i
, gives

V^{\prime}(\theta_{i})+i\mathscr{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\cot{\left(\frac{\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}}{2}\right)}.

(7)

It is clear from the left hand side of the equation that the saddles may be complex.

The next step is to introduce the spectral function, defined formally as

\bar{\rho}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\delta(\theta-\theta_{i}),

(8)

which allows the following replacement when taking the large
N
limit:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}f(\theta_{i})\to\int d\theta\,\bar{\rho}(\theta)f(\theta).

(9)

\bar{\rho}(\theta)
is normalized to unity by virtue of its definition (
8
). We define
z_{i}=e^{i\theta_{i}}
to return to the space of eigenvalues of
U
. The saddle point equation now takes the form

z_{i}V^{\prime}(z_{i})+\mathscr{N}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{z_{i}+z_{j}}{z_{i}-z_{j}}

(10)

and its large
N
limit is

zV^{\prime}(z)+\mathscr{N}=\mathscr{P}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{2\pi iz^{\prime}}\,\rho(z^{\prime})\,\frac{z+z^{\prime}}{z-z^{\prime}},\qquad z\in\mathcal{C}.

(11)

In the above relation
\mathscr{P}
denotes the principal value of the integral and
\rho(z):=2\pi\bar{\rho}(\theta)
for all
z\in\mathcal{C}
, where
\mathcal{C}
is the contour in the complex-eigenvalue space on which the saddle points coalesce (also called the support of
\rho(z)
). The density of the saddle points on
\mathcal{C}
is

D(z)=\dfrac{\rho(z)}{2\pi iz}.

(12)

The normalization of
\rho(z)
follows from that of
\bar{\rho}(\theta)
:

\int_{C}\dfrac{dz}{2\pi iz}\,\rho(z)=1.

(13)

We continue
\rho(z)
to the entire complex plane by using (
11
) as its definition.

The problem now is to find the spectral function
\rho(z)
and its support
\mathcal{C}
given the potential (
3
). This is a Reimann-Hilbert problem
7
;
25
, and it can be solved in a rather straight-forward manner if we restrict to solutions in which
\mathcal{C}
is a single continuous curve. Such a solution is called a single cut solution. If the potential is real and model is a U(
N
) matrix model (i.e.
\mathcal{N}=0
), then the saddle points are found exclusively on the unit circle and the support
\mathcal{C}
is simply the unit circle or an arc of a unit circle. In the present case the saddle points may be found off the unit circle.

The support
\mathcal{C}
can be found by solving the differential equation

\dfrac{dz}{d\theta}=\frac{iz}{\rho(z)},\qquad z\equiv z(\theta).

(14)

The expression for the expectation value (
2
) simplifies in the large
N
limit to

\langle f(U)\rangle=\int_{C}\dfrac{dz}{2\pi iz}\rho(z)f(z).

(15)

Lastly, the spectral function must satisfy the SU(
N
) constraint, which we can derive as follows:

\det U=1\implies\sum_{i}\theta_{i}=0\implies\int_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi iz}\rho(z)\ln{z}=0.

(16)

In the next section, we discuss the ungapped phases of the model.

Ungapped phases

z=-1/\gamma

We refer to any phase in which the spectral function has a closed support as an ungapped phase of the model. In this phase all integrals over the support, such as the saddle point equation (
11
)

\sigma\left(-1+\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha z}+\dfrac{1}{1+\gamma z}\right)+\mathscr{N}=\mathscr{P}\oint_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{2\pi iz^{\prime}}\,\rho(z^{\prime})\,\frac{z+z^{\prime}}{z-z^{\prime}},\qquad z\in\mathcal{C},

(17)

become closed contour integrals. Thus, we can immediately utilize the standard theorems of elementary Complex Analysis to solve the model and compute various quantities of interest.

We assume the following ansatz based on the poles of the potential (
z=-\frac{1}{\alpha},-\frac{1}{\gamma}
):

\rho(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{A_{n}}{z^{n}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{n}}{(z+1/\alpha)^{n}}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{C_{n}}{(z+1/\gamma)^{n}}.

(18)

It is necessary to include a pole at
z=0
in the ansatz in order to satisfy the normalization condition (
13
), and this pole must always be inside
\mathcal{C}
. The coefficients in the ansatz are determined by substituting it in (
17
) and comparing both sides. The normalization condition can be used to fix any one coefficient as well.

There are four different solutions for
\rho(z)
depending on whether the poles
z=-\frac{1}{\alpha}
and
z=-\frac{1}{\gamma}
are inside or outside
\mathcal{C}
. Each of these solutions corresponds to a distinct ungapped phase. We use the following nomenclature:

•

Phase A: Only the pole
z=-1/\gamma
lies inside
\mathcal{C}

•

Phase B: Both the poles lie inside
\mathcal{C}

•

Phase C: Only the pole
z=-1/\alpha
lies inside
\mathcal{C}

•

Phase D: No poles lie inside
\mathcal{C}

For brevity, we discuss phase A in detail and summarize the rest at the end of the section. This phase can be thought of as the small
\alpha
large
\gamma
phase. We substitute the ansatz (
18
) in (
17
) and compare sides to obtain the spectral function

\rho(z)=1+\sigma+\frac{\sigma}{1+\gamma z}-\frac{\sigma}{1+\alpha z}.

(19)

The comparison also shows that the Lagrange multiplier
\mathcal{N}
vanishes. With the spectral function known, the SU(
N
) constraint (
16
) simplifies to

(1+\sigma)\ln{r_{0}}+\sigma\ln{\left(\frac{1-r_{0}\alpha}{r_{0}\gamma-1}\right)}+\sigma\ln\gamma=0

(20)

where
r_{0}=|z(\pm\pi)|
is the distance to the intersection of
\mathcal{C}
and the negative real axis. Since the pole at
z=-\frac{1}{\alpha}
is outside
\mathcal{C}
, the number
r_{0}
is constrained to
\frac{1}{\gamma}<r_{0}<\frac{1}{\alpha}
. This number serves as an initial value for the differential equation (
14
), which can be solved to obtain the equation for the support
\mathcal{C}
:

\alpha|z|^{1+1/\sigma}=\left|z+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right|^{-1}\,\left|z+\frac{1}{\gamma}\right|.

(21)

Just as expected,
\mathcal{C}
is not a unit circle in this phase. See the left panel in Fig.
1
for plots of
\mathcal{C}
in phase
A
.

If for some value of
\alpha
the spectral function acquires a zero on the negative real axis, say
z=-r_{0}
, then we have a phase transition to another phase. We call this new phase the
gapped
phase as
\mathcal{C}
has acquired a gap at
z=-r_{0}
. Solving
\rho(-r_{0})=0
gives the phase boundary

\alpha=\frac{\gamma r_{0}-\sigma-1}{r_{0}(\gamma r_{0}(\sigma+1)-2\sigma-1)}

(22)

where
r_{0}
, given by (
20
), is itself a function of
\alpha
and
\gamma
. We solve this equation numerically and plot the phase diagram close to the transition between the ungapped phase A and the gapped phase in the right panel of Fig.
1
.

Next, we calculate the moments in this phase.

W_{n}:=\frac{1}{N}\langle\mathrm{tr}\,U^{n}\rangle=\oint_{C}\dfrac{dz}{2\pi iz}\rho(z)z^{n}=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n+1}\frac{\sigma}{\gamma^{n}}\hskip 42.67912ptn\geq 1\\
(-1)^{n+1}\sigma\alpha^{|n|}\hskip 34.14322ptn\leq-1\end{cases}

(23)

If our model results from a lattice gauge theory, then these moments are related to the winding Wilson loops over a single plaquette
31
. We observe that

W_{n}\neq W_{-n}

(24)

which indicates the presence of the sign problem. This behavior is completely expected, as our model’s potential is complex, in addition to having an SU(
N
) geometry. In the Gross-Witten-Wadia model
17
, the single-winding Wilson loop (
W_{1}
) is related the string tension
\tau
and the lattice spacing
a
as
W_{1}=e^{-a^{2}\tau}
. If we assume that this continues to hold if our model is connected to a lattice gauge theory, then the beta function of
1/\sigma
has the expression

\beta=\dfrac{2W_{1}}{\sigma^{2}}\dfrac{\ln W_{1}}{\partial_{\sigma}W_{1}}.

(25)

For phase A this yields
\beta=\dfrac{2}{\sigma}\ln{\dfrac{\sigma}{\gamma}}
.

Lastly, we look at the free energy, defined as

F=-\frac{1}{N^{2}}\ln Z.

(26)

As the free energy in the large
N
limit is just the rescaled effective action,
F=\frac{1}{N^{2}}S_{\text{eff}}
, the continuum version of (
6
) can be used to directly calculate the free energy. Another method is to first find the derivatives of the free energy with respect to each parameter of the model using

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}=\left\langle\frac{\partial V(U)}{\partial t}\right\rangle=\oint_{C}\frac{dz}{2\pi iz}\rho(z)\frac{\partial V(z)}{\partial t},\qquad t=\sigma,\alpha,\gamma,

(27)

and then integrate back. The derivatives are

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\alpha-\gamma},\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial\gamma}=-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\gamma-\alpha},\qquad\frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma}=2\sigma\ln{\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right)}.

(28)

Using either method, we find the free energy in phase A to be

F=\sigma^{2}\ln\left(1-\frac{\alpha}{\gamma}\right).

(29)

4
Gapped Phase

Figure 3
:
Schematic of the support
\mathcal{C}
of the spectral function in the gapped phase (dashed, both panels). The dots on the negative real axis are the poles of the potential (
z=-1/\alpha,-1/\gamma
). Solid closed curve in the left panel is the contour
\mathcal{C}^{*}
used to solve integrals in the gapped phase. It can be deformed continuously to that shown in the right panel. See Fig.
4
for the actual contours in the gapped phase.

In this phase the saddle points lie on an open contour. The
gap
develops about the negative real axis, similar to that in the Gross-Witten-Wadia model. The gapped phase can be studied by using the resolvent, which is defined as

R(z)=\left\langle\frac{z+U}{z-U}\right\rangle.

(30)

This definition implies the limiting values

R(z)=\begin{cases}-1\hskip 28.45274ptz\rightarrow 0,\\
+1\hskip 28.45274ptz\rightarrow\infty,\\
\end{cases}

(31)

and in the large-
N
limit the resolvent can be expressed as an integral over the support
\mathcal{C}

R(z)=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{2\pi iz^{\prime}}\,\rho(z^{\prime})\,\frac{z+z^{\prime}}{z-z^{\prime}}.

(32)

It is clear from the above expression that
R(z)
is analytic everywhere except for
z\in\mathcal{C}
, and that it has branch points at the ends of
\mathcal{C}
. We denote the ends of
\mathcal{C}
by
a
and
a^{*}
.

Figure 4
:
Support of the spectral function
\rho(z)
in the gapped phase.

By using the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula
28
, we find the two main expressions which make the resolvent a useful quantity. The first relates the potential to the resolvent:

zV^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{2}\,\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\Big(R(z+\epsilon)+R(z-\epsilon)\Big).

(33)

The second is an expression for the spectral function in terms of the resolvent:

\rho(z)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}R(z)

(34)

where
\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}R(z)
is the discontinuity in the resolvent across
\mathcal{C}
.

Motivated by (
33
) and (
34
), and the fact that
\mathcal{C}
can serve as a branch cut for
R(z)
, we write the following ansatz for the resolvent:

R(z)=zV^{\prime}(z)+g(z)Q(z).

(35)

Note that
V
in the above is the modified potential which includes the Lagrange multplier, as written in (
4
). The second term contains
g(z)
, a unknown function well-behaved on
\mathcal{C}
but possibly meromorphic outside it, and
Q(z)
, which contains the discontinuity in
R(z)
. The latter is defined as
Q(z)=\sqrt{z-a}\,\sqrt{z-a^{*}}
with branch cut
\mathcal{C}
. This definition ensures that
\rho(a)=\rho(a^{*})=0
. Using the ansatz in (
34
) gives

\rho(z)=\frac{1}{2}g(z)\,\Delta_{\mathcal{C}}Q(z).

(36)

Thus, the problem of finding the spectral density is equivalent to the problem of finding
g(z)
, the Lagrange multiplier
\mathscr{N}
and the endpoints
a,a^{*}
.

To find an expression for
g(z)
, we first note that (
34
) allows writing the expectation value of a quantity
f(U)
as a closed contour integral:

\langle f(U)\rangle=\int_{\mathcal{C}}\dfrac{dz}{2\pi iz}\,\rho(z)\,f(z)=\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{*}}\dfrac{dz}{4\pi iz}\,R(z)\,f(z),

(37)

where
\mathcal{C}^{*}
is a closed contour wrapped around the support
\mathcal{C}
, as shown in the left panel of Fig
3
. Then, a general expression for
g(z)
is

g(z)=\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{*}}\frac{z^{\prime}V(z^{\prime})}{(z^{\prime}-z)Q(z^{\prime})}\frac{dz^{\prime}}{2\pi i}.

(38)

This result is similar to a theorem by Tricomi
15
.

For the current model,
\mathcal{C}^{*}
can be continuously deformed to that shown in the right panel, which means that the integral in the above relation takes contributions only from the residues at
z=0,-\frac{1}{\alpha},-\frac{1}{\gamma},\infty
. Solving the integral we find

g(z)=\frac{\sigma}{(1+\gamma z)A_{\gamma}}+\frac{\sigma}{(1+\alpha z)A_{\alpha}}

(39)

where we have used the shorthands

A_{\alpha}=\left|\frac{1}{\alpha}+a\right|,\quad A_{\gamma}=\left|\frac{1}{\gamma}+a\right|.

For the remaining three quantities
a
,
a^{*}
and
\mathscr{N}
, we need three independent equations. As
g(z)
is now known, two equations are found immediately from the limiting behaviour of the resolvent as given in (
31
).

\mathscr{N}+\sigma-\sigma|a|\left(\frac{1}{A_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{A_{\gamma}}\right)=-1,\quad\mathscr{N}-\sigma+\sigma\left(\frac{1}{\alpha\,A_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{\gamma A_{\gamma}}\right)=1.

(40)

The third and last equation is found by enforcing the SU(
N
) constraint
\langle\ln U\rangle=0
. By using (
37
) this constraint can be rewritten in the following form

\oint_{\mathcal{C}^{*}}\dfrac{dz}{4\pi iz}\,R(z)\,\ln z=0

(41)

By deforming the contour
\mathcal{C}^{*}
as shown in Fig.
3
, we find the SU(
N
) constraint to be

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{A_{\alpha}}\left[\left(|a|+\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\ln|a|-\left(|a|-\dfrac{1}{\alpha}\right)\ln\cos^{2}\frac{\phi}{2}\right]-\text{arcsinh}\dfrac{(\alpha-|a|^{-1})A_{\alpha}}{1+\cos\phi}+\ln\alpha

(42)

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{A_{\gamma}}\left[\left(|a|+\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\right)\ln|a|-\left(|a|-\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\right)\ln\cos^{2}\frac{\phi}{2}\right]-\text{arcsinh}\dfrac{(\gamma-|a|^{-1})A_{\gamma}}{1+\cos\phi}+\ln\gamma=0,

where
\phi\in(0,\pi)
is the argument of the endpoint
a
. Simultaneously solving the equations (
40
), together with the SU(
N
) constraint gives the Lagrange multiplier
\mathscr{N}
and the endpoints
a,a^{*}
.

Due to the transcendental nature of the SU(
N
) constraint, the observables in this phase can not be expressed in closed-form in terms of the model parameters
\sigma,\alpha,\gamma
. For example, the single-winding Wilson loop expectation values

\displaystyle W_{1}=\dfrac{\sigma}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha}+\dfrac{1}{\gamma}\right)-\dfrac{\sigma}{2|a\alpha+1|}\left(\dfrac{1}{\alpha}+\text{Re}\,a\right)-\dfrac{\sigma}{2|a\gamma+1|}\left(\dfrac{1}{\gamma}+\text{Re}\,a\right)

(43)

\displaystyle W_{-1}=\dfrac{\sigma}{2}(\alpha+\gamma)-\dfrac{\sigma\alpha}{2|\alpha+1/a|}\left(\alpha+\dfrac{\text{Re}\,a}{|a|^{2}}\right)-\dfrac{\sigma\gamma}{2|\gamma+1/a|}\left(\gamma+\dfrac{\text{Re}\,a}{|a|^{2}}\right)

(44)

depend on the endpoints
a,a^{*}
which themselves depend transcendentally on the model parameters. The situation with the free energy is identical. Here we provide the derivative of free energy with respect to

6
Conclusions and Discussions

In this work, we have examined the large
N
limit of a SU(
N
) unitary matrix model with a complex potential containing Fisher-Hartwig singularities. The model exhibits four ungapped phases and a single gapped phase. The saddle points in the large
N
limit were found to lie off the unit circle in the complex plane for both ungapped and gapped phases, reflecting the inherent complex-valued nature of the model’s action. Transitions between the ungapped phases proceed through the intermediate gapped phase. The phase transitions are third order, similar to that observed in the Gross–Witten-Wadia model.

Exact and explicit expressions were obtained for all observables in the ungapped phases, whereas in the gapped phase the transcendental nature of the equations forced us to use numerical methods to study the observables. We have also connected our results to an existing low temperature QCD-like model to verify our results, and extend the existing results.

These results provide deeper insight into the phase structure of complex extensions of unitary matrix models with Fisher-Hartwig singularities and may offer useful analogies for understanding complex actions in QCD-like theories. Further extensions of this work could include incorporating finite-
N
effects or exploring the behavior under further complex deformations of the potential.
