Abstract
. We prove group existence and structure theorems in a general setting of tame topological theories. More precisely, we identify a linear/non-linear dividing line – called topological 1-basedness – among the class of t-minimal theories with the independent neighborhood property. This is a wide class including all visceral theories, as well as all dense weakly o-minimal and C-minimal theories (even those where exchange fails). Now assume ℳ \mathcal{M} is highly saturated and t-minimal with the independent neighborhood property. We show that if ℳ \mathcal{M} is non-trivial and topologically 1-based, it admits a type-definable abelian group ( G , + ) (G,+) with G G an open subset of M M . Moreover, we can ensure that G G is a topological group with the subspace topology inherited from M M ; and in this case, we show that the induced structure on G G satisfies an appropriate topological analog of the Hrushovski-Pillay classification of 1-based stable groups.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we introduce the notion of a
topologically 1-based t-minimal structure
, and prove that when such a structure is non-trivial, it admits an infinite type-definable abelian topological group, whose (local) structure we then proceed to study. These results can be viewed as a topological analogue of the literature on 1-based stable structures: on the one hand, the existence of a type-definable group resembles Hrushovski’s characterization of locally modular regular types (
[
9
]
, following earlier works of Zilber
[
21
, §3]
and Cherlin-Harrington-Lachlan
[
4
]
). On the other hand, our structural analysis of the group closely resembles the Hrushovski-Pillay analysis of 1-based stable groups (
[
10
]
).

Our main theorem reads as follows (see Theorem
11.15
; the relevant terms are defined in Definitions
2.2
,
3.1
,
9.7
, and
5.1
):

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure with the independent neighborhood property. Assume that
\mathcal{M}
is non-trivial and topologically 1-based. Then there are a countable parameter set
A
, and an
A
-type-definable abelian group
G
, such that the following hold:

(1)

G
is open in
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

G
is a topological group with the topology inherited from
M
.

(3)

G
is locally linear.

We explain the terminology more below; for now, we note that the above theorem encompasses all dense weakly o-minimal and C-minimal structures; in particular, as far as we know, it is the first group-existence theorem for (non-o-minimal) weakly o-minimal structures.

1.1.
Background

To recall, 1-basedness in stability is an abstract notion viewed as capturing those stable theories of
linear
complexity. Classical results in stability show that in non-trivial 1-based theories, any instance of non-triviality can be explained in terms of type-definable abelian groups (e.g.,
[
19
, Corollary 5.4.13]
). The key notion allowing the construction of such groups is the
germ
of a map on a complete (stationary) type – where the word
germ
here indicates that maps are viewed up to agreement on a non-forking extension of the domain.

Soon after the aforementioned works on 1-basedness in stable theories, it became clear that
o-minimality
provided a suitable tame (yet unstable) setting where many stability-like results could be developed. In particular, o-minimal structures were seen to exhibit a similar dimension theory to strongly minimal structures (and thus many notions could be translated from stability to o-minimality). To that end, early works of Peterzil and Loveys (
[
16
]
,
[
12
]
) developed a version of linearity for o-minimal structures, and obtained similar group-existence and structure theorems (which ultimately became part of the o-minimal trichotomy theorem,
[
18
]
). The key notion here was again the germ of a function – but in the o-minimal setting, germs retain their original topological meaning (one treats functions up to agreement on an interval).

Many aspects of Peterzil’s group-existence proof (
[
16
]
) are quite general, but the definition of linearity is rather specific to o-minimality. In a later work, Maalouf
[
13
]
adapted the definition of linearity to the related context of
C-minimality
, and proved a similar group existence theorem – however, Maalouf needed to assume the
exchange lemma
, which can fail in a general C-minimal structure.

The above works on o-minimality and C-minimality naturally lead to the following question: is there a general notion of linearity for ‘topologically tame’ structures, and can one use it to prove abstract group-existence and structure theorems? For example, what can be said for
weakly o-minimal
structures, and for C-minimal structures without exchange?

1.2.
T-minimality and Work of Johnson

Answering the above question would require a general setting for topological tameness, and this general setting should admit a suitable dimension theory for definable sets and types. Recently, Johnson
[
11
]
has shown that
t-minimality
provides such a setting. Recall that a (sufficiently saturated) structure is t-minimal if (1) it admits a uniformly
\emptyset
-definable Hausdorff topology, and (2) a unary definable set is infinite if and only if it has non-empty interior with respect to the given topology. T-minimality encompasses all dense weakly o-minimal and C-minimal structures (regardless of exchange), in addition to many valued fields and expansions thereof (e.g. all 1-h-minimal fields, and all unstable dp-minimal expansions of fields).

Johnson’s paper develops a rudimentary dimension theory assuming only t-minimality, then proceeds to develop stronger geometric properties (e.g. generic continuity of functions) assuming a stronger condition called
viscerality
(saying roughly that the topology is ‘uniform’). In our setting, we do not want to assume viscerality: the issue is that in many cases, viscerality follows from the existence of a group structure, and we want to work a priori without a group structure. For example, while all o-minimal groups are visceral, there are also non-visceral linear o-minimal structures (and our results should apply to them).

Instead, we augment t-minimality with a weaker condition, which we call the
independent neighborhood property
: roughly speaking, this property says that one can always ‘zoom in’ on a tuple without changing its dimension. The same property was already studied in the dp-minimal visceral context in
[
7
]
, where it proved quite useful. The key features of the independent neighborhood property in our case are:

•

It encompasses all visceral, weakly o-minimal, and C-minimal structures.

•

It implies many of the same geometric properties (including generic continuity) that Johnson’s paper proves for visceral structures.

In light of the above discussion, our main theorem shows that the known group-existence and structure theorems for o-minimal and C-minimal structures are special cases of a general result, valid for all t-minimal structures with the independent neighborhood property. Our proof follows a similar (but not identical) outline to the original proof of
[
16
]
, with various new challenges encountered throughout. In particular, note that we
do not
assume exchange (and this causes quite a few difficulties); as a tradeoff, our version of non-triviality needs to be a bit stronger than the usual one (see Definition
9.7
).

1.3.
Topological 1-basedness

The main task in our proof is to identify and study a linearity notion – namely
topological 1-basedness
– in the absence of either exchange or an order (recall that Peterzil and Loveys’ o-minimal version used both exchange and the order). Let us now discuss our version in more depth.

Just as
germs
play a key role in the stable and o-minimal group existence theorems, they also play a key role in our setting. In fact, it follows from Johnson’s paper that in a t-minimal structure, every complete type
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
has a well-defined germ at any realization
a
(see Lemma
2.8
), and this germ can be coded as an imaginary element
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
.

When constructing groups in the stable setting, germs are manifested definably as
canonical bases
. Thus, it is natural to view the imaginary
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
as a sort of ‘topological canonical base’ of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
, localized to the point
a
. There is good evidence for this line of thinking – for example, assuming the independent neighborhood property, if
B\supseteq A
then one has
\dim(a/B)=\dim(a/A)
if and only if
\operatorname{germ}(a/B)=\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
(see Lemma
3.7
).

In light of this analogy, our definition of topological 1-basedness essentially amounts to replacing canonical bases in the stability-theoretic definition with germs. There is one difference, however, which makes our definition a bit more general. Recall that in stability, 1-basedness requires the canonical base of a type to be algebraic over a single realization. Since the canonical base is imaginary, the word ‘algebraic’ here refers to the operator
\operatorname{acl}^{eq}
. In geometric structures, Gagelman extends the dimension theory to imaginary elements, where the
0
-dimensional closure may be a proper coarsening of
\operatorname{acl}^{eq}
. In our view, it is this 0-dimensional closure operator (rather than
\operatorname{acl}^{eq}
) which correctly captures linearity in our setting. Of course, 0-dimensionality only makes sense assuming exchange (i.e. in geometric structures). However, one can also characterize it topologically.
That is, in geometric t-minimal structures, 0-dimensional imaginaries are those associated with definable equivalence relations whose classes are
open
(or at least those which have interior in some ambient set). Thus, dropping the assumption of exchange and keeping only
t
-minimality (with independent neighbourhoods), our notion of linearity is the following:
say that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is
topologically 1-based over
A
if the following equivalent conditions hold (see Lemma
5.6
):

•

\dim(b/A\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab))=\dim(b/Aa)
.

•

The map
y\mapsto\operatorname{germ}(a/Ay)
is constant on a neighborhood of
b
in
\operatorname{tp}(b/Aa)
.

Then say that
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based if
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based over
\emptyset
for all real tuples
a
and
b
.

Note that this definition generalizes known versions of linearity in the literature. For example, our main structure theorem for topologically 1-based types, Theorem
6.4
, shows that topological 1-basedness is equivalent to an appropriately localized version of Pillay’s notion of weak normality. Outside of the stable context, in, e.g.
[
1
]
, Berenstein and Vassiliev develop a version of linearity in the related context of
geometric structures
, which they call
weak 1-basedness
. When restricted to o-minimality, weak 1-basedness is shown to capture exactly the linear o-minimal structures in the sense of Peterzil and Loveys. Now suppose
\mathcal{M}
is t-minimal with the independent neighborhood property, and
\mathcal{M}
moreover satisfies exchange. Then
\mathcal{M}
is a geometric structure. In Corollary
6.5
, we show that such an
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based if and only if it is weakly 1-based as a geometric structure – thus, topological 1-basedness also generalizes linearity in o-minimal structures (see Corollary
5.18
).

1.4.
Infinitesimals and Regularity

Once we have the notion of topological 1-basedness, our task is to use it to build type-definable groups. There are two key notions involved here. The first, following earlier works such as Peterzil’s o-minimal group configuration of
[
17
]
, is the
infinitesimal neighborhood

\mu(a/A)
of a tuple
a
over a parameter set
A
. This refers to the set of realizations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
from a highly saturated elementary extension which are ‘infinitesimally close’ to
a
in the sense of the original model (see Definition
4.3
). Infinitesimal neighborhoods allow us to replace finite-to-finite correspondences, locally, with homeomorphisms. For example, suppose
a
and
b
are interalgebraic over
A
; then using generic continuity, we show that
\mu(ab/A)
is the graph of a homeomorphism
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
(see Lemma
4.9
). By repeated applications of this fact, it follows that if
a
and
b
are interalgebraic over
At
, then
\mu(abt/A)
is the graph of a
\mu(t/A)
-indexed family of homeomorphisms
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
. Thus, we have a convenient tool for constructing families of homeomorphisms – and such objects will ultimately be the building blocks of a type-definable group action.

The other key notion is
regularity
. Using infinitesimal neighborhoods, one can construct (for example) three points
a,b,c\in M
, a set
A
, and three compatible type-definable families of homeomorphisms
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
,
\mu(b/A)\rightarrow\mu(c/A)
, and
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(c/A)
(compatible meaning that any composition
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)\rightarrow\mu(c/A)
agrees with one of the given maps
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(c/A)
). One then needs to find additional conditions assuring that (1) these families of maps arise from a group (or rather, at this level, a groupoid) and (2) the resulting groupoid is type-definable in an appropriate sense.

The key notion is regularity (inspired by regular group actions): say that a family of maps
f_{t}:X\rightarrow Y
is
regular
if for any
(x,y)\in X\times Y
there is
t
with
f_{t}(x)=y
, and if any two such maps either agree everywhere or agree nowhere. Regular families of maps can be seen as a manifestation of linearity (or 1-basedness), in the sense that a single point determines an entire map in the family. In Sections 7 and 8, we study type-definable systems of compatible regular families of maps in more detail, showing that in many cases such configurations give rise to type-definable group actions (this is in some sense a completely abstract group configuration theorem – see Theorem
8.8
). Then in Section 9, we show that such collections of regular families arise naturally under the assumption of topological 1-basedness. The key input here is a structure theorem for topologically 1-based types (see Theorem
6.4
), which roughly says that such types always look regular in an infinitesimal neighborhood (as mentioned above, this is a topological analog of the
weakly normal
decomposition in
[
10
]
):

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure with the independent neighborhood property. Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
.

(2)

Any two fibers in the projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
are either equal or disjoint.

So, condition (2) of the above theorem is how we prove that the families of homeomorphisms we build are regular, and thus gives rise to a type-definable group.

1.5.
Summary of the Paper

The paper is roughly organized into four main parts:

•

Sections 2-4 study t-minimal theories and the independent neighborhood property in general, focusing on germs and generic continuity. These sections do not involve topological 1-basedness or groups. Section 2 constructs and studies germs in arbitrary t-minimal theories; Section 3 adds the independent neighborhood property to the background assumptions and develops generic continuity; and Section 4 rewrites the key points of the previous sections in the language of infinitesimal neighborhoods.

•

Sections 5-6 introduce topological 1-basedness. Section 5 is concerned mainly with the definition and basic properties. For example, we prove that topological 1-basedness is preserved under adding and removing constants (Lemma
5.7
) and taking reducts (Lemma
5.9
), and that a topologically 1-based structure cannot admit an infinite definable field (Lemma
5.15
). Then Section 6 is devoted to the main structure theorem for topologically 1-based types, Theorem
2
above.

•

Sections 7-8 leave the topological setting entirely and focus on a completely abstract version of group configurations, which we call
regular groupoid spines
. This is something like a regular groupoid, but where only certain ordered pairs of objects have morphisms between them. Section 7 shows that any regular groupoid spine with at least 3 objects arises from a genuine regular groupoid (this is really a group configuration theorem). Then Section 8 shows that if a regular groupoid spine is type-definable in an appropriate sense, so is the groupoid it arose from (and thus so is the automorphism group at any of its objects).

•

Finally, Sections 9-11 combine the previous two parts to prove our main theorems. Suppose that
\mathcal{M}
is non-trivial and topologically 1-based. In Section 9 we use infinitesimal neighborhoods to construct a type-definable regular groupoid spine with three objects in
\mathcal{M}
(precisely, every required property other than regularity comes from the general theory of infinitesimal neighborhoods, and regularity comes from Theorem
2
). Thus, by the results of Sections 7-8, we obtain a type-definable group in an elementary extension of
\mathcal{M}
. Then in Section 10, we show that the group can be transferred down to
\mathcal{M}
, and that – by virtue of generic continuity – it can be tweaked slightly to make it a topological group with the induced topology from
\mathcal{M}
. Finally, Section 11 shows that the group is locally abelian and locally linear. The main tool here is again Theorem
2
. The idea is that any two infinitesimal translates of an infinitesimal neighborhood (in an appropriate sense) are equal or disjoint – and this implies that the given infinitesimal neighborhood is a coset of a subgroup. Thus, the local structure of definable sets can be analyzed entirely in terms of subgroups and their cosets, in a similar manner to the Hrushovski-Pillay analysis of 1-based stable groups (
[
10
]
).

We can also give some local analysis. Namely, using the notion of topologically 1-based types, we define what it means for a one-dimensional type to be
topologically locally modular
(even if the ambient structure is not topologically 1-based as a whole). In this case (assuming non-triviality), we are able to build a type-definable topological group on an open subset of the realizations of the type (see Theorem
10.11
). However, the structural results for the group (local linearity and local abelianity) need full topological 1-basedness, at least for all tuples from the group itself.

Finally, we give a brief appendix devoted to examples of t-minimal structures with the independent neighborhood property, covering some non-visceral structures, most notably weakly o-minimal and C-minimal structures. We also include a complementary appendix by Will Johnson, proving that visceral theories have the independent neighborhood property.

1.6.
Notation and model theoretic generalities

We use standard model-theoretic notation and terminology. Structures are denoted by calligraphic capital letters (
\mathcal{M},\mathcal{N}
etc.), and their universes are denoted by the corresponding Latin letters
M
,
N
. All tuples are assumed to be finite. As is customary, by
real
tuples, we refer to tuples in
M^{n}
(some
n
), as opposed to
imaginary
tuples which may reside in
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
.

Our structures will typically be assumed to be
sufficiently saturated
. To clarify, when we say
\mathcal{M}
is
sufficiently saturated
, we mean it is
\kappa
-saturated and
\kappa
-strongly homogenous for some uncountable cardinal
\kappa
strictly larger than the cardinality of the language of
\mathcal{M}
. Here,
\kappa
is unspecified but fixed to go along with the structure
\mathcal{M}
. In this case, the word
small
always means ‘of size less than
\kappa
’ – and by a
parameter set
, we mean a small subset of
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
. Thus, smallness and parameter sets are sensitive to the particular choice of model. For example, when studying infinitesimal neighborhoods, we will be working in an
|M|^{+}
-saturated model
\mathcal{N}\succ\mathcal{M}
. So
M
is small with respect to
\mathcal{N}
, but obviously not with respect to
\mathcal{M}
.

By a
type-definable set
, we mean the intersection of a small collection of definable sets. We use throughout that the projection of a type-definable set is again type-definable (over the same parameters). A complete type over a parameter set
A
is a maximally consistent type (in the language
\mathcal{L}
expanded with constants symbols for all
a\in A
). For every tuple
a
and parameter set
A
we denote by
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
the unique complete type over
A
realized by
a
.

2.1.
T-minimality

Recall the following definition (originally appearing in

Definition 2.2
.

The t-minimal structures form a fairly wide class, including most known instances of topological tameness in model theory. In particular, t-minimality encompasses all weakly o-minimal and C-minimal structures, as well as Henselian valued fields of characteristic zero.

Johnson (
[

2.3.

One of our main tools will be the existence of

(3)

2.5.
Codes of Germs

We remark here that definable germs can be viewed as elements of
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
. In particular, this will apply to
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
for any
a
and
A
.

Note that since the topology on
\mathcal{M}
is
\emptyset
-definable, every automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{M}
is a homeomorphism. It follows that if

is a germ at

, then
\sigma(g)
is a well-defined germ at
\sigma(a)
. Now we define:

Definition 2.15
.

Let
a\in M^{n}
, and let

be a germ at

. We say that

is a

for

if the following holds: let

be any automorphism of

. Then
\sigma(c)=c
if and only if

(so in particular

).

Remark 2.16
.

An immediate consequence of Definition
2.15
is that if
g
is a germ of

with code

, then
a\in\operatorname{dcl}(c)
.

Now we show:

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
a\in M^{n}
and let
g
be a definable germ at

. Then there is a code for
g
in
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
. In particular, for any parameter set
A
, there is a code for
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
in
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a definable set realizing

. Write
X=Y_{b}
for some
\emptyset
-definable family
\{Y_{t}:t\in T\}
of sets. Let
\sim
be the equivalence relation on
M\times T
given by
(x,s)\sim(y,t)
if
x=y
and
Y_{s}
and
Y_{t}
agree in a neighborhood of

. Then
\sim
is
\emptyset
-definable, so

is a sort in
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
. Now let

be the equivalence class of

. Then one checks easily that

is a code of

.
∎

Definition
2.15
should remind the reader of canonical bases in stability theory, particularly when considering germs of the form
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
. In fact, germs will serve as a replacement for canonical bases when interpreting a group later on. The main difference between the two notions is that germs at
a
contain the data of the point
a
, while canonical bases do not. This boils down to which equivalence relation is absorbed in the coding process: one should think of canonical bases (when they exist) as coding formulas in
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
up to agreeing
almost everywhere
, while germs code formulas in
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
up to agreement
in a neighborhood of
a
.

Note that there can be several codes of
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
, but any two are interdefinable over

. For this reason – as is often the case with canonical bases – we will abuse notation by identifying all codes of a germ with the germ itself. Thus, if

is a code of

, we will simply write

.

2.6.
D-approximations

Following (
[

, Definition 3.16]
), we also define:

Definition 2.18
.

Let

and let

be a parameter set. A

of

at

is a definable set

.

Note that a

For example, if

and

at

in

. In general, one should think of a d-approximation as analogous to a definable set of Morley degree one in an

-stable theory: in that context, any complete type

is (up to parallelism and passing to

) the unique generic type of some definable set

is the unique ‘generic at

.

We now give a couple of easy facts about d-approximations. First, a trivial but useful fact is the following:

Lemma 2.20
.

Let

, let

be a parameter set, and let

be a d-approximation of

at

. Then any

-definable property which holds of

in

.

Let

be an

-definable set containing

. By Lemma

(3), we compute:

∎

A restatement of Lemma
2.8
is the following:

Corollary 2.21
.

Let
a\in M^{n}
and

a parameter set. Then any minimal basic witness of

over

is a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at

.

Proof.

Let

be a minimal basic witness, corresponding to the projection

sending

. Lemma

gives that

3.
Independent Neighborhoods

We will need a natural strengthening of t-minimality, which we call the
independent neighborhood property
. This property has already been studied in the more restrictive context of SW-uniformities and related structures (
[
7
]
,
[
8
, §3.1]
). It is also the key geometric idea underlying the definition of
vicinic sets
in
\lx@sectionsign 4
of the same paper. In this section, we introduce the property in the abstract t-minimal setting and study its basic features. The theme will be that independent neighborhoods allow for stronger geometric properties of definable sets and types.

3.1.
Definition and Examples

Definition 3.1
.

\mathcal{M}
has the
independent neighborhood property
if for any tuple
a\in M^{n}
, any parameter set
A
, and any neighborhood
U
of
a
, there is a neighborhood
V
of
a
such that
V\subset U
and
V
is definable over a parameter
t
with
\dim(a/At)=\dim(a/A)
.

Definition
3.1
only requires the new parameters to preserve the dimension of the original tuple
a
. However, it follows that one can preserve the dimension of any tuple containing
a
:

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property. Let
a\in M^{m}
,
b\in M^{n}
, let
A
be a parameter set, and let
U
be a neighborhood of
a
. Then there is a neighborhood
V\subset U
of
a
defined over a tuple
t
with
\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
.

Proof.

The set
U\times M^{n}
is a neighborhood of
(a,b)
. By the independent neighborhood property, there is a neighborhood
W\subset U\times M^{n}
of
(a,b)
defined over a parameter
t
with
\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
. Let
\pi:M^{m}\times M^{n}\rightarrow M^{m}
be the projection. Then
\pi(W)\subset U
is a
t
-definable neighborhood of

.
∎

Most natural examples of t-minimal theories have the independent neighborhood property. For instance, it was shown in (
[
7
, Corollary 3.12]
) that all SW-uniformities have the independent neighborhood property, covering all dp-minimal expansions of topological groups. More generally, Johnson shows in Appendix
B
that all visceral theories have the independent neighborhood property, and thus so do
all
t-minimal topological groups. Finally, in Appendix A, we show that all C-minimal and (dense) weakly o-minimal theories have the independent neighborhood property.

The simplest example of a t-minimal theory without the independent neighborhood property is likely one pointed out by Johnson. Namely, consider a real closed field with the Sorgenfrey topology (generated by the half-open intervals of the form
[a,b)
). This gives a t-minimal theory. However, consider a generic singleton
a
, and any
b>a
. If
V\subset[a,b)
is any neighborhood of
a
, then
a
is definable over the canonical parameter of
V
(as the minimum element of
V)
. It follows that no such
V
can be defined independently of
a
.

Assumption 3.3
.

From now until the end of Section 6, assume
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property.

3.2.
Geometric Consequences of Independent Neighborhoods

Independent neighborhoods imply stronger geometric properties of definable sets. We now give various examples. The first lemma below shows that the local dimension of a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
is always
\dim(a/A)
. In contrast, without the independent neighborhood property one can have infinite discrete definable subsets of
M^{2}
(consider
\{(x,-x)\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Sorgenfrey topology; in that case, if

is generic then
\operatorname{germ}(a,-a/\emptyset)
is a single point).

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
a\in M^{n}
, let
A
be a parameter set, and let

be a d-approximation of

at

(1)

Let

be any definable set containing a neighborhood of

\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at

.

(2)

In particular,
\dim(\operatorname{germ}(a/A))=\dim(a/A)

We show (1); then (2) follows by definition.

To show (1), first note that

trivially realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
, because
X
does. The point is that we can also show

. For this, by assumption, there is a neighborhood

of

\dim(X)=\dim(a/A)
.
∎

3.4.
Generic Continuity

Johnson
[

points out that generic continuity can fail in a general t-minimal structure. His example is yet again a real closed field with the Sorgenfrey topology and the inverted diagonal (namely

Most of our generic continuity results need some local instance of additivity. Thus, we define:

Definition 3.12

Our main use of additivity, which we will exploit frequently, is the following:

\dim(ab/B)\leq\dim(b/B)+\dim(a/Bb)\leq\dim(b/A)+\dim(a/Ab)=\dim(ab/A).

3.5.
Uniqueness of the Topology

We end this section with an interesting fact that we nevertheless will not need. Recall that a structure can be t-minimal with respect to more than one topology (for example, consider a real closed field with either the order topology or the Sorgenfrey topology). On the other hand, it turns out that there can only be
one
such topology with the independent neighborhood property (at least up to perturbing a finite set) – and if this topology exists, it is the coarsest t-minimal topology on
\mathcal{M}
(again up to a finite set). Thus, if one t-minimal topology on a structure has independent neighborhoods, one might think of that topology as the ‘canonical’ one.

Recall that we have a fixed topology
\tau
on
\mathcal{M}
with the independent neighborhood property. Now we show:

Lemma 3.18
.

Let
\tau^{\prime}
be another
\emptyset
-definable topology on
\mathcal{M}
with respect to which
\mathcal{M}
is t-minimal.

(1)

There is a cofinite set
M^{\prime}\subset M
so that
\tau^{\prime}
refines
\tau
on
M^{\prime}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{M}
also has the independent neighborhood property with respect to
\tau^{\prime}
, then there is a cofinite set
M^{\prime}
such that
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
agree on
M^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We show (1). Then (2) follows by applying (1) twice (the second time with
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
reversed).

Now to prove (1), we will show that for cofinitely many
a\in M
, every definable
\tau
-neighborhood of
a
contains a definable
\tau^{\prime}
-neighborhood of
a
. Since this is a definable property of
a
, it will suffice to prove it for any

. So fix such an

.

Since
a\in M-\operatorname{acl}(\emptyset)
, we have
\dim(a)=1
. Now let
U
be a definable
\tau
-neighborhood of
a
. It is harmless to shrink
U
, so by the independent neighborhood property, we may assume

is definable over a tuple

with
\dim(a/t)=1
. By t-minimality, the
\tau^{\prime}
-boundary of

is finite, and is thus contained in
\operatorname{acl}(t)
. So
a
does not belong to the
\tau^{\prime}
-boundary of

, and thus
a
belongs to the
\tau^{\prime}
-interior of

. This shows that

contains a definable
\tau^{\prime}
-neighborhood of

, as desired.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

If
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property with respect to
\tau
, then one can completely describe
\tau
on

using only the model theory of
\mathcal{M}
(with no topology). Let

. Then by the independent neighborhood property,

has a neighborhood basis consisting of all

-definable sets containing

, where

ranges over all tuples with
a\notin\operatorname{acl}(t)
.

4.
Infinitesimal Neighborhoods

We now introduce the two-model setup that has become customary in treating infinitesimals in topological settings.

Assumption 4.1
.

From now until the end of Section 6, we fix
\mathcal{N}
, an elementary extension of
\mathcal{M}
which is sufficiently saturated with respect to
\mathcal{M}
.

By default (unless stated otherwise), we still perform our model-theoretic analysis in
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, parameter sets will continue to be small subsets of
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
.

Following previous works (see e.g.
[
17
]
), we use
\mathcal{N}
to discuss infinitesimal elements, and in particular infinitesimal neighborhoods of elements of
\mathcal{M}
. The idea is to prove statements about
\mathcal{M}
by translating them to cleaner statements about infinitesimals in
\mathcal{N}
.

The present section is divided into four parts. The first subsection makes the framework of infinitesimals precise. There should be no surprises here, as similar frameworks have become well-known in topological settings. The next subsection then rewrites some of our main topological lemmas on d-approximations in terms of infinitesimal neighborhoods. Again, this is a straightforward translation procedure, and we will be brief.

Finally, the next two subsections give two isolated but interesting results. First, we use infinitesimals to prove a very useful remnant of additivity that holds even if
\mathcal{M}
does not satisfy exchange. Then we show that if
\mathcal{M}
is saturated in its own cardinality, every infinitesimal neighborhood is the locus of a complete type.

4.1.
Basics

We begin with basic definitions:

.

\mu(a/A)=\mu(a/B)
then

\dim(a/A)=\dim(\mu(a/A))=\dim(\mu(a/B))=\dim(a/B),

so
a
is independent from
B
over
A
.

∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
a\in M^{m}
,
b\in M^{n}
, and
a\subset\mathcal{M}^{eq}
.

(1)

\mu(ab/A)\subset\mu(a/A)\times\mu(b/A)
; thus the natural projections map
\mu(ab/A)
into
\mu(a/A)
and
\mu(b/A)
, respectively.

(2)

\mu(ab/A)=\mu(a/A)\times\mu(b/A)
if and only if
a
and
b
are symmetrically independent over
A
.

Proof.

(1)

By Lemma
4.5
(3). Namely, suppose
(x,y)\in\mu(ab/A)
. Then
(x,y)\in\mu(a,b)
, so
x\in\mu(a)
and
y\in\mu(b)
. Moreover, we have
(x,y)\models\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
, and thus
x\models\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
and
y\models\operatorname{tp}(b/A)
. Thus, by Lemma
4.5
(3),
x\in\mu(a/A)
and
y\in\mu(b/A)
.

(2)

First suppose
a
and
b
are symetrically independent over
A
. By (1), we only need to show that if
x\in\mu(a/A)
and
y\in\mu(b/A)
, then
(x,y)\in\mu(ab/A)
. It is automatic that
(x,y)\in\mu(a,b)
, since
x\in\mu(a)
and
y\in\mu(b)
. So by Lemma
4.5
(2), it suffices to show that
(x,y)
belongs to some
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
. Now by Lemma
3.10
, there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximations
X
of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
and
Y
of
\operatorname{tp}(b/A)
at
b
, so that
X\times Y
is a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
. Since
x\in\mu(a/A)
and
y\in\mu(b/A)
, we have
x\in X
and
y\in Y
, thus
(x,y)\in X\times Y
as desired.

Now assume
\mu(ab/A)=\mu(a/A)\times\mu(b/A)
. Then

\dim(\mu(ab/A))=\dim(\mu(a/A))+\dim(\mu(b/A)),

so
\dim(ab/A)=\dim(a/A)+\dim(b/A)
, and thus
a
and
b
are symmetrically independent over
A
.

∎

Now we turn to generic continuity:

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
a\in M^{m}
,
b\in M^{n}
, and
A
a parameter set so that
(a,b)
is additive over
A
. Then:

(1)

The projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
is open and surjective.

(2)

If
a\in\operatorname{acl}(Ab)
, then the projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
is a homeomorphism.

(3)

In particular, if
a
and
b
are interalgebraic over
A
, then
\mu(ab/A)
is the graph of a homeomorphism
\mu(a/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
\pi
be the projection sending
(a,b)
to
b
. By Lemma
3.15
, there are
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximations
X
of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
, and
Y
of
\operatorname{tp}(b/A)
at
b
, so that
\pi
induces an open surjective map
X\rightarrow Y
. By Lemma
4.6
,
\mu(ab/A)
and
\mu(b/A)
are open in
X
and
Y
, respectively, which implies that the projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
is also open.

To show
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
is surjective, let
y\in\mu(b/A)
. By compactness, it suffices to show that for any
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximation
Z
of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
, we have
y\in\pi(Z)
. But by Lemma
3.14
,
\pi(Z)
contains an
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(b/A)
at
b
; thus
\pi(Z)
contains
\mu(b/A)
, and thus
y\in\pi(Z)
.

(2)

Repeat the proof of (1), but starting with d-approximations
X
and
Y
so that
X\rightarrow Y
is moreover injective (as provided by Lemma
3.16
).

(3)

Apply (2) to both
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(a/A)
and
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
.

∎

4.3.
The Fiber Germ Equation

We now give a rather surprising consequence of independent neighborhoods. Namely, we prove a fairly strong additivity-like statement that holds even if full additivity fails. This will be extremely useful throughout the rest of the paper.

Recall that our main uses of additive pairs will ultimately stem from Lemma
3.13
. Roughly, that lemma said that independence is transitive for additive pairs: if
(a,b)
is additive over
A
and independent from
B
over
A
, then all dimension computations in the additive decomposition of
\dim(ab/A)
remain unchanged over
B
.

The result of this subsection, then, is that one of the clauses of Lemma
3.13
(namely Lemma
3.13
(2)) does not require additivity at all. This seems to be a unique and pleasant feature of the topological setting (compared to other sub-additivite dimension theories).

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set.

(1)

\mu(ab/A)_{b}=\mu(a/Ab)
. That is, the fiber above
b
of the projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
is precisely
\mu(a/Ab)
.

(2)

\operatorname{germ}(ab/A)_{b}=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
– that is, the fiber germ of
\operatorname{germ}(ab/A)
above
b
is
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
.

(3)

If
B\supset A
and
\dim(ab/B)=\dim(ab/A)
, then
\dim(a/Bb)=\dim(a/Ab)
.

Proof.

(1) follows from Lemma
4.5
(3): we have
x\in\mu(a/Ab)
if and only if
x\in\mu(a)
and
x\models\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
; and this is equivalent to the assertion that
(x,b)\in\mu(a,b)
and
(x,b)\models\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
.

(2) now follows from (1) by applying Lemma
4.6
(2) to both sides of the equation. That is, by Lemma
4.6
(2), we can rewrite the desired statement as
\operatorname{germ}_{a}(\mu(ab/A)_{b})=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(\mu(a/Ab))
. But (1) gives that
\mu(ab/A)_{b}=\mu(a/Ab)
, so of course the two sets have the same germ at
a
.

Now for (3), assume
B\supset A
and
\dim(ab/B)=\dim(ab/A)
. By Lemma
3.7
,
\operatorname{germ}(ab/B)=\operatorname{germ}(ab/A)
. Now applying (2) gives

\operatorname{germ}(a/Bb)=(\operatorname{germ}(ab/B))_{b}=(\operatorname{germ}(ab/A))_{b}=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab).

Then by Lemma
3.7
again,
\dim(a/Bb)=\dim(a/Ab)
.
∎

In fact, Lemma
4.10
implies:

Corollary 4.11
.

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set. If
(x,y)\in\mu(ab/A)
, then the fiber
\mu(ab/A)_{y}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(x/Ay)
.

Proof.

Let
X
be an
A
-definable d-approxiation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
. By Lemma
4.10
(2), the fiber
X_{b}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
. Now if
(x,y)\in\mu(ab/A)
, then
\operatorname{tp}(xy/A)=\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
. So
X_{y}
must realize
\operatorname{germ}(x/Ay)
. Since
\mu(ab/A)
is open in
X
, the same germ is also realizeed by
\mu(ab/A)_{y}
.
∎

4.4.
Infinitesimal Neighborhoods as Complete Types

We end this section with another interesting fact about infinitesimal neighborhoods. Namely, in the case that
\mathcal{M}
is saturated in its own cardinality, Lemma
4.5
(in combination with Lemma
2.20
) implies that every infinitesimal neighborhood is, in fact, the locus of a complete type. We will not explicitly use this fact, as we might not have access to saturated models. Nevertheless, this seems interesting in its own right, so we include a proof sketch. Note that if we had a saturated model, some of our arguments could be expressed a bit more elegantly using this fact.

Lemma 4.12
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
is saturated. Let
a\in M^{n}
and
A
a parameter set. Then there is a set
B\subset M^{eq}
such that
A\subset B
,
\dim(a/B)=\dim(a/A)
, and
\mu(a/A)
is precisely the set of realizations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
(note that
B
will be small in the sense of
\mathcal{N}
, but not in the sense of
\mathcal{M}
).

Proof.

Let
\{X_{\alpha}:\alpha<\kappa\}
enumerate all
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
. We may assume that
\kappa
is a cardinal and
\kappa\leq|M|
. We will inductively build
b_{\alpha}
and
Y_{\alpha}
, for
\alpha<\kappa
, such that:

(1)

b_{\alpha}
is a finite tuple from
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

Y_{\alpha}\subset X_{\alpha}
is an
Ab_{\alpha}
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
.

(3)

\dim(a/A_{\alpha}b_{\alpha})=\dim(a/A_{\alpha})
, where
A_{\alpha}=A\cup\{b_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\}
.

Indeed, to build
b_{\alpha}
and
Y_{\alpha}
, one just needs to apply Lemma
2.20
to
X_{\alpha}
with parameter set
A_{\alpha}
. The key point making this work is that
|A_{\alpha}|<|M|
for all
\alpha
, hence (as
\mathcal{M}
is saturated) such a
b_{\alpha}
can be found inside
\mathcal{M}
.

Now suppose we have built
b_{\alpha}
and
Y_{\alpha}
for al
\alpha
. Let
B=A_{\kappa}
be
A
together with all
b_{\alpha}
for
\alpha<\kappa
– equivalently, the union of all
A_{\alpha}
for
\alpha<\kappa
. It follows by induction, that
\dim(a/A_{\alpha})=\dim(a/A)
for all
\alpha\leq\kappa
(for the limit step, use that any drop in dimension is witnessed by a formula with finitely many parameters). In particular,
\dim(a/B)=\dim(a/A)
.

Now we show that
\mu(a/A)
is the set of realizations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
. First assume
a^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
. Then for each
\alpha<\kappa
we have
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}/Ab_{\alpha})=\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab_{\alpha})
, thus
a^{\prime}\in Y_{\alpha}\subset X_{\alpha}
. So
a^{\prime}
belongs to all
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
, and thus
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/A)
.

Conversely, suppose
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/A)
. If
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}/B)\neq\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
, then there is
\alpha<\kappa
so that
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}/A_{\alpha})\neq\operatorname{tp}(a/A_{\alpha})
. But
A_{\alpha}
is a small set in
\mathcal{M}^{eq}
, and
\dim(a/A_{\alpha})=\dim(a/A)
; so we have
\mu(a/A_{\alpha})=\mu(a/A)
. Thus
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/A_{\alpha})
, and thus
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}/A_{\alpha})=\operatorname{tp}(a/A_{\alpha})
, a contradiction.
∎

5.
Topological 1-basedness

Throughout this section, we continue to fix a sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure
\mathcal{M}
with the independent neighborhood property, as well as a highly saturated elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
where we treat infinitesimal neighborhoods of elements of
\mathcal{M}
.

The purpose of this section is to introduce our notion of topological 1-basedness and study its basic properties. The first two subsections give some motivation and equivalent definitions. The next three subsections show that topological 1-basedness is preserved under topological reducts and expansions by constants, and that (similarly to strongly minimal theories) if exchange holds then topological 1-basedness can be checked at the level of 2-types. Finally, the last three subsections give examples of topologically 1-based and non-1-based structures.

5.1.
The Definition

We begin by defining topological 1-basedness.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
a\in M^{m}
,
b\in M^{n}
, and
A
a parameter set. We say that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is
topologically 1-based over
A
if
\dim(b/Aa)=\dim(b/A\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab))
. If
A=\emptyset
, we simply say that
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based.

Definition 5.2
.

We say that
\mathcal{M}
is
topologically 1-based
if
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is 1-based (over
\emptyset
) for all real tuples
a
and
b
.

Remark 5.3
.

Letting
g:=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
, an equivalent statement in Definition
5.1
would be that
\dim(b/Aag)=\dim(b/Aa)
, i.e.
b
is independent from
g
over
Aa
. This is equivalent because
a\in\operatorname{dcl}(g)
, so
\dim(b/Aag)=\dim(b/Ag)
is automatic.

Remark 5.4
.

If
\mathcal{M}
satisfies exchange, Definition
5.1
makes sense even if
b
is an imaginary tuple (that is, in that case the values
\dim(b/Ag)
and
\dim(b/Aa)
are well-defined). Without exchange, dimension theory only works for real tuples, and thus we must assume
b
is a real tuple. Note that if
\mathcal{M}
satisfies exchange, one could also allow imaginary
b
in Definition
5.2
, and this would result in the same notion of topological 1-basedness (this is essentially Lemma
5.5
).

Let us motivate the above definitions. As we stated in the introduction, we intend to treat germs as the topological analog of canonical bases. In stability, one would say
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is 1-based if
\operatorname{Cb}(\operatorname{tp}(a/\operatorname{acl}(b)))\in\operatorname{acl}(a)
. In our case, there is no need to pass from
b
to
\operatorname{acl}(b)
, as Lemma
3.7
gives that
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
and
\operatorname{tp}(a/\operatorname{acl}(b))
have the same germ at
a
. Thus, the most obvious translation of the definition from stability would simply be that
\operatorname{germ}(a/b)\in\operatorname{acl}(a)
.

Our chosen formulation is a bit more general. Indeed, let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b)
. Notice, then, that if
g\in\operatorname{acl}(a)
, then
\dim(b/g)=\dim(b/a)
, which is exactly our definition. On the other hand, the latter property is all we will need, so we have chosen to stick with it. In fact, we do not know whether the two potential definitions are equivalent.

5.2.
Equivalent Formulations

Recall that if
\mathcal{M}
satisfies exchange, one can extend the dimension theory to imaginary sorts (see
[
6
, §3]
) – with the caveat that there can be infinite zero-dimensional interpretable sets. For example, if
\mathcal{M}\models\mathrm{ACVF}
, then the set of all balls of a given radius is 0-dimensional (the intuition is that the data of a ball is ‘locally constant’, so the set of balls is topologically ‘small’). In this light, the following should help clarify our definition:

Lemma 5.5
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
satisfies exchange.

(1)

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples,
A
a parameter set, and
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
. Then
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
if and only if
\dim(g/Aa)=0
.

(2)

\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based if and only if for all real tuples
a
and parameter sets
A
, we have
\dim(\operatorname{germ}(a/A)/a)=0
.

Proof.

(1)

For ease of notation, assume
A=\emptyset
. Since
a\in\operatorname{dcl}(g)
, we have
\dim(b/g)=\dim(b/ga)
. Moreover, since
g\in\operatorname{dcl}(ab)
, we have
\dim(ab)=\dim(abg)
. Now combining with additivity, we compute:

\dim(a)+\dim(b/a)=\dim(ab)=\dim(abg)

=\dim(a)+\dim(g/a)+\dim(b/ga)=\dim(a)+\dim(g/a)+\dim(b/g).

Rearranging now gives

\dim(b/a)=\dim(g/a)+\dim(b/g).

In particular,
\dim(g/a)=0
holds if and only if
\dim(b/a)=\dim(b/g)
, as desired.

(2)

The right to left direction is trivial by (1); we show left to right. So assume
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based. Let
a
be a real tuple,
A
be a parameter set, and
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
. Let
c
be a finite tuple from
A
so that
\dim(a/c)=\dim(a/A)
, and thus
\operatorname{germ}(a/c)=g
. Then let
b
be a real tuple so that
c\in\operatorname{dcl}(b)
. We may choose
b
to be independent from
a
over
c
; thus
\operatorname{germ}(a/b)=g
as well. Then since
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, and by (1), we get that

\dim(\operatorname{germ}(a/b)/a)=\dim(g/a)=0,

which proves (2).

∎

Returning to the general case, one should think of topologically 1-based types as ‘locally linear’ – namely,
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based if in some neighborhood of
b
, any conjugate
b^{\prime}\equiv_{a}b
satisfies
\operatorname{germ}(a/b)=\operatorname{germ}(a/b^{\prime})
. In some sense, this is the most natural way to adapt the stability-theoretic definition using canonical bases to the topological setting. Namely, in a stable theory, the asserion that
\operatorname{Cb}(\operatorname{stp}(a/b))\in\operatorname{acl}(a)
really says that
\operatorname{Cb}(\operatorname{stp}(a/b^{\prime}))
is constant on a ‘big’ set of realizations
b^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(b/a)
(here ‘big’ means non-forking over
a
). In the topological setting, we make exactly the same statement but interpret ‘big’ as ‘on a neighborhood of
b
in
\operatorname{tp}(b/a)
’ (see Lemma
5.6
below). In general, passing from ‘
\operatorname{acl}^{eq}
’ to ‘constant on a neighborhood’ seems to be the correct philosophy in finding topological analogs of stability-theoretic phenomena.

Lemma
5.6
makes the above discussion precise:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
.

(2)

The map
y\mapsto\operatorname{germ}(a/Ay)
is constant for
y\in\mu(b/Aa)
.

Proof.

We may assume
A=\emptyset
.
Let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b)
. First assume
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based, and let
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/a)
. We show that
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b^{\prime})
. Indeed, by 1-basedness,
\dim(b/a)=\dim(b/ga)
, thus
\mu(b/a)=\mu(b/ga)
. So
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/ga)
, and thus
b^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(b/ga)
. So
\operatorname{tp}(abg)=\operatorname{tp}(ab^{\prime}g)
, and since
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b)
, we conclude that also
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b^{\prime})
.

Conversely, suppose
y\mapsto\operatorname{germ}(a/y)
is constant on
\mu(b/a)
. Then for any
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/a)
we have
\operatorname{tp}(abg)=\operatorname{tp}(ab^{\prime}g)
. Now since
\dim(\mu(b/a))=\dim(b/a)
, there is an element
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/a)
with
\dim(b^{\prime}/abg)=\dim(b/a)=\dim(b^{\prime}/a)
. It follows that
\dim(b^{\prime}/ag)=\dim(b^{\prime}/g)=\dim(b^{\prime}/a)
as well, and since
\operatorname{tp}(abg)=\operatorname{tp}(ab^{\prime}g)
, this implies
\dim(b/g)=\dim(b/a)
.
∎

5.3.
Expansion by Constants

Recall that the class of locally modular strongly minimal theories is preserved under naming constants and taking reducts (as is the analogous class of ‘linear’ o-minimal theories). In the next two subsections, we prove the same for topological 1-basedness.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
C
be a paraneter set, and let
\mathcal{M}_{C}
be
\mathcal{M}
expanded by naming the elements of
C
. Then
\mathcal{M}_{C}
is topologically 1-based if and only if
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based.

Proof.

Before starting the proof, we should note that
\mathcal{M}_{C}
is also t-minimal with the same topology. Moreover,
\mathcal{M}_{C}
also has independent neighborhoods. Indeed, given any
a
and
A
, the independent neighborhood property for
\mathcal{M}
gives us arbitrarily small neighborhoods
U
of
a
defined over parameters
t
with
\dim(a/CAt)=\dim(a/CA)
; and this is equivalent to the independent neighborhood property for
\mathcal{M}_{C}
. Thus, for the rest of the proof, we can use all of the results collected to this point on independent neighborhoods.

We now proceed with the proof. First suppose
\mathcal{M}_{C}
is topologically 1-based. To show
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, let
a
and
b
be real tuples. We wish to show that
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based. As this only depends on
\operatorname{tp}(ab)
, we may replace
(a,b)
with any other realization of the same type. In particular, we may assume that
(a,b)
is independent from
C
, i.e.
\dim(ab/C)=\dim(ab)
. By Lemma
4.10
, this implies that
\dim(a/Cb)=\dim(a/b)
and
\dim(b/Ca)=\dim(b/a)
.

Now let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b)
. Since
\dim(a/Cb)=\dim(a/b)
, we also have
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Cb)
. Then the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{C}
is topologically 1-based gives
\dim(b/Cg)=\dim(b/Ca)
. But now we compute:

\dim(b/g)\geq\dim(b/Cg)=\dim(b/Ca)=\dim(b/a),

and this implies that
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based.

Now assume
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based; we show that
\mathcal{M}_{C}
is topologically 1-based. To do this, fix real tuples
a
and
b
, and set
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Cb)
. We want to show that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Cb)
is topologically 1-based over
C
– i.e. that
\dim(b/Cg)=\dim(b/Ca)
.

Let
c
be a finite tuple from
C
such that
\dim(a/Cb)=\dim(a/cb)
, and
\dim(b/Cg)=\dim(b/cg)
. Then let
d
be a real tuple such that
c\in\operatorname{dcl}(d)
. Replacing
d
with an independent realization of
\operatorname{tp}(d/c)
, we may assume that
ab
and
d
are symmetrically independent over
c
(for example, this is, essentially,
[
11
, Corollary 2.16]
).

Let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Cb)
. By the choice of
c
we also have
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/cb)
. Now by the choice of
d
we have
\dim(ab/d)=\dim(ab/c)
, and thus Lemma
4.10
(2) gives
\operatorname{germ}(a/bd)=\operatorname{germ}(a/bc)=g
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, it follows that
\dim(bd/g)=\dim(bd/a)
. By Lemma
4.10
(3), this implies
\dim(b/dg)=\dim(b/da)
.

Next, recall that
\dim(ab/d)=\dim(ab/c)
. By Lemma
4.10
(3) again, this implies
\dim(b/da)=\dim(b/ca)
. Then, combining with the previous paragraph, we have:

\dim(b/Cg)=\dim(b/cg)\geq\dim(b/dg)

=\dim(b/da)=\dim(b/ca)\geq\dim(b/Ca),

which implies that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Cb)
is topologiclly 1-based over
C
.
∎

5.4.
Reducts

Now we show that topological 1-basedness is preserved under reducts in an appropriate sense. There is a subtlety here in that one needs to preserve the topology and independent neighborhoods for this to even make sense. Thus, we define:

Definition 5.8
.

An
admissible reduct of
\mathcal{M}
is a structure
\mathcal{M}^{-}
such that:

(1)

The universe of
\mathcal{M}^{-}
is
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

Every (parametrically) definable set in
\mathcal{M}^{-}
is also parametrically definable in
\mathcal{M}
.

(3)

With respect to the same topology
\tau
,
\mathcal{M}^{-}
is also t-minimal with the independent neighborhood property.

In all known examples of t-minimal structures with independent neighborhoods, the independent neighborhoods are witnessed by a fixed definable basis. This means there is a
\emptyset
-definable basis
\{U_{t}\}
such that for all
a\in M^{n}
and
A
, there are arbitrarily small
U_{t_{1}}\times\dots\times U_{t_{n}}
containing
a
and satisfying
\dim(a/At_{1}\dots t_{n})=\dim(a/A)
. In this case, any reduct of
\mathcal{M}
containing the same family
\{U_{t}\}
is admissible. In contrast, we do not know whether any reduct defining the same topology is admissible.

Now we show:

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
\mathcal{M}^{-}
be an admissible reduct of
\mathcal{M}
. If
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, then
\mathcal{M}^{-}
is topologically 1-based.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.7
, we may assume all basic relations of
\mathcal{M}^{-}
are definable without parameters in
\mathcal{M}
. Now we begin with:

Claim 5.10
.

If
X\subset M^{n}
is
\mathcal{M}^{-}
-definable, then
\dim(X)
is the same in
\mathcal{M}^{-}
and
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Working first in
\mathcal{M}^{-}
, let
\{X_{i}\}
be a weak cell-decomposition of
X
(in the sense of
[
11
, Proposition 2.50]
). So each
X_{i}
is a weak
k_{i}
-cell for some
k_{i}
. Then
X_{i}
is still a weak
k_{i}
-cell in the sense of
\mathcal{M}
, and it follows that in both structures
\dim(X)
is equal to the largest
k_{i}
.
∎

The claim also implies the analogous statement for germs: the dimension of a definable germ in
\mathcal{M}^{-}
remains the same when viewed as a definable germ in
\mathcal{M}
(on the other hand, the data of which germ is
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
for fixed
a
and
A
may change).

Now toward a proof of the lemma, fix real tuples
a
and
b
. We want to show that
\operatorname{tp}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based. We can freely replace
(a,b)
by any other realization of
\operatorname{tp}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(ab)
. In particular, by a compactness argument (using the above claim), one finds a realization such that
\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(ab)=\dim_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(ab)
. Now let
c
be a basis of
(a,b)
in the sense of
\mathcal{M}^{-}
; then one still has
(a,b)\in\operatorname{acl}_{\mathcal{M}}(c)
, and it follows that
c
is still a basis in the sense of
\mathcal{M}
. In particular, if
X
is an
(a,b)
-witness for
c
in the sense of
\mathcal{M}^{-}
, then
X
is also an
(a,b)
-witness for
c
in the sense of
\mathcal{M}
. It follows that
\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(a/b)
and
\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}}(a/b)
have a common realization, and thus are equal. Call this common germ
g
. By Lemma
4.10
(1),
\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(b/a)
and
\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}}(b/a)
are both the fiber germ
g_{a}
, and thus

\dim_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(b/a)=\dim(\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(b/a))=\dim(\operatorname{germ}_{\mathcal{M}}(b/a))=\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/a).

Now since
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, we have
\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/g)=\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/a)
. If
\operatorname{tp}_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(a/b)
were not topologically 1-based, then we would have

\dim_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(b/g)<\dim_{\mathcal{M}^{-}}(b/a)=\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/a)=\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/g).

So there is some
g
-definable set in
\mathcal{M}^{-}
which contains
b
and has dimension less than
\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/g)
. But this assertion is still valid in
\mathcal{M}
, so one obtains
\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/g)<\dim_{\mathcal{M}}(b/g)
, a contradiction.
∎

5.5.
Reduction to Plane Curves

A fundamental result in the theory of strongly minimal sets says that if such a set is locally modular, it is also 1-based; equivalently, non-1-basedness can always be witnessed in the plane. A similar result was proven for weakly 1-based geometric theories in
[
1
, Proposition 2.15]
. We do not know whether the analogous statement holds of topological 1-basedness in complete generality – however it does hold assuming exchange (essentially because of the result of
[
1
]
). Here we give a short topological proof.

Lemma 5.11
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
has exchange and is not topologically 1-based. Then there are
a,b\in M
and a real tuple
t
so that
\dim(a/t)=\dim(b/t)=\dim(ab/t)=1
and
\operatorname{tp}(ab/t)
is not topologically 1-based over
\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let
a\in M^{n}
and
t\in M^{m}
so that
\operatorname{tp}(a/t)
is not topologically 1-based over
\emptyset
, and so that the value of
n
is minimized among all such pairs. Note that
n\geq 2
(as one can easily check that all 1-types are topologically 1-based).

Let
d=\dim(a/t)
, and write
a=(e_{1},\dots,e_{d},f_{1},\dots,f_{n-d})
where
e:=(e_{1},\dots,e_{d})
is a basis for
a
over
t
. Clearly
n>d
, since otherwise
\operatorname{tp}(a/t)
would be topologically 1-based.

By Lemma
4.9
,
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/t)
is realized by the graph of a function
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(f_{1})\times\dots\times\mu(f_{n-d})
. This function is determined by its coordinate components
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(f_{i})
, and each of these components realizes
g_{i}:=\operatorname{germ}(ef_{i}/t)
. In other words,
g\in\operatorname{dcl}(g_{1},\dots,g_{n-d})
. Now by assumption (and Lemma
5.5
) we have
\dim(g/a)>0
, and thus some
\dim(g_{i}/a)>0
. Then
\operatorname{tp}(e_{1},\dots,e_{d},f_{i}/t)
is not topologically 1-based over
\emptyset
. By the minimality of
n
, we must have
a=(e_{1},\dots,e_{d},f_{i})
, so that
d=n-1
.

Now assume
n\geq 3
. Let
t^{\prime}\in\mu(t/a)
be independent from
t
over
a
. A short computation (using that
d=n-1
and
n\geq 3
) yields that
a\notin\operatorname{acl}(tt^{\prime})
. So we can write
a=(b,c)
where
b\in M
,
c\in M^{n-1}
, and
\dim(b/tt^{\prime})=1
.

Let
X
be a
\emptyset
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(cbt)
at
cbt
. By Lemma
4.10
, the fiber
X_{bt}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(c/bt)
. Since
\operatorname{tp}(cbt)=\operatorname{tp}(cbt^{\prime})
,
X_{bt^{\prime}}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(c/bt^{\prime})
.

Now by the minimality of
n
,
\operatorname{tp}(c/bt)
is topologically 1-based, so by Lemma
5.6
,
X_{bt}
and
X_{bt^{\prime}}
agree on some neighborhood
U
of
c
. By the independent neighborhood property, we may assume
U
is definable over a tuple
u
with
\dim(cbtt^{\prime}/u)=\dim(cbtt^{\prime})
. By additivity of
(c,btt^{\prime})
over
\emptyset
(i.e. Lemma
3.13
),
\dim(btt^{\prime}/u)=\dim(btt^{\prime})
, and thus
\dim(b/tt^{\prime}u)=\dim(b/tt^{\prime})=1
. Thus, there is a neighborhood
V
of
b
such that the fibers
X_{vt}
and
X_{vt^{\prime}}
agree on
U
for all
v\in V
. This means the fibers
X_{t}
and
X_{t}^{\prime}
agree on
U\times V
, so that
\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{t})=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{t^{\prime}})
.

Now similarly to above, since
X
is a
\emptyset
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(at)
at
at
, we have that
X_{t}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/t)
, and thus
X_{t^{\prime}}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/t^{\prime})
. It follows that
\operatorname{germ}(a/t)=\operatorname{germ}(a/t^{\prime})=g
, say. But then
g\in\operatorname{dcl}(at^{\prime})
, which gives

\dim(t/g)\geq\dim(t/at^{\prime})=\dim(t/a).

So
\operatorname{tp}(a/t)
is topologically 1-based afterall, a contradiction.

So we have
n=2
and
d=n-1=1
. Write
a=(a_{1},a_{2})
. If either
\dim(a_{i}/t)=0
then
\operatorname{tp}(a/t)
is easily seen to be topologically 1-based. Thus we have
\dim(a_{1}/t)=\dim(a_{1}a_{2}/t)=\dim(a_{2}/t)=1
, as desired.
∎

Remark 5.12
.

The proof above actually shows a bit more, in the sense that the statement can be localized. Namely, let
X\subset M
be type-definable over
A
, and suppose exchange holds for any
X
-tuples over
A
. Moreover, suppose there are
X
-tuples
a
and
t
with
\operatorname{tp}(a/t)
not topologically 1-based over
A
. Then one can find such tuples
a
and
t
so that
a\in X^{2}
(and so that the other dimension requirements of the lemma are satisfied). The reason this works is that the extra parameters we had to add to
t
during the proof came from the original tuple
a
(so we never introduced anything outside
X
; similarly,
t^{\prime}\in X
as well, though this is not needed).

5.6.
Locally Linear Groups

In the remainder of this section, we give examples of topologically 1-based and non-topologically-1-based structures, with the hope of convincing the reader that we have isolated the correct dividing line.

Recall that a 1-based group in stability theory has only group-like definable sets: namely, every definable set is either a subgroup, a coset of a subgroup, or a finite Boolean combination of those. In the topological setting, the analogous notion is
local linearity
:

Definition 5.13
.

Assume
(M,\cdot,\dots)
is an expansion of a topological group (with the given topology
\tau
). We say
\mathcal{M}
is
locally linear
if for all
a\in M^{m}
and
b\in M^{n}
, there is a definable subgroup
H\leq M^{m}
such that
\operatorname{germ}(a/b)=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(a\cdot H)
.

The following are all examples of locally linear t-minimal topological groups with independent neighborhoods:

•

Ordered vector spaces over any ordered division ring.

•

The reduct
(K,+,v)
whenever
(K,+,\cdot,v)\models ACVF
. One can make this more general by taking valued vector spaces (in a certain language) over any fixed valued field, but we omit the details as they will not be needed.

•

If
K
is a model of ACVF or RCVF, and
O
is the valuation ring, take
K/O
with its full induced structure (see
[
7
, Corollary 3.12, §6.3]
).

Now we show:

Lemma 5.14
.

Assume (in addition to our standing assumption of t-minimality and independent neighborhods) that
\mathcal{M}=(M,\cdot,\dots)
is an expansion of a topological group, and is locally linear. Then
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based.

Proof.

We use Lemma
5.6
. Namely, let
a\in M^{m}
and
b\in M^{n}
, and let
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/a)
– so by Lemma
4.10
,
(a,b^{\prime})\in\mu(ab/\emptyset)
. We will show that
\operatorname{germ}(a/b)=\operatorname{germ}(a/b^{\prime})
.

By local linearity, there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable subgroup
H\leq M^{m+n}
so that
\operatorname{germ}(ab/\emptyset)=\operatorname{germ}_{(a,b)}((a,b)\cdot H)
. By Lemmas
3.4
and
2.20
, there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximation
X
of
\operatorname{tp}(ab)
at
(a,b)
such that:

(1)

X
is an open subset of
(a,b)\cdot H
, and

(2)

X
is
t
-definable for some
t
with
\dim(ab/t)=\dim(ab/\emptyset)
.

Note that (2) implies
\mu(ab/t)=\mu(ab/\emptyset)
– thus
(a,b^{\prime})\in\mu(ab/t)
, and thus
\operatorname{tp}(abt)=\operatorname{tp}(ab^{\prime}t)
. It follows that
X
is also a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab^{\prime})
at
(a,b^{\prime})
.

Now let
X_{b}
and
X_{b^{\prime}}
be the fibers in
X
above
b
and
b^{\prime}
– so
a\in X_{b}\cap X_{b^{\prime}}
. Since
X
is open in
(a,b)\cdot H
,
X_{b}
is open in the corresponding fiber
((a,b)\cdot H)_{b}
– and likewise for
b^{\prime}
. But since
H
is a subgroup, the two fibers
((a,b)\cdot H)_{b}
and
((a,b)\cdot H)_{b^{\prime}})
are equal – namely, since they both contain
a
, they are both equal to
a\cdot N
where
N\leq M^{m}
is the kernel of the projection
H\rightarrow M^{n}
. It follows that
\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{b})=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{b^{\prime}})
.

Finally, by Lemma
4.7
we now have

\operatorname{germ}(a/b)=\operatorname{germ}(ab/\emptyset)_{b}=(\operatorname{germ}_{(a,b)}X)_{b}=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{b})

=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{b^{\prime}})=(\operatorname{germ}_{(a,b^{\prime})}X)_{b^{\prime}}=\operatorname{germ}(ab^{\prime}/\emptyset)_{b^{\prime}}=\operatorname{germ}(a/b^{\prime}),

which completes the proof.
∎

In fact, later on we will show that local linearity is equivalent to topological 1-basedness in the case of topological groups (see Theorem
11.10
). This is an analog of the Hrushovsk-Pillay classification of 1-based groups in stability theory (
[
10
]
). We postpone the proof of the full result as it requires more machinery than we have available at this point.

5.7.
1-basedness and Fields

On the opposite end of the previous lemma, let us now check that no infinite field is topologically 1-based:

Lemma 5.15
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
defines an infinite field – that is, assume there is an infinite definable
F\subset M^{n}
for some
n
with a definable field structure. Then
\mathcal{M}
is not topologically 1-based.

Proof.

Suppose
F\subset M^{n}
is an infinite definable field. By Lemma
5.7
, we may assume
F
(with its operations) is
\emptyset
-definable. Let
d=\dim(F)>0
. Let
P=L=F^{2}
, interpreted as the sets of points and (non-vertical) lines in the plane, and let
I\subset X\times T
be the incidence relation. Then one easily checks the following:

(1)

\dim(P)=\dim(L)=2d
and
\dim(I)=3d
.

(2)

If
(p,l)\in I
with
\dim(pl)=3d
then
\dim(p)=\dim(l)=2d
and
\dim(p/l)=\dim(l/p)=d
.

Now fix
(p,l)\in I
as above, so
\dim(pl)=3d
. Let
g=\operatorname{germ}(p/l)
. If
\mathcal{M}
were topologically 1-based, we would have

\dim(l/gp)=\dim(l/p)=d>0.

So there is
l^{\prime}\neq l
with
\operatorname{tp}(plg)=\operatorname{tp}(pl^{\prime}g)
, and thus
g=\operatorname{germ}(p/l^{\prime})
. It follows that
\operatorname{tp}(p/l)
and
\operatorname{tp}(p/l^{\prime})
have the same d-approximations at
x
, which gives that
\mu(p/l)=\mu(p/l^{\prime})
and
\dim(\mu(p/l))=d>0
. So there is
p^{\prime}\neq p
with
p^{\prime}\in\mu(p/l)\cap\mu(p/l^{\prime})
. Thus
\operatorname{tp}(pl)=\operatorname{tp}(p^{\prime}l)
and
\operatorname{tp}(pl^{\prime})=\operatorname{tp}(p^{\prime}l^{\prime})
. In particular,
p
and
p^{\prime}
are distinct intersection points of the lines
l
and
l^{\prime}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Remark 5.16
.

The above lemma fails if we replace ‘definable field’ with ‘interpretable field’. Indeed, for
K\models ACVF
, we already saw that
K/O
is locally linear, thus topologically 1-based; but it interprets a copy of the residue field
k
.

5.8.
The o-minimal Case

We conclude this section by pointing out that topological 1-basedness successfully captures the dividing line of the trichotomy for o-minimal structures.

Recall that every point in an o-minimal structure is either trivial, an element of a group interval with linear structure, or an element of a real closed field interval (see the main result of
[
18
]
). Call an o-minimal structure
linear
if only the first two cases occur. In
[
1
]
, Berenstein and Vassiliev generalize linearity in o-minimal structures to a general notion of
weakly 1-based
for geometric theories (including all t-minimal theories with exchange): a geometric theory is weakly 1-based if for all real tuples
a
and sets
B
, there is
a^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
so that
\dim(a/a^{\prime})=\dim(a/Ba^{\prime})=\dim(a/B)
.

Now return to our fixed t-minimal
\mathcal{M}
(and elementary extension
\mathcal{N}
) with independent neighborhoods. If
\mathcal{M}
has exchange, then
\mathcal{M}
is also geometric. As it turns out, in this case
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based in our sense if and only if it is weakly 1-based as a geometric structure. We will not fully prove this until the next section; for now, we give one implication in general, and the other implication only in the o-minimal case.

Lemma 5.17
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
has exchange. If
\mathcal{M}
is weakly 1-based, then
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based.

Proof.

We freely use the extension of dimension theory to imaginaries, given by (
[
6
]
). Fix real tuples
a
and
b
, let
d=\dim(a/b)
, and let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/b)
. By Lemma
5.5
, it suffices to show that
\dim(g/a)=0
.

Since
\mathcal{M}
is weakly 1-based, there is
a^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
with
\dim(a/a^{\prime})=\dim(a/ba^{\prime})=d
. Let
a^{\prime\prime}
be an independent realization of
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}/ab)
– i.e.
\operatorname{tp}(aba^{\prime})=\operatorname{tp}(aba^{\prime\prime})
and
\dim(a^{\prime\prime}/aba^{\prime})=d
. It follows easily that
\dim(a^{\prime\prime}/a)=\dim(a^{\prime\prime}/aa^{\prime})=d
, so that
a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}
are independent over
a
.

Now since
\dim(a/ba^{\prime})=\dim(a/b)=d
, we have
\operatorname{germ}(a/ba^{\prime})=g
. Then since
\dim(a/a^{\prime})=\dim(a/ba^{\prime})
, we also have
\operatorname{germ}(a/a^{\prime})=g
. Similarly,
\operatorname{germ}(a/a^{\prime\prime})=g
. It follows that
g\in\operatorname{dcl}(aa^{\prime})\cap\operatorname{dcl}(aa^{\prime\prime})
; and since
a^{\prime}
and
a^{\prime\prime}
are independent over
a
, it follows from additivity that
\dim(g/a)=0
as desired.
∎

Corollary 5.18
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
is o-minimal. Then
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based if and only if it is linear.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based, Lemma
5.15
gives that it does not define an infinite field, and thus is linear. If
\mathcal{M}
is linear, then by
[
1
, §3.2, Theorem 2.20]
it is weakly 1-based, so by Lemma
5.17
it is topologically 1-based.
∎

6.
The Structure Theorem for Topologically 1-based Types

In this section, we prove our main topological structure theorem for topologically 1-based types. Roughly speaking, we show that such types look like actual partitions of an ambient space, at least after zooming into an infinitesimal locus. This result and proof are inspired by arguments from Peterzil’s group construction in linear o-minimal theories
[
16
]
.

Before presenting the main result, we give two technical lemmas in the next two subsections.

6.1.
Additive Extensions

Our first lemma concerns taking independent extensions of types. Roughly, we want to know that we can always find independent realizations of a type without introducing new failures of additivity:

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set.

(1)

There is
c\models\operatorname{tp}(b/a)
such that
\dim(c/Aab)=\dim(b/Aa)
and
(c,ab)
is additive over
A
.

(2)

For any
c
as in (1), and any
B\supset A
with
\dim(abc/B)=\dim(abc/A)
, we have that
b
and
c
are symmetrically independent over
Ba
.

(3)

If
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
, then we can choose
c
as in (1) so that in addition
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ac)
.

Proof.

(1)

Let
e
be a basis for
b
over
Aa
. Then
e
is independent over
Aa
, so also over
A
, and thus
\dim(e/A)=\dim(e/Aa)=\dim(b/Aa)
. Note that this implies
\mu(e/Aa)=\mu(e/A)
, which we use below.

Next, note that
\dim(\mu(ae/A))=\dim(ae/A)=\dim(ab/A)
, and
\dim(\mu(e/A))=\dim(e/A)=\dim(b/Aa)
. So
X=\mu(ae/A)\times\mu(e/A)
is a type-definable set of dimension
\dim(ab/A)+\dim(b/Aa)
. By compactness, we can find elements of
X
of maximal dimension over
A
. Our main goal is to show that we can take the first coordinate of such an element to be
(a,e)
itself.

Claim 6.2
.

There is
f^{\prime}\in\mu(e/A)
such that
\dim(aef^{\prime}/A)\geq\dim(X)=\dim(ab/A)+\dim(b/Aa)
.

Proof.

Let
Y
be any d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(e/A)
at
e
. By compactness, it will suffice to find
y\in Y
so that
\dim(aey/A)\geqq\dim(X)
(noting that this last inequality is a type-definable condition in
y
).

By Lemma
3.5
, we may assume
Y
is definable over
At
where
\dim(ae/At)=\dim(ae/A)
, and thus
\mu(ae/At)=\mu(ae/A)
. Now since
Y
is a d-approximation, we have
\dim(Y)=\dim(e/A)=\dim(\mu(e/A))
. So
\dim(\mu(ae/A)\times Y)=\dim(X)
, and thus there is
(a^{\prime},e^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mu(ae/A)\times\mu(e/A)
with
\dim(a^{\prime}e^{\prime}y^{\prime}/A)\geq\dim(X)
. So
(a^{\prime},e^{\prime})\in\mu(ae/A)=\mu(ae/At)
, and thus
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}e^{\prime}t/A)=\operatorname{tp}(aet/A)
. So there is
y
with
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}e^{\prime}y^{\prime}t/A)=\operatorname{tp}(aeyt/A)
. Then
\operatorname{tp}(y/At)=\operatorname{tp}(y^{\prime}/At)
, implying
y\in Y
; and moreover
\dim(aey/A)=\dim(a^{\prime}e^{\prime}y^{\prime}/A)\geq\dim(X)
.
∎

Fix
f^{\prime}
satisfying the conclusion of the claim: i.e.,
f^{\prime}\in\mu(e/A)
and
\dim(aef^{\prime}/A)\geq\dim(X)
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is sufficiently saturated, there is
f
from
\mathcal{M}
so that
\operatorname{tp}(aef/A)=\operatorname{tp}(aef^{\prime}/A)
.

Now since
f^{\prime}\in\mu(e/A)=\mu(e/Aa)
, we have
\operatorname{tp}(ae/A)=\operatorname{tp}(af^{\prime}/A)=\operatorname{tp}(af/A)
. So there is
c
from
\mathcal{M}
so that
\operatorname{tp}(abe/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acf/A)
. Thus
c\models\operatorname{tp}(b/Aa)
. Moreover,
f
is also a basis for
c
over
Aa
; it follows that
bc
is interalgebraic with
ef
over
Aa
, so that

\dim(abc/A)=\dim(aef/A)=\dim(aef^{\prime}/A)\geq\dim(ab/A)+\dim(b/Aa)

=\dim(ab/A)+\dim(c/Aa)\geq\dim(ab/A)+\dim(c/Aab).

The reverse inequality is automatic by sub-additivity; thus, all inequalities above are equalities. We conclude that
(c,ab)
is additive over
A
, and that
\dim(c/Aab)=\dim(c/Aa)=\dim(b/Aa)
. Thus, we have now established (1).

(2)

Let
c
be as in (1), and let
B\supset A
so that
\dim(abc/B)=\dim(abc/A)
. By Lemma
4.10
(3), we get

\dim(c/Bab)=\dim(c/Aab)=\dim(b/Aa)=\dim(c/Aa)\geq\dim(c/Ba).

So
c
is independent from
b
over
Ba
.

On the other hand, we compute:

\dim(abc/B)=\dim(abc/A)=\dim(ab/A)+\dim(c/Aab)

=\dim(ac/A)+\dim(b/Aa)\geq\dim(ac/B)+\dim(b/Bac)

\geq\dim(abc/B).

So all inequalities above must be equalities, and this implies

\dim(b/Bac)=\dim(b/Aa)\geq\dim(b/Ba).

Thus
b
is also independent from
c
over
Ba
, which proves (2).

(3)

Now assume in addition that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
. We revisit the construction in (1) slightly to prove (3). First, choose
e
,
B
, and
f^{\prime}
as in the original construction. Now let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
, so by assumption
\dim(b/Aa)=\dim(b/Aga)
. It follows that
e
is also a basis for
b
over
Aga
, so that
\dim(e/Aa)=\dim(e/Aga)
, and thus
\mu(e/Aa)=\mu(e/Aga)
. Since
f^{\prime}\in\mu(e/Aa)
, we conclude that
f^{\prime}\in\mu(e/Aga)
, and thus
\operatorname{tp}(aeg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(af^{\prime}g/A)
.

Now we make two quick edits to the construction. Namely, we can first choose
f
to satisfy the stronger property that
\operatorname{tp}(aefg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(aef^{\prime}g/A)
, and thus
\operatorname{tp}(afg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(af^{\prime}g/A)=\operatorname{tp}(aeg/A)
. Then we can in turn choose
c
to satisfy the stronger property that
\operatorname{tp}(abeg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acfg/A)
(so in particular
\operatorname{tp}(abe/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acf/A)
as in (1)). Then the conclusions of (1) still follow; while we in addition have
\operatorname{tp}(abg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acg/A)
, thus
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ac)
. This proves (2).

∎

6.2.
Topological Characterization of Zero-Dimensionality

We also need one more lemma before the main argument. Recall that topologically 1-based types require a ‘low complexity germ’, in the sense that knowing the germ doesn’t lower the dimension of the parameter (and assuming exchange, this is equivalent to the germ having dimension zero). The following lemma gives a topological characterization of this phenomenon for general imaginaries. This result is likely well known, but we include a proof for completeness.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
a\in M^{n}
,
b\in\mathcal{M}^{eq}
, and
A
a parameter set. Assume that
b\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aa)
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\dim(a/Ab)=\dim(a/A)
.

(2)

There is an
A
-definable function
f:X\rightarrow Y
, where
X\subset M^{n}
and
Y\subset\mathcal{M}^{eq}
, which takes constant value
b
on a neighborhood of
a
.

Proof.

First, assume (1). Since
b\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aa)
, there are an
A
-definable function
f:X\rightarrow Y
, where
X\subset M^{n}
and
Y\subset\mathcal{M}^{eq}
, such that
f(a)=b
. Shrinking
X
if necessary, we may assume it is a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
. Now (1) implies that
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)=\operatorname{germ}(a/A)=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X)
. In particular, the
Ab
-definable condition ‘
f(x)=b
’ must hold on a neighborhood of
a
in
X
. This proves (2).

Now assume (2), and let
f:X\rightarrow Y
be the given function. Let
U
be an open set containing
a
such that
f
takes constant value
b
on
X\cap U
. By the independent neighborhood property, we may assume
U
is
t
-definable where
\dim(a/At)=\dim(a/A)
. But now
b
is
At
-definable, since
b
is the unique element of
f(X\cap U)
. Thus also
\dim(a/Ab)=\dim(a/A)
, which proves (1).
∎

6.3.
The Main Structure Result

Finally, we give the main result of this section:

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
a
and
b
be real tuples, and
A
a parameter set. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
.

(2)

Any two fibers in the projection
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
are either equal or disjoint.

Proof.

First assume
\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
is topologically 1-based. To prove (2), we chose tuples
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in\mu(ab/A)
. We want to show that also
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in\mu(ab/A)
. To show this, let
X
be an
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
; we show that
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in X
. By Lemma
2.20
, we may assume
X
is
t
-definable where
\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
.

Let
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
. Since
\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
, Lemma
4.10
(3) gives that also
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Abt)
. Now by Lemma
6.1
(3), there is
c\models\operatorname{tp}(b/Aat)
such that
\dim(c/Aabt)=\dim(b/Aat)
,
(c,ab)
is additive over
At
, and
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Act)
. Then
\operatorname{tp}(abgt/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acgt/A)
, since
g
is definable over
Aabt
and
Aact
by the same formula.

Since
\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
,
X
is also a d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/At)
at
(a,b)
. Then by Lemma
4.10
(2), the fiber
X_{b}
realizes
g=\operatorname{germ}(a/Abt)
. Since
\operatorname{tp}(abgt/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acgt/A)
,
X_{c}
also realizes
g
. So
X_{b}
and
X_{c}
agree on some neighborhood of
a
, say
U
. By Lemma
3.2
, we may assume
U
is
u
-definable where
\dim(abc/Atu)=\dim(abc/At)=\dim(abc/A)
. Note that by Lemma
6.1
(2) we know that
b
and
c
are symmetrically independent over
Aatu
; thus
\dim(b/Aactu)=\dim(b/Aatu)
.

Let
e
be a code of the set
X_{b}\cap U=X_{c}\cap U
. So
e\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aabtu)\cap\operatorname{dcl}(Aactu)
. Thus

\dim(b/Aatu)\geq\dim(b/Aaetu)\geq\dim(b/Aactu)=\dim(b/Aatu).

So equality holds throughout, and by Lemma
6.3
, there is an
Aatu
-definable function
f:Y\rightarrow Z
such that
b\in Y
and
f
takes constant value
e
in a neighborhood of
b
, say
V
. By Lemma
3.2
, we may assume
V
is
v
-definable where
\dim(abc/Atuv)=\dim(abc/Atu)=\dim(abc/At)
. Note, then, that
e\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aatuv)
, since it is the unique image of
f
on
Y\cap V
.

Now since
(c,ab)
is additive over
At
and
\dim(abc/Atuv)=\dim(abc/At)
, it follows by Lemma
3.13
that
\dim(ab/Atuv)=\dim(ab/At)=\dim(ab/A)
. Thus
\mu(ab/Atuv)=\mu(ab/A)
. In particular, we have

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime}),(a^{\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in\mu(ab/Atuv),

and thus each of these three pairs realizes
\operatorname{tp}(ab/Atuv)
. Let
e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}
be such that

\operatorname{tp}(abetuv/A)=\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}b^{\prime}e^{\prime}tuv/A)=\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}b^{\prime\prime}e^{\prime\prime}tuv/A).

Since
e\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aatuv)
(i.e. the
b
-coordinate is not needed to define
e
), it follows that
e^{\prime}=e^{\prime\prime}
. Thus
e^{\prime}
codes both
X_{b^{\prime}}\cap U
and
X_{b^{\prime\prime}}\cap U
, and thus
X_{b^{\prime}}\cap U=X_{b^{\prime\prime}}\cap U
. But since

\operatorname{tp}(abtuv/A)=\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime\prime}b^{\prime}tuv/A),

and
a\in X_{b}\cap U
, it follows that
a^{\prime\prime}\in X_{b^{\prime}}\cap U
(recall here that
X
and
U
are
Atu
-definable). Thus we also have
a^{\prime\prime}\in X_{b^{\prime\prime}}\cap U
, which shows that
(a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime})\in X
, as desired. We have now proved that (1) implies (2).

Now for the converse, assume that any two fibers of
\mu(ab/A)\rightarrow\mu(b/A)
are equal or disjoint. We show that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based. Since
\dim(\mu(b/Aa))=\dim(b/Aa)
, a compactness argument gives an element
c\in\mu(b/Aa)
with
\dim(c/Aab)\geq\dim(b/Aa)
. By Lemma
4.10
,
(a,c)\in\mu(ab/A)
. So by assumption, the fibers above
b
and
c
in
\mu(ab/A)
are equal.

Let
X
be a
\emptyset
-definable d-approximation of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)
at
(a,b)
. By Lemma
4.10
, the fiber
X_{b}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)
. Since
(a,c)\in\mu(ab/A)
, we have
\operatorname{tp}(ab/A)=\operatorname{tp}(ac/A)
, and thus the fiber
X_{c}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ac)
. But by Lemma
4.6
,
\mu(ab/A)
is open in
X
; thus the common fiber above
b
and
c
in
\mu(ab/A)
realizes both
\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{b})
and
\operatorname{germ}_{a}(X_{c})
. In particular,
\operatorname{germ}(a/Ab)=\operatorname{germ}(a/Ac)=g
, say. Then
\operatorname{tp}(abg/A)=\operatorname{tp}(acg/A)
, since
g
is definable over
Aab
and
Aac
by the same formula. Now since
g\in\operatorname{dcl}(Aab)
, we have

\dim(b/Aag)=\dim(c/Aag)\geq\dim(c/Aab)\geq\dim(b/Aa).

This implies that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based.
∎

We conclude, as promised earlier, that topological 1-basedness restricts exactly to the prior notion of weak 1-basedness for geometric theories:

Corollary 6.5
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
satisfies exchange. Then
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based if and only if it is weakly 1-based.

Proof.

We showed in Lemma
5.17
that weak 1-basedness implies topological 1-basedness. We now show the reverse implication. So assume
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based.

Recall that to show
\mathcal{M}
is weakly 1-based, we are given a real tuple
a
and a small set
B\subset M
. We need to find
a^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(a/B)
so that
\dim(a/a^{\prime})=\dim(a/Ba^{\prime})=\dim(a/B)
. By compactness (since
\mathcal{M}
is sufficiently saturated), it is enough to prove the result in the case where
B:=b
is a finite tuple.

First, since
\dim(\mu(a/b))=\dim(a/b)
, there is
c\in\mu(a/b)
with
\dim(c/ab)\geq\dim(a/b)
. In particular,
\operatorname{tp}(c/b)=\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
, and
\dim(c/ab)=\dim(a/b)
.

Similarly, there is
d\in\mu(b/a)
with
\dim(d/abc)=\dim(b/a)
. Then the fibers above
b
and
d
in
\mu(ab/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(b/\emptyset)
both contain
a
, and thus they are equal by Theorem
6.4
. In particular, since
(c,b)\in\mu(ab/\emptyset)
, it follows that
(c,d)\in\mu(ab/\emptyset)
as well. Thus
\operatorname{tp}(cd)=\operatorname{tp}(ab)
, and so
\dim(d/c)=\dim(b/a)=\dim(d/abc)
. It follows that
d
and
a
are independent over
c
, so that
\dim(a/c)=\dim(a/cd)
.

Now, since
(a,d)\in\mu(ab/\emptyset)
, we also have
\operatorname{tp}(ad)=\operatorname{tp}(ab)
, and thus

\dim(d/a)=\dim(b/a)=\dim(d/abc).

It follows that also
\dim(d/ab)=\dim(abc)
, so
d
and
c
are independent over
ab
. Thus also

\dim(c/abd)=\dim(c/ab)=\dim(a/b)=\dim(c/d).

It follows that
c
and
a
are independent over
d
. Thus we also have
\dim(a/cd)=\dim(a/d)
.

To recap, we have now shown that
\dim(a/c)=\dim(a/cd)=\dim(a/d)
. Now, since
(a,d)\in\mu(ab/\emptyset)
, we have
\operatorname{tp}(ab)=\operatorname{tp}(ad)
, and thus there is
a^{\prime}
so that
\operatorname{tp}(aba^{\prime})=\operatorname{tp}(adc)
. Since
\mathcal{M}
is sufficiently saturated, we may assume
a^{\prime}
is a tuple from
\mathcal{M}
. Then
\operatorname{tp}(a^{\prime}b)=\operatorname{tp}(cd)=\operatorname{tp}(ab)
, so that
a^{\prime}\models\operatorname{tp}(a/b)
– and moreover, since
\dim(a/c)=\dim(a/cd)=\dim(a/d)
, it follows that
\dim(a/a^{\prime})=\dim(a/a^{\prime}b)=\dim(a/b)
. This shows that
\mathcal{M}
is weakly 1-based.
∎

7.
Groupoid Spines

Later on we will show that under a suitable non-triviality assumption, any topologically 1-based t-minimal structure with the independent neighborhood property admits an infinite type-definable group. This amounts to a topological version of the group configuration theorem. Before giving the argument, we first separate its purely combinatorial content. Thus, the current section is independent from model theory and concerns only the recovery of a group (in fact, a groupoid) from a well-behaved family of bijections. The general idea is similar to
[
5
]
, but with details more suited to our needs. Then in the next section, we add type-definability requirements (but working in an arbitrary theory), before finally returning to our topologically 1-based setting in Sections 9-11.

7.1.
Basic Notions

The central object we will work with for now is:

Definition 7.1
.

A
groupoid spine
consists of:

•

A linear order
(I,<)
,

•

An
I
-indexed family of non-empty sets.
\mathcal{X}=\{X_{i}:i\in I\}
,

•

A non-empty relation
\emptyset\neq R\subset I^{2}
containing all pairs
(i,j)
with
i<j
, and

•

For all
(i,j)\in R
, a non-empty collection
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
of bijections
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}
(called
morphisms
),

such that the morphisms are relatively closed under the groupoid operations. That is:

(1)

If
(i,i)\in R
for some
i
then
\operatorname{id}:X_{i}\rightarrow X_{i}\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,i)
.

(2)

If
(i,j),(j,i)\in R
and
f\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
then
f^{-1}\in\operatorname{Mor}(j,i)
.

(3)

If
(i,j)(j,k),(i,k)\in R
,
f\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
, and
g\in\operatorname{Mor}(j,k)
, then
g\circ f\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,k)
.

We denote a groupoid spine by the data
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
, where
\mathrm{Mor}
is the family
\{\operatorname{Mor}(i,j):(i,j)\in R\}
.

Note that Definition
7.1
implies that for any
i,j\in I
, there is a composition of morphisms and their inverses sending
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
(which may be empty if
i=j
). This ensures that the groupoid determined by the data (if it exists) is connected.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
be a groupoid spine.

(1)

Suppose
R\subset S\subset I^{2}
. We say that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})

extends to
S
if one can define
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
for
(i,j)\in S-R
in such a way that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},S,\mathrm{Mor})
is a groupoid spine.

(2)

We say that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})

extends to a groupoid
, if it extends to
S=I^{2}
.

In other words,
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
extends to
S
if it can be grown to a groupoid spine on
S
without adding any new morphisms to pairs in
R
.

The following is then clear.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose
(I,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
is a groupoid spine that extends to a groupoid. Then the extension to a groupoid is uniquely determined. More precisely, the resulting groupoid is precisely the groupoid on
I
generated by all morphisms between pairs in
R
.

7.2.
Combinatorial Group Configuration Theorem

We now give a purely combinatorial analog of the group configuration theorem. The statement will say that a symmetric groupoid spine on at least three objects always extends to a groupoid. First, we define:

Definition 7.4
.

A groupoid spine
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
is
symmetric
if
R
is symmetric – that is,
(i,j)\in R
implies
(j,i)\in R
for all
i,j\in I
.

Equivalently, one easily checks that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
is symmetric if and only if it contains all pairs
(i,j)
with
i\neq j
. We now show:

Theorem 7.5
.

Let
(I,<\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
be a symmetric groupoid spine with
|I|\geq 3
.
Then
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\mathrm{Mor})
extends to a groupoid.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be the groupoid on
I
generated by all maps in the sets
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
for
(i,j)\in R
. It suffices to show that for
(i,j)\in R
, every
\mathcal{G}
-morphism
i\rightarrow j
already belongs to
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
.

So let
(i,j)\in R
, and let
f:X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}
be any
\mathcal{G}
-morphism. So
f
is a composition of maps from Mor and their inverses. On the other hand, by symmetry, inverses are not needed. So in fact,
f
is directly a composition of maps from Mor.

To be precise, we now obtain
i=i_{0},\dots,i_{n}=j\in I
such that each
(i_{l},i_{l+1})\in R
and
f
factors as a composition

X_{i_{0}}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}\dots\xrightarrow{f_{n}}X_{i_{n}},

where each
f_{i_{l}}\in\operatorname{Mor}(i_{l-1},i_{l})
. We may assume that
n
is minimal among all such decompositions of
f
. Our goal is to show that
n=0
or
n=1
(where we interpret
n=0
as
i=j
and
f=\operatorname{id}:X_{i}\rightarrow X_{i}
). So, assume
n\geq 2
.

Note that if
(i_{l},i_{l+2})\in R
for any
l
, then by closure under composition, the chain

X_{i_{l}}\xrightarrow{f_{l}}X_{i_{l+1}}\xrightarrow{f_{l+1}}X_{i_{l+2}}

can be shortened to a single morphism
X_{i_{l}}\rightarrow X_{i_{l+2}}
, contradicting the minimality of
n
. So each
(i_{l},i_{l+2})\notin R
. Since
R
contains all distinct pairs, this implies
i_{l+2}=i_{l}
whenever
0\leq l\leq n-2
. So the sequence
i_{0},\dots,i_{n}
alternates between the two values
i_{0}=i
and
i_{1}
, and
(i,i)\notin R
. Since
(i,j)\in R
by assumption, this implies
i\neq j
. This is only possible if
j=i_{1}
. That is, we deduce that
i
and
j
are distinct and the sequence
i_{0},\dots,i_{n}
alternates between
i
and
j
. Since
i_{n}=j
, it follows that
n
is odd, so in particular
n\geq 3
. So our decomposition starts with the chain

X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X_{j}\xrightarrow{f_{2}}X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}X_{j}.

We now show that this chain of three maps can be shortened to a single morphism
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}
, which will contradict the minimality of
n
and thus prove the theorem. To do this, recall the assumption that
|I|\geq 3
. Thus (as
i\neq j
) there is
k\in I
so that
i,j,k
are all distinct. Thus all non-diagonal pairs from
\{i,j,k\}
belong to
R
.

Now fix any morphism
g:j\rightarrow k
. By closure under inverses and composition, there is a morphism
h:k\rightarrow i
so that
h\circ g=f_{2}
. So the first three maps in our decomposition can be expanded to the chain

X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X_{j}\xrightarrow{g}X_{k}\xrightarrow{h}X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}X_{j}.

By closure under composition applied to each of
X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{1}}X_{j}\xrightarrow{g}X_{k}
and
X_{k}\xrightarrow{h}X_{i}\xrightarrow{f_{3}}X_{j}
, this chain simplifies to a composition of two morphisms
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{k}\rightarrow X_{j}
– which then simplifies for the same reason to a single morphism
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{j}
, as desired.
∎

7.3.
Regularity

Typically, one should not expect a groupoid spine to extend to a groupoid (at least, not without having many pairs
(i,j)\in R
with
i>j
) – and this is related to group configurations. For example, let
I=\{1,2,3\}
with the usual order
<
, fix a non-empty set
X
, and fix any non-empty family
F
of bijections
X\rightarrow X
. Now define:

•

X_{i}=X
for each
i
.

•

\operatorname{Mor}(1,2)=\operatorname{Mor}(2,3)=F
.

•

\operatorname{Mor}(1,3)=F\circ F
, the set of all compositions of two maps from
F
.

This data defines a groupoid spine (where
R=\{(1,2),(2,3),(1,3)\}
). One can check that if this groupoid spine extends to a groupoid, then
F
is a (left and right) coset of a group of permutations of
X
. In particular, given
f,g\in F
, the composition
f\circ g
is ‘independent’ from both
f
and
g
(meaning that for any
f^{\prime}
, there is
g^{\prime}
with
f\circ g=f^{\prime}\circ g^{\prime}
, and the analogous statement with
f
and
g
reversed). This independence of a composition with its factors is also the main idea of group configurations. So one can think of a groupoid spine with this property as an analog to a group configuration.

Typically, the independence property above is not expected. However, in stable 1-based theories, group configurations arise ‘automatically’: for example, in a locally modular strongly minimal theory, any composition of one-dimensional families of plane curves gives rise to a group configuration. This is roughly because the composite family cannot be too large, in the sense that a composition
f\circ g
is generically determined by one point. In the abstract language of groupoid spines, the analogous property is
regularity
:

Definition 7.6
.

Let
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
be a groupoid spine. We say that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
is
regular
if for all
(i,j)\in R
, and all
x\in X_{i}
and
y\in X_{j}
, there is exactly one morphism
f\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
with
f(x)=y
.

Note the analogy of the above definition with regular group actions. In particular, a group of permutations of a set induces a regular groupoid spine in the obvious way if and only if the corresponding group action is regular.

7.4.
The Main Result on Regular Groupoid Spines

We now show an analog to the fact that group configurations are automatic in the 1-based case. Namely, we show that for groupoid spines, regularity guarantees extendability.

Theorem 7.7
.

Let
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
be a regular groupoid spine. Then:

(1)

There is a symmetric set
S\supset R
such that
(I,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
extends to
S
.

(2)

In particular, if
|I|\geq 3
then
(I,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
extends to a groupoid.

Proof.

That (1) implies (2) is immediate by Theorem
7.5
. We only show (1).

Let
S
be the symmetric closure of
R
– so
S
adds to
R
all pairs
(i,j)\notin R
with
i>j
, and in particular
S
is symmetric. We will use multiple times the fact that
S
only adds strictly decreasing pairs to
R
– so if
(i,j)\in S
and
i\leq j
then
(i,j)\in R
.

For any pair
(i,j)\in S-R
, note that
(j,i)\in R
, so
\operatorname{Mor}(j,i)
is well-defined. Now set
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
to be the collection of all inverses of maps in
\operatorname{Mor}(j,i)
. All of the required properties in Definition
7.1
follow immediately, except closure under composition, which will occupy the rest of the proof.

So, let
i,j,k\in I
with
(i,j),(j,k),(i,k)\in S
, and consider a composition of morphisms
i\rightarrow j\rightarrow k
. We want to show that the composite map
X_{i}\rightarrow X_{k}
is a morphism
i\rightarrow k
. To do this, our first main goal is to convert everything into a problem in the original groupoid spine (by replacing certain maps with their inverses to reduce to a statement about only maps between
R
-pairs). We then apply regularity in the original spine to prove the resulting statement.

Let us proceed. First, note that if
i\leq j\leq k
, then
(i,j),(j,k),(i,k)\in R
, and the desired statement follows from closure under composition for
R
-pairs. The case
i\geq j\geq k
then follows similarly by replacing each map with its inverse.

Otherwise,
j
is either the maximum or minimum of
\{i,j,k\}
. We will prove the case that
j=\max\{i,j,k\}
, as the other case is similar. Assuming
j=\max\{i,j,k\}
, the problem is now symmetric in
i
and
k
– so without loss of generality, we assume
i\leq k
, and thus
(i,k)\in R
as well.

By replacing the given morphism
j\rightarrow k
with its inverse, we can now restate the problem in terms of
R
-pairs: we know that
(i,j),(k,j),(i,k)\in R
, and we have morphisms
f:i\rightarrow j
and
g:k\rightarrow j
. We want to find a morphism
h:i\rightarrow k
with
g\circ h=f
.

We now find such a morphism
h
using regularity. First, pick any element
y\in X_{k}
. Let
z=g(y)\in X_{j}
, and let
x\in X_{i}
with
f(x)=z
. By regularity, there is
h\in\operatorname{Mor}(i,k)
with
h(x)=y
. Thus
g\circ h
is a morphism
i\rightarrow j
sending
x
to
z
. By regularity, there is only one such morphism. So since
f(x)=z
as well, it follows that
g\circ h=f
, as desired.
∎

Before moving on, we also note that regularity is preserved under groupoid extensions:

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
(I,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
be a regular groupoid spine. If
(I,\mathcal{X},R,Mor)
extends to a groupoid, then the resulting extension is also regular.

Proof.

Let
(i,j)\in I^{2}
, and let
x\in X_{i}
and
y\in X_{j}
. We show that there is exactly one morphism
i\rightarrow j
(in the groupoid extension) sending
x
to
y
.

If
i<j
, then
(i,j)\in R
, and the desired statement follows from regularity for
R
-pairs. The case
i>j
then follows similarly, by taking inverses and finding a unique morphism
j\rightarrow i
sending
y
to
x
. Combining these two cases, the desired statement holds whenever
i\neq j
.

Now assume
i=j
, and from now on write
(i,j)
as
(i,i)
. If
(i,i)\in R
then we are done (by regularity for
R
-pairs), so we may further assume
(i,i)\notin R
. Recall that
R\neq\emptyset
(by the definition of groupoid spines); so there must be at least one other element of
I
, say
k
. Fix any morphism
g:X_{i}\rightarrow X_{k}
, and let
z=g(y)\in X_{k}
. By the groupoid axioms, the map
f\mapsto g\circ f
gives a bijective correspondence between
\operatorname{Mor}(i,i)
and
\operatorname{Mor}(i,k)
. Moreover, for
f:i\rightarrow i
, the condition
f(x)=y
is equivalent to
g\circ f(x)=z
. Since
i\neq k
(and this case was covered above), there is a unique morphism
i\rightarrow k
sending
x
to
z
. Passing back through our bijective correspondence, this implies there is a unique morphism
i\rightarrow i
sending
x
to
y
, as desired.
∎

8.
Type-Definability Requirements

We now revisit the constructions from the previous section in the context of a first-order theory, adding type-definability hypotheses in each step. The results of this section are valid in any first-order theory. In the following section, we then show that they apply in the setting of topological 1-basedness considered earlier. In this section, we work in a highly saturated first-order structure
\mathcal{M}
(with no topology assumptions).

8.1.
Type-Definability and Faithful Type-Definability

Assume that

In general, it is not clear that a type-definable groupoid spine should be faithfully type-definable: for example, the obvious definition of the formula

8.2.
Preservation Under Extensions

The main advantage of ordinary type-definability (versus faithful type-definability) is that it allows for a straightforward extension result:

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
A

-type-definable groupoid spine which extends to a groupoid. Then the resulting extension is also
A
-type-definable.

Proof.

Fix

when

8.3.
An Elimination Result

Our main goal is to interpret, under appropriate assumptions, a group from a type-definable groupoid spine. The group we hope to construct is the group of morphisms from an object to itself in an extension to a groupoid. Notice, though, that our definitions of type-definability and faithful type-definability have a flaw in this regard: a type-definition is allowed to repeat one map several times (i.e. the natural map
T_{ij}\rightarrow\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
is surjective but might not be injective). Thus, one cannot hope to build a group structure on the parameter space
T_{ii}
directly – instead, one needs to build a quotient of it. Forming the necessary quotient object (in a type-definable manner) is the main use of faithful type-definability – in this case, it essentially amounts to an elimination result for certain hyperimaginaries, which we treat separately below.

Proposition 8.5
.

Let
A
be a parameter set. Let

∎

9.
Construction of a Regular Groupoid Spine

We now return to the topological setting considered earlier in the paper. Thus, we fix

As before, we consider infinitesimal neighborhoods of

Our goal now is to show that if

9.1.
Families of Bijections

First, we show that from a certain configuration of interalgebraicities, we can construct a regular familiy of homeomrphisms between infinitesimal neighborhoods:

Let
(a,b,t)
be real tuples. Assume:

(1)

Any two of
a,b,t
are symmetrically independent.

(2)

a
and
b
are interalgebraic over
t
.

(3)

(t,ab)
is additive.

(4)

\operatorname{tp}(ab/t)
is topologially 1-based.

Then
\mu(abt/\emptyset)
is the graph of a regular family of homeomorphisms
\mu(a/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(b/\emptyset)
. More precisely, there is a family
F
of homeomorphisms
f:\mu(a/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(b/\emptyset)
such that:

(i)

For each
t^{\prime}\in\mu(t)
, the fiber
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime}}\subset\mu(ab/\emptyset)
is the graph of a homeomorphism
f_{t^{\prime}}\in F
.

(ii)

For each
f\in F
, there is
t^{\prime}\in\mu(t/\emptyset)
with
f_{t^{\prime}}=f
.

(iii)

For each
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/\emptyset)
and
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/\emptyset)
, there is exactly one
f\in F
with
f(a^{\prime})=b^{\prime}
.

Proof.

By symmetric independence, Lemma
4.8
gives:
\mu(at/\emptyset)=\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)
and
\mu(bt/\emptyset)=\mu(b/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)
. By Lemma
4.9
, since
a\in\operatorname{acl}(bt)
, projecting gives a homeomorphism

\mu(abt/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(bt/\emptyset)=\mu(b/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)

(technically this requires that
(a,bt)
is additive, but additivity follows automatically from
a\in\operatorname{acl}(bt)
). Similarly, the projection
\mu(abt/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)
is a homeomorphism. It follows that
\mu(abt/\emptyset)
is the graph of a family of homeomorphisms
\mu(a/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(b/\emptyset)
. Let
F
be the set of distinct homeomorphisms
\mu(a/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(b/\emptyset)
occurring in this way.

It remains to show that given
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/\emptyset)
and
b^{\prime}\in\mu(b/\emptyset)
, there is exactly one
f\in F
with
f(a^{\prime})=b^{\prime}
. First, the fact that there is at least one such
f
follows by the additivity of
(t,ab)
: indeed, since
a
and
b
are symmetrically independent, we have
\mu(ab/\emptyset)=\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(b/\emptyset)
; then by additivity and Lemma
4.9
, the projection

\mu(abt/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(ab/\emptyset)=\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(b/\emptyset)

is surjective.

It remains to show that there is at most one
f\in F
sending
a^{\prime}
to
b^{\prime}
. But this is exactly the content of Theorem
6.4
: if
f,g\in F
both send
a^{\prime}
to
b^{\prime}
, then there are
t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime}\in\mu(t/\emptyset)
so that
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime}}
is the graph of
f
and
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime\prime}}
is the graph of
g
. Then both
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime}}
and
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime\prime}}
contain
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})
– and since
\operatorname{tp}(ab/t)
is topologically 1-based, Theorem
6.4
gives that these two fibers are equal. That is,
f
and
g
have the same graph, and so they are the same function.
∎

9.2.
Pre-Group Configurations

We now aim toward a topological analog of a group configuration theorem. Toward that end, we define:

Definition 9.2
.

A
one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration
in
\mathcal{M}
is a 5-tuple
(a,b,c,t,u)
such that all of the following hold:

(1)

Each of
a,b,c,t,u
has dimension 1.

(2)

\dim(abctu)=3
.

(3)

a
and
b
are interalgebraic over
t
, and
b
and
c
are interalgebraic over
u
.

\textstyle{a}
\textstyle{b}
\textstyle{c}
\textstyle{t}
\textstyle{u}
\textstyle{\star}

Thus, a one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration consists of all but one point (denoted
\star
in the above diagram) in a conventional one-dimensional group configuration. One can easily check that all dimension computations in a one-dimensional pre-group configuration match those in a usual group configuration. Precisely, we leave the following to the reader:

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
(a,b,c,t,u)
be a one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration in
\mathcal{M}
. Separately, let
a^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime}
be
\mathbb{Q}
-linearly independent real numbers, let
b^{\prime}=a^{\prime}+t^{\prime}
, and let
c^{\prime}=b^{\prime}+u^{\prime}
. Then the map
f:\{a,b,c,t,u\}\mapsto\{a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime}\}
– sending each element to its primed counterpart – preserves all dimensions. More precisely, if
A,B\subset\{a,b,c,d,e\}
, then
\dim(A/B)
(in the sense of
\mathcal{M}
) is the same as the pregeometry dimension of
f(A)
over
f(B)
in the sense of the strongly minimal group
(\mathbb{R},+)
.

In particular, all dimension computations in a one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration have the additivity property:

Corollary 9.4
.

Let
(a,b,c,t,u)
be a one-dimensional pre-group configuration. If
A,B\subset\{a,b,c,t,u\}
, then
(A,B)
is additive.

Proof.

The analogous property holds in
(\mathbb{R},+)
, by exchange.
∎

9.3.
Group Configurations

We now define what it means for a one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration
(a,b,c,t,u)
to be an actual topological group configuration. In a stable theory, the requirement is the existence of a non-algebraic tuple
v\in\operatorname{acl}(ac)\cap\operatorname{acl}(tu)
(i.e. the sixth point of a conventional group configuration). In the topological setting, this is (a priori) too much to ask for. On the other hand, the same condition can be expressed using 1-basedness: if such a
v
as above exists in a stable context, then
v
is interalgebraic with
\operatorname{Cb}(\operatorname{stp}(ac/tu))
, which implies that
\operatorname{stp}(ac/tu)
is 1-based (and conversely, if
\operatorname{stp}(ac/tu)
is 1-based, one can take its canonical base for
v
). Thus, in the topological setting, we simply adapt the version of the definition using 1-basedness:

Definition 9.5
.

Let
(a,b,c,t,u)
be a one-dimensional topological pre-group configuration. We say that
(a,b,c,t,u)
is a
one-dimensional topological group configuration
if
\operatorname{tp}(ac/tu)
is topologically 1-based.

We now show:

Theorem 9.6
.

Let
(a,b,c,t,u)
be a one-dimensional topological group configuration in
\mathcal{M}
. Then there is an
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable group structure on the set
\mu(a/\emptyset)
with identity element
a
(and similarly for
b
and
c
).

Proof.

We will build an
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable regular groupoid spine
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\operatorname{Mor})
in the structure
\mathcal{N}
, where:

•

I=\{1,2,3\}
with the usual order.

•

X_{1}=\mu(a/\emptyset)
,
X_{2}=\mu(b/\emptyset)
, and
X_{3}=\mu(c/\emptyset)
.

•

R=\{(i,j):i<j\}
.

The Theorem will then follow by Theorem
8.8
. Precisely, Theorem
8.8
will provide an
\mathcal{M}a
-type-definable group structure on the set
X_{1}=\mu(a/\emptyset)
, with identity element
a
(and similarly for
b
and
c
). But since
a
,
b
, and
c
are tuples from
\mathcal{M}
, these groups are actually
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable.

Our task, then, is to define the sets
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
for
i<j
. We will do this by applying Lemma
9.1
to each of
(a,b,t)
,
(b,c,u)
, and
(a,c,tu)
. We leave it to the reader to check the various independence and algebraicity requirements needed to apply Lemma
9.1
in each of these cases; note that there is no need to check the additivity requirements, as Corollary
9.4
gives them for free.

So, in order to apply Lemma
9.1
, the main thing to check is that each of
\operatorname{tp}(ab/t)
,
\operatorname{tp}(bc/u)
, and
\operatorname{tp}(ac/tu)
is topologically 1-based. But indeed:

•

By assumption
t\in\operatorname{acl}(ab)
, so
\dim(t/ab)=0
, and thus
\dim(t/\operatorname{germ}(ab/t))
must also be 0. Thus
\operatorname{tp}(ab/t)
is topologically 1-based.

•

By the same reason as above,
\operatorname{tp}(bc/u)
is also topologically 1-based.

•

Finally,
\operatorname{tp}(ac/tu)
is topologically 1-based because of the assumption that
(a,b,c,t,u)
is a one-dimensional topological group configuration.

So, we may legally apply Lemma
9.1
in each of the above instances. Denote the three resulting families of bijections by
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
(in the obvious way, so that
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
is the family of maps from
X_{i}
to
X_{j}
).

If we can show that the data defined so far gives a groupoid spine, then it will automatically be
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable and regular. That is:

•

Each
X_{i}
is an infinitesimal neighborhood, so is
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable.

•

Similarly, each
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
is represented as in Definition
8.1
by an
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable family – precisely by
\mu(abt/\emptyset)
,
\mu(bcu/\emptyset)
, and
\mu(actu/\emptyset)
, respectively.

•

By Lemma
9.1
, each
\operatorname{Mor}(i,j)
has the required property for the definition of regularity.

Thus, our only remaining task is showing that
(I,<,\mathcal{X},R,\operatorname{Mor})
indeed gives a groupoid spine. And for this, the only thing to check is closure under composition. So let
f\in\operatorname{Mor}(1,2)
, and let
g\in\operatorname{Mor}(2,3)
. We want to show that
g\circ f\in\operatorname{Mor}(1,3)
.

Since
f\in\operatorname{Mor}(1,2)
, there is
t^{\prime}\in\mu(t/\emptyset)
so that the fiber
\mu(abt/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime}}
is the graph of
f
. Similarly, since
g\in\operatorname{Mor}(2,3)
, there is
u^{\prime}\in\mu(u/\emptyset)
so that the fiber
\mu(bcu)_{u^{\prime}}
is the graph of
g
. We will show that the fiber
\mu(actu/\emptyset)_{t^{\prime}u^{\prime}}
is the graph of
g\circ f
. Let
h:\mu(a/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(c/\emptyset)
be the bijection whose graph is the fiber above
(t^{\prime},u^{\prime})
. So we want to show that
g\circ f=h
.

To show this, let
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/\emptyset)
. Note that
abctu\in\operatorname{acl}(atu)
– thus
\dim(atu)=3
, and thus
\mu(atu/\emptyset)=\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)\times\mu(u/\emptyset)
. Moreover, by Lemma
4.9
, the projection gives a homeomorphism

\mu(abctu/\emptyset)\rightarrow\mu(atu/\emptyset)=\mu(a/\emptyset)\times\mu(t/\emptyset)\times\mu(u/\emptyset).

Since
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/\emptyset)
,
t^{\prime}\in\mu(t/\emptyset)
, and
u^{\prime}\in\mu(u/\emptyset)
, we conclude that
(a^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in\mu(atu/\emptyset)
, and thus there are
b^{\prime},c^{\prime}
so that
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in\mu(abctu/\emptyset)
. Thus:

•

(a^{\prime},b^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\mu(abt/\emptyset)
, and so
b^{\prime}=f(a^{\prime})
;

•

Similarly,
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in\mu(bcu/\emptyset)
, and so
c^{\prime}=g(b^{\prime})
;

•

And finally,
(a^{\prime},c^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime})\in\mu(actu/\emptyset)
, and so
c^{\prime}=h(a^{\prime})
.

It now follows that
g\circ f(a^{\prime})=h(a^{\prime})
. Since
a^{\prime}\in\mu(a/\emptyset)
was arbitrary, it follows that
g\circ f=h
, and this proves the theorem.
∎

Building a Topological Group Configuration

We now show that, combined with a non-triviality assumption, topological 1-basedness implies the type-definability of an infinite group.

Say that
\mathcal{M}
is
non-trivial
if for some parameter set
A
, there are
a,b,c\in M
with
\dim(abc/A)=2
and each of
a,b,c

.

Assume

a\in M
with

, and an infinite
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable group structure on the set

.

Let
A
be a parameter set and
(a,b,t)\in M^{3}
with

and each of

, we may assume

. By Lemma

, there is a pair
(c,u)\models\operatorname{tp}(at/b)
so that

It follows easily that

is a one-dimensional pre-group configuration in

. Since

is topologically 1-based,

is then automatically a one-dimensional topological group configuration in

to get an

.

We can also give some more local analysis:

(1)

So

Let

10.
Topologizing the Group

Throughout this section, assume
e\in M
with
\dim(e)=1
. Moreover, assume
(G_{0},\cdot)
is an
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable group with universe
\mu(e)
and identity element
e
.

Our goal will be to replace the group structure on
\mu(e)
with a suitable type-definable group over a small set in
\mathcal{M}
. A key feature will be that the group we build is a topological group with the topology inherited from the given t-minimal topology
\tau
.

10.1.
Step 1: A Type-Definable Group in
\mathcal{M}

First we build a type-definable supergroup of
G_{0}
, defined over a small set in
\mathcal{M}
:

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
A
be any parameter set such that
\dim(e/A)=1
. Then there are a countable set
B
, and a
B
-type-definable group
(G,\cdot)
with
G_{0}\leq G
so that the following hold:

(1)

G
is an open subset of
M
.

(2)

Every element of
G
realizes
\operatorname{tp}(e/A)
.

Proof.

Since
\dim(e/A)=1
, every formula in
\operatorname{tp}(e/A)
holds on an open neighborhood of
e
. So by Fact
2.1
, there is an open neighborhood
U_{0}
of
e
all of whose elements realize
\operatorname{tp}(e/A)
. Shrinking
U_{0}
if necessary, we may assume it is
\mathcal{M}
-definable.

Since
G
is type-definable over
\mathcal{M}
, its operation and inverse are given by (restrictions of)
\mathcal{M}
-definable functions, say
(x,y)\models m(x,y)
and
x\mapsto i(x)
.

Now recall that
G_{0}=\mu(e)
is the intersection of all
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhoods of
e
. By compactness, there is such a neighborhood
U_{1}\subset U_{0}
satisfying the universal group axioms. That is:

•

If
a,b,c\in U_{1}
then
m(m(a,b),c)
and
m(a,m(b,c))
are both defined and equal.

•

If
a\in U_{1}
then
m(a,e)=m(e,a)=a
.

•

If
a\in U_{1}
then
i(a)
is defined and
m(a,i(a))=m(i(a),a)=e
.

We now build a decreasing sequence of definable open neighborhoods
U_{i}\subset U_{1}
(for
i\geq 2
) whose intersection is closed under
m
and
i
, and so is a group. To do this, it suffices to check:

Claim 10.2
.

Let
W
be any
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhood of
e
. Then there is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhood
V\subset U_{1}
of
e
such that
m(V^{2}),i(V)\subset W
.

Proof.

Since
W
is
\mathcal{M}
-definable, we have
\mu(e)\subset W
, and thus all products and inverses of elements of
\mu(e)
belong to
W
. By compactness, the same holds after replacing
\mu(e)
with some
\mathcal{M}
-definable neighborhood of
e
.
∎

Now if we have constructed all
U_{i}
, then clearly
G=\bigcap U_{i}
is a group with the operations
m
and
i
. Thus
G_{0}
is a subgroup. Since
G
is an intersection of countably many definable open sets, it is open (by Fact
2.1
). Finally,
G
is defined over a countable set in
\mathcal{M}
(namely, the parameters used to define each
U_{i}
).
∎

10.2.
Step 2: A Locally Topological Group

Recall that a version of generic continuity holds in our setting (see Lemma
4.9
). It follows that the group
G
built above is a ‘generically topological group’: the operation and inverse are almost everywhere continuous. In
[
15
]
, Marikova initiated a general method for turning a ‘generically topological’ group into a genuinely topological group. We recall one version of Marikova’s method, following
[
2
]
:

Definition 10.3
.

Let
G
be a group, and
d\in G
. By
G_{d}
, we mean the group on underlying set
G
with operation
(x,y)\mapsto xd^{-1}y
and inverse
x\mapsto dx^{-1}d
.

The group
G_{d}
is obtained from
G
by a relabelling, translating every element by
d
. Thus
d
is the identity of
G_{d}
.

Definition 10.4
.

Let
G
be a group whose underlying set is also a topological space.

(1)

We say
G
is
generically topological
if there are dense open sets
U\subset G^{2}
and
V\subset G
such that
(x,y)\mapsto xy
and
x\mapsto x^{1}
are continuous on
U
and
V
, respectively.

(2)

We say
G
is
locally topological
if there is an open neighborhood
U\subset G
of the identity such that
(x,y)\mapsto xy
and
x\mapsto x^{-1}
are continuous on
U^{2}
and
U
, respectively.

The following is from
[
2
, §7]
:

Fact 10.5
.

Let
G
be a generically topological group. Then there is a dense open set
W
such that
G_{d}
is locally topological for all
d\in W
.

We also need the following reinterpretation of generic continuity:

Lemma 10.6
.

Let
U\subset M^{n}
be definable and open, and let
f:U\rightarrow M
be definable. Then
f
is continuous on a dense open set.

Proof.

Let
U^{\prime}
be the set of
u\in U
so that
f
is continuous on a neighborhood of
u
. We show that
U^{\prime}
is dense. If not, there is a definable open
V\subset U
disjoint from
U^{\prime}
. Let
A
be a set so that
f
and
V
are
A
-definable. Since
V
is open, there is
v\in V
with
\dim(v/A)=n
. Then by generic continuity, Corollary
3.17
,
f
is continuous on a neighborhood of
v
, a contradiction.
∎

We conclude:

Lemma 10.7
.

Let
G
be a type-definable group over a small set
A
, whose universe is an open subset of
M
. Then for any
d\in G-\operatorname{acl}(A)
, the group
G_{d}
is locally topological with the topology inherited from
M
.

Proof.

The operation and inverse of
G
are given by
A
-definable functions, say
m(x,y)
and
i(x)
. By compactness, there is an
A
-definable
X\supset G
so that
m
and
i
are defined on elements of
X
. By Lemma
10.6
,
m
and
i
are continuous on dense open subsets of
X^{2}
and
X
, respectively. But
G
is open in
X
, so it follows that
m
and
i
are also continuous on dense open subsets of
G^{2}
and
G
, respectively. Thus
G
is generically topological. By Fact
10.5
, there is a dense open set
W\subset G
so that
G_{d}
is locally topological for all
d\in W
. Now let
Y
be the set of all
d\in X
so that the maps
(x,y)\mapsto xd^{-1}y
and
x\mapsto dx^{-1}d
defined and continuous on neighborhoods of
(d,d)
and
d
, respectively. Then
Y
contains
W
and is
A
-definable.

Now let
d\in G-\operatorname{acl}(A)
. If
G_{d}
is not locally topological, then
d\notin Y
, and thus by t-minimality,
X-Y
contains a neighborhood of
d
. Since
G
is open,
X-Y
contains a neighborhood of
d
in
G
. This contradicts that
W\subset Y
and
W
is dense.
∎

10.3.
Step 3: A Topological Group

Finally, we show that from a locally topological group, one can always extract a genuinely topological group:

Lemma 10.8
.

Let
G
be a locally topological type-definable group whose underlying set is an open subset of
G
. Then there is a type-definable topological open subgroup of
G
defined over a countable set.

Proof.

By Fact
2.1
, there is a definable neighborhood
U_{0}
of the identity contained in
G
. Since
G
is locally topological, there is a definable open neighborhood
U_{1}\subset U_{0}
of the identity such that the operation and inverse are continuous on elements of
U_{1}
. By continuity, there is a definable open neighborhood
U_{2}\subset U_{1}
over the identity such that
U_{2}\cdot U_{2}
and
U_{2}^{-1}
are contained in
U_{1}
. Continue in this manner, constructing definable open neighborhoods
U_{2}\supset U_{3}\supset U_{4}\dots
of the identity such that
U_{i+1}\cdot U_{i+t}\subset U_{i}
and
U_{i+1}^{-1}\subset U_{i}
. Then
\bigcap U_{i}
satisfies the requirements of the lemma.
∎

We conclude the main result of this subsection. Recall that we have a fixed
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable group structure
G_{0}
on
\mu(e)
, with
e
as the identity. Now we show:

Proposition 10.9
.

Let
A
be any parameter set so that
\dim(e/A)=1
. Then there are a countable set
B
, and a
B
-type-definable group
G
, such that:

(1)

e
is the identity of
G
.

(2)

G
is open in
M
.

(3)

G
is a topological group with the topology inherited from
M
.

(4)

Every element of
G
realizes
\operatorname{tp}(e/A)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
10.1
, we can find
B
and
G
satisfying all of the given requirements except being topological. Let
d\in G-\operatorname{acl}(B)
. Then the group
G_{d}
is
Bd
-type-definable and topological. So the pair
(G_{d},Bd)
satisfies all requirements of the proposition except that its identity element is
d
, not
e
. But
d
and
e
have the same type over
A
, so there is an automorphism
\sigma
of
\mathcal{M}
fixing
A
and satisfying
\sigma(d)=e
. Now the pair
(\sigma(G),\sigma(Bd))
satisfies the requirements of the proposition.
∎

10.4.
Concluding

We now give our main results at the level we have reached thus far. So, we drop the assumptions from the beginning of this section; but we retain our fixed pair
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
of t-minimal structures with independent neighborhoods.

Theorem 10.10
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
is non-trivial and 1-based. Then there are a countable parameter set
B
, and a

-type-definable group
(G,\cdot)
such that:

(1)

G
is a non-empty open subset of
M
.

(2)

(G,\cdot)
is a topological group with the subspace topology inherited from

.

Proof.

By Corollary
9.8
, there are
a\in M
with
\dim(a)=1
, and an
\mathcal{M}
-type-definable group structure on
\mu(a)
. Now apply Proposition
10.9
.
∎

Theorem 10.11
.

Let
a\in M
and
A
a parameter set so that
\dim(a/A)=1
, and
p=\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
is non-trivial and topologically locally modular. Then there are a countable set
B
, and a

-type-definable group

such that:

(1)

The identity element of

is

.

(2)

is a non-empty open subset of

, and is contained in the realizations of

.

(3)

(G,\cdot)

.

Proof.

By Corollary

, there is an

∎

10.5.
Viscerality of T-minimal Groups

Before moving on, we point out an isolated fact that falls out of our work in this section and may be of interest. Roughly, we show that
every
t-minimal expansion of a group with independent neighborhoods is
already
both topological and visceral, at least up to editing the topology at finitely many points.

Recall that
\mathcal{M}
is a fixed sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure with the independent neighborhood property.

Theorem 10.12
.

Assume that
\mathcal{M}=(M,\cdot,e,...)
is an expansion of a group. Then there is a definable topology
\tau^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{M}
such that:

(1)

With respect to
\tau^{\prime}
,
\mathcal{M}
is a visceral topological group.

(2)

\tau^{\prime}
agrees with
\tau
on a cofinite subset of
M
.

Proof.

It is enough to find
\tau^{\prime}
satisfying (1). Then by Johnson’s appendix (Theorem
B.8
),
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property with respect to
\tau^{\prime}
; therefore, by Lemma
3.18
,
\tau^{\prime}
agrees with
\tau
on a cofinite set.

The rest of the proof finds
\tau^{\prime}
satisfying (1). First, let
d\in M
be generic, and consider the group
M_{d}
as in Definition
10.3
. By Lemma
10.7
,
M_{d}
is locally topological with respect to
\tau
. Moreover, if we construct
\tau^{\prime}
satisfying (1) for
M_{d}
, the same
\tau^{\prime}
will work for
M
. Thus, we assume moving forward that
M
is locally topological with respect to
\tau
.

By Lemma
10.8
, there is thus a type-definable open topological subgroup
H\leq M
, defined over a countable set. Absorbing parameters, we assume
H
is
\emptyset
-definable. We then obtain a definable uniform structure on
M
by taking translations of basic neighborhoods of
e
. Call the resulting topology
\tau^{\prime}
. So
\tau^{\prime}
is translation invariant (both left and right), and agrees with
\tau
on
H
.

We will show that
(M,\tau^{\prime})
is a topological group, and
\mathcal{M}
is visceral with respect to
\tau
’. We start with the first of these goals, i.e. showing
(M,\tau^{\prime})
is topological. By
[
8
, Lemma 2.11]
, it is enough to show that for each
g\in M
, conjugation by
g
is a
local homeomorphism
: this means there are
\tau^{\prime}
-open neighborhoods
e\in U,V\subset M
so that
u\mapsto gug^{-1}
induces a homeomorphism
U\rightarrow V
. Since
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
agree on
H
, it is equivalent to find such
U,V
which are
\tau
-open.

Before proceeding, we collect some easy claims. Throughout, by a
generic
element of
M
over
A
, we mean an element
g
with
\dim(g/A)=1
.

Claim 10.13
.

Every element of
M
is a product of two generic elements.

Proof.

Given
g\in M
, let
h
be generic over
g
, and write
g=h(h^{-1}g)
.
∎

Claim 10.14
.

Let
g,h\in G
with
\dim(g,h)=2
. Then the maps
x\mapsto gx
and
x\mapsto xg
define homeomorphisms
\mu(h)\rightarrow\mu(gh)
and
\mu(h)\rightarrow\mu(hg)
.

Proof.

This follows by Lemma
4.9
, noting that
\dim(h/g)=\dim(h)=1
, so
\mu(h/g)=\mu(h/\emptyset)=\mu(h)
(and similarly for
gh
).
∎

Claim 10.15
.

Let
h\in H
be generic. Then the maps
x\mapsto hx
and
x\mapsto xh
give homeomorphisms
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(h)
.

Proof.

This follows since
H
is topological, so these maps are homeomorphisms, and thus they preserve all
\mathcal{M}
-defianble open sets (see also Lemma
11.2
).
∎

Claim 10.16
.

Let
g\in M
be generic. Then
x\mapsto gx
and
x\mapsto xg
each give homeomorphisms
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(g)
.

Proof.

Let
h\in H
with
\dim(g,h)=2
, and write
g=ah
where
a=gh^{-1}
. So also
\dim(ah)=2
. By Claims
10.15
and
10.14
,
x\mapsto gx=ahx
is now a composition of translation homeomorphisms
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(h)\rightarrow\mu(g)
(translating by
h
then by
a
). The argument for
x\mapsto xg
is similar, using
b
so that
g=hb
.
∎

Finally, we conclude:

Claim 10.17
.

Let
g\in M
. Then conjugation by
g
induces a homeomorphism between neighborhoods of
e
. Thus,
(M,\tau^{\prime})
is a topological group.

Proof.

By Claim
10.13
, we may assume
g
is generic. Now using Claim
10.16
, write conjugation by
g
as a composition of translation homeomorphisms
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(g)\rightarrow\mu(e)
, sending
x\mapsto gx\mapsto gxg^{-1}
. So conjugation by
g
gives a homeomorphism
\mu(e)\rightarrow\mu(e)
, and thus (by compactness) also a homeomorphism between neighborhoods of
e
.
∎

We now turn to our second main goal mentioned above:

Claim 10.18
.

\mathcal{M}
is t-minimal, and thus visceral, with respect to
\tau^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Viscerality follows from t-minimality by definition, since
(M,\tau^{\prime})
is uniform. We show t-minimality. Recall this means showing that for definable
X\subset M
,
X
has
\tau
’-interior if and only if it is infinite.

First suppose
X
has
\tau^{\prime}
-interior. Then by translation invariance, some translation
Y
of
X
contains a
\tau^{\prime}
-neighborhood of
e
. Since
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
agree on
H
(i.e. near
e
),
Y
also contains a
\tau
-neighborhood of
e
. By t-minimality of
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
,
Y
is infinite – thus so is
X
.

Now suppose
X
is infinite. Say
X
is
A
-definable, and pick
a\in X
with
\dim(a/A)=1
. By t-minimality of
(\mathcal{M},\tau)
,
X
contains a neighborhood of
a
, and thus contains
\mu(a)
. By Claim
10.16
,
Y=a^{-1}X
contains
\mu(e)
, so in particular contains a
\tau
-neighborhood of
e
. Since
\tau
and
\tau^{\prime}
agree near
e
,
Y
contains a
\tau^{\prime}
-neighborhood of
e
. Then by translation invariance,
X
contains a
\tau^{\prime}
-neighborhood of
a
.
∎

We have now shown that
(\mathcal{M},\tau^{\prime})
is a visceral topological group, which completes the proof of (1), and thus of the theorem.
∎

Remark 10.19
.

One could also prove Theorem
10.12
by quoting upcoming results of Johnson. According to private communication, he shows that any visceral expansion of a group admits a definable visceral group topology. This allows us to skip the proof that
(M,\tau^{\prime})
is a topological group above, concentrating only on the proof that
(\mathcal{M},\tau^{\prime})
is t-minimal. Indeed, once we know
(\mathcal{M},\tau^{\prime})
is t-minimal (and thus visceral), we obtain from Johnson a second definable visceral topology
\tau^{\prime\prime}
with respect to which
\mathcal{M}
is a topological group. (In the end, one can show
\tau^{\prime\prime}=\tau^{\prime}
using independent neighborhoods and translation invariance, so Johnson’s argument really gives an alternate proof that
\tau^{\prime}
is a group topology).

11.
The Structure of the Group

In this section, we assume that
G
is a type-definable group on a non-empty open subset of
M
, and is a topological group with the subspace topology inherited from
M
. Absorbing parameters, we assume
G
is
\emptyset
-type-definable. We denote the identity of
G
by
e
.

Our goal is to prove results analogous to the Hrushovski-Pillay classification of 1-based stable groups (
[
10
]
). In a very coarse language, we will show:

(1)

G
is locally linear.

(2)

G
is locally abelian.

(3)

There are no large type-definable families of germs of subgroups of
G
.

11.1.
The Infinitesimal Subgroup

Let
\mu=\mu(e)
be the intersection of all
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhoods of
e
. So
\mu
is open and type-definable over
\mathcal{M}
. One easily checks:

Lemma 11.1
.

\mu
is a subgroup of
G
.

Proof.

Let
a,b\in\mu
. We show
ab^{-1}\in\mu
. Indeed, let
V
be any
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhood of
e
. Since
G
is topological, the set
\{(x,y):xy^{-1}\in V\}
is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhood of
e^{2}
, so contains a set
U^{2}
where
U
is an
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhood of
e
. By definition of
\mu
we get
a,b\in U
, and so
ab^{-1}\in V
. Since
V
was arbitrary,
ab^{-1}\in\mu
.
∎

Lemma 11.2
.

Let
a\in G
. Then
\mu(a)=a\cdot\mu=\mu\cdot a
.

Proof.

The maps
x\mapsto ax
and
x\mapsto xa
are
\mathcal{M}
-definable homeomorphisms, so preserve
\mathcal{M}
-definable open sets.
∎

Lemma 11.3
.

Let
A
be a parameter set, and
H\leq G^{n}
an
A
-type-definable subgroup. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

H
has non-empty interior.

(2)

H
is open.

(3)

\mu^{n}\subset H
.

(4)

\dim(H)=n
.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
: Let
a
be an interior point of
H
. For any
b\in H
, the map
x\mapsto ba^{-1}x
sends
a
to
b
and is a homeomorphism of both
H
and
G
. So
b
is also an interior point of
H
.

(2)\implies(3)
: If
H
is
\mathcal{M}
-definable and open, it contains the intersection of all
\mathcal{M}
-definable open neighborhoods of
e
, which is precisely
\mu^{n}
.

(3)\implies(4)
: If
H
contains
\mu^{n}
, then

n=\dim(\mu^{n})\leq\dim(H)\leq\dim(G^{n})=n,

so
\dim(H)=n
.

(4)\implies(1)
: If
\dim(H)=n
, then there is
h\in H
with
\dim(h/A)=n
. Then every
A
-definable superset of
h
contains a neighborhood of
h
, and by Fact
2.1
, so does
H
.
∎

11.2.
Characterization of Topological 1-basedness

In this subsection we prove the main geometric result concerning topological 1-basedness in
G
. This should be seen as analogous to the Hrushovski-Pillay characterization of 1-based stable groups.

We will need the following, which is an elementary group-theoretic exercise:

Fact 11.4
.

Let
H
be a group, and
X\subset H
a non-empty subset. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

Any two left translates of
X
are equal or disjoint.

(2)

Any two right translates of
X
are equal or disjoint.

(3)

X
is a left coset of a subgroup of
G^{n}
.

(4)

X
is a right coset of a subgroup of
G^{n}
.

(5)

X
is closed under the map
(x,y,z)\mapsto xy^{-1}z
.

Recall that in Section
5.6
we introduced
local linearity
for t-minimal topological groups. We now give this notion a more general definition in the type-definable setting.

Lemma 11.5
.

Let
a\in G^{n}
and
A
a parameter set. The following are equivalent:

(1)

There are a parameter set
A
, and an
A
-type-definable subgroup
H\leq G^{n}
, so that
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(a\cdot H)
.

(2)

There are a parameter set
A
, and an
A
-type-definable subgroup
H\leq G^{n}
, so that
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(H\cdot a)
.

(3)

\mu(a/A)
is a left coset of a subgroup of
\mu^{n}
.

(4)

\mu(a/A)
is a right coset of a subgroup of
\mu^{n}
.

Proof.

(1) and (2) are equivalent by Fact
11.4
. Similarly, (3) and (4) are equivalent by Fact
11.4
.

(1)\rightarrow(3)
: Let
A
be a parameter set, and
H\leq G^{n}
an
A
-type-definable subgroup, so that
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)=\operatorname{germ}_{a}(a\cdot H)
. Then
\mu(a/A)=\mu(a)\cap(a\cdot H)
. By Lemma
11.2
, this is the same as
a\cdot(\mu\cap H)
. By Lemma
11.1
,
\mu\cap H
is a subgroup of
\mu^{n}
. This implies (3).

(3)\rightarrow(1)
. Assume
\mu(a/A)
is a left coset of a subgroup. By Fact
11.4
,
\mu(a/A)
is closed under
(x,y,z)\mapsto xy^{-1}z
. Now using compactness, inductively build
\mathcal{M}
-definable d-approximations
X_{1}\supset X_{2}\dots
of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
at
a
so that
X_{i+1}X_{i+1}^{-1}X_{i+1}\subset X_{i}
for all
i
. Then
X=\bigcap X_{i}
is closed under
(x,y,z)\mapsto xy^{-1}z
, so is a left coset of the subgroup
a^{-1}X
. Since each
X_{i}
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
, Fact
2.1
gives that
X
also realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
. Finally,
a^{-1}X
is defined over countably many parameters from
\mathcal{M}
, so satisfies the requirements of (1).
∎

Definition 11.6
.

Let
a\in G^{n}
and
A
a parameter set. We say that
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
is
locally linear
if the equivalent conditions of Lemma
11.5
hold.

Definition 11.7
.

We say that
G
is
locally linear
if
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
is locally linear for all
a\in G^{n}
and all parameter sets
A
.

We also give a general definition of topological 1-basedness for
G
:

Definition 11.8
.

We say that
G
is
topologically 1-based
if for every
a\in G^{m}
, every
b\in G^{n}
, and every parameter set
A
,
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
.

Remark 11.9
.

By Remark
5.12
, if exchange holds then Definition
11.8
is equivalent to the analogous assertion restricted to the case when
n=2
.

In this language, we now prove our main analog of the Hrushovski-Pillay theorem on 1-based stable groups:

Theorem 11.10
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

G
is topologically 1-based.

(2)

G
is locally linear.

In particular, both conditions hold if
\mathcal{M}
is topologically 1-based.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
: Let
a\in G^{n}
and let
A
be a parameter set. Let
X=a^{-1}\cdot\mu(a/A)\subset\mu^{n}
, so that
\mu(a/A)=a\cdot X
. Then let
b\in G^{n}
so that
\dim(a,b/A)=\dim(a/A)+n
. So
\dim(b/A)=n
, which implies that
\mu(b/A)=\mu(b)=\mu^{n}\cdot b
. Also,
a
and
b
are symmetrically independent over
A
, and thus

\mu(a,b/A)=\mu(a/A)\times\mu(b/A)=(a\cdot X)\times(\mu^{n}\cdot b).

So for all
u\in\mu^{n}
, the fiber above
u\cdot b
in
\mu(a,b/A)
is
\mu(a/A)=a\cdot X
.

Now
(a,b)
and
(a\cdot b,b)
are interdefinable over
A
by the map
(x,y)\mapsto(x\cdot y,y)
. It follows that the same map gives a homeomorphism
\mu(a,b/A)\rightarrow\mu(a\cdot b,b/A)
. Thus, for
u\in\mu^{n}
, the fiber above
u\cdot b
in
\mu(a\cdot b,b/A)
is the right translate by
u\cdot b
of the corresponding fiber in
\mu(a,b/A)
, i.e.
a\cdot X\cdot u\cdot b
. Now, assuming (1), the type
\operatorname{tp}(a\cdot b/Ab)
is topologically 1-based over
A
. Thus, by Theorem
6.4
, any two of the sets
a\cdot X\cdot u\cdot b
for
u\in\mu^{n}
are equal or disjoint. The same holds after cancelling
a
on the left and
b
on the right. Thus, any two of the sets
X\cdot u
, for
u\in\mu^{n}
, are equal or disjoint. By Fact
11.4
, it follows that
X
is a left coset of a subgroup of
\mu^{n}
, and thus so is
a\cdot X=\mu(a/A)
.

(2)\implies(1)
: Let
a\in G^{m}
,
b\in G^{n}
, and
A
a parameter set. Assuming (2), the set
\mu(a,b/A)
is a left coset of
\mu^{m+n}
. It follows that there is a subgroup
H\leq G^{m}
so that each fiber
\mu(a,b/A)_{y}
for
y\in\mu(b/A)
is either empty or a left coset of
H
. Since any two left cosets of
H
are equal or disjoint, it follows by Theorem
6.4
that
\operatorname{tp}(a/Ab)
is 1-based over
A
.
∎

11.3.
Few Subgroups

Hrushosvki and Pillay also prove that 1-based stable groups have few definable subgroups, in the sense that every connected definable subgroup is defined over
\operatorname{acl}(\emptyset)
. To adapt to our setting, we make two changes:

(1)

Instead of connected subgroups, we consider germs of subgroups at the identity.

(2)

Instead of asking for the germ to be defined over
\operatorname{acl}(\emptyset)
, we require (as in the definition of topological 1-basedness) that the germ is constant on an open set.

Before proving a precise statement, we need a technical lemma. In the general t-minimal setting (even with independent neighborhoods), one can have a definable set – say over
A
– which is not a d-approximation for any of its elements over
A
. In contrast, Lemma
11.11
essentially says that groups and their cosets are always d-approximations at generic points:

Lemma 11.11
.

Let
X
be an
A
-type-definable left coset of a subgroup of
G^{n}
of dimension
d
. Let
a\in X
with
\dim(a/A)=d
. Let
b
be a basis of
a
over
A
, and
\pi
the corresponding projection sending
a
to
b
. Then:

(1)

\pi
is finite-to-one on
X
.

(2)

X
realizes
\operatorname{germ}(a/A)
.

Proof.

Note that (1) implies (2) by compactness, Lemma
3.6
, and Fact
2.1
(as cofinally many
A
-definable supersets of
X
are d-approximations of
\operatorname{tp}(a/A)
).

We show (1). Since
X
is a left coset, there is an
A
-type-definable subgroup
H\leq G^{n}
such that all non-empty fibers of
\pi
in
X
are left cosets of
H
(here we treat fibers a subsets of
G^{n}
, not
G^{n-d}
, as this will make the argument easier). So it suffices to show that
H
is finite.

Now assume
H
is infinite. Then there is
h\in H
with
\dim(a,h/A)=\dim(a/A)+\dim(H)>\dim(X)
.

Since the fiber
X_{b}
is a left coset of
H
and contains
a
, it follows that
a\cdot h\in X
, and so
\dim(a\cdot h/A)\leq\dim(X)<\dim(a,h/A)
. On the other hand, we claim that
a\cdot h
is interalgebraic with
(a,h)
over
A
, which will give a contradiction. Clearly
a\cdot h\in\operatorname{acl}(A,a,h)
. For the converse, since
\pi(a\cdot h)=b
, we have
b\in\operatorname{acl}(A,a\cdot h)
. But
a\in\operatorname{acl}(Ab)
by definition; thus
a\in\operatorname{acl}(A,a\cdot h)
, and thus
h\in\operatorname{acl}(A,a\cdot h)
as well, since
h=a^{-1}\cdot(a\cdot h)
.
∎

We now give our ‘few subgroups’ result:

Theorem 11.12
.

Assume
G
is topologically 1-based. Let
Y\subset G^{m}
be
A
-type-definable, let
\{H_{y}:y\in Y\}
be an
A
-type-definable family of d-dimensional subgroups of
G^{n}
, and let
b\in Y
. Then the germ of
H_{y}
at the identity is constant for all

Proof.

First, we need:

Claim 11.13
.

There is

\dim(a,b/A)=\dim(b/A)+d

Proof.

Let

, there is a finite-to-one projection

,

contains

. In particular,

. Let

∎

Now fix

is topologically 1-based, Theorem

gives that

is a left coset of a subgroup. In particular, all of the non-empty fibers

Let

at the identity is the same as the germ of

Now by the choice of

is additive over

, there is

is realized by

∎

11.4.
Local Abelianity

Recall that every 1-based stable group is abelian-by-finite (see
[

, Theorem 3.2]
). This is shown by applying the ‘few subgroups’ result in that context to the family of conjugation maps.

In the topological setting, the analogous statement is that

contains an open abelian subgroup – or equivalently,

is abelian. We now prove this, using essentially the same strategy from the stable case.

Theorem 11.14
.

Assume

is topologically 1-based.

(1)

There are a countable parameter set
A
, and an
A
-type-definable open abelian subgroup
H\leq G
.

(2)

In particular,
\mu
is abelian.

Proof.

The family of (graphs of) conjugation maps
x\mapsto axa^{-1}
is a
\emptyset
-type-definable family of subgroups of
G^{2}
. Let
b\in G
with
\dim(b)=1
. Then by Theorem
11.10
, the germ of

at the identity is constant for

.

Let
H
be the

of
G
– the set of elements

so that

is the identity on a neighborhood of
e
. Then
H
is a
\emptyset
-type-definable subgroup of
G
. Since any two elements of
\mu(b/\emptyset)
induce the same conjugation on a neighborhood of

. In particular,

is infinite, and so

. Let

with

, and then let

with

(so

and

). So

commutes with an

-definable neighborhood of the identity, and so

commutes with

from

Since

, the

-definable condition ‘

-definable neighborhood of

-definable open neighborhoods,

11.5.
Summarizing

Finally, we end the paper by giving our main theorem in full. Thus, we now drop all underlying assumptions that were present in this section until now.

Theorem 11.15
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure with the independent neighborhood property. Assume
\mathcal{M}
is non-trivial and topologically 1-based. Then there are a countable parameter set
A
, and an

, such that the following hold:

(1)

G
is open in
\mathcal{M}
.

(2)

is a topological group with the topology inherited from

.

(3)

G
is locally linear.

Proof.

By Theorem
10.10
, we can satisfy all requirements other than abelianity and local linearity. But local linearity follows by Theorem

; and by Theorem

, after further shrinking we can arrange that

is abelian.
∎

The following may also be worth noting. We leave the details for the interested reader to verify:

Remark 11.16
.

Let

. Let

Appendix A
Proving the Independent Neighborhood Property

Here, we include a brief appendix to demonstrate that various well-known structures have the independent neighborhood property. It is possible that the facts we present here were already known. In this case, our contribution is to give a unified treatment.

Assumption A.1
.

Throughout the appendix,
\mathcal{M}
is a sufficiently saturated t-minimal structure. We do not assume
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property.

A.1.
Broadness at a Point

In his paper, Johnson defines the class of

. Here we give a similar notion but applying locally near one point. This is not quite a direct translation, because Johnson’s notion of broadness is invariant under coordinate permutations and ours is not. Nevertheless, our notion will better suit the argument at hand.

Definition A.2
.

For each

-broad

-broad if there is an
a
-broad set

such that for each

is
a
-broad.

Remark A.3
.

By induction, note that the assertion ‘

is

-broad’ is

The main elementary fact we need about

.

Let

, and let

-broad.

Proof.

We induct on

. If

, this is just Fact

: if no

-broad, we can find open

containing

. Then

Now suppose

. Since

is
a
-broad, there is an
a
-broad set

so that for all

, let

be the set of

A.2.
Algebraicity over Broad Sets, Part 1

There are many examples of t-minimal theories admitting locally constant definable functions
f:M\rightarrow M
with infinite image (indeed, every failure of exchange in a t-minimal theory looks something like this). However, no such function can be a choice function: that is, one cannot hope that the constant value taken on an open set
U\subset M
always belongs to
U
(otherwise, the image of
f
would be infinite and discrete, which is impossible). Lemma
A.5
below captures this idea on a general level:

Lemma A.5
.

Let
a\in M
, let
A
be a parameter set, and let
B\subset M^{n}
be
a
-broad. If
a\in\operatorname{acl}(Ab)
for all
b\in B
, then
a\in\operatorname{acl}(A)
.

Proof.

We induct on
n
. The inductive step is straightforward: assume
n=m+1
for some
m\geq 1
, and further assume the statement is true for all values less than
n
. Let
C\subset M
be
a
-broad so that
B_{c}
is
a
-broad for each
c\in C
. For each
c\in C
, applying the inductive hypotheses to
B_{c}
over the parameter set
Ac
gives

. Now applying the base case to
C
gives that

.

The bulk of the work is the case
n=1
. So assume
B\subset M
is
a
-broad and
a\in\operatorname{acl}(Ab)
for all
b\in B
. By Lemma
A.4
, this algebraicity is witnessed uniformly on a broad set. That is, after potentially replacing
B
with a smaller (still
a
-broad) subset, we can find an
A
-definable

with the following properties:

(1)

(a,b)\in X
for all
b\in B
.

(2)

The second projection
\pi_{2}:M^{2}\rightarrow M
is at most
k
-to-1 on
X
for some integer

.

Let
\pi_{1}:M^{2}\rightarrow M
be the first projection. Let
Y
be the set of

so that the fiber
\pi_{1}^{-1}(y)\cap X
is
y
-broad. Note that
Y
is
A
-definable and contains
a
(as
\pi^{-1}(a)\supseteq B
). If

is a boundary point of

, then by t-minimality,

, and we are done. We show that

cannot be an interior point of

. Assume otherwise, and let

be an open neighborhood of

with

.

We now inductively construct elements
a_{1},a_{2},\dots\in U
and non-empty open sets

with the following properties:

(1)

a_{i}\in U_{j}
whenever
i>j
.

(2)

Each

.

Suppose
i\geq 1
and we have built all previous

and

. First, let

be the intersection of all previous

(so if

then

). Since the previous

are non-empty and form a descending chain,

is non-empty. Now let

be any element of

, noting that this will ensure (1) is satisfied.

Next, by definition

, so the fiber

is

-broad. In particular,

is infinite, so has non-empty interior by t-minimality. Now let

be any non-empty open subset of

, and note that this ensures we satisfy (2).

Finally, assume we have built

and

as above for all

. Then by (1) and (2), we have:

(3)

(a_{i},a_{j})\in X
whenever

.

Indeed, if

then (1) gives

, and so (2) gives

\pi_{2}^{-1}(a_{j})\cap X
become arbitrarily large as

, which contradicts that

is

-to-1 on

.
∎

A.3.
Algebraicity over Broad Sets, Part 2

Next we consider a counterpart to Lemma

where we consider two points. That is, suppose

is

-broad, and

for all

Definition A.6
.

if the following holds: let

be definable,
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}:M^{2}\to M

We give some examples:

.

Suppose

satisfies exchange. Then

has the few broad sets property. Indeed, let

and

be as in Definition

. If infinitely many fibers in

Example A.8
.

Say that

has the

if for all

•

Now we show that if

Example A.9
.

A.4.
Independent Parameters from a Broad Set

Assume
\mathcal{M}
has the few broad sets property. We now prove that given a tuple and a broad set at any of its coordinates, we can choose parameters from the broad sets without decreasing the dimension of the whole tuple. In many cases, this is sufficient to prove the independent neighborhood property.

We begin by proving the result at a single coordinate. We then conclude the full version inductively.

Lemma A.11
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
has the few broad sets property. Let

be

-broad for some

so that

.

Let

. Choose

so that if

belongs to any basis, then it belongs to

.

Assume

for each

. Then for each

, there is a coordinate

so that

. By Lemma

, after shrinking

x\in e

. We have two cases:

•

Case 1:

. Then applying Lemma

over the parameter set

over

.

•

Case 2:

over the parameter set

, we conclude that

. In particular, let

, thus

is also a basis for
a
over
A
. Then by the choice of

a_{i}\in e
. Thus

over

.

∎

Finally, we conclude:

Theorem A.12
.

Assume
\mathcal{M}
has the few broad types property. Fix
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in M^{n}
and a parametet set
A
. For each

, let

be an
a_{i}
-broad subset of

. Then there is

so that

.

Proof.

Choose the

inductively, at each stage working over

∎

A.5.
Concluding

Finally, we connect Theorem
A.12
to the topology:

Definition A.13
.

Say that
\mathcal{M}
is
locally broad
if the following holds: let
a\in M
and
U
an open neighborhood of
a
. Then there are
m\geq 1
and an
a
-broad set
B\subset M^{m}
so that for every
b\in B
, there is a
b
-definable open set
V_{b}
with
a\in V_{b}\subset U
.

Roughly speaking,
A.13
says that one can define small neighborhoods of
a\in M
using parameters near
a
– and in addition,
a
-broad many perturbations of those parameters should not move the neighborhood too much (or move
a
out of it). Weakly o-minimal and C-minimal theories easily have this property, as do most natural examples of t-minimal theories. For example, if
\mathcal{M}
is weakly o-minimal and
(b,c)
is a neighborhood of
a
, then one can define subneighborhoods using parameters
x
and
y
whenever
x\in(b,a)
and
y\in(a,c)
(and the set of such pairs
(x,y)
is
a
-broad). The C-minimal case follows by a similar argument.

On the other hand, a real closed field with the Sorgenfrey topology is
not
locally broad: for example, given
a
and a neighborhood
[a,b)
, the set of
(x,y)
with
a\in[x,y)\subset[a,b)
is
\{a\}\times(a,b]
, which is not
a
-broad.

Now we show:

Theorem A.14
.

Suppose
\mathcal{M}
is locally broad and has the few broad sets property. Then
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property.

Proof.

Combine the definition of local broadness with Theorem
A.12
.
∎

Corollary A.15
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is weakly o-minimal, or if
\mathcal{M}
is C-minimal, then
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property.

Corollary A.16
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is locally broad and satisfies exchange, then
\mathcal{M}
has the independent neighborhood property.

Appendix B
Independent neighborhoods in visceral theories, by Will Johnson

We show that the independent neighborhood property (Definition
3.1
) holds in any visceral theory. Recall that a
t
-minimal theory is
visceral
if it admits a definable uniformity (see, e.g.,
[
11
, Definition 1.17]
).
Dp-minimal
visceral theories were considered in
[
20
]
, and the independent neighborhood property was apparently proved for them in
[
8
, Corollary 3.12]
.

Work in a monster model
\mathcal{M}
of a visceral theory
T
in a language
\mathcal{L}
. Let
\mathcal{B}

be a definable basis for the uniformity, chosen so that basic
entourages are symmetric and open. If
E
is a basic entourage, and

a\in\mathcal{M}
, then
E[a]
denotes the “open ball”
\{x\in\mathcal{M}:(a,x)\in E\}
. The family
\{E[a]:E\in\mathcal{B}\}
is a
neighborhood basis of
a
. More generally if
\bar{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{M}^{n}
, then
E[\bar{a}]
denotes the product
\prod_{i=1}^{n}E[a_{i}]
. The family
\{E[\bar{a}]:E\in\mathcal{B}\}
is again a neighborhood basis of
\bar{a}
.

Remark B.1
.

The arguments below generalize to the case of a definable set
D

with a visceral uniformity, not necessarily definable in the induced
structure on
D
.

Recall that a partial
n
-type
p
is
broad
if, in some elementary extension, we can find infinite sets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{n}
such that every tuple in the product
S_{1}\times S_{2}\times\cdots\times S_{n}
realizes
p
. For more about broad types and sets, see
[
11
, §2.1]
.

Lemma B.2
.

For any small cardinal
\kappa
, there is a small cardinal
\lambda

such that if
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
are sets of size at least
\lambda
and
A\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is a set of size at most
\kappa
, then
there is
\bar{b}\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
such that
\operatorname{tp}(\bar{b}/A)
is
broad.

Proof.

Fix
\kappa
as in the statement. By the Erdős-Rado theorem, choose
\lambda
such that if
we color the
n
-element subsets of
\lambda
with

2^{|\mathcal{L}|+\kappa}
-many colors, then there is a homogeneous set of
size
\aleph_{0}
.

Now let
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n},A\subseteq\mathcal{M}
be given. Shrinking the

S_{i}
, we may assume
|S_{i}|=\lambda
for each
i
. Let

\{c_{i,\alpha}:\alpha<\lambda\}
be an enumeration of
S_{i}
for
each
i
. Consider the map from
n
-element subsets of
\lambda

to the type space over
A
given by

\{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\}\mapsto\operatorname{tp}(c_{1,\alpha_{1}},\ldots,c_{n,\alpha_{n}}/A)

for
\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
. Let
J\subseteq\lambda
be an infinite subset which is homogeneous of color
p
, so
that

\operatorname{tp}(c_{1,\alpha_{1}},\ldots,c_{n,\alpha_{n}}/A)=p

for all
\alpha_{1}<\cdots<\alpha_{n}
in

. Note that

(c_{1,\alpha_{1}},\ldots,c_{n,\alpha_{n}})\in\prod_{i=1}^{n}S_{i}
, so
it remains to show that

is broad, i.e., the set
p(\mathcal{M})
of
realizations is broad. Given any
k
, we can find an increasing

\beta_{1,1}<\beta_{1,2}<\cdots<\beta_{1,k}<\beta_{2,1}<\cdots<\beta_{2,k}<\cdots<\beta_{n,1}<\cdots<\beta_{n,k}

in
J
, because
J
is infinite. Then every element of

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n}\{c_{i,\beta_{i,1}},c_{i,\beta_{i,2}},\ldots,c_{i,\beta_{i,k}}\}

\displaystyle=\{c_{1,\beta_{1,1}},c_{1,\beta_{1,2}},\ldots,c_{1,\beta_{1,k}}\}\times\cdots\times\{c_{n,\beta_{n,1}},c_{n,\beta_{n,2}},\ldots,c_{n,\beta_{n,k}}\}

. As

∎

Lemma B.3
.

Let
(P,\leq)
be an
A
-definable preorder. Suppose
(P,\leq)
is
downward directed, in the sense that for any
x,y\in P
, there is

z\in P
with
z\leq x
and
z\leq y
. If
\operatorname{tp}(\bar{b}/A)
is broad
and

, there is

such that
\operatorname{tp}(\bar{b}/Ac^{\prime})

Proof.

Let
\kappa=|A|+\aleph_{0}
. Let
\lambda
be as in
Lemma
B.2
. Let

. Since

the set

contains a product

, with

\bar{e}\equiv_{A}\bar{b}

By saturation and directedness, there is some

Let

Lemma B.5
.

Let
p=\operatorname{tp}(\bar{a}/C)
, and let
p(\mathcal{M})
be the set of realizations.
Let
k=\dim(\bar{a}/C)=\dim(p)=\dim(p(\mathcal{M}))
. If
U\ni\bar{a}
is
any neighborhood, then
\dim(p(\mathcal{M})\cap U)=k
.

Proof.

Otherwise, there is a
C
-definable set

such
that
\dim(D\cap U)<k
by
[
11
, Corollary 2.42]
. Then

\dim_{\bar{a}}(D)<k
, where
\dim_{\bar{a}}(D)
is the local dimension of
D

at
\bar{a}
. Let
X=\{\bar{b}\in D:\dim_{\bar{b}}(D)<k\}\subseteq D
.
The set
X
contains
\bar{a}
and is
C
-definable (by definability of dimension
[
11
, Theorem 2.51]
). If
\bar{b}\in X

. So the local dimension of

is at most

Then
\bar{a}
belongs to the

-definable set

,

.
∎

Lemma B.6
.

Let
C
be a set. Let

be a

-definable basic entourage. For
any
\bar{b}
, there is
\bar{c}\in E[\bar{b}]\subseteq\mathcal{M}^{n}

\dim(\bar{b},\bar{c}/C)=\dim(\bar{b}/C)+\dim(\bar{c}/C)
.

Proof.

Take
E_{1}
a basic entourage such that
E_{1}\circ E_{1}\subseteq E
.
Let
C_{1}\supseteq C
be a small set defining

. Let
k=\dim(\bar{b}/C)
. Let

.
Let

be the type-definable set of tuples
\bar{b}^{\prime}
such that

\bar{b}^{\prime}\equiv_{C}\bar{b}
and
\bar{b}^{\prime}\in E_{1}[\bar{b}]

\dim(X)=\dim(\bar{b}/C)=k
. Then

\dim(X\times E_{1}[\bar{b}])=k+n

is type-definable over

is an

\displaystyle k+n=\dim(\bar{b}^{\prime},\bar{c}^{\prime}/C_{1}\bar{b})\leq\dim(\bar{b}^{\prime},\bar{c}^{\prime}/C)

\displaystyle\leq\dim(\bar{b}^{\prime}/C)+\dim(\bar{c}^{\prime}/C)\leq k+n.

it follows that

we have

\displaystyle\bar{b}^{\prime}\equiv_{C}\bar{b}

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\prime}\in E[\bar{b}^{\prime}]

\displaystyle\dim(\bar{b}^{\prime},\bar{c}^{\prime}/C)=\dim(\bar{b}^{\prime}/C)+\dim(\bar{c}^{\prime}/C)

Taking
