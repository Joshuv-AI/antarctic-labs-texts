A multicentre phase III clinical trial of dual-epigenetic therapy plus CHOP compared with CHOP in previously untreated patients with peripheral T-cell lymphoma
Authors: Chong Wei, Liping Su, Xiaobo Wang, Yang Song, Ningbin Chen, Yuanyan Tang, Aijun Liao, Wei Yang, Zhiming Li, Jing Shen, Daobin Zhou, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This phase 3 multicenter trial evaluated the efficacy and safety of adding dual epigenetic agents azacitidine and chidamide to CHOP (AC-CHOP) versus CHOP alone in previously untreated peripheral T-cell lymphoma (PTCL). A total of 128 patients were enrolled from 9 centers in China and assigned according to participating centers (AC-CHOP, N = 84; CHOP, N = 44). Azacitidine 200 mg on days 1–2, 100 mg on day 3 and chidamide 20 mg twice weekly were administered in the AC-CHOP arm. The AC-CHOP arm showed numerically higher ORR (60.7% vs. 54.6%) and CR rate (47.6% vs. 36.4%) than the CHOP arm, although the differences were not statistically significant. A trend toward improved PFS was observed in the AC-CHOP arm (median 10.2 months in the AC-CHOP arm vs. 8.4 months in the CHOP arm, P = 0.093), alongside a significantly improved OS (median 46.2 months vs. 24.1 months, P = 0.023). The safety profile was comparable between the two groups, with hematologic toxicity and infection being the most common adverse events. Genomic profiling showed that DNMT3A mutations were associated with lower response rates, while IDH2 and TP53 mutations were associated with inferior survival outcomes. In conclusion, the addition of chidamide and azacitidine to CHOP did not significantly improve ORR or PFS. Although OS was longer in the AC-CHOP arm, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the imbalances in post-protocol treatments between the two arms. Integration of molecular profiling may improve risk stratification and inform future biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies in PTCL.

Background

Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) are a heterogeneous group of mature T- and NK-cell neoplasms that encompass over 30 distinct entities in the 2022 WHO classification of haematolymphoid tumours [1]. PTCLs account for approximately 10% of all types of non-Hodgkin’s lymphomas (NHLs) in Western countries and up to 20%-30% in China [2, 3]. With the exception of anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL), of which the addition of brentuximab vedotin (BV) to the anthracycline-based regimen is considered the new standard treatment [4], uncertainty remains regarding the optimal front-line therapeutic regimen for most types of PTCLs. The treatment mainly follows the framework for aggressive B-cell NHLs, of which the CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) regimen remains to be the most commonly prescribed ‘standard’ treatment. Unfortunately, given the high risk of relapse, the prognosis of PTCLs is generally worse than their B-cell counterparts. Most types of aggressive PTCLs exhibit poor long-term survival of around 30 to 40% [5–7]. There is still a significant unmet need for the treatment of PTCLs.

Recent molecular studies revealed that PTCLs especially nodal T-follicular helper cell lymphoma (nTFHL) share recurrent mutations in epigenetic-modulating genes, such as TET2, DNMT3A, and IDH2 [8, 9]. Thus, therapies targeting epigenetic changes, such as histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors and hypomethylating agents, either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, are now being investigated in these diseases. Single-agent HDAC inhibitors, including romidepsin, belinostat, and chidamide (also known as tucidinostat) are associated with overall response rates of 25–38% in relapsed and refractory (R/R) PTCLs [10, 11]. In the phase 1b/2 study evaluating chidamide combined with CHOEP regimen in patients with untreated PTCLs, a modest efficacy was demonstrated with a complete remission (CR) rate of 40.7% and median progression-free survival (PFS) of 10.7 months [12]. In the multicenter phase 2 study of oral azacitidine plus CHOP regimen as initial treatment for PTCL, a high clinical activity with a CR rate of 75% and a 2-year PFS rate of 65.8% was reported [13]. However, the benefit of adding both azacitidine and chidamide to the CHOP regimen remains unclear.

In this study, we added azacitidine and chidamide to the CHOP backbone to form a dual-epigenetic chemotherapy regimen, termed AC-CHOP. Herein, we report the efficacy and safety of the phase 3 trial of AC-CHOP versus CHOP in patients with previously untreated PTCL.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is a multicenter, phase 3 trial conducted at 9 centers in China. Patients in the AC-CHOP group were included from the 4 leading sites, Peking Union Medical College Hospital (PUMCH), Shanxi Cancer Hospital, The Second Hospital Of Dalian Medical University, Central Hospital of Dalian University of Technology, whereas patients in the CHOP group were enrolled from the remaining five sites according to the center-assigned treatment allocation. Because treatment allocation was assigned by participating center, individual patient-level randomization and blinding were not performed. Subjects were enrolled between August 2021 and August 2024.

Eligible patients were aged 18 to 70 years and had newly diagnosed, histologically confirmed PTCL according to the 5th edition of the WHO classification of lymphoid neoplasms, including PTCL, not otherwise specified (PTCL-NOS), nTFHL (nTFHL, angioimmunoblastic T-cell lymphoma (AITL), nTFHL, follicular-type, nTFHL-NOS), ALK negative ALCL, intestinal T-cell lymphoma, and subcutaneous panniculitis-like T-cell lymphoma [1]. Central pathology review was required for all patients registered and was performed by at least two expert hematopathologists in PUMCH. The other key inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0 to 3; (2) At least one measurable lesion on computed tomography (CT) or positron emission tomography-CT (PET-CT); (3) Adequate hematopoietic function: neutrophil count ≥ 1.5 × 109/L, platelet count ≥ 80 × 109/L, hemoglobin ≥ 90 g/L; 4) Adequate organ function was required, including New York Heart Association functional class 0–2, alanine aminotransferase ≤ 3 times upper limit of normal (ULN), total bilirubin ≤ 2 times ULN, estimated glomerular filtration rate > 60 ml/min. Key exclusion criteria were: (1) ALK-positive ALCL and extranodal natural killer/T-cell lymphoma; (2) clinical evidence of central nervous system lymphoma; (3) patients combined with any unstable systemic diseases or uncontrolled active infections; (4) pregnant or lactating women.

This trial was conducted in accordance with the Good Clinical Practice Guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review boards at each participating institution. Informed consent was given by all patients. This clinical trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT05075460). The study protocol is available in Supplement material.

Procedures

The CHOP regimen consisted of cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m2 intravenously on day 1), doxorubicin (50 mg/m2 intravenously on day 1), vincristine (1.4 mg/m2 intravenously on day 1), and prednisone (60 mg/m2 orally on days 1–5). Each cycle of the AC-CHOP regimen consisted of azacitidine 200 mg subcutaneously on days 1–2 and 100 mg on day 3, chidamide 20 mg orally twice weekly, combined with the CHOP regimen. Both AC-CHOP and CHOP regimens were repeated every three weeks. Six cycles of treatment were planned in both groups.

Supportive care and post-remission management were not fully mandated by protocol and could vary according to institutional practice. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor and anti-infective prophylaxis, including antiviral prophylaxis such as acyclovir for herpesvirus prevention and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PJP) prophylaxis, were permitted according to individual institutional guidelines. Post-remission decisions regarding hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and chidamide maintenance were made according to site practice and investigator discretion and were not protocol-mandated components of the primary endpoint assessment. For maintenance therapy, chidamide was administered at 20 mg twice weekly until disease progression, unacceptable toxicity, or completion of 2 years of treatment.

Assessments

PET-CT was evaluated at baseline, interim (after three cycles), and final evaluation (6-8 weeks after completion of therapy) according to 2014 Lugano criteria for non-Hodgkin lymphoma [14]. Central response assessment was performed by PUMCH. Patients showing no response to chemotherapy (progressive disease or stable disease) at either the interim or end-of-treatment evaluation were withdrawn from the trial and administered salvage therapy. Following treatment completion, follow-up included clinical examinations every 3 months for the first 2 years and every 6 months for the subsequent 3 years. Surveillance contrast-enhanced CT or PET-CT scans were conducted every 6 months for the first 2 years and annually thereafter. Adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE), version 5.0. Efficacy and safety analyses were conducted in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint in this study was overall response rate (ORR) at the end of treatment, defined as the proportion of patients with CR or PR. The secondary endpoints included CR rate at the end of treatment, as well as PFS, overall survival (OS) and safety. PFS was defined as the time from the date of patient enrollment to the date of disease relapse, progression, death due to any cause, or receipt of subsequent systemic therapy to treat residual progressive disease, whichever came first. OS was defined as the time from the date of patient enrollment to the date of death from any cause and was censored at the date of the last follow-up. The ITT population was used for the primary and secondary end points analyses.

Targeted sequencing

Targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples using a custom panel encompassing 413 lymphoma-related genes (Oncolym Panel, Geneplus-Beijing, Beijing, China). The full list of genes is provided in the supplementary material (Supplementary Table S1). Matched buccal mucosa samples were collected and sequenced to exclude germline variants. Genomic DNA from both FFPE tissues and buccal samples was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina HiSeq 3000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), achieving an average sequencing depth of 1552.4 ± 464.3. Sequence reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using BWA aligner (version 0.5.9). Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions/deletions (indels) were annotated using the Exome Aggregation Consortium, 1000 Genome Project, and COSMIC database. Somatic SNVs and indels in both hotspot and non-hotspot regions were reported with a minimum of ≥4 and ≥8 supporting reads, respectively. A minimum variant allele frequency (VAF) of 1.0% in tumor genomic DNA was required for variant calling.

Sample size estimation and statistical analysis

Sample size determination was based on the primary efficacy endpoint ORR. A one-sided significance level (α) of 0.05 and a desired statistical power of no less than 80% were set. According to previous studies, the ORR of the CHOP regimen was estimated at 50% (null hypothesis), while the ORR of the AC-CHOP regimen was expected to reach 75% (alternative hypothesis), reflecting a 25% absolute difference between groups. Considering the feasibility of patient enrollment and the expected number of patients enrolled at each participating center, a 2:1 sample size allocation ratio (AC-CHOP: CHOP) was adopted. The calculated result indicated that at least 67 patients in the AC-CHOP group and 34 patients in the CHOP group would be required to achieve a power of 80.51% under the above assumptions. Assuming an approximate 20% dropout rate during the study period, the final planned enrollment was set at 84 patients in the AC-CHOP group and 43 patients in the CHOP group, totaling 127 patients. The planned accrual period was 36 months, with an additional follow-up duration of 12 months to observe the primary and secondary endpoints.

Differences between categorical variables and continuous variables were assessed using the χ2 test and the Mann-Whitney U test. Survival analyses were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival rates between groups were compared using the log-rank test. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Estimation of the hazard ratio (HR) was based upon the unstratified Cox regression model. Estimation of the odds ratio (OR) was based on the unstratified logistic regression model. All cases were analyzed on an intention-to-treat basis. Subgroup analyses of ORR, CR, PFS, and OS were not prespecified for formal hypothesis testing, were not adjusted for multiplicity, and should be considered hypothesis-generating. All data analyses were performed using the SPSS 20.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

From August 2021 to August 2024, a total of 136 patients were screened for eligibility and 128 were enrolled in the study. Patients were assigned according to participating centers, of which 84 were assigned to the AC-CHOP arm and 44 were assigned to the CHOP arm. Protocol adherence

A total of 56 patients (66.7%) in the AC-CHOP arm and 27 patients (61.4%) in the CHOP arm completed all six planned treatment cycles. The main reason for treatment discontinuation in both groups was insufficient efficacy. In the AC-CHOP group, 19 patients discontinued due to lack of response, 2 due to severe infections, 2 due to toxicity (including one case of renal dysfunction and one case of myelosuppression), and 5 patients withdrew consent. In the CHOP group, 13 patients discontinued due to lack of response, 2 due to severe infections, 1 due to toxicity (myelosuppression), and 1 withdrew consent.

Response to treatment

Post-protocol treatments

Post-protocol treatments were summarized in Supplementary Table S2. In total, 18 patients in the AC-CHOP arm underwent HSCT, including 12 who received auto-HSCT after first-line remission, 4 who received auto-HSCT following salvage therapy, and 2 who received allo-HSCT after salvage therapy. In the CHOP arm, 7 patients underwent HSCT, including 3 who received auto-HSCT after first-line remission, 3 who received auto-HSCT following salvage therapy, and 1 who received allo-HSCT after first-line remission. In addition, 25 patients (29.8%) in the AC-CHOP arm and 9 patients (20.5%) in the CHOP arm received chidamide maintenance after first-line remission, with a longer median duration of maintenance in the AC-CHOP arm than in the CHOP arm (16 months [range, 2–24] vs. 6 months [range, 3–24]). Furthermore, 43 patients (51.2%) in the AC-CHOP arm and 25 patients (56.8%) in the CHOP arm received salvage therapy, with a median of 1.5 (range, 1–4) and 1 (range, 1–4) subsequent treatment lines, respectively. Among patients who received salvage therapy, platinum/gemcitabine-based chemotherapy was the most common category in both arms. BV-containing regimens (37.2% in AC-CHOP arm vs. 4.0% in CHOP arm) and targeted therapies, including golidocitinib, linperlisib, and EZH2 inhibitors (48.8% in AC-CHOP arm vs. 12.0% in CHOP arm), were more frequently used in the AC-CHOP arm, whereas chidamide-containing salvage regimens were more common in the CHOP arm (48.0% in CHOP arm vs. 16.3% in AC-CHOP arm).

Survival analysis

As of September 2025, the median follow-up time was 21.3 months in the AC-CHOP group and 21.7 months in the CHOP group. As of the data cutoff, 57 deaths had occurred, including 31 in the AC-CHOP arm and 26 in the CHOP arm. The leading cause of death in both arms was disease progression. Other causes included severe pneumonia (n = 6, 2 in the AC-CHOP arm and 4 in the CHOP arm), 1 bloodstream infection in the CHOP arm, 1 septic shock in the CHOP arm, 1 cryptococcal meningitis in the AC-CHOP arm, 2 second primary malignancies (one in each arm), and 1 airway obstruction in the CHOP arm. The median OS was significantly longer in the AC-CHOP group at 46.2 months (95% CI: 30.1–46.2) compared with 24.1 months (95% CI: 21.5–27.3) in the CHOP group (HR: 0.55; 95% CI: 0.32–0.96; P = 0.023) (Fig. 2B). The 1-year and 2-year OS rates were 82.4% (95% CI: 74.0–90.8) and 69.2% (95% CI: 58.6–79.8), respectively, in the AC-CHOP group, versus 85.8% (95% CI: 75.3–96.4) and 50.1% (95% CI: 33.8–66.3) in the CHOP group.

In exploratory subgroup analyses of PFS and OS, a numerically favorable signal for AC-CHOP was observed for PFS among patients with Ann Arbor stage I–II disease (HR 0.27, 95% CI: 0.08–0.94; Figure S3), and for OS among patients with ECOG 0–1 (HR 0.52, 95% CI: 0.28–0.95), normal LDH (HR 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.80), and Ann Arbor stage I–II disease (HR 0.11, 95% CI: 0.01–0.91) (Figure S4). However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the subgroup analyses were underpowered, not prespecified for formal hypothesis testing, and not adjusted for multiplicity.

Among the different histologic subtypes, patients with nTFHL exhibited comparable PFS and OS to those with PTCL-NOS, both within the AC-CHOP arm (Figure S5) and across the entire cohort (Fig. 3A, B). Patients who achieved CR at the end of treatment had significantly longer PFS and OS compared with those who achieved PR or were non-responders (Figure S6). Mutational profile and prognostic analyses

NGS was performed on FFPE tumor samples from 51 patients in the AC-CHOP arm and 12 patients in the CHOP arm. The AC-CHOP cohort comprised nTFHL (N = 30), PTCL-NOS (N = 20), and intestinal T-cell lymphoma (N = 1), whereas the CHOP cohort included nTFHL (N = 5) and PTCL-NOS (N = 7). Somatic mutations were detected in 61/63 patients (96.8%), with a total of 509 mutations spanning 188 genes. The mutational landscape of both treatment arms is summarized in Fig. 4A, B, with separate oncoplots displayed for the AC-CHOP (Fig. 4A) and CHOP (Fig. 4B) cohorts. The recurrently affected pathways included DNA methylation (e.g., TET2, DNMT3A, and IDH2), histone modification (e.g., KMT2C, KMT2D, KDM6A, SETD2, CREBBP, and EP300), chromatin remodeler (e.g., ARID1A and ARID1B), TCR signaling (e.g., RHOA, CD28, FYN, PLCG1, and CARD11), JAK-STAT signaling (e.g., SOCS1, JAK3, and STAT3), PI3K-AKT signaling (e.g., PI3KR1, and AKT1), as well as tumor suppressor (e.g., TP53 and ATM). The median number of mutations per sample was 8.3 (range, 1–21), and the mean VAF was 14.1%. We next assessed the impact of tumor mutations on treatment response. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for CR and ORR, focusing on somatic mutations with a frequency ≥12% as well as TP53 mutation. In the AC-CHOP arm, DNMT3A mutations were significantly associated with inferior outcomes, correlating with lower CR rate (OR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.02–0.80, P = 0.029) and ORR (OR = 0.12, 95% CI: 0.02–0.76, P = 0.024) (Fig. 4D, F). In an exploratory pooled analysis including patients from both treatment arms, DNMT3A mutation showed a borderline association with lower CR rate (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.04–1.03, P = 0.055) (Fig. 4C).

To further evaluate the prognostic relevance of these mutations, multivariable Cox regression analyses were conducted for PFS and OS. In the AC-CHOP arm, IDH2 and TP53 mutations were associated with significantly worse OS (IDH2: HR = 12.61, 95% CI: 1.15–138.87, P = 0.038; TP53: HR = 6.85, 95% CI: 1.09–43.11, P = 0.040), whereas RHOA mutations were associated with improved OS (HR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.92, P = 0.042) (Fig. 4H). Similar patterns were observed in the pooled analysis including both arms (IDH2: HR = 17.92, 95% CI: 1.71–187.73, P = 0.016; TP53: HR = 13.37, 95% CI: 3.11–57.52, P < 0.001; RHOA: HR = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.01–0.84, P = 0.036) (Fig. 4G). No mutation was significantly associated with PFS in either the arm-specific or pooled analyses. In addition, TET2 mutations were not associated with treatment response or survival outcomes.

Safety

The overall safety profile was manageable in both arms (Table 3). Hematologic toxicities were the most common grade 3-5 adverse events in both groups. Grade 3-5 neutropenia (38.1% vs. 36.4%), febrile neutropenia (17.9% vs. 13.6%), thrombocytopenia (15.5% vs. 11.4%), and anemia (9.5% vs. 11.4%) were observed in the AC-CHOP and CHOP arms, respectively. Among non-hematologic adverse events, most were grade 1-2. Grade 3-5 ALT elevation was numerically more frequent in the AC-CHOP arm than in the CHOP arm (10.7% vs. 6.8%). Of note, 5 cases of PJP occurred in the AC-CHOP arm, whereas no cases were observed in the CHOP arm. PJP prophylaxis was not mandated by protocol, and none of the 5 affected patients had received prophylaxis before onset. Diagnosis was based on typical clinical manifestations, characteristic chest CT findings including ground-glass opacities and interstitial changes, and positive sputum PJP-DNA testing. All affected patients received sulfonamide-based therapy, including adjunctive corticosteroids in 4 patients for hypoxemia, and all recovered. Details of these cases are summarized in Supplementary Table S3. Neither EBV nor hepatitis B virus reactivation were observed in either group. Discussion

Epigenetic dysregulation plays an important role in the PTCL pathogenesis, particularly in nTFHL. There is emerging evidence supporting the use of epigenetic therapies either as single agents or in combination with chemotherapy for PTCL. The efficacy of single agent of HDAC inhibitors remains modest in patients with R/R PTCLs [10, 11]. In contrast, oral 5-azacitidine, a demethylating agent, has shown high efficacy in a retrospective series of R/R AITL patients, with an ORR of 75% and a median PFS of 15 months [15]. Further investigations into combination therapies have yielded encouraging results. For instance, the combination of oral 5-azacitidine with romidepsin for the treatment of treatment-naïve or relapsed/refractory PTCL achieved an overall response rate of 61% and a median progression-free survival of 8 months [16]. Building on these findings, recent studies have also explored epigenetic therapies combined with CHOP regimen as first-line treatment for PTCL. A phase II study evaluating the addition of oral 5-azacitidine to the CHOP regimen as an initial treatment for PTCL reported a CR rate of 75% and a 2-year PFS of 65.8% [13]. A phase II study of chidamide combined with CHOEP demonstrated moderate efficacy, with a CR rate of 40.7% and a median PFS of 10.7 months [12]. Romidepsin has been evaluated in combination with CHOP therapy in a phase III trial, but this combination did not improve PFS, response rates, or OS and was associated with increased toxicity in previously untreated PTCL patients [17].

In our study, the primary efficacy endpoint of ORR was not met. The addition of chidamide and azacitidine to CHOP did not confer a statistically significant improvement in ORR or CR rate compared with CHOP alone, with ORR/CR rates of 60.7%/47.6% versus 54.6%/36.4%, respectively. When interpreted in the context of prior epigenetic agent-containing regimens in PTCL (Table 4), the efficacy of AC-CHOP appears more modest. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, epigenetic drug exposure differed substantially. In the oral azacitidine plus CHOP study, CC-486 was administered at 300 mg daily for 14 days per cycle, whereas in our study azacitidine was given subcutaneously over only 3 days. The design of AC-CHOP was informed in part by our prior prospective experience with subcutaneous azacitidine plus chidamide in relapsed/refractory AITL, in which azacitidine was administered at 100 mg daily for 7 days every 4 weeks (total dose 700 mg) [18]. The present schedule used a reduced total dose of 500 mg over 3 days every 3 weeks to improve feasibility and facilitate integration with a 21-day CHOP backbone. This abbreviated schedule may have provided less sustained epigenetic modulation than more prolonged azacitidine exposure. Second, our study allowed broader inclusion of patients with poorer performance status, as reflected by the less restrictive ECOG eligibility criteria. Third, differences in histologic composition may also have contributed. The majority of enrolled patients in our cohort had PTCL-NOS or nTFHL, whereas some prior studies were more enriched for TFH-derived disease. Importantly, ALK-positive ALCL was excluded per protocol, and ALK-negative ALCL was also underrepresented, due to the widespread adoption of BV-CHP following the ECHELON-2 study. The secondary end point analysis demonstrated a borderline advantage in PFS (median 10.2 months vs. 8.4 months, P = 0.093) and significantly improved OS (median 46.2 months vs. 24.1 months, P = 0.023) in the AC-CHOP arm compared to the CHOP arm. Standardized reporting of post-protocol treatments demonstrated imbalances in post-remission consolidation, chidamide maintenance exposure, and subsequent salvage therapy between the two arms. In particular, patients in the AC-CHOP arm more frequently underwent upfront HSCT consolidation, had longer chidamide maintenance exposure, and were more often treated with BV-containing or targeted salvage regimens. In addition, differences in supportive care, post-protocol therapies, and access to salvage treatments across centers may also have contributed to the OS divergence despite similar initial response rates. Taken together, these factors represent important potential confounders, and the longer OS observed in the AC-CHOP arm should therefore not be attributed solely to the frontline regimen.

In previous studies, nTFHL exhibited higher response rate and prolonged response duration to epigenetic modulating therapy compared to non-nTFHL subtypes [13, 16]. In the exploratory analysis of the phase III trial of romidepsin combined with CHOP therapy, a significant PFS improvement was also observed in the Ro-CHOP arm for PTCL patients with a centrally confirmed TFH phenotype [17]. NTFHL also showed more favorable response to oral 5-azacitidine plus CHOP regimen and higher 2-year PFS compared with PTCL-NOS [13]. This lineage-selective sensitivity of nTFHL may be due to higher mutation rates of epigenetic genes in these patients [19]. Our study did not demonstrate a clear differential benefit in nTFHL versus PTCL-NOS subtypes. Although patients with nTFHL in the AC-CHOP arm had numerically higher ORR and CR rates, their PFS and OS were comparable to those with PTCL-NOS. This finding may reflect the modest sample size and the biological heterogeneity within histologic subtypes. Importantly, because PTCL-NOS, nTFHL, and rare entities were analyzed collectively in this study, the lack of benefit observed with AC-CHOP should not be definitively generalized to individual PTCL subtypes, particularly those with known sensitivity to epigenetic modulation. These results also underscore the need for molecular stratification beyond histological subtype when evaluating epigenetic-based therapies in PTCL. More broadly, the numerically favorable signals observed in selected exploratory subgroup analyses, including the PFS signal in patients with Ann Arbor stage I–II disease and the OS signals in patients with ECOG 0–1 and Ann Arbor stage I–II disease, should also be interpreted cautiously, as these analyses were exploratory, involved relatively small numbers of patients, and were not adjusted for multiplicity.

Regarding safety, both regimens were generally manageable. Hematologic toxicities were the most common adverse events in both arms, with grade 3-5 neutropenia and febrile neutropenia occurring numerically more frequently in the AC-CHOP arm, consistent with the added myelosuppressive effects of dual epigenetic therapy combined with CHOP. Among non-hematologic toxicities, most events were low grade, although grade 3-5 ALT elevation was also numerically higher with AC-CHOP, suggesting a modest increase in hepatic toxicity. Of additional concern, 5 cases of PJP were observed in the AC-CHOP arm, whereas none occurred in the CHOP arm. Because PJP prophylaxis was not mandated by protocol, variation in preventive practice across sites may have contributed in part to this imbalance. However, the clustering of all PJP cases in the AC-CHOP arm raises concern that dual epigenetic therapy combined with CHOP may confer additional immunosuppressive risk. These findings support close monitoring for opportunistic infections and suggest that routine consideration of PJP prophylaxis should be strongly considered in future studies evaluating dual epigenetic therapy plus CHOP.

The role of consolidation with autologous stem cell transplant (auto-SCT) remains an area of ongoing debate in PTCL. While studies such as the Nordic Lymphoma Group trial [20] and other registry-based analyses [5, 21] have supported its use in eligible patients, the real-world uptake remains limited. In our study, only 9.1% and 14.3% of patients in the CHOP and AC-CHOP arms, respectively, received upfront HSCT consolidation. This low percentage may reflect the relatively older age of the patient population, as well as geographic variations in economic status and clinical practice patterns, with many centers in China setting an upper age limit of 65 years for auto-SCT. Broadening access to auto-HSCT after first remission may further improve long-term outcomes of patients with PTCL. Interestingly, among patients in remission, chidamide maintenance was associated with a significant OS benefit. Similarly, our previous study also demonstrated that chidamide maintenance yielded OS outcomes comparable to those achieved with SCT and significantly improved compared to patients on observation [22]. These observations suggest that chidamide maintenance may be a feasible post-remission strategy for patients ineligible for HSCT due to age or comorbidities, although this finding requires prospective validation.

Consistent with prior genomic studies of PTCL, we observed frequent mutations in epigenetic regulators (TET2, DNMT3A, IDH2), signaling pathways (TCR JAK-STAT, PI3K-AKT), and tumor suppressors (TP53, ATM) [23, 24]. Multivariable analyses identified several mutations with potential prognostic relevance. DNMT3A mutations were significantly associated with lower CR in the AC-CHOP arm, and a borderline association was also observed in the pooled analysis, suggesting a consistent trend toward inferior response. These findings indicate that the adverse impact of DNMT3A mutations may not be fully mitigated in the context of the AC-CHOP regimen. One possible explanation is that DNMT3A mutations in AITL are frequently linked to clonal hematopoiesis in our previously published study, which has been associated with inferior outcomes and may reflect early, non–tumor-defining events [25]. In addition, DNMT3A mutations commonly co-occur with TET2 mutations, and the coexistence of these alterations may contribute to a more complex epigenetic landscape that is less responsive to hypomethylating therapy. IDH2 and TP53 were associated with inferior OS. Notably, TET2 mutations, despite their high frequency, were not independently associated with treatment response or survival outcomes, in contrast to prior reports [13]. These results suggest that mutational profiling may have potential value for risk stratification in PTCL, although its role in guiding therapeutic decision-making requires further validation. For example, patients harboring DNMT3A mutations, particularly those with concurrent clonal hematopoiesis, may represent a higher-risk subgroup that could benefit from further investigation of alternative or combination strategies beyond epigenetic-based regimens. Recent preclinical studies have suggested that metformin may influence the expansion of DNMT3A-mutant hematopoietic clones, offering a potential therapeutic strategy [26]. However, the clinical relevance of this approach in PTCL remains unclear and warrants further investigation. Similarly, TP53-mutant PTCL, often associated with primary chemoresistance, may warrant novel combination approaches, including immunotherapeutic strategies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, because PTCL is a rare and biologically heterogeneous disease, conducting randomized comparative trials in this setting is inherently challenging. Our study therefore adopted a center-assigned comparative phase III design to enhance feasibility. However, this approach may also have introduced selection bias and inter-center variation in supportive care, consolidation strategies, and post-protocol management. Second, the relatively small sample size, combined with the inherent heterogeneity of PTCL subtypes, reduced the statistical power of subgroup analyses. Third, the azacitidine dosing schedule was selected primarily for clinical feasibility and to facilitate integration with a 21-day CHOP backbone, rather than as a biologically optimized exposure schedule. Further studies are needed to determine the optimal regimen that balances tolerability with more effective and durable disease control. In addition, the number of patients with available sequencing data remained limited and imbalanced between the two treatment arms. As a result, treatment-by-biomarker interaction analyses were not feasible, and the genomic findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive conclusions.

Based on the findings of our study, the addition of chidamide and azacitidine to the CHOP regimen did not demonstrate a significant improvement in ORR and PFS compared to the CHOP regimen alone for patients with PTCL. Exploratory genomic analyses suggested that DNMT3A mutations were associated with lower response rates, whereas IDH2 and TP53 mutations were associated with inferior OS. Future research should incorporate molecular diagnostics to refine risk stratification and guide biomarker-driven therapeutic strategies.
