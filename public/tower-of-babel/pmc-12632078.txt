Interpretable machine learning for classification and risk factor identification of anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms after the full opening of China’s COVID-19 lockdown
Authors: Zhihao Li, Jinfeng Li, Anqi Zhang, Pengpeng Liu, Guanli Su, Zidan Wang, Yujing Zhang, Yujie Wang, Hao Wu, Yuxia Ma, Jun Ge, Mengyang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMental health disorders remain a critical global public health issue, exacerbated by the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Post-lockdown mental health assessments are scarce. This study aims to develop interpretable machine learning (ML) models to classify and identify key risk factors for anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms in mainland China after the opening of COVID-19 lockdown.MethodsCross-sectional data collected in 2023 and 2024 were used as the training set (with test set divided in a 7:3 ratio) and internal validation set, respectively. The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scale, and Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) scale were employed to assess symptoms of anxiety, depression, and insomnia, respectively. Seven interpretable machine learning models incorporating 27 relevant influencing factors were applied for the classification and risk factor identification of three mental health symptoms.ResultsThe survey included 65,292 respondents with 36,996 cases in the training set (with test set) and 28,296 cases in the external validation set. The Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) model achieved the highest Area Under the Curve (AUC) value among seven ML models, with 0.817, 0.829, and 0.819 for anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms in the test set, and with 0.815, 0.820, and 0.822 for anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms in the external validation set. Several factors associated with a lower prevalence of anxiety symptom, including satisfactory neighborhood relationship, extensive knowledge about COVID-19, maintaining a regular sleep-wake cycle, daily vegetable consumption, and sufficient time spent sunbathing. Conversely, factors associated with a higher prevalence comprised history of anxiety, history of insomnia, history of depression, external noise at home, and fear of COVID-19 infection. Similar results were observed for depression and insomnia symptoms.ConclusionsThe explainable ML model found that healthy habits and positive social ties were linked to lower anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms. In contrast, past mental health issues and stressors were tied to higher levels. Public health efforts should focus on encouraging these modifiable habits and strengthening community support, to help improve residents’ mental health, especially in the aftermath of a pandemic.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07569-7.Keywords: Mental health, Machine learning, Influencing factor, Categorical Boost, SHapley Additive exPlanations

Introduction

Since early 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic, officially designated as the coronavirus disease 2019, has profoundly affected daily life worldwide [1, 2], leading to significant physical, mental, and economic consequences. Mental disorders have emerged as a major social issue, exacerbated by the prolonged threat of the pandemic, economic instability, social isolation, and widespread uncertainty [3–5]. Common psychological responses include fear, anxiety, depression, traumatic stress, and insomnia [6–8]. Extensive evidence showed that the COVID-19 pandemic has intensified psychological distress [9], worsened chronic conditions [10], and increased substance abuse [11], with marginalized groups being particularly vulnerable [12].

Studies conducted in the early phases of the COVID-19 have revealed the significant mental health impacts of the pandemic. However, focusing solely on residents’ mental health during the pandemic is far from sufficient. With the opening up of epidemic prevention policies in various countries, the long-term trends of psychological well-being demand sustained attention. On December 7, 2022, China announced the full opening of COVID‑19 lockdown policies [13]. After approximately three years of pandemic restrictions, the normal production and daily life of residents have been greatly challenged, so it is crucial to give continuous and long-term monitoring of residents’ mental health.

The psychological well-beings of the residents are associated with a confluence of intricate factors, including but not limited to socioeconomic and demographic determinants, individual traits, disease characteristics, lifestyle, dietary habits, as well as external environmental factors. Individuals subjected to adverse socioeconomic conditions, such as poverty [14], educational disparities [15], and employment instability [16], are at an increased risk of experiencing mental health challenges. These factors intersect with demographic variables like age, gender, and ethnicity, further modulating the risk profile for mental disorders. Individual traits, such as personality, cognitive functioning, and genetic predisposition, play a crucial role in shaping mental health outcomes [17]. Disease characteristics, the presence of physical illnesses, their chronicity and management are associated with mental well-being [18]. Comorbidity of mental and physical health conditions is closely related to the exacerbated mental health issues [19]. Lifestyle and dietary habits are also important factors correlated with mental health. A survey study conducted in South Korea indicated that maintaining regular sleep patterns and engaging in consistent exercise are associated with a reduced risk of depression, while frequent smoking is linked to an increased risk [20]. Studies have also indicated that a dietary pattern characterized by high consumption of fruits and vegetables, positively correlated with psychological conditions among women [21]. In addition, a comfortable external environment characterized by good greenery, good air quality, and positive community relationships, is also associated with the maintenance of healthy mental health [22]. Therefore, it is crucial to clarify the comprehensive correlation of multiple factors on psychological well-being.

As for the statistical method used for the analysis of the influencing factors and mental health, traditional regression models such as linear regression and logistic regression are widely used because of their transparency and interpretability [23–26]. However, these linear regression models have several limitations in practical applications. On one hand, linear models assume that the independent variables are mutually independent, making it difficult to handle situations where multicollinearity exists between variables [27]. On the other hand, the linearity assumption poses challenges when dealing with complex nonlinear relationships between independent and dependent variables [28]. In contrast, machine learning (ML) methods can handle complex nonlinear problems, with examining a wider array of socioeconomic, demographic, individual, and environmental factors as potential predictors of COVID-19 related distress simultaneously in our study. Of course, machine learning models also have drawbacks. They are often criticized as “black boxes” because their decision-making process is opaque, making it difficult to explain how specific inputs lead to their final predictions [29]. This opacity poses significant challenges in domains where interpretability is essential for trust, accountability, and ethical deployment—such as clinical medicine and public health [30]. In response, interpretable ML approaches such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [31] have been developed to illuminate model mechanics and facilitate our understanding.

This study aims to classify three common mental health symptoms of anxiety, depression, and insomnia, and identify influencing factors among residents in mainland China, after the full lifting of COVID-19 lockdown. Based on data from two cross-sectional surveys conducted in 2023 and 2024, we employed machine learning and SHAP methods to identify and rank the importance of mental health-related factors, while also performing independent external validation for the three mental health symptoms using the 2024 dataset. This research holds significant implications for improving public mental health and facilitating the smooth recovery of social and economic activities in the post-COVID-19 era.

Methods

Study population

The presented data were obtained from two cross-sectional nationwide survey conducted by Hebei Medical University, in August 2023 and August 2024, respectively. Hebei Medical University recruited approximately 1,500 university student investigators through its official platform. The student investigators came from 31 provinces of the Chinese mainland, ensuring the representativeness of the survey data at a national level. Each investigator was assigned to survey more than 20 subjects, thereby ensuring a total of approximately 30,000 respondents. The electronic questionnaire was published through an online platform called “Wenjuanxing” (https://www.wjx.cn/), and convenient sampling methods were used to collect the respondents in mainland China. All survey data were collected in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. And all participants provided electronically signed informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire.

Several data quality control measures offered by the “Wenjuanxing” platform was implemented. First, forced response was enabled for all critical items to prevent missing data. Second, skip logic was applied to tailor the questionnaire flow based on previous answers, reducing respondent burden and improving relevance. Third, duplicate submission prevention (via IP address restriction) was activated to ensure the independence of each response. Additionally, we excluded participants with any of the following characteristics, non-residents of the Chinese mainland, aged younger than 18 years old, and the answer time is less than one minute. Of all the 70,849 participants in two surveys, 65,292 cases were included finally in this study, with 36,996 cases in the first survey and 28,296 cases in the second survey, respectively (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hebei Medical University (2023001).

The workflow of sample selection

Data processing

The mental health outcomes in this study were anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptom events. Three outcomes were assessed using the corresponding standardized scales. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale, a self-report questionnaire used for screening for anxiety symptoms [32]. GAD-7 scores range from 0 to 21, and a score greater than 4 was defined as the presence of anxiety symptoms [33]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.948 and 0.949 in the first and second study, respectively. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) scale [34]. PHQ-9 scores range from 0 to 27, and a score greater than 4 was defined as depression symptoms [35]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.941 and 0.942 in the first and second study, respectively. Insomnia symptoms were assessed using the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) scale [36]. ISI scores range from 0 to 28, and a score greater than 7 was defined as insomnia symptoms [37]. The Cronbach’s α coefficients were 0.929 and 0.931 in the first and second study, respectively.

Based on professional knowledge, literature reviews, and the characteristics of questionnaire-collected information [38–41], we have amassed a total of 27 factors that potentially associated with psychological health (see Additional file 1 for detailed information and sources). These factors spanned across economic and demographic characteristics, comorbidity spectra, individual’s lifestyle and dietary habits, living environmental elements, and COVID-19 related information (Table 1, Additional file 1). Socioeconomic and demographic features included age, gender, and per capita monthly income. Disease-specific features included chronic diseases history, past anxiety, past depression, and past insomnia. Individual’s lifestyle and dietary habits included smoking, drinking, diet type, daily routine habits, electronic entertainment time, exercise, and the frequency of consuming foods including vegetables, fruits, dairy products, and animal foods. Living environment features included whether there are pets, plants, sunlight, and external noise in the household, whether there is park or garden near home, whether there is a harmonious neighborhood relationship, and a harmonious community environment. COVID-19 related information included the infection status of the COVID-19, the knowledge about COVID-19, and the concern about the COVID-19.

Statistical analysis

A diverse suite of seven machine learning algorithms from four distinct methodological families was selected to analyze the relationship between the influencing factors and the three mental health outcomes. This multi-model approach was adopted to rigorously ensure the robustness and reliability of our findings, rather than relying on a single model’s potentially biased perspective. The seven ML algorithms included Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), logistic regression (LR), Adaptive Boosting (ADB), naive Bayes (NB), and neural network (NNET). Tree-based ensemble models including RF, XGB, CatBoost, and ADB, were highly effective at automatically detecting complex non-linear patterns and interactions without requiring prior specification, thereby reducing human bias. Linear model (represented by LR) served as a strong and interpretable baseline, helping to benchmark the performance of more complex models and validate core linear effects. Probabilistic model (represented by NB) provided a probabilistically distinct approach based on Bayesian principles, and its consistency with other models strengthened confidence in the results. Finally, the neural network (represented by NNET) was employed for its high flexibility to capture subtle and complex relationships that might be overlooked by other models. The hyper-parameters of each ML algorithm were listed in Additional file 2.

The data from 2023 was split into a training set and a test set in a 7:3 ratio, used for model training and parameter tuning, respectively. The data from 2024 was reserved as an external validation set to assess the model’s real-world generalizability. And the performance on the test set and external set served as the primary outcomes reported in this study. In addition, the models’ performance was evaluated using several key metrics, including the Area Under the Curve (AUC), Sensitivity (SE), Specificity (SP), and F1 Score (F1). These metrics collectively provided a robust and multidimensional assessment of the model’s predictive capability.

For the interpretation of the ML model results, we used the SHAP method, a method based on cooperative game theory that quantifies feature contributions to predictions [31]. SHAP values measure the marginal effect of each feature on the output by comparing predictions with and without that feature. Features with larger absolute SHAP values have greater influence. As a model-agnostic approach, SHAP is compatible with various algorithms including linear models, tree-based methods, and neural networks [42]. We aggregated and averaged SHAP values across all patients to assess feature importance. The summary plot displays the overall impact and ranking of features, while dependence plots show individual feature effects, with color indicating feature value (yellow for high, blue for low) [43]. For clarity, graphical representations used the top 50% of predictors in this study, with the top 14 features selected based on average absolute SHAP value.

Age variables with nonnormal distribution were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were presented as numbers and frequencies, and Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparisons. R software version 4.3.0 was used for all analysis in this study [44]. The “caret” package [45], “mlr3” package [46], and “mlr3” related packages (including mlr3measures, mlr3verse, mlr3tuning and mlr3extralearners) were used for machine learning analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P-values less than 0.05 as statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics

The survey included 65,292 respondents with 36,996 cases in the total training and test set (data in 2023) and 28,296 cases in the external validation set (data in 2024), respectively (Table 1). Specifically, in the total training and test set, the corresponding survey cases (proportion) of anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms were 11,517 (31.13%), 15,307 (41.37%), and 13,747 (37.16%), while in the external validation cohort, the corresponding survey cases (proportion) were 9,558 (33.78%), 12,032 (42.52%), and 10,657 (37.66%), respectively (Additional file 3). In the total training and test set, age was centered at 21 (20, 35) years, 41% were male, and 14% had chronic diseases. In the external validation set, age was also centered at 21 (20, 35) years, 40% were male, and 15% had chronic diseases. In both datasets, the demographic characteristics of the data were basically balanced and comparable. In addition, people who suffered from chronic diseases, had a history of mental diseases, had unhealthy lifestyles and eating habits, and had a bad living environment were associated with a greater risk of mental health problems (Table 1).

Variables selection

To select variables for the final model, separate analyses were conducted for anxiety (Fig. 2 A), depression, (Fig. 2 C) and insomnia (Fig. 2 E). All 27 variables from the dataset were initially included. Variables were iteratively removed based on the lowest SHAP values—reflecting their minimal contribution to predictive performance—until the highest AUC was achieved. As shown in Fig. 2, retaining all 27 variables yielded the highest AUC values across all three outcomes (Fig. 2 BDF). Therefore, the full set of features was selected as the final model, balancing predictive power and interpretability through SHAP-based evaluation.

SHAP importance plot and AUC screening curves. Figure 2 presents the SHAP importance plot and AUC screening curves illustrating the predictive performance of the training set for anxiety (A, B), depression (C, D), and insomnia (E, F)

Model evaluation and interpretation

For the diagnosing of three mental health outcomes in the test set, the AUC values of seven ML models ranged from 0.793 to 0.817 for anxiety, from 0.808 to 0.829 for depression, and from 0.797 to 0.819 for insomnia, respectively (Table 2, Fig. 3). The CatBoost model presented the optimal AUC in all the three outcomes. In terms of sensitivity, the RF model demonstrated the best performance across all three mental health outcomes, including anxiety, depression, and insomnia, with values of 0.922, 0.877, and 0.897, respectively. For specificity, the NB model showed the highest results, with the corresponding values of 0.583, 0.679, and 0.644. And regarding the F1-score, the RF model outperformed others, achieving scores of 0.854, 0.814, and 0.829 for three outcomes, respectively.

Similar to the results of the test set, the CatBoost model also achieved the highest AUC value among seven ML models in the external validation set, with 0.815, 0.820, and 0.822 for anxiety, depression, and insomnia, respectively (Table 2). Similarly, in terms of sensitivity, the RF model also achieved the highest values among all models for anxiety, depression, and insomnia, with scores of 0.922, 0.859, and 0.881, respectively. When evaluating specificity, the NB model performed best, obtaining values of 0.592, 0.686, and 0.680 across the three outcomes. As for the F1-score, the RF model again yielded the top performance, recording values of 0.844, 0.802, and 0.826. Given that AUC is one of the most widely used comprehensive metrics, the subsequent SHAP analysis mainly focused on the CatBoost model, which achieved the highest AUC.

The SHAP swarm plot and waterfall plot of the CatBoost model were used to illustrate the relationships between influencing factors and three mental health outcomes, respectively (Fig. 4). For the anxiety symptom, the top ten variables identified were past anxiety, past insomnia, past depression, worried about infection or reinfection of COVID-19, external noise at home, satisfactory neighborhood relationship, knowledge about COVID-19, regular sleep-wake, eating vegetables every day, and sunbathing time every day (Fig. 4A). The top ten variables related to depression and insomnia were similar to anxiety, and were illustrated in the SHAP swarm plot (Figs. 4C and 4E). The top 10 important features shared by the three outcomes were past anxiety, past insomnia, past depression, worried about infection or reinfection of COVID-19, regular sleep-wake, satisfactory neighborhood relationship, and eating vegetables every day. Based on the prediction model, a higher SHAP value of a feature indicated a greater likelihood of the three outcomes. Additionally, all seven machine learning models revealed that the most important influencing factor for anxiety symptoms was a prior history of anxiety, for depressive symptoms it was also a prior history of anxiety, and for insomnia symptoms it was a prior history of insomnia.

Discussion

This study utilized explainable machine learning models to identify and quantify key factors associated with mental health outcomes during the post-pandemic era. The CatBoost model demonstrated robust predictive performance, achieving high AUC values. Through SHAP value analysis, modifiable lifestyle factor, such as maintaining a regular sleep-wake cycle, daily vegetable consumption, and sufficient sun exposure, emerged as consistent and strong features associated with lower levels of anxiety, depression, and insomnia symptoms. Conversely, a history of mental health disorders and environmental stressors, such as home noise and fear of COVID-19 infection, were identified as the most significant contributors to higher risk.

Some of our findings aligned with well-established literature. For instance, while it may have seemed self-evident that a history of mental health was the strongest predictor of future recurrence [47, 48], our findings solidified this principle within our specific study context and quantified its overwhelming effect size compared to modifiable risk factors. This underscored the critical importance of relapse prevention and long-term management for individuals with a prior history. For individuals without a prior history, our model suggested that modifiable lifestyle and social factors became the primary targets for preventive intervention. Therefore, we suggested that future research should adopt a dual focus. On the one hand, studies should prioritize investigating the biological and psychological mechanisms that sustain vulnerability to relapse within high-risk populations with a history of mental health [49, 50], and evaluate targeted maintenance therapies tailored to this group. On the other hand, for the general population without such a history, efforts should concentrate on developing and implementing broad, population-level strategies that address modifiable risk factors.

Several noteworthy findings emerged in our study. It was particularly interesting that the protective effect of certain modifiable factors, such as daily vegetable intake, electronic entertainment time, sleep patterns, and sun exposure, rivaled or even exceeded that of some non-modifiable clinical factors, such as chronic diseases history in our model. Furthermore, these protective factors showed remarkable consistency across all three mental health outcomes. This suggested a potential common pathway—perhaps related to systemic inflammation, circadian rhythm regulation, or overall vitality—that underpins resilience against general psychological distress rather than disorder-specific symptoms [51–54]. For example, the protective mechanism of vegetables may be achieved through the rich antioxidants and anti-inflammatory compounds found in vegetables [55]. These bioactive substances can help mitigate oxidative stress and inflammation, which are implicated in the development of mental health issues [56].

In addition, unlike most previous studies in the field [57, 58], our analysis found that demographic factors such as age and gender exhibited relatively weak associations with mental health outcomes. This discrepancy could be attributed to several factors. First, our model included multiple strong modifiable lifestyle factors, which may have mediated or obscured the underlying influence of demographic variables [59]. Second, the study was conducted during the post-pandemic period, where widespread stressors likely attenuated differences across demographic groups. Additionally, whereas many previous studies focused on specific clinical diagnoses, which often demonstrate stronger demographic associations, our study measured general psychological distress. Future research should further explore the stability and boundary conditions of demographic factors in relation to mental health across varied cultural and temporal contexts.

As for the living environment, comfortable neighborhood relationships, satisfactory community environment, and sunbathing at home were significant protective factors in reducing mental health issues [60]. However, poor living environment, such as external noise at home, were risk factors for mental health. Cross-sectional data from the 2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) revealed that the neighborhoods have a stable and positive impact on the mental well-being of older adults [61]. A study conducted in Mexico found that, family education for relatives of patients with severe mental disorders, regardless of the relatives’ gender or age, such education helped reduce negative emotional attitudes of patients and improved their understanding of the illness [62]. In addition, a comfortable living environment, such as greenery, cleanliness, peacefulness, and safety, can reduce stress, encourage social interaction and physical activity, promote relaxation, and foster a sense of security and well-being [63].

As for COVID-19 related factors, the history of COVID-19 infection [64], knowledge of COVID-19, and concerns about COVID-19 were all strongly correlated with mental health issues. A large number of studies have consistently found that individuals recovering from COVID-19 may experience mental health challenges [65, 66]. The physiological impact of the infection, coupled with the psychological stress of isolation, fear of reinfection, and social stigma, can contribute to increased anxiety, depression, and other mental health issues. Moreover, the level of understanding about COVID-19 may be also related to the individual’s preventive behaviors and psychological state. Even though the epidemic prevention policy has been open for nearly two years, our study still found a strong positive correlation between COVID-19 related concerns and mental health problems, which indicated that the association of COVID-19 on residents’ mental health was significant and long-lasting.

This study provided valuable insights with significant theoretical, practical, and methodological implications. Theoretically, it leveraged explainable machine learning to systematically identify and quantify consistent associations between modifiable protective factors, such as sleep, diet, sunlight, and neighborhood relationships, and mental health outcomes during a public health crisis. Practically, the findings suggested that public health interventions could prioritize promoting these modifiable habits and strengthening community support to enhance psychological resilience, especially after large-scale stressful events. Methodologically, the application of explainable ML techniques offered a transparent and interpretable framework for analyzing complex multivariable data in mental health research, demonstrating a scalable approach for future studies in this field.

This study has several limitations. First, although our machine learning models were trained and tested on an independent large dataset in 2023, and validated using an external dataset in 2024, the temporal effect inevitably affects the prediction results. And further training and validation on more diverse populations are necessary to enhance the generalizability of the models. Second, due to the cross-sectional survey design, causal relationships between mental health problems and related variables cannot be established. Third, convenient sampling can lead to issues of sampling bias and data imbalance. Finally, questionnaire-based surveys were unable to gather broader and more detailed information (such as genetic and laboratory indicators data), nor can they provide a definitive clinical diagnosis of mental health disorders. Therefore, more in-depth studies are needed in the future to explore the causal relationships between various complex factors and mental health disorders.

Conclusion

The explainable ML approach robustly identified modifiable lifestyle factors (such as maintaining a regular sleep-wake cycle, daily vegetable consumption, sufficient sun exposure) and positive psychosocial factors (such as satisfactory neighborhood relationships) as features consistently and strongly associated with lower levels of anxiety, depression, and insomnia. Conversely, a history of mental health disorders and specific environmental stressors (such as external noise at home, fear of COVID-19 infection) were consistently associated with higher levels of these outcomes. Based on these observed associations, we suggest that future public health interventions could consider prioritizing the promotion of these modifiable health habits (sleep, diet, sunlight) and strengthening community support, which may help build psychological resilience during large-scale stressful events like a pandemic.
