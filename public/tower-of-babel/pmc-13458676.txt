Laparoscopic Lateral Suspension for Apical Pelvic Organ Prolapse: A Systematic Review of Current Evidence on Clinical Outcomes and Safety
Authors: Jamal Kamali, Soodabeh Darvish, Shakiba Amirjani, Behnaz Nouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo summarize current evidence on the clinical outcomes and safety of laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS) for apical pelvic organ prolapse and to distinguish direct comparative evidence from non-comparative outcome data.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidance. PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, CENTRAL, clinical trial registries, grey literature sources, and reference lists were searched from inception to May 2025. Eligible studies included adult women with apical pelvic organ prolapse of stage 2 or higher who underwent LLS with or without mesh. Both comparative and non-comparative clinical studies were eligible if they reported anatomical outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, complications, reoperation, recurrence, or perioperative outcomes. Two reviewers independently screened records and extracted data. Because of heterogeneity in study design, surgical technique, comparator status, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, findings were synthesized narratively and meta-analysis was not performed.ResultsThirteen studies were included: one randomized comparative study, four non-randomized surgical comparative studies, one quasi-experimental care-intervention study, and seven non-comparative observational studies. The available evidence suggested favorable apical and anterior anatomical outcomes after LLS, with reported apical success generally ranging from 87.3% to 100%. Patient-reported outcomes, including symptom improvement, satisfaction, quality of life, and sexual function, generally improved after surgery. Reported major complications and reoperations were uncommon, although safety outcomes were inconsistently defined and underpowered in several studies. Direct comparative evidence was limited and heterogeneous, and most studies were observational.ConclusionCurrent evidence suggests that LLS may provide favorable anatomical and patient-reported outcomes for apical and anterior compartment prolapse, but certainty remains limited because of heterogeneous, mostly observational evidence. Further multicenter studies with standardized long-term outcomes are needed.

Introduction

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) is a prevalent condition affecting up to 50% of parous women, with significant impacts on quality of life (QoL), sexual function, and daily activities.1 Apical prolapse, involving descent of the vaginal apex or uterus, is a critical component of POP, often requiring surgical intervention to restore anatomical and functional integrity.2 These symptoms impose significant physical, psychological, and socioeconomic burdens, with annual healthcare costs for POP surgery in the United States alone exceeding $1 billion. Surgical management of apical POP has evolved significantly, with approaches broadly categorized into native tissue repairs and mesh-based techniques. Traditional native tissue repairs, such as sacrospinous ligament fixation (SSF) and laparoscopic uterosacral ligament suspension (LHUS), offer minimally invasive options with low complication rates but may have variable long-term success, particularly for anterior compartment defects.4,5 However, these techniques may have variable long-term success, particularly for anterior compartment defects, with recurrence rates ranging from 10% to 30% in some cohorts.6 Conversely, mesh-based techniques like laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy (LSC) and sacrohysteropexy (LSH) provide durable apical support but are associated with mesh-related complications, including exposure and erosion, prompting regulatory restrictions in some regions.7,8

Another contemporary laparoscopic mesh-based option for apical prolapse is laparoscopic pectopexy. Unlike sacrocolpopexy, which fixes the vaginal apex or uterus to the sacral promontory, pectopexy uses the iliopectineal ligament as the lateral fixation site. It has been discussed as an alternative to sacrocolpopexy, particularly in patients in whom promontory dissection may be difficult or undesirable, such as those with obesity, adhesions, challenging vascular anatomy, or concern about bowel or hypogastric nerve injury.9 Pectopexy is therefore part of the contemporary surgical landscape for laparoscopic apical prolapse repair and should be acknowledged when discussing mesh-based alternatives. However, pectopexy differs from laparoscopic lateral suspension in its fixation site, anatomical rationale, and available evidence base. For this reason, pectopexy was considered relevant background context in the present review but was not treated as a primary comparator unless a study included an LLS arm or provided extractable LLS-specific outcome data.10

Laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS) has emerged as a promising alternative, addressing limitations of both native tissue and mesh-based repairs.11 By suspending the vaginal apex to the pelvic sidewalls bilaterally, LLS creates a hammock-like support, distributing tension more evenly than unilateral fixation methods like SSF, potentially reducing recurrence risk.12 The favorable anterior compartment outcomes reported in several studies may be related to the lateral direction of support created by LLS.13 This anatomical rationale is plausible, as the procedure aims to restore apical support while providing bilateral suspension that may influence the anterior vaginal wall.14 However, the included clinical studies were not designed to test biomechanical mechanisms directly. Therefore, these explanations should be interpreted as hypotheses rather than as proven mechanisms.15,16 Future studies combining standardized clinical outcomes with imaging, biomechanical assessment, or objective measures of vaginal axis restoration may help clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed clinical results.17

It is also important to distinguish mesh-based LLS from mesh-less or suture-based lateral suspension. Most published LLS techniques use a synthetic mesh to suspend the vaginal apex or uterus toward the abdominal wall or pelvic sidewall.18 These procedures may provide durable anatomical support, but they also require careful consideration of mesh-related complications, including exposure, erosion, infection, chronic pain, and the potential need for revision surgery. More recently, mesh-less or suture-based lateral suspension techniques have been described. These approaches attempt to reproduce the lateral support concept without implanting a synthetic mesh sheet and may be particularly relevant in settings where mesh use is restricted or where patients prefer to avoid synthetic material. Mesh-based and mesh-less LLS should therefore not be interpreted as identical interventions, because they may differ in fixation method, tissue response, durability, safety profile, and clinical implications.19,20

Despite increasing interest in LLS, its precise role in apical prolapse surgery remains insufficiently defined. Previous reviews of apical POP surgery have mainly focused on broader comparisons among established native-tissue repairs, sacrocolpopexy, sacrohysteropexy, and other surgical procedures. In those reviews, LLS has often been discussed only briefly or as part of a wider group of laparoscopic techniques rather than as the central intervention.21 At the same time, the primary LLS literature includes a mixture of randomized, prospective, retrospective, and non-comparative clinical studies. These studies differ in surgical technique, mesh use, patient selection, follow-up duration, anatomical definitions, subjective outcome measures, and complication reporting. Such variation makes it difficult to draw clear conclusions about the clinical performance of LLS or to compare it directly with established apical repair procedures.

A very recent systematic review and meta-analysis has specifically evaluated mesh-based LLS for POP and represents an important contribution to the field.22 The present review differs from that work in several ways. First, it focuses specifically on apical pelvic organ prolapse, including uterine prolapse and post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse, rather than treating POP as a broader anatomical category. Second, it includes both mesh-based and mesh-less or suture-based lateral suspension techniques when extractable LLS-specific outcome data are available. Third, it separates direct comparative evidence from non-comparative outcome data and considers contemporary comparator procedures, including SSF, LHUS, LSC, and LSH, when direct comparisons are reported. Finally, because of heterogeneity in study design, surgical technique, comparator status, follow-up duration, and outcome definitions, this review emphasizes structured narrative synthesis, risk-of-bias assessment, and certainty-of-evidence interpretation rather than presenting pooled estimates as the sole basis for inference.

This systematic review was designed to address that gap. Rather than presenting LLS as a uniformly comparative evidence base, the review summarizes the available clinical outcomes after LLS and distinguishes direct comparative evidence from non-comparative outcome data. The review evaluates anatomical success, patient-reported outcomes, perioperative findings, complications, recurrence, and reoperation after LLS for apical POP. It also considers how LLS fits within the broader context of native-tissue and laparoscopic mesh-based apical repairs. By clarifying both the strengths and limitations of the current evidence, this review aims to support clinical decision-making and identify priorities for future well-designed comparative studies.

Method

The research adhered to the systematic review guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Handbook23 and presented its findings in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement.24 The research protocol has been officially recorded in the International prospective register of systematic reviews.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

To identify relevant studies evaluating LLS compared to traditional apical native tissue repairs (eg, SSF and LHUS) or mesh-based techniques (eg, LSC, LSH) for apical pelvic organ prolapse (POP), a comprehensive search was performed across multiple electronic databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Additional sources included clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform), grey literature (Google Scholar, conference proceedings), and reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews. The search covered publications from database inception to May 20, 2025.

The search strategy, developed in collaboration with a medical librarian, used a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms tailored to each database. Key terms included “pelvic organ prolapse,” “apical prolapse,” “laparoscopic lateral suspension,” “sacrospinous ligament fixation,” “uterosacral ligament suspension,” “sacrocolpopexy,” and “sacrohysteropexy,” combined with Boolean operators (AND, OR). Filters were applied to restrict results to human studies and English-language publications, reflecting the study inclusion criteria. A sample PubMed search string was: (“pelvic organ prolapse”[MeSH Terms] OR “apical prolapse”[+] OR “vaginal prolapse”[+]) AND (“laparoscopic lateral suspension”[+] OR “sacrospinous ligament fixation”[+] OR “uterosacral ligament suspension”[+] OR “sacrocolpopexy”[MeSH] OR “sacrohysteropexy”[+]) AND (“surgery”[MeSH Terms] OR “surgical”[+]). Similar strategies were adapted for other databases, accounting for platform-specific syntax. The full search strategy for each database is available in Appendix A. All citations were exported to a reference management tool (EndNote) for deduplication and screening.

Eligibility Criteria

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (1) enrolled adult women with symptomatic apical pelvic organ prolapse of stage 2 or higher, as defined by the Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) system, including women with uterine prolapse or post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse; (2) evaluated laparoscopic lateral suspension, with or without mesh, as the primary surgical intervention; (3) reported at least one relevant clinical outcome, including objective anatomical success, POP-Q measurements, subjective improvement, patient-reported outcomes, quality of life, sexual function, complications, reoperation, recurrence, or perioperative outcomes such as operative time, blood loss, or length of hospital stay; (4) used an experimental or observational clinical study design, including randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, quasi-experimental studies, or other eligible clinical observational designs; and (5) were peer-reviewed, English-language publications available from database inception to May 20, 2025.

Both comparative and non-comparative clinical studies were eligible. Comparative studies were included when they evaluated LLS against other apical prolapse procedures, such as sacrospinous ligament fixation, laparoscopic uterosacral ligament suspension, laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, or laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy. Non-comparative studies were also included when they provided clinically relevant outcome data for LLS. This approach was used because the available literature on LLS remains limited and heterogeneous, and restricting eligibility only to direct comparative studies would have excluded important clinical evidence on efficacy and safety.

Studies were excluded if they: (1) focused primarily on non-apical pelvic organ prolapse or non-surgical management; (2) evaluated procedures that were not laparoscopic lateral suspension or did not provide extractable LLS-specific outcome data; (3) lacked relevant anatomical, subjective, safety, reoperation, recurrence, or perioperative outcomes; (4) were case reports, case series with fewer than 10 patients, reviews, editorials, letters, conference abstracts without sufficient full-text data, or expert opinion articles; or (5) were non-English publications or unpublished reports. Pectopexy-only studies were not eligible unless they included an LLS arm or provided extractable LLS-specific outcome data, because the objective of this review was to synthesize clinical evidence on laparoscopic lateral suspension rather than to review all laparoscopic mesh-based apical suspension procedures.

This review was designed as an LLS-centered systematic review rather than a full comparative review of all available apical prolapse procedures. Therefore, both comparative and non-comparative clinical studies were eligible when they reported extractable outcomes after laparoscopic lateral suspension. Comparative studies were considered when LLS was evaluated against other apical repair procedures, including sacrospinous ligament fixation, laparoscopic uterosacral ligament suspension, laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, or laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy. Non-comparative LLS studies were retained because the clinical literature on LLS remains limited and because excluding these studies would have removed clinically relevant safety and outcome data. Pectopexy-only studies were not eligible unless they included an LLS arm or provided extractable LLS-specific data, because the objective was to synthesize evidence directly informing the use of LLS rather than to review all laparoscopic mesh-based apical suspension procedures.

Selection Process

A two-stage process was employed. First, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria, excluding studies not addressing LLS, SSF, LHUS, LSC, or LSH for apical POP. Second, full texts of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and assessed independently by the same reviewers to confirm inclusion based on population (women with apical POP ≥ stage 2), intervention, comparators, outcomes (eg, POP-Q, PGI-I, complications), and study design. Discrepancies at either stage were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage included non-apical POP focus, lack of relevant outcomes, or ineligible study designs (eg, case reports). The Covidence platform facilitated screening and tracked decisions.

Data Extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed to collect relevant information from included studies evaluating LLS compared to SSF, LHUS, LSC, or LSH for apical POP. Two independent reviewers extracted data, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer to ensure accuracy. The form was piloted on a subset of studies to refine its structure.

Extracted data included: (1) study characteristics: author, publication year, country, study design (eg, RCT, cohort), sample size, and follow-up duration; (2) patient characteristics: age, BMI, parity, menopausal status, and POP-Q stage (≥ stage 2); (3) interventions and comparators: type of LLS (with/without mesh), surgical details (eg, suture material, concomitant procedures), and comparators (SSF, LHUS, LSC, LSH); (4) outcomes: objective anatomical success (POP-Q stage 0 or 1, points Ba, C, Bp), subjective outcomes (eg, PGI-I, PFDI-20, PFIQ-7, PISQ-12, FSFI, POP-SS), complications (eg, mesh exposure, bleeding), reoperation rates, and perioperative metrics (eg, operative time, blood loss, hospital stay, de novo SUI); and (5) other: statistical methods and funding sources. Missing or unclear data were noted, and attempts were made to contact study authors for clarification, though no additional data were obtained. Extracted data were compiled in a spreadsheet to facilitate narrative synthesis, as described in the synthesis methods.

Risk-of-Bias Assessment

Risk of bias was assessed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Because the evidence included comprised different study designs, design-appropriate appraisal approaches were used. The randomized controlled trial was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Non-randomized comparative and quasi-experimental studies were assessed using ROBINS-I principles, with attention to confounding, participant selection, intervention classification, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Non-comparative observational studies were assessed using relevant Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal domains for case series or cohort-type evidence, including clarity of inclusion criteria, representativeness of the sample, completeness of follow-up, reliability of outcome measurement, and adequacy of statistical reporting. Risk-of-bias judgments were not used to exclude studies, but they were incorporated into the interpretation of the narrative synthesis.

Certainty of Evidence

The certainty of evidence was assessed at the outcome level using GRADE principles. Because meta-analysis was not appropriate, certainty judgments were based on study design, risk of bias, consistency of findings, directness of evidence, precision, and reporting limitations. Certainty was considered separately for the main outcome domains: apical anatomical success, anterior compartment outcomes, patient-reported outcomes, perioperative outcomes, complications, recurrence, and reoperation. The certainty assessment was used to guide the strength of the conclusions and to distinguish consistent clinical signals from findings supported by limited or indirect evidence.

Data Synthesis and Analysis

A meta-analysis was not conducted because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across the included studies. The studies differed in design, comparator group, type of LLS procedure, use of mesh, patient population, follow-up duration, anatomical definitions, subjective outcome measures, and reporting of complications and reoperations. Therefore, findings were synthesized narratively. To improve interpretability, the synthesis was structured by study design, type of evidence, comparator, LLS technique, follow-up duration, and outcome domain. Comparative studies were interpreted separately from non-comparative studies, and mesh-based LLS was distinguished from mesh-less LLS when the available data allowed. Outcome interpretation also considered the risk-of-bias assessment and the certainty of evidence for each outcome domain.

Result

The results are presented as a structured narrative synthesis because the included studies varied in design, comparator, surgical technique, follow-up duration, and outcome definition. To avoid overstating the strength of the evidence, comparative and non-comparative data are described separately where possible. Findings are also interpreted according to the type of LLS procedure, including mesh-based and mesh-less techniques, and according to the main outcome domains: anatomical success, patient-reported outcomes, perioperative findings, complications, recurrence, and reoperation. Given the predominance of small observational studies, the results should be interpreted as a summary of the available clinical evidence rather than as definitive evidence of superiority over established apical repair procedures.

Study Selection

The database search identified 1649 records. After removal of 573 duplicate records, 1076 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 857 records were excluded because they were not relevant to the review question. A total of 219 reports were sought for retrieval, and 48 reports were assessed for eligibility. Thirty-five reports were excluded after full-text assessment, including 29 reports without relevant outcomes, three non-English reports, and three conference abstracts. Citation searching and grey literature searches identified 27 additional records; however, no additional eligible study was added from these sources because the reports assessed from this route were duplicate publications already identified through database searching. Overall, 13 studies were included in the final synthesis. The corrected study selection process is shown in Figure 1.17,25–36

A total of 13 studies were included in this systematic review, evaluating the efficacy and safety of LLS compared to traditional apical native tissue repairs for the treatment of apical pelvic organ prolapse (POP). The studies encompassed a variety of designs, settings, and patient populations, providing a broad perspective on the surgical techniques under investigation. The included studies were conducted across multiple countries, predominantly in Europe (Turkey,25,26,28,31 Italy,29 Switzerland27) and Asia (China),33,34,36 reflecting diverse clinical settings. Study designs varied, including retrospective cohort studies (n=5), prospective observational studies (n=4), one randomized controlled trial, one quasi-experimental study, and one prospective analysis. The time periods ranged from 2010 to 2023, with most studies conducted in the last decade. Patient populations primarily included women with symptomatic apical prolapse ≥ stage 2, with some studies focusing on specific subgroups (eg, PHVP or uterine prolapse). Sample sizes ranged from 2126 to 134 patients,30 with most studies having fewer than 100 participants, highlighting a limitation in statistical power. The randomized study was judged as having some concerns rather than low risk of bias. Although it used a prospective randomized design and reported both objective and patient-reported outcomes, the sample size was small, allocation concealment was not fully described, blinding was limited, and follow-up was restricted to 12 months. Among the non-randomized comparative studies, the overall risk of bias ranged from moderate to serious. These studies provided clinically useful comparative data, particularly for LLS versus sacrospinous fixation, sacrohysteropexy, sacrocolpopexy, or high uterosacral ligament suspension. However, their interpretation was limited by retrospective design in several studies, non-randomized treatment allocation, potential baseline imbalance, small sample sizes, limited adjustment for confounding, and variation in follow-up duration.

The single-arm LLS studies were generally judged to be at moderate or moderate-to-serious risk of bias. Their main strengths were the use of clinical POP-Q assessment, reporting of perioperative and complication outcomes, and, in several studies, follow-up extending beyond 12 months. Nevertheless, these studies lacked comparator groups and therefore could not support firm conclusions about the relative effectiveness of LLS compared with other apical prolapse procedures. Several studies also had limited sample sizes, incomplete reporting of participant selection, no blinded outcome assessment, and inconsistent reporting of recurrence, reoperation, mesh-related complications, and patient-reported outcomes.

Overall, the quality assessment indicates that the current evidence base supports a cautious interpretation of LLS outcomes. The available studies suggest favorable anatomical and subjective outcomes after LLS, but the certainty of comparative inference remains limited. The main methodological limitations were the predominance of observational designs, small cohorts, lack of randomization, limited control for confounding, heterogeneous outcome definitions, and variable follow-up. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as encouraging clinical evidence rather than definitive proof that LLS is superior to established apical prolapse repairs.

Objective Anatomical Outcomes

Across the 13 studies included in this systematic review, anatomical success, typically defined as Pelvic Organ Prolapse Quantification (POP-Q) stage 0 or 1 (ie, points Ba, C, or Bp < −1 cm or no prolapse beyond the hymen), was a primary measure of efficacy for laparoscopic LLS compared to traditional apical native tissue repairs (eg, SSF, LHUS) or other mesh-based techniques (eg, LSC and LSH). The studies collectively demonstrated high anatomical success rates for LLS, with outcomes generally comparable to or slightly superior to those of comparator procedures, particularly in addressing anterior and apical compartment prolapse.

In a retrospective study conducted in Turkey, Satır Özel et al compared LSC and LLS in 21 patients with uterine prolapse, finding no significant differences in most POP-Q points or recurrence rates between the two techniques. Notably, Point C was more proximal in the LSC group (−6.6 cm) compared to the LLS group (−5.2 cm, P=0.037), yet no apical recurrences were observed in either group, suggesting robust anatomical correction for both approaches.26 Similarly, Wang et al, evaluating a mesh-less LLS technique for post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse in China, reported an impressive 100% anatomical cure rate (POP-Q < stage 2) at 1 year, which decreased slightly to 95% at 3 years, accompanied by significant improvements in POP-Q measurements (eg, Point C improved from 3.75 ± 1.78 cm preoperatively to −5.80 ± 1.06 cm at 1 year, P<0.001)35 (Table 3).

Prospective studies further corroborated the efficacy of LLS. Baki Erin et al, in a Turkish cohort of 105 patients, found comparable anatomical cure rates at 2 years between LLS (apical: 96.1%, anterior: 96.0%, posterior: 75.0%) and SSF (apical: 90.5%, anterior: 90.6%, posterior: 69.4%), with no statistically significant differences (P>0.05).28 Likewise, Chatziioannidou et al reported an 87.3% objective cure rate for LLS in a Swiss cohort at a mean follow-up of 3.4 years, with only one anterior and two apical recurrences among 79 patients.17 Moreover, Mereu et al, in a two-center Italian study, achieved anatomical success rates of 94.2% for the anterior compartment and 94.9% for the apical compartment at 2 years, underscoring the durability of LLS outcomes.29

Several studies highlighted LLS’s particular strength in addressing anterior compartment prolapse. Doğan et al, in a randomized controlled trial in Turkey, observed significant improvements in apical staging (Point C, P<0.001) for both LLS and LSH, but LLS demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the anterior compartment (Point Ba), unlike LSH (P=0.130).37 Similarly, Yu et al reported anatomical success rates of 93.44% for LLS and 93.85% for LHUS at 12 months in a Chinese cohort, with LLS showing superior improvement in Point Ba, suggesting enhanced correction of anterior wall defects.36 Wang et al, also in China, reported anatomical cure rates of 88.3% (anterior), 100% (apical), and 93.3% (posterior) at 2 years for LLS, reinforcing its efficacy in severe prolapse cases.33

Other studies provided additional evidence of LLS’s anatomical efficacy. Schiavi et al, in a multicenter Italian study with a median follow-up of 3.8 years, noted significant reductions in POP-Q scores for both anterior and apical compartments following LLS.30 Yurtcu et al found no significant differences in anatomical success between LLS and SSF in a Turkish cohort, with logistic regression indicating that factors such as menopausal status or surgery type did not significantly influence outcomes.25 Also, Dubuisson et al, in a Swiss series, reported an overall anatomical cure rate of 87.7%, with a related study citing rates of 91.6% (anterior), 93.6% (apical), and 85.3% (posterior).27

Overall, LLS consistently achieved anatomical success rates ranging from 87.3% to 100% for the apical compartment across studies, with rates for the anterior compartment similarly high (88.3–96.0%). These outcomes were generally comparable to those of SSF, LHUS, LSC, and LSH, with some studies suggesting a slight advantage for LLS in anterior compartment correction. Variations in follow-up duration (ranging from 12 months to 3.8 years) and definitions of anatomical success highlight the need for standardized reporting to facilitate direct comparisons.

Subjective Outcomes

The subjective outcomes reported across the 13 studies included in this systematic review provided critical insights into patient satisfaction, quality of life (QoL), and sexual function following LLS compared to SSF, LHUS, LSC and LSH. These outcomes, assessed through validated tools such as the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I), Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory (PFDI-20), Pelvic Floor Impact Questionnaire (PFIQ-7), Pelvic Organ Prolapse/Urinary Incontinence Sexual Questionnaire (PISQ-12), Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), and Pelvic Organ Prolapse Symptom Score (POP-SS), consistently demonstrated high patient satisfaction and functional improvements. Moreover, LLS frequently exhibited outcomes comparable to or slightly superior to its comparators, particularly in reducing prolapse-related symptoms and enhancing sexual function, though variability in follow-up periods and assessment tools underscored the need for standardized reporting.

In a retrospective analysis conducted in Turkey, Satır Özel et al observed no significant differences in PISQ-12 scores (P=0.481) or postoperative symptom prevalence (P=0.676) between LSC and LLS among 21 patients with uterine prolapse, suggesting that both techniques offered equivalent symptom relief and sexual function outcomes. However, a Chinese study by Wang et al, evaluating a mesh-less LLS approach for post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapses, reported exceptional subjective success, with all patients achieving a PGI-I score of ≤2 at 1 year, sustained in 97.5% at 3 years, alongside significant improvements in PFDI-20, PFIQ-7, and PISQ-12 scores (P<0.001) among sexually active individuals. This contrast highlights the potential influence of surgical technique and patient population on subjective outcomes, with mesh-less approaches showing pronounced benefits in specific contexts.

In addition, prospective studies reinforced the subjective efficacy of LLS. For instance, Baki Erin et al, in a Turkish cohort of 105 patients, reported subjective cure rates of 88.4% for LLS compared to 83.0% for SSF at 2 years (P=0.32). Notably, LLS was associated with significant improvements in FSFI and POP-SS scores and a lower incidence of dyspareunia, suggesting a functional advantage over SSF. Similarly, Chatziioannidou et al documented a 96.2% subjective success rate (PGI-I ≤2) for LLS at a mean follow-up of 3.4 years in Switzerland, reflecting sustained symptom resolution. Furthermore, Mereu et al, in a two-center Italian study, found that 89% of patients were asymptomatic 2 years post-LLS, underscoring its capacity to alleviate prolapse-related complaints effectively.

Conversely, some studies reported more comparable outcomes between LLS and other techniques. Doğan et al, in a randomized controlled trial in Turkey, noted equivalent improvements in P-QoL, POP-SS, FSFI, and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores for LLS and LSH, indicating no significant differences in patient-reported outcomes. Likewise, Yurtcu et al (2025) found no notable differences in PISQ-12 scores between LLS and SSF among sexually active patients in Turkey, suggesting similar sexual function benefits. In a different context, Yu et al (2023) reported comparable enhancements in PFDI-20, PFIQ-7, and PGI-I scores for LLS and LHUS at 12 months in a Chinese cohort, though LLS showed slight advantages in overall symptom relief, possibly due to its efficacy in addressing anterior compartment defects.

Additionally, studies exploring broader QoL impacts provided further insights. Wang et al, in a Chinese study, demonstrated that comprehensive perioperative care for LLS significantly improved QoL scores (95.00 ± 1.26 vs 87.44 ± 2.57, P<0.001) compared to conventional care, highlighting the role of adjunctive care in enhancing subjective outcomes. Moreover, Schiavi et al, in a multicenter Italian study with a median 3.8-year follow-up, reported significant improvements in QoL and sexual function (via PISQ-12, FSFI, and FSDS), including increased monthly intercourse frequency, following LLS for high-grade uterine prolapse. Wang et al, also in China, achieved a 93.3% subjective cure rate for LLS at 2 years, with significant improvements in PFDI-20 and PFIQ-7 scores (P<0.05), though PISQ-12 and ICI-Q-SF scores remained unchanged (P=0.317 and P=0.551, respectively). However, subjective outcome data were limited for Dubuisson et al, with related studies only referencing satisfactory outcomes, and for Xu et al, where excerpts lacked specific details.

In summary, the subjective outcomes across these studies underscored high patient satisfaction, with subjective cure rates ranging from 83.0% to 100% and notable improvements in QoL and sexual function for LLS. While LLS generally performed comparably to SSF, LHUS, LSC, and LSH, certain studies suggested advantages in reducing dyspareunia and enhancing symptom relief, particularly in anterior compartment correction. Nevertheless, variations in follow-up duration (12 months to 3.8 years) and the diversity of questionnaires used highlight the need for standardized outcome measures to strengthen cross-study comparisons.

Other Outcomes

In addition to anatomical and subjective outcomes, the 13 studies included in this systematic review reported a range of perioperative and postoperative metrics, including operative time, blood loss, hospital stay, and the incidence of de novo symptoms such as stress urinary incontinence (SUI). These outcomes provided valuable insights into the procedural efficiency and postoperative recovery associated with LLS compared to traditional apical native tissue repairs other mesh-based techniques. Overall, LLS demonstrated favorable perioperative profiles, with short operative times, minimal blood loss, and brief hospital stays, though the occurrence of de novo symptoms varied across studies, reflecting differences in surgical techniques and patient populations.

For instance, Wang et al, evaluating a mesh-less LLS approach for post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse in China, reported a mean operative time of 53.8 ± 22.3 minutes, blood loss of 45.5 ± 23.5 mL, and an average hospital stay of 2.7 days, indicating a highly efficient procedure with rapid recovery. In contrast, Wang et al, also in China, focused on the impact of perioperative care, finding that comprehensive care significantly reduced time to mobilization (12.28 ± 0.83 vs 18.87 ± 0.95 hours, P<0.001), pain duration (2.91 ± 0.33 vs 4.31 ± 0.11 days, P<0.001), and hospital stay (4.56 ± 0.61 vs 5.67 ± 0.74 days, P<0.001) compared to conventional care, highlighting the role of enhanced recovery protocols in optimizing outcomes for LLS.

Moreover, studies comparing LLS to other techniques provided additional context. Yu et al, in a Chinese cohort, found no significant differences in operative time, blood loss, or hospital stay between LLS and LHUS, suggesting comparable procedural efficiency. However, the study noted the occurrence of de novo urinary incontinence, though specific rates were not detailed, limiting direct comparisons. Similarly, Baki Erin et al, in a Turkish study of 105 patients, recorded operative time and blood loss for LLS and SSF but did not provide specific values in the available excerpts, precluding a detailed assessment of these metrics.

Regarding de novo symptoms, Satır Özel et al, in a retrospective Turkish study, reported a 28% incidence of de novo incontinence in the LLS group compared to 0% in the LSC group (P=0.100), though the difference was not statistically significant, possibly due to the small sample size (n=21). Additionally, the study noted a longer mean follow-up in the LSC group (1,014.7 days) compared to LLS (598.4 days, P=0.013), which may have influenced the detection of postoperative symptoms. In contrast, Chatziioannidou et al (2022), in a Swiss cohort, did not explicitly quantify de novo SUI but suggested improvements in pre-existing SUI following LLS, indicating potential benefits for urinary function in some patients. Furthermore, Wang et al, in China, reported a low incidence of de novo symptoms after LLS surgery, though specific details were not provided, reinforcing the generally favorable postoperative profile of this technique.

In summary, the additional outcomes reported across these studies underscored the procedural efficiency of LLS, characterized by short operative times, minimal blood loss, and brief hospital stays, often enhanced by comprehensive perioperative care. While de novo symptoms, particularly urinary incontinence, were noted in some studies, their incidence was generally low or not statistically significant, though data limitations in certain studies (eg, Baki Erin et al, 2023; Yu et al, 2023) hindered comprehensive comparisons. Nevertheless, variability in reporting and follow-up duration (ranging from months to over 2.5 years) highlights the need for standardized metrics to fully elucidate these outcomes across diverse surgical approaches.

For patient-reported outcomes, including symptom improvement, satisfaction, quality of life, and sexual function, the certainty was judged to be low to very low. Most studies reported improvement after surgery, but different instruments were used across studies, baseline severity varied, and several studies lacked comparator groups. The certainty for perioperative outcomes was low because operative time, blood loss, hospital stay, and recovery measures were inconsistently reported and were influenced by local surgical practice, perioperative care protocols, and patient selection.

Discussion

This systematic review summarizes the current clinical evidence on laparoscopic lateral suspension for apical pelvic organ prolapse. Overall, the included studies suggest that LLS may be associated with favorable anatomical and patient-reported outcomes, particularly for apical and anterior compartment support. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The evidence base remains limited by small sample sizes, the predominance of observational and non-randomized designs, heterogeneous surgical techniques, variable follow-up duration, inconsistent outcome definitions, and limited direct comparative data. For this reason, the present review should not be interpreted as establishing superiority of LLS over other apical prolapse procedures. Rather, it provides a structured synthesis of an evolving and methodologically heterogeneous body of evidence.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis has provided an important synthesis of mesh-based LLS for pelvic organ prolapse.22 The present review adds to that work by using a more focused apical-prolapse framework and by including both mesh-based and mesh-less or suture-based lateral suspension techniques when LLS-specific outcome data were available. It also separates direct comparative studies from non-comparative clinical evidence and considers comparator procedures such as sacrospinous fixation, laparoscopic uterosacral ligament suspension, laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, and laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy when direct comparisons were reported. This distinction is clinically important because pooled estimates from heterogeneous LLS studies may not fully capture differences in technique, comparator status, mesh use, follow-up duration, and outcome definition. The contribution of the present review is therefore not to replace previous meta-analytic work, but to clarify the scope, limitations, and certainty of the available evidence for LLS in apical prolapse surgery.

The anatomical efficacy of LLS, with apical success rates of 87.3%–100%, stems from its unique biomechanical design. Unlike SSF, which relies on unilateral fixation to the sacrospinous ligament, LLS distributes tension bilaterally across the pelvic sidewalls, creating a hammock-like support that stabilizes both apical and anterior compartments.5,38 This mechanism explains LLS’s superior anterior compartment correction, as evidenced by significant improvements in Point Ba in Doğan et al31 and Yu et al36 compared to LSH and LHUS. Finite element modeling studies suggest that lateral suspension reduces stress concentrations on the vaginal wall, minimizing tissue strain and recurrence risk.39 Moreover, the mesh-less LLS approach, as reported by Wang et al,35 uses suture-based fixation to replicate this support, achieving a 100% cure rate at 1 year, consistent with cadaveric studies showing that high-strength sutures can approximate mesh tensile properties.40

In contrast, LSC’s sacral mesh anchoring provides deep apical support but may be less effective for anterior defects, as Satır Özel et al26 noted a more proximal Point C with LSC but no anterior advantage.41 This biomechanical versatility positions LLS as a bridge between the durability of mesh-based repairs and the biological compatibility of native tissue techniques, particularly for complex prolapse cases involving anterior wall defects. Furthermore, the durability of LLS, sustained up to 3.8 years in Schiavi et al30 may result from reduced gravitational stress on lateral fixation points, as suggested by biomechanical analyses.42

Subjective success and quality of life improvements with LLS are driven by its minimally invasive approach and preservation of pelvic function.13 Low dyspareunia rates, as in Baki Erin et al, likely result from avoiding transvaginal incisions, which can cause scarring and nerve irritation in SSF.28 Additionally, LLS’s laparoscopic technique minimizes tissue trauma, reducing postoperative pain and accelerating recovery, as evidenced by Wang et al’s (2024, mesh-less) 2.7-day hospital stay.33 Comprehensive perioperative care further enhances QoL through enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) principles, such as early mobilization and pain management, aligning with evidence-based protocols.43 Notably, high subjective success (eg, 96.2% PGI-I ≤2 in Chatziioannidou et al17) reflects LLS’s alignment with patient-centered care models prioritizing functional outcomes.44 These results are comparable to LSC’s 85–95% satisfaction rates, positioning LLS as a competitive alternative.45

The safety profile of LLS is underpinned by meticulous surgical technique and patient selection. Mesh exposure, reported at 0%–8.2%, is lower than transvaginal mesh (10–20%) due to extraperitoneal mesh placement, which avoids vaginal mucosal disruption.46 In addition, mesh-less LLS eliminates this risk, addressing regulatory concerns in regions with mesh restrictions.47 Low severe complication rates compared to SSF reflect LLS’s laparoscopic precision, minimizing risks like bladder injury or infection.48 Furthermore, short operative times and brief hospital stays reduce anesthesia exposure and costs, aligning with value-based care.49

LLS may offer a safety-related anatomical rationale because it avoids sacral promontory dissection, which is required in sacrocolpopexy and may expose patients to presacral vascular or nerve-related risks. However, this potential advantage should be interpreted cautiously. LLS remains a technically demanding laparoscopic procedure and requires appropriate training, careful pelvic sidewall dissection, and standardized complication reporting.22

This review’s strengths include diverse settings (Europe, Asia), validated measures (eg, POP-Q, PGI-I), and inclusion of mesh and mesh-less LLS, enhancing generalizability. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting this review. First, the evidence base included both comparative and non-comparative studies, which limits the ability to draw firm conclusions about the relative effectiveness of LLS compared with other apical prolapse procedures. Second, most studies were small and observational, and several had important risks of bias related to patient selection, confounding, incomplete reporting, and lack of standardized outcome assessment. Third, outcome definitions varied across studies, particularly for anatomical success, subjective improvement, recurrence, and complications. Fourth, follow-up duration was inconsistent and often too short to assess long-term durability. Fifth, the certainty of evidence was limited across most outcome domains, mainly because of study design limitations, imprecision, and heterogeneity. These limitations support a cautious interpretation of the findings and highlight the need for well-designed multicenter comparative studies.

Clinically, LLS is a versatile option for apical POP, particularly for patients needing anterior support or preferring mesh-less surgery. However, decisions should consider prolapse stage, comorbidities, and surgeon expertise. Future research should prioritize multicenter RCTs comparing LLS to LSC and native tissue repairs, standardized long-term outcomes (≥5 years), cost-effectiveness studies, and subgroup analyses (eg, elderly patients) to optimize its use.

Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that laparoscopic lateral suspension may be a promising surgical option for women with apical pelvic organ prolapse, particularly when clinically relevant anterior compartment support is also needed. The available studies generally report favorable anatomical and patient-reported outcomes after LLS, including improvement in POP-Q measures, prolapse-related symptoms, quality of life, and sexual function. Reported rates of major complications and reoperation were generally low. This favorable safety profile may partly reflect the avoidance of sacral promontory dissection and extensive deep posterior pelvic dissection, which may reduce operative difficulty in selected patients. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the certainty of evidence remains limited.

The current evidence base is characterized by small sample sizes, heterogeneous study designs, variable LLS techniques, inconsistent outcome definitions, short- to mid-term follow-up, and a predominance of observational or non-randomized studies. In addition, no pooled analysis was performed because of substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity. Therefore, the comparative effectiveness and long-term safety of LLS remain insufficiently established. LLS should be considered an evolving option within apical prolapse surgery rather than a procedure with proven superiority over other contemporary apical suspension techniques. An important anatomical limitation is that LLS primarily addresses apical and anterior compartment support. Its role in posterior compartment prolapse is less clear, and clinically relevant posterior defects may require concomitant posterior repair or alternative surgical strategies. This limitation should be considered when selecting patients and interpreting postoperative anatomical outcomes. Future research should prioritize well-designed multicenter randomized or prospective comparative studies. These studies should use standardized anatomical and patient-reported outcome definitions, longer follow-up, transparent reporting of complications and reoperations, and clear stratification of mesh-based and mesh-less or suture-based LLS techniques.
