Association between diabetic retinopathy and neurodegenerative diseases in the All of Us research program
Authors: Ha-Neul Yu, Jocelyn He, Benjamin Kim, Gui-Shuang Ying
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose:While diabetic retinopathy (DR) has previously been linked to neurodegenerative diseases, it remains unclear whether DR independently reflects neurodegenerative diseases beyond those attributable to diabetes itself. In this study, we leveraged data from the All of Us Research Program to assess whether DR serves as an independent marker of neurodegenerative disease among individuals with diabetes.Methods:A matched case-control, cross-sectional study was conducted using data from the All of Us Research Program (US-based EHR database). Three groups (exactly matched by age, sex, and race) were created and compared: individuals with both DR and DM (DR+DM, n = 7629), individuals with DM but no DR (DM-only, n = 22,887), and individuals without DM (n = 22,887). Outcomes included dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and multiple sclerosis (MS). Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed, adjusting for demographics, comorbidities and diabetes-related mediators.Results:In multivariable analysis, DM-only was associated with increased odds of dementia (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.28, 95 % CI: 1.08–1.51; p = 0.004). However, DR in the setting of DM (DM+DR vs. DM-only) was not associated with further increased odds of neurodegenerative disease outcome in multivariate models (aOR for dementia 1.18, 95 % CI: 0.94–1.49). No significant associations were identified for AD, PD, or MS (all p ≥ 0.10).Conclusion:Diabetic retinopathy was not associated with increased rates of neurodegenerative diseases beyond that conferred by diabetes itself, and the relationship may be mediated by diabetes severity and related comorbidities.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Neurodegenerative disease, All of Us research

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia, which over time leads to widespread damage in multiple organs, including the retina and brain.1 Increasing evidence suggests a strong association between DM and neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s (AD) and Parkinson’s disease (PD), with diabetic individuals facing up to a 70 % higher risk of dementia.2–5 However, phenotypic markers to identify DM patients at heightened risk for neurodegeneration remain elusive, highlighting a critical need for accessible and early indicators, especially amid the global rise in both DM and neurological diseases.6,7

One innovative area of investigation lies in exploring the retina as a gateway to understanding brain health, where early retinal changes in diabetic patients could offer clues to underlying brain pathology. Among the various diabetic complications, diabetic retinopathy (DR) offers a particularly valuable opportunity to explore this connection. With an estimated 529 million individuals living with diabetes globally—representing 6.1 % of the global population—the prevalence of DR among diabetic patients is approximately 27 %, affecting an estimated 104 million people.8,9 DR, long regarded as a microvascular complication of DM, is now recognized for its neurodegenerative features—including neuronal apoptosis and glial activation—that may precede vascular damage.10 Given the shared mechanisms between DR, AD, and PD (e.g., neuroinflammation, α-synuclein accumulation, and disrupted neurotrophic signaling), retinal changes may help identify systemic neurodegeneration.11,12 This study aims to evaluate the association between DR and neurodegenerative diseases through the analysis of data from the All of Us Research Program.

Methods

This study employed amatched cross-sectional design to evaluate the association between DR and neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs) in individuals with and without the presence of DR from the All of Us Research Program (AoURP), a large-scale, nationwide initiative funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH) aimed at advancing precision medicine and promoting diversity in biomedical research. AoURP provides a rich dataset that includes electronic health records, demographic information, biometric and lifestyle data, and genetic data from over 600,000 participants across the United States (as of February 2025), with a goal of reaching 1 million participants. Participants represent a wide spectrum of ethnic, geographic, and socioeconomic backgrounds. Because the data used were fully de-identified, the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board waived the requirement for ethical approval.

Three cohorts were constructed by 1:3 individual exact matching on age, sex at birth, and race/ethnicity. The case group consisted of individuals diagnosed with both diabetes and diabetic retinopathy (DR + DM; n = 7629). The first control group included individuals with diabetes but no diabetic retinopathy (DM-only; n = 22,887), as determined by the absence of a diagnosis code for DR. The second control group comprised individuals without diabetes and DR (Non-DM; n = 22,887). These diagnoses were identified using structured electronic health record data available in the AoURP (Diabetes – SNOMED 73,211,009; DR SNOMED – 4855,003). Inclusion criteria required participants to be adults aged 18 years or older with complete medical records. Participants were excluded if they had missing demographic or diagnostic data. The primary outcomes assessed were diagnoses of neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia (SNOMED 52,448,006), Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (SNOMED 26,929,004), Parkinson’s disease (PD) (SNOMED 49,049,000), or multiple sclerosis (MS) (SNOMED 24,700,007).

Data processing and statistical analysis

Data were extracted from All of Us v8 using SQL in BigQuery and processed in R. Survey variables were recoded into analytic categories (e.g., collapsing “Skip” and “Prefer not to answer” into “Unknown”), and demographic, social, and clinical covariates were standardized. For disease conditions, participants were classified as having the condition (“Yes”) if any relevant ICD or SNOMED code was present in their EHR, and as “No” otherwise. For the missing data in covariates such as HbA1c, DR severity, and diabetes type, we created one category of “unknown” as shown in Table 1, so that those with missing data were not excluded from the analysis.

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R within the secure All of Us Researcher Workbench environment. Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics across the three groups using mean, standard deviation (SD), median, and interquartile range for continuous measures, using counts and percentages for categorical measures. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression model with demographic matching variables as covariates were performed to assess whether DR was associated with increased prevalence of each neurodegenerative condition (dementia, AD, PD, and MS) as compared to those with DM only or without DM. Two multivariate logistic regression models with demographic matching variables as covariates were performed. The first multivariable model (intermediate multivariable model) was adjusted by potential confounders, including smoking, alcohol intake, hypertension (38,341,003), obesity (414,916,001), dyslipidemia (370,992,007), brain injury (127,294,003; both traumatic or nontraumatic brain injury), and cardiovascular disease (698,247,007; 230,690,007; 42,343,007; 22,298,006). The second multivariable model was adjusted by both potential confounders in the model 1 and diabetes-related mediators including diabetic neuropathy (230,572,002), diabetic nephropathy (127,013,003), hemoglobin A1c (Standard LOINC 4548–4), insulin use, DR severity (No DR, Mild NPDR, Moderate NPDR, Severe NPDR, Proliferative DR, and Unknown), and diabetes types (no diabetes, type 1, type 2, other, unknown). From these univariable and multivariable logistic regression models, we estimated odds ratios (ORs), adjusted odds ratios (aORs), and their confidence intervals. We also tested the interactions between diabetes/diabetic retinopathy status and demographics (age, sex at birth, race/ethnicity) or clinical moderators in multivariable models. When significant interaction (p < 0.05) between diabetes/diabetic retinopathy status and demographics was found, stratified analysis by the demographic variable was performed. Two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics

As shown in Table 1, the mean age in each group was approximately 64.1 years (SD = 12.3), 50 % female, 37 % non-Hispanic White, 26 % non-Hispanic Black, and 26 % Hispanic or Latino. Hypertension was present in 93.2 % of the DM + DR group, 84.8 % in the DM-only group, and 27.2 % in Non-DM group. Prior cardiovascular disease events were 55.9 % in DM + DR group, 42.3 % in DM-only group, and 13.5 % in Non-DM group. In addition, obesity (65.2 % vs. 55.8 % vs. 13.3 %), diabetic neuropathy (53.8 % vs. 19.0 % vs. 0.2 %), and diabetic nephropathy (53.9 % vs. 21.4 % vs. 0.3 %) were different among groups. Mean HbA1c was 5.78 % in the DM + DR group, 5.65 % in the DM-only group, and 5.45 % in the Non-DM group. Lifestyle factors included alcohol use rate of 83.4 % in the DM + DR group, 83.5 % in the DM-only group, and 80.5 % in the Non-DM group, as well as smoking rates of 42.0 % in the DM + DR group, 45.9 % in the DM-only group, and 40.5 % in the Non-DM group.

Association between DR and neurodegenerative diseases

The rates of neurodegenerative conditions were examined among Non-DM, DM, and DM + DR groups (Table 2, Fig. 1). Dementia was observed in 6.0 % of the DM + DR group, 4.4 % in the DM-only group and 1.2 % in Non-DM group. Alzheimer’s disease had a prevalence of 0.9 % in the DR+DM group, 0.7 % in the DM-only group, and 0.2 % in Non-DM group. Parkinson’s disease was reported in 1.3 % of DM+DR group, 1.4 % of DM-only group, and 0.5 % of Non-DM group. Multiple sclerosis had a prevalence of 0.9 % in the DM+DR and DM-only groups and 0.5 % in Non-DM group.

Table 2 reported results from the univariable and multivariable analyses for associations of DM and DR with each neurodegenerative disease. Adjusted OR and their 95 % CIs were reported from two multivariable models, one with adjustment for potential confounders (intermediate model), and another model (fully adjusted model) adjusted for both potential confounders and potential mediators (diabetic neuropathy, diabetic nephropathy, HbA1c levels, insulin use, and DR severity). In the fully adjusted multivariable model, DM-only had significantly increased odds of dementia, with an adjusted OR of 1.28 (95 % CI: 1.08–1.51; p = 0.004) compared to Non-DM. DM+DR had significantly increased odds of dementia compared to the Non-DM group in the full model with aOR of 1.52 (95 % CI: 1.15—2.00; p = 0.004). Compared to DM, DM+DR did not have increased odds of dementia (aOR=1.18; 95 % CI: 0.94—1.49; p = 0.15) in the full multivariate model.

Compared to Non-DM group, DM-only had significantly increased odds of Alzheimer’s disease in the intermediate multivariable model (aOR 1.49; 95 % CI: 1.05–2.10; p = 0.02), but this association was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (aOR 1.23; 95 % CI: 0.84–2.09; p = 0.86). DM+DR had significant increase in odds of Alzheimer’s disease compared to Non-DM group in the intermediate model (aOR 1.57; 95 % CI: 1.05–2.35; p = 0.03) but not significant in full multivariable models (aOR 1.06; 95 % CI: 0.54–2.09; p = 0.86). Compared to DM-only, the presence of DR did not significantly increase the odds of Alzheimer’s (aOR 0.87; 95 % CI: 0.49–1.54; p = 0.63) in the full multivariate model.

For Parkinson’s disease, DM-only had significantly increased odds compared to Non-DM group in the intermediate multivariate model (aOR 1.36; 95 % CI: 1.06–1.74; p = 0.02), but this was not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model (aOR 1.09; 95 % CI: 0.95–1.72; p = 0.10). DM+DR showed no significant increase in odds in either intermediate (aOR 1.03; 95 % CI: 0.71–1.51; p = 0.87) or fully adjusted models (aOR 0.57; 95 % CI: 0.83–1.43; p = 0.53). Compared to DM-only, the presence of DR was not associated with increased odds of Parkinson’s disease (aOR 0.69; 95 % CI: 0.42–1.11; p = 0.12) in the full multivariate model.

Regarding multiple sclerosis, neither DM-only nor DM+DR had significantly increased odds compared to Non-DM group in the intermediate model (DM-only aOR 1.14; 95 % CI: 0.85–1.52; p = 0.38; DM+DR aOR 1.06; 95 % CI: 0.74–1.52; p = 0.74) or fully adjusted models (DM-only aOR 1.06; 95 % CI: 0.78–1.44; p = 0.71; DM+DR aOR 1.34; 95 % CI: 0.76–2.35; p = 0.31). The comparison between DM+DR and DM-only also showed no significant difference in odds of multiple sclerosis in either model (p ≥ 0.30).

For dementia, we found significant interaction between diabetes/diabetic retinopathy and age in the intermediate adjusted model (p < 0.001) and fully adjusted model (p = 0.002) (Supplementary Table 1). The stratified analysis by age (≤65 years, >65 years) found that the significant association between diabetes/diabetic retinopathy and dementia was only observed in those with age>65 years, and their association was not statistically significant in those with age ≤65 years (Table 3). For other factors for each neurodegenerative disease, we did not find any significant interaction between diabetes/diabetic retinopathy and demographics (sex, race/ethnicity, all p > 0.05 in the full multivariable model, Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

This matched case-control analysis of the AoURP data, encompassing over 54,000 individuals, explored the relationship between diabetes, diabetic retinopathy, and the odds of neurodegenerative diseases, including dementia, AD, PD, and MS. Our results highlight several key findings. Both the DM and DM+DR groups had higher odds of dementia compared to the Non-DM group (aOR = 1.28 and 1.52, respectively) in the multivariable model adjusted for confounders and mediators. However, the DM+DR group did not exhibit significantly greater odds than the DM-only group. Similarly, both DM and DM+DR groups demonstrated increased odds of Alzheimer’s disease compared to Non-DM (aOR = 1.49 and 1.57, respectively) in models adjusted for confounders, but these associations were no longer significant after further adjustment for diabetes-related mediators. The lack of increased odds in the DM+DR group compared to the DM-only group suggests that the elevated odds observed among those with DR are primarily mediated by factors related to overall diabetes severity rather than DR-specific mechanisms.

Our findings contrast with a study by Pedersen et al. of a Danish registry-based nationwide cohort study, which reported that individuals with DR had a higher associations of incident AD compared to both diabetic individuals without DR (adjusted hazard ratio (HR) = 1.34; 95 % CI: 1.18–1.53) and individuals without diabetes (adjusted HR = 1.24; 95 % CI: 1.08–1.43).13 They suggested that non-vascular factors may play an important role in the relationship between DR and AD, and that retinal and cerebral neurodegeneration may share common risk factors. Similarly, Lee et al., in a retrospective cohort study of 536 individuals with diabetes, found that having DR for more than five years was strongly associated with an increased risk of all-cause dementia and AD dementia, even after adjusting for time-varying indicators of diabetes severity (HR = 1.80; 95 % CI: 1.15–2.82).14 However, in other studies, the association between DR and NDDs is less clear. For example, Hwang et al. found that in participants from the Cardiovascular Health Study Cognition Study comparing ophthalmic conditions, including cataracts, age-related macular degeneration, DR, and glaucoma, there was no association between DR and AD (HR= 0.46; 95 % CI: 0.06–3.33), although their study was limited by a small sample size.15 Similarly, the AGES-Reykjavik cross-sectional study compared the presence of cerebral microbleeds or retinopathy in relation to AD and found no significant increase in the risk of AD among participants with retinopathy.16 It is worth noting that this study examined retinopathy overall and did not focus specifically on DR caused by diabetes, which may have diluted potential associations.

When investigating the association between DR and other neurodegenerative diseases, such as PD, results have also been mixed. In a large-scale study using the Korean National Health Insurance Service Database, Lee et al. reported that the incidence of PD was 2.74, 8.39, and 15.51 per 10,000 person-years for non-diabetes, diabetes without DR, and diabetes with DR groups, respectively.17 Multivariate Cox models showed that individuals with DR had a significantly higher odds of PD (HR = 1.75; 95 % CI: 1.64–1.86) than those with diabetes but without DR (HR = 1.33; 95 % CI: 1.29–1.38) and non-diabetic individuals (HR = 1; reference). In contrast, Mauricio et al. reported that, in a comparison between type 2 diabetes patients with and without DR, DR was not associated with increased PD odds after adjusting for various confounders, including age, sex, duration of diabetes, smoking, BMI, HbA1c levels, and comorbidities.18 Similarly, Larsen et al. found that, compared to individuals without diabetes, neither diabetes without DR (adjusted OR = 0.76; 95 % CI: 0.68–0.85; adjusted HR = 0.91; 95 % CI: 0.80–1.04) nor diabetes with DR (adjusted OR = 0.93; 95 % CI: 0.72–1.21; adjusted HR = 0.77; 95 % CI: 0.56–1.05) was associated with prevalent or incident PD.19 These discrepancies in findings may stem from differences in study design, population characteristics, diagnostic definitions, and follow-up duration. While some studies have proposed biological mechanisms linking DR and PD—such as disruption of dopaminergic pathways, increased α-synuclein expression, and altered neurotrophic factor signaling11,12—epidemiological evidence for these associations remains inconsistent. Our study adds to the literature suggesting that DM-only or DM+DR is not associated with the prevalence of PD.

Another potential explanation for the inconsistency across studies is the potential for DR to be more closely linked to vascular components of brain changes, such as vascular dementia, rather than to neurodegenerative conditions like AD or PD. For example, Chai et al. reported that DR and its subtypes may increase the risk of vascular dementia, but found no significant association between DR and AD.20 Vascular dementia arises from impaired cerebral blood flow, often secondary to microvascular pathology. Similarly, DR is characterized by retinal microvascular changes—such as capillary dropout, microaneurysms, and neovascularization—which may reflect parallel processes occurring in the cerebral microvasculature.21 These shared vascular insults could explain why DR may be more strongly linked to dementia—which encompasses both vascular and neurodegenerative forms—than to other forms of neurodegeneration. Given that community diagnoses of AD, without the use of cerebrospinal fluid or PET biomarkers, can be incorrect 25–30 % of the time—often due to the presence of vascular dementia—further delineating dementia subtypes could help clarify underlying associations.22 Future studies should, therefore, consider including vascular dementia and other cerebrovascular outcomes as endpoints when exploring the relationship between DR and neurocognitive changes. The exploration of vascular dementia was not conducted in this study due to an insufficient number of cases in the AoURP.

Our study has several limitations. First, due to sample size constraints and the lack of DR severity reporting in many cases, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses based on DR severity, such as distinguishing between non-proliferative and proliferative DR. Similarly, subset analysis could not be performed based on the types of diabetes, as the number of patients with type 1 diabetes was very small, and some patients had missing information regarding whether they had type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Additionally, our findings may underestimate potential associations due to limitations in the AoURP dataset. For instance, unlike Lee et al.’s study, which emphasized duration of over five years with DR as a key factor in dementia risk, our dataset lacked information on the duration of either diabetes or DR, which may be crucial for detecting associations that evolve over time. Furthermore, the AoURP population may not be representative of the general U.S. population. For example, while approximately 10 % of older Americans are estimated to have dementia, the prevalence reported among Non-DM group in our dataset was only 1.3 %, suggesting potential underreporting or selection bias.23 This notably low prevalence suggests underreporting or underdiagnosis, which may bias the observed associations. One likely explanation is that enrollment in All of Us requires a signed consent form, excluding individuals unable to provide consent, such as those with advanced neurodegenerative disease. This requirement likely resulted in a healthier sample and underrepresentation of more severe cases. As such, this implies under-ascertainment and consent-related selection of a healthier sample, which may bias the results. Additionally, patients with advanced dementia or other neurodegenerative diseases may be less likely to participate in research studies or to have undergone eye exams, further contributing to selection bias. As a result, our dataset may contain fewer and milder cases of dementia, AD, or other neurodegenerative disorders, which should be considered when interpreting the findings. Moreover, we used EHR-derived outcomes, which may be prone to under-ascertainment and misclassification due to incomplete or inaccurate diagnostic coding, missed diagnoses outside the health system, or fragmented care. Additionally, there were missing data in the All-of-US dataset. Although we coded individuals with a diagnosis based on SNOMED as having the disease, and those without a diagnosis as not having the disease, we could not determine whether the absence of a diagnosis reflected true absence of disease or missing data in SNOMED. Missing data in either covariates or outcomes may bias the evaluation of the associations. These factors could result in some true cases of neurodegenerative disease being undetected or incorrectly classified. Nondifferential misclassification may have biased associations toward the null, while differential misclassification—such as potential increased surveillance among DM+DR patients—could have either exaggerated or obscured true associations. Additionally, other neurological confounders such as depression and APOE status were not considered, along with potential residual confounding, which may have led to an overestimation of the observed associations.

Future investigations are needed to clarify the causal relationships and underlying mechanisms linking DR with neurodegeneration, particularly in ways that can inform clinical risk stratification and screening. While DR may reflect underlying neurovascular dysfunction, it is unlikely to serve as a sufficient standalone marker for cognitive decline in diabetes. Future longitudinal studies should integrate multimodal data—including clinical indicators of diabetes severity and duration such as longitudinal HbA1c trajectories, cumulative glycemic burden, and presence of systemic complications)—alongside retinal imaging metrics such as optical coherence tomography (OCT)-derived neuroretinal and vascular thinning. Combining these with neurocognitive assessments, brain imaging, and claims-confirmed dementia diagnoses could offer a more robust approach to identifying individuals at elevated risk for neurodegeneration. Such research would support the development of comprehensive models that reflect shared neurovascular pathways and improve the accuracy and feasibility of cognitive screening strategies in diabetic populations. Moreover, future work should distinguish between dementia subtypes, such as vascular versus neurodegenerative, to determine whether DR’s vascular or neurodegenerative features are more relevant to cognitive outcomes, thereby refining both mechanistic understanding and clinical applications.
