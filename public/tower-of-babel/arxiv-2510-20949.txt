Robust Synchronization of Time-Fractional Memristive Hopfield Neural Networks
Yuncheng You

Abstract
. In this paper we study robust synchronization of time-fractional Hopfield neural networks with memristive couplings and Hebbian learning rules. This new model of artificial neural networks exhibits strong memory and long-range path-dependence in learning processes. Through scaled group estimates it is proved that under rather general assumptions the solution dynamics is globally dissipative. The main result established a threshold condition for achieving robust synchronization of the neural networks if it is satisfied by the interneuron coupling strength coefficient. The synchronizing threshold is explicitly computable in terms of the original parameters and strictly decreasing for the fractional order α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) .

Consider a fractional-order memristive Hopfield neural network composed of
m
neuron nodes and denoted by
\mathcal{NW}=\{\mathcal{N}_{i}:i=1,2,\cdots,m\}
. The state variable
u_{i}(t)
for each neuron
\mathcal{N}_{i},1\leq i\leq m
, the symmetric weights
w_{ij}(t)
updated by the Hebbian learning rule
[
27
,
28
]
before synaptic activation functions
f_{j}(u_{j})
, and the memristor’s window function
\rho(t)
are governed by fractional differential equations of the same fractional order
\alpha
in this model:

(1.1)

\begin{split}&D_{c}^{\alpha}u_{i}(t)=-a_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{ij}\,f_{j}(u_{j})+k\,\psi_{i}(\rho)u_{i}+J_{i}-P\sum_{j=1}^{m}(u_{i}-u_{j}),\;\;1\leq i\leq m,\\
&D_{c}^{\alpha}w_{ij}(t)=-c_{ij}\,w_{ij}+\lambda_{ij}\,f_{i}(u_{i})f_{j}(u_{j}),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq m,\\
&D_{c}^{\alpha}\rho(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}u_{i}-b\rho,\quad\text{where}\quad t>0,\quad\alpha\in(0,1).\end{split}

In this model, the Caputo type fractional time derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
denoted by
D_{c}^{\alpha}
of a scalar function
y(t)
is defined by

D_{c}^{\alpha}y(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}\,y^{\prime}(s)\,ds,\quad t>0,

and
\Gamma(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\,z>0,
is the Gamma function, for
y(\cdot)\in C[0,\infty)
and
y^{\prime}(\cdot)\in L^{1}_{loc}[0,\infty)
. The coefficient
P>0
in (
1.1
) is called the interneuron coupling strength. Note that the state variable
u_{i}(t)
simulates the electric potential of a biological neuron. The initial states of the system (
1.1
) will be denoted by

(1.2)

u_{i}^{0}=u_{i}(0),\quad w_{ij}^{0}=w_{ij}(0),\quad\rho^{0}=\rho(0),\quad 1\leq i,j\leq m.

Here
w^{0}_{ij}=w^{0}_{ji}
for any
i,j
. The memristive feature of this model is reflected by the memductance-potential synapse term
k\psi_{i}(\rho)u_{i}
for each neuron
\mathcal{N}_{i}
. The Hebbian learning rule used in (
1.1
) for synaptic weights
w_{ij}(t)
demonstrated good performance in unsupervised machine learning tasks on certain datasets like MNIST datasets and CIFAR datasets in image recognition and classification
[
29
]
.

We assume that the scalar functions
f_{i}(s),1\leq i\leq m
are locally Lipschitz continuous functions and the memristor window’s functions are
\psi_{i}(s)=s(\eta_{i}-s),1\leq i\leq m
. The parameters
a_{i},b,k,\eta_{i},c_{ij}
can be any given positive constants, while the input/bias constants
J_{i}
and coefficients
\lambda_{ij},\gamma_{i}
can be any given real numbers. More specific assumptions on parameters and element functions will be made in Section 3.

In this paper we shall define robust synchronization for artificial neural network models and investigate this new model (
1.1
) of fractional-order memristive Hopfield neural networks to rigorously prove that robust synchronization of this fmHHNN will be triggered if the network coupling strength coefficient

satisfies a computable threshold condition.

In Section 3 we show the dissipative dynamics of global solutions to the initial value problem (

In Section 4 we shall prove the main result on robust synchronization of the fractional memristive Hopfield-Hebbian neural networks by the approach of direct uniform estimates of the interneuron differencing equations. Conclusions will be in the end.

2.
Fractional Analysis Lemmas

Fractional-order derivatives and fractional calculus broadly expanded the mathematical fields in modeling physical processes and phenomenological dynamics by fractional differential equations (FDE). The framework of FDE provides a flexible incorporation of longterm memory of continuum past states in the trajectories of real time evolution. The basic theory and some applications of fractional calculus and FDE can be found in books
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
.

Caputo fractional derivative
D_{c}^{\alpha}y(t)
defined in Section 1 is strictly called the left-sided Djrbashian-Caputo fractional derivative
[
32
,
33
]
, usually for absolutely continuous functions
y\in AC[0,T)
which annihilated the singularity at the initial point
t=0
. Compare with Riemann-Liouville fractional derivative and Grünwald-Letnikov fractional derivative, Caputo fractional derivative can easily handle the initial conditions in a way that matches the physically observable ordinary derivatives at
t=0
, making it most suitable and popular for modeling initial value problems of evolutionary equations in real world applications. But the product rule and the chain rule for the ordinary derivatives are not valid for Caputo fractional derivatives in analysis.

First we present a useful Lemma
[
34
]
with a brief proof, which will be used in conducting
a priori
estimates of solutions for FDE.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
\alpha\in(0,1)
and any given
T>0
, if
f\in AC[0,T]
, then it holds that

(2.1)

\frac{1}{2}\,D_{c}^{\alpha}f^{2}(t)\leq f(t)D_{c}^{\alpha}f(t),\quad\text{for}\;\;t\in(0,T).

Proof.

By change order of integration and then integration by parts, we have

\begin{split}&\Gamma(1-\alpha)\left[f(t)D_{c}^{\alpha}f(t)-\frac{1}{2}\,D_{c}^{\alpha}f^{2}(t)\right]\\
=&\,f(t)\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds-\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f(s)f^{\prime}(s)\,ds\\
=&\,\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,[f(t)-f(s)]\,ds=\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\int_{s}^{t}f^{\prime}(x)\,dx\,ds\\
\overset{\text{CoI}}{=}&\,\int_{0}^{t}f^{\prime}(x)\int_{0}^{x}(t-s)^{\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds\,dx=\int_{0}^{t}(t-x)^{\alpha}\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{(t-x)^{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{x}(t-s)^{\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds\,dx\\
=&\,\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(t-x)^{\alpha}\frac{d}{dx}\left(\int_{0}^{x}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds\right)^{2}dx\\
\overset{\text{IbP}}{=}&\,\frac{\alpha}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(t-x)^{\alpha-1}\left(\int_{0}^{x}(t-s)^{-\alpha}f^{\prime}(s)\,ds\right)^{2}dx\geq 0,\quad t\in(0,T).\end{split}

Since
\Gamma(1-\alpha)>0
for
\alpha\in(0,1)
, the inequality (
2.1
) is proved.
∎

The Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(z)
and the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(z)

[
35
,
36
]
defined by

E_{\alpha}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{\Gamma(n\alpha+1)},\quad E_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}}(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n}}{\Gamma(n\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},

have an essential role to play in fractional differential equations and analysis.
E_{\alpha}(z)
is an entire function and
E_{1}(z)=e^{z}
. The function
E_{\alpha}(-x)
with real variable
x>0
is especially more involved in treatment of FDE. It is completely monotone shown in
[
32
, Corollary 3.2]
meaning the derivatives
(-1)^{n}E_{\alpha}^{(n)}(-x)\geq 0
for
x>0
and all positive integer orders
n
.

The following Lemma provides a very useful tool in study of dynamics with respect to global solutions of fractional-order differential equations.

Lemma 2.2
.

(Fractional Gronwall Inequality)

If a nonnegative function
x(t)
is absolutely continuous for
t\geq 0
and satisfies the inequality

(2.2)

D_{c}^{\alpha}x(t)\leq p-q\,x(t),\quad\text{for}\;\;t>0,

where the fractional order is
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
p
and
q
are positive constants, then it holds that

(2.3)

\begin{split}x(t)&\,\leq x(0)\,E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+\,\frac{p}{q}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\left(1-E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})\right)\\
&\,<x(0)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+qt^{\alpha}}\right)+\,\frac{p}{q}\,\Gamma(\alpha),\quad\text{for}\;\;t>0.\end{split}

Proof.

Parallel to the variation of constants formula for linear ODEs, it can be shown
[
32
, Proposition 4.5]
that
x(t)
in the inequality (
2.2
) satisfies

(2.4)

\begin{split}x(t)&\,=x(0)E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+p\int_{o}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-q(t-s)^{\alpha})\,ds\\
&\,-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{R(t)}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-q(t-s)^{\alpha})\,ds\\
&\,\leq x(0)E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+p\int_{o}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-q(t-s)^{\alpha})\,ds,\quad t>0,\end{split}

where
-R(t)
is the nonpositive gap function between the two sides of the inequality (
2.2
) and for
0<\alpha<1
the function
E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-x)
is always positive on
x>0
due to the complete monotonicity as mentioned.

By the integral relation with the two-parameter Mittag-Leffler functions
[
36
, Section 4.4]
, we have

(2.5)

\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{o}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}E_{\alpha,\alpha}(-q(t-s)^{\alpha})\,ds=t^{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-q\,t^{\alpha}).

From (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) it follows that

(2.6)

x(t)\leq x(0)E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+p\,\Gamma(\alpha)\,t^{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-q\,t^{\alpha}),\quad t>0.

Note that
[
31
, Theorem 4.2]

E_{\alpha,\beta}\,(x)=x\,E_{\alpha,\alpha+\beta}\,(x)+\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}.

Then we have

(2.7)

t^{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-q\,t^{\alpha})=\frac{1}{q}\left[\frac{1}{\Gamma(1)}-E_{\alpha,1}\,(-q\,t^{\alpha})\right]=\frac{1}{q}\left[1-E_{\alpha}\,(-q\,t^{\alpha})\right].

Moreover, Theorem 3.6 in
[
32
]
shows that the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(-x)
admits the two-side bounds

(2.8)

\frac{1}{1+\Gamma(1-\alpha)x}\leq E_{\alpha}(-x)\leq\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+x},\quad x>0.

Finally substitute (
2.7
) and then (
2.8
) with
x=q\,t^{\alpha}
into (
2.6
), we end up with

\begin{split}x(t)&\,\leq x(0)E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+p\,\Gamma(\alpha)\,t^{\alpha}E_{\alpha,\alpha+1}(-q\,t^{\alpha})\\[4.0pt]
&\,=x(0)E_{\alpha}(-q\,t^{\alpha})+\frac{p}{q}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\left(1-E_{\alpha}\,(-q\,t^{\alpha})\right)\\
&\,\leq x(0)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+q\,t^{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{p}{q}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1-\alpha)q\,t^{\alpha}}{1+\Gamma(1-\alpha)q\,t^{\alpha}}\right)\\
&\,<x(0)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+q\,t^{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{p}{q}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\,,\quad t>0.\end{split}

The fractional Gronwall inequality (
2.3
) is proved.
∎

In regard to fractional-order differential equations, the existence of global solutions for an initial value problem needs to be carefully addressed. Continuation of a local solution in time is a subtle issue because two local solution segments in time may not be simply concatenated like ODEs. In fact two different solution trajectories of a Caputo FDE can intersect
[
37
, Theorem 6.1]
.

The next lemma presents an implementable sufficient condition, other than the stringent global Lipschitz condition, for the existence of global solutions in time with respect to initial value problems (IVP) of autonomous fractional differential equations.

Lemma 2.3
.

For an initial value problem of autonomous Caputo fractional differential equation
(
s
)

D_{c}^{\alpha}x(t)=f(x),\quad x(0)=x_{0}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

if the scalar or vector function
f(x)
is locally Lipschitz on its bounded or unbounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a solution of this IVP satisfies

(2.9)

\|x(t)\|\leq\|x_{0}\|\,C(t)+Q,\qquad t\in I_{max}=[0,T_{max}),

where
C(t)
is a positive non-increasing continuous function,
C(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, and
Q>0
is a constant, then the maximal existence interval of this solution
I_{max}=[0,\infty)
so that
x(t)
is a global solution in time.

Proof.

According to the dissipative condition (
2.9
), any solution trajectory satisfies
\{x(t):t\in I_{max}\}\subset B_{x_{0}}=\{x\in\Omega:\|x\|\leq\|x_{0}\|C(0)+Q+1\}
, which is a bounded ball in the state space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the locally Lipschitz continuous condition, the vector field
f(x)
is globally Lipschitz within the bounded set
B_{x_{0}}
. Then we can apply the standard existence and uniqueness theorems
[
31
,
32
,
38
]
to assert that this solution
x(t)
is a unique global solution,
I_{max}=[0,\infty)
, of this initial value problem.
∎

Absorbing set
[
39
]
defined below is an important concept in study of dissipative evolutionary dynamics.

Definition 2.4
.

For a
d
-dimensional system
S
of time-fractional differential equations, a bounded subset
B^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called an absorbing set if for any given bounded set
B
in the state space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
there is a finite time
T_{B}\geq 0
such that all the solution trajectories of this system
S
initially started in the set
B
will permanently enter the set
B^{*}
when
t>T_{B}
. A system of fractional differential equations is called
dissipative
if there exists an absorbing set.

The Young’s inequality is widely seen in management of differential inequalities. It states that for any positive numbers
x
and
y
, if
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
and
p>1,q>1
are constants, then one has

(2.10)

x\,y\leq\frac{1}{p}\,\varepsilon\,x^{p}+\frac{1}{q}\,C(\varepsilon,p)\,y^{q}\leq\varepsilon\,x^{p}+C(\varepsilon,p)\,y^{q},

where
C(\varepsilon,p)=\varepsilon^{-q/p}
and the constant
\varepsilon>0
can be arbitrarily given.

Finally in this section, we introduce the definition of robust synchronization.

Definition 2.5
.

For an artificial neural network model
\mathcal{NW}
in terms of time-fractional differential equations such as (
1.1
), define its synchronous degree to be

(2.11)

\text{deg}_{s}(\mathcal{NW})=\sup_{u^{0}\in\,\Omega\,\subset\,\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\max_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}|u_{i}(t)-u_{j}(t)|\right\}\right\}.

where
u^{0}=(u_{1}(0),\cdots,u_{d}(0))
is any initial state of the neurons in the domain
\Omega
of the state space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The neural network
\mathcal{NW}
is called robust synchronizable if for any small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an interneuron coupling threshold
P^{*}(\varepsilon)>0
such that
\text{deg}_{s}(\mathcal{NW})<\varepsilon
for all
P>P^{*}(\varepsilon)
.

3.
Dissipative Dynamics of Fractional Hopfield Neural Networks

The mathematical model (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) of the Caputo fractional neural network
\mathcal{NW}
with Hebbian learning rule can be formulated into an initial value problem of the evolutionary equation:

(3.1)

\begin{split}&D_{c}^{\alpha}\,g(t)=F(g),\quad\>\;t>0,\\[3.0pt]
&g(0)=g^{0}\in X=\mathbb{R}^{m(1+m)+1}.\end{split}

In (
3.1
) the column vector function
g(t)=\text{col}\left(u_{i}(t),w_{ij}(t),\rho(t)):1\leq i,j\leq m\right)
and the initial state vector
g(0)=g^{0}=\text{col}((u_{i}^{0},\,w_{ij}^{0},\rho^{0}):1\leq i,j\leq m)
.

In (
3.1
) the
m(1+m)+1
-dimensional nonlinear vector function

(3.2)

F(g)=\begin{pmatrix}-a_{i}u_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{ij}f_{j}(u_{j})+k\,\psi_{i}(\rho)u_{i}+J_{1}-P\,\sum_{j=1}^{m}(u_{i}-u_{j}):1\leq i\leq m\\[10.0pt]
-c_{ij}w_{ij}+\lambda_{ij}f_{i}(u_{i})f_{j}(u_{j}):1\leq i,j\leq m\\[10.0pt]
-b\,\rho+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\gamma_{i}u_{i}\end{pmatrix}

is locally Lipschitz continuous. (
3.1
) is the concise version of the model (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) of the fractional memristive Hopfield-Hebbian neural networks.

Here are some remarks on the versatility and features: [1] The activation functions
\{f_{i}(x):1\leq i\leq m\}
can be heterogeneous for different neurons, including Softplus
\textup{Sp}(x)=\ln\,(1+e^{x}),\,\textup{ReLU}(x)
,
\textup{GeLU}(x)=(x/2)[1+\textup{Erf}\,(x/\sqrt{2})]
, which are plausibly assumed to be uniformly bounded functions since the analogous potential of biological neurons are bounded from the resting level up to the bursting limit. [2] This model is compatible with typical memristor (chips or circuits) window functions such as sigmoidal function
\tanh(s)
, linear function
1-\eta|s|
, quadratic function
1-\eta s^{2}
, Jogelker memristor
1-(2s-1)^{2q}
, and Strukov-Williams memristor
s(\eta-s)
which is taken in this model and used in CMOS technology. [3] The synaptic weights
w_{ij}(t)
updated real time by the Hebbian rule actually incarnate the key biological neuron factors of locality, synaptic dissipativity, cooperative (
\lambda_{ij}>0
) or competitive (
\lambda_{ij}<0
) attributes. The matrix of Hebbian coefficients
\{\lambda_{ij}\}
describes topological pathways of information through the network and can be sparse in training or learning networks.

We make the following Assumption and set up new notations:

(3.3)

\begin{split}&a=\min\,\{a_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\}>\frac{1}{2}\,k\eta^{2},\;\;|f_{i}(s)|\leq\beta,\;\;J=\max\,\{|J_{i}|:1\leq i\leq m\},\\
&\psi_{i}(s)=s(\eta_{i}-s),\;\eta=\max\,\{\eta_{i}:1\leq i\leq m\},\;\gamma=\max\,\{|\gamma_{i}|:1\leq i\leq m\},\\[2.0pt]
&\lambda=\max\,\{|\lambda_{ij}|:1\leq i,j\leq m\},\quad c=\min\,\{c_{ij}:1\leq i,j\leq m\}>0.\end{split}

First we address the existence of global solutions for this initial value problem (
3.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Given any initial state
g^{0}\in X
, there exists a unique global solution
g(t;g^{0}),\;t\in[0,\infty),
for the initial value problem (
3.1
) of the fractional memristive Hopfield neural network
\mathcal{NW}
described by the model (
1.1
) with the Assumption (
3.3
).

Proof.

By the standard theorems on solutions of Caputo fractional differential equations
[
31
,
32
]
and the locally Lipschitz continuous condition here, for any given initial state
g^{0}\in X
there exists a unique local solution
g(t,g^{0})
of (
3.1
), whose maximum existence interval is
I_{max}=[0,T_{max}(g^{0}))
may depending on
g^{0}
.

Multiply the
w_{ij}
-equation in (
1.1
) by
w_{ij}(t)
. Lemma
2.1
with (
3.3
) shows that

(3.4)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}D_{c}^{\alpha}w^{2}_{ij}(t)&\leq w_{ij}(t)D_{c}^{\alpha}w_{ij}(t)=-c_{ij}\,w^{2}_{ij}(t)+\lambda_{ij}\,w_{ij}(t)f_{i}(u_{i}(t))f_{j}(u_{j}(t))\\
&\leq-c\,w^{2}_{ij}(t)+\lambda\,\beta^{2}\,w_{ij}(t)\leq-\frac{1}{2}\,c\,w^{2}_{ij}(t)+\frac{1}{2c}\,\lambda^{2}\beta^{4},\quad t\in I_{max}.\end{split}

Then by Lemma
2.2
we have

(3.5)

w^{2}_{ij}(t)\leq w^{2}_{ij}(0)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+c\,t^{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{4}}{c^{2}}\Gamma(\alpha)\leq w^{2}_{ij}(0)+\frac{\lambda^{2}\beta^{4}}{c^{2}}\Gamma(\alpha),\;\;1\leq i,j\leq m.

Denote by
W_{0}=[\max\{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w^{2}_{ij}(0):i=1,\cdots,m\}]^{1/2}
. It follows that

(3.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}w^{2}_{ij}(t)\leq mW_{0}^{2}\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+c\,t^{\alpha}}\right)+\frac{m^{2}\lambda^{2}\beta^{4}}{c^{2}}\Gamma(\alpha),\;\;t\in I_{max}.

Next multiply the
u_{i}
-equation in (
1.1
) by
C_{1}u_{i}(t)
for
1\leq i\leq m
and sum them up. Using (
3.3
) and (
3.6
) we get

(3.7)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}D_{c}^{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}u^{2}_{i}+\frac{1}{2}C_{1}P\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\,(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}\leq C_{1}u_{i}D_{c}^{\alpha}u_{i}+\frac{1}{2}C_{1}P\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\,(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}\\
=&\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[-a_{i}u^{2}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{ij}(t)f_{j}(u_{j})u_{i}+k\psi_{i}(\rho)u_{i}^{2}+J_{i}u_{i}\right]\\
\leq&\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[-au_{i}^{2}+\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{m}w_{ij}^{2}(0)G_{\alpha}(t)+\frac{m\lambda^{2}\beta^{4}}{c^{2}}\Gamma(\alpha)}\;\beta|u_{i}|+\left[k\rho(\eta_{i}-\rho)u_{i}^{2}+J|u_{i}|\right]\right]\\
\leq&\,-\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[a-\frac{1}{2}k\eta^{2}\right]u_{i}^{2}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[J+\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]\right]|u_{i}(t)|\\
\leq&\,-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[a-\frac{1}{2}k\eta^{2}\right]u_{i}^{2}(t)+\frac{1}{2}\,C_{1}m\frac{\left[J+\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]\,\right]^{2}}{a-\frac{1}{2}k\eta^{2}}\end{split}

for
t\in I_{max}
, where
C_{1}>0
is a scaling constant yet to be specified, the double sum of interneuron coupling terms in the first inequality of (

\displaystyle-C_{1}P\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\left[(u_{j}-u_{i})u_{i}+(u_{i}-u_{j})u_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=-C_{1}P\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq m}(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}C_{1}P\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\,(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}

and moved to the left side with the derivative terms. In the third inequality of (

G_{\alpha}(t)=\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+c\,t^{\alpha}}\leq 1\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}G_{\alpha}(t)=0.

We also treated the memductance synaptic term in the third inequality of (

\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}&\,\left[\frac{m\gamma_{i}^{2}}{2b}u_{i}^{2}(t)+\frac{b}{2m}\rho^{2}(t)\right]-b\rho^{2}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{m\gamma_{i}^{2}}{2b}\,u_{i}^{2}(t)-\frac{b}{2}\,\rho^{2}(t),\;\;t\in I_{max}.\end{split}

Combine the two differential inequalities (

\begin{split}&D_{c}^{\alpha}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}u^{2}_{i}+\rho^{2}(t)\right)+C_{1}P\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\,(u_{i}-u_{j})^{2}\\
\leq&-\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{1}\left[a-\frac{1}{2}k\eta^{2}\right]u_{i}^{2}(t)+C_{1}m\frac{\left[J+\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]\,\right]^{2}}{a-\frac{1}{2}k\eta^{2}}\\
&+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{m\gamma^{2}}{b}\,u_{i}^{2}(t)-b\,\rho^{2}(t)\right),\quad t\in I_{max}.\end{split}

Now we choose

\leq&\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(C_{1}u_{i}^{2}(0)+\rho^{2}(0)\right)E_{\alpha}(-\delta\,t^{\alpha})+\,\frac{C_{2}(W_{0})}{\delta}\,\Gamma(\alpha)\left(1-E_{\alpha}(-\delta\,t^{\alpha})\right)\\
\leq&\,\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left(C_{1}u_{i}^{2}(0)+\rho^{2}(0)\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+\delta\,t^{\alpha}}\right)+\,\frac{C_{2}(W_{0})}{\delta}\,\Gamma(\alpha),\quad\text{for}\;\;t\in I_{max}.\end{split}

where

(3.18)

for

4.
Main Result on Robust Synchronization

In this section we shall prove the main result on robust synchronization of the fractional memristive Hopfield-Hebbian neural networks. The approach is to tackle the fractional differencing equations of the pairwise gap functions of the neuron state variables between any two nodes in the network. Then conduct
a priori
estimates to sharply derive a computable threshold condition in terms of the known parameters, which will trigger occurrence of robust synchronization of the entire neural network once this condition is satisfied by the interneuron coupling strength.

The gap functions between any two neuron nodes
\mathcal{N}_{i}
and
\mathcal{N}_{j}
are denoted by

\displaystyle U_{ij}(t)=u_{i}(t)-u_{j}(t),\quad\text{for}\;\;1\leq i\neq j\leq m.

These gap functions satisfy the fractional differencing equations

(4.1)

\begin{split}D_{c}^{\alpha}U_{ij}(t)=&\,-\left(a_{i}U_{ij}+(a_{i}-a_{j})u_{j}\right)+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\,\left(w_{i\ell}-w_{j\ell}\right)f_{\ell}(u_{\ell})\\
&\,+k\,(\psi_{i}(\rho)u_{i}-\psi_{j}(\rho)u_{j})+(J_{i}-J_{j})-mPU_{ij},\end{split}

where the last term is
-P\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}[(u_{i}-u_{\ell})-(u_{j}-u_{\ell})]=-P\,\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}(u_{i}-u_{j})=-mPU_{ij}
.

We introduce the following secondary parameters involved in the estimates of the fractional differencing equations (
4.1
) of the gap functions:

\displaystyle a^{*}=\max\{|a_{i}-a_{j}|:1\leq i,j\leq m\},

\displaystyle\eta^{*}=\max\{|\eta_{i}-\eta_{j}|:1\leq i,j\leq m\},\quad J^{*}=\max\{|J_{i}-J_{j}|:1\leq i,j\leq m\}.

The main result of this work is the following theorem on the threshold condition for the interneuron coupling strength to achieve a robust synchronization of the entire fractional neural network
\mathcal{NW}
described in (
1.1
). The methodology in the proof is based on the global dissipative dynamics of this neural network and through analytic
a priori
estimation of the fractional differencing equations.

Theorem 4.1
.

The time-fractional memristive Hopfield-Hebbian neural network
\mathcal{NW}
presented in the model (
1.1
) with the Assumption (
3.3
) is robust synchronizable. For any prescribed small gap
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant threshold

(4.2)

P_{\alpha}^{*}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{m\,\varepsilon}\left[a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right]>0,

where
M(\alpha)
is shown in (
3.18
), such that if the interneuron coupling strength coefficient
P>P_{\alpha}^{*}(\varepsilon)
, then the neural network
\mathcal{NW}
is robust synchronized. Namely, for any prescribed small gap
\varepsilon>0
,

(4.3)

\deg_{s}\,(\mathcal{NW})=\sup_{(u_{1}^{0},\,\cdots,\,u_{m}^{0},\,\rho^{0})\,\in\,\mathbb{R}^{m+1}}\left\{\max_{1\leq i<j\leq m}\left\{\limsup_{t\to\infty}\,|u_{i}(t)-u_{j}(t)|\right\}\right\}<\varepsilon.

Moreover, the synchronization threshold
P_{\alpha}^{*}(\varepsilon)
is strictly decreasing with respect to the fractional-order
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

In the sequel of this proof we can denote
U_{ij}(t)
by
U(t)
for notational simplicity. For any given
1\leq i\neq j\leq m
, multiply the
U_{ij}
-equation (
4.1
) by
U_{ij}(t)
. With (
3.5
) and (
3.20
) we have

(4.4)

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\,D_{c}^{\alpha}U^{2}(t)\leq U(t)D_{c}^{\alpha}U(t)=-a_{i}U^{2}(t)-(a_{i}-a_{j})u_{j}(t)U(t)\\
&+\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\,\left(w_{i\ell}(t)-w_{j\ell}(t)\right)f_{\ell}(u_{\ell}(t))U(t)\\[4.0pt]
&+k(\psi_{i}(\rho(t))-\psi_{j}(\rho(t)))u_{i}(t)U(t)+k\psi_{j}(\rho(t))U^{2}(t)+(J_{i}-J_{j})U(t)-mPU^{2}(t)\\[3.0pt]
\leq&-aU^{2}(t)+a^{*}|u_{j}(t)||U(t)|+2\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]|U(t)|\\[7.0pt]
&\,+k|\eta_{i}-\eta_{j}|\rho(t)|u_{i}(t)||U(t)|+k\left(\eta_{j}\rho(t)-\rho^{2}(t)\right)U^{2}(t)+J^{*}|U(t)|-mPU^{2}(t)\\[2.0pt]
\leq&-aU^{2}(t)+a^{*}|u_{j}(t)||U(t)|+2\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]|U(t)|\\[2.0pt]
&\,+k\eta^{*}|\rho(t)||u_{i}(t)||U(t)|+\frac{1}{2}\,k\,(\eta^{2}-\rho^{2}(t))U^{2}(t)+J^{*}|U(t)|-mPU^{2}(t)\\
\leq&\,-\left[a-\frac{1}{2}k\,\eta^{2}+mP\right]U^{2}(t)\\
&\,+\left[a^{*}|u_{j}(t)|+k\eta^{*}|\rho(t)||u_{i}(t)|+2\beta\left[W_{0}\sqrt{G_{\alpha}(t)}+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right]|U(t)|.\end{split}

According to the ultimate bounding estimate (
3.20
) and (
3.8
), for any given initial state and weights
g^{0}=(u_{i}^{0},w_{ij}^{0},\rho^{0})
, there is a finite time
T(g^{0})>0
such that

Therefore the above differential inequality (
4.4
) implies that, for
t>T(g^{0})
,

(4.5)

\begin{split}&D_{c}^{\alpha}U^{2}(t)+2\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\,k\eta^{2}+mP\right)U^{2}(t)\\
\leq&\,2\left(a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right)|U(t)|.\end{split}

Use Cauchy inequality to split the product on the right-hand side of the inequality (

) into two terms. Then we obtain

(4.6)

\begin{split}&D_{c}^{\alpha}U^{2}(t)+\left(a-\frac{1}{2}\,k\eta^{2}+mP\right)U^{2}(t)\\
\leq&\,\frac{\left(a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right)^{2}}{a-\frac{1}{2}\,k\eta^{2}+mP},\quad t>T(g^{0}).\end{split}

Denote by

(4.7)

\mu=a-\frac{1}{2}\,k\eta^{2}+mP.

From (
4.6

(4.8)

D_{c}^{\alpha}U^{2}(t)+\mu U^{2}(t)\leq\frac{1}{\mu}\left(a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right)^{2}

for

. Apply the fractional Gronwall inequality (

) and (

) in Lemma
2.2
to the fractional differential inequality (
4.8

\begin{split}&U_{ij}^{2}(t)\leq U_{ij}^{2}(T_{g^{0}})\left(\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{\Gamma(1+\alpha)+\mu(t-T(g^{0}))^{\alpha}}\right)\\
+&\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)}{\mu^{2}}\left[a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right]^{2},\;t>T(g^{0}).\end{split}

Finally, for any prescribed small gap

\begin{split}&\limsup_{t\to\infty}|u_{i}(t)-u_{j}(t)|=\limsup_{t\to\infty}|U_{ij}(t)|\\[3.0pt]
\leq&\,\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma(\alpha)}}{\mu}\left[a^{*}M^{1/2}(\alpha)+k\eta^{*}M^{3/2}(\alpha)+2\beta\left[1+\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{m\Gamma(\alpha)}\lambda\beta^{2}\right]+J^{*}\right]\\

In particular, by the strict decreasing property of

Although Hopfield neural networks are cross-coupled networks but not multilayer or deep neural networks, the procedures and techniques in this work can be applied to study of interlayer and intralayer synchronization of multilayer learning neural networks as well as the cluster synchronization of many multiplex networks.

Conclusions

We summarize the contributions in this paper and outlook for further extensions.

First we proposed and investigated a new model.of Caputo type time-fractional memristive Hopfield-Hebbian neural networks. This model incorporates multiple features of long-term memory, efficient stinulus responses in brain-machine learning, and Hebbian learning rules in unsupervised meaning.

The fractional analysis of three Lemmas are presented and proved as a foundation for conducting the scaled
a priori
estimates of solutions, especially the fractional Gronwall inequality not just in the form of Mittag-Lefflrter functions but also in the explicit form in terms of Gamma functions of a fractional order, which enables us to effectively establish the global solution existence and the ultimate absorbing bound. It paves the way to explore the asymptotic dynamics of the time-fractional neural networks.

The main contribution of this paper is Theorem
4.1
. It rigorously proved a sufficient threshold condition to be satisfied by a single interneuron coupling strength coefficient for achieving robust synchronization of the Caputo time-fractional neural networks. The usefulness and merit of this result lie in the explicit expression of the threshold and the convergence rate, which are computable in terms of the original parameters. Moreover, threshold is shown to be decreasing when the fractional order
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

The methodology and analysis in this work potentially can be generalized to study other types of time-fractional artificial neural networks, especially for multilayer forward neural networks with semi-supervised data sets and for multiplex networks.

Mathematical research on predicable or complex asymptotic dynamics including synchronization of artificial neural networks is meaningful in deep learning performances but remains challenging and widely open, especially for training and learning with backpropagation algorithms.
