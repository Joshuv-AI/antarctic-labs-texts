pH-Responsive Carboxymethyl Cellulose-Encapsulating Hesperidin–Selenium Nanoparticles Attenuate Paracetamol-Induced Acute Kidney Injury via Keap-1/Nrf2, NF-κB, and Mitochondrial Apoptosis Modulation
Authors: Mohamed H. A. Gadelmawla, Khaled M. Alam-Eldein, Afnan Saleh, Sama Adel, Haneen Ali, Shaza Ayman, Esraa Tarek, Nievin Ahmed Mahran, Ahmed M. Ashour, Nasser M. Alorfi, Fahad S. Alshehri, Wessam N. El-Sayed, Ahmed Hassan Ibrahim Faraag, Ali Khames, Salma M. Selim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paracetamol overdose is a major cause of drug-induced acute kidney injury (AKI), driven by oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and tubular apoptosis. This study evaluated the nephroprotective efficacy of pH-responsive carboxymethyl cellulose-encapsulated hesperidin-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (CMC@HES-SeNPs) against paracetamol-induced AKI in rats. HES-SeNPs were synthesized using hesperidin as a reducing/stabilizing agent and further coated with CMC. The nanoparticles were characterized by DLS, zeta potential, TEM, and in vitro release kinetics at pH 7.4 and 5.5. 42 Male rats were allocated into groups of control, paracetamol (PAR), paracetamol treated with sodium selenite (PAR&Se), paracetamol treated with hesperidin (PAR&HES), paracetamol treated with hesperidin-loaded selenium nanoparticles (PAR&HES-SeNPs), and paracetamol treated with carboxy methyl cellulose-coated hesperidin-loaded selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs). Paracetamol markedly impaired renal function, increasing creatinine, urea, NGAL, KIM-1, and cystatin-C, and induced oxidative/nitrosative stress, Keap-1 upregulation, Nrf2 suppression, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, cytochrome-C release, Bax/Bcl-2 imbalance, caspase-3 activation, and severe renal histopathological injury. CMC@HES-SeNPs displayed sustained, pH-enhanced hesperidin release and produced the strongest renoprotective response, restoring renal biomarkers, associated with restoration of Keap-1/Nrf2-related antioxidant markers, reduction in TNF-α, IL-6, NF-κB, and caspase-3, increased IL-10, and preservation of renal architecture. Collectively, these results indicate that CMC@HES-SeNPs represent a promising multifunctional nanoplatform for mitigating paracetamol-induced AKI in association with coordinated modulation of redox, inflammatory, and mitochondrial apoptotic markers, and highlight CMC encapsulation as a rational strategy to enhance selenium–flavonoid delivery and efficacy for future drug-induced AKI management and translation.

1. Introduction

One of the most used analgesics and antipyretics, paracetamol (acetaminophen) has been linked with serious adverse effects, especially when used in high doses [1]. Although usually safe under recommended dosage, overdose of paracetamol may cause severe hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity [2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a well-reported complication of paracetamol toxicity, in which the kidney experiences oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular apoptosis leading to renal dysfunction [3]. Acute and chronic kidney damage caused by paracetamol has been widely researched, and the pathophysiology of nephrotoxicity is usually attributed to the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the loss of antioxidant defenses in renal cells [4].

Although there are therapeutic interventions, the existing pharmacological therapies for kidney damage due to paracetamol are limited in number [5]. Conventional therapeutic agents like N-acetylcysteine (NAC) offer partial protection and are well known to have side effects, thereby requiring investigation into other treatment options [6,7,8,9]. To this end, there has been a lot of interest in the potential of herbal medicine for kidney protection [8,9]. Many plant-based compounds have been shown to have nephroprotective effects due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects, making them a potential candidate as adjunctive therapeutic agents in kidney injury management [10,11,12].

Of these, selenium (Se) has been a promising element because of its well-documented antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, which play a critical role in the reduction in renal damage [13]. In the nanoparticulate form, selenium has been revealed to be more bioactive than its bulk counterpart. Biosynthesized selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs), and specifically those produced by using natural plant extracts, provide benefits over chemically produced nanoparticles, including toxicity and biocompatibility [14]. In addition, functionalization of SeNPs with natural stabilizers also increases their stability and bioavailability, which makes them a good candidate in renal protection [15,16].

Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) is also a biocompatible and biodegradable polysaccharide that has found extensive application in the formulation of nanoparticles as a stabilizing agent [17,18]. The fact that CMC can increase the solubility and stability of nanoparticles, in addition to having the capability to regulate drug release, makes it a perfect choice to increase the bioavailability of selenium nanoparticles [19]. Through stabilizing the SeNPs, CMC is known to improve the pharmacokinetic properties of these nanoparticles and optimize their effectiveness in treating kidney injury [20].

This paper seeks to assess the renal protective properties of CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles biosynthesized with the help of hesperidin, which is a flavonoid with antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. We are interested in the possibilities of this nanoparticle formulation to reverse acute kidney injury caused by paracetamol, which can shed light on new and sustainable methods of kidney protection.

2. Results

2.1. Drug Release Profiles of CMC@HES-SeNPs

Hesperidin release from CMC@HES-SeNPs followed a biphasic pattern at both pH values tested, consisting of a moderate initial burst phase followed by a prolonged, near-linear sustained release phase (Figure S1). At physiological pH 7.4, an initial burst release of approximately 18.4 ± 1.6% was observed within the first two hours, attributable to rapid diffusion of surface-associated hesperidin molecules. This was followed by a controlled release phase reaching 62.3 ± 2.8% cumulative release at 48 h, with a plateau of 68.7 ± 3.1% by 72 h. The restrained release at physiological pH reflects the electrostatic shielding provided by the negatively charged CMC coat, which slows matrix erosion and reduces drug–medium interactions.

At pH 5.5, release was notably accelerated throughout. The burst phase was more pronounced (28.6 ± 2.2% within 2 h), and cumulative release reached 81.4 ± 3.4% at 48 h and 89.2 ± 2.9% at 72 h. This pH-responsive behavior is mechanistically explained by partial protonation of the carboxymethyl groups on the CMC backbone under acidic conditions, which reduces crosslink density, increases polymer chain swelling, and facilitates hesperidin diffusion through the matrix. This pH-dependent release is particularly relevant given that paracetamol-induced AKI generates a locally acidic renal microenvironment, suggesting that CMC@HES-SeNPs will preferentially release their therapeutic cargo at the injury site. By contrast, free hesperidin solution achieved greater than 95% release within 8 h at both pH values, confirming that the dialysis membrane itself was not rate-limiting and that delayed release from CMC@HES-SeNPs reflects genuine encapsulation-controlled kinetics.

Kinetic modeling results are summarized in Table S1 and Figure S1. The Korsmeyer–Peppas model provided the best fit at both pH values (R2 = 0.994 at pH 7.4 and 0.991 at pH 5.5). The release exponent n was 0.54 ± 0.03 at pH 7.4 and 0.61 ± 0.04 at pH 5.5, both falling in the anomalous (non-Fickian) transport range (0.43 < n < 0.85), indicating that hesperidin release is governed by the combined contributions of Fickian diffusion and polymer chain relaxation rather than diffusion alone. The higher n value at pH 5.5 suggests a greater contribution from polymer swelling at lower pH, consistent with the protonation-driven swelling mechanism described above.

These findings confirm the pH-responsive behavior of CMC@HES-SeNPs, with significantly faster hesperidin release under acidic conditions than at physiological pH, while the complete release curves are provided in Figure S1.

2.2. Physicochemical Characterization of Nanoparticles (Zeta Potential, Zeta Size, TEM, XRD, and FTIR)

Zeta-potential analysis demonstrated a clear change in surface charge following CMC coating. Uncoated HES-SeNPs exhibited a negative zeta potential of −29.9 mV, with a single peak representing 100% of the measured area. After coating with CMC, the zeta potential became more negative, reaching −44.50 mV, while maintaining a single distribution peak accounting for 100% of the measured area. This shift of approximately 14.6 mV toward a more negative value indicates modification of the nanoparticle surface and may be attributed to the ionized carboxylate groups of CMC. The increased negative surface charge suggests stronger electrostatic repulsion and potentially improved colloidal stabilization of CMC@HES-SeNPs compared with HES-SeNPs (Figure 1A,G).

Dynamic light scattering also revealed a marked alteration in the measured hydrodynamic size after CMC coating. HES-SeNPs showed a Z-average diameter of 62.8 nm and a low PDI of 0.154. In contrast, CMC@HES-SeNPs exhibited a lower Z-average diameter of 29.5 nm and a PDI of 0.321. The coated formulation displayed a single intensity peak at 29.5 nm, representing 100% of the measured intensity, with no detectable secondary peak. Therefore, CMC coating was associated with a reduction in the measured hydrodynamic diameter and disappearance of the minor large-size population, although the higher PDI indicated greater variability within the coated nanoparticle population (Figure 1B,H).

TEM examination supported the morphological differences between the two formulations. HES-SeNPs appeared predominantly spherical and relatively well dispersed, whereas CMC@HES-SeNPs were observed as electron-dense nanoparticles embedded within or surrounded by a less electron-dense polymeric matrix. The coated nanoparticles showed spherical-to-oval morphology with localized clustering and a visible CMC-associated layer. Notably, the more negative zeta potential, reduced measured hydrodynamic diameter, disappearance of the minor aggregated population, and polymer-associated TEM appearance support successful surface modification of HES-SeNPs by CMC (Figure 1C–F).

XRD and FTIR Analysis Supporting Successful Incorporation

The XRD pattern of CMC@HES-SeNPs displayed diffraction peaks at approximately 2θ = 23.8°, 29.5°, 41.2°, 43.4°, 45.3°, 49.2°, 52.7°, 55.8°, 62.8°. The presence of distinct diffraction peaks confirmed the crystalline nature of the selenium phase, whereas the broad background was attributed to the amorphous contribution of the CMC and hesperidin organic matrix. The coexistence of crystalline selenium-related reflections and an amorphous polymeric background supports the successful incorporation of SeNPs into the CMC–hesperidin nanocomposite (Figure 2A).

The FTIR spectra of free hesperidin (HES), HES-SeNPs, and CMC@HES-SeNPs showed clear spectral changes supporting the successful incorporation of hesperidin into the selenium nanoparticle system and subsequent CMC coating. Free HES exhibited characteristic broad O–H stretching bands at approximately 3242.7 and 3578.0 cm−1, together with bands assigned to aromatic C=C, C–O, C–O–C, and C–H vibrations. Following the formation of HES-SeNPs, these bands underwent noticeable shifts and changes in intensity, including the appearance of a broad O–H band around 3406.2 cm−1 and altered C–O and aromatic-region signals, indicating interactions between hesperidin functional groups and the selenium nanoparticle surface. After CMC encapsulation, the CMC@HES-SeNP spectrum showed further shifts and broadening, particularly in the O–H region around 3370.8 cm−1, together with modified C–O, C–H, and carbonyl-related bands (Figure 2B).

2.3. Immunohistochemistry

To visualize the protein expression in tissue, IHC analysis was performed, as NF-κB was expressed as marked nuclear brown color in the PAR group, while it was moderately expressed in the PAR&HES group, and mild immunostaining in other groups. PAR showed a remarkable rise in NF-κB immunostaining compared with other groups (p < 0.0001). There was no substantial variance between the control and PAR&CMC@HES-SeNP groups regarding NF-κB immunostaining and between PAR&CMC@HES-SeNP, PAR&HES-SeNP, and PAR&Se groups (Figure 3). On the contrary, caspase-3 immunostaining showed remarkable variation between the control group and other treatment groups (p < 0.001) except for PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs (p = 0.9862). The protective treatments against PAR revealed a substantial decline in caspase-3 expression (p < 0.001). PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs showed the second lowest cytoplasmic caspase-3 expression after the control group, with mild expression, while the PAR group showed the highest immunostaining (Figure 4).

2.4. Histopathological Assessment

Concerning histopathological evaluation of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue examined under a light microscope, the control group showed normal arrangement of renal tubules originating from the renal corpuscles in the renal cortical area. The PAR group revealed remarkable alteration of the histological architecture of the renal cortex with prominent amorphous eosinophilic hyalin necrotic areas, karyolitic cells, degenerated renal tubules, infiltration with inflammatory cells, and interstitial hemorrhage as evidence for the harmful effect of paracetamol on renal tissue. Additionally, the PAR&HES group showed limited improvement in the renal tissue, as interstitial hemorrhage, degenerated cells, and interglomerular hemorrhage were still apparent. A better protective impact was revealed in the PAR&Se groups despite the detection of interglomerular hemorrhage and obliterated tubules. Administration of PAR&HES-SeNPs had a remarkable effect, while PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs revealed the best protective effect with improved renal cortical histoarchitecture, with minimal interglomerular hemorrhage, highlighting the improved protective effect of PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs (Figure 5).

2.5. Biochemistry

2.5.1. Assessment of Serum Kidney Function Tests

According to Figure 6, AKI induced by paracetamol (PAR) greatly increased the serum concentration of creatinine, urea, NGAL, KIM1, and cystatin-C, which are indicators of impaired renal function. Sodium selenite (PAR&Se) treatment had some protective effect, which lowered these markers relative to the PAR group. Hesperidin (PAR&HES) supplementation led to a relatively minor change, with a decrease in the kidney function markers, but not as significant as in the PAR&Se group (p < 0.05).

It is worth noting that hesperidin-biosynthesized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&HES-SeNPs) were the most associated with the protective effects, and all kidney functional parameters were significantly reduced, approaching normal levels. The kidney functionality was also greatly enhanced with CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs), which further validated the protective role of these nanoparticles against the kidney threat caused by paracetamol.

2.5.2. Oxidative Stress Assessment

Data in Figure 7 illustrate the evaluation of oxidative stress markers, including MDA, NO, and Keap-1, following paracetamol (PAR)-induced acute kidney injury (AKI). Paracetamol administration significantly increased MDA and NO levels, indicating enhanced oxidative stress. MDA and NO concentrations were notably higher in the PAR group compared to the control, with reductions observed in groups treated with sodium selenite (PAR&Se), hesperidin (PAR&HES), and selenium nanoparticles biosynthesized using hesperidin (PAR&HES-SeNPs), and CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs) also showed a significant reduction in oxidative stress markers, demonstrating their protective potential p < 0.05.

The expression of Keap-1, a key regulator of oxidative stress, was significantly upregulated in the PAR group, as shown by both protein and gene expression data. Treatment with sodium selenite (PAR&Se) and hesperidin (PAR&HES) resulted in partial downregulation of Keap-1, while selenium nanoparticles (PAR&HES-SeNPs) and CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs) significantly reduced Keap-1 expression, both at the protein and gene levels, approaching near-normal values.

2.5.3. Anti-Oxidative Stress Assessment

Oxidative stress caused by paracetamol (PAR) was observed, as indicated in Figure 8 by noticeable changes in the concentration of the major antioxidant stress markers. MDA and NO were significantly increased in the PAR group, which demonstrates increased lipid peroxidation and production of nitric oxide. But when sodium selenite (PAR&Se), hesperidin (PAR&HES), and selenium nanoparticles biosynthesized with hesperidin (PAR&HES-SeNPs) were used, the levels of these markers decreased significantly, which indicated the presence of antioxidant protection. Protective effects were also exhibited by CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs), which showed a significant decrease in MDA and NO levels, as shown in Figure 7.

Likewise, the concentrations of GSH, SOD, and GPx were lower in the PAR group, which proves the disruption of the antioxidant defense system, notably HMOX1, which has been increased in the PAR group. The addition of sodium selenite and hesperidin led to partial recovery of these markers, but selenium nanoparticles (PAR&HES-SeNPs and PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs) demonstrated the greatest improvements, restoring the oxidative balance to more normal levels (p < 0.05).

Moreover, the Nrf2 mRNA expression, which is one of the main regulators of the cellular antioxidant response, was significantly suppressed in the PAR group. Protein and gene expression of the antioxidant defense system regarding Nrf2 were upregulated in both selenium- and hesperidin-treated groups, but most significantly in the PAR&HES-SeNP and PAR&CMC@HES-SeNP groups, which could indicate the potential of these treatment options to stimulate the antioxidant defense system at the molecular level.

2.5.4. Inflammatory/Anti-Inflammatory Marker Assessment

Data shown in Figure 9 indicate that paracetamol (PAR)-induced acute kidney injury (AKI) greatly increased the serum levels of TNF-α (A), IL-6 (B), cytochrome-C (C), NF-κB mRNA expression (D), NF-κB protein level (E), and IL-10 (F), which showed a strong inflammatory response. This was significantly higher in the rats treated with PAR compared to those in the control group, hence renal inflammation. The sodium selenite (PAR&Se) treatment led to a substantial decrease in the levels of TNF-α, IL-6, cytochrome-C, NF-κB, as well as the downregulation of NF-κB; on the other hand, an increase in IL-10 indicates that the inflammatory response was partially suppressed. These markers were also reduced with hesperidin treatment (PAR&HES), but the extent of the normalization was smaller than in PAR&Se (p < 0.05).

Surprisingly, the biosynthesis of selenium nanoparticles with hesperidin (PAR&HES-SeNPs) demonstrated the strongest anti-inflammatory properties, with significant reductions in TNF-α, IL-6, cytochrome-C, NF-κB protein and gene expression levels, which were near normal levels, and an increase in IL-10. Large normalization of these inflammatory markers was also observed in CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs), which also provides additional evidence of the potential of these nanoparticles to reduce paracetamol-induced renal inflammation.

2.5.5. Apoptotic/Anti-Apoptotic Marker Assessment

Acute kidney injury (AKI) caused by paracetamol (PAR) significantly disturbed the apoptotic balance, as indicated by the upregulation of the pro-apoptotic protein and gene, Bax, and the downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein and gene, Bcl2. Relative to the control, there was a significant increase in the Bax expression and a decrease in Bcl2, which is indicative of amplified apoptotic signaling and cell death in the kidney, as shown in Figure 10.

These changes were partially inhibited by sodium selenite (PAR&Se) treatment, which was indicated by a decrease in the level of Bax and a moderate recovery of the Bcl2 expression. The same applied to Hesperidin supplementation (PAR&HES), which had only a slight effect on the apoptotic markers; the effects were not as significant as those in the selenium-treated group.

It is important to note that the biosynthesis of selenium nanoparticles with the help of Hesperidin (PAR&HES-SeNPs) showed a better anti-apoptotic effect, as it dramatically decreased Bax and reinstated Bcl2 levels to normal levels. Also, the protective effects of CMC-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (PAR&CMC@HES-SeNPs) were no less or better, and the apoptotic/anti-apoptotic equilibrium was restored at both the molecular and protein levels p < 0.05.

3. Discussion

Acute kidney injury caused by paracetamol is a significant toxicological issue, as the kidneys are extremely susceptible to oxidative, inflammatory, and mitochondrial injury. While liver damage is the most frequent complication of paracetamol toxicity, the kidneys can also be damaged, especially in the case of chronic high-dose use or overdose [21]. There is a need for multifunctional nephroprotective strategies to target the interrelated mechanisms of oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and tubular structural injury, which are the primary mechanisms of drug-induced AKI, in addition to current therapeutic approaches that are largely supportive [22]. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate the protective effect of hesperidin-stabilized selenium nanoparticles, especially of carboxymethyl cellulose encapsulated selenium nanoparticles, against paracetamol-induced renal injury.

The improved performance of CMC@HES-SeNPs can be attributed, in part, to their carefully designed chemical structure and the interactions among their individual components. During nanoparticle synthesis, hesperidin serves as both a reducing and stabilizing agent, with its hydroxyl-rich structure facilitating selenium reduction while forming a protective layer around the nanoparticles to improve their stability. Coating the nanoparticles with carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) provides an additional negatively charged polymeric shell that minimizes particle aggregation, enhances colloidal stability, and improves dispersibility in aqueous media. Crosslinking of the CMC matrix with calcium ions further strengthens the nanostructure and enables sustained, pH-responsive release of hesperidin, allowing faster drug release under the mildly acidic conditions associated with injured tissues while limiting premature release under physiological conditions. Collectively, these complementary chemical interactions generate a stable multifunctional nanoplatform that combines the antioxidant properties of selenium and hesperidin with the protective and controlled-release characteristics of CMC. This structure–function relationship likely contributes to the improved bioavailability, prolonged therapeutic activity, and enhanced nephroprotective efficacy observed with CMC@HES-SeNPs compared with the non-encapsulated formulations, as shown in Figure 11.

The observed changes in oxidative, inflammatory, and apoptotic biomarkers are consistent with coordinated modulation of these biological processes; however, they do not establish direct pathway activation or causal signaling. These changes were, however, significantly inhibited by HES-SeNPs and more so by CMC@HES-SeNPs. This suggests that CMC@HES-SeNPs are a multi-target nanoprotective system and not just an antioxidant agent, and they have coordinated effects on the redox–inflammatory–apoptotic axis.

The successful formation of stable nanosized HES-SeNPs was confirmed by physicochemical characterization. The negative zeta potential indicates good electrostatic interactions between the nanoparticles, which is essential for minimizing aggregation and ensuring the colloidal stability of the system [23]. It is believed that this surface stability is caused by the functional groups of hesperidin that play a role in selenium reduction, capping, and stabilization. A low polydispersity index suggests that the population of nanoparticles is relatively homogeneous [24]; TEM confirmed that the particles were mainly spherical and well-dispersed. Such properties are of biological significance, as they may lead to enhanced tissue interaction, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy due to the size, stability, and uniformity of the surface, as shown in Figure 11.

The CMC coating and negative surface charge may improve the colloidal stability of CMC@HES-SeNPs by providing steric and electrostatic protection against aggregation [19]. However, serum protein adsorption may alter nanoparticle size, surface charge, release behavior, and biological distribution. Because storage and serum stability were not directly evaluated, future studies should assess changes in particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and drug retention under defined storage and physiological conditions.

One major benefit of CMC@HES-SeNPs was their pH-dependent release characteristics. The release of hesperidin exhibited a moderate initial burst followed by a prolonged release up to 72 h. The release was slower at pH 7.4 (physiological) and faster at acidic pH 5.5, which could be attributed to protonation of carboxymethyl groups in the CMC matrix, decreased crosslinking, increased polymer swelling, and increased diffusion of hesperidin. The best fit to the Korsmeyer–Peppas model suggests that release took place via anomalous transport, which is a combination of diffusion and polymer relaxation. The pH-sensitive release may be beneficial for the liberation of hesperidin in injured and inflamed renal tissues with controlled release under normal physiological conditions [25].

Paracetamol was found to significantly affect renal function by raising the level of serum creatinine and urea, which demonstrated decreased renal clearance and filtration capacity [26]. The increased levels of NGAL, KIM-1 and cystatin-C further validated active tubular injury and early renal dysfunction [27]. NGAL is a marker of tubular epithelial stress, KIM-1 is a close associate of proximal tubular injury, while cystatin-C is a sensitive marker of filtration disturbance [28]. Partially, these biomarkers were normalized by sodium selenite and free hesperidin, HES-SeNPs and CMC@HES-SeNPs showed better normalization. This indicates that the delivery of nanoselenium contributes to augmenting the nephroprotective effects, probably due to increased bioavailability, prolonged release, and the synergistic antioxidant effects of nanoselenium and hesperidin.

The role of oxidative stress was one of the key mechanisms involved in renal injury caused by paracetamol [4]. Lipid peroxidation and membrane damage are reflected by the increase in MDA, and elevated NO reflects nitrosative stress, which may exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and tubular injury [29,30]. Concurrently, paracetamol resulted in elevated expression of Keap-1 and HMOX-1, as well as decreased levels of antioxidant proteins, such as GSH, SOD, GPx, and Nrf2. The dysregulation of Nrf2 reduces the transcription of antioxidants and cytoprotective genes and results in a collapse of renal redox defense, owing to the negative regulation of Nrf2 by Keap-1, as shown in Figure 10.

CMC@HES-SeNPs restored redox balance by decreasing the levels of MDA, NO, and Keap-1, and increasing the levels of GSH, SOD, GPx, and Nrf2. These findings are consistent with restoration or modulation of Keap-1/Nrf2-related antioxidant defenses. Selenium might be able to support selenoenzyme-dependent antioxidant activity, particularly GPx, and hesperidin could directly scavenge free radicals as well as be able to modulate redox-sensitive signaling [31,32]. The CMC coating is likely to have contributed to such effects by stabilizing the nanoparticles and providing for a slow release [19]. Thus, CMC@HES-SeNPs mitigated renal tissue damage both by decreasing oxidative damage and restoring endogenous antioxidant defense.

The observed increase in HMOX-1 in the PAR group could be a compensatory mechanism to counter higher pro-oxidative stress caused by severe paracetamol intake and does not indicate increased antioxidant status [33,34]. HMOX-1 is a highly inducible cytoprotective enzyme, which is induced in renal tubular cells after oxidative stress/inflammation and is responsible for heme degradation to reduce toxic effects from heme. Hence, its elevation could be a sign of a built-in defense mechanism for stress in the presence of renal damage. However, the biological relevance of HMOX-1 induction might vary based on the level, length, and cellular context of HMOX-1 expression, as excessive expression may lead to increased intracellular free iron and to ferroptotic signaling.

Paracetamol-induced renal injury was also significantly associated with inflammation. TNF-α and IL-6 were elevated, showing activation of a pro-inflammatory cytokine response, and NF-κB gene/protein expression, as demonstrated by immunohistochemistry, was elevated, demonstrating activation of a central inflammatory pathway [35]. Activated NF-κB may stimulate additional cytokine production and exacerbate renal inflammation through these mechanisms. Mitochondrial injury and apoptotic activation are also correlated with increased cytochrome-C and inflammatory stress [16,36,37].

Treatment with HES-SeNPs and CMC@HES-SeNPs was associated with attenuation of the inflammatory response, TNF-α, IL-6, NF-κB, and cytochrome-C, and an increase in IL-10. IL-10 is an anti-inflammatory cytokine that helps to control excessive immune activation and promote tissue repair. We speculate that the anti-inflammatory activity of CMC@HES-SeNPs may be partially mediated through activation of the Nrf2 pathway since the Nrf2 pathway can block the NF-κB signaling pathway. Furthermore, oxidative stress is reduced, resulting in lower redox-dependent inflammatory activation. So, the formulation is shown to act on the inflammation process in a coordinated way, through its antioxidant and its NF-κB-suppressive effects.

Paracetamol also caused an imbalance in apoptotic mechanisms by upregulating the expression of Bax and downregulating Bcl-2 [38]. Bax promotes permeabilization of the mitochondrial membrane and the release of cytochrome-c, while Bcl-2 maintains the integrity of the mitochondria [39]. The expression of caspase-3, which is further confirmed by the increase in immunostaining, confirms the activation of the execution phase of apoptosis. These results point to the pro-apoptotic effect of paracetamol on renal tubular cells by favoring mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis.

CMC@HES-SeNPs treatment was associated with attenuation of mitochondrial apoptotic markers, as reflected by restoration of the Bax/Bcl-2 balance and reduced caspase-3 expression, as their ability to restore the Bax/Bcl-2 ratio and decrease the expression of caspase-3 was observed. This anti-apoptotic role is attributed to the upstream regulation of oxidative and inflammatory stress, maintenance of mitochondrial integrity, and decrease in the release of cytochrome c [40]. This increased response for CMC@HES-SeNPs is indicative of the importance of polymer encapsulation to sustain antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities for the purposes of interrupting apoptosis.

Biochemical and molecular results were reconfirmed by histopathological examination. Severe renal cortical damage characterized by tubular degeneration, necrotic changes, inflammatory infiltration, congestion, hemorrhage, and nuclear alterations was observed in the kidney as a result of paracetamol [41]. These lesions are typical of toxic tubular necrosis caused by oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis [42]. Partial structural improvement was obtained by sodium selenite and free hesperidin, and good protection was obtained with HES-SeNPs. The CMC@HES-SeNP group exhibited the best preservation of renal architecture, thus highlighting its protective effect at the tissue level.

Several integrated factors could explain the improvement in the efficiency of CMC@HES-SeNPs. Nanoparticulate selenium could offer greater biological activity than inorganic selenium [43,44]. Hesperidin is responsible for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity and stabilization of the surface of the nanoparticles [45]. CMC improves colloidal stability, controls release, and allows for pH-responsive delivery [46]. These components can be simultaneously disrupted to modulate Keap-1/Nrf2, oxidative/nitrosative stress, NF-κB inflammation, release of cytochrome-C from mitochondria, imbalance of Bax/Bcl-2, activation of caspase-3, and renal structural damage.

Although the present findings were obtained in a rat model of paracetamol-induced acute kidney injury, the underlying mechanisms targeted by CMC@HES-SeNPs, including oxidative stress, Keap-1/Nrf2 dysregulation, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis, are highly conserved across mammalian species and are also implicated in human acute kidney injury. Therefore, the observed renoprotective effects may have broader biological relevance beyond this experimental model. Nevertheless, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to humans because species-specific differences in paracetamol metabolism, selenium pharmacokinetics, nanoparticle biodistribution, immune responses, and renal physiology may influence therapeutic efficacy and safety. Moreover, the current study employed a single toxin-induced AKI model and evaluated only short-term outcomes.

The rat model of paracetamol-induced acute kidney injury was selected because it closely reproduces the principal pathological features observed in human drug-induced AKI, including tubular epithelial injury, oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis. Rats exhibit reproducible biochemical, molecular, and histopathological responses following paracetamol overdose, making them a well-established preclinical model for evaluating nephroprotective interventions. Moreover, the Keap-1/Nrf2 antioxidant pathway, NF-κB inflammatory signaling, and mitochondrial apoptotic mechanisms targeted by CMC@HES-SeNPs are highly conserved between rodents and humans, supporting the biological relevance of this model. Although interspecies differences in drug metabolism, selenium handling, nanoparticle pharmacokinetics, and renal physiology may influence therapeutic responses, this model provides a robust platform for mechanistic investigation and preclinical proof-of-concept before validation in larger animal models and ultimately human clinical studies.

This study has several limitations. An N-acetylcysteine positive-control group was not included, preventing direct comparison with a conventional therapy for paracetamol toxicity. In addition, the absence of a CMC@HES-SeNP-only group limits definitive evaluation of the formulation’s intrinsic safety. Future studies should include quantitative determination of encapsulation efficiency using validated analytical methods such as HPLC or UV-Vis spectroscopy to further optimize formulation performance and enable more accurate dose standardization. Other limitations include the use of a single AKI model and dose, short-term assessment, and the lack of pharmacokinetic, biodistribution, serum-stability, and long-term toxicity studies. Future investigations should address these limitations before clinical translation.

4. Materials and Methods

4.1. Biosynthesis of Selenium Nanoparticles Using Hesperidin

Selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs) were prepared by reducing sodium selenite with hesperidin. To begin, 86.5 mg of sodium selenite was dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water, and the resulting solution was stirred at 500 rpm. Separately, 0.1 g of hesperidin was dissolved in 10 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and added to the selenium mixture dropwise. The solution was stirred until it became clear. Then, 1 mL of ascorbic acid (240 mM) was added dropwise to the mixture. The entire reaction mixture was kept under continuous stirring at 500 rpm in a dark environment for 4 h [25].

4.2. Preparation of Cmc-Coated Selenium Nanoparticles Using Hesperidin

Carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) solution was prepared by dissolving 1 gm of sodium CMC in 100 mL of distilled water. The mixture was then incubated on a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm until the solution became clear and homogeneous. The previously synthesized selenium nanoparticles (SeNPs), biosynthesized using hesperidin, were added to the CMC solution at a 1:1 ratio, followed by sonication for 5 min to achieve uniform dispersion. The resulting mixture was then incubated on a magnetic stirrer at 300 rpm for 30 min. To facilitate crosslinking, 500 μL of 18 mM CaCl2 solution was added dropwise to the SeNP-CMC mixture and incubated for 1 h. The nanoparticles were then collected by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 20 min, resuspended with distilled water, and stored at room temperature for future use [25].

4.3. Determination of Drug Release Profiles of CMC@HES-SeNPs

The in vitro release profile of hesperidin from CMC@HES-SeNPs was evaluated using the dialysis membrane diffusion method under sink conditions at two physiologically relevant pH values: pH 5.5, simulating the acidic environment of lysosomes and inflamed tissues, and pH 7.4, representing physiological plasma conditions. Briefly, 1 mL of the CMC@HES-SeNP suspension, prepared according to the theoretical formulation composition, was transferred into pre-soaked dialysis bags (molecular weight cut-off 12–14 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and sealed at both ends. The dialysis bags were immersed in 30 mL of the corresponding release medium, either phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) or acetate buffer (pH 5.5), in sealed Falcon tubes and incubated at 37 ± 0.5 °C under continuous horizontal shaking at 100 rpm (Innova 40 incubator shaker, New Brunswick Scientific, Edison, NJ, USA). Released hesperidin was quantified spectrophotometrically at predetermined time intervals, and cumulative drug release was expressed as the percentage of the theoretical hesperidin content in the formulation.

At designated time points of 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h, 1 mL aliquots of the release medium were withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh, pre-warmed buffer to maintain sink conditions throughout. The concentration of released hesperidin in each withdrawn aliquot was quantified spectrophotometrically at 284 nm (UV-1900 UV-Vis spectrophotometer, Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan), using a freshly prepared calibration curve of hesperidin in the respective buffer (0.5–100 ug/mL; R2 > 0.999). Free hesperidin solution was subjected to the same dialysis procedure as a control to confirm that the membrane itself did not impose a rate-limiting barrier. All experiments were conducted in triplicate and cumulative drug release was calculated as:Cumulative Drug Release (%) = (Ct × V)/Drugtotal × 100 where Ct is the hesperidin concentration (ug/mL) at time t, V is the total volume of release medium (mL), and Drugtotal is the total hesperidin content (ug) initially loaded into the dialysis bag.

To characterize the release mechanism, cumulative release data were fitted to five kinetic models: zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Hixson–Crowell, and the Korsmeyer–Peppas power-law model. The Korsmeyer–Peppas equation Qt/Qinf = kptn was applied to the initial 60% of release data, where n is the release exponent and kp is the rate constant. For spherical matrices, n < 0.43 indicates Fickian diffusion, 0.43 < n < 0.85 indicates anomalous (non-Fickian) transport, and n > 0.85 indicates Case II transport. The best-fit model was identified by the highest R2 and lowest root-mean-square error (RMSE).

4.4. Characterization of Nanoparticles

4.4.1. Zeta Potential and Zeta Size

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was used to measure the zeta size and zeta potential of the hesperidin-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (HES-SeNPs) and carboxymethyl cellulose coating hesperidin-stabilized selenium nanoparticles (CMC@HES-SeNPs) at the Faculty of Engineering, Nano Center, Capital University, Cairo, Egypt. The tests were carried out in ultrapure water at 25 °C to determine the size distribution and surface charge of the nanoparticles. The measurements were noted and examined to assess the stability and dispersion of the nanoparticles in aqueous solutions.

4.4.2. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) was used to investigate the morphology and size distribution of the HES-SeNPs and CMC@HES-SeNPs in the Central Laboratory, Al-Azhar University, Cairo, Egypt. Initially, the SeNPs were suspended in distilled water, and a drop of the suspension was placed on a copper grid covered with carbon. The grid was subsequently air-dried, and the TEM images were taken at different magnifications to examine the shape, size, and dispersion of the nanoparticles at high resolution.

4.4.3. XRD Analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of the prepared CMC–hesperidin–selenium nanoparticles (CMC–HSP–SeNPs) was performed at Al-Azhar University, Cairo, Egypt, to evaluate the crystalline nature and phase characteristics of the synthesized nanocomposite. The dried nanoparticle powder was finely ground to obtain a homogeneous sample and then placed on the XRD sample holder. The diffraction pattern was recorded over a 2θ scanning range of approximately 10–90°. The obtained diffractogram was used to identify the characteristic diffraction peaks of selenium and to assess the possible contribution of the polymeric/organic matrix formed by carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) and hesperidin. Peak positions were expressed as 2θ values, while the diffraction intensity was recorded as counts.

4.4.4. Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR was performed at the Nano Center, Faculty of Engineering, Capital University, Egypt, to identify the characteristic functional groups and evaluate the interactions among HES, HES-SeNPs, and CMC@HES-SeNPs. The obtained spectra were processed and analyzed using Spectroscopic Suite Analyzer Pro software (version YFGMS 5.1.1.2026), and the major absorption bands were assigned according to their corresponding vibrational modes.

4.5. Animals

Forty-two male Albino rats (100–120 g) were kept in controlled environmental conditions in the animal facility of the School of Biotechnology, Badr University in Cairo, Egypt. The rats were adjusted to a temperature of 25 ± 2 °C at 12 light/dark cycles (12 L/12 D) and were allowed free access to tap water and normal rodent food. All the processes used in this research were accredited by the institutional animal ethics committee, as per the ethical provisions stated under approval number BUC-IACUC/BIOT/138/A/2026.

The sample size of seven rats per group was selected based on comparable published studies and the principle of animal reduction. No formal a priori power analysis was performed.

4.7. qRT-PCR Analysis of Gene Expression

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-qPCR) was conducted to evaluate renal gene expression. Total RNA was extracted from kidney tissues and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The mRNA levels of Keap-1, Nrf2, NF-κB, Bax, and Bcl2 were quantified using gene-specific primers, with GAPDH serving as the internal reference for normalization. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in the analysis are listed in Table 1.

4.8. Renal Immunohistochemistry for NF-κB and Caspase-3

To evaluate renal inflammatory and apoptotic responses, immunohistochemical analysis was performed to assess the expression of NF-κB and caspase-3 in kidney tissue. Renal specimens were first cut into 4 μm thick sections, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration through a graded series of alcohols. Antigen retrieval was achieved by microwaving the slides in a citrate buffer (pH 6.0). To block endogenous peroxidase activity, the tissue sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and non-specific binding was minimized by incubating the slides with 5% bovine serum albumin.

The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary monoclonal antibody targeting NF-κB and caspase-3, host: Rabbit (1:100 and 1:200 dilution, respectively; Cat. No. ab32536, ab184787 and Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After thorough washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the sections were exposed to a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogenic substrate, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin for tissue contrast. The stained slides were analyzed under a light microscope, and representative images were captured to assess the expression and localization patterns in the kidney tissue [51,52].

4.9. Quantitative Assessment of IHC Staining

For quantitative assessment, ImageJ Fiji software (v. 1.54n) was used to perform a semi-quantitative analysis of the staining, with a focus on the intensity and distribution of NF-κB and caspase-3 expression. The percentage of positively stained areas was calculated at a magnification of ×400, allowing for a consistent comparison of NF-κB and caspase-3 expression across all experimental groups [53,54,55,56].

4.10. Histopathological Examination

The renal tissue samples were fixed and subjected to the usual procedures and embedded in paraffin wax and cut into thin sections of about 4–5 µm thickness. The resulting sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to enable detailed histopathological examination. The prepared slides were observed under the light microscope to measure the change in structure in the kidney tissue. Representative photomicrographs were examined to assess the main histopathological parameters, such as tubular necrosis, vascular congestion, and inflammatory cell infiltration [57,58].

4.16. Statistical Analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the results confirmed a parametric distribution. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using the Brown–Forsythe test. Accordingly, all data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

5. Conclusions

In conclusion, paracetamol-induced AKI was linked to renal dysfunction, tubular injury, oxidative/nitrosative stress, Keap-1/Nrf2/HMOX-1 axis disruption, NF-κB-mediated inflammation, mitochondrial apoptosis, and severe histopathological damage. These pathological changes were significantly reduced by HES-SeNPs, especially following CMC encapsulation. The nephroprotective effects were associated with restoration of antioxidant defenses, modulation of Nrf2-related cytoprotective markers, attenuation of NF-κB-associated inflammation, improvement of IL-10-related anti-inflammatory balance, normalization of mitochondrial apoptotic markers, restoration of anti-inflammatory balance mediated by IL-10, stabilization of mitochondrial apoptotic regulators, and preservation of the renal histoarchitecture. Overall, CMC@HES-SeNPs are a promising multifunctional nanoformulation for reducing paracetamol-induced renal injury through integrated regulation of redox, inflammatory and apoptotic pathways.
