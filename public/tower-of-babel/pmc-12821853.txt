Callus and endosperm green fluorescence reporter‐assisted selection system in maize CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing
Authors: Lei Ge, Wenbo Li, Yiming Dou, Yu Ma, Mengyao Sun, Xiaoya Chen, Xue Feng, Yubin Li, Qian Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenome editing using CRISPR/Cas9 (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats/CRISPR-associated protein 9) has emerged as a promising approach for functional gene analysis and genetic improvement. Since stable transformation remains the primary method for implementing this system, the ultimate goal in crop breeding programs would require the selection of transgene-free plants with the CRISPR/Cas expression cassette removed.ResultsIn this study, we developed an endosperm-specific fluorescence reporter-assisted selection system for CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing (pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9) in maize (Zea mays L.), utilizing enhanced green fluorescent protein (eGFP) expressed specifically in the endosperm to facilitate the easy identification of transgenic and transgene-free plants from the T1 generation on. In addition, the 22 kDa alpha zein (z1C1_10) promoter from maize, employed in this system, has been shown to be active in both callus and endosperm, thereby being able to enhance the accuracy of transformant identification during the tissue culture process by reducing false positives compared to the traditional selective media methods. Our studies targeting the ZmSnRK2.1 or Dwarf1 (D1) genes demonstrated a reasonable editing efficiency, with rates ranging from 56.3% for T0 plants targeting ZmSnRK2.1, to 87.5% and 100% for T1 plants targeting D1 and ZmSnRK2.1, respectively. In addition, we successfully identified 1 transgene-free homozygous d1 mutant in the T1 generation and 7 transgene-free homozygous snrk2.1 mutants in the T2 generation.ConclusionsThe pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system provides an efficient tool for gene editing, transformant selection and transgene status identification in maize breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07429-2.Keywords: PAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9, Seed fluorescence reporter, Endosperm-specific promoter, Gene editing, Maize, Mutagenesis efficiency

Background

Genome editing using Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) and the CRISPR- associated protein (Cas) has become a cornerstone of breeding-by-design in agriculture [36]. The CRISPR/Cas9 system consists of two key components: a single guide RNA (sgRNA) for precise genome targeting and a Cas9 nuclease for nucleotide manipulation [17]. With its unprecedented technical advantages, this technology has revolutionized crop trait improvement, enhancing grain yield and quality, abiotic stress resistance, nutrient transport, male sterility, seed propagation for clonal hybrids and virus resistance, while also enabling multiplex mutations [3, 5, 19, 25, 29]. Although gene editing through “DNA-free” or transient expression systems using Cas9–gRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes is technically feasible [34], the stable transformation of a CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette remains the predominant approach for generating single, multiplex, or mutant libraries in plants for a higher editing efficiency [35].

The stable transformation strategy necessitates the constant production of CRISPR/Cas9 in vivo to induce targeted mutations at the desired sites. Subsequently, the CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette can be removed through genetic segregation to prevent continuous editing activity and address concerns regarding transgenic plants in agriculture [2, 13]. However, verifying or screening for the presence or absence of transgenes can be labor-intensive and time-consuming. To streamline this process, several reporter systems have been developed to couple the CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette with various reporters. These include an anthocyanin biosynthesis gene expression cassette [22], an RNA interference element targeting a herbicide-resistance P488 enzyme [23], a mCherry fluorescence marker [11], a pair of suicide transgenes that drives the expression of a toxin in the embryo, along with a cytoplasmic male sterility gene, resulting in male gametophyte death [15], and a synthetic open reading frame RUBY that converts tyrosine to red betalain [14]. Additionally, the gene encoding the red fluorescent protein DsRED2 driven by endosperm- and/or embryo-specific promoters has been employed to facilitate easy and efficient seed sorting [35]. Each reporter system has its own rationale, efficiency and application scenario, with a general emphasis on early and easy detection.

In this report, we present a selection system we developed, in which the enhanced green fluorescent protein coding sequence (eGFP) is driven by the callus/endosperm-specific promoter of z1C1_10 (pAZS22), and is coupled with the CRISPR/Cas9 expression cassette (pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9). The pAZS22 promoter, native to maize, is specifically active in callus and endosperm [20, 32], and the expression of eGFP is a reliable indicator for the presence of the transgenes. This system thus enables the selection of transgene presence, facilitated by fluorescent signal from callus formation, which is the earliest stage of plant regeneration following transformation. Additionally, it allows for the detection of transgenes in seeds produced by T0 plants. On the other hand, GFP/eGFP is the most widely applicable fluorescent protein with multiple advantageous attributes. The visualization of green fluorescence by GFP is well-established for long, known for its simplicity and ease of application. Notably, utilizing handheld lamps in the field under natural daylight can achieve satisfactory discrimination results, which is more convenient than fluorescence signals that can only be detected in darkness. The mutagenesis efficiency of the system was tested using guide RNAs targeting the Gibberellin 3-oxidase Dwarf1 (D1) gene and the Sucrose non-fermenting-1-related protein kinase 2 (ZmSnRK2.1) gene in maize.

The two genes for our case study were chosen for their potential in germplasm innovation. The d1 null mutation has been reported to be associated with the easily recognizable dwarf phenotype, which is convenient for visual phenotyping [7]. The D1 gene in maize encodes a GA 3-oxidase that catalyzes the final step of bioactive gibberellin (GA) synthesis. Currently, all known d1 alleles, such as d1-3286, d1-6039, d1-4, and d1-6016, contain mutations that result in gene knockout. For instance, d1-4 and d1-6016 exhibit large deletions in the coding region, d1-6039 has a one base pair deletion leading to an early frameshift in translation, while d1-3286 contains a 4.5 kb transposon-derived copia-like element in an exon [7]. These significant mutations result in the loss of gene function, severely disrupting endogenous GA synthesis and leading to the dwarf phenotype in plant stature. However, knockdown mutations in D1 which might be more useful for maize breeding is lacking.

SnRK2 kinases have been extensively studied in model organism Arabidopsis for their regulatory role in abscisic acid (ABA) response in plants [10]. The synergistic link between SnRK2 and ABA signaling in the plant response to stress, such as drought and shoot growth, has been revealed. SnRK2 kinases play a dual role: promoting plant growth under optimal conditions in the absence of ABA, while inhibiting growth in response to ABA. However, the gene function of ZmSnRK2.1 in maize is not verified yet. So we test our system with ZmSnRK2.1 while aiming to create its mutant lines for future gene function study as well.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The transgenic lines in the background of maize inbred line B104 were generated by BomeiXingao Technology Co., Ltd (Bejing, China). Maize plants were cultivated in the green house under 12 h lighting at the campus of Qingdao Agricultural University.

pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 construction

The callus/endosperm-specific eGFP expression cassette was combined with the CRISPR/Cas9 stable expression cassette (Fig. 1A). The promoter of z1C1_10 (Zm00001d048816); [32] designated as pAZS22, was PCR-amplified from the genomic DNA of maize inbred line B73. The CRISPR/Cas9 stable expression cassette, sourced from Rentao Song’s lab, contains a maize codon-optimized open reading frame of Cas9 driven by the maize Ubiquitin2 promoter [24]. A monopartite SV40 nuclear localization signal (NLS) was added to the N terminus of Cas9 and a bipartite nucleoplasmin nuclear localization signal (BiNLS) was added to the C-terminus to facilitate nuclear localization. The Cas9 element was cloned between the HindIII site and PlmI site of the pCAMBIA3301 vector [24]. The expression of the sgRNA was driven by the maize U6 promoter (U6p, Chr 8: 165,525,624–165,548,023) with the corresponding U6 terminator (U6t) used as well [24]. D1 (Zm00001d039634) and ZmSnRK2.1 (Zm00001d047220) were chosen as the target genes. The B104 reference genome was utilized to design the target sequences (gRNA spacers) of sgRNAs, which were confirmed to be intact in the inbred line B104 used for transformation through Sanger sequencing. One sgRNA design for each gene was introduced between U6p and U6t, and was cloned between the SamI and PstI sites of the pCAMBIA3301 vector. Finally, the pAZS22 promoter-driven eGFP and the CaMV poly(A) signal element were cloned between the PstI and HindIII sites for maize transformation.

Maize transformation

All constructs intended for plant transformation were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. Immature maize embryos from inbred B104 were transformed with the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 constructs via Agrobacterium-mediated stable transformation. Transformations and plant regeneration were performed through a commercial service provided by BomeiXingao Technology Co., Ltd (Bejing, China). The protocol was modified after [16] (Protocol S1). Glufosinate was used as the selection agent for herbicide-resistance resulted from the presence of Bar gene.

Imaging and fluorescence observation of eGFP

Maize seed kernels, ears and whole plants were photographed using a Canon 70D digital camera (Canon, Japan). The ears and kernels of eGFP/Cas9 transformants were observed under a Dark Reader Handlamp HL34T (Claire Chemical Research, USA) equipped with the 480 nm excitation wavelength through the 510 nm filter glasses, and photographed in a dark room under the same handlamp through the 510 nm filter lens. Kernels emitting green fluorescence under the Dark Reader Handlamp were scored as eGFP/Cas9-positive, while kernels without obvious green fluorescence were scored as eGFP/Cas9-negative. Longitudinal sections of eGFP/Cas9-positive kernels were photographed under excitation at 480 nm with a Canon 70D digital camera (Canon, Japan) through a 510 nm filter.

Genomic DNA extraction and PCR/sequencing assay

For each transgenic line, genomic DNA was extracted from seedling leave samples using a DNA extraction kit (Roche). Target regions were amplified using specific primer pairs flanking the designated target sites (Table S1) with Pfu DNA polymerase (ThermoFisher) to detect the presence of transgenes and mutagenesis at the desired sites. The PCR program consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C of 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 2 min. The PCR products were separated on a 1% agarose gel and stained with ethidium bromide. The stained gels were imaged using the Gel Doc XRS system (Bio-Rad). Selected PCR products were sent for DNA sequencing through a commercial service provided by Sangon Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The raw sequence data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive [6] in National Genomics Data Center [8], China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA: CRA028316). The sequencing chromatograms were analyzed by SnapGene v. 2.0.1.

Results

Construct design of pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing system and generation of Bar&eGFP-positive callus in immature embryo tissue culture

To establish a fluorescent reporter system in maize, we constructed an expression cassette consisting of the endosperm-specific promoter (the 277 bp fragment upstream of the transcriptional initiation site) derived from the maize z1C1_10 gene [20, 32], which encodes an alpha zein protein expressed in seeds. This promoter drives an eGFP gene (Table S2) and is positioned between the gRNA expression cassette and the Cas9 expression cassette (Fig. 1A). The maize codon-optimized CRISPR/Cas9 system was adapted from Rentao Song’s Lab [24].

To test the efficiency of the system, we transformed immature maize embryos with pCAMBIA3301 harboring pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 targeting D1 or ZmSnRK2.1 via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation (Table S3, Protocol S1). Green fluorescence was observed during the embryonic callus formation stage on selective culture media, which proved that pAZS22 was not only active in endosperm but also active in callus. This feature of pAZS22 was harnessed to facilitate transformant identification. The callus cell clumps that grew on Bar selective media and exhibited a green fluorescence signal (Fig. 1B&C) were selected for further plant regeneration.

Phenotypic characterization of pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 transformed maize ears and kernels

To evaluate the effectiveness of our engineered eGFP as a seed reporter, we assessed green fluorescence in maize ears and longitudinal sections of kernels harvested from pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 transformants targeting D1 and SnRK2.1 (Fig. 2). In both maize ears and T1 kernels, individual fluorescent kernels harboring the eGFP transgene were easily distinguished by a blue hand lamp inspected through the proper filter (Figure S1; Fig. 2B,C,D&H). In contrast, non-fluorescent kernels, which presumably do not carry the transgenes, were also readily identifiable (Fig. 2A&G). The correlation of the presence or absence of green fluorescence in kernels with the presence or absence of the transgenes in seedling leaves was verified by PCR amplification of fragments of eGFP and Cas9 genes (Figure S2).

Case study using pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 targeting D1 gene

To evaluate the efficiency of the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system, we analyzed transformants targeting the D1 gene (Fig. 3). From the plants regenerated from primary T0 transformants, six successfully set seeds after self-pollination or crossing with wild-type when self-pollen was not available due to differences in maturation time of male and female flowers. To illustrate the segregation of the transgene, we photographed two exemplar maize ears under fluorescence excitation (Fig. 2B&C). From these ears, we selected 10 kernels exhibiting fluorescence (GFP seeds), indicative of the presence of the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 cassette, and 12 kernels without fluorescence (non-GFP seeds), indicative of its absence. These kernels were planted for phenotyping. Among the 10 plants grown from GFP seeds, 9 displayed a dwarf phenotype, while 1 out of the 12 plants from non-GFP seeds exhibited the same phenotype (Table S4). The dwarf phenotype suggests successful knockout of both d1 alleles [7].

Additionally, we sequenced the D1 region spanning the target site in 4 plants from GFP seeds and 4 plants from non-GFP seeds to assess the editing status. This analysis identified 7 T1 lines carrying at least one mutant allele within the target region (Fig. 3B; Figure S3; Table S4). All dwarf plants were found to bear homozygous d1 edits due to frameshifts, while plants with only one mutant allele of d1 did not exhibit a dwarf phenotype (Table S4). Small edits like the addition of 1 base in D1CR3 resulted in frameshift from the 28th aa, and a premature stop codon at the 163rd codon (Table S4). Notably, we identified 1 transgene-free T1 line that was a homozygous mutant of d1 and displayed the dwarf phenotype (Table S4; Fig. 3C).

These results therefore indicated that the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system works well for genome editing of single targets, and transgene-free homozygous mutant lines can be obtained from T1 generation. The strategies to obtain transgene-free homozygous mutants employing the system of pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 are summarized in Fig. 4. The case study of D1 exemplified the feasibility of strategy A (Fig. 4A). The frequency in our D1 case study is 25%.

Case study using pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 targeting ZmSnRK2.1

To evaluate the efficiency and heritability of our system, we analyzed transformants of the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 construct targeting ZmSnRK2.1. We obtained 16 primary T0 regenerated plants, extracted DNA from the T0 plants and sequenced the region spanning the target site. In 9 of the 16 samples successful mutagenesis was identified (Fig. 5). The mutagenesis rate in T0 plants was 56.3%. Small edits like the addition of 1 base in ZmSnRK2.1CR1 resulted in frameshift from the 7th aa, and a premature stop codon at the 42th codon (Table S5). For the T1 plants we genotyped 28 plants grown from GFP seeds, and the mutagenesis rate in T1 plants was 100% while 7 homozygous snrk2.1 mutants were identified (Table S5). Selected seeds from T1snrk2.1 mutants were planted to generate T2 generation. To obtain transgene-free mutants, we genotyped 22 T2 plants grown from non-GFP seeds set by selfing T1snrk2.1 mutants. The mutagenesis rate in T2 plants was 100%, and 7 out of 22 were homozygous snrk2.1 mutants (Table S5). Hence, 7 transgene-free homozygous snrk2.1 mutants were obtained from T2 generation.

The results therefore indicated that the mutations in T0 plants caused by the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system can be stably transmitted to the T1 generation, and transgene-free homozygous mutant lines can be successfully obtained form T2 generation. The case study of ZmSnRK2.1 exemplified the feasibility of strategy B (Fig. 4B). The frequency in our ZmSnRK2.1 case study is 31.8%.

Discussion

Features of the pAZS22 driven eGFP reporter system confer multiple merits for transformant selection and tracking

The pAZS22 driven eGFP system serves as a good screening marker which allows tracking of transformation during callus selection and seed selection steps with the help of strong visual signal from the green fluorescent protein.

In the realm of transgenic technology, the selection process for transformants is pivotal to the overall success of the procedure. The traditional method involving selective media often yields false positive, as cells proliferating above the culture media may escape the selective pressure exerted by toxic substances in the media. Different selection marker gene and reagent pairs may yield varied escape rates. For example, the Bar/bialaphos pair is known for its high escape rate, especially for quick transformation protocols, which does not include a long callus-proliferation step [18] [1]. Other selection marker gene and reagent pairs may have lower escape rates, however, an additional round of selection—using methods such as the Bar strip test or PCR—is still frequently required to confirm the presence or absence of the transgene in T1 plants as deployed by other reporter systems [22, 23, 35]. In our study, as shown in Fig. 1C, it is evident that the callus grown on selective media harbored cells that did not express eGFP, indicating the absence of the desired expression cassette. Therefore, callus tissue exhibiting a green fluorescence signal was selected for further plant regeneration. This strategy reduces the likelihood of false positive selections and subsequently lessens the associated workload. Actually, other maize transformation studies have also successfully applied GFP expression driven by a constitutive promoter as a visual screening marker for tracking transformation in all tissues at all developmental stages [31] In comparison, the tissue-specificity of pAZS22 allows a focus on the target tissues and stages, which could evade the potential metabolic burden to the hosts [30].

With regard to the gene editing technology, the transgenes are the tools but not the aims. In edited germplasms, the transgenes are preferred to be segregated out to avoid continuous editing. Currently, there have been several reporter systems, as mentioned above, to facilitate the screening and eliminating of the transgenes. The pro and cons of available reporter systems has been comprehensively reviewed by Yan et al., 2021 [35]. Hence there is still room for developing alternative reporter systems that are versatile or handy in maize. To take visible pigment reporters as comparisons, anthocyanin biosynthesis is inhibited in some germplasms of maize [4]. Even if the reporter pigments were not inhibited, reporters like anthocyanin and red betalain would be inapplicable for maize cultivars with pigmented kernels in the same color system, for example, the W22 inbred, which produces anthocyanin in kernels and is widely used in maize genetic studies [33]. In contrast, the eGFP marker has been shown to be able to work well in purple kernels [20]. With regard to manipulability, the coding sequence of eGFP is only 720 bp in size and easy to manipulate, well as that of pigment reporters like PAP1 (PRODUCTION OF ANTHOCYANIN PIGMENTS 1) or Ruby is over 2 kb or up to 4 kb, respectively [14, 22], which might be more difficult for cloning or transformation [12, 26].

In the choice of fluorescent proteins, GFP/eGFP is currently the most widely used one for its overall performance in regard of brightness, photostability, toxicity etc. [27]. For example, the brightness of eGFP is 34 (mM·cm)−1, while that of mCherry is 16 (mM·cm)−1 and monomeric DsRed is 3.5 (mM·cm)−1, more than twofold and almost tenfold weaker than that of eGFP, respectively [28]. Besides, the photostability of eGFP is the best among all fluorescent protein variants [28]. Our observations indicate that the green fluorescence signal from the transgenic eGFP is strong, unambiguous, and clearly visible under inspection light equipped with the appropriate excitation wavelength/filter, even in bright ambient conditions (personal observations). In contrast, fluorescence from red fluorescent proteins typically requires a dark room for effective inspection. Thus, phenotyping using the eGFP reporter system is more adaptable to various lighting conditions and is even feasible in field settings when equipped with blue LED hand lamps (Figure S1). It has been demonstrated that a mCherry fluorescent marker in Arabidopsis seeds resulted in a reduction of the genotyping workload by over 75% [11]. Likewise, being able to identify transgene-free seeds by our reporter system would significantly reduce the genotyping workload. And since transporting maize ears from the field to lab is much more laborious than in the case of Arabidopsis, being able to screen in the field would save even more related workload for maize researchers.

Reasonable mutagenesis efficiency of the pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system in our study

The CRISPR/Cas9 system employed in this study is adapted from Rentao Song’s lab [24], with the Cas9 open reading frame codon-optimized for maize by Jinsheng Lai’s lab [37]. In Qi et al.’s 2016 study, the mutagenesis rates in the T0 generation of transgenic plants targeting a single site in the MADS/MYBR/AP2 gene were reported as 57.1%, 66.7% and 71.4%. In our research, out of the 16 T0 plants expressing Cas9 and sgRNA targeting SnRK2.1, 9 exhibited successful edits, resulting in a mutagenesis rate of 56.3%, comparable to the prototype without the eGFP module [24] and most reported cases in maize [9, 35]. In the T1 and T2 samples we tested, the SnRK2.1 mutagenesis rates were all 100%.

Regarding D1 mutagenesis, we planted 22 T1 plants expressing Cas9 and sgRNA targeting D1, of which 10 displayed a dwarf phenotype, indicating successful homozygous or bi-allelic knockout of d1 in the genome. Among the 8 plants we genotyped (including 4 from GFP seeds and 4 from non-GFP seeds), 7 contained at least one mutant allele of d1 (Table S1), yielding a high mutagenesis efficiency of 87.5%. Notably, all plants derived from GFP seeds, where the transgene cassette is still present, were homozygous mutants, which implies a high mutagenesis rate in the T0 generation.

Among the T1 plants derived from non-GFP seeds—often missed when using only selective media due to the lack of evidence of transformation—we identified one homozygous mutant and two uni-allelic mutants in the D1 case study that lacked the transgenic cassette (Table S4). In most CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing scenarios, uni-allelic mutants are typically obtained in the T1 generation, while transgene-free homozygous mutants are screened starting from the T2 generation (Fig. 4B). The speed at which we obtained a homozygous mutant is noteworthy. Additionally, in situations where screening of T2 or later generations is required, the eGFP reporter-assisted selection enabling phenotyping by visualization in the field would significantly reduce the workload, especially at an industrial scale.

Choice of D1 and ZmSnRK2.1 as target genes for testing the reporting system

In the present study, we tested the efficiency of our system by targeting the D1 and ZmSnRK2.1 genes in maize. The two genes were chosen for their potential in future germplasm innovation.

Instead of the currently available knockout mutations of the D1 gene, we aim to apply gene editing tools to create more subtle mutations, such as small insertions or deletions (indels), that hopefully do not cause frameshifts. This approach could potentially lead to a knockdown of the gene rather than a complete knockout, allowing for a spectrum of GA-deficient phenotypes and variation in plant stature. Although we have not yet achieved such mutations in the T1 generation, we plan to continue screening in later transgenic generations as well as incorporating more sgRNAs for more target sites, as the CRISPR/Cas9 system remains active.

Considering that mutations in genes related to GA signaling, such as Reduced Height Protein 1, or biosynthesis, like Gibberellin 20-Oxidase, have contributed to the development of semidwarf wheat (Triticum durum) and rice (Oryza sativa) during the Green Revolution, which improved yield and resilience [39], we are hopeful that we can harness this system to engineer the D1 gene and create a potent semidwarf maize variety.

The gene ZmSnRK2.1 was chosen for similar reasons. SnRK2 kinases have been shown to play an important regulatory role in abscisic acid (ABA) response in Arabidopsis [10]. However, studies of this family are very limited in other organisms. Recently, it is reported that ZmSnRK2.2 is involved in drought tolerance in maize through the ZmSnRK2.2-ZmHsf28-ZmJAZ14/17 module coordinating ABA and JA signaling [21]. By editing other members of the family, we plan to characterize the gene function and explore its role in breeding to improve drought resistance in maize.

Conclusions

The pAZS22-eGFP/CRISPR/Cas9 system contributes to the repertoire of reporter-assisted gene editing system available for maize breeding, providing an efficient tool for gene editing, transformant selection and transgene status identification.
