Central limit theorems for the nearest neighbour embracing graph in Euclidean and hyperbolic space
Holger Sambale, Christoph Thäle, Tara Trauthwein

Abstract
Consider a stationary Poisson process η \eta in the d d -dimensional Euclidean or hyperbolic space and construct a random graph with vertex set η \eta as follows. First, each point x ∈ η x\in\eta is connected by an edge to its nearest neighbour, then to its second nearest neighbour and so on, until x x is contained in the convex hull of the points already connected to x x . The resulting random graph is the so-called nearest neighbour embracing graph. The main result of this paper is a quantitative description of the Gaussian fluctuations of geometric functionals associated with the nearest neighbour embracing graph. More precisely, the total edge length, more general length-power functionals and the number of vertices with given outdegree are considered. Keywords . Central limit theorem, hyperbolic stochastic geometry, nearest neighbour embracing graph, Poisson process, random geometric graph, stabilizing functional, stochastic geometry. MSC . 60D05, 60F05, 60G55.

Introduction

Consider a stationary Poisson process
\eta
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We sequentially construct a random graph with vertex set
\eta
by connecting each point
x\in\eta
to its nearest neighbour, then to its second nearest neighbour and so on. This is continued until
x
is contained in the convex hull of the points already connected to
x
, that is, until
x
lies in the convex hull of its
k
nearest neighbours, where
k
is chosen to be minimal. The resulting random graph is called the
nearest neighbour embracing graph
, in short
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
. It was introduced by S. N. Chiu and I. Molchanov in
[
8
]
, and has found applications in good illumination problems
[
1
,
2
]
. We also refer to
[
6
]
for a construction algorithm and to Figure
1
for a simulation in Euclidean space.

In the present paper we deal with geometric functionals of the nearest neighbour embracing graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and in the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Studying random graphs in hyperbolic space of constant negative curvature has several motivations. First, changing the space underlying a geometric random graph model allows to distinguish between its universal properties and those which are curvature-dependent. Moreover, many real-world networks display a hyperbolic structure, which is well reflected by geometric random graphs as studied in
[
3
,
10
,
12
]
. To deal with both set-ups simultaneously we denote the underlying space by
\mathbb{X}^{d}
with
\mathbb{X}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{H}\}
and by
d(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
the standard Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{X}^{d}
. Moreover, we write
vol
for the Riemannian volume on
\mathbb{X}^{d}
. For
x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
and
r>0
, the notation
B(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}:d(x,y)\leq r\}
stands for the closed ball of radius
r
centred at
x
. If
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we also use the notation
B_{r}:=B(0,r)
for the ball of radius
r
centred at the origin and we also denote for
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{H}^{d}
by
B_{r}
the ball of radius
r
centred at an arbitrary but fixed point
p\in\mathbb{H}^{d}
, which we also refer to as the origin.

Now, let
\eta
be a stationary Poisson process on
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, that is,
\eta
is a Poisson process on
\mathbb{X}^{d}
with intensity measure equal to the Riemannian volume. Based on
\eta
we construct the nearest neighbour embracing graph
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
in
\mathbb{X}^{d}
as described above. Furthermore, we denote the set of
outgoing
neighbours of
x\in\eta
by
N(x,\eta)
, i.e., the set of neighbours the point
x
connects to in order to close its convex hull. Finally, the set of
all
neighbours of
x
will be denoted by
\tilde{N}(x,\eta)
. The first class of geometric functionals associated with
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
we are interested in are of the form

F_{t}^{(\alpha)}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\sum_{y\in\tilde{N}(x,\eta)}d(x,y)^{\alpha}

for some exponent
\alpha\geq 0
and
t\geq 2
. Here,
\eta|_{B_{t}}
denotes the restriction of
\eta
to
B_{t}
. Note that in
F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
, every edge between two points within
B_{t}
is counted with weight
1
, while edges which only have one endpoint in
B_{t}
are counted with weight
1/2
.

Our first result is a quantitative central limit theorem for
F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
, where the speed of convergence is measured by the Wasserstein and the Kolmogorov distance. We recall that the Wasserstein distance between the laws of two (real-valued) random variables
X
and
Y
is defined by

\textup{dist}_{W}(X,Y)=\sup\{|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(Y)|\colon h\in\mathrm{Lip}(1)\},

where
\mathrm{Lip}(1)
is the set of all functions
h\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
which are Lipschitz continuous with constant at most
1
. The Kolmorogov distance between the laws of
X
and
Y
is given by the supremum of the distance of their distribution functions, i. e.,

\textup{dist}_{K}(X,Y)=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq x)|.

We can now formulate our first main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N
be a standard Gaussian random variable and
\diamondsuit\in\{K,W\}
. Then, for any
\alpha\geq 0
and any
t\geq 2
, we have

\textup{dist}_{\diamondsuit}\bigg(\frac{F_{t}^{(\alpha)}-\mathbb{E}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}}},N\bigg)\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\textup{vol}(B_{t})}},

where
c>0
is a constant only depending on the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
and on
\alpha
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on the Malliavin–Stein method for functionals of Poisson processes. More precisely, we will use a normal approximation bound for such Poisson functionals as developed in
[
14
]
with an extension taken from
[
19
]
. We remark at this point that an essential step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
will consist in establishing a lower variance bound for the functionals
F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
. More precisely, we will shown in Lemma
4.2
below that for any
t\geq 2
one has that

\operatorname{Var}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\geq c\,\textup{vol}(B_{t}),

where
c>0
is a constant depending only on
\alpha
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
.

Remark 1.2
.

We only consider non-negative exponents
\alpha\geq 0
. It is reasonable to expect that similar central limit results can be achieved when considering negative exponents
\alpha>-\frac{d}{2}
, by combining the proof of our Theorem
1.1
with the bounds developed for the nearest neighbour graph in
[
19
]
. The speed of convergence we expect is then of the order of
\textup{vol}(B_{t})^{-1/2}
for
\alpha>-d/4
and
\textup{vol}(B_{t})^{-1+1/p}
for
-d/2<\alpha\leq-d/4
, where
p\in(1,2]
is chosen such that
2p\alpha+d>0
.

Recalling that
N(x,\eta)
is the set of neighbours necessary to close the convex hull around
x
, we write

\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)=\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x,\eta):=|N(x,\eta)|.

In
[
8
]
, the quantity
|N(x,\eta)|
is referred to as the degree of
x
in
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
, even if it strictly speaking differs from the actual degree of
x
in the graph-theoretic sense (which equals
|\tilde{N}(x,\eta)|
). We will call
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)
the outdegree of
x
in
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
. We denote the number of points of
\eta
in
B_{t}
with fixed outdegree
k
by

G_{t}^{(k)}:=\sum_{x\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)=k\}},

where we assume
k\geq d+1
since we almost surely need at least
d+1
points to close the convex hull around
x
.

In
[
8
, Section 3]
, a central limit theorem for
G_{t}^{(k)}
was proven for
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the usual Euclidean metric under the assumption that the rescaled asymptotic variance is positive. Our second main contribution consists in a quantification of this result and in a removal of the additional assumption on the asymptotic variance.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
N
be a standard Gaussian random variable and
\diamondsuit\in\{K,W\}
. Then, for any
k\geq d+1
and any
t\geq 2
, we have

\textup{dist}_{\diamondsuit}\bigg(\frac{G_{t}^{(k)}-\mathbb{E}G_{t}^{(k)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}G_{t}^{(k)}}},N\bigg)\leq\frac{c}{\sqrt{\textup{vol}(B_{t})}},

where
c>0
is a constant only depending on the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
and on
k
. Moreover,

\textup{dist}_{K}\bigg(\frac{G_{t}^{(k)}-\mathbb{E}G_{t}^{(k)}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}G_{t}^{(k)}}},N\bigg)\geq\frac{\hat{c}}{\sqrt{\textup{vol}(B_{t})}}

for another constant
\hat{c}>0
depending on the same parameters.

As already anticipated above, an essential step in the proof of Theorem
1.3
is a lower variance bound for
G_{t}^{(k)}
of the form

\operatorname{Var}G_{t}^{(k)}\geq c\,\textup{vol}(B_{t}),

where
c>0
is a constant depending only on
k
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
. In the case of the Euclidean space, this shows that the variance assumption made in
[
8
]
is always fulfilled. Furthermore, the proof of the upper bound in Theorem
1.3
is again based on the Malliavin–Stein method for Poisson functionals in the form taken from
[
14
,
19
]
. However, we note that in contrast to Theorem
1.1
we also have here a lower bound on the rate of convergence if
\diamondsuit=K
. We expect the same to be true for
\diamondsuit=W
and, similarly, for Theorem
1.1
as well.

The remaining parts of this paper are structured as follows. In Section
2
we collect some background material about Poisson functionals and gather some geometric facts which will be used throughout the paper. In Section
3
we consider the changes the nearest neighbour embracing graph undergoes if a new point is added to the generating point cloud. We study the local nature of these changes by analysing properties of the so-called radius of stabilization. The proofs of our main results are the content of the final Section
4
.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Poisson functionals

Throughout this section we consider an arbitrary metric measure space, denoted by
(\mathbb{Y},d,\lambda)
, and a Poisson process
\eta
on
\mathbb{Y}
with intensity measure
\lambda
. As a general reference to Poisson processes we refer to the monograph
[
13
]
. A random variable
F=f(\eta)
only depending on
\eta
is known as a Poisson functional. For
x\in\mathbb{Y}
and a Poisson functional
F=f(\eta)
we denote by
D_{x}F:=f(\eta+\delta_{x})-f(\eta)
the difference operator, where
\eta+\delta_{x}
is the configuration which arises if we add the point
x
to
\eta
. Second order differences
D_{x,y}F
are defined by iteration, i. e.,
D_{x,y}F:=D_{y}(D_{x}F)=f(\eta+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta+\delta_{x})-f(\eta+\delta_{y})+f(\eta)
for
x,y\in\mathbb{Y}
.

The main tools we use in order to derive our central limit theorems are bounds established by Last, Peccati and Schulte in
[
14
]
, with an extension taken from
[
19
]
. In particular, we use
[
14
, Theorem 6.1]
with
p_{1}=p_{2}=1
for the Wasserstein distance and derive an analogous bound from
[
19
, Theorem 3.4]
with
p=2
for the Kolmogorov distance.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
F
be an integrable Poisson functional such that
\mathbb{E}\int(D_{x}F)^{2}\lambda(dx)<\infty
and
\operatorname{Var}F>0
, and let
N
be a standard Gaussian random variable. Assume there are constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{x}F|^{5}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\qquad\text{for $\lambda$-a.e. $x\in\mathbb{Y}$},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{x,y}F|^{5}

\displaystyle\leq c_{2}\qquad\text{for $\lambda^{2}$-a.e. $(x,y)\in\mathbb{Y}^{2}$}.

(2.2)

Writing
\bar{c}=\max\{1,c_{1},c_{2}\}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{W}\Big(\frac{F-\mathbb{E}F}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}F}},N\Big)\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{3\bar{c}}{\operatorname{Var}F}\Big[\int\Big(\int\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F\neq 0)^{1/20}\lambda(dy)\Big)^{2}\lambda(dx)\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{\bar{c}}{(\operatorname{Var}F)^{3/2}}\int\mathbb{P}(D_{x}F\neq 0)^{2/5}\lambda(dx)

as well as

\displaystyle d_{K}\Big(\frac{F-\mathbb{E}F}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}F}},N\Big)\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{3\bar{c}}{\operatorname{Var}F}\Big[\int\Big(\int\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F\neq 0)^{1/20}\lambda(dy)\Big)^{2}\lambda(dx)\Big]^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{2\bar{c}}{\operatorname{Var}F}\left(\int\mathbb{P}(D_{x}F\neq 0)^{1/5}\lambda(dx)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle+\frac{13\bar{c}}{\operatorname{Var}F}\Big(\int\int\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F\neq 0)^{1/20}\lambda(dy)\lambda(dx)\Big)^{1/2}.

To derive a central limit theorem using Proposition
2.1
, it is crucial to show a lower bound for the variance
\operatorname{Var}(F)
of the right order. To do so in our set-up, we shall use
[
18
, Theorem 1.1]
, which reads as follows.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
F
be an integrable Poisson functional such that
\mathbb{E}\int(D_{x}F)^{2}\lambda(dx)<\infty
and

\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(D_{x,y}F)^{2}\lambda^{2}(d(x,y))\Big]\leq c\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(D_{x}F)^{2}\lambda(dx)\Big]

(2.3)

for some constant
c\geq 0
. Then,

\operatorname{Var}F\geq\frac{4}{(c+2)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\int(D_{x}F)^{2}\lambda(dx)\Big].

2.2
Geometric ingredients

In this section we gather the geometric ingredients needed for our purpose, including in particular basic definitions and facts from hyperbolic geometry. As a general references for hyperbolic geometry we mention the monographs
[
5
,
7
,
16
]
. The hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{d}
is the unique simply connected
d
-dimensional Riemannian manifold of constant negative curvature
-1
. We denote the corresponding Riemannian metric by
d(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
and the Riemannian volume by
vol
. There are several models of the hyperbolic space like the Beltrami–Klein model, the Poincaré ball model or the Poincaré half-plane model. We emphasize that all our results are model independent.

By
p
we denote an arbitrarily chosen fixed point of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
, referred to as the origin. Recalling the notation
B_{r}=B(p,r)
for the closed ball of radius
r>0
centred at
p
it holds that

\textup{vol}(B_{r})={d\kappa_{d}}\int_{0}^{r}\sinh^{d-1}(u)\,du,

(2.4)

where
\kappa_{d}:=\textup{vol}(B(0,1))=\frac{\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(1+d/2)}
is the volume of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball, see
[
16
, Eq. (3.26)]
. In fact, this identity is a direct consequence of the polar integration formula in hyperbolic geometry, which states that

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}f(x)\,\textup{vol}(dx)={d\kappa_{d}}\int_{S^{d-1}(p)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sinh^{d-1}(u)f(\exp_{p}(uv))\,du\sigma_{p}(dv),

(2.5)

see
[
7
, pages 123–125]
. Here,
S^{d-1}(p)
denotes the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere in the tangent space
T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{d}
of
\mathbb{H}^{d}
at
p
, the measure
\sigma_{p}
is the normalized spherical Lebesgue measure on
S^{d-1}(p)\subset T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{d}
, and
\exp_{p}\colon T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
stands for the exponential map which in our case is applied to the point
uv\in T_{p}\mathbb{H}^{d}
. The point
\exp_{p}(uv)
in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
arises from
p
by traveling in
\mathbb{H}^{d}
distance
v>0
along a geodesic ray in direction
u\in S^{d-1}(p)
.

As can be seen from (
2.4
), the growth of
\textup{vol}(B_{r})
as a function of
r
is exponential, which is in sharp contrast to the corresponding situation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
\textup{vol}(B_{r})=\kappa_{d}r^{d}
. In particular, it holds in the hyperbolic setting that

\gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)}\leq\textup{vol}(B_{r})\leq\Gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)},

(2.6)

for all
r\geq 2
, where
\gamma_{d},\Gamma_{d}>0
are dimension-dependent constants, see
[
11
, Lemma 7]
or
[
15
, Lemma 4]
.

In our proofs it will often turn out to be possible to treat the
d
-dimensional hyperbolic space and the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space simultaneously. We shall use for this generic space the notation
\mathbb{X}^{d}
with
\mathbb{X}\in\{\mathbb{H},\mathbb{R}\}
. Moreover, we let
p\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
be a fixed origin, which for
\mathbb{H}^{d}
we choose as above and for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
we take for
p
the point with Euclidean coordinates
(0,\ldots,0)
. For
z\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
we denote by
T_{z}\mathbb{X}^{d}
the tangent space of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
at
z
. We mention that in the Euclidean case, this is just
\mathbb{R}^{d}
itself. Similarly, we let
\exp_{p}:T_{p}\mathbb{X}^{d}\to\mathbb{X}^{d}
be the exponential map at
p
, which in the hyperbolic case was introduced above. If
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the function
\exp_{p}
is just the identity map on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

3
The radius of stabilization

A central element of our proofs is the fact that for the nearest neighbour embracing graph
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
, there exists a “radius of stabilization” for the difference operator
D_{x}
, i. e., a random variable
R(x,\eta)
such that all changes between
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta)
and
\mathrm{NNE}(\eta+\delta_{x})
happen within
B(x,R(x,\eta))
. The purpose of this section is to develop properties of this stabilization radius.

Lemma 3.1
.

For
x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
, define

R(x,\eta):=\max\{R_{1}(x,\eta),2R_{2}(x,\eta)\},

where

\displaystyle R_{1}(x,\eta):=\max_{z\in N(x,\eta)}d(z,x),

\displaystyle R_{2}(x,\eta):=\max\{d(z,y):z\in\eta,x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x}),y\in N(z,\eta)\}.

Then for all
x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\geq d+1
, it holds that

\displaystyle D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}(\eta)=D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}(\eta\cap B(x,R(x,\eta)))

and

\displaystyle D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}(\eta)=D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}(\eta\cap B(x,R(x,\eta))).

Proof.

If
x
is added to the configuration, any new edge must have
x
as one of its endpoints. Hence, there are two possibilities for new edges:

•

edges from
x
to one of its nearest neighbours, whose lengths are smaller than
R_{1}(x,\eta)
by definition,

•

edges from
x
to some
z\in\eta
such that
x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})
. In particular,
d(x,z)\leq\sup_{y\in N(z,\eta)}d(z,y)
and hence,
z\in B(x,R_{2}(x,\eta))
.

On the other hand, edges which are deleted upon the addition of
x
are edges from
z\in\eta
to
y\in N(z,\eta)
such that
x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})
and
\textup{conv}(x,N(z,\eta)\setminus\{y\})\subset\textup{conv}(N(z,\eta))
, i. e.,
x
completes the convex hull around
z
before
y
can get added to
N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})
. This implies necessarily that
d(x,z)\leq\sup_{w\in N(z,\eta)}d(w,z)
and also
d(x,y)\leq 2\sup_{w\in N(z,\eta)}d(w,z)
, so that
z,y\in B(x,2R_{2}(x,\eta))
. Hence, all changes to the edges or node degree of the graph (and, by extension, to
F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
or
G_{t}^{(k)}
) caused by insertion of the point
x
happen within
B(x,R(x,\eta))
.
∎

To derive properties of the radius of stabilization
R(x,\eta)
of Lemma
3.1
we start with the following technical result. Recall that we denote by
S^{d-1}(z)\subset T_{z}\mathbb{X}^{d}
the unit sphere in the tangent space at
z
. For
v\in S^{d-1}(z)
we let
H_{v}(z)
be the halfspace in
T_{z}\mathbb{X}^{d}
containing
v
, whose bounding hyperplane passes through
z
and which has normal vector
v-z
. Further, for
z\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
,
v\in S^{d-1}(z)
and
r>0
we put

S(z,v,r):=B(z,r)\cap\exp_{z}(H_{v}(z)),

(3.1)

where we recall that
\exp_{z}:T_{z}\mathbb{X}^{d}\to\mathbb{X}^{d}
stands for the exponential map. We remark that in the Euclidean case
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
the set
S(z,v,r)
is just the half-sphere centred at
z
with radius
r
pointing in the direction of
v
. Moreover, in order to treat the Euclidean and hyperbolic cases in parallel, we introduce the function
\rho:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,\infty)
, defined by

\rho(r):=\begin{cases}\kappa_{d}r^{d}&\text{in the Euclidean case}\\
\gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)}\mathbbm{1}_{\{r\geq 2\}}&\text{in the hyperbolic case.}\end{cases}

Here, we recall that

\kappa_{d}
denotes the volume of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball, and
\gamma_{d}
is the constant from (
2.6
). In other words,
\rho(r)
equals the volume of a ball of radius
r
in the Euclidean case, while in the hyperbolic case, it is a lower bound on the volume of a ball of radius
r
which as
r\to\infty
is of the same order as
\textup{vol}(B(p,r))
by (
2.6
).

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
r>0
and
z\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}\in(0,\infty)
depending on
d
only such that

\mathbb{P}(\exists\,v\in S^{d-1}(z)\text{ such that }\eta(S(z,v,r))=0)\leq c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}\rho(r)).

Proof.

By stationarity of
\eta
we may assume that
z
equals the origin
p
.
We cover the tangent space
T_{p}\mathbb{X}^{d}
, which we can identify with
\mathbb{R}^{d}
in both cases, with cones
C_{1},\ldots,C_{K}
centered at
p
having angular radius
\pi/4
. Now, let
r>0
and
v\in S^{d-1}(p)
be such that
\eta(S(p,v,r))=0
.
Then
v\in C_{i}\subset H_{v}(p)
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and thus
D_{i}:=\exp_{p}(C_{i})\subset\exp_{p}(H_{v}(p))
. Hence,
D_{i}\cap B(p,r)\subset S(p,v,r)
, so that
\eta(D_{i}\cap B(p,r))=0
. Application of the union bound leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\exists\,v\in S^{d-1}(p)\text{ such that }\eta(S(p,v,r))=0)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}(\eta(D_{i}\cap B(p,r))=0)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\exp(-\textup{vol}(D_{i}\cap B(p,r))).

Since each set of the form
D_{i}
, with
i\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, can be transformed into
D_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
using an isometry of the space
\mathbb{X}^{d}
,
\textup{vol}(D_{1}\cap B(p,r))=\ldots=\textup{vol}(D_{K}\cap B(p,r))=A\textup{vol}(B(p,r))
for some constant
A>0
. Using the definition of the function
\rho(r)
and putting
c_{1}:=K
and
c_{2}:=A
, this leads to the desired result.
∎

The previous result allows us to derive tail estimates for the radius of stabilization. They will turn out to be of crucial importance in later parts of the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, let
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
be distinct points. Then there are constants
c_{0},C_{0}>0
depending on the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only such that

\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{m}})>s)\leq(m+1)C_{0}\exp(-c_{0}\rho(s/2)),\qquad s>0.

Proof.

We start with the case
m=0
. Recall the definitions of
R(x,\eta),\ R_{1}(x,\eta)
and
R_{2}(x,\eta)
from Lemma
3.1
. First, we establish estimates for
R_{1}(x,\eta)
. Note that the event
\{R_{1}(x,\eta)>s\}
implies that
x
is not inside the convex hull of its neighbours within
B(x,s)
, which means there must be a set of the form (
3.1
) which does not contain any points of
\eta
. In other words,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{1}(x,\eta)>s)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\,v\in S^{d-1}(x)\text{ such that }\eta(S(x,v,s))=0\right)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}\exp(-c_{2}\rho(s)),

(3.2)

where the second bound follows from Lemma
3.2
.

We now deal with
R_{2}(x,\eta)
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\max\{d(z,y):z\in{\eta},x\in N(z,{\eta}+\delta_{x}),y\in N(z,{\eta})\}>s\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(z,y)>s\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{y\in N(z,\eta)\}}\,\eta(dz)\eta(dy).

Applying the Mecke equation for Poisson processes
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s)\\
\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(z,y)>s\}}\mathbb{P}(x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}))^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(y\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}))^{1/2}\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy).

Note that adding points to
\eta
decreases the probability that
x
is a neighbour of
z
, therefore

\mathbb{P}(x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}))\leq\mathbb{P}(x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})),

and likewise for
y
. Moreover,
x
can only be among the convex neighbours of
z
if there is a half-ball of radius
d(x,z)
centred at
z
which does not contain any points of
\eta
. We thus have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(z,y)>s\}}\mathbb{P}(x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x}))^{1/2}\mathbb{P}(y\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{y}))^{1/2}\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(z,y)>s\}}\mathbb{P}(\exists\,v\in S^{d-1}(z)\text{ such that }\eta(S(z,v,d(x,z)))=0)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\times\mathbb{P}(\exists\,w\in S^{d-1}(z)\text{ such that }\eta(S(z,w,d(y,z)))=0)^{1/2}\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy).

(3.3)

By Lemma
3.2
we deduce that (
3.3
) is bounded by

\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}c_{1}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(z,y)>s\}}\exp(-c_{2}\rho(d(z,x)))\exp(-c_{2}\rho(d(z,y)))\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy).

(3.4)

Since distance and volume are invariant under isometries, in both the Euclidean and the hyperbolic case we may assume that the point
x
coincides with the origin. Then, in the Euclidean case, (
3.4
) is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}c_{1}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert z-y\rVert>s\}}\exp(-c_{2}\kappa_{d}\lVert z\rVert^{d})\exp(-c_{2}\kappa_{d}\lVert z-y\rVert^{d})\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy).

Substituting
y=z+v
, this is

c_{1}^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\lVert v\rVert>s\}}\exp(-c_{2}\kappa_{d}\lVert v\rVert^{d})\,\textup{vol}(dv)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\exp(-c_{2}\kappa_{d}\lVert z\rVert^{d})\,\textup{vol}(dz).

Transformation into spherical coordinates and the subsequent change of variables
c_{2}\kappa_{d}r^{d}=u
yields that this is the same as

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}(d\kappa_{d})^{2}\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{-c_{2}\kappa_{d}r^{d}}{r^{d-1}}\,dr\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-c_{2}\kappa_{d}r^{d}}{r^{d-1}}\,dr=\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}}\int_{c_{2}\kappa_{d}s^{d}}^{\infty}e^{-u}\,du\,\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}\,du=\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}}e^{-c_{2}\kappa_{d}s^{d}},

and we conclude that

\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s)\leq\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}}\exp(-c_{2}\kappa_{d}s^{d})=\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{c_{2}^{2}}\exp(-c_{2}\rho(s)).

(3.5)

In the hyperbolic case, the right hand side of (
3.4
) is equal to

\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}c_{1}^{2}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(y,z)>s\}}\exp\left(-c_{2}(\rho(d(p,z))\right)\exp(-c_{2}\rho(d(y,z)))\,\textup{vol}(dz)\textup{vol}(dy).

Writing again
\exp_{z}:T_{z}\mathbb{H}^{d}\to\mathbb{H}^{d}
for the exponential map at
z
and substituting
y=\exp_{z}(ru)
with
r>0
and
u\in S^{d-1}(z)\subset T_{z}\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Then the last expression can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d\kappa_{d}\int_{\mathbb{H}^{d}}\int_{S^{d-1}(z)}\int_{s}^{\infty}c_{1}^{2}(\sinh r)^{d-1}\exp(-c_{2}\rho(d(p,z)))\exp(-c_{2}\rho(r))\,dr\sigma_{z}(du)\textup{vol}(dz),

where
\sigma_{z}
is the normalized spherical Lebesgue measure on
S^{d-1}(z)
.
Next, we notice that this expression does not depend on the direction
u
. Together with the introduction of hyperbolic spherical coordinates as in (
2.5
), this allows us to reduce the previous expression to

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}(d\kappa_{d})^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}(\sinh r)^{d-1}e^{-c_{2}\rho(r)}\,dr\int_{s}^{\infty}(\sinh r)^{d-1}e^{-c_{2}\rho(r)}\,dr.

To derive an upper bound for the last expression we use that
\sinh x=(e^{x}-e^{-x})/2\leq e^{x}/2\leq e^{x}
for
x>0
. Then, using the substitution
u=c_{2}\gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)}
and assuming that
s\geq 2
, we arrive at

\displaystyle c_{1}^{2}(d\kappa_{d})^{2}\Big(\int_{0}^{2}e^{r(d-1)}\,dr+\int_{2}^{\infty}e^{r(d-1)}e^{-c_{2}\gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)}}\,dr\Big)\Big(\int_{s}^{\infty}e^{r(d-1)}e^{-c_{2}\gamma_{d}e^{r(d-1)}}\,dr\Big)

\displaystyle=c_{1}^{2}(d\kappa)_{d}^{2}\Big(c_{3}+\frac{1}{(d-1)\gamma_{d}c_{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u}\,du\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{(d-1)c_{2}\gamma_{d}}\int_{c_{2}\gamma_{d}e^{s(d-1)}}^{\infty}e^{-u}\,du\Big)

\displaystyle=A_{1}e^{-c_{2}\gamma_{d}e^{s(d-1)}}

\displaystyle=A_{1}e^{-c_{2}\rho(s)},

where
c_{3},A_{1}>0
are constants only depending on
\mathbb{H}^{d}
. Altogether we derive the bound

\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s)\leq A_{1}e^{-c_{2}\rho(s)}

(3.6)

in the case that
s\geq 2
. Note that if
0<s<2
, the bound continues to hold trivially, provided that we choose
A_{1}\geq 1
large enough. Summarizing, this shows that the statement of the lemma holds in the case
m=0
in both the Euclidean and the hyperbolic settings.

Let us now study
R(x,\mu+\delta_{y})
, where
x,y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
are distinct points and
\mu\subset\mathbb{X}^{d}
is a locally finite collection of points. We have

R(x,\mu+\delta_{y})=\max\{R_{1}(x,\mu+\delta_{y}),2R_{2}(x,\mu+\delta_{y})\}.

Note that the addition of a point
y
can only make the convex hull of neighbours of
x
shrink, hence

R_{1}(x,\mu+\delta_{y})=\max\{d(x,z):z\in N(x,\mu+\delta_{y})\}\leq\max\{d(x,z):z\in N(x,\mu)\}=R_{1}(x,\mu).

As for
R_{2}(x,\mu+\delta_{y})
, we have

R_{2}(x,\mu+\delta_{y})=\max\{d(z,w):z\in\mu+\delta_{y},x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}),w\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{y})\}.

We first treat the case
z=y
. If
x\in N(y,\mu+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})
, then the maximum reduces to

\max\{d(y,w):w\in N(y,\mu+\delta_{y})\}\leq R_{1}(y,\mu).

We are left to find a bound for

\max\{d(z,w):z\in\mu,x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}),w\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{y})\}.

(3.7)

If
y\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{y})
, then necessarily
d(y,z)\leq\max\{d(z,w):w\in N(z,\mu)\}
, and hence (
3.7
) is bounded by

\max\{d(z,w):z\in\mu,x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}),w\in N(z,\mu)\}.

(3.8)

Lastly, we observe that
x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})
implies that
x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x})
, since adding points can only shrink the convex hull. Thus (
3.8
) is smaller than

\max\{d(z,w):z\in\mu,x\in N(z,\mu+\delta_{x}),w\in N(z,\mu)\}=R_{2}(x,\mu).

Combining the above estimates, we get

R(x,\mu+\delta_{y})\leq\max\{R_{1}(x,\mu),2\max\{R_{1}(y,\mu),R_{2}(x,\mu)\})=\max\{R(x,\mu),{2}R_{1}(y,\mu)\}.

By induction, it now follows that

R(x,\mu+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{m}})\leq\max\{R_{1}(x,\mu),2R_{2}(x,\mu),2R_{1}(y_{1},\mu),\ldots,2R_{1}(y_{m},\mu)\}.

Hence,

\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{m}})>s)\\
\leq\mathbb{P}(R_{1}(x,\eta)>s)+\mathbb{P}(R_{2}(x,\eta)>s/2)+\mathbb{P}(R_{1}(y_{1},\eta)>s/2)+\ldots+\mathbb{P}(R_{1}(y_{m},\eta)>s/2).

Using (
3.2
), (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), we deduce that

\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{m}})>s)\leq C_{0}(m+1)\exp\left(-c_{0}\rho(s/2)\right),

for well-chosen constants
C_{0},c_{0}>0
depending on the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only.
∎

In the next lemma, we prove an upper bound on moments of the number of points of
\eta
inside a ball of radius
R(x,\eta)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{X}^{d}
be a finite set of points and let
x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
. For any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there is a constant
c>0
only depending on
m
,
\mathcal{A}
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
such that

\mathbb{E}\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A}))^{m}\leq c.

Proof.

For any natural number
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A})))^{m}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(x,y)\leq R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A})\}}\,\eta(dy)\Big)^{m}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}(m)\mathbb{E}\sum_{(y_{1},\ldots,y_{\ell})\in\eta_{\neq}^{\ell}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(x,y_{1})\leq R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A})\}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(x,y_{\ell})\leq R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A})\}}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}(m)\int_{(\mathbb{X}^{d})^{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(x,y_{1})\leq R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{A})\}}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathbbm{1}_{\{d(x,y_{\ell})\leq R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{A})\}}\Big]\,\textup{vol}(dy_{1})\ldots\textup{vol}(dy_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}(m)\int_{(\mathbb{X}^{d})^{\ell}}\mathbb{P}(d(x,y_{1})\leq R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{A}))^{1/\ell}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathbb{P}(d(x,y_{\ell})\leq R(x,\eta+\delta_{y_{1}}+\ldots+\delta_{y_{\ell}}\cup\mathcal{A}))^{1/\ell}\,\textup{vol}(dy_{1})\ldots\textup{vol}(dy_{\ell}),

where
p_{\ell}(m)
is the number of possibilities to partition
\{1,\ldots,m\}
into
\ell
non-empty subsets, see
[
4
, Section 5.2]
, and
\eta_{\neq}^{\ell}
denotes the set of
\ell
-tuples of distinct points of
\eta
. We have used the Mecke equation for Poisson processes of
[
13
, Theorem 4.4]
to achieve the third line and Hölder’s inequality for the last line. By Lemma
3.3
, we derive that

\mathbb{E}\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta\cup\mathcal{A})))^{m}\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}p_{\ell}(m)\left(\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}C_{0}(\ell+|\mathcal{A}|+1)\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}}{\ell}\rho(d(x,y))\right)\,\textup{vol}(dy)\right)^{\ell}\leq c

for a suitable constant
c>0
only depending on
m
,
\mathcal{A}
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, using similar arguments as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
∎

4
Proofs of the main results

4.1
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The proof of Theorem
1.1
requires estimates for the add-one costs as well as a lower variance bound. We start with the add-one cost bounds and introduce for
x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
and a set
A\subset\mathbb{X}^{d}
the notation
d(x,A):=\inf\{d(x,a):a\in A\}
for the distance of
x
to
A
. Moreover, we shall write
\partial B_{r}=\{x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}:d(x,p)=r\}
for the sphere in
\mathbb{X}^{d}
of radius
r>0
centred at
p
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta>0
. Then there is a constant
\tilde{C}_{1}>0
depending on
\beta,\alpha
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
and
t\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left|D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\right|^{\beta}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left|D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\right|^{\beta}\leq\tilde{C}_{1}.

(4.1)

Moreover, there are constants
\tilde{c}_{2},\tilde{C}_{2}>0
depending on the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}
and
t\geq 2
,

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0\right)\leq\tilde{C}_{2}\exp(-\tilde{c}_{2}\rho(d(x,y)/2)),

and, if
x\notin B_{t}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0\right)\leq\tilde{C}_{2}\exp(-\tilde{c}_{2}\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t})/2))

and

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0\right)\leq\tilde{C}_{2}\exp(-\tilde{c}_{2}\rho(d(x,y)))\exp(-\tilde{c}_{2}\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t}))).

Proof.

Let us first check (
4.1
). If a point
x
is added, it follows from Lemma
3.1
that the number of edges which are newly gained is bounded by
\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))
. On the other hand, a rough bound on the number of edges which are lost is given by the total number of possible edges within
B(x,R(\eta))
, which is bounded by
\eta(B(x,R(\eta)))^{2}
. Altogether, this yields

|D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}|\leq 2\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{2}R(x,\eta)^{\alpha},

and hence, for any
\beta>0
,

\mathbb{E}|D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}|^{\beta}\leq 2^{\beta}\mathbb{E}[\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{4\beta}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}[R(x,\eta)^{2\alpha\beta}]^{1/2}.

We have that for
m=\lceil 4\beta\rceil
,

\mathbb{E}[\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{4\beta}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{m}]^{4\beta/m},

which, by Lemma
3.4
, is bounded by a constant depending on
\beta
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only. Furthermore, the term
\mathbb{E}[R(x,\eta)^{2\alpha\beta}]
is bounded as well by a constant depending on
\alpha,\beta
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
by Lemma
3.3
.

By similar arguments as we used for
D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
, we have

|D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}|\leq 2\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{2}R(x,\eta)^{\alpha}+2(\eta+\delta_{y})(B(x,R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})))^{2}R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})^{\alpha},

and hence, for any
\beta>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}|^{\beta}\leq\,

\displaystyle 2^{2\beta}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta)))^{4\beta}]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[R(x,\eta)^{2\alpha\beta}]^{1/2}\bigg)

\displaystyle+2^{2\beta}\bigg(\mathbb{E}[\big(\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})))+1\big)^{4\beta}]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}[R(y,\eta+\delta_{x})^{2\alpha\beta}]^{1/2}\bigg).

This is again bounded by a constant depending on
\alpha,\beta
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
.

To find a bound on
\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)
, recall that as argued in
[
14
]
(cf. the discussion below Proposition 1.4 there, for instance), if
d(x,y)>R(x,\eta)
and
R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})=R(x,\eta)
, we have
D_{x,y}F=0
for any Poisson functional
F
with radius of stabilization
R(x,\eta)
. In view of the definition of
R(x,\eta)
from Lemma
3.1
, we see that
R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})\neq R(x,\eta)
is possible if any of the following situations occur:

•

y\in N(x,\eta+\delta_{y})
, in which case we must have
d(x,y)\leq\sup_{z\in N(x,\eta)}d(x,z)\leq R(x,\eta)
,

•

there exists
z
such that
x\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{x})
,
y\in N(z,\eta+\delta_{y})
, which implies that
d(y,z)\leq\sup_{w\in N(z,\eta)}d(z,w)
and hence
d(x,y)\leq d(x,z)+d(y,z)\leq R(x,\eta)
,

•

x\in N(y,\eta+\delta_{x})
, in which case
d(x,y)\leq\sup_{z\in N(y,z)}d(y,z)\leq R(y,\eta)
.

Altogether, this leads to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)

\displaystyle\leq 3\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta)\geq d(x,y))+\mathbb{P}(R(y,\eta)\geq d(x,y))

\displaystyle\leq 4C_{0}\exp(-c_{0}\rho(d(x,y)/2))

(4.2)

using Lemma
3.3
.

Now note that if
x\notin B_{t}
and
d(x,\partial B_{t})>R(x,\eta)
, then any edge which is affected by adding
x
has both endpoints outside
B_{t}
, and consequently,
D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}=0
. This yields

\mathbb{P}(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)\leq\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta)\geq d(x,\partial B_{t}))\leq C_{0}\exp(-c_{0}\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t})/2)),

again by Lemma
3.3
.

To show the last bound, we need to combine both of the previous arguments. Assume that
x\notin B_{t}
. First of all, note that, as above, if
d(x,y)>R(x,\eta)
and
R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})=R(x,\eta)
then
D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}=0
. Moreover, if

d(x,\partial B_{t})>\max\{R(x,\eta+\delta_{y}),R(x,\eta)\},

then both
D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}(\eta)=0
and
D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}(\eta+\delta_{y})=0
and consequently
D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}=0
. Hence, reusing arguments from the beginning of the proof, we deduce that
D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0
implies
d(x,y)<\max\{R(x,\eta),R(y,\eta)\}
and
d(x,\partial B_{t})<\max\{R(x,\eta),R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})\}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta)>\max\{d(x,y),d(x,\partial B_{t})\})

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\mathbb{P}(R(y,\eta)>\max\{d(x,y),d(x,\partial B_{t})\})

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq}+\mathbb{P}(R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})>\max\{d(x,y),d(x,\partial B_{t})\})

\displaystyle\leq 3C_{0}\exp(-c_{0}\rho(\max\{d(x,y),d(x,\partial B_{t})\}/2)).

Note that
\rho(\max\{a,b\})=\max\{\rho(a),\rho(b)\}\geq\frac{1}{2}(\rho(a)+\rho(b))
, since
\rho
is a non-decreasing function. We thus derive that

\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)\leq 3C_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}}{2}\rho(d(x,y)/2)\right)\exp\left(-\frac{c_{0}}{2}\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t})/2)\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

In a next step we derive a lower variance bound for
F_{t}^{(\alpha)}
based on Proposition
2.2
.

Lemma 4.2
.

For
t\geq 2
it holds that

\operatorname{Var}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\geq c\rho(t),

where
c>0
is a constant depending on
\alpha
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only.

Proof.

We use Proposition
2.2
. First note that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}\{D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0\}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}]\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{P}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\neq 0)^{1/2}(\mathbb{E}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{4})^{1/2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy).

By Lemma
4.1
, this can be bounded by

C\int_{B_{t}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\exp(-c\rho(d(x,y)/2))\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)\\
+C\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\setminus B_{t}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\exp(-c\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t})/2))\exp(-c\rho(d(x,y)/2))\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)

for some constants
C,c>0
depending only on
\alpha
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
. Using standard spherical coordinates in the Euclidean case and the polar integration formula (
2.5
) for the hyperbolic space, it is not hard to see that

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\exp(-c\rho(d(x,y)/2))\,\textup{vol}(dy)<\infty

and hence we are left to bound

C\textup{vol}(B_{t})+C\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\setminus B_{t}}\exp(-c\rho(d(x,\partial B_{t})/2))\,\textup{vol}(dx).

(4.3)

In the Euclidean case,
\rho(t)=\textup{vol}(B_{t})
and the second term, after changing to polar coordinates, can be written as

\displaystyle Cd\kappa_{d}\int_{t}^{\infty}r^{d-1}\exp(-c\kappa_{d}2^{-d}(r-t)^{d})\,dr

\displaystyle=Cd\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}(u+t)^{d-1}\exp(-c\kappa_{d}2^{-d}u^{d})\,du

\displaystyle\leq Cd\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}2^{d-1}(u^{d-1}+t^{d-1})\exp(-c\kappa_{d}2^{-d}u^{d})\,du

\displaystyle=O(t^{d-1}).

Hence there is a constant
C_{1}>0
depending on
\alpha
(and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
) only such that

\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)\leq C_{1}\kappa_{d}t^{d}=C_{1}\rho(t)

for
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In the hyperbolic case, by (
2.6
), one has that

\textup{vol}(B_{t})\leq\frac{\Gamma_{d}}{\gamma_{d}}\rho(t).

For the second term of (
4.3
), it holds that for
x\notin B(p,t)
, one has
d(x,B(p,t))=d(x,p)-t
. By changing to hyperbolic polar coordinates using (
2.5
), as done in the proof of Lemma
3.3
, we deduce this term is bounded by

Cd\kappa_{d}\int_{S^{d-1}(p)}\int_{t}^{\infty}\sinh(r)^{d-1}\exp(-c\rho((r-t)/2))\,dr\sigma_{p}(dv).

Using again that
\sinh(x)\leq e^{x}
for
x>0
, and introducing the change of coordinates
u=r-t
, it follows that the above is bounded by

Cd\kappa_{d}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp((d-1)(u+t))\exp(-c\rho(u/2))\,du=\frac{Cd\kappa_{d}}{\gamma_{d}}\rho(t)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{(d-1)u-c\rho(u/2)}\,du\leq C_{2}\rho(t)

for some constant
C_{2}>0
depending on
\alpha
(and
\mathbb{H}^{d}
) only. Thus, we conclude that in the hyperbolic case
\mathbb{X}^{d}=\mathbb{H}^{d}
,

\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}\times\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x,y}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\textup{vol}(dy)\leq C_{3}\rho(t),

for some constant
C_{3}>0
which depends on
\alpha
and
\mathbb{H}^{d}
.

It remains to show a lower bound of order
\rho(t)
for the quantity

\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx).

Let
x\in B_{t}
such that
B(x,1)\subset B_{t}
. Recalling the notation established in (
3.1
), we start by
claiming
that there exists an
\epsilon>0
small enough such that for any
y\notin B(x,1-\epsilon)
and any
v\in S^{d-1}(y)\cap H_{\exp_{y}^{-1}(x)}(y)
, the set
S(y,v,d(x,y))
contains a subset of the form
D\cap B(x,1)\setminus B(x,1-\epsilon)
, where
D=\exp_{x}(C)
for some cone
C\subset T_{x}\mathbb{X}^{d}
, centred at
x
and of angular radius
\beta
, where
\beta
depends only on
\epsilon
. This is represented by the red area in Figure

. To

, define
x^{\prime}:=\exp_{y}^{-1}(x)
and denote by

the hyperplane in
T_{y}\mathbb{X}^{d}
passing through
y
and has normal vector
x^{\prime}-y
. Let
v^{\prime}
be the projection of
v
onto
\mathcal{H}_{x^{\prime}}(y)
. Then it holds that
H_{v^{\prime}}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y)\subset H_{v}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y)
. Indeed, one has

v^{\prime}=v-\frac{\langle x^{\prime}-y,v-y\rangle}{\lVert x^{\prime}-y\rVert^{2}}(x^{\prime}-y).

Hence for any
w\in H_{v^{\prime}}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y)
, we have

\langle v-y,w-y\rangle=\underbrace{\langle v^{\prime}-y,w-y\rangle}_{\geq 0\text{, as }w\in H_{v^{\prime}}(y)}+\frac{1}{\lVert x^{\prime}-y\rVert^{2}}\underbrace{\langle x^{\prime}-y,v-y\rangle}_{\geq 0\text{, as }v\in H_{x^{\prime}}(y)}\cdot\underbrace{\langle x^{\prime}-y,w-y\rangle}_{\geq 0\text{, as }w\in H_{x^{\prime}}(y)}\geq 0,

therefore
w\in H_{v}(y)
. Next, let
y^{\prime}
be the projection of
y
onto
\partial B(x,1-\epsilon)
for some small
\varepsilon
. Since
\exp_{y}^{-1}(y^{\prime})\in H_{x^{\prime}}(y)

\exp_{y}(\exp_{y}^{-1}(y^{\prime})+H_{v^{\prime}}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y))\subset\exp_{y}(H_{v^{\prime}}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y))\subset\exp_{y}(H_{v}(y)).

Moreover,
B(y^{\prime},d(x,y^{\prime}))\subset B(y,d(x,y))
and we note that, due to the fact that the hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}_{x^{\prime}}(y)
and
\mathcal{H}_{v^{\prime}}(y)

\exp_{y}(\exp_{y}^{-1}(y^{\prime})+H_{v^{\prime}}(y)\cap H_{x^{\prime}}(y))\cap B(y^{\prime},d(x,y^{\prime}))\cap B(x,1)\setminus B(x,1-\epsilon)

is always the same up to isometry, independently of the choice of
y
and

\epsilon>0
small enough such that there is a cone

with opening angle
\beta>0
depending on
\epsilon
such that for

, the intersection

is included in this set. This proves the claim.

We choose
\epsilon,\beta>0
as above. Cover

\beta/2
with apex at
x
. Choose
K

such that

and
z_{i}
lies within the projection

of cone
C_{i}
. We
claim
that for each point

outside

is completely covered by the union
\bigcup_{0<d(y,z_{i})<d(x,y)}S(y,\exp_{y}^{-1}(z_{i}),1)
. To

, assume this is not the case. Then there is a point

outside

and a point

such that the interior of

contains non of the points

. However, by the claim we proved earlier in this proof, the set

always contains a set of the form

, where

and

is a cone of apex

and angular radius
\beta
. The cone
C
must contain one of the cones

, and hence

is included inside of

.

Note that the above discussion continues to hold if we disturb the points

by some

chosen small enough.

Assume now that the point set
\eta
is such that

is empty,

contains exactly one point

in each of

and no other points of

are within

.

In this situation, points outside

(including

) might gain edges, but will not lose any if

, at least

, since

connects to its neighbours until the convex hull is completed. As

, we conclude that

|D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}|\geq(d+1)(1-\epsilon)^{\alpha}\mathbbm{1}\Big\{\eta(B(z_{1},\epsilon^{\prime}))=\ldots=\eta(B(z_{K},\epsilon^{\prime}))=1,\eta\Big(B(x,1)\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{K}B(z_{i},\epsilon^{\prime})\Big)=0\Big\}.

It follows that
\mathbb{E}(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\geq c>0
for some constant

depending, by isometry invariance of the volume, only on

and the choice of
\epsilon
,
\epsilon^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, and hence,

\int_{\mathbb{X}^{d}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\geq\int_{B_{t-1}}\mathbb{E}(D_{x}F_{t}^{(\alpha)})^{2}\,\textup{vol}(dx)\geq c\textup{vol}(B_{t-1})\geq c^{\prime}\rho(t)

for another constant

only depending on the same parameters. In particular, this proves (

) for a suitable constant

only depending on
\alpha
and the choice of
\epsilon
and
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, so that we obtain

\operatorname{Var}F_{t}^{(\alpha)}\geq c\rho(t).

This completes the argument.

Large parts of the proof of Theorem

. The only major difference lies in the proof of an analogue of Lemma

, i.e., a lower variance bound for

.

Lemma 4.3
.

For

it holds that

\operatorname{Var}G_{t}^{(k)}\geq c\rho(t),

where

is some constant depending on
k
and the choice of

only.

Proof.

Let

such that

. We construct three rings around

by defining

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}\colon\frac{2}{3}<d(x,y)<\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{9}\Big\},

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}\colon\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{9}<d(x,y)<\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{9}\Big\},

\displaystyle R_{3}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}\colon\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{9}<d(x,y)<1\Big\}.

See Figure

for an illustration. Split
\mathbb{X}^{d}
into two half-spaces

,

such that

(1)

Let

such that

is included in the convex hull of

.

(2)

Let

.

(3)

Let

which are closer to

than

.

for an illustration.

The validity of the construction (

) is equivalent to choosing
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d+1}
with
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d}\in R_{1}\cap H_{2}
and
z_{d+1}\in R_{2}\cap H_{1}
such that for every

, the set
S(x,v,8/9)
is non-empty. This can be achieved by constructing cones in the tangent space and projecting them down to
\mathbb{X}^{d}
, making sure that every projection contains a point. To see (
3
), we argue as follows.
Let
y\in\mathbb{X}^{d}\setminus B(x,\frac{2}{3})
and define
y^{\prime}
as the projection of
y
onto
\partial B(x,\frac{2}{3})
. Then
B(y^{\prime},d(x,y^{\prime}))\subset B(y,d(x,y))
and the set
B(y^{\prime},d(x,y^{\prime}))\cap B(x,1)
is, up to isometry, the same irrespective of the choice of
y
. Moreover,
B(y^{\prime},d(x,y^{\prime}))\cap B(x,1)
contains a set of the form
D\cap B(x,1)
, where
D=\exp_{x}(C)
for some cone
C\subset T_{x}\mathbb{X}^{d}
with apex at
x
and a given angular radius
\beta>0
. Now, cover
T_{x}\mathbb{X}^{d}
with cones
C_{1},\ldots,C_{K}
with apex at
x
and angular radius
\frac{\beta}{2}
and put
k+2
points
y_{i},\ldots,y_{i+k+1}
into
\exp_{x}(C_{i})\cap R_{3}
. There is now a cone
C_{i}
such that
\exp_{x}^{-1}(y)\in C_{i}
. Moreover,
C_{i}\subset C
, and so
\exp_{x}(C_{i})\cap R_{3}\subset D\cap B(y,d(x,y))
. Hence, all points
y_{i},\ldots,y_{i+k+1}
are closer to
y
than
x
.

Assume now that
\eta
contains the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d+1},w_{1},\ldots,w_{k-d-1},y_{1},\ldots,y_{K}
and no other points within
B(x,1)
. Then, upon the addition of
x
, the following holds: first,
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)=k
. Indeed, the half-ball
H_{1}\cap B(x,\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{9})
does not contain any point of
\eta
, and the half-ball
H_{1}\cap B(x,\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{9})
only contains the point
z_{d+1}
. Hence,
x
must connect to
z_{d+1}
. Since

for all

and

for all

, the point
x
must also connect to
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d},w_{1},\ldots,w_{k-d-1}
. The placement of the points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{d+1}
ensures that the convex hull is complete, and hence,
x
will not connect to any point outside of
B(x,\frac{2}{3}+\frac{2}{9})
.

Moreover, any point
y\in\eta
such that
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)\neq\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})
is such that
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)>k
and
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})>k
. To see this, first note that
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y)
can only change with the addition of

if
y
connects to
x
. In this case,
y
must also connect to any point of
\eta
within
B(y,d(x,y))
. Since
\eta\cap B(x,\frac{2}{3})=\varnothing
, we have

of the points

. It follows that
\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})\geq k+1
. Moreover,

must have been connected to all points of
\eta
within

.

\displaystyle D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)=k\}}+\sum_{y\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})=k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)\neq k\}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}-\sum_{y\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})\neq k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)=k\}}

for some constant

only depending on

and the choice of

. The remaining parts of the proof follow from arguments similar to the ones used in the proof of Lemma

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

To begin, recall that
R(x,\eta)
from Lemma
3.1
is a radius of stabilization for
G_{t}^{(k)}
. Moreover, note that

\displaystyle D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(x)=k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{x\in B_{t}\}}+\sum_{y\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})=k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)\neq k\}}-\sum_{y\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})\neq k\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)=k\}}

and hence,

\displaystyle|D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\sum_{y\in\eta|_{B_{t}}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta)\neq\overline{\mathrm{deg}}(y,\eta+\delta_{x})\}}\leq 1+\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta))).

In particular, applying Lemma
3.4
yields

\mathbb{E}\left|D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}\right|^{r}\leq C

for any
r>0
, where
C>0
is a constant which depends on
r
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle|D_{x,y}G_{t}^{(k)}|

\displaystyle\leq|D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}(\eta)|+|D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}(\eta+\delta_{y})|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta))+1+1+\eta(B(x,R(x,\eta+\delta_{y})).

This shows that we also have

\mathbb{E}\left|D_{x,y}G_{t}^{(k)}\right|^{r}\leq C

for any
r>0
, where
C>0
is a constant which depends on
r
and the choice of
\mathbb{X}^{d}
only. Furthermore, the probabilities of
D_{x}G_{t}^{(k)}\neq 0
and
D_{x,y}G_{t}^{(k)}\neq 0
are bounded akin to the case of the edge-sums discussed in Lemma
4.1
, as the reasoning in the proof of the latter is solely based on the behaviour of the radii of stabilization. Together with Lemma
4.3
, we may therefore complete the proof of the upper bound for both
\diamondsuit=W
and
\diamondsuit=K
in the same way as the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

To deduce a corresponding lower bound on the rate of convergence with
\diamondsuit=K
we observe that
G_{t}^{(k)}
is a square-integrable integer-valued random variable. It follows from an argument of Englund
[
9
]
(see also
[
17
, Section 3.3]
) that for such random variables the rate of convergence measured with respect to the Kolmogorov distance is always bounded by a constant multiple of the minimum of one and
1
divided by the square-root of the variance. The lower bound thus follows from this in conjection with Lemma
4.3
and the definition of
\rho(t)
.
∎
