Transgenerational inheritance of altered H3K27me3 in wild-type Caenorhabditis elegans
Authors: Isa Özdemir, François-Xavier Stubbe, Kamila Delaney, Joanna M Wenda, Florian A Steiner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epigenetic inheritance provides mutation-free mechanisms for phenotypic adaptation, including modifications to DNA or DNA-associated proteins that create patterns of heritable gene regulation. However, the limits and regulation of this inheritance remain incompletely understood. Here, we developed a C. elegans system to study the transgenerational epigenetic inheritance of H3K27me3, a conserved histone posttranslational modification associated with gene repression. We find that transiently induced alterations of the genome-wide H3K27me3 landscape and the associated fertility defects persist for many generations in genetically wildtype descendants under selective pressure. We uncover that the inheritance of the altered H3K27me3 landscape involves an initiation phase that relies on SET-32/H3K23me3 and a maintenance phase that requires MES-4/H3K36me3. Our results indicate that epigenetic inheritance can act as a mutation-independent, heritable mechanism of adaptation.

Introduction

Natural selection is based on the survival and reproduction of individuals with the best-adapted traits for a given environment, so that the genomes of these individuals are more likely to be inherited by the next generations. Adaptation relies on the random occurrence of mutations in the genome and is therefore slow and untargeted. However, epigenetic factors such as small RNAs, DNA methylation, or histone posttranslational modifications (PTMs) can affect gene expression in a heritable way, and can change in response to environmental influences, providing a potential mechanism for fast, targeted, and reversible adaptation (Waddington, 1942).

Histone PTMs can act as chromatin-based epigenetic factors to influence gene expression without alterations in DNA sequence. Therefore, the mitotic transmission of histone PTMs is one of the most-studied subjects of epigenetic inheritance (EI) (Özdemir and Steiner, 2021). There is consensus for a semi-conservative chromatin replication model, in which the parental chromatin serves as a template to replicate chromatin landscapes through the action of reader and writer proteins that recognize and catalyze histone PTMs, respectively (Alabert and Groth, 2012). Thus, in addition to the genetic material, the landscape of some histone PTMs is replicated in cis during the cell cycle. Typical examples of such “replicative EI” are the maintenance of repressive histone PTMs on “selfish” elements (Yang et al, 2019), or the barrier to cellular reprogramming in differentiated cell lineages (Nicetto and Zaret, 2019). However, not all histone PTMs are heritable. In cases such as the deposition of histone PTMs in response to environmental stimuli, the epigenetic remodeling during the germline-to-soma transition, or the lineage differentiation during development, “reconstructive EI” takes place, where histone PTMs are targeted de novo to specific genomic regions (Özdemir and Steiner, 2021; Alabert and Groth, 2012; Stewart-Morgan et al, 2020; Reverón-Gómez et al, 2018; Petryk et al, 2021; Yu et al, 2018; Xia et al, 2019; Zheng et al, 2016). The epigenome of any given cell results from both replicative EI and reconstructive EI, thereby enabling the germ line to give rise to highly specialized, yet totipotent germ cells (Jablonka, 2013, 2017; Margueron and Reinberg, 2010).

Examining EI from parents to offspring or across multiple generations is more challenging due to the scarcity of a practical experimental model. Long generation times, epigenome mixing during sexual reproduction, and varying epigenetic buffering capacities among individuals pose challenges for studying transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (TEI) in mammalian systems. In contrast, self-fertilizing, isogenic Caenorhabditis elegans is convenient for such studies owing to the fast reproduction, invariant development, and large brood sizes with little genetic variability between individuals, while at the same time sharing similar chromatin-based epigenetic systems with humans. C. elegans has been extensively used as a model system for the study of small RNA-based TEI, which in many cases involves changes to the histone PTMs at the targeted genomic loci (Woodhouse et al, 2018; Kalinava et al, 2018; Buckley et al, 2013). However, whether TEI can be based directly on initial chromatin changes is less well understood (Fitz-James and Cavalli, 2022).

Typically, investigating EI of histone PTMs relies on the genetic removal of reader, writer or eraser proteins, and the observation of the subsequent changes in the patterns of the histone PTMs (defects in replicative EI), or the failure to initiate them de novo (defects in reconstructive EI). For instance, the Polycomb histone PTM H3K27me3 is essential for germ line maintenance and cell differentiation (Cockrum and Strome, 2022; Gaydos et al, 2014, 2012; Kaneshiro et al, 2022, 2019). H3K27me3 is associated with repressed genes (Cao et al, 2002) and can be subjected to replicative EI and reconstructive EI. Mutating Polycomb group genes (PCGs) results in progressive and detrimental phenotypes such as mortal germ line (Mrt) or maternal-effect sterility (Mes) in C. elegans, which are linked to the failure to maintain or de novo initiate H3K27me3-mediated gene silencing. While such analyses allow mechanistic insight into the short-term maintenance of H3K27me3, the sterility phenotypes limit the study of H3K27me3 long-term inheritance (Gaydos et al, 2012, 2014).

To overcome these challenges and study the long-term inheritance of H3K27me3 patterns across generations, we developed a C. elegans model to alter H3K27me3 landscapes by the transient expression of a mutant histone H3.3 (H3.3K27M). We then analyzed the inheritance of the altered H3K27me3 landscapes and the associated fertility defects among the wildtype offspring of the subsequent generations. H3.3K27M was originally identified as a dominant-negative driver mutation of human high-grade gliomas, and we previously showed that its expression in C. elegans leads to an autosomal loss of H3K27me3 in germ cells, resulting in aberrant gene expression, endomitotic oocytes, and low fertility (Delaney et al, 2019; Lewis et al, 2013). Here, we show that the H3K27me3 landscapes altered by H3.3K27M expression and the associated fertility defects are epigenetically inherited for at least 15 generations of genetically wildtype offspring in a metastable way. We found that the inheritance is divisible into an initiation phase relying on SET-32-mediated deposition of H3K23me3, and a maintenance phase requiring MES-4-mediated deposition of H3K36me3. Our findings provide a mechanistic understanding of how organisms can transiently adapt their epigenomes and inherit these changes in subsequent generations.

Results

An inducible C. elegans system to transiently alter H3K27me3 epialleles

To investigate the inheritance of H3K27me3 patterns across generations, we first designed an approach to transiently alter the H3K27me3 landscape in the C. elegans germ line. We aimed to induce changes in the parental generation to mimic an environmental event, and then to monitor the inheritance (replicative EI) or recovery (reconstructive EI) in the offspring generations in the absence of the initial trigger (Fig. 1A). We had previously shown that the expression of a dominant-negative lysine 27 to methionine mutant of histone H3.3 (H3.3K27M) results in a redistribution of H3K27me3 in C. elegans germ cells, where it becomes restricted to the X chromosomes and a few autosomal domains (Delaney et al, 2019). As a consequence of this redistribution, genes in the autosomal regions that lose H3K27me3 become ectopically expressed, resulting in endomitotic oocytes and low fertility (Delaney et al, 2019). The genome-wide redistribution of H3K27me3 is easily visible by immunofluorescence (IF), enabling an assessment of cell-to-cell variability within a single worm. This provides a major advantage over TEI studies using reporter genes, where the detection of chromatin changes requires ChIP-seq experiments, and the signal from many cells and individuals is averaged. To restrict the expression of the H3.3K27M protein to the parental generation, we designed a tetracycline-inducible transgene (H3.3K27Mtet) (Fig. 1B). The H3.3K27M responder transgene is under the control of a constitutive driver transgene that expresses both an activator (rtTA(Q); rTetR fused to the QFAD activation domain from the fungal transcription factor QF) and a repressor (tTS; TetR fused to the repressor domain from C. elegans PIE-1) (Mao et al, 2019). In the absence of tetracycline, the repressor binds to the Tetracycline Response Element (TRE) and prevents expression of the responder transgene, and in the presence of tetracycline, the activator replaces the repressor at the TRE to drive H3.3K27M expression (indicated by a beige background or labeling in all the figures) (Fig. 1B, C). The inducible expression of H3.3K27M mimics an environmental event that changes the H3K27me3 epigenome, and we aimed to investigate the transmission of the changed epigenomes to the wild-type offspring. To exclude the analysis of worms with leaky expression of the transgene in the inheriting generations, we delivered the transgenes in the form of an extrachromosomal array that has a low meiotic transmission rate and is lost in part of the offspring. The driver transgene also encodes a fluorescent mKate protein, so that worms containing the array (H3.3K27Mtet) can easily be distinguished by their red fluorescence from worms that have lost the array (WT). This allowed us to study the inheritance of altered H3K27me3 landscapes in genetically wildtype offspring (Fig. 1C).

First, we verified by IF that H3.3K27Mtet, but not wildtype worms, showed a redistribution of the H3K27me3 landscape upon tetracycline treatment, with a global depletion of the H3K27me3 signal on autosomes, and at least a partial retention on chromosome X (Fig. 1D). The persistence of the H3K27me3 signal on the X chromosome upon H3.3K27Mtet induction was previously verified and explained by both low levels of H3.3 and high pre-existing levels of H3K27me3 on the X chromosome (Delaney et al, 2019). The H3.3K27Mtet-induced changes in the H3K27me3 landscape led to a strong decrease in brood sizes at 25 °C in most of the worms, with some escapers (Fig. 1E). To use fertility as a selection criterion, we classified the phenotypes into subfertile, arbitrarily defined as 0–50 embryos (including animals without germ line), and fertile, defined as >50 embryos. Wildtype worms typically had 200–250 offspring at 25 °C (Fig. 1E). The subfertile phenotype was detected almost exclusively in worms carrying the H3.3K27Mtet array, and only upon tetracycline induction (Fig. 1E,F). It became more penetrant with increasing tetracycline concentrations used for H3.3K27Mtet-induction (Fig. 1F). We selected a level of induction that caused the fertility defects, but also allowed for sufficient fertility to propagate the next generations (1 ng/µl tetracycline). Consistent with our previous observations, the H3.3K27Mtet-induced fertility defects were partially suppressed by the depletion of kgb-1, a MAPK subfamily serine-threonine kinase that becomes derepressed in the germline of worms expressing H3.3K27M (Fig. 1G) (Delaney et al, 2019). Taken together, the system allowed us to use tetracycline treatment of worms carrying the H3.3K27Mtet array to induce a redistribution of H3K27me3 that resulted in the expected and previously characterized fertility defects, and then to select genetically wildtype offspring that had lost the array for further analysis.

Altered H3K27me3 landscapes and the resulting fertility defects are epigenetically inherited for at least 15 generations in genetically wildtype progeny

Next, we designed a transgenerational assay to investigate the transmission of the H3.3K27Mtet-induced defects to wildtype offspring (Fig. 2A). We induced the expression of H3.3K27Mtet in the parental (P-1 and P0) generations, singled >50 array-containing individuals (P0array), and categorized them into fertile and subfertile according to their brood sizes. Among the offspring of the P0array generation, we only selected offspring that had lost the H3.3K27Mtet array (genetically wildtype) and no longer expressed H3.3K27M. To assess the inheritance of fertility defects, we identified subfertile P0array individuals (defined as ≤50 viable offspring) and singled >50 of their F1 offspring. When these F1subfertile reached adulthood, we classified their fertility (fertile or subfertile). For each subsequent generation Fnsubfertile, we again identified subfertile individuals, singled >50 of their offspring (the Fn+1subfertile), and classified the fertility (fertile or subfertile) of these offspring (Fig. 2A). Worms that did not contain the H3.3K27Mtet array in the P-1 and P0 generations and therefore never expressed H3.3K27M, as well as their offspring, were used as wildtype control (P0wt and Fwt). In the P0 generation, 74% of the worms carrying the H3.3K27Mtet array (P0array) showed fertility defects, while less than 7% of the P0wt worms were subfertile at 25 °C (Fig. 2B). Remarkably, the H3.3K27Mtet-induced fertility defects were still present among the genetically wildtype F1 offspring of subfertile P0array animals. 37% of these F1subfertile inherited fertility defects (Fig. 2B). We expanded the TEI assays to generations F2 to F15 (Fig. 2A). We found that when selecting subfertile individuals in generation Fnsubfertile, the fertility defects were present in 25–40% of their offspring in the generation Fn+1subfertile (Fig. 2B). This demonstrates that under selection, the phenotypic consequences of the H3.3K27M expression in the P0 generation can be heritable for at least 15 generations in a population of wildtype worms. The offspring of subfertile worms displayed both subfertile and fertile phenotypes in each generation, implying that individual worms can both replicate the H3.3K27Mtet-induced changes or reconstruct the wildtype H3K27me3 landscape, respectively, in different germ cells. The fertility defects among the Fwt (wildtype control) remained low and ranged between 3 and 12% for all generations analyzed (Fig. 2B).

To obtain a quantitative measure of the fertility defects, we plotted the brood sizes of the P0wt/Fwt and P0array/Fsubfertile worms (Fig. 2C). Most P0wt and Fwt animals had >200 offspring. H3.3K27Mtet-induction resulted in a reduction of the brood size to less than 50 for 74% of the P0array worms. The brood sizes of Fsubfertile remained, on average, consistently lower compared to Fwt for at least 15 generations, with a large variability. This variability in brood sizes of the Fsubfertile in every generation again implies that most worms inherit the fertility defects but are also able to reconstruct the wildtype H3K27me3 landscape in part of the germ cells, resulting in partially or fully recovered brood sizes.

Next, we tested whether the inheritance of the fertility defects was based on the inheritance of the altered H3K27me3 landscape. We used IF to detect H3K27me3 in germ cells of P0wt and P0array and in Fwt and Fsubfertile worms from generations F1 to F15. IF allows the detection of altered chromatin landscapes within single cells and is therefore ideally suited to probe patterns that are inherited with low penetrance and are heterogeneous within individuals. As described above (Fig. 1D), H3.3K27Mtet-induced P0array worms showed germ cells with an altered H3K27me3 landscape, where the H3K27me3 signal is depleted from autosomes but retained on chromosome X. Strikingly, the changed H3K27me3 landscape was present in all of the next 15 Fsubfertile generations, even though the H3.3K27M protein was no longer detectable in any of the F generations (Figs. 2D and EV1). Fwt worms did not show altered H3K27me3 landscapes in any generation (Fig. 2D). We quantified the penetrance of the altered H3K27me3 landscape within individual worms, and assessed if a high penetrance of the chromatin defects resulted in a low brood size of that individual. We estimated whether individual P0wt/Fwt or P0array/Fsubfertile animals were fertile or subfertile by letting them lay eggs until the second day of adulthood, and then analyzed the H3K27me3 patterns of the germ cells of these same individuals by IF. To quantify the penetrance of the chromatin changes, we determined the percentage of nuclei with clearly recognizable altered H3K27me3 patterns per gonad (Fig. 2E). Most P0array/Fsubfertile individuals contained a mix of germ cells with altered H3K27me3 landscapes and with fully or partially reconstructed wildtype-like H3K27me3 landscapes (Fig. 2F). For the quantification of the penetrance, we only counted nuclei with completely aberrant landscapes as “altered”, thus likely underestimating the penetrance of the defects. P0wt and Fwt individuals did not show any altered H3K27me3 patterns and faithfully replicated the wildtype H3K27me3 landscapes (Fig. 2E). Germ lines of P0array animals classified as subfertile (brood size <50) contained on average 60% nuclei with altered patterns (Fig. 2E). Strikingly, the P0array individuals that were classified as fertile (“escapers”; brood size >50) had on average only 4% nuclei with altered H3K27me3 patterns, confirming a causal link between altered H3K27me3 patterns and reduced fertility. Consistent with a robust epigenetic inheritance, we observed germ cells with altered H3K27me3 patterns with a penetrance of on average 30–50% in subfertile individuals of the F1subfertile to F15subfertile generations. Some Fsubfertile individuals still showed 75% germ cells with altered H3K27me3 landscapes in generation F15, demonstrating that they can be robustly transmitted across generations (Fig. 2E). Germ lines of Fsubfertile individuals classified as subfertile contained more nuclei with altered patterns than individuals classified as fertile (the latter were only assessed in F1–F3). The partial penetrance of the altered H3K27me3 patterns within individual germ lines explains how each individual in each generation can give rise to both fertile and subfertile offspring. The variability of the H3K27me3 patterns both between and within individuals also emphasizes the need for methods that allow the assessment of individual worms and cells (such as IF) instead of methods that average the signals of many cells from many individuals (such as ChIP-seq).

Taken together, our observations imply that during the transition from one Fsubfertile generation to the next, some germ cells reconstruct the wildtype H3K27me3 landscape and give rise to fertile offspring, while other germ cells replicate the altered H3K27me3 patterns and give rise to subfertile offspring. When selecting for subfertile individuals, the H3.3K27Mtet-induced chromatin changes can be epigenetically inherited for at least 15 generations in a population of genetically wild-type worms.

Downstream effects of altered H3K27me3 patterns persist during TEI

In our previous analysis of a strain with constitutive expression of H3.3K27M, we had shown that the loss of autosomal H3K27me3 results in ectopic activation of many genes that are normally silenced in the germline. This resulted in fertility defects at 25 °C that could be suppressed by the depletion or inhibition of KGB-1, or by growing the worms at 20 °C (Delaney et al, 2019). Given that we observed an inheritance of the changed H3K27me3 chromatin patterns in our TEI assay, we hypothesized that the fertility defects in the inheriting generations should also depend on the expression of kgb-1 and the growth at 25 °C. To test this hypothesis, we depleted KGB-1 by RNAi from P0array to F1subfertile, or in the subfertile generations (F1 to F2, F3 to F4, or F4 to F5) (Fig. 3A). In a parallel experiment, we shifted the worm cultures from 25 °C to 20 °C in the same generations as for the KGB-1 depletion experiment (Fig. 3B). Both depletion of KGB-1 and a temperature shift to 20 °C suppressed the fertility defects in the subsequent Fsubfertile generations (Fig. 3A,B). To assess whether the inheritance of the changed chromatin patterns resulted in similar patterns of aberrant gene expression in the P0array and the Fsubfertile generations, we dissected germ lines of adult individuals in the P0 and F3 generations and analyzed their gene expression by RNA-seq (Figs. 3C–F and EV2A,B). We compared fertile individuals from P0wt (wildtype control), subfertile individuals from P0array, subfertile individuals from F3subfertile, and fertile individuals from F3subfertile. We pooled 20 dissected germ lines per sample, and compared the P0array and F3subfertile samples to the P0wt sample. The sets of ectopically expressed genes for both the P0array and subfertile individuals from the F3subfertile were enriched for somatic genes (Fig. 3E), consistent with the ectopic de-silencing of somatic genes in the germ line upon loss of H3K27me3. Spreading of H3K36me3 promotes TEI of altered H3K27me3 patterns

Next, we wondered what prevented the reconstruction of wildtype H3K27me3 patterns in the offspring that inherited the changed H3K27me3 distribution. The histone PTM H3K36me3 has previously been shown to antagonize H3K27me3 in the C. elegans germ line. H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 form mutually exclusive domains across the genome of germ cells to allow the expression of germline genes and to repress somatic genes, respectively (Gaydos et al, 2012). We therefore hypothesized that genomic regions that lose H3K27me3 upon expression of H3K27M gain ectopic H3K36me3. These altered H3K36me3 epialleles may then antagonize the reconstruction of wild-type H3K27me3 epialleles during TEI.

We co-stained H3K36me3 and H3K27me3 in pachytene nuclei and quantified the H3K36me3 chromatin occupancy by comparing the H3K36me3 signal with the DAPI signal. This analysis revealed an increased signal of H3K36me3 in Fsubfertile pachytene nuclei where H3K27me3 was reduced (Fig. 4A,B). However, the increase in H3K36me3 was not observed in the P0array generation (Fig. 4A,B). To test if the spreading of H3K36me3 is required for the replicative TEI of the H3.3K27Mtet-induced defects, we depleted MES-4, the histone methyltransferase required for the maintenance of H3K36me3 (Gaydos et al, 2012; Rechtsteiner et al, 2010; Furuhashi et al, 2010), during the transition from one generation to the next. Loss of MES-4 results in a maternal-effect sterile (Mes) phenotype (Furuhashi et al, 2010), and we could therefore only analyze the generation immediately following the start of the mes-4(RNAi) treatment. Mes-4(RNAi) treatment of the P0wt/Fwt had no effect on the fertility of the following generation, but maintaining the RNAi-mediated depletion of MES-4 for two generations resulted in the expected Mes phenotype with >90% penetrance, demonstrating that the depletion was efficient. Depletion of MES-4 reduced, but did not completely abolish H3K36me3 signal in pachytene nuclei (Fig. 4C), likely because of the activity of other H3K36 HMTs such as MET-1 (Kreher et al, 2018; Cockrum and Strome, 2022). When we started the exposure to mes-4(RNAi) in generation F1subfertile or in later generations (Fnsubfertile, with n = 2, 5, 6, or 7), we observed a full suppression of the replicative EI of the H3.3K27Mtet-induced defects, and worms of the subsequent generation showed H3K27me3 landscapes and fertility comparable to wildtype controls (Figs. 4C,D and EV3A). These results suggest that in the absence of MES-4, the lower levels of H3K36me3 allow the reconstruction of wild-type H3K27me3 domains during the development of the subsequent generation, thus restoring fertility. They also demonstrate that the ectopic H3K36me3 epialleles formed upon redistribution of H3K27me3 are required for the replicative TEI of the altered H3K27me3 epialleles and the resulting fertility defects. Surprisingly, starting the mes-4(RNAi) treatment at P0array generation had no suppressive effect on the inherited fertility defects or on the altered H3K27me3 landscape in F1subfertile worms (Fig. 4C,D). These results suggest that MES-4 is required for maintaining the ectopically initiated H3K36me3 epialleles in Fsubfertile, but not for initiating the TEI during the transition from P0array to F1subfertile. Spreading of H3K23me3 is required for the initiation of TEI of altered H3K27me3 patterns

Previous studies implicated the methyltransferase SET-32 in the initiation of small-RNA-mediated epigenetic inheritance (Woodhouse et al, 2018; Spracklin et al, 2017). SET-32 catalyzes the methylation of H3K23, and no H3K23me3 is observed in pachytene nuclei in set-32 mutants, indicating that SET-32 is the main HMT for H3K23me3 at the pachytene stage of the C. elegans germ line (Fig. EV3B). We hypothesized that H3K23me3 replaces H3K27me3 upon H3K27Mtet-induction and initiates EI of the altered epialleles. To test this hypothesis, we detected H3K23me3 by IF from P0 to F8 in P0wt/Fwt and P0array/Fsubfertile animals, and quantified the H3K23me3 chromatin occupancy by comparing the H3K23me3 signal with the DAPI signal. In P0wt/Fwt animals, H3K23me3 appeared as localized spots on chromatin, consistent with previous observations in wildtype worms (Vandamme et al, 2015) (Fig. 5A). Upon the induction of H3.3K27Mtet in the P0array animals, the H3K23me3 signal increased and appeared as a continuous pattern on the chromosomes in P0array nuclei with altered H3K27me3 landscapes. The increase in H3K23me3 persisted in the F1-F2subfertile generations, but subsequently returned to wildtype levels and distributions after F2subfertile (Fig. 5A,B). These results suggest that H3K23me3 replaces H3K27me3 in genomic regions where it is lost upon H3.3K27Mtet induction, initiating the EI of the altered H3K27me3 patterns. We next examined whether the increased deposition of H3K23me3 is required for the replicative TEI of the H3.3K27Mtet-induced phenotypes, and whether it could bridge the reduction of H3K27me3 and the increase of H3K36me3 during the P0array to F1subfertile transition. We hypothesized that the expression of H3.3K27Mtet induces the SET-32-mediated deposition of H3K23me3 in the P0array generation, and that the ectopic H3K23me3 epialleles counteract the reconstructive EI of wildtype H3K27me3 domains during the transition to the F1subfertile generation. To test this hypothesis, we depleted SET-32 by RNAi from P0 to F1 and examined the inheritance of H3.3K27Mtet-induced fertility defects. Depletion of SET-32 did not affect fertility in the P0array (nor in the P0wt or Fwt). However, it fully suppressed the H3K27Mtet-induced fertility defects and restored the wildtype H3K27me3 landscape in F1subfertile generations (Figs. 5C,D and EV3C). In contrast, depleting SET-32 in the F generations, either during F1-F2subfertile or during F5-F7subfertile transitions, did not suppress the fertility defects or their inheritance to the subsequent generations (Fig. 5D). These results demonstrate that SET-32 plays a major role in the intergenerational inheritance of H3K27Mtet-induced defects from P0array to F1subfertile generations, and in initiating TEI. They suggest that SET-32 deposits H3K23me3 on autosomal regions where H3K27me3 is lost in the P0array generation. During the transitions from F1-F2subfertile generations, H3K23me3 is then replaced by H3K36me3.

Based on these observations, we propose a model for the TEI of altered H3K27me3 domains. In the P0array, H3.3K27Mtet expression causes a depletion of autosomal H3K27me3 domains. SET-32 methylates H3K23 in the genomic regions where H3K27me3 is lost, which is required for the EI of the altered H3K27me3 patterns to the F1 generation (initiation phase). During the transition from F1/F2 to the later F generations, H3K36me3 replaces H3K23me3 and is required for the maintenance of the altered H3K27me3 patterns. During the maintenance phase, some germ cells can reconstruct wild-type H3K27me3 epialleles through the de novo writer activity of PRC2. In other germ cells, MES-4 maintains the ectopic H3K36me3 epialleles, thus antagonizing the reconstruction of wild-type H3K27me3 domains (Fig. 6). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate the TEI of an altered H3K27me3 landscape for many generations in a wild-type C. elegans population. Previous studies that have investigated EI have focused on the heritable (de)silencing of reporter transgenes by an acutely applied environmental stress (Klosin et al, 2017) or exogenously supplied small RNAs (Buckley et al, 2013; Priyadarshini et al, 2022; Ni et al, 2014; Ashe et al, 2012; Houri-Ze’evi et al, 2016; Lev et al, 2017). These studies have demonstrated that the effects on gene expression states can be passed on to unexposed offspring across several generations, requiring the RNAi machinery and involving chromatin modifications associated with gene silencing (Buckley et al, 2013; Demoinet and Roy, 2018). In parallel, studies describing the genetic removal of chromatin readers and writers provided insight into how chromatin marks are deposited and maintained (Stewart-Morgan et al, 2020; Greer et al, 2014; Heestand et al, 2018; Kaneshiro et al, 2022). Together, these studies clearly demonstrated the existence of TEI in C. elegans, but left open the questions of whether TEI can rely on histone PTMs as inheriting factors, and how the changes in chromatin were maintained across generations. Here, we revealed that an altered H3K27me3 landscape and the associated fertility defects can be transgenerationally heritable. Visualization of the H3K27me3 redistribution by IF allowed us to determine the persistence and penetrance of the inheritance in individual worms. We found that the TEI comprises two distinct steps: initiation, and maintenance (Fig. 6). In the initiation phase, the reduction of H3K27me3 results in an increase in SET-32-mediated deposition of H3K23me3. During the transition from initiation to maintenance (F1 to F2 generations), H3K23me3 levels diminish and MES-4-mediated deposition of H3K36me3 increases. The prediction from this model that the same genomic regions or nucleosomes that are marked by H3K23me3 during initiation become marked by H3K36me3 remains to be tested. Both during initiation and maintenance of TEI, in every individual worm, some germ cells escape the maintenance of the altered H3K27me3 epialleles and reconstruct wild-type H3K27me3 patterns. This competition between the replicative TEI and the reconstructive TEI results in a partial penetrance of the inherited fertility defects even within individual worms.

Genomic domains enriched in H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 are mutually exclusive in several model organisms (Kaneshiro et al, 2022; Schmitges et al, 2011; Dorighi and Tamkun, 2013). We found that the switch from H3K27me3 domains to H3K36me3 domains required the transient deposition of H3K23me3 by SET-32. Maybe because of the transient nature of H3K23me3, point mutations at H3K23 or loss-of-function mutations of H3K23me3 writer or reader proteins are not associated with any obvious defects (Zhebrun et al, 2025), and mutants of set-32 are viable (Woodhouse et al, 2018; Andersen and Horvitz, 2007; Kalinava et al, 2017). SET-21 has been described as the second H3K23me3 HTM that acts in parallel to SET-32 (Zhebrun et al, 2025). SET-21 appears to be most expressed during diakinesis and contributes less to the overall H3K23me3 levels than SET-32 (Zhebrun et al, 2025), and we therefore did not test it in our transgenerational assay. SET-32 was previously identified in epigenetic suppressor screens (Greer et al, 2014; Okabe et al, 2021; Wan et al, 2021). It was found to be required for the initiation of heritable epigenetic phenotypes such as an increased lifespan caused by the reduction of H3K4me3 levels or the silencing of a GFP reporter transgene (Woodhouse et al, 2018; Kalinava et al, 2018; Ashe et al, 2012; Greer et al, 2014). In line with these previous studies, we found that removal of SET-32 does not have a suppressive effect on H3.3K27Mtet-expressing P0, but that SET-32 is required for the initiation of replicative EI during the transition from P0 to F1. We propose that H3K23me3 is a transitory histone PTM required to initiate a heritable epigenetic state in response to induced changes in the epigenome. We can only speculate what mediates the transition from H3K23me3 to H3K36me3. Methylation at position K23 may be incompatible with additional methylation at position K36. If this is the case, MES-4-mediated deposition of H3K36me3 would require either the activity of a yet to be identified demethylase, or a replication-dependent nucleosome turnover during germline development of the subsequent generation.

To experimentally select subfertile animals in each generation, we used an arbitrary cutoff of <50 offspring, resulting in an observation of 30–40% replicative TEI and 60–70% reconstructive TEI in each generation (Fig. 2B). Similarly, for quantifying germ cells with altered H3K27me3 landscapes, we only scored those as “altered” that resembled cells expressing H3.3K27M in the P0 (Fig. 2F). However, we observed a spectrum for both phenotypes, with a broad range of embryos laid (Fig. 2C), and intermediate stages of reconstruction of the wildtype H3K27me3 landscape (Fig. 2F). We therefore likely underestimate the penetrance of the TEI. However, our results resemble the low penetrance of epigenetically inherited phenotypes observed in previous studies (Barucci et al, 2020; Demoinet and Roy, 2018; Greer et al, 2016, 2014, 2011; Heestand et al, 2018; Houri-Zeevi et al, 2021; Kalinava et al, 2018, 2017; Kaneshiro et al, 2022; Klosin et al, 2017; Lee et al, 2019; Lev et al, 2017; Posner et al, 2019; Toker et al, 2022; Woodhouse et al, 2018). The spectrum of germ cells with either replicated or reconstructed H3K27me3 distributions is observed within individuals (Fig. 1E), and not just within the population, explaining why in every generation, individual worms can give rise to a mix of fertile and subfertile offspring. We have previously shown that the global loss of H3K27me3 causes gene expression changes at many genes, but that de-silencing of the kgb-1 gene is mainly responsible for the reduction in brood size (Delaney et al, 2019). The observed partial reconstruction of H3K27me3 domains in some germ cells (Fig. 2F) can therefore explain the imperfect correlation between chromatin defects and reduced brood sizes, as the latter depends on whether the kgb-1 locus is targeted by the partial reconstruction. How specific regions are targeted by PRC2 for H3K27me3 reconstruction is not well understood, and the amount of PRC2 is likely limiting this process. Similar observations were made for sperm-inherited epialleles lacking H3K27me3, where the majority of loci regained H3K27me3 in the offspring, while few regions could not reconstruct H3K27me3, and instead maintained H3K36me3 marks (Kaneshiro et al, 2022, 2019). These observations support the concept that the heritability of altered H3K27me3 and H3K36me3 chromatin landscapes is subject to the continuous competition between PRC2 and MES-4.

PRC2 is essential for development, as homozygous mutations in PRC2 result in defective lineage specification and body patterning in flies and mammals (Grijzenhout et al, 2016; Lewis, 1978; O’Carroll et al, 2001; Pasini et al, 2004). Additionally, several monoallelic, recessive loss-of-function mutations in PRC2 (likely reducing the overall HMT activity) are associated with developmental disorders such as Weaver Syndrome, suggesting that not only the zygotic presence, but also the post-embryonic maintenance of H3K27me3 epialleles is important in humans (Lui et al, 2018; Imagawa et al, 2017). Therefore, defining the mechanisms underlying the maintenance of H3K27me3 is crucial for understanding cellular epigenetic memory in health and disease. In C. elegans, mutations in PRC2 result in a Mes phenotype due to the failure in the maintenance of silencing X-linked genes in the germ line (Gaydos et al, 2012; Bender et al, 2006). This Mes phenotype can be interpreted as an epigenetic phenotype, as loss of maternal PRC2 only affects the grandchildren, but the sterility prevents the analysis of future generations (Gaydos et al, 2014). Here, we take advantage of the inhibition of PRC2 by the transient expression of H3.3K27M (Delaney et al, 2019) to demonstrate that EI can persist beyond the following generation. This approach resembles the transient inhibition of PRC2 recently described in Drosophila, which leads to an irreversible derepression of genes linked to tumorigenesis (Parreno et al, 2024). Our results show that such a derepressed state can be heritable and under selection extend for many generations in C. elegans.

The balance between reconstructive and replicative TEI by opposing histone PTMs in the germ line appears to be a general principle for heritable gene expression states, and may provide plasticity and epigenetic variation across generations (Jablonka, 2013; Patel and Wang, 2013). Here, we have shown the long-term inheritance of an altered H3K27me3 landscape, which is maintained by a transient spreading of H3K23me3 followed by H3K36me3. We propose that this inheritance of epigenome-based phenotypes can provide a mutation-independent mechanism for adaptation to the environment.

There are several limitations to our study. It investigates transgenerational epigenetic inheritance and probes the epigenome at every generation in a specific developmental window of the germline. This allows us to determine what changes from one generation to the next, but does not allow us to follow the dynamic processes that occur during development. The stochastic nature of the epigenetic inheritance and the relatively low penetrance of the phenotypes prevent us from generating large epigenetically homogenous populations that would be required for omics analyses in later generations, e.g., to probe if the same nucleosomes or genomic regions are marked by H3K23me3 during establishment and by H3K36me3 during maintenance. Detailed epigenome profiling of individual germ cells is currently hampered by the technical limitations to obtaining completely resolved single-cell epigenomes for C. elegans.

C. elegans strains and culture

C. elegans strains were grown using standard conditions for maintenance at 20 °C (Brenner, 1974). All experiments were performed at 25 °C unless otherwise stated. Animals were maintained on regular NGM (0.25% peptone, 0.3% NaCl, 1.5% agar, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 25 mM KH2PO4, pH 6.0, 25 mM K2HPO4, pH 6.0, and 5 µg/mL cholesterol) seeded with E. coli OP50 or HT115. N2 (Bristol strain) was used as the wildtype. The generation of tetracycline-inducible transgenes expressing H3.3K27M was based on the sequence of the previously published his-72(uge72) allele containing a C-terminal OLLAS tag (Delaney et al, 2019). The transgene (referred to as H3.3K27Mtet) was expressed from an extrachromosomal array obtained by microinjection of plasmids TC374 (containing the transgenic tetracycline-inducible driver) (Mao et al, 2019) and pIO1 (containing the his-72K27M::OLLAS responder transgene) into N2 (Fig. 1B). This resulted in the strain FAS158(ugeEx108[rpl-28p::rTetR::QF::P2A::mKate::T2A::TetR::pie-1 + 7xTetO::pes-10p::his-72[K27M]::ollas his-72 3’UTR]), which was used for all experiments. H3.3K27Mtet induction was done on 1 ng/µl tetracycline-containing NGM plates seeded with HT115 unless otherwise indicated.

Transgenerational subfertility assay

The approach for initiating fertility defects and following their inheritance is described in (Fig. 2A). In all experiments that assess fertility, we singled worms at larval stages before any germline or fertility phenotypes were visible to avoid observer bias. About 10–12 P-1 animals (parents of P0 generation) were selected based on the presence or absence of the extrachromosomal H3.3K27Mtet array and placed at 25 °C when they were at the L3-L4 larval stage. From these P-1 plates, >50 L3/early L4 animals (before any phenotypes were apparent) of the P0 generation were singled. P0array animals contained the H3.3K27Mtet array, while P0wt animals were derived from wildtype P-1 animals and thus did not contain the array. After 3–4 days, the brood sizes of these P0 animals were scored under a dissecting microscope (Leica S9E stereo microscope) and categorized into fertile and subfertile. Fertile animals lay more than 50 viable embryos, while subfertile worms lay less than 50 viable embryos. For the analysis of the inheritance, only individuals without the H3.3K27Mtet array were selected, so that all worms in the F generations were genetically wildtype. For the F1 generation, >50 offspring of subfertile animals from the P0array were randomly selected at the L3/L4 stage and singled for the analysis of phenotypes in the F1subfertile generation. As control, >50 offspring of fertile animals from the P0wt were randomly selected at the L3/L4 stage and singled for the analysis of phenotypes in the F1wt generation. This procedure was repeated for subsequent F generations, with the selection and analysis of >50 offspring of subfertile Fsubfertile animals and >50 offspring of fertile Fwt animals in each generation. Fertility level comparisons were performed in three biological replicates. Statistical tests applied for each experiment were specified in the corresponding figure legends.

RNAi treatment and temperature shift assay

The standard RNAi by feeding protocol (Kamath et al, 2001) was used. Briefly, overnight-grown RNAi precultures in LB medium containing ampicillin at 37 °C were diluted 10x in the same medium and incubated for 3–4 h at 37 °C (until OD values reached up to 0.6–0.8), followed by 4 mM IPTG induction for another 4 h at 37 °C. The cultures were concentrated by centrifugation before seeding onto freshly prepared RNAi NGM (±1 ng/µl tetracycline, 1 mM IPTG, and 25 µg/ml carbenicillin). mes-4(RNAi) and set-32(RNAi) clones pIO47 and pIO9 were generated by Gibson assembly using L4440 as a backbone (Gibson et al, 2009).

For mes-4(RNAi), L3/L4 from the P0, F1, F2, F5, F6, and F7 generations were exposed to RNAi by feeding, and 40–50 offspring were singled on individual plates for fertility assessment. Only the first generation following mes-4(RNAi) could be assessed, as the second generation showed the expected Mes phenotype. For set-32(RNAi), worms were exposed to RNAi by feeding from P0-F2, F1-F2, and F5-F8. For kgb-1(RNAi), worms were exposed to RNAi by feeding from P0-F1, F1-F2, F3-F4, and F4-F5. As a control for the RNAi treatment, worms were continuously exposed to bacteria containing the empty L4440 RNAi vector. Temperature shift experiments were done as RNAi experiments, except that plates were shifted from 25 °C to 20 °C from P0-F1, F1-F2, F3-F4, and F4-F5.

Immunofluorescence staining and image analysis

Worm gonads were dissected in 4 µl of anesthetizing DB buffer (1.6% sucrose, 73 mM HEPES, pH 6.9, 40 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM EGTA, pH 7.5, 0.1% NaN3) on ten-well microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a 21 G needle (BD Microlance). Dissected gonads were transferred to poly-L-lysine-coated (0.1% (w/v); Sigma-Aldrich) three-square-well microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Slides were placed on a dry-ice cold metal plate for at least 30 min. Samples were fixed in methanol for 10 min. The fixatives were stored at −20 °C and reused for a maximum of ten experiments. Slides were washed three times in PBS for 5 min and incubated with 1:300 anti-OLLAS (Novus Biologicals NBP1-06713), 1:300 anti-H3K27me2/3 (Active motif 39535), 1:600 anti-H3K23me3 (Active motif 61499) or 1:2000 anti-H3K36me3 (Abcam ab9050) antibodies overnight at 4 °C in a humid chamber. Samples were washed in PBS-Tween-20 three times for 5 min and incubated with 1:1000 times diluted Cy3- (Jackson ImmunoResearch) or Alexa488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) secondary antibodies for 1.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Samples were washed with PBS-Tween-20 three times for 5 min, stained with 2 µg/ml DAPI for 15 min, and mounted with VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium (H-1200). 2D and confocal images were obtained using a Leica DM5000B with 63x oil objectives (NA = 1.25) and a Leica LSM800 confocal microscope with 63x oil objectives (NA = 1.40), respectively. Representative confocal images show maximum projections of 0.1-μm Z-sections of the entire pachytene nuclei.

For quantifying the extent of co-localization of histone marks on the chromatin of stained pachytene nuclei, MMMEA macro and EZcolocalization plugin in Fiji were used (Stauffer et al, 2018; Schindelin et al, 2012). The maximum Z projections for each nucleus were stacked based on the DAPI signal. Graphs were generated based on the user-defined parameters and the obtained measurements using a custom R script. The percentage of pachytene cells showing altered H3K27me3 patterns per gonad were scored manually using Fiji (Schindelin et al, 2012).

Western blotting

Western blotting was carried out according to standard procedures. For detecting H3.3K27M expression, 40 worms per condition were picked into 20 µL of M9 buffer, and an equal volume of 2X SDS-PAGE Loading Buffer was added. Samples were sonicated using a Bioruptor (Eppendorf metallic stand) at high power for ten cycles of 30 s ON/30 s OFF at 4 °C. Lysates were then boiled for 3 min and 20 µL were used for Western blot. OLLAS-tagged H3.3K27M was detected with an anti-OLLAS antibody (Novus Biologicals NBP1-06713), and H3 (loading control) was detected using an anti-H3 antibody (Abcam Ab1791). HRP-coupled secondary antibodies (Rat: Cell Signaling #7077, Rabbit: Cell Signaling #7074) were revealed by ECL (Bio-Rad #1705061) using an Amersham ImageQuant 800.

RNA-seq and expression analysis

For the RNA-seq analysis, we collected germ lines from P0wt (wildtype control; n = 3), subfertile individuals from P0array (n = 2), subfertile individuals from F3subfertile (n = 2), and fertile individuals from F3subfertile (n = 2). We estimated whether individual animals were fertile or subfertile by letting them lay eggs until the second day of adulthood, and then dissected germ lines for RNA extraction. Worms were washed in PBS and transferred to PBS supplemented with 0.1% sodium azide and 0.01% Tween-20. Gonads were extruded and severed from the carcass by cutting worms with a 26-gauge needle. Individual gonads were transferred using glass needles into 10 µl of TRIzol (kept on ice) in a three-well glass block, and dissolution of the gonad was confirmed visually under the microscope. Approximately 20 gonads were collected per sample. The TRIzol volume was adjusted to 100 µl, and RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

RNA-seq libraries were prepared by the iGE3 Genomics Platform (University of Geneva) using the Illumina Stranded mRNA protocol with polyA selection, and sequenced with the 50nt single-end protocol on a NovaSeq 6000. Single-end sequencing reads were quasi-mapped to the C. elegans transcriptome (WBcel235) using Salmon v1.10.3 (Patro et al, 2017) with GC bias correction and mapping validation. A decoy-aware index was constructed using the full WBcel235 genome sequence as a decoy. Transcript-level quantifications were imported into R and summarized to gene level using tximport v1.34.0 (Soneson et al, 2015). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 v1.46.0 (Love et al, 2014). Genes were considered differentially expressed at a Fold Change of ≥2 and an FDR of ≤0.01.

Data representation and statistical analysis

All data analysis, statistical significance testing, and plot rendering were performed in R using tidyverse v2.0.0 (Wickham et al, 2019). The figures were assembled in Affinity Designer and Inkscape. Quantification of brood sizes, fertility, nuclei per gonad, or chromatin occupancy are derived from biological replicates.
