Single-cell multiomics identifies an ALDH9A1–carnitine signaling axis driving resistance of NSCLC to immunotherapy
Authors: Hailei Du, Tong Lang, Xiaoxue Zha, Chao Qu, Ling Chen, Shihua Yao, Xing Feng, Zhaohui Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceAlthough immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the clinical management of non–small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs), majority of patients ultimately develop primary or acquired resistance, limiting long-term therapeutic benefits. Here, we show that ALDH9A1, the committed enzyme in carnitine biosynthesis, is a previously unrecognized regulator of immune evasion in NSCLC and an important functional contributor to immunosuppressive remodeling and response to anti-PD-1 therapy. This resistance can be overcome by IL-1β neutralization or pharmacologic ALDH9A1 inhibition with DEAB. Clinical analyses further reveal a strong association between dysregulation of the ALDH9A1/IL-1β axis and reduced survival in patients with NSCLC, highlighting this pathway as a promising therapeutic target for next-generation immunotherapy strategies. These findings warrant further investigation into its clinical utility.

Even though Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized the clinical management of non–small cell lung cancers (NSCLCs), majority of patients ultimately develop primary or acquired resistance (1–3). Among the diverse mechanisms implicated in ICI failure, reprogramming of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is now recognized as a major driver of both extrinsic and intrinsic resistance pathways (4, 5). TIME consists of multiple immune and stromal cellular groups whose functional states and spatial configuration critically determine tumor development and therapeutic effectiveness (6, 7). However, most existing studies rely on simplified 2D systems or conventional murine models that fail to recapitulate structural complex and cellular interactions present in human NSCLCs.

Advances in single-cell spatial transcriptomics and codetection by indexing (CODEX) imaging provide an opportunity to dissect the spatially resolved cellular ecosystems underlying ICI responsiveness (8–10). To identify determinants of immunotherapy resistance, we applied an integrative multiomics strategy including CODEX, single-cell spatial transcriptomics, metabolomics, and proteomics to the study of NSCLCs from patients with or without resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. This analysis revealed a previously uncharacterized association between anti-PD-1 nonresponsiveness and aldehyde dehydrogenase 9A1 (ALDH9A1), a metabolic enzyme required for carnitine biosynthesis (11). Although ALDH9A1 has been implicated in tumor metabolism and immune regulation in other contexts (12–14), its function in NSCLC and its relationship with the TIME remain poorly defined.

We observed that ALDH9A1 expression is significantly higher in the immunosuppressive environments of NSCLCs and hypothesized that it contributes to immune evasion. Using immunocompetent and immunodeficient mouse models, we show that genetic ablation or pharmacologic inhibition of ALDH9A1 suppresses NSCLC progression and overcomes resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. Mechanistically, ALDH9A1 loss increases tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation while reducing tumor-infiltrating MDSCs, suggesting a central role of ALDH9A1 in shaping the TIME. These findings highlight ALDH9A1 as a promising intervention target for improving immunotherapy responsiveness in NSCLC.

Results

Single-Cell Spatial Transcriptomics and Lipidomics Assays Identify ALDH9A1 as a Candidate Gene Related to NSCLC Resistance to Anti-PD-1 Antibody.

To identify novel genes that dictate the response of NSCLCs to ICIs, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on 24 NSCLC tumors collected from patients with either durable response or poor response to anti-PD-1 therapy (SI Appendix, Table S1). Integrated analysis identified multiple major cellular populations, including tumor cells, T cells, myeloid cells, fibroblast/CAF (Cancer-associated fibroblasts) populations, endothelial cells, NK cells, mast cells, pericytes, and B/plasma cells (Fig. 1 A–C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B and Table S2).

Immune populations were further subclustered into activated T cells, CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, exhausted T cells, NK cells, dendritic cells, PMN-MDSCs, M-MDSCs, tumor-associated macrophages, B/plasma cells, and Treg cells based on canonical marker expression (Fig. 1 D and E). Comparative analysis revealed substantial differences in immune composition between responder and nonresponder tumors. In particular, responder tumors exhibited increased CD8 T cell infiltration, whereas PMN-MDSCs were enriched in nonresponder tumors (Fig. 1 F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C).

Differential expression analysis of tumor cells further demonstrated marked transcriptional differences between the two groups (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Table S3). Gene ontology analysis revealed enrichment of metabolic and oxidative stress-related pathways in nonresponder tumors (Fig. 1J and SI Appendix, Table S4). Among the upregulated genes, ALDH9A1 emerged as one of the most significantly elevated metabolic genes in resistant tumors (Fig. 1 I and K). Consistent with the known role of ALDH9A1 in carnitine-associated fatty acid metabolism (14), lipidomics analysis demonstrated increased accumulation of multiple carnitine-related metabolites in nonresponder tumors (Fig. 1L). Together, these findings identified ALDH9A1-associated metabolic remodeling and immunosuppressive myeloid enrichment as prominent features of NSCLC resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy.

CODEX Assays Further Verify the Spatial Relationship Between High ALDH9A1 Protein Levels and NSCLC Immune Evasion in Clinical Patients.

Next, at the protein level, we sought to further verify whether there is a positive correlation between ALDH9A1 upregulation, tumor progression, and the spatial landscape of immune and cancer cells in NSCLCs. To map a comprehensive single-cell spatial profile and visualize markers of distinct cellular types, we collected 20 microdissected NSCLC specimens (SI Appendix, Table S1) with varied ALDH9A1 expression levels, and ten out of these 20 samples are PD-1 responders while the rest are not. Second, tissue microarrays (TMAs) were constructed using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE)-optimized technology. Third, we used multiplex staining of 35 antibodies, including an anti-ALDH9A1 antibody, to pinpoint every cell group within the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Finally, the spatial organization of the TIME was investigated by employing CODEX platform (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B).

After cell segmentation, quantification and normalization of unique markers, unsupervised learning, and cluster analysis, 11 major cellular populations were identified within NSCLCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C–E). Comparative spatial analysis revealed distinct immune architectures between tumors with relatively high versus low ALDH9A1 expression. Representative CODEX images demonstrated that ALDH9A1-high tumors exhibited reduced CD8 T cell infiltration and increased enrichment of MDSC myeloid populations within tumor regions (Fig. 2 A and B). By contrast, ALDH9A1-low tumors displayed increased CD8 T cell accumulation together with higher densities of IFNG-high and Granzyme B-high cells, consistent with enhanced cytotoxic immune activity (Fig. 2C).

Quantitative analysis across all patient samples further demonstrated increased CD8 T cell abundance in responder tumors, whereas MDSC populations were relatively enriched in nonresponder tumors (Fig. 2 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). To further characterize spatial cellular interactions, we performed tumor-centered neighborhood analysis by quantifying neighboring cell populations within a 25-μm radius surrounding individual tumor cells. Responder tumors exhibited increased CD8 T cell and B cell neighborhoods, whereas nonresponder tumors displayed greater enrichment of MDSC cells surrounding tumor cells (Fig. 2 F–H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3B and Table S5). Importantly, ALDH9A1 protein intensity negatively correlated with tumor-centered CD8 T cell neighborhood scores and positively correlated with MDSC neighborhood scores across NSCLC samples (Fig. 2 I and J and SI Appendix, Table S6). Furthermore, TLS was also quantified, and we found a negative correlation between ALDH9A1 expression and TLS (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Together, these findings suggest that elevated ALDH9A1 expression is associated with an immunosuppressive spatial microenvironment in NSCLC.

Aldh9a1 Deficiency Significantly Limits Lung Cancer Progression and Promotes the Maturation of Tertiary Lymphoid Structures in Immunocompetent Mice.

Our data suggested a potential effect of ALDH9A1 on NSCLC immunotherapy. To validate this hypothesis, a tamoxifen (TAM)-inducible Aldh9a1 knockout was performed in mouse KP lung cancer model. Briefly, we crossed UBC-Cre-ERT2, KrasFSF-G12D/+, and Trp53frt/frt (KP) mice with Aldh9a1f/fmice to produce KrasFSF-G12D/+, and Trp53frt/frt, Aldh9a1f/f; UBC-Cre-ERT2/+mice (Fig. 3A). intranasal Ad-FlpO administration was performed to initiate lung cancer development in 8-wk-old Aldh9a1f/f mice. After 10 wk, we injected TAM to induce Aldh9a1 knockout (Fig. 3B). Six weeks later, tumor burden in Aldh9a1+/+ KP mouse was bigger than that in Aldh9a1−/−KP mouse (Fig. 3 C and D). And Aldh9a1−/− KP mice showed prolonged survival compared to Aldh9a1+/+ counterparts (Fig. 3E). Additionally, compared with wild-type LLC1 cells, Aldh9a1 knockdown in LLC1 cells significantly inhibited subcutaneous tumor growth in immune‐competent C57BL/6J but not in nude mice (Fig. 3 F and G), suggesting that ALDH9A1 contributes to NSCLC progression by remodeling TIME rather than directly acting within tumor cells.

Thus, immune cells from tumor regions of Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− KP mice were isolated by FACS sorting based on CD45 expression and followed by bulk RNA sequencing. RNA sequencing of tumor tissues indicated significantly upregulated and downregulated genes in these two groups (Fig. 3H and SI Appendix, Table S7). Pathway analysis and gene ontology (GO) enrichment revealed increased chemokine production and inflammatory response in Aldh9a1−/− tumors compared to Aldh9a1+/+ tumors (Fig. 3I), indicating a proinflammatory tumor microenvironment in Aldh9a1−/− mice. Notably, Aldh9a1 knockout enhanced the maturation of tertiary lymphoid structures (mTLS) in KP tumors, which was defined with primary (CD21+ follicular dendritic cells [DCs]) follicles, and have been previously shown to be positively associated with enhanced responsiveness to ICIs and improved survival (15, 16) (Fig. 3K). Aldh9a1 loss was associated with increased intratumoral lymphocyte infiltration, including higher CD4+ and CD8+ T cell densities by image-based quantification. These results demonstrate that ALDH9A1 is a candidate predictive biomarker for responsiveness to ICIs.

CyTOF and Pseudotime Trajectory Analysis Reveal That ALDH9A1 Drives the Polarization of Immunosuppressive MDSCs and CD8+ T Cell Exhaustion in Mice Lung Cancer Milieu.

We then investigated the effects of ALDH9A1 on the TIME using cytometry by time of flight (CyTOF) on lung tumor tissues from Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− KP mice (Fig. 4A and SI Appendix, Table S8). CyTOF profiling revealed distinct immune cell clusters characterized by discrete median-scaled expression patterns of lineage-defining and functional markers (Fig. 4B). These signatures allowed clear discrimination of major myeloid (Ly6G+ neutrophils, Ly6C+ monocytes/MDSCs, CD11c+ dendritic cells) and lymphoid subsets (γδ T/ILC populations, CD4+/CD8+ T cells, B cells, and NK cells).

Compared to the Aldh9a1+/+ group, the TIME of Aldh9a1−/− mice exhibited a reduction in tumor-promoting myeloid cell populations, including monocytic(M)-MDSCs and polymorphonuclear (PMN)-MDSCs (CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6Clo) (Fig. 4 C and D). Simultaneously, as shown in Fig. 4E, the enrichment of anticancer T lymphocytes was markedly increased in the Aldh9a1−/− group. Given that MDSCs are a major immunosuppressive “brake” on CD8+ T cells against NSCLCs and promote tumor progression (17, 18), we performed single cell RNA seq analysis of tumor immune cells from tumor regions of Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− KP mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Pseudotime trajectory analysis of MDSCs indicated that M-MDSC and PNM-MDSC differentiate from a common precursor and then bifurcate into either cell types (Fig. 4 F and G). Gene changes as MDSCs developed were divided into three groups associated with regulation of anti-inflammatory response, endocytosis, and chemotaxis, respectively (Fig. 4 H and I).

Next, we explored the relationship between MDSCs and T cells in this murine cancer model. CellChat-based computational cell–cell communication inference revealed that both M-MDSCs and PMN-MDSCs primarily communicated with CD8+ T cells, and they also showed strong communication with each other (Fig. 4 J and K and SI Appendix, Table S9). Among all the interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), we identified coinhibitory signaling interactions such as CD80-CTLA4 and NECTIN2-TIGIT between PMN-MDSCs and CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4L). We also found an IL-1β signaling interaction between PMN-MDSCs and M-MDSCs (Fig. 4M). Furthermore, CD80 and NECTIN2 were highly expressed in PMN-MDSCs (Fig. 4 N–Q) and are closely linked to the immune suppression of CD8+ T cells, warranting deeper scrutiny. Thus, our data emphasize that ALDH9A1-mediated MDSCs induce CD8+ T cell exhaustion in lung cancer TIME through cytokine paracrine action.

ALDH9A1 Inhibitor Reverses MDSC-Driven CD8+ T Cell Exhaustion and Elevates the Efficacy of Immunotherapy Against Lung Cancers.

Next, Diethylaminobenzaldehyde (DEAB), a well-known inhibitor of ALDH9A1 (11), was used to treat a syngeneic LLC1 lung tumor model with ALDH9A1 upregulation. DEAB not only significantly inhibited the development of lung tumors but also protected against MDSC-mediated blockade of CD8+ T cell proliferation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–C). We observed increased numbers of activated CD69+CD44+CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues from DEAB-treated mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). DEAB also increased antitumor genes (Ifng, Gzma, and Gzmb) but reduced the levels of exhaustion-associated markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E and F). However, this phenotype did not happen in Rag1−/− immunodeficient mice, highlighting an indispensable and crucial role of CD8+ T cells in DEAB’s antineoplastic action (SI Appendix, Fig. S5G).

Moreover, anti-Gr1 antibody-mediated loss of MDSC-like cells significantly inhibited tumor growth in vehicle-treated models but not in DEAB-treated counterparts (SI Appendix, Fig. S5H). Either DEAB or anti-Gr1 antibody treatment expanded recently primed CD69+CD44+CD8+ T cells in TIME while coadministration of DEAB with anti-Gr1 antibody failed to upregulate these cancer-reactive CD8+ T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5I), showing that DEAB converts MDSCs into T cell–stimulating cells. We also wondered whether the synergy between DEAB and ICIs was possible. DEAB was administered to LLC1-bearing mice alone or in combination with anti-PD-1 antibody. A stronger antitumor effect was observed in the combined treatment group compared with either treatment alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S5J). Notably, in OVA+ adoptive T cell transfer (ACT) LLC1 tumor models, ACT combined with DEAB promoted superior cancer control and rapid enrichment of IFNγ+ CD8+ T cells, a phenomenon not observed with either treatment alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 K and L). To confirm the specificity of DEAB effect in potentiating anti-PD-1 therapy, we utilized a nanoparticle-based small interfering RNA (siRNA) approach to target ALDH9A1. Pyrimidine bases (C/U) in siRNAs were modified with 2′-O-methylation to ensure RNA stability (19). Nanoparticle-siNC or nanoparticle-siALDH9A1 was administered to LLC1-bearing mice alone or in combination with anti-PD-1 antibody. This independent genetic silencing recapitulated the phenotype as DEAB treatment, as a stronger antitumor effect was observed in the combined treatment group compared with either treatment alone (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Our data indicate that DEAB enhances immunotherapy efficacy by transforming MDSCs into tumor-reactive T cell–stimulating cells in lung cancers.

Spatial Proteomic Mapping Reveals DEAB-Induced Remodeling of The NSCLC TIME.

To characterize how pathway inhibition reshapes TIME, we performed high-dimensional spatial proteomics using a 31-marker CODEX panel covering tumor, stromal, immune, and functional states (Fig. 5 A and B). Histopathological annotation coupled with multiplexed protein imaging enabled us to quantify cellular composition, spatial relationships, and functional states across matched vehicle- and DEAB-treated NSCLC lesions. CODEX imaging demonstrated a marked reduction in tumor epithelial regions in DEAB-treated samples, accompanied by expansion of immune-rich niches (Fig. 5C). In vehicle-treated tumors, CD8+ T cells displayed a dominant PD-1+ exhausted (Tex) phenotype. DEAB treatment markedly decreased PD-1 expression, indicating a shift toward effector and cytotoxic activation (Fig. 5D). Stromal compartment markers, including SMA and Podoplanin, displayed distinct spatial organization patterns after DEAP treatment (Fig. 5E). Unsupervised clustering followed by cell-type annotation identified major stromal, epithelial, and immune lineages across all samples (Fig. 5 F–H). DEAB treatment markedly enhanced the percentage of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, dendritic cells, NK cells, and macrophages, while reducing tumor epithelial cells and MDSCs (Fig. 5G). These findings indicate a global transition from an immunosuppressive to a more immune-permissive TIME.

To dissect CD8 compartment heterogeneity, we performed high-resolution clustering of all CD8 T cells. UMAP projection revealed five major states: Tmem (Memory T cell), Teff (Effector T Cell), Terminal effector, and Tex (Fig. 5I). Vehicle-treated tumors were dominated by Tex, whereas inhibitor treatment expanded Tmem and effector-like subsets, coupled with a striking reduction in exhausted states (Fig. 5 J and K). Differential expression analysis confirmed upregulation of GZMB, IFNG, and CD44 and reduction of PD-1 and TOX in DEAB-treated effector populations (Fig. 5J). Collectively, spatial proteomics reveals that DEAB treatment reduces tumor mass, diminishes suppressive myeloid populations, and restores CD8+ T cell effector function via spatially coordinated remodeling of TIME.

ALDH9A1-Enhanced IL-1β Signaling Dictates TIME Reprogramming in Lung Cancers.

Since our previous RNA sequencing data from KP tumor immune cells identified altered inflammatory pathways (Fig. 3I), we sought to clarify the key downstream effector of ALDH9A1 in TIME reprogramming by focusing on possible alteration in chemokines and cytokines. We performed qRT-PCR of these targets identified IL-1β as the top hit (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Cell–cell communication assays from single cell RNA sequencing result (Fig. 4J) also revealed one strong IL-1β-IL-1R1 signaling interaction between lung tumor cells and MDSCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C). To validate whether IL-1β contributes to the effect of ALDH9A1 on TIME, an in vitro MDSC suppression experiment was performed by coculturing MDSCs isolated from lung cancer tissue with CD8+ T cells. MDSCs from mice bearing Aldh9a1−/− LLC1 cells lost the ability to inhibit CD8+ T cell proliferation, while the introduction of recombinant IL-1β (rIL-1β) regained the inhibitory capacity of MDSCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D). Transwell migration assays indicated that Aldh9a1 transfection in A549 cells markedly promoted MDSC chemotaxis, whereas anti-IL-1β reversed this phenotype (SI Appendix, Fig. S7E). Conversely, Aldh9a1 deficiency in LLC1 cells decreased the enrichment of MDSCs, which was reversed by rIL-1β treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7F). These data suggest that ALDH9A1 promotes the infiltration of MDSCs into lung cancer tissues through the IL-1β–IL-1R1 axis.

In vivo, a TAM-inducible Aldh9a1 transgenic (TG) mouse model was generated (SI Appendix, Fig. S7G). Compared to control mice, Aldh9a1-TG mice exhibited a marked increase in tumor weight while this phenotype was reversed by anti-IL-1β (SI Appendix, Fig. S7H). As expected, Aldh9a1-TG mice showed reduced survival compared to controls, while anti-IL-1β treatment significantly improved survival (SI Appendix, Fig. S7I). Upregulation of IL1β was also observed in Aldh9a1-TG mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S7J). We also observed a significant MDSC enrichment and a marked decrease in CD8+ T cell populations in Aldh9a1-TG mice tumor, which were reversed by anti-IL-1β treatment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 K and L). Clinically, a high immune signature for ALDH9A1 and IL-1β levels correlated with poor survival in NSCLC patients (SI Appendix, Fig. S7M and SI Appendix, Table S10). We also validate our findings in human tumors by analyzing bulk RNA-seq data from the Stand Up To Cancer-Mark Foundation (SU2C-MARK) NSCLC cohort, comprising 152 RNA-seq specimens from patients treated with immune checkpoint blockade (20). ALDH9A1 expression showed only a modest, nonsignificant trend toward enrichment in nonresponders (SI Appendix, Fig. S8A). However, ALDH9A1 was significantly positively correlated with IL1B expression and an MDSC-like immunosuppressive myeloid signature in human NSCLC (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 B and C). Immune deconvolution analyses further demonstrated strong associations between ALDH9A1 and multiple myeloid cell populations, whereas correlations with lymphoid populations were limited (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 D and E). Collectively, these findings support an association between ALDH9A1 and a myeloid-dominant immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

Together, our data suggest that ALDH9A1 levels are closely associated with IL-1β-mediated MDSC infiltration into tumors, contributing to lung tumor progression.

ALDH9A1-Induced cis-Regulation Remodels Chromatin Accessibility and Activates the Il1b Superenhancers in NSCLCs.

To elucidate how ALDH9A1 drives the production of IL-1β in NSCLC, we first performed ATAC-seq with Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− lung tumor cells (Fig. 6A). Annotating peaks of accessibility to the nearest genes transcription start sites indicated decreased chromatin openness in Aldh9a1−/− cells (Fig. 6 A, Left-panel). Correspondingly, the heatmap of average ATAC-seq signals highlighted a more closed chromatin state (Fig. 6 A, Right-panel). Moreover, across all genomic elements, the distribution of the open peaks exhibited a decrease within 1 kb of the promoter regions in Aldh9a1−/− lung tumor cells (19.41%) when compared to Aldh9a1+/+ cells (26.87%) (Fig. 6B). A total of 2354 differential ATAC-seq peaks were identified in Aldh9a1−/− cells in comparison to Aldh9a1+/+ cells (Fig. 6C). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of these peaks elucidated marked remodeling of chemokine and immune signal pathways, supporting the role of ALDH9A1 in adjusting NSCLC TIME (Fig. 6D).

Given that murine Il1b locus was among the top hit, we integrated ATAC-seq and publicly available ChIP-seq data in bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), which identified seven distal regions of Il1b locus as SE (21). We found that in Aldh9a1−/− lung tumor cells, at least six distal regions of Il1b locus showed reduced signals compared to Aldh9a1+/+ lung tumor cells (Fig. 6E). This finding led us to hypothesize that ALDH9A1 controls Il1b level via cis-regulation. To investigate whether Il1b SE are controlled by ALDH9A1, we performed H3K27ac ChIP-seq with Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− lung cancer cells (Fig. 6F). As expected, the peak distribution analysis across different genomic locations revealed that majority of them fell into intergenic and intronic locations (Fig. 6G). We then performed superenhancer (SE) analysis to annotate SEs using H3K27ac signal (Fig. 6H), and identified reduced H3K27ac signal at peak 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 (Fig. 6I). We then conducted ChIP-qPCR against H3K27ac marks and confirmed this reduction (Fig. 6J). These findings demonstrate that ALDH9A1 drives the production of IL-1β via activating the Il1b SE in NSCLCs.

We also try to dissect whether ALDH9A1-driven carnitine metabolism increases acetyl-CoA availability by performing high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) (22) with Aldh9a1+/+ and Aldh9a1−/− lung tumor cells. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S9, a reduced acetyl-Co-A flux and histone acetylation was observed in Aldh9a1−/− lung tumor cells compared to Aldh9a1+/+, indicating ALDH9A1supports acetyl-CoA abundance and histone acetylation, and reshapes chromatin accessibility at Il1b superenhancer regions.

IL-1β or PD-1 Inhibitor Activates Binding of RelA to Aldh9a1 Promoter, Forming a Positive ALDH9A1/ IL-1β Circuit in NSCLC Cells.

To further elucidate the crosstalk between MDSCs and NSCLC cells, we investigated the effect of MDSC-associated cytokines on Aldh9a1 transcription in lung tumors. A549 cells were stimulated with IL-1β, CSF1, TNFα, or control medium, respectively. IL-1β, rather than the other cytokines, significantly activated Aldh9a1 transcription in A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10A). IL-1β-induced ALDH9A1 upregulation was time dependent (SI Appendix, Fig. S10B). Notably, a PD-1 inhibitor also upregulated ALDH9A1 levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C). We postulated that IL-1β and anti-PD-1 antibodies might activate a common transcription factor (TF) to enhance ALDH9A1 expression in A549 cells. RNA-seq and stable isotope labeling by amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) were then carried out to clarify this TF. IL-1β and anti-PD-1 were added separately to A549 cell cultures, and RNA-seq indicated 13 deregulated genes, with NF-κB/RelA identified as the top TF candidate (SI Appendix, Fig. S10D). GSEA showed that RelA-induced transcripts were enriched in IL-1β-treated A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10E). However, IL-1β failed to activate RelA or induce ALDH9A1 expression in RelA-deficient A549 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S10F), confirming that NF-κB/RelA is required for ALDH9A1 transcription in response to IL-1β and anti-PD-1.

ENCODE ChIP-seq data identified five potential RelA binding sites at the promoter of ALDH9A1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S10G). ChIP experiments indicated that only BS4 is a major binding site for RelA in the ALDH9A1 promoter (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 H and I), indicating that ALDH9A1 is a previously unidentified gene responsive to both IL-1β and anti-PD-1. RelA activation contributes to this regulation, establishing an ALDH9A1-IL-1β feedback loop (SI Appendix, Fig. S10J).

Discussion

A major barrier to effective immunotherapy in NSCLC is the accumulation of immunosuppressive MDSCs, which establishes a treatment-protective niche within the TIME (23, 24). Our study identifies ALDH9A1 as a critical regulator of MDSC recruitment and function. ALDH9A1-expressing NSCLC cells elevate IL-1β production, which acts through IL-1R1 on MDSCs to promote their infiltration and immunosuppressive polarization. In turn, IL-1β activates NF-κB to further induce ALDH9A1, forming a feed-forward ALDH9A1-IL-1β circuit that sustains immunosuppression and resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy. Disrupting this loop, via ALDH9A1 genetic deletion, pharmacological inhibition, or IL-1β/IL-1R1 blockade, reduces MDSC accumulation, alleviates CD8+ T cell exhaustion, enhances TLS maturation, and prolongs survival (25). Although KP and LLC murine models do not fully capture the complexity and heterogeneity of human NSCLC immunobiology or clinical ICB responsiveness, the consistency between our mechanistic mouse studies and expanded human single-cell, spatial CODEX, and clinical datasets supports a conserved association between the ALDH9A1/IL-1β axis and immunosuppressive remodeling in NSCLC.

Distinguishing PMN-MDSCs from activated neutrophil states in human tumors remains an evolving challenge due to overlapping transcriptional and phenotypic features (26). Therefore, the granulocytic populations identified in this study likely represent a spectrum of immunosuppressive myeloid states within the NSCLC microenvironment.

Multiomics analysis reveals that ALDH9A1-driven carnitine metabolism increases acetyl-CoA availability and reshapes chromatin accessibility at Il1b superenhancer regions, thereby linking metabolic reprogramming with inflammatory cytokine regulation. These findings highlight a broader principle whereby aberrant carnitine metabolism orchestrates immune suppression and resistance to therapy. Although other ALDH family members are implicated in cancer, ALDH9A1 is uniquely positioned to influence carnitine biosynthesis (27, 28), suggesting a tumor type-specific and metabolic-context-dependent role. Our preliminary observations further suggest that ALDH9A1 may also modulate B cell differentiation, raising the possibility that ALDH9A1-driven carnitine dysregulation affects multiple immune compartments.

We additionally observed sex-dependent differences in the survival benefits conferred by Il1b deletion or IL-1β blockade, consistent with known sex-specific differences in carnitine metabolism, IL-1β biology, and immune regulation (29–34). Further studies are warranted to delineate how sex hormones, metabolic states, and immune responses shape ALDH9A1’s role in NSCLC.

In summary, our work defines an ALDH9A1–carnitine–Il1b axis as a key regulatory pathway of NSCLC immune evasion and demonstrates that targeting ALDH9A1 or IL-1β signaling can reprogram the TIME and restore sensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy. These insights nominate ALDH9A1 as a promising therapeutic target and provide a rationale for developing ALDH9A1-directed interventions in immunotherapy-refractory NSCLC.

Materials and Methods

Tumor specimens were collected from patients with NSCLC prior to ICI therapy at Ruijin Hospital after informed consent and institutional review board approval. Clinical responses were assessed according to RECIST v1.1 criteria, and patients were classified as responders or nonresponders.

Single-cell RNA sequencing was performed on dissociated tumor samples. After quality control filtering, transcriptomic profiles were analyzed using Seurat for dimensionality reduction, clustering, and cell-type annotation based on canonical lineage markers. Tumor and immune cell populations were further subclustered for downstream analyses. Differential expression between responders and nonresponders was evaluated using pseudobulk DESeq2 models. Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler.

To characterize spatial immune organization, multiplex CODEX imaging was performed on tumor tissues using a 35-antibody panel of immune, stromal, and tumor markers (35). Tumor and immune cell populations were further subclustered for downstream analyses. Differential expression between responders and nonresponders was evaluated using pseudobulk DESeq2 models. Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analyses were performed using clusterProfiler.

To characterize spatial immune organization, multiplex CODEX imaging was performed on tumor tissues using a 35-antibody panel of immune, stromal, and tumor markers. Cell segmentation, phenotypic annotation, and neighborhood analyses were conducted using established image-processing pipelines. Additional immune profiling was performed using CyTOF.

Functional studies were performed using genetically engineered mouse models, syngeneic tumor transplantation models, adoptive T cell transfer, and nanoparticle-mediated ALDH9A1 silencing. Transcriptomic, epigenomic, and chromatin analyses were conducted using bulk RNA sequencing, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq. Cytokine production, immune suppression, migration, and signaling assays were evaluated by ELISA, flow cytometry, Western blotting, and in vitro functional assays.

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism and R. Unless otherwise indicated, comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed Student’s t test or Mann–Whitney U test. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in SI Appendix, Materials and Methods.
