Invisible pressure: a qualitative study of parental mobbing experienced by preschool teachers
Authors: Sevtap Samancı Bağcı, Ayşegül Ayyıldız Asil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to examine parental mobbing behaviors directed toward preschool teachers and to explore the effects of these behaviors on teachers. Employing a qualitative research method, the study analyzes the characteristics of parents who engage in mobbing, the definitions, the causes, the impacts, and coping strategies related to mobbing based on teachers’ lived experiences. The study group consisted of 17 female preschool teachers. The findings indicate that teachers who were exposed to mobbing were predominantly early-career or moderately experienced professionals. According to participants’ accounts, mobbing behaviors were more frequently exhibited by female parents. In addition, parents with both low and high educational levels were prone to mobbing, driven by distinct contextual motivations, those with a small number of children, high-income parents were found to be more prone to engaging in mobbing behaviors toward teachers. Teachers identified several key factors contributing to mobbing, including difficulties in setting boundaries with parents, lack of administrative support. Mobbing behaviors reported by teachers included being perceived as caregiver, interference in classroom practices, phone harassment, verbal abuse, and threatening language. The most commonly reported effects of mobbing on teachers were fear of job loss, obsessive thoughts, and increased stress levels. Regarding coping strategies, seeking mediation from school administrators was the most frequently used approach, followed by face-to-face communication. Other strategies included changing communication styles and setting clearer boundaries. Overall, parental mobbing experiences create significant individual and institutional consequences, negatively affecting teachers’ professional motivation and overall quality of life. The findings highlight the urgent need for the development of policies and practices aimed at preventing mobbing in educational settings and strengthening support mechanisms for teachers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04605-3.Keywords: Mobbing, Parent, Preschool teacher

Introduction

The term mobbing, originally used in the 19th century in English to describe the behavior of birds flying around their nests to drive away the aggressor, is now used to describe psychological violence in the workplace. It was developed in the 1960s by Konrad Lorenz to describe the protective behavior of animals against a threat or stranger. Lorenz used the term to describe the attacks of geese aimed at frightening a fox [16]. In humans, mobbing first emerged in the 1970s in a study conducted on students, and it was found to cause psychological and physiological distress by damaging self-confidence [60].

In its dictionary definition, mobbing means encirclement, collective attack, and harassment [71]. Mobbing is a set of disturbing, unpleasant, and humiliating behaviors carried out continuously and systematically by individuals in equal positions, superiors, or subordinates towards an individual [22]. Zapf [75], defines mobbing as a form of long-term social stress, noting that it is usually perpetrated by a single individual and deepened by a group supporting the mobbing. It is stated that the psychological and emotional harm experienced by teachers in educational institutions has negative consequences at both individual and institutional levels, reducing teacher performance while also affecting students and parents [17, 65]. In this context, the direction of mobbing behaviors in the education sector can manifest in different ways. Mobbing is a situation that everyone is at risk of experiencing. Research shows that both weaker and less capable individuals, as well as reliable, creative, and successful people, can be victims of mobbing [52]. While there has been an increase in research on mobbing in Türkiye in recent years, most of these studies focus on general education levels, and data on preschool teachers remains quite limited [35].

The first six years of a child’s life are a critical period in which they develop learning skills through interaction with their environment and observation, and the foundations of their basic education are laid [46]. Preschool education is a process that forms the cornerstones of children’s social, emotional, cognitive, and physical development. During this period, it is of great importance for teachers to provide a healthy learning environment for their students and to support them not only academically but also psychologically. Understanding the effects of mobbing originating from parents is considered critical for the success of the preschool education system. This study aims to understand the mobbing behaviors of preschool teachers originating from parents and the effects of these behaviors on teachers. Investigating this issue will draw attention to the mobbing behaviors from parents that teachers encounter and will allow for the development of various solutions. It is believed that this research, by revealing the causes and effects of mobbing and shedding light on the problems faced by preschool teachers, will be an important guide for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers, and thus will make it possible for teachers to have a healthier, more supportive, and more productive working environment.

Literature review

Mobbing

Mobbing is a workplace disorder whose existence was largely unknown until the 1970s. Initially used to describe aggressive behavior exhibited by groups of people exhibiting animal-like behaviors, the concept of mobbing later came to be used to describe hostile behavior between individuals in the workplace [41]. Mobbing is derived from the Latin phrase “mobile vulgus” (unstable crowd) [37]. The English verb “mob” means to gather, attack, or harass [16]. In the literature, it is expressed with various terms such as “violence,” “psycho-terror,” “emotional abuse,” and “workplace aggression” [44]. Mobbing is a concept expressed with various terms in different countries and languages. In English-speaking countries, this phenomenon is generally described as “mobbing” or “bullying” while in Europe, North America, and Australia it is called “mobbing” and in Canada it is referred to as “bullying” [74]. Although definitions vary from country to country, in an agreement reached in 2007, European Union countries defined mobbing as continuous and intentional threats, harassment, and humiliating behaviors directed at employees [24].

The key feature that distinguishes mobbing from ordinary daily disagreements and conflicts is the continuous repetition of these behaviors. It is stated that mobbing behaviors must occur at least once a week and last for six months [48]. Mobbing is a continuous, focused, and damaging form of communication applied by powerful individuals to weaker ones in the workplace [58]. Although not as direct as aggression, mobbing is usually verbal, passive, and ambiguous [6]. The definition of mobbing is evaluated based on the experiences of the victims and the effects such as the health problems they suffer [79]. Moayed et al. [47], defined mobbing in more detail, stating that it is the repeated negative behavior exhibited by one person or group towards another person or group when disagreements and conflicts in the workplace cannot be resolved through normal means. Mobbing is defined as emotional pressure and psychological terrorism, preventing individuals from fulfilling their work duties and negatively affecting their social identities [20].

Mobbing process

German and Swedish occupational psychologists have developed various models to understand this process. The most well-known of these models is Leymann’s five-stage model [67]. According to Leymann, mobbing is a five-stage process that leaves harmful effects on both the physical and psychological health of the individual. However, individuals do not have to experience all stages; the severity of mobbing behaviors tends to increase as the process progresses [41]. Leymann’s mobbing model is considered suitable for Scandinavian countries, but insufficient for Mediterranean countries. Therefore, Harold Ege developed the “Italian-Aegean Model” specific to Mediterranean cultures, as an alternative to Leymann’s five-stage mobbing model [1]. This model consists of six stages, as seen below [15]:

Parents engaged in mobbing behaviors toward teachers

The International Labour Organization (ILO), in a report published in 1998, emphasized that teachers are in a high-risk group for mobbing [78]. Parents and teachers reported that they approach each other with certain preconceptions [55]. Teachers feel uncomfortable when their professional competencies are questioned by parents, while parents experience fear of being held responsible by teachers for their children’s academic failures [11]. These disagreements between teachers and parents may result in parents engaging in mobbing behaviors toward classroom teachers [69]. Findings from Özge Sağbaş and Özkan [54] reveal that conflicts teachers experience with parents lead to decreased motivation in their lessons, difficulty in conducting classes, and problems with concentration. Disagreements between teachers and parents stem from differences in educational goals and child-centered perspectives; teachers tend to prioritize the class as a whole, while parents tend to prioritize their own children [3]. Martino et al. [19], listed the most common mobbing behaviors encountered by teachers as follows: being ignored, physical violence, abuse, being constantly the subject of gossip, constant criticism of their efforts and services, and being assigned tasks below their capacity. The main causes of mobbing are the hierarchical and bureaucratic management style in schools and the ineffective behavior of educational leaders; furthermore, the ineffectiveness of unions hinders teachers’ pursuit of their rights. Preventing mobbing requires new ethical codes in the short term and changes in management style and culture in the long term [17]. A recent study in the United States conducted by McMahon et al. [42], with a sample of 11,814 teachers across all 50 states, found that 63% of teachers reported experiencing verbal and threatening behaviors from parents, while 26% reported being subjected to physical violence by parents over the past year.

According to Babaoğlan and Turap [4], the prominent mobbing behaviors of parents include disturbing by phone calls, being disturbing by text messages, threatening, complaining about teacher, slandering, blaming, gossiping about teacher, questioning teaching of teacher and sabotaging lessons. The causes of mobbing were examined as organizational and personal factors. Teachers reported that parents who have communication problems, are gossipy, selfish, or have psychological issues, as well as students with anger issues, low academic performance, or psychological problems, contribute to mobbing. According to teachers, the main reason for mobbing is trying to do their job correctly. The most prominent organizational outcomes of mobbing were identified as loss of motivation and unwillingness to go to school. The consequences of mobbing on teachers included family unrest, anger problems, and physical fatigue. Teachers reported coping with mobbing through strategies such as trying to be strong, seeking support from the Ministry of National Education, obtaining union or legal support, receiving support from colleagues, and seeking guidance services. The findings of Berkowitz et al. [5], indicate that parents’ violence toward teachers manifests in various forms, most commonly rudeness, shouting, intimidation, and verbal threats. These behaviors create complex challenges that affect teachers both personally and professionally, requiring coping mechanisms at societal, school, community, and individual levels. Effective strategies include improving the social and organizational climate in schools, providing mentoring and support, offering conflict management training for educators, and implementing comprehensive family–school partnership policies at the school level. According to Şenaras and Çetin [63], teachers are most frequently subjected to pressure from parents regarding grades, assignments, and in-class issues, whereas school principals face pressure from parents related to teacher assignments, enrolling students in specific classes, or changing classes or teachers. Teachers reported that, as a result of these pressures, they may experience emotional and psychological difficulties and that the pressure often affects their personal lives.

Causes of mobbing

To fully uncover the true causes of mobbing, it is necessary to consider different dimensions together. To reach the real causes, many factors such as the organization, the victim, and the perpetrator must be examined simultaneously. However, methodological difficulties hinder empirical research [75]. Mobbing is also linked to societal and social conditions [25]. For example, a negative organizational climate can cause mobbing, and mobbing can also negatively affect the organizational climate. In her research on the causes of mobbing, Vartia [70] found that factors such as organizational structure, job characteristics, leadership style, organizational culture, and group processes are related to mobbing. Crawford [14], states that organizations with rigid hierarchies have a culture that encourages mobbing behaviors; in such organizations, only obedience is expected from employees, and insincere relationships create fertile ground for mobbing. Einarsen and Skogstadd [23] state that mobbing incidents occur more frequently in large organizations than in smaller ones. It has also been noted that mobbing behaviors are more common in organizations where work is poorly organized and job satisfaction is low (Cassitto et al., 2003). Managers influence employees within the scope of the authority given to them, but they cannot use this authority arbitrarily; they must act in accordance with established rules [34]. The structure of organizational management can significantly affect the organizational climate. A negative climate can cause employees to be disadvantaged and exposed to mobbing behaviors [21]. Individuals who interact with a culture of marginalization and violence may reflect these experiences in the organizational environment, thus preparing the ground for mobbing behaviors in the social structure [30].

Consequences of mobbing

Lamontange [38] states that mobbing has emotional and cognitive effects on individuals. Emotionally, it leads to anger, sadness, and disappointment, discouraging the victim, while causing them to feel inadequate in learning processes, making them hesitant to ask questions and speak. This situation can negatively affect self-confidence and personal development. 41% of mobbing victims experience depression, while 31% of women and 21% of men are diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder, rendering them unable to work again [49]. Mobbing behavior leads to frequent illness and absenteeism for employees exposed to mobbing. This creates economic problems for the organization by requiring temporary workforce [26]. In a study by Mikkelsen and Einarsen [45], 27% of those exposed to mobbing experienced psychological health complaints, and 10% experienced psychosomatic health complaints. Victims may experience mental health problems such as depression and panic attacks, and may even be at risk of suicide attempts. Therefore, it is recommended that victims seek clinical help in cases where mobbing cannot be prevented [28].

Strategies to cope with mobbing

The intense competition in educational institutions, the misguided leadership perceptions of administrators, and ineffective management practices are also significant factors in the intimidation of teachers [7]. Since mobbing in institutions leads to many problems for individuals, combating mobbing has become necessary. The reason why it is difficult for victims to fight mobbing is that definitive evidence of mobbing is rare and difficult to prove [31]. Since the psychological tension created by mobbing in working life creates significant costs for individuals, institutions, and society, combating this problem is essential. To eliminate mobbing, the causes of these behaviors must first be determined, and accurate analysis and diagnosis must be made. Afterwards, administrators should take appropriate measures to transform workplaces into social structures that provide satisfaction and commitment [43].

To cope with mobbing and prevent its spread, administrators must first define mobbing and be aware of the factors that can lead to it. Being aware of these factors is the first step for a good administrator to cope with mobbing. Managers and team leaders should take precautions against conflicts that may arise within the organization and prevent problems before they escalate [27]. To prevent mobbing, managers and employees should be informed about this issue. Establishing ethical rules and supporting behavior that conforms to them, trusting in professionalism, providing a tolerant environment, and deterring inappropriate behavior can be effective solutions [12].

Contribution of this study

A review of the literature reveals that Branch et al. [9], and Branch [8], addressing the vertical and upward forms of mobbing, demonstrate that managers can be both perpetrators and victims, and that a lack of managerial support, in particular, deepens the victimization; while Chyra and Czerkawski [13] show that prospective teachers are frequently exposed to mobbing behaviors such as questioning their professional competence, excessive workload, and criticism. [77] study reveals that mobbing affects not only victims but also witnesses with high stress and health problems; while Leymann [41] emphasizes that mobbing is mostly perpetrated by more than one person and is particularly concentrated in public sectors such as education. Hubert and Veldhoven [32], state that the prevalence of mobbing in the education sector is remarkably high, while Dick and Wagner [18] and Lewis [40] reveal that teachers and academics experience exclusion, shame, silence, and organizational withdrawal as a result of mobbing. Nanto [50], and Shallcross [61] state that control, neglect, and a dysfunctional organizational culture are among the main causes of mobbing; while Salin (2003) has shown that mobbing has multifaceted negative effects on job satisfaction, commitment, and health. Rosander and Nielsen [56], and Rossiter and Sochos [57] emphasize that mobbing has damaging effects not only on victims but also on witnesses and the organizational climate. While Thomas [66] and Tomic [68] detail the physical, psychological, and professional consequences of mobbing, all of these studies have primarily focused on mobbing through internal workplace actors (managers, colleagues, organizational structure). In contrast, studies examining teachers’ experiences of parent-induced mobbing, which directly affects their professional roles but originates from outside the workplace, particularly in the context of preschool education and through a qualitative approach based on teachers’ experiences, are extremely limited. This study contributes significantly to the literature on mobbing by filling a crucial gap, making visible the use of power by parents over teachers, and highlighting the need to address mobbing as a structural problem in educational settings. Method

This research employed a qualitative research method. Qualitative research is a type of inquiry that employs methods such as observation, interviews, and document analysis to address a research problem [59]. In this study, an exploratory qualitative research design was adopted due to the limited previous research on parental mobbing. Exploratory research refers to research that seeks to gain initial insights into a topic that has not been studied in depth, allowing researchers to understand the nature of a phenomenon and identify new aspects or patterns through flexible, open-ended qualitative methods [62].

Study group

The participants in this study consisted of 17 preschool teachers who were victims of mobbing and working in state kindergartens in Hakkari province as of the 2024–2025 academic year. The sample of the research was determined using the convenience sampling method. In this method, the researcher can prefer participants who are readily accessible and in their immediate environment to make the data collection process more practical [73].

As shown in Table 1 all teachers participating in the study (100%) were women. Regarding age distribution, 41.2% were 30 years old or younger, while 58.8% were 31 years old or older. The vast majority of participants (94.1%) held a bachelor’s degree, with only one participant (5.9%) having a master’s degree. This finding indicates that a bachelor’s degree is considered the standard level of education in the institutions where the research was conducted. When examining the distribution of teachers according to their children groups served, it was determined that 11.8% worked with 3-year-olds, 35.3% with 4-year-olds, 35.3% with 5-year-olds, and 17.6% with mixed 4–5 year-old groups. In terms of professional experience, 52.9% of participants had 1–5 years of experience, 41.2% had 6–10 years, and 5.9% had 11 years or more.

Data collection

Within the scope of the research, data were collected through a semi-structured Interview Form on Mobbing Behaviors Experienced by Preschool Teachers from Parents. The development process of the interview form was carried out systematically. First, a conceptual framework was established through a review of the literature on workplace mobbing, teacher victimization, and parent–teacher conflict. Based on this framework and the research questions, five main themes were identified: (1) characteristics of parents who engage in mobbing, (2) types of mobbing behaviors, (3) reasons for mobbing, (4) effects of mobbing, and (5) coping strategies. These themes were selected in alignment with both the key concepts highlighted in the literature and the aims of the study. The identified themes were grounded in both commonly examined dimensions in the mobbing literature and the objectives and research questions of the study. Mobbing is not limited to specific behaviors; rather, it is addressed holistically in terms of its sources, forms, consequences, and coping processes. Accordingly, the theme of “characteristics of parents” aims to understand the individual and socio-demographic factors that may contribute to mobbing behaviors. The theme of “mobbing behaviors” seeks to reveal the types and nature of actions experienced by teachers, while the theme of “reasons for mobbing” focuses on explaining the personal and institutional factors underlying these behaviors. The theme of “effects of mobbing” aims to identify the psychological, emotional, physical, and professional consequences for teachers. Finally, the theme of “coping strategies” is intended to understand how teachers deal with these negative experiences and what strategies they develop. This thematic structure enables a multidimensional examination of the mobbing phenomenon and contributes to a comprehensive analysis of the research topic.

Following the identification of the themes, open-ended questions were developed for each theme to elicit participants’ experiences in depth. The initial draft of the interview form was reviewed by two experts in educational sciences and qualitative research. The experts evaluated the form in terms of content validity, clarity, and alignment with the research questions. Based on their feedback, minor revisions were made to improve the wording and clarity of several questions. A pilot study was conducted with two preschool teachers to test the applicability of the interview form. Based on the pilot results, the clarity of the questions and the flow of the interview were reviewed, and minor adjustments were made. The teachers who participated in the pilot study were not included in the main sample or in the data analysis in order to ensure methodological consistency. The interview form consisted of two sections. The first section included demographic information about the participants, while the second section contained open-ended questions related to the research themes. The interview form was developed specifically for this study and has not been used in any previous research; it is provided as a supplementary file. Data collection process was carried out through face-to-face interviews with preschool teachers who reported experiencing mobbing by parents. With the participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded.

Regarding reliability and validity, the criteria proposed by [76] were taken into consideration. Guba and Lincoln conceptualized trustworthiness under four dimensions: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability.

In this study, credibility was ensured through participant confirmation (member checking) and expert review. During the data collection process, participants were informed in detail about the purpose and scope of the study, and efforts were made to ensure that they expressed their experiences of mobbing openly and accurately. In addition, the interview form was reviewed by field experts, and necessary revisions were made based on their feedback.

Dependability was established by providing a clear and systematic description of the research process, including data collection and data analysis procedures. The steps followed during the content analysis (coding, categorization, and theme development) were explicitly defined to ensure transparency and consistency.

Confirmability was ensured by grounding the findings in participants’ statements and supporting them with direct quotations. In the context of this study, teachers’ experiences of parental mobbing were presented as expressed by the participants, minimizing researcher bias and ensuring that interpretations were based on actual data.

Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the research context, participant characteristics, and the nature of mobbing behaviors experienced by preschool teachers. This allows readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar educational settings.

Furthermore, to enhance the overall reliability of the study, the purpose and method of the research were clearly explained to the participants, and the data were analyzed in a systematic and consistent manner [72].

The researchers acknowledged their prior experiences as teachers and consciously set aside personal assumptions through bracketing, focusing on participants’ own descriptions of parental behaviors. Bracketing was applied during coding and theme development to ensure that the analysis reflected teachers’ perspectives rather than researchers’ preconceptions.

Discussion

According to the research findings, female parents are the most likely to engage in mobbing against teachers. Interestingly, parents with both low and high levels of education were observed to engage in such behaviors, indicating that mobbing is not limited to a single educational group. Parents with lower education levels may struggle to understand school processes and teachers’ decisions due to limited knowledge and experience. In contrast, highly educated parents, being more aware of their rights, may engage in intrusive monitoring of teachers to ensure their child’s academic and social development. Higher-income parents were also reported to engage in mobbing more frequently. Teachers noted that the majority of parents exhibiting mobbing behaviors were housewives, followed by women employed as civil servants. The findings further indicated that some parents leveraged their spouse’s professional status to exert pressure on teachers. Additionally, parents with fewer children were more likely to engage in mobbing behaviors. These patterns can be explained by several socio-psychological and contextual factors. Female parents, particularly housewives, may have more time for daily interactions with the school, creating opportunities for mobbing behaviors to emerge. High-income parents or those with professional connections may feel empowered to exert influence over teachers, sometimes perceiving their social or professional status as granting authority in the school context. Parents with fewer children may focus more intensively on the education of their single child, resulting in increased oversight and pressure on teachers. Overall, these findings suggest that parental mobbing behaviors are influenced not only by individual and socio-economic characteristics but also by perceived power dynamics and the opportunities for involvement provided by the rural school environment. Leymann’s [41] study found that 45% of male employees and 55% of female employees experienced mobbing. 76% of men and 3% of women engage in mobbing by individuals of the same gender. Rossiter and Sochos [57], in a study examining the relationship between workplace mobbing behaviors and employee burnout levels, determined that high levels of emotional support were associated with mobbing. Research shows that young people are more resilient to mobbing, women are more exposed to mobbing than men, and teachers are more often victims of mobbing compared to other professional groups.

In teachers’ perceptions of behaviors they define as mobbing, the most prominent codes relate to the damage of professional reputation, such as being perceived as merely a caregiver or receiving personalized criticism. This suggests that parental actions are not only evaluated in terms of overt hostility but also in terms of their impact on the teacher’s professional identity. Teaching involves a significant amount of emotional and care labor, as teachers routinely manage their own emotions while responding to students’ and parents’ emotional needs. This emotional labor is associated with both the relational aspects of the job and the emotional demands placed on educators [33]. Such behaviors may undermine teachers’ authority in the classroom and contribute to a sense of professional insecurity, aligning with Leymann’s [41] definition of mobbing as systematic social and psychological harassment aimed at an individual’s professional standing. Regarding psychological and social pressure, teachers reported being more frequently pressured through the administration. This indicates that the school’s institutional structure may inadvertently enable parental mobbing behaviors, either by inaction or by inconsistent responses to complaints. In terms of interference in private space, parents harassing teachers by phone exemplifies how seemingly ordinary parental involvement can escalate into intrusive and persistent behaviors, consistent with the literature on third-party harassment [17]. Intrusive and persistent questioning, reflecting the use of hierarchical power, was identified as the most common mobbing behavior from parents. This highlights how parents may leverage perceived social, professional, or institutional authority to assert control over the teacher, creating chronic stress and a sense of vulnerability. Similarly, unrealistic and coercive performance expectations, coded as external expectations, reveal how pressure framed as advocacy or concern can cross the line into mobbing when it becomes persistent and non-negotiable. Finally, in the context of communication and behavioral pressures, insulting and threatening speech was the most frequently reported behavior. This demonstrates that verbal aggression remains a key component of parental mobbing and underscores the psychological impact on teachers, who may experience heightened stress, anxiety, and feelings of powerlessness. Overall, these findings suggest that parental mobbing encompasses a combination of identity-threatening behaviors, coercive pressure, and verbal aggression, shaped both by the social power of parents and the institutional environment in which teachers operate. Chyra and Czerkawski [13], in their study conducted in Poland, examined mobbing behaviors towards 97 prospective teachers. According to the research results, the most common types of mobbing against teachers include impersonal insinuations, criticisms of duties and professional competence, excessive workload, and abandonment of assigned tasks. In Gülenç’s [29] study, it was determined that the most common mobbing behavior teachers are subjected to is “not taking their opinions and views into consideration”. In contrast, the least common negative behavior they encounter is “insults and humiliating statements about personality, attitude, or private life”. This finding shows that teachers mostly encounter exclusionary and dismissive behaviors; that ignoring their opinions, rather than direct insults, is a common form of mobbing.

When examining the underlying causes of parental mobbing, the primary factors identified were the abuse of good intentions by parents and teachers’ inability to set boundaries, categorized as personal factors. This suggests that teachers’ interpersonal skills and conflict management abilities may influence their vulnerability to mobbing. The second major category involved institutional and structural factors, particularly the administration leaving teachers unsupported. Teachers reported that administrators often adopted an overly permissive approach toward parents, failing to intervene when mobbing behaviors occurred. This aligns with prior research indicating that organizational inaction or lack of clear policies can exacerbate workplace bullying, as power imbalances remain unaddressed [53]. Additional contributing factors included parents leveraging their social or professional status and belittling the teaching profession. These behaviors reflect how external social hierarchies and societal perceptions of teaching as a low-status profession can embolden parents to exert undue pressure. When combined with insufficient administrative support, these factors create an environment in which teachers are more susceptible to repeated psychological and social harassment. Overall, these findings suggest that parental mobbing is multifactorial, arising from a combination of personal vulnerabilities, institutional deficiencies, and broader socio-cultural power dynamics. Addressing these causes requires both empowering teachers to establish boundaries and implementing structural protections to limit the misuse of parental authority. A study by Borg and Riding [7] identified factors such as the low status of the profession, managerial attitudes and behaviors, interpersonal relationships, role conflict, supervision, and communication styles as contributing factors to mobbing behaviors in educational organizations. Parents being obsessive, interfering, dissatisfied, and selfish are also among the other personal factors that contribute to mobbing. In Vartia’s (1996) study, a large majority of mobbing victims (63%) cited jealousy as the main cause of these behaviors. Zapf [75], also identified jealousy as a trigger for mobbing.

The findings indicate that teachers subjected to parental mobbing experience a wide range of psychological, emotional, physical, and professional effects, highlighting the pervasive impact of these behaviors. The most commonly reported psychological effect was obsessive thoughts, followed by stress, suggesting that repeated exposure to mobbing triggers cognitive rumination and sustained mental strain. Brewer and Whiteside [10], found in their research that experiencing or witnessing mobbing causes high levels of stress in individuals. Emotionally, teachers most frequently reported a shock response, followed by sadness and fear, with many experiencing frequent crying episodes. These reactions reflect the intense emotional burden of parental mobbing, which may erode teachers’ sense of safety, self-efficacy, and professional identity. The physical consequences, including weakness, appetite loss, sleep disturbances, stress-related illnesses (headaches, neck stiffness, hives, palpitations), and even premature birth, further underscore the somatic toll of chronic psychological stress induced by mobbing. Stress-related illnesses were particularly frequent or served as triggering factors, demonstrating the direct link between sustained psychological pressure and physical health outcomes. Khoo [36], researching the consequences of mobbing, stated that it often leads to health problems such as anxiety, depression, digestive problems, headaches, fatigue, insomnia, and dermatological skin problems. Alfano and Fraccaroli [2], found that approximately half of employees experiencing mobbing in the workplace exhibited symptoms of PTSD and that mobbing can lead to suicidal thoughts. The social repercussions were also significant, with many teachers reporting increased family problems. This indicates that the negative effects of parental mobbing extend beyond the workplace, affecting teachers’ personal lives and relationships. Professionally, mobbing led to tangible impacts on job motivation and engagement, with two teachers requesting reassignment and eleven reporting a loss of interest in their work. Research on mobbing shows that it leads to problems such as decreased effectiveness and productivity in organizations, job stress and dissatisfaction, reduced organizational commitment and trust, loss of motivation, absenteeism, and job turnover [51]. Overall, these results highlight that parental mobbing is not merely an episodic inconvenience but a chronic stressor with far-reaching psychological, emotional, physical, social, and professional consequences. According to a 2019 report by La Trobe University (VIC, Australia) [39], it was found that teacher targeted bullying and harassment by students and parents has detrimental effects on a teacher’s sense of self-efficacy and wellbeing and may contribute to an unsafe workplace. Addressing these effects requires both preventive strategies, such as institutional support and clear policies for managing parental aggression, and individual coping mechanisms, including counseling and boundary-setting training for teachers. Such interventions are critical to safeguard teachers’ well-being and maintain the quality of education in rural schools.

The research findings indicate that teachers develop multidimensional coping strategies in response to parental mobbing. These strategies can be categorized into individual solutions, the use of administrative and support mechanisms, resorting to formal procedures, passive coping strategies, and seeking alternative solutions. The most frequently used coping strategy was relying on the administration’s mediating role, which suggests that teachers require institutional support rather than relying solely on individual efforts in such situations. This finding highlights the critical role of school administration in mobbing processes and suggests that effective administrative intervention can be decisive in protecting teachers. The second most common strategy, face-to-face communication, indicates that teachers tend to address problems directly and manage conflicts actively. Similarly, strategies such as changing communication methods and setting boundaries demonstrate that teachers develop more proactive and protective approaches over time. On the other hand, the use of passive coping strategies, such as remaining silent, suggests that some teachers adopt a more passive stance due to power imbalances and a lack of institutional support. Resorting to formal procedures (such as objecting and defending oneself) and alternative solutions (such as requesting reassignment, self-improvement, and self-sacrifice) indicates that teachers seek solutions at both institutional and individual levels when faced with mobbing. However, strategies such as requesting reassignment point more toward withdrawal from the environment rather than resolving the issue, suggesting that the structural dimension of mobbing is not being adequately addressed. Overall, these findings demonstrate that teachers’ coping strategies in response to parental mobbing are shaped by institutional support, individual competencies, and perceived power dynamics. This implies that effective intervention requires not only strengthening individual coping strategies but also ensuring active involvement of school administrations, the development of clear policies, and the enhancement of support mechanisms for teachers.

Conclusion

When the overall results of the research are examined, it is seen that parent’s mobbing has multifaceted and profound psychological effects on preschool teachers. Mobbing behaviors are shaped not only by individual factors but also by institutional, societal, and cultural factors, negatively impacting teachers’ professional reputation, psychological health, and social lives. The fact that all participants were female teachers suggests that this group may be more vulnerable to mobbing. The fact that most participants were in the early stages of their professional lives indicates that inexperience and lack of institutional support may increase the risk of mobbing. Mobbing behaviors frequently manifest as interference with the teacher’s expertise, boundary violations, and disregard for professional value. This situation leads not only to teachers resorting to individual coping strategies but also to serious consequences such as reassignment or disillusionment with the profession. The research reveals that parent’s mobbing needs to be addressed systematically, teachers need to be supported, and school-family cooperation needs to be established on healthy foundations.

Limitations

Firstly, the study was conducted in a rural province of Türkiye with unique socio-cultural, economic, and geographical characteristics. Therefore, the findings may not fully reflect the experiences of preschool teachers working in metropolitan areas or regions with different socio-economic structures. Secondly, the research was conducted using a qualitative design with 17 female preschool teachers. The limited number of participants and the fact that all participants were female prevented a comparative examination of the potential effects of parental mobbing on teachers of different genders. Furthermore, the research is cross-sectional and therefore limited in its ability to reveal the long-term psychological and professional effects of parental mobbing on teachers. Finally, the study’s limitation to teachers working in state preschool institutions prevents inferences from being made about how parent-teacher relationships are shaped in the context of private schools.

Recommendations for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers

Recommendations for researchers

Similar studies can be conducted in different provinces and educational levels.

Research methods can be diversified, and research can be conducted with administrators, teachers, and students.

Recommendations for practitioners

The importance and limitations of school-teacher collaboration can be conveyed through information seminars for parents.

Ethical awareness-raising activities can be conducted regarding parent communication.

Psychological counseling and guidance services within schools can be strengthened to support teachers experiencing mobbing.

Recommendations for policymakers

Fair evaluation mechanisms can be developed to protect teachers’ right to defend themselves against parental complaints.

The Ministry of National Education and the Council of Higher Education can support academic studies on the causes, effects, and solutions of parental mobbing.

Safe and confidential application channels can be created for teachers to report their complaints.

Social awareness can be raised by giving coverage to the difficulties teachers face in this regard in the media.
