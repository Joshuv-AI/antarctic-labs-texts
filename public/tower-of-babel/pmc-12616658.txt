Sleep quality after caesarean delivery: associated factors and effects on quality of life and early breastfeeding
Authors: Thatchanan Jintadawong, Patchareya Nivatpumin, Manussawee Swaengrujitham, Wiruntri Punchuklang, Tripop Lertbunnaphong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe investigated the prevalence and associated factors of poor sleep quality after caesarean delivery on the first postoperative night. We also evaluated its impact on quality of life and early breastfeeding effectiveness.MethodsThis single-centre, cross-sectional study collected data from February 2023 to March 2024. We enrolled patients who underwent caesarean delivery under spinal anaesthesia. We gathered perioperative data and utilized the Thai version of the Richards–Campbell Sleep Quality Questionnaire (RCSQ) to evaluate sleep quality. An overall score of below 50 indicated poor sleep quality. The Thai version of the EuroQol Five Dimensions Five Levels (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire was used to assess quality of life, and the latch, audible swallowing, type of nipple, comfort and hold (positioning) (LATCH) score was employed to evaluate breastfeeding effectiveness. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyse associated factors, with data presented as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe analysis included 284 patients. Seventy-nine patients (27.8%) demonstrated poor sleep quality after caesarean delivery. Three independent factors correlated with poor sleep quality were maternal age ≥35 years (1.880 [1.086–3.252], P = 0.024), urgent/emergency caesarean delivery (1.998 [1.098–3.637], P = 0.023) and moderate to severe pain (numerical rating scale ≥ 4; 1.718 [1.001–2.948], P < 0.049). The Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between the EQ-5D-5L health scale score and the RCSQ score was 0.442 (P < 0.001). The LATCH scores and hospital stay durations were not significantly different between the groups.ConclusionThe findings underscore the importance of recognizing patients at higher risk for poor sleep quality. Careful pain monitoring, timely support for urgent or emergency cases and early identification of potential sleep disturbances may guide individualized postoperative care.Keywords: Breastfeeding, caesarean delivery, EQ-5D-5L, postoperative pain, sleep quality, quality of life

Background

Sleep quality significantly impacts physical and emotional well-being [1–3]. Surgery often leads to decreased sleep duration, increased awakening, reduced sleep quality and frequent nightmares [4]. The total sleep duration decreases by up to 80% during the first three postoperative nights [4]. Normal sleep patterns include two distinct forms: rapid eye movement sleep and nonrapid eye movement sleep. Early postoperative periods involve the absence of rapid eye movement sleep and reduced slow-wave sleep [4]. Sleep patterns typically normalize within 1 week after surgery [5].

Previous studies reported sleep disturbance rates ranging from 22.8% to 44.0% in female surgical patients [6,7]. Multiple perioperative factors contribute to sleep pattern disruption. These include increased levels of surgical stress hormones, enhanced sympathetic activity, elevated inflammatory markers and fever [4]. Direct surgical effects such as postoperative pain and starvation also impact sleep [4,8]. Additionally, hospitalization-related factors, for example, noise, light, temperature and nursing procedures, can increase patient anxiety and discomfort [4,9]. Additional risk factors include advanced patient age; preexisting obstructive sleep apnoea or coronary artery disease; preoperative sleep disturbance; extended surgery duration; surgical trauma severity; and anaesthesia type [4,7, 10,11].

Poor postoperative sleep quality is associated with multiple complications, including delirium in older adults, heightened pain sensitivity and elevated cardiovascular risk, such as myocardial infarction and cardiogenic death [8,11–14]. Sleep disturbances can delay postsurgical recovery [7]. Maternal sleepiness and fatigue affect early postpartum breastfeeding patterns [15,16], and poor maternal sleep is linked to breastfeeding difficulties [17]. Recent evidence suggests that inadequate maternal sleep can delay the onset of lactogenesis and reduce early breastfeeding effectiveness [17]. Consequently, maternal fatigue and poor sleep quality lead to delayed breastfeeding initiation and reduced feeding frequency [15–17].

At our institution, the caesarean delivery rate reached 48.9% in 2017, and projections indicate a continued upward trend [18]. Compared with vaginal birth, caesarean delivery entails distinct postoperative challenges, notably pain management and physiological recovery from anaesthesia. Meta-analyses also associate caesarean delivery with increased risk of postpartum depression, particularly after emergency caesarean delivery [19]. These rising rates raise public health concerns regarding maternal physical recovery, mental health and breastfeeding initiation, all of which may be further affected by sleep quality. However, comprehensive data on postcaesarean sleep quality remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to examine sleep quality after caesarean delivery, identify factors associated with poor sleep and evaluate the effects of sleep quality on early breastfeeding and quality of life.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted this single-centre, cross-sectional study at Thailand’s largest university hospital. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (approval number Si-898/2022). We registered the protocol with www.clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05757401) on 26 January 2023. All patients provided informed consent. Our reporting aligns with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [20].

Study population and eligibility criteria

Patient enrolment occurred from 1 February 2023 to 12 March 2024. We included patients over 18 years of age with a gestational age exceeding 28 weeks who underwent caesarean delivery under spinal anaesthesia. All the participants needed Thai language comprehension abilities. We excluded patients who required conversion to general anaesthesia or who received epidural or truncal nerve blocks. Additional exclusion criteria were patients who needed endotracheal intubation, those requiring intensive care unit admission postoperatively and those for whom surgery was completed between 24:00 and 06:00 h. We also excluded patients who were unable to complete postsurgical questionnaires.

Perioperative care protocol

All patients underwent elective or urgent/emergency caesarean delivery under spinal anaesthesia with local anaesthetic and intrathecal morphine. Standard protocols guided intraoperative monitoring and care. After surgery, all patients were transferred to the postanaesthetic care unit for approximately 2 h before ward admission. Standard postpartum care protocols were followed in the ward.

Data collection procedures

Data collection began after the first postoperative night. We used two paper-based questionnaires: the Richards–Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) and the EuroQol Five Dimensions Five Levels (EQ-5D-5L). This study received authorization to utilize the Thai version of the RCSQ and the Thai version of the EQ-5D-5L health questionnaire for research purposes. We also collected demographic data, clinical characteristics and perioperative information. We documented latch, audible swallowing, type of nipple, comfort and hold (positioning) (LATCH) scores and neonatal formula supplementation volumes on postoperative day 1. Additional neonatal data included birthweight and weight measurements on postoperative day 1 and the discharge day.

Primary outcome assessment

We assessed sleep quality via the Thai version of the RCSQ, which was translated and validated by Kittisin et al. [21] The Thai-RCSQ has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.96 for internal reliability [21]. The questionnaire was used to evaluate five sleep components from the first postoperative night: sleep depth, sleep latency, number of awakenings, ability to return to sleep and overall sleep quality. A total score ≥50 indicates good sleep quality [22].

Secondary outcome measures

We evaluated quality of life with the Thai EQ-5D-5L questionnaire, which assesses mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each domain uses a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘No problems’ to ‘Unable to/Extreme problem’. The EuroQol Visual Analog Scale (EQ VAS) measures health status from 0 (worst imaginable) to 100 (best imaginable) [23,24]. Postoperative pain was measured using an 11-point numerical rating scale (NRS); scores ≥4 indicated moderate to severe pain [25]. Patients self-reported the maximum pain intensity at rest and with movement within the first 24 h after surgery. We categorized postoperative nausea and vomiting into four grades: none, nausea, vomiting once or twice and vomiting more than two times and requiring treatment. Itching severity was classified as none, mild itching or severe itching requiring treatment.

The LATCH score was used to evaluate breastfeeding effectiveness by assessing infant latch, audible swallowing, maternal nipple type, comfort level and positioning assistance needs [26]. The score was recorded on postoperative day 1 and on the day of maternal discharge from the hospital.

Statistical analysis and sample size determination

Sample size calculations were derived from previous research demonstrating a 31.7% prevalence of poor sleep quality in females undergoing total abdominal hysterectomy under spinal anaesthesia with intrathecal morphine [7]. We hypothesized a similar rate (32%) for caesarean delivery patients. Using a 95% confidence level, two-sided testing and a margin of error of 0.055, we calculated a required sample of 277 patients. We added approximately 5% for potential data loss, yielding a final target of 286 patients.

We performed analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and MedCalc version 22.023 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium). We assessed the normality of the data distribution via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical data, presented as numbers and proportions (percentages), were analysed with chi-square tests. Continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges.

Risk factor evaluation employed chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests for univariable analysis. We used forward stepwise multivariable logistic regression to identify factors associated with poor sleep quality. Results are reported as crude and adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to determine the optimal age cut-off, and variance inflation factors (VIFs) were reported to assess multicollinearity among factors. The EQ-5D-5L utility score, which represents overall quality of life, was calculated as 1 minus the coefficient of each health dimension [27]. We assessed the internal reliability of the RCSQ via Cronbach’s alpha. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationships between sleep quality and quality of life and between sleep quality and breastfeeding [28]. Wilson’s method was employed to calculate the 95% CI for poor sleep quality prevalence. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-sided).

Results

Of the 286 patients enrolled in the study, 284 completed it, as two patients did not complete the questionnaire. Participant flow is shown in Figure 1. Medical and obstetric characteristics

Underlying medical conditions included thyroid diseases, thalassaemia, myoma uteri, chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, systemic lupus erythematosus, anaemia, dyslipidaemia, obesity and HIV infection. American Society of Anesthesiologist Physical Status Classification III patients presented with preeclampsia with severe features, Liddle syndrome, valvular heart disease (mitral valve regurgitation) and chronic kidney disease. Pregnancy-associated problems were gestational diabetes, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia and antepartum haemorrhage.

Sleep quality assessment

The mean total RCSQ score was 62.4 ± 20.6 mm (range 0–100), with a median of 64 (interquartile range 48–80). The RSCQ scores for individual items are illustrated in Figure 2. The RCSQ had a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.919.

Perioperative management and complications

All patients received 0.5% hyperbaric bupivacaine with intrathecal morphine 200 mcg. No patients experienced failed spinal anaesthesia requiring general anaesthesia. Eighteen patients (6.3%) needed additional intraoperative analgesic medications (ketamine and fentanyl) because of pain. Although 14 patients (4.9%) experienced postpartum haemorrhage (blood loss ≥ 1000 mL), none required hysterectomy, reoperation or intensive care admission. Postoperative care and nursing activities

Nighttime nursing activities, including vital sign measurements, medication administration and pad changes, were not significantly different between the poor and good sleep quality groups. The groups had similar LATCH scores and neonatal supplementation milk volumes on the first operative day.

The receiver operating characteristic curve analysis for maternal age revealed an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.579 (P = 0.037). Discussion

Prevalence and general characteristics

Our study found that 27.8% of women reported poor sleep quality on postoperative night 1 after caesarean delivery. Three key factors were associated with poor sleep quality: maternal age ≥ 35 years, urgent/emergency caesarean delivery and moderate to severe postoperative pain. The observed prevalence aligns with rates reported among gynaecologic surgery patients, which range from 22.8% to 44.0% [6,7].

The early postoperative period typically involves sleep–wake cycle disruption due to multiple factors, particularly the postsurgical stress response [8–11]. Sleep disturbances arise from heightened postoperative sympathetic activity and elevated catecholamine levels, as increased noradrenergic activity promotes wakefulness [4,29].

Age-related sleep disturbance

Previous studies identified advanced age as a significant factor in postoperative sleep disturbance [10]. However, our study population consisted of childbearing-aged women rather than elderly patients, with ages ranging from 19 to 46 years. Despite this younger age range, we found that an age ≥35 years was significantly associated with poor sleep quality. These results parallel those of Naik et al. who reported significantly greater ages in the poor sleep quality group among intensive care unit patients (median age 42.8 vs 31.4 years) [22].

Multiple studies corroborate an age-related gradient: advancing age correlates with poorer sleep quality, and sleep quantity typically decreases with age [30–32]. Older adults also spend more time in lighter stages and experience fewer, shorter sleep cycles [31]. These alterations manifest as shorter total sleep, longer sleep-onset latency and greater fragmentation [31]. In our analysis, however, maternal age showed limited discriminatory ability for predicting postoperative sleep quality (AUC 0.579 at a cut-off of >35 years). Clinically, age alone should not guide risk stratification and is best considered alongside perioperative and psychosocial factors when assessing vulnerability to postoperative sleep disturbance.

Impact of emergency caesarean delivery

Urgent or emergency caesarean delivery has been found to be significantly correlated with poor sleep quality. A systematic review revealed higher complication rates for infection, fever, urinary tract infection, wound dehiscence, disseminated intravascular coagulation and reoperation, in emergency caesarean deliveries [33]. Emergency caesarean deliveries also impact psychological outcomes. Dekel et al. reported an increased risk for acute stress reactions and post-traumatic stress symptoms [34]. These deliveries are also correlated with higher postpartum depression rates [35]. Such psychological responses can manifest as intrusive thoughts, heightened arousal and sleep difficulties during the immediate postoperative period.

Timing of caesarean delivery

Multiple studies indicate that surgical timing influences sleep quality because disrupted melatonin and cortisol production perturbs circadian rhythms [36,37]. These effects lead not only to sleep disturbance but also to impaired cognition and slower recovery [36,37]. We excluded patients whose caesarean delivery ended between 24:00 and 06:00 h. Such exclusion may introduce selection bias, given the established influence of circadian factors on sleep quality and duration. We excluded these cases for two reasons. Firstly, nighttime operations are usually emergencies, which heighten perioperative physiologic stress and more often necessitate general rather than regional anaesthesia, adding confounding beyond circadian disruption [38]. Secondly, the number of cases during this interval at our institution was small, limiting reliable inference from that subgroup.

Relationship between pain and sleep quality

Moderate to severe postoperative pain significantly affected first-night sleep quality. Previous research at our institute revealed greater pain intensity on day 1 postcaesarean with spinal anaesthesia and intrathecal morphine than on day 2 [39]. Pain and sleep quality demonstrate a bidirectional relationship: postoperative pain disrupts sleep quality and poor sleep quality increases pain sensitivity and perception [8]. Pain triggers increased production of inflammatory cytokines and the release of stress hormones (cortisol and adrenocorticotropic hormone), disrupting normal sleep patterns [8].

Our regression analysis identified moderate to severe postcaesarean pain as associated with poor sleep quality. However, this association was not a strong predictor, with an aOR of 1.718 (95% CI 1.001–2.948) indicating limited precision. Greater pain often increases opioid consumption, and opioid use is correlated with abnormal sleep architecture and poorer sleep quality [7,40]. While extended surgery duration and surgical trauma severity can adversely affect postoperative sleep [4,8,29], we found no significant associations between uterine atony or postpartum haemorrhage and sleep quality.

Environmental and hospital factors

Environmental factors such as unfamiliar surroundings, noise and light are well-recognized contributors to postoperative sleep disturbance [41]. In our cohort, however, sleep quality did not differ significantly by ward type or in relation to nighttime nursing activities. This pattern likely reflects standardized hospital routines, consistent nurse staffing and controlled ward environments, which together minimize environmental disruptions. Interindividual variability in sensitivity to noise, light and other stimuli may also have contributed to the relatively uniform sleep quality across ward settings. Therefore, within our institution, ward type and routine nursing activities were not major determinants of early postoperative sleep quality.

Impact on recovery and quality of life

Sleep disturbance is associated with longer surgical recovery [7]. In our cohort, patients with good sleep had marginally shorter hospital stays than those with poor sleep, although the between-group difference was not statistically significant. Findings from gynaecologic surgery similarly link better first-night sleep to shorter recovery, improved healing and function and reduced length of stay [7].

Sleep disturbance also adversely affects emotional well-being and physical health [1–3]. A recent systematic review reported that the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the EQ-5D-5L index varies by population and treatment [42]. Nonetheless, the review authors recommended overall mean MCIDs of −0.04 for the EQ-5D-5L index and −6.5 for the EQ VAS to denote clinically important worsening [42]. In our study, group differences in EQ-5D-5L index scores were statistically significant but did not exceed the MCID threshold (mean difference 0.03 favouring the good sleep group). By contrast, the mean EQ VAS difference was 11.9 points, well above the MCID, indicating a clinically meaningful disparity in perceived health between good and poor sleep groups. Because postoperative EQ-5D-5L health status reflects multiple domains, the activity, pain and anxiety/depression dimensions included in the EQ-5D-5L likely contribute to the observed correlation between poorer sleep and lower scores [23].

Optimal perioperative care, including multimodal analgesia, prophylaxis for nausea and vomiting, and early mobilization, facilitates recovery and is codified in the enhanced recovery after caesarean delivery (ERAC) pathway [43]. Our findings suggest that structured sleep optimization should be an explicit component of postpartum recovery and may be incorporated within ERAC protocols.

Effects on breastfeeding

We found no significant differences in early breastfeeding performance, measured by LATCH scores, between the good sleep and poor sleep groups. Prior research has linked reduced sleep duration and efficiency with delayed onset of lactation [17,44]. Our null finding may reflect the timing of our assessments, which occurred only on postoperative day 1 and at hospital discharge. These early time points may not capture breastfeeding difficulties that emerge later as maternal fatigue accumulates and circadian disruption intensifies.

Breastfeeding success in the immediate postpartum period is multifactorial and extends beyond institutional support. Delivery mode is influential, with emergency caesarean delivery often associated with greater breastfeeding difficulty than elective caesarean delivery or vaginal birth [45]. Other reported predictors include high prepregnancy body mass index (BMI), gestational complications, thyroid disease, low serum albumin, parity, caesarean history, gestational age, birth weight, breastfeeding guidance and feeding frequency [44]. Maternal sleepiness and fatigue can delay initiation and reduce feeding frequency [15,16].

In our study, parity, prior breastfeeding experience, partner or family support, and maternal mental health were not reported or were not adjusted in our multivariable models. Omission of these factors may have masked associations between sleep quality and breastfeeding outcomes. Overall, breastfeeding performance reflects interacting biological, psychological and social determinants, of which maternal sleep quality is only one component. Therefore, mothers with poor sleep should receive comprehensive lactation support from midwives and other healthcare professionals to optimize maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Implications of maternal sleep disturbance in the postpartum period

Our study adds to evidence that sleep quality is a critical determinant of recovery and well-being after caesarean delivery. Postpartum sleep quality is associated with how mothers perceive and experience relationships with their infants [46]. Large-scale survey data show that poor sleep, postpartum depression, childbirth-related post-traumatic stress and low social support predict impaired mother–infant bonding, with depression the strongest negative factor [47]. Comparative studies of first-time parents using continuous actigraphy show greater sleep disturbance, daytime sleepiness and functional impairment in mothers and fathers than in nonparent controls [48]. These observations indicate that maternal sleep after caesarean delivery reflects not only postoperative complications, surgical recovery and hospital factors, but also ongoing family dynamics and caregiving responsibilities.

Accordingly, sleep after caesarean delivery should be viewed as a multidimensional construct intertwined with psychological health, social support and family adjustment, rather than as a purely physiologic recovery variable. A holistic approach, with interventions targeting sleep and psychosocial domains, should be offered to all patients undergoing caesarean delivery to improve maternal recovery and foster positive mother–infant relationships.

Study limitations and future directions

This study’s limitations include its single-institution design, which restricts the generalizability of the findings. Future research should incorporate multicentre and national-level studies to enhance applicability of the findings. Preoperative sleep quality and psychological factors, such as anxiety, were not assessed because data collection occurred postoperatively and included both elective and emergency caesarean deliveries. Future prospective studies should incorporate standardized preoperative assessments, such as the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, to disentangle these potential confounders. We excluded patients whose caesarean delivery ended between 24:00 and 06:00 h to minimize heterogeneity introduced by urgent or emergent nighttime procedures. However, this exclusion may limit generalizability because circadian factors during nighttime surgery can materially influence postoperative sleep quality and recovery. Future studies should include patients undergoing surgery across the full 24-h period to address circadian variation.

Additionally, the main findings were derived from questionnaires rather than actigraphy measurements. Future research should evaluate the correlation between RCSQ scores and actigraphy in postcaesarean patients. Prospective studies should also examine the long-term effects of poor early postoperative sleep on breastfeeding outcomes, postpartum depression and maternal–child bonding.

Conclusion

Nearly one-third of patients experienced poor sleep quality after caesarean delivery. Advanced maternal age, urgent or emergency caesarean delivery and moderate to severe postoperative pain were significantly associated with poorer sleep quality. Poor sleep was moderately correlated with lower quality-of-life scores, whereas no significant association was observed with early breastfeeding effectiveness. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive perioperative assessment; however, causal relationships require confirmation through longitudinal studies.
