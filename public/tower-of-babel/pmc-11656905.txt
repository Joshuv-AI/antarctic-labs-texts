Associations between internet addiction and suicidal ideation in depressed adolescents: the mediating effect of insomnia as well as sex differences
Authors: Lewei Liu, Yinghan Tian, Haojie Fan, Jiawei Wang, Changhao Chen, Zhiwei Liu, Feng Geng, Daming Mo, Xiangfen Luo, Xiangwang Wen, Xin Zhao, Mingru Hao, Lei Xia, Huanzhong Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInsomnia may mediate the association between internet addiction (IA) and suicidal ideation (SI). However, such association has not been thoroughly investigated in adolescents with major depressive disorder (MDD). Thus, our study aimed to explore whether insomnia significantly affects the association between IA and SI in adolescents with MDD, as well as any sex differences.MethodsFrom January 2021 to September 2023, this cross-sectional study included 502 adolescents with MDD and 123 healthy controls (HCs). The Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S), the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), the Insomnia Severity Index Scale (ISI) and the Positive and Negative Suicidal Ideation Scale (PANSI) were used to assess subjects’ severity of depression, IA, insomnia and SI. And we employed the PROCESS macro program to examine the mediating role of insomnia between IA and SI.ResultsCompared to HCs, adolescents with MDD had significantly higher levels of SI and their prevalence of IA and insomnia were 50.0% and 35.7%. In patients, PANSI scores correlated negatively with age and age of onset, and positively with disease duration as well as the scores of the CGI-S, IAT, and ISI (all p < 0.01). Moreover, above-mentioned correlations were more significant in girls. Mediation analyses showed that insomnia mediated the effect of IA on SI, and the total, direct, and indirect effects were 0.197 (95% CI: 0.132—0.262), 0.157 (95% CI: 0.096—0.218) and 0.040 (95% CI: 0.014—0.068), respectively. In female patients, the total, direct, and indirect effects were 0.224 (95% CI: 0.151—0.296), 0.191 (95% CI: 0.124—0.257) and 0.033 (95% CI: 0.002—0.068), respectively. In male patients, insomnia acted as a fully mediated effect between IA and SI, with an effect value of 0.045 (95% CI: 0.008—0.093).ConclusionDepressed adolescents have higher suicide risk, and SI is significantly associated with IA and insomnia, especially in girls. Moreover, insomnia may mediate the association between IA and SI. However, given the small sample size of this study and the limitations of assessment tools used, the results need to be viewed with caution. Regardless, clinical interventions should be strengthened for IA and insomnia in adolescents with MDD to reduce suicide risk.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent global public health issue. In recent years, there has been a rise in the incidence of MDD at younger ages. For example, a survey conducted between 2009 and 2019 in the United States showed that the prevalence of depressive episode increased from 8.1% to 15.8% among adolescents [1]. Not only can MDD significantly impair adolescents’ quality of life, but it can also greatly increase suicidal ideation (SI) risk [2]. According to epidemiological surveys, the prevalence of SI in the past 2 weeks was much higher in Chinese adolescents with MDD than in healthy adolescents (42.1% vs. 22.1%) [3, 4]. Furthermore, SI can significantly predict future suicide attempts and completions [5, 6]. Therefore, it is essential to comprehend the specific mechanisms underlying SI, such as Internet addiction (IA) and insomnia, to prevent suicidal deaths in adolescents with MDD.

A recent series of studies on SI found that IA may be one of the potential risk factors in adolescents with MDD. IA is very common among adolescents and is emerging as a significant public health concern [7]. Studies shown that the prevalence of IA ranged from 12.8% to 23.7% in Chinese general adolescents [8, 9], while the rate was as high as 46.8% among those with MDD [10]. In addition, IA may also result in SI. A survey of 12,507 adolescents revealed that those with IA had a 2.2 times higher SI risk [11]. However, the potential mechanisms underlying the association between IA and SI have not received thorough investigation in adolescents with MDD.

SI resulting from IA may be linked to insomnia in adolescents with MDD. Insomnia is a common symptom of MDD, and is often associated with a worse prognosis [12]. A study of healthy adolescents found that problematic Internet use was significantly associated with sleep problems [13]. The adolescent period is a crucial time for developing healthy sleep patterns [14]. Insomnia symptoms during this period can trigger negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression, and in severe cases, even prompt suicidal behaviors [15, 16]. For instance, a meta-analysis shown that sleep disorders increased suicide risk by 2.45 times in patients with MDD [17]. Additionally, a randomized controlled trial found that treating insomnia symptoms also significantly reduced SI in insomnia patients [18], which further confirmed the close link between insomnia and suicide. To date, a series of studies have tentatively confirmed that insomnia may play a mediating role in the association between IA and SI in the general population [19, 20]. For example, through path analyses, Zhang and colleagues found that problematic Internet use can lead to SI through insomnia, frequent nightmares, and social jetlag [20]. However, this mediating role has not been well studied in adolescents with MDD. Adolescents with MDD are at higher risk for IA and SI due to their immature psycho-cognitive development, lack of self-regulation and control, and greater emotional instability [3, 4, 8, 10]. Hence, it is necessary to explore the potential impact of insomnia on the association between IA and SI in this group for preventing suicidal behaviors and improving prognosis.

In addition, potential sex difference is noteworthy when examining the associations between IA, insomnia, and SI. For example, Huang and colleagues found that problematic Internet use among female adolescents was more likely to lead to suicide risk than among boys [11]. On the other hand, Maurya and colleagues found that female adolescents were more likely to experience sleep problems as Internet use increased [21]. And in terms of mediating effects, a study conducted with healthy adolescents found that compared to boys, insomnia symptoms were more significantly mediated between problematic Internet use and SI in girls [20]. The above differences may be related to factors such as earlier puberty in girls, differences in sex houngans levels, and different types of Internet use [22, 23]. However, whether this sex difference exists in adolescents with MDD is currently inconclusive.

In summary, while some studies have linked insomnia to IA and SI, it remains unclear whether this association also exists in adolescents with MDD. The present study primarily analyzed the factors associated with SI in adolescents with MDD, with a focus on determining whether insomnia significantly affects the association between IA and SI, as well as any sex differences in these associations. Based on the above aims, we hypothesized that (a) adolescents with MDD are at higher overall risk for SI, IA and insomnia, and SI is independently associated with general demographic factors such as sex, age, etc.; (b) IA and insomnia are facilitators of SI in adolescents with MDD; (c) insomnia plays a mediating role in the association between IA and SI; and (d) there are sex differences in the above associations and mediating effect.

Methods

Study design and participants

From January 2021 to September 2023, we conducted this cross-sectional study among psychiatric patients at eight hospitals (Chaohu Hospital of Anhui Medical University, Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, Bozhou People’s Hospital, Suzhou Second People’s Hospital, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Bengbu Medical College, Ma’anshan Fourth People’s Hospital, Fuyang Third People’s Hospital) in Anhui Province, China. These eight hospitals were located in the northern, central and southern regions of Anhui Province, and they were all well-known local hospitals with a total of over 3,000 psychiatric beds, which ensured the reliability of the study sample to a certain extent. All enrolled patients met the following criteria: (1) aged 12–18 years; and (2) diagnosed with MDD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition (DSM-5); and (3) had the ability to cooperate through the entire research process. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients were excluded if they had a comorbid diagnosis or a previous diagnosis of other types of mental disorders to ensure consistency of the sample; and (2) patients with severe infections or certain major physical illnesses (e.g. organic brain disease, cancer, thyroid dysfunction, etc.) were also excluded considering that such illnesses may interfere with the assessment of mental status. At the same time, we also recruited healthy controls (HCs) aged 12–18 years in Anhui Province. The processes of recruiting HCs included publicizing this study in local communities and healthcare facilities, and initially contacting potential participants by phone or WeChat to invite them for a screening meeting with the aim of assessing their eligibility.

The Ethics Committee of Chaohu Hospital, Anhui Medical University approved the current study (202009-KYXM-04). And the written informed consents were signed by all subjects and their parents before study commencement. In order to safeguard participants’ privacy, this study adopted de-identification measures in both the data collection and analyses phases. Meanwhile, all study members signed a confidentiality agreement. We also informed all participants about these privacy protection measures so as to collect the most accurate data.

Measuring instruments

Socio-demographic characteristics

This study collected data on the socio-demographic characteristics of the subjects through a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire included information on sex, age, body mass index (BMI), single-child family status, first-episode MDD, age at onset, duration of illness, and use of antidepressant medication.

Clinical severity of depressive symptoms

Our study assessed patients’ clinical depression severity using Clinical Global Impression-Severity (CGI-S) [24, 25]. The CGI-S was designed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and is a widely used tool in psychiatric treatment and research [26, 27]. The scale ranges from 1 to 7, with the higher the score, the more severe the illness.

Severity of IA

The Internet Addiction Test (IAT) was used to assess subjects’ severity of IA [28]. Developed by Kimberly Young, the IAT has been widely used to assess IA since 1998 and is recognized globally. The scale is short and convenient, consisting of 20 questions related to frequency and scenarios of Internet usage. Each question is rated on a 5-point scale (1–5), resulting in a total score range of 20 to 100. In this study, IAT scores ≥ 50 were defined as the presence of IA [29]. Previous studies have shown that the Chinese version of the IAT is reliable and valid for adolescents [30].

Severity of insomnia symptoms

The Insomnia Severity Index Scale (ISI) was used to measure the severity of insomnia symptoms experienced by the subjects in the past two weeks [31]. The ISI is an adjunct to the diagnosis of insomnia in DSM-IV, and the scale consists of 7 insomnia-related items, each of which is rated on a five-point scale ranging from ‘0 = none’ to ‘4 = very severe’. The ISI total score ranges from 0 to 28, with higher scores indicating more severe insomnia symptoms in the subjects. In our study, the ISI total score ≥ 15 was defined as the presence of insomnia symptoms [32]. Currently, the ISI has been widely used in adolescents with MDD and has good reliability [33, 34].

Severity of SI

The Positive and Negative Suicidal Ideation Scale (PANSI) was used to assess subjects’ severity of SI [35, 36]. The PANSI was developed to assess protective and risk factors associated with SI in adolescents and adults and consists of two dimensions: positive suicidal ideation (PANSI-PSI, 6 items) and negative suicidal ideation (PANSI-NSI, 8 items). Each item of the scale is rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). The sum of the total PANSI-PSI score (reverse scoring) and the total PANSI-NSI score (forward scoring) is the total PANSI score. The higher the PANSI total score, the higher the level of SI in individuals. Additionally, the Chinese version of the PANSI has been used extensively in Chinese adolescents with MDD [37].

Statistical analysis

SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed to perform statistical descriptions, one-way analyses, correlation analyses, linear regression analyses, and mediation analyses. Firstly, the normal distribution of continuous variables was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (quartiles) [M (P25, P75)] were used to express continuous variables, while categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%). In univariate analyses, independent samples t-test, Mann–Whitney U test and Chi-square test were used to compare demographic and clinical variables between different groups. Because patients and HCs were not matched for sex and age, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. Pearson’s or Spearman correlation analyses were used to examine the correlation between PANSI score and with demographic and clinical variables in patients by sex. Further, multivariate linear regression analyses were performed using the “stepwise” method to investigate which factors (variables with P ≤ 0.10 in the correlation analyses) were most significantly affecting PANSI score (dependent variable), and to construct models that were more concise and easier to interpret. At the same time, we combined expertise and theoretical guidance in the variable selection process to ensure that important variables were not excluded from the stepwise regression analyses. Finally, we conducted mediation analysis using Model 4 on the PROCESS macro [38], and we performed 5,000 bootstrapped samples to assess the significance of the mediation effect. The Bootstrap method is a non-parametric statistical method that constructs the distribution of a statistic by random sampling with replacement, thus eliminating the need to rely on assumptions about data distribution. This method can help to reduce the estimation bias associated with small samples and provide more precise confidence intervals. P ≤ 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics in patients and HCs

A total of 522 adolescents with MDD were recruited, and we included 502 patients in the final statistical analyses due to incomplete questionnaires provided (n = 20). Additionally, this study recruited 123 healthy adolescents as a control group. The patients’ mean age was 15.30 (SD = 1.65), and 72.9% were girls. Compared to HCs, patients had higher scores of IAT, ISI and PANSI (all p < 0.001) (Table 1). When the effect of age and sex was examined by adding them to the ANCOVA as covariates, the above differences remained significant (all p < 0.001). The prevalence of IA and insomnia in patients were 50.0% and 35.7%, respectively (Table 1). Furthermore, compared to male patients, female patients were younger, lower BMI, higher likelihood of not being the only child, earlier onset, and greater SI (all p < 0.05) (Table 2). The mediating effect of insomnia on the relationship between IA and SI in patients

In the mediation analysis, IA served as the independent variable, while SI was the dependent variable and insomnia served as the mediating variable. As presented in Table 5, insomnia mediated the impact of IA on SI in the total sample, female patients and male patients. In the total sample, the total, direct, and indirect effects were 0.197 (95% CI: 0.132—0.262), 0.157 (95% CI: 0.096—0.218) and 0.040 (95% CI: 0.014—0.068), respectively (Fig. 1). In female patients, the total, direct, and indirect effects were 0.224 (95% CI: 0.151—0.296), 0.191 (95% CI: 0.124—0.257) and 0.033 (95% CI: 0.002—0.068), respectively (Fig. 2). In male patients, insomnia acted as a fully mediated effect between IA and SI, with an effect value of 0.045 (95% CI: 0.008—0.093) (Fig. 3). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the mediating role of insomnia in the association between IA and SI in adolescents with MDD, as well as any potential sex differences. In our study, the level of SI was significantly higher in adolescents with MDD than in HCs, which was consistent with the results of previous studies [4, 39–41]. Individuals who experience depression often interpret, think about, and respond to events in a negative and despairing manner, which can increase the risk of SI [42]. Additionally, our study found that 50.0% of adolescents with MDD had IA, and this percentage was similar to the previously reported percentage of Chinese adolescents with MDD (36.9%—46.8%) [10, 43]. A meta-analysis of 42 studies confirmed a higher risk of IA in adolescents with MDD [44]. All of these studies indicate that IA is common in adolescents with MDD and may impact the patients’ prognosis. And finally, similar to previous studies [45], the present study also showed the prevalence of insomnia symptoms in 35.7% of adolescents with MDD. Given that insomnia has detrimental effects on the clinical prognosis of adolescents with MDD [12], clinical attention should be paid to the sleep health of this population.

Further correlation and regression analyses showed that general demographic factors such as sex, age, age at onset, and duration of illness were independent influences on SI, which was consistent with the findings of previous studies [23, 46]. Firstly, in our study, female patients have higher levels of SI. On the one hand, this may be attributed to the changes in estrogen levels during adolescence [47]. On the other hand, the female patients are more prone to negative emotions such as anger, despair, and guilt, and consequently have an increased risk of suicide [48]. Secondly, there was a negative correlation between age and SI level, which may be related to younger patients’ cognitive immaturity and high impulsivity. Finally, there were also higher levels of SI in patients with younger age at onset and longer duration of illness. This might be due to the fact that the persistence of the disease exposed patients to prolonged periods of mental anguish, social isolation, and persistent negative symptoms, and these experiences might make them feel hopeless and helpless, thus increasing SI risk [49]. Therefore, clinicians should focus on screening for the risk of SI in patients with these characteristics.

In addition, we found that the more severe the IA, the higher the level of SI in adolescents with MDD. Indeed, the close association between IA and SI has been repeatedly confirmed in different studies [46, 50–52]. For instance, a study conducted on healthy Chinese adolescents revealed that Internet gaming disorder significantly increased the risk of SI [46]. Another survey conducted in the United States also discovered a dose–effect relationship between using the Internet for ≥ 5 h daily and SI (OR = 1.7; 95% CI: 1.3—2.1) [51]. In addition, Gansner and colleagues found a positive correlation between the severity of problematic Internet use and past-year suicidality in adolescents with psychiatric disorders [50]. The close association between IA and SI may involve the following mechanisms. First, adolescents with IA often have poorer self-control and higher levels of impulsivity [53], and these personality traits can greatly increase an individual’s risk of SI [54]. Adolescents with MDD and comorbid IA are highly likely to face additional life stressors because of difficulties in meeting their social and academic responsibilities. When individuals encounter stressful life events, they may be driven by impulsivity to adopt negative coping mechanisms, such as suicidal behaviors [55]. Additionally, the Internet functions as a platform for disseminating information, which can lead to the rapid spread of suicide-related content [56, 57]. Adolescents are in a critical period of psychological and emotional development, and they may be more susceptible to the influence of the Internet environment [58]. Excessive use of the Internet may lead to their exposure to more negative information and increase their risk of suicide. Finally, overuse of the Internet may increase the incidence of cyberbullying among adolescents, especially for those already suffering from MDD [59, 60]. And cyberbullying may worsen their depressive symptoms, reduce their self-esteem and life satisfaction, and consequently increase the risk of suicidal behaviors [60, 61]. Interestingly, in our study, adolescent female patients had a greater correlation between SI and IA than adolescent male patients, which may involve sex differences in the types of Internet use, in addition to being related to girls’ greater susceptibility to suicide. Previous studies have established that female adolescents are more likely to use social media or engage in information searching, while boys tend to use the Internet primarily for online gaming [62–64]. As a result, female adolescents are more likely to be exposed to suicide-related information, which in turn increases the risk of suicide in this group. For example, Choi and colleagues found that female adolescents were more likely to use the Internet for information searches on self-harm and suicide-related terms, and that the volume of searches was positively associated with suicide risk [62]. In summary, to safeguard adolescents with MDD from the detrimental effects of IA, it is imperative for families, schools, society, and Internet platforms to collaborate and enhance supervision, offer mental health support, and educate this population on appropriate Internet usage.

In this study, insomnia was another independent risk factor for SI in adolescents with MDD. Similarly, Fan and colleagues found more sleep disturbance in the SI group in a cross-sectional study of 576 adolescents with MDD [65]. A meta-analysis of 39 studies also pointed to sleep disorders, such as insomnia and nightmares, as risk factors for SI [66]. Insomnia may have a range of negative effects on adolescents, such as reduced attention and judgment, worsened mood, and reduced regulatory control, which can further lead to impulse control disorders and thus to risky behaviors, including suicide [67]. In addition, the close association between insomnia and SI may also be related to the common biological mechanisms they share. For instance, previous studies found that serotonin and inflammation play important moderating roles between sleep disorders and suicidal behavior, respectively [68, 69]. As a result, sleep disorders in adolescents with MDD should be adequately emphasized and intervened in clinical practice, which may help to reduce the risk of suicide in this population.

More importantly, our study also suggested that insomnia played a mediating role in the relationship between IA and SI in adolescents with MDD. Previous studies conducted in healthy adolescents have repeatedly validated the pathway process [19, 20]. For example, Guo and colleagues found that sleep disorders mediated the association between IA and suicidal behaviors through a national survey [19]. It is reasonable to explain that excessive Internet use before bedtime may lead to disturbed sleep–wake patterns in adolescents with MDD [70, 71]. Higuchi and colleagues conducted an experiment that found playing computer games before bedtime could affect sleep latency and the period of rapid eye movement sleep [70]. Additionally, excessive Internet use can cause physical symptoms in adolescents, such as dizziness and muscle pain, which may further lead to insomnia [72]. Subsequently, insomnia, in turn, increases the likelihood of adolescent suicide through corresponding biological mechanisms, worsened mood, impulse control disorders, and other pathways [73]. The study also identified sex differences in the mediating role mentioned earlier. Specifically, insomnia partially mediated the relationship between IA and SI in female patients, while in male patients, it fully mediated the relationship. We speculated that this difference could also be caused by different types of Internet usage. As previously mentioned, female adolescents tend to use social media more frequently [23], which can increase their exposure to suicide-related information. Therefore, IA in female adolescents may directly contribute to an increased risk of suicide. In contrast, the type of Internet use among male adolescents is more dominated by game playing [23]. Some longitudinal studies have suggested that claims about the negative effects of electronic games might be overstated [74], and there were even studies suggesting that electronic games could promote positive mental health in adolescents by providing a way to reduce stress and relax [75]. However, there were studies that came to inconsistent conclusions. For example, Yu and colleagues found that online gaming disorder could directly lead to increased risk of SI in adolescents [46]. However, this study did not explore underlying sex differences. Kerr and colleagues showed through a large sample that never participating in Internet gaming was associated with lower risk of SI in girls, but not in boys, which also revealed that boys and girls may be affected differently by Internet gaming [76]. These inconsistent findings might suggest that Internet gaming is not always relaxing and is a stressor in many cases. This is commonly observed in team-based competitive games where less-skilled players are often harshly berated by their teammates for how they play [77, 78]. In some extreme cases, so-called “toxic” players will go so far as to tell their teammates to “kill yourself”. Such phenomenon may explain some of the conflicting results regarding the benefits and detriments of online gaming in this population. However, it should be noted that excessive Internet use, including the use of social media and gaming, can lead to elevated risk of suicide by causing sleep disorders in adolescents [20, 79]. Based on the results of this study, our recommendations are as follows: (a) healthcare professionals and parents need to be aware of IA’s possible negative outcomes such as suicidal risk for adolescents with MDD; (b) appropriate clinical interventions (e.g., disease education, psychotherapy, etc.) and family support should be provided to help them develop moderate Internet behaviors and good sleep habits; (c) the potential sex differences in understanding the associations between SI and IA as well as insomnia should be taken into account.

There were several limitations to this study. First, the cross-sectional study design resulted in the inability of the present study to make causal inferences about the findings. Therefore, it is necessary to replicate the mediating role of insomnia in the relationship between IA and SI in longitudinal studies. Second, this study did not document the types of subjects’ Internet use in detail, so the speculations we made in the paper still need to be validated by further studies. And our study only investigated a limited number of hospitals in Anhui Province, China, and the sample size was small, which might limit the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, we will consider the current study as a preliminary study to be followed by a validation study with a larger sample size to verify the results of this study. Regarding the assessment of depressive symptoms, this study used the CGI-S scale, which consists of only 7 items and is not designed specifically for assessing depressive symptoms, and thus may not be as robust a measure of depression severity as other depression scales, such as the Hamilton Rating Scale for Depression [80]. Finally, this study used only self-assessment questionnaires to assess insomnia in the subjects, which might be affected by individuals’ subjective feelings and memory bias, resulting in bias between the reported results and the actual situation. Therefore, future studies can incorporate objective sleep monitoring devices, such as polysomnography, to provide a more comprehensive assessment of insomnia.

Conclusion

In the current study, adolescents with MDD had higher overall risks for IA, insomnia, and SI, and there were significant correlations between SI and IA as well as insomnia, especially in girls. Furthermore, insomnia fully mediated the association between IA and SI in adolescent girls with MDD, and partially mediated this association in adolescent boys with MDD. Hence, we suggest that clinicians and parents should assist adolescents with MDD in developing healthy sleep habits and Internet usage patterns to reduce the suicidal risk in this population.
