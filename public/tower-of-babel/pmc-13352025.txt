Autosomal Type IV Collagen Genes Display Sex Differences in Genetic Risk for Hematuria
Authors: Frida Lona-Durazo, Ian R. Dinsmore, Michelle T. McNulty, Matthew G. Sampson, Léonie Forest, Tooraj Mirshahi, Alexander R. Chang, Andrew D. Paterson, Moumita Barua, Sarah A. Gagliano Taliun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHematuria is an understudied condition associated with kidney disease and related outcomes. Sex and gender influence many aspects of human health, including kidney traits. However, sex-differential genetic effects, which can be attributed to hormones, gene regulation, and other factors, have not been comprehensively explored.MethodsTo identify genetic effects that differ by sex for hematuria, we performed sex-specific genome-wide association analyses in the UK Biobank (UKB), with replication results in 2 independent cohorts: the Million Veteran Program (MVP) and Geisinger MyCode. Sex-differential genetic effects were evaluated using locus-specific sex-by-genotype interaction analysis. We further performed observational analyses between hematuria and sex hormones (estradiol and testosterone) in the UKB.ResultsWe identified significant sex-differential effects in the UKB (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.006), corresponding to larger effect sizes in females than in males, but in the same direction of effect, at genetic variants within 2 autosomal type IV collagen genes that encode key structural components of the glomerular basement membrane: COL4A3 and COL4A2.ConclusionAlthough males are known to experience more severe kidney disease than females in X-linked Alport syndrome, our findings highlight important sex differential effects in hematuria, where the presence of COL4A3 variation in females results in stronger association effect sizes than in males, which replicated in an independent cohort. In addition, we identify that COL4A2 is associated with hematuria, also with greater effect size in females than in males, only in the UKB.

Sex differences play a significant role in the development and progression of kidney disease. Epidemiological studies show that chronic kidney disease is more prevalent in females, but males are more likely to experience faster progression to end-stage kidney disease.1, 2, 3 Biological factors, including variations in immune response, oxidative stress, expression of kidney transporters and structural proteins, and the effects of sex hormones could contribute to sex-differential susceptibility.2,4, 5, 6, 7 Social and behavioral factors, such as health care access and comorbidities, may further modulate outcomes.3 Understanding sex differences and potential mechanisms involved is increasingly recognized as essential for improving precision medicine and developing tailored therapeutic strategies in kidney disease.

Sex differences in kidney disease development and progression have become increasingly recognized, with accumulating evidence suggesting that estrogen exerts a protective influence on kidney physiology and pathology. For example, estrogen appears to have protective effects on kidney structure and function, whereas testosterone may promote fibrosis and injury.3,7 Decline in kidney health in premenopausal females has been shown to be slower than in age-matched males; however, this protective effect disappears in postmenopausal females. Indeed, clinical and experimental studies have shown that estrogen-estrogen receptor interactions, with receptors expressed throughout the kidney, plays an important role in regulating renal homeostasis, blood flow, cytokine signaling and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system.8, 9, 10, 11, 12

In addition, sex-associated differences in kidney health have been attributed to regulation of genes on the sex chromosomes.13,14 For example, genetic variation in the α-5 chain type IV collagen gene (COL4A5) is responsible for X-linked recessive Alport syndrome, resulting in more severe disease in males than in females. In contrast, female carriers show various degrees of disease severity, possibly due to random X chromosome inactivation.15 Autosomal genes may contribute to sex-associated differences. For example, a large sex-stratified meta-analysis of estimated glomerular filtration rate, identified a sex-differential association on chromosome 17 (SLC47A1), that is hypothesized to be regulated by testosterone and/or estrogen.16

Hematuria is an understudied sign of kidney disease. In the UKB, the prevalence of hematuria is higher in males (5%) than in females (3%). Although previous research has investigated the genetic underpinnings of hematuria in large-scale cohorts,17,18 sex-differential genetic effects remain underexplored, which are relevant because hematuria is considered an early manifestation of glomerular pathology.

Here, we performed sex-stratified genome-wide association analyses for hematuria in the White British subset of the UKB, and report replication results in 2 independent cohorts (the MVP and Geisinger MyCode). In addition, we quantified possible sex-differing effects for hematuria using locus-specific sex-by-genotype interaction analysis and 2 complementary approaches (case-only genome-wide interaction analysis and sexually dimorphic effects). Finally, we performed observational analyses to explore the association of sex hormone levels and hematuria.19, 20, 21 Identifying sex differences in hematuria through the lens of genetics has the potential to guide the development of personalized therapies for kidney disease, by better informing about biological factors that differentially influence kidney health by sex.

Methods

We followed the STREGA reporting guidelines.22

Sex-Specific Genome-Wide Association Study of Hematuria

Hematuria cases and controls were defined using PheCode 593 based on the PheWAS R package23 as used in our previous sex-combined hematuria analysis.17 PheCode 593 was defined based on the following criteria: individuals with at least one of the following codes were defined as cases: “hematuria,” 599.7 (International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9) and R31 (ICD10); “hematuria unspecified,” 599.70 (ICD9) and R31.9 (ICD10); and “gross hematuria,” 599.71 (ICD9) and R31.0 (ICD10). ICD codes were derived from hospital inpatient discharge data. Individuals with any genitourinary PheCodes (range 590–593.99, excluding 593), encompassing codes for infections of kidney, acute or chronic pyelonephritis, renal and perinephric abscess, urinary tract infection, cystitis, trigonitis, and urethritis were excluded from the analysis (i.e., were assigned as neither cases nor controls). This phenotype definition resulted in 9774 hematuria male cases and 177,666 male controls, and 7061 female cases and 213,033 female controls.

We carried out female-only and male-only single variant genome-wide association analyses using SAIGE (v0.42)24 across variants on the autosomes and chromosome X in the White British subset of the UKB, using an additive model and including the following covariates: the first 10 principal components of genetic ancestry and year of birth as continuous variables. SAIGE allows for the inclusion of related individuals as well as case-control imbalance. The UKB data-field for genetic sex (field 22001), which was derived from sex inference based on chromosome X- and Y-probe intensities, was used to define male and female groups. Nonpseudoautosomal regions of chromosome X for males were modelled by multiplying the dosages by 2, whereas nonpseudoautosomal regions for females were treated as autosomes. Pseudoautosomal regions were excluded. The Haplotype Reference Consortium–imputed dataset was used.25 We retained variants with a minor allele count ≥ 20 and an imputation score > 0.4. We defined a genome-wide significant signal using the standardly accepted P-value cutoff of 5 × 10−8. To investigate if multiple independent signals are present in the sex-specific hematuria genome-wide association study (GWAS), we performed conditional analyses of association using the Trans-omics for Precision Medicine–imputed reference panel (Supplementary Methods).

Replication in Independent Cohorts

To validate our sex-specific hematuria GWAS signals, we checked for replication of the independent lead variants identified in the UKB Trans-omics for Precision Medicine–imputed reference panel through conditional analyses, in the European genetic ancestry subset of the Geisinger MyCode 175k freeze cohort using dipstick hematuria as done in a previous hematuria GWAS,26 and in the MVP, which definition of hematuria cases is based on the same PheCode 593 as in the UKB (Supplementary Methods). We applied a Bonferroni P-value correction to account for the number of variants tested for replication (i.e., 5 variants across males and females) and accounting for consistency in the direction of effect (i.e., 1-sided test) by setting the P-value cutoff to 0.01 (0.05/10) × 2.

Hematuria Prevalence by Sex

We estimated the prevalence of hematuria in the White British subset of the UKB for each sex across age at recruitment deciles. To investigate the genetic component of the differences in hematuria prevalence across female age groups, we performed genetic associations across the female-specific genome-wide significant and conditionally independent lead (i.e., most significantly associated) variants (P < 5 × 10−8), stratified by menopause status (Supplementary Methods). Sample sizes for each group are in Supplementary Table S1. For comparison, we assessed genetic associations in males stratified by 2 age groups as follows (UKB field 21022): (i) aged < 50 years and (ii) aged ≥ 50 years. We used the same covariates as in the initial association analysis (i.e., year of birth and the first 10 principal components of genetic ancestry) and performed the analysis using REGENIE (v3.2.1),27 which accounts for case control imbalance using the Firth correction and applies a default minor allele count filter of ≥5.

Assessment of Sex-Differential Effects in Hematuria

We applied 3 quantitative approaches to identify potential sex-differential effects in hematuria. As our primary approach, we carried out a sex by genotype interaction analysis at specific loci, which is described below. As secondary analyses, we conducted a case-only genome-wide interaction test of sex in hematuria cases, as well as an estimation of sexually dimorphic effects analysis (Supplementary Methods; Supplementary Table S2). Identified variants with suggested sex-differing effects were assessed for overlap with predicted motifs of sex hormone receptors (Supplementary Table S3).

Given that the power to identify interaction effects without prior screening in our current dataset was very low (0.0004; Supplementary Methods), we implemented prior screening to increase power.14 Nine variants were assessed. Specifically, we selected the lead genetic variants, defined by our conditional analysis of association as well as the lead variants of the genome-wide significant signals from the published Haplotype Reference Consortium–imputed sex-combined hematuria GWAS,17 which have been previously replicated (Supplementary Methods). We performed the genotype-by-sex interaction analysis with REGENIE (v3.2.1)27 using the Trans-omics for Precision Medicine–imputed White British subset of the UKB dataset (cases = 16,833 and controls = 390,639). We included in the prediction model, sex, year of birth, and the first 10 genetic principal components of genetic ancestry as well as a sex by genotype interaction term. We applied a Bonferroni-corrected P-value threshold of 0.006 (0.05/9) for the interaction effect, to account for the 9 variants tested. We then replicated the significant signals in Geisinger MyCode, where the interaction analysis was run in REGENIE (v4.0) using the same parameters and covariates as in the replication described above.

Association of Sex Hormone Levels With Hematuria in the UKB

We tested estradiol levels for association with hematuria status in the White British subset of the UKB, focusing primarily on a binary indicator of estradiol detection (field 30806.0; detectable or not), and secondarily on quantitative estradiol levels (field 30800.0.0). We performed the analysis across all females and stratifying by menopause status (Supplementary Methods).

In addition, we performed associations between testosterone levels and hematuria status, stratifying by sex, using quantitative testosterone (field 30850.0.0) and a binary indicator of testosterone detection across both sexes (field 30856.0.0) (Supplementary Methods).

Sex-Specific Transcriptome-Wide Association Study of Hematuria

We performed sex-specific transcriptome-wide association study of hematuria with Omnibus Transcriptome Test using Expression Reference Summary data,28 using sex-specific cis expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) from human kidney samples from the NEPTUNE study29 (Supplementary Methods; Supplementary Results; Members of the NEPTUNE study network).

Replication in Independent Cohorts

We followed-up the sex-specific hematuria lead variants identified in the UKB (Trans-omics for Precision Medicine–imputed) in 2 independent cohorts: the Geisinger MyCode cohort, for which sex-stratified GWAS were performed for hematuria, as well as in MVP, consisting of >90% male participants (Supplementary Table S9). We focused our replication on cohorts of European genetic ancestry, but we also report for reference the summary statistics in the African and admixed American subsets of MVP, which have smaller sample sizes than the European subset (Supplementary Table S9). We considered a variant to replicate if the P was < 0.01 and the direction of effect (positive or negative) was consistent.

In Geisinger MyCode, the lead variant in COL4A4 (rs35138315) was not significant in the male-specific GWAS (effect size of main genetic effect [BetaG] ± standard error [SE] = 0.85 ± 0.85, P = 0.31); whereas in the female-specific GWAS, it was significant (BetaG ± SE = 1.65 ± 0.53, P = 0.002). A likely explanation for the absence of significance in Geisinger MyCode male-specific GWAS is the lack of power, because there were overall 35 carriers of the effect allele (A-allele) in the cohort. The low number of carriers in MyCode compared with UKB was expected because the A-allele is known to be at higher frequency in the British population (due to a founder effect).30 In contrast, in MVP, that same COL4A4 variant was the lead single-nucleotide polymorphism in the region (BetaG ± SE = 1.55 ± 0.03, P = 1.08 × 10−19), and with the same direction of effect. The conditionally independent signal on chromosome 2 (COL4A3 rs4305262) identified in the female-specific UKB GWAS, was significant only in the female-specific analysis in Geisinger MyCode (female-specific P = 1.48 × 10−7); whereas in the male-specific, it was not significant (P = 0.72). In addition, the effect sizes for COL4A3 rs4305262 in the analyses across male-specific cohorts (as well as in MVP) were of similar magnitude, whereas the effect sizes across the female-specific cohorts were of larger magnitude than across the male-specific cohorts.

The signal we identified on COL4A2 for females (rs1927355), was similarly replicated in Geisinger MyCode, in which it was genome-wide significant in the female-specific GWAS (BetaG ± SE = 0.08 ± 0.01, P = 9.25 × 10−10), and nominally significant in the male-specific GWAS (BetaG ± SE = 0.05 ± 0.02, P = 0.01). In MVP, this variant had a similar effect size as in the male-specific UKB GWAS, and it was significant (BetaG ± SE = 0.03 ± 0.01, P = 1.02 × 10−5).

The signal we identified in LGALS9C in the male-specific UKB GWAS was not replicated (P > 0.05) in either replication cohort despite having estimated perfect power to detect the association in MVP. We discarded the possibility of this signal to be a UKB-specific signal because of comparable allele frequencies across cohorts (Supplementary Results; Supplementary Table S10). Therefore, it is likely that this signal is a false positive, and we therefore excluded it from downstream analyses.

Finally, the signal in CCDC97 was genome-wide significant in Geisinger MyCode, in both sexes, as well as in MVP (P < 5 × 10−8), with the same direction of effect (Supplementary Table S9).

Hematuria Prevalence Increases by Age in Both Sexes

In the White British subset of the UKB, the prevalence of hematuria in females is 3%, whereas it is 5% in males. We stratified the prevalence of hematuria by age deciles, using the age at recruitment, for males and females separately, to investigate how prevalence varies across adulthood. We observed that hematuria prevalence increases with age across both males and females, but the magnitude of increase for males is higher than for females (Figure 3). The age range for males and females in the cohort is similar (female range: 40–71 years; male range: 39–73 years), as well as the sample size per age group (Supplementary Figure S2). In addition, we identified a significant effect of the interaction between age and sex for hematuria (Betaage∗sex ± SE = 0.02 ± 0.002; P = 1.31 × 10−11) (Supplementary Methods; Supplementary Table S11). Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with urolithiasis, or urologic or gynecologic malignancy ICD codes were consistent (Supplementary Figure S3; Supplementary Table S12; Supplementary Information). Finally, stratification of females by menopause status did not reveal significant differences at the variants assessed (chr2: COL4A4 rs35138315 and COL4A3 rs4305262, chr13: COL4A2 rs1927355, chr19: CCDC97 rs73045269; Supplementary Figure S4A), suggesting that menopause has minimal effects on the increase in hematuria prevalence across age groups at these loci. However, we acknowledge the relatively small sample size in the premenopausal group in the UKB, which could potentially affect the statistical power (Supplementary Table S1), as well as the likely under ascertained cases in young female age groups because of the nature of the ICD-code phenotype. For comparison, male stratification by age groups (i.e., aged < 50 years and aged ≥ 50 years) showed a stronger effect in the older compared with the younger age group at the COL4A4 rs35138315 locus (P = 0.03) (Supplementary Figure S4B). Assessment of Sex-Differential Effects in Hematuria Reveals Potential Genetic Differences in Autosomal Type IV Collagen Genes

Our primary approach consisted of performing a genotype-by-sex interaction analysis for the lead significant variants identified in the sex-specific association analysis (Table 2), to assess the presence of an effect of sex on hematuria (because we did not have sufficient statistical power to perform a genome-wide interaction analysis; Methods). In addition, we included the lead variants of the genome-wide significant signals from the sex-combined hematuria GWAS,17 as well as conditionally independent variants in the COL4A3/COL4A4 region (Methods). Of the 9 unique variants assessed, the variants in chromosomes 2 (COL4A3 rs4305262) and 13 (COL4A2 rs1927355) had a significant interaction effect (P < 0.006; Table 3), supporting the presence of a sex-differential signal. In contrast, the interaction term for the other 7 variants was not significant. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with urolithiasis, or urologic or gynecologic malignancy ICD codes were consistent (Supplementary Table S13; Supplementary Information). Association of Sex Hormone Levels With Hematuria in the UKB

Given the observed sex-differential signals for hematuria, we performed observational exploratory analyses in the White British subset of the UKB, to assess if there is a relationship between hormone levels and hematuria. We examined estradiol levels in females, including stratification by menopause status, and testosterone levels in both sexes and stratified by sex. When stratifying by menopause status, we observed a significant association between detectable estradiol and hematuria in the premenopausal group (odds ratio [95% confidence interval] = 0.58 [0.51–0.67]; P < 1 × 10−16), suggesting that higher estradiol levels are associated with decreased hematuria risk, but not in the postmenopausal group (P > 0.05). Analysis using quantitative estradiol support the results of a significant association between estradiol and hematuria in the premenopausal group (Betahematuria ± SE = −166.9 ± 20.97; P = 1.73 × 10−15) (Supplementary Table S14; Supplementary Results).

We identified a significant relationship between testosterone levels and hematuria status in males only (Betahematuria ± SE = −0.21 ± 0.04; P = 2.08 × 10−7), suggesting that lower testosterone levels are associated with a higher risk of hematuria. In the sex-combined analyses, we observed a significant relationship between detectable testosterone and hematuria status, with the same direction of effect (odds ratiotestosterone [95% confidence interval] = 0.91 [0.85–0.96]; P = 0.002), and significant differences between the sexes (odds ratiotestosterone∗sex [95% confidence interval] = 0.37 [0.26–0.53]; P = 9.91 × 10−8) with a stronger negative association between testosterone levels and hematuria status in males than in females (Supplementary Table S14). However, sensitivity analyses do not support the observation of significant differences between the sexes (P = 0.20) (Supplementary Results).

Discussion

In the present study, we performed sex-specific GWAS of hematuria and tested for sex-differential effects using multiple approaches. This approach has led to elucidating sex-differential genetic effects at autosomal loci and uncovered a novel locus for hematuria. By performing sex-interaction analyses of hematuria in the UKB, we identified a signal on chromosome 13 within COL4A2, and a signal on chromosome 2 within COL4A3, with sex-differential effects, corresponding to a larger effect size in females than in males, but with consistent direction of effect. In an independent cohort, Geisinger MyCode, we observed a significant sex-differential effect, with a larger effect in females than in males at the COL4A3 variant, and in the same direction of effect across sexes. In addition, in the largely-male MVP cohort, the effect size of the top variant at COL4A2 aligned with the effect size observed in our male-specific UKB and Geisinger MyCode analyses. However, we note that the effect sizes, as commonly seen for genetic variant-trait associations, are small for both COL4A2 and COL4A3. As expected, there is incomplete penetrance of rs4305262 (COL4A3) and rs1927355 (COL4A2) in UKB: penetrance is 4% for both variants, compared with rs35138315 on COL4A4 (i.e., Alport syndrome pathogenic variant), which has an overall penetrance of 20%.

Sex-specific genome-wide rare variant analyses of hematuria was performed, in which we identified a COL4A4 signal across both sexes, the effect being driven mostly by the Ser959Ter rare variant (rs35138315) in all analyses (sex-combined female-only and male-only). Given that the pleiotropic COL4A2 region has been associated across multiple kidney and cardiovascular traits with female-biased effects, we performed female-specific colocalization analyses and identified a common putative causal variant across hematuria and cardiovascular traits (Supplementary Methods, Supplementary Results, Supplementary Figures S11, S12–S14; Supplementary Tables S22–24).

A limitation of the present work is that our primary and replication cohorts were of European genetic ancestry, and our results may not be transferable to individuals of other genetic ancestries. Even though we reported replication results in the African and admixed American cohorts of MVP, these are mostly underpowered because of small sample sizes. Owing to limited statistical power to detect sex-by-genotype interactions of modest effect at genome-wide significance, we assessed interactions at associated loci present in any of the hematuria GWAS stratifications (sex-combined, female- or male-specific). Further investigation of sex-differential effects is warranted in larger sample sizes and in other genetic ancestries. Similarly, potential sex-differential effects via transcriptome-wide association study or proteome-wide association study may shed new insights as sample sizes of cell-type specific eQTL and pQTL data become larger. Finally, heterogeneity introduced because of differences in hematuria phenotyping across datasets (ICD code–based definitions in UKB and MVP vs. dipstick-based in Geisinger MyCode) could limit statistical power to replicate significant sex-differential effects. Notably, there are differences in hematuria prevalence between the UKB and MVP, suggesting that there may be additional unknown biases across biobanks based on geography or other factors (e.g., reimbursement practices) even when using the same phenotyping strategy.

It is known that both sex and gender differences can affect hematuria prevalence, as well as other related kidney disorders.3 In the present study we focused on genetically determined sex to assess potential differences between males (XY) and females (XX) using genetic information. However, we note that in the UKB there are 306 participants with discordant self-reported and chromosomal sex, which could partially be explained by differences between gender and chromosomal sex.32 We cannot discard the influence that gender may have on hematuria, but that remains outside the scope of the current study. Larger samples are needed to disentangle the independent effects of sex and gender in hematuria.

To the extent of our knowledge, the present study is the first to report significant sex-differential genetic effects of hematuria in type-IV collagen genes (COL4A3 and COL4A2), both with consistent direction, but larger effects in females than in males, noting that in Geisinger MyCode, only the COL4A3 signal was replicated. A previous study assessing sex differences in hematuria identified nominally significant heterogeneity between males and females in the chromosome 6 HLA region, confirmed by a sex by genotype interaction analysis.18 No sex differences were identified at other tested loci, such as COL4A3 or COL4A4, but the COL4A1 or COL4A2 locus was not assessed, because it was not genome-wide significant in the GWAS.18

COL4A2 encodes the α2 chain of type IV collagen, one of the ubiquitous type IV collagen proteins that constitutes basement membranes in many tissues.33 COL4A2 and COL4A1 reside end to end on chromosome 13 in humans. Pathogenic variants in these 2 genes have been described to be causal for vascular phenotypes including HANAC syndrome (hereditary angiopathy, nephropathy, aneurysms, and muscle cramps).34, 35, 36, 37

It is possible that COL4A1 or COL4A2 are influenced by sex hormones through complex interactions. Possible support is found from a study in mouse mesangial cells which demonstrated that TGF-β1 stimulates α1(IV) transcription through an interaction with Sp1 transcription factor, and that estradiol acts as an antagonist of this stimulatory effect, possibly by interacting with Sp1.38 In summary, our results at this locus suggest that the effect of COL4A2 on hematuria is larger in females than in males in the UKB, but the signal was not replicated in Geisinger MyCode. Given the sex-differential effects observed at this locus for other traits (i.e., blood pressure), further investigation is required to provide a definitive conclusion.

By contrast, COL4A3 and COL4A4 are more restricted in their expression, localizing to basement membranes in the glomerulus, ear, and eye. In GTEx v8 there are no evident gene expression differences in COL4A3/COL4A4 between males and females across tissues.31 They reside end to end on chromosome 2 and pathogenic variants in these 2 genes are responsible for Alport syndrome. Although sex differences in autosomal forms of Alport syndrome have not been rigorously evaluated, individuals heterozygous for pathogenic variants in COL4A3 and COL4A4 are reported to display broad clinical spectrum. One report suggests that a kidney-specific polygenic risk score may account for this variability39; however, our results suggest sex may also be a contributor.

One of the factors that could differentially influence the genetic effect across tissues and between sexes is the effect of sex hormones. Previous studies in mammalian models and in humans have shown that sex hormones play an important role in maintaining kidney function, and that estrogen has a protective role in kidney health.40, 41, 42, 43 Our observational analysis of estradiol levels in the UKB support its potential protective effect for hematuria in premenopausal females, although further confounding factors can bias these exploratory analyses (e.g., body mass index, medication use, and comorbidities).

In conclusion, we used robust computational approaches and replication cohorts to identify novel sex-differential autosomal loci associated with hematuria status. As the field of personalized medicine moves forward, it is essential to identify sex-differential genetic effects to better design and deliver therapeutic strategies. Future work investigating the mechanisms by which sex-differential effects occur is necessary to elucidate the biological processes contributing to hematuria.
