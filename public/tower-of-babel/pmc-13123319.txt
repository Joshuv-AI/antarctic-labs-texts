Isolation of Leishmania infantum from a red fox (Vulpes vulpes) and vector competence of Phlebotomus perniciosus
Authors: Marcos Antonio Bezerra-Santos, Mariaelisa Carbonara, Renata Fagundes-Moreira, Antonio Camarda, Petr Volf, Filipe Dantas-Torres, Domenico Otranto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) have been reported as potential reservoirs of Leishmania infantum in the Mediterranean region; however, their capacity to infect sand fly vectors has not been previously demonstrated. Here, we isolated L. infantum from lymph node aspirates of a naturally infected red fox and, for the first time, performed xenodiagnosis in this wild canid species using Phlebotomus perniciosus sand flies. Promastigotes were detected in the guts of 92.1% (n = 151/164) dissected sand fly females, with a heavy parasite load observed in 58.9% (n = 89/151) and moderate in 27.1% (n = 41/151). Parasites retrieved from sand flies were molecularly confirmed as L. infantum. Isolation from the lymph node aspirate was successful, with promastigotes first detected after 4 days of culturing, followed by a marked increase thereafter. These findings demonstrate that L. infantum can persist and visceralize in red foxes and that symptomatic foxes could be highly infectious to P. perniciosus. Collectively, the results support the role of red foxes as reservoirs of L. infantum in endemic areas and highlight their relevance for disease control programs within a One Health framework.

Introduction

The geographical expansion of red foxes (Vulpes vulpes) into many urban and periurban areas [1], [2] has resulted in increased interactions with domestic animals and humans [3], [4]. This proximity raises concerns regarding the transmission dynamics of zoonotic pathogens, as these wild canids are known to host a broad spectrum of parasites of public health importance, including Leishmania infantum [5]. This protozoan species is the etiological agent of zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis, which is transmitted by sand flies of the genus Phlebotomus in the Old World and by species from several genera, previously grouped under the genus Lutzomyia, in the Americas [6], [7].

Dogs are the primary reservoir host for L. infantum, playing a central role in its zoonotic transmission cycle [8], [9], [10]. However, a growing body of evidence suggests that red foxes may also serve as reservoirs of L. infantum, potentially contributing to the parasite's transmission cycle [11], [12]. Molecular surveys have reported prevalence rates of up to 74% in red fox populations, which indicates a high level of exposure to the parasite in this species [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]. Nevertheless, the reservoir competence of red foxes remains largely anecdotal, as it requires proof that a host can effectively transmit the parasite to competent sand fly vectors, thus sustaining the parasite life cycle [19], [20]. Without such evidence, the role of a given host species in maintaining parasite circulation within an endemic area remains ambiguous [8], [21], [22], [23].

In this context, xenodiagnosis is a direct and sensitive method for assessing host infectiousness to competent vectors, as it evaluates parasite development in the sand fly gut following a natural blood meal [9], [24], [25], [26], [27]. In addition, isolating parasites from a vertebrate host is a critical step in confirming an active infection and for genotyping strains across different host species (e.g., humans, dogs, and other animals) to elucidate their roles in the transmission cycle [11]. For instance, L. infantum has been routinely isolated from different canine samples (e.g., bone marrow, lymph node, and spleen aspirates) for elucidating the pathogenic mechanisms [28], [29], [30], [31]. In contrast, direct culture isolation of L. infantum in red foxes has been achieved long ago in two animals from France using bone marrow samples [32]. In addition, a Leishmania isolate obtained from a dead red fox isolated in vivo (i.e., by a hamster inoculation) was characterized based on isoenzyme analysis [33].

In the present study, we isolated L. infantum from the lymph node of a naturally infected red fox and performed xenodiagnosis using P. perniciosus sand flies, providing definitive evidence of the reservoir potential of this widespread canid species.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

A female red fox was admitted to a Wildlife Rescue Centre in the Apulia region (southern Italy) due to pathological conditions (i.e., intense pruritus, apathy, cachexia, pale mucous membranes, diffuse alopecia, diffuse desquamative and ulcerative skin lesions, and generalized peripheral lymphadenomegaly). The fox was kept for clinical stabilization, treatment, and eventual release, in accordance with current wildlife protection guidelines. During clinical examination, blood samples and lymph node aspirates (from enlarged popliteal lymph nodes) were collected from the fox. All procedures performed herein complied with Italian wildlife rescue regulations and animal welfare standards (Ethical Committee of the University of Bari, approval number 59/24).

Diagnosis of Leishmania infantum infection

Diagnosis was established using direct and indirect tests, including serological assays (e.g., indirect immunofluorescence antibody test [IFAT] and SNAP Leish 4Dx® Test) and molecular assays. For the latter, genomic DNA of blood and lymph node samples was extracted using a commercial kit (DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed for detection of Leishmania donovani complex kDNA minicircle fragment [34], followed by conventional PCR (cPCR) targeting the Leishmania ITS-1 region [35] and the hsp70 gene [36]. Amplicons from cPCRs were purified and sequenced in both directions using the same primers as for PCR amplification, with BigDye Terminator v3.1 chemistry on a 3130 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Resulting sequences were edited and analyzed using BioEdit software version 7.7 [37] and compared with those available in the GenBank database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST; http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) for species identification.

Isolation of Leishmania infantum

For parasite isolation, lymph node aspirates and buffy coat samples were incubated at 26 °C in a solid Novy-MacNeal-Nicolle (NNN) base overlaid with complete Schneider's Drosophila Medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), antibiotics, and 5% human urine according to standard protocols [38], [39]. Cultures were evaluated weekly and considered negative after 35 days of inoculation. Positive cultures were cryopreserved at −80 °C.

Xenodiagnosis, sand fly dissection and microscopic examination

Xenodiagnosis was conducted using laboratory-reared, Leishmania-free P. perniciosus females maintained under controlled insectary conditions [40]. For each selected anatomical region of the red fox (i.e., internal ear and right and/or left flank in areas with alopecia), 50 adult females were allowed to feed for 20 min using a feeding chamber applied to the skin (Fig. 1). The animal was not anesthetized due to its critical clinical condition, which increased the risks associated with anesthesia. Engorged females were separated and maintained at 26 °C, 75–80% relative humidity, and a 12:12 h light-dark cycle, with access to a sterile sucrose solution. Flies were kept for 5–8 days post-blood meal (PBM) to allow parasite development. Mortality was monitored daily, and dead specimens were collected for molecular detection of Leishmania parasites.Fig. 1Blood feeding of Phlebotomus perniciosus sand flies on a Leishmania infantum naturally infected red fox (Vulpes vulpes).Fig. 1

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Epitools - Epidemiological Calculators (available online at http://epitools.ausvet.com.au) and GraphPad Prism® software version 9.5. Significance was set at p < 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the Wilson method. Infectiousness was calculated as the proportion of fed females that became infected, while feeding success was assessed as the proportion of fully engorged females. Fisher's exact test was used to compare the differences between blood meals at different time points, and infection intensity between D5 and D8 PBM was compared using the chi-square test.

Results

The red fox scored positive for L. infantum by IFAT (antibody titer 1:10,240) and by the SNAP Leish 4Dx® Test. At qPCR, both blood (Ct value: 27) and lymph node aspirates (Ct value: 17) were positive. Similarly, both samples scored positive by cPCR, with BLAST analysis of the obtained sequences showing 99.0% (ITS-1 reference strain: AM502245) and 99.6% (hsp70 reference strain: XM_001470287) nucleotide identity with L. infantum sequences from GenBank. Both sequences from this study were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers PX497855 for ITS1 and PX557817 for hsp70.

Feeding success of P. perniciosus during xenodiagnosis (Supplementary video 1) was 93.0% (n = 186/200; 95% CI: 89.0–96.0). In total, 88.1% (n = 164/186; 95% CI: 83.0–92.0) of blood-fed sand flies survived, and L. infantum promastigotes were observed in the guts of 92.1% (n = 151/164; 95% CI: 87.0–95.0) of dissected females, with heavy parasite load recorded in 58.9% (n = 89/151; 95% CI: 51.0–66.0), moderate in 27.1% (n = 41/151; 95% CI: 21.0–35.0), and light in 13.9% (n = 21/151; 95% CI: 9.0–20.0) (Table 1; Fig. 2; Supplementary video 2). Comparison of infectiousness between D5 and D8 PBM using Fisher's exact test showed no statistically significant differences between time points (OR = 1.62; 95% CI: 0.48–5.47; p = 0.58), with no difference in infection intensity (p = 0.95; Table 1).

Parasites retrieved from the sand flies were molecularly confirmed as L. infantum by both cPCR and qPCR. In the latter, Ct values ranged from 13.0 to 35.5, with a mean of 26.6. In addition, L. infantum DNA was detected in 17 out of the 22 blood-fed females that died before dissection (Ct range: 27.2–28.8; mean: 28.0). Discussion

Based on xenodiagnosis results, the L. infantum-naturally infected and symptomatic red fox herein evaluated was highly infectious to P. perniciosus. The majority of sand fly female blood-fed on the fox developed heavy infections, further confirming the vector competence of this sand fly species. These findings suggest that red foxes can serve as reservoirs of L. infantum, contributing to the maintenance and transmission cycles of the parasite. In parallel, the isolation of L. infantum from the infected red fox's lymph node provides additional evidence that this wild canid can harbor viable, infective parasites, reinforcing their reservoir status.

The role of red foxes in the transmission cycle of L. infantum has long been debated [11]. Although these wild canids often test positive through molecular or serological assays, such diagnostic methods alone cannot definitively establish their reservoir competence [11], [43]. Under the above circumstances, xenodiagnosis using P. perniciosus females showed a high infection rate (i.e., 92.1%), indicating that this wild canid species can be highly infectious to sand flies. Comparable xenodiagnostic data have been reported for P. perniciosus sand flies fed on naturally infected dogs, with infection rates ranging from 2.8% to 92.0% (mean of 39.0%) in a group of 16 dogs [19], and from 6.0% to 100% (mean 52.5%) in a group of 24 dogs [44]. In cats, a single study reported an infection rate of 21.0% in P. perniciosus following feeding on a naturally infected animal [20]. Furthermore, xenodiagnostic studies on naturally infected wild lagomorphs implicated in a human leishmaniasis outbreak in Madrid [45] demonstrated infection rates of up to 2.0% and 10.6% in P. perniciosus fed on apparently healthy rabbits and hares, respectively [46], [47]. In contrast, studies conducted in Brazil with asymptomatic crab-eating foxes (Cerdocyon thous) showed a low level of infectiousness to Lutzomyia longipalpis, suggesting a minor contribution of these wild canids to L. infantum in this country [48].

The above findings suggest that symptomatic dogs and red foxes may be similarly infectious to sand fly vectors, exhibiting comparable levels of infectiousness. Nonetheless, infection rates are likely influenced by parasite burden, particularly the number of amastigote forms present in the skin, which tend to be higher in severely diseased animals [49]. This aspect is especially relevant in endemic areas such as Brazil, where control strategies based on removal of infected dogs have failed to reduce visceral leishmaniasis cases [10], [50]. Considering that the red fox examined in this study was clinically ill, further xenodiagnosis studies evaluating the infectiousness of asymptomatic and apparently healthy L. infantum-infected red foxes to P. perniciosus are advocated to better clarify the epidemiological role of this wild canid in parasite transmission.

The isolation of L. infantum from a red fox further supports the involvement of this species in the transmission cycles of zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis. Multiple studies have isolated L. infantum from dogs and humans [29], [51], as well as from P. perniciosus sand flies [52] in southern Italy. Collectively, these results provide evidence of active transmission among foxes, dogs, and sand fly vectors within the same geographic area.

The confirmed reservoir competence of red foxes suggests that wildlife surveillance programs should integrate L. infantum monitoring in red fox populations, especially in interface areas where wildlife, domestic animals, and humans interact frequently [10], [11]. Moreover, recognizing the role of red foxes in the leishmaniasis transmission calls for their inclusion in comprehensive One Health control strategies that incorporate wildlife monitoring and broader ecological considerations, such as landscape changes, urban/wildlife interfaces, sand fly habitat suitability, and trophic interactions influencing host abundance. While there is currently no evidence supporting interventions (e.g., elimination, treatment) targeting wild reservoirs to control zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis, wild hosts such as red foxes may serve as valuable sentinels of infection risk in specific areas. Therefore, the findings of this study are highly relevant and can inform public health policies aimed to control zoonotic visceral leishmaniasis in the Mediterranean region.
