Privacy for Free in the Overparameterized Regime
Simone Bombari, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Differentially private gradient descent (DP-GD) is a popular algorithm to train deep learning models with provable guarantees on the privacy of the training data. In the last decade, the problem of understanding its performance cost with respect to standard GD has received remarkable attention from the research community, which formally derived upper bounds on the excess population risk ℛ P \mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}} in different learning settings. However, existing bounds typically degrade with over-parameterization, i.e. , as the number of parameters p p gets larger than the number of training samples n n – a regime which is ubiquitous in current deep-learning practice. As a result, the lack of theoretical insights leaves practitioners without clear guidance, leading some to reduce the effective number of trainable parameters to improve performance, while others use larger models to achieve better results through scale. In this work, we show that in the popular random features model with quadratic loss, for any sufficiently large p p , privacy can be obtained for free, i.e. , | ℛ P | = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \left|\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}\right|=o(1) , not only when the privacy parameter ε \varepsilon has constant order, but also in the strongly private setting ε = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \varepsilon=o(1) . This challenges the common wisdom that over-parameterization inherently hinders performance in private learning.

Introduction

Deep learning models have been shown to be vulnerable to various attacks directed to retrieve information about the training dataset
(
Fredrikson et al., 2015
;
Carlini et al., 2019
;
Carlini et al., 2021
;
Haim et al., 2022
)
. This vulnerability is a concern when sensitive, private data is included in the training set of a learning pipeline. To allow developers and model providers to still utilize these data,
differential privacy
(DP)
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
consolidated as the golden standard for privacy in learning algorithms. In fact, this framework comes with algorithmic methods
(
Shokri and Shmatikov, 2015
;
Abadi et al., 2016
)
that provide formal protection guarantees for each individual sample in the training set, which becomes safeguarded (up to some level) by any adversary with access to the trained model and the rest of the dataset.

Neural networks can be trained in a differentially private fashion via algorithms such as DP (stochastic) gradient descent (DP-GD)
(
Abadi et al., 2016
)
. This method involves minimizing the training loss with additional “tweaks” to guarantee protection, which typically boil down to
(i) clipping
the per-sample gradients before averaging,
(ii)
perturbing the parameters updates with
random noise
, and
(iii)
limiting the number of training iterations with
early stopping
(see Algorithm
1
).
While these adjustments provide privacy guarantees, they often come with a performance cost with respect to standard (stochastic) GD algorithms
(
Tramer and Boneh, 2021
;
Kurakin et al., 2022
)
. The impact on performance tends to worsen as the privacy requirements become stronger, as this requires greater perturbations to the gradients. Thus, private training of deep learning models involves carefully tuning additional hyper-parameters, e.g., the clipping constant, the magnitude of the noise, and the number of training iterations, in order to reach the best performance for a given privacy requirement. Notably, this comes at a remarkable computational cost, also considering the higher training times and memory loads that DP optimization has with respect to standard GD algorithms, mainly due to the computation of the per-sample gradients
(
De et al., 2022
)
.

The challenging problem of optimizing neural networks with an assigned privacy guarantee has motivated a thriving field of research proposing novel architectures and training algorithms
(
Tramer and Boneh, 2021
;
Papernot et al., 2021
;
De et al., 2022
)
. Concurrently, theoretical studies have emerged with the scope of better understanding and quantifying the
privacy-utility
trade-offs in different learning settings. Here,
privacy
is often defined via the pair of parameters
(\varepsilon,\delta)
: the impact of a single data point on the output of the algorithm is controlled by
\varepsilon
with probability
1-\delta
, see Definition
3.1
for the formal introduction of
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. In order to provide meaningful protection, practitioners pick constant-order values of
\varepsilon
,
e.g.
,
\varepsilon\in\{1,2,4,8\}
, and
\delta<1/n
, where
n
is the number of training samples
(
Bassily et al., 2019
)
.
The
utility
, in this context, is typically measured as the degradation in generalization performance of the DP-trained solution
\theta^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
compared to a non-private baseline
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where
p
denotes the number of parameters of the model. In particular, considering the standard supervised setting and denoting by
(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}
an input-label pair distributed accordingly to the data distribution
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
, the
excess population risk
is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}}\left[\ell(x,y,\theta^{p})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}}\left[\ell(x,y,\theta^{*})\right],

(1.1)

where
\ell\left(x,y,\theta\right)
is the loss over the sample
(x,y)
of the model evaluated in
\theta
. Intuitively, the excess population risk worsens with more stringent privacy requirements on
\theta^{p}
(
i.e.
, with smaller values of
\varepsilon
and
\delta
), and a rich line of work spanning over a decade has investigated this trade-off in various settings, see e.g.
(
Kifer et al., 2012
;
Jain and Thakurta, 2014
;
Bassily et al., 2014
;
Bassily et al., 2019
;
Song et al., 2021
;
Varshney et al., 2022
;
Brown et al., 2024
)
.

Despite this flurry of research, existing results are unable to address the over-parameterized regime,
i.e.
,
p=\Omega(n)
. In fact, in this regime, taking
\varepsilon
of constant order (
e.g.
,
\varepsilon=1
) typically makes the upper bound on the excess population risk to read (at best)
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
, which is vacuous as the performance of a trivial model that constantly outputs zero is of the same order. For additional details, we refer to the discussion on related works in Section
2
. This result is sometimes understood via the qualitative argument that the noise introduced at each iteration by DP-GD increases with the dimension of the parameter space. More specifically, if each entry of
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is perturbed with standard Gaussian noise, the
\ell_{2}
norm of the perturbation scales as
\sqrt{p}
, which suggests that the privacy-utility trade-off worsens as
p
grows
(
Yu et al., 2021a
;
Mehta et al., 2022
)
. This motivated a line of research that focuses on DP algorithms acting over lower dimensional subspaces, both in theory
(
Zhou et al., 2021
;
Asi et al., 2021
)
and in practice
(
Yu et al., 2021b
;
Golatkar et al., 2022
)
.

However, reducing the dimensionality of architectures is in stark contrast with the current trend of increasingly large networks in deep learning. In the non-private setting, the apparent contradiction between excellent generalization of over-parameterized models and classical bias-variance trade-off has been the subject of intense investigation, which has unveiled phenomena such as benign overfitting
(
Bartlett et al., 2021
;
Bartlett et al., 2020
)
and double descent
(
Belkin et al., 2019
;
Mei and Montanari, 2022
)
.

Recently, larger models have also shown benefits on down-stream tasks requiring private fine-tuning
(
Li et al., 2022b
;
Yu et al., 2022
)
, which motivated theoretical studies that provide refined privacy-utility trade-offs in this specific setting
(
Wang et al., 2024
;
Li et al., 2022a
)
.
Perhaps surprisingly, the recent work by
De et al. (2022)
gives evidence of the benefits of scale even in the absence of public pre-training data, as the generalization performance is shown to improve with model size on datasets such as CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, following an accurate hyper-parameter search.
In Figure
1
, we investigate the interplay between privacy and over-parameterization in a simpler and more controllable setting: training a 2-layer, fully-connected ReLU network on MNIST (
n=6\cdot 10^{4}
) with DP-GD (see Algorithm
1
). We vary the network width, spanning both the under-parameterized (
p\sim 10^{4}
) and over-parameterized (
p\sim 10^{7}
) regime, questioning if the algorithm suffers as the number of parameters grows larger.
The plot shows that this is not the case: the test accuracy initially increases until the network is wide enough (
p\sim 10^{5}
), and then plateaus without showing any decreasing trend in the right side of the figure.

Then, are larger models truly disadvantageous when doing private optimization? How can the result in the simple experiment in Figure
1
be reconciled with existing theoretical work on privacy-utility trade-offs in the over-parameterized regime? How can one solve the disagreement between the experimental evidence in
De et al. (2022)
and the widespread idea that more parameters hurt the performance of DP-GD?
These questions call for a principled understanding of private training in deep learning. This in particular amounts to
(i)
establishing meaningful generalization guarantees (i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}
significantly smaller than trivial guessing at test time) for over-parameterized models, and
(ii)
characterizing how the hyperparameters introduced by DP-GD (clipping constant, noise magnitude, and number of training iterations) affect performance.

1.1
Main contribution

In this work, we provide privacy-utility guarantees
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=o(1)
under over-parameterization – not only when
\varepsilon
has constant order, but also in the strongly private setting
\varepsilon=o(1)
. We frame this result as achieving
privacy for free
in the over-parameterized regime.

We consider a family of machine learning models where the number of parameters
p
is a free variable that can be significantly larger than the number of samples
n
and the input dimension
d
, just as in the experimental setup of Figure
1
. This excludes, for example, linear regression, where
p=d

(
Varshney et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
;
Brown et al., 2024
)
. Specifically, we focus on the widely studied
random features
(RF) model
(
Rahimi and Recht, 2007
)
with quadratic loss, which takes the form

\ell(x,y,\theta)=\left(f_{\textup{RF}}(x,\theta)-y\right)^{2},\qquad f_{\textup{RF}}(x,\theta)=\phi(Vx)^{\top}\theta,

(1.2)

where
f_{\textup{RF}}
is a generalized linear model,
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
a non-linearity applied component-wise to the vector
Vx\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
a random weight matrix. The RF model can be regarded as a 2-layer fully-connected neural network, where only the second layer
\theta
is trained and the hidden weights
V
are frozen at initialization. Its appeal comes from the fact that it is simple enough to be analytically tractable and, at the same time, rich enough to exhibit properties occurring in more complex deep learning models
(
Bartlett et al., 2021
;
Mei and Montanari, 2022
)
.

The DP-trained solution
\theta^{p}
is obtained via DP-GD as reported in Algorithm
1
. Differently from
(
Abadi et al., 2016
)
, we do not perform stochastic batching of the data when aggregating the gradients. The cost of privacy typically refers to the performance gap between an algorithm that enforces
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP and one that does not. Thus, we set the non-private baseline
\theta^{*}
in (
1.1
) to be the solution obtained by (non-private) GD. In the RF model, the test error of this baseline has been characterized precisely in a recent line of work
(
Mei and Montanari, 2022
;
Mei et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2024
)
and, for a class of data distributions,
\theta^{*}
also corresponds to the Bayes-optimal solution given by
\textup{arg\,min}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}}\left[\ell(x,y,\theta)\right]
. At this point, we can present an informal version of our result (formally stated as Theorem
1
).

Theorem

(main result – informal)
.

Consider the RF model in (
1.2
) with input dimension
d
and number of features
p
. Let
n
be the number of training samples and
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}
be defined according to (
1.1
), where
\theta^{*}
is the solution of GD and
\theta^{p}
is the
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private solution of DP-GD (Algorithm
1
).
Then, for all sufficiently over-parameterized models, under some technical conditions, the following holds with high probability

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}\right|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d}{n\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{d^{3/2}}}\right)=o(1).

(1.3)

In words, in the regime
d\ll n\ll d^{3/2}
– considered e.g. in
(
Mei et al., 2022
;
Bombari et al., 2023
)
, see Assumption
4.1
for details – we show that

\left|\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}\right|=o(1)
as long as
\varepsilon\gg d/n
. In fact, when
d\ll n\ll d^{3/2}
and
\varepsilon\gg d/n
, the three terms
\frac{d}{n\varepsilon}
,
\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}
and
\sqrt{\frac{n}{d^{3/2}}}
appearing in the RHS of (
1.3
) become
o(1)
. We make two observations:
(i)
as
d\ll n
, our result guarantees vanishing excess population risk, even with a strong privacy requirement
\varepsilon=o(1)
;
(ii)
the bound in (
1.3
) does not depend on the number of parameters
p

and Assumption
4.1
only requires a lower bound on
p
,
which allows for arbitrarily large over-parameterization in the model. As typical in the related literature, the dependence of
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}
on
\delta
is only logarithmic and, therefore, it is neglected in the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\cdot)
that hides poly-logarithmic factors in
\delta
and
n
.

Achieving
privacy for free
might seem surprising, so we now comment on the intuition behind the result. In the regime
n\gg d
, there is a surplus of samples that can be used to learn privately. In fact, in our model, the test error of (non-private) GD plateaus when
n
is between
d
and
d^{3/2}

(
Mei et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2024
)
, hence
\Theta(d)
samples are enough to achieve
utility
, with the remaining ones playing the role of achieving
privacy
.
The plateau in the test loss of GD has been shown for other kernel ridge regression models
(
Ghorbani et al., 2021
)
, and in RF it is exhibited when
d^{l}\ll n\ll d^{l+1}
for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
, as long as
p\gg n
, see Figure 1 of
Hu et al. (2024)
. We expect that DP-GD catches up with the performance of GD in any of these plateaus. However, as
n
approaches
d^{l+1}
, the test loss of GD sharply decreases and it is unclear whether DP-GD has the same rate of improvement.
This suggests that our result could be extended to the regime
d^{l}\ll n\ll d^{l+1}
with
p\gg n
. In the present paper, we focus on
d\ll n\ll d^{3/2}
and
p=\Omega(n^{2})
due to the additional challenges in the analysis of clipping, see the discussion after Lemma
6.5
in Section
6
.

Figure
2
displays
the intuition of having a surplus of samples
. There, we plot the test accuracy of a fixed model as the number of training samples
n
increases, both for GD and DP-GD
, keeping
p
fixed and much larger than
n
. In the left side of the figure, the performance of
\theta^{*}
sharply increases with
n
, while the private algorithms have very low utility. Moving towards the right side of the plot, the performance of GD tends to saturate, thus approaching the regime where there is a surplus of samples required to learn the task. Here, the extra samples are used to achieve privacy: the utility of DP-GD increases, eventually approaching the non-private baseline.

Our main result proves that, in the RF model, this heuristic picture holds for any sufficiently large degree of over-parameterization. The key technical hurdle is to formalize these intuitions.
Specifically, while
Mei et al. (2022)
;
Hu et al. (2024)
establish the asymptotic test error of
\theta^{*}
at convergence, we need a
non-asymptotic
control (in terms of
n,d,p
) on the
whole DP-GD trajectory
to understand the impact of clipping and early stopping. This in turn leads to a completely different proof strategy: we first frame DP-GD as the Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme of a stochastic differential equation (SDE); then, exploiting a number of concentration arguments (Dudley, Sudakov-Fernique, and Borell-TIS inequalities), we establish the size of a set containing the full trajectory of the SDE (with high probability), which allows to set the clipping constant; finally, we study the DP-trained solution
\theta^{p}
via an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, which allows to set the early stopping. Remarkably, our approach provides insights on the problem of hyper-parameter optimization. In fact, as a byproduct of our analysis, we establish the scaling of key hyper-parameters (clipping constant, noise magnitude and number of training iterations) in terms of the learning setup (input dimension
d
, number of parameters
p
) for random features.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss relevant related work. In Section
3
, we introduce the DP-GD algorithm, and we formally define the DP-trained solution
\theta^{p}
as well as the non-private baseline
\theta^{*}
that is considered in the excess population risk (
1.1
). In Section
4
, we formally state our main result (Theorem
1
). In Section
5
, we provide supporting numerical evidence, including a discussion on the role of hyper-parameters. In Section
6
, we provide the proof of Theorem
1
, deferring some technical parts to the appendix, and we conclude with a discussion in Section
7
.

2
Related work

Differentially private empirical risk minimization is the problem of minimizing the empirical risk
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(x_{i},y_{i},\theta)/n
while also ensuring privacy guarantees, where
(x_{i},y_{i})
represents an input-label pair in the training set and
\ell
is a loss function. Different algorithms have been proposed to tackle this problem, and the performance is measured through the excess risks of their solution
\theta^{p}
with respect to the baseline
\theta^{*}
. In particular, we can define the
excess empirical risk
as the difference in the training losses
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{E}}=\mathcal{L}(\theta^{p})-\mathcal{L}(\theta^{*})
, and the
excess population risk

\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}
as the difference in the test losses, see (
1.1
). These quantities capture the privacy-utility trade-off of the DP algorithm, as excess risks worsen with an increase in the privacy guarantees (
i.e.
, with smaller values of the privacy parameter
\varepsilon
). The seminal works by
Chaudhuri and Monteleoni (2008)
;
Chaudhuri et al. (2011)
quantitatively investigate this trade-off for strongly convex losses in the context of
\varepsilon
-DP (obtained by setting
\delta=0
), both for output and objective perturbation methods.

Constrained optimization.

Kifer et al. (2012)
extend the analysis of the objective perturbation by
Chaudhuri et al. (2011)
, providing a bound on the empirical excess risk of
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{E}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{p}/(n\varepsilon))
for constrained, strongly convex
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP optimization (see their Theorem 4). The subsequent work by
Bassily et al. (2014)
focuses on a variant of DP-GD previously studied by
Williams and Mcsherry (2010)
, and it bounds the excess empirical risk as
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{E}}=\beta L\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{p}/(n\varepsilon))
(see their Theorem 2.4.2).

Bassily et al. (2014)

n
. This makes the bounds of Theorem 2.4.2 therein larger by a factor

Here,

\beta
is the diameter of the (bounded) optimization domain
\mathcal{B}
,
L
is the Lipschitz constant of the loss with respect to the parameters of the model, and the baseline is defined as the training loss minimizer

. We note that, in this setting,

also provide an excess population risk bound of the form
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(p^{1/4}/\sqrt{n\varepsilon})

Unconstrained optimization.

In the setting of (convex) unconstrained optimization,
Jain and Thakurta (2014)
aim to remove the dependence of
p
in excess risk bounds for
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, which was present in earlier work by
Chaudhuri et al. (2011)
. They focus on generalized linear models (GLMs,
i.e.
,

\ell(x,y,\theta)=\ell(\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta,y)
where
\varphi
is the feature map), assume
L
-Lipschitz loss with a strongly convex regularization term, and bound the excess population risk with respect to the Bayes optimal solution
\theta^{*}
as
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(L\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n}\varepsilon)
. The more recent work by
Song et al. (2021)
also considers unconstrained convex Lipschitz GLMs, without the assumption on the strong convexity.
Using the same non-private baseline
\theta^{*}
and denoting by
M
the projector on the column space of
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}[\varphi(x)\varphi(x)^{\top}]
, the previous bound is improved by
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}]=L\left\|M\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}\,\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(1/\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\operatorname{\mathop{rank}}(M),n\right)}/(\varepsilon n)\right)
(see their Theorem 3.2).

Li et al. (2022a)
consider Lipschitz losses with
\ell_{2}
regularization and introduce the notion of restricted Lipschitz continuity, which gives dimension-free bounds. However, in the setting of GLMs, this approach leads to the same bounds as in
(
Song et al., 2021
)
. A similar approach is taken by
Ma et al. (2022)
that remove the dependence on
p
at the cost of an additional factor
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\tilde{H})
, where
\tilde{H}\succeq\sup_{\theta}\nabla^{2}_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{P}_{XY}}[\ell(x,y,\theta)]
. The work by
Arora et al. (2022)
considers GLMs with a loss that is not necessarily Lipschitz, and it also recovers the result of
Song et al. (2021)
in the Lipschitz setting with
\varepsilon=\Omega(1)
.

Importantly, even in the prototypical case of an RF model, existing bounds do not cover the over-parameterized regime. We now provide a detailed discussion explaining why this is the case. First, note that the RF model in (
1.2
) has a non-Lipschitz (quadratic) loss, which does not allow a direct application of most previous bounds. Thus, to ensure a fair comparison, one can estimate
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
and evaluate the Lipschitz constant of the training loss restricted to a bounded set
\mathcal{B}
with radius
\beta=\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
. This provides the scaling of the effective Lipschitz constant of the model, as if the optimization was bounded to the set
\mathcal{B}
. From our results in the later sections, it can be shown that
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{n/p})
, which in turn gives
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(x_{i},y_{i},\theta)\right\|_{2}=\Theta(n)
. This makes the bound by
Jain and Thakurta (2014)
read
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\sqrt{n}/\varepsilon)
, which is vacuous for
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
. A similar discussion holds for the bound by
Bassily et al. (2019)
. Furthermore, even by assuming that the loss function in (
1.2
) is Lipschitz, the result improves only by a factor
\sqrt{n}
, which gives
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1/\varepsilon)
and, therefore, a trivial bound when
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
. Similar considerations apply to the bound by
Song et al. (2021)
(and, thus, to the ones by
Li et al. (2022a)
;
Arora et al. (2022)
), since in this setting we have that
\left\|M\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}=\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
and
\operatorname{\mathop{rank}}(M)\geq n
. As concerns the result by
Ma et al. (2022)
, it can be verified that
\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits(\tilde{H})\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\Theta(p)
, which reintroduces the dependence on the number of parameters
p
, preventing an improvement upon
Jain and Thakurta (2014)
. Finally, while
Arora et al. (2022)
give bounds for quadratic losses, the reasoning resembles the one above on the effective Lipschitz constant in
\mathcal{B}
and it does not lead to an improvement with respect to the Lipschitz case. The detailed calculation of the quantities mentioned in this paragraph is deferred to Appendix
A
.

Linear regression.

This model reads
\ell(x,y,\theta)=(x^{\top}\theta-y)^{2}
, where
y=x^{\top}\theta^{*}+\sigma
,
\theta^{*}
is the ground-truth solution and
\sigma
independent label noise.
Varshney et al. (2022)
assume that each point is sampled from a standard Gaussian distribution and obtain a sample complexity of
d/\varepsilon
via gradient descent with adaptive clipping
(
Andrew et al., 2021
)
. This improves over
(
Wang, 2018
;
Cai et al., 2021
;
Milionis et al., 2022
)
, and it matches the result of earlier work by
Liu et al. (2022)
, which however proposes a computationally inefficient method.

relax the assumption on the data (sub-Weibull concentration) and improve the dependence of the sample complexity on the condition number of the covariance matrix.

Brown et al. (2024)
remove the need for adaptive clipping, focusing on a setting where the clipping constant is large enough so that clipping will most likely never happen.

3
Differentially Private Gradient Descent

The formal definition of DP builds on the notion of
adjacent datasets
. In our supervised learning setting, a dataset
D^{\prime}
is said to be adjacent to a dataset
D
if they differ by only one sample.

Definition 3.1

(
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP
(
Dwork et al., 2006
)
)
.

A randomized algorithm satisfies
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differential privacy if for any pair of adjacent datasets
D,D^{\prime}
, and for any subset of the parameters space
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(D)\in S\right)\leq e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta.

(3.1)

Here, the probability is with respect to the randomness induced by the algorithm, and the inequality has to hold uniformly on all the adjacent datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
.

Different methods have been proposed to train machine learning models while enforcing
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP. An approach is to rely on the scheme of
output perturbation
, which adds noise to the output of empirical risk minimization
(
Dwork et al., 2006
;
Chaudhuri et al., 2011
;
Kifer et al., 2012
)
. In deep learning, however, it is not easy to characterize the final parameters of the model, and therefore to estimate the minimal amount of noise required to guarantee protection. For this reason, a popular choice relies on DP-GD algorithms, which involve perturbing the individual updates during training time, thus providing privacy guarantees based on the size of the perturbation and the number of iterations
(
Song et al., 2013
;
Bassily et al., 2014
;
Abadi et al., 2016
)
. In this work, we consider Algorithm
1
, which can be regarded as a variant of the well-established method by
Abadi et al. (2016)
without stochastic batching. Its privacy guarantees are reported below.

Algorithm 1

DP-GD

0:

Number of iterations
T
, learning rate
\eta
, clipping constant
C_{\textup{clip}}
, noise
\sigma
, initialization
\theta_{0}
.

for

t\in[T]

do

Compute the gradient
g(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})\leftarrow\nabla_{\theta}\ell(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})

Clip the gradients
g_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})\leftarrow g(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})/\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits\left(1,\frac{\|g_{t}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})\|_{2}}{C_{\textup{clip}}}\right)

Aggregate the gradients
g_{\theta_{t-1}}\leftarrow\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}g_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1})

Update the model parameters adding noise
\theta_{t}=\theta_{t-1}-\eta g_{\theta_{t-1}}+\sqrt{\eta}\,\frac{2C_{\textup{clip}}}{n}\sigma\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})

Model parameters
\theta_{T}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

For any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,8\log(1/\delta))
, if we set

\sigma\geq\sqrt{\eta T}\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon},

(3.2)

then Algorithm
1
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private.

The proof follows the strategy of
Abadi et al. (2016)
and is based on the moment accountant method. As our formulation is slightly different, we provide the complete argument in Appendix
B
.

While existing work tackles the problem of understanding utility through stability arguments
(
Bassily et al., 2019
;
Song et al., 2021
)
, we propose a novel approach, which focuses on the implicit bias of DP-GD. The idea is to resort to a continuous process defined by Algorithm
1
in the limit of small learning rates
\eta\to 0
.
This has proven effective in the non-private setting, where the
gradient flow
equation helped understanding the properties of GD in over-parameterized models
(
Mei and Montanari, 2022
;
Mei et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2024
)
.
However, two challenges arise in the analysis of DP-GD:
(i)
the
clipping
of the per-sample gradients, and
(ii)
the injection of
noise
. To overcome the former, we define an auxiliary
clipped loss

\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\theta)
that incorporates clipping. As for the latter, we consider the stochastic differential equation (SDE) obtained by adding a Wiener process to the gradient flow. This motivates the scaling of the standard deviation of the injected noise as
\sqrt{\eta}
, as e.g. considered by
Wang et al. (2019)
.

More specifically, similarly to
Song et al. (2021)
, we note that the clipping step in Algorithm
1
can be reformulated as the optimization of the surrogate loss
\ell_{i,C_{\textup{clip}}}(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i})
, whose derivative for the
i
-th training sample reads

\ell_{i,C_{\textup{clip}}}^{\prime}(z)=\ell^{\prime}(z)\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(1,\frac{C_{\textup{clip}}}{\left|\ell^{\prime}(z)\right|{\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}}}\right).

(3.3)

This is formalized by the result below, whose proof is deferred to Appendix
B
.

Proposition 3.3
.

For any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and any clipping constant
C_{\textup{clip}}>0
, we have that

g_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta)=\nabla_{\theta}\ell_{i,C_{\textup{clip}}}\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta_{t-1}-y_{i}\right)
,
where
g_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta)
is defined in the clipping step of Algorithm
1
.

In other words, running Algorithm
1
is equivalent to running the same algorithm, without the clipping step, on the
clipped loss

\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell_{i,C_{\textup{clip}}}(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i})
.
Hence, we can write the
t
-th iteration of Algorithm
1
as a noisy GD update on
\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\theta)
,
i.e.
,

\theta_{t}-\theta_{t-1}=-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\theta_{t-1})+\sqrt{\eta}\frac{2C_{\textup{clip}}}{n}\sigma\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p}).

(3.4)

This update rule corresponds to the Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme of the SDE

\textup{d}\Theta(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\Theta(t))\textup{d}t+\frac{2C_{\textup{clip}}}{n}\sigma\,\textup{d}B(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\Theta(t))\textup{d}t+\Sigma\,\textup{d}B(t),

(3.5)

with discretization
\eta
(see Section 10.2 of
Kloeden and Platen (2011)
). Here,
B(t)
represents the standard
p
-dimensional Wiener process, we introduce the shorthand
\Sigma=2C_{\textup{clip}}\sigma/n
, and we set the initial condition of (
3.5
) to correspond to the initialization of Algorithm
1
,
i.e.

\Theta(0)=\theta_{0}
.
The strong convergence of the Euler-Maruyama method guarantees that, for any
\tau=\eta T
, the solution
\theta_{T}
of Algorithm
1
approaches the solution
\Theta(\tau)
of the SDE in (
3.5
) as the learning rate
\eta
gets smaller.

We note that previous work
(
Paquette et al., 2024
)
has considered a similar SDE to analyze the effects of stochastic batching, separating the dynamics in a gradient flow plus a Wiener process. Thus, the approach developed here could prove useful also to study DP-SGD, after incorporating an additional independent Wiener process in (
3.5
).

Importantly, privacy guarantees analogous to those of Proposition
3.2
hold for
\Theta(\tau)
, as formalized in the result below (the proof is deferred to Appendix
B
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\ell:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function with Lipschitz-continuous derivative. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\varepsilon\in(0,8\log(1/\delta))
, if we set

\Sigma\geq\frac{2C_{\textup{clip}}\sqrt{\tau}}{n}\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon},

(3.6)

the solution
\Theta(\tau)
of the SDE (
3.5
) at time
\tau
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private.

Armed with Proposition
3.4
, the rest of the paper focuses on the analysis of the DP-trained solution
\Theta(\tau)
. In fact, by strong convergence, the privacy-utility trade-offs of
\theta_{T}
approach the ones of
\Theta(\tau)
, for sufficiently small learning rates. As a non-private baseline, we consider the solution of the gradient flow equation

\textup{d}\hat{\theta}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\hat{\theta}(t))\textup{d}t,\qquad\theta^{*}=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\hat{\theta}(t),

(3.7)

where
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i})
is the original training loss (see Section 5.1 of
Bartlett et al. (2021)
for more details on the convergence). Note that (
3.7
) resembles (
3.5
), without the effects of clipping and the introduction of noise, and
\theta^{*}
is defined at convergence, as it does not require early stopping.

4
Main result

For simplicity, we set the initialization
\theta_{0}=0
, although similar results can be obtained for a broad class of initializations. The random features matrix
V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
is a normalized Gaussian matrix,
i.e.
,
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, and the data distribution is scaled as follows.

Assumption 1

(Data distribution)
.

The training samples
\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,(x_{n},y_{n})\}
are
n
i.i.d. samples from the joint distribution
\mathcal{P}_{XY}
, such that the marginal distribution
\mathcal{P}_{X}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}
is sub-Gaussian, with
\left\|x\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
.

2.

The data
x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}
are normalized such that
\left\|x\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
.

3.

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]\right)=\Omega(1)
,
i.e.
, the second-moment matrix of the data is well-conditioned.

Furthermore, we assume all the labels
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
to be bounded.

The first assumption requires the data to have well-behaved tails. It is commonly used in the literature
(
Liu et al., 2023
)
, and it covers a number of important cases such as data respecting Lipschitz concentration
(
Bubeck and Sellke, 2021
)
(
e.g.
, standard Gaussian
(
Varshney et al., 2022
;
Brown et al., 2024
)
or uniform on the sphere
(
Mei and Montanari, 2022
)
). The second assumption (and the boundedness of the labels) can be achieved via data pre-processing. The third assumption is also required in related work to achieve the best rates in linear regression
(
Varshney et al., 2022
;
Liu et al., 2023
)
.
The scaling of input data and random features
V
guarantees that the pre-activations of the model (
i.e.
, the entries of
Vx
) are of constant order. We then process the entries of
Vx
via the following family of activation functions.

Assumption 2

(Activation function)
.

The activation function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-linear, Lipschitz continuous function such that
\mu_{0}=\mu_{2}=0
and
\mu_{1}\neq 0
, where
\mu_{k}
denotes the
k
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
.

This choice is motivated by theoretical convenience, and it covers a wide family of activations, including all odd ones (
e.g.
,
\tanh
). We believe that our result can be extended to a more general setting, as the one in
Mei et al. (2022)
, at the cost of a more involved analysis.

Assumption 3

(Parameter scaling)
.

n=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),\qquad\log n=\Theta\left(\log p\right),\qquad n=\omega\left(d\log^{2}d\right),\qquad n=o\left(\frac{d^{3/2}}{\log^{3}d}\right).

(4.1)

We consider an over-parameterized setting in which the number of parameters
p
is at least of order
n^{2}
. To guarantee that the RF model interpolates the data, it suffices that
p\gg n
(see e.g.
(
Mei et al., 2022
;
Wang and Zhu, 2023
)
), and we expect our result to hold under this milder assumption. This would however need a different argument, specifically for Lemma
6.5
.
The requirement
\log n=\Theta\left(\log p\right)
is mild and it could be relaxed at the expenses of a poly-logarithmic dependence on
p
in our final result in Theorem
1
. Finally, we focus on the regime
d\ll n\ll d^{3/2}
, which corresponds to standard datasets, such as CIFAR-10 (
n=5\cdot 10^{4}
,
d\approx 3\cdot 10^{3}
), or ImageNet as considered in
(
Zhang et al., 2017
)
(
n\approx 1.3\cdot 10^{6}
,
d\approx 9\cdot 10^{4}
).

Assumption 4

(Privacy budget)
.

\delta\in(0,1),\qquad\varepsilon\in(0,8\log(1/\delta)),\qquad\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}}=\omega\left(\frac{d\log^{5}n}{n}\right).

(4.2)

The first two conditions on
\delta,\varepsilon
are standard in differential privacy. The lower bound on
\varepsilon
coming from the third condition still allows for strong privacy regimes with
\varepsilon=o(1)
, as
n\gg d
from Assumption
3
. To state our main result, we set the hyper-parameters in the SDE (
3.5
) as

\tau=\frac{d\log^{2}n}{p},\qquad C_{\textup{clip}}=\sqrt{p}\log^{2}n,\qquad\Sigma=\frac{2C_{\textup{clip}}\sqrt{\tau}}{n}\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}.

(4.3)

Theorem 1
.

Consider the RF model in (
1.2
) with input dimension
d
and number of features
p
. Let
n
be the number of training samples and
\mathcal{R}_{\textup{P}}
be defined in (
1.1
), where
\theta^{*}
is given by (
3.7
) and
\theta^{p}
is the solution
\Theta(\tau)
of the SDE (
3.5
) at time
\tau
, with hyper-parameters set as in (
4.3
). Let Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
hold. Then, we have that
\theta^{p}
is
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private, and that

\displaystyle\left|\mathcal{R}_{P}\right|

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d}{n\varepsilon}\,\log^{5}n\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{n\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d}{n\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{d^{3/2}}}\right)=o(1),

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

The strong convergence of the Euler-Maruyama discretization scheme guarantees that, for sufficiently small learning rates
\eta
, the solution
\theta_{T}
of Algorithm
1
also satisfies (
4.4
), which establishes the utility of DP-GD.

Theorem

proves that, in the RF model, over-parameterization is not inherently detrimental in private learning. To verify this numerically, we plot in the first panel of Figure

Proof of Theorem
1

Notation.

All logarithms are in the natural basis. Random variables are defined on different probability spaces, which describe the randomness in the data, the random features and the private mechanisms. All complexity notations are understood for sufficiently large data size
n
, input dimension
d
and number of parameters
p
.
We indicate with
C
and
c
absolute, strictly positive, numerical constants, that do not depend on the scalings of the problem and whose value may change from line to line.
Given a positive number
s
,
[s]
denotes the set of positive numbers from
1
to
s
. Given a vector
v
,
\left\|v\right\|_{2}
denotes its Euclidean norm. Given a matrix
A
,
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
denotes its operator norm,
\left\|A\right\|_{F}
its Frobenius norm and
A^{+}
its Moore-Penrose inverse.
We use
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
to denote the data matrix (containing the
i
-th element of the training set
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in its
i
-th row),
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
to denote the feature matrix (containing
\varphi(x_{i}):=\phi(Vx)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
in its
i
-th row) and
K:=\Phi\Phi^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
to denote the kernel associated with the feature map.

6.1
Technical outline

To circumvent the difficulty in explicitly solving the SDE in (
3.5
) (
i.e.
, in characterizing the probability density function of
\Theta(\tau)
), we instead consider the SDE

\textup{d}\hat{\Theta}(t)=-\nabla\mathcal{L}(\hat{\Theta}(t))\textup{d}t+\Sigma\,\textup{d}B(t)=-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}}{n}\left(\Phi\hat{\Theta}(t)-Y\right)\textup{d}t+\Sigma\,\textup{d}B(t),

(6.1)

where
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
is the original (quadratic) training loss and
B(t)
is the same standard Wiener process as in (
3.5
). The solution of the SDE in (
6.1
) is a multi-dimensional Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process which admits a closed form (see, e.g., Section 4.4.4 in
Gardiner (1985)
).
Let us then define

\mathcal{C}:=\left\{\theta\quad\textup{s.t.}\quad\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i})\|_{2}<C_{\textup{clip}}\quad\textup{ for all }i\in[n]\right\},

(6.2)

which corresponds to the subset of the parameters space where
clipping does not happen
,
i.e.
, where
\mathcal{L}_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}(\theta)
. If the full path of the process
\Theta(t)
happens in this region (
i.e.

\Theta(t)\in\mathcal{C}
for all
t\in[0,\tau]
), then
\Theta(\tau)=\hat{\Theta}(\tau)
. This corresponds to the event

\hat{\Theta}(t)\in\mathcal{C},\textup{ for all }t\in[0,\tau],

(6.3)

which is easier to control, as
\hat{\Theta}(t)
is an OU process. The first part of our proof consists in showing that, for our choice of the hyper-parameters in (
4.3
), this event happens with high probability. To do so, we consider the decomposition

\hat{\Theta}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\hat{\Theta}(t)\right]+\tilde{\Theta}(t)=\hat{\theta}(t)+\tilde{\Theta}(t),

(6.4)

where we introduce the notation
\tilde{\Theta}(t):=\hat{\Theta}(t)-\mathbb{E}_{B}[\hat{\Theta}(t)]
and use that the expectation of an OU process corresponds to the gradient flow
\mathbb{E}_{B}[\hat{\Theta}(t)]=\hat{\theta}(t)
defined in (
3.7
).
Then,
\mathcal{C}
can be characterized as

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\theta\quad\textup{s.t.}\quad\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i})\right\|_{2}<C_{\textup{clip}}\quad\textup{ for all }i\in[n]\right\}

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\left\{\theta\quad\textup{s.t.}\quad\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\theta-y_{i}\right|<\frac{C_{\textup{clip}}}{2\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}}\quad\textup{ for all }i\in[n]\right\},

which implies that the probability of the event in (
6.3
) is lower bounded by the probability of the event

\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)\right|+\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\hat{\theta}(t)-y_{i}\right|\leq\frac{C_{\textup{clip}}}{2\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}},\quad\textup{ for all }i\in[n],\quad\textup{for all }t\in[0,\tau].

(6.6)

As we set
C_{\textup{clip}}=\sqrt{p}\log^{2}n
(see (
4.3
)) and
\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{p})
with high probability (see the argument in (
C.97
) and (
C.98
)),
we therefore want to show that, for all
i\in[n]
and for all
t\in[0,\tau]
,
|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)|=o(\log^{2}n)
and
|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\hat{\theta}(t)-y_{i}|=o(\log^{2}n)
with high probability. The term
|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\hat{\theta}(t)-y_{i}|
is handled by the lemma below.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold. Then, we have that, jointly for all
i\in[n]
,

\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\hat{\theta}(t)-y_{i}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right),

(6.7)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

We now briefly sketch the argument to obtain (
6.7
), deferring the full proof to Section
6.2
.
As the initialization
\theta_{0}=0
, the solution of the gradient flow in (
3.7
) takes the form
\hat{\theta}(t)=(1-e^{-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}\Phi}{n}t})\Phi^{+}Y
. Since
K
is invertible with high probability (due to Lemma
C.5
), the quantity of interest can be expressed as

y_{i}-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\hat{\theta}(t)=\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}e^{-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}\Phi}{n}t}\Phi^{+}Y.

Our approach consists in iteratively controlling this quantity introducing auxiliary
leave-one-out
variables,
i.e.
,
\Phi_{-i}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times p}
and
Y_{-i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
, defined without the
i
-th sample.
The first step involves showing that

\left\|\Phi^{+}Y-\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}\right\|_{2}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(p^{-1/2}\right),

which implies that we can focus the analysis on
\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}e^{-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}\Phi}{n}t}\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}|
. This result comes from the
stability
of GD, as shown in Lemma
6.5
, and is a consequence of lower bounding the smallest eigenvalue of the kernel
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)
.

The second step aims to introduce a leave-one-out variable in the exponent, as we show that

\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}e^{-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}\Phi}{n}t}\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}\right|\leq 2\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}e^{-\frac{2\Phi_{-i}^{\top}\Phi_{-i}}{n}t}\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}\right|.

This result is achieved via an explicit computation based on
Lie’s product formula
for the matrix exponential, which is carried through in Lemma
6.7
.

Finally, since the only dependence on
x_{i}
is via the term
\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}
, we can conclude the argument with
Dudley’s (chaining tail) inequality
, upper bounding the covering number of the set described by the curve
\gamma(t)=V^{\top}e^{-\frac{2\Phi_{-i}^{\top}\Phi_{-i}}{n}t}\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}
in Lemma
6.8
.

Next, we handle the term
|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)|
via the lemma below, also proven in Section
6.2
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
, and
4
hold. Then, we have that, jointly for all
i\in[n]
,

\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right),

(6.8)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)-2n\exp\left(-cp\right)
over
B
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant and
B
refers to the probability space of the private mechanism,
i.e.
, the noise in (
6.1
).

To prove (
6.8
), we start by noticing that
\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)
evolves as a Gaussian random variable with time-dependent variance. The idea is to upper bound this variance with that of the auxiliary process

\textup{d}z_{i}(t)=\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\Sigma\,\textup{d}B(t),

which is obtained by removing the
attractive drift

[-2\Phi^{\top}(\Phi\hat{\Theta}(t)-Y)/n]
from (
6.1
). Intuitively, the removal of the attractive drift increases the variance, as the process becomes less concentrated around the mean. This is formalized in Lemma
6.9
, where an application of the
Sudakov-Fernique inequality
gives that

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{z_{i}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|z_{i}(t)\right|\right].

(6.9)

We note that the RHS of (
6.9
) is easier to control than the LHS, as
z_{i}(t)
is a Wiener process and, therefore, its variance is bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(\Sigma^{2}\tau\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)
via the
reflection principle
. Then, given our hyper-parameter choice in (
4.3
) and the lower bound on
\varepsilon
in Assumption
4
, we have that
\Sigma^{2}\tau\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
. To conclude, we exploit the
Borell-TIS inequality
to show that, with high probability,

\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)|\leq\mathbb{E}_{B}[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)|]+\log n,

which concludes the argument.

The combination of Lemma
6.1
and
6.2
gives that
the event in (
6.3
) happens with high probability, which allows us to study the utility of
\Theta(\tau)
through the closed form of the OU process
\hat{\Theta}(\tau)
, as
\Theta(\tau)=\hat{\Theta}(\tau)
. This, in turn, boils down to controlling the effects of the noise and of the early stopping that are decoupled via the decomposition
\hat{\Theta}(\tau)=\hat{\theta}(\tau)+\tilde{\Theta}(\tau)
. In fact, on the one hand,
\tilde{\Theta}(\tau)
is a mean-0 random variable in the probability space of
B
and it captures the effect of the noise; on the other hand,
\hat{\theta}(\tau)

is the deterministic component (with respect to
B
) describing the flow and it captures the effect of the early stopping. We show that both the noise and the early stopping provide negligible damage to utility in the following two lemmas, whose proofs are contained in Section
6.3
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
4
hold, and let
d=o(p)
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[\left(\varphi(x)^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(\tau)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d^{2}\log^{10}n}{n^{2}}\,\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d^{2}}{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}\right)=o(1),

(6.10)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
B
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Similarly to Lemma
6.2
, Lemma
6.3
also uses that

\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(\tau)
is a Gaussian random variable with variance increasing linearly in
\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}
,
\tau
, and
\Sigma^{2}
. Then, the claim in (
6.10
) is a consequence of the choice of the hyper-parameters in (
4.3
) and Assumption
4
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
hold. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[\left(\varphi(x)^{\top}\left(\hat{\theta}(\tau)-\theta^{*}\right)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{n\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d}{n}+\frac{n}{d^{3/2}}\right)=o(1),

(6.11)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

The idea of the argument is to decompose the LHS of (
6.11
) through two disjoint subspaces,
i.e.
,
\varphi(x)^{\top}(P_{\Lambda}+P_{\Lambda}^{\perp})(\hat{\theta}(\tau)-\theta^{*})
, where
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is the projector on the space spanned by the eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
. The rationale is that there is a
spectral gap
between the
d
-th and the
(d+1)
-th eigenvalue of the kernel
K
, when sorting them in non-increasing order, see Lemma
C.5
.
We note that this result also uses the well conditioning of the data covariance (
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]\right)=\Omega(1)
), see the discussion right after the proof of Lemma
6.4
in Section
6
.

As a consequence of the spectral gap, the term
\|P_{\Lambda}(\hat{\theta}(\tau)-\theta^{*})\|_{2}
is negligible, since in this subspace
\hat{\theta}(\tau)
is
already close to convergence
, despite the early stopping. To control the other subspace, we consider the decomposition
\phi(Vx)=\mu_{1}Vx+\tilde{\phi}(Vx)
, where
\mu_{1}
is the first Hermite coefficient of
\phi
. Then, we show that
(i)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[(\tilde{\phi}(Vx)^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}(\hat{\theta}(\tau)-\theta^{*}))^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(d/n+n\log^{3}d/d^{3/2})
, exploiting the upper bound on
\|\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tilde{\phi}(Vx)\tilde{\phi}(Vx)^{\top}]\|_{\textup{op}}
in Lemma
C.15
, and
(ii)

\mathbb{E}_{x}[(x^{\top}V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}(\hat{\theta}(\tau)-\theta^{*}))^{2}]=\mathcal{O}\left(d/n+n\log^{3}d/d^{3/2}\right)
exploiting the bound on
\|V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\Phi^{+}\|_{\textup{op}}
in
Lemma
C.14
.

Finally, denoting by
\hat{\mathcal{R}}
and
\mathcal{R}^{*}
the generalization error of
\hat{\Theta}(\tau)
and
\theta^{*}
respectively, Lemmas
6.3
-
6.4
(together with some additional manipulations) guarantee that

|\hat{\mathcal{R}}-\mathcal{R}^{*}|=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{d}{n\varepsilon}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{d^{3/2}}}\right).

As
\Theta(\tau)=\hat{\Theta}(\tau)
(due to Lemmas
6.1
-
6.2
), the result of Theorem
1
follows. The details are deferred to the end of Section
6.3
.

6.2
Analysis of clipping

This section contains the proofs of Lemmas
6.1
and
6.2
, including a number of auxiliary results required by the argument.
We make use of the notation introduced in the previous section, including the leave-one-out variables (
\Phi_{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times p}
,
Y_{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,
X_{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times d}
,
K_{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(n-1)\times(n-1)}
) and the OU process
\hat{\Theta}(\tau)=\hat{\theta}(\tau)+\tilde{\Theta}(\tau)
. We also consider the hyper-parameter choice in (
4.3
). When stating that a random variable
Z
is sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential), we implicitly mean
\left\|Z\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
(
\left\|Z\right\|_{\psi_{1}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
),
i.e.
, its sub-Gaussian (sub-exponential) norm does not increase with the scalings of the problem.
Given a p.s.d. matrix
A
,
\lambda_{j}(A)
denotes its
j
-th eigenvalue sorted in non-increasing order (
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(A\right)=\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{s}(A)=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(A\right)
).
We indicate with
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
the projector on the space spanned by the eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
. This quantity is well defined due to Lemma
C.5
which proves a spectral gap, and we will condition on the result of this lemma throughout the paper. It is convenient to define the function
\tilde{\phi}(z):=\phi(z)-\mu_{1}z
, where
\mu_{1}
is the first Hermite coefficient of
\phi
. Note that this function is Lipschitz continuous and has the first 3 Hermite coefficients equal to 0, due to Assumption
2
. We then define the shorthands
\tilde{\varphi}(x)=\tilde{\phi}(Vx)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\tilde{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
as the matrix containing
\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
in its
i
-th row, and
\tilde{K}=\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
. Then, we have

\left\|\Phi^{+}Y-\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

(6.12)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

From Lemma
C.5
, we have that
K
is invertible with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
. Conditioning on such high probability event, we have that also
K_{-1}
is invertible and, therefore, we can write
\Phi^{+}=\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}
and
\Phi^{+}_{-1}=\Phi_{-1}^{\top}K^{-1}_{-1}
.
Thus, from the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
Bombari and Mondelli (2024)
(see their Equation (44)), we have

\Phi^{+}Y-\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}=\frac{P_{\Phi_{-1}}^{\perp}\varphi(x_{1})}{\left\|P_{\Phi_{-1}}^{\perp}\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(y_{1}-\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right),

(6.13)

where
P_{\Phi_{-1}}
is the projector over the span of the rows of
\Phi_{-1}
.

To bound the term
\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}
, we decompose
\varphi(x_{1})=\mu_{1}Vx_{1}+\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})
. Thus, an application of the triangle inequality gives

\left|\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right|\leq\left|\mu_{1}x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right|+\left|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right|.

(6.14)

We bound the two terms in (
6.14
) separately. As for the first term, we have that

\left\|V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

where the second step is a consequence of Lemma
C.10
, and holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X_{-1}
. In fact, notice that considering
\Phi
or
\Phi_{-1}
in Lemma
C.10
does not change the argument, and therefore the final result. Then, conditioning on this high probability event, since
x_{1}
is sub-Gaussian, we have that the first term of (
6.14
) reads

\left|\mu_{1}x_{1}^{\top}V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right),

(6.15)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
.

As for the second term in (
6.14
), by triangle inequality, we have that

\left\|\left(\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|K_{-1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]\right\|_{2}+\left\|K_{-1}^{-1}\left(\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]\right)\right\|_{2}.

(6.16)

We bound the two terms in (
6.16
) separately. As for the first term, using indices
i\in[n-1]
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}=p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(x_{i+1}^{\top}v)\tilde{\phi}(x_{1}^{\top}v)\right]=p\sum_{l=3}^{+\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\left(\frac{x_{i+1}^{\top}x_{1}}{d}\right)^{l},

(6.17)

where we use the Hermite decomposition of the functions
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
. Recall that the first 3 Hermite coefficients of
\tilde{\phi}
are 0, and the others correspond to the ones of
\phi
, which we denote as
\mu_{l}
.
Since
x_{1}
is sub-Gaussian and independent from the
x_{i+1}
-s, with
\left\|x_{i+1}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, we have that

\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\in[n-1]}\left|x_{i+1}^{\top}x_{1}\right|\leq\sqrt{d}\log n,

(6.18)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}n\right)
over
x_{1}
. Thus, conditioning on this high probability event, (
6.17
) gives

\left|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}\right|\leq p\left(\frac{\left|x_{i+1}^{\top}x_{1}\right|}{d}\right)^{3}\sum_{l=3}^{+\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\frac{\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(6.19)

which implies

\left\|K_{-1}^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert K_{-1}^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(6.20)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
. Here, the last passage also uses Lemma
C.5
, which provides a lower bound on
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K_{-1}\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(p)
.

As for the second term of (
6.16
), we have

\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\phi(x_{i+1}^{\top}v_{k})\tilde{\phi}(x_{1}^{\top}v_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\phi(x_{i+1}^{\top}v_{k})\tilde{\phi}(x_{1}^{\top}v_{k})\right].

(6.21)

All terms in the previous sum are independent, sub-exponential, mean-0 random variables, since both
\phi
and
\tilde{\phi}
are Lipschitz. Thus, Bernstein inequality (see Theorem 2.8.1 of
Vershynin (2018)
) gives

\left|\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]_{i}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\sqrt{p}\right),

(6.22)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
. Then, performing a union bound over all the indices
i\in[n-1]
, we obtain

\left\|K_{-1}^{-1}\left(\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\Phi_{-1}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right]\right)\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{p}\sqrt{np}\log n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\right),

(6.23)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
. Then, (
6.23
) and (
6.20
) make (
6.16
) read

\left\|\left(\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right)^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}+\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(6.24)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{8}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
, where the last step is a consequence of
p=\Omega(n^{2})
. Plugging (
6.24
) and (
6.15
) in (
6.14
) provides the upper bound

\left|\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}Y_{-1}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\log n\right),

(6.25)

which holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{9}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
.

To bound the term
\left\|P_{\Phi_{-1}}^{\perp}\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}
in (
6.13
), we can use Lemma B.1. in
Bombari and Mondelli (2024)
, which gives

\left\|P_{\Phi_{-1}}^{\perp}\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)}=\Omega(\sqrt{p}),

(6.26)

where the last step is a consequence of Lemma
C.5
, and holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{10}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
and
X
. The combination of (
6.13
), (
6.25
) and (
6.26
) readily gives the desired result.
∎

After performing a union bound on all
i\in[n]
, the result of Lemma
6.5
guarantees that, with high probability,

\left\|\Phi^{+}Y-\Phi_{-i}^{+}Y_{-i}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{p}}\right),\qquad\textup{for all }i\in[n].

This will be key in our later proof of Lemma
6.1
, where we will use Lemma
6.5
and the fact that it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
. We note that the proof strategy of Lemma
6.5
is specifically designed to obtain such a probability guarantee, and it differs from previous work, especially in the argument to show

\|(\Phi_{-i}^{+})^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\qquad\textup{for all }i\in[n],

see (
6.24
).
Note that, for a fixed
i
, this can be proved via Markov inequality through an upper bound on the second moment of the LHS of the equation above, which in turn reduces to an upper bound on
\|\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}]\|_{\textup{op}}
. This resembles the strategy followed by
Mei et al. (2022)
(see their Proposition 7.(b)) to prove that this term is negligible when estimating the test loss of the RF model, which is their object of interest. However, this approach would lead to vacuous probability guarantees after performing a union bound on all the training samples, due to the polynomial tail bound given by Markov inequality. To solve the issue, we pursue our desired result via the argument between (
6.16
) and (
6.20
), which leads to

\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}=O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

and therefore to our assumption
d^{3/2}\gg n
, see (
4.1
). Similarly, in the argument between (
6.21
) and (
6.23
), we upper bound
\|(\Phi_{-i}^{+})^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[(\Phi_{-i}^{+})^{\top}\tilde{\varphi}(x_{i})\right]\|_{2}
via Bernstein inequality on the separate indices of the argument vector, which at the end relies on the bound

\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}\log n}=O\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

leading to our assumption
p=\Omega(n^{2})
, see (
4.1
).

Lemma 6.6
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n=\omega\left(d\log^{2}d\right)
, and
n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}/\log^{3}d\right)
. Then, we have that, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
,

\frac{\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}}e^{-\frac{2\Phi^{\top}\Phi}{n}t}\frac{\varphi(x_{1})}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}}<\frac{\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}}e^{-\frac{2\varphi(x_{1})\varphi(x_{1})^{\top}}{n}t}\frac{\varphi(x_{1})}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}},

(6.27)

holds uniformly for all
t\in(0,\tau]
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
P_{\Lambda}
be the projector on the space spanned by the eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
,
P_{0}
be the projector on the kernel of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
, and
P_{\lambda}=I-P_{\Lambda}-P_{0}
. By definition, we have that

t,t^{\prime}\in[0,\tau]

\left\|\gamma(t)-\gamma(t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}=\left\|V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}e^{-\frac{2K_{-1}}{n}t}\left(I-e^{-\frac{2K_{-1}}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)}\right)Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}.

(6.67)

Assuming, without loss of generality,
t^{\prime}\geq t
, we have that

\left\|\gamma(t)-\gamma(t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(6.68)

where the last step is a consequence of Lemma
C.10
and it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X_{-1}
and
V
(note that the argument of Lemma
C.10
goes through equivalently when considering
\Phi_{-1}
instead of
\Phi
). This proves the desired result on the diameter.

Following a similar strategy, we can prove that, for all
t^{\prime}>t
s.t.
\left|t-t^{\prime}\right|\leq\Delta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\gamma(t)-\gamma(t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert I-e^{-\frac{2K_{-1}}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}

(6.69)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2\lambda_{\rm max}\left(K_{-1}\right)}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)}\right)\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\frac{2\lambda_{\rm max}\left(K_{-1}\right)}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi_{-1}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y_{-1}\right\|_{2}\frac{2\lambda_{\rm max}\left(K_{-1}\right)}{n}\Delta,

where the second step is a consequence of the fact that
1-e^{-\frac{2K_{-1}}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)}
is a p.s.d. matrix with eigenvalues given by
1-e^{-\frac{2\lambda_{i}\left(K_{-1}\right)}{n}(t^{\prime}-t)}
, and the third step follows from the inequality
z\geq 1-e^{-z}
, for
z\geq 0
.
Since
\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right)
jointly for all
i\in[n]

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}p\right)
over
V
(see the argument carried out in (
C.97
) and (
C.98
)), we have that

\lambda_{\rm max}\left(K_{-1}\right)=\left\lVert\Phi_{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\leq\left\|\Phi_{-1}\right\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(np\right)
. Thus, using again Lemma
C.10
, we get that, for every
\Delta>0
and for all
t^{\prime}>t
s.t.
\left|t-t^{\prime}\right|\leq\Delta
,

\left\|\gamma(t)-\gamma(t^{\prime})\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}p\Delta,

(6.70)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X_{-1}
and
V
, where
C_{1}
is an absolute constant independent from the scalings of the problems and from
\Delta
.
This means that we can cover
T
using
\left\lceil\tau/\Delta\right\rceil=\left\lceil d\log^{2}n/(p\Delta)\right\rceil
balls with radius
C_{1}p\Delta
.
Then, setting
\epsilon=C_{1}p\Delta
, this implies

\mathcal{N}(T,\epsilon)\leq\left\lceil\frac{d\log^{2}n}{p}\frac{C_{1}p}{\epsilon}\right\rceil=\left\lceil\frac{C_{1}d\log^{2}n}{\epsilon}\right\rceil.

(6.71)

Notice that, for
\epsilon\geq\textup{diam}(T)
, we simply have
\mathcal{N}(T,\epsilon)=1
. For smaller values of
\epsilon\leq\textup{diam}(T)\leq C_{2}
, the previous expression reads
\mathcal{N}(T,\epsilon)\leq\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{\epsilon}
, where
C_{3}
is another absolute constant set large enough such that
\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{\epsilon}\geq 2
, for all
\epsilon\leq C_{2}
(this allows to remove the ceiling function), and
C_{3}d\log^{2}n\geq eC_{2}
(which will be used in the next inequality). Then, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(T,\epsilon)}\textup{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\sqrt{\log\mathcal{N}(T,\epsilon)}\textup{d}\epsilon

(6.72)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{\epsilon}\right)}\textup{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\sqrt{\log\left(\frac{eC_{2}}{\epsilon}\right)+\log\left(\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{eC_{2}}\right)}\textup{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{eC_{2}}{\epsilon}\right)}\textup{d}\epsilon+\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{eC_{2}}\right)}\textup{d}\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{C_{2}}\log\left(\frac{eC_{2}}{\epsilon}\right)\textup{d}\epsilon+C_{2}\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{C_{3}d\log^{2}n}{eC_{2}}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}C_{2}+C_{4}\log n,

which concludes the argument.

Lemma 6.9
.

For all
i\in[n]
, we have that

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)\right)^{2}\right]\leq\Sigma^{2}\left|t-s\right|\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(6.83)

Proof.

Let us define the matrix
O\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as the orthogonal matrix such that
\Phi^{\top}\Phi=ODO^{\top}
, with
D\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
being a p.s.d. diagonal matrix with eigenvalues sorted in non-increasing way (which we indicate as
D_{k}
,
k\in[p]
, for simplicity). Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)\right)^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}OO^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}OO^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)\right)^{2}\right].

(6.84)

Note that, multiplying both sides of (
6.1
) by
O^{\top}
we get

\textup{d}\left(O^{\top}\hat{\Theta}(t)\right)=-\frac{2D}{n}\left(O^{\top}\hat{\Theta}(t)\right)\textup{d}t+\frac{2O^{\top}\Phi^{\top}Y}{n}\textup{d}t+\Sigma\,\textup{d}\left(O^{\top}B(t)\right).

(6.85)

Since
D
is diagonal, we have that each coordinate of
O^{\top}\hat{\Theta}(t)
evolves as an independent, one dimensional, OU process (without drift, eventually), since each coordinate of
O^{\top}B(t)
evolves as an independent Wiener processes, for rotational invariance of the Gaussian measure.
This implies that, for all
s,t\in[0,\tau]
, and for all
k\neq k^{\prime}
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k^{\prime}}\right]=0.

(6.86)

This, together with (
6.84
), gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\sum_{k=1}^{p}[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]

(6.87)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right].

Since
[O^{\top}\hat{\Theta}(t)]_{k}
describes an OU process with drift
\Delta_{k}=\frac{2D_{k}}{n}
, standard results on the variance and the auto-correlation of OU processes (see Section 4.4.4 in
Gardiner (1985)
) give (supposing
\Delta_{k}\neq 0
)

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}^{2}\right]=\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{2\Delta_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\Delta_{k}t}\right),

(6.88)

and

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right]=\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{2\Delta_{k}}\left(e^{-\Delta_{k}|t-s|}-e^{-\Delta_{k}(t+s)}\right),

(6.89)

which implies that

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{\Delta_{k}}\left(1-\frac{e^{-2\Delta_{k}t}+e^{-2\Delta_{k}s}}{2}-e^{-\Delta_{k}|t-s|}+e^{-\Delta_{k}(t+s)}\right).

(6.90)

Then, assuming
t\geq s
without loss of generality, and introducing the shorthand
\delta=t-s
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma^{2}}{\Delta_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\Delta_{k}s}\left(\frac{1+e^{-2\Delta_{k}\delta}-2e^{-\Delta_{k}\delta}}{2}\right)-e^{-\Delta_{k}\delta}\right)

(6.91)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Sigma^{2}\left(1-e^{-\Delta_{k}\delta}\right)}{\Delta_{k}}\left(1-e^{-2\Delta_{k}s}\left(\frac{1-e^{-\Delta_{k}\delta}}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Sigma^{2}\left(1-e^{-\Delta_{k}\delta}\right)}{\Delta_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\Sigma^{2}\delta=\Sigma^{2}|t-s|,

where the inequality in the fourth line holds as
1-e^{-x}\leq x
for all
x\geq 0
. Note that, when
[O^{\top}\hat{\Theta}(t)]_{k}
does not have a drift term (
\Delta_{k}=0
), it is a Wiener process, which implies that
\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]=\Sigma^{2}|t-s|
(see Section 3.8.1 in
Gardiner (1985)
).

Thus, plugging (
6.91
) in (
6.87
) we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left(\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)-\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{p}[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\left([O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)]_{k}-[O^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(s)]_{k}\right)^{2}\right]

(6.92)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{p}[O^{\top}\varphi(x_{i})]_{k}^{2}\,\Sigma^{2}\left|t-s\right|

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{2}\left|t-s\right|\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2},

which gives the desired result.

Proof of Lemma

.

where

V

Note that, by Lemma

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)\right|\right]\leq\Sigma^{2}\left|t-s\right|\left\|\varphi(x_{i})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{z_{i}}\left[\left(z_{i}(t)-z_{i}(s)\right)^{2}\right].

, for every

\mathbb{E}_{B}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(t)\right|\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{z_{i}}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,\tau]}\left|z_{i}(t)\right|\right]\leq C_{1},

(6.99)

where the last step is a consequence of (
6.96

6.9

\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\tilde{\Theta}(0)=0

7
Concluding remarks

In this work, we show that privacy can be obtained for free, i.e., the excess population risk is negligible even when
\varepsilon=o(1)
, in sufficiently over-parameterized random feature models. This proves that over-parameterization is not at odds with private learning.

We focus on the regime where the number of samples
n
scales with the input dimension
d
as
d\ll n\ll d^{3/2}
, but we believe that our approach can be applied in much wider generality. In fact, we crucially leverage the fact that the test loss of the GD solution
\theta^{*}
does not decrease with respect the case
n=\Theta(d)
. This in turn means that there is a surplus of samples that can be used to learn privately.
A similar plateau in the test loss has been demonstrated for kernel ridge regression by a recent line of work
(
Ghorbani et al., 2021
;
Mei et al., 2022
;
Hu et al., 2024
)
tackling increasingly general settings, and showing privacy for free here is an exciting direction for future research.

Appendix A
Further discussion on related work

In Section
2
, we have discussed how existing bounds are unable to tackle an over-parameterized RF model when
\varepsilon=\Theta(1)
. This requires the characterization of different quantities,
i.e.
, the Lipschitz constant of the loss
L
, the norm of the baseline solution
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
, the rank of the matrix
M
, defined as the projector on the column space of
\mathbb{E}_{x}[\varphi(x)\varphi(x)^{\top}]
, and the quantity
\left\|M\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
. We provide the desired characterization in the following paragraphs.

Estimate of
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}
.

Recall that
\theta^{*}=\Phi^{+}Y
, which readily implies that
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y\right\|_{2}
. An application of Lemma
C.5
gives that, with high probability,
\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{-1/2}\right)
. Then, as the labels are bounded, we have
\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n/p}\right)
. This upper bound is tight in different settings,
e.g.
, when the labels contain random noise independent from the data. In this case, an argument similar to the one in Theorem 2 of
Bombari et al. (2023)
, together with Lemma
C.5
, guarantees that
\|\theta^{*}\|_{2}=\Theta(\|\Phi^{+}\|_{F})=\Theta(\sqrt{n/p})
.

Estimate of
L
via a bounded set
\mathcal{B}
with radius
\beta=\Theta(\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|)
.

Denoting with
(x,y)
a generic input-label pair, we can write

L=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}}\left\|\nabla_{\theta}\ell(\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta-y)\right\|_{2}=\sup_{\theta\in\mathcal{B}}2\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|\left|\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta-y\right|=\Theta\left(\beta\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right),

(A.1)

where we use Cauchy-Schwartz inequality in the last step and the fact that
\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}\beta\gg|y|
in our data scaling. Then, noting that
\left\|\varphi(x)\right\|_{2}=\Theta(\sqrt{p})
(which holds with high probability) and plugging the value
\beta=\Theta(\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|)
obtained in the previous paragraph, we obtain

, and consequently

We start by introducing the notion of
sensitivity
. Let
\mu:\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be an arbitrary, deterministic,
p
-dimensional function, where
\mathcal{D}
represents the space of datasets. We define its
\ell_{2}
sensitivity to be

\Delta_{2}\mu=\sup_{D\textup{ adjacent with }D^{\prime}}\left\|\mu(D)-\mu(D^{\prime})\right\|_{2},

(B.1)

where
D\textup{ adjacent with }D^{\prime}
means that the two datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
differ by only one training sample.
The DP-GD algorithm is obtained through the composition of
T
independent Gaussian mechanisms (see
Dwork and Roth (2014)
, Theorem A.1), as every iteration includes a (clipped) gradient update and a Gaussian noise injection. In this case, we have that Algorithm
1
is a randomized mechanism
\mathcal{A}
consisting of a sequence of adaptive (Gaussian) mechanisms
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{T}
where
\mathcal{M}_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
for all
t\in[T]
. One option is then to use the advanced composition Theorem (see Theorem 3.20 in
(
Dwork and Roth, 2014

)
to compute the privacy guarantees of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
. Unfortunately, this approach is not fruitful in our case,
as it would involve an additional
\log T
term in our final result in Theorem
3.2
, which would make the variance of the noise added at each iteration to diverge, in the limit of
\eta\to 0
,
T\to+\infty
, with
\eta T\to\tau
.
Thus, to prove Proposition
3.2
, we calculate the privacy guarantees through the moment accountant method, also used by
Abadi et al. (2016)
to show their Theorem 2 (which we restate in Theorem
2
, for the sake of completeness and notation). This allows for a tighter tracking of the privacy budget, exploiting the independence between the Gaussian mechanisms at every iteration of Algorithm

.

While our approach is conceptually similar to the one used in Theorem 1 of
Abadi et al. (2016)
, in contrast with
Abadi et al. (2016)
we consider full batch gradient descent and make explicit the dependence on the learning rate
\eta
, given our choice of scaling for Algorithm

.
We rely on the notion of
privacy loss
, which describes the difference that a randomized mechanism has on two adjacent datasets. More specifically, given two adjacent datasets
D,D^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}
, a randomized mechanism
\mathcal{M}_{t}\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathcal{D}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
and an auxiliary input
\theta_{t-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, the

at the output

takes the form

\gamma(\theta;\mathcal{M}_{t},\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime})=\log\frac{p\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D)=\theta\right)}{p\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D^{\prime})=\theta\right)},

(B.2)

where the notation

indicates the value of the law of a random variable
Z
evaluated in
\theta
. As in
Abadi et al. (2016)
, we define the
\lambda

as the logarithm of the moment generating function of the privacy loss evaluated in
\lambda

i.e.
,

\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda;\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime})=\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D)}\left[\exp(\lambda\gamma(\theta;\mathcal{M}_{t},\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime}))\right].

(B.3)

Note that the expectation is taken with respect to the probability distribution of the parameters given by the mechanism applied to the dataset
D
. Then, we can define

\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda)=\sup_{\theta_{t-1},D\textup{ adjacent with }D^{\prime}}\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda;\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime}),

(B.4)

where the supremum is taken over all possible
\theta_{t-1}
and all the adjacent datasets
D,D^{\prime}
.

We now re-state Theorem 2 of
Abadi et al. (2016)
which translates the moment accountant to
(\varepsilon,\delta)
privacy guarantees. Next, we bound the sensitivity of each iteration of Algorithm
1
and, therefore, the corresponding value of
\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda)
(see Lemmas

3.2
will then follow.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}_{t}
be the randomized mechanism induced by the
t

\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda)=\sup_{\theta_{t-1},D\textup{ adjacent with }D^{\prime}}\alpha_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda;\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime})\leq\frac{\eta}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\lambda+\lambda^{2}\right).

Proof.

As

\mu_{\theta_{t-1},D}

\mu_{\theta_{t-1},D}=\theta_{t-1}-\frac{\eta}{n}\sum_{(x_{i},y_{i})\in D}g_{C_{\textup{clip}}}(x_{i},y_{i},\theta_{t-1}).

(B.12)

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle(\theta;\mathcal{M}_{t},\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log\frac{p\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D)=\theta\right)}{p\left(\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D^{\prime})=\theta\right)}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle{}_{\mathcal{M}_{t}}(\lambda;\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\log\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{M}_{t}(\theta_{t-1},D)}\left[\exp(\lambda\gamma(\theta;\mathcal{M}_{t},\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime}))\right]

\displaystyle=\log\left(\exp\left(\lambda\frac{\left\|\Delta_{\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime}}\right\|_{2}^{2}}{2\varsigma^{2}}\right)\mathbb{E}_{\theta\sim\mathcal{M}(\theta_{t-1},D)}\left[\exp\left(-\lambda\frac{(\theta-\mu_{\theta_{t-1},D})^{\top}\Delta_{\theta_{t-1},D,D^{\prime}}}{\varsigma^{2}}\right)\right]\right)

j>j^{*}

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\theta^{j}_{T}-\Theta(\tau)\right\|_{2}\right]<h.

(B.31)

Proposition
B.4
formalizes in which sense the SDE in (
3.5
) is a continuous limit of Algorithm
1
. This is done through the family of algorithms with progressively smaller learning rates and larger number of iterations. It is important to notice that all these algorithms provide the same DP guarantee, as
\eta_{j}T_{j}=\eta T
for every
j
, and the result of Proposition
3.2
depends on learning rate and number of iterations only through their product
\eta T
.

Lemma B.5
.

We have that, for any open ball
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

\lim_{j\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j}_{T}\in S\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\in S\right).

(B.32)

Proof.

By contradiction, let the thesis be false. Then, there exists a ball
S(r_{0},r)
with center
r_{0}
and radius
r
s.t. there is
h^{*}>0
and
j
arbitrarily large that satisfy

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j}_{T}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)\right|>h^{*}.

(B.33)

We first suppose that we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j}_{T}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)>\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)+h^{*},

(B.34)

for
j
arbitrarily large.
Then, there is a sequence of events
\omega_{j}
s.t.
\mathbb{P}\left(\omega_{j}\right)>h^{*}
and

\theta^{j}_{T}(\omega_{j})\in S(r_{0},r),\qquad\Theta_{\tau}(\omega_{j})\notin S(r_{0},r).

(B.35)

As mentioned previously in this section, the law
p(\Theta_{\tau})
is continuous, and therefore bounded in the closed set
S(r_{0},r^{\prime})
for any
r^{\prime}
. This implies that there exists
r^{*}>0
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\notin S(r_{0},r)\,\textup{ and }\,\Theta_{\tau}\in S(r_{0},r+r^{*})\right)<\frac{h^{*}}{2},

(B.36)

which in turn implies that there is a sequence of events
\omega^{*}_{j}
, such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\omega_{j}^{*}\right)>h^{*}/2
and

\theta^{j}_{T}(\omega^{*}_{j})\in S(r_{0},r),\qquad\Theta_{\tau}(\omega^{*}_{j})\notin S(r_{0},r+r^{*}).

(B.37)

Note that, for all such events, by triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\theta^{j}_{T}(\omega^{*}_{j})-\Theta_{\tau}(\omega^{*}_{j})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\Theta_{\tau}(\omega^{*}_{j})-r_{0}\right\|_{2}-\left\|\theta^{j}_{T}(\omega^{*}_{j})-r_{0}\right\|_{2}

(B.38)

\displaystyle\geq\left(r+r^{*}-r_{0}\right)-\left(r-r_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=r^{*},

which implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\theta^{j}_{T}-\Theta_{\tau}\right\|_{2}\right]\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\omega_{j}^{*}\right)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left\|\theta^{j}_{T}-\Theta_{\tau}\right\|_{2}\right|\omega_{j}^{*}\right]\geq\frac{h^{*}r^{*}}{2}.

(B.39)

This last equation holds for arbitrarily large
j
, which provides the desired contradiction with Proposition
B.4
.

If instead of (
B.34
) we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)>\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j}_{T}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)+h^{*},

(B.40)

for
j
arbitrarily large,
then the argument follows in the same way. In fact, we exploit the continuity of the law
p(\Theta_{\tau})
to state that there exists
r^{*}>0
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}\notin S(r_{0},r-r^{*})\,\textup{ and }\,\Theta_{\tau}\in S(r_{0},r)\right)<\frac{h^{*}}{2},

(B.41)

thus giving a sequence of events
\omega^{*}_{j}
, such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\omega_{j}\right)>h^{*}/2
and

\theta^{j}_{T}(\omega^{*}_{j})\notin S(r_{0},r),\qquad\Theta_{\tau}(\omega^{*}_{j})\in S(r_{0},r-r^{*}).

(B.42)

At this point, we can again apply the triangle inequality to obtain the same contradiction as in (
B.39
).
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

The assumption on
\Sigma
guarantees, due to Theorem
3.2
, that for all
j>0
, the discretizations
\theta^{j}_{T}
of the SDE (
3.5
) are, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon\in(0,8\log(1/\delta))
,
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-differentially private, since

\sigma=\frac{n}{2C_{\textup{clip}}}\Sigma\geq\sqrt{\tau}\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\eta_{j}T_{j}}\frac{\sqrt{8\log(1/\delta)}}{\varepsilon},

(B.43)

where the first step is due to (
3.6
).

By contradiction, suppose that
\Theta_{\tau}
is not
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP.
This means that there exists a point in the parameters space
\bar{\theta}
such that, on adjacent datasets
D
and
D^{\prime}
, we have

p\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D)=\bar{\theta}\right)>e^{\varepsilon}p\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D^{\prime})=\bar{\theta}\right)+\delta.

(B.44)

Since both the laws in the previous equation are continuous, we also have that there exists an open ball
S
centered in
\bar{\theta}
, such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D)\in S\right)>e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta,

(B.45)

which we rewrite as

\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D)\in S\right)=e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta+h,

(B.46)

for some
h>0
. Now, by Lemma
B.5
, we have that there exists
j^{*}
large enough s.t.

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}(D)\in S\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D)\in S\right)\right|<\frac{h}{2},

(B.47)

and

\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)\right|<\frac{he^{-\varepsilon}}{2}.

(B.48)

Hence, putting together (
B.46
), (
B.47
), and (
B.48
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}(D)\in S\right)

\displaystyle>\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D)\in S\right)-\frac{h}{2}

(B.49)

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\Theta_{\tau}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta+h-\frac{h}{2}

\displaystyle>e^{\varepsilon}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)-\frac{he^{-\varepsilon}}{2}\right)+\delta+h-\frac{h}{2}

\displaystyle=e^{\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}\left(\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}(D^{\prime})\in S\right)+\delta.

The previous equation implies that the discretization
\theta^{j^{*}}_{T}
is not
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-DP, therefore contradicting the thesis of Theorem
3.2
. This provides the desired result. ∎

This section uses the following additional notation. We denote by
\mathbf{1}:\mathbb{R}\to\{0,1\}
the indicator function,
i.e.
,
\mathbf{1}(z)=1
if
z>0
, and
0
otherwise.
Given a matrix
A
, we indicate with
\sigma_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}(A)=\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(A^{\top}A\right)}
its smallest singular value. Given a p.s.d. matrix
A
,
\lambda_{j}(A)
denotes its
j
-th eigenvalue sorted in non-increasing order (
\lambda_{\rm max}\left(A\right)=\lambda_{1}(A)\geq\lambda_{2}(A)\geq\ldots\geq\lambda_{s}(A)=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(A\right)
).
Given two matrices
A\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s_{1}}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s_{2}}
, we denote by
A\ast B=[(A_{1:}\otimes B_{1:}),\ldots,(A_{s:}\otimes B_{s:})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s_{1}s_{2}}
their row-wise Kronecker product (also known as Khatri-Rao product), and
A_{j:}
denotes the
j
-th row of
A
. Given a natural number
l
, we denote
A^{*l}=A\ast A^{*(l-1)}
, with
A^{*1}=A
. We remark that
\left[\left(A^{*l}\right)\left(A^{*l}\right)^{\top}\right]_{ij}=\left(\left[AA^{\top}\right]_{ij}\right)^{l}
. Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
, we use the notation
A\preceq B
(
B\succeq A
) to indicate that
B-A
is p.s.d.
As in Section
6
, we indicate with
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
the projector on the space spanned by the eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
, and we condition on the high probability event described by Lemma
C.5
, which guarantees a spectral gap in
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
(and, hence, the well-posedness of
P_{\Lambda}
).

Lemma C.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold, and let
x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Then, we have that

\left\|\mathbb{E}[x]\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.1)

and

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}[xx^{\top}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(C.2)

Proof.

The first statement is a direct consequence of
x
being sub-Gaussian. In fact, this implies

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x^{\top}\frac{\mathbb{E}[x]}{\left\|\mathbb{E}[x]\right\|_{2}}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.3)

as the second moment of sub-Gaussian random variables is bounded. Thus, we have

\left\|\mathbb{E}[x]\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\left[x^{\top}\frac{\mathbb{E}[x]}{\left\|\mathbb{E}[x]\right\|_{2}}\right]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x^{\top}\frac{\mathbb{E}[x]}{\left\|\mathbb{E}[x]\right\|_{2}}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.4)

which provides the desired result.

For the second statement, we have, for every
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1
,

u^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[xx^{\top}\right]u=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x^{\top}u\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.5)

where the second step holds as the second moment of sub-Gaussian random variables is bounded. Since
\mathbb{E}\left[xx^{\top}\right]
is p.s.d., its operator norm is
\sup_{\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}u^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[xx^{\top}\right]u
, and the second statement readily follows.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold, and let
d=o(n)
and
d=o(p)
.
Then, we have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)=\Theta(n),

(C.6)

and

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right),

(C.7)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-cd\right)
over
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Also, we have that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(V^{\top}V\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{p}{d}\right),

(C.8)

and

\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{d}}\right),

(C.9)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-cd\right)
over
V
.

Proof.

Notice that
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is a matrix with i.i.d. sub-Gaussian rows with second moment matrix
\Psi=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}\left[xx^{\top}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Thus, by Remark 5.40 in
Vershynin (2012)
, we have that

\left\lVert X^{\top}X-n\Psi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n\frac{d}{n}\right)=o(n),

(C.10)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
. Then, conditioning on this high probability event, by Weyl’s inequality, we have

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)\leq\left\lVert X^{\top}X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert n\Psi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert X^{\top}X-n\Psi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n\right),

(C.11)

where the last step is a consequence of Lemma
C.1
. This proves the upper bound in the first statement as well as the second statement.
Furthermore, again by Weyl’s inequality, we have

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(n\Psi\right)-\left\lVert X^{\top}X-n\Psi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\Omega(n),

(C.12)

where the last step is a consequence of
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\Psi\right)=\Omega(1)
, true by Assumption
1
. Merging this with (
C.11
) concludes the proof of the first statement.

The third statement can be proven in the same exact way, considering
\sqrt{d}V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
, a matrix with i.i.d. standard Gaussian (and therefore sub-Gaussian) rows with second moment matrix equal to the identity
I\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Finally, the fourth statement is a consequence of Theorem 4.4.5 of
Vershynin (2018)
and the scaling
d=o(p)
, and it holds with probability
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}d\right)
. This concludes the proof and provides the desired results.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
.
Then with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
, all the following hold

\lambda_{d}(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K])=\Omega\left(\frac{pn}{d}\right),

(C.13)

\lambda_{d+1}(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K])=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.14)

\lambda_{n}(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K])=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)=\Omega(p),

(C.15)

where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

As
K_{ij}=\varphi(x_{i})^{\top}\varphi(x_{j})
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}[\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{i})\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{j})]=p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}[\phi(v^{\top}x_{i})\phi(v^{\top}x_{j})],

(C.16)

where in the last step we exploited that the
v_{k}
-s are identically distributed, and introduced the shorthand
v
to indicate a random variable distributed as
v_{1}
.
Since
\left\|x_{i}\right\|=\sqrt{d}
for all
i\in[n]
, and
v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, we have that
\rho_{1}:=v^{\top}x_{i}
and
\rho_{2}:=v^{\top}x_{j}
are two standard Gaussian variables with correlation
x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}/d
. Thus, exploiting the Hermite expansion of
\phi
, we can write

\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]_{ij}=p\sum_{l=0}^{+\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\frac{\left(x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right)^{l}}{d^{l}}=p\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\frac{\left[\left(X^{*l}\right)\left(X^{*l}\right)^{\top}\right]_{ij}}{d^{l}},

(C.17)

where
\mu_{l}
is the
l
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
.
Since
\phi
is such that
\mu_{l}=0
for
l=\{0,2\}
,
\mu_{1}\neq 0
, and there exists
l\geq 3
such that
\mu_{l}\neq 0
(since
\phi
is non-linear), we can write the previous sum with the two non-0 terms

\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]_{ij}=\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}p}{d}XX^{\top}+p\sum_{l=3}^{+\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\frac{\left[\left(X^{*l}\right)\left(X^{*l}\right)^{\top}\right]_{ij}}{d^{l}}.

(C.18)

We analyze the terms of the sum separately.
By Lemma
C.2
we have that, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}d\right)
over
X
,

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)=\Theta(n).

(C.19)

As
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, with
d<n
, this means that (conditioning on this high probability event)
XX^{\top}
is a matrix with rank equal to
d
, and that its first
d
eigenvalues (when sorted in non-increasing order) are all
\Omega(n)
. This implies that

M_{1}:=\frac{\mu_{1}^{2}p}{d}XX^{\top}

(C.20)

has the first
d
eigenvalues of order
\Omega(pn/d)
, and the remaining
n-d
equal to 0.

We now consider the second term of (
C.18
):

M_{2}=p\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\frac{\left[\left(X^{*l}\right)\left(X^{*l}\right)^{\top}\right]_{ij}}{d^{l}}.

(C.21)

Let us define

\tilde{M}_{2}=p\left(\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\right)I=CpI,

(C.22)

corresponding to the diagonal elements of
M_{2}
, where
C
is a constant depending only on
\phi
. Notice that
C
is strictly positive, as
\phi
is non-linear.
For
i\neq j
, since
x_{i}
is sub-Gaussian and independent from
x_{j}
, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|>\log n\sqrt{d}\right)<2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right).

(C.23)

Performing a union bound we also get

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i,j}\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|>\log n\sqrt{d}\right)<2n^{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)<2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right).

(C.24)

This implies that

\displaystyle\left\lVert M_{2}-\tilde{M}_{2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|M_{2}-\tilde{M}_{2}\right\|_{F}

(C.25)

\displaystyle=p\left\|\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\left(\frac{\left(X^{*l}\right)\left(X^{*l}\right)^{\top}}{d^{l}}-I\right)\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq p\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\left\|\frac{\left(X^{*l}\right)\left(X^{*l}\right)^{\top}}{d^{l}}-I\right\|_{F}

\displaystyle\leq p\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}\sqrt{n^{2}\left(\frac{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i,j}\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|^{l}}{d^{l}}\right)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq p\sqrt{n^{2}\left(\frac{\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i,j}\left|x_{i}^{\top}x_{j}\right|^{3}}{d^{3}}\right)^{2}}\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(p\frac{n\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=o(p).

Merging (
C.22
) and (
C.25
) together, a standard application of Weyl’s inequality gives

\lambda_{\rm max}\left(M_{2}\right)=\Theta(p),\qquad\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{2}\right)=\Theta(p),

(C.26)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right)
.
Thus, recalling that
\lambda_{i}(\cdot)
denotes the
i
-th eigenvalue of a matrix sorted in non-increasing order and applying Weyl’s inequality, we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\left(M_{1}+M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{i}\left(M_{1}\right)-\lambda_{\rm max}\left(M_{2}\right)=\Omega(pn/d),\qquad\textup{for }i\in[d],

(C.27)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\left(M_{1}+M_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{i}\left(M_{1}\right)+\lambda_{\rm max}\left(M_{2}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),\qquad\textup{for }d<i\leq n,

where in both lines we used our argument in (
C.20
) and (
C.26
). Since
\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]=M_{1}+M_{2}
, we have proved that the spectrum of
\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]
has a gap, as

\lambda_{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{pn}{d}\right)=\omega(p),

(C.28)

and

\lambda_{d+1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right).

(C.29)

This proves (
C.13
) and (
C.14
). To show (
C.15
), it suffices to note that

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(M_{2}\right)=\Theta(p),

(C.30)

where the first step is true since
M_{1}
is p.s.d., and the second step follows from (
C.26
).
∎

Lemma C.4
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
.
Then, we have that

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right),

(C.31)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Consider the truncated function

\bar{\phi}(z):=\phi(z)\mathbf{1}\left(|z|\leq\frac{\log p}{L}\right),

(C.32)

where
L
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. Define also the truncated kernel
\bar{K}
accordingly

\bar{K}_{ij}:=\bar{\phi}(Vx_{i})^{\top}\bar{\phi}(Vx_{j}).

(C.33)

We now compare
\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]
. Let
v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I/d)
and define the random variable

E_{ij}:=\mathbf{1}\left(|v^{\top}x_{i}|>\frac{\log p}{L}\;\textup{ or }\;|v^{\top}x_{j}|>\frac{\log p}{L}\right).

(C.34)

As
E_{ij}
is an indicator,
E_{ij}=E_{ij}^{2}
and

\mathbb{P}_{V}(E_{ij}=1)\leq\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(|v^{\top}x_{i}|>\frac{\log p}{L}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(|v^{\top}x_{j}|>\frac{\log p}{L}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}p\right),

(C.35)

where the second step holds as
v^{\top}x_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
v^{\top}x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in the probability space of
V
. Thus, a union bound over
i
and
j
gives

K=\bar{K},

(C.36)

with probability at least
1-2n^{2}\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}p\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
. Furthermore, we can write

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}_{ij}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K_{ij}]\right|

\displaystyle=p\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\phi(v^{\top}x_{i})\phi(v^{\top}x_{j})\,E_{ij}\right]\right|

(C.37)

\displaystyle\leq p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(\phi(v^{\top}x_{i})\phi(v^{\top}x_{j})\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[E_{ij}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(\phi(v^{\top}x_{i})\right)^{4}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}_{V}(E_{ij}=1)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2p\,C_{1}^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}\log^{2}p}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2p\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}p\right).

Here, the second and third line follow from Cauchy-Schwartz inequality; the fourth line is a consequence of (
C.35
) and
\phi(v^{\top}x_{i})
being a sub-Gaussian random variable (
\phi(z)
is Lipschitz) and thus with bounded fourth moment (see Equation 2.11 in
Vershynin (2018)
).
This result holds for any
i,j\in[n]
, and therefore implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right\|_{F}

(C.38)

\displaystyle\leq 2pn\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}p\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}p\right).

Then, by Weyl’s inequality, we have

\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]\right)\geq\lambda_{\rm min}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]\right)-\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\Omega(p),

(C.39)

where the last step follows from Lemma
C.3
and it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
.

We now prove the bound in (
C.31
) on the truncated kernel
\bar{K}
. Let us then define the matrix
H_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as

H_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}.

(C.40)

This definition requires
\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]
to be invertible, and it is therefore conditioned on the event described by (
C.39
). We will condition on this high probability event over
X
until the end of the proof. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}\right].

(C.41)

Note that there exists a constant
C
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[H_{k}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\sup_{v_{k}}\left\|\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.42)

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\sup_{v_{k}}\left\|\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}}{p}\leq C\frac{n}{p}\log^{2}p,

where the first step is true because of Jensen and triangle inequality, the third step is true because of (
C.39
), and the last step holds because, for every
i\in[n]
,

\left|\bar{\phi}(v_{k}^{\top}x_{i})\right|\leq\left|\phi(0)\right|+L\frac{\log p}{L}=\left|\phi(0)\right|+\log p\leq C_{3}\log p,

(C.43)

where we use that
\phi
is
L
-Lipschitz and the definition in (
C.32
).
We also have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k}^{\top})\right]\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}

(C.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}I,

which allows us to write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left(H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[H_{k}]\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}\right]^{2}

(C.45)

\displaystyle\preceq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\preceq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert H_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}H_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\preceq C\frac{n\log^{2}p}{p}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[H_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=C\frac{n\log^{2}p}{p^{2}}I,

where the third line holds since
H_{k}
is p.s.d. for all
v_{k}
, the fourth line is a consequence of (
C.42
), and the last step of (
C.44
). This readily gives

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left(H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[H_{k}]\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{Cn\log^{2}p}{p^{2}}.

(C.46)

Thus, (
C.41
) is the sum of
p
independent (in the probability space of
V
), mean-0,
n\times n
symmetric random matrices, such that
\left\lVert H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[H_{k}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{Cn\log^{2}p}{p}
almost surely for all
k
. Furthermore, we also have
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left(H_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[H_{k}]\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{Cn\log^{2}p}{p^{2}}
. Then, by Theorem 5.4.1
Vershynin (2018)
, we get

\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq t\right)\leq 2n\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\frac{Cn\log^{2}p}{p}+\frac{Cn\log^{2}p}{3p}t}\right).

(C.47)

Setting

t=\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log p\log n\leq C_{4},

(C.48)

where the last step holds since
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)

(C.49)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\log n-\frac{\log^{2}n}{2C+2Ct/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(\log n-\frac{\log^{2}n}{2C+2CC_{4}/3}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}n\right),

which gives the result in (
C.31
) on the truncated kernel
\bar{K}
. We will now translate this in the desired result.
Note that, by (
C.36
), we have

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},

(C.50)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
. Applying the triangle inequality we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.51)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\frac{C_{5}}{p}\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}p\right),

where the last step follows from (
C.38
) and Lemma
C.3
, and it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.52)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},

where the last step holds since

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.53)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert I\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{C_{5}}{p}\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}p\right),

and the last step of (
C.53
) follows from (
C.38
) and Lemma
C.3
, and it holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
.
Thus, using consecutively (
C.50
), (
C.51
), and (
C.52
), we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.54)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\frac{C_{5}}{p}\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}p\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\left(\bar{K}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\frac{C_{5}}{p}\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}p\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right),

where the last step follows from (
C.49
), and the steps jointly hold with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{8}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma C.5
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
.
Then, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, all the following hold

\lambda_{d}(K)=\Omega\left(\frac{pn}{d}\right),

(C.55)

\lambda_{d+1}(K)=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.56)

\lambda_{n}(K)=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(p),

(C.57)

where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

By Lemma
C.4
, we have that, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, for all
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have

u^{\top}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{-1/2}\right)u\leq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\left\|u\right\|_{2}^{2},

(C.58)

where
C
is an absolute constant.
Then, setting
u=\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]^{1/2}\hat{u}
, we have that, for all
\hat{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\hat{u}^{\top}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\hat{u}\leq\hat{u}^{\top}\left(C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\,\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\hat{u},

(C.59)

which reads

K-\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\preceq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\,\mathbb{E}_{V}[K].

(C.60)

In the same way, considering instead
\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]-K\right)
in the very first equation, we can also derive

\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]-K\preceq C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\,\mathbb{E}_{V}[K],

(C.61)

which therefore gives

\left(1-C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\preceq K\preceq\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K].

(C.62)

By the Courant–Fischer–Weyl min-max principle, we can write

\lambda_{i}(K)=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{S}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in S,\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}Ku\right),

(C.63)

where
S
is any
i
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
S^{*}=\arg\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{S}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in S,\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}Ku\right)
. Then, by (
C.62
), we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}(K)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in S^{*},\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}Ku\right)

(C.64)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in S^{*},\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}\left(\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)u\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{S}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits_{u\in S,\,\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1}\left(u^{\top}\left(\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)u\right)

\displaystyle=\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right),

where in the last line we use the same principle to express the
i
-th eigenvalue of
\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]
.
Following the same strategy, we can therefore conclude that

\left(1-C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)\leq\lambda_{i}\left(K\right)\leq\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right).

(C.65)

By Lemma
C.3
, we have that, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log n\right)
over
X
,
\lambda_{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)=\Omega(np/d)
, and that
\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}_{V}[K]\right)=\Theta(p)
, for
d+1\leq i\leq n
. This implies

\lambda_{d}(K)=\Omega\left(\left(1-C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)\frac{np}{d}\right)=\Omega\left(\frac{pn}{d}\right),

(C.66)

\lambda_{d+1}(K)=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(1+C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)p\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.67)

\lambda_{n}(K)=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega\left(\left(1-C\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\right)p\right)=\Omega(p),

(C.68)

where we use
n\log^{2}n=o(p/\log^{2}p)
. This gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
d=o(p)
and
d=o(n)
.
Then, we have

\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{np}{d}}\right),

(C.69)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-cd\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The
k
-th row of
\Phi^{\top}
takes the form
\phi(Xv_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since the Gaussian distribution is Lipschitz concentrated (see Theorem 5.22 in
Vershynin (2018)
), we have that

\left\|\phi(Xv_{k})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\left\|\phi(Xv_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\phi(Xv_{k})\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}\frac{\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{\sqrt{d}},

(C.70)

where the first step holds since
the 0-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
is zero
, and the second step holds since
\phi
is Lipschitz. Notice that the term
\sqrt{d}
is due to the fact that
\sqrt{d}\,v_{k}
is standard Gaussian. We remark that the sub-Gaussian norm in this equation is intended in the probability space of
v_{k}
.
By Lemma
C.2
, we have that

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right),

(C.71)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}d\right)
over
X
. Thus, conditioning on this high probability event, due to (
C.70
), we have that
\Phi^{\top}
is a
p\times n
matrix whose rows are i.i.d. mean-0 random vectors with sub-Gaussian norm
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n/d}\right)
. Then, by Lemma B.7 of
Bombari et al. (2022)
, we have

\left\lVert\Phi^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{np}{d}}\right),

(C.72)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}n)
over
V
, which provides the desired result.
∎

Lemma C.7
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n\log^{3}n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}\right)
. Then, we have

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{K}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.73)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The proof follows the same strategy as the proof of Lemma
C.3
, with the difference that now there is no term
M_{1}
. In fact,
\tilde{\phi}
has the same Hermite coefficients as
\phi
, except that
\tilde{\mu}_{1}=0
. The thesis then follows from (
C.26
). We remark that to prove this result, differently from the proof of Lemma
C.3
,
n=\omega(d)
is not required.
Also, we require only
n\log^{3}n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}\right)
instead of
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
. In fact, we do not need a bound on the smallest eigenvalue of
M_{2}
(see (
C.26
)), and therefore we just need
\left\lVert M_{2}-\tilde{M}_{2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right)
(see (
C.25
)).

∎

Lemma C.8
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
and
n\log^{3}n=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}\right)
.
Then, we have

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.74)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Consider the truncated function

\bar{\phi}(z):=\tilde{\phi}(z)\mathbf{1}\left(|z|\leq\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}\right),

(C.75)

where
\tilde{L}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\tilde{\phi}
. Define the truncated kernel
\bar{K}
accordingly

\bar{K}_{ij}:=\bar{\phi}(Vx_{i})^{\top}\bar{\phi}(Vx_{j}).

(C.76)

We now compare
\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]
and
\mathbb{E}_{V}[\tilde{K}]
. Let
v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I/d)
and define the random variable

E_{ij}:=\mathbf{1}\left(|v^{\top}x_{i}|>\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}\;\textup{ or }\;|v^{\top}x_{j}|>\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}\right).

(C.77)

Note that since
E_{ij}
is an indicator we have
E_{ij}=E_{ij}^{2}
, and

\mathbb{P}_{V}(E_{ij}=1)\leq\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(|v^{\top}x_{i}|>\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}\right)+\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(|v^{\top}x_{j}|>\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}\right)\leq\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}p\right),

(C.78)

where the second step holds as
v^{\top}x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in the probability space of
V
.

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}_{ij}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\tilde{K}_{ij}]\right|

\displaystyle=p\left|\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{i})\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{j})\,E_{ij}\right]\right|

(C.79)

\displaystyle\leq p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{i})\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{j})\right)^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[E_{ij}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq p\,\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left(\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{i})\right)^{4}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{P}_{V}(E_{ij}=1)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq p\,C^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{1}\log^{2}p}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq p\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}p\right).

Here, the second and third lines follow from Cauchy-Schwartz inequality; the fourth line is a consequence of (
C.78
) and
\tilde{\phi}(v^{\top}x_{i})
being a sub-Gaussian random variable (
\tilde{\phi}(z)
is Lipschitz), and thus with bounded fourth moment (see Equation 2.11 in
Vershynin (2018)
). This result holds for any
i,j
, and therefore implies

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\tilde{K}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\tilde{K}]\right\|_{F}

(C.80)

\displaystyle\leq pn\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}p\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}p\right),

where in the last step we used
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right)
, which follows from Assumption
3
.
This last equation, together with Lemma
C.7
, gives

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{K}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.81)

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}n\right)
. We will condition on this high probability event until the end of the proof.

We now define the matrix
\bar{H}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as

\bar{H}_{k}=\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top},

(C.82)

which implies

\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\bar{H}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{H}_{k}\right].

(C.83)

Now, we have

\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert\bar{H}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{H}_{k}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq 2\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert\bar{H}_{k}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq 2\sup_{v_{k}}\left\|\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2n\log^{2}p,

(C.84)

where the first step is true because of Jensen and triangle inequality, and the last step holds because, for every
i\in[n]
,

\left|\bar{\phi}(v_{k}^{\top}x_{i})\right|\leq\tilde{L}\frac{\log p}{\tilde{L}}=\log p,

(C.85)

given the definition in (
C.75
).
Also, note that

\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{H}_{k}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})\bar{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\right]=\frac{1}{p}\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{K}],

(C.86)

which gives, together with (
C.81
),

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{H}_{k}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right).

(C.87)

Then, we can use (
C.84
) and (
C.87
), together with the same argument in (
C.45
), to get

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left(\bar{H}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{H}_{k}]\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(n\log^{2}p\right).

(C.88)

Thus, (
C.83
) is the sum of
p
independent (in the probability space of
V
), mean-0,
n\times n
symmetric random matrices, such that
\left\lVert\bar{H}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{H}_{k}]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq 2n\log^{2}p
almost surely for all
k
, and
\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left(\bar{H}_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{V}[\bar{H}_{k}]\right)^{2}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq Cn\log^{2}p
, for some constant
C
. Then, by Theorem 5.4.1 of
(
Vershynin, 2018
)
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq t\right)\leq 2n\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{Cnp\log^{2}p+2tn\log^{2}p/3}\right).

(C.89)

Setting
t=p
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq p\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{5}\frac{p}{n\log^{2}p}\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}n\right),

(C.90)

where in the last step we used
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
. To conclude, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}(\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\neq\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits_{i\in[n],k\in[p]}|v_{k}^{\top}x_{i}|>\log p/\tilde{L}\right)

(C.91)

\displaystyle\leq 2np\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}p\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}p\right),

where the second step holds as
v_{k}^{\top}x_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
in the probability space of
V
, and the last step holds because of the assumption
n=\mathcal{O}\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
. Putting (
C.90
) and (
C.91
) together, we finally get

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\bar{\Phi}\bar{\Phi}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p\right),

(C.92)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, which gives the thesis. ∎

Lemma C.9
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and
p=\omega(n)
. Then, we have

\left|\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-Mp\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\log n\right),

(C.93)

\left|\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-M_{1}p\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\log n\right),

(C.94)

with
M
and
M_{1}
being two positive constants only depending on
\phi
, with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c\log^{2}n)
over
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Furthermore, we have that
M-M_{1}=\mu_{1}^{2}>0
, which, conditioning on the previous result, implies

\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}-\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{p}).

(C.95)

Proof.

We have

\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\phi(v_{k}^{\top}x_{1})^{2},

(C.96)

where we use the shorthand
v_{k}
to indicate the
k
-th row of
V
. As
\phi
is Lipschitz,
v_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I/d)
and
\left\|x_{1}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, we have that
\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}
is the sum of
p
independent sub-exponential random variables, in the probability space of
V
.
Thus, by Bernstein inequality (see Theorem 2.8.1 in
Vershynin (2018)
), we have

\left|\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\log n\right),

(C.97)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log^{2}n)
, over the probability space of
V
. Exploiting the Hermite expansion of
\phi
, we get

\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=p\,\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{(}0,1)}\left[\phi(\rho)^{2}\right]=pM,

(C.98)

where we set
M=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}
.

The same argument applied on
\tilde{\phi}
, which is also Lipschitz, guarantees

\left|\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-M_{1}p\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\log n\right),

(C.99)

where
M_{1}=\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}
, since the 0-th, 1st and 2nd Hermite coefficients of
\tilde{\phi}
are 0. Thus, we readily get

M-M_{1}=\mu_{1}^{2}.

(C.100)

To conclude, it is sufficient to notice that, conditioning on the previous two high probability events, we have that
\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}
and
\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}
are both
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right)
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}-\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}+\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}}

(C.101)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(M-M_{1})p-\left|\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-pM\right|-\left|\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}^{2}-M_{1}p\right|}{\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}+\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}}

\displaystyle=\Omega(\sqrt{p}),

where we use the triangle inequality twice in the second line and conclude using
\sqrt{p}=\omega(\log n)
.
∎

Lemma C.10
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
. Then, we jointly have

\left\lVert X^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(C.102)

\left\lVert V^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{p}}\right),

(C.103)

\left\lVert\mu_{1}V^{\top}-X^{+}\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right),

(C.104)

\left\lVert\mu_{1}V^{\top}\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(C.105)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

The first two statements easily follow from Lemma
C.2
, which gives

\left\lVert X^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)^{-1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(C.106)

\left\lVert V^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(V^{\top}V\right)^{-1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{p}}\right),

(C.107)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}d\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
. For the third statement, we condition on this high probability event, and notice that

\Phi=\mu_{1}XV^{\top}+\tilde{\Phi},

(C.108)

which gives

\left\lVert\mu_{1}V^{\top}-X^{+}\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert X^{+}\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert X^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right),

(C.109)

where the last step holds because of Lemma
C.8
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right)
. For the fourth statement, we write

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mu_{1}V^{\top}\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\mu_{1}V^{\top}-X^{+}\Phi\right)\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert X^{+}\Phi\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.110)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mu_{1}V^{\top}-X^{+}\Phi\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert X^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\,\sqrt{\frac{1}{p}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

where the second step follows from

\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)^{-1/2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\right),

(C.111)

which holds with probability
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}n\right)
because of Lemma
C.5
. This final result provides the thesis.
∎

Lemma C.11
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
. Let
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be the projector on the span of the
d
eigenvectors corresponding to the
d
highest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
, and
P_{V}
be the projector on the span of the columns of
V
. Then, we have that

\left\lVert P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right),

(C.112)

and

\left\lVert P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}P_{V}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right),

(C.113)

jointly hold with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a vector such that
\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1
. Then, we have

\left\|\Phi P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{d+1}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi)}=\sqrt{\lambda_{d+1}(K)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.114)

where the first inequality follows by the definition of
P_{\Lambda}
, and
\lambda_{d+1}(K)
(
\lambda_{d+1}(\Phi^{\top}\Phi)
) is the
(d+1)
-th eigenvalue of
K
(
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
) when sorting them in non-increasing order. Then, the last equality holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, because of Lemma
C.5
. Then, conditioning on the previous high probability event, since
\Phi=\tilde{\Phi}+\mu_{1}XV^{\top}
, we can write

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)}\left\|V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mu_{1}XV^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}

(C.115)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\tilde{\Phi}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}+\left\|\Phi P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|u\right\|_{2}+\left\|\Phi P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

where the last step is a consequence of Lemma
C.8
, and it holds with probability
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
.
By Lemma
C.2
, we have that
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)=\Theta(n)
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}d\right)
. Conditioning on such high probability events, (
C.115
) gives

\left\|V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{n}}\right),

(C.116)

which provides the first part of the thesis, since it uniformly holds for every
u
(as we did not condition on any event dependent on
u
itself).
To conclude, since we have

P_{V}=(V^{+})^{\top}V^{\top},

(C.117)

we can use Lemma
C.10
to write

\left\|P_{V}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}=\left\|(V^{+})^{\top}V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}\leq\left\lVert V^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}u\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right),

(C.118)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}d\right)
over
V
. Since again this result holds uniformly on all
\left\|u\right\|_{2}=1
and
\left\lVert P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}P_{V}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\left\lVert P_{V}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
, the thesis readily follows.
∎

Lemma C.12
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)
and
n=\omega(d)
. Let
P_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be the projector on the span of the
d
eigenvectors corresponding to the
d
highest eigenvalues of
\Phi^{\top}\Phi
. Then, we have

\left\|P_{\Lambda}\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{p}),

(C.119)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over

and
V
, where

is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Multiple applications of the triangle inequality yield

\displaystyle\left\|P_{\Lambda}\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}

C.11

\left\|\varphi(x_{1})\right\|_{2}-\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(x_{1})\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{p}),

and

which readily gives the thesis.
∎

Lemma C.13
.

We have that

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{pn}{d}}\log n\right),

(C.123)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Note that
\tilde{\phi}
is Lipschitz (since
\phi
is Lipschitz by Assumption
2
). During the proof, we condition on the event
\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
, which is true with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}d\right)
by Lemma
C.2
, and we use the shorthand
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to denote a random vector such that
\sqrt{d}\,v
is a standard Gaussian vector,
i.e.
, it has the same distribution as the rows of
V
.
This implies

\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right),\qquad\left\|\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{d}}\right),

(C.124)

and

\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|v\right\|_{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),\qquad\left\|\left\|v\right\|_{2}-\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\left\|v\right\|_{2}\right]\right\|_{\psi_{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(C.125)

where both sub-Gaussian norms are meant on the probability space of
v
. Here, the very first equation follows from the discussion in Lemma C.3 in
Bombari et al. (2022)
, and the upper bounds on the sub-Gaussian norms follow from the Lipschitz concentration property of
\sqrt{d}v
.
Then, there exists an absolute constant
C_{1}
such that we jointly have

\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Xv\right)\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{n},\qquad\left\|v\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1},

(C.126)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
over
v
.

Let
E_{k}
be the indicator defined on the high probability event above with respect to the random variable
v_{k}:=V_{:k}
(the
k
-th row of
V
),
i.e.
,

E_{k}:=\mathbf{1}\left(\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\textup{ and }\;\left\|\tilde{\phi}\left(Xv_{k}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq C_{1}\sqrt{n}\right),

(C.127)

and we define
E\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
as the diagonal matrix containing
E_{k}
in its
k
-th entry. Notice that we have
\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=0
with probability at least
1-2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
, and
\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right]\leq 2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)
.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}\left(I-E\right)V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert 1-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right]

(C.128)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert I-E\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{4}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{4}\right]^{1/4}\left(2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|\tilde{\Phi}\right\|^{4}_{F}\right]^{1/4}\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\left\|V\right\|^{4}_{F}\right]^{1/4}\left(2p\exp\left(-c_{2}d\right)\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=o(1),

where the last step holds because of our initial conditioning on
X
: the first two terms are the sum of finite powers of sub-Gaussian random variables (the entries of
\tilde{\Phi}
and
V
), and thus (see Proposition 2.5.2 in
Vershynin (2018)
) the first two factors in the third line of the previous equation will be
\mathcal{O}\left(p^{\alpha}\right)
for some finite
\alpha
, which gives the last line due to Assumption
3
.

Exploiting the Hermite expansion of
\tilde{\phi}
we can write

\left[\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right]_{ij}=p\left[\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv)v^{\top}\right]\right]_{ij}=\frac{p}{\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{E}_{v}\left[\tilde{\phi}\left(x_{i}^{\top}v\right)\left(e_{j}^{\top}\left(\sqrt{d}v\right)\right)\right]=0,

(C.129)

where the last step holds since the first Hermite coefficient of
\tilde{\phi}
is 0. Thus, the application of the triangle inequality to this last equation and (
C.128
) gives

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}\left(I-E\right)V\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=o(1),

(C.130)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}d\right)
over
X
.

Let’s now look at

\tilde{\Phi}EV-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{p}\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]=:\sum_{k=1}^{p}W_{k},

(C.131)

where we defined the shorthand
W_{k}=\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]
. (
C.131
) is the sum of
p
i.i.d. mean-0 random matrices
W_{k}
(in the probability space of
V
), such that

\displaystyle\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{v_{k}}\left\lVert\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.132)

\displaystyle=2\sup_{v_{k}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})\right\|_{2}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}E_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2C_{1}^{2}\sqrt{n},

because of (
C.127
). Then, by matrix Bernstein’s inequality for rectangular matrices (see Exercise 5.4.15 in
Vershynin (2018)
), we have that

\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}EV-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq t\right)\leq\left(n+d\right)\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}/2}{\sigma^{2}+2C_{1}^{2}\sqrt{n}t/3}\right),

(C.133)

where
\sigma^{2}
is defined as

\sigma^{2}=p\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits\left(\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}},\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[W_{k}^{\top}W_{k}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\right).

(C.134)

For every matrix
A
, we have
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(A-\mathbb{E}[A]\right)\left(A-\mathbb{E}[A]\right)^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[AA^{\top}\right]-\mathbb{E}[A]\mathbb{E}[A]^{\top}\preceq\mathbb{E}\left[AA^{\top}\right]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[W_{k}W_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}v_{k}E_{k}\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.135)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{v_{k}}\left(E_{k}\left\|v_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}^{2}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{1}^{2}}{p}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{K}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

where the last step is a direct consequence of Lemma
C.7
, and holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
.
For the other argument in the
\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits
in (

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[W_{k}^{\top}W_{k}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[v_{k}E_{k}\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})^{\top}\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})E_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.136)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{v_{k}}\left[v_{k}v_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{v_{k}}\left(E_{k}\left\|\tilde{\phi}(Xv_{k})\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d}\,C_{1}^{2}n

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n}{d}\right).

Then, plugging these last two equations in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{V}\left(\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}EV-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[\tilde{\Phi}EV\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\geq\sqrt{\frac{pn}{d}}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(n+d\right)\exp\left(-\frac{(pn/d)\log^{2}n/2}{C_{2}(pn/d)+2C_{1}^{2}\sqrt{np}\sqrt{n/d}\log n/3}\right)

(C.137)

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp\left(-c_{5}\log^{2}n\right),

where we used Assumption
3
. Then, applying the triangle inequality and using (
C.130
) and (
C.137
), we get

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}EV\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{pn}{d}}\log n\right),

(C.138)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X,V
.
To conclude, since

with probability at least

∎

Lemma C.14
.

We have that

\left\lVert V^{\top}P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.139)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}:=\sum_{l=3}^{\infty}\mu_{l}^{2}
, where
\mu_{l}
denotes the
l
-th Hermite coefficient of
\phi
, and let
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the matrix defined as

E=K-p\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right).

(C.140)

Note that

\displaystyle\left\lVert EK^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert K-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]-p\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.141)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]^{-1/2}\left(K-\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]\right)\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]^{-1/2}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mathcal{O}\left(p\frac{n\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\,\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{n}{p}}\log n\log p\,\frac{np}{d}\,\frac{1}{p}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}}{d}\log^{2}n+\frac{n\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right),

where in the second line we used (
C.25
) and
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(p)
, which holds by Lemma
C.5
; in the third line we used Lemma
C.4
,
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K\right)=\Omega(p)

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{V}\left[K\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(np/d\right)

C.20
) and (
C.26

\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{n}\right)
, given by Lemma
C.2
); in the fourth line we used
p=\Omega(n^{2})
, and the last step holds due to Assumption
3
. The full equation as a whole holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
.

By the Woodbury matrix identity (or Hua’s identity), we have

\displaystyle K^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left(p\left(\mu_{1}^{2}\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}I\right)+E\right)^{-1}

(C.142)

\displaystyle=\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}-\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}EK^{-1}.

Then, since we have

\left\lVert\frac{pX^{\top}}{d}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{1}^{2}\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(X^{\top}X\right)}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right),

(C.143)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
due to Lemma
C.2
, (
C.142
) allows us to write

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{pX^{\top}}{d}\left(K^{-1}-\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{pX^{\top}}{d}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}EK^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.144)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right),

where we used (
C.141
) and (
C.143
) in the last step, which then holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
. Notice that, with this same probability, the application of the triangle inequality to (
C.143
) and (
C.144
), together with Assumption
3
, gives

\left\lVert\frac{pX^{\top}}{d}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

(C.145)

Recalling that
\Phi=\mu_{1}XV^{\top}+\tilde{\Phi}
, another triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}-\frac{\mu_{1}pX^{\top}}{d}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert V^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mu_{1}\left\lVert\frac{d}{p}V^{\top}V-I\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\frac{pX^{\top}}{d}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.146)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{pn}{d}}\log n\,\frac{1}{p}+\sqrt{\frac{d}{p}}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{dn}}\right),

where the second line holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
because of Lemma
C.13
, Theorem 4.6.1 in
Vershynin (2018)
and (
C.145
), and the last step is a consequence of Assumption
3
.

We now define
\varrho_{\Lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
as the projector on the span of the eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of
K
. This implies

P_{\Lambda}\Phi^{\top}=\Phi^{\top}\varrho_{\Lambda}
, and therefore
P_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\Phi^{+}=\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{\perp}
. Hence, we have

\left\lVert\mu_{1}VX^{\top}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\left\lVert\Phi^{\top}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}^{\top}\varrho_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\sqrt{\lambda_{d+1}(K)}+\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\varrho_{\Lambda}^{\perp}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.147)

where the last step is a consequence of Lemma

C.8

and

, which holds with probability at least

over

\left\lVert X^{\top}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq\frac{1}{\mu_{1}\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(V^{\top}V\right)}}\left\lVert\mu_{1}VX^{\top}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\right).

(C.148)

Thus, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{7}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert V^{\top}P^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{\mu_{1}pX^{\top}}{d}K^{-1}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi^{\top}K^{-1}-\frac{\mu_{1}pX^{\top}}{d}K^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

(C.149)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{\mu_{1}pX^{\top}}{d}\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle+\left\lVert\frac{\mu_{1}pX^{\top}}{d}\left(K^{-1}-\left(\mu_{1}^{2}p\frac{XX^{\top}}{d}+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}pI\right)^{-1}\right)\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{dn}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(\mu_{1}X^{\top}X+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}dI/\mu_{1}\right)^{-1}X^{\top}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log n}{\sqrt{dn}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\mu_{1}X^{\top}X+{\tilde{\mu}}^{2}dI/\mu_{1}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert X^{\top}\varrho^{\perp}_{\Lambda}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\,\sqrt{d}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n}+\frac{\sqrt{n}\log^{3}n}{d^{3/2}}\right),

where the second step holds due to (
C.146
), the third step due to (
C.144

C.148

Lemma C.15
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
p=\omega(d)
and
\log p=\Theta(\log n)=\Theta(\log d)
. Let
x\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Then, we have

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}n+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.150)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c\log^{2}n\right)
over
V
, where
c
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
N
be a positive natural number that will be defined later, and let
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
be a matrix containing
\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x}_{i})
in its
i
-th row, where every
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
is sampled independently from
\mathcal{P}_{X}
. Importantly,
\hat{x}_{i}
is different from
x_{i}
(as they are defined as auxiliary random variables only useful for the purposes of this proof), but
\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x}_{i})=\tilde{\phi}(V\hat{x}_{i})
is defined with the same random features
V
in (
1.2
).
Set

n^{\prime}=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\left\lfloor\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\right\rfloor,\left\lfloor\frac{d^{3/2}}{\log^{3}d}\right\rfloor\right),\qquad N=p^{2}n^{\prime},

(C.151)

where
n^{\prime}
is a positive integer (as
p
is large enough). Note that this definition guarantees that the triple
(n^{\prime},d,p)
satisfies the scalings

n^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{p}{\log^{4}p}\right),\qquad n^{\prime}\log^{3}n^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}\left(d^{3/2}\right),

(C.152)

which provide the sufficient hypotheses to apply Lemma
C.8
on a
n^{\prime}\times p
block of
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}
.

Then,
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}
can be seen as the vertical stacking of
p^{2}
matrices with size
n^{\prime}\times p
. All these matrices are independent copies of each other (in the probability space of the
\hat{x}_{i}
-s), and each of these has operator norm
\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right)
by Lemma
C.8
, with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n^{\prime}\right)
. Thus, performing a union bound over these
p^{2}
matrices, we get

\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(p^{2}\,p\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{Np}{n^{\prime}}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(N\log^{4}p+\frac{Np\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.153)

with probability at least

1-2p^{2}\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n^{\prime}\right)\geq 1-2p^{2}\exp\left(-c_{2}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\log^{2}p,\log^{2}d\right)\right)

(C.154)

over
V
and
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Note that the rows of
\tilde{\Phi}_{N}
are identically distributed and such that

\sup_{\hat{x}_{i}}\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x}_{i})\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{0})\right\|_{2}+\tilde{L}\sup_{\hat{x}_{i}}\left\|V\hat{x}_{i}\right\|_{2}\leq\left\|\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{0})\right\|_{2}+\tilde{L}\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\sup_{\hat{x}_{i}}\left\|\hat{x}_{i}\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right),

(C.155)

where we denote with
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
a vector of zeros,
\tilde{L}
is the Lipschitz constant of
\tilde{\phi}
, and the last step follows from the bound on
\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}
given by Lemma
C.2
, which holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{3}d\right)
over
V
(high probability event over which we will condition until the end of the proof). This readily gives, for some constant
C_{1}
,

\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x}_{i})\right\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq C_{1}\sup_{\hat{x}_{i}}\left\|\tilde{\varphi}(\hat{x}_{i})\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\right).

(C.156)

We remark that the sub-Gaussian norm in (
C.156
) is intended in the probability space of
\hat{x}_{i}
, and that the bound holds jointly for every
i\in[N]
. Then, there exists a sufficiently small absolute constant
C_{2}
such that
C_{2}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}/\sqrt{p}
is a matrix with independent sub-Gaussian rows, with unit sub-Gaussian norm. Then, by Theorem 5.39 in
Vershynin (2012)
(see their Remark 5.40 and Equation (5.25)), we have that

\frac{C_{2}^{2}}{p}\left\lVert\frac{\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{p}{N}}\right),

(C.157)

with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{4}p\right)
over
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}}{N}-\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}+\frac{\left\lVert\tilde{\Phi}_{N}^{\top}\tilde{\Phi}_{N}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}}{N}

(C.158)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(p\sqrt{\frac{p}{N}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{p}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log^{4}p}{p}}+\sqrt{p}\,\sqrt{\frac{\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}p+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

where the first step follows from the triangle inequality, the second step is a consequence of (
C.157
) and (
C.153
), and the third step is a consequence of (
C.151
). Taking the intersection between the high probability events in (
C.153
) and (
C.157
), we have

\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\log^{4}n+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right),

(C.159)

with probability at least
1-2p^{2}\exp\left(-c_{5}\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits\left(\log^{2}p,\log^{2}d\right)\right)\geq 1-2\exp\left(-c_{6}\log^{2}n\right)
over
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
V
.
Note that the LHS of the previous equation does not depend on
\{\hat{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, which were introduced as auxiliary random variables. Thus, the high probability bound holds restricted to the probability space of
V
, and the desired result follows.
∎

Lemma C.16
.

Let Assumptions
1
and
2
hold, and let
n=o\left(p/\log^{4}p\right)
,
n\log^{3}n=o\left(d^{3/2}\right)

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

and
V

First, we can upper bound the LHS of (

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\varphi(x)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right]\leq 2\mu_{1}^{2}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(x^{\top}V^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right].

, it is sub-Gaussian with

\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(x^{\top}V^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right]\leq C\left\|V^{\top}\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left\lVert V^{\top}\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\|Y\right\|_{2}^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\,n\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right),

(C.162)

where the third step holds with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{1}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
because of Lemma
C.10
.
Next, we bound the second term of (
C.161
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\left(\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\theta^{*}\right)^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\theta^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\theta^{*}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\lVert\Phi^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}^{2}\left\|Y\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\left(\log^{4}n+\frac{p\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)\,\frac{1}{p}\,n\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{n\log^{4}n}{p}+\frac{n\log^{3}d}{d^{3/2}}\right)=o\left(1\right),

where the fourth line holds because of Lemmas
C.15
and
C.5
with probability at least
1-2\exp\left(-c_{2}\log^{2}n\right)
over
X
and
V
. The last line provides the desired result. ∎
