A 30 m aboveground biomass dataset for multiple vegetation types in China (2020)
Authors: Xiaoya Wang, Bo Zhong, Bo Chang, Yulin Zhang, Shuaifeng Li, Bailin Du, Yang Qiao, He Cai, Junjun Wu, Aixia Yang, Yi Jian, Jianrong Su, Genxu Wang, Qinhuo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately estimating aboveground biomass (AGB) is crucial for understanding terrestrial carbon cycling and informing climate policy. China’s diverse topography and rich vegetation types make it a significant contributor to the global carbon stock. However, existing AGB products often lack sufficient spatial resolution, data consistency, and accessibility to fully capture biomass patterns across the country’s varied ecosystems. Here, we present a 30 m-resolution, nationwide dataset of AGB density (AGBD) for China in 2020, which integrates multiple vegetation types. This product was developed using openly accessible, multi-source remote sensing data including LiDAR, optical, and radar imagery and enables consistent mapping of forests, grasslands, shrublands, croplands and wetlands. Systematic validation using field observations, national statistical yearbooks, and spatial distribution patterns, alongside comparisons with existing AGB products, demonstrates the model’s high accuracy with an average R2 of 0.85, RMSE of 31.26 Mg/ha and rRMSE of 50.04%, estimating a total carbon stock of 20.20 Pg across China’s vegetation areas. This dataset provides an updated, comprehensive baseline for biomass assessment, supporting carbon accounting and biodiversity monitoring in China.Subject terms: Forestry, Ecosystem ecology, Carbon cycle

Background & Summary

Accurate estimation of biomass is fundamental to understanding the global carbon cycle and monitoring ecosystem health1. Terrestrial ecosystems such as forests, croplands, shrublands, and grasslands play a critical role in mitigating climate change by acting as major carbon sinks2. China, with its diverse range of vegetation types from tropical rainforests to temperate grasslands makes a substantial contribution to global biomass carbon storage. Therefore, developing a baseline map of biomass across China’s terrestrial ecosystems can provide essential policy support for national climate strategies and emissions trading systems.

Total plant biomass comprises both aboveground biomass (AGB) and belowground biomass, such as living roots. In practice, AGB is typically the focus of estimation because belowground biomass is complex to measure accurately in the field3. Traditional methods for measuring AGB rely on field-based approaches, either through destructive sampling (cutting and weighing sample trees) or through non-destructive techniques that apply allometric models using tree dimensions such as height and diameter at breast height (DBH)4,5. In recent years, remote sensing-based approaches have increasingly supplanted field measurements and become a major focus of research. Satellite-based AGB estimation has advanced significantly over the past few decades, employing a range of sensors including passive optical instruments (e.g., Landsat, MODIS), active microwave sensors (e.g., ALOS PALSAR, ENVISAT ASAR), and active Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) systems (e.g., GLAS, ICESat). The increasing availability and accessibility of high-resolution, multi-source satellite imagery now enable timely, efficient, and large-scale estimation of AGB and monitoring of its dynamics at national and global scales6.

Compared to time-consuming and destructive field measurements, LiDAR sensors typically offer the most accurate and efficient estimates of AGB, making them a preferred method. However, despite their high precision, the substantial cost and logistical demands of LiDAR limit its scalability at national and global levels. As a result, previous studies have primarily focused on localized AGB estimation7–10. Launched in 2019, NASA’s Global Ecosystem Dynamics Investigation (GEDI) mission uses an orbital laser altimeter aboard the International Space Station to scan the Earth’s surface between latitudes 51.6°N and 51.6°S. The mission provides sparse AGB estimates across its observational domain, calibrated using coincident airborne LiDAR data and ground inventory plots. These estimates represent the largest existing reference dataset for AGB and can be integrated with remote sensing data and machine learning approaches to advance large-scale AGB estimation efforts.

Most existing regional and global AGB products are derived using high-resolution satellite remote sensing (SRS) data. Some rely on field-measured biomass as reference data, such as the Climate Change Initiative (CCI) global forest AGB maps, which were generated from satellite SAR imagery at a 100 m resolution over multiple years11, and a 30 m resolution forest AGB map of China for 2015–2021, produced using 6,667 field plots as training data12. Other studies use GEDI biomass estimates as reference, including an enhanced biomass product that integrates GEDI, TanDEM-X, and Landsat data13, as well as biomass maps for Australia and the United States developed using GEDI and SRS data2. However, many of these products depend heavily on extensive field inventory data or proprietary commercial imagery, such as TanDEM-X and PlanetScope, which are not openly accessible and present challenges for broad usability. Moreover, due to the limited penetration capability of optical and microwave electromagnetic signals through dense vegetation canopies, AGB retrieval from such imagery often suffers from saturation effects, meaning that beyond a certain biomass threshold, the received signal no longer increases14.

In China, several studies have specifically mapped the spatial distribution of forest AGB15–17. However, these efforts either have low spatial resolution or rely on data collected before 2010, making them insufficient to represent the current status of forest AGB in the country. Moreover, due to inconsistencies in the sources and acquisition times of ground inventory data, remote sensing imagery, and forest mask datasets used in these studies, substantial discrepancies exist among existing forest AGB products18. These inconsistencies pose challenges for accurate national carbon stock assessments. In addition, some studies have produced biomass maps for other vegetation types, such as grasslands19, croplands20, and shrublands21, but these estimates are mostly limited to specific regions or single vegetation types. Comprehensive assessments that integrate multiple vegetation types across the entirety of China remain scarce. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a systematic and up-to-date estimation of biomass across diverse vegetation types in China to establish a robust baseline for future AGB inversion research.

This study presents a 30-meter resolution AGB dataset for China in the year 2020, generated by integrating GEDI spaceborne LiDAR data, various SRS datasets, field survey data, and multiple Random Forest (RF) models. The product provides a comprehensive biomass estimation that covers all major vegetation types, including forests, grasslands, shrublands, and croplands, within a unified mapping framework. To demonstrate the strengths of this dataset, we conducted an extensive validation process, including spatial pattern analysis and accuracy assessments using field inventory data and national statistical yearbooks. In addition, the dataset was systematically compared with existing biomass products to highlight its unique advantages.

Methods

Study area

The study area encompasses the entirety of China, situated in eastern Asia along the western coast of the Pacific Ocean, covering approximately 9.6 million square kilometers. China’s vast territory and extensive geographical span result in a complex and diverse array of terrain and vegetation types. According to the 2020 WorldCover map22, the primary vegetation cover types in China for 2020 include trees (25.02%), grassland (22.57%), and cropland (15.58%) (Fig. 1). Coniferous forests constitute a significant proportion of the tree cover (52%)23.

Data sources and pre-processing

All satellite remote sensing datasets used in this study are openly accessible through the Google Earth Engine (GEE). These include NASA’s GEDI Level 4 A Version 2.1 data24, Copernicus Sentinel-2 Level 2 A multispectral imagery25, the GLO-30 Digital Elevation Model (DEM)26, and Phased Array L-band Synthetic Aperture Radar (PALSAR) Version 2 data provided by the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA)27.

GEDI footprint-level aboveground biomass data

GEDI is a spaceborne altimetry mission operated from the International Space Station (2018–2023, 2024–present), providing high-resolution laser observations of the Earth’s three-dimensional structure. The GEDI instrument acquires day and night time 1064 nm laser waveforms in 25 m diameter footprints. The AGBD is derived by applying an allometric model to relative canopy height estimates retrieved at each GEDI footprint waveform28. Due to its sampling strategy, GEDI offers near-global but spatially sparse coverage. For this study, we downloaded all available GEDI Level 4 A (Version 2.1) footprints across China for the year 2020. Previous research suggests an upper limit of approximately 800 Mg/ha for global forests AGB5,29. To mitigate the influence of anomalous extreme values potentially arising from noise or complex terrain within the GEDI data, we retained only biomass values within the range of [0, 800] Mg/ha. In accordance with guidance from the GEDI science team, we excluded invalid AGBD measurements that did not meet the quality criteria of the Level 4 A product (‘l4_quality_flag’ = 0 and ‘degrade_flag’ >0)30.

GEDI-derived AGBD measurements with a relative standard error (RSE) greater than 50% (i.e., ‘agbd_se’ / ‘agbd’ >50%) were also excluded due to their unreliability. However, this filtering step disproportionately removes most GEDI observations with AGBD values below 6 Mg/ha, which typically correspond to low-biomass ecosystems such as shrublands, grasslands, and croplands. To address the potential prediction bias caused by the underrepresentation of low-value samples, we implemented a targeted sample augmentation strategy. Specifically, we generated 1,865,634 low-biomass samples based on literature-derived estimates for representative regions, including the grasslands of Inner Mongolia7,9,10,19, the croplands and grasslands of the North China Plain9,31,32, and the temperate grasslands and shrublands of northern Xinjiang9,21,33,34. Concretely, based on field survey data and empirical biomass statistics from the literature mentioned above, we generated a portion of the low-biomass samples by sampling from a normal distribution. These synthetic samples were then mapped to nearby pixels with similar spatial and spectral properties, considering factors such as regional vegetation types, vegetation cover, and normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) similarity, thereby expanding the sample size. These synthetic samples were uniformly distributed across the regions, with biomass values ranging from 0.71 to 6.02 Mg/ha.

In addition, terrain slope can lead to overestimation or underestimation of elevation and height-related metrics, which in turn affect GEDI-derived AGBD estimates35–37. Recent studies have highlighted that solar noise interacts with terrain slope during daytime data acquisition, causing significant overestimation of the RH95 metric on steep slopes greater than 30 degrees. Since RH95 is closely related to AGBD38, such biases can substantially affect biomass estimation. To mitigate these effects, we excluded GEDI measurements collected on slopes steeper than 30° (derived from the GLO-30 DEM), as well as daytime GEDI observations (‘solar_elevation’ >0).

Satellite remote sensing data

Sentinel-2 is a European multispectral imaging mission that provides high-resolution imagery with a revisit frequency of five days at the equator. We first applied cloud and cloud-shadow masking using Google’s CloudScore + dataset, setting a threshold of 0.5 to filter the original imagery39. Digital number (DN) values were then converted to surface reflectance using the formula: (DN+BOA_offset)/10,000, where BOA_offset equals 0 prior to January 2022 and −1000 thereafter. Next, we applied median compositing to mosaic all available high-quality images from January to December 2020. All 12 available spectral bands were initially used as input features. Based on multiple repeated experiments and previous studies on biomass feature importance2,5,16,18, we further selected five key normalized difference spectral indices (NDSI). This reduction was necessary to enhance computational efficiency, as including too many input features did not result in significant improvements in model accuracy.

The use of spectral indices helps mitigate calibration issues associated with individual spectral bands caused by atmospheric conditions, illumination variability, and terrain effects, thereby enhancing consistency in large-scale analyses.

PALSAR-2, onboard the ALOS-2 satellite, is a synthetic aperture radar (SAR) system capable of capturing imagery at night and through clouds, making it a 24-hour, all-weather technology. The original SAR data were acquired in Fine Beam Dual-polarization (FBD) mode, which provides both HH and HV polarization channels. All SAR image strips from the year 2020 were mosaicked into a seamless composite covering the study area. DN values were then converted to γ0 in decibels using the equation: γ0=10−log10DN2−83.

Copernicus DEM GLO-30 is primarily derived from the TanDEM-X mission conducted by the German Aerospace Center (DLR). TanDEM-X, along with its twin satellite TerraSAR-X, employs SAR and interferometric SAR (InSAR) techniques to generate a global, high-resolution digital elevation model. The dataset has been locally refined using ancillary products derived from multiple satellite imagery26. DEM was subsequently used to calculate terrain elevation and slope in this study.

Land cover data

WorldCover, produced by the European Space Agency (ESA), is a 10-meter resolution land cover dataset available for the years 2020 and 2021. The product was generated using Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 imagery, in combination with the Copernicus Global 30 m DEM and various auxiliary datasets, through a gradient-boosted decision tree classifier40. WorldCover achieves a global overall accuracy of 74.4% and is publicly accessible at https://esa-worldcover.org22. In this study, we used the 2020 WorldCover land cover product as a base map to mask and extract China’s major vegetation types, including forests, croplands, grasslands, shrublands, and wetlands, for biomass estimation and reporting.

AGB estimation

Given the horizontal geolocation uncertainty of ±10 meters at the center of each GEDI footprint41, directly aligning GEDI data with SRS imagery can introduce spatial mismatches. To mitigate this issue, all input datasets (GEDI, SRS, and land cover data) were resampled to a common spatial resolution of 30 meters within GEE. This approach also helps reduce noise and contributes to improved performance of the machine learning models. Bilinear resampling was applied to the continuous GEDI and SRS data, while majority resampling was used for the categorical land cover data.

We employed the RF regression model on the GEE platform to extrapolate the discrete GEDI observations into spatially continuous AGBD estimates. Numerous studies have demonstrated that, compared with other commonly used regression models such as support vector machines and artificial neural networks, RF performs better in handling high-dimensional data. It is capable of modeling large numbers of non-independent or non-linearly separable predictor variables and is less sensitive to noise and feature selection. These properties make RF particularly well suited for monitoring complex ecosystems using diverse remote sensing variables, including forest height, biomass estimation, and forest classification42–45.

In our regression model, 22 predictor variables (listed in Table 1) derived from Sentinel, PALSAR, and DEM datasets were used as input features, while GEDI AGBD values served as the predicted variable. A total of 54,393,995 GEDI observations across China were stratified and randomly split into a training dataset (70%) and a holdout testing dataset (30%) based on AGBD value distributions. The parameter numberOfTrees refers to the number of decision trees, while bagFraction defines the fraction of training samples randomly selected for building each decision tree (with the remaining samples left out for out-of-bag validation). We used an iterative search method to identify the optimal parameter combination. Specifically, the numberOfTrees was varied from 10 to 150 in steps of 10, and bagFraction from 0.1 to 0.9 in steps of 0.1. To improve computational efficiency and generalization performance, the optimal model configuration—defined by the highest prediction accuracy—was found to be numberOfTrees = 50 and bagFraction = 0.6.

Due to the enormous volume of remote sensing data at fine spatial resolution across a vast area, training a global RF model is computationally intensive and time-consuming. Moreover, the GEE platform imposes certain limitations in terms of processing capacity and memory usage. To balance prediction accuracy and computational efficiency, we adopted a locally adaptive regression approach. Specifically, China was divided into 508 geographic tiles (Fig. 2) and an independent RF model was trained within each tile to generate the national AGB map.

Overview of the 508 geographical tiles used for local adaptive modeling. Seven blue rectangular tiles indicate selected representative vegetation regions for subsequent model performance evaluation: (a) Central Tibetan Plateau: Grasslands are the dominant vegetation type. To reduce noticeable boundary effects, where AGB values predicted by different RF models may vary significantly at tile edges, we implemented a neighborhood-based training strategy. For each tile, the RF model was trained using samples from a 3 × 3 surrounding tile window, and predictions were generated for the center tile only.

Spatial and temporal dependencies within the training and validation datasets may lead to overestimation of model accuracy46. To account for such dependencies and assess the extent to which each model generalizes to new data, we employed five-fold cross-validation. For each tile, we calculated the standard deviation (SD) of the root mean square error (RMSE) across multiple training-testing splits as a measure of overall model uncertainty. In addition, model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), RMSE and relative root mean square error (rRMSE), all computed based on the mean values from the validation datasets.

In this study, model validation was performed using 30% of the in situ GEDI measurements. Gaussian kernel density estimation was applied to visualize the density of scatter points. The prediction model achieved an average R2 of 0.85, RMSE of 31.26 Mg/ha and rRMSE of 50.04% (Fig. 3(a)). In addition, the average SD across 5-fold cross-validation was 6.28 Mg/ha. Validation results for the locally adaptive RF models in seven representative regions are shown in Fig. 3(b–h). Across all 508 tiles, the R2, RMSE, rRMSE and SD ranged from 0.72 to 0.93, 5.72 to 59.90 Mg/ha, 25.47% to 85.69%, and 0.74 to 15.78 Mg/ha, respectively. These statistics indicate a strong correlation between the estimated AGBD and the GEDI observations, suggesting that the RF model generalizes well to new data. However, it tends to slightly overestimate low biomass and underestimate high biomass values. This tendency can be attributed to potential design bias in GEDI’s model-based predictions, as discussed by Ståhl et al.47. They show that even high-quality remote sensing predictions can exhibit systematic biases due to limitations in the model training data, especially in regions where the model was trained with sparse or unbalanced field data.

Using the conversion factor of 0.47 between AGB and carbon stock48, the total AGB storage across China’s vegetated areas—including croplands, forests, grasslands, shrublands, and wetlands—was estimated at 20.20 Pg, with a national mean of 44.03 Mg/ha. Among these vegetation types, forests accounted for the largest share of AGB, totaling 13.50 Pg with an average of 119.36 Mg/ha, followed by grasslands (3.71 Pg; 36.34 Mg/ha), croplands (2.88 Pg; 40.92 Mg/ha), wetlands (0.06 Pg; 60.97 Mg/ha), and shrublands (0.04 Pg; 42.13 Mg/ha).

Data Records

This all-vegetation-type biomass product integrates multi-source remote sensing data acquired in 2020, including GEDI, Sentinel-2, PALSAR, WorldCover, and the GLO-30 DEM. As a result, it represents a temporally consistent biomass map for the year 2020. The dataset is freely available from the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center via 10.11888/Terre.tpdc.30279749. It is provided as GeoTIFF files at a spatial resolution of 30 meters, with AGB values expressed in units of megagrams per hectare (Mg/ha).

Technical Validation

Due to the limited availability of field-based biomass measurements that are temporally consistent with 2020 (most publicly available field datasets are concentrated between 2010 and 2015 or from other years), we adopted the following strategies to validate the biomass dataset presented in this study:

Comparison to existing forest AGB maps

To comprehensively assess the spatial patterns of the predicted AGB values, we compared our forest AGB estimates at the pixel level with three existing forest AGB products. These comparison datasets were selected based on temporal proximity (around the year 2020) or similar spatial resolution ( ≥100 m). The selected products include: (1) a global forest AGB map for 2010 developed by Santoro et al., which integrates high-resolution satellite remote sensing data including ICESat/GLAS LiDAR, Sentinel-1, Envisat ASAR along with 110,897 field measurements11; (2) a 30-meter resolution forest AGB map of China for 2019 produced by Guo and Yang et al., based on over 4,700 nationwide field plots and incorporating canopy height products, multispectral imagery, and environmental variables using a RF model5; and (3) a product by He and Zhang et al., which estimates forest AGB across China from 2013 to 2020 by integrating high-resolution satellite data (e.g., the Gaofen series, Landsat, Sentinel) with forest assessment indicators under the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) framework12.

To ensure comparability among the four AGB maps, we reprojected and resampled the three external products to match the projection and spatial resolution of our dataset using nearest-neighbor resampling. These harmonized datasets were then used to analyze differences in spatial distribution patterns and total forest carbon stocks across China.

We masked and compared the estimated forest AGB from our study with other forest AGB maps, revealing broadly similar spatial patterns but also some notable differences (Fig. 6). Across all four maps, high AGB values are consistently concentrated in southern Tibet and the Sichuan–Yunnan region, while lower values dominate northern and central China. Additionally, our AGB map displays some high values in Taiwan, whereas the Guo map shows high biomass in northeastern China, and the He map highlights high values in Hainan. These spatial patterns are not present in the CCI map.

Spatial distribution of forest AGB across China.

We further examined the histogram distributions of forest AGB values across the four maps (Fig. 7). The histogram derived from our study most closely resembles a normal distribution, and the majority of forest AGB values across all maps fall within the range of 50–150 Mg/ha. The CCI map displays a substantial proportion of low AGB values in the 0–30 Mg/ha range, which deviates significantly from the others. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences among the products, such as the lower spatial resolution, forest masking criteria, the temporal mismatch with the 2010 reference year, or potential local deviations as a global dataset. The peak frequency of our distribution aligns most closely with the He map, both centered around 100–120 Mg/ha. In terms of variability, our map has a standard deviation similar to those of the CCI and He maps, while the Guo map shows the smallest SD at 31.67 Mg/ha.

Frequency Distributions of AGB maps.

Some of the differences in mean AGB and total carbon stock shown in Table 2 are primarily attributable to variations in the total forest area used in the calculations. These differences arise from divergent definitions of forests, which can significantly affect carbon stock estimates. According to the China Forestry and Grassland Statistical Yearbook, the national forest area was reported as 218 million hectares in 202050. In our study, non-forest areas were masked using the ESA WorldCover land cover product. The He map used a forest cover layer generated within their research, while the Guo map employed the Globeland30 forest cover product. These three maps have relatively similar forest area estimates, with the Guo map being the closest to the official statistical yearbook. In contrast, the CCI map utilized the Hansen global forest dataset for forest masking, which resulted in a significantly different forest area. Moreover, the CCI product reports carbon stocks for the year 2010, making it temporally inconsistent with the other datasets. Total aboveground carbon stock for China’s forests was estimated by summing the carbon content of all forest pixels in each AGB map. Our study estimated China’s total forest aboveground carbon stock at 13.50 Pg C, which is approximately 2.27 times higher than the value from the CCI map, and about 15% higher than the estimates from the Guo and He maps (Table 2).

Validation using field data

To evaluate the accuracy and applicability of the AGB map produced in this study, we collected field survey data from 78 forest plots in Sichuan Province (Fig. 8). These include five 667 m2 plots surveyed in 2020, twenty-two 400 m2 plots from 2023, and fifty-one 400 m2 plots from 2024. The plots were established using RTK-based positioning, with a preference for monospecific or relatively homogeneous stands. Tree height (H) and diameter at breast height (DBH) were measured within each plot, and the average AGB was calculated to serve as ground-truth data (see Eqs. 10,11) for comparison with the satellite-derived biomass estimates. To ensure temporal consistency between the ground samples and the biomass product, we also generated biomass maps for Sichuan Province for the years 2023 and 2024.

In addition, we extracted 76 observation records from 2020, spanning various months (mainly from May to October) across nine monitoring stations referenced in three previous studies, using data available from the National Tibetan Plateau Data Center (TPDC). These include two grassland ecosystem stations (Haibei and Inner Mongolia) and five desert ecosystem stations (Ordos, Fukang, Linze, Naiman, and Shapotou) used by Guo et al. for observations in China’s arid and semi-arid regions51. Additional records include AGB measurements at different phenological stages for soybean and millet at the Ansai station from Wang et al.20, and seasonal AGB data from fenced plots at different slope positions in the Yinshan Mountains foothills reported by Yao et al.52. As the AGB values produced in this study represent annual mean aboveground biomass density, we calculated the annual mean AGB for each of the nine stations based on multiple monthly observations. These averaged values were then georeferenced and used as independent validation points against our biomass map.

The estimated AGB showed strong agreement with field-measured AGB at the pixel level. For the forest plots in Sichuan Province, the R2 and RMSE were 0.76 and 43.22 Mg/ha, respectively (Fig. 9(a)). For the grassland and cropland plots obtained from the TPDC, the R2 and RMSE were 0.50 and 9.33 Mg/ha, respectively (Fig. 9(b)). These results indicate that the model performs better in high-biomass forests than in low-biomass vegetation types. One contributing factor is the data preprocessing step, in which GEDI measurements with RSE greater than 50% were removed. This led to an underrepresentation of low AGB samples (e.g., <6 Mg/ha), which may have resulted in overestimation in low-biomass regions.

Assessment of AGB map against independent field data.

It is important to acknowledge the uncertainties associated with validation using the above field samples. First, the number of available ground-truth samples for grasslands, croplands, and shrublands was limited and not representative at the national scale. Second, there is a spatial resolution mismatch between our AGB map and the field plots, which could lead to geolocation discrepancies and imperfect spatial alignment. Therefore, establishing a more comprehensive and standardized vegetation observation network, along with developing biomass maps at higher spatial resolution, should be key priorities for future research.

Additionally, we compared the average AGB values across 32 first-level administrative regions in China (excluding Hong Kong and Macao) as derived from the yearbook and forest biomass products. The results are shown in Fig. 10. Our AGB product demonstrates a strong agreement with the yearbook-based estimates across the 32 regions (R2 = 0.78, RMSE = 22.22 Mg/ha; Fig. 10(a)). Compared to the statistical data, our estimates are higher in 25 provinces and lower in 7 provinces. Notably, the absolute errors in Ningxia and Hubei provinces exceed 30 Mg/ha. The AGB estimates from Guo’s map also show good agreement with the statistical data (R2 = 0.74), with an RMSE that is 0.87 Mg/ha lower than that of our product. In contrast, the He map (R2 = 0.48) and the CCI map (R2 = 0.47) exhibit weaker correlations with the statistical data. In particular, the AGB values estimated by the CCI product are consistently below the 1:1 reference line (Fig. 10(b)), indicating a systematic underestimation compared to the yearbook reference values.

Based on the evaluation using field measurements and comparisons with statistical data and existing AGB maps, we propose that this new dataset represents a reliable estimate of China’s total carbon stock and its spatial distribution for the year 2020. It can inform forest and grassland management decisions and support national carbon accounting efforts. The proposed modeling framework addresses key gaps in current approaches and can be extended to produce annual, wall-to-wall AGB maps at a 30-meter resolution for other regions, potentially at the global scale. While several previous studies have developed separate models for different plant functional types4 or land cover classes54, our findings demonstrate that a unified model trained across multiple vegetation types is feasible. This approach reduces training time and effectively mitigates the accuracy loss associated with limited training samples in specific categories.

Usage Notes

The dataset presented in this study is publicly available for free download. It is worth noting that the modeling approach involved dividing the entire territory of China into 508 grid cells, within which locally adaptive models were trained. This grid-based partitioning may have segmented ecologically or climatically similar regions into separate training zones, potentially introducing slight boundary effects in transitional areas between adjacent tiles. Future work could improve the spatiotemporal consistency of AGB estimates in these transition zones by training local models based on ecological regions, vegetation zones, or administrative boundaries such as provinces. Additionally, GEDI L4A AGBD outputs can exhibit regional biases, particularly in areas like Asia where few calibration plots were used to train the L4A model. Users with access to more field inventory data from specific regions of interest could apply a local calibration curve for pre-training calibration, which helps reduce the propagation of residual design bias from the L4A inputs into the model, thereby improving the accuracy of biomass estimates55. Finally, when applying this map to ecological modeling or carbon accounting in non-forest areas, users should fully account for the limitations associated with higher prediction uncertainty in low-biomass ecosystems, such as grasslands, shrublands, and croplands, due to the sparsity of field validation plots. Future efforts should focus on increasing field measurements in low-biomass regions to strengthen model calibration and validation.
