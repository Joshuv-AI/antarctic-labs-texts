Host-guided microbiome-metabolite interactions enable cross-kingdom SynComs for disease suppression
Authors: Shanshan Liu, Shuxian Wang, Jingyuan Zhang, Chengyuan Tao, Mohammadhossein Ravanbakhsh, Xu Xu, Kaiqi Wen, Dandan Xiang, Ou Sheng, Zongzhuan Shen, Chunyu Li, Rong Li, Qirong Shen, George A Kowalchuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe plant microbiome plays a crucial role in enhancing disease resistance, yet microbiome-based plant protection strategies remain limited by an incomplete understanding of how host selection, microbial interactions, and rhizosphere chemistry jointly shape pathogen suppression.ResultsHere, we adopt a “learning from nature” approach to design synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) that recapitulate naturally evolved disease-suppressive interactions, using banana Fusarium wilt as a model system. High-throughput profiling revealed that both bacterial and fungal communities contribute to varietal resistance. Resistance-associated microbial taxa were identified and isolated to assemble bacterial, fungal, and cross-kingdom SynComs representative of resistant versus susceptible hosts. SynComs derived from resistant varieties suppressed pathogen growth more effectively than those from susceptible hosts, with cross-kingdom SynComs exhibiting the strongest effects. Cross-kingdom SynCom inoculation significantly reduced disease severity and restructured both the composition and functional potential of the rhizosphere microbiome. Integrative transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed coordinated host metabolic reprogramming, characterized by increased accumulation of diverse metabolites, including alkaloids, amino acids, and flavonoids. Notably, supplementation with resistance-associated rhizosphere metabolites, such as stearic acid and shikimic acid, further enhanced disease suppression.ConclusionsTogether, our findings establish a mechanistic framework in which host-guided microbiome assembly and metabolite-mediated interactions jointly enable effective cross-kingdom SynComs for disease suppression, providing ecological principles for microbiome-based plant protection strategies. Introduction

Soil-borne plant pathogens represent a serious threat to crop yields and global food security [1, 2]. Breeding efforts have traditionally focused on plant traits associated with resistance, such as defense gene expression and antimicrobial compounds [3, 4]. Increasing evidence shows that the rhizosphere, a key hub of plant–microbe interactions, strongly influences plant health, with the microbiome functioning as an extension of the plant’s immune system [5, 6]. Recent studies have shown that the disease resistance level of resistant varieties is not only a function of the plant’s inherent ability to resist pathogen invasion, but also depends on the enrichment of functional microorganisms in the rhizosphere [7–9]. We posit that the natural assembly of disease-suppressive microbiomes can be used as a blueprint for the development of effective microbial amendment strategies to inhibit pathogen outbreaks.

Soil microbiomes have long been recognized as potential sources of plant-beneficial microbes, including those that help resist pathogen attack [10, 11]. The design of inoculation strategies has typically relied on the screening of microbial isolates for inhibitory effects on the pathogen followed by trials involving plant-soil systems. This stepwise approach allows researchers to first identify candidate microbes with strong antagonistic potential and then evaluate their effectiveness and stability under more complex, naturalistic conditions. While this has proven successful in some cases, such approaches are often unsuccessful or give unpredictable results. This stems primarily from an incomplete understanding of the ecology and interactions of the organisms involved [12, 13]. Increasing evidences suggest that disease suppression is a collective outcome of microbial consortia rather than individual bacterial or fungal species [14, 15], and a diverse microbial community typically results in more effective disease resistance than any single microbial taxon [16]. Also, it has been suggested that the combined effects of bacteria and fungi are important in determining disease-suppressive activities [17]. Thus, recent research has sought to engineer synthetic communities (SynComs) comprised of multiple strains, with particular attention to how to construct the most effective strain combinations for optimal SynCom effectiveness. In addition, plant exudates are known to be critical for the assembly of microbial populations in the rhizosphere [18], and shifts in exudation patterns upon pathogen attack have been linked with enhanced disease suppression of the rhizosphere microbiome [19]. It has therefore been suggested that the use of such metabolites (for instance as prebiotics) may enhance the effectiveness of microbial amendments [20]. Given the findings of this previous research, we propose that the engineering of disease-suppressive SynCom applications could benefit from a “learning from nature” approach, in which the ecological interactions of disease-suppressive crop varieties are used as a blueprint (Fig. 1). We proposed the following steps for the development of effective inoculation strategies, based on a general model of plant-pathogen interactions: (1) determine the taxa enriched by resistant plant varieties, targeting both bacterial and fungal communities due to their importance; (2) Recover isolates that correspond as well as possible to these taxa; (3) test their interactions with the pathogen individually and in combination (including combining bacteria and fungi); (4) combine this with the metabolites that the plants use to stimulate recruitment of these organisms in the rhizosphere.

Fusarium wilt of banana is a prevalent soil-borne disease caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense Tropical Race 4 (Foc TR4), posing a significant threat to the global banana industry [21]. In our previous study, it was shown that disease-resistant levels of banana can be explained to a large extent by a variety’s ability to recruit a disease-suppressive microbiome [9]. Here, we built upon this knowledge using three representative banana varieties with high (highly resistant variety: Zhongjiao no.9), medium (resistant variety: Kangku no.5), and low (susceptible variety: Baxi) levels of resistance to apply the strategy outlined above to design disease-suppressive intra-kingdom and cross-kingdom SynComs. We then validated the disease-suppressive effects of cross-kingdom SynComs in vivo and used multi-omics approaches to explore the underlying mechanisms. We further profiled metabolites to select compounds specific to the highly resistant variety and examined their ability to enhance the disease-suppressive activities of our SynComs. We hypothesized that (1) Plants selectively shape their rhizosphere microbiomes by recruiting specific microbiome components (both bacterial and fungal) through the mediation of plant-derived metabolites, thereby enhancing resistance to pathogens in the rhizosphere; (2) cross-kingdom SynComs composed of both bacterial and fungal members will provide stronger disease suppression than intrakingdom SynComs, with plant metabolites potentially enhancing this effect; and (3) cross-kingdom SynComs may enhance pathogen suppression through multiple mechanisms, including antagonism and resource competition. Although the learning-from-nature approach has been widely applied to plant-microbiome research to identify potentially beneficial microbes, our study aims to establish a comprehensive framework for designing and testing SynComs derived from different banana genotypes, integrating the roles of key plant metabolites. Although based on a specific plant-pathogen system, this framework is intended to provide insights for broader applications. We highlight the interplay between microbial consortia and plant metabolites in disease resistance and demonstrate how this understanding can inform the development of more effective disease suppression strategies. Furthermore, we demonstrated the potential effectiveness of such a learning-from-nature approach, in which natural assembly, microbial interactions and key metabolites are used to steer the development of robust plant protection strategies.

Materials and methods

Field experiment description, soil sampling and DNA extraction

In previous work, we examined a range of banana varieties representing a disease resistance gradient and demonstrated that microbiome assembly in the rhizosphere explained a significant amount of variation in plant disease resistance [9]. For the current study, we chose three representative varieties, including one susceptible, one resistant and one highly resistant variety (Baxi, Kangku no.5, Zhongjiao no.9). Some of the analyses within the current manuscript, including the field experimental settings, DNA extractions, and fungal community profiling, were performed as part of our previous study [9], in which the fungal ITS amplicon sequencing data (PRJNA830664) were published. Bacterial sequencing libraries were constructed as reported previously [22, 23]. In brief, the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 520 F and 802R (Table S1). The purified PCR products were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq PE 250 platform at Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China).

Bioinformatic analyses

Amplicon reads were processed using standard pipelines, including quality filtering in QIIME [24–26], operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% similarity with UPARSE [27], and taxonomic assignment was performed against the RDP Bacterial 16S database for 16S rRNA sequences (version 2.13) and the UNITE Fungal ITS database for ITS1 sequences (version 8.3). Bacterial sequence data were deposited in the NCBI SRA database under the accession number PRJNA1156543.

For visualizing the variance among samples in species complexity, Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was performed. Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was conducted to evaluate the effects of banana varieties on the whole soil microbial community using the R vegan package [28, 29]. Linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify differentially abundant taxa across varieties, combining Kruskal–Wallis and Wilcoxon tests followed by linear discriminant analysis (LDA) to estimate effect sizes [30]. Bacteria and fungi with markedly increased numbers were defined as those with an alpha value < 0.05 and an LDA score (log10) > 2 and displayed using iTOL [31].

Rhizosphere metabolite collection and GC-MS analysis

For the current, we used the rhizosphere metabolite data of three banana varieties from a previous publication to explore the interactions between rhizosphere metabolites and bacterial and fungal communities [9]. Briefly, experimental soil was collected from the field site mentioned above, sieved through a 2-mm mesh, and used to grow sterile banana seedlings in polypropylene pots filled with 2 kg of dry soil. Each variety was replicated six times under controlled conditions. After three months, roots were gently removed from the soil, and the tightly adhering soil was carefully collected from the root surface. The collected soil samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen for five minutes and then sent to BIOTREE Technology Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for GC–MS-based non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) based on the covariance matrix was performed to explore the differences of rhizosphere metabolites. PERMANOVA was conducted to evaluate the differences in metabolites across varieties. To trace significant differences in the composition of metabolites across different banana varieties, LDA was performed with an alpha value < 0.05 and a LDA score > 2 [30].

Isolation and identification of culturable rhizosphere microbes

Bacterial and fungal isolates were recovered from the rhizosphere soils of three banana varieties. For bacterial isolation, diluted soil suspensions were plated on 1/10 tryptic soy agar (TSA) and incubated at 30 °C for 3 days. For fungal isolation, soil suspensions were plated on Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol agar and colonies were transferred to Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) after 7 days at 28 °C. All strains were purified and identified by Sanger sequencing using 27F/1492R (bacteria) and ITS1/ITS4 (fungi) [32, 33], with taxonomic assignments confirmed by BLAST. Phylogenetic analyses were performed in Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis Version 7.0 [34] and visualized using iTOL.

Effect of bacterial and fungal isolates on pathogen growth

For bacterial strains, the antagonistic activity was evaluated using the modified dual culture assays [35]. In brief, approximately 1 × 1 cm of freshly grown Foc TR4 fungal hyphal mat was inoculated in the middle of the PDA plate, and 1μL of the bacterial suspension was inoculated in the eight corners of the plate (Fig. S1A). The PDA plate without inoculation of tested bacterial strains was used as the control treatment. Then the plates were placed for 7 d at 28 °C, and the effectiveness of each tested bacterial strain was observed and judged following the method provided in Fig. S1B.

For fungal strains, approximately 1 × 1 cm of freshly grown Foc TR4 fungal hyphal mat was inoculated on one side of the PDA plate; meanwhile, a hyphal mat of the test fungal isolates was inoculated on the opposite side of the plate [36] (Fig. S2A). The PDA plate inoculated with Foc TR4 served as the control treatment. Interactions between Foc TR4 and fungal isolates were scored by the criteria provided in Fig. S2B following 7 days post-incubation at 25 °C.

To complement the results of dual culture assays, we further tested the bacterial and fungal strains on the growth of Foc TR4 using supernatant assays (Fig. S3). For each isolate, sterile culture supernatants were mixed with a standardized Foc TR4 spore suspension, and pathogen growth was quantified by OD₇₅₀ after 48 h [37]. Growth promotion or inhibition was determined relative to negative and positive controls. Detailed procedures are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Design of SynComs to mimic consortia of different banana varieties

Based on sequence similarity and the source of bacterial and fungal strains, bacterial and fungal strains were selected to construct SynComs (Table S2). We constructed 19 SynComs in total, including 9 SynComs associated with the highly resistant variety, 7 SynComs associated with the susceptible variety, and 3 SynComs composed of strains from both varieties. To compare strains with different pathogen suppression capacities, the “High Suppressive” SynComs included the three strains with the strongest suppression, the “Low Suppressive” included the three strains with the weakest suppression, and “All” contained all strains isolated from the respective variety. The “All” was designed to test the combined effects and potential interactions among all representative strains. The total inoculum applied was standardized across all SynComs, ensuring comparability of results. For detailed design of SynComs, see Supplementary Methods. For the bacterial SynCom, strains were grown in TSB and resuspended in sterile water to a final density of 10⁷ cells mL−1. Fungal strains were cultured on PDA, and spores were collected and adjusted to 105 cells mL−1. Each of the bacterial or fungal strains was mixed in equal ratios, respectively. The microbial compositions of cross-kingdom SynComs comprised a combination of bacteria and fungi and were adjusted to a biomass ratio of 4:1 (bacteria to fungi) [38].

Soil incubation with SynComs associated with different varieties

Soil incubation experiments were performed in natural and sterilized soils to evaluate the pathogen-suppression ability of the 19 SynComs (Fig. S4). Each SynCom was added to 10 g soil at a final density of 10⁷ cells g−1, with sterile water as the control. After a one-week pre-incubation at 28 °C, soils were inoculated with Foc TR4 (105 cells g−1) and incubated for one month. Soil DNA was then extracted and Foc TR4 abundance was quantified by qPCR using primers FocSc-1/FocSc-2 (Table S1) [39]. Each analysis was performed in triplicate, and the results were expressed as log10 values (target copies per gram of soil).

To investigate the direct antagonistic effects of SynComs on the pathogen, we selected highly suppressive SynComs associated with a highly resistant variety for a supernatant assay. Detailed procedures and experimental design are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Disease suppression of cross-kingdom SynCom in vivo

To assess the in vivo disease-suppression ability of the bacterial-fungal cross-kingdom SynCom associated with the highly resistant banana variety, a pot experiment was conducted at Nanjing Agricultural University. Banana seedlings Baxi were transplanted into pots containing 2 kg of natural soil. After one week, plants were inoculated with the SynCom (sterile water as control), with six replicates per treatment. One week after SynCom inoculation, all plants were inoculated with the pathogen (105 cells g−1 soil). After one month, soil and roots were collected, disease severity was quantified as the proportion of corm blackening, and rhizosphere DNA was extracted for 16S rRNA gene, ITS region and metagenomic sequencing. Amplicon data were processed as described above. For metagenomic sequencing data, reads were quality-filtered (FastQC), adapters were removed and assembled using MetaSPAdes [40]. Redundant contigs and genes were removed using MMseqs2 to obtain a non-redundant gene catalog. Gene prediction was performed with Prodigal, and clean reads were mapped using Bowtie2 to calculate gene abundances [41]. Functional annotation was conducted using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [42]. Root samples were collected following the method described by Cao et al. [43]. Transcriptomic and widely targeted metabolomic analyses of root samples were performed by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Effects of rhizosphere metabolites on cross-kingdom SynCom-mediated pathogen inhibition and microbial abundance

An additional soil incubation experiment was conducted to explore how metabolites from highly resistant variety influence the pathogen-suppressive ability of cross-kingdom SynComs and microbial abundance. Each well of a 6-well plate was filled with 10 g of sterilized soil. The soil was inoculated with bacterial, fungal, or cross-kingdom SynComs assembled from highly resistant variety, with SynComs treatments from the susceptible variety serving as negative controls. Metabolite treatments mimicking the enriched metabolites of highly resistant (HRM) and susceptible (SM) plant varieties were prepared, with soils without any added metabolites serving as blanks (NM). The experiment included 18 treatment groups with 6 replicates per treatment. A detailed experimental design is provided in the Supplementary Methods. The same procedures as above were used for pathogen inoculation and DNA extraction. QPCR was employed to determine the abundances of total bacteria and total fungi, using primers Eub338F/Eub518R, ITS1/5.8 s (Table S1) [44], respectively.

In vivo effects of metabolite addition on disease suppression by SynComs

To evaluate the in vivo disease-suppressive effects of metabolite addition on cross-kingdom SynComs associated with the highly resistant variety, a pot experiment was conducted at Nanjing Agricultural University. Banana plantlets Baxi were transplanted into pots containing 1 kg of natural soil. After one week, plants were subjected to inoculation treatments comprising six groups: (1) control; (2) cross-kingdom SynCom from the highly resistant variety; (3) metabolites from the highly resistant variety; (4) cross-kingdom SynCom from the highly resistant variety combined with its metabolites; (5) metabolites from the susceptible variety; and (6) cross-kingdom SynCom from the highly resistant variety combined with metabolites from the susceptible variety. One week after inoculation with SynComs and/or metabolites, all treatments were inoculated with the pathogen at a final concentration of 105 cells g−1 soil. Disease severity was assessed one month later as described above.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS and R. A structural equation model was fitted with the lavaan package, and model fit was evaluated using standard criteria [45, 46]. Variance partitioning analysis was used to quantify the contributions of bacterial communities, fungal communities, and rhizosphere metabolites to pathogen density [47]. Multiple comparisons were assessed using Tukey’s test, while pairwise differences were examined using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Differentially abundant metagenomic genes were identified with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Transcriptomic differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes defined as |log2Fold Change|≥ 1 and p < 0.05. Differential metabolites were identified using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP) > 1 and |log2Fold Change|≥ 1.

Results

Profiles of bacterial and fungal communities across banana varieties

To explore the relationship between microbiome composition and disease resistance, we profiled the bacterial and fungal communities in the rhizospheres of three banana varieties with differing resistance levels. We measured community richness and diversity using the Chao 1 and Shannon indices. The results revealed that the highly resistant variety exhibited significantly lower bacterial community richness and diversity compared to the other varieties, with the highest richness and diversity found in the susceptible variety (Fig. S5A and S5B). For the fungal community, significantly higher Shannon diversity was observed in the resistant variety compared to the other varieties, while no significant difference in richness was found among the three varieties (Fig. S5C and S5D). PCoA based on the Bray–Curtis distance revealed significant differences in bacterial and fungal communities across the banana varieties (p (bacteria) = 0.002, p (fungi) = 0.006), with the first two axes explaining over 90% of the variation (Fig. 2A). PCA based upon metabolite profiles also revealed clear differences among the banana varieties (p = 0.004) (Fig. S6).

The structural equation model revealed that pathogen density was strongly associated with microbial community compositions (Fig. 2B). Specifically, bacterial community composition (R = − 0.680, p < 0.001) and fungal community composition (R = − 0.716, p < 0.001) were negatively correlated with pathogen density, while both bacterial (R = 0.918, p < 0.001) and fungal (R = 0.953, p < 0.001) community compositions were impacted by rhizosphere metabolite compositions. Furthermore, variance partitioning analysis showed that the combined influence of metabolites, bacterial community composition, and fungal community composition had the highest explanatory power in relation to pathogen density (Fig. 2C).

Rhizosphere microbial communities and metabolites compositions differing across banana varieties

To assess differences in rhizosphere microbial communities and metabolite compositions across banana varieties, we analyzed both bacterial and fungal communities as well as metabolite profiles from the rhizospheres of the highly resistant, resistant, and susceptible banana varieties. The bacterial and fungal communities were dominated by a few phyla, with Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Chloroflexi representing the majority of bacterial sequences, and Ascomycota and Zygomycota predominated in the fungal communities (Fig. S7). At the genus level, the highly resistant variety showed significant enrichment of Burkholderia, Enterobacter, Microbacterium, Rhodanobacter, Dyella, Penicillium, Humicola, Myxocephala, and Mortierella compared with the other two varieties (Fig. S8).

Further investigation using LDA revealed 104 bacterial OTUs and 56 fungal OTUs that significantly differed across the banana varieties (Fig. 2D and E). These differentially enriched microbial taxa primarily included bacterial groups from the phyla Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, and Actinobacteria, and fungal communities that were predominantly enriched in Ascomycota (Fig. S9). Metabolite profiling showed distinct rhizosphere metabolite compositions among the varieties. Specifically, stearic acid, silane, and shikimic acid were enriched in the highly resistant variety, while nonanoic acid was more abundant in the resistant variety compared to both the highly resistant and susceptible varieties. In contrast, the susceptible variety exhibited significant enrichment of eight compounds, including palmitic acid, myo-inositol, D-(+)-talose, D-(−)-rhamnose, stigmasterol, ketone_C, glyceryl-glycoside, and 3-hydroxybutyric acid (Figs. S10 and). These findings highlight distinct microbial and metabolite signatures across banana varieties, providing a foundation for exploring their role in disease suppression.

Cultivation of bacterial and fungal isolates from rhizosphere soils

A total of 329 bacterial isolates were recovered from the three banana varieties, with 109 isolates from the highly resistant variety, 109 isolates from the resistant variety, and 111 isolates from the susceptible variety, respectively (Fig. 3A). For bacteria, the isolates predominantly belonged to Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, and Firmicutes, with minor representation from Bacteroidetes. Additionally, the cultured microbes encompass several dominant genera identified in the sequencing analyses, including Enterobacter, Burkholderia, Microbacterium, and Sphingobium. A total of 270 fungal isolates representing 14 genera were recovered across the three varieties (Fig. 3B). The fungal isolates were classified into Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, which were also represented among the major fungal groups identified in the culture-independent profiling. Among the fungal genera, Aspergillus, Penicillium, and Trichoderma were the most abundant. These results suggest that our culture-based approach effectively captured a significant portion of the rhizosphere microbiome.

Pathogen suppression potential of selected bacterial and fungal strains

After integrating the results of high-throughput sequencing and culture collection, only a subset of detected genera contained OTUs that were also found in our culturable collection (Fig. S11). Subsequently, based on OTU and strain sequence similarity, as well as the origin of the isolates, we selected representative strains from each banana variety to construct SynComs and assessed their impact on pathogen growth (Figs. S12 and S13). We then tested the effects of the selected strains on pathogen growth (Figs. S1–S3). For the highly resistant variety, the bacterial SynCom was assembled from isolates of Enterobacter, Burkholderia, Microbacterium, Dyella, and Streptomyces, most of which showed strong inhibitory activity (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the SynCom from the susceptible variety comprised a broader range of genera with lower pathogen suppression. In total, bacterial strains from the highly resistant variety exhibited stronger pathogen inhibition compared to those from the susceptible variety (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.001). For the fungal SynCom, isolates of Penicillium, Trichoderma, Humicola, and Aspergillus from the highly resistant variety were included, whereas only Penicillium and Fusarium isolates were selected for the susceptible variety (Fig. 3D). Fungal isolates from the highly resistant variety exhibited stronger pathogen inhibition than those from the susceptible variety (Mann–Whitney U test, p < 0.01).

Cross-kingdom SynComs from the highly resistant variety effectively suppress pathogen

To evaluate the pathogen-suppressive effects of SynComs, we constructed several SynComs derived from the rhizospheres of both the highly resistant and susceptible banana varieties. These SynComs were tested in natural and sterilized soils to assess their ability to suppress pathogen abundance. The SynComs included bacterial-only, fungal-only, and cross-kingdom (bacterial and fungal) combinations, with different treatments for each variety. In natural soil, SynComs constructed from the highly resistant variety significantly reduced pathogen abundance compared to the control where only the pathogen was inoculated into the soil (t-test: p < 0.05), except for the SynCom composed of low-suppressive bacterial strains (t-test: p > 0.05). The cross-kingdom SynCom derived from the highly resistant variety showed the strongest suppression (Fig. 4A). For SynComs derived from the susceptible variety, the SynCom composed of bacterial strains with high pathogen suppression abilities and the SynCom composed of bacterial strains with high pathogen suppression abilities combined with fungi significantly reduced pathogen abundance (t-test: p < 0.05), whereas the SynCom composed of bacterial strains with low pathogen suppression abilities and the SynCom composed of low pathogen-suppressing bacteria and fungi led to an increase in pathogen abundance (t-test: p < 0.05). Overall, SynComs associated with the highly resistant variety exhibited stronger pathogen suppression than those derived from the susceptible variety. Furthermore, SynComs composed of bacterial and fungal strains from both the highly resistant and susceptible varieties also exhibited significant pathogen suppression (t-test: p < 0.05). However, the cross-kingdom SynCom derived from the combination of both varieties showed a markedly reduced suppression effect compared to the cross-kingdom SynCom based solely on the highly resistant variety (Fig. S14). Similar results were obtained in sterilized soil (Figs. S15 and S16). Based on their pathogen-suppressive effects, the cross-kingdom SynCom associated with the highly resistant variety, which exhibited the strongest inhibitory activity, was selected for further investigations.

In supernatant assays, the pathogen suppression was significant across bacterial, fungal, and cross-kingdom SynCom treatments compared to the control, with the bacterial SynCom supernatant showing the strongest effect (Fig. S17). In vivo experiments further confirmed the disease-suppressive capacity of the cross-kingdom SynComs associated with the highly resistant variety. SynCom inoculation significantly reduced disease severity in banana plants (t-test, p < 0.01; Fig. 4B). Quantification of pathogen abundance revealed that SynCom-treated plants harbored significantly lower pathogen densities compared to the control (t-test, p < 0.01; Fig. 4C).

SynCom treatment alters rhizosphere microbial community composition and function

To further elucidate the SynCom-induced changes in microbial composition and function, we performed amplicon and metagenome sequencing on rhizosphere soil samples from SynCom-treated and control groups. Microbial community analysis revealed that SynCom inoculation induced marked shifts in both bacterial and fungal community compositions (p (bacteria) = 0.016, p (fungi) = 0.007; Fig. 4D). Genes obtained from metagenomic sequencing were annotated using the KEGG database. Compared to the control, the SynCom treatment significantly enriched several metabolism-related pathways, including glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, butanoate metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and starch and sucrose metabolism (Fig. 4E).

SynCom treatment induces distinct transcriptomic and metabolic changes in banana roots

To evaluate how SynCom influenced host molecular responses, we performed transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on root samples. PCA revealed a clear separation in both transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles between the SynCom-treated and control plants (Fig. 5A). Differential gene expression analysis identified 286 genes that were significantly upregulated and 923 genes that were significantly downregulated in response to SynCom treatment compared to the control. Metabolomic analysis revealed that, compared to the control, the SynCom treatment significantly increased the abundance of 475 metabolites and decreased 134 metabolites (Fig. 5B). KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes showed that the most significantly enriched pathways were primarily associated with metabolism, particularly carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and amino acid biosynthesis (Fig. 5C). Clear differences were observed across multiple metabolite categories between SynCom-treated and control plants (Fig. 5D). Notably, metabolites belonging to categories such as alkaloids, amino acids and their derivatives, and flavonoids were significantly upregulated.

Addition of metabolites from the highly resistant variety enhanced SynCom-mediated pathogen suppression

To investigate the impact of plant-derived metabolites on SynCom-mediated pathogen suppression, we added metabolites from both the highly resistant and susceptible banana varieties to the SynComs. Compared with control, metabolites from the highly resistant variety did not alter pathogen abundance, whereas those from the susceptible variety significantly promoted pathogen growth (Fig. 6A). For the treatment involving the cross-kingdom SynCom of the highly resistant variety, which consisted of co-inoculation with the cross-kingdom SynCom and Foc TR4, adding its own metabolites enhanced pathogen suppression, while metabolites from the susceptible variety reduced pathogen suppression (Fig. 6B). Similarly, the inclusion of metabolites from the susceptible variety in SynComs of susceptible variety co-inoculated with Foc TR4 led to a significant increase in pathogen abundance compared to the control treatment, whereas the addition of metabolites from the highly resistant variety did not impact pathogen abundance (Fig. 6C).

Compared with the treatment that did not include any metabolites, metabolites from the highly resistant variety did not significantly affect the biomass of the bacterial SynCom but did promote fungal growth (Fig. 6D and E). For the cross-kingdom SynComs, these metabolites significantly increased both bacterial and fungal abundance. However, for SynComs derived from the susceptible variety, metabolites from the highly resistant variety had no significant effect on either bacterial or fungal biomass (Fig. S18).

In vivo pot experiments, in which banana plantlets were inoculated with pathogen following application of SynComs and/or metabolites, further demonstrated the disease-suppressive capacity of the cross-kingdom SynCom derived from the highly resistant variety. Disease severity measurements showed that application of this SynCom, both alone and in combination with metabolites from the highly resistant variety, significantly reduced disease severity compared to treatments without SynCom addition, with the combined treatment exhibiting the lowest disease severity (Fig. 6F). Metabolites from the highly resistant variety, in the absence of SynComs, showed a trend toward reduced disease severity following pathogen inoculation, whereas those from the susceptible variety increased disease severity.

Discussion

The rhizosphere microbiome plays a critical role in determining plant disease resistance. Building on this, our study moves beyond descriptive associations and establishes a framework for designing microbiome-based disease suppression strategies by leveraging microbial taxa and metabolites associated with disease-resistant varieties. Specifically, we adopted a “learning from nature” approach, in which the natural assembly of bacteria and fungi in resistant plants were used to guide the construction of cross-kingdom SynComs, incorporating both microbial members and plant metabolites. Within this framework, metabolite-mediated cross-kingdom SynComs comprising bacteria and fungi exhibited strong pathogen suppression.

Consistent with previous work showing that resistance arises from both plant traits and microbiome activity [7, 48], our results indicate that the assembly of specific rhizosphere microbes is closely associated with variation in plant pathogen resistance. However, the relative contributions of host genetic resistance and microbiome-mediated effects cannot be fully disentangled within our experimental framework, and the observed patterns likely reflect an integrated plant–microbiome system rather than independent effects of either component. In this context, our results further highlight that both bacterial and fungal communities contribute to pathogen suppression, addressing the limitations of previous studies that focused solely on the role of either bacteria or fungi in plant disease resistance [49, 50].

Previous studies have shown that multispecies microbial consortia can outperform single-strain inoculations in disease suppression [16, 51], with some cases showing that disease suppression depends on the synergistic activities of microbial populations recruited to the plant rhizosphere [52]. With these previous findings in mind, we designed multispecies SynComs comprised of both bacterial and fungal isolates phylogenetically related to taxa enriched in the rhizosphere of both highly resistant and susceptible varieties. Although such SynComs represent a simplified subset of the native microbiome, they provide a tractable system to capture key interaction patterns. In agreement with our expectations, SynComs derived from the highly resistant variety outperformed those derived from the susceptible variety, whereas SynComs derived from the susceptible variety and those incorporating microbes from both highly resistant and susceptible varieties showed reduced suppressive capacity. These patterns are indicated by our results showing that the highly resistant variety is enriched in strains with strong inhibitory activity, such as Enterobacter, Burkholderia, Trichoderma, and Penicillium [53–56]. In contrast, some strains isolated from the susceptible variety can promote pathogen growth, suggesting that their inclusion may counteract the effects of antagonistic taxa or increase competition among SynCom members [57, 58]. Furthermore, we found that cross-kingdom SynComs derived from the highly resistant variety exhibited stronger pathogen suppression than single-kingdom SynComs. This highlights the advantage of integrating bacterial and fungal members in constructing disease-suppressive microbiomes, consistent with previous studies showing that cross-kingdom microbial consortia can enhance disease suppression [59].

In vivo experiments showed that SynCom inoculation enhanced plant resistance, reducing both disease severity and pathogen density. These effects were accompanied by shifts in the composition and functional potential of rhizosphere bacterial and fungal communities, indicating successful establishment of the introduced consortium and its influence on the native microbiome [60]. Transcriptomic analyses revealed a broad downregulation of host gene expression, whereas metabolomic profiling showed increased accumulation of defense-related and signaling-related metabolites such as alkaloids, amino acids, and flavonoids [61, 62]. These changes likely reflect the overall impact of SynCom inoculation on plant health and microbial community dynamics. However, the precise mechanisms behind these effects, such as whether they involve immune modulation or other pathways, remain to be fully elucidated. Future studies will need to investigate the specific roles of key genes, metabolites, and microbial taxa in mediating these outcomes, providing a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms that drive SynCom-induced disease suppression.

Previous studies have shown that variation in rhizosphere metabolite composition among plant varieties is associated with the assembly of their rhizosphere microbiomes [63]. We extend this paradigm using specific rhizosphere metabolites from highly resistant varieties to enhance the disease-suppressive activities of variety-specific SynComs. We found that pathogen suppression of our best-performing SynComs was enhanced in the presence of the metabolite mixture composing stearic acid and shikimic acid from the highly resistant variety, while metabolites associated with the susceptible variety were linked to increased pathogen density. While these findings support a role for rhizosphere metabolites in shaping microbial interactions and enhancing disease suppression, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely resolved. Moreover, our metabolite-based assays were conducted under simplified conditions and do not fully capture the complexity of in situ rhizosphere processes. In particular, the extent to which these effects are mediated through direct microbial responses to plant-associated metabolites versus indirect shifts in community structure requires further investigation.

Our results suggest that bacterial and fungal communities may contribute to pathogen suppression through distinct processes. Bacterial isolates showed stronger antagonistic activity in supernatant assays, while fungal isolates exhibited distinct resource utilization patterns in response to metabolite addition. These observations are consistent with the idea that bacteria primarily suppress pathogens through antagonism [64], and fungi contribute to pathogen suppression through resource competition [65]. In addition, the ability of fungi to decompose complex substrates may indirectly influence bacterial carbon acquisition strategies [66], potentially leading to an increase in bacterial abundance observed in cross-kingdom SynComs following metabolite addition.

In conclusion, our study suggests that pathogen resistance in resistant plant varieties is associated with the joint action of both bacteria and fungi. Cross-kingdom SynComs, comprising both bacterial and fungal members, were more effective than intra-kingdom SynComs in reducing pathogen infection and reshaping the rhizosphere microbiome, accompanied by broad transcriptional and metabolic changes in the host. Metabolites from highly resistant varieties further enhanced these suppressive effects. By identifying the microbial taxa and metabolites naturally associated with highly resistant plant varieties, we can engineer SynComs that mimic these beneficial interactions (Fig. 7). This strategy leverages the inherent ecological principles of the rhizosphere to maximize disease suppression, providing a blueprint for rational design of microbiome-based, sustainable plant protection strategies.
