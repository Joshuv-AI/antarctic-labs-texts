Dynamic data structures for twin-ordered matrices
Bartłomiej Bosek, Jadwiga Czyżewska, Evangelos Kipouridis, Wojciech Nadara, Michał Pilipczuk, Karol Węgrzycki, Anna Zych-Pawlewicz

Abstract
We present a dynamic data structure for representing binary n × n n\times n matrices that are d d -twin-ordered, for a fixed parameter d d . Our structure supports cell queries and single-cell updates both in 𝒪 ⁡ ( log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) \mathcal{O}(\log\log n) expected worst case time, while using 𝒪 d ​ ( n ) \mathcal{O}_{d}(n) memory; here, the 𝒪 d ​ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{O}_{d}(\cdot) notation hides a constant depending on d d .

Introduction

The notion of
twin-width
, introduced by Bonnet, Kim, Thomassé, and Watrigant
[
9
]
, has rapidly emerged as a unifying structural parameter for graphs (both sparse and dense), matrices, and permutations. The basic idea behind twin-width, expressed through the underlying decomposition notion called a
contraction sequence
, is to “fold” the given structure (be it a graph, a matrix, or a permutation) by iteratively merging elements into larger and larger parts. At each point, we require that every part has a homogeneous relation to all the other parts, except for a bounded number of exceptions. The best possible bound on the number of exceptions is then precisely the twin-width. This concept has a surprisingly wide range of applicability: for example,
H
-minor-free graphs (for a fixed
H
), graphs of bounded cliquewidth, and pattern-free permutations all have bounded twin-width
[
9
]
.

Despite being a relatively new concept, the fundamental character of twin-width has already been well-understood. Twin-width uncovers deep links between structural graph theory and algorithmic finite model theory, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
6
,
9
,
10
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
18
]
. In this direction, probably the most important result is the theorem of Bonnet et al.
[
9
]
stating that the model-checking problem for first-order logic is fixed-parameter tractable on graph classes of bounded twin-width, provided a suitable contraction sequence is given as input. Combinatorial aspects of twin-width were explored in
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
8
,
11
,
17
]
, among many other works. Several further algorithmic applications can be found in
[
5
,
7
,
16
]
.

In this work, we study binary matrices of bounded twin-width – let us now define the notion precisely. We consider a binary matrix
M
, i.e., one with entries in
\{0,1\}
. A contraction sequence for
M
is a sequence of pairs

(\mathcal{R}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0}),\ (\mathcal{R}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{1}),\dots,\ (\mathcal{R}_{p},\mathcal{C}_{p}),

where for every
i=0,1,\dots,p
we have
\mathcal{R}_{i}
and
\mathcal{C}_{i}
are partitions of rows and columns of
M
into row blocks and column blocks respectively. Moreover,

•

in
(\mathcal{R}_{0},\mathcal{C}_{0})
each row and each column belongs to a separate block;

•

in
(\mathcal{R}_{p},\mathcal{C}_{p})
all rows belong to a single block and all columns belong to a single block;

•

(\mathcal{R}_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i})
is obtained from
(\mathcal{R}_{i-1},\mathcal{C}_{i-1})
by either merging two consecutive row blocks into a single row block or merging two consecutive column blocks into a single column block.

For an example of a contraction sequence, see Figure
1

For a row block
R\in\mathcal{R}
and a column block
C\in\mathcal{C}

M[R,C]
is the submatrix consisting of all the cells at the intersection of the rows of
R
and the columns of

if it contains both an entry
0
and an entry

. The

of the contraction sequence is the least

d

is

if it admits a contraction sequence of width at most
d
, and

has

at most
d
if one can permute rows and columns in order to obtain a

-twin-ordered matrix. Throughout this paper we consider the order of the rows and columns to be fixed, and we always assume that the considered matrix is
d
-twin-ordered.

The connection between graphs and matrices of bounded twin-width lies at the heart of the theory of twin-width and was observed already in the original work of Bonnet et al.
[
9
]
. Later, Bonnet, Giocanti, Ossona de Mendez, Simon, Thomassé, and Toruńczyk studied the twin-width of matrices from the combinatorial and the model-theoretic viewpoints
[
6
]
, whereas Bonnet, Giocanti, Ossona de Mendez, and Thomassé investigated algorithmic applications of twin-width for problems such as matrix multiplication
[
5
]
. The matrix viewpoint was also pivotal in the work on
\chi

[
11
,
17
]
.

The starting point of our work is the result of Pilipczuk, Sokołowski, and Zych-Pawlewicz
[
16
]
, who approached the setting of matrices of bounded twin-width from the point of view of data structures, or
compact representations
. Precisely, they designed a data structure that stores a
d
-twin-ordered binary
n\times n
matrix
M
using
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
1
1

1

The
\mathcal{O}_{d}(\cdot)
notation hides multiplicative factors that may depend on
d
.
bits (which is asymptotically optimal) while supporting single-entry queries in time
\mathcal{O}_{d}(\log\log n)
. Their data structure, however, is fundamentally static: once it is initialized on a matrix
M
, it cannot be easily modified upon updates to
M
. It is natural to ask whether a similarly compact and efficient data structure can be also proposed in the dynamic setting, where
M
can be updated subject to a guarantee that it always stays
d
-twin-ordered.

Our results.

In this work, we address the above question and present a dynamic data structure for
d
-twin-ordered binary matrices that supports both queries and updates efficiently, while guaranteeing near-linear space complexity. Formally, we show the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
and
M\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be a binary matrix
that is updated over time subject to a guarantee that
M
is

d
-twin-ordered at every moment. Then, there exists a dynamic data
structure for maintaining
M
that uses
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
memory and supports
the following operations:

•

\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
: initialize the data structure
with a list
\cal K
of disjoint rectangles representing all ones
in
M
in
\mathcal{O}_{d}((n+|{\mathcal{K}}|)\log\log n)
time.

•

\mathtt{QueryBit}(i,j)
: return the
entry
M[i,j]
in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
expected worst case
time.

•

\mathtt{Update}(i,j)
: flip the entry
M[i,j]

in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
expected worst case time.

Throughout the paper we work in the standard word RAM model with words
of length
\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, hence space complexity
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
means storing
the matrix using
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n\log n)
bits.
In the static setting,
Pilipczuk et al.
[
16
]
managed to obtain a representation that
uses only
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
bits, hence our result trades a slight increase in the space complexity for making the data structure dynamic.
Note that only the space complexity of the data structure and
its initialization time depend on the parameter
d
, whereas the
bounds on the time complexity of all the other operations do not
involve
d
.
Following
[
16
]
we allow our data structure to be
initialized on a compact description of the matrix through a list
of disjoint rectangles comprising all the entries
1
(which we formally define in Section
2
and call
slabs
); so in
particular, one may efficiently initialize a dense matrix.
It is known (see
[
16
, Lemma 10]
for details) that such a
description with
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
rectangles can be easily extracted
from a contraction sequence of width
d
.

All logarithms in this paper are base

. We assume the standard RAM model of computation. For

[a,b]\coloneqq\{a,a+1,\ldots,b\}

Twin-ordered matrices.

The basic terminology for matrices as well as the definition of
a
d
-twin-ordered matrix have already been discussed in
Section
1
.
For the rest of the paper we work with a binary
n\times n
matrix
M

that is at every point
d
-twin-ordered. The entry of
M
at row
i
and column
j
is denoted by
M[i,j]
.

In our data structure we do not rely on contraction sequences at all,
but rather exploit structural properties of matrix
M
following from
the assumption that it is
d
-twin-ordered. To state these properties,
we need a few definitions. Recall that a
(row/column) block
is
a contiguous set of rows/columns, and for a row block
R
and a column
block
C
, the
zone

M[R,C]
is the submatrix consisting of all
the cells at the intersection of
R
and
C
.

A
corner
in

is a
2\times 2
zone whose both rows are different and both columns are different. We have the following fact about the number of corners in a
d
-twin-ordered matrix.

Lemma 1
(
[
16
, Theorem 5 and Lemma 8]
)

Let
f_{d}=\frac{16}{3}(2d+3)^{2}2^{4(2d+2)}
. Any
d
-twin-ordered binary
n\times n
matrix
contains at most
f_{d}\cdot(n+2)\in\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
corners.

For the purpose of this paper, a
slab
in

is defined as a zone

entirely filled with ones, where
R
is a row block and

is a column block. We can also represent a slab as

by
the four coordinates
(a,b,c,d)
, with

and
a\leqslant b
and

. This is in particular the representation that we use when working with slabs in algorithmic procedures.
A

of

is a set
\mathcal{K}
of pairwise disjoint slabs in

Theorem 2.1

Dictionaries.

In this work we make extensive use of
van Emde Boas
dictionaries,
called also van Emde Boas trees
[
19
]
. The
van Emde Boas tree
maintains a set of keys
S
, which is a subset of the
domain
\{1,\ldots,U\}
for
U\in\mathbb{N}
, by supporting the following
operations:

•

\mathtt{Insert}(\mathtt{x})
:
insert key
\mathtt{x}
to
S
;

•

\mathtt{Delete}(\mathtt{x})
: remove key
\mathtt{x}

from
S
;

•

\mathtt{Lookup}(\mathtt{x})
: verify whether
\mathtt{x}

belongs to the maintained set of keys
S
;

•

\mathtt{Successor}(\mathtt{x})
: find the smallest key in

S
greater than
\mathtt{x}
;

•

\mathtt{Predecessor}(\mathtt{x})
: find the largest key
in
S
smaller than
\mathtt{x}
.

All operations work in worst case time
\mathcal{O}(\log\log U)
and
the
required space is
\mathcal{O}(U)

[
19
]
.

Recall that a
segment

[a,b]
, for
a,b\in\mathbb{N},a~\leqslant b
, is defined as the
set of consecutive natural numbers between its coordinates
a
and
b
:

[a,b]=\{a,a+1,\ldots,b-1,b\}
. Two segments
[a,b]
and

[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
are called

adjacent
if either
b+1=a^{\prime}
or
a-1=b^{\prime}
.
In the next lemma we describe a modification of van Emde Boas trees, which gives
us the data structure to maintain a set of
disjoint and non-adjacent segments with coordinates in
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, which we shall call an
adhesive segment set
.

Lemma 3

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
. There is an
adhesive segment set
data
structure
\mathbb{S}
that
maintains a
dynamic set
S
of
pairwise disjoint and pairwise non-adjacent segments
whose coordinates belong to
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
The data structure supports
the following methods:

•

\mathtt{Containing}([a,b])
: Returns the segment
of
S
that contains

[a,b]
, if existent.

•

\mathtt{Adjacent}([a,b])
: Returns the (at most two)
segments in
S

adjacent to
[a,b]
.

•

\mathtt{Disjoint}([a,b])
: Returns whether
[a,b]
is disjoint from all the segments in
S
.

•

\mathtt{Merge}([a,b])
: If
[a,b]
is not disjoint from all the segments in
S
, the method does nothing, and otherwise it performs the following modification. If
[a,b]
is not adjacent to any segment in
S
, the method adds
[a,b]
to
S
. If
[a,b]
is adjacent to one segment
s\in S
, the method replaces
s
with
s^{\prime}\coloneqq s\cup[a,b]
. If
[a,b]
is adjacent to two segments
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
, the method replaces
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
with
s^{\prime}\coloneqq[a,b]\cup s_{1}\cup s_{2}
.

•

\mathtt{Split}([a,b])
: If
[a,b]

is contained
in some segment
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\in S
, the method
replaces the segment
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
in

S
with segments
[a^{\prime},a-1]
and
[b+1,b^{\prime}]
. If any of these segments turns out to be empty, it is not added to
S
. If there is no segment
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\in S
containing
[a,b]
, the method does nothing.

The data structure occupies
\mathcal{O}(n)
memory and all operations
run in
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
worst case time. The data structure can be initialized on an empty
S
in time
\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof

We use the van Emde Boas dictionary
\mathbb{D}
with universe
[1,n]
. We store in
\mathbb{D}

the coordinates of the segments of
S
. Additionally, for each coordinate we store an extra bit determining if it is the first or the
second coordinate. These extra bits are stored in a static array with entries
[1,n]
: entry
i
stores the suitable piece of data for the coordinate equal to
i
.

To implement
\mathtt{Containing}([a,b])
we issue

a^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathtt{Predecessor}(a+1)
and

b^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathtt{Successor}(b-1)
to
\mathbb{D}
. We
return
[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]
, provided
a^{\prime}
is the first coordinate of a segment,
b^{\prime}
is the second coordinate of a segment, and
b^{\prime}=\mathtt{Successor}(a^{\prime})
; otherwise there is no segment in
S
containing
[a,b]
. Queries
\mathtt{Adjacent}([a,b])
and
\mathtt{Disjoint}([a,b])
are implemented
analogously.

To implement
\mathtt{Merge}([a,b])
, we first check whether
[a,b]
is disjoint from all the segments in
S
by invoking
\mathtt{Disjoint}([a,b])
.
If that is not the case, we do nothing.
Otherwise, we issue

a^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathtt{Predecessor}(a)
and

b^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathtt{Successor}(b)
queries to
\mathbb{D}
.
Since
[a,b]
is disjoint from segments in
S
,
a^{\prime}
is the second
and
b^{\prime}
is the first coordinate of a segment in
S
. If
a^{\prime}+1=a
,
we issue
a^{\prime\prime}:=\mathtt{Predecessor}(a^{\prime})
query to

\mathbb{D}
to find the first coordinate of the segment

[a^{\prime\prime},a^{\prime}]\in S
. Then, we delete
a^{\prime}
from
\mathbb{D}
and
insert
b
, indicating that this is the second coordinate of a segment.
Then we
proceed with
b^{\prime}
in an analogous fashion.

To implement
\mathtt{Split}([a,b])
, we issue

[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]=\mathtt{Containing}([a,b])
to find
the interval

[a^{\prime},b^{\prime}]\in S
containing
[a,b]
. If there is no such interval, there is nothing to do.
Otherwise, we insert to

\mathbb{D}
key
a-1
indicating that it is the second coordinate,
and we insert key
b+1
indicating that it is the first coordinate.
∎

Canonical slab decomposition

From Lemma
2
we know that for any
d
-twin-ordered
n\times n
matrix there always exists a slab decomposition into
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
slabs, which is a crucial property of our dynamic data structure. However, we need to provide an efficient algorithm to compute such decomposition.
In this Section, we describe the canonical slab decomposition, which is a slab decomposition with additional structural properties. This allows us to use the adhesive segment set data structure, described in Lemma
3
, to compute a desired decomposition in Theorem
3.1
.

The slab decomposition we are going to compute is, in some sense, canonical. To describe it, we need a few definitions. Recall that
M
is a binary
n\times n
matrix. For
i\in[1,n]
, an
i
-strip
is a slab of the form
(a,b,i,i)
for some
1\leqslant a\leqslant b\leqslant n
such that neither
(a-1,b,i,i)
nor
(a,b+1,i,i)
is a slab. In other words, an
i
-strip is a slab of width
1
contained in the
i
-th column such that it is not possible to extend this slab upwards or downwards, due to either finding an entry
0
there or the border of the matrix
M
.
By
\mathtt{strips}_{i}
we denote the set of all
i
-strips; and
\mathtt{strips}\coloneqq\bigcup_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mathtt{strips}_{i}
is the set of all the strips. For example, if
(0,1,1,0,1,0,1,1,1)
are the consecutive entries of the
ith
column of a matrix, the set
\mathtt{strips}_{i}
is equal to
\{(2,3,i,i),(5,5,i,i),(7,9,i,i)\}
.

We say that strips
(a,b,i,i)\in\mathtt{strips}_{i}
and
(a,b,j,j)\in\mathtt{strips}_{j}
are
siblings
if for every
k\in[i,j]
we have
(a,b,k,k)\in\mathtt{strips}_{k}
.
Note that the relation of being a sibling is an equivalence relation on
\mathtt{strips}
. We call the equivalence classes of this relations
canonical slabs
; we identify each canonical slab with its union, which is always a slab in
M
. Noting that each strip belongs to exactly one canonical slab, we define the
canonical slab decomposition
of
M
as the family of all canonical slabs of
M
. For examples of the canonical slab decomposition see Figure
3
.

In the next lemma we show that the canonical slab decomposition of a

Lemma 4

Let
\cal R
be the canonical slab decomposition of a binary
n\times n
matrix
M
that is
d
-twin-ordered. Then
|{\cal R}|\leqslant 4f_{d}\cdot(n+2)+4n\in\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
, where
f_{d}
is
the constant dependent on
d
defined in Lemma
1
.

Proof

Let
(a,b,c,d)
be a canonical slab in
M
with
1<a\leqslant b<n
and
1<c\leqslant d<n
.
Our goal is to show that
(a,b,c,d)
must intersect a corner in
M
. This will prove the claim, as there can be at most
4n
canonical slabs satisfying
a=1
or
b=n
or
c=1
or
d=n
, whereas the number of corners in
M
is at most
f_{d}(n+2)
by Lemma
1
and each corner in
M
can intersect at most
4
slabs of
\cal R
.

Let
Z=M[[a,b],[d+1,d+1]]
.
As
(a,b,c,d)
is a canonical slab, we have
(a,b,d+1,d+1)\notin\mathtt{strips}_{d+1}
; that is,
Z
is not a
(d+1)
-strip. If
Z
contains both an entry
0
and an entry
1
, then the zone
M[[a,b],[d,d+1]]
must contain a corner that intersects
(a,b,c,d)
. If all the entries of
Z
are

There is an algorithm

An amortized data structure

In this section we present a weaker variant of the data structure promised in Theorem
1.1
, where we allow amortized running time of the updates. We will de-amortize this data structure using standard methods in Section

.

Theorem 4.1

There is a data structure
\mathbb{A}
that maintains a

d
-twin-ordered
n\times n
binary matrix
M

by supporting the following operations:

1.

\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
: Initialize the data structure
with a slab decomposition
\cal K
of
M
in

\mathcal{O}((n+|{\cal K}|)\log\log n)

\mathtt{QueryBit}(i,j)

\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)

\mathtt{Update}(i,j)

\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)

\mathbb{A}
uses
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
space.

Proof

The data structure
\mathbb{A}
internally maintains:

1.

a static 2D orthogonal point location data structure provided by Theorem
2.1
, referred to as
\mathbb{P}
, storing at most

4f_{d}(n+2)+4n\in\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)

\{1,\ldots,n\}
; and

2.

a dictionary (implemented as a hash table)
\mathbb{Q}
where
the keys are
from universe

\{1,\ldots,n\}\times[1,n]
, the values are
boolean,
and the number of elements that will be added is bounded by

8f_{d}(n+2)\in\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
. We assume a standard implementation
of a hash

The intuition behind maintaining
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}

is the following:
\mathbb{P}
stores a slab decomposition of

computed at some point in the past. When the next updates arrive,

.

belongs to one of the

\mathbb{P}
.

.

(i,j)

\mathbb{P}

In this section we show how to de-amortize the data structure

. Precisely, we prove the following statement, which immediately implies Theorem

.

Theorem 5.1

There is a data structure
\mathbb{W}
, which maintains a

d
-twin-ordered
n\times n
binary matrix
M

by supporting the following operations:

1.

\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
: Initialize the data structure
with a slab decomposition
\cal K
of
M
in

\mathcal{O}((n+|{\cal K}|)\log\log n)
time.

2.

\mathtt{QueryBit}(i,j)
: Return the entry
M[i,j]
in

\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
expected worst case time.

3.

\mathtt{Update}(i,j)
: Flip the entry
M[i,j]
in

\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
expected worst case time.

The data structure
\mathbb{W}
uses
\mathcal{O}_{d}(n)
space.

Proof

We apply a standard de-amortization technique to the data structure

\mathbb{A}
of Theorem
4.1
. For the simplicity of exposition, we firstly present a slightly flawed simpler version of the argument, and then we explain what was the issue and how to fix it.

Let
L=8f_{d}(n+2)
be the limit on the size of
\mathbb{Q}
from Theorem
4.1
and let us partition all updates into epochs of
L
updates. Let us denote the
i
th epoch as
E_{i}
, with the first epoch being
E_{0}
. For each valid
i\geqslant 1
, let us define the data structure
\mathbb{A}_{i}
that will exist throughout epochs
E_{2i-2},E_{2i-1},E_{2i},E_{2i+1}
and the data structure
\mathbb{A}_{0}
that will exist throughout epochs
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
(in particular, there will be only two different data structures existing at any point of time). The data structure
\mathbb{A}_{0}
will be initialized with the
\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
call and responsible for answering queries of epochs
E_{0}
and
E_{1}
. The data structure
\mathbb{A}_{i}
for
i\geqslant 1
will be initialized throughout epochs
E_{2i-2}
and
E_{2i-1}
and responsible for answering queries during epochs
E_{2i}
and
E_{2i+1}
. At the start of epoch
E_{2i-2}
, we run
\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
, where
\cal K
is the slab decomposition obtained from the current state of
\mathbb{A}_{i-1}
, similarly as in the case of recomputation of the data structure in the proof of Theorem
4.1
. We have that
|\mathcal{K}|=\mathcal{O}(L)
, so that call takes
\mathcal{O}(L\log\log n)
total time. Hence, we can distribute this work over
L
updates of epoch

adding
\mathcal{O}(\log\log n)
worst case time overhead to each update. At the end of

the
\mathtt{Init}(n,\cal K)
call will be finished, but
\mathbb{A}_{i}
will consequently be
L
updates behind the current state, as its state reflects
