Abstract

Localized Kernel Methods for Signal Processing

By

Sippanon Kitimoon

Claremont Graduate University: 2025

This dissertation presents two signal processing methods using specially designed localized kernels for parameter recovery under noisy condition. The first method addresses the estimation of frequencies and amplitudes in multidimensional exponential models. It utilizes localized trigonometric polynomial kernels to detect the multivariate frequencies, followed by a more detailed parameter estimation. We compare our method with MUSIC and ESPRIT, which are classical subspace-based algorithms widely used for estimating the parameters of exponential signals. In the univariate case, the method outperforms MUSIC and ESPRIT under low signal-to-noise ratios. For the multivariate case, we develop a coordinate-wise projection and registration approach that achieves high recovery accuracy using significantly fewer samples than other methods.

The second method focuses on separating linear chirp components from time-localized signal segments. A variant of the Signal Separation Operator (SSO) is constructed using a localized kernel. Instantaneous frequency estimates are obtained via FFT-based filtering, then clustered and fitted with piecewise linear regression. The method operates without prior knowledge of the number of components and is shown to recover intersecting and discontinuous chirps at SNR levels as low as
-30
dB.

Both methods share an idea based on localized kernels and efficient FFT-based implementation, and neither requires subspace decomposition or sparsity regularization. Experimental results confirm the robustness and tractability of the proposed approaches across a range of simulated data conditions. Potential extensions include application to nonlinear chirps, adaptive kernel design, and signal classification using extracted features.

Chapter 1
Introduction

This dissertation focuses on two core applications in signal processing:

•

Robust and Tractable Multidimensional Exponential Analysis

•

Localized Kernel Method for Separation of Linear Chirps

The foundation of both applications is built upon the constant parameter problem formally defined in Section
1.1.3
, where the goal is to recover unknown parameters of signals composed of complex exponentials or chirp components from noisy observations. By designing and utilizing localized kernel methods, we develop robust algorithms that perform well in the presence of sub-Gaussian noise. Since the two applications addressed in this dissertation focus on solving distinct signal processing problems, the literature reviews for each application are presented separately in their respective chapters.

In this chapter, we will focus on defining the constant parameter problem that underlies both applications and introducing the state-of-the-art methods used to solve the problem.

1.1
Background and problem statement

We begin with a brief review of the sub-Gaussian noise model in Section
1.1.1
, followed by a definition of the signal-to-noise ratio used throughout this work in Section
1.1.2
. Section
1.1.3
concludes with a formal statement of the constant parameter problem, which serves as the foundational model for the applications developed in later chapters.

1.1.1
Sub-Gaussian noise

In this section, we review certain properties of sub-Gaussian random variables. Intuitively, a sub-Gaussian random variable is one whose probability tails decay at least as fast as those of a Gaussian random variable; i.e. extreme deviations are exponentially unlikely and no heavier than in the normal distribution. The material in this section is based on
[
1
, Section 2.3]
, with a slight change of notation.
A mean zero real valued random variable
X
is called sub-Gaussian with parameter
\nu
(
X\in\mathcal{G}(\nu)
) if

\log\mathbb{E}(\exp(tX))\leq(t\nu)^{2}/2.

(1.1.1)

Examples include Gaussian variables and all bounded random variables.
For a sub-Gaussian variable, it is shown in
[
1
, Section 2.3]
that condition (
1.1.1
) implies the tail bound

\mathsf{Prob}(|X|>t)\leq 2\exp(-t^{2}/(2\nu^{2})).

(1.1.2)

Conversely, by
[
1
, Section 2.3, Theorem 2.1]
, the tail bound in (
1.1.2
) implies

X\in\mathcal{G}(4\nu)
.

We will say that a complex valued random variable
X
is in
\mathcal{G}(\nu)
if both the real and imaginary parts of
X
are in
\mathcal{G}(\nu)
.
We observe that if
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
|z|>t
then
\max(|\Re e(z)|,|\Im m(z)|)>t/\sqrt{2}
.
So, for such variables, we have

\mathsf{Prob}(|X|>t)\leq 4\exp(-t^{2}/(4\nu^{2})).

(1.1.3)

It is not difficult to see that if
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
are i.i.d., complex valued variables all in
\mathcal{G}(\nu)
,
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
|\mathbf{a}|_{n}^{2}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}a_{\ell}^{2}
, then
\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}a_{\ell}X_{\ell}\in\mathcal{G}(|\mathbf{a}|_{n}\nu)
.
Therefore, (
1.1.3
) implies

\mathsf{Prob}\left(\left|\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}a_{\ell}X_{\ell}\right|>t\right)\leq 4\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{4|\mathbf{a}|_{n}^{2}\nu^{2}}\right),\qquad t>0.

(1.1.4)

1.1.2
Signal to noise ratio

The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) measures how strong the signal is compared to the noise; i.e., higher SNR values indicate clearer signals, while lower values indicate signals that are more difficult to distinguish from noise. In this dissertation, we used a synthetically generated dataset to evaluate our methods under controlled conditions. The original signal
f(t)
was constructed, and Gaussian noise was introduced to simulate noisy environments.

The SNR in dB is defined as

\text{SNR}=20\log_{10}\left(\frac{\|\mathbf{f}\|_{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\|_{2}}\right),

(1.1.5)

where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is an L2 norm,
\mathbf{f}
is the vector of the original clean signal
f(t)
, and
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}
is the vector of noise
\epsilon(t)
.

To control the SNR, we generate a vector of Gaussian noise
\mathbf{n}
and compute

\boldsymbol{\epsilon}=\frac{\|\mathbf{f}\|_{2}}{10^{\text{SNR}/20}\cdot\|\mathbf{n}\|_{2}}\cdot\mathbf{n},

(1.1.6)

where SNR is the desired signal-to-noise ratio in decibels (dB).

We then obtain the noisy signal using the formula

\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{f}+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(1.1.7)

where
\mathbf{F}
is the vector of the noised signal
F(t)
.

By scaling the Gaussian noise
\mathbf{n}
using Equation
1.1.6
, we ensure that the resulting noisy signal
\mathbf{F}
achieves the desired SNR. This allows us to evaluate the performance of our experiments under varying noise levels.

1.1.3
Problem statement

In this section, we consider the following univariate set up.
Let
\mathbb{T}={\mathbb{R}}/(2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
(i.e.,
x,y
are considered equal if
x=y\mbox{ mod }2\pi
),
K\geq 1
be an integer,
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{T}
,
k=1,\cdots,K
,
A_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}
for
k=1,\cdots,K
.
We define

\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}},\quad\hat{\mu}(\ell)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\exp(-i\ell\lambda_{k}),\qquad\ell\in{\mathbb{Z}},

(1.1.8)

where
i=\sqrt{-1}
,
\delta_{\lambda}
denotes the Dirac delta supported at
\lambda
. We then formulate our problem statement as follow:

Point source separation problem.

Given finitely many noisy samples

\tilde{\mu}(\ell)=\hat{\mu}(\ell)+\epsilon_{\ell},\qquad|\ell|<n,

(1.1.9)

where
n\geq 1
is an integer, and
\epsilon_{\ell}
are realizations of a sub-Gaussian random variable, determine
K
,
A_{k}
, and
\lambda_{k}
,
k=1,\cdots,K
.

The harder part of the problem is to determine
K
and the
\lambda_{k}
’s.
The coefficients
A_{k}
can then be determined by solving a linear system of equations.
Many algorithms to do this are known, e.g.,
[
51
]
.
Therefore, we will focus on the task of finding
K
and the
\lambda_{k}
’s.

Well-known solutions

In this section, we will briefly review classical and modern methods developed to solve the point source separation problem introduced in Section
1.1.3
. These include Prony’s

]
, MUSIC
[
50
, Section 10.2.1]

[
50

methods, which have served as state of the art tools for parameter recovery in exponential models.

1.2.1
Prony’s method

Prony’s method, originally proposed in
[
11
]
, is a classical approach for estimating the parameters of exponential sums from uniformly spaced data.

Let
\mu(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\delta_{\lambda_{k}}(t)
, where
A_{k}
’s are complex numbers,
\lambda_{k}
’s are complex numbers with
-\pi<\Im m(\lambda_{k})\leq\pi
, and
\delta_{\lambda}
is the Dirac delta supported at
\lambda
.
The Prony method seeks to find
K
and
\lambda_{k}
’s given data of the form

\hat{\mu}(\ell)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\exp(-i\lambda_{k}\ell),\qquad\ell=0,\cdots,n-1,

(1.2.1)

where
n\geq 2K
is an integer.

Writing
z_{k}=\exp(-i\lambda_{k})
, we introduce the Prony polynomial

P(z)=\prod_{k=1}^{K}(z-z_{k})=\sum_{j=0}^{K}c_{j}z^{j},

(1.2.2)

where
c_{K}=1
.
Obviously, for every integer
\ell
,
0\leq\ell\leq K-1
,

\int z^{\ell}P(z)d\mu(z)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}z_{k}^{\ell}P(z_{k})=0.

(1.2.3)

Since

\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}z_{k}^{\ell}P(z_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}z_{k}^{\ell}\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}z_{k}^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}z_{k}^{\ell+j}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}c_{j}\hat{\mu}(j+\ell),

(1.2.4)

we see that the coefficients of the Prony polynomial are solutions of the equations

\sum_{j=0}^{K}c_{j}\hat{\mu}(j+\ell)=0,\qquad\ell=0,1,\cdots,n-K-1.

(1.2.5)

Prony’s method consists of solving this system for the coefficients
c_{j}
,
j=1,\cdots,K-1
, and then find the zeros
z_{k}=\exp(-i\lambda_{k})
of the Prony polynomial.

The points
z_{k}
can be determined directly as joint eigenvalues.
Let
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.
We consider the matrix
\mathbf{M}(z)
with entries given by

M_{k,\ell}(z)=\hat{\mu}(\ell+k+1)-z\hat{\mu}(\ell+k),\qquad k=1,\cdots,K,\ \ell=0,\cdots,K-1,

(1.2.6)

and observe that the determinant

Q(z)=\det(\mathbf{M}(z))
is a polynomial in
z
of degree
\leq K
.
We consider the lower triangular matrix
\mathbf{L}(z)
given by

L_{j,k}=\begin{cases}z^{j-k},&\mbox{ if $0\leq k\leq j\leq K-1$},\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(1.2.7)

Since
\det(\mathbf{L}(z))=1
for all
z
, we have

\det(\mathbf{L}(z)\mathbf{M}(z))=\det(\mathbf{M}(z))=Q(z).

(1.2.8)

We note that

\displaystyle(\mathbf{L}(z)\mathbf{M}(z))_{j,\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}L_{j,k}M_{k,\ell}=\sum_{k=0}^{j}z^{j-k}\hat{\mu}(k+\ell+1)-\sum_{k=0}^{j}z^{j-k+1}\hat{\mu}(k+\ell)

(1.2.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j+1}z^{j-k+1}\hat{\mu}(k+\ell)-\sum_{k=0}^{j}z^{j-k+1}\hat{\mu}(k+\ell)=\hat{\mu}(\ell+j+1)-z^{j+1}\hat{\mu}(\ell).

Consequently,

\int z^{\ell}(\mathbf{L}(z)\mathbf{M}(z))_{j,\ell}d\mu(z)=0,\qquad j=1,\cdots,K,\ \ell=0,\cdots,K-1,

(1.2.10)

and hence, also that

\int z^{\ell}Q(z)d\mu(z)=0,\qquad\ell=0,\cdots,K-1.

(1.2.11)

This is the same system of equations as (
1.2.3
).
So,
Q
is a constant multiple of
P
.

Thus, the
z_{k}
’s are joint eigenvalues as indicated by the matrix
\mathbf{M}(z)
; i.e. the zeros of
\det(\mathbf{M}(z))
.

1.2.2
MUSIC method

The Multiple Signal Classification (MUSIC) algorithm
[
50
, Section 10.2.1]
is a technique for recovering the parameters of an exponential sum, particularly when the data is affected by noise. It operates on the principle of decomposing a trajectory matrix (a Hankel matrix formed from the data) into signal and noise subspaces using singular value decomposition (SVD).

To proceed, we assume that a parameter
L\in\mathbb{N}
is selected as an upper bound on
K
, satisfying the condition

K\leq L\leq n-K+1,

(1.2.12)

We define the
L
-trajectory matrix
\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times(n-L+1)}
constructed from this time series by

\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}:=\left(\hat{\mu}(\ell+m)\right)_{\ell=0,\ldots,L-1;\,m=0,\ldots,n-L},

(1.2.13)

where
L\in\{K,\ldots,n-K+1\}
is called the
window length
. Note that
\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}
is a rectangular Hankel matrix, with constant entries along each anti-diagonal.

Given a sequence of noisy samples
\tilde{\mu}(\ell)=\hat{\mu}(\ell)+\epsilon_{\ell}
, one constructs a Hankel matrix
\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times(n-L+1)}
. In the ideal (noiseless) case, the column space of this matrix coincides with the span of
K
exponential vectors
\{e_{L}(\varphi_{j})\}_{j=1}^{K}
, where

e_{L}(\varphi)=\left(e^{i\varphi\ell}\right)_{\ell=0}^{L-1}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}.

(1.2.14)

This span defines the signal space
\mathcal{S}_{L}
, while its orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{C}^{L}
is the noise space
\mathcal{N}_{L}
.

Let
Q_{L}
be the orthogonal projector onto
\mathcal{N}_{L}
. Since the true signal vectors
e_{L}(\varphi_{j})\in\mathcal{S}_{L}
, they are orthogonal to the noise space, and thus satisfy

Q_{L}e_{L}(\varphi_{j})=0,\quad j=1,\dots,K.

(1.2.15)

Hence, the parameters
\varphi_{j}
can be recovered as the zeros of the so-called noise-space correlation function

\mathcal{N}_{L}(\varphi):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\|Q_{L}e_{L}(\varphi)\|_{2}

(1.2.16)

(see Figure
1.1
(b)) or, equivalently, as the peaks of the MUSIC imaging function

\mathcal{J}_{L}(\varphi):=\sqrt{L}\cdot\|Q_{L}e_{L}(\varphi)\|_{2}^{-1}.

(1.2.17)

In practice,
Q_{L}
is computed via the SVD of
\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}
:

\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}=U_{L}D_{L}W^{H},

(1.2.18)

where
U_{L}=[u_{1},\dots,u_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times L}
contains the left singular vectors.

Let
U^{(2)}_{L,L-M}=[u_{M+1},\dots,u_{L}]\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times(L-M)}
be the orthonormal basis of the noise space. Then,

Q_{L}=U^{(2)}_{L,L-M}(U^{(2)}_{L,L-M})^{H},

(1.2.19)

and

\mathcal{N}_{L}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\left\|(U^{(2)}_{L,L-M})^{H}e_{L}(\varphi)\right\|_{2}.

(1.2.20)

1.2.3
ESPRIT method

The Estimation of Signal Parameters via Rotational Invariance Techniques (ESPRIT) is a classical subspace-based method for estimating exponential parameters from sampled data. It builds on the structure of the Hankel matrix associated with the signal and leverages a matrix pencil framework to compute the exponential nodes
[
50
, Section 10.2.2]
.

Assume that the observed data
\tilde{\mu}(\ell)=\hat{\mu}(\ell)+\epsilon_{\ell}
follow an exponential model of the form (
1.1.9
). As in MUSIC, we construct a rectangular Hankel matrix
\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}\in\mathbb{C}^{L\times(n-L+1)}
and perform singular value decomposition (SVD):

\mathbf{H}_{L,n-L+1}=\widetilde{U}_{L}\widetilde{D}_{L}\widetilde{W}^{H}_{n-L+1}.

(1.2.21)

The numerical rank
K
is determined by examining the decay of the singular values, e.g., by selecting the largest
K
such that
\sigma_{K}\geq\tau\sigma_{1}
, where
\tau>0
is a chosen tolerance (see Figure
1.1
(a)).

Let
\widetilde{W}_{n-L+1,K}\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-L+1)\times M}
contain the first
K
right singular vectors. Removing the last and first rows respectively gives matrices
\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(0)
and
\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(1)
. The key insight of ESPRIT is that the signal model implies a shift-invariance property between these two matrices, leading to the generalized eigenvalue problem:

z\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(0)^{H}-\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(1)^{H}.

(1.2.22)

The ESPRIT nodes
\{z_{j}\}
are computed as the eigenvalues of the matrix

\widetilde{F}^{\text{SVD}}_{K}:=\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(1)^{H}\left(\widetilde{W}_{n-L,K}(0)^{H}\right)^{\dagger},

(1.2.23)

where
(\cdot)^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse. The estimated frequencies
\varphi_{j}
are then recovered via

\varphi_{j}:=\log\left(\frac{z_{j}}{|z_{j}|}\right),\quad j=1,\ldots,K,

(1.2.24)

taking the principal branch of the complex logarithm.

Finally, the amplitudes
\{c_{j}\}
are determined by solving the least squares problem

\min_{c\in\mathbb{C}^{K}}\left\|V_{n,K}(z)c-\begin{bmatrix}\hat{\mu}(0)&\cdots&\hat{\mu}(n-1)\end{bmatrix}^{T}\right\|_{2},

(1.2.25)

where
V_{n,K}(z)
is the Vandermonde matrix associated with the recovered nodes
z=\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{K}
.

1.2.4
Examples of Prony’s, MUSIC, and ESPRIT methods

Before illustrating the methods with an example, we briefly summarize their key differences. Prony’s method is a classical algebraic approach that reconstructs the signal parameters by solving a system of linear equations derived from the data, and it performs best in low-noise settings. MUSIC is a subspace-based method that estimates the signal frequencies by analyzing the orthogonality between signal and noise subspaces via eigen-decomposition of a Hankel matrix. ESPRIT also uses subspace information, but exploits a shift-invariance structure in the signal model to compute frequencies. While MUSIC and ESPRIT generally perform better in noisy environments, they require singular value decompositions and are computationally more demanding.

Chapter 2
Exponential Parameter Estimation

The goal of this chapter is to develop a robust and tractable method for estimating the parameters of a finite sum of exponential signals from noisy observations. Specifically, we aim to recover the number of components, their frequencies, and amplitudes. Traditional methods, such as Prony’s method or subspace-based techniques like MUSIC and ESPRIT, face limitations in the presence of noise. In contrast, our approach builds on the use of
localized trigonometric kernels
to isolate and estimate signal parameters without requiring matrix decompositions, enabling accurate parameter recovery under sub-Gaussian noise.

This dissertation is based on localized trigonometric polynomial kernels developed in
[
41
]
. We introduce our localized trigonometric kernels in Section
2.1
and reconstruction operator in Section
2.2
. Our main theorem is stated in Section
2.3
, and proved in Section
2.5
.
Section
2.4
discusses the numerical implementation of this theorem.

2.1
Localized trigonometric kernels

The main idea is to use a low pass filter and the corresponding localized kernel
\Phi_{n}
.
A
low pass filter
is an infinitely differentiable function
H:{\mathbb{R}}\to[0,1]
such that
H(t)=H(-t)
for all
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
H(t)=1
if
|t|\leq 1/2
, and
H(t)=0
if
|t|\geq 1
.

With a low pass filter
H
, we now define

\hbar_{n}=\left\{\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)\right\}^{-1},\quad\Phi_{n}(x)=\hbar_{n}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)e^{i\ell x},\qquad x\in\mathbb{T},\ n>0.

(2.1.1)

We note that the normalizing factor
\hbar_{n}
is chosen so that

\max_{x\in\mathbb{T}}|\Phi_{n}(x)|=\Phi_{n}(0)=1.

(2.1.2)

An important property of
\Phi_{n}
is the following localization estimate given in
[
17
]
.

Let

H_{1}:=\|H\|_{1}=\int_{-1}^{1}H(t)\,dt>0.

(2.1.3)

Since
H(t)\in[0,1]
for all
t\in[-1,1]
, it follows that
H_{1}\leq 2
.

The following Theorem
2.1.1
gives the upper bound of
|\Phi_{n}(x)|
.

Theorem 2.1.1

Let
H
be a smooth low pass filter,
p\geq 1
and
S\geq 2
be integers.
We have

(a)

\left|\sum_{k=-n}^{n}H\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{p}-n\int_{-1}^{1}H(x)^{p}~dx\right|\leq\frac{3p}{12}\cdot\frac{\|H^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}}{n}.

(2.1.4)

In particular, if

n\geq\sqrt{\frac{p\|H^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}}{\|H^{p}\|_{1}}},

(2.1.5)

then

\left|\sum_{k=-n}^{n}H\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{p}-n\int_{-1}^{1}H(x)^{p}~dx\right|\leq(n/4)\int_{-1}^{1}H(x)^{p}~dx.

(2.1.6)

In particular, if
p=1
,

\left|\frac{1}{n\hbar_{n}\|H\|_{1}}-1\right|\leq\frac{1}{4}.

(2.1.7)

Hence, the lower and upper bounds of
n\hbar_{n}
are

\frac{4}{5\|H\|_{1}}\leq n\hbar_{n}\leq\frac{4}{3\|H\|_{1}}.

(2.1.8)

(b)
For
x\in[-\pi,\pi]
,
x\not=0
,
n\geq 1
, we have

|\hbar_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}\sqrt{2\pi}n}{(n|x|)^{S}}\cdot\frac{7}{2}.

(2.1.9)

In particular, if
n
satisfies (
2.1.5
) with
p=1
, then

|\Phi_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{14\sqrt{2\pi}}{3}\cdot\frac{\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}}{\|H\|_{1}}\cdot\frac{1}{\max(1,(n|x|)^{S})}.

(2.1.10)

The following proposition summarizes some facts which we will need in the proof.
We recall that the Fourier transform (respectively, inverse Fourier transform) of
f
is defined by

\hat{f}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t)\exp(it\omega)dt,\qquad\check{f}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(\omega)\exp(it\omega)d\omega.

(2.1.11)

Proposition 2.1.1

(a)
If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is twice continuously differentiable and
n\geq 1
is an integer, then

\left|\sum_{|k|\leq n}f(k)-\int_{-n}^{n}f(t)dt\right|\leq\frac{3}{24}\int_{-n}^{n}|f^{\prime\prime}(t)|dt.

(2.1.12)

(b)
If
\check{f}
is integrable and has bounded total variation then

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}f(k)\exp(ikx)=\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\check{f}(x+2\ell\pi).

(2.1.13)

Proof.

Part (a) is a consequence of the Euler-Maclaurin formula (cf.
[
44
, Formulas[Chapter 8, (2.01), (2.04)]
.
Part (b) is the Poisson summation formula
[
3
, Proposition 5.1.30]
.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem
2.1.1
.

In order to prove part (a), we use Proposition
2.1.1
(a) with
H(\cdot/n)
in place of
f
and recall that
H(1)=H(-1)=0
to conclude that

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=-n}^{n}H\left(\frac{k}{n}\right)^{p}-\int_{-1}^{1}H(x)^{p}\,dx\right|\leq\frac{3}{24n^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}|(H^{p})^{\prime\prime}(t)|dt.

(2.1.14)

If
p=1
, this proves (
2.1.4
).
Let
p\geq 2
.
Using integration by parts, we see that

(p-1)\int_{-1}^{1}H^{p-2}(t)H^{\prime}(t)^{2}dt=-\int_{-1}^{1}H^{p-1}(t)H^{\prime\prime}(t)dt.

(2.1.15)

Since
(H^{p})^{\prime\prime}(t)=pH^{p-1}(t)H^{\prime\prime}(t)=p(p-1)H^{p-2}(t)H^{\prime}(t)^{2}
, we conclude that

\int_{-1}^{1}|(H^{p})^{\prime\prime}(t)|dt\leq 2p\|H^{\prime\prime}\|_{1}.

(2.1.16)

Together with (
2.1.14
), this leads to (
2.1.4
) also in the case when
p\geq 2
.

In order to prove part (b), we use Proposition
2.1.1
(b) with
f(t)=H(t/n)
, so that
\check{f}(x)=n\check{H}(nx)
.
Hence,

\hbar_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n}(x)=\sum_{|k|<n}H(k/n)\exp(ikx)=\sqrt{2\pi}n\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb{Z}}\check{H}(n(x+2\ell\pi)).

(2.1.17)

Since
h
is
S
times continuously differentiable, then an integration by parts shows that

\check{H}(nx)=\frac{(-1)^{S}}{(nix)^{S}}\check{H^{(S)}}(nx),

(2.1.18)

and hence

|\check{H}(n(x+2\ell\pi))|\leq\frac{\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}}{n^{S}|x+2\ell\pi|^{S}}

(2.1.19)

If
|x|<\pi
, and
\ell\not=0
, then
|x+2\ell\pi|\geq 2|\ell|\pi-|x|\geq(2|\ell|-1)\pi\geq(2|\ell|-1)|x|
.
So,

|\hbar_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}n\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}}{(n|x|)^{S}}\left\{1+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(2\ell-1)^{-S}\right\}.

(2.1.20)

We have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(2\ell-1)^{-S}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{-S}-(1/2^{S})\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell^{-S}=(1-2^{-S})\zeta(S),

(2.1.21)

where
\zeta(S)
is the Riemann zeta function. Note that when
S=2
,

\left(1+2(1-2^{-S})\zeta(S)\right)=\left(1+2(1-1/4)\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)\approx 3.4674<\frac{7}{2}.

(2.1.22)

As
S\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\zeta(S)\rightarrow 1
and
2^{-S}\rightarrow 0
. Thus,

\lim_{S\rightarrow\infty}\left(1+2(1-2^{-S})\zeta(S)\right)=\left(1+2(1-0)(1)\right)=3.

(2.1.23)

Hence, it holds that

\left(1+2(1-2^{-S})\zeta(S)\right)<\frac{7}{2}.

(2.1.24)

Together with (
2.1.20
), this leads to

|\hbar_{n}^{-1}\Phi_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}n}{(n|x|)^{S}}(1+2(1-2^{-S}))\zeta(S)<\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}n}{(n|x|)^{S}}\cdot\frac{7}{2}.

(2.1.25)

This proves (
2.1.9
).
Additionally,

\displaystyle H(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{t}H^{\prime}(u)\,du

(2.1.26)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{t}\left(\int_{-1}^{u}H^{\prime\prime}(v)\,dv\right)du\qquad-1\leq v\leq u\leq t

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{t}(t-v)H^{\prime\prime}(v)\,dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cdots~=~\frac{1}{(S-1)!}\int_{-1}^{1}(t-v)_{+}^{S-1}H^{(S)}(v)\,dv.

Thus,

\int_{-1}^{1}H(t)~dt=\frac{1}{(S-1)!}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(\int_{-1}^{1}(t-v)_{+}^{S-1}~dt\right)H^{(S)}(v)\,dv.

(2.1.27)

Since
(t-v)_{+}^{S-1}=0
for
t<v
, we simplify the inner integral:

\int_{v}^{1}(t-v)^{S-1}\,dt=\int_{0}^{1-v}u^{S-1}\,du=\frac{(1-v)^{S}}{S}

(2.1.28)

Hence,

\int_{-1}^{1}H(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{S!}\int_{-1}^{1}(1-v)^{S}H^{(S)}(v)\,dv

(2.1.29)

To estimate this, note that
(1-v)^{S}\leq 2^{S}
for
v\in[-1,1]
. Therefore,

\int_{-1}^{1}H(t)\,dt\leq\frac{2^{S}}{S!}\int_{-1}^{1}H^{(S)}(v)\,dv.

(2.1.30)

It follows that

\frac{\|H^{(S)}\|_{1}}{\|H\|_{1}}\geq\frac{2^{S}}{S!}\geq 1.

(2.1.31)

This to together with (
2.1.8
) and (
2.1.9
) prove (
2.1.10
), and completes the proof.
\blacksquare

Clearly, the parameter
S
controls the localization of the kernel, with higher smoothness of
H
implying better localization.
We refer to
[
40
]
for a more detailed discussion of the effect of
S
.
The estimate (
2.1.10
) implies that
\Phi_{n}(x)
is an approximation to the Dirac delta
\delta_{0}
.
Figure
2.1
illustrates the localization property of the kernel.

There are, of course, many possible choices for a low pass filter.
The actual choice is not so critical for our theory.
In the rest of this dissertation, we fix a low pass filter.
All constants may depend upon this filter, and the filter will be omitted from the notation.

2.2
Reconstruction operator

Constant convention

The letters
c,c_{1},\cdots
will denote generic positive constants depending on
H
and
S
alone. Their values might be different at different occurrences within single formula.
The notation
A\lesssim B
means
A\leq cB
,
A\gtrsim B
means
B\lesssim A
, and
A\sim B
means
A\lesssim B\lesssim A
.
Constants denoted by capital letters, such as
L
,
C
, etc. will retain their values.

The localization property (
2.1.10
) implies that

\sigma_{n}(\hat{\mu})(x)=\hbar_{n}\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)\hat{\mu}(\ell)\exp(i\ell x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\Phi_{n}(x-\lambda_{k})\approx\mu(x).

(2.2.1)

Thus, the “prominent” peaks of
|\sigma_{n}(\hat{\mu})(x)|
will occur at (or close to) the points
\lambda_{k}
and the value of
\sigma_{n}
at these points lead to the corresponding complex amplitudes
A_{k}
.
It turns out that the values of
A_{k}
are actually determined fairly accurately simply by evaluating
\sigma_{n}(\mu)(x)
at the estimated value of
\lambda_{k}
.

The main difficulty is to make this more precise, and quantify the approximation error in terms of
n
and the properties of the noise.

2.3
Main theorem

The purpose of this section is to make precise the idea as to how the use of the localized kernel facilitates the solution of the point source separation problem, giving precise error bounds.
We assume the notation in (
1.1.9
), and make further notation as follows.
Let

M=\sum_{k=1}^{K}|A_{k}|,\ \ \mathfrak{m}=\min_{1\leq k\leq K}|A_{k}|,\ \ \eta=\min_{\ell\not=k}|\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{\ell}|,

(2.3.1)

We further assume that each
\epsilon_{\ell}
is a realization of a sub-Gaussian random variable in
\mathcal{G}(V)
.

We will write

\sigma_{n}(x)=\sigma_{n}(\hat{\mu})(x)=\hbar_{n}\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)\tilde{\mu}(\ell)\exp(i\ell x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T}.

(2.3.2)

Writing

E_{n}(x)=\sigma_{n}(\{\epsilon_{\ell}\})(x)=\hbar_{n}\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)\epsilon_{\ell}\exp(i\ell x),\quad x\in\mathbb{T},

(2.3.3)

we observe that

\sigma_{n}(\tilde{\mu})(x)=\sigma_{n}(\hat{\mu})(x)+E_{n}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}\Phi_{n}(x-\lambda_{k})+E_{n}(x).

(2.3.4)

With this set up, our main theorem concerning the recuperation of point sources can be stated as follows.

Theorem 2.3.1

Let
0<\delta<1
,

\mathbb{G}=\{x\in[-\pi,\pi]:|\sigma_{n}(x)|\geq\mathfrak{m}/2\}.

(2.3.5)

and (cf. (
2.1.10
), (
2.3.1
))

C=\max\left(1,\left(\frac{16ML}{\mathfrak{m}}\right)^{1/S}\right).

(2.3.6)

For sufficiently large
n
(cf. (
2.5.13
)), each of the following statements holds with probability exceeding
1-\delta
.

•

(
Disjoint union condition
)

the set
\mathbb{G}
is a disjoint union of exactly
K
subsets
\mathbb{G}_{\ell}
,

•

(
Diameter condition
)

for each
\ell=1,\cdots,K
,
\mathsf{diam}(\mathbb{G}_{\ell})\leq 2C/n
,

•

(
Separation
)

\mathsf{dist}(\mathbb{G}_{\ell},\mathbb{G}_{k})\geq\eta/2
for
\ell\neq k
,

•

(
Interval inclusion
)

For each
\ell=1,\cdots,K
,
I_{\ell}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}:|x-\lambda_{\ell}|\leq 1/(4n)\}\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{\ell}
.

Moreover, if

\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathbb{G}_{\ell}}|\sigma_{n}(x)|,

(2.3.7)

then

|\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}|\leq 2C/n.

(2.3.8)

Remark 2.3.1

Equation (
2.5.13
) shows that the value of
n
should be at least proportional to the reciprocal of the minimal separation,
\eta
, among the
\lambda_{\ell}
’s. There is no direct connection with the number
K
of signals, except the fact that there can be at most
{\cal O}(\eta^{-1})
values of
\lambda_{\ell}
in
[-\pi,\pi]
.
For example, our method would require a much larger value of
n
if there are two signals which are very close by compared to the case when they are far apart.
Equation (
2.5.13
) also demonstrates the connection between
n
, the noise variance
V
, the minimal amplitude
\mathfrak{m}
, and the desired level of certainty
1-\delta
. Since the value of
\mathfrak{m}
is unknown, we estimate the threshold
\tau
(
3\mathfrak{m}
in (
2.3.5
)) by taking a percentile of the histogram of
|\sigma_{n}(x)|
.
As a result, any signal with amplitude less than
\tau
will not be detected by our algorithm.
\blacksquare

Chapter 3
Robust and Tractable Multidimensional Exponential Analysis

The content presented in this chapter is adapted from our paper titled
“Robust and Tractable Multidimensional Exponential Analysis”

[
39
]
.

3.1
Introduction

In continuation of the point source separation problem discussed in Section
1.1
, this chapter addresses its multidimensional extension, which appears in various applications such as tomographic imaging (including Computerized Tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), radar and sonar imaging), wireless communication, antenna array processing, sensor networks, and automotive radar, among others.
Mathematically, the problem can be formulated as follows.
Given a multidimensional signal of the form

f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\exp(-i\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}_{k}\rangle),\quad\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}_{1},\cdots,\mathbf{w}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},

(3.1.1)

find the number
K
of components, and the parameters
a_{k}
and
\mathbf{w}_{k}
’s .
Of course, this is a problem of inverse Fourier transform if we could observe the function
f
at
all
values of
\mathbf{x}
. In practice, however, one can observe (after some sampling and renaming of the variables) the values of
f
at only
finitely many
multi-integer values of
\mathbf{x}
.
In this case, it is not possible to distinguish values of
\mathbf{w}_{k}
which are equal modulo
2\pi
in all variables. So, this is a special case of the ancient trigonometric moment problem
[
59
]
, except that we do not have
all
the trigonometric moments (i.e., the samples
f(\boldsymbol{\ell})
) for all values of
\boldsymbol{\ell}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{q}
. Thus, the problem is the ill-posed problem known often as the super-resolution problem: knowing the information in a finite domain of the frequency space, we need to extend it to the entire frequency space. The important problem in this connection is to determine the relationship between the number of samples
f(\boldsymbol{\ell})
needed to recuperate the desired quantities up to a given accuracy.

In the univariate case, there are many methods to solve the problem of parameter estimation in exponential sums, we refer to
[
50
,
13
]
for a good introduction.

Under the rubric of target estimation or localization, which is one of the fundamental problems in radar signal processing with many civilian and military applications including landmine detection and geolocations (cf.
[
67
]
), a broad set of techniques has emerged for solving exponential analysis problems. Most of these methods are categorized broadly as subspace methods
[
30
]
, and are based on statistical considerations, rather than the nature of the signal itself. In fact, quite a few papers, e.g.,
[
63
,
54
]
, are interested in testing a statistical hypothesis on whether or not there exists a signal at all. There is a theoretical limit on how much SNR can be tolerated, depending upon the number of antenna elements and number of observations
[
63
]
, in spite of a huge computational cost. On the other hand, beamforming methods
[
30
]
take into account the nature of the signal but focus again on noise, and try to maximize the SNR.

Contrary to the remarks in
[
30
]
, methods based on filtered inverse Fourier transform were developed in
[
41
,
17
,
6
]
, and shown to work very well and faster than subspace methods.

Over the past several decades, several approaches to multivariate exponential analysis have been investigated.
Many of these are extensions of the classical Prony method, and variations of MUSIC and ESPRIT, which are designed to stabilize this classical method.
For example,
[
53
]
discusses multivariate extensions of MUSIC and ESPRIT algorithms.
The number of samples required to recuperate the
\mathbf{w}_{k}
’s up to an accuracy of
\mathcal{O}(1/n)
is typically on the order of
{\cal O}(n^{q})

[
13
,
52
,
56
,
31
,
49
]
.
Under some extra assumptions, it is shown in
[
14
]
that this can be improved to
{\cal O}(n\log^{q-1}n)
, and to
(q+1)n^{2}\log^{2q-2}n

[
57
]
.
In
[
6
]
, we have discussed an an analogue of the beam-forming methods in multivariate setting.
However, this requires
{\cal O}(n^{q})
samples.

In
[
8
,
7
]
, the authors proposed a method to solve the problem using a combination of Prony’s method and a based method, so that number of samples required is
{\cal O}(qn)
.
Methods based on Padé approximation and orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle are explored, especially in the univariate case
[
17
,
12
]
.
The methods described in the paper
[
8
]
are also connected with Padé approximation, and the paper
[
2
]
develops this method further to obtain accurate solution to the multivariate problem in the presence of a moderate Gaussian noise with a sub-Nyquist sampling rate.

In this chapter, we propose a novel method based on localized trigonometric polynomial kernels developed in
[
41
]
.
Our method utilizes
{\cal O}(qn)
samples as well, but is faster and far more robust under noise.
In contrast to the subspace based methods, our method takes into account the nature of the signal, resulting in a significant noise reduction with only a small number of observations per signal, and yields accurate results with theoretical guarantees.

The rest of this chapter is organized as follows. Section
3.2
introduces a system model for tomographic imaging which illustrates how the problem of multidimensional exponential analysis arises in signal processing. The algorithms to implement these theorems are given in Section
3.3
, and demonstrated in the case of the three examples explored in
[
8
]
.

3.2
System model

As mentioned in Section
3.1
, the multi-dimensional exponential model in (
3.1.1
) arises in many applications in science and engineering.
In this section, we illustrate the details of one such application, namely, tomographic imaging.

In tomographic imaging, an object of interest being imaged is probed by a sequence of monochromatic tones swept through a frequency range
[\Omega_{init},\Omega_{fin}]\in{\mathbb{R}}
(in units of Hertz).
The sensor transmits a signal onto a scene with respect to various angles
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}=(\theta_{m},\phi_{m})\}_{m}
, where
\theta_{m}\in[\Theta_{init},\Theta_{fin}]\subseteq[0,2\pi]
and
\phi_{m}\in[\Phi_{init},\Phi_{fin}]\subseteq[0,\pi]
, are the azimuth and elevation angles, with respect to the sensor (such as a radar
[
25
]
), respectively.
The scene reflectivity is a complex-valued function over the spatial coordinates,
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in 3D imaging (units of meters).
In this chapter, this is modeled as a distribution,
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\delta_{\mathbf{w}_{k}}
, where
\delta
denotes the Dirac delta.
For the sake of simplicity, we will use
\delta
regardless of whether it is applied to vectors in different dimensions or scalars.
With rescaling and shifting, we may assume that the domain of
\mu
is a subset of
[-\pi,\pi]^{3}
.

We define the center reference point (CRP) to be the center of mass of the scene to be reconstructed, and the line of sight (
los
) as the unit vector,
\mathbf{i}_{\mbox{los}}
, that points from the transmitter to the CRP of the scene.
The distance along the
los
from the transmitter to the voxel location (placing the origin at the transmitter),
\mathbf{r}_{0}
, of interest is called the ‘downrange’
r_{0}=|\mathbf{r}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{i}_{\mbox{los}}|
(where
\mathbf{r}_{0}
is the position vector of a scatterer in the scene, and
\cdot
denotes the inner product operation). Given this, it can be shown that the backscattered signal at downrange
r_{0}
from the sensor, when viewed at angle
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, is given by
[
25
,
55
,
24
]

\Upsilon(t)|_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\mathbf{r}_{0}}=[R_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\left\{\mu\right\}(r_{0})]\chi\left(t-\frac{2r_{0}}{\nu_{p}}\right)+\check{\epsilon}(t),

(3.2.1)

where
2r_{0}/\nu_{p}
denotes the two-way time delay,
\nu_{p}
represents the speed of wave propagation,
\chi
is the transmitted waveform,
\check{\epsilon}(t)
is the measurement noise, and
R_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
is the Radon transform of the scene,
\mu
, with respect to angle
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, and evaluated at the downrange location
\mathbf{r}_{0}
.
This corresponds to the integral across sensor returns from all points along a hyperplane perpendicular to the downrange location
\mathbf{r}_{0}
, commonly referred to as an ‘iso-range contour’.

Therefore the complete response at time
t
from all ranges, along the line formed by intersecting the scene at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, is rewritten as

\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t)=\int[R_{\mathbf{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}\left\{\mu\right\}(r)]\chi\left(t-\frac{2r}{\nu_{p}}\right)\,dr+\check{\epsilon}(t).

(3.2.2)

This can be reformulated in convolution form (after a choice of units, without loss of generality, so that
\nu_{p}=2
) as:

\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t)=\left(R_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mu)\ast\chi\right)(t)+\check{\epsilon}(t),

(3.2.3)

where
\ast
denotes the convolution operation.
Equation (
3.2.3
) can be interpreted as the response to a linear time invariant (LTI) system
[
45
]
with an input signal
\chi(t)
and an distributional impulse response
\mu_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t)
which characterizes the interaction between the transmit waveform and the scene with respect to sensing angle
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta,\phi)
(where
\theta
and
\phi
are the azimuth and elevation angles respectively).
The received signal
\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t)
, is the output of this LTI system and is subsequently sampled at the receiver.
Given
\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(t)
for a finite grid
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{m}\}
as described earlier, our problem is to estimate the underlying scene reflectivity
\mu
i.e. to form the image.

Taking the Fourier transform, we obtain

\frac{\widehat{\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(u)}{\widehat{\chi}(u)}=\widehat{R_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\{\mu\}}(u)+\epsilon(u),

(3.2.4)

where
\epsilon
is derived from the Fourier transform of
\check{\epsilon}
in an obvious way.
In this chapter, we will treat
\epsilon
itself as the noise in the obervations.
The division on the left hand side of (
3.2.4
) might amplify the noise in the original observations, but we will relate the noise level and the number of observations etc. in Theorem
2.3.1
.

When
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\theta,\phi)
, the Fourier projection slice theorem implies that the one dimensional Fourier transform
\widehat{R_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}(\mu)}(u)
is given by
\mathfrak{F}({\mu})(u\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi),u\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi),u\sin(\phi))
, where
\mathfrak{F}
denotes three dimensional Fourier transform.
Writing

\mathbf{x}=(u\cos(\theta)\cos(\phi),u\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi),u\sin(\phi)),

(3.2.5)

equation (
3.2.4
) becomes

\frac{\widehat{\Upsilon_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}}(u)}{\widehat{\chi}(u)}=\mathfrak{F}({\mu})(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon(\mathbf{x}).

(3.2.6)

Although the vector
\mathbf{x}
is in spherical coordinates here, one interpolates the actual data to obtain (a noisy version of)
\mathfrak{F}({\mu})(\mathbf{x})
at
\mathbf{x}
in a Cartesian grid, so as to facilitate the use of fast Fourier transform
[
25
]
. In particular, the problem of finding the reflexivity function
\mu
reduces to the problem mentioned in Section
3.1
.

In this chapter we present a novel approach for solving the multidimensional analysis problem by resampling the Fourier space
\mathfrak{F}
in a computationally efficient manner, while showing substantial improvements over state-of-the-art techniques including the MUSIC and ESPRIT approaches.

It is important to note that this chapter offers a general efficient tool for solving multidimensional exponential problems with applications beyond tomographic imaging such as signal source separation and direction-of-arrival estimation in multi-channel radar systems.

After finding the estimate

Throughout the experiments in Table

\mbox{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}|\mathbf{w}_{k}-\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{k}|^{2}}.

(3.3.1)

We then reported the number of points reconstructed, run-time, memory, accuracy, and RMSE by taking average over 16 trials. Finally, we computed the standard deviation of the RMSE.

3.4
Conclusions

The problem of multidimensional exponential analysis arises in many areas of applications including tomographic imaging, ISAR imaging, antenna array processing, etc.
The problem is essentially to develop an efficient algorithm to obtain the inverse Fourier transform of a multidimensional signal, based on finitely many equidistant samples of the signal.
We have given a very simple algorithm based on localized trigonometric kernel, which reduces the problem to a series of one dimensional problems.
Our algorithm works with a tractable number of samples, gives high accuracy, and is very robust even in the presence of noise as high as -10dB.
We have proved theoretical guarantees under the assumption that the noise is sub-Gaussian, a significantly weaker assumption than that of white noise common in the literature.

4.1
Introduction

The task of separating a superposition of signals into its individual components is a common challenge encountered in various signal processing applications, especially in domains such as audio
[
37
,
42
,
65
]
and radar
[
28
,
18
,
15
,
5
,
43
,
36
]
. Due to the extensive set of applications and the frequent occurrence of signal source separation challenges, methodologies are often tailored to the specific field in question, as well as the level of prior knowledge of both the component signals and the mixing mechanism.
We focus on a specific example that occurs in radar signal processing, specifically in the context of electronic warfare (EW) and electronic intelligence (ELINT).

In these applications, the RF system makes measurements of the electromagnetic spectrum with a large instantaneous bandwidth, typically above
1
GHz and continuing to increase with continual improvements in hardware capabilities.
The resulting data may consist of tens to hundreds of signals mixed together after just a few seconds to minutes of recording, many overlapping in time and/or frequency and measured at low signal-to-noise (SNR).
Most radars transmit a signal that consists of a sequence of linear frequency modulated (LFM) pulses, commonly referred to as Chirp signals.

A linear chirp is a signal (pulse) of the form
A(t)\exp(i\phi(t))
, where
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\phi
is a quadratic polynomial, so that the instantaneous frequency
\phi^{\prime}(t)
is a linear function.
If
\gamma
is the starting time of the pulse,
d
is its duration,
\theta
is the initial frequency,
B
is the bandwidth, then the phase of the pulse is given by

\phi(t)=\phi(\gamma,\theta,B,d;t)=\theta(t-\gamma)+(B/d)(t-\gamma)^{2}.

(4.1.1)

The pulse is assumed to repeat periodically in
m
bursts with a pulse repetition interval PRI being the time between the end of one burst and the beginning of the next.
Thus, for a signal
s
starting at
t=t_{0}^{*}
, its
n
-th burst is given by

s_{n}(t)=s_{n}(A,\theta,B,t_{0}^{*},d,m,\mbox{PRI};t)=\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} &\displaystyle A(t)\exp\left(i(\theta(t-n*\mbox{PRI})+(B/d)(t-n*\mbox{PRI})^{2})\right),&\\
&\mbox{ if $t_{0}^{*}+n*\mbox{PRI}\leq t\leq t_{0}^{*}+n*\mbox{PRI}+d$, \ $n=0,\cdots,m-1$,}\\
&0,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.1.2)

Naturally, the chirp signal has the form
s=\sum_{n}s_{n}
.

The first step in processing the electromagnetic spectrum is the detection and parameterization step, where the time and frequency locations of individual pulses are determined, and their parameters estimated.
The second step in the EW receiver is deinterleaving, where groups of pulses are associated with each other to obtain the different radar signals, from which further insights are extracted.
Here, we focus on the first problem of the detection, separation, and parameter estimation of individual radar pulses.

Thus, the problem which we intend to solve is the following.
We observe a signal of the form

F(t)=f(t)+\epsilon(t),

(4.1.3)

where
\epsilon
is a sub-Gaussian random noise, and
f
is the ground truth signal

f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}s(A_{j},\theta_{j},B_{j},t_{0,j}^{*},d_{j},m_{j},\mbox{PRI}_{j};t),\qquad t\in[0,T].

(4.1.4)

From equidistant observations
F(\ell\delta)
,
\ell=0,\cdots,N
, we wish to determine the number of pulses at any time
t
as well as the parameters
A_{j},\theta_{j},B_{j},t_{0,j}^{*},d_{j},m_{j},\mbox{PRI}_{j}
of the pulses.
In this chapter, we will assume that all
A_{j}
’s are
\equiv 1
.

The instantaneous frequency of the chirp
s_{n}
in (
4.1.2
) at time
t
is defined by
\theta+(2B/d)(t-n*\mbox{PRI})
.
Our approach is to assume (following
[
9
,
6
]
) that on a sufficiently small interval of the form
[t-\Delta,t+\Delta]
, this frequency is approximated by a constant frequency at the center of the interval.
We will then use specially constructed localized kernels to solve the problem for each of this interval as described in
[
41
,
6
]
.
A more elaborate subdivision is necessary when the instantaneous frequencies of different pulses cross each other.
We will demonstrate experimentally that our method is very stable, capable of working with a very small SNR if the sampling rate is sufficiently high.
Thus, the only limitation is the hardware capability, not a theoretical limitation.
We will analyze theoretically the connection between
\Delta
and the various parameters of the pulses and the accuracy.

The main contributions of this chapter are the following.

1.

This chapter is the first work of its kind that illustrates the use of SSO in the presence of a very high noise, crossover frequencies, and various components of the signal having different start and end points.

2.

In particular, we describe how the SSO algorithm needs to be modified to detect the start and stop points of the various signal components and crossover frequencies.

3.

We examine the theory behind the SSO to highlight the connection between the noise level, the sampling frequency, and the length of the snippet required for SSO to work properly.

4.

Our algorithms are fully automated, assuming only the minimal separation among the instantaneous frequencies. The minimal separation can be treated as a tunable parameter, but its choice can affect the performance of the algorithm (cf.
[
39
]
).

5.

Our algorithm works based on FFT, and hence, is very fast.

We will describe some related work in Section
4.2
.
The mathematical foundation behind our work is explained in Section
4.3
, including the intuition and definition of the Signal Separation Operator (SSO) introduced in
[
6
]
.
The algorithms and methodology to use SSO for the chirp separation problem are described in Section
4.4
with step-by-step illustrations on a few examples.
In particular, the examples demonstrate the connection between the sampling rate, noise level, minimal separation among the instantaneous frequencies.
A full report on the entire dataset which we worked on is presented in Section
4.5
.

4.2
Related works

There are several general ways to approach the problem of radar signal detection and separation.

In the case when the frequencies are constant, the problem is known as parameter estimation in exponential sums as we introduced in Chapter
2
.
If the radar pulses have sufficiently high SNR, then they can be detected using an energy-based envelope detector operating on the complex valued time-series data, known as In-phase and Quadrature (IQ) data
[
32
,
29
,
19
]
.
It is actually more common for modern solid state radars to transmit low probability of intercept (LPI) waveforms that utilize LFM pulses at low and negative SNR
[
64
,
46
]
; in this case, the envelope detector will no longer be suitable.
In the low power setting, there are two broad classes of methods, the first operates on the IQ data directly, while the second utilizes time-frequency transforms approaching detection and separation as a 2D signal processing problem.
In the time domain, there are analytic methods that operate on the IQ data directly, such as variations of independent components algorithm (ICA)
[
28
,
34
,
26
,
58
]
, with extensions to nonlinear chirps
[
48
,
66
]
, and incorporation of simple neural networks
[
47
]
.
A more common approach is to utilize time-frequency transforms and process the result as an image instead of operating on the IQ data directly
[
27
,
61
,
4
,
21
,
35
,
60
]
.

A general framework that includes both the problems of chirp separation and parameter estimation in exponential sums is the following.
One observes a signal of the form

f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t)\exp(i\phi_{j}(t)),\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,

(4.2.1)

where the instantaneous amplitudes (IA)
A_{j}
’s are complex valued, and
\phi_{j}
’s are differentiable functions of time.

The quantity
\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t)
is known as the instantaneous frequency (IF) of the component
f_{j}
.
The problem is to estimate the IFs and the IAs.
This is also a very important and old problem, starting with a paper by a Nobel Laureate, Gabor
[
20
]
.
One of the first approaches to solve the problem is the Hilbert-transform based empirical mode decomposition (EMD) by Huang
[
22
]
.

EMD.

The following introduction of the EMD method is provided in
[
6
]
. To accommodate multiple frequency components as in the constant parameter problem with
K>1
, Huang et al.
[
22
,
23
]
introduced a technique called
empirical mode decomposition
(EMD). This method decomposes a signal
G(t)
into a finite sum of
intrinsic mode functions
(IMFs), along with a slowly varying residual trend. Each IMF is then transformed into an AM–FM signal using the Hilbert transform, allowing for a time-frequency representation of
G(t)
.

In our setting, we consider the empirical mode decomposition of
G(t)
as follows:

G(t)=f(t)+T(t),

(4.2.2)

where
f(t)
denotes the sum of IMFs and
T(t)
is the residual term. More specifically, we write:

\left\{\begin{aligned} f(t)&=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t),\\
f_{j}(t)&=A_{j}(t)\cos\left(2\pi\phi_{j}(t)\right),\quad j=1,\dots,K,\end{aligned}\right.

(4.2.3)

where each
f_{j}(t)
represents an AM–FM component with instantaneous amplitude
A_{j}(t)
and instantaneous phase
\phi_{j}(t)
.

The EMD scheme relies on the presence of sufficiently many local extrema in the given real-valued signal
G(t)
. However, in practical settings where the trend
T(t)
is rapidly varying, it is common to encounter segments of the signal where local maxima or minima are sparse or entirely absent. To handle such cases, the standard EMD procedure must be refined. This leads to various improved EMD strategies proposed in
[
22
,
23
]
.

A typical modified EMD scheme proceeds as follows. Let the initial signal be denoted by
g_{1,0}:=G
. The algorithm constructs two cubic spline interpolants—one through the local maxima and the other through the local minima of
g_{1,0}
. These interpolants define the upper and lower envelopes, respectively. Their average is denoted by
m_{1,1}
, and the first intermediate signal is defined as

g_{1,1}:=g_{1,0}-m_{1,1}.

(4.2.4)

This procedure is repeated iteratively: for each
\ell\geq 1
,

g_{1,\ell}:=g_{1,\ell-1}-m_{1,\ell},

(4.2.5)

where
m_{1,\ell}
is the mean of the upper and lower envelopes of
g_{1,\ell-1}
. This iterative refinement is called the
sifting process
, and it terminates when the resulting function satisfies two empirical criteria: (i) the number of zero crossings and local extrema differ by at most one, and (ii) the envelopes are approximately symmetric with respect to the time axis.

The output of this procedure is the first intrinsic mode function (IMF), denoted
f_{1}
. The residue
g_{2,0}:=g_{1,0}-f_{1}
is then subjected to the same sifting process to extract
f_{2}
, and so on. Formally, the
j
-th IMF is computed from

g_{j,0}:=g_{1,0}-f_{1}-\cdots-f_{j-1},

(4.2.6)

followed by iterative sifting until convergence.

This iterative process yields a decomposition of the signal
G(t)
into
K
IMFs and a residual trend term
T_{K}(t)
, i.e.,

G(t)=f_{1}(t)+\cdots+f_{K}(t)+T_{K}(t),

(4.2.7)

where
T_{K}(t)
is a slowly varying function.

However, there is no mathematical theory behind this approach.
A mathematically rigorous approach, synchrosqueezing transform (SST), was given by Daubeschies and her collaborators
[
9
,
10
]
.
These seminal papers have given rise to a lot of variations and improvements.
We refer to
[
33
]
for more references on this topic.
In this chapter, we will use the algorithm given in
[
62
]
for comparing our method with SST.

SST.

To formalize the types of signals SST can handle, Daubechies et al.
[
9
]
define a class of functions called intrinsic mode type (IMT) components.

Definition 4.2.1
(Intrinsic Mode Type (IMT) Function)

A function

f(t)=A(t)e^{i\phi(t)}

(4.2.8)

is said to be an IMT function with accuracy
\epsilon>0
if:

•

A\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
, and
\phi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,

•

\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(t)>0
and
\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\phi^{\prime}(t)<\infty
,

•

\left|A^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq\epsilon\phi^{\prime}(t)
and
\left|\phi^{\prime\prime}(t)\right|\leq\epsilon\phi^{\prime}(t)
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 4.2.2
(Well-Separated Superposition of IMT Components)

A function
f(t)
is a superposition of
K
well-separated IMT components up to accuracy
\epsilon
and separation
d>0
if:

f(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}f_{k}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}A_{k}(t)e^{i\phi_{k}(t)},

(4.2.9)

where each
f_{k}
is an IMT function, and their respective instantaneous frequencies
\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)
satisfy

\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)>\phi_{k-1}^{\prime}(t),\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\left|\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)-\phi_{k-1}^{\prime}(t)\right|}{\phi_{k}^{\prime}(t)+\phi_{k-1}^{\prime}(t)}\geq d,\quad\forall t\in\mathbb{R}.

(4.2.10)

We denote the set of such functions by
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon,d}
.

The SST algorithm first computes the continuous wavelet transform
W_{f}(a,b)
of a signal
f\in\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon,d}
, and then sharpens the representation by reallocating the time-scale coefficients to the time-frequency plane using an estimated instantaneous frequency.

The central result justifying the method is the following:

Theorem 4.2.1
(Daubechies et al.
[
9
, Theorem 3.3]
)

Let
f\in\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon,d}
and define
\tilde{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{1/3}
. Let
\psi
be a Schwartz-class wavelet whose Fourier transform
\hat{\psi}
is supported in
[1-\Delta,1+\Delta]
, where
\Delta<\frac{d}{1+d}
.

Define the synchrosqueezed transform
S^{\delta}_{f,\tilde{\epsilon}}(b,\omega)
by

S^{\delta}_{f,\tilde{\epsilon}}(b,\omega):=\int_{A_{\tilde{\epsilon},f}(b)}W_{f}(a,b)\cdot\frac{1}{\delta}h\left(\frac{\omega-\omega_{f}(a,b)}{\delta}\right)a^{-3/2}\,da,

(4.2.11)

where
A_{\tilde{\epsilon},f}(b)=\{a>0:|W_{f}(a,b)|>\tilde{\epsilon}\}
, and
\omega_{f}(a,b)
is the instantaneous frequency estimate.

Then, for sufficiently small
\epsilon
, the following holds:

1.

|W_{f}(a,b)|>\tilde{\epsilon}
only when
(a,b)
satisfies
|a\phi_{k}^{\prime}(b)-1|<\Delta
.

2.

For such
(a,b)
, the estimated frequency satisfies
|\omega_{f}(a,b)-\phi_{k}^{\prime}(b)|\leq\tilde{\epsilon}
.

3.

For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
,

\left|\int_{|\omega-\phi_{k}^{\prime}(b)|<\tilde{\epsilon}}S^{\delta}_{f,\tilde{\epsilon}}(b,\omega)\,d\omega-A_{k}(b)e^{i\phi_{k}(b)}\right|\leq C\tilde{\epsilon},

(4.2.12)

for some constant
C
.

This theorem shows that SST achieves a sharp concentration in the time-frequency plane and allows for the accurate recovery of each component’s instantaneous frequency and amplitude. Consequently, SST serves as a rigorous and adaptive alternative to empirical mode decomposition (EMD), especially for signals composed of well-separated AM-FM components.

In
[
6
]
, we have applied the technique based on localized trigonometric kernels to define a signal separation operator (SSO) to solve the constant parameter problem under conditions weaker than those in
[
9
]
.
Our conditions do assume that all the components are present in the interval
[0,T]
; i.e., the number of signals
f_{j}
is the same throughout the interval, and there is no discontinuity in any of these.
Our method finds the IFs by looking at the peaks of a power spectrum. The IAs are estimated by a simple substitution compared to the limiting process required in SST.
Finally, our method can be implemented very fast using FFT.

The paper
[
33
]
deals specifically with the separation of chirplets, based on a modification of a filter described in
[
6
]
.
It is not clear when the various conditions in this chapter are satisfied.
Also, the method requires a three dimensional search.
In this chapter, we will demonstrate how the methods developed in
[
6
]
can be adapted for the chirp separation problem, including the case of crossover frequencies.

4.3.1
Reduction to the constant parameter case

In this section, we describe certain conditions under which the data of the form (
4.2.1
) can be reduced to the constant parameter data as in Chapter
2
.
Thus, we are interested in finding the instantaneous frequencies
\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})
and amplitudes
A_{j}(t^{*})
in the signal

f(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t)\exp(i\phi_{j}(t)),

(4.3.1)

for some point
t^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
A_{j}
’s are continuous and
\phi_{j}
’s are continuously differentiable functions on an interval of the form
[t^{*}-\Delta,t^{*}+\Delta]
.
We introduce the notation (abusing the notation introduced in (
2.3.1
))

\mathbf{M}(t^{*})=\sum_{j=1}^{K}|A_{j}(t^{*})|,\qquad B(t^{*})=\max_{1\leq j\leq K}|\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})|.

(4.3.2)

The following theorem is a reformulation of
[
6
, Theorem 4.2]
.
We will reproduce the proof for the sake of completeness.

Note that our Theorem
4.3.1
does not require the conditions in Definition
4.2.1
and Definition
4.2.2
, unlike the SST method.

Theorem 4.3.1

Let
t^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\alpha,\Delta>0
, and for
|u|\leq\Delta
,

|A_{j}(t^{*}+u)-A_{j}(t^{*})|\leq\alpha|u||A_{j}(t^{*})|,\qquad|\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*}+u)-\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})|\leq\alpha|u||\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})|.

(4.3.3)

Then we have

\left|f(t^{*}+u)-\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t^{*})\exp\left(i\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})u\right)\right|\leq\alpha\mathbf{M}(t^{*})(B(t^{*})\Delta+1)\Delta,\qquad|u|\leq|\Delta|.

(4.3.4)

Proof.
In this proof, we write
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}(t^{*})
and
B=B(t^{*})
. Let
|u|\leq\Delta
.
We observe that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t^{*}+u)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u))-\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t^{*})\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}))\exp(iu\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*}))\right|

(4.3.5)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(A_{j}(t^{*}+u)-A_{j}(t^{*})\right)\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u))\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t^{*})\left(\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u))-\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}))\exp(iu\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*}))\right)\right|

In view of the fact that

|e^{ix}-e^{iy}|=2|\sin((x-y)/2)|\leq|x-y|,

(4.3.6)

and (
4.3.3
), we deduce that
for each
k
,

\displaystyle\left|\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u))\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}))\exp(iu\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*}))\right|\leq\left|\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u)-\phi_{j}(t^{*})-u\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})\right|

(4.3.7)

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{0}^{u}\left(\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*}+v)-\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})\right)dv\right|\leq\alpha B\left|\int_{0}^{u}|v|dv\right|\leq\alpha B|u|^{2}\leq\alpha B\Delta^{2}.

Using (
4.3.3
) again, we see that

\left|\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left(A_{j}(t^{*}+u)-A_{j}(t^{*})\right)\exp(i\phi_{j}(t^{*}+u))\right|\leq\mathbf{M}\Delta.

(4.3.8)

Together with (
4.3.7
) and (
4.3.5
), this leads to (
4.3.4
).

\blacksquare

4.3.2
Signal separation operator

In this section, we fix
t^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
The quantities denoted below by
\alpha
,
\Delta
,
R
are independent of
t^{*}
, and so are the constants involved in
\lesssim
,
\gtrsim
, and
\sim
, but the other quantities will depend upon
t^{*}
without its mention in the notation.

Let
R>0
denote the sampling frequency. We write

\lambda_{j}=\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})/R,\quad n=\lfloor R\Delta\rfloor,\quad\mu=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t^{*})\delta_{\lambda_{j}},\quad\hat{\mu}(\ell)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t^{*})\exp(-i\ell\lambda_{j}).

(4.3.9)

Then using Theorem
4.3.1
, it is easy to deduce that (cf. (
2.3.2
))

\left|\hbar_{n}\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)f(t^{*}-\ell/R)e^{i\ell x}-\sigma_{n}(\hat{\mu})(x)\right|\leq\alpha\mathbf{M}(t^{*})(B(t^{*})\Delta+1)\Delta.

(4.3.10)

This leads to the following definition (cf.
[
6
, Definition 2.3]
)

Definition 4.3.1

Let
F:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
,
t^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
R>0
.
We define the
Signal Separation Operator
SSO by

\mathcal{T}_{n,R}(F)(t^{*};x)=\hbar_{n}\sum_{|\ell|<n}H\left(\frac{|\ell|}{n}\right)f(t-\ell/R)e^{i\ell x},\qquad x\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(4.3.11)

We will use the operator to separate the components
f_{j}(t^{*})
and the instantaneous frequencies
\phi_{j}^{\prime}(t^{*})
based on the noisy observations

F(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}f_{j}(t)+\epsilon(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{K}A_{j}(t)\exp(i\phi_{j}(t))+\epsilon(t),

(4.3.12)

where for each
t
,
\epsilon(t)
is a realization of a complex sub-Gaussian random variable in
\mathcal{G}(V)
.
Theorem
2.3.1
can be translated into Theorem
4.3.2
below, where we abuse the notation to make the comparison easier.
We note that Theorem
4.3.2
itself is a restatement of
[
6
, Theorems 2.4,4.1]
in a somewhat modified form taking the noise into account.
In order to state this theorem, we write (in addition to (
4.3.2
))

\ \mathfrak{m}(t^{*})=\min_{1\leq\ell\leq K}|f_{\ell}(t^{*})|,\ \ \eta(t^{*})=\min_{\ell\not=k}|\phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(t^{*})-\phi_{k}(t^{*})|,\ \eta^{*}=\eta(t^{*})/R.

(4.3.13)

For brevity, we will omit the mention of
t^{*}
from our notation, unless we feel that this might cause some confusion.

Theorem 4.3.2

Let
0<\delta<1
,
t^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
R,\Delta>0
, and the assumptions of Theorem
4.3.1
be satisfied.
We assume that
n=\lfloor R\Delta\rfloor
satisfies the condition (
2.5.3
), and in addition,
n\geq 4C/\eta^{*}
.
We assume further that

\alpha\mathbf{M}(B\Delta+1)\Delta\leq\mathfrak{m}/4.

(4.3.14)

We define

\mathbb{G}(t^{*})=\{x\in\mathbb{T}:|\mathcal{T}_{n,R}(F)(t^{*};x)|\geq 3\mathfrak{m}/4\}.

(4.3.15)

Then
each of the following statements holds with probability exceeding
1-\delta
.

•

(
Disjoint union condition
)

The set
\mathbb{G}(t^{*})
is a disjoint union of exactly
K
subsets
\mathbb{G}_{\ell}(t^{*})
,

•

(
Diameter condition
)

For each
\ell=1,\cdots,K
,
\mathsf{diam}(\mathbb{G}_{\ell}(t^{*}))\leq 2C/n
,

•

(
Separation
)

\mathsf{dist}(\mathbb{G}_{\ell}(t^{*}),\mathbb{G}_{j}(t^{*}))\geq\eta/2
for
\ell\neq k
,

•

(
Interval inclusion
)

For each
\ell=1,\cdots,K
,
I_{\ell}=\{x\in\mathbb{T}:|x-\lambda_{\ell}|\leq 1/(4n)\}\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{\ell}(t^{*})
.

Moreover, if

\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}=\arg\max_{x\in\mathbb{G}_{\ell}(t^{*})}|\mathcal{T}_{n,R}(F)(t^{*};x)|,\quad\widehat{\phi_{\ell}^{\prime}(t^{*})}=R\hat{\lambda}(\ell),

(4.3.16)

then

|\hat{\lambda}_{\ell}-\lambda_{\ell}|\leq 2C/n.

(4.3.17)

Remark 4.3.1

For the convenience of the reader, we summarize the conditions on the sampling frequency
R
below.
We assume that for every
t^{*}
of interest,

R\Delta\geq\max\left(C_{1},4C/\eta^{*}\right)

(4.3.18)

and
R
is large enough so that with probability
>1-\delta
,

|E_{n}(x)|\leq C_{2}V\sqrt{\frac{(\log(C_{2}R\Delta)/\delta)}{R\Delta}}

(4.3.19)

We note that the condition (
4.3.18
) puts a lower bound on
\Delta
, while the condition (
4.3.14
) is an upper bound on the Lipschitz constant
\alpha
.
In particular, the choice of
\Delta
is a theoretically delicate one.
In our experiments, this was done by experimentation.

\blacksquare

Remark 4.3.2

In the case of a chirp

\phi^{\prime}(t)=\begin{cases}\omega+\frac{B}{d}(t-\gamma),&\mbox{ if $t_{0}\leq t\leq t_{0}+d$}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(4.3.20)

we have for
t^{*}\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]

|\phi^{\prime}(t^{*}+u)-\phi^{\prime}(t^{*})|=\left|\frac{B}{d}\right||u|,\qquad|\phi^{\prime}(t^{*})|=\left|\omega+\frac{B}{d}(t^{*}-\gamma)\right|

(4.3.21)

So, we may take

\alpha=\frac{|B/d|}{\min_{t\in[t_{0},t_{0}+d]}|\omega+(B/d)(t-\gamma)|}.

(4.3.22)

\blacksquare

4.4
Algorithms

In this section, we describe how the SSO can be used for signals of the form (
4.1.4
).
The basic idea behind our algorithms is to apply Theorem
4.3.2
to different snippets of the signal.
Thus, we choose a small
\Delta
and divide the signal duration into
D
overlapping time intervals
I_{k}=[t_{k}-\Delta,t_{k}+\Delta]
,
k=1,\cdots,D
.
The
t_{k}
’s are chosen such that
I_{k}\cap I_{k+1}\neq\emptyset
. This is to ensure that all the sampling data are considered in this experiment.
Theorem
4.3.2
gives guidelines on how to select
\Delta
.
In practice, one has to treat this as a tunable parameter.
In our experiments reported here, we chose
\Delta
by experimentation so that it is large enough to eliminate the noise effect, but small enough to give an accurate estimation.
A large value of
\Delta
implies that there are more data points to average out the noise, but this has higher chance that the signal will start or end within the interval which leads to inaccurate estimation.

The signal restricted to
I_{k}
has the form (cf. (
4.1.4
)):

F_{k}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{J_{k}}f_{j,k}(t)+\epsilon(t),\qquad f_{j,k}(t)=A_{j,k}(t)\exp(i\phi_{j,k}(t)),

(4.4.1)

where
\epsilon(t)
represents the noise and

\phi_{j,k}(t)=\omega_{j,k}(t-\gamma_{j,k})+a_{j,k}(t-\gamma_{j,k})^{2}/2.

(4.4.2)

The SSO will be used with each
F_{k}
to obtain the parameter values for each
f_{j,k}
.
In our experiments, we have focused on IFs, assuming all the amplitudes to be
1
.
Taken together, the set
(t_{k},\widehat{\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t_{k})})
is called the
SSO diagram
.
Once we determine the IFs, the amplitudes can be determined by simply substituting the estimated IFs in SSO and more accurately by solving an appropriate least square problem.
We postpone a more detailed investigation of this question to a future work.

Our methodology requires a prior knowledge of

1.

a minimum separation
\eta
among all the instantaneous frequencies

2.

the receiver bandwidth

B_{\mbox{\scriptsize{rec}}}\geq\max_{j,k,t}\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t)-\min_{j,k,t}\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t).

(4.4.3)

Out of these,
B_{\mbox{\scriptsize{rec}}}
is a system parameter and is always known a priori.
The condition implies that all the signals are fully captured in the measurements.

The minimal separation
\eta
can be found experimentally as well, and its choice will affect the performance of our algorithms.
We have demonstrated this in detail in Chapter
2
.

The clusters defined in Theorem
4.3.2
are found, using a clustering algorithm. For example, in this chapter we used DBSCAN
[
16
]
.
The notation we use in our algorithm is

([\mbox{scatter data}],[\mbox{label}])\leftarrow\mbox{DBSCAN}([\mbox{scatter data}],[\mbox{neighborhood radius}],[\mbox{minimum number of neighbors}]).

(4.4.4)

Here,
[\mbox{scatter data}]
is a data matrix, and the algorithm attaches a label
1
for the cluster of interest (and
-1
otherwise), based on the number of points neighboring the so called core points in a ball of radius denoted above by
[\mbox{neighborhood radius}]
.

4.1
.

4.5
Numerical experiments

4.5.1
Numerical results

To assess the robustness and performance of the proposed Signal Separation Operator (SSO) method, we conducted a series of controlled numerical experiments across a variety of scenarios. These experiments were designed to evaluate the impact of key signal characteristics, including minimal frequency separation, SNR, sampling rate, and the presence of frequency crossovers.

The details for all the experiments are listed in Tables
4.3
and
4.4
.

Minimal separation

The algorithm demonstrates strong performance when signal components are sufficiently separated in frequency. As illustrated in Figure
4.10
, the method accurately recovers the instantaneous frequencies and associated parameters in both non-intersecting and cleanly intersecting cases. However, performance degrades in scenarios where the minimal separation between components becomes too small. In such cases, the spectral peaks associated with different components may become indistinct, leading to inaccurate clustering and parameter estimation. This limitation is primarily due to the inherent resolution bounds imposed by the kernel size and the sampling interval.

Figure 4.10:
Ground truth vs regression result plots for example 1 (top left), 2 (top right), 3 (bottom left), and 4 (bottom right) with SNR -10 dB and sampling rate 0.5 GHz. Our algorithm works well in case of no crossover and clear crossover signals, but does not work so well when the minimum separation among signals is low.

Robustness to noise

The method remains effective even under high noise conditions.
To illustrate, we compute the the root mean square error (RMSE) in each experiment by

\mbox{RMSE}=\mbox{mean}\left(\sqrt{\frac{1}{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\sum_{j=1}^{J_{k}}\left(\frac{\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t_{k})-\widehat{\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t_{k})}}{\phi_{j,k}^{\prime}(t_{k})}\right)^{2}}\right).

(4.5.1)

In our experiments, we took the mean over 16 trials for each choice of the signal, the sampling rate, and SNR.
As shown in Figure
4.11
, the root mean square error (RMSE) remains low for SNR levels ranging from
10
dB to
-30
dB.
The algorithm maintains RMSE values within acceptable bounds, provided that the sampling rate is sufficient.
The combination of localized kernel averaging and peak detection in the frequency domain allows the method to suppress noise effectively.

Figure 4.11:
The sampling rate performance plots of vary SNR vs RMSE for example 1 (top left), 2 (top right), 3 (bottom left), and 4 (bottom right). The RMSE was comparing between the ground truth and the estimation parameters of
\phi(t)
in (
4.1.1
).

Sampling Rate

Sampling rate plays a critical role in frequency resolution and noise resilience. As shown in Figure
4.12
, increasing the sampling rate from 0.5 GHz to 25 GHz significantly improves the accuracy of instantaneous frequency estimation, particularly in cases with minimal separation or closely spaced crossovers. In this figure, a heat map is used to visualize the estimation error computed in equation
(
4.4.6
)
, where the color indicates the magnitude of error. This color-coded representation helps to clearly highlight the improvement in performance with higher sampling rates. Higher sampling rates yield a finer time grid, which enhances the operator’s ability to resolve subtle frequency variations and distinguish between overlapping components.

Figure 4.12:
Heat map of the errors for example 1, 2, 3, and 4 with SNR -10 dB at sampling rate 0.5 GHz (left) vs sampling rate 25 GHz (right). Ones may notice that the higher sampling rate examples can deal with the smaller minimal separation among signals better.

We recall that the most often used method for finding IF’s is EMD, which is purely heuristic.
The method known as synchrosqueezing transform method (SST) is based on a solid mathematical foundation, and works better than EMD.
In this section we compare our results with those obtained by using SST.
As mentioned earlier, there are many versions of SST.

4.6
Conclusions

Separation of a superposition of blind source signals is an important problem in audio and radar signal processing with many applications, including electronic warfare and electronic intelligence.
In this chapter, we have modified a method proposed in
[
6
]
for solving this problem in the case when the instantaneous frequencies of the components are linear.
The method is based on a filtered FFT, and hence, is very fast.
While the paper
[
6
]
assumes the frequencies to be slowly and continuously varying, our modification includes the case when the signals are discontinuous and when there are crossover frequencies present.
We analyze theoretically the behavior of SSO in the presence of noise, giving insight into the relationship between the sampling frequency, desired accuracy, and noise.
We have illustrated the effectiveness our method with a few well chosen examples, and tested it on a small database of 7 signals, demonstrating a consistent behavior.
Our method outperforms the SST algorithm given in
[
62
]
.

Chapter 5
Conclusion and Future Work

This dissertation introduced two robust, tractable, and theoretically grounded methods for the decomposition and analysis of structured signals in both stationary and nonstationary settings. The central theme across both contributions is the use of localized trigonometric polynomial kernels to recover signal parameters efficiently and accurately under noisy condition.

5.1
Summary of results

This dissertation presents two algorithmic frameworks designed for signal recovery in distinct but related contexts: parameter estimation in multidimensional exponential sums, and separation of linear chirp components from short-time signal observations. Both approaches are grounded in localized trigonometric polynomial kernels and rely on efficient FFT-based computations.

In Chapter
3
, we developed a multidimensional exponential analysis framework for estimating frequencies and amplitudes from a finite sum of complex exponentials sampled on a grid. Our method uses localized kernel reconstructions to directly estimate parameters while bypassing traditional subspace or eigenstructure methods. We established theoretical guarantees under sub-Gaussian noise assumptions and demonstrated the algorithm’s superiority over classical methods like MUSIC and ESPRIT, particularly in high-dimensional and low-SNR regimes.

In Chapter
4
, we extended the methodology based on localized kernels to nonstationary signals modeled as linear chirps. The proposed Signal Separation Operator (SSO) extracts instantaneous frequencies from short-time signal segments by filtering in the frequency domain. The method is automatic, noise-robust, and well-suited for handling crossover signals. Through synthetic radar scenarios, we demonstrated its accuracy in recovering complex chirp trajectories without prior information about the number of components.

Together, these techniques provide a unified and scalable framework for signal recovery in structured and noisy environments.

5.2
Future research directions

Several promising directions emerge from this work:

•

Generalization to Nonlinear Chirps:
Extending the SSO method to handle higher-order chirps could broaden its application to more realistic radar and communication signal models.

•

Adaptive Kernel Design:
Future work could explore adaptive or data-driven kernel designs that optimize localization properties for specific signal models or noise conditions. For example, wider kernels may be beneficial in high-noise regimes to suppress spurious peaks, whereas narrower kernels could improve resolution in closely spaced frequency clusters. Designing such adaptive filters in a principled way could enhance both the robustness and resolution of the parameter recovery process.

•

Learning-Based Classification and Prediction:
By converting recovered signal parameters into feature representations, these methods can be integrated into pipelines for supervised classification or prediction tasks.

•

Application to Other Domains:
The underlying mathematical ideas may be applied to problems in biomedical signal processing, wireless sensor networks, and geophysical exploration, where sparse and structured signal recovery is essential.

By advancing tractable and robust techniques grounded in harmonic analysis, this dissertation contributes new tools to the broader effort of reliable signal inference in the presence of uncertainty and complexity.
