Liposomal bupivacaine: a delay rather than elimination of rebound pain in mouse postoperative model
Authors: Hao Wu, Jiayu Jin, Di Zhou, Lihai Chen, Liu Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiposomal bupivacaine (LB) provides prolonged analgesia, but it remains unclear whether it prevents rebound pain or merely delays its onset. This study investigated the effect of LB on rebound pain in a mouse model of peripheral nerve block to determine whether its resolution results in a pain response exceeding that of unblocked controls.MethodsMethylene blue staining was employed to compare the success rate of nerve stimulator-guided versus anatomical landmark-based techniques for combined sciatic and femoral nerve blockade.Postoperative rebound pain following liposomal bupivacaine administration under nerve stimulator guidance was assessed at multiple time points using von Frey and Hargreaves tests. Histological analyses of perineural inflammation and Wallerian degeneration were conducted on postoperative days 2 and 28.ResultsNerve stimulator-guided blocks had high success rates (sciatic: 93.3%, P=0.035; femoral: 73.3%, P=0.030).LB extended thermal (24h, P<0.05) and mechanical (36h, P<0.05) analgesia. Transient thermal rebound pain occurred at 48h (P=0.003), but no mechanical rebound was observed. LB group showed significantly more severe Wallerian degeneration and Neural inflammation in both the sciatic and femoral nerves compared to the S group at day 2 (P<0.05).ConclusionsLiposomal bupivacaine did not prevent rebound pain but merely delayed its onset in a mouse model of combined sciatic and femoral nerve block.

Introduction

Rebound pain is a common postoperative phenomenon characterized by a sudden intensity increase following the resolution of peripheral nerve block (PNB) [1]. Epidemiological data suggest the overall incidence of rebound pain after PNB remission was found to be as high as 61.7% in Ethiopia [2].It undermines nerve block efficacy, leading to higher opioid consumption and significantly impeding recovery [3].Liposomal bupivacaine (LB) provides analgesia for up to 72 h and may potentially prevent rebound pain [4].However, existing studies have focused mainly on its analgesic duration rather than a critical question: whether prolonged analgesia truly prevents rebound pain.

The pathogenesis of rebound pain, frequently linked to neuroinflammatory responses and neurotoxicity following the abrupt cessation of neural blockade, suggests that its prevention may require mechanisms beyond prolonged analgesia [5]. While LB extends the analgesic window, it might only delay this neuroinflammatory process rather than abolish it. The sustained release of bupivacaine could potentially induce localized neurotoxicity or alter neuronal recovery patterns, predisposing patients to a delayed yet heightened pain state [6]. Previous animal studies on LB’s effects on neuroinflammation have yielded conflicting results [7], leaving its modulatory role in rebound pain mechanisms uncertain.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether liposomal bupivacaine prevents rebound pain or merely delays its onset in a mouse model of combined sciatic and femoral nerve block. Utilizing a nerve stimulator-guided technique to ensure precise and consistent block administration, we evaluated pain behaviors over 120 h to comprehensively capture the resolution of analgesia and any subsequent rebound pain. We specifically tested the hypothesis that the resolution of LB blockade would be associated with a pain response exceeding that in unblocked controls. Our results provide critical insights into the true nature of recovery following LB-mediated analgesia and its implications for clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Animals

All experiments described here were conducted in strict accordance with institutional guidelines. Male C57BL/6J mice (weighing 25–30 g; aged 8 weeks; Wandong Biotechnology Corporation of Nanjing; n = 67) were housed in the same room at a constant temperature (22 ± 2 °C) and humidity (55 ± 5%) and maintained on a 12:12 h light-dark cycle with free access to water and food.

Experimental groups and study design

This study consisted of three independent experiments designed to evaluate nerve block techniques, pain behaviors, and histological outcomes. Experiment 1: Comparative Evaluation of Nerve Block Techniques. A total of 30 sciatic and femoral nerves from 15 mice were randomly assigned to two groups: (1) nerve stimulator-guided technique (Group N, n = 15) and (2) anatomical landmark-based technique (Group A, n = 15). This experiment was designed to compare the success rates of sciatic and femoral nerve blocks between the two techniques. Experiment 2: Assessment of Postoperative Pain Behaviors.24 mice were randomly allocated into three groups for behavioral analysis: (1) sham group (n = 8), (2) S group(n = 8), and (3) liposomal bupivacaine group (LB group, n = 8). Experiment 3: Histological and Inflammatory Analyses. 28 mice were assigned to the S group and the LB group for histological and inflammatory analyses at early (day 2, n = 7) and late (day 28, n = 7) time points to assess postoperative neural damage and neuroinflammation.

Animal model of tibial fracture

The Tibial Fracture model was established using the method described by Khajuria et al. [8]. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and fixed in a right lateral recumbent position. and open mid-diaphyseal tibial fractures were induced in the left hindlimb as previously described. Postoperatively, the LB group was administered 35 mg/kg 1.3% liposomal bupivacaine solution in the vicinity of the sciatic and femoral nerves [9], guided by a nerve stimulator. Concurrently, the S group only received a tibial fracture surgery. After sealing the injection hole and closing the wound, mice were resuscitated on a heated blanket.

Combined sciatic and femoral nerve block under nerve stimulator guidance

A combined sciatic and femoral nerve block was executed under nerve stimulator guidance [9, 10]. For the sciatic nerve block, the anesthetized mouse was positioned in right lateral recumbency, with the left hind limb and inguinal region prepared by shaving and cleansing, aligning the limb perpendicular to the torso. The puncture site was identified in the posterior lateral thigh muscle, and a 22G, 5 cm needle (B. Braun Melsungen AG, model 4894146 N) was utilized. The needle was attached to a nerve stimulator (Stimuplex HNS12, B. Braun Melsungen AG, Germany), and the tip was directed towards the posterior mid-femur. The initial nerve stimulator current is set at 0.2 mA, frequency at 2 Hz, aiming for ankle contraction or extension in mice. The needle position is adjusted until, at 0.08–0.12 mA current, ankle movement occurs. After confirming no blood return,35 mg/kg 1.3% liposomal bupivacaine is injected, causing ankle movement loss. If, upon resetting the current to 0.2 mA, the response doesn’t recover, the sciatic nerve block is successful. For the femoral nerve block, the mouse was repositioned to supine, with the puncture site chosen at the mid-to-lower inguinal region. The needle was inserted at a 30° angle to the skin, toward the midpoint of the inguinal region. The initial current was set at 0.2 mA. The target motor responses in mice were quadriceps contraction and knee extension. The needle position was adjusted until these responses occurred at 0.08 ~ 0.12 mA. After confirming no blood return, 35 mg/kg 1.3% liposomal bupivacaine was injected, causing knee movement loss. If, upon resetting the current to 0.2 mA, the response didn’t recover, the nerve block was successful.

Sciatic nerve and femoral nerve block under anatomical landmark location

A combined sciatic and femoral nerve block was executed under anatomical landmark location. For the sciatic nerve block, the anesthetized mouse was positioned in right lateral recumbency, with the left hind limb and inguinal region prepared by shaving and cleansing, aligning the limb perpendicular to the torso. The puncture site was identified in the posterior lateral thigh muscle, and a 29G needle was utilized. The needle was introduced posteromedially towards the greater trochanter in an anteromedial direction. After encountering the ischial tuberosity, 35 mg/kg 1.3% liposomal bupivacaine was injected [11]. For the femoral nerve block, referring to the method of femoral nerve block guided by nerve stimulator, the mice were placed in supine position, and the puncture needle was inserted into the middle third of the groin at 30° from the skin. The depth of the puncture needle was 5 mm, and 35 mg/kg 1.3% liposomal bupivacaine was injected.

Staining study

To assess the success rate of nerve blocks,15 mice were injected around both the femoral and sciatic nerves on each side. One side was randomly selected for injection using the anatomical landmark guidance method(Group A) [9], while the other side used the nerve-stimulator guidance method (Group N). A volume of 0.085 mL of 0.1% methylene blue solution was injected around each nerve [12].

The anatomical landmark-guided approach for sciatic and femoral nerve block is as follows: Sciatic Nerve Block: Referring to Liljana’s research [9], the mouse is fixed in a lateral decubitus position, and the middle and posterior-lateral muscle area of the thigh is chosen as the puncture site. A 29-gauge needle is directed posterior to the midpoint of the femur. After the puncture needle encounters the femur and no blood is aspirated, 0.085 mL of 0.1% methylene blue is injected. Femoral Nerve Block procedure: Referring to the anatomical structure of the femoral nerve and the muscle contraction response under neurostimulation guidance, targeted improvements are made. Except for not using a neurostimulator, the rest of the operation is the same as above, with the puncture needle depth set to 5 mm, and 0.085 mL of 0.1% methylene blue is injected.

This procedure led to a total of 30 methylene blue stainings for the sciatic nerves and 30 methylene blue stainings for the femoral nerves across the left and right hind limbs. One hour after injection, the mice were euthanized under isoflurane anesthesia and dissected to assess whether the nerve staining was successful and to determine the success rates of sciatic and femoral nerve blocks. This assessment was utilized to determine the efficacy of nerve stimulation-guided blocks of the sciatic and femoral nerves in mice. The presence of staining on the nerves indicated a successful block, while the absence of staining suggested a failed block [12].

Definition of Rebound Pain.

The phenomenon of rebound pain was defined as “a common, yet under-recognised acute increase in pain severity after a peripheral nerve block (PNB) has receded, typically manifesting within 24 h after the block was performed [13].For the purpose of this study, rebound pain was pre-defined as a statistically significant exacerbation (P < 0.05) in pain hypersensitivity (measured by mechanical or thermal withdrawal threshold) in the LB group, compared to the S group, at any pre-specified time point within the expected block resolution window (e.g., 24–48 hours post-operation).

Nociceptive behavioral tests

Two tests were used to assess pain behavior: (i) Mechanical nociception assessed by the withdrawal response to von Frey filament application, (ii) Thermal nociception assessed by the withdrawal response to thermal stimulus.

Mechanical nociception

Unrestricted mice were positioned under a clear plastic chamber that was elevated on a mesh floor, allowing them to acclimate to the environment. Mechanical withdrawal responses were assessed using a series of calibrated von Frey filaments (ranging from 0.04 to 4 g) [14, 15]. These filaments were applied from beneath the cage, through the mesh floor openings, to the plantar surface of the hind paw, specifically targeting the middle area of the left leg. The filament was pressed until it produced a slight bend and was then held in this position for 6 s. Beginning with a filament force of 0.6 g, we determine the 50% paw withdrawal threshold utilizing Dixon’s up-down method (with cutoff thresholds of 4 g [maximum] and 0.04 g [minimum]).

Thermal nociception

Thermal withdrawal latencies were evaluated utilizing the Hargreaves method. Animals were placed in a plastic chamber on an elevated glass platform. A radiant heat source (SH-1000 Plantar Hot Plate Tester, Shanghai Vax Biotech Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) was positioned beneath the glass to direct heat towards the plantar surface of the paw. The intensity of the radiant heat was calibrated to achieve an average withdrawal latency of approximately 10 s for the paws of naive animals. Each paw was subjected to heat stimulation three times with a minimum interval of 3 min between applications, and the average withdrawal latency was calculated by averaging these values (with a cutoff time of 15 s) [16, 17].

Histological Evaluation(HE)

Twenty-eight mice were divided into the liposomal bupivacaine and surgical groups, and 7 mice in each group were selected for detection on day 2 and day 28, respectively. These mice were anesthetized as described above, followed by gentle exposure and removal of approximately 8–10 millimeters of the sciatic and femoral nerves. This time interval was chosen correspond to short-term and long-term nerve tissue damage. The specimens were fixed in 4 formalin for 48 h, washed with ethanol, and embedded in paraffin.

A pathologist, blinded to the experimental treatment, analyzed the slides using standardized scales for perineural inflammation. As shown in Fig. 1, edema and lymphocyte infiltration are characteristic features of inflammation. Euthanasia

This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nanjing Medical University (Approval No. DWSY-23062366). All experimental procedures were performed in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the institutional ethical guidelines. Animals were euthanized via a continuous 5% isoflurane exposure until 1 min after the breath stop. Then gastrointestinal tissue was obtained in these tests.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 via a repeated-measures one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post-tests for evaluating the effects of liposomal bupivacaine (LB) on PWL and 50% paw withdrawal threshold. The dependent variable for these analyses was time in hours. Chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact probability method were used to examine the success rates of sciatic nerve and femoral nerve blocks between nerve stimulator guidance and anatomical landmark localization methods. For ordinal categorical variables (such as histological inflammation and Wallerian degeneration scores), comparisons between groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test due to violation of the chi-square test assumptions (expected cell counts < 5). The statistical significance was determined as P < 0.05. All data were presented as means ± standard error of mean (SEM).

Discussion

Our in vivo findings demonstrate that in a mouse model, nerve stimulator-guided administration of liposomal bupivacaine (LB) via a combined sciatic and femoral nerve block extended the duration of thermal and mechanical analgesia beyond 24 h. However, upon the resolution of this blockade, a transient thermal rebound pain was observed at the 48-hour time point. To our knowledge, the temporal association between prolonged LB analgesia and the subsequent emergence of rebound pain has not been extensively characterized in preclinical settings. The underlying mechanisms may involve LB-induced neuroinflammation and transient neural injury.

The analgesic duration of LB in animal models has been variable in prior reports, often lasting only a few hours when administered via traditional landmark techniques [9, 20]. In the present study, the use of nerve stimulator guidance likely improved the precision of perineural injection [21], and the combined nerve block approach may have contributed to the observed extension of sensory blockade. This methodological refinement provided a stable model to investigate the post-block analgesic course of LB.

The phenomenon of rebound pain following peripheral nerve block resolution, sometimes described as a “burning” sensation in clinical reports [22, 23], has also been noted in preclinical models using conventional local anesthetics [24]. Our observation of transient thermal rebound pain at 48 h after LB resolution is consistent with this clinical neurobehavioral phenomenon, suggesting it may occur even after extended-duration analgesia.

A pivotal finding of our study is the selective manifestation of rebound hyperalgesia to thermal, but not mechanical, stimuli at the 48-hour time point. This dissociation suggests that the underlying mechanisms are modality-specific, rather than representing a generalized state of hyperexcitability. We postulate that this selectivity can be explained by the differential involvement and sensitization of primary afferent fibers. Thermal pain perception, particularly heat hyperalgesia, is predominantly mediated by unmyelinated C-fibers [25]. The prolonged blockade of these fibers by the local anesthetic may induce a compensatory upregulation of key transducers and receptors, such as the Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channel, upon block resolution [26, 27]. This ‘molecular rebound’ could render C-fibers hyper-responsive to heat stimuli. In contrast, mechanical pain involves a more complex interplay of fibers, including thinly myelinated Aδ-mechanonociceptors and low-threshold mechanoreceptors that may undergo different forms of plasticity [28, 29]. The post-block inflammatory milieu appears to bias signaling towards thermal pathways, as inflammatory mediators (e.g., NGF, PGE2) are potent sensitizers of TRPV1 on C-fibers, dramatically lowering their activation threshold to heat [26]. While some of these mediators also contribute to mechanical sensitization, the latter is more dependent on processes like central sensitization and alterations in inhibitory controls in the spinal dorsal horn, which may not have been sufficiently engaged or may have been countered by intact compensatory mechanisms within our observed timeframe.

Our histological assessments revealed early-stage neuroinflammation and axonal degeneration in the LB group, a finding temporally correlated with the behavioral manifestation of rebound pain. Local anesthetics, including bupivacaine, have documented neurotoxic potential [30, 31]. We speculate that LB may initiate a cascade involving initial axonal injury, followed by the release of proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) [32, 33]. These factors could subsequently sensitize peripheral nociceptors and potentially contribute to central sensitization [34], ultimately promoting a state of rebound pain.

Notwithstanding the coherent narrative outlined above, we fully acknowledge that the causal relationship between neuroinflammation and rebound pain remains to be definitively established. The present study primarily provides correlative evidence. Future work must include direct mechanistic validation. This would entail: (a) Quantitative profiling of key inflammatory mediators (e.g., TNF-α, IL-1β, PGE2) in the DRG and spinal cord via ELISA or multiplex assays to objectively correlate their levels with pain behavior. (b) Detailed immunohistochemical quantification of microglial (Iba1) and macrophage (e.g., CD68) activation states to spatially and temporally map the neuroimmune response. Most importantly, (c) functional interventional studies will be crucial. The administration of specific inhibitors—such as minocycline to suppress microglial activation or antagonists targeting TNF-α or PGE2 receptors—prior to the anticipated onset of rebound pain, would provide the most compelling evidence. A significant attenuation of thermal hyperalgesia following such interventions would powerfully demonstrate that neuroinflammation is a driving cause, rather than a passive consequence, of rebound pain.

Furthermore, it is important to note that the von Frey and thermal pain tests applied to the hindpaw primarily evaluate the functional status of the sciatic nerve blockade. While the involvement of the femoral nerve was confirmed histologically, the lack of a standardized, reliable sensory test for the femoral nerve territory (e.g., the medial knee) in mice represents a limitation, and our behavioral data chiefly reflect the duration and sequelae of sciatic nerve blockade. The development of more suitable methods to specifically evaluate femoral nerve block efficacy in mice would strengthen future experimental designs.

Finally, as with any preclinical investigation, the translational relevance of these findings must be approached with caution. Given the inherent differences between animal models and human physiology, our results require validation through targeted clinical research to determine their applicability to the clinical phenomenon of rebound pain.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that in a mouse model, nerve stimulator-guided liposomal bupivacaine provides prolonged analgesia but is followed by a transient period of thermal rebound pain upon block resolution. Histological evidence indicates concomitant early neuroinflammation and axonal injury. These findings suggest that the extended analgesia afforded by liposomal bupivacaine may delay, rather than prevent, the onset of rebound pain in this model. The observed thermal-specific rebound pain is potentially mediated through neuroinflammatory mechanisms that preferentially target thermal pain pathways. Further research is necessary to validate these mechanisms and to explore their translational relevance in clinical settings.
