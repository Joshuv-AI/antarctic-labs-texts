Cardiac tumors are uncommon, with primary lesions occurring in only 0.02% of the population; three-fourths of them are benign. Myxomas account for roughly half of all primary cardiac tumors in adults and are typically sporadic (95%), occasionally familial or syndromic. They show a female predominance and arise most often in the left atrium (75%). Less frequent sites include the right atrium (15%), the left and right ventricles (3% each), and both atria simultaneously in about 2.5% of cases, while truly multiple locations are exceptional (1%).1,2

Biatrial myxomas are usually separate masses, and their clinical presentation depends on size and chamber involvement, yet many patients remain asymptomatic and the myxomas are discovered incidentally. Multimodality imaging clarifies anatomy and guides surgical planning, and complete resection remains the definitive treatment, preventing embolic events and minimizing recurrence.

The present report describes an exceedingly rare incidentally detected biatrial myxoma connected across the fossa ovalis in an asymptomatic elderly woman, emphasizing the diagnostic value of comprehensive cardiovascular imaging and timely surgical management in this setting.

History of presentation

A 74-year-old woman, entirely asymptomatic, was referred after screening transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed 2 mobile atrial masses. She reported no chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, syncope, or embolic events. Vital signs were stable, and physical examination was unremarkable.

Past medical history

The patient had well-controlled systemic hypertension managed with losartan, hydrochlorothiazide, and amlodipine. There was no history of structural heart disease, arrhythmia, malignancy, or thromboembolic events.

Differential diagnosis

The initial differential for biatrial masses included cardiac thrombus, primary benign tumors (myxoma, papillary fibroelastoma, lipoma), and metastatic cardiac disease.

Investigation

Initial blood test revealed a white blood cell count of 8,900/mm3 (normal range: 3,500-10,500/mm3), hemoglobin of 13.9 g/dL (normal range: 12-15 g/dL), and platelet count of 365,000/mm3 (normal range: 150,000-450,000/mm3). The electrocardiogram showed first-degree atrioventricular block, without other arrhythmia. TTE demonstrated a 3.1 × 1.4 cm pedunculated left atrial mass and a 3.6 × 1.7 cm right atrial mass, both attached to the interatrial septum at the fossa ovalis, with no obstruction of atrioventricular inflow (Figure 1). Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed prolapse of each mass into the respective ventricle, ill-defined, suggestive of a myxoma (Figure 2, Video 1).

Management

Given the embolic risk of a tumor spanning both atria, surgery was performed. Through median sternotomy and cardiopulmonary bypass, the interconnected masses were excised en bloc along with a cuff of septal tissue (Figure 4); the interatrial septum was closed primarily. Histopathology confirmed myxoma (Figure 5).

Discussion

Biatrial myxomas, comprising only 2.5% of cases, are often misdiagnosed as unilateral myxomas; when they occur, it is essential to consider genetic syndromes such as multiple endocrine neoplasia, von Hippel-Lindau syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, and Carney complex. Familial syndromes tend to show multiple locations in 45% of cases.3,4

Although they are frequently asymptomatic, complications may occur and are primarily determined by mobility, attachment site, and surface characteristics.1,2 Pedunculated tumors may prolapse through atrioventricular valves during diastole, producing a dynamic obstruction often described as a “tumor plop” phenomenon.5 In left atrial tumors, this may mimic mitral stenosis physiology, resulting in exertional dyspnea, syncope, pulmonary congestion, or sudden cardiac death.2 Right-sided involvement may similarly interfere with tricuspid inflow, producing signs of right heart failure or functional tricuspid stenosis.6

Systemic embolization remains the most frequent complication and may occur even in minimally symptomatic patients. The friable and heterogeneous architecture of myxomas predisposes to fragmentation or thrombus formation along the tumor surface.2 Cerebral embolism is most common, though coronary, renal, and peripheral embolic events are well described. Importantly, embolic risk is not strictly proportional to tumor size, reinforcing the rationale for prompt intervention once diagnosis is established.1

Although TTE is typically sufficient for detection, diagnostic suspicion should not rely solely on the identification of a pedicle. Features that support the diagnosis of myxoma include septal origin, marked mobility, heterogeneous echotexture, and dynamic valve interaction.1,5 In biatrial presentations, careful evaluation of septal integrity is essential to distinguish 2 independent masses from a single tumor extending across the interatrial septum.

While TTE is the primary tool, transesophageal echocardiography refines the assessment of stalk insertion and chamber involvement. In complex cases, cardiac magnetic resonance enhances tissue characterization, differentiating myxoma from thrombus or other primary cardiac neoplasms through perfusion patterns and late gadolinium enhancement.7 In this case, comprehensive multimodality imaging was critical not only for diagnosis but also for defining the anatomical relationship across the septum, thereby facilitating surgical strategy.

Complete resection of the tumor and surrounding tissue minimizes recurrence, which occurs in approximately 1% to 5% of sporadic cases, but increases to 15% to 22% in patients with Carney complex.3,8 Operative mortality in experienced centers is low, and the favorable postoperative course observed in this patient reinforces the benefit of timely intervention, even in the absence of symptoms.2,6

Postoperative complications often include supraventricular arrhythmias due to atrial manipulation, with some risk of recurrent interatrial communication in biatrial myxoma cases.2

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course in our patient was notable only for transient sinus bradycardia requiring a temporary pacemaker for 72 hours. She was discharged on day 6. At the 1-year follow-up, she remained asymptomatic; TTE showed no residual mass or shunt and normal biventricular function.

Conclusions

Biatrial myxoma connected across the fossa ovalis is exceptionally uncommon yet carries dual-chamber embolic risk. Multimodality imaging secures diagnosis and anatomic definition, while prompt en bloc surgical excision ensures definitive cure with excellent prognosis. Clinicians should suspect this entity whenever mobile, opposing atrial masses share a septal stalk to avert complications.
