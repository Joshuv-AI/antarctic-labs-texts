SAT-Based Search for Minwise Independent Families
Enrico Iurlano Günther R. Raidl

Abstract
Proposed for rapid document similarity estimation in web search engines, the celebrated property of minwise independence imposes highly symmetric constraints on a family ℱ \mathscr{F} of permutations of { 1 , … , n } \{1,\ldots,n\} : The property is fulfilled by ℱ \mathscr{F} if for each j ∈ { 1 , … , n } j\in\{1,\ldots,n\} , any cardinality- j j subset X ⊆ { 1 , … , n } X\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\} , and any fixed element x ∗ ∈ X x^{\ast}\in X , it occurs with probability 1 / j 1/j that a randomly drawn permutation π \pi from ℱ \mathscr{F} satisfies π ⁡ ( x ∗ ) = min ⁡ { π ⁡ ( x ) : x ∈ X } \pi(x^{\ast})=\min\{\pi(x):x\in X\} . The central interest is to find a family with fewest possible members meeting the stated constraints. We provide a framework that, firstly, is realized as a pure SAT model and, secondly, generalizes a heuristic of Mathon and van Trung to the search of these families. Originally, the latter enforces an underlying group-theoretic decomposition to achieve a significant speed-up for the computer-aided search of structures which can be identified with so-called rankwise independent families. We observe that this approach is suitable to find provenly optimal new representatives of minwise independent families while yielding a decisive speed-up, too. As the problem has a naive search space of size at least ( n ! ) n (n!)^{n} , we also carefully address symmetry breaking. Finally, we add a bijective proof for a problem encountered by Bargachev when deriving a lower bound on the number of members in a minimal rankwise independent family.

Introduction

The concept of (
k
-restricted) minwise independence was introduced by Broder et al.
[
4
]
in the context of rapid similarity estimation within large document collections on the World Wide Web.
The result of their considerations is ultimately the
MinHash
algorithm, which is widely used by practitioners in the field of big data (see
[
26
]
as an example of thousands of other works that build on
[
4
]
).
It can accurately approximate the pairwise Jaccard similarity between high-dimensional data by precomputing in linear time the so-called
sketches

[
4
]
associated to the data.
Let us take a closer look at the most important observation used for this:
For estimating the Jaccard similarity
r(A,B):=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|
between two sets
A,B\subseteq\left[n\right]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, it was figured out
[
4
]
that, with
E_{\pi;A,B}
describing the scenario of coincidence

\min\{\pi(a):a\in A\}=\min\{\pi(b):b\in B\},

we have

\operatorname{Pr}\left[E_{\pi;A,B}\,|\,\pi\text{ drawn uniformly at random from }S_{n}\right]=r(A,B),

(1)

where
S_{n}
stands for the symmetric group consisting of all permutations, i.e., bijective functions from
\left[n\right]
onto
\left[n\right]
.
Hence, the Jaccard similarity can be characterized by the (conditional) probability stated in (
1
).
However, the difficulty of accurately approximating this similarity measure
r
via random sampling lies in the fact that, for larger values of
n
, it is hard to draw a permutation from
S_{n}
—even approximately—uniformly at random.
The reason is the loss of accuracy of pseudorandom number generators in large numeric scopes.
Therefore, Broder et al.
[
4
]
studied families of permutations, which they called
minwise independent
, having less members than
S_{n}
and yet ensuring the identity in (
1
).

Minwise independence later became a useful concept and tool for many other applications, including, but not limited to, the detection of spam
[
18
]
, machine learning (dimensionality reduction)
[
26
]
, and derandomization as well as computational geometry
[
20
]
.

We now formally introduce the most basic concepts and properties of minwise independence.
Following
[
4
]
, a finite non-empty family
\mathscr{F}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})
of permutations
\pi_{i}\in S_{n}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, together with a probability distribution on the members of
\mathscr{F}
,
p=(p_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in[0,1]^{d}
, is
k
-restricted minwise independent
if for each
j\in\left[k\right]
for each
X\subseteq\left[n\right]
with
|X|=j
, and each
x^{\ast}
in
X
,

\operatorname{Pr}\left[\pi(x^{\ast})=\min_{x\in X}\pi(x)~|~\pi\text{ drawn from }\mathscr{F}\text{ randomly according to }p\right]=\frac{1}{j}.

(2)

If not stated otherwise, in the following, we will always assume a uniform probability distribution on the members of a family, i.e.,
p=(1/d)_{i=1}^{d}
.
Let us denote by
S_{n,k}
the set of injective functions with domain
\left[k\right]
and codomain
\left[n\right]
, henceforth called
k
-subpermutations
, and let us introduce
\mathsf{SOP}(n,j):=\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{j})\in\left[n\right]^{j}:s_{2}<s_{3}<\ldots<s_{j}\wedge s_{1}\not\in\{s_{2},\ldots,s_{j}\}\}
with
1\leq j\leq n
, which we call the set of
semiordered patterns
.
We can state the equality (
2
) equivalently as

\lvert\{i\in\left[d\right]:\pi_{i}(s_{1})=\min\{\pi_{i}(s_{1}),\pi_{i}(s_{2}),\ldots,\pi_{i}(s_{j})\}\}\rvert=\frac{d}{j},

(3)

for each
j\in\left[k\right]
and each
(s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{j})\in\mathsf{SOP}(n,j)
, implying that
d
must be a multiple of
\operatorname{lcm}\left(\left[k\right]\right)
, the least common multiple of the numbers
1,2,\ldots,k
.
We call a
k
-restricted minwise independent family
\mathscr{F}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})\subseteq S_{n}

optimal
if for all
\tilde{d}<d
there is no
k
-restricted minwise independent family
\widetilde{\mathscr{F}}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{\tilde{d}})\subseteq S_{n}
.

Example 1
.

The family
\mathscr{F}:=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{6})\subseteq S_{4}
given by

((2,3,1,4),(1,4,2,3),(4,1,2,3),(2,3,4,1),(2,1,4,3),(4,3,2,1))

is
3
-restricted minwise independent—the permutations are here given in tuple notation.
Notice that for
(2,1,4)\in\mathsf{SOP}(4,3)
, we have
2=|\{\pi_{3},\pi_{5}\}|=6/3
and
\pi_{3}(2)=1=\min\{1,4,3\}=\min\{\pi_{3}(2),\pi_{3}(1),\pi_{3}(4)\}
as well as
\pi_{5}(2)=1=\min\{1,2,3\}=\min\{\pi_{5}(2),\pi_{5}(1),\pi_{5}(4)\}
.
A respective observation can be made for all
12
elements in
\mathsf{SOP}(4,3)
, all
12
elements in
\mathsf{SOP}(4,2)
, and all four elements in
\mathsf{SOP}(4,1)
.

In

Finally, let us recall some elementary and here crucial concepts from group theory: For any group
A
with binary group operation
\circ:A\times A\to A
, neutral element
e\in A
, and element inversion
(\cdot)^{-1}:A\to A
, for each subgroup
G
of
A
we can form a so-called left-coset by picking an arbitrary
a\in A
and forming
aG:=\{a\circ g:g\in G\}
.
Similarly, right-cosets
Ga:=\{g\circ a:g\in G\}
are formable for
a\in A
.
We will exclusively focus on subgroups of the symmetric group
S_{n}
with the composition of functions as operation (see also Remark
8
).

For minwise independence, the following result seems to be the first in the literature which (after casting the original assertion to the present group theoretical setting) proves the existence of a minwise independent family by a particular decomposition into
right-cosets
.

Theorem 6

(
[
2
]
)
.

Let

be odd and
\mathscr{F}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d})\subseteq S_{n}
be

-restricted minwise independent.
With
G=\{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\}:=\{\operatorname{id},\sigma\}
denoting the subgroup of
S_{n}
containing the identity permutation and the order reversing permutation
\sigma:\left[n\right]\to\left[n\right]
,

, we have that
(\gamma_{\ell}\circ\theta_{m}:\ell\in\left[d\right],m\in\left[2\right])
is a
(k+1)
-restricted minwise independent family counting
2d
members.

We also emphasize that a connection of minwise independence to group theory has been studied within a more restrictive setting in
[
5
]
.

The goal of this paper is to provide a SAT framework enabling the study—for reasonably small scales of
n
and
k
—the existence and nature of
k
-restricted minwise independent families
\mathscr{F}\subseteq S_{n}
with a pre-specified number of members
d
.
Analogously to
[
21
,
11
]
, a particular emphasis will be set on the question whether (near-) optimal such families can be “spanned” by suitable—possibly large—subgroups of
S_{n}
and to quantify a potential speed-up in terms of solving time.

2
A SAT approach

The main idea shares the natural approach from
[
1
]
(in the setting of Answer Set Programming) where permutations are represented via their order theoretic incidence structure.
In fact, if
\pi\in S_{n}
is a permutation, it is disambiguously determined by full knowledge on
X^{\pi}=(x^{\pi}_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
capturing by
x^{\pi}_{i,j}
the information if
\pi(i)<\pi(j)
(
1
in the affirmative case, otherwise
0
).
In the following it will become clear that considering just the strict upper-diagonal entries of
X^{\pi}
is sufficient (see later asymmetry).
Note that
X^{\pi}
can be seen as incidence matrix of a strict total order on
\left[n\right]
representing the permutation.
We can in fact recover the
j
-th entry of the permutation via
\pi(j)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}x^{\pi}_{i,j}
.
All solutions for
x_{i,j}^{\pi}
subject to the following constraints (irreflexivity, asymmetry, and transitivity) determine hence a permutation:

\displaystyle\neg x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{i,i}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d\right]~\forall i\in\left[n\right],

(5)

\displaystyle\neg x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{i,j}\vee\neg x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{j,i}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d\right]~\forall i\in\left[n\right]~\forall j\in\left[n\right],

(6)

\displaystyle\neg x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{i,j}\vee\neg x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{j,h}\vee x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{i,h}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d\right]~\forall i\in\left[n\right]~\forall j\in[i+1\!:\!n]~\forall h\in\left[n\right]\setminus\{i,j\}.

(7)

These constraints are already stated in conjunctive normal form (CNF), and for two integers
p
and
p
, we denote by
[p\!:\!q]:=\{p,p+1,\ldots,q\}
.

To model property (
3
) in the definition of minwise independence, we need to impose specific cardinality constraints which are stated in (
8
)—note that
j\leq 3
is intentionally excluded due to a more favorable replacement in (
9
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left[\bigwedge_{h=2}^{j}x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{s_{1},s_{h}}\right]\leq d/j

\displaystyle~~~\forall j\in[4\!:\!k]~\forall s\in\mathsf{SOP}(n,j),

(8)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left[\bigwedge_{h=2}^{3}x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{\sigma(h-1),\sigma(h)}\right]\leq d/3!

\displaystyle~~~\forall\sigma\in S_{n,3}.

(9)

In fact, we avoid the higher upper bound
d/3
(which in (
8
) would result for
j=3
) and also can relinquish the case
j=2
—this is justified by the aforementioned particularity that
3
-restricted minwise independence is precisely
3
-rankwise independence.

Note that upper bounds instead of equalities appear in (
8
)–(
9
) which provide a considerable simplification in view of a conversion to CNF.
These seemingly weaker conditions are however equivalent to the ones with equalities: Any not attained upper bound in (
8
)–(
9
) would imply a strict excess of the upper bound for some other
s^{\prime}\in\mathsf{SOP}(n,j)
respectively
\sigma^{\prime}\in S_{n,3}
—infeasibility would be a consequence; in fact, the number of patterns/subpermutation conditions met by a family
\mathscr{F}
is fixed.
How the cardinality constraints are technically translated to CNF is deferred to Section
3
.

Remark 7
.

If the interest is in modeling
k
-rankwise independence, we can simply replace (
8
)–(
9
) with
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}\left[\bigwedge_{h=2}^{k}x^{\pi_{\ell}}_{\sigma(h-1),\sigma(h)}\right]\leq d/k!~~\forall\sigma\in S_{n,k}
.

For symmetry breaking, for a permutation
\pi
, we can consider the binary string resulting from
X^{\pi}=(x^{\pi}_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}
after the following procedure: All side-diagonals of
X^{\pi}
lying above the main diagonal are concatenated as string denoted as
z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi}]:=(X^{\pi}_{i+1,i+j}:j=2,\ldots n,i=0,\ldots,n-j)
.
It is then natural, without loss of generality, to enforce an ordering on
x^{\pi_{\ell}}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d
, which is based on the lexicographical ordering of their associated strings
z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi_{\ell}}]
.
Thus, when
\preceq
denotes the lexicographical ordering relation on binary strings (e.g.,
\texttt{0100}\preceq\texttt{0110}
), we require

\displaystyle z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi_{m+1}}]\preceq z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi_{m}}]

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d\right].

(10)

The identity permutation, whose upper diagonal part of the incidence matrix consists of exclusively
1
-entries, is hence the minimum element with respect to this ordering.
For
\pi\in S_{n}
the string
z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi}]
is of length
n(n-1)/2
.
Therefore, we might prefer the following weakened constraint depending on a chooseable “accuracy” parameter
H\in[0\!:\!n(n-1)/2]
; for a string
s
we denote the substring consisting of the first
H
entries of
s
by
s_{[1..H]}
:

\displaystyle z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi_{m+1}}]_{[1..H]}\preceq z_{\textrm{cat}}[X^{\pi_{m}}]_{[1..H]}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d\right].

(11)

In
[
22
, entry A036604]
one can look up
\mathsf{A}(n)
, the number of pairwise comparisons (dynamically chooseable) needed to uniquely determine a strict total order on
\left[n\right]
; see also the follow-up paper
[
23
]
.
In the following we regard the choice
H:=\mathsf{A}(n)
as meaningful guideline but could also change this value for
H
, if a different trade-off between symmetry breaking and lower model complexity is preferred.
Finally, in this setting we can add without loss of generality the following constraint.
As in
[
21
, Lemma 4.2]
, we preserve minwise independence by composing each permutation in the family with
\pi_{1}^{-1}
and afterwards bringing the transformed in lexicographic order:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}=\operatorname{id}\text{, i.e., unit clauses~~~}x^{\pi_{1}}_{i,j}

\displaystyle~~~\forall i\in\left[n\right]~\forall j\in[i+1\!:\!n].

(12)

We now transfer to our setting a heuristic approach relying on group theory due to Mathon and van Trung
[
19
]
.
Its successful applicability to the search for perfect sequence covering arrays of higher “coverage-multiplicity” has been recently shown
[
21
]
.
In the spirit of these two previous works, we will assume that our families
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})
with
\pi_{i}\in S_{n}
and where
\operatorname{lcm}\left(\left[k\right]\right)
is a divisor of
d
have the following underlying structure:
There is a subgroup
G=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{q}\}
of
S_{n}
whose order
q=|G|
is a divisor of
d
and for which there are “offsets”
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{d/|G|}\in S_{n}
such that

\mathscr{F}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})=(\theta_{\ell}\circ\gamma_{m})_{(\ell,m)\in\left[d/|G|\right]\times\left[|G|\right]}.

(13)

Condition (
13
) models the coincidence of
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})
with a union of
d/|G|
not necessarily different left-cosets of a subgroup
G
of
S_{n}
.
Hence, having fixed a non-trivial subgroup
G
of
S_{n}
, the number of decision variables is reduced, as every choice for an “offset”
\theta_{i}
automatically fixes a larger set of size
|G|
of permutations in
\mathscr{F}
.
Note that we will always assume that per default
\gamma_{1}:=\operatorname{id}
in our enumeration of
G
.

A model for this heuristic approach in the above given setting consists of the following parts:
Decision variables
X^{\theta_{\ell}}
,
\ell=1,\ldots,d/|G|
, with permutation constraints as in (
5
)–(
7
), cardinality constraints

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/|G|}\sum_{m=1}^{|G|}\left[\bigwedge_{h=2}^{j}x^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\gamma_{m}(s_{1}),\gamma_{m}(s_{h})}\right]\leq d/j

\displaystyle~~~\forall j\in[4\!:\!k]~\forall s\in\mathsf{SOP}(n,j),

(14)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/|G|}\sum_{m=1}^{|G|}\left[\bigwedge_{h=2}^{3}x^{\theta_{\ell}}_{\gamma_{m}(\sigma(h-1)),\gamma_{m}(\sigma(h))}\right]\leq d/3!

\displaystyle~~~\forall\sigma\in S_{n,3},

(15)

and lexicographical symmetry breaking, compare (
11
),

\displaystyle z_{\textrm{cat}}[\theta_{\ell+1}]_{[1..H]}\preceq z_{\textrm{cat}}[\theta_{\ell}]_{[1..H]}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d/|G|-1\right].

(16)

Fixing the trivial subgroup consisting just of the identity permutation
\{\gamma_{1}\}=\{\operatorname{id}\}
of
S_{n}
, we recover precisely the original, non-heuristic model (
5
)–(
9
), (
11
).

Remark 8
.

For clarity, we emphasize that our notation
\alpha\circ\beta
specifies the function resulting from applying function
\alpha
to the output of function
\beta
, i.e.,
\alpha(\beta(\cdot))
—in contrast to the notation of
[
21
]
.

In
[
21
]
, also right-cosets are examined, where the postulated equality (
13
) translates to

\mathscr{F}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{d})=(\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell})_{(\ell,m)\in\left[d/|G|\right]\times\left[|G|\right]}.

(17)

In the framework of SAT, this needs a more elaborated modeling approach:
Although we notice that
\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell}={(\theta_{\ell}^{-1}\circ\gamma_{m}^{-1})}^{-1}
, leading to the idea to use
\theta_{\ell}^{-1}
directly as new decision variable, there is then no direct way to recover via an encoding in CNF the incidence matrix of the inverse of a permutation (where the latter is itself specified by its incidence matrix).
In fact we now face the difficulty to let a symbolic permutation specified by decision variables take action on the domain of a given collection of parameter permutations and return the output of the latter.
This considerably differs from the left-coset approach where the need is to access the incidence matrix of a symbolic permutation in entries pre-permutated by concretely given parameter permutations.

We circumvent this issue by modeling the offsets
\theta_{m}
as permutation matrices
T^{\theta_{\ell}}=(t_{ij}^{\theta_{\ell}})_{i,j=1}^{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
whose bi-stochasticity we enforce via

\displaystyle\bigvee_{j=1}^{n}t_{ij}^{\theta_{\ell}}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d/q\right]~\forall i\in\left[n\right],

(18)

\displaystyle\neg t_{i,j}^{\theta_{\ell}}\vee\neg t_{i,r}^{\theta_{\ell}}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d/q\right]~\forall i\in\left[n\right]~\forall\{j,r\}\in\binom{\left[n\right]}{2},

(19)

\displaystyle\neg t_{i,j}^{\theta_{\ell}}\vee\neg t_{r,j}^{\theta_{\ell}}

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d/q\right]~\forall j\in\left[n\right]~\forall\{i,r\}\in\binom{\left[n\right]}{2}.

(20)

The following constraint (
21
) stores the incidence matrix of
\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell}
for any fixed
\gamma_{m}
and a symbolic
\theta_{\ell}
inside the fresh variables
x_{i,r}^{\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell}}
,
(i,r)\in\left[n\right]^{2}
.
The formulation depends on the permutation matrix
T^{\theta_{\ell}}
of
\theta_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle x_{i,r}^{\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell}}\leftrightarrow\left((t_{\gamma_{m}(r),j}^{\theta_{\ell}})_{j=1}^{n}\preceq(t_{\gamma_{m}(i),j}^{\theta_{\ell}})_{j=1}^{n}\right)

\displaystyle~~~\forall\ell\in\left[d/q\right]\forall m\in\left[q\right]\forall(i,r)\in\left[n\right]^{2}.

(21)

Regarding (
21
), note that firstly, row
i
is a lexicographical successor of row
r
of the permutation matrix of an arbitrary permutation
\pi
iff
\pi(i)<\pi(r)
; secondly, the permutation matrix of
\gamma_{m}\circ\theta_{\ell}
can be recovered from
T^{\theta_{\ell}}
by applying the permutation
\gamma_{m}
to its rows, as illustrated in the following example.

Example 9
.

Let
(\theta(i))_{i=1}^{4}=(4,1,3,2)
,
(\gamma(i))_{i=1}^{4}=(4,2,3,1)
.
Then,
(\theta\circ\gamma(i))_{i=1}^{4}=(2,1,3,4)
.
The permutation matrix, respectively incidence matrix, of
\theta
is

T=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&1\\

4
Bijecting derangements onto non-waste permutations

This section settles the challenge of Bargachev
[
3
, p. 6]
to construct a bijection between two permutations classes exploited for obtaining the best known lower bound in Theorem
4
(
iii
).
For this, the (later defined) class of permutations having
k

waste indices
, denoted as
\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)\subseteq S_{n}
, is introduced.
The pursued approach relies then on an algebraic proof of the coincidence of the cardinality of
\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
with the count of
k
-partial derangements
in
S_{n}
, i.e., permutations with exactly
k\in[0\!:\!n]
fixed points, whose class is henceforth denoted as
\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)\subseteq S_{n}
.
However, Bargachev emphasizes the lack of a known explicit bijection
\Phi:\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)\to\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
, even for
k=0
.

We fill this gap by more generally giving a bijection for
arbitrary

k
having a remarkably succinct description.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
.
An index
j\in\left[n\right]
is
waste

[
3
]
if either the special case
j=n
and
\min\{\pi(\ell):\ell=1,\ldots,n\}=\pi(n)=1
applies, or alternatively if we have
j<n
with

\min\{\pi(\ell):\ell=1,\ldots,j\}=\pi(j)\text{ and }\pi(j)>\pi(j+1).

(28)

Denote by
\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)\subseteq S_{n}
the set of all permutations possessing precisely
k\in[0:n]
waste indices.

We now mimic and generalize the approach in
[
27
, Proposition 2.1]
relying on the cycle notation of permutations; see also
[
7
]
.
Retrospectively, this will highlight that analogues of the permutation classes introduced out of combinatorial interest in
[
27
]
actually appear in more applied contexts.
Moreover, this gives a simpler alternative to the proof of
[
3
, Lemma 4]
, the latter requiring to solve a technical recurrence relation.

Theorem 13
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in[0\!:\!n]
there is an (explicitly describable) bijection
\Phi:\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)\to\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
.

Proof.

Suppose
\pi\in\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)\subseteq S_{n}
.
Let us define the transformed
\Phi(\pi)
as the result of the following procedure:
Write down
\pi
in cycle notation, where we assume that cycles coincide with their cycle-representative placing the smallest entry on the cycle’s first (i.e., lefter-most) position.
Afterwards, the cycles are ordered from left to right such that their respective first entries decrease; see Example
14
.
The final output
\Phi(\pi)
is then defined as the permutation which maps
1,\ldots,n
to the cycles’ entries read from left to right.

Now observe that the result
\Phi(\pi)
is an element of
\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
:
By construction, there will be precisely
k
length-
1
cycles in the cyclic notation of
\pi
.
Each position of a length-
1
cycle has an associated waste index:
Note that the minimization in (
28
) but also the the second, decreasing, behavior in (
28
) holds true due to the ranked ordering of cycles.
On the other hand, in cycles of length at least two, by construction, cycle-entries appearing not in the first position cannot be minimizers in (
28
).
Moreover, the first entries of the cycles cannot meet the second condition of decrease in (
28
).
No additional waste-indices originate from cycles of length at least two and therefore
\Phi(\pi)\in\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
.

We show that
\Phi
is surjective.
For any
\tau\in\mathsf{Wst}(n,k)
we find
\pi\in\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)
such that
\Phi(\pi)=\tau
:
Determine all indices
j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{\ell}
that satisfy the minimization-condition in (
28
).
Using these indices, segment
\tau
into
\ell
blocks

(\tau_{j_{1}},\tau_{j_{1}+1},\ldots,\tau_{j_{2}-1}),(\tau_{j_{2}},\tau_{j_{2}+1},\ldots,\tau_{j_{3}-1}),\ldots,(\tau_{j_{\ell}},\tau_{j_{\ell}+1},\ldots,\tau_{n}).

(29)

Let
\pi
be the permutation which results from interpreting the
\ell
blocks in (
29
) as cycles, i.e.,

\pi=(\tau_{j_{1}}\tau_{j_{1}+1}\cdots\tau_{j_{2}-1})(\tau_{j_{2}}\tau_{j_{2}+1}\cdots\tau_{j_{3}-1})\cdots(\tau_{j_{\ell}}\tau_{j_{\ell}+1}\cdots\tau_{n}).

(30)

As there are precisely
k
non-waste indices for
\tau
, there are precisely
k
length-
1
blocks in (
29
) and therefore
k
length-
1
cycles in (
30
).
Eventually, this means that
\pi
has exactly
k
fixed points and that
\Phi(\pi)=\tau
.

Next we show the injectivity of
\Phi
.
The transition from a permutation to its decomposition into cycles is injective.
For permutations from
\mathsf{Derang}(n,k)
, this extends to the linear traversal of its cycles’ members:
In fact, the imposed decreasing ordering among the cycles’ leaders and the minimum value attained by each leader in its cycle permit just one unique segmentation into cycles.
∎

.

Let
(\rho(j))_{j=1}^{9}=(1,5,3,4,6,2,8,7,9)
, and thus
\rho\in\mathsf{Derang}(9,4)

Then, its cycle notation, respectively
\Psi(\rho)

\rho=(9)(78)(4)(3)(256)(1),\text{ respectively }\Phi(\rho)=(9,7,8,4,3,2,5,6,1).

Theorem

automatically implies the validity of
[

5
Conclusion

We developed SAT models useful for examining the existence of (near-) optimal
k
-restricted minwise independent families
\mathscr{F}\subseteq S_{n}
for reasonably small values of
n
and
k
.
Besides finding many so-far unknown minwise independent families, our results show the following key observation concerning these structures:
Many optimal representatives of such families carry indeed the aforementioned group theoretic structure.
A further benefit of the decomposition is that it allows to find feasible representatives by avoiding prohibitively long running times, which in the setting of a purely non-heuristic approach, in contrast, would be needed.
We rounded our considerations up by describing a simple bijection leading to a short variant of a proof due to Bargachev
[
3
]
that addresses rankwise independence.

The following aspects seem interesting for further work.
Firstly, examining the performance of an Integer Linear Programming approach, which could address the cardinality constraints straightforwardly, would be interesting, too.
Secondly, it would be interesting to see if instances so-far unsolved could be attacked by an incremental SAT approach.
Finally, given the large pool of subgroups for the right-coset approach, a further question is, if a solver can learn clauses from a small subpool of the subgroup-associated instances and then run faster on the remaining instances.
