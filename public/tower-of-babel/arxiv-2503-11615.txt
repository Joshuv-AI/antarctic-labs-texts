From Score Matching to Diffusion: A Fine-Grained Error Analysis in the Gaussian Setting
Samuel Hurault, Matthieu Terris, Thomas Moreau, Gabriel Peyré

Abstract
Sampling from an unknown distribution, accessible only through discrete samples, is a fundamental problem at the core of generative AI. The current state-of-the-art methods follow a two-step process: first, estimating the score function (the gradient of a smoothed log-distribution) and then applying a diffusion-based sampling algorithm—such as Langevin or diffusion models. The resulting distribution’s correctness can be impacted by four major factors: the generalization and optimization errors in score matching, and the discretization and minimal noise amplitude in the diffusion. In this paper, we make the sampling error explicit when using a diffusion sampler in the Gaussian setting. We provide a sharp analysis of the Wasserstein sampling error that arises from these four error sources. This allows us to rigorously track how the anisotropy of the data distribution (encoded by its power spectrum) interacts with key parameters of the end-to-end sampling method, including the number of initial samples, the stepsizes in both score matching and diffusion, and the noise amplitude. Notably, we show that the Wasserstein sampling error can be expressed as a kernel-type norm of the data power spectrum, where the specific kernel depends on the method parameters. This result provides a foundation for further analysis of the tradeoffs involved in optimizing sampling accuracy.



1
Introduction

Sampling from an unknown distribution based only on a finite dataset is a core challenge in modern generative modeling. Score-based methods address this by estimating the gradient of a smoothed log-density – known as the score function – and then using it to guide sample generation via a stochastic differential equation (SDE). Because this score is not available in closed form,
[
21
]
introduced score matching to estimate it directly from data, and later,
[
39
]
showed that the optimal score estimator in the minimum mean squared error sense is a Gaussian noise denoising function. This connection laid the foundation for modern denoising score matching, where a neural network is trained to predict clean samples from noise-corrupted inputs using stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
Once trained, the learned score is used to guide sampling through a discretized diffusion process, such as Langevin dynamics or a discretized diffusion stochastic process
[
36
,
37
]
. This two-stage pipeline has achieved state-of-the-art results in generative tasks across images, audio, and videos
[
18
,
19
,
11
,
24
]
.

Despite their strong empirical performance, score-based diffusion models suffer from biases introduced at both stages of the pipeline: training yields an approximate score function, constrained by limited data and optimization errors, while sampling relies on discretized diffusion dynamics that introduce integration errors and noise scheduling truncation.
This paper investigates how these four sources of error interact within a simplified, analytically tractable setting.
Specifically, we focus on Gaussian data and linear scores, which enables exact explicit computation of how each error source affects the resulting sampled distribution.
In this simplified setting, computing the explicit errors already involves cumbersome calculations that uncover complex interactions between the four error sources, data geometry, and algorithmic parameters.
More specifically, our contributions are:

Error Analysis in SGD-based Denoising Score Matching:

In Section
3
, we conduct a detailed analysis of the errors introduced by stochastic gradient descent (SGD) when approximating the score function using denoising score matching, focusing on the Gaussian data setting. Theorem
1
characterizes the covariance structure of the resulting error in the parameter space of a linear score model. Our analysis accounts for both the
generalization error
, arising from the use of a finite dataset of size
N
, and the
optimization error
, stemming from a positive constant learning rate
\tau
. This explicit derivation of the stationary distribution of SGD for a denoising objective is novel and provides new insights into the magnitude and structure of these two sources of error.

Error Analysis in Diffusion-Based Sampling:

In Sections
4.1
and
4.2
, we present a parallel analysis of the sampling error introduced by Langevin and diffusion models. Theorems
2
and
3
provide closed-form asymptotic expressions for the Wasserstein error resulting from (i) the use of an approximate score function (ii) the effects of time discretization with finite stepsize
\gamma
and (iii) the presence of a nonzero final noise amplitude, modeled as a fixed noise level
\sigma
for Langevin and a nonzero terminal diffusion time
T-t_{K}
for diffusion models.

Unified Analysis of the Full Pipeline:

Finally, in Corollaries
1
and
2
, we combine these two error analyses, so as to analyze how the power spectrum of the initial data distribution influences the final sampling error. Notably, we demonstrate that, for both Langevin and diffusion sampling, the overall error can be expressed as a kernel norm on the power spectrum of the data. We explicitly characterize this kernel in terms of the parameters involved in the training and sampling algorithms.

All theoretical error predictions are carefully validated numerically in Figures
1
,
2
and
3
through empirical evaluations across Gaussian data experiments.

Background

Denoising Score Matching (DSM) and SGD

Consider the problem of sampling from an unknown data distribution
p_{\mathrm{data}}
.
Recent approaches
[
39
,
36
,
26
]
leverage an approximation of the score
\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{data}}
with a denoising neural network
D_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
with trainable parameters
\theta
.
The denoiser is trained by minimizing the
L^{2}
denoising loss
\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left\|{x-D_{\sigma}(x+\sigma w,\theta)}\right\|^{2}\right]
. According to Tweedie’s formula
[
16
]
, the optimal estimator for this loss, called the
MMSE denoiser
, satisfies
D_{\sigma}^{\star}(x)=x+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log p_{\sigma}(x),
with
p_{\sigma}=p_{\mathrm{data}}*\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Id})
the distribution of the data
p_{\mathrm{data}}
, smoothed by a Gaussian of standard deviation
\sigma
. Therefore, a denoiser trained with the
L^{2}
loss provides an approximation of the score of the smoothed distribution
p_{\sigma}
. Denoting the approximated score
v_{\sigma}(x,\theta)
, the
L^{2}
denoising loss can be rewritten as the denoising score matching loss

F(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p,w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})}\left[l(x,w,\theta)\right];\quad\text{ with }l(x,w,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\|{v_{\sigma}(x+\sigma w,\theta)+\frac{w}{\sigma}}\right\|^{2}

(1)

To learn the score function, one needs to minimize (
1
) with respect to
\theta
. Due to its expectation structure, a convenient algorithm is the Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD), which reads:

\begin{split}\text{For $k\geq 0$},\quad x_{k}&\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\quad w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}),\quad\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\tau_{k}\nabla l(x_{k},w_{k},\theta_{k}).\end{split}

(2)

When using a decaying stepsize
\tau_{k}
that satisfies
\sum_{k}\tau_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k}\tau_{k}^{2}<\infty
, the sequence defined by (
2
) converges to the minimizer of
F

[
31
]
. In contrast, when the learning rate
\tau
is constant—a common practice in training denoising neural networks—the sequence
\theta_{k}
typically does not converge but eventually oscillates around a stationary point, leading to a biased estimate that we refer to as the
optimization error
. Additionally, since each SGD update samples
x_{k}
from only a finite dataset of size
N
drawn from
p_{\mathrm{data}}
, this introduces a second source of bias, which we term the
generalization error
. Following the interpretations in
[
1
,
12
]
, we view the SGD iterates as an ergodic Markov chain that converges to a unique stationary distribution dependent on both
\tau
and
N
.

Langevin sampling

Langevin dynamics
[
25
]
is a stochastic method for sampling from a smooth distribution
p
. The continuous-time dynamics follow the stochastic differential equation (SDE):

y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad dy_{t}=-\nabla\log p(y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dw_{t},

where
w_{t}
is a standard Wiener process. The drift term guides samples toward high-density regions of
p
, while the diffusion term enables exploration. To simulate these dynamics, the Unadjusted Langevin Algorithm (ULA) applies an Euler–Maruyama discretization:

y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad y_{k+1}=y_{k}+\gamma\nabla\log p(y_{k})+\sqrt{2\gamma}\,w_{k},

(3)

where
w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
and
\gamma>0
is the stepsize. Under assumptions such as strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of
-\log p

[
15
,
14
,
10
]
, ULA converges to a biased distribution due to discretization.
Plug-and-play
Langevin methods
[
36
,
26
]
replace the unknown score
\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{data}}
with a learned approximation
v_{\sigma}(\cdot,\theta)
, trained via denoising score matching (
1
) at fixed noise level
\sigma>0
, introducing further bias. The error of Langevin sampling therefore depends on both the discretization stepsize
\gamma
and the considered noise level
\sigma
.

Diffusion model sampling

Diffusion models generate samples from the data distribution by reversing a forward stochastic noising process. Several formulations have been proposed, including Variance Preserving (VP)
[
37
,
18
]
, Variance Exploding (VE)
[
37
]
, DDIM
[
35
]
, and EDM
[
23
]
.
We adopt a unified formulation of these variants, and describe the forward noising process by the SDE:

x_{0}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}},\quad dx_{t}=-\beta_{t}x_{t}\,dt+\sqrt{2\xi_{t}}\,dw_{t}\kern 5.0pt.

(4)

The solution has a marginal distribution given by
p_{t}=p_{\mathrm{data}}(s_{t}\cdot)*\mathcal{N}\left(0,(s_{t}\sigma_{t})^{2}\operatorname{Id}\right)
, where
B_{t}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\beta_{s}\,ds
and
s_{t}\coloneqq e^{-B_{t}}
is a time-dependent scaling factor. This corresponds to a rescaled version of the Gaussian-smoothed data distribution
p_{\sigma_{t}}
, with noise standard deviation
\sigma_{t}\coloneqq\left(2\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{s}e^{2B_{s}}\,ds\right)^{1/2}
.
The VP diffusion, where
\beta_{t}=\xi_{t}
, converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{Id})
, while the VE model sets
\beta_{t}=0
, leading to an exploding variance as
t\to\infty
. As discussed in
[
37
]
, sampling from
p_{\mathrm{data}}
can then be done by reversing this process, from time
T>0
to
0
. As shown in Appendix
C.1
, this reverse-time process
q_{t}\coloneqq p_{T-t}
, follows the SDE

y_{0}\sim p_{T},\quad dy_{t}=\beta_{T-t}y_{t}\,dt+(1+\alpha)\xi_{T-t}\nabla\log p_{T-t}(y_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2\alpha\xi_{T-t}}\,dw_{t}.

(5)

where
\alpha\geq 0
is a parameter controlling the diffusion coefficient. In this work, we consider the Euler–Maruyama discretized version of this process: for a stepsize
\gamma>0
, and timesteps
t_{k}\coloneqq k\gamma
, with
k\in\llbracket 1,K\rrbracket
and
t_{K}\leq T
:

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1+\gamma\beta_{T-t_{k}})y_{k}+\gamma(1+\alpha)\xi_{T-t_{k}}\nabla\log p_{T-t_{k}}(y_{k})+\sqrt{2\alpha\gamma\xi_{T-t_{k}}}\cdot w_{k}

(6)

As in plug-and-play Langevin methods, the unknown score
\nabla\log p_{T-t}
is approximated using denoising score matching (
1
), but with a time-dependent noise level
\sigma_{t}>0
. In practice, for optimal sampling performance, the diffusion is early-stopped at
t_{K}<T
. The bias of diffusion sampling thus depends on the stepsize
\gamma
and the final time
t_{K}
.

In the remainder of this paper, we assume that the following assumption holds.

Assumption 1
.

The data distribution is Gaussian,
p_{\mathrm{data}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{data}},C_{\mathrm{data}})
with
C_{\mathrm{data}}\succ 0
.

We denote
(\lambda_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
the positive eigenvalues of
C_{\mathrm{data}}
with associated eigenvectors
(u_{i})_{1\leq i\leq d}
.

Goal of the paper.

We analyze the sampling error of Langevin and diffusion models as a function of

(\tau,1/N,\gamma)
, which are assumed to be small, as well as the noise parameters
\sigma
(for Langevin) and the stopping time
t_{K}
(for diffusion), with explicit constants depending only on the power spectrum
(\lambda_{i})
of the covariance of the data.

Modeling the Error in Denoising Score Matching

In this section, we analyze the training of a linear parametric model
v_{\sigma}(x,\theta)
to approximate the score of the smoothed distribution
\nabla\log p_{\sigma}
by minimizing the denoising score matching loss (
1
). More precisely, we consider a score parametrized as the affine operator

v_{\sigma}(x,\theta)=-Ax+b\quad\text{with parameters }\theta=(A,b)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

Under Assumption
1
, denoting
C_{\sigma}=C_{\mathrm{data}}+\sigma^{2}\operatorname{Id}
, the true score
\nabla\log p_{\sigma}
is the affine operator
v_{\sigma}^{\star}(x)=\nabla\log p_{\sigma}=-C_{\sigma}^{-1}x+C_{\sigma}^{-1}\mu_{\mathrm{data}}
. This implies that, under the Gaussian assumption, the chosen affine parameterization of the score function does not introduce any approximation error.
We consider training the parameter vector
\theta=\operatorname{vec}\left[-A^{\top}\;b^{\top}\right]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)d}
using the SGD algorithm (
2
) with a fixed learning rate
\tau>0
, and a finite dataset
{X_{N}=\{x_{i}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\}_{0\leq i<N}}
of size
N
:

\text{For $k\geq 0$},\quad x_{k}\in X_{N},\quad w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}),\quad\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\tau\nabla l(x_{k},w_{k},\theta_{k}),

(7)

The objective of this section is to provide an explicit approximation of the stationary distribution sampled by this process, with respect to the choice of learning rate
\tau
and the number of data samples
N
. We thus consider two sources of error.

Generalization error

It arises from the limited number
N
of samples drawn from
p_{\mathrm{data}}
used for training the score model.
To account for this error source, we assume that, at each step of the SGD algorithm (
7
), the data sample is not chosen in
X_{N}
but is drawn from the empirical Gaussian distribution
p_{N}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{N},\hat{C}_{N})
, where

\hat{\mu}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{i}\in X_{N}}x_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\hat{C}_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x_{i}\in X_{N}}(x_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{N})(x_{i}-\hat{\mu}_{N})^{\top}.

(8)

We thus analyze the following SGD iterations

\text{For $k\geq 0$},\quad x_{k}\sim p_{N},\quad w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\operatorname{Id}),\quad\theta_{k+1}=\theta_{k}-\tau\nabla l(x_{k},w_{k},\theta_{k}).

(9)

Note that this is an approximation of the actual algorithm (
7
) used in practice, allowing for a simplified mathematical analysis. We empirically verify Figure
1
the validity of this approximation.

Optimization error

The iterates
(\theta_{k})
of (
9
) define a homogeneous Markov chain that, for small learning rate
\tau
, converges to an invariant distribution
\pi_{\tau,N}(\theta)

[
32
,
1
]
. As shown in
[
1
]
, for a quadratic loss, a constant learning rate
\tau>0
introduces an error due to nonzero second and higher moments of
\pi_{\tau,N}
.
[
12
, Proposition 3]
provides a closed-form second moment via the covariance of the per-step SGD error. However, this general result does not capture the specific structure of the denoising score matching loss (
1
). We refine this analysis by deriving a precise expression for the denoising case. The following theorem provides an explicit expansion, for small
\tau
and large
N
, of the mean and covariance of the stationary distribution of the parameters
A
and
b
, trained via (
9
), in terms of the noise level
\sigma
and data geometry
C_{\mathrm{data}}
. The proof is given in Appendix
A.1
.

Theorem 1

(Optimization and generalization errors in SGD for denoising score matching)
.

Under Assumption
1
, for fixed stepsize
\tau<\frac{2}{\max\left(\lambda_{max}+\sigma^{2},1\right)},
the SGD iterates (
9
) converge to a stationary distribution on the parameters
A
and
b
. At stationarity,
b=\kappa+A\mu_{\mathrm{data}}
, with
\kappa
and
A
uncorrelated random variables with means

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\kappa]=o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[A]=C_{\sigma}^{-1}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)

and covariances (denoting
P_{\sigma}\coloneqq C_{\sigma}^{-1}C_{\mathrm{data}}
and
Q_{\sigma}\coloneqq C_{\sigma}^{-2}C_{\mathrm{data}}
)

\begin{split}\operatorname{Cov}(\kappa)&=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}}P_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{N}Q_{\sigma}+\frac{\tau}{N\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\langle P_{\sigma},C_{\mathrm{data}}\rangle\operatorname{Id}-P_{\sigma}^{2}\right)+o\left(\tau,\frac{1}{N}\right)\end{split}

\begin{split}\operatorname{Cov}(A)=\frac{\tau}{2\sigma^{2}}P_{\sigma}\otimes\operatorname{Id}+\frac{1}{N}\left(Q_{\sigma}\otimes Q_{\sigma}\right)(I_{d^{2}}+P_{trans})-\frac{\tau}{N\sigma^{2}}P_{\sigma}^{2}\otimes\operatorname{Id}+o\left(\tau,\frac{1}{N}\right).\end{split}

P_{trans}
denotes the transpose in the vectorized space
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}}
i.e.
P_{trans}[vec(X)]=vec(X^{\top})
.

The bias of SGD thus arises from the covariances of
A
and
b
. We expressed these covariances as the sum of three terms, respectively, in
O(\tau)
,
O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
, and
O\left(\frac{\tau}{N}\right)
.
Note that the constants in each term depend only on the geometry of the data, characterized by
C_{\mathrm{data}}
, and on the noise standard deviation
\sigma
. In particular, the covariances of
\kappa
and
A
are diagonal, respectively in the eigenbasis of
C_{\mathrm{data}}
and in the eigenvector outer-product basis
(u_{i}\otimes u_{j}^{\top})_{ij}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d^{2}\times d^{2}}
, with diagonal coefficients that depend (above
\tau
and
N
), on the eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
of
C_{\mathrm{data}}
(the power spectrum) and on
\sigma
.

Langevin and diffusion model sampling errors

In this section, we present a parallel analysis of the sampling errors introduced by Langevin dynamics (Section
4.1
) and diffusion models (Section
4.2
) with linear scores. For each algorithm, Theorems
2
and
3
provide similar closed-form asymptotic expressions for the resulting Wasserstein error, capturing the effects of: (i) discretization due to finite stepsize, (ii) nonzero noise level or finite diffusion time, and (iii) inexact linear score functions. These results are then specialized, in Corollaries
1
and
2
, to a linear score trained by denoising score matching, based on the analysis from the previous section.

4.1
Langevin sampling error

We first consider the Unadjusted Langevin (ULA) sampling algorithm (
3
) with constant stepsize
\gamma>0
and with the unknown true score
\nabla\log p_{\mathrm{data}}
replaced by the linear score
v(x,\theta)=-Ax+b
.
When
A
is symmetric, this is equivalent to assuming Gaussian data with mean
\mu=A^{-1}b
and covariance
A^{-1}
.
We first analyze the sampling error of this algorithm arising from both the discretization of the Langevin dynamics and the inexact approximation of the score function.

Discretization error

The ULA iterates (
3
) then sample a Gaussian distribution with explicit mean and variance given in the following Lemma. The discretization error
appears in the covariance of the sampled distribution, which differs from
A^{-1}
due to the positive stepsize
\gamma>0
. This is not a new result, but an important result for the rest of our analysis. The covariance is written using the inverse of the Lyapunov operator
L^{\gamma}_{C}
, defined, for
C\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\gamma>0
by
L^{\gamma}_{C}[X]=CX+XC^{\top}-\gamma CXC^{\top}
.

Lemma 1

(ULA solution with linear score, proof in Appendix
B.1
))
.

Assuming
\operatorname{Re}(\operatorname{eig}(A))>0
, and
{\gamma<\frac{2}{\lambda_{max}(A)}}
, the ULA algorithm (
3
) with linear score
v(x,\theta)=-Ax+b
converges to

q=\mathcal{N}(A^{-1}b,\Sigma^{\gamma}_{A})\quad\text{with}\quad\Sigma^{\gamma}_{A}\coloneqq(L^{\gamma}_{A})^{-1}[2\operatorname{Id}].

Score estimation error

We now analyze the impact of a small perturbation of the score on the above asymptotic distribution. We saw in Section
3
, that under the Gaussian assumption
1
, the optimal score for the denoising score matching loss corresponds to
A^{*}=C_{\sigma}^{-1}
and
b^{*}=C_{\sigma}^{-1}\mu_{\mathrm{data}}
. Instead of using this true score, and following the form of the error on
b
identified in Theorem
1
, we suppose that we have its following random perturbed version

v_{\sigma}(x,\theta)=-A_{\varepsilon}x+b_{\varepsilon},\quad\text{with}\quad A_{\varepsilon}=A^{*}+\varepsilon\Delta,\quad b_{\varepsilon}=b^{*}+\varepsilon(\delta+\Delta\mu_{\mathrm{data}}),

(10)

where
\Delta
and
\delta
are uncorrelated and zero-mean random variables taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Using the score (
10
), applying Lemma
1
, we get that, for small enough
\gamma
, the ULA iterates sample the invariant Gaussian distribution

Theorem 2

For a small enough stepsize

Diffusion model sampling error

We now conduct a similar error analysis on the discretized diffusion sampling algorithm (

Discretization error

The discretized version (

In this work, we do not explicitly study the discretization error of diffusion models. However,
[

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[f(y_{k})]-\mathbb{E}[f(y_{t_{k}})]=\gamma\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\psi_{s}(y_{s})ds+o(\gamma)

[
20

. With this result, we get that the discretization error on the mean and covariance of the sampled distribution is of order

Lemma 2

(Discretized diffusion with linear score, proof in Appendix

Under Assumption

The explicit calculation of the above matrices

Score estimation error

The objective of this section is to determine the influence of

and covariance

Conclusion

This work provides a first analytical step toward understanding how the data geometry, encoded by the power spectrum, interacts with key parameters of Langevin and diffusion model sampling pipelines. While limited to linear models and Gaussian data, the framework uncovers structural dependencies that offer valuable guidance about the trade-off to select various parameters such as the noise parameter of Langevin or the stopping times of diffusion models. Extending these results to nonlinear neural networks remains an open and difficult challenge.

Lemma 3

in

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{N}=\mu+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mu^{(1)}+\frac{1}{N}\mu^{(2)}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)\quad\text{where}\quad\mu^{(1)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,C_{\mathrm{data}})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}[\mu^{(2)}]=0

We can also get a similar central limit result for the empirical covariance.

(ULA solution with linear score))

{\gamma<\frac{2}{\lambda_{max}(A)}}

Proposition 2

(Second order Taylor expansion of the Bures distance)
.

For
\Sigma,H_{0}\in\operatorname{Sym}^{++}(d)
, let

\displaystyle=\mathcal{B}^{2}(\Sigma,H_{0})+\varepsilon\operatorname{Tr}\left({H_{1}}\right)-2\varepsilon\operatorname{Tr}\left({L_{\left({\Sigma}^{1/2}H_{0}{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}}^{-1}\left[{\Sigma}^{1/2}H_{1}{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right]}\right)

\displaystyle+2\varepsilon^{2}\operatorname{Tr}\left({L_{\left({\Sigma}^{1/2}H_{0}{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}}^{-1}\left[\left(L_{\left({\Sigma}^{1/2}H_{0}{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right)^{1/2}}^{-1}\left[{\Sigma}^{1/2}H_{1}{\Sigma}^{1/2}\right]\right)^{2}\right]}\right)+o(\varepsilon^{2})

Proof.

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}\left({\Sigma+H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1}-2\left(\Sigma^{{}^{1/2}}(H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1})\Sigma^{{}^{1/2}}\right)^{1/2}}\right)

(48)

In order to prove the result, we need to calculate the second-order expansion of the matrix square root.

Lemma 8

(Second order Taylor expansion of the matrix square root)
.

For

, let

such that
{H_{0}\pm H_{1}\in\operatorname{Sym}^{++}(d)}

\displaystyle(H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1})^{1/2}=H_{0}^{1/2}+\varepsilon L_{H_{0}^{1/2}}^{-1}[H_{1}]-\varepsilon^{2}L_{H_{0}^{1/2}}^{-1}\left[\left(L_{H_{0}^{1/2}}^{-1}[H_{1}]\right)^{2}\right]+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

Proof.

We consider the second-order development

\displaystyle(H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=H_{0}^{1/2}+\varepsilon X_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}X_{2}+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

Then by taking the square on both sides,

\displaystyle H_{0}+\varepsilon H_{1}

\displaystyle=H_{0}+\varepsilon(H_{0}^{1/2}X_{1}+X_{1}H_{0}^{1/2})+\varepsilon^{2}(H_{0}^{1/2}X_{2}+X_{2}H_{0}^{1/2}+X_{1}^{2})+o(\varepsilon^{2})

we thus get

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=L_{H_{0}^{1/2}}^{-1}[H_{1}]

\displaystyle X_{2}

\displaystyle=-L_{H_{0}^{1/2}}^{-1}[X_{1}^{2}]

∎

\displaystyle\left.\left.-\varepsilon^{2}L_{\Sigma^{1/2}H_{0}\Sigma^{1/2}}^{-1}\left[\left(L_{\Sigma^{1/2}H_{0}\Sigma^{1/2}}^{-1}[\Sigma^{1/2}H_{1}\Sigma^{1/2}]\right)^{2}\right]\right)\right)+o(\varepsilon^{2}).

which simplifies to the desired result.

Order

Calculation of

:

Reminding that for

,

, the discretized stochastic process writes:

By unrolling the recursion, we obtain:

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{k,0}y_{0}+\gamma\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\Psi_{k,\ell}r_{\ell}+\sum_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\Psi_{k,\ell}\sqrt{2\alpha\xi_{T-t_{\ell}}\gamma_{k}}\cdot w_{\ell},

\displaystyle=\Psi_{k,0}y_{0}+\phi_{k}+\eta_{k}

with
\phi_{k}
deterministic and

\displaystyle\eta_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Psi_{k,i}\sqrt{2\alpha\xi_{T-t_{i}}\gamma}\cdot w_{i}

which follows a Gaussian distribution with zero-mean, independent of
y_{0}
.
Thus

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(y^{(0)}_{k},y^{(0)}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\Psi_{k,0}\operatorname{Cov}(y_{0})\Psi_{\ell,0}+\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}].

Using independence between the
(w_{i})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=2\alpha\gamma\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\sum_{l=0}^{j-1}\Psi_{k,i}\Psi_{\ell,l}\sqrt{\xi_{T-t_{i}}\xi_{T-t_{l}}}\mathbb{E}[w_{i}w_{l}]

\displaystyle=2\alpha\gamma\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\Psi_{k,i}\Psi_{\ell,i}\xi_{T-t_{i}}

\displaystyle=2\alpha\gamma\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{k},t_{i}}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{\ell},t_{i}}\xi_{T-t_{i}}

where we remind that

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{t,s}

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(Q_{s}-Q_{t})}\left(\Sigma_{T-t}\Sigma_{T-s}^{-1}\right)^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\left(\operatorname{Id}-\frac{\gamma}{2}(H_{t}-H_{s})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\gamma).

\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}]
is thus the left-endpoint rectangle discretization, with interval
\gamma
, of the integral:

\displaystyle 2\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{\ell}}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{k},s}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{\ell},s}\xi_{T-s}ds

and we have, from Lemma
9
, at zeroth-order in
\gamma

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{\ell}}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{k},s}\hat{\Psi}_{t_{\ell},s}\xi_{T-s}ds+\mathcal{O}(\gamma)

(93)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\left(\Sigma_{T-t_{k}}\Sigma_{T-t_{\ell}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}e^{-\alpha(Q_{t_{k}}+Q_{t_{\ell}})}\int_{0}^{t_{\ell}}e^{2\alpha Q_{s}}\xi_{T-s}\Sigma_{T-s}^{-(\alpha+1)}ds+\mathcal{O}(\gamma).

(94)

To solve the first integral, we use that

\displaystyle d[e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}]

\displaystyle=e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}d[\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}]-2\alpha\beta_{T-t}e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=2\alpha e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}(\xi_{T-t}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-1}+\beta_{T-t}\text{Id})-2\alpha\beta_{T-t}e^{2\alpha t}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle=2\alpha\xi_{T-t}e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-(\alpha+1)}

to get

\displaystyle 2\alpha\int_{0}^{t_{k}}\xi_{T-s}e^{2\alpha Q_{s}}\Sigma_{T-s}^{-(\alpha+1)}ds=\int_{0}^{t_{k}}d[e^{2\alpha Q_{s}}\Sigma_{T-s}^{-\alpha}]=e^{2\alpha Q_{t}}\Sigma_{T-t}^{-\alpha}-\Sigma_{T}^{-\alpha}.

And thus (
94
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}]

\displaystyle=e^{-\alpha(Q_{t_{k}}+Q_{t_{\ell}})}\left(\Sigma_{T-{t_{k}}}\Sigma_{T-{t_{\ell}}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\left(e^{2\alpha Q_{t_{\ell}}}\Sigma_{T-{t_{\ell}}}^{-\alpha}-\Sigma_{T}^{-\alpha}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\gamma).

The cross covariance
\operatorname{Cov}(y^{(0)}_{k},y^{(0)}_{\ell})
then writes

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(y^{(0)}_{k},y^{(0)}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\Psi_{k,0}\operatorname{Cov}(y_{0})\Psi_{\ell,0}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}[\eta_{k}\eta_{\ell}^{\top}]

(95)

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(B_{T-t_{k}}-B_{T-t_{\ell}})}E_{T-t_{k},T-t_{\ell}}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma),

(96)

where we define, for

\begin{split}E_{t,s}&=e^{-2\alpha(B_{T}-B_{s})}\Sigma_{t}^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\Sigma_{T}^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\operatorname{Cov}(y_{0})\Sigma_{T}^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\Sigma_{s}^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\\
&+(\Sigma_{t}\Sigma_{s})^{\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\left(\Sigma_{s}^{-\alpha}-e^{-2\alpha(B_{T}-B_{s})}\Sigma_{T}^{-\alpha}\right).\end{split}

For perfect initialization of the covariance
\operatorname{Cov}(y_{0})=\Sigma_{T}
,

simplifies to

\displaystyle E_{t,s}

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{t}\left(\Sigma_{t}\Sigma_{s}^{-1}\right)^{(\alpha-1)/2}

In other words,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}(y^{(0)}_{k},y^{(0)}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=e^{\alpha(B_{r_{k}}-B_{r_{\ell}})}\hat{E}^{\gamma}_{r_{k},r_{\ell}}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})

where,

\displaystyle\hat{E}^{\gamma}_{r_{k},r_{\ell}}=E_{r_{k},r_{\ell}}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma)

Plugging (
95
) into (

), we get the expected second-order expansion of the covariance.

. Using that
{\lambda^{r}_{i}=s_{r}^{2}(\lambda^{r}_{i}+\sigma_{r}^{2}\operatorname{Id})}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(0)}_{r}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\left(1-s_{r}\sqrt{1+\frac{\sigma_{r}^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}}\right)^{2}+(1-s_{r})^{2}\left\|{\mu}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-s_{r})^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}-(1-s_{r})\sigma_{r}^{2}d+\sigma_{r}^{4}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{4\lambda_{i}}+(1-s_{r})^{2}\left\|{\mu}\right\|^{2}+o((1-s_{r})^{2},\sigma_{r}^{4})
