AVF Occlusion Unveils True Hemodynamics
Authors: Kenneth S. Paik, Aderonke O. Adeniyi, Priyesh A. Patel, Swethika Sundaravel, Olivia Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHigh-flow hemodialysis arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) can augment measured cardiac output and confound transplant hemodynamic assessment.Case SummaryA 58-year-old man with end-stage heart failure and end-stage renal disease awaiting simultaneous heart-kidney transplantation had recurrent presyncope, volume intolerance, and ventricular ectopy despite preserved cardiac output on previous right heart catheterization with AVF occlusion using a blood pressure cuff. History of Presentation

A 58-year-old man with long-standing biventricular end-stage heart failure (HF) (left ventricular ejection fraction <15%), World Health Organization group 2 pulmonary hypertension, end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and a mature left upper-extremity hemodialysis arteriovenous fistula (AVF), who was receiving home dobutamine at 3 μg/kg/min, presented for a third hospitalization within 4 weeks with presyncope, volume overload, and frequent ventricular ectopy. He was listed as a United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) Status 4 by exception for simultaneous heart-kidney transplantation (SHKT) after symptomatic improvement on dobutamine. However, previous hemodynamic testing without dobutamine continued to show preserved cardiac output (CO) and cardiac index (CI), despite AVF occlusion by inflation of a blood pressure cuff until no thrill was palpable.

On admission, blood pressure was 89/75 mm Hg (mean arterial pressure 80 mm Hg), heart rate 92 beats/min, respiratory rate 15 breaths/min, and temperature 35.6 °C. He was diaphoretic with jugular venous distention to 15 cm H2O, tepid extremities, and trace lower-extremity edema. Laboratory testing demonstrated mild hyponatremia (sodium 132 mmol/L), ESRD with azotemia (creatinine 5.85 mg/dL, estimated glomerular filtration rate 10 mL/min/1.73 m2, blood urea nitrogen 87 mg/dL), and elevated B-type natriuretic peptide (1,602 pg/mL). Telemetry and implantable cardioverter-defibrillator interrogation showed frequent ventricular ectopy and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia.

Past Medical History

His history included advanced HF requiring ambulatory inotropic support, pulmonary hypertension, ESRD requiring hemodialysis through a mature upper-extremity AVF, and active SHKT listing. The AVF had a palpable thrill, but its flow had not previously been quantified during invasive hemodynamic assessment.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included decompensated advanced HF with true low-output physiology, AVF-mediated high-output physiology, mixed low-output and shunt-augmented physiology, arrhythmia-related presyncope, or residual volume overload. The key diagnostic uncertainty was whether measured CO and derived pulmonary vascular indices reflected intrinsic cardiac reserve or were distorted by high-flow AVF shunting.

Investigations

Right heart catheterization (RHC) was performed after dobutamine was held. To avoid the limitations of subjective thrill disappearance, AVF compression was performed with real-time duplex ultrasound confirmation. Baseline duplex-estimated AVF flow was approximately 2.8 L/min. Hemodynamic measurements were obtained after 1 minute of sustained manual AVF compression, during which duplex ultrasound confirmed near-complete cessation of access flow to <300 mL/min. With confirmed occlusion, Fick CO decreased from 5.2 to 3.2 L/min and Fick CI decreased from 2.7 to 1.6 L/min/m2. Thermodilution CO similarly decreased from 4.8 to 3.2 L/min, and thermodilution CI decreased from 2.5 to 1.7 L/min/m2. Pulmonary artery oxygen saturation fell from 65% to 49%, cardiac power output decreased from 0.78 to 0.58 W, and systemic vascular resistance rose from 983 to 1,650 dyn·s·cm−5. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) decreased from 2.1 to 1.2 WU, transpulmonary gradient (TPG) decreased from 10 to 4 mm Hg, and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) increased from 23 to 32 mm Hg.

Management

The duplex-confirmed occlusion study reframed the patient's physiology. Relatively normal baseline CO reflected AVF augmentation rather than adequate intrinsic forward flow. The marked decreases in pulmonary artery oxygen saturation, Fick and thermodilution CO and CI, and cardiac power output during Doppler-confirmed shunt interruption supported clinically meaningful low-output HF physiology. The concomitant improvement in TPG and PVR suggested that the baseline pulmonary vascular profile was at least partly flow-mediated rather than indicative of fixed pulmonary vascular disease. The rise in PCWP during occlusion, particularly with increased afterload, further suggested limited left-sided reserve.

These findings were reviewed during a multidisciplinary discussion involving advanced HF and surgical teams. Because the patient poorly tolerated inotropic therapy because of symptomatic ventricular ectopy and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, the team planned temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) with an Impella 5.5 as a bridge to SHKT. Temporary MCS eliminated the need for inotropes, reduced ventricular arrhythmias, and provided a stable platform. His hemodynamics post-AVF occlusion supported the escalation to UNOS Status 2 with the temporary MCS device.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The immediate outcome of the diagnostic procedure was clarification of transplant-relevant hemodynamics. Duplex-confirmed AVF occlusion aligned invasive data with the patient's clinical low-output syndrome and provided more accurate estimates for MCS selection and SHKT planning. The patient remained clinically stable on temporary MCS support without hypotension or end-organ dysfunction and underwent successful SHKT after 37 days at UNOS Status 2. The AVF was ligated and excised by the vascular surgery team at the time of heart transplantation.

Discussion

Hemodialysis AVFs create a low-resistance circuit that increases venous return and may substantially elevate measured CO.1,2 In patients with preserved cardiac reserve, this physiology can be well tolerated; however, in advanced HF, high-flow access may obscure a low-output state and complicate interpretation of RHC.1,2 Previous studies and contemporary reviews have shown that temporary AVF compression can reduce measured CO and that high-flow access may contribute to high-output HF and pulmonary hypertension.3,4 This case extends that concept to transplant candidacy by showing that a preserved baseline CI can be misleading when a large shunt contributes substantially to measured output. Objective methods should, therefore, be considered to demonstrate nearly complete occlusion and define the severity of HF.

The most important feature of this case is not simply that AVF occlusion changed hemodynamics, but that the occlusion was objectively verified. Traditional bedside maneuvers, such as cuff inflation until thrill disappearance, assume complete shunt interruption. In this patient, a previous study using that approach did not demonstrate a low-output state, whereas duplex-guided compression confirmed reduction of AVF flow and unmasked a major decrease in CI. Without real-time confirmation, incomplete occlusion could falsely reassure clinicians that intrinsic CO is adequate, as originally happened with this patient.

The pulmonary hemodynamic changes were also clinically relevant. Baseline PVR was not severely elevated, but during confirmed occlusion the TPG and PVR decreased further, supporting a flow-dependent component to the PVR measurements. For transplant candidates, this distinction matters because fixed pulmonary vascular disease carries different implications from reversible, flow-mediated pulmonary hypertension.4 At the same time, the increase in PCWP during occlusion demonstrated that AVF occlusion did not simply normalize hemodynamics; instead, it revealed limited cardiac reserve and reduced forward flow under higher systemic vascular resistance.

This case supports a practical diagnostic approach for patients with advanced HF with high-flow hemodialysis access: measure access flow, perform RHC with standardized AVF occlusion, confirm occlusion with duplex ultrasound, and interpret derived indices in the context of shunt status. Standardized duplex-based protocols may improve reproducibility, particularly when decisions involve inotropes, MCS, pulmonary hypertension assessment, or transplant listing.

Conclusions

In patients with advanced HF with hemodialysis AVFs, measured CO and derived pulmonary vascular indices can be misleading when shunt flow contributes substantially to systemic output.
