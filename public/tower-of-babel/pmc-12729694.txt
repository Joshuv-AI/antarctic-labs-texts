Association between age and angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage: a secondary analysis
Authors: Jianhui Huang, Cunrong Chen, Tianlai Lin, Zhirong Ding, Rongda Cai, Youli Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAge is defined as the patient’s chronological age (in years) at hospital admission. The association between age and angiographic vasospasm remains controversial. This study aimed to explore the association between age and angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH).MethodsA secondary analysis was performed based on a retrospective study conducted from 2010 to 2015 in the intensive care unit (ICU) of a French university hospital. Factors associated with angiographic vasospasm were identified using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. The non-linear relationship between age and angiographic vasospasm was assessed using restricted cubic spline regression, and the threshold effect was evaluated using a two-piecewise linear regression model.ResultsThe study included 231 mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH. Their median age was 56 years (range, 19–84 years). Patients were divided into two groups based on the median age of 56 years: younger (< 56 years) and older (≥ 56 years). The rate of angiographic vasospasm was 34.63% (N = 80). After adjusting for potential confounders, patients in the older group were less likely to develop angiographic vasospasm [odds ratio (OR), 0.40; 95% confidence interval (CI), 0.19–0.85; p = 0.017] compared with those in the younger group. After adjusting for confounders, when age was ≤ 53 years, the risk of angiographic vasospasm increased by 13% for each year increase in age (OR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.05–1.22; p = 0.002). For age > 53 years, the risk of angiographic vasospasm decreased by 17% for each year increase in age (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.77–0.89; p < 0.001).ConclusionAge was non-linearly associated with the risk of angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH.

Background

The global incidence of subarachnoid hemorrhage is estimated to be 6.1 cases per 100,000 person-years [1, 2]. It has been reported that 38.5% of patients with aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) require mechanical ventilation, with an in-hospital mortality rate as high as 54.6% [3].

Angiographic vasospasm is a serious but common complication after aSAH [4]. Previous studies have produced inconsistent results regarding the association between age and the risk of angiographic vasospasm. In this context, age is defined as the patient’s chronological age (in years) at hospital admission. Some studies have reported that older age is associated with a lower risk of angiographic vasospasm [5, 6] and that older age (e.g., > 65 years) has a protective effect [7, 8]. However, other studies have found no association between age and angiographic vasospasm [9, 10]. Therefore, the relationship remains controversial.

This study aimed to evaluate the association between age and angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH.

For patients in age groups at high risk for angiographic vasospasm, neurologic examinations and transcranial Doppler monitoring could be appropriately intensified to identify those who may require confirmatory imaging [11, 12]. Early detection and intervention for angiographic vasospasm may help improve patient prognosis [13].

Methods

Study design and participants

This secondary analysis used data from a retrospective study conducted in the intensive care unit (ICU) of a French university hospital between 2010 and 2015 [14]. A total of 236 patients with aSAH requiring mechanical ventilation were initially included. 5 patients with missing data on the duration of mechanical ventilation were excluded, resulting in a final cohort of 231. The research protocol (Q-2015–09-07) and the relevant ethical issues for the original study were published previously, and these were waived for this secondary analysis [14]. The study was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. Data were obtained from the Dryad Digital Repository (10.5061/dryad.47d7wm3b4). The dataset is made available under the Creative Commons Zero (CC0) 1.0 Universal Public Domain Dedication, which explicitly permits unrestricted reuse, reanalysis, and dissemination for any purpose. The original study explored risk factors for poor 1-year outcome (survival and degree of disability) [14], whereas our secondary analysis investigates the association between age and angiographic vasospasm.

Data collection

The data included sex, age (in years) at hospital admission, tobacco use, alcohol abuse, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, aneurysm location, Glasgow Coma Scale, World Federation of Neurosurgical Societies (WFNS) score, Fisher’s score, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), aneurysm-securing delay, aneurysm-securing procedure, intracerebral hemorrhage, early brain injury, hydrocephalus requiring external ventricular drain (EVD), rebleeding, angiographic vasospasm, length of ICU stay, and withdrawal of care. SAPS II scores were dichotomized at the median.

Angiographic vasospasm was defined as a reduction of ≥ 1/3 in arterial diameter, as assessed by computed tomography angiography or digital subtraction angiography [14]. Angiographic vasospasm could occur in all intracranial arteries, but most commonly affects the internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, and anterior communicating artery on the side ipsilateral to the ruptured aneurysm [11, 15, 16].

Transcranial Doppler monitoring was performed daily to detect ultrasonographic vasospasm [14]. If ultrasonographic vasospasm was suspected, computed tomography angiography or digital subtraction angiography was performed to confirm angiographic vasospasm [14].

Statistical analysis

Patients were divided into two groups based on the median age of 56 years: younger (< 56 years) and older (≥ 56 years). The median split is widely used in research as a nonparametric, data-driven approach that avoids bias introduced by selecting arbitrary thresholds [17, 18]. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). For categorical variables, the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore the association between age and angiographic vasospasm. Stratified logistic regression was used to examine the effect of age on angiographic vasospasm across different strata. Restricted cubic spline regression was used to assess the non-linear association between age and angiographic vasospasm. A two-piecewise logistic regression model was then applied to identify the threshold effect along the fitted curve [19, 20]. The inflection point was calculated automatically by the recursive method using the maximum-likelihood model [19, 20]. A log-likelihood ratio test (LRT) was also performed, and the one-line linear regression model was compared with the two-piecewise linear model [19, 20]. All statistical analyses were performed with EmpowerStats (www.empowerstats.com, X&Y solutions, Boston, Massachusetts, USA) and R software version 3.6.1 (http://www.r-project.org). A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Non-linear association and threshold effect of age on angiographic vasospasm

In both unadjusted and adjusted models, age showed a non-linear association with angiographic vasospasm (Fig. 1). After adjusting for confounders, the risk of angiographic vasospasm increased with age and then gradually declined, with the highest risk observed at 53 years. As shown in Table 5, before the age of 53 years, the risk increased by 13% for each additional year (OR, 1.13; 95% CI, 1.05–1.22). After the age of 53 years, the risk decreased by 17% for each additional year (OR, 0.83; 95% CI, 0.77–0.89). Angiographic vasospasm often occurred between days 4 and 14 after aSAH onset [6]. Discussion

In this study, the association between age and angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH was analyzed. The incidence of angiographic vasospasm was 34.63% (80 patients), consistent with previous studies [5, 6, 10, 21]. Risk factors for angiographic vasospasm were identified, including SAPS II, aneurysm-securing delay, aneurysm-securing procedure, early brain injury, hydrocephalus requiring EVD, and length of ICU stay; these findings were consistent with previous studies [22–25].

Among mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH, older patients were less likely to develop angiographic vasospasm. Furthermore, a non-linear association between age and the risk of angiographic vasospasm was observed. For patients aged ≤ 53 years, the risk of angiographic vasospasm increased with each additional year. For patients aged > 53 years, the risk decreased with each additional year.

The pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. In relatively young aSAH patients requiring mechanical ventilated, the risk of angiographic vasospasm increases with age, may reflect age-related declines in cerebrovascular autoregulation [26]. Additionally, this may be associated with the observation that the benefits of nimodipine in preventing cerebral vasospasm are greater in younger patients [27]. In relatively older patients, the risk of angiographic vasospasm may decrease with age. Potentially due to age-associated arteriosclerosis and an attenuated inflammatory response that can attenuate vascular reactivity to constrictive stimuli after aSAH [8]. Age-related arteriosclerosis may impair the contractility and elasticity of small arteries and arteriolar branches [28]. Vascular responsiveness to inflammatory mediators may also decrease with advancing age [29].

Angiographic vasospasm was defined as the narrowing of intracranial arteries [11, 15, 16]. It could occur in all intracranial arteries, but most commonly in the internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery, and anterior communicating artery on the side ipsilateral to the ruptured aneurysm [10, 30]. Since this study was a secondary analysis, it was unclear whether the original authors assessed all intracranial arteries or only the specific vascular territories associated with the ruptured aneurysm.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center retrospective cohort study, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader populations. Second, because the secondary analysis was restricted to patients with aSAH who required mechanical ventilation, the findings may not be generalizable to patients managed without mechanical ventilation. The findings still offer a reference for the association between age and angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients. Third, the very elderly subgroup was small (only 4 patients aged ≥ 80 years), which limited the applicability to this population. Fourth, despite adjustment for potential confounders, unmeasured variables may have influenced the results. Fifth, owing to limitations inherent in the primary dataset, the association between the modified Fisher’s score and angiographic vasospasm could not be assessed.

Conclusion

Age was non-linearly associated with angiographic vasospasm in mechanically ventilated patients with aSAH.
