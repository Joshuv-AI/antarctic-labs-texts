Singularities of the Lagrangian mean curvature flow at the critical Lagrangian phase
Arunima Bhattacharya, Ravi Shankar, Jeremy Wall, and Diego Yepez

Abstract
. We establish interior estimates for singularities of the Lagrangian mean curvature flow when the Lagrangian phase is critical, i.e., | Θ | ≥ ( n − 2 ) ​ π 2 |\Theta|\geq(n-2)\tfrac{\pi}{2} , and extend our results to the broader class of Lagrangian mean curvature type equations. Our gradient estimates require certain structural conditions, and we construct C α C^{\alpha} singular viscosity solutions to show that criticality of the phase is necessary, and that these conditions cannot be removed in dimension one. We also introduce a new method for proving C 2 , α C^{2,\alpha} estimates by exponentiating the arctangent operator into a concave one when | Θ | ≥ ( n − 2 ) ​ π 2 |\Theta|\geq(n-2)\tfrac{\pi}{2} and n > 2 n>2 .



1.
Introduction

We say that
u
solves a
Lagrangian mean curvature type equation
in the complex Euclidean space when

(1.1)

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=\Theta(x,u,Du)\in(-n\frac{\pi}{2},n\frac{\pi}{2}),

where
\lambda_{i}
’s are the eigenvalues of the Hessian of
u
. The quantity
\Theta
, determined by

(1.2)

\Theta=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i},

is called the Lagrangian phase or angle of the Lagrangian submanifold
L=(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. By Harvey-Lawson
[
HL82
]
, the Lagrangian phase determines the mean curvature vector of
L
:

(1.3)

\vec{H}=J\nabla_{g}\Theta,

where
\nabla_{g}
is the gradient operator for the induced metric
g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+u_{ik}\delta^{kl}u_{lk}
on
L
, and
J
is the
\frac{\pi}{2}
rotation matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. When the Lagrangian phase is constant, i.e.,
\Theta=c
, one gets the
special Lagrangian equation
. In this case,
H=0
, and
L
is a volume-minimizing submanifold in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
A dual form of the special Lagrangian equation is the Monge-Ampére equation
\det D^{2}u=c
.
This is the potential equation for special Lagrangian submanifolds in
(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n},dxdy)
as interpreted by Hitchin in
[
Hit97
]
.

We say that
u
solves the potential equation for prescribed
Lagrangian mean curvature
when

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=\Theta(x).

A family of Lagrangian submanifolds
X(x,t):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
evolves by
Lagrangian mean curvature flow
(LMCF) if it solves

(X_{t})^{\bot}=\Delta_{g}X=\vec{H}.

After a change of coordinates, one can locally write
X(x,t)=(x,Du(x,t))
, such that
\Delta_{g}X=(J\bar{\nabla}\Theta(x,t))^{\bot}
. This means a local potential
u(x,t)
evolves by the parabolic equation

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle u(x,0):=u(x).

Symmetry reductions of the Lagrangian mean curvature flow reduce the above local parabolic representation to an elliptic equation for
u(x)
, which models singularities of the mean curvature flow (see Chau-Chen-He
[
CCH12
]
). If
u(x)
solves

(1.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=s_{1}+s_{2}(x\cdot Du(x)-2u(x)),

then
X(x,t)=\sqrt{1-2s_{2}t}\,(x,Du(x))
is a
shrinker
or
expander
solution of the LMCF, if
s_{2}>0
or
s_{2}<0
respectively. If
u(x)
solves

(1.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}\cdot x+\gamma_{3}\cdot Du(x),

then
X(x,t)=(x,Du(x))+t(-\gamma_{3},\gamma_{2})
is a
translator
solution of the LMCF. If
u(x)
solves

(1.6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{i}=r_{1}+\frac{r_{2}}{2}(|x|^{2}+|Du(x)|^{2}),

then
X(x,t)=\exp(r_{2}tJ)(x,Du(x))
is a
rotator
solution of the LMCF.

Notation

Before we present our main results, we clarify some terminology.

I.

By
B_{R}
we denote a ball of radius
R
centered at the origin.

II.

We denote the oscillation of

in
B_{R}
by
\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}(u)
.

III.

Let
\Gamma_{R}=B_{R}\times u(B_{R})\times Du(B_{R})\subset B_{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}
be constants such that for
\Theta(x,z,p)
, we assume the following structure conditions

(1.7)

\displaystyle|\Theta_{x}|,|\Theta_{z}|,|\Theta_{p}|

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{1}(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u,\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}),

\displaystyle|\Theta_{xx}|,|\Theta_{xz}|,|\Theta_{xp}|,|\Theta_{zz}|,|\Theta_{zp}|

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{2}(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{R}}u,\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}),

are treated as independent of each other. Observe that this indicates that the above partial derivatives do not have any

Our main results are the following.

Theorem 1.1

(Hessian Estimates)
.

If

is a smooth solution of (

Theorem 1.2

(Gradient Estimates)
.

Let

|Du(0)|\leq C(n,\|\Theta_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1})})\left(1+(\text{osc}_{B_{1}}u)^{2}\right).

In Section

, we construct singular

.

For solutions of the Lagrangian mean curvature equation, Hessian estimates for convex smooth solutions with

Bhattacharya-Wall obtained a priori Hessian bounds for singularities of the LMCF and for the broader class of equations (

[

]
. In the supercritical range, one has

Let us give a brief overview of the methods used in our proofs. To prove gradient estimates, we follow the approach of
[
BMS22
]
. Since the phase depends on
(x,u(x),Du(x))
, the appearance of terms involving the gradient of the phase prevents direct application of earlier methods. We treat each gradient contribution separately and derive structural conditions under which a gradient estimate holds. We further construct
C^{\alpha}
singular viscosity solutions demonstrating that none of the conditions can be removed in dimension one, and that the criticality condition on the phase is necessary in higher dimensions.

It is interesting to note that despite the fact that the Lagrangian angles of these solutions can be interpreted as smooth functions on the ambient space
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, these viscosity solutions have no geometrical interpretation as Lagrangian submanifolds, and the mean curvature is not bounded. This is in contrast to the
C^{1,1}
Lagrangian submanifolds of the Lipschitz viscosity solutions in
[
MS23
]
, and the analytic submanifolds of the
C^{1,\alpha}
viscosity solutions in
[
NV10
,
WY13
]
. There are non-Dini viscosity solutions for the complex Monge-Ampère equation with constant coefficients in
[
WW25
]
.

The main difficulty in deriving Hessian estimates for (
1.1
) arises from the use of the Michael-Simon Sobolev inequality
[
MS73
, Theorem 2.1]
. Because
\Theta
depends on the gradient, substituting the mean curvature term into the Sobolev inequality introduces additional Hessian-dependent terms. To control these, we use an idea of
[
Din24
]
, which removes the Hessian dependence at the cost of introducing higher-order dependence on the gradient.

Once the Hessian is bounded, the main obstruction to obtaining
C^{2,\alpha}
estimates arises from the lack of concavity of the arctangent operator and the dependence of the phase on the potential and its gradient. To overcome this, we develop a new technique that exponentiates the arctangent operator at the critical value, exploiting the boundedness of the Hessian and the assumption that
n\geq 3
. Note that this approach had previously been available only for the supercritical phase, where the exponentiation method of
[
CPW17
,
CW19
]
relied on
\delta>0
, and no such method was known at the critical phase (
\delta=0
). While applicable in higher dimensions
n\geq 3
, Yuan’s counterexamples
[
Yua
]
show that the technique fails in dimension two. In this situation, an upwards rotation employed in Bhattacharya-Ogden
[
BO25
]
can yield the estimate. We conclude by presenting alternative approaches to derive the
C^{2,\alpha}
estimate in Remark
6.2
.

3.
Proof of the Gradient Estimates

Proof.

The method of this proof is inspired by
[
BMS22
]
. We denote
ca\leq b\leq Ca
by
a\sim b
. Let
M=\mathrm{osc}_{B_{1}}u
and replace
u
with
u-\inf_{B_{1}}u+M
so that
M\leq u\leq 2M
. We will use the test function
w=\eta|Du|+Au^{2}/2
where
\eta=1-|x|^{2}
and
A=3\sqrt{n}/M
. Suppose
w
achieves its maximum at
x_{0}\in\bar{B}_{1}
. If
x_{0}\in\partial B_{1}
, then we are done so suppose
x_{0}\in B_{1}
. Diagonalize
D^{2}u(x_{0})=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
. We will assume
u_{n}\geq|Du|/\sqrt{n}>0
and observe that for each
k
, at the maximum point
x_{0}
,

0=\partial_{k}w(x_{0})=\eta\frac{u_{k}\lambda_{k}}{|Du|}+\eta_{k}|Du|+Auu_{k}.

By taking
A=3\sqrt{n}/M
, we see that

(3.1)

\eta\lambda_{n}\frac{u_{n}}{|Du|}\in(-c(n),-C(n))|Du|.

Thus
\lambda_{n}<0
, and

\eta\sim\frac{|Du|}{|\lambda_{n}|}.

Since
|Du|\lesssim\eta|\lambda_{n}|
we may assume
|\lambda_{n}|>1
as otherwise we would be done.

At
x_{0}
, we also have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq g^{ij}\partial_{ij}w(x_{0})

(3.2)

\displaystyle=|Du|g^{ij}\eta_{ij}+2g^{ij}\eta_{i}|Du|_{j}+\eta g^{ij}|Du|_{ij}+Aug^{ij}u_{ij}+Ag^{ij}u_{i}u_{j}.

As in
[
BMS22
]
, we get

(3.3)

\displaystyle|Du|g^{ij}\eta_{ij}

\displaystyle=-2\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{j}^{2}}|Du|\gtrsim-\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}|Du|\sim\frac{\eta}{|\lambda_{n}|},

(3.4)

\displaystyle 2g^{ij}\eta_{i}|Du|_{j}

\displaystyle\overset{x_{0}}{\gtrsim}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+\lambda_{j}^{2}}\frac{u_{j}\lambda_{j}}{|Du|}\gtrsim-\frac{1}{|\lambda_{n}|},

(3.5)

\displaystyle Aug^{ij}u_{ij}

\displaystyle=Au\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{j}}{1+\lambda_{j}^{2}}\gtrsim-\frac{1}{|\lambda_{n}|},

(3.6)

\displaystyle Ag^{ij}u_{i}u_{j}

\displaystyle\geq A\frac{u_{n}^{2}}{1+\lambda_{n}^{2}}\sim A\frac{|Du|^{2}}{\lambda_{n}^{2}}\sim A\frac{\eta|Du|}{|\lambda_{n}|}.

The remaining term is

\displaystyle\eta g^{ij}|Du|_{ij}

\displaystyle=\eta g^{ij}\frac{u_{ijk}u_{k}}{|Du|}+\eta\sum_{j=1}^{n}g^{jj}\frac{(|Du|^{2}-u_{j}^{2})\lambda_{j}}{|Du|^{3}}

\displaystyle\geq\eta\frac{u_{k}}{|Du|}\partial_{k}\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\eta\frac{u_{k}}{|Du|}\left(\Theta_{x_{k}}(x,u,Du)+\Theta_{z}(x,u,Du)u_{k}+\Theta_{p_{k}}(x,u,Du)\lambda_{k}\right).

We note that by the argument in
[
BMS22
]
, we get that

(3.8)

\Theta-\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}\leq\frac{1}{|\lambda_{n}|}.

By the structure condition (
1.7
) on
\Theta
, we will here assume
|\Theta_{xx}|\leq\|\Theta_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1})}

\|\Theta_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1})}
is assumed independent of

and
Du

If we also assume (
1.8
), i.e.,

|\Theta_{x}|\lesssim_{\Theta_{xx}}\left(\Theta-\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

which is similar to the interpolation inequality
[

, Lemma 7.7.2]
, then

(3.9)

\eta\frac{u_{k}}{|Du|}\Theta_{x_{k}}\gtrsim_{\Theta_{xx}}-\eta|\lambda_{n}|^{-1/2}.

To bound the middle term of (
3.7
), we assume (
1.9
), that is,
\Theta_{z}\geq 0
. Note that such a condition plays a role in the
C^{0}
maximum principle used in existence and uniqueness theory.
Hence

(3.10)

\eta\frac{u_{k}^{2}}{|Du|}\Theta_{z}=\eta|Du|\Theta_{z}\geq 0.

To bound the final term of (
3.7
), we will assume (
1.10
), namely,

\lvert p\rvert\,\lvert D_{p}\Theta(x,z,p)\rvert\;\leq\;C\Bigl(\Theta(x,z,p)-\tfrac{(n-2)\pi}{2}\Bigr).

Then, using (
3.1
) we see that

\displaystyle\eta\frac{u_{k}\lambda_{k}}{|Du|}\Theta_{p_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\Theta_{p_{k}}(\eta_{k}|Du|+Auu_{k})

\displaystyle\geq-C(n)|Du\|D_{p}\Theta|

\displaystyle\geq-C(\Theta(x,z,p)-\tfrac{(n-2)\pi}{2})

(3.11)

\displaystyle\geq-C|\lambda_{n}|^{-1}.

Rearranging the terms of (
3.2
), using all of the above estimates (
3.3
), (
3.4
), (
3.5
), (
3.6
), and inserting (
3.9
), (
3.10
), and (
3.11

\eta|Du|\lesssim_{\Theta_{xx}}M(1+\eta|\lambda_{n}|^{1/2})\lesssim M(1+(\eta|Du|)^{1/2}).

By Young’s inequality, we then get

\eta|Du(x_{0})|\leq C(\|\Theta_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1})},n)(1+(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{1}}u)^{2})

which gives

|Du(0)|\leq w(0)\leq w(x_{0})\leq C(\|\Theta_{xx}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1})},n)(1+(\mathrm{osc}_{B_{1}}u)^{2}),

concluding the proof. ∎

4.
Singular Non-Lipschitz Viscosity Solutions

Using counterexamples, we show that
\Theta\geq(n-2)\pi/2
is necessary for Lipschitz regularity, and that none of the conditions in Theorem
1.2
can be removed in dimension
n=1
. In dimension
n=1
, we construct Hölder-but-not-Lipschitz viscosity solutions for
C^{2}
phases which fail at least one of each of the conditions (
1.8
), (
1.9
), and (
1.10
). In higher dimensions, the quadratic lifts of these
n=1
solutions provide non-Lipschitz viscosity solutions with subcritical phase.

We note that Hölder viscosity solutions are not known for the constant phase special Lagrangian equation, or even for
F(D^{2}u)=\Theta(x)
with
\Theta\in C^{1,\alpha}
. The reason is that the even function
u=|x_{1}|^{\alpha}
is not a supersolution. More generally, homogeneous functions with sign-constant determinant will not be admissible
[
Moo22
]
.

If
Du
dependence in
\Theta
is allowed, then we can instead look for odd functions
u=\text{sign}(x_{1})|x_{1}|^{\alpha}
. If we identify this as a viscosity solution of
F(D^{2}u)=\Theta(x)
, then
\Theta(x)
must be discontinuous at
x_{1}=0
. But since
Du
is itself singular, there are smooth combinations of
x_{1},u,
and
Du
which agree with
D^{2}u
.

4.1.
Fails condition (
1.9
)

Let
u(x_{1})=\operatorname{sign}(x_{1})\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\alpha}
for
0<\alpha<1
. Then

\displaystyle u^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\alpha(\alpha-1)\,\operatorname{sign}(x_{1})\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\alpha-2},

\displaystyle u^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\alpha\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\alpha-1}.

Hence

u\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\;=\;\alpha^{q}\,\operatorname{sign}(x_{1})\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\alpha(1+q)-q}\;=\;\alpha^{q-1}(\alpha-1)^{-1}\,u^{\prime\prime}

provided
\alpha(1+q)-q=\alpha-2
, i.e.,
q\alpha=q-2
, so
\alpha=(q-2)/q
. Therefore
u
is a viscosity solution of

\arctan(u^{\prime\prime})\;=\;-\,\arctan\!\bigl((1-\alpha)\,\alpha^{1-q}\,u\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\bigr).

This clearly fails condition (
1.9
), due to the sign of
\Theta_{u}
.

More generally, for integers
m\geq 0
,

u^{2m+1}\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\;=\;C\,\operatorname{sign}(x_{1})\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{\alpha(2m+1+q)-q}\;=\;-C_{1}\,u^{\prime\prime}

if
\alpha-2=\alpha(2m+1+q)-q
, i.e.,
\alpha=(q-2)/(2m+q)
. In this case,

\arctan(u^{\prime\prime})\;=\;-\,\arctan\!\bigl(C_{1}^{-1}\,u^{2m+1}\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\bigr).

4.2.
Fails condition (
1.8
)

For integers
m\geq 0
,

x_{1}^{2m+1}\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\;=\;\operatorname{sign}(x_{1})\,\lvert x_{1}\rvert^{2m+1+q\alpha-q}\;=\;C\,u^{\prime\prime}

if
2m+1+q\alpha-q=\alpha-2
, i.e.,
\alpha=(q-3-2m)/(q-1)
. In this case,

\arctan(u^{\prime\prime})\;=\;-\,\arctan\!\bigl(C^{-1}\,x_{1}^{2m+1}\,(u^{\prime})^{q}\bigr).

This fails the uniform
C^{2}
bound in
x
, since taking two derivatives yields additional chain rule
factors of
(u^{\prime})^{q}
, which range over the entire real line. Hence the resulting function cannot be
bounded in the variable
x_{1}^{2m+1}(u^{\prime})^{q}
.

4.3.
Fails conditions (
1.10
) and (
1.8
)

Let
L=-\log\lvert x_{1}\rvert
be a positive even function near
x_{1}=0
, and set
u^{\prime}(x_{1})=L^{1/2}
with

u(x_{1})\sim x_{1}L^{1/2}+o\bigl(x_{1}L^{1/2}\bigr).

Then

2u^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\;=\;-\,L^{-1/2}\,x_{1}^{-1}\;=\;-x_{1}\,L^{-1/2}\,x_{1}^{-2}\;=\;-\,x_{1}\,e^{2(u^{\prime})^{2}}/u^{\prime}.

Therefore, near
x_{1}=0
,
u
is a viscosity solution of an equation of the form

\arctan u^{\prime\prime}\;=\;-\,\arctan\!\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\,x_{1}\,g(u^{\prime})\right],\qquad g(u^{\prime})=e^{2(u^{\prime})^{2}}/u^{\prime}\quad\text{near }\lvert u^{\prime}\rvert=\infty.

This fails condition (
1.10
) due to Gaussian growth, and it also fails condition (
1.8
).

4.4.
Higher dimensions

Let
v(x_{1})
be the non-Lipschitz viscosity solution of Section
4.2
constructed to
\arctan v^{\prime\prime}=\Theta_{1}(x_{1},u_{1})\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
. For
a_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
, we define a solution in separated variables

u(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})=v(x_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}^{2}/2.

Then
u
is a non-Lipschitz viscosity solution of

(4.1)

\displaystyle F(D^{2}u)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{1}(x_{1},u_{1})+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\arctan a_{i}=:\Theta(x,Du).

Near
\Theta_{1}=-\pi/2
, it is not possible to choose
a_{i}
so that
\Theta\geq(n-2)\pi/2
, so the total phase
\Theta
attains subcritical values. This shows that
\Theta(x,u,Du)\geq(n-2)\pi/2
cannot be dropped from the gradient estimate.

4.5.
Remarks

It is an interesting question to find a solution that fails (
1.10
) alone, but it must be noted that (
1.10
) cannot be dropped. It is also interesting to see whether any of these conditions can be dropped in higher dimensions, provided that
\Theta\geq(n-2)\pi/2
. The
n=1
lifts drop below the critical value
(n-2)\pi/2
near the singularities.

From a geometric point of view, if
\Theta=\Theta(x,Du)
, then the mean curvature is the normal projection
H=(J\nabla_{(x,y)}\Theta(x,y))^{\bot}
of the smooth ambient gradient
\nabla\Theta=(\Theta_{x},\Theta_{y})
restricted to
(x,Du)
. Note that the mean curvature is not bounded for these examples near the singularity.

Our examples reflect that smooth such data
\Theta(x,y)
for the mean curvature of a Lagrangian submanifold is not sufficient to make the submanifold
(x,Du)
continuous, or even bounded. Note that although the viscosity solutions in
[
MS23
]
are Lipschitz and semi-convex, the submanifolds themselves are
C^{1,1}
. The solutions in
[
NV10
,
WY13
]
are
C^{1,\alpha}
, while the submanifolds are analytic. Our examples are not well defined in any sense except the viscosity one.

where
L_{\sigma_{k}}
is the matrix
(\frac{\partial\sigma_{k}}{\partial u_{ij}})
. As
\sigma_{k}>0
for each
0\leq k\leq n-1
, we use a cuttoff function
\phi
such that
0\leq\phi\leq 1
,
\phi=1
on
B_{2}
, and
|D\phi|<2
. We get

\displaystyle\int_{B_{2}}b\sigma_{k}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{B_{3}}\phi b\sigma_{k}dx=\int_{B_{3}}\phi b\frac{1}{k}\text{div}(L_{\sigma_{k}}Du)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\int_{B_{3}}-\langle bD\phi+\phi Db,L_{\sigma_{k}}Du\rangle dx

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}}\bigg[\int_{B_{3}}b\sigma_{k-1}

(5.18)

\displaystyle\hskip 108.405pt+\int_{B_{3}}[|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}+\text{tr}(g^{ij})]\sqrt{\det g}dx\bigg]

where the last inequality comes from the argument in
[
WY14
]
.

Using (
5.13
) and (
5.16
), it follows that

(5.19)

\int_{B_{r}}\phi^{2}|\nabla_{g}b|^{2}dv_{g}\leq C(n,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(1+\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}})\int_{B_{r+1}}\text{tr}(g^{ij})\sqrt{\det g}dx.

Combining (
5.19
) and (
5.18
), we get the inductive inequality

\int_{B_{r}}b\sigma_{k}dx\leq C(n,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}}\left[\int_{B_{r+1}}b\sigma_{k-1}dx+(1+\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}})\int_{B_{r+2}}\text{tr}(g^{ij})\sqrt{\det g}dx\right].

Using this induction for each
\sigma_{k}
, with
0\leq k\leq n-1
, altogether we get

\displaystyle(b-2c_{0})(0)

\displaystyle\leq C(n,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(1+\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{r+n+2})})\Bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r+n+2})}^{j}

(5.20)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n+2}\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r+n+2})}^{j}\right)\int_{B_{r+n+2}}\text{tr}(g^{ij})\sqrt{\det g}dx\Bigg)

where we use that

\int_{B_{r}}bdx\leq C(n)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r})}

as in
[
WY14
]
.

In order to bound the remaining integral, we use the conformality identity (
5.17
) to get

(5.21)

\int_{B_{r}}\text{tr}(g^{ij})\sqrt{\det g}dx\leq C(n)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\int_{B_{r+1}}\sigma_{j}dx.

The argument above with
b=1
gives the inductive inequality

\int_{B_{r}}\sigma_{k}dx\leq C(n)\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r+1})}\int_{B_{r+1}}\sigma_{k-1}dx,

which gives

\int_{B_{r}}\text{tr}(g^{ij})\sqrt{\det g}dx\leq C(n)\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\|Du\|^{j}_{L^{\infty}(B_{r+n})}.

Plugging these into (
5.20
), and using Young’s inequality, we get

(b-2c_{0})(0)\leq C(n,\nu_{1},\nu_{2})(1+\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{2n+1})})\left[\|Du\|^{2n+1}_{L^{\infty}(B_{2n+1})}+1\right].

Adding
2c_{0}
across and exponentiating yields

|D^{2}u(0)|\leq C_{1}\exp{[C_{2}\|Du\|^{2n+3}_{L^{\infty}(B_{2n+1})}]}

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
depend on
n,\nu_{1}
, and
\nu_{2}
.

∎

6.
C^{2,\alpha}
Regularity

Once Hessian and gradient estimates are established, a final challenge remains in proving
C^{2,\alpha}
estimates. For general equations
F(D^{2}u,Du,u,x)=0
, higher regularity results require both concavity and uniform ellipticity
[
Kry83
]
. Without concavity, known results apply to uniformly elliptic equations of the form
F(D^{2}u)=f(x)
with interior estimates
[
Caf89
]
or convex level sets of the graph
[
CY00
]
.

Here we will prove that the arctangent operator can be modified to a concave operator in dimensions
n>2
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
f(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\arctan(\lambda_{j})
be defined on
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;F(D^{2}u)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{j}\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}\}
and suppose
u
is a solution of (
1.1
) on
B_{R}
, then there exists a constant
A
depending on
\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}
and
n>2
such that
-e^{-Af(\lambda)}
is a concave function.

Remark 6.1
.

Note this exponentiation technique does not work in two dimensions due to counterexamples of Yuan
[
Yua
]
.

Proof.

Using the calculation as in
[
CPW17
, Lemma 2.2]
, observe

(6.1)

e^{Af}\partial^{2}_{ij}e^{-Af}=-A^{2}f_{i}f_{j}-Af_{ij}=-A\left(\frac{A+2\lambda_{i}\delta_{ij}}{(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})(1+\lambda_{j}^{2})}\right):=-AH_{ij}

where

\det H_{ij}=\frac{1}{\prod(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})^{2}}(A2^{n-1}\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda)+2^{n}\sigma_{n}(\lambda)).

If
\lambda_{n}\geq 0
, then we are done, so suppose that
\lambda_{n}<0
. We show that
\sigma_{n-1}\geq C>0
. Let
\theta_{j}=\arctan(\lambda_{j})
and observe that

\frac{(n-2)\pi}{2}-\theta_{n}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\theta_{j}\leq(n-2)\arctan(\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})})+\theta_{n-1}.

Rearranging the above, noting that
-\theta_{n}>0
, and taking the tangent of both sides, we obtain

(6.2)

\lambda_{n-1}>\tan(T)

where
T=\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan(\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})})
. Note that
n>2
is crucial here.

Fix

(6.3)

\epsilon=\frac{s}{(n-1)}\quad\text{where}\quad s=\frac{\tan(T)}{2},

and suppose that
0>\lambda_{n}\geq-\epsilon
. We denote

\hat{\lambda}_{j}=\prod_{i\neq j}^{n}\lambda_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}}=\prod_{k\neq i,j}^{n}\lambda_{k}.

We then observe

\displaystyle\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\hat{\lambda}_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\hat{\lambda}_{n}-s\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\widehat{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\left[(\lambda_{j}-s)\widehat{\lambda_{j}\lambda_{n}}\right]}{n-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(n-1)(\lambda_{n-1}-s)(\lambda_{n-1})^{n-2}}{n-1}>\frac{1}{2}[\tan(T)]^{n-1}=C

where the last inequality comes from (
6.2
) and (
6.3
).

On the other hand, if
\lambda_{n}<-\epsilon
, we get

-\frac{2(n-1)}{\tan(T)}>\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}},

which combined with

\frac{(n-1)}{\tan(T)}>\frac{(n-1)}{\lambda_{n-1}}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}

gives

0>-\frac{(n-1)}{\tan(T)}>\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}}=\frac{\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda)}{\sigma_{n}(\lambda)}.

Multiplying across by
\sigma_{n}(\lambda)<0
flips the inequalities and we get

0<\frac{(n-1)(-\sigma_{n}(\lambda))}{\tan(T)}<\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda).

Noting that

-\sigma_{n}(\lambda_{n})\geq|\lambda_{n}|^{n}>\epsilon^{n}=\frac{[\tan(T)]^{n}}{2^{n}(n-1)^{n}}

gives
\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda)\geq C>0
.

Now, choose
A
large enough so that

\displaystyle\det H_{ij}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\prod(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})^{2}}(A2^{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\lambda)+2^{n}\sigma_{n}(\lambda))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\prod(1+\lambda_{i}^{2})^{2}}(A2^{n-1}C-2^{n}\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}^{n})>0,

that is,

A>\frac{2\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}}{C},

and this completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
u
be a
C^{1,1}
solution of (
1.1
) on
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{1/2})
with

|D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}u(0)|\leq C(\|u\|_{C^{2}(B_{1})},\nu_{1},\nu_{2},n)|x|^{\alpha}.

Corollary 6.1
.

Let
u
be a
C^{1,1}
solution of (
1.4
), (
1.5
), or (
1.6
) on
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{1/2})
with the same estimate above where
C
also depends on
s_{2}
for (
1.4
),
\gamma_{2},\gamma_{3}
for (
1.5
), or
r_{2}
for (
1.6
).

Proof.

We modify the second order, uniformly elliptic equation (
1.1
) to a concave one via exponentiation from Lemma
6.1
. Applying
[
CC03
, Cor. 1.3]
, we get
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{1/2})
with the estimate

|D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}u(0)|\leq C(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|e^{-A\Theta}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})})|x|^{\alpha}

where
C
depends on the uniform ellipticity constants and the dimension
n
.

By Taylor expanding the exponential, we get

\|e^{-A\Theta}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}\leq A\|\Theta\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}.

To bound
\|\Theta\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}
, we Taylor expand
F(x)=\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))
to get

\Theta(x_{1},u(x_{1}),Du(x_{1}))-\Theta(x_{2},u(x_{2}),Du(x_{2}))=DF(\xi)\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})

for
\xi
between
x_{1},x_{2}
, where

\displaystyle|DF(\xi)|

\displaystyle=|\Theta_{x}(\xi,u(\xi),Du(\xi))+\Theta_{z}(\xi,u(\xi),Du(\xi))Du(\xi)+\Theta_{p}(\xi,u(\xi),Du(\xi))D^{2}u(\xi)|

(6.4)

\displaystyle\leq\nu_{1}(1+\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+\|D^{2}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})})

which bounds
\|\Theta\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}
.

When
u
solves the translator equation (
1.5
), we get that

\displaystyle\frac{|\Theta(x_{1},u(x_{1}),Du(x_{1}))-\Theta(x_{2},u(x_{2}),Du(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}=\frac{|\gamma_{2}\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})+\gamma_{3}\cdot(Du(x_{1})-Du(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

(6.5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq|\gamma_{2}|+|\gamma_{3}|\|Du\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}.

When
u
solves the shrinker/expander equation (
1.4
), we add and subtract
x_{1}\cdot Du(x_{2})
in the numerator to get

\displaystyle\frac{|\Theta(x_{1},u(x_{1}),Du(x_{1}))-\Theta(x_{2},u(x_{2}),Du(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=|s_{2}|\frac{|x_{1}\cdot Du(x_{1})-x_{2}\cdot Du(x_{2})-2(u(x_{1})-u(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=|s_{2}|\frac{|x_{1}\cdot(Du(x_{1})-Du(x_{2}))+Du(x_{2})\cdot(x_{1}-x_{2})-2(u(x_{1})-u(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

(6.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq|s_{2}|\left(\|Du\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}+\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}+2\|u\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}\right).

Lastly, when
u
solves the rotator equation (
1.6
), we use that

||v|^{2}-|w|^{2}|=|\langle v+w,v-w\rangle|\leq|v+w||v-w|

to get

\displaystyle\frac{|\Theta(x_{1},u(x_{1}),Du(x_{1}))-\Theta(x_{2},u(x_{2}),Du(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{|r_{2}|}{2}\frac{||x_{1}|^{2}-|x_{2}|^{2}+|Du(x_{1})|^{2}-|Du(x_{2})|^{2}|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}

(6.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\frac{|r_{2}|}{2}\left(2+2\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}\|Du\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{1})}\right).

The estimates in (
6.4
), (
6.5
), (
6.6
), and (
6.7
) complete the proof.
∎

6.1.
Discussion: alternate approaches

Here we outline an alternate proof using the upward rotation method of Bhattacharya-Ogden
[
BO25
, Lemma 4.1]
, which is based on a rotation of Yuan
[
Yua06
]
. Note that this will work in dimension two.

Lemma 6.2

(Lemma 4.1,
[
BO25
]
)
.

Let
u\in C^{1,1}(B_{R})
for
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let

K=\sup_{B_{R}}|D^{2}u|\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan K\right)

Then the Lagrangian submanifold
L=(x,Du(x))\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be represented as a gradient graph
(\bar{x},D\bar{u}(\bar{x}))
given by

\begin{pmatrix}\bar{x}\\
\bar{y}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\gamma&-\sin\gamma\\
\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}.

The new potential is given by

(6.8)

\bar{u}(x)=u(x)-\sin\gamma\cos\gamma\frac{|Du|^{2}-|x|^{2}}{2}-\sin^{2}\gamma(x\cdot Du(x)).

The rotated coordinates are Lipschitz with respect to the original coordinates by

|\bar{x}_{1}-\bar{x_{2}}|\geq(\cos\gamma-K\sin\gamma)|x_{1}-x_{2}|

The rotated gradient graph in the
\bar{x}
coordinates is Lipschitz with constant

|D\bar{u}(\bar{x_{1}})-D\bar{u}(\bar{x}_{2})|\geq\left(\frac{\sin\gamma+\|u\|_{C^{1,1}(B_{R})}}{\cos\gamma-K\sin\gamma}\right)|\bar{x_{1}}-\bar{x_{2}}|.

Now we prove the
C^{2,\alpha}
estimates.

Proof of the
C^{2,\alpha}
estimates.

We will prove these estimates on a neighborhood of

U_{0}=\{x\in B_{\frac{1}{2}}|\;\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))\geq(n-2)\pi/2\}

as by symmetry they will hold for the set where
\Theta\leq-(n-2)\pi/2
. Let
\Omega=\bar{x}(U)
for
U=\{x\in B_{1}|\;\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))\geq(n-2)\frac{\pi}{2}-(n-1)\gamma\}\supset U_{0}
. On
\Omega
,
\bar{u}
satisfies

(6.9)

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\arctan\bar{\lambda}_{j}=\bar{\Theta}(\bar{x},\bar{u}(\bar{x}),D\bar{u}(\bar{x})):=g(\bar{x})

where the
\bar{\lambda}_{j}
are the eigenvalues of
D^{2}\bar{u}
. As the upward rotation increases the phase, we get

g(\bar{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\arctan\bar{\lambda}_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\arctan\lambda_{j}+n\gamma=\Theta(x,u(x),Du(x))+n\gamma

which shows that
g(\bar{x})\geq(n-2)\pi/2+\gamma
in
\Omega
. We then have for
\bar{r}=(\cos\gamma-K\sin\gamma)\rho/2
where
B_{\bar{r}}\subset\Omega
and
B_{\rho}\subset U
, that
g\in C^{\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}})
with

\frac{|g(\bar{x}_{1})-g(\bar{x}_{2})|}{|\bar{x}_{1}-\bar{x}_{2}|^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{|\Theta(x_{1},u(x_{1}),Du(x_{1})-\Theta(x_{2},u(x_{2}),Du(x_{2}))|}{|x_{1}-x_{2}|^{\alpha}}(\cos\gamma-K\sin\gamma)^{-\alpha}.

We modify the uniformly elliptic second order equation (
6.9
) to a concave one via exponentiation (see
[
CW19
, p. 24]
or
[
CPW17
, Lemma 2.2]
). Applying
[
CC03
, Cor. 1.3]
, we get
\bar{u}\in C^{2,\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}/2})
with the estimate

|D^{2}\bar{u}(\bar{x})-D^{2}\bar{u}(0)|\leq C\left(\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\bar{r}})}+\|e^{-Ag}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}})}\right)|\bar{x}|^{\alpha}

where
C
depends on
K
and
n
, and
A
depends on
\gamma
. We rotate the gradient graph
\{(\bar{x},D\bar{u}(\bar{x}))|\bar{x}\in B_{\bar{r}/2}\}
back down by an angle of
\gamma
to the original coordinates:

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\gamma&\sin\gamma\\
-\sin\gamma&\cos\gamma\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\bar{x}\\
\bar{y}\end{pmatrix}.

We then connect the original Hessian to the rotated Hessian using

\frac{dx}{d\bar{x}}=\cos\gamma I_{n}+\sin\gamma D^{2}_{\bar{x}}\bar{u}(\bar{x})=\left(\frac{d\bar{x}}{dx}\right)^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{\cos\gamma+K\sin\gamma}I_{n}

to get, for
x\in B_{r_{0}}\subset\bar{x}^{-1}(B_{\bar{r}/2})
,

|D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}u(0)|\leq C\left(\|\bar{u}\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\bar{r}})}+\|e^{g}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}}(0))}\right)(\cos\gamma+K\sin\gamma)^{2+\alpha}|x|^{\alpha}.

We note that by Taylor expanding the exponential we get

\|e^{-Ag}\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}})}\leq A\|g\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{\bar{r}})}.

Using (
6.8
), connecting the
C^{\alpha}
norms, and bounding
\|\Theta\|_{C^{\alpha}(B_{\rho})}
as in Proposition
6.1
, we get

\displaystyle|D^{2}u(x)-D^{2}u(0)|

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}+R\|Du\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}

\displaystyle\quad+R^{2}/2+\|Du\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{R})}/2+2\lambda^{\alpha}\bar{r}C_{\Theta}\bigg)|x|^{\alpha}

where
C_{\Theta}
is given by the estimates in (
6.4
), (
6.5
) (
6.6
), or (
6.7
). ∎

Remark 6.2
.

We mention another approach. One can show that the second fundamental form of the level set is uniformly convex for
n>2
following the proof in
[
Yua06
]
with strict inequalities. From there one can exponentiate as in (
6.1
) where we choose a basis of vectors given by the
(n-1)
tangential directions and one normal direction to the level set. As the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
upper left block matrix is the second fundamental form, it is positive definite by the uniform convexity of the level set. Choosing
A
large enough in the remaining lower right block matrix makes the whole matrix positive definite.
