Maternal Serum Nitrate and Nitrite Associations With Birth Outcomes: A Case‐Control Study
Authors: Wael K. Al‐Delaimy, Omar F. Khabour, Yousef Khader, Fayez A. Abdulla, Hanan Hammouri, Nour Abdo, Razan A. Saleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitrate and nitrite contamination of groundwater and surface water is a global public health concern, often linked to the increasing use of nitrogen‐based fertilizers. In Jordan's Ghor region, where agriculture is intensive and fertilizer use is widespread, the issue is particularly relevant. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum nitrate and nitrite levels with low birth weight and preterm birth. A total of 659 participants were enrolled, comprising 452 controls (normal birth weight and full‐term delivery) and 207 cases (low birth weight/preterm delivery). High maternal serum nitrite levels were significantly associated with increased odds of low birth weight delivery in both the unadjusted model (odds ratio [OR] = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.14–1.80, P = 0.002) and the a priori confounder set adjusted model (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.02–1.74, P = 0.038). Nitrite levels were also significantly associated with the combined outcome of preterm and/or low birth weight delivery in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.01–1.57, P = 0.036), and this association remained significant after a priori adjustment in a multiple‐imputation sensitivity analysis (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.03–1.63, P = 0.029). In conclusion, although metabolically interrelated, nitrite and nitrate levels in pregnant women may differentially impact pregnancy outcomes in the largely low income agricultural communities in the Ghor region in Jordan.

Introduction

Nitrate contamination of water resources is a global health problem (Patel et al., 2022), primarily driven by the extensive use of nitrogen fertilizers (Ward, 2009). Studies also indicate that climate change affects nitrate/nitrite leaching through temperature rising and rainfall fluctuations (Boyacioglu et al., 2024). For example, heat waves enhance the nitrification process and the subsequent nitrate accumulation (Dannenmann et al., 2016), while drought season negatively impacts the clearance of nitrate by plants (Lv et al., 2024). Consequently, human exposure to nitrate/nitrite may be heightened during climatic fluctuations.

Humans absorb nitrates from soil through the food chain and from drinking water. Once ingested, it enters the bloodstream and is secreted into saliva, where it is reduced by oral microflora to nitrite (Washio & Takahashi, 2025). Upon swallowing, nitrite may either enter systemic circulation or be further reduced to nitric oxide (NO) in the acidic gastric environment (Lidder & Webb, 2013). In states of hypoxia, circulating nitrite can also be converted to NO, which plays an important physiological role in the body (Christie et al., 2023). However, exposure to high levels of nitrate/nitrite has been associated with several health consequences, including adverse pregnancy outcomes, gastrointestinal cancers, autoimmune diseases, and others (Habermeyer et al., 2015).

Preterm birth (PB) and low birth weight are major drivers of neonatal mortality, resulting in serious immediate and long‐term health, developmental, and cognitive issues for infants (Averin et al., 2023; Frontera et al., 2024). Immediate effects include breathing problems, infections, feeding challenges, and temperature instability, while long‐term consequences involve chronic illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, and others (Jańczewska et al., 2023). Therefore, preventing low birth weight and preterm delivery is important and achievable if exposure to modifiable risk factors can be prevented or limited.

Studies have reported a potential impact of maternal exposure to nitrate/nitrite on newborn birth weight and PB with some controversy (Blake, 2014; Coffman et al., 2021; Ferguson et al., 2013; Jensen et al., 2023; Migeot et al., 2013; Reuther et al., 2025; Stayner et al., 2017; Temkin et al., 2019). For example, two studies from California, USA, reported an association between low birth weight and PB and nitrate exposure from air and water (Reuther et al., 2025; Sherris et al., 2021). A study conducted in Iowa, USA showed that early prenatal exposure to drinking water nitrate levels (below the 10 mg/L standard) were linked to PB and low birth weight (Semprini, 2025). A recent study from USA indicated that nitrate exposure from drinking water during the second trimester, even at concentrations below regulatory thresholds, is associated with a 1.2 and 1.0% point increase in the risk of PB and low birth weight, respectively (Montoya, 2025). A study from Denmark showed an association between prenatal nitrate exposure from household tap water and small‐for‐gestational‐age fetuses (Jensen et al., 2023). A study conducted in Prince Edward Island, Canada, showed that agricultural contamination of groundwater is a risk factor for low birth weight and PB (Bukowski et al., 2001). On the other hand, no association between nitrate exposure from water/food sources and low birth weight and PB has been reported by studies from different countries (Blake, 2014; Coffman et al., 2021; Stayner et al., 2017). Notably, most prior studies have assessed exposure via diet or drinking water, which might be modulated by metabolic variability between individuals. Serum nitrite and nitrate levels are objective markers representing cumulative exposure to all environmental sources. Although these levels can be influenced by diet, metabolism, and physiology, they represent a more direct internal measurement of human exposure than water‐source monitoring.

In Jordan, the Ghor region (locally known as Al Ghawr) presents a compelling setting for investigating nitrate/nitrite exposure due to its intensive agricultural activity and heavy reliance on fertilizers, as well as sensitivity to drought and heatwaves (Alwedyan & Taani, 2021; Anbar, 1983; Mahmoud, 1983). The Ghor extends approximately 110 km between Lake Tiberias and the Dead Sea and is below sea level. Agricultural activity relies on water from the East Ghor Canal, which receives water primarily from the Yarmuk River, several dams, and reused water (Anbar, 1983). People living in this region are low income farmworkers and day laborers who are likely exposed to high levels of nitrate/nitrite from water and food sources (Alwedyan & Taani, 2021; Mahmoud, 1983). The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between serum nitrate and nitrite levels (as biomarkers of environmental exposure during pregnancy) at delivery and low birth weight and PB in the Ghor region.

Methods

Study Design and Study Participants

A case‐control study was conducted to investigate the association between maternal serum levels of nitrate or nitrite (as environmental exposure biomarkers) and low birth weight and/or PB (cases) in the Ghor. Data collection took place in four maternity hospitals in cities serving the region. The hospitals were: Muath bin Jabal Hospital in Alshuna Alshamalia city, Abu‐Obaidah Hospital in Pella village, Princess Badea Hospital in Irbid city, and Al‐Hussain Hospital in Salt city. At hospitals serving women from regions other than Ghor, only women residing in Ghor were eligible for inclusion. Cases included women aged 18–49 years who delivered babies with low birth weight and/or PB at or after 24 weeks of gestation. Low birth weight was defined as birth weight less than 2,500 g, as per the World Health Organization (WHO). PB was defined as any birth occurring before 37 weeks of gestation, as per WHO. For each low birth weight or PB case, which was selected consecutively from each participating hospital, two or three controls of normal birth weight deliveries were selected. Controls were frequency‐matched to cases by maternal age group and newborn sex. Controls were selected from the hospital/region of the case to allow for better representation of deliveries occurring in the two Ghor regions (North and South). In addition, cases and controls were recruited during the same time period to make sampling and handling constant between the two groups. Exclusion criteria for both cases and controls included having a history of diabetes mellitus, gestational diabetes, hypertension before pregnancy, pre‐eclampsia, eclampsia, severe anemia, cardiovascular diseases, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, renal diseases, active tuberculosis or hepatitis infection, epilepsy, or pregnancy complications.

Ethical Approval

The study was approved by the “Institutional Review Board (IRB) at King Abdullah University Hospital/Jordan University of Science and Technology” (ID number: 47/151/2022). Written informed consent was obtained from all participating women after a full explanation of the study objectives and procedures.

Serum Sampling

Maternal blood samples (3 mL) were collected in plain tubes upon admission to the maternity unit in the included hospitals. In brief, after blood sampling, the blood tubes were kept at room temperature (30 min) to complete the coagulation process. Blood tubes were then centrifuged at 1,500×g. Serum fraction was then ultra‐filtered through a 30 kDa molecular weight cutoff filter. The filtered samples were then stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Measurements of Serum Nitrate/Nitrite

A calorimetric assay based on Griess reagents was adopted to measure serum nitrate and nitrite levels (Miranda et al., 2001; Sukuroglu et al., 2015). The assay was developed by Cayman Chemical Company (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Filtered serum samples were diluted 1:3 in “sample diluent” provided by the assay kit. Part of the diluted serum was treated with nitrate reductase enzyme to measure total nitrate/nitrite in the sample. Untreated remaining filtered serum was used to measure nitrite levels. Samples were assayed in duplicates in ELISA plate format. Standard curve samples were assayed in triplicates. ELISA plates were read at 540 nm using a plate reader from Bio‐Tek (Winooski, USA). Nitrate levels were calculated by subtracting the nitrite level from the total nitrate and nitrite levels.

Study Questionnaire and Data Collection

In each hospital, a trained midwife was responsible for collecting the necessary data from women using a structured questionnaire through face‐to‐face interviews before hospital discharge. The questionnaire included inquiries about socio‐demographic factors (e.g., age, gender, education, occupation, and residence), pre‐pregnancy body mass index, weight gain during pregnancy, lifestyle factors, type and sources of water consumed (municipal/bottled/well/other), history of anemia, and maternal smoking during or before pregnancy. The last part of the questionnaire included information on clinical data, obstetric characteristics, pregnancy outcomes, including gestation age and the weight of newborns.

Sample Size Calculations

The sample size was calculated to detect an odds ratio (OR) of 1.75 for the association between elevated maternal serum nitrate or nitrite levels and the risk of PB and/or low birth weight. This effect size was selected to fall within the range of associations reported for higher versus lower gestational nitrate/nitrite exposure in prior studies (e.g., adjusted ORs of 1.47–2.52 for PB reported by Sherris et al. (2021)), representing a clinically meaningful but conservative target. Assuming a two‐sided significance level of 0.05, a power of 80%, and a 30% prevalence of elevated nitrate or nitrite exposure among controls—a moderate, conservative assumption for an intensively agricultural region with documented groundwater nitrate contamination, given that power is maximized as the exposed proportion approaches 50%—the required sample size was estimated to be 490 participants (163 cases and 327 controls) for a 1:2 case‐to‐control ratio. The sample size was calculated using EpiCalc 2000.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 24. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations and medians. Comparisons between cases and controls for categorical variables were performed using the Chi‐square test. For continuous variables, independent samples t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were applied based on data normality.

Associations between maternal serum nitrate and nitrite levels and birth outcomes were examined using logistic regression models. Unconditional logistic regression was employed, as matching was performed at the group level rather than through individual matching. Separate models were fitted for each outcome (low birth weight, PB, and a combined outcome). Nitrate and nitrite exposures were modeled as continuous variables to preserve statistical power.

Confounders were selected a priori on the basis of established causal knowledge rather than by stepwise statistical selection, which is not recommended for effect estimation. A directed acyclic graph (DAG; Figure 1) was used to encode assumed causal relationships and to distinguish confounders from mediators and from determinants of exposure. The minimal sufficient adjustment set, forced into all models irrespective of statistical significance or goodness of fit, comprised maternal age, household income, maternal education, smoking during pregnancy, pre‐pregnancy body mass index, parity, anemia during pregnancy, and maternal history of low birth weight. Variables judged to lie on the causal pathway from exposure to outcome were not adjusted for in the primary models: water source was treated as an upstream determinant of the serum biomarker, and gestational hypertension as a probable mediator of the NO—related vascular pathway. To confirm the adjustment set, the change‐in‐estimate method (10% threshold) was applied to the primary nitrite—low birth weight association by adding each candidate covariate individually to the base model. Gestational hypertension was additionally examined in a sensitivity analysis, and multiple imputation by chained equations (m = 20, pooled using Rubin's rules) was used to assess the influence of missing covariate data. ORs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for both crude (mutually adjusted) and adjusted models.

Directed acyclic graph summarizing the assumed causal structure used to select the adjustment set. A priori confounders (maternal age, household income, maternal education, smoking during pregnancy, pre‐pregnancy body mass index, parity, anemia during pregnancy, and history of low birth weight) are common causes of both exposure and outcome and were forced into all models. Water source was treated as an upstream determinant of the serum biomarker, and gestational hypertension as a probable mediator; both were therefore excluded from the primary adjustment set.

Analyses were conducted for the overall study population (North and South Ghor combined) and stratified by region to explore potential geographic differences. Regional variation (North vs. South Ghor) was assessed through stratified analyses to evaluate potential heterogeneity in associations, rather than including region as a covariate in the primary models. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐tailed p‐value < 0.05. Because within‐region sample sizes were modest relative to the number of covariates, the region‐stratified adjusted models were regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, and the combined‐region models (with multiple‐imputation sensitivity analysis) were used as the primary basis for inference.

Results

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

A total of 659 women were included in the study, comprising 452 in the control group (full‐term, normal birth weight deliveries) and 207 in the case group (preterm and/or low birth weight deliveries). Of these, 381 women resided in North Ghor (264 controls and 117 cases), while 278 lived in South Ghor (188 controls and 90 cases). The gender of newborns for the cases were 48% males and 52% females and for the control 48.7% males and 51.3% females. The socio‐demographic characteristics of the study participants were compared between the two groups, and no statistically significant differences were observed (Table 1). The mean age of the mothers of cases was 29.96 years (SD 6.32) and for controls 29.24 years (SD 6.19). Most mothers in both groups were aged 26–35 years (52.5% in controls vs. 50.5% in cases, p = 0.26). The majority of women (84.8%) in the control group and 81.1% in the case group had a high school education or less, while 15.2% and 18.9%, respectively, had a diploma or higher (p = 0.235). Women's employment status did not significantly differ between the two groups, with most being housewives (92.6% in controls vs. 90.7% in cases, p = 0.404). Household income levels were also similar, with approximately half of participants in both groups reporting an income of 201–300 JOD (p = 0.652), which is considered low to very low income in Jordan.

In both regions combined, serum nitrite levels were significantly higher in mothers of low birth weight infants compared to those with normal birth weight (0.83 vs. 0.61 μM, p = 0.014), while no significant differences were observed for nitrate levels (p = 0.243). Multivariate Analysis

In the combined analysis of the North and the South Ghor (Table 4), higher maternal serum nitrite levels were significantly associated with increased odds of low birth weight delivery in both the unadjusted model (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.14–1.80, P = 0.002), and the adjusted model with an a priori confounder set (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.02–1.74, P = 0.038). Nitrite was also significantly associated with increased odds of the combined outcome of preterm and/or low birth weight delivery in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.01–1.57, P = 0.036). After a priori confounder adjustment, the inverse association between nitrate and preterm delivery was attenuated and no longer statistically significant (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–1.00, P = 0.104). In the North Ghor region, nitrite levels remained significantly associated with low birth weight in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.02–1.79, P = 0.034), and nitrate levels were significantly, but weakly, associated with the combined outcome in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.00–1.04, P = 0.033). In South Ghor, the inverse association between nitrate and preterm delivery did not persist after a priori adjustment (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01, P = 0.140), while nitrite levels were significantly associated with low birth weight in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.02–2.68, P = 0.040). These findings suggest that elevated maternal nitrite may increase the risk of low birth weight. The inverse association between nitrate and preterm delivery, by contrast, was weak and did not remain statistically significant after a priori confounder adjustment, and should be interpreted with caution. In a multiple‐imputation sensitivity analysis accounting for missing covariate data, nitrite remained significantly associated with low birth weight (OR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.13–1.86, P = 0.003) and with the combined outcome of preterm and/or low birth weight (OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.03–1.63, P = 0.029).

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant association between elevated maternal serum nitrite levels (as biomarkers of environmental exposure) and an increased risk of delivering low birth weight infants consistent across both unadjusted and adjusted models. Higher maternal serum nitrate levels showed a weak inverse association with PB that did not remain statistically significant after a priori confounder adjustment. These findings underscore the complex and potentially divergent roles of nitrate and nitrite in pregnancy physiology.

Previous research has reported mixed findings regarding gestational exposure to nitrate and low birth weight/preterm pregnancy outcomes (Blake, 2014; Coffman et al., 2021; Ferguson et al., 2013; Jensen et al., 2023; Migeot et al., 2013; Reuther et al., 2025; Stayner et al., 2017; Temkin et al., 2019). A large population‐based study in California involving 1.4 million births reported a higher risk of very early PB among women exposed to elevated nitrate levels in drinking water (Sherris et al., 2021). Similarly, Bukowski et al. (Bukowski et al., 2001) identified a dose–response relationship between groundwater nitrate and PB in Prince Edward Island. Conversely, other studies have found minimal or no associations (Albouy‐Llaty et al., 2016; Stayner et al., 2017), reflecting inconsistencies in existing literature. Super et al. (1981), for instance, reported no direct association between high water nitrate levels and outcomes such as birth weight or PB, although increased infant mortality was observed among previous offspring of mothers residing in high‐nitrate areas. Serum‐based assessments of nitrate and nitrite, while less commonly employed, offer valuable insights. One such study found that serum nitrite concentrations were significantly lower during both induced and spontaneous preterm labor compared to controls, whereas serum nitrate levels did not differ significantly (Diejomaoh et al., 2003). In addition, prenatal exposure to nitrosatable drugs in conjunction with higher levels of dietary nitrite intake have been shown to increase the risk of PB (Vuong et al., 2016). Methodological limitations, including potential exposure misclassification, small sample sizes, and inconsistent outcome definitions, limit the comparability and generalizability of many previous studies (Manassaram et al., 2007; Ward, 2009).

From a biological perspective, nitrate may exert a protective effect through its role as a precursor of NO, a key mediator of vasodilation and uteroplacental blood flow (Mukosera et al., 2020). Nitric oxide is essential in maintaining placental function and supporting fetal growth (Pels et al., 2023; Zullino et al., 2018). As a reservoir for NO, nitrate may enhance placental perfusion, reduce uterine contractility, and support full‐term gestation. Thus, elevated nitrate levels could indicate adequate NO bioavailability, contributing to a decreased risk of PB, which is what we found in our study. Because we measured serum levels near delivery, our findings may not reflect exposures during critical earlier stages of pregnancy, limiting our ability to draw causal conclusions. Prior studies have shown that maternal nitrate and nitrite levels vary across trimesters, with levels peaking during the second and third trimesters and declining near term (Diejomaoh et al., 2003), suggesting that the timing of exposure measurement is an important consideration.

On the other hand, the observed association between elevated maternal serum nitrite and the increased risk of low birth weight may reflect underlying oxidative or nitrosative stress that may reflect pathological conditions (Hanff et al., 2017; Rozanova et al., 2012). Nitrite may indicate a disturbance in the NO homeostasis and the production of NO metabolites, which may impair the physiological function of the placenta and the transport of nutrients (Mukosera et al., 2020, 2022). Given that pregnancy is already a state characterized by heightened oxidative stress, elevated nitrite levels may further exacerbate placental dysfunction and fetal compromise. In support of this, NO levels in the fetal‐placental circulation have been shown to be elevated in intrauterine growth restriction (Lyall et al., 1996). The changes of nitrite levels during pregnancy and the factors associated with them have not been adequately investigated. One study showed that nitrite concentrations in amniotic fluid remain stable throughout pregnancy until the 37th week, after which they begin to decrease (Morris et al., 1995). A previous study comparing nitrite and nitrate levels in newborns found that urinary excretion levels were significantly higher in preterm infants than in full‐term infants (Tsukahara, Hiraoka, et al., 1997; Tsukahara, Takeuchi, et al., 1997). An alternative mechanism by which maternal blood nitrite might affect birth weight is by modulation of the insulin sensitivity pathway. Nitrite has been shown to stimulate insulin secretion and to increase insulin sensitivity of body cells (Nyström et al., 2012). High circulating nitrite levels might lead to low blood sugar levels and, subsequently, low fetal weight gain. In support of this, maternal diabetes is associated with increased birth weight (Silveira et al., 2021). Finally, serum nitrite levels may reflect pregnancy‐related infections and placental dysfunction. For example, PB and low birth weight have been shown to be associated with asymptomatic bacteriuria (Romero et al., 1989). In addition, nitrite and nitrate levels in vaginal secretions are higher in PB compared to full term birth (Nakatsuka et al., 2000). In this case, vaginal and urinary tract infections can lead to elevated blood nitrite levels by bacterial conversion of nitrate to nitrite and subsequent body reabsorption. Moreover, Placental insufficiency presents a paradox: while NO‐mediated signaling is required, traditional production methods fail. Nitrite serves as a vital, stress‐responsive reservoir that converts into NO to compensate, yet it contributes to damaging stress when this system is overwhelmed and nitrite levels buildup (Hollenberg & Cinel, 2009). Thus, the relationships between nitrite and pregnancy outcomes seem to be complex and require further studies. Moreover, because elevated nitrite levels could be a consequence, rather than a cause of PB or fetal growth restriction, the interpretability of these findings is limited.

It is worth noting that nitrite and nitrate play beneficial roles in the body, such as protecting the cardiovascular system, regulating blood pressure, and the proper functioning of the immune system (Ashworth & Bescos, 2017; Bondonno et al., 2016). Nitrate supplements are suggested to be effective in the management of preeclampsia (Ormesher et al., 2018). The human body appears to require adequate levels of nitrate and nitrite, and low or high levels may be associated with health conditions, as observed in the current study. Furthermore, the relationship between nitrate or nitrite and health appears complex and may depend on an individual's health status.

Finally, the study showed slight regional differences (North Ghor vs. South Ghor) in the observed associations between nitrite and nitrite and pregnancy outcomes. The degree of exposure to these particles depends on several factors, including the quality of water used for irrigation and drinking, dietary lifestyles, oral microbiome, and environmental/climate factors (Blekkenhorst et al., 2018; Dirnböck et al., 2016; Hord et al., 2009).

Unlike prior studies that primarily assessed environmental or drinking water nitrate exposure, this study focused on maternal serum concentrations, which may more accurately reflect individual internal exposure. However, the observed inverse association between nitrate levels and PB should be interpreted with caution. The effect size was modest, and the CI approached the null value, suggesting that the finding may be attributable to residual confounding or chance. Moreover, given the multiple comparisons conducted across outcomes, exposures, and models, some borderline associations, particularly the inverse relationship between nitrate and PB, should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, given the observational nature of this study, causality cannot be inferred. Moreover, blood nitrite levels, in addition to environmental exposure, are strongly influenced by infection, inflammation, and oral microbiome activity, which should be accounted for in future studies. We also did not directly validate serum nitrate and nitrite levels against environmental measurements (e.g., drinking water sources). Although these biomarkers reflect internal exposure from multiple sources, they are also influenced by physiological and metabolic factors during pregnancy, which limits the ability to attribute observed associations to specific exposure pathways during the full period of pregnancy.

Additional research is warranted to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms including sources of exposure and the impact of climate change. Future work should link nitrate and nitrite serum levels with drinking‐water measurements, diet, and repeated pregnancy sampling across gestation. Exposure levels according to reported agricultural work, water source, or region should be evaluated. Finally, more studies with larger samples are needed to better examine regional differences in exposure and nitrite/nitrate serum levels.

Conclusion

The study indicates that higher maternal serum nitrite near delivery is linked to increased odds of low birth weight in an agricultural Jordanian population. A weak inverse association between nitrate and PB was observed in some models but did not remain statistically significant after a priori confounder adjustment, and therefore warrants cautious interpretation. These findings could be useful in interventions aimed at managing low birth weight and preterm pregnancy outcomes in the Ghor region of Jordan. Potential preventive interventions should focus on water quality monitoring, maternal nutrition, or broader agricultural‐environmental controls.
