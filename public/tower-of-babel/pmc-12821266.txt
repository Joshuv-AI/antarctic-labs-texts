Associations between rivaroxaban dose, gut microbiota, and coagulation parameters in a rat model
Authors: Yuanyuan Liu, Yi Zhang, Yang Yang, Chang Han, Yong Zhang, Jingtao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRivaroxaban, a direct oral anticoagulant, is widely prescribed for thrombotic disorders, yet growing evidence suggests that gut microbiota may influence its pharmacodynamic effects. This study investigated dose-dependent associations between rivaroxaban administration, gut microbiota composition, and coagulation parameters in a rat model. Twenty-four rats were assigned to control, low-, medium-, and high-dose rivaroxaban groups. After 30 days of administration, 16S rRNA sequencing and plasma coagulation analyses were performed. Rivaroxaban treatment was associated with significant, dose-related alterations in gut microbiota diversity and composition, notably decreasing Allobaculum and increasing Ligilactobacillus faecis abundance. Additionally, Lactobacillus johnsonii exhibited an initial increase followed by a decline at higher doses. Coagulation parameters—prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and thrombin time (TT) were prolonged, and fibrinogen (FIB) levels decreased in a dose-dependent manner. Correlation analyses revealed associations between microbial shifts and coagulation indices. These findings indicate associations rather than causation and highlight the need for further mechanistic studies, including models with altered baseline microbiota, to determine whether specific microbial changes modulate rivaroxaban response.Keywords: Rivaroxaban, Gut microbiota, 16S rRNA, Microbial diversity, Anticoagulation, Coagulation parameters

Introduction

Rivaroxaban is a representative novel oral anticoagulant (NOAC) that acts on coagulation factor Xa and is widely used in the prevention and treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF) and systemic embolic events. Currently, in China, prescriptions for rivaroxaban account for more than 60% of the total market value of oral anticoagulants [1]. However, clinical trials and real-world use of oral anticoagulants have revealed substantial inter-individual variability in efficacy and drug interactions. This variability is attributed to differences in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics among individuals, leading to challenges in achieving optimal anticoagulant effects. Consequently, a significant proportion of patients may experience subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic anticoagulation, underscoring the need for personalized approaches to anticoagulant therapy [2, 3].

An increasing body of evidence suggests that an individual’s unique microbiome is a key determinant of drug response. The gut microbiota exhibits complex interactions with therapeutic drugs, influencing drug metabolism, transformation, and ultimately, treatment efficacy [4]. Rivaroxaban is primarily metabolized by CYP3A, and inter-individual differences in human gut microbiota or specific taxa have been shown to influence hepatic CYP3A expression and P-gp function. These microbiota–host interactions may therefore contribute to variability in oral anticoagulant exposure and effect [5–7]. Thus, exploring the individual differences in anticoagulant efficacy through gut microbiota modulation represents a novel and promising direction.

In this study, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was employed to characterize changes in gut microbiota and coagulation parameters across different rivaroxaban dose groups in rats, and to explore microbial taxa that may be associated with rivaroxaban intervention as preliminary candidates for further mechanistic and translational investigation.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

A total of 24 male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats (SPF grade), aged 6 weeks and weighing approximately 200–220 g, were purchased from Sipeifu (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Rats were housed under standard laboratory conditions (temperature 20–25 °C, humidity 40–70%, 12 h light/dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. Rivaroxaban (Batch No. BJ73222) was obtained from Bayer Healthcare Co., Ltd. Rats were group-housed (3 rats per cage). For both coagulation and microbiome analyses, each rat was treated as an individual experimental unit.

Grouping and drug administration

Following one week of acclimatization, rats were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 6 per group): control group (CGA), and three experimental groups receiving low (EGAL), medium (EGAM), and high (EGAH) doses of rivaroxaban. The control group received an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) via oral gavage. Rivaroxaban was administered by oral gavage (i.g.) once daily for 30 days at 0.9, 2.7, and 18 mg/kg, corresponding to human-equivalent doses of approximately 10, 30, and 200 mg/day, respectively. Human-equivalent doses were calculated by the body-surface-area (BSA) method using standard interspecies conversion factors. The lowest dose was selected to approximate clinically relevant exposure after BSA conversion, the medium dose to explore a graded pharmacodynamic response at higher exposure, and the highest dose (18 mg/kg, corresponding to a supratherapeutic ∼200 mg human-equivalent dose) was intentionally included to model potential overdose/high-exposure scenarios and to maximize detectable changes in coagulation parameters and gut microbiota within a tolerable range in rats. All animals were euthanized after the final dose on Day 30.

Sample collection

Fresh fecal pellets were collected 08:00–10:00 (24 h after the final gavage) after an overnight fast (water ad libitum) and were obtained per animal to avoid cage-level pooling. Samples were stored at − 80 °C in sterile cryovials for subsequent DNA extraction. Blood samples were collected under isoflurane anesthesia (2.0% in oxygen at 1.0 L·min⁻¹, maintained 5–10 min). Blood was collected from the abdominal aorta under deep anesthesia. Plasma was separated and stored at − 80 °C for measurement of coagulation parameters.

Coagulation assays

At the end of the 30-day treatment, rats were anesthetized and approximately 2 mL of blood was collected from the abdominal aorta into 3.2% sodium citrate tubes (9:1, v/v; vacuum system). Tubes were gently inverted 8–10 times and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min to obtain platelet-poor plasma, which was stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Before testing, samples were rapidly thawed at 37 °C, mixed gently, and re-centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C if visible particulates were present. Prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), thrombin time (TT), and fibrinogen (Fbg) were measured on a semi-automated coagulometer using commercial reagents from Wuhan Zhongtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China): PT kit (223052, PX1201), APTT kit (322123, AX1201), TT kit (522124, TX1201), and fibrinogen kit (623051, FX1201). Fibrinogen was determined by the Clauss method according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For PT, 50 µL citrated plasma was prewarmed at 37 °C for 3 min and 100 µL PT reagent was added; for TT, 100 µL plasma was prewarmed at 37 °C for 3 min and 100 µL TT reagent was added; for APTT, 50 µL plasma and 50 µL APTT reagent were incubated at 37 °C for 3 min followed by addition of 50 µL CaCl₂; for Fbg, plasma was diluted 1:10 in imidazole buffer prior to incubation with Fbg reagent. All measurements were performed in duplicate, and mean values were used for analysis.

16S rRNA gene sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the MN NucleoSpin 96 Soil DNA Kit. DNA quantity and quality were assessed prior to sequencing. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at a 97% similarity threshold, and OTUs with a relative abundance below 0.005% of total reads were filtered. Sequencing was conducted by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), and subsequent analyses included microbial abundance, composition, structure, and diversity.

Bioinformatics analysis

Sequence data were processed using QIIME2 and R statistical software. Alpha diversity metrics (including OTU richness and evenness) were calculated using QIIME2. Beta diversity analysis was performed with the “vegan” package in R. Python was used to generate visualizations. All diversity analyses used the rarefied feature table at 48,166 reads/sample (minimum Effective reads; 24/24 retained). Beta diversity used Bray–Curtis, Jaccard, and UniFrac (weighted & unweighted) with PCoA ordination; a phylogenetic tree was built with MAFFT + FastTree. Taxonomy was assigned against SILVA 138 using the QIIME2 feature-classifier (Naïve Bayes).

Statistical analysis

STAMP and R software were used to assess intergroup differences. Normality was assessed by Shapiro–Wilk. For normal data, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc was used; for non-normal data, Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s post-hoc was applied. BH-FDR was used to correct for multiple testing (two-sided, q < 0.05). For beta diversity, group differences were assessed by ANOSIM with 999 permutations (R package vegan); significance was set at p < 0.05. For differential abundance and correlations, multiple testing was controlled by Benjamini–Hochberg FDR (two-sided, q < 0.05). Correlations between taxa and coagulation indices were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation.

Results

Coagulation parameters

Significant changes in coagulation parameters were observed following rivaroxaban administration. Compared with the control group (CGA: PT 11.58 ± 0.19 s, TT 33.83 ± 1.02 s, APTT 23.15 ± 0.37 s, FIB 1.53 ± 0.04 g/L), all treatment groups exhibited prolonged coagulation times and reduced fibrinogen levels. Specifically, EGAL showed mild prolongation (PT 12.90 ± 0.75 s, TT 35.65 ± 0.43 s, APTT 26.28 ± 1.97 s, FIB 1.32 ± 0.19 g/L), whereas EGAM and EGAH demonstrated a dose-dependent enhancement in anticoagulant effect (EGAM: PT 15.25 ± 0.38 s, APTT 37.50 ± 2.76 s, FIB 0.69 ± 0.05 g/L; EGAH: PT 16.60 ± 0.84 s, APTT 54.83 ± 6.75 s, FIB 0.58 ± 0.04 g/L), indicating a consistent pharmacodynamic response to rivaroxaban (Fig. 1).

Sequencing data and OTUs

A total of 6,145 operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were identified at a 97% similarity threshold. Among them, CGA, EGAL, EGAM, and EGAH contained 2,728, 2,758, 3,695, and 3,990 OTUs respectively. The number of group-specific OTUs were 431 (CGA), 379 (EGAL), 501 (EGAM), and 665 (EGAH). Richness (ACE/Chao1) increased in EGAM/EGAH vs. CGA/EGAL (q < 0.05), whereas CGA vs. EGAL showed no significant difference (Figs. 2 and 3).

Alpha diversity

Alpha diversity indices revealed dose-dependent microbial diversity changes. While no significant differences in ACE and Chao1 indices were observed between CGA and EGAL, both EGAM and EGAH showed significantly increased richness compared to CGA and EGAL. Shannon and Simpson indices did not differ significantly among groups (q > 0.05), whereas richness (ACE/Chao1) increased in EGAM/EGAH versus CGA/EGAL (Fig. 3).

Beta diversity

Beta-diversity analysis by ANOSIM demonstrated significant differences in microbial community structure among groups (R = 0.665, p = 0.001), with inter-group dissimilarity exceeding intra-group dissimilarity (Fig. 4A). Consistently, PCoA based on Bray–Curtis, unweighted UniFrac, and weighted UniFrac distances further illustrated group-level separation (Fig. 4B–D), indicating compositional shifts of the gut microbiota following rivaroxaban exposure.

Taxonomic differences among groups

At the phylum level, Firmicutes remained dominant in all groups, but its relative abundance showed a gradual decrease from low- to high-dose rivaroxaban, accompanied by a stepwise increase in Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria. At the order and family levels, Erysipelotrichales and Lactobacillaceae exhibited a similar dose-related decline. At the genus level, Allobaculum and Limosilactobacillus also decreased consistently with escalating rivaroxaban doses. These taxa therefore represent bacterial lineages that appear sensitive to rivaroxaban exposure in this rat model (Table 1).

Correlation between microbiota and coagulation parameters

Correlation analysis revealed that Allobaculum abundance was negatively correlated with APTT, PT, and TT, but positively associated with FIB (q < 0.05). Ligilactobacillus showed a significant negative correlation with FIB, while its correlation with APTT, PT, and TT was positive but not statistically significant. Other genera, including Limosilactobacillus, Lactobacillus, and unclassified Lachnospiraceae, exhibited weak correlations without statistical significance (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Rivaroxaban is a representative non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant (NOAC) that exerts its effect by inhibiting factor Xa. It is widely used in clinical settings for the prevention and treatment of atrial fibrillation (AF) and systemic thromboembolic events. Several studies have reported that higher doses of rivaroxaban are associated with increased bleeding risk [8]. Given the rapidly aging population and the increasing incidence of thrombotic diseases in older adults, the safety of anticoagulant therapy has become critically important. Although NOACs have demonstrated a favorable safety profile, accumulating real-world evidence has revealed significant inter-individual differences in their clinical efficacy [9]. These differences cannot be fully explained by pharmacogenomics alone, and conflicting results across studies have further limited the clinical utility of genetic testing in guiding rivaroxaban use. Consequently, the identification of new markers for evaluating drug safety has become imperative. Although rivaroxaban exposure was not directly quantified in plasma or feces in this study, we deliberately employed a broad dose range (0.9–18 mg/kg) in rats, which includes supratherapeutic doses relative to standard clinical regimens. These doses were chosen to elicit clear pharmacodynamic responses and to explore potential dose-dependent alterations in coagulation parameters and gut microbiota, rather than to replicate routine human exposure. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted as relative pharmacodynamic and microbiome changes within this experimental model, rather than as a direct validation of established rivaroxaban pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships.

The gut microbiota, often referred to as a “hidden organ,” constitutes a modifiable genome within the human body and plays a pivotal role in health and disease. Variations in gut microbial composition have been shown to influence drug metabolism and therapeutic outcomes [10]. Emerging studies suggest that not only do intestinal microbes affect drug metabolism and toxicity, but complex host–microbiota interactions can also modulate drug efficacy [11]. A better understanding of these interactions will enable targeted modulation of gut microbiota to improve drug safety and therapeutic outcomes.

In the present study, 16S rRNA sequencing was employed to investigate changes in fecal microbiota in rats exposed to different doses of rivaroxaban. Previous studies have reported that AF patients exhibit increased abundance of Ruminococcus and decreased levels of Lachnobacterium and Bacteroides [12, 13]. These findings are cited as clinical context rather than a direct disease model (we used health rats). In our study, Bacteroidia increased in the medium- and high-dose groups, whereas Clostridia remained overall comparable across groups, suggesting that these taxa may be responsive to rivaroxaban exposure in this rat model and warrant further evaluation in relation to coagulation phenotypes.

Previous reports also indicated that Lachnospira increases and Escherichia-Shigella decreases in AF patients undergoing perioperative management. In our study, a downward trend in Lachnospiraceae abundance was observed following rivaroxaban treatment, which may be consistent with a potential protective shift reported in previous studies [14]. Erysipelotrichaceae, which includes genera such as Clostridium and Lachnoclostridium, has been linked to dietary choline metabolism and the production of trimethylamine (TMA), a precursor of trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a metabolite associated with cardiovascular events. Erysipelatoclostridium has also been identified as a risk factor for AF [15, 16]. In our study, the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae decreased with increasing rivaroxaban dosage. Notably, Allobaculum—a genus within this family—showed a significant negative correlation with APTT, PT, and TT, and a positive correlation with FIB, suggesting that Allobaculum may be a preliminary taxon of interest associated with rivaroxaban-related coagulation changes in this rat model; however, this observation is exploratory and requires further mechanistic and clinical validation. Consistent with our observations, a recent mouse study reported that rivaroxaban alleviates hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome via gut microbiota modulation and noted an association between small-intestinal Allobaculum abundance and rivaroxaban administration [17].

Notably, we observed modest but statistically significant changes in fibrinogen levels and TT in rivaroxaban-treated rats compared with controls. This appears to differ from reports in human plasma, where therapeutic rivaroxaban typically prolongs PT (and to a lesser extent APTT) while exerting little or no effect on Clauss fibrinogen and TT when standard reagents are used [18, 19]. Several factors may explain this discrepancy. First, our experiments used a rat model with 30 days of once-daily rivaroxaban, including a supratherapeutic high dose (18 mg·kg⁻¹), which resembles exposure regimens used in preclinical studies investigating pleiotropic effects of rivaroxaban in chronic disease models rather than standard clinical dosing in humans. Such studies in cirrhotic or experimental disease models typically apply 2–30 mg·kg⁻¹ for 2–4 weeks and report marked pharmacodynamic effects without a uniform pattern on conventional coagulation assays, underscoring that species-, dose- and context-dependent responses are expected in this setting [20, 21]. Also, in our model, all groups were tested within a single analytical system using identical reagents and procedures, so these findings should primarily be interpreted as relative changes within this experimental context rather than as absolute diagnostic cut-offs. Therefore, while our data indicate that long-term, especially high-dose, rivaroxaban exposure modulates coagulation profiles in this rat model, they do not contradict the clinical evidence that standard TT and Clauss fibrinogen are unreliable for quantitative monitoring of rivaroxaban exposure in humans. From a thrombosis and hemostasis perspective, the findings of this study are the measurable alterations in coagulation function across rivaroxaban dose groups and the subsequent microbiota analyses were designed to explore whether specific bacterial taxa track with, or potentially modulate, these coagulation phenotypes.

Ligilactobacillus was negatively correlated with FIB and has been reported as a probiotic capable of modulating hypercoagulability via SCFA production, TMAO inhibition, and immune regulation [22]. These findings are associative and do not establish that rivaroxaban exerts anticoagulant effects via microbiota modulation; causal mechanisms require dedicated experimentation.

Species-level evidence indicates that certain Lactobacillus-lineage strains support intestinal barrier function. For example, Ligilactobacillus murinus alleviates intestinal ischemia/reperfusion injury in mice by promoting IL-10 release from M2 macrophages via TLR2 signaling and thereby protecting epithelial integrity [23]. Selected Lactobacillus johnsonii strains have been shown to enhance gut barrier integrity through interactions with tight junction proteins such as JAM-2 and to protect against experimental colitis, further supporting a barrier-stabilizing role for this lineage [24, 25]. In our study, Lactobacillaceae abundance was higher than in controls at low and medium rivaroxaban doses but declined at the high dose, and Limosilactobacillus, Lactobacillus and Ligilactobacillus showed similar non-monotonic trends. These taxa were not significantly correlated with coagulation parameters, but such fluctuations are biologically plausible because Lactobacillus-lineage bacteria are sensitive to luminal drug, bile acid and nutrient conditions. At supratherapeutic rivaroxaban doses, high intraluminal drug concentrations may act as a xenobiotic stressor with direct growth-modulating or inhibitory effects on susceptible taxa, consistent with toxicomicrobiomics/pharmacomicrobiomics data showing that multiple oral drugs reshape gut microbial communities independently of the primary disease process [26, 27]. In addition, dose-dependent changes in bile acids, pH, nutrient availability and host secretions may alter ecological niches and interspecies competition, providing an alternative explanation for the observed non-monotonic responses without implying a causal role in coagulation or bleeding. Clinical and translational studies indicate that direct oral anticoagulants, including rivaroxaban, are associated with gastrointestinal bleeding in some settings, but the mechanisms are multifactorial and remain incompletely defined, and current evidence does not demonstrate microbiota-mediated causality [28, 29]. Emerging human data further describe bidirectional associations between oral anticoagulant therapy and gut microbiota composition, supporting a complex interaction rather than a simple linear pathway [5, 30]. Since intestinal permeability, mucosal histology and bleeding outcomes were not evaluated in this rat model, any proposed link between altered Lactobacillus-lineage abundance, barrier integrity and gastrointestinal bleeding risk should be regarded as a hypothesis for future mechanistic studies, rather than a conclusion derived from the present data.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a rat model using a broad rivaroxaban dose range, including supratherapeutic levels, which may not fully replicate human gut microbiota, immune status, or pharmacokinetics; therefore, the findings should not be directly extrapolated to standard clinical dosing. Second, only 16S rRNA sequencing was applied, which limits functional resolution; metagenomic and metabolomic approaches are needed to clarify mechanistic links between microbial functions and coagulation phenotypes. Third, rivaroxaban concentrations were not measured in plasma or feces, so quantitative pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships and intestinal exposure could not be established. Our observations should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger animal studies and human cohorts. Fourth, the cross-sectional assessment at a single time point precludes causal inference regarding the directionality of associations between rivaroxaban dose, gut microbiota, and coagulation parameters; our data cannot distinguish whether microbial changes are causes, consequences, or merely correlates of altered hemostasis.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that different rivaroxaban doses in rats are associated with dose-dependent changes in PT, APTT, TT, and FIB, as well as with shifts in gut microbiota composition. Several bacterial taxa showed exploratory associations with coagulation parameters, suggesting that the gut microbiota may reflect or accompany rivaroxaban-related hemostatic effects in this experimental setting. However, these observations are correlational and do not establish that microbiota alterations causally mediate anticoagulant responses. Further mechanistic experiments and clinical studies are required to clarify causality, define the underlying pathways, and evaluate the translational relevance of microbiota signatures for anticoagulant safety and response.
