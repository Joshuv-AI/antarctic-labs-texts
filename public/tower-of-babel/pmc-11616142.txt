Kissing balloon treatment for stenosis of the internal and external carotid artery orifice associated with moyamoya disease: illustrative case
Authors: Takayuki Morimoto, Kenta Fujimoto, Sung-Chul Ko, Toshikazu Nishioka, Hidemori Tokunaga
Published: 2024

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare cerebrovascular disorder that is characterized by progressive stenosis of the terminal portion of the internal carotid artery (ICA) with the development of small collateral vessels.1 MMD is one of the major causes of ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke.1, 2 Although the steno-occlusive changes in patients with MMD mainly occur in the intracranial lesion, the involvement of the extracranial ICA has been increasingly reported.3–6 External carotid artery orifice (ECAO) or vertebral artery orifice (VAO) stenosis,7, 8 which interferes with superficial temporal artery (STA)–middle cerebral artery bypass, is also rare in patients with MMD. In this report, we present a case of MMD involving stenosis of the ICA orifice (ICAO), ECAO, and VAO, which was treated with percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) using the kissing balloon technique (KBT), followed by direct and indirect revascularization.

Illustrative Case

A 48-year-old female patient with no past medical history presented to our emergency department with right hemiparesis and motor aphasia. Head magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed acute infarction in the bilateral frontal lobes, which was dominant in the left lobe, and magnetic resonance angiography showed stenosis in the terminal portion of bilateral ICAs with bilateral posterior cerebral artery involvement (Fig. 1). She was admitted while on a regimen of double antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) consisting of aspirin and clopidogrel, and she was transferred to a rehabilitation center on day 21 of her hospitalization. After rehabilitation therapy, she came back to our outpatient department, and we performed digital subtraction angiography (DSA). Common carotid artery (CCA) angiography revealed stenosis in the terminal portion of the bilateral ICAs, along with basal moyamoya and ethmoidal moyamoya vessels, which is classified as stage III in Suzuki staging (Fig. 2A and B). CCA angiography also showed stenosis in the left ICAO and ECAO and right ICAO (Fig. 2C and D). Vertebral artery (VA) angiography demonstrated stenosis in the bilateral VAOs (Fig. 2E and F). We planned to perform bilateral direct and indirect revascularization, but there were some interferences in her cervical lesions. First, because of the ECAO stenosis, the external carotid artery (ECA) would not be able to maintain sufficient blood flow to the STA. Second, the stenosis in the bilateral ICAOs would possibly cause a future stroke due to progressive steno-occlusive changes in the ICAO. Furthermore, unless we performed direct revascularization, the anterograde blood flow from the ICA would be necessary for maintaining the cerebral blood flow. To resolve these problems, we performed PTA for bilateral stenosis in the cervical lesions. We did not perform stenting because the patient was going to be on a regimen of DAPT for at least 3 months after the procedure, which increased the risk of perioperative hemorrhagic complications. Because we planned the revascularization early to prevent an additional stroke, we selected PTA for the cervical lesion. In particular, her left cervical lesion was involved with the ICAO and ECAO, so we performed PTA using the KBT to preserve the patency of the ICA and ECA. FIG. 1. We first performed PTA using the KBT on the left cervical lesion because it was the symptomatic side of the intracranial lesion. Under local anesthesia, 7- and 6-Fr FUBUKI catheters (Asahi Intecc) were navigated into the left CCA using a 4-Fr SY3 catheter and a 0.035-inch guiding wire, respectively. Next, a distal filter protection device (FilterWire EZ, Boston Scientific) was advanced to the proximal petrosal portion of the ICA from the 7-Fr FUBUKI catheter. A CHIKAI 14 guidewire (Asahi Intecc) was used for lesion cross of ECA stenosis. PTA was performed with a 3.0 × 200–mm high-pressure balloon (SHIDEN, Kaneka Medics) for each lesion using the KBT (Fig. 3A). No debris was observed in the retrieved filter. After PTA, stenosis of the ICA and ECA was improved, and recoil was not observed (Fig. 3B). After an interval of 1 month, the right cervical lesion of the ICAO was treated with PTA, and the stenosis was improved (Fig. 3C and D). Following PTA for the bilateral cervical lesions, bilateral direct and indirect revascularizations were performed 4 months later (Fig. 4A and B). She presented with cerebral hyperperfusion syndrome after the left revascularization, but the symptoms improved in a few weeks. Follow-up DSA was performed 2 weeks after the final revascularization surgery, with right and left CCA angiography revealing patency of the donor vessels and the ICAO and ECAO (Fig. 4C and D). The patient was discharged home without new neurological deficit. Six months after discharge, DSA revealed restenosis in the left ECAO, so we performed PTA, and the patency in the left ECA was good. Discussion

Observations

We report a rare case of MMD with ICAO and ECAO stenosis that was treated with the combination of endovascular therapy and revascularization. Although patients with MMD have affected intracranial vessels, especially the terminal portion of the ICA, some reports have indicated that extracranial vessels are also involved.3, 7, 9–11 Jee et al. reported MMD with extracranial systemic arteriopathy; they concluded that young adults with MMD may have concomitant extracranial arteriopathy and that patients with RNF213 variants, especially the p.Arg4810Lys homozygous variant, should be screened.9 It was also reported that stenosis in the extracranial ICA was frequently observed in patients with MMD, which is associated with stroke events after revascularization treatment. However, in their study, the extracranial ICA stenosis was the type with a champagne bottle neck sign, which is determined as the phenomenon in which the diameter of the ICA at the proximal portion was rapidly reduced to less than half the diameter of the CCA.3, 4 In this case, the left ICA stenosis was located just in the orifice of the ICA. At the same time, the right ICA stenosis was located in the proximal orifice with a short lesion, which is seen in the arterial dissection as the string sign. These ICA lesions were different from the extracranial stenosis reported in previous studies. In addition, in this case, stenosis of the left ECAO was observed. This is a rare occurrence in MMD, and limited studies have been reported. Lee and Ahn reported that a patient with MMD had stenosis of the proximal ECA, and they performed ECA stenting.7 Furthermore, in this case, stenosis of the VAO was revealed by DSA. Häckel et al. reported the case of a patient with MMD who had VAO stenosis, and they performed a left VA-CCA transposition.8 In summary, we report a rare case of MMD with ICAO, ECAO, and VAO stenosis. The patient was treated with the combination of endovascular treatment and surgery, leading to a good outcome.

In this case, we performed PTA using the KBT for left stenosis in the ICAO and ECAO. Only three cases of surgical treatment for ICA stenosis with MMD have been reported.10–12 Two of the three cases were treated with carotid artery stenting (CAS), and one was treated with carotid endarterectomy. The KBT is frequently used for coronary bifurcation lesions, restoring the volume and area of the affected vessel as well as preventing loss of the vessel wall.13 For this technique, two balloon catheters are placed in both of the affected vessels and inflated simultaneously to preserve the patency of both vessels. There are few reports about PTA using the KBT for carotid bifurcation stenosis. Mori et al. reported a patient with carotid bifurcation stenosis coupled with the proatlantal intersegmental artery, which was dilated with the KBT.14 This is the first report of PTA using the KBT for stenosis in the ICAO and ECAO in a patient with MMD. For a patient with MMD who is planned for revascularization surgery, blood flow in the ECA is essential because the STA, which is used for bypass surgery, is a branch of the ECA, and transdural anastomosis is supplied by the MMA, which is another branch of the ECA. The ECA is one of the blood-supplying vessels for cerebral tissue. Unlike CAS for the ICA, the KBT for carotid bifurcation would be able to preserve both ICA and ECA patency.

Lessons

It is controversial to perform revascularization in patients with MMD who have intracranial and cervical lesions. First, we have to decide which treatment should be performed first. We selected endovascular revascularization for cervical lesions as the first treatment. We first performed PTA because we thought that anterograde revascularization would decrease the risk of perioperative complication; if an embolism occurred in the distal ECA, it would lead to cerebral infarction before the STA-MCA bypass. When the bypass was performed first, the embolism of the distal ECA during PTA would contribute to cerebral infarction, and occlusion due to recoil after PTA would possibly result in cerebral infarction. Second, endovascular therapy for cervical lesions requires antiplatelet medication therapy, which increases the risk of perioperative complications during surgery. In particular, patients treated with CAS must be given DAPT for at least 3 months, so we have to delay the next surgery for a long time. Unlike CAS, PTA does not require DAPT after the procedure, so it is easy to perform the next surgery. In this case, we performed PTA for cervical lesions followed by revascularization surgery, and the patient developed no new neurological deficits or recurrence of ischemic stroke.
