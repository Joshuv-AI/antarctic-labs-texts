Does the Use of Direct Oral Anticoagulants Influence the Incidence of Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension After Acute Pulmonary Embolism?
Authors: Takeshi Ogo
Published: 2024

Acute pulmonary embolism can lead to serious, long‐lasting complications known as post–pulmonary embolism (PE) syndrome. 1 , 2 Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) is the most severe type of post‐PE syndrome. 3 CTEPH is associated with poor quality of life and a poor prognosis, especially if left untreated. 4 Despite the lack of clarity regarding the underlying mechanism of CTEPH development following acute PE, it is widely accepted that it occurs due to inadequate resolution of the acute embolism. 4 However, CTEPH can occur in patients without a history of acute PE, and the mechanism underlying its development remains poorly understood. Therefore, it is crucial to acquire more precise incidence data to detect CTEPH more effectively after acute PE.

Although the precise incidence of CTEPH after acute PE remains unclear, estimates vary significantly, ranging from 0.1% to 11.8%. 5 These studies on the incidence of CTEPH after acute PE used vitamin K antagonists as acute anticoagulant therapy. Recently, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have been widely used for the treatment of acute PE in place of vitamin K antagonists. However, the effect of DOACs on the development of CTEPH after acute PE has not been fully explored. The research article “Incidence of Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension After Pulmonary Embolism in the Era of Direct Oral Anticoagulants: From the COMMAND VTE Registry‐2” by Ikeda et al in this issue of the Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), added an intriguing finding to the detection of CTEPH after acute PE in the era of DOAC therapy. 6 This study retrospectively enrolled and followed up 2787 patients with acute PE over a 3‐year period in expert centers for venous thromboembolic disease. The authors reported that the cumulative incidence of CTEPH 3 years after acute pulmonary embolism was 2.3%. This study provides data on the incidence of CTEPH after acute PE in the DOAC era, as >80% of patients were treated with DOACs for acute PE. Furthermore, the study identified the possible risk factors for the development of CTEPH after acute PE in the era of DOAC therapy. This may also be useful for screening populations at high risk for CTEPH after acute PE in the DOAC era.

Does the Use of DOACs Influence the Incidence of CTEPH After Acute PE?

The incidence of CTEPH in survivors of acute PE ranges between 2% and 4% during the time when vitamin K anticoagulants are commonly used. 5 , 7 However, caution should be exercised when interpreting the incidence of CTEPH. The incidence of CTEPH after acute PE is influenced by several factors, including the accuracy of the CTEPH diagnosis. A meta‐analysis of 4047 patients with acute PE, who were followed up for the occurrence of CTEPH, revealed that its incidence was 3 times higher (9.1%) in studies in which the diagnosis of CTEPH was based solely on echocardiographic parameters, and significant overdiagnosis has been reported. This underscores the importance of right‐heart catheterization in the diagnosis of CTEPH. 7 In this study, the diagnosis of CTEPH was made by venous thromboembolism specialists from expert centers in accordance with pulmonary hypertension guidelines with right heart catheterization, which enhanced the reliability of the information for the diagnosis of CTEPH. Furthermore, CTEPH was diagnosed when suspected during follow‐up after an acute PE, which suggests that the incidence of CTEPH in this study is comparable with that observed in real‐world clinical practice.

Recently, the FOCUS (Follow‐Up After Acute Pulmonary Embolism) study investigated screening for the development of CTEPH after acute PE and implemented a comprehensive 18‐item follow‐up program at 3, 6, and 12 months after acute PE in preselected centers with a high standard of care. 8 The results of the FOCUS study, which was conducted during the DOAC era, showed that the incidence of CTEPH was 2.3% ≈2 years after acute PE, which is comparable with the incidence observed in Ikeda's study. Therefore, DOACs may not have influenced the incidence of CTEPH after acute PE compared with vitamin K anticoagulants. This study highlights the importance of awareness regarding the development of CTEPH as a post‐PE syndrome, even in the context of the contemporary DOAC era.

Risk Factors of CTEPH After Acute PE

One of the key elements of this study was the investigation of the risk factors associated with the development of CTEPH after acute PE in the era of DOAC therapy. By identifying patients with acute PE who are at a risk of developing CTEPH, it may be possible to decrease the incidence of undiagnosed CTEPH and allocate resources more effectively to high‐risk populations. This study identified possible risk factors for CTEPH after acute PE, including female sex, longer interval between symptom onset and diagnosis, hypoxemia at diagnosis, right heart load, low d‐dimer levels, and unprovoked PE. These risk factors may be useful for risk stratification during the long‐term follow‐up after acute PE. A longer interval between symptom onset and diagnosis suggests that the more challenging clot dissolution and reduced anticoagulation effectiveness may have influenced the development of CTEPH.

Acute‐on‐Chronic PE?

One limitation in identifying the incidence of CTEPH after acute PE is the mixture of incident cases and preexisting CTEPH. 9 , 10 Acute PE may be diagnosed when additional acute thromboembolism is present in individuals who already have CTEPH. This study included patients with preexisting CTEPH at the time of acute pulmonary diagnosis. Therefore, the incidence of CTEPH may overestimate the “true” risk of CTEPH, as a cohort study has shown that CTEPH can often be misclassified as acute PE. 11 This study showed that one of the risk factors for CTEPH was the degree of right heart load, the definition of which included a high tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient on echocardiography. This indicates that CTEPH may have been present during acute PE, which is in agreement with previous studies. 11 , 12 Therefore, it is possible that CTEPH diagnosed in this study existed before acute PE, and the reason why the incidence of CTEPH has not decreased in the DOAC era may be due to the inclusion of acute‐on‐chronic cases. Radiologic findings on computed tomography pulmonary angiography at the time of acute PE may help reveal chronic thrombotic lesions. 13 This study reminds that clinicians should be aware that patients diagnosed with CTEPH following acute PE may have preexisting CTEPH.

In conclusion, this study indicates that DOACs do not influence the incidence of CTEPH after acute PE. The risk factors for CTEPH after acute PE identified in this study may suggest the presence of preexisting CTEPH. Therefore, the incidence of “true” CTEPH development after acute PE in the DOAC era remains unclear. However, this study emphasizes that the development of CTEPH, the most severe post‐PE syndrome, is rare but should be considered during follow‐up after acute PE, even in the modern anticoagulant therapy era. This study also indicates the presence of preexisting CTEPH at the time of acute PE and implies the difficulty in determining the true incidence of CTEPH after acute PE.
