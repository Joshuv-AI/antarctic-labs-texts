On the entropy growth of sums of iid discrete random variables
Riccardo Castellano, Pavel Sekatski

Abstract
We derive an asymptotic lower bound on the Shannon entropy H H of sums of N N arbitrary iid discrete random variables. The derived bound H ≥ r ⁡ ( X ) 2 ​ log ⁡ ( N ) + 𝑐𝑠𝑡 H\geq\frac{r(X)}{2}\log(N)+{\it cst} is given in terms of the incommensurability rank r ⁡ ( X ) r(X) of the random variable – a positive integer quantity that we introduce. The derivation does not rely on central limit theorems, but builds upon the known expressions of the asymptotic entropy of the multinomial distribution and sums of iid lattice random variables, which correspond to the case r ⁡ ( X ) = 1 r(X)=1 .



I
Introduction and background

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (iid) random variables that take values in a countable subset of real numbers
\chi\subset\mathbb{R}
, and have a finite variance
{\rm Var}(X)
. We are interested to lower bound the Shannon entropy
H
of the sum
T_{N}:=\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}X_{i}
with an expression of the form

H(T_{N})\geq\frac{r(\chi)}{2}\log(N\lambda(X))+o(1).

(1)

Historically, it is the case of continuos random variables that attracted more attention
Linnik 1959
;
Barron 1986
;
Artstein et al. 2004
;
Madiman and Barron 2007
. Here, it is the
differential
Shannon entropy
h(T_{N})\to\frac{1}{2}\log(N)+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e {\rm Var}(X))
that converges
Barron 1986
to that of a Gaussian random variable from below
Artstein et al. 2004
;
Madiman and Barron 2007
.

In the case of discrete random variables,
T_{N}
is supported on a discrete set, which plays a crucial role for the calculation of the Shannon entropy
H(T_{N})
. A bound of the form (
1
) is known for the particular case of
lattice
random variables, which we now define.

Definition 1
.

X
is a
lattice random variable
, if it takes values in a
lattice set

\chi
, i.e. such that

\displaystyle\chi\subset\big\{h\,n+a:n\in\mathbb{Z}\big\}

(2)

for some
h,a\in\mathbb{R}
. The
maximal span

h(X)=h(\chi)
is the largest real number
h
for which Eq. (
2
) can be fulfilled.

Notice that random variables that only take one or two values (
|\chi|\leq 2
) are always a lattice.

Theorem 2
.

Gavalakis and Kontoyiannis 2024
;
Takano 1987

Let
X
be a lattice random variable. Then in the
N\rightarrow\infty
limit

H(T_{N})=\frac{1}{2}\log(N)+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e \frac{{\rm Var}(X)}{h(\chi)^{2}})+o(1).

(3)

Upon a shift by
a
and a rescaling by
\frac{1}{h}
, irrelevant for the entropy, the values of a lattice random variable are integer
n_{i}\in\mathds{Z}
. Intuitively, if
\chi
is not an affine transformation of a set of integers, but contains numbers that are somehow
incommensurable
, there can only be fewer different combinations of outcomes that sum up to the same value of
T_{N}
, making the entropy
H(T_{N})
larger. The goal of this paper is to formalize this intuition and derive a generalized version of Theorem
2
that applies for all discrete random variables.

As a last preliminary result we recall that the asymptotic entropy of a multinomial distribution has a simple expression.

Theorem 3
.

Kaji 2015

Let
\bm{n}(N,\bm{p})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
be a multinomial random variable describing
N
trials with
d
different outcomes of probabilities
\bm{p}:=(p_{1},..p_{d})
. In the large
N
limit its entropy is given by

\displaystyle H(\bm{n}(N,\bm{p}))=\frac{d-1}{2}\log(2\pi e N \sqrt[d-1]{p_{1}..p_{d}})+o(1)

(4)

II
Incommensurable partitions and the first generalization

First, we need to define some notion of “incommensurability” of the set of values
\chi
of the random variables
X_{i}
, which would quantify how far we are from the case of a lattice random variable.
Consider a
partition
of a set

\chi=\underset{j=1}{\overset{k}{\bigcup}}\chi_{j}

into
k
disjoint subsets
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}
. For each of these sets let

\mathcal{S}_{m}(\chi_{j}):=\left\{\sum_{x_{i}\in\chi_{j}}n_{i}x_{i}:n_{i}\in\mathbb{N},\sum_{i}n_{i}=m\right\}

(5)

be the set of numbers that can be obtained by summing
m
, not necessarily different, numbers from
\chi_{j}
. For convenience we also introduce the following notation

\mathbb{N}^{k}_{N}:=\left\{\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}=N\right\},

(6)

for the set of natural
k
-vectors
\bm{n}
whose elements sum up to
N
(with
0\in\mathbb{N}
). With its help, we define the following property.

Definition 4
.

A collection of disjoint sets
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}
of
real numbers (e.g. a partition of
\chi
) is called
incommensurable
(or
k
-incommensurable) if

1.

All
\chi_{j}
are non-empty and
\chi_{j}\neq\{0\}
.

2.

For all
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{m},\bm{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}_{N}
and
{\bm{y}}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
,
{\bm{y}}^{\prime}=(y_{1}^{\prime},\dots,y_{k}^{\prime})
with
y_{j}\in S_{m_{j}}(\chi_{j})
and
y_{j}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}_{n_{j}}(\chi_{j})
we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}y_{j}^{\prime}\implies y_{j}=y_{j}^{\prime}\quad\forall j\in[1..k].

(7)

In words, for a random variable
X
taking values in
\chi
, which admits a
k
-
incommensurable
partition, any possible value
T_{N}=t
can be
uniquely
decomposed as
t=y_{1}+\dots+y_{k}
, where each
y_{j}
is a sum of numbers sampled from
\chi_{j}
and there are
N
sampled numbers in total. This property implies a bijection between the outcome of
T_{N}
and the
k
outcomes of the following random variables

Y_{j}^{(N)}:=\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}X_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\chi_{j}}(X_{i}),\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbf{1}_{\chi_{j}}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\in\chi_{j}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(8)

are indicator functions verifying if
X_{i}
takes value in
\chi_{j}
, and each
Y_{j}^{(N)}
only sums the values drawn from
\chi_{j}
.

Some examples and observations on how to construct an incommensurable partition of
\chi
can be found in App.
A
.
In all of the examples the sets
\chi
admit incommensurable partitions in sets
\chi_{j}
which are lattices. For such cases, the entropy of
T_{N}
can be bounded with the help theorems
2
and
3
, indeed we now prove the following bound.

Theorem 5
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be a collection of iid discrete random variables taking values in the set
\chi
, which admits an
k
-incommensurable partition
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1,\dots,k}
, with
p_{j}={\rm Pr}(X_{i}\in\chi_{j})
, and such that each
\chi_{j}
is a lattice, and
\chi_{1},\dots\chi_{s}
are the only sets with a single element. Then, the following bounds hold

\displaystyle s=k:\,\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{k-1}{2}\log\left(2\pi eN\sqrt[k-1]{p_{1}p_{2}..p_{k}}\right)+o(1)

(9)

\displaystyle s\leq k-1:\,\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j})\right)+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( p_j \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})+o(1)

(10)

\displaystyle s\leq k-2:\,\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{k+(k-s-1)}{2}\log(2\pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( p_j^2 \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})+o(1)

(11)

where the conditional random variables
\tilde{X}^{(j)}
have probability distribution fixed as
{\rm Pr}(\tilde{X}^{(j)}=x)={\rm Pr}(X=x|X\in\chi_{j})
and
h(\chi_{j})
is the maximal span of
\chi_{j}
. In addition, for
s=k-1
the expression (
10
) is an equality.

Proof.

Consider the following random variables

\displaystyle n_{N}^{(j)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\chi_{j}}(X_{i})\qquad\text{with}\qquad\bm{1}_{\chi_{j}}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\in\chi_{j}\\
0&x\notin\chi_{j}\end{cases}

(12)

\displaystyle Y_{N}^{(j)}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\,\mathbf{1}_{\chi_{j}}(X_{i})

(13)

where
n_{N}^{(j)}
counts the number of times the variables
X_{i}
took values in each set
\chi_{j}
, and
Y_{N}^{(j)}
is the sum of these values, with
T_{N}=\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}X_{i}=\underset{j=1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}Y_{N}^{(j)}
. Since the sets
\chi_{j}
are incommensurable, we known that each value
T_{N}=t
admits a unique decomposition
t=\underset{j}{\sum}y_{j}
as a sum of values of the variable
Y_{N}^{(j)}
. Hence, there is a bijection between the random variable
T_{N}
and the vector of random variables
\bm{Y}_{N}=(Y_{N}^{(1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)})
, implying

H(T_{N})=H(\bm{Y}_{N}).

(14)

Recall that only the first
s\leq k
sets
\chi_{j}
contain a single element
x_{j}\neq 0
(by definition
4
). These sets are special, as for them the identity
Y_{N}^{(j)}=x_{j}n_{N}^{(j)}
gives a bijection between
Y_{N}^{(j)}
and
n_{N}^{(j)}
. Using the chain rule we can thus write

\displaystyle H(\bm{Y}_{N})

\displaystyle=H(Y_{N}^{(1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(s)})+H(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|Y_{N}^{(1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(s)})

(15)

\displaystyle=H(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})+H(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)}).

(16)

Here, the first term
H(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})
can be readily computed. To do so we will distinguish two cases. For
s=k=|\chi|
, we have
n_{N}^{(1)}+\dots+n_{N}^{(s)}=N
and the vector
(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})
follows a multinomial distribution with
s=k=|\chi|
outcomes of probabilities
p_{1},\dots p_{s}
. By Theorem
3

we find

H(\bm{Y}_{N})=H(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})=\frac{k-1}{2}\log\left(2\pi eN\sqrt[k-1]{p_{1}p_{2}..p_{k}}\right)+o(1)

(17)

proving the theorem for the special case.
For
s<k
,
n_{N}^{(1)}+\dots+n_{N}^{(s)}\neq N
in general and the vector of random variables
(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})
follows the multinomial distribution with N trials and
s+1
events of probabilities
p_{1},\dots,p_{s},1-\underset{j=1}{\overset{s}{\sum}}p_{j}
, where the last event corresponds to
X_{j}\notin\chi_{1},..\chi_{s}
. Again, using Theorem
3
we find

\displaystyle H(n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)})

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\log\left(2\pi eN\big(p_{1}\dots p_{s}(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j})\big)^{1/s}\right)-o(1)

(18)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{2}\log( 2 \pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j})\right)-o(1)

(19)

To bound the second term in Eq. (
16
) we note that the knowledge of all
n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)}
does not reveal more information on the remaining variables
Y_{N}^{(j)}
(with
j\geq s+1
) than the knowledge of their sum
\underset{j=1}{\overset{s}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}
, therefore

\displaystyle H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|n_{N}^{(1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(s)}\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=1}^{s}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)

(20)

Since the total number of trials is fixed, knowing
\underset{j=1}{\overset{s}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}
is equivalent to knowing
\underset{j=s+1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}=N-\underset{j=1}{\overset{s}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}
.

Next, we separately discuss the lower and upper-bounds on
H(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|\underset{j=s+1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)})
. For the lower bound, by data processing inequality we conclude that the conditional entropy could only be lower if we knew all the individual values of
n_{N}^{(j)}
, hence

H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)\geq H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}\right).

(21)

For the upper bound, we essentially use the processing inequality with the chain rule again to get

\displaystyle H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)},n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle=H\left(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}\right)+H\left(n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)

(23)

Remark that both bounds (
21
,
22
) are tight for
s=k-1
where
n_{N}^{(k)}=\underset{j=s+1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}
.

Let us first compute the last term in the upper bound (
23
). To do so introduce
q=\underset{j=s+1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}p_{j}
, and note that for
n=\underset{j=s+1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}n_{N}^{(j)}
the numbers
\left(n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}\right)
follow a multinomial distribution with
k-s
outcomes of probability
\frac{p_{j}}{q}
. Therefore, by Theorem
3
we get

\displaystyle H\left(n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\,q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\,H\left(n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)}|\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}n_{N}^{(j)}=n\right)

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\,q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\frac{k-s-1}{2}\log\left(2\pi en\big(\frac{p_{s+1}}{q}\dots\frac{p_{k}}{q}\big)^{1/(k-s-1)}\right)+o(1)

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{k-s-1}{2}\log\left(2\pi eN\right)+\frac{1-\delta_{1,k-s-1}}{2}\log\left(p_{s+1\dots p_{k}}\right)+o(1),

(26)

where we used the fact that for the binomial distribution
\mathds{E}[\log(n)]=\log(q N)+o(1)
in the assymptotic limit.

Next, compute the expression on the rhs of (
21
), which also appears in the upper bound. In fact, when the total numbers of time
n_{N}^{(j)}
the outcomes of
X_{i}
fall into each set
\chi_{j}
are known, the variables
Y_{N}^{(j)}
become independent. Therefore,

H(Y_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,Y_{N}^{(k)}|n_{N}^{(s+1)},\dots,n_{N}^{(k)})=\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}H(Y_{N}^{(j)}|n_{N}^{(j)}),

(27)

and it remains to bound the individual entropies. To do so note that each
n_{N}^{(j)}
follows a binomial distribution so

\displaystyle H(Y_{N}^{(j)}|n_{N}^{(j)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\,p_{j}^{n}(1-p_{j})^{N-n}H(Y_{N}^{(j)}|n_{N}^{(j)}=n)

(28)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}\,p_{j}^{n}(1-p_{j})^{N-n}H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}\right),

(29)

were
\tilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}\in\chi_{j}
with
{\rm Pr}(\tilde{X}_{i}^{(j)}=x)={\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x|X_{i}\in\chi_{j})=\frac{{\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x)}{p_{j}}
. Since each
\chi_{j}
is a lattice by assumptions, using Theorem
2

we find

\displaystyle H(Y_{N}^{(j)}|n_{N}^{(j)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(N p_j)+\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1)

(30)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log( p_j \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1),

(31)

Combining all the terms together we thus find

\displaystyle H(T_{N})\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j}\right)\right)+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( p_j \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1),

(32)

which is tight for
s=k-1
, and for
s<k-1

\displaystyle H(T_{N})\leq\frac{k+(k-s-1)}{2}\log(2\pi e N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}p_{j}\right)\right)+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( p_j^2 \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1).

(33)

∎

By the lemma
13
when the partition of
\chi
in sets with single elements
\{\chi_{j}=\{x_{j}\}\}_{j=1,\dots d}
is incommensurable, so is the the partition obtained by merging two sets into one, e.g.
\chi_{j}=\{x_{j}\}
for
j=1,\dots d-2
and
\chi_{d-1}=\{x_{d-1},x_{d}\}
. One can verify that using the Theorem
5
for these two partitions gives exactly the same bound for the entropy. Hence, in principle, one never needs to invoke the theorem for the case
s=k
.

III
Incommensurability rank and the main result

Upon further inspection one realises that the random variables for which
\chi
admits an incommensurable partition in lattices are very special, hence the applicability of Theorem
5
is quite limited. To obtain a bound applicable to all discrete random variables, we now introduce a notion which only characterizes the incommensurability of a subset of
\chi
.

Definition 6
.

Consider a discrete set of real numbers
\chi
. A collection of disjoint sets
\{\chi_{1},..\chi_{k}\}
is called a
k-canonical prepartition
(or canonical prepartition) of
\chi
if it satisfies the following properties:

1.

\chi_{j}\subset\chi

2.

\chi_{j}
is a non-empty lattice for
j=1,..,k
(definition
1
).

3.

\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1,..,k}
is incommensurable (definition
4
).

If
|\chi_{j}|=1
for all
j=1,\dots,k
we say that the canonical prepartition is
degenerate
.

Note that when
\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\chi_{j}=\chi
, the collection
\{\chi_{1}\dots,\chi_{k}\}
is an incommensurable partition of
\chi
in lattices, recovering the setting of Theorem
5
.

For a general random variable characterizing all the canonical prepartitions is non-trivial (see the examples in the App.
A
). Nevertheless, the following general result is enlightening. To state it we recall the notion of the
rational span
of a set
\chi\subset\mathbb{R}
, given by

{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\chi):=\left\{\underset{x\in\chi}{\sum}q_{x}x:q_{x}\in\mathbb{Q}\right\}.

(34)

That is the set containing all linear combination of elements of
\chi
with
rational
coefficients.

Lemma 7
.

Any finite set
\chi=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\}
admits a
q
-canonical prepartition, where
q:={\rm dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}[{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\chi)]
is the dimension of the rational span of
\chi
seen as vector space over the field of rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

First, reorder the values
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
such that the first
q
values
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{q}
are linearly independent and form a basis of the vector space
{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\chi)
(which implies that non of these elements is zero). Then, define the following sets

\chi_{j}:={\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{j})\cap(\chi\setminus\{0\})\quad\text{for}\quad j=1,\dots,q\qquad\text{and}\qquad\overline{\chi}=\chi\setminus\underset{j=1}{\overset{q}{\bigcup}}\chi_{j}.

(35)

Linear independence of
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{q}
ensures that that
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{q}\}
are disjoint and incommensurable (implying 2), while
|\chi|=d
with
x_{j}\in\chi_{j}\subset{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{j})
ensures that they are non-empty lattices with
\chi_{j}\neq\{0\}
(implying 1 and 3). Hence
\{\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{q},\overline{\chi}\}
is a
q
-canonical prepartition of
\chi
.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of the paper. But first we give a technical lemma used in the proof.

Lemma 8
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be a collection of iid random variable taking values in some set
\chi
. For any bipartition
\chi=\chi_{g}\cup\chi_{g}^{c}
in two disjoint sets
\chi_{g}\cap\chi_{g}^{c}=\varnothing
, with
{\rm Pr}(X_{i}\in\chi_{g})=q
, define the random variables

Y_{i}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad{\rm Pr}(Y_{i}=x)={\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x|X_{i}\in\chi_{g})=\frac{{\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x)}{q}

(36)

than the following bound holds

H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right).

(37)

Proof.

Each random variable
X_{i}
can be decomposed as
X_{i}=C_{i}Y_{i}+(1-C_{i})Z_{i}
where
Y_{i},Z_{i},C_{i}

are independent,
C_{i}\in\{0,1\}
is Bernoulli with
{\rm Pr}(C_{i}=1)=q
,
Y_{i}
is given in Eq. (
36
) and

Z_{i}\qquad\text{such that}\qquad{\rm Pr}(Z_{i}=x)={\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x|X_{i}\in\chi_{g}^{c})=\frac{{\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x)}{1-q}.

(38)

This decomposition correspondences to first sampling a biased coin
C_{i}
deciding if
X_{i}
is in the set
\chi_{g}
or
\chi_{g}^{c}
, and then sampling the value in these sets from the conditional distributions. We will use the notation
\bm{C}=C_{1}\dots C_{N}
,
\bm{Z}=Z_{1}\dots Z_{N}
and
\lVert\bm{c}\rVert:=\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}c_{i}
counting the number of ones in a bitstrings (the value of
\bm{C}
). For these variables we have

\displaystyle H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(C_{i}Y_{i}+(1-C_{i})Z_{i})\right)

(39)

\displaystyle\geq H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}(C_{i}Y_{i}+(1-C_{i})Z_{i})\big|\bm{C},\bm{Z}\right)

(40)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{c},\bm{z}}Pr(\bm{C}=\bm{c})Pr(\bm{Z}=\bm{z})\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}Y_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-c_{i})z_{i}\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{c}}Pr(\bm{C}=\bm{c})\,\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}c_{i}Y_{i}\right)=\sum_{\bm{c}}Pr(\bm{C}=\bm{c})\,\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\lVert\bm{c}\rVert}Y_{i}\right)

(42)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right).

(43)

∎

Theorem 9
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be a collection of iid random variable taking values in
\chi
, a set of real number that admits a k-canonical prepartition
\{\chi_{1},..\chi_{k}\}
(definition
6
) with
p_{j}={\rm Pr}(X_{i}\in\chi_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,k
, and
q=\underset{j=1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}p_{j}
. In addition, let
\chi_{1},..\chi_{s\leq k}
be the only subsets with one element, then the following bounds hold

\displaystyle s<k:\quad H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e N \lambda_1)-o(1)\qquad\quad\,\,\,\,\lambda_{1}:=\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right)\prod_{j=s+1}^{k}p_{j}\frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde{X}^{(j)})}{h(\chi_{j})^{2}}\right)^{1/k}

(44)

\displaystyle s=k:\quad H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k-1}{2}\log(2\pi e N \lambda_{2})-o(1)\qquad\lambda_{2}:=\left(\frac{p_{1}\dots p_{k}}{q^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}

(45)

Where
\tilde{X}^{(j)}
is the random variable such that
{\rm Pr}(\tilde{X}^{(j)}=x)={\rm Pr}(X_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}}=x|X_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}1}}\in\chi_{j})
and
h(\chi_{j})
is the maximal span of the lattice
\chi_{j}
.

Proof.

The proof simply consists of combining the Theorem
5
with the Lemma
8
. First, with the help of Lemma
8
we get rid of all the outcomes in
\overline{\chi}:=\chi\setminus(\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\chi_{j})
to obtain

H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right),

(46)

where
Y_{i}
are the conditional random variables

Y_{i}\in\chi\setminus\overline{\chi}\qquad\quad{\rm Pr}(Y_{i}=x)={\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x|X_{i}\notin\overline{\chi})=\frac{{\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x)}{q}\quad\text{for}\quad x\notin\overline{\chi}.

(47)

Then we apply the Theorem
5
to bound each term
H\left(\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}Y_{i}\right)
.

For
s<k
we find (using the fact that
\chi_{j}
are lattices)

H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e \, n)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{p_{1}}{q}\dots\frac{p_{s}}{q}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right)\right)+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( \frac{p_j}{q} \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1),

(48)

since
\tilde{X}^{(j)}=\tilde{Y}^{(j)}
. In the large
N
limit we know that
\underset{n=0}{\overset{N}{\sum}}\binom{N}{n}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\log(n)=\log(qN)+o(1)
, leading to

\displaystyle H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e \, q N )+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{p_{1}}{q}\dots\frac{p_{s}}{q}\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right)\right)+\sum_{j=s+1}^{k}\frac{1}{2}\log( \frac{p_j}{q} \frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde X^{(j)})}{h(\chi_j)^{2}})-o(1)

(49)

\displaystyle=\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e \, N)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(p_{1}\dots p_{s}\prod_{j=s+1}^{k}p_{j}\frac{{\rm Var}(\tilde{X}^{(j)})}{h(\chi_{j})^{2}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1-\sum_{j=1}^{s}\frac{p_{j}}{q}\right)-o(1).

(50)

For
s=k
we get

H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)\geq\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi e \, n)+\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{p_{1}}{q}\dots\frac{p_{k}}{q}\right)-o(1),

(51)

where once again we use that in the large
N
limit
\mathds{E}[\log(n)]=\log(q N)+o(1)
, completing the proof.
∎

Remark that if the sets
\chi_{1},..\chi_{k}
all have a single element (i.e. the prepartition is degenerate), one can merge any two of them to obtain a
(k-1)
-canonical prepartition (non-degenerate). One can verify that with the above result, the two prepartitions lead to identical bounds. This observation, and the fact that the bound on entropy we obtained is linear in
k
motivates the following definition.

Definition 10
.

Let
\chi
be a discreet set of real numbers with
|\chi|\geq 2
. We define its
incommensurability rank

r(\chi)
as the maximal integer
1\leq r(\chi)\leq|\chi|-1
such that
\chi
admits a non-degenerate
r
-canonical prepartition. For a singleton set
|\chi|=1
we set
r(\chi)=0
.

The lower bound
r(\chi)\geq 1
follows from the observation that
\chi_{1}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
is a non-degenerate
1
-canonical prepartition for each
\chi
. While the upper-bound
r(\chi)\leq|\chi|-1
trivially follows from the observation that a set can not be partitioned in more that
|\chi|-1
disjoint subsets which are not all singleton.

Definition 11
.

Let
X
be a discrete non-deterministic random variable
X
taking values in
\chi\subset\mathds{R}
, we call its
incommensurability rank

r(X)
the maximal integer
1\leq r(X)\leq|\chi|-1
such that a shifted set
\chi^{\prime}=\{x+a:x\in\chi\}
with
a\in\mathds{R}
admits a non-degenerate
r
-canonical prepartition. For a deterministic random variables we set
r(X)=0
.

Note that a priori, the incommensurability rank of a set
\chi
can be increased by a shift of the set, see example around Eq. (
56
). This is why we have split the definition of the rank for a set and for a random variable. Nevertheless, the Lemma
14
in the appendix shows that they always coincide, i.e. the incommensurability rank of a set
\chi
can not be increased by a shift. Additionally, Lemma
15
demonstrate that lattice random variables have unit rank
r(X)=1
, and, conversely, any random variable with finite domain
|\chi|<\infty
and unit rank is lattice.

We can now state the equivalent of Theorem
9
in terms of the incommensurability rank of
X
, that is for the canonical prepartition with the optimal scaling of the entropy in the large
N
limit.

Corollary 12
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be a collection of iid discrete random variable taking values in a set
\chi
of real numbers. Then the following bounds holds

\displaystyle H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)\geq\frac{r(\chi)}{2}\log( 2\pi e \lambda_1 N )-o(1)

(52)

where
r(\chi)
is the incommensurability rank of
X
and
\lambda_{1}
is given in Eq. (
44
) for any non-degenerate
r(\chi)
-canonical partition of
\chi
.

IV
conclusion

While we do not posses explicit examples of random variables not saturating corollary
12
in the scaling, we suspect that the lower bound is not tight in general. Instead, it would be interesting to establish an upper-bound in the general case, theorem
5
suggests that the entropy cannot scale faster than
H\leq\frac{2r(\chi)-1}{2}\log(N)+{\it cst}
.

Finally, it is also worth mentioning that for a random variable
X
admitting a
k
-canonical prepartition
\chi_{g}=\cup_{i=1}^{k}\chi_{i}\subset\chi
with
|\chi_{i}|=1
iff
i=1,\dots s
and
{\rm Pr}(X_{i}\in\chi_{g})=q
, the lower bound in theorem
9
is obtained by combining the inequalities (
37
),(
16
) and (
21
). Those can be summarized as

\displaystyle H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{n=0}^{N}\binom{N}{n}q^{n}(1-q)^{N-n}\,H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)

(53)

\displaystyle H\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq H(n_{n}^{(1)},\dots,n_{n}^{(s)})+H(Y_{n}^{(s+1)}|n_{n}^{(s+1)})+\dots+H(Y_{n}^{(k)}|n_{n}^{(k)})

(54)

with the random variables
Y_{i}
with
{\rm Pr}(Y_{i}=x):={\rm Pr}(X_{i}=x|X_{i}\in\chi_{g})
and
n_{n}^{(j)}=\underset{i=1}{\overset{n}{\sum}}\bm{1}_{\chi_{j}}(Y_{i})
. Both expressions are simple consequences of the data processing inequality for the conditional entropy (in addition to the incommensurability of the sets
\chi_{i}
), and hence directly apply to any entropy that has this property.

Appendix A
Examples of incommensurability partitions

A.1
Illustrative examples

Let us illustrate the notion of incommensurability partitions with a pair of examples.
Consider the set
\chi=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\chi_{j}
which is not necessarily finite. Let
\chi_{j}\subset\{h_{j}n:n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}\}
with
h_{j}>0
. If the real numbers
(h_{1},\dots,h_{k})
are linearly independent over the field of rational numbers, i.e.
\underset{j=1}{\overset{k}{\sum}}q_{j}h_{j}=0\implies q_{j}=0
for
q_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then the partition
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}
is incommensurable. Indeed, linear independence guarantees that for integers
n_{i}
the equation
t=n_{1}h_{1}+\dots n_{k}h_{k}
has at most a unique solution.

Constructing partitions such that it’s subsets are not linear independent (over
\mathbb{Q}
) is also straitforword, for example consider
\chi=\{1,\pi,1+\pi\}
into
\chi_{1}=\{1\},\chi_{2}=\{\pi\}
and
\chi_{3}=\{1+\pi\}
. Indeed, the following system of equations for natural variables
n_{1},n_{2}
and
n_{3}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}n_{1}+n_{2}\pi+n_{3}(1+\pi)=t=k+k^{\prime}\pi,\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=N.\end{array}\right.\iff\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}n_{1}+n_{3}=k,\\
n_{2}+n_{3}=k^{\prime},\\
n_{1}+n_{2}+n_{3}=N.\end{array}\right.

(55)

has at most one solution. For this case, is crucial that in the definition of an incommensurable partition, the total number
N
of sampled random variables is fixed.

A.2
An example of
r(\chi)>{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}[{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\chi)]

Finally we make an example were the best
k
-canonical partition i.e. the one that yields the stronger inequality when applied to result
9
, is not given by the identification
\chi_{j}={\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(x_{j})\cap\chi
suggested by lemma
7
. Consider the set
\chi:=\{1,2,\pi,1+\pi,\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\}
and the following two canonical partitions

1.

\chi_{1}=\{1\},\;\chi_{2}=\{\pi\},\;\chi_{3}=\{\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\},\;\overline{\chi}=\{1+\pi\}
, is a non-degenerate
3
-canonical partition, obtained with the procedure indicated in lemma
7
. Accordingly
{\rm dim}_{\mathbb{Q}}[{\rm span}_{\mathbb{Q}}(\{1,\pi,1+\pi,\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\})]=3
. By using theorem
9
we would get an entropy lower bound scaling as
\frac{3}{2}\log{N}
.

2.

\chi_{1}:=\{1\},\;\chi_{2}:=\{\pi\},\;\chi_{3}:=\{1+\pi\},\;\chi_{4}:=\{\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\}
is a non-degenerate
4
-canonical partition, yielding a lower bound on the entropy scaling as
2\log(N)
by theorem
9
or equivalently by Corollary
12
.

The partition 2. proves that
\chi
has
r(\chi)\geq 4
, in fact, because the set has
5

. Thus, the construction in Lemma

\chi:=\{r,2r,r_{1},r_{2},r_{1}+r_{2},r_{3},r_{4},r_{3}+r_{4},\ldots,r_{2d-1}+r_{2d}\}.

Then we have

while the partition made by
\chi_{0}=\{r,2r\}

(3d+1)

A.3
Incommensurability is not invariant under uniform translations.

Here, we justify why the maximization over shifts in definition
11
was introduced. We show explicitly that the notion of incommensurable partition in not invariant under uniform translations. Consider two linearly independent real numbers
r_{1},r_{2}
and
\chi=\{r_{1},2r_{1},r_{2},2r_{2}\}
admits the incommensurable partition
\chi_{1}=\{r_{1},2r_{1}\}
with
\chi_{2}=\{r_{2},2r_{2}\}
, since
t=n\,r_{1}+m\,r_{2}
determines the naturals
n
and
m
uniquely. In contrast, for the shifted set
\chi=\{r_{1}+\epsilon,2+\epsilon,r_{2}+\epsilon,2r_{2}+\epsilon\}
the partition
\chi_{1}=\{r_{1}+\epsilon,2r_{1}+\epsilon\}
with
\chi_{2}=\{r_{2}+\epsilon,2r_{2}+\epsilon\}
is no longer incommensurable. Indeed, for
N=3
we can decompose
t=\underset{i=1}{\overset{N}{\sum}}X_{i}
in two different ways

t=2r_{1}+2r_{2}+3\epsilon=2(r_{1}+\epsilon)+(2r_{2}+\epsilon)=(2r_{1}+\epsilon)+2(r_{2}+\epsilon)

(56)

admits decompositions with different
\bm{y}=\binom{2(r_{1}+\epsilon)}{2r_{2}+\epsilon}\neq\bm{y}^{\prime}=\binom{2r_{1}+\epsilon}{2(r_{2}+\epsilon)}
, where we used the notation introduced in
4
.

Appendix B
Some properties of the incommensurability rank

First, we list some properties of incommensurable partitions that follow immediately from the definition and will be used later:

Lemma 13
.

Let
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}
be an incommensurable collection of disjoint sets of real numbers, then the following collections are also incommensurable

1.

Any collection where we remove one set
\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}\setminus\{\chi_{i}\}
.

2.

Any collection where we remove a subset from a set
(\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}\setminus\{\chi_{i}\})\cup\{\chi_{i}^{sub}\}
, with
\emptyset\neq\chi_{i}^{sub}\subset\chi_{i}
and
\chi_{i}^{sub}\neq\{0\}
.

3.

Any collection where we merge two sets
(\{\chi_{j}\}_{j=1..k}\setminus\{\chi_{i},\chi_{i^{\prime}}\})\cup\{\chi_{i}\cup\chi_{i^{\prime}}\}
.

Proof.

All the points follow as particular cases of the implication (
7
). For the point 1, take the vectors
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
with
y_{i}=m_{i}=y_{i}^{\prime}=n_{i}=0
and such that
\underset{j\neq i}{\sum}(y_{j}-y_{j}^{\prime})
, Eq. (
7
) implies
y_{j}=y_{j}^{\prime}
of all
j\neq i
implying that the collection without the set
\chi_{i}
is incommensurable.

For the point 2, take two vectors
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
such that
y_{i}
and
y_{i}^{\prime}
are obtained without using the values from
\chi_{i}^{sub}
. Again Eq. (
7
) implies
y_{j}=y_{j}^{\prime}
of all
j\neq i
.

For the point 3, for any pair of
k
-vector
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
with
\underset{j}{\sum}(y_{j}-y_{j}^{\prime})=0
define the
(k-1)
vectors
\tilde{\bm{y}}
and
\tilde{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
obtained by summing the values
\tilde{y}_{i}=y_{i}+y_{i^{\prime}}
, with

\underset{j}{\sum}(y_{j}-y_{j}^{\prime})=0\Longleftrightarrow\underset{j}{\sum}(\tilde{y}_{j}-\tilde{y}_{j}^{\prime})=0
. Since the original collection is incommensurable
\underset{j}{\sum}(\tilde{y}_{j}-\tilde{y}_{j}^{\prime})=0
uniquely specifies the vectors
\bm{y}
with
\bm{y}^{\prime}
and
\tilde{\bm{y}}
with
\tilde{\bm{y}}^{\prime}
.
∎

We now prove that the incommensurability rank of a random variable can always be obtained without shifting it’s outcomes.

Lemma 14
.

For a discrete random variable
X
taking values in
\chi\subset\mathds{R}
one has
r(\chi)=r(X)
.

Proof.

By construction we know that
r(\chi)\leq r(X)
. Consider the shifted set
\chi^{\prime}:=\{x+c:x\in\chi\}
such that
r(\chi^{\prime})=r(X)
. It admits a canonical prepartition
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{r}
. By lemma
13
we can drop elements from these sets until obtaining the incommensurable prepartition

\chi_{1}^{\prime}=\{x_{0}^{\prime},x_{1}^{\prime}\},\,\chi_{2}^{\prime}=\{x_{2}^{\prime}\},\,\dots\chi_{r}^{\prime}=\{x_{r}^{\prime}\}

(57)

with
x_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0
for
i\geq 2
. We thus have that for all
n_{0}+n_{1}+\dots n_{r}=N\in\mathbb{N}
the sum

\displaystyle t^{\prime}=n_{0}x_{0}^{\prime}+n_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}+\dots n_{r}x_{r}^{\prime}

(58)

implies a unique decomposition of
t^{\prime}=\underset{i=1}{\overset{r}{\sum}}y_{r}^{\prime}
with
y_{1}^{\prime}=n_{0}x_{0}^{\prime}+n_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}
,
y_{2}=n_{2}x_{2}^{\prime}\,\dots\,y_{r}^{\prime}=n_{r}x_{r}^{\prime}
.
In turns, this implies that
n_{2},\dots,n_{r}
, and thus
n_{0}+n_{1}=N-\underset{i=2}{\overset{r}{\sum}}n_{i}

are uniquely specified by
t^{\prime}
. But
y_{1}^{\prime}=n_{0}x_{0}^{\prime}+n_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}
then also has a unique solution for

n_{1}
and
n_{2}
. Hence the value of
t^{\prime}=\underset{i=0}{\overset{r}{\sum}}n_{0}x_{0}^{\prime}+n_{1}x_{1}^{\prime}+\dots n_{r}x_{r}^{\prime}
specifies all
n_{i}
uniquely.

Now consider the shifted values
x_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}-a
form the original set. The observed total
t=\underset{i}{\sum}n_{i}x_{i}
must also determine all the
n_{0},\dots,n_{r}
, since it determines
t^{\prime}=t+Na
, which determines all the
n_{i}
. Finally, to obtain a non-degenerate
r
-canonical prepartition from the singleton sets
\{x_{0}\},\dots\{x_{r}\}
simply group any two elements into a set with two elements. In particular, if
x_{i}=0
it must one of the grouped values. This shows that if the shifted set
\chi^{\prime}
admits a
r
-canonical prepartition, so does the set
\chi
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 15
.

A non-deterministic lattice random variable has rank
r(X)=1
, conversely any random variable
X
with finite domain
|\chi|=d
and rank
r(X)=1
is a lattice random variable.

Proof.

A deterministic random variable has
r(X)=0\neq 1
. A random variable that takes two values is always lattice and has
r(X)=1
. Hence, we now assume
|\chi|\geq 3
.

We will first demonstrate that a non-deterministic lattice random variables
X
has
r(X)=1
. We need to show that such
X
admits no non-degenerate canonical
k
-partition with
k\geq 2
. By Lemma
13
it is sufficient to show that any partition
\chi_{1}=\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
and
\chi_{2}=\{x_{3}\}
and
\overline{\chi}=\chi\setminus\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
for any
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\chi
is not
2
-canonical, since by applying the operations 1 and 2 from the Lemma any
k
-canonical non-degenerate partition can be reduced to such a
2
-canonical non-degenerate partition. Since
\chi
is a lattice it is thus sufficient to show that the two set

\{a+h\,k_{1},a+h\,k_{2}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{a+h\,k_{3}\}\qquad\text{with}\qquad k_{1},k_{2},k_{3}\in\mathbb{Z},

(59)

are commensurable. To see this, for some
n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime},m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the two following vectors of natural numbers

\bm{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}n_{1}=n_{1}^{\prime}|k_{2}-k_{3}|\\
n_{2}=n_{2}^{\prime}|k_{1}-k_{3}|\\
n_{3}=N-n_{1}-n_{2}\end{array}\right),\quad\bm{m}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}m_{1}=m_{1}^{\prime}|k_{2}-k_{3}|\\
m_{2}=m_{2}^{\prime}|k_{1}-k_{3}|\\
m_{3}=N-m_{1}-m_{2}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{N}_{N}^{3}

(60)

where
N
is chosen large enough such that
n_{3},m_{3}\geq 0
. This choice defines the following real vectors

\bm{y}=\binom{y_{1}}{y_{2}}=\binom{n_{1}x_{1}+n_{2}x_{2}}{n_{3}x_{3}}\quad\text{and}\qquad\bm{y}^{\prime}=\binom{y_{1}^{\prime}}{y_{2}^{\prime}}=\binom{m_{1}x_{1}+m_{2}x_{2}}{m_{3}x_{3}}.

(61)

By definition
4
this choice exhibits the commensurability of the sets if
y_{1}+y_{2}=y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime}
and
y_{2}\neq y_{2}^{\prime}
. We now show that there is always a choice of
n_{1}^{\prime},n_{2}^{\prime},m_{1}^{\prime},m_{2}^{\prime}
fulfilling both conditions. The second condition can be rewritten as

\displaystyle y_{2}\neq y_{2}^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n_{1}+n_{2}\neq m_{1}+m_{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(n_{1}^{\prime}-m_{1}^{\prime})|k_{2}-k_{3}|+(n_{2}^{\prime}-m_{2}^{\prime})|k_{1}-k_{3}|\neq 0.

(62)

In turn, for the first one we get

\displaystyle y_{1}+y_{2}=y_{1}^{\prime}+y_{2}^{\prime}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n_{1}x_{1}+n_{2}x_{2}+(N-n_{1}-n_{2})x_{3}=m_{1}x_{1}+m_{2}x_{2}+(N-m_{1}-m_{2})x_{3}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow

(63)

\displaystyle n_{1}(x_{1}-x_{3})+n_{2}(x_{2}-x_{3})=m_{1}(x_{1}-x_{3})+m_{2}(x_{2}-x_{3})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow

(64)

\displaystyle n_{1}^{\prime}|k_{2}-k_{3}|(k_{1}-k_{3})+n_{2}^{\prime}|k_{1}-k_{3}|(k_{2}-k_{3})=m_{1}^{\prime}|k_{2}-k_{3}|(k_{1}-k_{3})+m_{2}^{\prime}|k_{1}-k_{3}|(k_{2}-k_{3})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow

(65)

\displaystyle(n_{1}^{\prime}\pm n_{2}^{\prime})|k_{2}-k_{3}|(k_{1}-k_{3})=(m_{1}^{\prime}\pm m_{2}^{\prime})|k_{2}-k_{3}|(k_{1}-k_{3})\qquad\Longleftrightarrow

(66)

\displaystyle(n_{1}^{\prime}\pm n_{2}^{\prime})=(m_{1}^{\prime}\pm m_{2}^{\prime}),

(67)

where
\pm={\rm sign}(k_{1}-k_{3})(k_{2}-k_{3})
