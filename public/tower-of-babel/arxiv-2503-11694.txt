Abstract

Does
14
have a friend? Till now, this is an open question, i.e., we do not know the answer. In this note, we prove that a potential friend
F
of
14
is an odd, non-square positive integer.
7
appears in the prime factorization of
F
with even exponent while at most two prime divisors of
F
can have odd exponents in the prime factorization of
F
. If
p\mid F
such that
p
is congruent to
7
modulo
8
then
p^{2a}\mid\mid F
, for some positive integer
a
. Further, no prime divisor of
F
has an exponent congruent to
7
modulo
8
. The primes
3,5
can not appear simultaneously in the prime factorization of
F
. If
(3,F)>1
or
(5,F)>1
then
\omega(F)\geq 4
, otherwise
\omega(F)\geq 8
.

1
Introduction

For any positive integer
F
, the abundancy index
I(F)
is defined as
I(F)=\frac{\sigma(F)}{F}
, where
\sigma(F)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid F\\
d\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}d
. Positive integers
F
and
E
are friends if
F\neq E

and
I(F)=I(E)
. If there is no positive integer
E
other than
F
such that
I(F)=I(E)
, we say that
F
has no friends, that is,
F
is a solitary number. Many authors have already studied problems related to the abundancy index, in fact, one of the well-known open problems is the existence of an odd perfect number. To prove a number to be solitary, or giving a general method of finding a solitary number, is believed to be as difficult as of finding an odd perfect number.
10
is the smallest positive integer which is solitary or friendly is still unknown and thus to seek the answer, many authors
[
9
,
1
,
6
,
7
]
have given necessary conditions for a friend of
10
.
10
is not the only number whose classification is unknown to us, the status of
14,15,20
and many others are active research topics. In
[
8
]
, the author describes the nature of a friend of
15
while in
[
2
]
, the authors establish the necessary conditions for a friend of
20
. In this paper, we investigate friends of
14
and we give certain properties of a potential friend of 14.

2
Properties of the Abundancy Index

Some elementary properties of the abundancy index are below, the proofs of the following lemmas may be found in
[
4
,
10
]
.

Lemma 1
.

I(n)
is weakly multiplicative, that is, for any two co-prime positive integers
n
and
m
we have
I(nm)=I(n)I(m)
.

Lemma 2
.

If
\gamma,n
are two positive integers and
\gamma>1
. Then
I(\gamma n)>I(n)
.

Lemma 3
.

If
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
,
p_{3}
,…,
p_{k}
are
k
distinct prime numbers and
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
,
\gamma_{3}
,…,
\gamma_{k}
are positive integers then

\displaystyle I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}\biggl)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\biggl(\sum_{j=0}^{\gamma_{i}}p_{i}^{-j}\biggl)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}+1}-1}{p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}(p_{i}-1)}.

Lemma 4
.

If
p_{1}
,…,
p_{k}
are distinct prime numbers,
q_{1}
,…,
q_{k}
are distinct prime numbers such that
p_{i}\leq q_{i}
for all
1\leq i\leq k
. If
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},...,\gamma_{k}
are positive integers then

\displaystyle I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}\biggl)\geq I\biggl(\prod_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}\biggl).

Lemma 5
.

If
n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\gamma_{i}}
, then
I(n)<\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{p_{i}}{p_{i}-1}
.

3
Main Results

Note that,
I(14)=\frac{12}{7}
, therefore, a positive integer
F
is said to be friend of
14
if
I(F)=I(14)=\frac{12}{7}
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
F
be a friend of
14
then
F
is an odd positive non-square integer.

Proof.

Let a positive integer
F
be a friend of
14
then
F
must be greater than
14
as for any positive integer less than
14
,
I(F)\neq\frac{12}{7}
. Since
I(F)=\frac{12}{7}
i.e.,
\frac{\sigma(F)}{F}=\frac{12}{7}
i.e.,
7\sigma(F)=12F
, we have
7\mid F
and
12\mid\sigma(F)
as
(7,12)=1
, therefore we can write
F=7F^{\prime}
for some positive integer
F^{\prime}>2
. If possible, let
F^{\prime}
be an even positive integer. Then we can rewrite
F
as
F=14F^{\prime\prime}
for some positive integer
F^{\prime\prime}>1
, but then
I(F)>I(14)
by Lemma
2
. Therefore we can conclude that
(F^{\prime},2)=1
i.e.,
F
is an odd positive integer. Now if
F=7^{a}
for some positive integer
a>2
then it can not be a friend of
14
as by Lemma
5
,
I(7^{a})<\frac{7}{6}<\frac{12}{7}
. Therefore,
F
must be written as
F=7^{a_{1}}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
(
p_{1}=7
) where
p_{i}
are prime numbers and
a_{i}
are positive integers. If possible, suppose that all
a_{i}
are even. Then

I(F)=\frac{\sigma(F)}{F}=\frac{12}{7}

by Lemma
1
, we have

\displaystyle I(7^{a_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}I(p_{i}^{a_{i}})=\frac{12}{7}.

This implies

\displaystyle\sigma(7^{a_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}\sigma(p_{i}^{a_{i}})=12\cdot 7^{{a_{1}}-1}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{a_{i}},

that is, we get

\displaystyle(1+7+...+7^{a_{1}})\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}(1+p_{i}+\dots+p_{i}^{a_{i}})=12\cdot 7^{{a_{1}}-1}\cdot\prod_{i=2}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{a_{i}}.

Since
p_{i}>2
, for all
1\leq i\leq\omega(F)
the right hand side of the above expression is an even integer but the left hand side is odd since
a_{i}
are even which immediately implies
(1+p_{i}+\dots+p_{i}^{a_{i}})
is odd. Hence, all
a_{i}
are not even integers. Therefore,
F
is a non-square positive integer. This proves that
F
is an odd positive non-square integer.
∎

Remark 1
.

If
F
is friend of
14
, then from the proof of Theorem
1
, we can note that
4\mid\mid\sigma(F)
, as
F
is an odd positive integer.

Remark
1
is very crucial as we will be using it enormously in the upcoming proofs.

Theorem 2
.

Let
F
be a friend of
14
. If
p
is a prime divisor of
F
such that
p\equiv 7\pmod{8}
, then
p
appears in the prime factorization of
F
to an even exponent.

Proof.

Let
p
congruent to
7
modulo
8
be a prime divisor of
F
with odd exponent, that is,
p^{2a+1}\mid\mid F
, for some non-negative integer
a
. Then

\sigma(p^{2a+1})=1+p+p^{2}+\dots+p^{2a+1}\equiv 1-1+1\dots-1\pmod{8}=0.

This implies that
8\mid\sigma(p^{2a+1})
and so
8\mid\sigma(F)
but which is impossible as
F
is a friend of
14
, we have
4\mid\mid\sigma(F)
(from Remark
1
). Therefore, the exponent of
p
in the prime factorization of
F
must be an even positive integer.

∎

An immediate consequences of the preceding theorem, the following corollary describes that the exponent of the prime divisor
7
of
F
can not be odd.

Corollary 1
.

If
F
is a friend of
14
, then
7^{2a}\mid\mid F
, for some positive integer
a
.

Proof.

Since
F
is a friend of
14
,
7
is a prime divisor of
F
. As
7
is a prime satisfying
7\equiv 7\pmod{8}
, it follows from Theorem
2
that
7^{2a}\mid\mid F
, for some positive integer
a
.
∎

Theorem 3
.

If
F
is a friend of
14
then no prime divisor of
F
has exponent congruent to
7
modulo
8
.

Proof.

Let
p
be a prime divisor of
F
with exponent congruent to
7
modulo
8
, that is,
p^{a}\mid\mid F
where
a\equiv 7\pmod{8}
. Then

\displaystyle\sigma(p^{a})=1+p+\dots+p^{a}\equiv\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+(\pm 1)+\dots+(\pm 1)^{a}\pmod{8}\\
1+(\pm 3)+\dots+(\pm 3)^{a}\pmod{8}\end{array}\right.

since
a\equiv 7\pmod{8}
,

1+(\pm 1)+\dots+(\pm 1)^{a}\equiv 0\pmod{8}

and

1+(\pm 3)+\dots+(\pm 3)^{a}\equiv 0\pmod{8}.

This shows that
8\mid\sigma(p^{a})
, which implies
8\mid\sigma(F)
but
4\mid\mid\sigma(F)
(from Remark
1
). Hence, no prime divisor of
F
has exponent congruent to
7
modulo
8
.
∎

We may ask how many distinct prime divisors of
F
can have odd exponents in the prime factorization of
F
? The following theorem answers the question.

Theorem 4
.

If
F
is a friend of
14
then at most two distinct prime divisors of
F
have odd exponents in the prime factorization of
F
.

Proof.

If possible, suppose that,
F
has three distinct prime divisors
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}
with odd exponents
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
respectively in the prime factorization of
F
. Then

\sigma(p_{i}^{a_{i}})=1+p_{i}+\dots+p_{i}^{a_{i}}\equiv 1+1+\dots+1\pmod{2}=0.

This implies that
2\mid\sigma(p_{i}^{a_{i}})
, for
i=1,2,3
. Thus, we get
8\mid\sigma(p_{1}^{a_{1}})\sigma(p_{2}^{a_{2}})\sigma(p_{3}^{a_{3}})
, that is,
8\mid\sigma(F)
. But, since
F
is a friend of
14
,
4\mid\mid\sigma(F)
. Therefore, we conclude that at most two distinct prime divisors of
F
can have odd exponents in the prime factorization of
F
.
∎

Theorem 5
.

If
3
is a divisor of a friend
F
of
14
, then
3\mid\mid F
.

Proof.

Let
F=3^{a}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot m
be a friend of
14
, where
a,b,m\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}
. Then, using Lemma
2
we get

I(3^{a}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot m)\geq I(3^{3}\cdot 7^{2})=\frac{760}{441}>I(14).

Therefore
a
must be less than
3
. If possible, suppose that
a=2
. Then

I(3^{2}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot m)=\frac{12}{7}

which is equivalent to,

7\cdot\sigma(3^{2})\cdot\sigma(7^{2b})\cdot\sigma(m)=12\cdot 3^{2}\cdot 7^{2b-1}\cdot m

since
\sigma(3^{2})=13
,
13\mid 12\cdot 3^{2}\cdot 7^{2b-1}\cdot m
, that is,
13\mid m
. Therefore, let
m=13m^{\prime}
, for some positive integer
m^{\prime}
. Then we have
F=3^{2}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot 13m^{\prime}
. Using Lemma
2
we get that

I(F)\geq I(3^{2}\cdot 7^{2}\cdot 13)=\frac{38}{21}>I(14).

Hence
a
can not be
2
.This proves the theorem.
∎

Lemma 6
.

If
F
is a friend of
14
, then
3
and
5
can not appear simultaneously in the prime factorization of
F
.

Proof.

Let
F
be a friend of
14
and if possible, assume that
3,5
appears simultaneously in the prime factorization of
F
. Then using Lemma
2
we have

I(F)\geq I(3\cdot 5\cdot 7^{2})=\frac{456}{245}>I(14).

Therefore, it follows that either
3
or
5
can appear in the prime factorization of
F
but not together.
∎

We now give lower bounds for
\omega(F)
according to the prime divisors of
F
.

Theorem 6
.

If
F
is a friend of
14
, then
\omega(F)\geq 4
whenever
3\mid F
or
5\mid F
. Further, if
(3,F)=(5,F)=1
then
\omega(F)\geq 8
.

Proof.

Let
F
be a friend of
14
. If
3\mid F
then
5\nmid F
by Lemma
6
, therefore all prime divisors of
F
are greater than
5
. If possible, suppose that
F
has exactly three distinct prime divisors, that is,
F=3\cdot 7^{2a}\cdot p^{b}
, where
p>7
is a prime and
a,b\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}
. Then using Lemma
1
, Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
we have

I(F)\leq I(3\cdot 7^{2a}\cdot 11^{b})=I(3)\cdot I(7^{2a}\cdot 11^{b})<\frac{4}{3}\cdot\frac{7}{6}\cdot\frac{11}{10}=\frac{77}{45}<I(14).

Therefore,
F
can not have exactly three distinct prime divisors, hence
\omega(F)\geq 4
.

If
5\mid F
then
3\nmid F
by Lemma
6
, therefore all prime divisors of
F
are greater than
3
. If possible, suppose that
F
has exactly three distinct prime divisors, that is,
F=5^{a}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot p^{c}
, where
p>7
is a prime and
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}
. Then using Lemma
4
and Lemma
5
we have

I(F)\leq I(5^{a}\cdot 7^{2b}\cdot 11^{c})<\frac{5}{4}\cdot\frac{7}{6}\cdot\frac{11}{10}=\frac{77}{48}<I(14).

Therefore,
F
can not have exactly three distinct prime divisors, hence
\omega(F)\geq 4
.

Let
(3,F)=(5,F)=1
. Then every prime divisors of
F
are greater than
5
. If possible, suppose that
F
has at most seven distinct prime divisors, that is,
F=7^{2a}\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{\omega(F)}p_{i}^{a_{i}}
, where
p_{i+1}>p_{i}>7
are prime numbers,
a,a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}
and
\omega(F)\leq 6
. Then, by Lemma
2
, Lemma
4
, and Lemma
5
we get

\displaystyle I(F)

\displaystyle\leq I(7^{2a}\cdot\prod_{i=5}^{\omega(F)+4}q_{i}^{a_{i-4}})

\displaystyle\leq I(7^{2a}\cdot 11^{a_{1}}\cdot 13^{a_{2}}\cdot 17^{a_{3}}\cdot 19^{a_{4}}\cdot 23^{a_{5}}\cdot 29^{a_{6}})~~~~~~~(q_{i}\text{ is the i-th prime number})

\displaystyle<\frac{7\cdot 11\cdot 13\cdot 17\cdot 19\cdot 23\cdot 29}{6\cdot 10\cdot 12\cdot 16\cdot 18\cdot 22\cdot 28}=\frac{2800733}{1658880}<I(14).

Therefore,
F
can not have at most seven distinct prime divisors, hence
\omega(F)\geq 8
. This completes the proof.
∎
