Abstract
While the literature on grouped patterns in panel data analysis has received significant attention, little to no results are available on testing for their presence. We propose using existing tools for testing slope homogeneity in panels for this purpose. We highlight the key advantages and limitations of the available testing frameworks under a sequence of doubly local alternatives, where slopes are divided into dominant and remainder groups, with the size of the remainder groups and the slopes differences shrinking at a certain rate as the sample size increases. A Monte Carlo study corroborate our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Driven by empirical demand, the econometric literature on grouped structure in panel data has received significant attention in both econometrics and economics. Most contributions to date focus on identification, estimation, and inference for individual group membership and group-specific parameters; see, e.g.,
Bonhomme and
Manresa 2015
,
Bester and
Hansen 2016
,
Su
et al. 2016
,
Lu and
Su 2017
, and
Dzemski and
Okui 2024
. Before estimating such structures, an applied researcher typically faces a more basic diagnostic question: is there evidence of any grouped structure in the first place?
This paper propose a simple and readily implementable solution. We repurpose well-known test for slope homogeneity as diagnostics for the presence of a grouped structure, without estimating group membership or the number of groups. We show that these tests provide exactly the needed diagnostic when evaluated under appropriately designed sequences of local alternatives that encode grouped patterns, and we develop theory that clarifies when and why these tests have nontrivial power.

Our approach is guided by practicality: we rely on well-known tools that are readily available in standard software. The literature on testing slope homogeneity is long-standing. A seminal starting point is
Swamy 1970
’s a test statistic, based on deviations of individual slope estimates from the pooled estimator; refinements such as
Pesaran and
Yamagata 2008
remain among the most widely used tools for panel data models. In parallel, several LM tests have been developed for random-coefficient models, where the null of slope homogeneity is equivalent to the variance of the random coefficients being zero. Building on
Breusch and
Pagan 1979
(see also
Hsiao 2003
, Sec. 6.2.2.4),
Juhl and
Lugovskyy 2014
propose LM statistics for zero variance, and
Breitung
et al. 2016
further extend the methodology to empirically relevant settings. Other LM tests assess whether the regressors have predictive power for the restricted residuals under the null of slope homogeneity–
Su and
Chen 2013
in models with interactive fixed effects and
Lu and
Su 2017
, originally developed to determine the number of groups.

We analyze the power of established test statistics—
Pesaran and
Yamagata 2008
,
Su and
Chen 2013
, and the LM-type test of
Breitung
et al. 2016
—under a sequence of
doubly local alternatives
designed to capture grouped patterns. These alternatives are doubly local in the sense that (i) the population is split into a dominant group and a remainder set of units (possibly partitioned into several groups), and (ii) the slope differences across groups shrink at a controlled rate. This design allows us to characterize how power depends jointly on the convergence rate of group-specific differences and the relative size of the remainder group(s). We consider this setup across several scenarios: two groups; multiple groups, where the number of groups is either fixed or increases with the cross-sectional dimension
N
; and both large-
N
, large-
T
panels and large-
N
, fixed-
T
panels, where
T
denotes the time dimension.

Our analysis yields several findings. We derive the boundaries that characterize when grouped patterns are detectable by linking the rate at which slope differences vanish,
\gamma
, to the size of the remainder group(s),
M
. In large-
N
, large-
T
panels, the boundary is governed by
\gamma\asymp M^{1/2}T^{1/2}/N^{1/4}
. This result nests the classical
N^{1/4}\sqrt{T}
rate—when
M/N\to c>0
—reported in
Pesaran and
Yamagata 2008
,
Su and
Chen 2013
, and
Breitung
et al. 2016
, derived under analogous Pitman-drift sequences. Importantly, the tests remain informative even if
M/N\to 0
, provided
MT\to\infty
and the boundary condition above is satisfied, making sparse grouped alternatives detectable as well. When
T
is fixed, detectability requires

to be on the order of
\sqrt{N}
or larger; below this threshold, grouped heterogeneity is undetectable. Finally, if the number of groups increases with
N
, detectability depends on the total number of units in the remainder groups, rather than on the sheer count of groups. A detailed analysis of each scenario is presented in Section
4

5

The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section

3

Preliminaries

Consider the linear panel data model

\displaystyle y_{i}=x_{i}^{\prime}\beta_{g(i)}+\epsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,N,

(1)

where

We are interested in testing for the presence of a grouped structure in the slopes by means of existing slope homogeneity tests. Specifically, we study the local power of such tests against alternatives in which the slope coefficients differ across groups of individuals. The null hypothesis of identical slopes is formulated as,

\displaystyle H_{0}:\beta_{g(i)}=\beta,\text{ for all }i\in\mathcal{N},

H_{1}:\exists\ G\subset\{1,...,N\}:\;G\neq\emptyset\;\text{and}\;\beta_{g(i)}\neq\beta_{g(j)}\;\text{for}\;i\in G\;\text{and}\;j\in\mathcal{N}\backslash G.

As discussed in the introduction, a variety of well-established tests for slope homogeneity are available in the literature. To analyze their asymptotic behavior within a unified framework, we adopt a common set of assumptions sufficiently broad to capture their essential features.

\mathcal{S}(\bar{\sigma}^{2}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{WLS}(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i})\right)^{\prime}\left(\frac{x_{i}^{\prime}x_{i}}{\bar{\sigma}^{2}_{i}}\right)\left(\hat{\beta}_{i}-\hat{\beta}_{WLS}(\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{i})\right),

\Delta=\frac{\mathcal{S}_{PY}-NK}{\sqrt{2NK}}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad\text{ as }\quad N,T\to\infty.

4
Asymptotic Local Power Analysis

We study the local power of
\Delta
,
\mathcal{J}
, and
LM
tests under grouped alternatives, focusing on (i) how large slope differences must be to be detected and (ii) how large the group must be for detection. To this end, we introduce a sequence of
doubly local alternatives
that extends the classical Pitman framework and integrates these two aspects. We first present the results for the case where the sample is partitioned into two groups,
\{G_{1},G_{2}\}
, with
N,T\to\infty
. Group
G_{1}
is treated as the “dominant” group and
G_{2}
as the “alternative” one, containing the remaining individuals. We then extend these results to panels with
N\to\infty
and fixed
T
, as well as to settings with more than two groups.

Formally, we define

\displaystyle H_{1,n}:\beta_{g(i)}=\begin{cases}\beta,&\text{if }i\in G_{1},\\
\beta+\lambda_{2}/\gamma,&\text{if }i\in G_{2},\end{cases}

where
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
is a
K\times 1
vector, and
\{G_{1},G_{2}\}
form a partition of
\mathcal{N}
. The number of individuals in each group is

\#G_{2}=M,\qquad\#G_{1}=N-M,

where
M\to\infty
as
N\to\infty
and
\gamma=\gamma(N,T,M)
diverges with
N
,
T
, and
M
.

Allowing the scaling factor
\gamma
to depend on
M
as well as on
N
and
T
reflects that our proposed local alternatives control for both the slope difference and the size of the heterogeneous group. This extension is essential: the relative magnitude of
M
with respect to
N
and
T
is crucial in determining when and why slope homogeneity tests can detect group-based heterogeneity. Theorem
1
formalizes this statement.

Theorem
1

\gamma\asymp\frac{M^{1/2}T^{1/2}}{N^{1/4}},\qquad M,N,T\to\infty,

Specifically, if

It is worth emphasizing when the result in (
5

If the fraction of alternative units remains bounded away from zero,
m_{0}>0
(so that

O(\sqrt{T}N^{1/4})

In this case, our framework nests the local power behavior established by PY, SC, and BRS, providing a consistency check with existing results. The theorem further derives noncentrality parameters that differ from those in the original papers and clarify the distinct role of group-based heterogeneity in local power.

Further novel implications arise when the alternative group is small relative to the dominant one, i.e., when
m_{0}=0

In this case, the rate in (

This contrasts with the conventional intuition in the grouped-patterns literature, where heterogeneous slopes are typically assumed to affect a non-negligible share of the sample
(

;

Su 2017

.
These results highlight the usefulness of slope homogeneity tests in empirical contexts where the presence of small, systematically different subgroups matters for inference and policy.

4.2
Panels with increasing number of groups

The main message of our analysis will remain valid even if we allow the number of groups to grow with

\displaystyle H_{2,n}:\beta_{g(i)}=\begin{cases}\beta,&\mbox{if }i\in G_{1},\\

The analysis of local power when

6
Conclusions

We propose repurposing existing slope-homogeneity tests to detect grouped patterns in panels. The power of the tests is evaluated under a sequence of doubly local alternatives, linking the degree of heterogeneity across groups and each group size to its impact on test performance. Our analysis shows that slope-homogeneity tests are effective in detecting such structures, including sparse groups whose cardinality is negligible relative to the cross-sectional size. Monte Carlo simulations support similar conclusions in finite samples.
