Forced oscillations for generalized Φ \Phi -Laplacian equations with Carathéodory perturbations Thanks: The authors are members of the Gruppo Nazionale per l’Analisi Matematica, la Probabilità e le loro Applicazioni (GNAMPA) of the Istituto Nazionale di Alta Matematica (INdAM), and are supported by PRIN 2022 - Progetti di Ricerca di rilevante Interesse Nazionale, “Nonlinear differential problems with applications to real phenomena” (Grant Number: 2022ZXZTN2)
Alessandro Calamai, Maria Patrizia Pera, Marco Spadini

Abstract
. Using topological methods, we study the structure of the set of forced oscillations of a class of parametric, implicit ordinary differential equations with a generalized Φ \Phi -Laplacian type term. We work in the Carathéodory setting. Under suitable assumptions, involving merely the Brouwer degree in Euclidean spaces, we obtain global bifurcation results. In some illustrative examples we provide a visual representation of the bifurcating set.

34C25, 34A09, 34B08, 47H11

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to study the structure of the set of forced oscillations of a class of parametric
ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
involving a generalized
\Phi
-Laplacian type differential operator.

More specifically, we consider the following two different problems which, although similar, have to be handled separately:

(1)

[\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)]^{\prime}=\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

and

(2)

[\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)]^{\prime}=g\big(x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

where
\lambda
is a real parameter and, for a given open set
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the map
g\colon U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuous, whereas the perturbing term
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is Carathéodory and
T
-periodic in the first variable,
T>0
being given. We study the set of periodic solutions that depend on the parameter
\lambda
. More precisely, see Definition
3.4
, we investigate the set of those pairs
(\lambda,x)
, which we call
T
-forced pairs
of (
1
) (resp. (
2
)), where
x
is a
T
-periodic solution corresponding to
\lambda
of (
1
) (resp. (
2
)).
Our main goal will be to determine conditions ensuring the existence of a “branch”, consisting of nontrivial (again see Definition
3.4
)
T
-forced pairs emanating for
\lambda=0
from the set of stationary solutions.

Concerning the mapping
\phi\colon[0,\infty)\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that appears on the left-hand side in (
1
) and (
2
), it is assumed to be continuous and such that
\phi(\lambda,p,\cdot)
is one-to-one and onto for each
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and
p\in U
.
Loosely speaking, we view
\phi(\lambda,p,q)
as a sum
\phi_{0}(q)+\lambda\delta(\lambda,p,q)
, where
\phi_{0}
is a homeomorphism and
\delta(\lambda,p,q)
is a suitable perturbation, see Section
3
for more details.

As a special case when
n=1
we recover the classical
p
-Laplacian operator
\Phi(x):=x|x|^{p-2}
, with
p>1
, or, more generally, the so-called
\Phi
-Laplacian
: meaning a strictly increasing homeomorphism
\Phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
. Scalar equations with
\Phi
-Laplacian, of the form

(\Phi(x^{\prime}(t)))^{\prime}=\mathfrak{k}(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t))

are well-studied since they arise in some applicative models: for example, in non-Newtonian fluid theory,
nonlinear elasticity, diffusion of flows in porous media, theory of capillary surfaces
and, more recently, the modeling of glaciology
(see, e.g.,
[
2
,
10
,
33
]
).
From a mathematical viewpoint, different kinds of boundary conditions can be associated to these equations;
for instance, the existence of heteroclinic solutions can be established using the method of
upper/lower solutions (see, e.g.,
[
7
,
12
,
14
,
19
,
21
]
).
Concerning the study of periodic solutions, both in the scalar case and for equations in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
topological methods have been employed by many authors.
We cite, for instance,
[
3
,
4
,
6
,
11
,
22
,
34
]
.

Recently, in
[
16
]
we investigated a similar problem but with the stronger condition that the map

f
in (
1
) and (
2
) is
continuous
, and not merely Carathéodory.
Here we focus on the Carathéodory setting so that, with respect to
[
16
]
, we have to weaken the definition of solution of the ODEs (
1
) and (
2
), cf. Definition
3.2
,
and as a tool we need to use some more recent results obtained in
[
17
]
.
We stress that our results cannot be deduced from the analogous ones in
[
16
]
.

The approach used in this paper to study the set of
T
-periodic solutions of (
1
) and (
2
) can be summarized as follows. We observe that these two equations, by means of the introduction of an auxiliary variable, can be regarded as equivalent systems in
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
written in normal form. These systems can be studied, in a similar way as in
[
16
]
, through earlier results, obtained by the authors with M. Furi, about periodically perturbed ODEs on differentiable manifolds in the Carathéodory setting, see

[
25
,
35
]
.
In fact here we exploit some very recent results on system of coupled equations on manifolds,
obtained in
[
17
]
, that in a certain sense extend and unify the aforementioned ones.
Let us point out that the cited results rely upon the topological notion
of degree (also called
Euler characteristic
) of a tangent vector field, see the classical books

[
28
,
29
,
31
]
.
However, in the present paper all the technicalities are hidden in the proofs so that
our main results require only the well-known Brouwer degree in Euclidean spaces;
no advanced tools from differential topology are needed here.

Our main results, Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
below,
can be described as
global bifurcation results
, or
atypical bifurcation results
in the sense of Prodi-Ambrosetti.
Roughly speaking, we obtain the existence of an unbounded connected set – a “branch” – made up of
“nontrivial”
T
-periodic solution pairs
(\lambda,x)
of (
1
) and (
2
), resp.,
that emanates from the set of the “trivial” ones
– see Section
3
for more precise definitions.
To prove the existence of such a branch we need only suitable assumptions on the Brouwer degree of some maps in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
related with the terms
f
and
g
in the right-hand side of (
1
)
and (
2
).
Notice that the Brouwer degree can be computed explicitly; therefore it is possible, in some concrete situations, to verify the validity of those assumptions.

We observe that our results are related to similar ones, concerning the set of
T
-periodic solutions of periodically perturbed ODEs: for the case of differential equations on manifolds we cite the already quoted papers
[
17
,
25
,
35
]
.
Concerning equations in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we mention the recent
[
4
]
in which the authors study a case analogous to (
1
) but with a different topological tool, namely the coincidence degree developed by J. Mawhin.

Moreover, our problems are linked to some class of
differential-algebraic equations, cf.
[
9
,
13
,
18
,
37
]
. In fact, under suitable conditions, also in that case it is possible to relate
such equations to ODEs on manifolds, and to obtain the desired properties only in terms of the Brouwer degree in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

An interesting question, that we postpone to further research, is whether it would be possible to find a bridge between Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
. In fact, Theorem
4.2
does something similar for Theorems
4.4
and
4.5
, see Section
4
and the pertinent discussion in
[
36
]
. Another attractive line of study, not addressed here, is the investigation of the set of
T
-periodic solutions of equations (
1
) and (
2
) when a dependence on delayed arguments is introduced in
\phi
and
f
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we summarize the concept of Brouwer degree in Euclidean spaces in a slightly extended version. In Section
3
we list the main assumptions and state the global bifurcation results: namely, Theorems
3.5
and
3.6
. Section
4
is devoted to some preliminary results about

, we rewrite our equations in a way that allows the application of the results of the previous section.
Section
6
contains the proof of the main results.

In the last Section
7
we present some purely illustrative examples, for which we show a possible way to represent the set of
T
-forced pairs by means of suitable, finite-dimensional projections. More in detail, the visual representation of branches is obtained by plotting respectively, the
C^{1}
-norm of the solution
x
in any
T
-forced pair
(\lambda,x)
, and the set of “starting points”: meaning those triples
(\lambda,p,v)
such that
(p,v)
are the initial conditions, at time
t=0
, of a
T
-periodic solution that corresponds to
\lambda
.
In a sense, the numerical examples of Section
7
aim to illustrate different possible behaviors of the set of
T
-forced pairs.

2.
A quick refresher on the Brouwer degree in Euclidean spaces

In our argument the notion of Brouwer degree is only a tool whose use is hidden in the proofs. However, in order to make the paper self-contained, we briefly recall the definition and some properties since we will make use of this concept in a slightly extended version (see e.g.
[
5
,
20
,
32
]
). A reader which is already familiar with it can skip this section.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
,
\mathcal{F}
a continuous
\mathbb{R}^{s}
-valued map, defined (at least) on the closure
\overline{\mathcal{O}}
of
\mathcal{O}
, and
q\in\mathbb{R}^{s}
. We say that the triple
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)
is
admissible (for the Brouwer degree)
if the set
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(q)\cap\mathcal{O}
is compact.

The Brouwer degree is an integer-valued function which to any admissible triple
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)
assigns
\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)\in\mathbb{Z}
, called the
Brouwer degree of
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathcal{O}
with target
q
.
One can say informally that this number counts algebraically the solutions in
\mathcal{O}
of the equation
\mathcal{F}(p)=q
. In fact, one of the properties of this integer-valued function is given by the following
computation formula
, that holds in the smooth regular case.

Recall that, given a map
\mathcal{F}\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
of class
C^{1}
and a point
p\in\mathcal{O}
,
p
is said to be a
regular point
(of
\mathcal{F}
) if the differential of
\mathcal{F}
at
p
,
d\mathcal{F}_{p}
, is surjective. The points that are not regular are called
critical points
.
The
critical values
of
\mathcal{F}
are those points of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
which belong to the image of the set of the critical points.
Any
q\in\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
which is not in the image of the set of the critical points is a
regular value
. So,
q
is a regular value for
F
in
\mathcal{O}
if and only if
\det(d\mathcal{F}_{p})\neq 0
,
\forall p\in\mathcal{F}^{-1}(q)\cap\mathcal{O}
. Note that any element of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
which is not in the image of
\mathcal{F}
is a regular value. The following formula is actually the basic definition of the Brouwer degree.

Computation formula.

If
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)
is admissible,
\mathcal{F}
is smooth, and
q

is a regular value for
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathcal{O}
, then

(3)

\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)\;=\sum_{p\in\mathcal{F}^{-1}(q)\cap\mathcal{O}}\sign\det(d\mathcal{F}_{p}).

Let now
(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{O},r)
be any admissible triple. Then,
\deg(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{O},r)
is defined by taking
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)
such that
\mathcal{F}
and
q
are “sufficiently close” to
\mathcal{G}
and
r
and satisfy the assumptions of the Computation Formula and setting

(4)

\deg(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{O},r):=\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q).

It is known that this definition is well-posed.

We point out that the more classical, and well-known, definition of Brouwer degree is usually given in the subclass of triples
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},q)
such that
\mathcal{F}\colon\overline{\mathcal{O}}\to\mathbb{R}^{s}
is continuous,
\mathcal{O}
is bounded and
q\notin\mathcal{F}(\partial\mathcal{O})
. However, all the standard properties of the degree, such as
homotopy invariance, excision, additivity, existence
, still hold in this more general framework.
For a detailed list of such properties we refer, e.g., to
[
29
,
30
,
32
]
.

In this paper the target point
q
will always be the origin of
\mathbb{R}^{s}
; therefore, for the sake of simplicity, we will adopt the shorter notation
\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O})
instead of
\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O},0)
.
In this context, we will say that
p\in\mathcal{F}^{-1}(0)
is a
nondegenerate zero
(of
\mathcal{F}
) if
\det(d\mathcal{F}_{p})\neq 0
; this means, equivalently, that
p
is a regular point.
Accordingly, we will also say that
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O})
is an
admissible pair
(or that the map
F
is admissible in
\mathcal{O}
) if so is the triple
(F,\mathcal{O},0)
.
Observe that
\deg(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{O})
can be regarded also as the degree (or characteristic, or rotation) of the map
F
seen as a tangent vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{s}
. This remark is crucial for the
extension of the above definitions to the context of differentiable manifolds (see, e.g.,
[
28
,
29
,
31
]
).

3.
Setting of the problem and statement of the main results

As pointed out in the Introduction, we study the following two equations:

(5)

[\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)]^{\prime}=\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

and

(6)

[\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)]^{\prime}=g\big(x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big),\quad\lambda\geq 0.

Throughout the paper we will make the following assumptions on the map
\phi
that appears in (
5
) and (
6
).

\bullet
The map
\phi\colon[0,\infty)\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is assumed to be continuous and such that
\phi(\lambda,p,\cdot)
is one-to-one and onto for each
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and
p\in U
.

The latter condition means that
\phi(\lambda,p,\cdot)
is invertible with respect to the third variable in the following sense: for each
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exist a unique
q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, depending on
(\lambda,p)
, such that

u=\phi(\lambda,p,q).

We will denote this “partial inverse” as
\psi(\lambda,p,\cdot)
, so that
q=\psi(\lambda,p,u)
.

In the following we will also denote by “
\partial_{i}
” the partial differentiation with respect to the
i
-th variable.
We assume that:

\bullet
The map
\psi\colon[0,\infty)\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined above is continuous and, for each
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\in U
, the map

\lambda\mapsto\partial_{1}\psi(\lambda,p,u)

is also continuous.

Finally, we suppose that:

\bullet

\phi(0,\cdot,\cdot)
depends only on the third variable; so that, setting
\phi_{0}(q):=\phi(0,p,q)
thus we have
\psi(0,p,u)=\phi_{0}^{-1}(u)
.

This last assumption means that we regard
\phi(\lambda,\cdot,\cdot)
as a perturbation of some (nonlinear) homeomorphism depending only on the third variable. With this in mind, we view
\phi(\lambda,p,q)
as a sum
\phi_{0}(q)+\lambda\delta(\lambda,p,q)
, where
\phi_{0}
is a homeomorphism and
\delta(\lambda,p,q)
is a perturbation such that the above assumptions are satisfied.

The set of assumptions on
\phi
may seem complicated but in the more regular case they are quite straightforward, as shown in the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

When the map
\phi
is of class
C^{1}
, by the Implicit Function Theorem, we have that the above conditions involving the map
\psi
are satisfied if, for any
(\lambda,p,q)\in[0,\infty)\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the partial derivative
\partial_{3}\phi(\lambda,p,q)
is invertible.

Hereafter by
L^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})
we denote the Banach space, isomorphic to
L^{1}((0,T),\mathbb{R})
, of the
L^{1}_{\textrm{loc}}
maps
\xi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that are
T
-periodic, in the sense that
\xi(t)=\xi(t+T)
for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that a map
f\colon\mathbb{R}\times U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
as in (
5
) and (
6
) is said to be
T
-periodic Carathéodory when:

(F1)

f(t+T,p,q,\lambda)=f(t,p,q,\lambda)
,
\forall(p,q,\lambda)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
and for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(F2)

t\mapsto f(t,p,q,\lambda)
is measurable,
\forall(p,q,\lambda)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
;

(F3)

(p,q,\lambda)\mapsto f(t,p,q,\lambda)
is continuous, for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(F4)

for any compact set
K\subseteq U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[0,\infty)
, there exists a nonnegative function
\sigma_{K}\in L^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R})
such that

|f(t,p,q,\lambda)|\leq\sigma_{K}(t)
,
\forall(p,q,\lambda)\in K
and for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Let us now clarify the meaning of solution of (
5
) and (
6
).

Definition 3.2
.

Let
I\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be an interval, and
\lambda\geq 0
be given. A function
x\in W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
is said to be a solution of (
5
) if the function

y\colon I\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad y(t)=\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)

is in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
too and the equality
y^{\prime}(t)=\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big)
holds for a.e.
t\in I
.

Analogously, a function
x\in W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
is said to be a solution of (
6
) if the function
y
as above is in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
and the equality
y^{\prime}(t)=g\big(x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)+\lambda f\big(t,x(t),x^{\prime}(t),\lambda\big)
holds for a.e.
t\in I
.

We denote by
C^{r}_{T}(U)
,
r\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the set of the
U
-valued,
T
-periodic,
C^{r}
functions with the standard
C^{r}
topology. For simplicity, when
r=0
, we write
C_{T}(U)
.

Remark 3.3
.

Observe that when
x
is a solution of (
5
) or of (
6
), the function
y
introduced in Definition
3.2
is in particular continuous being in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
. By the definition of
\psi
we have
x^{\prime}(t)=\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)
for all
t\in I
so,
\psi
being continuous, we find that
x
is actually
C^{1}
.

Remark
3.3
justifies the following definition.

Definition 3.4
.

A pair
(\lambda,x)\in[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
, such that
x
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
5
) (resp. (
6
)), is said to be a
T
-forced pair
for (
5
) (resp. (
6
)). A
T
-forced pair
(\lambda,x)
is called
trivial
if
x
is constant and
\lambda=0
.

In this paper we investigate the set of nontrivial
T
-forced pairs of (
5
) and (
6
). Before stating our main results we need some further notation.
Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
. Define the open subset
\Omega_{U}
of
U
as follows:

\Omega_{U}=\big\{p\in U:(0,\overline{p})\in\Omega\big\}.

where
\overline{p}
denotes the constant function
p(t)=p
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
.

Concerning the set of
T
-forced pairs of equation (
5
) we will prove the following result:

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
. Define the vector field
w\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows:

w(p):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(t,p,0,0)\,\mathrm{d}t,

and assume that
\deg(w,\Omega_{U})
is well-defined and nonzero. Then there exists a connected set
\Gamma
of nontrivial
T
-forced pairs in
\Omega
of (
5
) whose closure in
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
intersects the set
\big\{(0,\overline{p})\in[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U):p\in w^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega_{U}\big\}
and is not contained in any compact subset of
\Omega
. In particular, when
U=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Omega=[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then
\Gamma
is unbounded.

Note that this theorem is not applicable to (
6
): compare with Remark
3.7
. For this equation, instead, the pertinent result is as follows:

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\Omega
be an open subset of
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
. Let
\gamma(p):=g(p,0)
and assume that
\deg\big(\gamma,\Omega_{U}\big)
is well-defined and nonzero. Then there exists a connected set
\Gamma
of nontrivial
T
-forced pairs in
\Omega
of (
6
) whose closure in
[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
intersects the set
\big\{(0,\overline{p})\in[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U):p\in\gamma^{-1}(0)\cap\Omega_{U}\big\}
and is not contained in any compact subset of
\Omega
. In particular, when
U=\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\Omega=[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
then
\Gamma
is unbounded.

We will prove these theorems by transforming equations (
5
) and (
6
) into a form suitable for investigation by the results of
[
17
]
. We will also need a few technical facts about the topological degree of vector fields many of which carry over from
[
16
]
.

Remark 3.7
.

Observe that the above Theorems
3.5
and
3.6

have a similar statement and yield similar conclusions.
Nevertheless, it is not possible to consider one as a particular case of the other, even in the case when
g
vanishes identically so that equation (
6
) reduces to (
5
).
To see this, notice that the degree of the average wind
w
, that is crucial for Theorem
3.5
, plays no role in Theorem
3.6
: in principle, it could not be even defined. Conversely, for equation (
5
), the degree of
\gamma
does not even make sense.

4.
Preliminary results

In order to pursue our investigation on the set of
T
-forced pairs of (
5
) and (
6
) we need some previous results about periodic perturbations of autonomous ODEs on manifolds, that have been first obtained in
[
24
,
27
,
36
]
within the framework of continuous vector fields, and then extended in
[
25
,
35
]
in the Carathéodory setting.

In this section we present some recent facts on system of coupled equations on manifolds obtained in
[
17
]
, that in a certain sense extend and unify the aforementioned ones. However, since in the context that we consider here it is not necessary to treat with equations on manifolds, we will present the indispensable results in a form simplified and significantly weaker but adequate to our purposes here.

Let
V,W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
be open sets, and
consider the following system parametrized by
\lambda\geq 0
on
V\times W
:

(7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{\xi}(t)=\lambda\mathfrak{f}\big(t,\xi(t),\eta(t),\lambda\big),\\

5.
Rearrangement of equations (
5
) and (
6
)

We now rewrite equations (
5
) and (
6
) into a form suitable for the application of the results of the previous section.
More precisely,
it is convenient to rewrite equations (
5
) and (
6
) as systems of ODEs in normal form. To do so, suppose that
x
is a solution of (
5
) and introduce the function
y(t):=\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)

which belongs to
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
. Thus
x^{\prime}(t)=\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)

and

y^{\prime}(t)=\lambda f\Big(t,x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\lambda\Big)

so that the function
t\mapsto\big(x(t),y(t)\big)
, taking values in
U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(I)
and satisfies the system:

(10)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}(t)=\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\\
y^{\prime}(t)=\lambda f\Big(t,x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\lambda\Big),\end{array}\right.

for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
. In other words
(x,y)
is a solution of (
10
).

In a similar way, if
x
is a solution of (
6
) we find that
t\mapsto\big(x(t),y(t)\big)
satisfies

(11)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}(t)=\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\\
y^{\prime}(t)=g\Big(x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\Big)+\lambda f\Big(t,x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\lambda\Big),\end{array}\right.

for a.e.
t\in\mathbb{R}
, that is
(x,y)
is a solution of (
11
).

Conversely, consider a solution
t\mapsto\big(x(t),y(t)\big)\in U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
of (
10
) (resp. (
11
)) and note that, by definition,
(x,y)\colon J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
is in
W_{\mathrm{loc}}^{1,1}(J)
,

being an interval. By the first equation of (
10
) (resp. (
11
)) we get
y(t):=\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)
, thus we have that

x
is a solution of (
6
) (resp. (
5
)).

Our purpose now is to rewrite equations (
10
) and (
11
) into a more useful form by “isolating”
\lambda
from inside
\psi
as well as
g
in these systems. A crucial step is the following simple version of Hadamard’s Lemma, compare e.g.
[
1
]
:

Lemma 5.1

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
\phi
be as in equations (
5
) and (
6
). Let also
p\in U
,
q,u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
be such that
u=\phi(\lambda,p,q)
. Then there exists a continuous function
h
such that
q=\phi_{0}^{-1}(u)+\lambda h(\lambda,p,u)
.

Proof.

Put
q=\psi(\lambda,p,u)
. It is enough to observe that

\displaystyle q-\phi_{0}^{-1}(u)=q-\psi(0,p,u)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\phantom{s}}{\,\mathrm{d}s}\,\psi(s\lambda,p,u)\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\partial_{1}\psi(s\lambda,p,u)\,\mathrm{d}s=\lambda h(\lambda,p,u),

where
h(\lambda,p,u)=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{1}\psi(s\lambda,p,u)\,\mathrm{d}s
.
∎

By Lemma
5.1
we see that

(12)

\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)=\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)+\lambda h\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)

for some mapping
h
. Hence system (
10
) can be rewritten as

(13)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}(t)=\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)+\lambda h\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\\
y^{\prime}(t)=\lambda f\Big(t,x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\lambda\Big),\end{array}\right.

Let us now deal with system (
11
). Using (
12
) we obtain

g\Big(x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\Big)=g\Big(x(t),\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)+\lambda h(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\Big),

and using again Lemma
5.1
, we get, for an appropriate function
\widehat{h}
, the equality:

g\Big(x(t),\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)+\lambda h(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)\Big)=g\Big(x(t),\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)\Big)+\lambda\widehat{h}(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big).

Thus we can rewrite (
11
) as

(14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}x^{\prime}(t)=\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)+\lambda h(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\\
y^{\prime}(t)=g\Big(x(t),\phi_{0}^{-1}\big(y(t)\big)\Big)+\lambda F\big(t,x(t),y(t),\lambda\big).\end{array}\right.

where
F
collects all the
\lambda
-dependent terms in the second equation of (
11
), that is:

(15)

F\big(t,x(t),y(t),\lambda\big)=\widehat{h}(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big)+f\Big(t,x(t),\psi\big(\lambda,x(t),y(t)\big),\lambda\Big).

6.1.
Proof of Theorem

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
3.5
regarding the structure of the set of solutions of of (
5
). As a first step, we relate the set
X\subseteq[0,\infty)\times C^{1}_{T}(U)
of
T
-forced pairs of (
5
) with the set
Y\subseteq[0,\infty)\times C_{T}(U\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
T
-triples of (
13
), see Definition
4.1
. To do so, we define the map
\mathfrak{H}\colon Y\to X
by
\mathfrak{H}\colon(\lambda,x,y)\mapsto(\lambda,x)
, and observe that
\mathfrak{H}
is continuous.

Lemma
6.1
below is analogous to Lemma 7.1 in
[
16
]
. We give the proof for completeness.

Lemma 6.1
.

The map
\mathfrak{H}
is a homeomorphism that respects the notion of triviality, in the sense that it makes
trivial
T
-triples correspond to trivial
T
-forced pairs and vice versa.

Proof.

Let
(\lambda,x,y)
be a
T
-triple of (
13
); then, by definition,
x
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
5
) corresponding to
\lambda
. Now, if
(\lambda,x,y)
is trivial, this means that
\lambda=0
and
x
is constant: therefore
(\lambda,x)
is a trivial
T
-forced pair.

Conversely, let
(\lambda,x)
be a
T
-forced pair of (
5
); this means, as already observed in Remark
3.3
, that
x
is
C^{1}
and, by the continuity of
\phi
, the map
t\mapsto y(t)=\phi\big(\lambda,x(t),x^{\prime}(t)\big)
is continuous. Note that
y
is
T
-periodic and that
(x,y)
is a
T
-periodic solution of (
13
) corresponding to
\lambda
. Namely,

\mathfrak{H}^{-1}(\lambda,x)=\left(\lambda,x,\phi\big(\lambda,x,x^{\prime}\big)\right)=(\lambda,x,y).

It is not difficult, using the continuity of
\phi
, to show that
\mathfrak{H}^{-1}\colon X\to Y
is a continuous map.

Finally, if
x
is constant so is
y
, hence the image under
\mathfrak{H}^{-1}
of any trivial
T
-forced pair is a trivial
T
-triple.
∎

Consider the following vector field, defined on
U\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\nu(p,u):=\left(\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f\big(t,p,\phi_{0}^{-1}(u),0\big)\,\mathrm{d}t\;,\;\phi_{0}^{-1}(u)\right).

In view of Theorem
4.2
and Lemma
6.1
one sees that the degree of
\nu
plays a crucial role in our argument. However, as shown by the following lemma, one does not need to compute the degree of
\nu
. In fact, since
\phi_{0}^{-1}
is a homeomorphism, the computation of the degree of
\nu
can be reduced to that of the “average wind” i.e.

(16)

w(p):=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}f(t,p,0,0)\,\mathrm{d}t.

Again, Lemma
6.2
below is similar to Lemma 7.2 in
[
16
]
.
We sketch the proof for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
16
]
)
.

Let
W\subseteq U
be open. The vector field
\nu
is admissible for the degree in
W\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if so is
w
in
W
, and

(17)

\deg\big(\nu,W\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\big)=\pm\deg\big(w,W\big).

Proof.

To prove the first assertion, let
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the unique point such that
\phi_{0}^{-1}(u_{0})=0
and observe that

(18)

\nu^{-1}(0)=\big\{(p,u_{0}):p\in w^{-1}(0)\big\};

thus,
w^{-1}(0)\cap W
is compact if and only if so is
\nu^{-1}(0)\cap(W\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Hence
\nu
is admissible in
W\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if so is
w
in
W
.

Now, the proof of formula (
17
) follows from an application of the properties of the degree.
First, by the excision property, we get

(19)

\deg\big(\nu,W_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\big)=\deg\big(\nu,W_{1}\times W_{2}\big)

where
W_{1}\subseteq W
and
W_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are open and bounded sets such that
\nu^{-1}(0)\subseteq W_{1}\times W_{2}
.
Let now
\nu_{1}
be the first component of
\nu
. By known transversality theorems, we can approximate
\nu_{1}
with a smooth map
\hat{\nu}_{1}
with only isolated zeros and also approximate
\phi_{0}
with a smooth diffeomorphism
\hat{\phi}_{0}
. Let

\hat{\nu}(p,u)=\big(\hat{\nu}_{1}(p,u)\;,\;\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1}(u)\big).

We can choose the approximations so close, in a way that the homotopy

H(s,p,u)=s\hat{\nu}(p,u)+(1-s)\nu(p,u)\\
=\Big(s\hat{\nu}_{1}(p,u)+(1-s)\nu_{1}(p,u)\;,\;s\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1}(u)+(1-s)\phi_{0}^{-1}(u)\Big),

has no zeros on the boundary of the bounded open set
W_{1}\times W_{2}
for
s\in[0,1]
. Thus
H
is admissible in
W_{1}\times W_{2}
, and the homotopy property of the degree yields

(20)

\deg\big(\nu,W_{1}\times W_{2}\big)=\deg\big(\hat{\nu},W_{1}\times W_{2}\big).

Similarly, defining
\hat{w}(p)=\hat{\nu}_{1}(p,0)
, we have

(21)

\deg(w,W_{1})=\deg(\hat{w},W_{1}).

To conclude, let
(p_{0},u_{0})
be an isolated zero of
\hat{\nu}
. We have

\det\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(p_{0},u_{0})=\det\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{1}\hat{\nu}_{1}(p_{0},u_{0})&0\\
\partial_{2}\hat{\nu}_{1}(p_{0},u_{0})&(\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1})^{\prime}(u_{0})\end{pmatrix}=\det\hat{w}^{\prime}(p_{0})\det(\phi_{0}^{-1})^{\prime}(u_{0}).

As in (
18
) we have

\hat{\nu}^{-1}(0)=\big\{(p,u_{0}):p\in\hat{w}^{-1}(0)\big\}.

Also,
\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1}
being a diffeomorphism,
\sign\det(\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1})^{\prime}(u_{0})=\pm 1
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\deg(\hat{\nu},W_{1}\times W_{2})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(p_{0},u_{0})\in\hat{\nu}^{-1}(0)\cap W_{1}\times W_{2}}\sign\det\hat{\nu}^{\prime}(p_{0},u_{0})

\displaystyle=\sign\det(\hat{\phi}_{0}^{-1})^{\prime}(u_{0})\sum_{p_{0}\in\hat{w}^{-1}(0)\cap W_{1}}\sign\det\hat{w}^{\prime}(p_{0})

\displaystyle=\pm\deg(\hat{w},W_{1}).

The assertion now follows from equations (
21
), (
20
) and (
19
).
∎

.

Recall that the standard

\Gamma
