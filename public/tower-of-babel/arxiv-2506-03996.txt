Optimal Spiking Brain Compression: Improving One-Shot Post-Training Pruning and Quantization for Spiking Neural Networks
Lianfeng Shi, Ao Li, Benjamin Ward-Cherrier

Abstract
Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have emerged as a new generation of energy-efficient neural networks suitable for implementation on neuromorphic hardware. As neuromorphic hardware has limited memory and computing resources, weight pruning and quantization have recently been explored to improve SNNs’ efficiency. State-of-the-art SNN pruning/quantization methods employ multiple compression and training iterations, increasing the cost for pre-trained or very large SNNs. In this paper, we propose a new one-shot post-training pruning/quantization framework, Optimal Spiking Brain Compression (OSBC), that adapts the Optimal Brain Compression (OBC) method of [Frantar, Singh, and Alistarh, 2023] for SNNs. Rather than minimizing the loss on neuron input current as OBC does, OSBC achieves more efficient and accurate SNN compression in one pass by minimizing the loss on spiking neuron membrane potential with a small sample dataset. Our experiments on neuromorphic datasets (N-MNIST, CIFAR10-DVS, DVS128-Gesture) demonstrate that OSBC can achieve 97% sparsity through pruning with 1.41%, 10.20%, and 1.74% accuracy loss, or 4-bit symmetric quantization with 0.17%, 1.54%, and 7.71% accuracy loss, respectively. Code is available on GitHub.

Introduction

Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs) have gained significant attention as a promising power-efficient alternative to traditional Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs). Driven by spikes, only active neurons need to be updated. SNNs enjoy many of the benefits of bio-plausibility, such as low computational power and compatibility with temporal data
[
1
]
, which makes them suitable to be implemented on neuromorphic chips like True North
[
2
]
, Loihi 1 and 2
[
3
]
or SpiNNaker
[
4
]
. However, as these chips have limited computing power and memory, finding ways to improve SNNs’ efficiency on neuromorphic hardware has been an important research question
[
5
]
.

Neural pruning and quantization have emerged as essential methods for reducing neuron activations and weight storage for SNNs. These optimizations enable the deployment of more capable and efficient models in the same hardware, promoting practical deployments in edge computing and robotics scenarios.

Some other notable methods of improving SNN efficiency include knowledge distillation
[
6
,
7
]
, where a bigger model is used to train a smaller model, spike rate reduction
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
, hardware-specific optimizations
[
11
,
12
]
, etc. Due to the scope of this paper, these methods will not be discussed in depth, but they have the potential to be used alongside SNN pruning and quantization methods
[
13
,
14
]
.

SNN pruning techniques

Several methods for pruning SNNs have emerged in the past five years.
Shi et al. 2024
finds that aggressive unstructured pruning (> 90% sparsity) can yield 10-fold efficiency increase. Combining SNN weight and neuron pruning, it achieves 99.37% sparsity with 2.16% drop in accuracy on non-neuromorphic datasets.
Guo et al. 2020
proposed an unsupervised online pruning technique for SNNs.
Kim et al. 2022
explored the lottery ticket hypothesis (LIH) for Spiking Neural Network, and used it as the basis for their unstructured weight pruning method. However, most state-of-the-art SNN pruning methods require the pruning step to happen during training, or adopt an iterative pruning technique, where multiple pruning and training iterations are performed. This is computationally expensive, especially for pre-trained and deep spiking neural networks. Moreover, with the adaptation of the transformer architecture for SNNs
[
18
,
19
]
, the size of future state-of-the-art SNN models could cause the retraining cost to become prohibitively large. A more computationally efficient pruning algorithm is urgently needed for the deployment of future SOTA SNNs.

SNN quantization techniques

Efforts to apply quantization to SNNs on neuromorphic hardware have recently gained traction. There are two big categories in neural network quantization: Quantization-Aware Training (QAT) and Post Training Quantization (PTQ). QAT allows models to adapt to the quantization through retraining, and there have been several articles written on QAT for SNNs, such as
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. However, PTQ for SNNs is still relatively underdeveloped, especially compared to its ANN counterparts, where the desire to reduce the size of Large Language Models (LLMs) to run on smaller or even edge computing hardware has inspired many algorithms, such as AdaQuant
[
23
]
, Optimal Brain Quantizer(OBQ)
[
24
]
, and GPTQ
[
25
]
.

In this paper, we explored using a second-order approximation of SNNs to prune and/or quantize an SNN in one shot, without retraining, inspired by the Optimal Brain Compression (OBC) algorithm
[
24
]
, a widely used ANN pruning and quantization framework.

We discovered that naive implementation of OBC to SNNs does not yield the best result, due to the disconnect between OBC’s objective function and SNNs’ layer-wise output spike train. We proposed an innovative objective function, named surrogate membrane potential, that is closely aligned with changes in SNN layer-wise output spike train and is efficient to compute. We call our method Optimal Spiking Brain Compression (OSBC).

The main contributions of this work can been summarized as follows:

•

We empirically demonstrate that the second-order-approximation based pruning and quantization method is effective on SNNs.

•

We design a novel dedicated SNN pruning and quantization framework (OSBC) that includes an innovative objective function and shows improved performance relative to OBC.

•

Experimental results on neuromorphic datasets indicate that our pruning and quantization framework achieves SOTA one-shot post-training pruning/quantization of SNNs. For instance, we achieved a 1.74% drop in accuracy at 97% sparsity on the DVS128-Gesture dataset.

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{\tau_{m}}\right)V[t-1]+\frac{1}{\tau_{m}}I[t]

\displaystyle S[t]

\displaystyle=\Theta(U[t]-V_{th})

(2)

\displaystyle V[t]

\displaystyle=U[t](1-S[t])

(3)

To avoid confusion,

All neural network compression methods that use second-order approximation are extensions of the OBS framework

(4)

c_{w_{p}}

c_{w_{p}}=w_{p}-\hat{w_{p}}
where
\hat{w_{p}}
is the nearest quantized expression of
w_{p}
).
[\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}]_{pp}
denotes the
p^{th}
diagonal of inverse Hessian,
[\boldsymbol{H}^{-1}]_{:p}
is its
p^{th}
column.

3
Methods

3.1
Module-wise compression for SNNs

Since the mathematics behind pruning and quantization in this paper is equivalent, we call the pruning and quantization problems
compression problems
.

Module-wise compression for SNNs in this paper largely follows the structure of Optimal Brain Compression by
Frantar et al. 2023a
. However, compressing linear and convolution layers alone, as is done in ExactOBS, is insufficient for SNNs. The output of linear and convolution layers in SNNs, either directly or indirectly through a batch normalization layer, acts as the external input current to the LIF neuron layer. However, minor errors in external input current accumulate in the LIF neurons’ membrane potential over time, resulting in significant output spike train deviation. Work by
Thieu and Melnik 2022
also shows that small Gaussian noise on external input current significantly increases the output irregularity of LIF neurons. On the other hand, the membrane potential
U[t]
directly indicates the LIF neuron’s "readiness" to fire at time
t
. Section
4.1
will empirically show that a LIF neuron’s membrane potential
U
is a superior objective function to constrain the change in the neuron’s output spike train
S
compared to the external input current
I
.

To have
U
in the objective function, we group the LIF neuron layers alongside the previous linear/convolution layers. We define modules in "module-wise compression" for SNN as each LIF neuron layer and its immediate preceding Linear, Convolutional, and Batch Normalization layers. Figure
1
shows the module concept in an SNN.

From now on, this paper considers the problem of SNN post-training compression as multiple independent sub-problems of per-module post-training compression, where each layer consists of a linear/convolution layer, possibly a batch normalization layer, and an LIF neuron layer. This follows the ANN layer-wise pruning/quantization convention well.

Following the convention of ANN pruning/quantization frameworks, OBC
[
24
]
and AdaPrune
[
29
]
, we focus on compressing linear and convolution layers within the modules. Convolution layers can be unrolled into a linear layer with unrolled input and flattened weights (see AdaPrune
[
29
]
for precise derivation). Batch Normalization layers can also be linearized. Appendix A shows that both convolution and batch normalization layers in SNNs can be converted into linear layers. Therefore, under this equivalence, this paper considers only a single module type in the module-wise SNN compression problem: one linear layer followed by one LIF neuron layer.

3.2
Problem definition and background

We model each SNN module as a function
f(X,W)
acting on a single neuromorphic input
X
-a
d_{time}\times d_{input}
matrix, where
d_{time}
is the temporal length of a single input data- and layer weight
W
- a
d_{input}\times d_{output}
matrix, where
d_{output}
denotes the number of LIF neurons in the module- . Note that the function
f
outputs the membrane potentials
U
and a
d_{time}\times d_{output}
matrix.

The goal of module-wise compression is to find a "compressed"
W
, which we denote
\hat{W}
, that performs as similarly to the original weights as possible. More formally, the compressed weights
\hat{W}
should minimize the expected output change as measured by some loss function
\mathcal{L}
(usually squared L2 loss) while satisfies some constrains on
\hat{W}
, expressed as
\mathcal{C}(\hat{W})>C
:

argmin_{\hat{W}}E_{X}(||f(X,W)-f(X,\hat{W})||_{2}^{2})\text{, subject to }\mathcal{C}(\hat{W})>C

(5)

Expectation is usually approximated by the average over a small number
N
of sample inputs
X
. For a differentiable
f
at
W
, the loss can be motivated via the second-order approximation.

3.3
Optimal Spiking Brain Compression (OSBC)

This paper proposes a framework for finding a differentiable
f
and obtaining the resulting objective function’s Hessian
\boldsymbol{H}
for general SNNs. We call it Optimal Spiking Brain Compression, mimicking its ANN counterpart Optimal Brain Compression (OBC).

f
can be rewritten as the composition
u\circ i
. Function
i
takes
X
,
W
and produces the neuron input current over time
I_{d_{time}\times d_{output}}
. Function
u
takes neuron input current over time, and produces neuron membrane potential over time
U_{d_{time}\times d_{output}}
.
i(X,W)=XW
since the first layer is always linear.

f(X,W)=u\circ i(X,W)=u(XW)

(6)

The membrane potential of an LIF neuron depends only on its own external input current, which is why function
u
can be rewritten as the concatenations of each individual LIF neuron membrane potential
[u_{1}(I_{:,1}),u_{2}(I_{:,2}),...u_{output}(I_{:,output})]^{T}
where
u_{1}=u_{2}=...=u_{output}
for LIF layers with identical neurons. We call the function that produces the membrane potential for a single neuron
v
. Then the square loss in Eq. (4) can be expressed as the sum of each LIF neuron’s membrane potential’s square loss.

\displaystyle||f(X,W)-f(X,\hat{W})||_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{output}}||v(XW_{:,j})-v(X\hat{W_{:,j}}))||_{2}^{2}

(7)

This means we can calculate the Hessian for the synaptic connections to each LIF neuron (each column of weights in W) separately, as there are no neuron dependencies. This is similar to
[
24
]

3.3.2
Surrogate Membrane Potential

Calculating precise
M_{j}
requires
O(d_{time}^{3}\cdot d_{output}\cdot N)
time complexity for a sample dataset of size N, since
M_{j}
is unique for each spike train, for each layer. A good approximation of
M_{j}
can be obtained with a true convolution kernel K shown in Figure
2
c).
M_{j}
calculated with K has low complexity
O(d_{time}^{2})
, since it depends only on the membrane time constant and is identical for all neurons in a typical LIF neuron layer. We will empirically show that K is a good substitute for
K_{t}
in section
4.1
. We call the membrane potential obtained through K surrogate membrane potential
U_{surrogate}
.

K[k]=\begin{cases}(1-\frac{1}{\tau_{m}})^{k}\frac{1}{\tau_{m}}&\text{if $k\geq 0$}\\
0&\text{if $k<0$}\end{cases}

(11)

This paper will use
M_{j}
calculated from the surrogate membrane potential for PTC. Such
M_{j}
is identical across LIF neurons in the same layer. We denote this matrix by
M
. We can then express the objective function for
W_{:,j}
(synaptic connection to neuron j) as
||MXW_{:,j}-MX\hat{W_{:,j}}||_{2}^{2}
. Since Eq.
7
tells us we can calculate the Hessian of each column of weights separately, the expected Hessian matrix for
W_{:,j}
, which has standard least square form, can be expressed as
\boldsymbol{H}=E_{X}[2(MX)^{T}MX]\approx\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}2(MX_{n})^{T}MX_{n}
, where
X_{n}
is the
n^{th}
sample input for this layer.
\boldsymbol{H}
can be used for module-wise pruning/quantization following the OBS framework mentioned in Eq.
4
.

The key observation here is that any operations expressed as a left matrix multiplication to the linear layer output
XW
have an objective function in least squares form. Thus, the objective function has a very easy-to-compute Hessian matrix that can be used for unstructured pruning and quantization.

Another key property of our compression method is that it can handle different membrane time constants
\tau
within an LIF neuron layer. However, to the best of our knowledge, no mainstream training method exists for such LIF neuron layers.
Fang et al. 2021
incorporated trainable time constants for each LIF neuron layer, but no intra-layer time constant difference.

4.1
Correlation study of objective functions and neuron output spike train loss

Since our method relies on minimizing the loss on an objective function, examining the relationship between the objective functions used and the loss in the output spike train is crucial. Following convention, we used the squared Van-Rossum Distance(VRD)
Rossum 2001
between pre- and post-compression output spike trains to represent the output spike train loss. The squaring is to be consistent with the squared L2 norm in objective functions. We examined the objective function of ExactOBS, OSBC with precise membrane potential, and OSBC with surrogate membrane potential.

The per-neuron objective function for ExactOBS can be expressed as the squared L2 norm of the change in neuron external input current
I
. For OSBC, the objective function can be described as the square L2 norm of the change in precise/surrogate membrane potential
U
/
U_{surrogate}
, calculated from the input current. Thus, for this study, we only model the input current of a spiking neuron instead of the weights and synapses before it.

The experiment is conducted on a LIF neuron with a firing threshold
V_{th}=1.0
and time constant
\tau_{m}\in[2.0,10.0]
, the latter of which was uniformly selected from its range. The timestep is chosen as 20. Input current at each timestep is drawn from a Gaussian distribution with mean
\mu\in[0.3,1.2]
and variance
\rho\in[0.05,0.2]
. A Gaussian noise with random mean and variance is introduced to each timestep of the external input current
I^{\prime}=I+\mathcal{N}(\mu,\rho)
to simulate the compression process. Then
U
,
U_{surrogate}
, and
S
of the LIF neuron are recorded with input
I
and
I^{\prime}
. Squared L2 norms of the objective functions and VRD of output spike trains are calculated. This experiment is repeated 10,000 times. Figure
3
shows the result. We can see that the VRD of the output spike train correlates well with the objective function of both OSBC methods, with correlation at 0.982 and 0.924, respectively, much higher than that of the ExactOBS method, at 0.104. Surrogate membrane potential retained much of the correlation compared to precise membrane potential, but requires much less computation, which validates our choice in section
3.3.2
.

4.2.2
Quantization

We compared OSBC’s quantization accuracy with a baseline method and vanilla implementation of GPTQ
[
25
]
, the state-of-the-art quantization derivative of OBC, on SNN. The quantization experiment used the same trained models and sample data as the pruning experiment. The models were quantized to 4, 3, and 2 bit widths.

We used the GPTQ framework for our quantization experiments. GPTQ quantizes each linear/convolution ANN layer according to standard uniform per-channel symmetric quantization on the min-max grid, similar to
Dettmers et al. 2022
. We chose a symmetric quantization grid for the low computing cost when deploying to neuromorphic hardware. The weights are then quantized in the ascending diagonal inverse Hessian order, which results in a quantization order from biggest to smallest loss in an equal spacing quantization grid according to Eq.
4

OSBC’s performance is generally better than that of RTN and GPTQ. It can quantize the CIFAR10-DVS model to 3-bit precision with a 1.86% drop in accuracy. However, OSBC does not consistently outperform RTN and GPTQ in higher-precision quantization for CIFAR10-DVS and DVS128-Gesture. Nevertheless, OSBC achieves the best quantization result out of the three methods at the lowest bit representation tested (2bit), outperforming the next best method by 28.11%, 1.30%, and 2.01% respectively.

4.3
Limitations and future works

Being a purely spatial SNN compression framework, we did not compress the temporal axis, which has the potential to improve the time-to-prediction of our underlying model. Also, we observed the out-of-bound problem with the min-max quantization grid, and a more rigorous study of the relationship between the choice of the quantization grid and the quantization performance is needed.

The key insight for OSBC is that any operation expressed as a left matrix multiplication to a linear layer output has its square L2 loss in least squares form, which enables easy Hessian matrix calculation for pruning or quantizing the layer. This indicates that future works on SNN compression can explore different methods to constrain the module-wise output spike train loss that can be expressed as a left matrix multiplication. Future work on combining pruning and quantization is also worth exploring.

5
Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper proposed a one-shot, efficient, and unified post-training pruning and quantization framework for SNNs, utilizing a second-order approximation of per-layer membrane potential loss to compress and compensate for the compression dynamically. Through theoretical analysis and empirical demonstration, we showed our method’s effectiveness in pruning and quantizing SNNs trained for neuromorphic datasets, outperforming current SOTA on SNN post-training one-shot pruning and quantization methods, approaching the accuracy of iterative retraining methods. We expect this work to pave the way for the efficient compression of very large SNNs such as deep SNNs and Spiking Transformers for low-power and edge computing use cases.
