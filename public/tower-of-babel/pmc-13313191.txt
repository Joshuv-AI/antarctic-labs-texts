In Vivo Evaluation of Peripapillary Scleral Architecture in Myopia and Glaucoma Using Polarization-Sensitive Optical Coherence Tomography
Authors: Kaho Akiyama, Shuichiro Aoki, Asahi Konagai, Masahiro Yamanari, Michiaki Okamoto, Megumi Honjo, Hitomi Saito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo quantitatively characterize the in vivo peripapillary sclera (PPS) architecture using polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) and evaluate its association with axial length (AL) and glaucoma.MethodsA total of 94 eyes from 94 participants (AL, 23.52–31.31 mm), including 64 normal-tension glaucoma (NTG) eyes and 30 nonglaucomatous eyes, were enrolled in this retrospective study. PS-OCT optic axis maps identified a two-layered structure consisting of an outer circumferential ring (CR) encircling the optic nerve head (ONH) and an inner radial layer (IRL). CR width was measured at the temporal side of the ONH. The IRL and peripapillary choroidal thickness were measured at seven temporal-half sectors. Associations with AL and sectoral variations in these associations were analyzed using multiple linear regression and linear mixed-effects models. A subanalysis compared the PPS structure of age-, sex-, and AL-matched NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes.ResultsCR width was positively associated with AL (P = 0.006). IRL thickness was thinnest in inferior sectors and negatively associated with the AL (P < 0.017), with no significant slope differences across temporal-half sectors (AL × sector interaction P = 0.578). The association between peripapillary choroidal thickness and AL showed significant sectoral heterogeneity (AL × sector interaction P < 0.006), with more negative slopes in temporal sectors (P < 0.006). No significant differences in PPS parameters were observed between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes (all P > 0.05).ConclusionsPS-OCT enabled the in vivo quantitative assessment of the PPS architecture, revealing layer-specific structural associations with AL (with sectoral differences) and comparable PPS features between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes. These layer-specific scleral characteristics may provide a biomechanical framework for understanding myopia-related ONH deformation.

Axial elongation in myopia is associated with structural alterations of the globe. Ex vivo studies have shown that the posterior sclera, including the peripapillary sclera (PPS), is thinner in eyes with high myopia (HM).1 The PPS between the optic nerve head (ONH) border and the dura mater, histologically termed the scleral flange, is the thinnest part of the sclera and is reported to thin even further in eyes with HM.2 HM is also known to increase the risk of open‑angle glaucoma,3 independent of IOP.4 Clinical and experimental evidence indicates that structural alterations of the ONH associated with axial elongation may increase its susceptibility to glaucomatous damage,1 potentially contributing to the high prevalence of NTG in East Asia.5 Given that PPS provides structural support to the lamina cribrosa (LC), its structural alteration may affect the biomechanical stability of the LC and contribute to ONH vulnerability.6–8 Therefore, investigating PPS structural characteristics in relation to myopia is essential for building a foundation to study their associations with vision-threatening diseases such as myopia-related optic neuropathy and glaucoma.

Ex vivo methods such as polarized light microscopy and X-ray or light scattering have revealed layered scleral architecture but have been limited to postmortem samples.9–11 Polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) enables the noninvasive, in vivo visualization of scleral collagen fiber orientations through tissue birefringence-derived optic axis (OA). Previous PS-OCT studies have described a characteristic PPS architecture: a circumferential ring of fibers encircling the ONH and a two-layered peripapillary structure with a radially oriented inner layer and a circumferentially oriented outer layer.12–14 Furthermore, a recent investigation has also identified the presence of inner scleral fiber penetration toward the LC, suggesting an additional mechanical linkage between the PPS and the ONH.15 However, existing PS-OCT studies have only provided qualitative descriptions or limited quantitative data in healthy eyes or pathologically myopic (PM) eyes with extensive ONH deformation. It remains unclear how PPS architecture varies across non-PM eyes with different axial lengths (ALs) and whether glaucoma-related structural differences are also present.

In this study, we applied PS‑OCT technology to quantify in vivo PPS collagen architecture in non-PM eyes with a broad range of ALs. We hypothesized that the PPS architecture is associated with myopia and may vary across eyes with different ALs. We quantitatively assessed layer-specific PPS architecture and evaluated its sectoral relationship with AL. As an exploratory subanalysis, we compared PPS structure between NTG eyes and age-, sex-, and AL-matched nonglaucomatous eyes focusing on configurations potentially linked to glaucoma. By establishing objective, quantitative PPS structure descriptors, this study aimed to elucidate the in vivo PPS architectural characteristics associated with myopia and explore their potential relevance to glaucoma.

Methods

Participants

Protocols for this retrospective observational study were approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine and Faculty of Medicine at The University of Tokyo and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The need for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Participants in this study included participants with and without NTG aged ≥18 years who consulted for a regular eye examination and underwent PS-OCT examinations, AL measurements and visual field (VF) examinations at the University of Tokyo Hospital (Tokyo, Japan) between October 2023 and April 2025. Exclusion criteria were (1) poor-quality PS-OCT images with quality index of <7 (due to media opacities including corneal opacity and clinically significant cataract); (2) insufficient scleral backscattering signal to construct OA images; (3) any history of intraocular surgery except for uncomplicated cataract surgery; (4) retinal diseases; (5) nonglaucomatous optic neuropathy, disc anomalies including ONH hypoplasia and inverted ONH; (6) unreliable VF results (fixation losses >20%, false positives >15%, or false negatives >15%); and (7) myopic maculopathy of category ≥2 based on the META-PM (Meta-Analysis for Pathologic Myopia) study classification,16 as determined from color fundus photographs, as well as eyes showing plus lesions such as lacquer cracks, myopic choroidal neovascularization, Fuchs’ spot, or posterior staphyloma.16 If both eyes of a participant with unilateral NTG were eligible, the eye diagnosed with NTG was selected. Otherwise, the right eye was selected. Figure 1 presents the inclusion and exclusion flowchart for study participants. We restricted the glaucomatous cohort to NTG eyes to minimize the potential influence of markedly elevated IOP-related structural deformation, such as posterior LC displacement and PPS deformation.7

Inclusion and exclusion flowchart of this study.

All participants included in this study underwent the following ocular examinations: refraction and corneal curvature radius measurements (TONOREF II, NIDEK, Aichi, Japan), best-corrected visual acuity, AL measurements (OA-2000, TOMEY, Aichi, Japan), slit-lamp examination, IOP measurements with Goldmann applanation tonometry, PS-OCT imaging (Tomey Corporation), and Humphrey Field Analyzer (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA) testing with the 24-2 Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm standard strategy.

Diagnosis of each eye was conducted independently by two glaucoma specialists (KA, HS) and disagreements were resolved by a third adjudicator (SA). NTG eyes had open angles on gonioscopy, glaucomatous ONH changes (i.e., neuroretinal rim narrowing, notching, and the presence of retinal nerve fiber layer defects) confirmed on fundus examination, stereophotographs, and en face OCT RNFL maps, an IOP of ≤21 mm Hg, and VF abnormality in accordance with glaucomatous ONH changes. VF results were diagnosed as abnormal if one of the following criteria was met. (1) The pattern deviation probability plot showed a cluster of three or more points with a probability of <5% and at least one point with a probability of <1% in an expected location. (2) The pattern standard deviation had a probability of <5%. (3) The glaucoma hemifield test indicated that the field is out of normal limits, according to the criteria described by Anderson and Patella.17 Nonglaucomatous eyes had normal optic disc appearances on fundus examination and fundus stereo photographs, IOP of ≤21 mm Hg, no abnormal VF results, and no abnormal findings on slit-lamp examination and fundus examination.

PS-OCT Imaging and Processing

PS-OCT imaging was performed with the ROCTIA system (Tomey Corporation) using a wavelength-sweeping laser centered at 1050 nm. Comprehensive details of this system have been previously described elsewhere.18,19 A 20° × 20° ONH-centered scan with 512 horizontal A-lines × 512 vertical B-scans was acquired after pupillary dilation. The system achieved an axial resolution of 7.3 µm and a depth measurement range of 4.49 mm in tissue. Raw polarization-diverse interferograms were reconstructed into complex Jones matrices.20 From the corrected Jones matrices, local OA orientation was obtained with a robust procedure described elsewhere13 (Figs. 2A, 2B). The OA corresponds with the local slow axis of birefringent tissue, reflecting the dominant alignment of scleral fibers. The OA represents the orientation of the slow axis projected onto the plane perpendicular to the incident OCT beam. Accordingly, color coding in cross-sectional OA images reflects this projected in-plane orientation derived from the volumetric dataset. En face scleral OA maps were derived by averaging the OA values along the axial dimension over 15 pixels (67 µm) beneath the choroid–sclera interface (Fig. 2C). Because OA values are relative to an arbitrary reference, a single global offset was determined by aligning the en face OA map with known peripapillary fiber architectures: circumferential fibers encircling the disc and radially oriented peripapillary retinal nerve fiber bundles.

The clinical disc margin was determined using Fiji ImageJ 1.54f (Java 1.8.0_322 64 bit, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) upon en face maximum intensity projection images concurrently obtained with PS-OCT scanning to ensure exact alignment with en face OA images. A best-fit ellipse was applied to determine the optic disc centroid (Fig. 3A). The corresponding en face OA image was imported into Fiji ImageJ, and the circumferential ring (CR) width was measured along the fovea–disc axis passing through the optic disc centroid (Fig. 3B). The mean CR width measured at seven locations along the temporal side of the ONH (0°, ±5°, ±10°, and ±15° relative to the fovea–disc axis) was used for analysis. Inner radial layer (IRL) and peripapillary choroidal thicknesses were manually measured at seven temporal points separated by a 30° interval on a 2-mm peripapillary circle centered on the optic disc centroid (−90°, inferior; 0°, temporal; +90°, superior) (Figs. 3C, 3D). CR width and measurement circle diameter for IRL and peripapillary choroidal thicknesses were corrected for magnification error using the modified Littmann's formula.21,22

The presence of penetrating inner scleral fibers, a recently reported structure,15 was identified on horizontal OA images and corresponding birefringence images (Fig. 4) by two observers (SA and KA) and disagreements were resolved by a third adjudicator (HS). A penetrating inner scleral fiber was considered present when radially oriented fibers derived from the inner layer penetrated through or beneath the circumferential arrangement of the outer layer in three or more consecutive slices.

Statistical Analysis

All data are reported as mean ± SD unless otherwise specified. Association between the AL and CR width was evaluated using multiple linear regression adjusted for age and sex, with AL × age and AL × sex interaction terms also assessed by likelihood ratio tests. For sectoral thickness repeated measures within the same eye, variation across temporal-half sectors in the IRL and peripapillary choroidal thickness were evaluated using one-way repeated-measures ANOVA, followed by post hoc paired t-tests for all sector pairs. Associations between the AL and IRL or peripapillary choroidal thickness were evaluated using linear mixed-effects regression to account for within-eye correlation across sectors. The models included a random intercept for eye, with fixed effects for AL, age, sex, sector, and the AL × sector interaction. To evaluate heterogeneity in the association between the AL and thickness measurements across sectors, models with and without the AL × sector term were compared using likelihood ratio tests. The AL × age and AL × sex interactions were assessed similarly. When the interaction was significant, pairwise Wald t-tests of slope differences were performed using Kenward–Roger adjusted degrees of freedom. Sector-wise linear regressions are presented for descriptive purposes only. Additional exploratory analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between PPS parameters and glaucoma-related structural and functional measurements, including VF mean deviation and global circumpapillary retinal nerve fiber layer thickness, using linear regression or linear mixed-effects regression models in both the whole cohort and the NTG subgroup.

Comparisons between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes were conducted on a 2:1 propensity score matched dataset to minimize the confounding effects of relevant covariates (age, sex, and AL). CR width was compared between groups using the Mann–Whitney U test. Group differences in IRL and peripapillary choroidal thickness were assessed by comparing averaged values across seven temporal sectors per eye between groups using Mann–Whitney U tests, as no a priori hypothesis regarding sector-specific group differences was postulated and to avoid inflation of type I error from multiple comparisons of within-eye correlated sectors. Exploratory temporal-half sector-wise comparisons using Mann–Whitney U tests for descriptive purposes only. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests or Fisher's exact tests when expected counts were <5.

P values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Interobserver reliability for IRL and peripapillary choroidal thickness measurements by two observers (SA and KA) was assessed in a randomly selected subset of 15 eyes by two-way random-effects, single-measure, absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (2,1). Interobserver agreement for the presence of penetrating inner scleral fiber was assessed in a randomly selected subset of 30 eyes using Cohen's κ coefficient. Statistical analyses were performed with Python (version 3.8.2).

Results

A total of 270 eyes from 139 participants met the inclusion criteria. After excluding 116 eyes of 74 participants (62 eyes for insufficient quality of PS-OCT scan, 9 eyes for insufficient scleral backscattering signal, 3 eyes for a history of vitreous or filtration surgery, 10 eyes for retinal disease, 9 eyes for optic disc abnormality, and 23 eyes for PM), 1 eye of each participant was selected. As a result, 94 non-PM eyes of 94 participants spanning a broad range of ALs (23.52–31.31 mm), including 64 NTG eyes and 30 nonglaucomatous eyes, were enrolled. Background characteristics are shown in Table 1. Interobserver intraclass correlation coefficients were 0.87 and 0.98 for IRL and peripapillary choroidal thickness, respectively, indicating excellent reliability. Interobserver agreement for the presence of penetrating inner scleral fibers was almost perfect (κ = 0.85, 93.3% agreement). Penetrating inner scleral fibers were identified in 78 eyes (83.0 %) of the whole cohort.

Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant differences across temporal-half sectors in both IRL (F = 28.52; P < 0.001) and peripapillary choroidal thickness (F = 78.54; P < 0.001). Post hoc testing indicated that the IRL showed the lowest thickness values in the inferior sectors (−90°, −60°), with a relatively uniform thickness from the superior to the temporal regions. The peripapillary choroid was thickest in the superior sector (90°) and showed decreasing thickness toward the inferior temporal sectors (−60°, −30°) (Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Fig. S1).

Table 2 summarizes the associations between PPS parameters and the AL. After adjustment for age and sex, CR width was positively associated with AL (β = 16.68’ 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.91–28.46; P = 0.006) (Fig. 5A), and this association did not differ by age or sex (AL × age, P = 0.917; AL × sex ,P = 0.166). IRL thickness was negatively associated with AL (β = −8.52; 95% CI, −11.76 to −5.28; P < 0.001), and older age was independently associated with thinner IRL (P = 0.002), whereas sex was not (P = 0.106). The AL–IRL thickness association did not differ significantly across temporal-half sectors, age, or sex (AL × sector, P = 0.573; AL × age, P = 0.696; AL × sex, P = 0.423). Peripapillary choroidal thickness was negatively associated with both AL (β = −8.78; 95% CI, −12.88 to −4.68; P < 0.001) and age (P < 0.001). The AL–choroid association showed significant heterogeneity across temporal-half sectors (P < 0.001) (Supplementary Fig. S2), indicating that the slope of the association between the AL and peripapillary choroidal thickness differed across sectors. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that slopes in the temporal sectors (–30°, 0°, and 30°) were significantly more negative than that at −60° (all P < 0.05; Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted) (Supplementary Table S2). Representative scatter plots for the temporal sector (0°) are shown in Figures 5B and 5C. The association between AL and peripapillary choroidal thickness showed significant interactions with age and sex (AL × age, P = 0.001; AL × sex, P = 0.005), with steeper AL-associated thinning observed in younger and male participants.

Discussion

In this study, we used PS-OCT to quantify the in vivo architecture of PPS in a non-PM cohort and examined its associations with myopia and glaucoma. Our results demonstrated that CR width was positively associated with AL, whereas IRL thickness showed a negative association with AL without significant differences in the association across temporal-half sectors. Peripapillary choroidal thickness demonstrated a significant negative association with AL with a temporal predominance. PPS parameters and the prevalence of penetrating inner scleral fibers were similar between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes matched for age, sex, and AL. These findings offer new insights into PPS architectural associations with myopia and provide a structural basis for considering the potential role of scleral biomechanics.

The PPS contains a circumferential collagen architecture surrounding the scleral canal that plays an important biomechanical role in stabilizing the ONH.8 Previous ex vivo studies using wide-angle X-ray scattering, polarized light microscopy, small-angle light scattering, and second harmonic generation imaging have demonstrated that collagen fibers in the PPS are predominantly arranged circumferentially around the LC.8–11 In the present study, we quantitatively assessed this circumferential structure in vivo using PS-OCT and found that CR width showed a significant positive association with AL. Prior studies have implicated scleral remodeling in myopic ONH deformation through reports of AL elongation–induced deep ONH structure misalignment and scleral flange extension contributing to the enlargement of the parapapillary-gamma and -delta zones.2,23,24 The observed association between CR width and AL may, therefore, reflect remodeling or redistribution of circumferential fibers within the outer scleral layer in eyes with a longer AL. A recent PS-OCT study of eyes with varying PM severities reported early loss of radial fibers in the inner scleral layer in PM progression, while the outer scleral layer, including the CR structure, remained relatively preserved, even in the advanced stages of PM.14 This observation suggests that the overall circumferential architecture of the PPS is maintained, even in highly elongated eyes. However, preservation of the overall circumferential architecture does not necessarily imply unchanged biomechanical behavior. This CR ring structure is thought to limit scleral canal expansion and reduce strain within the LC under IOP loading.6,8,25 Experimental models have also shown that the structural balance between radial and circumferential fibers is critical for maintaining LC stability.6 The greater CR width observed in our eyes with a longer AL may be associated with differences in load distribution around the scleral canal, which could influence the mechanical environment of the ONH.

Previous postmortem studies have reported thinning of the full-thickness posterior sclera in eyes with a longer AL.26,27 However, such studies are subject to inherent limitations, including tissue shrinkage and the loss of physiological loading conditions. In vivo evaluation of full-thickness scleral structures also remains challenging, because current OCT technology does not allow consistent full-thickness scleral measurements, except in advanced PM eyes with extremely thin choroid and sclera. Therefore, we used PS-OCT to target the inner scleral layer. Our IRL thickness results (sector average. 95.5 µm) were thinner than those reported in a recent PS-OCT study by Verdonk et al.28 in 17 healthy eyes (120.8–122.8 µm). Differences in study populations and measurement strategies may partly explain this discrepancy. Our cohort included eyes with mild myopia to HM, whereas Verdonk et al.28 evaluated emmetropic to mildly myopic eyes. In addition, their measurements were derived from deep learning–based segmentation of B-scans and thickness values were averaged across the segmented region,28 whereas our measurements were obtained manually at a fixed radial distance from the ONH centroid. This approach allowed not only standardized measurements relative to the ONH center, but also sector-by-sector quantification of scleral thickness.29 Thus, PS-OCT–derived IRL thickness may serve as a practical in vivo indicator of structural variation within the PPS, although further validation against full-thickness scleral measurements is required.

Our temporal-half sectoral analysis showed that IRL thickness was thinnest in the inferior sectors and thickest in the superior and temporal sectors. Previous ex vivo studies have also demonstrated regional heterogeneity in posterior scleral thickness,27 and our findings align with histological studies of donor eyes that have reported a thinner PPS in the inferior sector.29 Developmental anatomy may provide one possible explanation for this inferior susceptibility. The optic fissure lies inferiorly and is the last portion of the optic cup to close during embryogenesis, plausibly resulting in a developmentally thinner and mechanically weaker inferior sclera. This is consistent with inferiorly biased outpouching of the sclera in posterior staphyloma and inferonasal scleral ectasia in tilted disc syndrome.30,31

The negative association between AL and IRL thickness observed in our study may reflect several possibilities. AL elongation may lead to structural thinning of the inner scleral layer during globe expansion. Conversely, eyes with a thinner IRL may be more susceptible to AL elongation. The cross-sectional nature of the present study does not allow us to determine the direction of this relationship and future longitudinal studies are needed to further investigate these possibilities. In addition, AL-associated IRL thinning did not differ significantly across temporal-half sectors on an absolute scale (µm/mm AL). However, regions with a thinner absolute thickness show greater proportional differences for the same absolute difference, suggesting that equivalent absolute differences in IRL thickness carry greater proportional weight in sectors where the tissue is thinner. The radially oriented collagen fibers of the IRL have been shown in biomechanical models to resist IOP-induced radial strain.6 Sectors with a lower IRL thickness may contain a reduced complement of radially oriented fibers and thus be mechanically less resistant to the same deforming force, a hypothesis that warrants direct biomechanical investigation.

Peripapillary choroidal thickness was also thinner in eyes with a longer AL, consistent with previous reports.23,32 Furthermore, the association between AL and choroidal thickness showed clear sectoral heterogeneity, with the steepest slope observed in the temporal sectors. Gupta et al.32 reported the peripapillary choroid to be thickest temporally in emmetropic eyes, but thickest superiorly and thinnest inferiorly in myopic eyes, suggesting that the spatial distribution of the peripapillary choroid changes with AL. This pattern supports the sector-dependent association between AL and choroidal thickness observed in our cohort.32 This finding contrasts with the relatively uniform association of IRL thickness with AL across temporal-half sectors on an absolute scale. This difference in sectoral association patterns may relate to their distinct tissue composition. The IRL serves as a load-bearing collagenous shell, whereas the choroid is a highly vascular tissue influenced by multiple factors, including myopic expansion, aging, and regional variations in perfusion.32,33 In addition, myopia-related ONH remodeling such as enlargement of peripapillary-beta zone may further contribute to sectoral differences.34

The layered PPS architecture is thought to stabilize the ONH and help to mitigate LC deformation under mechanical load.6 Under the hypothesis that alterations in this structural support may contribute to NTG pathogenesis, we examined whether PPS structural parameters differed between NTG eyes and nonglaucomatous eyes. However, after controlling for key covariates, we found no significant differences in CR width, temporal-half sectoral IRL thickness, or choroidal thickness between the two groups. These findings indicate that the overall macroscopic PPS architecture was largely comparable between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes when major confounding factors were considered.

Given the proximity of CR structure to the prelaminar tissue and the histological continuity of the LC with the inner scleral layer,35 CR width alone may not fully capture structural features relevant to LC deformation dynamics. Similarly, the lack of difference in IRL thickness between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes was consistent with a previous histological study reporting that, although the full-thickness sclera was thinner in eyes with a longer AL, it did not differ between eyes with and without glaucoma.36 However, other aspects of the PPS microstructure beyond simple geometric parameters, including collagen fiber diameter and density, extracellular matrix composition, lamellar interweaving, and collagen crimp,8 may contribute to NTG susceptibility. Indeed, previous experimental studies have reported that glaucomatous sclera may exhibit alterations in the collagen microarchitecture.37 Building on this, future work integrating PS-OCT–derived macrostructure with tissue microstructure could provide valuable insights into how subtle microstructural alterations contribute to ONH vulnerability and the development of glaucoma, particularly in myopic eyes.

The presence of penetrating inner scleral fibers has recently been characterized using PS-OCT as radially oriented fibers extending from the inner scleral layer through the circumferential outer layer toward the LC.15 In this report, these fibers were identified in 85.5% of eyes with extensive myopia (AL, 30.0 ± 1.98 mm) and demonstrated substantial interobserver agreement (κ = 0.68), supporting their reproducibility as a structural feature.15 In our cohort spanning mild myopia to HM, similar fiber orientation patterns were observed, and their prevalence was comparable between NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes. This structure has been proposed to serve as a mechanical anchor stabilizing the CR structure and the LC.15 The preservation of these fibers in both NTG and nonglaucomatous eyes in our study suggests that they may represent a common architectural component of the PPS rather than a structure associated with NTG.

There are some limitations to our study. First, the penetration depth of current OCT technology limited the assessment of IRL on the nasal side. However, the temporal peripapillary region, which is frequently involved in myopia-related structural changes and glaucomatous damage, was well visualized, allowing evaluation of the PPS structure in this region. Second, PS-OCT can evaluate the dominant orientation of scleral fibers, but cannot directly capture microstructural features. Because the estimation of local OA requires spatial filtering of polarization signals, the effective spatial resolution of OA images is reduced compared with OCT intensity images. Future studies integrating microstructural characteristics with the present geometric measurements may provide a more comprehensive understanding of scleral biomechanics in myopia and glaucoma. Third, our study included only Japanese participants. Variations in scleral thickness reported across ethnicities29 may have influenced the absolute values of thickness measurements. However, the sectoral distribution pattern has been reported to be consistent among different races,29 suggesting that the overall trends observed in this study may be applicable to other populations. Fourth, differentiating glaucomatous optic neuropathy from nonglaucomatous optic neuropathy is known to be challenging, particularly in eyes with HM.38 Although we applied strict multimodal diagnostic criteria requiring concordant structural and functional findings and excluded eyes with significant myopic maculopathy, residual diagnostic uncertainty may remain in cross-sectional studies of myopic populations.

Conclusions

PS-OCT enabled quantitative in vivo characterization of layer-specific PPS architecture in non-PM eyes across a broad range of ALs. Our findings suggest that eyes with a longer AL exhibit distinct layer-specific PPS architectural patterns, characterized by greater circumferential width and a thinner inner layer, indicating that the PPS architecture differs across structural components in relation to AL. These findings provide an anatomical and biomechanical framework for future studies investigating how myopia-related PPS architecture may influence ONH deformation and vulnerability to glaucomatous damage.
