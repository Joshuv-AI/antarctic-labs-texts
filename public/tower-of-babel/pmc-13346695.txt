Structure-based virtual screening identifies novel small-molecule inhibitors targeting the endonuclease active site of APE1
Authors: Tianzhu Shen, Hongfei Shen, Yun Kong, Weidong Qiang, Xinwei Yu, Jiaping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1) is a key enzyme in the base excision repair (BER) pathway, and its aberrant overexpression is closely associated with poor prognosis, enhanced invasiveness, and therapeutic resistance in multiple cancers, making it an attractive anticancer drug target. In this study, a systematic structure-based virtual screening workflow was established using the crystal structure of the APE1 endonuclease active pocket (PDB ID: 7TC2) to screen approximately 1.529 million small molecules collected from the DrugBank, TargetMol, Specs, and ChemDiv databases. Through multi-step filtering involving drug-likeness evaluation, molecular docking, interaction fingerprint (IFP) screening, conformational strain energy assessment, and MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations, a set of candidate compounds with predicted affinity toward APE1 was identified. Representative hit compounds from different databases were further subjected to binding mode analysis and 100 ns molecular dynamics simulations. Computational analyses suggested that these candidate compounds were able to maintain hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions with key residues in the APE1 active site. Among them, DB02187 and T9286 exhibited more favorable computational performance than the reference ligand in multiple dimensions, including complex stability, per-residue energy contribution, and free energy landscape profiles. In contrast, although HIT107168463 exhibited favorable static binding energy, it showed relatively poor dynamic stability. Overall, DB02187 and T9286 displayed favorable predicted binding features and may represent candidate scaffolds for future experimental validation and lead optimization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at10.1038/s41598-026-51975-0.

Introduction

Apurinic/apyrimidinic endonuclease 1 (APE1) is a multifunctional protein with dual roles in DNA repair and redox regulation, playing a critical role in maintaining genomic stability and modulating cellular stress responses1. As a core enzyme in the BER pathway, APE1 recognizes and cleaves apurinic/apyrimidinic (AP) sites, representing a key step in the repair of damaged bases. In addition to DNA repair, APE1 also regulates the redox state of multiple transcription factors and thereby participates in cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammation, and tumor progression2. Accordingly, aberrant expression or dysregulation of APE1 has been closely associated with tumor cell survival, chemoresistance, invasiveness, and metastasis, making it an attractive therapeutic target in cancer treatment3,4.

Several small-molecule APE1 inhibitors have been reported, including APX33305 and related analogues targeting its redox activity, as well as other lead compounds with potential inhibitory effects on DNA repair function6,7. However, these compounds still show limitations in terms of selectivity, in vivo efficacy, safety, and clinical translatability, highlighting the need to identify novel and more effective APE1 inhibitors8. Compared with conventional experimental screening, structure-based virtual screening offers advantages such as low cost, high throughput, and strong target specificity, and has become an important strategy in lead discovery9,10.

In recent years, in-silico approaches have been widely applied in protein–ligand and protein–inhibitor studies, including identification of putative drug-binding pockets, analysis of ligand recognition mechanisms, and prioritization of novel bioactive compounds. For example, computational studies have been widely applied to identify novel epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors through structure-based virtual screening and molecular dynamics analysis11, as well as to discover poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) inhibitors by integrating molecular docking, binding free-energy calculations12. These examples further demonstrate the broad utility of molecular modeling, docking, and simulation techniques in modern drug discovery.

In the present study, we focused on the APE1 endonuclease active pocket and established a hierarchical and progressively refined virtual screening strategy. This site was selected because it is supported by an experimentally resolved co-crystal structure, which enables accurate definition of the binding cavity and key recognition residues for structure-based screening. In contrast, the binding region associated with the redox regulatory function of APE1 remains less clearly characterized and lacks consistent structural evidence, making it less suitable for reliable docking-based screening. First, the active site was defined based on a known co-crystal structure, and the reliability of the docking protocol was validated by re-docking. Next, large-scale compound libraries from multiple commercial databases were screened by molecular docking and interaction fingerprint (IFP) analysis based on the key interaction pattern of the reference ligand. Candidate molecules were then further evaluated through conformational strain energy and MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations. Finally, representative compounds were subjected to molecular dynamics simulations, per-residue energy decomposition, and free energy landscape analyses to comprehensively assess their binding stability. This study aimed to identify novel potential APE1 inhibitors from a million-scale chemical space and to provide a theoretical basis for subsequent experimental validation and lead optimization.

Materials and methods

Compound library preparation

A virtual screening library containing 1,529,230 small molecules was assembled from four commonly used databases, namely DrugBank FDA approved set13, TargetMol, Specs, and ChemDiv. These databases include approved or clinical-stage compounds, natural products, commercial small molecules, and highly diverse chemical scaffolds, thereby providing a broad chemical space for the discovery of APE1 inhibitors.

Drug-likeness filtering and 3D structure preparation

Compounds from the ChemDiv and Specs databases were filtered for drug-likeness using RDKit v2021.9 according to Lipinski’s rule of five and the Veber criteria, thereby excluding molecules with unfavorable molecular weight, excessive flexibility, or poor physicochemical properties. These preprocessing steps were applied because ChemDiv and Specs are large commercial screening libraries with broad chemical diversity and wide physicochemical property distributions. The retained compounds were converted into three-dimensional structures using OpenBabel v2.4.114, and their protonation states were assigned at pH 7.4. Compounds from the TargetMol natural product library and the DrugBank database were directly used for subsequent screening without additional drug-likeness filtering. The DrugBank subset mainly contains approved or investigational compounds with prior pharmaceutical relevance, whereas the TargetMol natural product subset was retained to preserve structurally diverse scaffolds that may fall outside conventional Lipinski space.

Protein preparation and binding site definition

The crystal structure of APE1 (PDB ID: 7TC2)15 was obtained from the Protein Data Bank and preprocessed using PyMOL v2.5.516 by removing water molecules, salt ions, and the co-crystallized ligand while retaining the protein structure. The docking grid was defined based on the position of the original co-crystallized ligand to fully cover the active pocket. Because the redox-related inhibitory site of APE1 lacks definitive structural evidence, this study focused on the endonuclease active pocket defined by the 7TC2 co-crystal structure.

Re-docking validation

To validate the reliability of the docking parameters17, the co-crystallized ligand in the 7TC2 structure was extracted and re-docked into the APE1 active pocket. The root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the predicted pose and the crystal pose was then calculated.

Molecular docking and IFP screening

Molecular docking was performed using smina program18, All processed receptor proteins and small-molecule ligands were converted into the PDBQT format required for molecular docking by ADFRsuite 1.019. The docking grid was centered at coordinates (− 7.49, − 25.54, 24.51) with dimensions of 25 × 25 × 25 Å, which fully covered the APE1 endonuclease active pocket. During docking, the exhaustiveness parameter was set to 8, and the best-ranked binding pose was retained for each compound.

Protein-ligand interaction fingerprints were calculated using PyPLIF-HIPPOS20. The key interactions observed for the reference ligand included hydrogen bonds with GLN-137 and SER-164, a salt bridge with ARG-136, and hydrophobic interactions with PHE-162, LEU-318, ILE-91, LEU-62, and PHE-165. Compounds showing at least one of these key interaction patterns and a docking score better than that of the reference ligand (− 5.4 kcal/mol) were retained.

Conformational strain energy evaluation

Conformational strain energy was evaluated using an RDKit-based workflow with the MMFF94 force field21. After hydrogen addition, the docked pose was energy-minimized using MMFF94, and the minimum energy was recorded. The difference between the energy of the original docked conformation and the minimized conformation was defined as the strain energy. Compounds with strain energy lower than 10 kcal/mol were retained for further analysis.

ADMET prediction

ADMET-related properties of the selected compounds were evaluated using the QikProp module implemented in Schrödinger v2022-3 together with the pkCSM online platform (http://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/pkcsm/). In this study, particular emphasis was placed on key drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic indicators, including predicted aqueous solubility (QPlogS), predicted hERG potassium channel inhibition risk (QPlogHERG), predicted Caco-2 cell permeability (QPPCaco), and predicted human oral absorption. These parameters were used to preliminarily assess the developability, absorption potential, and safety liabilities of the prioritized hit compounds.

Molecular dynamics simulation (MD)

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed using AMBER 24 based on the docked protein-ligand complexes. Small molecules were parameterized using the AM1-BCC charge27 model and the GAFF2 force field28 by antechamber module29, whereas the protein was described using the ff14SB force field. Each system was solvated in a truncated octahedral TIP3P water box with a 10 Å buffer around the solute, and Na+/Cl− ions were added to neutralize the system. The simulation workflow included energy minimization, 200 ps heating, 500 ps NVT equilibration, 500 ps NPT equilibration, and a 100 ns NPT production run. Non-bonded interactions were truncated at 10 Å, and long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method30. SHAKE was used to constrain hydrogen bond lengths31, while temperature control was maintained using the Langevin thermostat32, with a collision frequency of 2 ps⁻¹. A 2 fs integration time step was used, and trajectory snapshots were saved every 10 ps.

Trajectory analyses included ligand RMSD, complex RMSD, protein RMSF, radius of gyration (Rg), the number of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, and solvent-accessible surface area (SASA). In addition, per-residue energy decomposition and free energy landscape (FEL) analyses were performed to comprehensively evaluate binding stability and conformational distribution features of the candidate compounds.

Results and discussion

Selection and validation of the APE1 endonuclease pocket

As show in Fig. 1, previous crystallographic studies revealed the binding mode of 5-nitroindole-2-carboxylic acid with APE115, thereby providing direct structural evidence for the endonuclease active pocket. In contrast, the redox-related inhibitory site of APE1 lacks clear and consistent structural evidence. Therefore, the 7TC2 co-crystal structure was considered a suitable template because it captures a ligand-bound conformation of APE1 within the experimentally characterized endonuclease catalytic pocket, providing direct information on pocket geometry, residue orientation, and key recognition interactions. Therefore, it offers a reliable structural basis for screening compounds targeting the endonuclease repair function of APE1.

The co-crystallized reference ligand (5-nitroindole-2-carboxylic acid) was found to be well accommodated within the catalytic pocket of APE1, exhibiting a stable binding mode governed by a combination of polar and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 2A). Specifically, the ligand forms two key hydrogen bonds with GLN-137 and SER-164, while simultaneously establishing a salt bridge with ARG-136, which likely plays a critical role in anchoring the ligand within the active site. In addition, extensive hydrophobic contacts with residues PHE-162, PHE-165, LEU-318, ILE-91, and LEU-62 further contribute to the stabilization of the ligand by shaping a complementary nonpolar environment. Together, these interactions define a well-organized binding microenvironment that is essential for ligand recognition and retention. Based on these structural insights, the identified key residues were incorporated into the interaction fingerprint (IFP)-based virtual screening workflow to improve the structural and biological relevance of screened candidates.

To validate the reliability of the docking protocol, a redocking experiment was performed using the co-crystallized ligand from the APE1 complex (PDB ID: 7TC2). The predicted binding pose exhibited a high degree of agreement with the crystallographic conformation, with an RMSD of 0.4851 Å (Fig. 2B), which is significantly lower than the commonly accepted threshold of 2.0 Å. This result demonstrates that the adopted docking parameters and grid definition are capable of accurately reproducing the experimentally observed binding mode. Therefore, the docking protocol was considered robust and reliable, providing a solid foundation for subsequent large-scale virtual screening and downstream structure-based drug design efforts.

Hierarchical virtual screening workflow and hit prioritization

A hierarchical virtual screening workflow was established to improve the reliability and structural relevance of hit identification (Fig. 3). A total of 1,529,230 compounds collected from DrugBank, TargetMol, Specs, and ChemDiv were initially subjected to physicochemical filtering based on Lipinski’s rule of five, excluding DrugBank and TargetMol subsets due to their intrinsic drug-likeness. The remaining compounds were then evaluated by molecular docking, and only those with binding affinities better than − 5.4 kcal/mol (reference ligand threshold) were retained. To further refine the screening, an interaction fingerprint (IFP)-based filter was applied, requiring candidates to reproduce at least one key interaction observed in the reference complex, including hydrogen bonds with GLN-137 or SER-164, a salt bridge with ARG-136, or hydrophobic contacts with PHE-162, LEU-318, ILE-91, LEU-62, or PHE-165. No single interaction was considered mandatory; compounds satisfying any of these key recognition patterns were retained to preserve chemical diversity while maintaining structural relevance. After this step, 1280 compounds were selected for subsequent analyses. Subsequently, conformational strain energy was assessed, and compounds with strain energy lower than 10 kcal/mol were selected33. Finally, MM/GBSA binding free energy calculations were performed to prioritize the top 100 candidates. Compared to conventional single-step docking ranking, this multi-layered strategy integrates binding interactions, conformational feasibility, and energetic stability, thereby significantly enhancing the robustness and credibility of the screening outcomes.

As shown in Fig. 4, statistical analysis indicated substantial differences in the contribution of each database to the final hits. ChemDiv provided the largest number of candidates, reflecting its high chemical diversity. Compounds from the TargetMol natural product library generally exhibited better docking scores, suggesting favorable spatial compatibility of natural product scaffolds with the APE1 active pocket. Molecules from the SPECS library showed overall better MM/GBSA performance, whereas DrugBank offered compounds with potential repurposing value.

The best-performing compounds from each database and their predicted ADMET-related properties are shown in Fig. 5A; Table 1. Among these candidates, T9286 exhibited the most favorable docking score (− 8.376 kcal/mol), whereas HIT107168463 showed the most favorable MM/GBSA binding free energy (− 72.35 kcal/mol). All four compounds possessed moderate molecular weights (268–398 Da) and acceptable lipophilicity (AlogP 3.5–4.6), indicating generally suitable drug-like characteristics. Predicted aqueous solubility (QPlogS) values were within an acceptable range, with T9286 showing relatively better solubility. Regarding safety-related parameters, all compounds displayed QPlogHERG values above the major risk threshold, suggesting manageable predicted hERG liability. In addition, AN-652/42917906 and DB02187 showed excellent predicted Caco-2 permeability (> 1000 nm/s), while T9286 also demonstrated favorable membrane permeability. All compounds exhibited high predicted human oral absorption (87.9–100%), supporting their potential for oral exposure.

In addition, structural similarity analysis between the identified hits and previously reported APE1 inhibitors (Fig. 5B, CRT0044876 and 5-nitroindole-2-carboxylic acid) revealed generally low Tanimoto coefficients (Fig. 5C, 0.08–0.16), suggesting that these compounds may represent chemically distinct scaffolds. Overall, these representative compounds effectively occupied the APE1 active site, although differences were observed in the balance and anchoring patterns of their interaction networks (Table 2).

As shown in Fig. 6A–D, all four representative compounds adopt well-defined binding conformations within the APE1 active pocket, stabilized by a combination of hydrophobic and polar interactions. In particular, AN-652/42917906 (Fig. 8A) forms extensive hydrophobic contacts with PHE-162, ILE-91, GLN-137, ASP-90, and LEU-62, while simultaneously establishing multiple hydrogen bonds with PHE-165, GLN-137, SER-164, ASP-90, and ARG-136, resulting in a dense interaction network that strongly stabilizes the ligand. Similarly, DB02187 (Fig. 8B) exhibits broad hydrophobic interactions involving PHE-162, ILE-91, GLN-137, LEU-318, ASP-90, and LEU-62, accompanied by key polar interactions with SER-164, ASP-90, ARG-136, and GLN-137, suggesting favorable binding affinity within the catalytic pocket.

HIT107168463 (Fig. 8C) maintains stable binding through conserved hydrophobic contacts with PHE-162, ILE-91, GLN-137, ASP-90, and LEU-62, and further reinforces binding via multiple hydrogen bonds with ASP-163, GLN-137, SER-164, ASP-90, and ARG-136. Notably, T9286 (Fig. 8D) displays a comparable interaction pattern, featuring strong hydrophobic contacts with PHE-162, PHE-165, ILE-91, GLN-137, and LEU-62, together with a well-defined hydrogen-bonding network involving SER-164, ASP-90, ARG-136, and GLN-137. In addition, π–π stacking interactions with PHE-165 further contribute to binding stabilization. Overall, these compounds consistently reproduce the key interaction motifs observed in the reference complex, highlighting their favorable binding characteristics toward APE1.

Molecular dynamics evaluation of representative complexes

Energetic decomposition and free energy landscape analysis

As shown in Fig. 9A, per-residue energy decomposition revealed distinct interaction patterns among the five complexes. In the reference system, SER162 and LEU316 exhibited the strongest contributions, indicating their critical roles in ligand stabilization. AN-652/42917906 displayed a similar interaction profile to the reference, involving key residues such as LEU62, PHE160, and SER162, although with slightly weaker overall contributions. In contrast, DB02187 exhibited a more evenly distributed energy contribution across multiple residues, including LEU62, ASP90, and GLN135, suggesting the formation of a broader and more cooperative interaction network. HIT107168463 showed dispersed and relatively weak residue contributions, lacking a dominant interaction core, indicative of suboptimal binding. Notably, T9286 demonstrated strong contributions from residues such as LEU62, PHE160, and THR61, highlighting its ability to establish stable anchoring interactions through both hydrophobic and polar contacts.

Free energy landscape (FEL) analysis showed that all five systems displayed a single and relatively deep energy basin, indicating that they mainly occupied stable low-energy conformational regions during the simulations (Fig. 9B). Compared with the reference system, DB02187 and T9286 showed more concentrated lowest-energy regions, suggesting a stronger tendency to maintain a dominant binding conformation. HIT107168463 provides a representative example of the value of a multi-layer screening strategy: although it showed favorable docking and MM/GBSA performance in static evaluations, it failed to maintain comparable binding stability during molecular dynamics simulations. This discrepancy highlights that reliance on a single computational metric may overestimate certain candidates, whereas integrating docking, IFP filtering, strain energy, MM/GBSA, and MD enables more robust prioritization of truly promising compounds. In contrast, DB02187 was not necessarily the best according to every static metric, yet it displayed the best overall performance in terms of balanced residue interactions, dynamic stability, and concentrated free energy landscape distribution. T9286 highlights the unique value of natural product scaffolds in APE1 inhibitor development, with its stability being mainly driven by hydrophobic interactions and anchoring at key sites. These observations demonstrate the importance of integrating docking, IFP, strain energy, MM/GBSA, and MD into a multidimensional screening strategy for identifying truly promising APE1 inhibitor candidates.

Conclusion

In this study, a hierarchical structure-based virtual screening strategy was established for the APE1 endonuclease active pocket, and a set of potential APE1 inhibitor candidates was identified from approximately 1.529 million small molecules collected from the DrugBank, TargetMol, Specs, and ChemDiv databases. The combination of re-docking validation, key interaction fingerprint screening, conformational strain energy evaluation, and MM/GBSA calculations substantially improved the reliability of candidate selection. Binding mode analyses of representative hit compounds showed that these molecules could effectively interact with key residues in the APE1 active pocket. Further molecular dynamics simulations, per-residue energy decomposition, and free energy landscape analyses demonstrated that DB02187 and T9286 outperformed the reference ligand in terms of complex stability, key-site anchoring, and maintenance of dominant conformations, making them the two most promising candidates identified in this study. Among them, DB02187 showed the best overall performance, whereas T9286 highlighted the unique potential of natural product scaffolds in the development of APE1-targeted inhibitors.

In contrast, HIT107168463 exhibited favorable static binding scores but relatively weaker dynamic stability, underscoring the importance of integrating multiple complementary computational filters during hit prioritization. Nevertheless, as this study is based entirely on computational analyses, further biochemical, cellular, and selectivity experiments are required to validate the predicted activities of these candidate compounds. Overall, this study identified DB02187 and T9286 as computationally prioritized candidate compounds targeting the APE1 endonuclease pocket through an integrated virtual screening workflow. These findings provide a useful basis for future biochemical validation and lead optimization.
