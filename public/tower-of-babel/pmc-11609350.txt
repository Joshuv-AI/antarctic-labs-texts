Protection conferred by booster vaccine doses in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 during the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 epidemics from 2022 to 2023 in Greece
Authors: Helena C Maltezou, Maria N Gamaletsou, Maria Chini, Vasileios Petrakis, Vasiliki Rapti, Theodoros V Giannouchos, Eleni Karantoni, Konstantinos Kounouklas, Panagiota Stamou, Αmalia Karapanou, Dimitrios Basoulis, Andrianna-Chrysovalanto Verykokkou, Kyriakos Souliotis, Periklis Panagopoulos, Dimitrios Hatzigeorgiou, Garyfalia Poulakou, Konstantinos N Syrigos, Nikolaos V Sipsas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We assessed protection of COVID-19 vaccine doses in 962 hospitalized patients in 2022–2023.•Patients with ≥3 vaccine doses had lower odds of experiencing any adverse outcome.•Patients with 1–2 or ≥3 vaccine doses had decreased length of stay in hospital.•Older age, having ≥3 comorbidities, or admission ≥2 weeks post-diagnosis were associated with adverse outcomes.

Introduction

Although coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)-associated morbidity and mortality have decreased substantially since the emergence of the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) in late 2019, COVID-19 continues to burden society and healthcare facilities globally [[1], [2], [3]]. Its persistent morbidity has been primarily attributed to the emergence of new highly transmissible variants to which neither natural infection nor existing vaccines induce permanent immunity, as well as to low global COVID-19 vaccine uptake rates, even among individuals with high-risk comorbidities [4,5]. Our previous multicenter study conducted during the 2021–2022 season when the Delta (B.1.617.2) and Omicron (B.1.1.529) variants were dominant in Greece showed that a booster vaccine dose significantly protected against admission to an intensive care unit (ICU), invasive mechanical ventilation, in-hospital death, and prolonged hospitalization [6].

As SARS-CoV-2 becomes endemic, real-world studies are needed to estimate the severity of COVID-19 and the benefits conferred by booster vaccine doses during seasons when different subvariants are dominant. The findings of such studies may guide COVID-19 vaccination policies as well as the preparation of healthcare facilities during periods of peak healthcare demand and hospitalizations.

The present study aimed to describe the clinical course of patients admitted to hospitals with COVID-19 and estimate the protection conferred by booster doses during the Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 subvariant epidemics in 2022–2023 in Greece.

Methods

Study setting

This prospective observational study was conducted in Greece from November 14, 2022 (week 46/2022) to May 28, 2023 (week 21/2023), applying the same methodology as described in our previous study [6]. The following tertiary-care hospitals participated in the study: Laiko General Hospital (Athens), Sotiria General Hospital (Athens), Korgialeneio-Benakeio Red Cross Hospital (Athens), 251 Hellenic Air Force General Hospital (military hospital in Athens), and University Hospital of Alexandroupolis (Alexandroupolis).

The SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2 and BA.5 subvariants dominated during the study period, with the former prevailing until week 6/2023 and the latter prevailing from week 7/2023 onwards [7]. Among BA.2 subvariants, BA.2.75 prevailed during the first half of the study period, while XBB1.5 dominated from week 7/2023 onwards [7]. Seasonal influenza activity extended from week 44/2022 to week 20/2023, with an early peak almost exclusively caused by influenza A/H3N2 and a second peak caused by influenza B [7].

Study population

Adult patients consecutively admitted with laboratory-confirmed COVID-19 were included. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years of age, had an asymptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection, or had healthcare-associated COVID-19.

Data collection

The following data were collected prospectively for each COVID-19 case: demographic characteristics, comorbidities (chronic cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, malignancy, obesity, chronic neuromuscular disorder, chronic pulmonary disease, and chronic renal disease), history of COVID-19 vaccination (including number, dates, and brand names of vaccine doses), history of SARS-CoV-2 infections in the past, history of seasonal influenza vaccination, date of onset of COVID-19-associated symptoms, date of COVID-19 diagnosis, virus co-infection, admission to ICU, invasive mechanical ventilation, duration of hospitalization, and outcome (discharge or in-hospital death). History of COVID-19 vaccination was retrieved from the national COVID-19 vaccination registry.

The following COVID-19 vaccines licensed by the European Medicines Agency were/are used in Greece: Comirnaty (Pfizer, Inc.; NY, USA), Spikevax (Moderna; MA, USA), Vaxzervria (AstraZeneca; United Kingdom), Janssen (Johnson & Johnson; Belgium), and Novavax (Novavax Inc.; MD, USA). The COVID-19 vaccination campaign in Greece started on December 28, 2020 [8], with Comirnaty introduced in late December 2020, Spikevax in January 2021, Vaxzevria in February 2021, Janssen in May 2021, and Novavax in March 2022 [8]. The distribution of vaccine doses in this study was as follows: Pfizer mRNA vaccine: 1550 doses (90.7%), Moderna: 111 doses (6.5%), AstraZeneca: 27 doses (1.6%), and Johnson & Johnson: 20 doses (1.2%). Since published evidence from large studies supports the use of heterologous booster doses [9,10], vaccine brands were not considered in our statistical analysis.

Definitions

COVID-19 cases were defined as patients with symptoms compatible with COVID-19 and a positive SARS-CoV-2 reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and/or rapid antigen detection test (RADT). Adverse outcomes included admission to ICU, invasive mechanical ventilation, and/or death. Healthcare-associated COVID-19 was defined as COVID-19 acquired during hospitalization in a patient whose cause of admission was not COVID-19. History of SARS-CoV-2 infection was defined as a laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection before the current COVID-19 illness. Virus co-infection was defined as laboratory-confirmed concomitant infection with SARS-CoV-2 and another respiratory virus [11]. Invasive mechanical ventilation was defined as the delivery of mechanical breaths under positive pressure from a mechanical ventilator to the lungs using an endotracheal tube. Length of stay was defined as the time elapsed from admittance to hospital to discharge. Booster COVID-19 vaccine doses refer to any vaccine dose administered beyond the primary vaccination series.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis was conducted to characterize the overall study population, further stratified by COVID-19 vaccine doses at the time of the encounter (no vaccination, one to two doses, or three or more doses). We further conducted similar analyses across all collected demographic, clinical, and contextual variables to separately assess unadjusted differences between individuals experiencing any adverse outcome and the average in-hospital length of stay. We used chi-squared tests and analysis of variance or Kruskal Wallis tests for categorical and continuous variables, respectively, to assess statistically significant differences across subgroups. We then conducted univariate (unadjusted) and multivariable (adjusted) logistic regressions to estimate the association between the occurrence of any adverse outcome during hospitalization (ICU admission, invasive mechanical ventilation, or in-hospital mortality) with the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses at the time of admission. Similarly, we used univariate (unadjusted) and multivariable (adjusted) negative binomial regression models to estimate the association between in-hospital length of stay (days) with the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses at the time of admission, as the number of days in-hospital was expected to be right-skewed. Estimates of the negative binomial regression analyses were transformed using marginal effects to show differences in the average length of stay in days. Both multivariable models controlled for the factors and characteristics included in the descriptive analyses (age groups in years [18–59, 60–69, 70–79, or ≥ 80 years old], sex [male or female], number of comorbidities [none, one or two, or three or more], weeks for diagnosis to admission [within 1 week, 1 to 2 weeks, or 2 weeks or more], influenza vaccination [no or yes], and past SARS-CoV-2 infection [no or yes]) as well as hospital and month fixed effects to control for unobserved differences. Standard errors were clustered at the hospital level. Data were collected using Microsoft Excel and all statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical issues

The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of the hospitals involved in the study [approval numbers: 2218/27-01-2022 (Sotiria General Hospital), 684/27-10-2021 (Laiko General Hospital), 43561/2021 (University Hospital of Alexandroupolis), 665/21-01-2022 (Korgialeneio-Benakeio Red Cross Hospital), and 17/4003/9-12-21 (251 Hellenic Air Force General Hospital)]. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

During the 28-week study period, 962 adult patients (median age: 78 [interquartile range: 69–86] years) were consecutively admitted with COVID-19 in the participating hospitals for a mean of 9.2 (standard deviation [SD]: 8.4) days (Table 1). Of the 962 patients, 847 (88.0%) had at least one comorbidity. In terms of adverse outcomes, 39 (4.0%) of the 962 patients were admitted to ICU, 44 (4.6%) received invasive mechanical ventilation, and 110 (11.4%) died in hospital. Overall, 118 (12.3%) of the 962 patients with COVID-19 had at least one adverse outcome.

Of the 962 patients with COVID-19, 184 (19.1%) were unvaccinated against COVID-19, 125 (13.0%) had received 1–2 vaccine doses, and 653 (67.9%) had received ≥ 3 doses. Patients who had received ≥ 3 vaccine doses were older compared with unvaccinated patients and patients who had received 1–2 vaccine doses (mean ages: 76.4, 73.7, and 74.0 years, respectively; p = 0.009), had higher influenza vaccination rates (40.1%, 25.0%, and 32.0%, respectively; p < 0.001), were less likely to have a past SARS-CoV-2 infection (9.5%, 18.5%, and 20.0%, respectively; p < 0.001), and were more frequently admitted to hospital within ≤ 1 week after COVID-19 diagnosis (75.7%, 63.0%, and 68.8%, respectively; p = 0.012).

Table 2 shows the descriptive differences between individuals with adverse outcomes during hospitalization and those without, as well as the univariate and multivariable logistic regression estimates on the association between the occurrence of any adverse outcome during hospitalization with the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses and other covariates at the time of admission. Compared with those who did not experience any adverse outcome, individuals ≥ 80 years (42.2% versus 61.0%, respectively; p < 0.001), those ≥ 3 comorbidities (34.2% versus 38.1%, p = 0.022), and those admitted 2 weeks or more after their diagnosis (5.2% versus 11.9%; p = 0.011) were more likely to experience any adverse outcome during hospitalization. No statistically significant differences were observed between the three categories of vaccination status and adverse outcome incidence in descriptive and unadjusted regression analyses. After controlling for baseline characteristics, the multivariable regression estimates indicated that patients who had received ≥ 3 vaccine doses had lower odds of experiencing any adverse outcome during hospitalization compared with unvaccinated patients (adjusted odds ratio [aOR]: 0.57; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.37–0.86).

Table 3 shows the descriptive differences across various characteristics and factors in the average length of hospital stay, as well as the univariate and multivariable negative binomial regression estimates on the association between length of stay with the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses and other covariates at the time of admission. Compared with those aged 18 to 59 years, all other age groups exhibited longer average length of stay, with those ≥ 80 years experiencing almost five more days of hospitalization (5.9 versus 10.6 days, respectively; p < 0.001). Having at least one comorbidity was also an indicator of longer length of stay, with those having ≥ 3 comorbidities exhibiting more than four days of additional stay compared with those with no reported comorbidity (11.2 versus 6.8 days; p < 0.001). Individuals who were admitted to the hospital 2 weeks or more after their diagnosis had about 4 additional days of hospital stay compared with those who were admitted within one week from their diagnosis (13.0 versus 9.1 days; p = 0.001). Those who had a previous SARS-CoV-2 infection had a lower length of stay by about 2.7 days compared with those who did not (p < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between the three categories of vaccination status and length of stay in descriptive and unadjusted regression analyses. However, after controlling for baseline characteristics and differences, COVID-19 vaccination was significantly associated with shorter length of stay; on average, individuals who had received 1–2 vaccine doses had about 1.5 fewer days of in-hospital stay (95% CI: −2.6 to −0.4) and those with ≥ 3 doses had about 2.8 fewer days of in-hospital stay (95% CI: −4.1 to −1.4) compared with unvaccinated individuals.

Beyond the number of COVID-19 vaccine doses, compared with patients 18–59 years old, patients aged 60–69, 70–79 years, and ≥ 80 years had a significantly increased risk for any adverse outcome (aOR [95% CI]: 5.14 [1.27–20.71], 5.53 [1.55–19.76], and 9.13 [2.60–32.5], respectively) and prolonged length of stay (margins in days [95% CI]: 3.0 [1.8–4.3], 3.5 [2.5–4.6], and 4.7 [2.9–6.6], respectively). Compared with patients with no comorbidities, patients with 1–2 or ≥ 3 comorbidities had higher odds of any adverse outcome (aOR [95% CI]: 2.65 [1.13–6.27] and 2.64 [1.25–5.57], respectively), and patients with ≥ 3 comorbidities had significantly prolonged hospitalization (margins in days [95% CI]: 2.7 [0.6–4.9]). Compared with patients admitted ≤ 1 week after COVID-19 diagnosis, patients admitted 1–2 weeks after diagnosis and those admitted ≥ 2 weeks after diagnosis had higher odds of any adverse outcome (aOR [95% CI]: 1.53 [1.32–1.79] and 2.11 [1.58–2.81], respectively) while patients with a history of past SARS-CoV-2 infection were hospitalized for about one fewer day (margins [days]: 1.0 [95% CI: −2.0 to 0.0]).

Discussion

This was a multicenter, prospective study conducted to estimate the protection conferred by booster vaccine doses in hospitalized patients with COVID-19 in Greece from 2022 to 2023. Compared with being unvaccinated, receiving ≥ 3 doses of a COVID-19 vaccine was associated with decreased odds of experiencing any in-hospital adverse outcomes. As has been shown in previous studies [6,12], a history of any vaccine dose shortened the length of stay compared with unvaccinated patients. Both findings were significant after adjusting for hospital and month fixed effects and individual level covariates, particularly given the observed association of older age, multimorbidity, time from diagnosis to admission, and previous SARS-CoV-2 infection with adverse outcomes and longer length of stay; these characteristics were also correlated with vaccination receipt. This highlights the importance of controlling for baseline differences when estimating vaccine effectiveness. Our findings have implications for COVID-19 vaccination policies to protect patients and healthcare facilities during periods of peak healthcare demand and hospitalizations.

The profile of patients hospitalized with COVID-19 changed over the past two seasons in terms of age (mean age: 76.6 years in 2022–2023 versus 66.6 years in 2021–2022) and comorbidities (88% versus 72%, respectively) [6]. The cause for the aforementioned trends can be mainly attributed to the fact that most individuals have some level of protection against COVID-19, particularly against severe disease and death, because of past asymptomatic or undiagnosed infection and/or past COVID-19 vaccination [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]]. The shift from a mean age of 66.6 (2021–2022) to 76.6 years (2022–2023) indicates a need for vaccine prioritization for those ≥ 70 years of age. The timing of booster vaccination should also be considered to sustain vaccine-induced immunity [[19], [20], [21]]. Advanced age and comorbidities have been consistently identified as independent factors for adverse outcomes since the onset of the pandemic [6,[22], [23], [24]], which was also noted in our study. However, despite the more vulnerable profile of COVID-19 patients in 2022–2023 compared with those in 2021–2022, more favorable outcomes were recorded (4.0% admission to ICU, 4.6% invasive mechanical ventilation, 11.4% in-hospital deaths, and 12.3% with ≥ 1 adverse outcome) compared with the past season (6.6% admission to ICU, 5.6% invasive mechanical ventilation, 15.1% in-hospital deaths; and 18.0% with ≥ 1 adverse outcome) [6].

Of concern is the fact that one in every four patients with COVID-19 was admitted more than 1 week after diagnosis. Time from diagnosis to admission was an independent factor for increased risk for adverse outcomes. SARS-CoV-2 diagnostic tests are widely available in pharmacies and private laboratories in Greece. Since most of our patients were older than 75 years with several comorbidities, barriers to accessing healthcare services and medical evaluation attendance despite a positive SARS-CoV-2 test cannot be ruled out. Further studies are needed to elucidate the socioeconomic status of patients currently hospitalized with COVID-19 in Greece. Overall, strategies to reach this age group of patients early in their course of illness should be investigated.

The present study is potentially subject to certain limitations. The small number of cases admitted to ICU, on invasive mechanical ventilation, and those who succumbed to the disease did not allow us to estimate the impact of COVID-19 vaccination for each adverse outcome separately or by SARS-CoV-2 subvariant. Additionally, specific anti-COVID-19 treatment was not considered in the analysis, though all patients were treated upon admission. Our findings also may not apply to populations outside Greece or during different seasons. We were not able to control for other variables that might be independently associated with the outcomes of the study because of data availability, meaning that unobserved confounding factors are also possible. The findings of our study are also only able to indicate associations, not direct causal relationships, between vaccine doses and outcomes because of the study design.

In summary, receipt of ≥ 3 COVID-19 vaccine doses significantly reduced the likelihood of adverse outcomes among patients hospitalized with COVID-19 during the 2022–2023 season in Greece; a history of ≥ 1 vaccine dose was associated with a significant reduction in hospital stay length. The profile of patients hospitalized with COVID-19 in Greece changed over the last two seasons; patients admitted during 2022–2023 were significantly older and more often had comorbidities compared with those admitted during 2021–2022. Despite this, more favorable outcomes were recorded in 2022–2023 compared with the past season. Overall, older adults, patients with comorbidities, and those admitted ≥ 1 week after diagnosis were highly vulnerable to severe COVID-19 morbidity and outcomes. Our findings may guide COVID-19 vaccination policies to protect patients and healthcare systems.
