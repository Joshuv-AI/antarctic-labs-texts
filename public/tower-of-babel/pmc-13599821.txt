Perspectives on sex and gender inclusion in clinical practice guidelines: a qualitative study
Authors: Humaira Maheen, Jennifer Ervin, Marya Yenita Sitohang, Tania King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionClinical practice guidelines (CPGs) provide standardised direction for the diagnosis, management and treatment of medical conditions. However, sex and gender, critical determinants of health, are often inconsistently incorporated within guideline development. This study explored expert opinions on how sex and gender are currently considered in CPGs, the barriers to meaningful integration and practical strategies to strengthen equity-focused guideline development.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 28 participants representing clinical practice, sex and gender research, public health and health and advocacy organisations. A framework analysis approach was used for data analysis.FindingsTwo overarching challenges were identified as limiting the integration of sex and gender in CPGs: (1) persistent evidence gaps, including limited clinical data disaggregated by sex and gender and (2) politicisation of terminology, which undermines clarity and consistency in guideline processes. Participants highlighted the need for stronger evidence stratified by sex and gender, clear reporting standards, inclusive and appropriate language and greater representation of women and gender-diverse people on guideline committees. An intersectional approach was viewed as essential to ensure guidelines recognise structural, cultural and social contexts that shape differing health needs.ConclusionThis study outlines practical, internationally relevant actions to improve the integration of sex and gender into CPGs. Strengthening evidence, reporting standards and inclusivity within guideline processes provides a clear pathway to enhance clinical quality, improve equity and better support diverse populations.Keywords: Clinical Decision-Making, Health Education, Guideline Adherence

Introduction

Sex and gender are fundamental determinants of health.1 Sex and gender differences in health arise from complex interactions across multiple domains, including genetics, epigenetics, hormones, immune function, neurocognition, vascular biology and behaviour, and are not simply related to chromosomes or reproductive organs.2–4 These domains create sex-specific and gender-specific patterns in disease susceptibility, ageing and physiological processes across the life course.5 6 They also shape differential responses to diagnostics and therapeutics, underscoring the need for sex-informed and gender-informed medicine.5 7 Despite growing evidence of sex and gender differences in health and clinical outcomes more generally, differences remain inconsistently integrated into research, policy and clinical practice. This risks undermining the effectiveness of health interventions and perpetuating inequities across population groups.

Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) provide standardised guidance for the diagnosis and treatment of medical conditions. Based on scientific evidence, guideline developers evaluate the benefits and harms of diagnostic tests and treatments and provide recommendations accordingly. Several recent reviews on sex and gender representation in clinical settings show that these terms are not well incorporated into most guidelines.8–13 These reviews also identified substantial inconsistencies in the reporting of sex and gender, with terms often used interchangeably and sex and gender differences often omitted from guidelines. The limited inclusion of sex and gender considerations in clinical guidelines negatively impacts the health and well-being of groups across the population, particularly women.7 For example, the historical reliance of cardiovascular disease (CVD) guidelines on male data has been shown to negatively affect female clinical treatment protocols. This includes female patients with CVD being more likely to receive less aggressive treatment and fewer diagnostic and interventional procedures compared with males.14 15 Similarly, in non-small cell lung cancer, evidence has largely been generated from male populations due to historically higher smoking prevalence among males, resulting in screening criteria and treatment recommendations misaligned with females’ risk profiles.16 Another example is the CVD risk in the transgender population, who are shown to have a higher risk of myocardial infarction and CVD risk factors compared with cisgender men and women.17 18 These examples demonstrate the importance of including sex and gender in clinical guidelines and highlight the implications of their exclusion.

In Australia, clear guidance exists to promote equity in CPGs; however, current evidence indicates gaps in sex-inclusion and gender-inclusion within these resources.10 Drawing on experts’ insights, this study aimed to scope perspectives on the inclusion and exclusion of sex and gender in CPGs, as well as perceived barriers and strategies for better incorporating sex and gender considerations for guideline developers.

Methods

Participant recruitment

Participants were recruited through multiple sources. First, we sought assistance from two advisory panels (one scientific and one clinical) at the Centre for Health Equity, University of Melbourne, who shared our study invitation and information sheet (a plain-language summary of the project) with relevant or potential research participants. Interested individuals contacted the research team directly to confirm their participation. the study invitation and information sheet (a plain-language summary of the project) with relevant or potential participants. Interested individuals contacted the research team directly to confirm their participation. Second, we identified and directly contacted clinicians, clinical guideline developers, and experts from the field of sex and gender studies providing them with study information (a plain-language summary of the project) and a consent form. Within this recruitment strategy, we sought to ensure representation from individuals who either identified as First Nations, or gender-diverse/transgender or had experience working with people with disabilities and neurodivergent individuals to ensure intersectional representation within the study. Third, we used snowball sampling, asking participants who had already agreed to take part to identify and suggest other potentially eligible experts, to whom they forwarded the study details. Individuals who were interested then contacted the research team for participation.

Recruitment occurred between 1 July 2025 and 5 September 2025. We reached out to 41 potential participants; of them, 28 participated in interviews. Data collection

All participants were interviewed online by two researchers (HM and TK). Interviews were typically about 30–60 min and were transcribed verbatim using Zoom recording and transcription features. The researchers checked transcripts for accuracy by listening to audio recordings while cross-checking transcripts. The interview guide was informed by the existing literature on sex and gender bias in CPGs (see online supplemental file 1 - Interview guide) and sought to elicit participants’ perspectives on:

Data analysis

For qualitative analysis, we used Ritchie and Spencer’s framework analysis,19 a commonly used approach in applied policy research. Framework analysis involves five interconnected stages: familiarisation, identifying a thematic framework, indexing, charting and mapping and interpretation19 (p 178). Given the applied nature of the study and the need for both inductive and deductive approaches to coding and analysis, this method was particularly suitable.19 20

Two researchers (HM and JE) independently coded half of the interviews during the familiarisation stage and collaboratively developed a preliminary coding framework. These codes were created using both inductive coding, based on participants’ perspectives, and deductive coding, grounded in the current evidence on how CPGs are developed in Australia. These concepts helped structure the coding framework and ensured that key areas relevant to the study objectives were systematically examined. This framework was then applied to all 28 transcripts (indexing). Through an iterative process, the research team then developed themes and charted the data (see online supplemental file 2 - Coding framework). The final stage, mapping and interpretation, involved collaborative team discussions to refine the themes and ensure clarity. Data saturation was not formally assessed. NVivo (V.15) was used to support data management and analysis.21

Researchers’ reflexivity

Reflexivity was actively maintained throughout the study, including during analysis and writing, through documentation and discussion. Although some participants were known to the interviewers, this familiarity facilitated rapport and open dialogue. None of the participants had a direct supervisory or team relationship that could create power imbalances. The research team did not include a clinical practitioner, which helped minimise potential conflicts arising from personal clinical experiences or preferences. The coding process was iterative, and two researchers independently coded all interviews to reduce individual bias (see online supplemental file 2). The final analytic framework was developed collaboratively through team discussions, refinement of the coding structure and incorporation of all team members’ perspectives.

Positionality statement

The research team’s backgrounds and expertise shaped the framing, conduct and interpretation of this work. HM’s work explores the intersection of gender and culture, and how this shapes health for both men and women. JE’s research centres on gender as a social determinant of health. MYS brings expertise in identifying interventions to reduce gender inequity and improve health outcomes. TK’s research examines how gender, gender norms and gendered processes, including bias, influence health outcomes. We also acknowledge that our position as a women-only research team may have shaped the research process, for example, in how participants engaged with the topic depending on the perceived gender of the interviewer, and in which themes we were more readily attuned to during coding. To mitigate this, we drew on our collective and varied expertise across masculinity, culture and care to ensure the coding framework reflected the full range of participant viewpoints. We sought to remain attentive to the risk of overemphasising or underemphasising gender-related findings given this shared disciplinary focus, including through discussion within the team during analysis.

Patient and public involvement statement

The research question was initially defined in response to the funding agency’s priorities. Following funding approval, individuals with lived experience relevant to the study objective were engaged to review and refine the final research question to ensure its relevance and appropriateness. People with lived experience reviewed and provided feedback on the interview guidelines before data collection. Their input informed the wording, sequencing and framing of questions. A guideline framework was produced as a key research output and developed in consultation with lived experience contributors. The funding agency will make the framework accessible and disseminate it to relevant stakeholders. The present paper reports the qualitative findings that informed the development of this framework.

Results

All participants demonstrated a clear understanding of sex as a biological construct and gender as a social construct, and they recognised the critical importance of including them in clinical guidelines. We identified two critical themes that informed our research questions.

Theme 1: challenges/constraints that undermine the integration of sex and gender in CPGs

Insufficient evidence constrains treatment guidance

Participants consistently framed the lack of sex-disaggregated and gender-disaggregated evidence as a structural barrier to producing credible and equitable CPGs. They argued that the dominance of sex-neutral evidence limits guideline developers’ capacity to produce differentiated recommendations, particularly in areas where biological and social differences are clinically relevant. Some participants linked this gap to systemic biases within research design, highlighting that the historical exclusion of women from clinical trials resulted in limited evidence on sex-specific diagnosis, prognosis and treatment. Others argued that even in studies where women are included in the sample, the results are often not stratified by sex, limiting their usefulness for sex-specific recommendations within CPGs.

Evidence to decision framework really runs on the strength of recommendations, which is linked to the strength of evidence, so until the evidence is (stratified by sex) […] there is not sufficient power […]to implement sex-specific recommendations. (participant 1, guideline developer)

Sex and gender are part of personalised care. We […] need to stop treating sex as a covariate in a regression equation and analyse the data separately for males and females. There is a need for global recognition of these differences, and they will only be addressed if they’re acknowledged. (participant 2, clinician)

Some participants argued that over the past decade, sex inclusion has become less of a problem, with increasing numbers of clinical studies improving inclusivity of both males and females. They attributed this to updated requirements by some funding agencies and because some journals now insist on reporting sex-specific results. They suggested that in some cases, the roadblock to better inclusion of sex and gender considerations may not be related to a lack of current evidence, but rather due to the fact that CPGs need to be updated more regularly in line with new evidence. Despite better inclusion of sex considerations, a key evidence gap remains in gender-stratified results, because gender inclusion is still not a standard practice in clinical studies.

I do not agree that there is a universal lack of evidence on sex and gender […]. I think looking back, we were pretty bad at this, but I do not think that is the state of play for most things nowadays. It is true that 10 or 20 years ago, most clinical studies included men only, but that is no longer the case. [Although] I am not sure if the guidelines have caught up, the studies definitely have. I think it is time to get over the idea that we are not studying people appropriately—if anybody’s understudied, it’s trans populations, pregnant women, and children. (participant 3, clinician)

These gaps can result in diagnostic uncertainty, where clinicians sometimes feel unsure when making treatment decisions for certain gender-related health conditions. For example, one expert in trans health explained how limited research on how hormone therapy interacts with autoimmune conditions directly impacts the quality of care for trans patients.

In my work, particularly with trans populations undergoing hormone replacement therapy (HRT), it is clear how essential inclusive research is. For instance, lupus is more commonly diagnosed in people assigned female at birth. But when working with trans women or trans men undergoing HRT, questions often arise, such as whether shifting hormone levels could influence autoimmune conditions like lupus. Unfortunately, we do not yet have clear answers, because the research has not been done. (participant 4, clinician)

Participants also consistently highlighted the lack of precise mechanisms for operationalising sex and gender considerations in clinical practice. While there is broad willingness to improve the quality of data, some health professionals expressed scepticism about the utility of incorporating sex and gender, particularly in cases where they perceive these constructs as adding little clinical value. Others argued that this scepticism largely stems from the limited evidence base. Ultimately, this creates a problematic cycle: the absence of evidence weakens the willingness to act, and the lack of action perpetuates the evidence gap.

Politicisation erodes clarity and consistency in guideline development

Another barrier identified by participants was the frequent politicisation of sex and gender. Debates over equality and women’s rights often spill over into healthcare. One participant highlighted how political tactics and misinformation, including framing trans inclusion as a threat to women’s rights, create confusion and resistance among both the public and clinicians. They noted that clinicians are not immune to the influence of media narratives, which can generate anxiety and suspicion when health systems introduce changes such as recording gender in electronic health records. This underscores how politicisation can derail evidence-based progress by shaping perceptions and creating resistance to change.

The attempt to kind of split… let us get women worried that trans women are taking away their rights, like, that’s just a tactic. To actually oppress both sides. (participant 5, clinician)

Other participants noted the broader effects of politicisation on trans and gender-diverse health, describing the measurable mental health consequences of exposure to anti-trans rhetoric:

Exposure to anti-trans discrimination in the news media, social media, etc., strongly suggested an association with higher risk of anxiety, depression, self-harm, and suicidality. (participant 6, gender and sex researcher)

Such politicisation is also visible at the policy level, where structural exclusion undermines access to care. Taken together, these accounts demonstrate that politicisation permeates all levels of the system, from abstract debates over equality to clinical stereotypes in guidelines to discriminatory rhetoric and policies. This entanglement of politics and healthcare undermines the consistent and evidence-based inclusion of sex and gender in CPGs.

Theme 2: enablers that drive systematic integration of sex and gender in CPGs

Embedding sex and gender in evidence reviews strengthens guideline credibility

The Australian CPG development process is guided by the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. GRADE provides a structured and transparent approach to guideline development, ensuring that recommendations are based on the best available evidence and that decision-making processes are explicit, systematic and reproducible. The framework consists of two components: evidence review and the evidence-to-decision component. In order to improve the integration of sex and gender considerations into CPGs, participants highlighted that the evidence review stage serves as a critical facilitator.

You review evidence using GRADE principles—imprecision, inconsistency, and risk of bias—applied to the body of evidence. When you move into the evidence-to-decision phase, you sit with your committees to review the collated evidence, considering feasibility, applicability, and generalisability. You look at the evidence with experts, people with lived experience, and those to whom the guideline is relevant. That’s where the opportunity lies for including sex and gender considerations. These are important not just when looking for evidence—trials do not necessarily report male-female differences, and the evidence base may take time to mature—but also in the first instance, we can start with the evidence-to-decision. (participant 1, guideline developer)

Participants emphasised that by systematically incorporating sex and gender from the beginning, such as through explicit keyword strategies and tailoredProblem/Population, Intervention, Compairson, and Outcome (PICO) questions, evidence reviews are more likely to obtain evidence that supports inclusivity. This, in turn, ensures that the evidence informing the CPG recommendations is comprehensive and representative. This approach also enables the lack of evidence to be acknowledged in the evidence-to-decision component.

Institutional accountability and clear processes embed consistency

Most of the participants argued that sex and gender inclusion should be made mandatory across all major funding agencies. Although some agencies already offer guidance, a more direct and enforceable approach is required to ensure consistent compliance. Similar expectations should apply within academic publishing. Participants suggested that journals and publishers adopt mandatory requirements for the inclusion and reporting of sex and gender across disciplines. Embedding such conditions within both funding and publication processes would help close persistent evidence gaps and strengthen understanding of how sex and gender shape health outcomes.

That is where funding bodies can help. They can say, as part of the grant criterion, that you need to look at male vs female responses and whether the intervention differs by sex. Some journals are starting to do that, too—like how they now require explicit involvement of lived experience. (participant 13, clinician)

In the US and some other countries, it is now mandatory to include a certain percentage of women in trials, or it can affect funding. They do not require equal numbers, but representation proportionate to prevalence. For example, hypertension is more prevalent in women, so hypertension studies should have more women than men. (participant 12, clinician)

Participants also noted that to support the inclusion of sex and gender in CPGs, all parts of the health system need to work together. This includes health information systems, most of which currently do not capture both sex and gender, undermining data quality and limiting clinical inclusiveness. Participants emphasised that routinely collecting sex at birth alongside preferred gender would strengthen the accuracy and relevance of patient records and support clinicians and healthcare staff to deliver respectful patient-centred care consistently.

When you update your patient information system, it helps your clinical guidelines, because if you do not update it, the system will always keep it as male or female. So, it becomes a system failure, not a human failure. The barrier is changing the system to allow sex at birth and preferred gender—you cannot have a preferred sex. Having this in your policies makes it easier for clinicians to ask, ‘What is your preferred gender?’ and it’s especially important for reception staff, who are the first point of contact. (participant 10, public health expert)

Inclusive committees and capacity building strengthen equity and expertise

The composition of guideline committees was identified as a critical factor in developing inclusive CPGs. Women’s representation was specifically highlighted, noting that in Australia, current the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) recommendations advise a minimum of 40% female membership. While participants acknowledged that some guideline groups achieve this benchmark, they noted that this was not consistently the case. They recommended that guideline developers justify when this participation level is not met, including what efforts they made to achieve it, particularly in fields where women are known to have substantial expertise. One participant also noted a lack of clarity about including people from gender-diverse populations on these committees, which may explain why their representation has not been well operationalised to date.

NHMRC now have a requirement for 40% of committees to be women, but it does not specify anything about transgender people. The trans and gender-diverse (TGD) individuals and those with lived experience are in the TGD category. This way, the committee composition remains at least 40% women and 40% men, and the TGD group has its own category. However, this has not been fully reflected in the makeup of the committees. (participant 14, guideline developer)

Participants involved in guideline development committees described structural barriers that limit committee diversity. Development teams often depend on external stakeholders, such as professional colleges and specialist organisations, to nominate members, which limits their control over the initial pool of candidates. In some cases, depending on the specialty, nomination lists could be overwhelmingly male, making it difficult to create a more representative group. Clear and early setting of representation standards was identified as a way to address this issue. This means that nomination boards are aware of the standards and are tasked from the outset to seek candidates from a diverse pool.

Committee diversity is constrained by nomination processes because we sometimes have to rely on colleagues and other organisations and have limited control over the pool. Setting expectations early—that the committee must meet representativeness requirements and asking nominators to consider this—can help. It is not just about fulfilling requirements but improving the quality and equity of the guideline. (participant 11, guideline developers)

To promote equity, participants also recommended including people from Indigenous and multicultural backgrounds, gender-diverse and transgender populations, people with lived experiences and, where appropriate, accounting for other intersections (such as disability and people living in rural areas). However, participants cautioned that achieving this may not always be feasible. They emphasised the importance of taking reasonable steps to integrate equity considerations, balancing what is required with what can be realistically achieved. In some cases, equity may be advanced by incorporating relevant research evidence, while in others, representation of lived experience may be necessary to ensure that diverse perspectives are adequately reflected in the guideline development process.

Language consideration in guideline development

There were some differences in opinions on how language pertaining to sex and gender within CPGs should be used. This likely reflects broader politics around sex and gender, and the politicisation of transgender rights, and there were mixed positions on this. Regarding clinical guidelines, some participants supported the use of gender-inclusive language but also wanted to ensure that women maintained clear recognition of their identity. The use of gender-neutral or organ-based terms was supported by some participants as being key to ensuring that the needs of the population were met, but there was a perceived need to be mindful that some patients experienced such language as depersonalising or diminishing their identity.

When gender is not [necessarily] relevant, we can use neutral language. But terminology like ‘chest feeding’ [instead of breast feeding] is tricky, as many women may want to identify as mothers. […] In other instances, simple alternatives, like using ‘antenatal care’ instead of ‘maternity care,’ can be used, which do not necessarily define specific genders—they are just different [more inclusive] terminology. (participant 7, gender and sex researcher)

The practicalities of guideline development were also described as a significant constraint. Participants noted that tight word limits, strict page requirements, accelerated timelines and limited training in gender-inclusive terminology create pressure that can hinder thoughtful integration of sex and gender. Even when intentions are strong, these structural demands can make it difficult to use inclusive language consistently. One participant pointed out that such practical burdens can undermine conceptual commitments to gender inclusivity.

It was suggested that those developing guidelines should use a standard glossary of sex and gender terms, and that guideline developers be encouraged to adopt and employ gender-inclusive terminology. Some participants mentioned that this issue could be addressed by including a gender expert on the guideline development committee. While this advice is valuable, it raises questions about practicality—specifically, the feasibility of having a gender expert on every guideline committee.

Adopting intersectional approaches broadens the scope of integration

Participants noted that focusing on sex or gender in isolation risks oversimplifying complex health patterns and overlooking broader social influences. Sex and gender intersect with each other, as well as other identities. As an example, women’s higher rates of adverse drug reactions cannot be attributed solely to biology. Still, sex intersects with gendered patterns of healthcare use, prescribing practices and diagnostic bias to impact health outcomes.

One example comes from a […] study; they were interested in the fact that women are overrepresented in adverse drug outcomes. The assumption is that it’s because of androcentrism in drug safety testing, and that’s why we need sex-specific medicine. But they also point out cultural factors that could explain this disparity: women are more likely to utilise healthcare, more likely to be prescribed drugs, and may face bias and misdiagnosis. They might also be more likely to report adverse effects and have pre-existing social and structural vulnerabilities. So again, it is a way of not letting the pendulum swing too far, seeing bodies as purely ‘sexed bodies’. (participant 8, gender and sex researcher)

This example shows how sex-based interpretations can obscure the influence of gendered healthcare behaviours, clinical decision-making and structural vulnerabilities. A similar finding was reflected in discussions on trans health. While hormone therapy carries physiological considerations, participants stressed that social conditions such as minority stress also shape health in significant ways.

Gender-affirming hormones can have a huge impact on heart health, for example, but [so as] minority stress can, even if you are not on hormones. Being a trans person can also have an impact on your heart health. (participant 6, gender and sex researcher)

These insights point to a broader issue: when CPGs treat sex and gender as fixed categories, they risk overlooking how intersecting forms of marginalisation shape people’s experiences of health. Sex and gender also intersect with other identities such as race, disability and migration status to compound these experiences, influencing access to care, patterns of diagnosis and the quality of treatment offered. One participant highlighted this in relation to women with disabilities:

For women with disabilities, there is a stigma surrounding disability and assumptions about their sexuality or their ability to bear and care for children. As a result, women with disabilities encounter barriers in sexual and reproductive health, maternity care, and parenting support. Parents with disabilities, especially those with intellectual disabilities, are also more likely to have their children removed rather than receive adequate support. (participant 9, gender and sex researcher)

Within some rural Indigenous communities, some cultural avoidance practices specify the separation of men’s business and women’s business. This can make healthcare access particularly challenging for sister girls (trans women) and brother boys (trans men), who do not fit neatly into binary expectations.

In Indigenous clinics in (some) remote communities, it is males on one side and females on the other. But you now have sister girls and brother boys. Where do they fit? It gets complex because it has not been articulated (anywhere in CPG). One of my trans clients went to see a female GP and was told, ‘No, you are not female, you are actually male,’ and she said, ‘I’m a trans… I’m a sister girl, but this wasn’t seen. (participant 10, clinician and researcher)

Taken together, these accounts show that without an intersectional and personalised approach, CPGs risk reproducing simplified or incomplete understandings of health differences. Embedding sex and gender within broader equity frameworks is therefore vital, as it enables guidelines to better reflect the structural and social contexts that shape diverse population needs.

Discussion

This study is among the first to synthesise insights from experts to articulate the ongoing challenges of integrating sex and gender into CPGs. It explains why meaningful integration remains difficult and presents practical, evidence-based strategies to overcome these barriers. In doing so, the study responds to international reviews8–14 22 that have consistently highlighted the lack of sex and gender considerations in CPGs and offers clear pathways to strengthen health equity, enhance the quality of clinical care, and improve patient experiences.

Current guidelines lack sufficient integration of sex and gender considerations. This is despite widespread acknowledgement among experts of the necessity to incorporate these factors and a clear understanding of their definitions across various contexts. Our findings revealed two overarching barriers as central to this issue: persistent evidence gaps and the politicisation of sex and gender. These barriers are interlinked. When evidence is incomplete, inconsistently reported or absent, sex and gender considerations become more vulnerable to political contestation in clinical and policy settings. These dynamic risks entrench health inequities and limit CPGs’ ability to address population diversity.

Although sex-related reporting in some clinical evidence bases has improved over the past two decades,5 7 these advances have not been consistently translated into clinical guidelines. This means that even where relevant evidence now exists, many CPGs have not yet been updated to reflect it. At the same time, significant gaps in sex-related and gender-related evidence persist across many clinical areas. Addressing these gaps will require coordinated action across the research ecosystem, including stronger requirements from funding agencies and academic publishers23 to ensure sex-disaggregated and gender-disaggregated data are collected and reported.24 While some mechanisms are already in place, universal and consistent enforcement is still needed.25 26

This study highlights essential steps for enhancing the integration of sex and gender in CPGs. Although these recommendations were developed within the Australian context, they have clear global relevance and can serve as a benchmark for improving CPG development worldwide. (Online supplemental file 3 summarises our major findings along with recommendations to improve the guideline development process to better include sex and gender.

First, our findings emphasise the importance of the evidence review process, which underpins any guideline. Sex and gender considerations must be incorporated at every stage of this process, including the formulation of PICO questions, search strategies, outcome selection and reporting. When sex-disaggregated and gender-disaggregated data are not available, this should be explicitly documented to ensure guideline developers, clinicians and other users understand the limitations of the evidence base. Transparent reporting of these gaps is crucial for rigorous interpretation and guiding future research priorities.

Second, the composition of guideline development committees must be genuinely inclusive. This requires intentional efforts to ensure adequate representation of women,9 25 not merely in numerical terms but also in terms of expertise and decision-making influence. Current practices often rely on professional networks that tend to favour men or include women only to meet minimum requirements, rather than to draw on their substantive expertise. Where appropriate, inclusive committees should also incorporate people with lived experience and ensure representation from First Nations communities, gender-diverse groups, culturally and linguistically diverse populations and those living in rural and remote regions. Such diversity strengthens the relevance, legitimacy and equity of guideline recommendations.

Additionally, this study highlights the importance of language in communicating sex and gender in CPGs. Using gender-inclusive and additive terminology, such as women, men and gender-diverse people, tailored to the clinical context, is essential for clarity and for ensuring that guidelines remain relevant to all population groups affected by a condition. While the use of inclusive language guidelines for sex and gender (SAGER) has been adopted by some medical journals, significant gaps remain across the field.27 Including a gender expert on guideline committees, where feasible, may also strengthen the accuracy and appropriateness of language. Guideline and standards authorities could support this by mandating certain requirements.

Finally, it is also noteworthy that the politicisation of sex, gender and diversity and inclusion more broadly was noted by many respondents. Recent policy developments, particularly shifts in some parts of the world that emphasise binary sex categories or restrict the relevance of gender, pose significant risks to recent progress.28 These challenges reinforce the need for leadership from progressive countries to maintain momentum and ensure that sex and gender remain integral to evidence generation rather than reverting to narrow or exclusionary models.

Limitations

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting this analysis. Interviews were scheduled for 30–60 min, with some ending closer to 30 min to accommodate participants’ time-pressured schedules. These shorter interviews may have affected the depth of the discussion on some topics. Social desirability bias could also be a further limitation that cannot be fully eliminated in any face-to-face interview setting. However, we sought to mitigate that by assuring participants of the anonymity of their responses, the right to withdraw from participation (at any stage) and providing a collegial, non-judgmental interview style. We also used snowball sampling as a supplementary recruitment strategy, which carries some risk of participants nominating others with similar views; however, as this was not our main recruitment strategy, it is unlikely to have a substantial impact on our findings. Furthermore, since all our participants are experts from Australia, our findings are therefore best understood as reflective of the Australian context.

Our findings are shaped by the composition of the stakeholders interviewed, who included guideline developers, clinicians with diverse specialisations and academics. Participants’ views on sex and gender were diverse, with some regarding the issue primarily in terms of biological sex as a variable in clinical evidence, and others having more expansive conceptualisations of gender identity (and its relevance to health and clinical practice). This range reflects a broader tension in the field between the binary sex categories that dominate clinical datasets and guideline evidence, and calls for guidelines to better accommodate more expansive understandings of gender. Our sample of 28 participants does not, and cannot, represent the diversity of perspectives held within clinical practice (and society more broadly).

This has practical implications: recommendations that assume a more expansive framing of gender may face resistance or slower uptake among parts of the guideline development community (and clinical practice) still working within binary sex-based data conventions. At the same time, recommendations focused solely on binary sex categories risk perpetuating the exclusion of gender-diverse populations from clinical evidence. Future work engaging a broader stakeholder group may involve different perspectives. Guideline developers should therefore treat our recommendations as a starting point for these approaches. In addition, some issues raised extended beyond the study’s focus and were more broadly related to social determinants of health, which could not be examined in this paper. Despite these constraints, including both practice and policy stakeholders strengthens the relevance and applicability of the findings.

Conclusion

Integrating sex and gender into CPGs is both necessary and feasible. Practical steps to improve the inclusion of sex and gender in clinical practice guidelines identified in this study include strengthening evidence reviews, diversifying guideline committees and using inclusive language. While grounded in the Australian context, these recommendations may offer relevant insights for guideline developers internationally and provide a pathway to more equitable, higher-quality clinical guidelines.
