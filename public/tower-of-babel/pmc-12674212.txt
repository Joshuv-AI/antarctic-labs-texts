Two decades of FFAR4 biology: From nutrient sensing to therapeutic targeting
Authors: Yulin Kong, DaYoung Oh, Shenglong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYFree fatty acid receptor 4 (FFAR4; also known as GPR120) serves as a key lipid-sensing G-protein-coupled receptor that mediates the physiological actions of long-chain fatty acids, particularly omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids. Over the past two decades, studies of FFAR4 have revealed its pivotal role in metabolic regulation, inflammation resolution, and energy balance. This review integrates recent advances from structural biology, physiology, and translational research to provide an updated framework of FFAR4 biology. In particular, we highlight new cryo-electron microscopy-based insights into receptor activation and ligand recognition; the expanding roles of FFAR4 in non-metabolic systems, such as the central nervous system and kidney; and recent progress in the clinical development of selective FFAR4 agonists. By bridging molecular mechanisms with therapeutic translation, this review offers a comprehensive perspective on FFAR4’s functions in health and disease.

INTRODUCTION

The global rise in obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) has reached alarming levels. This escalating burden underscores an urgent need for therapeutic approaches targeting the underlying mechanisms of metabolic dysregulation. These disorders share a multifactorial pathophysiology characterized by impaired nutrient sensing, chronic inflammation, and disrupted energy homeostasis.

Free fatty acid receptor 4 (FFAR4; also known as GPR120) is a lipid-sensing G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) activated by long-chain fatty acids, particularly omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA).1 The receptor links dietary lipid composition to metabolic and immune regulation by coupling to Gαq/11 and β-arrestin-dependent pathways.2 FFAR4 is expressed across multiple metabolic and immune tissues, including the intestine, adipose tissue, liver, pancreatic islets, and macrophages, where it coordinates endocrine and inflammatory responses.3–5 In addition, FFAR4 signaling in the central nervous system (CNS) contributes to appetite control and behavioral regulation.6

To place FFAR4 in the broader context of lipid metabolism, we briefly compare it with other fatty-acid-sensing systems, including FFAR1 (also known as GPR40), cluster of differentiation 36 receptor (CD36), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs). While FFAR1 primarily mediates acute insulinotropic responses in pancreatic β cells7 and CD36 facilitates fatty acid uptake and taste perception, FFAR4 transduces lipid-derived signals that promote long-term metabolic adaptation.8–10 These receptors also intersect functionally with PPAR signaling pathways, reflecting an integrated network that coordinates cellular lipid utilization and storage.11–13 Furthermore, the temporal appearance and localization of omega-3 PUFAs near FFAR4-expressing cells depend on dietary intake, lipid absorption, and redistribution during inflammation and its resolution.

While several reviews have summarized canonical FFAR4 signaling,14–19 this work provides a distinct and updated perspective by integrating three complementary dimensions. First, it synthesizes recent advances in receptor structural biology, including cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM)-resolved receptor-ligand complexes and mechanisms of allosteric modulation. Second, it expands the scope of FFAR4 biology beyond metabolic regulation to include its emerging roles in neuroinflammation, gut-brain communication, renal function, and cancer. Third, it provides a critical evaluation of ongoing clinical studies of selective FFAR4 agonists, highlighting both their therapeutic promise and translational challenges, including altered receptor expression in obesity and limitations in omega-3 PUFA bioavailability. Together, these elements establish FFAR4 as a multifaceted metabolic sensor and a promising yet complex therapeutic target.

STRUCTURAL PLASTICITY OF FFAR4: BEYOND CANONICAL GPCR TOPOLOGY

FFAR4 belongs to the rhodopsin-like class A GPCR family and is located on human chromosome 10q23.33.20 The originally cloned human FFAR4 gene encodes a 377-amino-acid protein (UniProt: Q5NUL3) with a canonical seven-transmembrane (7TM) helical topology.1,2,21 Alternative splicing of exon 3 generates two functionally distinct isoforms: the short variant (GPR120S; 361 residues, UniProt: Q5NUL3–2) and the long isoform (GPR120L; 377 residues, UniProt: Q5NUL3),22,23 which differ by a 16-amino-acid insertion (residues 231–247) within the third intracellular loop (ICL3) (Figure 1A). This insertion modulates coupling to G proteins and β-arrestin.21 While human and murine FFAR4 share 86% sequence identity overall, GPR120L is uniquely expressed in humans, suggesting species-specific regulatory mechanisms.22

Beyond the canonical GPCR architecture, FFAR4 displays structural plasticity that contributes to signaling diversity and pharmacological complexity. Ligand binding, post-translational modifications, and signaling-state transitions drive conformational rearrangements across the TM helices and ICLs. Structural analyses indicate that key ligand-binding residues cluster within TM domains (TMDs) 3, 4, 5, and 6, forming an orthosteric pocket adapted for long-chain fatty acids. Recent cryo-EM studies of FFAR4 bound to synthetic and endogenous ligands demonstrate ligand-dependent shifts in TMD3 and TMD5 that influence the balance between G-protein and β-arrestin recruitment.1,24–26 Divergence between GPR120S and GPR120L, particularly within ICL3, contributes to signaling bias. GPR120S preferentially engages β-arrestin-2 over Gαq/11 and promotes anti-inflammatory signaling in macrophages,25–27 whereas GPR120L shows dual-coupling capacity and may enable broader physiological effects.25 Phosphorylation of serine and threonine clusters within the C terminus regulates receptor desensitization, internalization, and recycling in a tissue-dependent manner.21,28,29

High-resolution studies further indicate that FFAR4 can engage Gαq/11, Gαs, and Gαi depending on ligand structure and receptor conformation23,30 (Figure 1B). These observations support ligand-directed signaling bias, in which subtle structural features shape downstream outputs. Moreover, FFAR4 can form homodimers or heterodimers, for example, with FFAR1, which can influence ligand selectivity and signaling specificity in a tissue- and species-dependent manner.29

This structural adaptability underlies FFAR4’s capacity to integrate diverse nutrient signals with tissue-specific responses. The same complexity poses challenges for drug discovery. Therapeutic advances will likely require selective targeting of receptor conformations linked to beneficial metabolic and anti-inflammatory effects while avoiding off-target signaling.

FFAR4 DISTRIBUTION AND PHYSIOLOGY

FFAR4 is widely expressed across metabolic, immune, and neural tissues, including the intestine, adipose tissue, liver, pancreas, brain, and kidney.1,4,31 This broad distribution supports a role in systemic energy homeostasis, inflammation, and neuroendocrine signaling (Figures 1C–1E).

In the intestine, FFAR4 on enteroendocrine L and I cells stimulates the secretion of incretin and gut peptides, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP), and cholecystokinin (CCK), which contribute to glucose regulation and satiety.5,32,33 Dietary omega-3 fatty acid intake modulates colonic FFAR4 expression.34 In adipose tissue, FFAR4 facilitates adipocyte differentiation and promotes lipid storage while enhancing insulin sensitivity through anti-inflammatory actions.5,35–38 In the liver, FFAR4 suppresses hepatic lipogenesis and gluconeogenesis, helping to mitigate hepatic steatosis and improve glucose metabolism.39,40 In pancreatic β cells, FFAR4 modulates insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner, linking nutrient sensing to islet hormone output.41,42 In macrophages, FFAR4 activation attenuates inflammatory signaling by promoting polarization toward an M2 phenotype and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine production.5 In the brain, FFAR4 is enriched in dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area and is present in microglia and astrocytes, where it modulates neuroinflammation and contributes to appetite regulation through brain-gut signaling.43–46 These diverse physiological actions position FFAR4 as a central node in interorgan communication that coordinates nutrient, hormonal, and immune signals to maintain metabolic balance.

FFAR4 ORCHESTRATES METABOLIC HOMEOSTASIS

FFAR4 in obesity pathogenesis

Loss of FFAR4 function is linked to susceptibility to obesity. FFAR4-deficient mice develop severe diet-induced obesity with increased body weight, hepatic steatosis, enhanced adipogenesis, and adipose tissue inflammation.3 Complementary findings indicate impaired CCK secretion and reduced GIP production through paracrine mechanisms.47 Although the missense variant (R270H) was initially associated with obesity in human cohorts,3 larger studies did not confirm a consistent relationship.48,49 Discrepancies likely reflect the genetic heterogeneity of FFAR4 across populations, diet-dependent receptor activity, and methodological factors. Variants such as R270H can have population-specific effects that depend on observed discrepancies in allele frequency and linkage disequilibrium.3,48 As a nutrient sensor, FFAR4 shows ligand-dependent signaling that is sensitive to dietary lipid composition.3,5,50,51 Environmental and lifestyle factors, gene-environment interactions, and phenotypic variability add further complexity.13,52,53 Inconsistent sample sizes, population stratification, technical biases, divergent statistical methods, and nonstandardized endpoints also limit reproducibility.52,53 Future work in large, well-phenotyped human cohorts with integrated diet and genetic data will be required to resolve these contradictions.

Dietary lipid excess promotes obesity by stimulating hyperphagia and increasing adipogenesis.54 Beyond modulating fat taste preference, FFAR4 regulates the release of enteroendocrine hormones in a bidirectional manner. Pharmacological FFAR4 activation increases GLP-1 and CCK secretion while suppressing somatostatin and ghrelin in gastric cells.55 In adipose tissue, FFAR4 upregulation during adipogenesis drives terminal adipocyte differentiation.4,35 FFAR4 activation also induces the secretion of fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), which stimulates brown adipose tissue thermogenesis and promotes the browning of white adipose depots, effects that may protect against diet-induced obesity.56 Through nutrient sensing, hormonal regulation, and thermogenic activation, FFAR4 links dietary fat signals to systemic energy balance (Figure 2).

FFAR4 in T2DM pathogenesis

Genetic variation in FFAR4 has been associated with altered susceptibility to T2DM.57 FFAR4 is highly expressed in the intestinal epithelium and contributes to systemic glucose and insulin regulation. Direct mechanisms include three major effects on glycemic control. First, FFAR4 promotes AMPK-mediated GLUT4 translocation in insulin-sensitive tissues, which enhances peripheral glucose uptake.5,58 Second, it stimulates GLP-1 stimulation, which improves insulin secretion and satiety.59 Third, it protects β cells from inflammatory stress, preserving function and viability.60 Activation of FFAR4 improves insulin sensitivity in part by enhancing GLP-1 and CCK release from enteroendocrine L and I cells, which increases β cell responsiveness.32,61

In addition to these direct effects, FFAR4 engages immune mechanisms that contribute to metabolic repair. Activation of FFAR4 in islet macrophages restores obesity-impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion,62 and omega-3 fatty acids promote systemic insulin sensitivity through polarization of adipose tissue macrophages toward an anti-inflammatory phenotype. Pharmacological FFAR4 activation by omega-3 fatty acids or fatty acid esters of hydroxy fatty acids (FAHFAs) produces anti-diabetic effects through the preservation of β cell mass and the suppression of macrophage-driven inflammation.5,60

FFAR4 in hepatic inflammation and metabolic regulation

FFAR4 in Kupffer cells regulates liver inflammation and protects against MAFLD (Figure 2). Activation of FFAR4 suppresses nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) and JNK signaling, thereby reducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and limiting hepatocellular injury. This mechanism also protects against ischemiareperfusion injury.40 Clinical observations further support FFAR4’s role in hepatic homeostasis. In pediatric patients with obesity, carriers of the R270H loss-of-function FFAR4 variant display elevated alanine aminotransaminase (ALT) levels, suggesting increased susceptibility to hepatic injury.63 Conversely, the rs11187533 variant is associated with improved glycemic control and reduced biomarkers of hepatocyte damage.64 Both dietary and pharmacological interventions targeting FFAR4 demonstrate hepatoprotective effects. DHA attenuates hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation through FFAR4-dependent signaling.65–67 Fish-oil-enriched parenteral nutrition prevents the progression of liver injury clinically, although this benefit is lost in FFAR4-deficient models.68 Icosabutate, a semi-synthetic dual-FFAR1/FFAR4 agonist and liver-targeted EPA analog, improved hepatic fibrosis in the ICONA phase 2b trial in patients with MAFLD.69 The therapeutic efficacy of icosabutate appears constrained by several interrelated factors. Incomplete activation of FFAR4-specific signaling, likely influenced by structural determinants of receptor engagement, may limit its full therapeutic potential. Its partial agonism at FFAR1 may also dilute FFAR4-specific efficacy.70 Trial design features, including a modest cohort size (n = 187), fixed doses of 300 and 600 mg, and a 52-week observation period,71 may have limited the assessment of outcomes. Larger, longer trials with systematic dose exploration are warranted. Collectively, these findings position FFAR4 as a validated therapeutic target for hepatic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. Ongoing efforts to develop selective FFAR4 agonists may yield novel interventions for MAFLD and related liver disease.

FFAR4 in renal pathogenesis

Evidence implicates FFAR4 in renal health, including fibrosis resolution, acute injury repair, and attenuation of aging-related decline (Figure 2). In obstructive nephropathy, TUG-891 shifts macrophages toward an M2 phenotype, suppresses tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin (IL)-6, and reduces collagen deposition and tubular atrophy.72 In cisplatin-induced acute kidney injury (AKI), FFAR4 activation attenuates tubular damage by limiting endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-mediated apoptosis,73 and the downregulation of FFAR4 during AKI can be reversed by TUG-891.74 Podocyte-specific FFAR4 deficiency accelerates cellular senescence and disrupts lipid metabolism via the CAMKKβ-AMPK signaling pathway.75 In aging and glomerular disease models, FFAR4 agonists preserve renal structure and function.

Clinical translation remains in its early stages. Current investigations focus on immunoglobulin (Ig)A nephropathy,76–78 with emerging work in lupus nephritis,79 polycystic kidney disease,80 and other glomerulopathies.81,82 Notably, renal biopsy studies indicate that reduced FFAR4 expression correlates with impaired renal function, including higher 24-h urine protein excretion, higher serum creatinine, and a lower estimated glomerular filtration rate.75 These early human observations, while suggestive, underscore the need for more targeted clinical trials to systematically evaluate the therapeutic potential of FFAR4 modulation in kidney diseases. These observations support further targeted clinical evaluation of FFAR4 modulation in kidney disease.

FFAR4 in neuromodulation and brain-gut dialogue

FFAR4 connects dietary lipid sensing to central pathways involved in appetite, energy balance, and glucose homeostasis (Figure 2). FFAR4 is expressed in reward-related nuclei, including the arcuate nucleus, the nucleus accumbens, and the ventral tegmental area.83 In midbrain regions, FFAR4 localizes to dopaminergic neurons that regulate food and water intake.6 In the hypothalamus, FFAR4 shows enrichment in microglia and is present in astrocytes, where it modulates neuroimmune signaling.43–45,84 Activation of microglial FFAR4 suppresses NF-κB nuclear translocation and promotes anti-inflammatory polarization.43,85,86 Microglial deletion increases neuroinflammation, anxiety-like behaviors, and cognitive decline.44,87 Astrocyte-specific FFAR4 dampens NLRP3 priming and enhances IL-10 secretion.43 In vivo, omega-3 fatty acid supplementation alleviates depression-like behaviors and restores hippocampal plasticity.45 Because FFAR4 influences feeding and drinking behaviors, prolonged central activation could disrupt hypothalamic control circuits and contribute to undesired outcomes, such as anorexia.6

FFAR4 also plays a key role in brain-gut communication. In the gut, FFAR4 activation on enteroendocrine cells stimulates GLP-1 and peptide YY (PYY) release, which act on vagal afferents and central circuits to regulate appetite, support intestinal barrier integrity, and reduce inflammation.10,88,89 GLP-1 signaling further stimulates hepatic FGF21 release, which feeds back to influence sugar preference and systemic energy balance.90–92 Gut microbiota interactions add another layer of regulation. Loss of intestinal FFAR4 reduces Bacteroides vulgatus and its metabolite pantothenic acid, leading to lower GLP-1, reduced hepatic FGF21, and impaired nutrient preference.93 Microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids influence barrier function, immune signaling, and neurotransmitter production, thereby affecting CNS activity.94,95

FFAR4 in cancer

Omega-3 PUFAs affect cancer cell proliferation, cell cycle regulation, and cell death.96 Clinical and preclinical studies support the tumor-suppressive effects of omega-3 supplementation in multiple malignancies, including lung,97,98 prostate,99,100 pancreas,101 colon,102–104 and breast cancers.105,106 These effects are partially mediated through FFAR4, which can inhibit proliferation in prostate cancer107 and attenuate motility in lung cancer.108 In contrast, several studies report tumor-promoting roles for FFAR4 in lipid-rich microenvironments. Elevated FFAR4 correlates with poor differentiation in breast and colorectal carcinomas109,110 and can promote migration through MMP2 suppression111 or invasion through PI3K/AKT activation.112,113 These tumor-promoting observations are primarily preclinical and lack human clinical confirmation. The prevailing consensus remains that omega-3 PUFAs are broadly tumor suppressive, but FFAR4’s role may be context dependent. FFAR4 activation can upregulate lipogenic enzymes and modify membrane lipid composition,114 which may reduce chemotherapeutic permeability and contribute to drug resistance.115 Overall, FFAR4 exhibits functional duality in tumorigenesis that likely reflects ligand bias and microenvironmental influences.

MULTIMODAL FFAR4 SIGNALING IN METABOLIC REGULATION

FFAR4 exhibits ligand-directed signaling bias that allows regulation of diverse metabolic and immune processes according to ligand structure, tissue context, and available signaling components.

β-arrestin-2-dependent signaling

Upon activation, β-arrestin-2 translocates to the plasma membrane and forms a complex with FFAR4. The complex internalizes and binds to transforming growth factor β-activated kinase 1 (TAK1)-binding protein (TAB1), thereby disrupting the TAB1-TAK1 interaction.5,116,117 Inhibition of TAK1 suppresses downstream IKKβ/NF-κB and MKK4/JNK pathways. This mechanism enhances insulin sensitivity by increasing PDX1 expression in β cells and reducing chemokines such as CXCL1 and CCL2.118

Gαq/11-dependent signaling

FFAR4 coupling to Gαq/11 stimulates phospholipase Cβ (PLCβ) to hydrolyze phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2) into diacylglycerol (DAG) and inositol trisphosphate (IP3).119 IP3-driven intracellular calcium release promotes the secretion of gut hormones such as GLP-1, GIP, and CCK from enteroendocrine cells. In adipocytes, this pathway induces ERK1/2 activation and PPARγ expression, facilitating adipogenesis.120,121 Gαq/11 signaling also contributes to the suppression of pro-inflammatory gene expression122 and can promote ERK and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-Akt-mediated cell survival,123 although in some contexts, it may enhance migration and invasion via COX-2 and MMP-9 upregulation.124

Gαs and Gαi signaling

FFAR4 coupling to Gαs stimulates cAMP production and activates exchange protein activated by cAMP (EPAC)-mediated signaling to promote adipogenesis.35 Conversely, Gαi coupling suppresses intracellular cAMP levels, which contributes to the inhibition of ghrelin and somatostatin release.125

Together, these signaling pathways enable FFAR4 to integrate nutrient sensing with diverse downstream effects, including glucose regulation, lipid metabolism, adipocyte differentiation, and immune modulation. The tissue-specific balance among these pathways forms the mechanistic foundation for FFAR4’s broad physiological roles and underscores the therapeutic relevance of biased agonism (Figure 3).

FFAR4 AGONISTS

Metabolic disorders involve dysregulation across multiple tissues with altered adipocyte function, enteroendocrine signaling, and pancreatic endocrine regulation. Duodenal biopsies show downregulation of FFAR1 and FFAR4 in obese individuals compared with lean controls.53 Decreased FFAR4 expression in leukocytes from both obese humans and animals has also been reported.87 Given FFAR4’s role in nutrient sensing, energy homeostasis, and inflammation, there is strong interest in selective FFAR4 agonists (Figure 4; Table 1). Natural ligands

FFAR4 responds to long-chain fatty acids from omega-3, omega-6, and omega-9 families, with preferential activation by omega-3 PUFAs such as α-linolenic acid (ALA), DHA, and EPA.1,5 Because endogenous interconversion among omega-3 fatty acid species is limited in humans, targeted dietary supplementation remains the most effective approach to elevate systemic levels.138 After ingestion, these fatty acids circulate, bind to lipoproteins or albumin,4,59 and accumulate near FFAR4-expressing cells with peak availability postprandially. Bioavailability is time dependent because omega-3 PUFAs progressively integrate into membranes, which supports full biological efficacy and generally requires sustained supplementation.139 Once integrated, these lipids enhance membrane fluidity, promote FFAR4 activation, and engage in crosstalk with PPARγ to potentiate anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing responses.11 DHA and ALA exhibit high potency, albeit with concomitant FFAR1 activation.1,140,141 Although omega-3 supplementation improves some metabolic parameters (Table 2), non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) trials often did not meet primary endpoints.142–144 Heterogeneity in study design, dose, duration, and endpoints likely contributes to variability.143,145–147 Bioavailability differences are another factor. In obesity, omega-3 PUFAs are preferentially sequestered in adipose triglycerides and phospholipids, which may reduce availability at pancreatic, hepatic, immune, and neural sites, potentially lowering receptor engagement below an activation threshold.148,149 Absorption efficiency follows a hierarchy of free fatty acids greater than triglycerides greater than ethyl ester.150,151 Genetics, microbiota, diet, and concomitant therapies can further modulate responses.147

Other natural agonists include grifolic acid, which has partial FFAR4 selectivity129,165 but induces cell death in macrophages and GH3 cells through FFAR4-independent mechanisms.166 FAHFAs have emerged as endogenous FFAR4 ligands that enhance GLUT4 trafficking and glucose utilization and reproduce FFAR4’s anti-inflammatory and insulin-sensitizing actions.60,167,168 Silencing FFAR4 abolishes these effects.60

Synthetic ligands

Early synthetic agonists such as GW9508 exhibited activity at both FFAR1 and FFAR4, limiting their utility in mechanistic studies.126,169 Optimization yielded more selective compounds, including NCG21, GSK137647A, and TUG-891,129 ,130,170 which show about 100-fold selectivity for FFAR4 over FFAR1,128 although species-dependent pharmacodynamics complicate translation.51 Icosabutate, a liver-targeted EPA analog, has progressed to phase 2 trials for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).69 Structural modifications promote portal uptake and hepatic accumulation while limiting systemic exposure.70,171 In ICONA, icosabutate improved liver fibrosis but did not meet the primary endpoint of MASH resolution, which suggests partial engagement of protective pathways.

Challenges and opportunities

Many FFAR4 agonists share carboxylic acid or sulfonamide motifs and lack tissue selectivity.72 Future work should prioritize the discovery of new chemotypes and rational design of agonists that minimize β-arrestin-mediated desensitization, for example, by stabilizing underexplored conformations in TM6 and TM7.23 Allosteric modulators may further refine bias and tissue selectivity. Another promising strategy is to exploit synergistic interactions between FFAR4 and other metabolic receptors, including PPARγ, FFAR1, and GPR84, to develop dual- or multi-target agonists with refined specificity and fewer off-target effects. Preclinical studies indicate that FFAR4 combined with PPARγ,13,164,172,173 FFAR1,174 or GPR84175 can improve glucose tolerance and reduce inflammation. There remains a need for new chemotypes, deeper structure-activity relationships (SARs), and allosteric approaches to achieve precise control of receptor function.

CONCLUSIONS

Since its discovery in 2005, FFAR4 has emerged as a regulator of glucose and lipid metabolism, immune-metabolic interactions, and systemic energy balance.1 These pleiotropic functions make FFAR4 an attractive target for obesity, T2DM, MAFLD, and related inflammatory disorders. However, FFAR4 activation is not universally beneficial. In certain malignancies, context-dependent FFAR4 signaling may promote tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis through altered lipid metabolism.110,113,115 These effects underscore the need to define tissue- and disease-specific mechanisms before clinical translation.

Drug development faces several challenges. Many candidate molecules share structural features that limit selectivity and pharmacokinetics. Although FFAR1 and FFAR4 can couple to Gαq/11, FFAR4 exhibits signaling bias across tissues through Gαs, Gαi, and β-arrestin, with crosstalk to PPARγ that shapes efficacy and risk profiles. The partial efficacy of icosabutate in the ICONA phase 2 trial may reflect insufficient engagement of FFAR4-specific mechanisms.71 Priorities for future research include the rational design of biased agonists that preferentially engage anti-inflammatory, anti-fibrotic, or insulin-sensitizing pathways and the development of allosteric modulators for tissue targeting. With rigorous preclinical and clinical evaluation, next-generation FFAR4-targeted therapies may restore interorgan metabolic balance and counteract chronic metabolic disease. A deeper understanding of receptor biology will be essential to translate FFAR4 pharmacology into precision metabolic medicine.
