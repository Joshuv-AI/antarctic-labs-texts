Epidemiology, clinical features, and outcome of the hospitalized patients with Kerion in Fars Province, Iran: an eleven-year retrospective study
Authors: Fateme Khosravi, Mohammad Mahdi Parvizi, Maryam Sadat Sadati, Mohammadreza Khosravi, Mehdi Ghahartars
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKerion, a severe inflammatory type of tinea capitis, presents with purulent, boggy swelling and alopecia. Few articles in the literature provide information on this uncommon manifestation of tinea capitis (TC). This study aimed to determine the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of patients with Kerion over an 11-year period in an inpatient setting.MethodsIn this retrospective cross-sectional study, we enrolled all the admitted patients with inflammatory TC in the dermatology department of Shahid Faghihi referral hospital during 2011–2022. The data included demographic factors, animal contact, treatment, outcome, and past medical profiles. We used STATA 14.2 to analyze the data.ResultsOne hundred and thirty-two patients with Kerion were enrolled in to the study. the mean age of patients was 7.6 ± 5.8 years. Boys (82.5%, n = 109) significantly outnumbered girls (17.5%, n = 23), and 82.2% of patients were under 11 years of age. Eight patients were one to two years old, two were infants under one year old, and five were adults. More than half (52.2%) of the patients were from rural areas. Most of the cases had heavily utilized systemic antifungal therapy, antibiotics, and corticosteroids. In addition, 43 patients reported positive animal contact. Over the course of an eleven-year period, there was a downward trend in the number of hospitalized patients.ConclusionWe report one of the largest studies on Kerion patients over an eleven-year period. We have expanded the information on Kerion patients’ epidemiology, transmission mode, and clinical outcomes. To develop preventative strategies for Kerion, future studies should focus on additional risk factors and individual susceptibilities.

Background

Tinea capitis (TC), a cutaneous fungal infection of the scalp, remains a public health concern worldwide [1–3]. The disease has inflammatory or non-inflammatory presentations, and Kerion is an uncommon inflammatory subtype [3]. Kerion derives its name from the Greek word for honeycomb [4] Back in time, Kerion was considered a sign of poverty [5]. Fortunately, the development of antifungal treatments and improvements in public health have effectively controlled the prevalence of inflammatory TC [6]. Kerion is more common in rural areas, and factors such as low socioeconomic status, poor hygiene, and crowded living can increase the frequency [7]. T-cell lymphocytes mediate a vigorous, hypersensitive immune response to pathogens, which primarily causes disease [3]. Kerion predominantly affects children between the ages of 3 and 7, with boys more commonly affected than girls [3].

The disease manifests as tender, pustular, boggy, inflammatory plaques (Fig. 1) [3]. Alopecia, changes in skin color, and bacterial superinfection are potential complications that may mimic other conditions such as abscesses, cellulitis, psoriasis, alopecia areata, atopic dermatitis, and seborrheic dermatitis, potentially leading to a misdiagnosis [8, 9]. The absence of a standardized guideline for diagnosing Kerion can lead to delays in accurate diagnosis and treatment [3]. Diagnosis is typically based on clinical suspicion, followed by further confirmation through lesion culture or microscopic identification using direct testing with potassium hydroxide preparation (KOH) [3]. Researchers rarely investigate the transmission mode of this uncommon disease [10]. Also, information on the condition is primarily derived from case report studies [5, 7, 11–13], highlighting the importance of larger studies.

The incidence of Kerion hasn’t yet been studied in referral hospitals in Iran. With respect to demographic factors, mode of transmission, and treatment outcomes, the present study retrospectively reviews cases with clinically diagnosed inflammatory TC treated in the dermatology department of Shahid Faghihi referral hospital to expand the information on the condition.

Methods

Study design, study population, and data gathering

With the extraction of information from the electronic database in the dermatology department of Shahid Faghihi Hospital, we identified 132 patients with tinea capitis between April 2011 and August 2022 (11 years). Patients with a clinical diagnosis of tinea capitis or those diagnosed through a KOH test or pathogen culture met the inclusion criteria. The study excluded patients whose final diagnosis was not tinea capitis. The following factors were ascertained: age, gender, place of residence, familial history, medical history, contact with animals, history of other fungal infections, date of admission and discharge, systemic symptoms such as lymphadenopathy and fever, laboratory profile, systemic and topical antifungal treatment. An expert physician conducted telephone conversations with patients to collect information on treatment outcomes. Despite conducting a 6-week follow-up after patients’ discharge, the absence of a registration system in an outpatient setting prevented the documentation of outcome data in electronic databases.

Ethical statement

The study protocol received approval from the Research Ethics Committees of the School of Medicine, Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, located in Shiraz, Iran (ethics code: IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1401.148). The data for this retrospective study came from the patients’ hospital records. According to the research ethics consideration in biomedical sciences approved by the Health Ministry of Iran, upon admission to the hospital, all patients (or parents or legal guardians for patients under 18 years of age) signed a consent form authorizing the use of their hospital records for research purposes, anonymously.

Statistical analyses of the data

We analyzed the data using SPSS version 25 and STATA 14.2. We used chi-square, independent t-test, and ANOVA tests. Using descriptive statistics, we defined the presence of scalp ringworm as well as current clinical, demographic, and epidemiological patterns. We used the chi-square test to examine the relationship between gender and factors, such as age and place of residence. We used the independent t-test to investigate the relationship between the number of hospital days and clinical symptoms. The p-value of equal to or less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall, 132 patients with TC were admitted in our dermatology ward, all of whom had the clinical presentation of Kerion. Table 1 presents the patients’ characteristics enrolled in the study. Among the patients, 23 (17.5%) were female and 109 (82.5%) were male. The average age of the patients was 7.6 (standard deviation (SD): 5.8), [range: 6 months to 48 years]. The mean age was 7.6 years (SD: 9.4) for males and 7.7 years (SD: 4.8) for females, with no significant difference between the groups (p = 0.121). Most participants were in the 6–11 age category (49.2%), followed by 0–5 (32%), 11–15 (13.6%), 16–18 (0.7%), and > 18 (5.3%) (Fig. 2). We identified TC in five adult patients (above 18 years old). Among which, three cases were adult men and two were adult women. Regarding geographic distribution, 52.2% of the population were from rural areas and 47.8% of patients were from urban areas. There was a significant association between male gender and residence in rural areas (p < 0.001). Two patients had known underlying diseases, including epilepsy and brain abscess.

Regarding history of animal contact, we identified positive animal contact in 44 patients (sheep: 40, dog: 3, bird: 1). One patient reported development of disease after head trauma and one after wrestling. Of the patients younger than 2 years, three reported exposures to sheep. Most of the infants were from urban areas. Moreover, the 48-year-old patient in our study also reported contact with sheep. The identical infection was not detected among the family members of patients. We should note that 75 patients had missing data on the mode of transmission.

The mean duration of hospital stay to manage inflammation and super infection was 8.7 days (SD: 5.6). There was no significant difference observed between boys (mean: 8.9, SD: 5.9) and girls (mean: 7.6, SD: 3.3). Among the patients, 35 were hospitalized for more than 10 days, with 5 patients requiring more than 20 days. The longest recorded hospital stay was 46 days, for a 6-year-old boy with no underlying diseases.

Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of 132 Kerion patients by gender and year of admission. The number of admissions varied annually, with peaks in 2014 and 2015 at 19 admissions each, and lower numbers in other years. Throughout all years, males consistently outnumbered females in admissions. This data suggests a gender disparity in Kerion prevalence, with males being more affected and seeking treatment at this dermatology center more frequently than females.

During the clinical examination, all patients displayed the typical presentation of Kerion. Thirty-two patients had fever, and 42 had cervical lymphadenopathy. The analysis revealed differences in the prevalence of fever, lymphadenopathy, and remote dermatophytosis between males and females. For fever, 5.3% of males and 18.9% of females reported experiencing it, suggesting a higher likelihood in females. Conversely, lymphadenopathy was present in 25.7% of males and 6% of females, indicating a higher likelihood in males. Similarly, remote dermatophytosis was reported in 17.4% of males and 2.2% of females, suggesting a higher likelihood in males (Fig. 4). However, these differences were not statistically significant, meaning they could have occurred by chance and may not represent true relationships between gender and these conditions (the p-values for fever, lymphadenopathy, and remote dermatophytosis were 0.450, 0.739, and 0.381, respectively). The results showed no correlation between the presence of these systemic symptoms and the length of hospital stay (p-values of 0.758 and 0.415 for fever and lymphadenopathy, respectively). Additionally, 26 patients had fungal infections other than tinea capitis, and in these patients, no significant relationship was found between the length of hospital stay and the presence of non-scalp fungal infection (p-value = 0.707). These findings suggest that the presence of systemic symptoms or non-scalp fungal infections did not impact the length of hospital stay in this study population.

Laboratory results showed that thrombocytosis, one of the acute phase reactions resulting from infection, was observed in only 16 patients. Lymphocytosis, also a sign of acute infection, was observed in almost half of the patients (52.27%). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated in 57 out of 132 patients, representing 43.18% of the total sample.

The systematic antifungal drug regimen consisted of griseofulvin, terbinafine, and itraconazole that were used based on standard dosing based on weight. In the study, systemic drug treatment varied, with 40.91% receiving one agent, 54.55% receiving two agents, and smaller proportions receiving three agents (3.03%). Two patients didn’t receive any systemic antifungal agents. In six patients, liver enzymes (AST and ALT) were elevated above normal levels. Out of the total cases, 94 received systemic prednisolone, and 116 received a systemic antibiotic. Topical antifungal agents that were used for treatment were ketoconazole shampoo, terbinafine, sertaconazole ointment and clotrimazole solution till the eradication of the infection. For topical antifungal agents, most participants received three agents (68.18%), followed by two agents (19.70%), one agent (2.27%), and the smallest proportion receiving four agents (8.33%). In our hospital, ketoconazole shampoo (n = 90), clotrimazole solution (n = 60) and terbinafine ointment (n = 21) were the most used topical agents. Furthermore, 100 patients received potassium permanganate soaks that have antifungal and antibacterial capacity (Table 2).

We conducted telephone follow-ups with fifty-seven patients. The study’s outcome indicates that out of the 57 cases analyzed, 17 resulted in a complete cure and normal hair growth; 28 had hair regrowth with some scarring; and 12 had no hair regrowth. Seventy-five cases were lost to follow-up. Overall, the findings suggest varying degrees of success in the treatment outcomes, with a substantial portion of cases showing some level of improvement.

Discussion

Globally, people widely recognize tinea capitis as a public health concern, especially in developing countries [1, 2]. In this study, we reported one of the largest epidemiological studies conducted among Kerion patients admitted in a tertiary referral hospital in Iran. The major findings of our study were the decreased number of hospitalized Kerion patients over the last decade and the high incidence of the disease among boys under the age of 11. Many patients identified close contact with sheep as a source of infection. Hair regrowth with some scarring was the most frequently reported treatment outcome.

Kerion is an intense type of TC caused by a hypersensitivity reaction to pathogen mediated by T-cell lymphocytes. While it is commonly linked to zoophilic dermatophytes like Microsporum canis, anthropophilic agents such as Trichophyton tonsurans have become increasingly responsible, particularly in urban areas [14–16]. In our study, nearly half of the Kerion cases were from urban areas, reflecting these recent observations [14–16]. A recent study also reported a similar even distribution of the disease [7]. Increased human-animal contact in urban settings and greater interaction between urban and rural populations, facilitated by improved transportation, could contribute to these findings. Understanding these epidemiological trends is crucial in developing targeted preventative strategies to manage tinea capitis.

Direct contact with humans, soil, or infected animals can transmit keritosis, but pinpointing the precise source of infection is frequently challenging and rarely investigated [7, 17]. We discovered that 43 patients had a history of animal contact, with sheep being the most frequent source, which is particularly noteworthy given the cultural context in Iran. Iran is an Islamic country with relatively low pet ownership, where most of the animal contacts are with sheep that are held for dairy farming. This finding highlights the significance of our results, as future preventative measures could address this possible infection source. The literature further provides multiple infection sources. In a study involving 121 inflammatory TC patients in Tunisia, 29 cases reported positive animal contact, primarily with cats and dogs [7]. The source of infection in some case report studies has been identified as dog [5, 17], cat [10] shared comb use [18], handmade belt [19], family member [20], and soil [21]. Furthermore, one of our patients developed Kerion after head trauma, reflecting the same scenario in a previous study [21]. We do not identify head injury as a potential predisposing factor for Kerion; however, given the multifactorial nature of the disease, a thorough patient history can enhance our understanding of the condition.

As was found in our study, the majority of TC patients were boys, which is consistent with patterns seen in previous studies [7, 10, 22–24] and in Iran [10, 23, 25]. Huyen et al. reported that 64% of the 25 inpatient Kerion cases were males [5]. However, the differences in patient gender have not always been identified [26]. The gender of patients may influence their vulnerability to certain types of fungal infections, but the relationship remains incompletely understood. The chemical and physical structure of the skin, along with its normal microbiota, play a crucial role in antifungal resistance [27]. In this case, a study suggests that progesterone and related compounds may help limit infections in women by slowing the growth of dermatophytes. This may allow the host immune system to clear the infection [28]. This observation could help explain why males are more susceptible to tinea capitis. Future research should explore the interactions between hormonal factors, skin structure, and the suitability of fungi.

Our study result was similar to prior studies, indicating that TC mostly appeared in children between 6 and 11 years of age [23, 25, 29, 30]. A study among 25 inpatient Kerion cases found that children between ages four and eleven mostly experience the disease [10]. The resistance of adult hair to TC is believed to be the fungistatic property of fatty acids, as well as colonization of sebum by the fungus Pityrosporum orbiculare [7]. Although Kerion mostly appears in children, we had five adult Kerion patients without any predisposing factors, such as immunosuppression. Half of them were from rural areas, and the other half were from urban areas. Previous reports have documented a few cases of adult Kerion, even among immunocompetent individuals [7, 12, 31–35]. Therefore, it is not implausible to consider Kerion as a potential differential diagnosis in adults. Two of our patients were infants from both rural and urban areas, neither of whom reported any history of animal contact. It’s likely that asymptomatic households might have been the source of infection. Family members have been the previously reported source of infant contamination [7, 13].

In our dermatology department, patients were typically hospitalized for an average of 8.7 days. The longest duration of hospitalization was 46 days. All the patients were discharged when the severe skin inflammation was subsided. We were able to follow up with 57 patients, among whom 79% had hair regrowth, 21% had cicatricial alopecia, and 49% had some scarring left. In a previous study conducted in an inpatient setting, the average duration of hospital stay was 9.9 days [36]. All Kerion patients had hair regrowth and 69.6% had some scars [5]. In this study, a decreasing trend was observed in the incidence of Kerion, which could be attributed to increased hygiene levels, better access to primary care, improvements in treatment, reduced hospital visits during the COVID-19 pandemic years, or a combination of these factors.

Systemic antifungal therapy is the choice for treatment of all TC types because topical agents don’t penetrate the hair shaft [3]. Griseofulvin is the first choice, however, recent clinical trials have found equal efficacy of newer drugs, including itraconazole and terbinafine [3]. In our hospital, either a single systemic drug or a combination of Griseofulvin, terbinafine or itraconazole were frequently prescribed. Systemic corticosteroid and antibiotics were widely used. Systemic corticosteroid therapy has been reported to expedite the subsidence of inflammation [37, 38]. Some studies have reported dramatic responses to oral corticosteroid in Kerion patients [37]. The use of oral corticosteroids for Kerion patients is controversial. However, in our hospital, most of the patients received oral corticosteroid during the hospitalization period [36]. Also, no definite evidence supports the use of antibiotics in treating Kerion [5]. However, our preference was to use antibiotics for the treatment of severe inflammation.

Our study was subject to several limitations. First, our patients had typical manifestations of Kerion, and further mycological tests were not a routine in our hospital, and we don’t have the pathogenic spectrum of our patients. Additionally, the 6-week follow-up visits of our patients were done in an outpatient setting, and data were not recorded at the time, and we had missing data on treatment results. Furthermore, this study cannot be generalized to a broader population for the following reasons: First, the demographic distribution in our study may only represent Kerion patients in our region. Second, our study sample includes hospitalized patients and does not involve outpatients. Furthermore, the majority of patients with tinea capitis seek care at primary healthcare centers, and tertiary hospitals refer selected cases, thereby introducing unavoidable referral bias. Finally, this study covers an 11-year period, and our sample size is limited.

In this regard, future research on tinea capitis could explore various aspects that may enhance public health outcomes. We recommend additional research to examine the role of domestic and farm animals in the transmission of tinea capitis, as well as the effectiveness of educational campaigns in reducing infection rates across various communities. Furthermore, genetic research could shed light on why individuals in specific regions are more susceptible to developing Kerion. Lastly, upcoming research could investigate the role of telehealth in diagnosing and treating patients in remote regions, aiming to improve access to care while reducing health costs.

Conclusion

Kerion is an uncommon manifestation of tinea capitis, and early detection of the disease is necessary to manage the complications. Our research represented one of the largest epidemiological investigations into Kerion in an eleven-year period. The epidemiologic profile of our patients shows that development of the disease is not unlikely in urban areas. Many of our patients had a history of animal contact with sheep. It’s important to note that while Kerion is more common in children, it can still occur in adults and should be taken into account in a differential diagnosis. Based on our experience in the dermatology clinic, the majority of patients experienced hair regrowth, albeit with some residual scalp scarring. In general, we recommend systemic corticosteroids in patients with severe inflammatory symptoms to control the inflammation.
