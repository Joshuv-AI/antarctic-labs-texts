Fast Polarisation-Aware Decoder for Non-Binary Polar Codes
Joseph Jabour, Ali Chamas Al-Ghouwayel, Emmanuel Boutillon

Abstract
The paper explores the emerging field of low-complexity non-binary (NB) polar decoders. It demonstrates that customising each kernel of a NB polar code (PC) through an offline analysis can lead to a significant reduction in the decoder’s complexity. The proposed decoder, termed the fast successive cancellation polarisation-aware (FSC-PA) , achieves this by determining the minimum computational load of the parity check nodes that share the same level of input polarisation. The NB polar decoder is designed for both binary phase shift keying (BPSK) and cyclic-code shift keying (CCSK) modulations. The FSC-PA algorithm results in an overall reduction of 60% in field additions and a reduction of 30% in real additions compared to the state-of-the-art extended min-sum algorithm, with a negligible impact on performance ( with less than 0.2 dB degradation).



I
Introduction

Recent papers highlight the interest in coupling
non-binary (NB)
codes with orthogonal modulations as an effective solution to send short messages with low-rate spectral efficiency (long-range application). In
[
1
]
,
non-binary low-density parity check (NB-LDPC)
coded modulation schemes are presented. This scheme exhibits a gain of 0.8 dB compared to state-of-the-art solutions. In
[
2
]
, the interest of such schemes is also highlighted; however, the complexity of
NB
decoders is circumvented by using multilevel coding with small cardinality
NB
codes at the cost of a small performance penalty. However, having a
NB
code that is easy to decode on a high cardinality
Galois field (GF)
would open many new opportunities to construct an effective short-frame message for long-range transmission.

Polar codes (PCs)
are the latest capacity-approaching codes introduced by Arikan
[
3
]
in 2009. The binary
PC
has been adopted in different standards, such as the fifth generation technology standard for broadband cellular networks (5G). Under similar decoding algorithm and coding rate,
non-binary polar codes (NB-PCs)
often exhibit superior decoding performance compared to their binary counterparts, particularly in short-packet transmission scenarios. Additionally,
NB-PCs
can be associated with an orthogonal modulation of the same cardinality as the
NB
code. In this paper,
cyclic-code shift keying (CCSK)
modulation is praised for its advantages in terms of demodulation and synchronisation
[
4
,
5
]
. However, other orthogonal modulations, such as the
chip spectrum spreading (CSS)
modulation used in
long range wireless area network (LoRaWan)

[
6
]
would give identical performance.

The
successive cancellation (SC)
based decoding suffers from high latency due to the recursive nature of the decoding process. Thus, a fast decoding approach has been proposed in
[
7
]
to reduce the computational complexity of the binary
PC
decoder and increase its throughput. In
[
8
]
, a generalised approach to fast decoding of binary
PCs
is introduced to further reduce the decoding latency of the
SC
decoder. The idea is to prune some of the decoder’s nodes and reduce the message traversal by performing non-recursive operations based on soft values inputted into these specific nodes. These substitutes are termed in the literature as constituent codes.
In this work, the constituent codes adopted for fast decoding are Rate-0, Rate-1, and repetition (REP) codes for
NB-PC
decoders. In addition to that, the single-parity check constituent code is adopted for the binary
PC
decoder.

A concrete design methodology is proposed in
[
9
]
for
NB-PCs
along with their kernel coefficients.

The work in
[
10
]
introduces a
SC
decoder based on the
min-sum (MS)
approximation, which operates in the
log-likelihood ratio (LLR)
domain. The logarithmic representation of the beliefs replaces all multiplication operations with additions. Furthermore, the authors in
[
11
]
show that selecting unitary kernel coefficients maintains the decoder’s performance, particularly in the context of CCSK modulation; thus omitting all
GF
multiplication operations. In addition, a restricted set of input messages is proposed in the kernel’s
check nodes (CNs)
to reduce decoding complexity with minimal performance impact compared to the conventional
MS
decoding algorithm.

To further simplify the decoding algorithm,
[
12
]
employs the
extended min-sum (EMS)
algorithm used for
NB-LDPC
decoders
[
13
]
to reduce the complexity of the
SC
decoder without sacrificing performance. This approach is later denoted FSC-EMS. Moreover,
SC
decoding with
asymmetrical extended min-sum (AEMS)

[
14
]
introduces an asymmetric approach to processing the CN inputs for output generation and is denoted as FSC-AEMS in the sequel. This decoding approach leverages the efficient L-bubble algorithm in
[
15
]
to process the reduced input set within the
CN
. This optimised implementation ensures streamlined and effective CN processing with a decoding performance similar to the conventional FSC-EMS decoder when associated with
CCSK
modulation. However, using the L-bubble sorter with asymmetric processing degrades the decoding performance under
binary phase shift keying (BPSK)
modulation.

This paper proposes a practical optimisation based on statistical estimates to reduce the complexity of the
NB-PC
decoder. The optimisation observes the polarisation effect on the kernels and utilises the statistical information to generate a polarisation-aware decoder that only computes the information that is likely to be useful. This proposed optimisation approach can be applied naturally to the fast decoding algorithms proposed in
[
7
,
8
]
to allow further reduction of the computational complexity of the decoder, by shortening the sizes of the messages entering the nodes in the upper layers of the
NB-PC
decoder. Such constituent nodes, which kept their initial recursive processing of message traversal unchanged, strongly dominate the global complexity of the NB-PC decoder.

The paper is outlined as follows: Section
II
introduces the
PCs
and the SC decoder. Section
III
discusses the
fast successive cancellation polarisation-aware (FSC-PA)
. In Section
IV
, the complexity and simulation results of
FSC-PA
decoders are compared with the state-of-the-art
SC
decoders. Lastly, the main points are concluded in Section
VI
.

II
Non-Binary Polar Codes and Decoders

An
NB-PC
is a
PC
defined over GF
(q=2^{p})
with
p>1
. The structure of a
PC
depends on the adopted kernel. A kernel
G_{2}
is a generator matrix for
N=2
and is considered the basic transformation unit. The adopted kernel
G_{2}
transforms an input
u=(u_{0},u_{1})
into an output
x=(x_{0},x_{1})
using the following relation.

\begin{cases}x_{0}=u_{0}\oplus u_{1}\\
x_{1}=\gamma\circledast u_{1},\end{cases}

(1)

where
\circledast
represents the field multiplication and
\oplus
represents the field addition over GF
(q)
, and
\gamma\in
GF
(q)
.

The polar encoder generates a codeword based on the polar transformation that leads to channel polarisation. The encoder encodes the message
m
of size
K
into a codeword
c
of size
N=2^{n}
by allocating the message
m
into the
K
most reliable channel positions (having the least error probability
\mathcal{P}_{\epsilon}
) such that
u_{i}=m_{j}:i\in\mathcal{A}_{D},0\leq j\leq K
where
\mathcal{A}_{D}
represents the set of the
K
most reliable input positions (noiseless virtual channels). The remaining
N-K
noisiest channels are frozen so that
u_{i}
is equal to the null element of GF
(q)\ \forall\ i\notin\mathcal{A}_{D}
. The frozen symbol channels are determined by estimating the error rate in each channel using a genie-aided decoder
[
3
]
.

Subsequently, the message
u
is transformed into the codeword
x
using a generator matrix
G_{N}
generated as the
n^{th}
Kronecker power of
G_{2}
, i.e.,
G_{N}=G_{2}^{\otimes n}
, with
\otimes
representing the Kronecker product.

On the receiver side, the
SC
decoder generates an estimate
\hat{u}
of the information message
u
from the received noisy codeword
y
. The input vectors of the polar decoder
L^{(0)}_{i}
are the intrinsic
LLR
information observed from the channel and can generally be expressed as

L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha(k))=\log\mathbb{P}(y_{i}|\hat{\alpha})-\log\mathbb{P}(y_{i}|\alpha),\\
\forall\ i\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket,\alpha\in\text{GF}(q),

(2)

where
\hat{\alpha}=\underset{\alpha\in GF(q)}{\arg\max{\mathbb{P}(y_{i}|\alpha)}}
, that is,
\hat{\alpha}
is the hard decision.

II-A

Log-Likelihood Ratio Estimation in
BPSK
Modulation

The intrinsic
LLR
input
L^{(0)}_{i}\ \forall i\in\llbracket 0,N-1\rrbracket
of a binary polar decoder can be deduced in the context of BPSK modulation over binary
additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)
channel as

\Lambda^{(0)}_{i}=\frac{2y_{i}}{\sigma^{2}},

(3)

where
\sigma^{2}
denotes the noise variance.

For
NB
polar decoding, each
p
-bits correspond to a given symbol
\alpha
. An input
LLR

L^{(0)}_{i}
is a vector of
q
corresponding to the reliability of each possible symbol
\alpha\in GF(q)
. Thus, can be expressed as

L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{p-1}\frac{y_{i}(k)}{\sigma^{2}}(\hat{\alpha}(k)-\alpha(k))\ \forall\ \alpha\in\text{GF}(q),

(4)

where
\alpha(k)
denotes the
k^{\text{th}}
bit in the binary representation of the symbol
\alpha\in\text{GF}(q)
.

II-B

Log-Likelihood Ratio Estimation in
CCSK
Modulation

On the other hand,
CCSK
modulation
[
16
]
is a spread spectrum modulation that spreads an encoded symbol of
p-
bits into a modulated symbol of
q=2^{p}
bits (chips) using a
pseudo-random noise (PN)
sequence. Let
\psi=(\psi_{x_{0}},\psi_{x_{1}},\cdots,\psi_{x_{N-1}})
denote the
CCSK
modulated frame of a codeword
x=(x_{0},\cdots,x_{N-1})
where
x_{i}\in\text{GF}(q)
. The
i^{th}
transmitted
CCSK
symbol
\psi_{x_{i}}
is received as a vector
y_{i}=(y_{i}(k))_{k=0,1,\ldots,q-1}
with
y_{i}(k)=(2\psi_{x_{i}}(k)-1)+w_{i}(k)
where
w_{i}(k)
represents a sample of real additive noise. Therefore, the intrinsic
LLR
vectors
L^{(0)}_{i}
can be expressed in CCSK modulation over an
AWGN
channel as

L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha)=\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\frac{y_{i}(k)}{\sigma^{2}}(\psi_{\hat{\alpha}_{i}}(k)-\psi_{\alpha}(k))\ \forall\ \alpha\in\text{GF}(q),

(5)

where
\hat{\alpha}_{i}
represents the maximum likelihood decision on
y_{i}
and defined as

\hat{\alpha}_{i}=\underset{\alpha\in\ GF(q)}{\mathrm{argmax}}\{\sum_{k=0}^{q-1}\frac{y_{i}(k)}{\sigma^{2}}\psi_{\alpha}(k)\}.

(6)

Note that, by construction, the
LLR
values are all positive and
L^{(0)}_{i}(\hat{\alpha}_{i})=0
.

For binary decoding over
CCSK
modulation, binary marginalisation is performed to determine the bit-level reliabilities from the symbol reliabilities. For each symbol
x_{i}
, the intrinsic LLR vector
L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha)
is obtained first in
\text{GF}(q)
. Then, for each bit position
k\in{0,1,...,p-1}
of the symbol’s binary representation, a corresponding binary LLR using is approximated as

\Lambda_{i}^{(0)}(k)=\max\limits_{\alpha_{0}\in\text{GF}(q):\alpha_{0}(k)=0}{L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha_{0})}-\max\limits_{\alpha_{1}\in\text{GF}(q):\alpha_{1}(k)=1}{L^{(0)}_{i}(\alpha_{1})}.

(7)

Polar Successive Cancellation Decoder

A polar decoder consists of
n=\log_{2}(N)
layers, where each layer has
N/2
kernels, as shown in Fig.
1
. Each kernel comprises a single-parity check node indicated simply as
CN
and a repetition node called a

.

A kernel at instance

has two input vectors denoted as
L_{\theta_{t}^{l-1}}^{(l-1)}
and

at indices
\theta_{t}^{l-1}

deduced as follows:

\begin{gathered}\theta_{t}^{l-1}=2t-(t\bmod 2^{n-l})\\
\phi_{t}^{l-1}=2^{n-l}+2t-(t\bmod 2^{n-l}),\end{gathered}

(8)

for
t=0,\dots,N/2-1
. The kernel instance
t
and the layer
l
are omitted from the indices
\theta_{t}^{l-1}
and
\phi_{t}^{l-1}
in the sequel for better readability of the notation. As an example, the red kernel presented in Fig.

l=2
and instance

. Therefore, according to (

), the corresponding
LLR
inputs
L_{\theta_{t}^{l-1}}^{(l-1)}
and
L_{\phi_{t}^{l-1}}^{(l-1)}
are the vectors with indices
\theta_{3}^{1}=5
and
\phi_{3}^{1}=7
, respectively, that is, the corresponding

input vectors of the kernel at instance

and the layer

are
L_{5}^{(1)}
and

.

The inputs
L_{\theta}^{(l-1)}
and
L_{\phi}^{(l-1)}
are processed by the kernel to obtain the output vectors
L_{\theta}^{(l)}
and
L_{\phi}^{(l)}
using the
CN
and
VN
update processing, respectively, as illustrated in Fig.
2
.

The
CN
update of a kernel can be expressed using the
MS
approximation as

L_{\theta}^{(l)}(\beta)=\min_{{\eta\in\text{GF}(q)}}{(L_{\theta}^{(l-1)}(\beta\oplus\eta)+L_{\phi}^{(l-1)}(\gamma\circledast\eta))},\\
\forall\ \beta\in\text{GF}(q).

(9)

Similarly, the
VN
update of a kernel can be expressed as

L^{(l)}_{\phi}(\eta)=L_{\theta}^{(l-1)}(\hat{u}^{(l)}_{\theta}\oplus\eta)+L_{\phi}^{(l-1)}(\gamma\circledast\eta),\ \forall\ \eta\in\text{GF}(q),

(10)

where
\hat{u}_{\theta}^{(l)}
is the estimated symbol at index
\theta
of layer
l
. To avoid arithmetic overflow, the vector
L^{(l)}_{\phi}
is normalised as follows.

L^{(l)}_{\phi}(\eta)=L^{(l)}_{\phi}(\eta)-\min(L^{(l)}_{\phi}),\ \forall\ \eta\in\text{GF}(q).

(11)

The hard decision
\hat{u}^{(n)}_{i}
at layer
l=n
and for a node
i=0,1,\dots,N-1
is estimated as

\hat{u}^{(n)}_{i}=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }i\notin\mathcal{A}_{D}\\
\underset{\alpha\in\ \text{GF}(q)}{\arg\min}\ L^{(n)}_{i}(\alpha),&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{A}_{D}.\end{cases}

(12)

Once computed, the estimated symbols
\hat{u}_{\theta}^{(l)}
and
\hat{u}_{\phi}^{(l)}
at layer
l
are back-propagated to layer
l-1
as

\begin{gathered}\hat{u}_{\theta}^{(l-1)}=\hat{u}_{\theta}^{(l)}\oplus\hat{u}_{\phi}^{(l)},\ \ \hat{u}_{\phi}^{(l-1)}=\gamma\circledast\hat{u}_{\phi}^{(l)}.\end{gathered}

(13)

III
Successive Cancellation Polarisation-Aware Decoder

The
FSC-PA
decoding algorithm is a simplified decoding approach for
NB-PC
. It is based on investigating the statistical behaviour of the computations performed within the
CNs
at different layers.

In the domain of NB-LDPC codes, equations of density evolution have been derived
[
17
]
. In the case of
NB
-
PCs
, these equations are also applicable. However, two observations prevent us from mathematically formalising the density function of incoming/outgoing messages from the kernels. First, they are difficult to handle in the general case, and Monte Carlo methods are used in practice to evaluate the decoding threshold of NB-LDPC codes. Last but not least, the theoretical analysis of the proposed polarisation-aware algorithm requires knowledge of the probability distribution of the first
n_{m}
most reliable decisions of the messages. This problem cannot be tackled simply because it requires manipulation of the distributions over
[0,1]^{n_{m}}
. Consequently, Monte Carlo simulation is relied upon in the sequel.

III-A

Definition of Nodes Clusters

In a polar decoder of size
N
, there are
N/2
kernels in each of the
n
layers, each consisting of a
CN
and a
VN
. Nodes that share the same polarisation level can be clustered
[
11
]
based on their polarisation parameter (mutual information, Bhattacharyya parameter or synthesised channel error rate).

In a similar pattern, let a cluster of kernels
S_{s}^{(l)}
at layer
l
with index
s=0,\dots,2^{l-1}-1
be a set of kernels at instances
t=s\times(2^{n-l})
up to
t=(s+1)\times 2^{n-l}-1
. Thus, a cluster
S_{s}^{(l)}
includes
2^{n-l}
kernels with all
CNs
having similar polarisation levels and similarly all
VNs
having similar polarisation levels (which differ from those of
CNs
). In this approach,
VNs
of the kernels are considered to belong to the same cluster as
CNs
as they both share the same input messages. Thus, any optimisation at
CNs
will have a similar impact on their corresponding
VNs
.

Hence, the cluster definitions serve as a foundation for dynamically computing the kernel message sizes within each cluster, based on statistical estimations at the
CNs
.

III-B

Check Node Processing Prerequisite

Assume an
EMS
-based CN processing at layer
l
, and kernel
t
with the two sorted input messages
M^{(l-1)}_{\theta}
and
M^{(l-1)}_{\phi}
(most reliable candidates of
L^{(l-1)}_{\theta}
and
L^{(l-1)}_{\phi}
respectively) each of size
n_{m}
. Each element of the input messages consists of a 2-tuple whose first and second instances represent, respectively, the
GF
symbol and its associated
LLR
value. For clarity, the
GF
vector of the message
M^{(l-1)}_{\theta}
is denoted as
M^{(l-1)\oplus}_{\theta}
and the
LLR
vector is denoted as
M^{(l-1)^{+}}_{\theta}
.

Let the vectors
M_{L}
and
M_{H}
correspond to the least reliable input vector and the highest reliable input vector, respectively, of the inputs
M^{(l-1)}_{\theta}
and
M^{(l-1)}_{\phi}
. Relative reliability
[
14
]
is estimated by comparing an
LLR
value of each input
M^{(l-1)}_{\theta}
and
M^{(l-1)}_{\phi}
with an index
z=2
such that

\begin{gathered}(M_{L},M_{H})\leftarrow\\
\begin{cases}(M^{(l-1)}_{\theta},M^{(l-1)}_{\phi})&\text{ If }(M^{(l-1)}_{\theta}(z)<M^{(l-1)}_{\phi}(z))\\
(M^{(l-1)}_{\phi},M^{(l-1)}_{\theta})&\text{ Otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{gathered}

(14)

Furthermore, let the matrix
T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}
be the intermediate matrix of all potential candidates to generate the output
M_{\theta}^{l}
such that

\begin{gathered}T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(i,j)=M_{H}(i)\boxplus M_{L}(j):\ 0\leq i,j<n_{m},\end{gathered}

(15)

where
\boxplus
corresponds to a
GF

(\oplus)
and an
LLR
addition
(+)
operations.

Thus, the most reliable (distinct)
n_{m}
symbols
{T^{\prime}}_{\Sigma}^{\oplus}
of
{T^{\prime}}_{\Sigma}
with their corresponding
LLRs

{T^{\prime}}_{\Sigma}^{+}
are sorted to generate the message
M_{\theta}^{(l)}
.

III-C

Statistical Computation Using EMS-based Check Nodes

The relative reliability approach defined in
[
14
]
, allows to estimate the potential elements generated in (
15
). This helps reduce the overall complexity of the SC decoder and can be achieved by computing the bubble pattern matrices
\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}
.

A bubble pattern matrix is defined as a matrix of size
n_{m}\times n_{m}
which includes the probability that the element (bubble) of
T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(i,j)
is selected in any output element of
M^{(l)}_{\theta}
in layer
l
and CN at kernel instance
t
. To do so, for a decoded codeword, a bubble pattern matrix
\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}
can be computed as follows.

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}(i,j)=\begin{cases}1&\text{If }T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}(i,j)\in\ M^{(l)}_{\theta}\\
0&\text{Otherwise}\end{cases},\\
\forall\ 0\leq t<N/2;\ 1\leq l<n.\end{gathered}

(16)

Estimating the average behaviour of the bubble pattern of a given CN gives useful insight into partitioning its bubbles (the element of
T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}
) into two sets: the set of useful bubbles (i.e. the ones that are frequently used) and the set of useless bubbles (i.e., the ones that are never, or only rarely used). This partition allows for reducing the complexity of the CN processing by only computing the set of useful bubbles. Experimentation shows that the
CNs
with similar polarisation effects have similar sets of useful bubbles. Thus, the accumulation of statistical information can help increase the accuracy of the average behaviour of bubbles.

A contribution rate matrix
\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{s}
is defined as the aggregated statistical information
\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}
of
CNs
at instances
t\in\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{s}
. Hence, the contribution rate matrix
\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{s}
at layer
l
and cluster
s
can be expressed as

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{s}=\frac{1}{2^{n-l}}\sum_{t=s.2^{n-l}}^{(s+1).2^{n-l}-1}\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}.\end{gathered}

(17)

The bubble pattern matrices
\mathcal{B}^{(l)}_{t}
are taken into account if and only if the frame is successfully decoded to avoid biasing the statistical results by a faulty decision. The estimation of
\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{s}
is accumulated for
N_{r}
well-decoded frames. The contribution rate matrices are normalised such that

C^{(l)}_{s}=\frac{\mathcal{C}^{(l)}_{s}}{N_{r}}.

(18)

The last layer
l=n
includes simple
CNs
that perform a field addition of the most reliable element of the two inputs to obtain the estimate
\hat{u}_{i}\ \forall\ i\mod 2=0
. Therefore, they are excluded from the simplification process.

III-D

Bubbles Pruning Process

The pruning process is performed offline to obtain a pruned form of
T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}
, denoted as
T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}
, for all clusters
s=0,\dots,2^{l-1}-1
in layers
l=1,\dots,n-1
. In the pruning process, a threshold
\mathcal{P}_{t}
is defined as the minimum contribution rate value for a bubble to be computed. Therefore, any bubble with a contribution rate
C^{(l)}_{s}(i,j)<\mathcal{P}_{t}
is omitted from the
CN
processing within the cluster
\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{s}
.

The indicator matrices
\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}
can be found for all clusters
s=0,1,\dots,2^{l-1}-1
over the
l=1,2,\dots,n-1
layer as follows:

\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ If }C^{(l)}_{s}(i,j)>\mathcal{P}_{t}\\
0&\text{Otherwise.}\end{cases}

(19)

These
\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}
matrices indicate which bubbles of
T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}
should be exclusively computed to generate
M^{(l)}_{\theta}
, that is,

\begin{gathered}T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}(i,j)=\begin{cases}M_{H}(i)\boxplus M_{L}(j)&\text{If }\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}(i,j)=1\\
(0,+\infty)&\text{Otherwise.}\end{cases}\\
:0\leq i,j<n_{s^{\prime}}^{(l-1)}.\end{gathered}

(20)

where
s^{\prime}=\lfloor s/2\rfloor
.

The tuple
(0,\infty)
represents the
GF
and
LLR
values resulting from the saturation process, in which the element
T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}(i,j)
is excluded from the output generation.

Moreover, since the bubbles in the first row are generated directly from the corresponding input
M^{(l-1)}_{L}
, the omitted bubbles consequently reduce the maximum size of the propagated messages so that the size of the CN input messages within the node cluster
\mathcal{S}^{(l)}_{s}
is

\begin{gathered}n^{(l)}_{s}=\sum_{j=0}^{n^{(l-1)}_{s^{\prime}}}{\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}(0,j)},\end{gathered}

(21)

The size of the inputs at layer
l=1
denoted as
n_{0}^{(0)}
is equal to the chosen
EMS
size
n_{m}
, i.e.,
n_{0}^{(0)}=n_{m}
.

As a result, both the
CNs
and
VNs
in cluster
S^{(l)}_{s}
have an output size of
n^{(l)}_{s}
elements but different input sizes. A
CN
in a cluster
S_{s}^{(l)}
generates an output of
n^{(l)}_{s}
elements and has inputs of
n^{(l-1)}_{s^{\prime}}
elements. Therefore, only
n^{(l)}_{s}
among the
n^{(l-1)}_{s^{\prime}}
elements of the inputs are sufficient to generate
n^{(l)}_{s}
reliable elements (see summation boundary of (
20
)). Nonetheless, the reason for generating
n^{(l-1)}_{s^{\prime}}
elements in the previous layer is that the
VNs
in cluster
S^{(l)}_{s}
require
n^{(l-1)}_{s^{\prime}}
input elements to produce
n^{(l)}_{s}
reliable outputs. This is due to the processing nature of the
VN
which combines the reliability of the common
GF
elements to generate meaningful results.

Algorithm
1
summarises the design procedure for the proposed
FSC-PA
decoder, based on the approach described above.

III-E

Proposed Design of FSC-PA Decoder

The design of the
FSC-PA
decoder depends on two parameters, the first is the maximum input size indicated by
n_{0}^{(0)}
, and the second is the inclusion threshold
\mathcal{P}_{t}
. The former can be found by estimating the decoding performance of the conventional
EMS
-based decoder at different message sizes
n_{m}
. Once the good
n_{m}
is found, it can be adopted in the design stage of the
FSC-PA
decoder, i.e.,
n_{0}^{(0)}=n_{m}
.

As an example, let the desired code be
N=64
with
K=42
on GF
(64)
using
CCSK
modulation. The first requirement is to find the decoding performance for different
n_{m}
using Monte Carlo simulation (or a mathematical approximation as in
[
9
]
). The decoding performance of the
EMS
-based decoder at different sizes
n_{m}
is simulated as shown in Fig.
4
using
CCSK
modulation and over an
AWGN
channel. As shown, the parameter
n_{m}=18
is the minimum size that maintains good performance at
FER

\approx 10^{-4}
. Hence, the parameter
n_{0}^{(0)}
is set to 18 for the statistical analysis carried out in a
FER
region of
10^{-2}
, that is, at an
SNR
of
-7.5
dB.

Similarly, the parameter
n_{0}^{(0)}
is deduced for other codes using the same approach. The value
n_{0}^{(0)}=18
is found to be the minimum size that maintains good performance at
FER

\approx 10^{-4}
for all code lengths
N\leq 512
.

IV
Complexity Analysis of FSC-PA Decoders

The complexity of the proposed
FSC-PA
decoder is compared with the FSC-EMS decoder using a message size of
n_{m}
, the FSC-AEMS decoder with message sizes
n_{L}=8
and
n_{H}=20

[
14
]
, and the binary
CRC-aided fast successive cancellation list of size
L=32
(CA-FSCL32)
decoder. A conclusive complexity analysis can be drawn when comparing the number of arithmetic and field operations across the
NB

SC
decoders. However, this comparison becomes less straightforward when including the binary
CA-FSCL32
decoder due to intricate sorting procedures, which are highly dependent on the decoder’s structure and implementation, and cannot be accurately estimated. To address this, the number of arithmetic operations is considered to provide a reasonable insight into the computational complexity. Thus, shows whether real implementation is worth or not.

Three types of constituent codes have been considered for
NB
-
SC
decoders
[
18
]
: Rate-0 codes, Rate-1 codes and REP codes of size
N_{v}=4
. In the binary
CA-FSCL32
decoder, a fourth constituent code type, single parity check (SPC), is incorporated to improve the decoding efficiency. However, this constituent code is not used in the
NB
decoders due to its high computational complexity in the
NB
domain
[
18
]
. Furthermore, in the proposed
FSC-PA
decoder, the final layers involve a limited processing, resulting in a minimal computational load. As a result, incorporating the SPC constituent code in this context would offer negligible additional complexity reduction. For further details on fast decoding, the reader is referred to
[
7
,
8
,
18
]
.

However, it is worth noting that, in any
NB
-
SC
decoder, for Rate-1 constituent codes, the
N_{v}
propagated messages consist of a single element, the hard decision. In the case of Rate-0 codes, recursive processing is completely bypassed, as all associated nodes correspond to frozen bits and are assigned a predetermined symbol, typically zero. For repetition (REP) codes, all
N_{v}
nodes represent the same transmitted symbol; therefore, the
N_{v}

LLR
input vectors each of size
q
elements are aggregated through summation, and the symbol with the smallest
LLR
value is decided.

The complexity of a binary punctured
[
19
]

CA-FSCL32
decoder is evaluated for
K_{b}=Kp
and
N_{b}=Np
. The complexity of the
CA-FSCL32
decoder is assumed to be 32 more complex than that of the fast binary
SC
decoder. It is clearly an underestimate of the complexity, since
CA-FSCL32
requires many additional sorting operations between competing solutions. Without fast decoding, a binary
SC
decoder performs a total of
\frac{N^{{}^{\prime}}}{2}\log_{2}(N^{{}^{\prime}})
real additions with
N^{\prime}=2^{\lceil\log_{2}N_{b}\rceil}
. With fast decoding, the
N_{v}/2
additions and comparisons performed by the conventional code are omitted when rate-0 and rate-1 codes are substituted, replaced by
N_{v}
additions for REP code, and replaced by
N_{v}
comparators when SPC is substituted. The total number of additions performed by a
CA-FSCL32
decoder is provided in Table
I
.

Alternatively, in
NB
FSC-EMS decoders using the bubble check approach
[
20
]
, a
CN
that generates an output of size
n_{m}
requires
O^{\oplus}_{\text{EMS}}

GF
additions and
O^{+}_{\text{EMS}}

LLR
additions, which are given by:

\begin{gathered}O^{\oplus}_{\text{EMS}}=n_{m}\sqrt{n_{m}};\ O^{+}_{\text{EMS}}=n_{m}\sqrt{n_{m}}-2n_{m}+1.\end{gathered}

(22)

Furthermore, an
AEMS
-based
CN
requires candidates
O^{\oplus}_{\text{AEMS}}

GF
and candidates
O^{+}_{\text{AEMS}}

LLR
estimated as follows.

\begin{gathered}O^{\oplus}_{\text{AEMS}}=2(n_{L}+n_{H})-4;\ O^{+}_{\text{AEMS}}=n_{L}+n_{H}-3.\end{gathered}

(23)

On the other hand, an
FSC-PA

CN
has a unique intermediate matrix
T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}
depending on its corresponding layer
l
and cluster
s
. Therefore, the numbers of operations are:
O^{l\oplus}_{s_{\text{PA}}}

GF
additions and
O^{l+}_{s_{\text{PA}}}

LLR
candidates that can be expressed as:

\begin{gathered}O^{l\oplus}_{s_{\text{PA}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n^{(l)}_{s}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n^{(l)}_{s}-1}{\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}(i,j)};\ O^{l+}_{s_{\text{PA}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{(l)}_{s}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n^{(l)}_{s}-1}{\mathcal{R}^{(l)}_{s}(i,j)}.\end{gathered}

(24)

It is noticeable that the number of real additions is less than the field additions in all estimations. This is because the processing of the first row and column of any intermediate matrix (
T^{\prime}_{\Sigma}
or
T^{\prime(l)}_{\Sigma_{s}}
) requires only
GF
additions (the
LLR
value of
M_{H}(0)
and
M_{L}(0)
is always zero due to normalisation).

The total number of
GF
(denoted as
T^{\oplus}
) and
LLR
(denoted as
T^{+}
) additions performed by the FSC decoder (after applying fast decoding) is shown in table
I
for each code length
N
and rate
r
.

For the
VNs
processing, no optimisation or simplification is assumed for all
NB
decoders for ease of evaluation. A

10

q

\frac{N}{2}\log_{2}(N)q

VI
Conclusion

This paper introduces an
FSC-PA
decoder for
NB
-
PCs
, offering a simplified yet effective approach to reducing decoding complexity through per node clustering dedicated check node optimisation. The proposed
FSC-PA
decoder achieves a 60 % reduction in field additions and a 30 % reduction in real additions compared to the FSC-EMS decoder for codes over GF(64). Under
CCSK
modulation (a quasi-orthogonal modulation scheme) the proposed decoder outperforms the state-of-the-art binary
CA-FSCL32
decoder by up to 1.7 dB, while maintaining significantly lower complexity. In contrast, under
BPSK
modulation, the
CA-FSCL32
retains a performance advantage, albeit at the cost of higher computational complexity. The ability of the
FSC-PA
decoder to surpass
CA-FSCL32
under CCSK modulation (both in decoding performance and complexity) highlights its potential as a promising solution for low power wide area network.
