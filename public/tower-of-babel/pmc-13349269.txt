Authors’ reply- Educational and Socioeconomic Correlates of Stroke Risk Behaviours: Findings from SPRINT INDIA Trial
Authors: Shweta Jain Verma, Arya Devi, Jeyaraj D Pandian
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We thank the authors for their thoughtful and valuable comments on our article titled “Educational and Socioeconomic Correlates of Stroke Risk Behaviour: Findings from the SPRINT INDIA Trial.”[12] Our analysis was conducted as a post-hoc, exploratory assessment of educational strata within the SPRINT INDIA trial, which was originally powered to evaluate the effectiveness of a structured prevention intervention rather than to detect differences across educational subgroups.[34] Given the multiple comparisons performed across baseline characteristics, behavioral factors, and laboratory parameters, the findings should be interpreted cautiously as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.[5] We acknowledge that educational status is closely interlinked with demographic and socioeconomic variables such as age, sex, geographic region, occupation, and baseline stroke severity, which may influence outcomes.[67] In this context, we applied Chi-square tests and ANOVA for descriptive comparisons across groups, which we considered appropriate for an exploratory post-hoc analysis aimed at identifying potential associations rather than establishing causality. We agree that future studies incorporating pre-specified subgroup analyses and multivariable regression adjusting for key covariates will be necessary to confirm these findings.

We also recognize that the requirement for smartphone access for intervention delivery and the observed attrition at one-year follow-up may limit generalizability, particularly among lower-income or rural populations, as the intervention relied on mobile-based communication.[3] Although 71.1% of participants completed follow-up, exploratory assessments did not suggest differential attrition across educational strata or treatment arms; however, we agree that additional sensitivity analyses such as multiple imputation or inverse probability weighting could further strengthen the robustness of the findings. Educational attainment was categorized into three groups to maintain consistency with the main SPRINT INDIA trial and to ensure adequate sample sizes within each category, acknowledging that this approach may mask non-linear relationships but was necessary to balance interpretability with statistical stability. Similarly, the use of composite outcomes was aligned with the primary trial design to maintain consistency and statistical power, although individual outcome components were presented separately for transparency.

We acknowledge that certain continuous variables demonstrated wide variability, and although ANOVA was used as a standard comparative approach in this exploratory context, we recognize potential impact of skewness and outliers on statistical assumptions. This analysis was conducted at the participant level without modelling center-level clustering, as randomization was not stratified by site and the analysis remained descriptive in nature. Additionally, several outcomes were based on self- or caregiver-reported measures and may be subject to reporting bias. No separate sample size calculation was performed for education-based subgroup analyses, and education was used as a pragmatic proxy for socioeconomic status, which does not fully capture its multidimensional nature and may introduce residual confounding.

We agree that some interpretations in the ‘Discussion’, particularly regarding pathways linking education to outcomes such as medication adherence and lifestyle behaviors, are hypothesis-generating and not supported by formal mediation analyses. While education is a commonly used and relatively stable indicator in epidemiological research, the absence of direct measures such as income or wealth remains a limitation. We also appreciate the concern that our conclusions may appear to extend beyond the presented findings, particularly in suggesting education-tailored prevention strategies despite no significant differences in primary vascular outcomes; we intended to highlight observed differences in behavioral risk factors and engagement patterns, which may still have implications for secondary prevention, though these should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, we acknowledge that the analysis primarily relied on cross-sectional comparisons at follow-up, and that longitudinal or repeated-measures approaches would provide more robust insights into within-individual changes over time.

In summary, our study was intended to generate hypotheses and highlight the potential role of social determinants in stroke risk behaviors within the Indian context. We appreciate the reviewers’ insights, which have helped refine the interpretation of our findings, and we agree that future research incorporating robust statistical adjustments, longitudinal designs, and comprehensive socioeconomic measures will be essential to strengthen causal inference and inform targeted interventions.
