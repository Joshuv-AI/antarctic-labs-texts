Geng Liu, Junjie Mu, Li Feng, Mengxiao Zhu, Francesco Pierri

Abstract
. Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly integrated into search services, providing direct answers that can reduce users’ reliance on traditional result pages. Yet their factual reliability in non-English web ecosystems remains poorly understood, particularly when answering real user queries. We introduce a fact-checking dataset of 12 161 12\,161 Chinese Yes/No questions derived from real-world online search logs and develop a unified evaluation pipeline to compare three information-access paradigms: traditional search engines, standalone LLMs, and AI-generated overview modules. Our analysis reveals substantial differences in factual accuracy and topic-level variability across systems. By combining this performance with real-world Baidu Index statistics, we further estimate potential exposure to incorrect factual information of Chinese users across regions. These findings highlight structural risks in AI-mediated search and underscore the need for more reliable and transparent information-access tools for the digital world.

The World Economic Forum has indicated online misinformation and disinformation as the top global short-term risks in 2024 and 2025.

This shift from traditional retrieval toward AI-generated answers raises new concerns: users may receive fluent but incorrect responses, and the mechanisms driving these outputs remain largely opaque

.
Recent work highlights that AI-enhanced search engines may amplify misinformation risks, as users increasingly treat model-generated answers as authoritative despite their potential inaccuracies

Despite growing attention to AI-mediated search in English-language environments, little is known about how these systems behave in non-English ecosystems, particularly within China’s highly centralized search infrastructure.
Existing evaluations rely mainly on curated benchmarks rather than real user queries, leaving open questions about their reliability, consistency, when confronted with everyday factual questions

Prior work on automated fact-checking has focused primarily on English benchmarks such as FEVER

, with limited attention to real user queries or non-English ecosystems.
Research on Chinese search behavior has examined query logs and domain taxonomies
(

Meanwhile, studies using Baidu Index show that search patterns can reveal public information demand and health-related attention
(

To address these gaps, we investigate how factual information is mediated by different Chinese information-access paradigms. Figure

shows the pipeline of our work. We focus on two main research questions:

•

RQ1:
How reliably do Chinese search engines, standalone LLMs, and AI Overview systems answer factual Yes/No questions drawn from real user queries?

•

RQ2:
What is the potential exposure to misinformation of Chinese online users and how it is distributed geographically?

Our contributions are threefold.
First, we construct a large-scale factual query dataset grounded in real-world search behavior based on Chinese search logs, we extract
12\,161
verifiable Yes/No factual questions, creating the first dataset designed specifically to evaluate factual answering across Chinese information-access systems. This dataset moves beyond curated benchmarks by capturing the types of factual claims users actually search for.

Second, we introduce a unified evaluation framework that compares three major information-access paradigms—retrieval-based search engines (Baidu, Sogou, Bing China), standalone LLMs (Qwen, DeepSeek, LLaMA), and Baidu’s AI Overview, under controlled and comparable conditions.
Applying this framework reveals clear differences in the percentage of correct predictions and topic-level reliability across paradigms, providing the first systematic cross-system comparison within the Chinese web ecosystem.

Third, we connect system reliability with real-world information demand by combining health-related Baidu Index signals with model accuracy.
This yields a province-level exposure metric that highlights substantial geographic disparities in potential exposure to incorrect health information, pointing to structural inequalities in access to reliable factual content across China.

To facilitate reproducibility and future research, we will release the dataset, preprocessing scripts, and full evaluation pipeline after the review process.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Automated fact-checking

Automated fact-checking is traditionally framed as a pipeline of claim detection, evidence retrieval, and veracity prediction. This paradigm was consolidated with datasets such as FEVER
(
30
)
, and later extended with improved retrieval and entailment models
(
36
)
. However, these systems rely on curated English corpora and controlled evidence sources, limiting their applicability to real-world user queries.

Recent work has explored using LLMs for fact-checking, either as veracity classifiers or as automatic annotators. While LLMs can achieve moderate agreement with human judgments, they often hallucinate and behave inconsistently, raising concerns about their reliability in realistic settings
(
23
)
. Building on these concerns, DeVerna et al. show that even when LLMs are equipped with explicit reasoning steps and web-search evidence, they still struggle to reliably verify political claims without curated context
(
9
)
.

Closer to our study, Fontana et al. evaluate open-source LLMs and show that they perform well on claims already covered by fact-checking articles but struggle with fresh factual news
(
12
)
. Overall, existing evidence suggests that LLMs remain limited when applied beyond curated English benchmarks.

Most of these studies focus on English claims and fact-checking corpora, whereas much less is known about how LLMs and search-based tools behave on real user queries in the Chinese web ecosystem. Our work addresses this gap by evaluating search engines, standalone LLMs, and AI Overview modules on a large set of Chinese Yes/No factual questions derived from real search logs.

2.2.
Analysis of search engine queries

Prior work has examined how search queries capture users’ information needs and has introduced a range of topical taxonomies. Foundational analyses of Google search logs identify broad query categories such as health, entertainment, commerce, and navigation
(
5
)
. More recent studies extend this line of work by analyzing domain-specific behaviors and the reliability of health-related search interactions
(
1
;
25
;
27
;
15
;
2
)
. In the Chinese context, related research proposes comparable taxonomies: Ye et al. examine Sogou search behavior during the COVID-19 pandemic
(
34
)
, and the T2Ranking dataset provides structured domain labels across education, healthcare, IT, and finance
(
33
)
. We adopt and refine the T2Ranking taxonomy in our study to better represent the topical distribution of real Chinese search queries.

2.3.
Baidu Index and Public Information Demand

Baidu Index is widely used to monitor public attention and information-seeking behavior in China, providing regional and temporal signals that reflect large-scale search interest. Prior studies have used the platform to track population responses during health crises
(
13
;
32
)
, environmental and societal risks, and mental health and well-being
(
28
)
. These findings demonstrate that search-volume patterns closely approximate real-world information demand and reveal substantial geographic inequalities.

Despite these applications, Baidu Index has rarely been connected to evaluations of system-level reliability. Existing research measures what people search for, but not whether regions with higher search demand are more exposed to inaccurate or misleading answers. Our work bridges this gap by linking Baidu Index search intensity with factual accuracy measurements from search engines and LLMs, enabling a geography-aware assessment of disparities in access to reliable information across China.

3.
Data and Methods

3.1.
Data Collection

To construct a dataset suitable for evaluating automated fact-checking performance in Chinese, we build on the publicly available T2Ranking dataset, which is derived from real user search logs collected by Sogou
(
33
)
.
Sogou is one of the major Chinese web search engines, and its large-scale query logs have been widely adopted
(
35
)
in the information retrieval literature.
Because T2Ranking contains authentic search queries paired with human-annotated passages, it provides a reliable foundation for extracting verifiable Yes/No factual questions and for studying system behavior on real-world information needs.

This dataset contains
307\,706
users’ queries and corresponding snippets retrieved from the search engine, with the goal of providing a comprehensive benchmark for training and evaluating passage retrieval and re-ranking algorithms in Chinese information retrieval.
Each passage was annotated by professional annotators on a four-level relevance scale (Level 0–Level 3), with
Level 0
indicating that the passage bears no meaningful relation to the query and offers no useful information for answering it and
Level 3
indicating that the passage fully meets the information need and explicitly contains the answer.
We retained only queries that can be expressed in binary (‘‘Yes’’ or ‘‘No’’) form and involve verifiable factual claims.
This choice enables objective and reproducible evaluation, as binary questions admit a definitive answer and minimize interpretive ambiguity across systems. To ensure that each Yes/No judgment was grounded in explicit evidence, we further restricted the dataset to queries whose associated passages were labeled as Level 3 — passages that fully satisfy the information need and directly contain the factual answer.
We further ensured that each retained query–passage pair could be unambiguously resolved as either ‘‘Yes’’ or ‘‘No,’’ based on the evidence contained in the passage.
In particular, we employed DeepSeek
1
1

1

https://api-docs.deepseek.com/
, to assist with Yes/No identification, fact-checking classification, and answer labeling during preprocessing. To validate the reliability of these model-assisted annotations, we manually reviewed a stratified subset of samples at each step, ensuring that the resulting dataset reflects consistent and high-quality factual judgments.

3.1.1.
Query Classification

To identify which user queries are relevant for factual verification, we first instructed DeepSeek to classify each query into two types: those that can be answered with a Yes/No response and those that require a more elaborate, open-ended answer.
Within the Yes/No category, we further filtered out opinion-based or subjective questions to retain only those that involve factual verification.
This filtering step isolates objectively verifiable queries, which serve as the basis for our subsequent fact-checking analysis.
Prompt templates are reported in Appendix
A.1
and
A.3
.

3.1.2.
Answer Labeling

For each fact-checking query, we determine whether the associated passage provides a verifiable answer by prompting DeepSeek to assign one of three labels:
Yes
,
No
, or
Uncertain
. A passage is labeled
Yes
if it directly supports the proposition in the query,
No
if it explicitly contradicts it, and
Uncertain
if it lacks sufficient information to infer a factual judgment.

To illustrate the labeling criteria, consider the following representative cases. For the query
“Does malnutrition cause myopia?”
, a passage stating that
“malnutrition can affect ocular development and increase the risk of myopia”
constitutes affirmative evidence (
Yes
). For the query
“Does fluorescence imaging require fasting?”
, a passage noting that the procedure
typically does not require fasting”
provides a negative answer (
No
). When the retrieved text contains content unrelated to the factual claim—for example, administrative information about business licenses—it is labeled
Uncertain
.

This procedure enforces a consistent interpretation of evidence across all query–passage pairs and constrains the LLM to operate strictly on the retrieved content. The full prompt template is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

3.1.3.
Topic Definition

To examine the topic distribution of fact-checking queries, we adopt the domain taxonomy from T2Ranking, refining several broad categories to improve interpretability.
Core domains (e.g., “Education”, “Health”) are retained, while the residual “General” class is subdivided into “Society” and “Finance”.
We further rename “E-commerce” to “Shopping” and add an “Adult” domain, consistent with prior work
(
16
;
5
)
.
The final taxonomy includes ten top-level domains, with prompt design details in Appendix
A.4
.

3.1.4.
Manual Evaluation of LLM Annotations for Pre-processing

To assess the reliability of the LLM-assisted labeling, three annotators manually reviewed a stratified subset of samples from each preprocessing stage. Agreement was measured against the majority human label. In the Yes/Noclassification task (
100
queries;
50
Yes/No and
50
non Yes/No), agreement with the LLM was approximately 95%. For fact-checking classification (
100
queries;
50
factual and
50
non-factual), all factual labels matched the LLM output, but 24 of the non-factual queries were incorrectly labeled as factual. Given this substantial error rate, all LLM-labeled non–fact-checking queries were removed from subsequent analyses. We also validated answer labeling (
33
Yes,
33
No,
34
Uncertain) and topic labeling (
20
queries per domain), both of which achieved agreement rates close to 95%.

After applying all filtering and validation steps, the resulting dataset consists of
12\,161
verified fact-checking queries, each associated with a definitive “Yes” or “No” answer.
As shown in Figure
2
, these queries are highly concentrated in the Health domain, with Technology and Finance forming the next largest groups, while categories such as Entertainment, Adult, and Sports appear much less frequently.
This skewed distribution highlights that real-world factual information needs on the Chinese web are dominated by health-related concerns, with a long tail of less common topics.

3.2.
Automated Fact-Checking Techniques

Given the integration of retrieval and generation in modern search engines, we design a unified framework to evaluate three paradigms: online search engines, zero-shot LLMs, and Baidu’s AI Overview.
These paradigms correspond to retrieval-based, generative, and hybrid mechanisms, representing how users access factual information.
To ensure comparability, each system is tested using the same set of real user queries.
We then evaluate whether each module provides a correct Yes/No answer. Because search engines often return multiple results, we focus on the top
10
entries and employ an LLM-based agent to determine whether the retrieved passages actually answer the query.
We explicitly instruct the model to rely solely on the retrieved web content when answering the query.
Conceptually, AI Overview systems operate as hybrid RAG-style pipelines that combine internal retrieval with large language model generation.
However, their retrieval mechanisms are opaque, and we cannot verify whether the evidence they use corresponds to the top-ranked documents returned by the search engine, or whether additional internal sources are incorporated.
As a result, AI Overview should be considered as a black-box hybrid system rather than a controlled retrieval-based module.

Such a design ensures a fair comparison of factual answering abilities across different paradigms.

3.2.1.
Online search engines

To assess how Chinese search engines handle fact-checking, we designed a multi-stage evaluation pipeline that simulates real-world information retrieval by submitting each query to Baidu, Bing, and Sogou — the three most popular Chinese search engines in China
2
2

2

https://gs.statcounter.com/search-engine-market-share/all/china/
.

To simulate standard user search behavior when using search engines, we employed a locally deployed SearXNG
3
3

3

https://docs.searxng.org/
instance to retrieve search results through its API interface.
Given that users typically focus on the first page results
(
11
)
, we restricted our analysis to the top
10
ranked results.
The system returned both the titles and content snippets of the retrieved entries.
Subsequently, we performed preprocessing on the collected webpage content, including the removal of redundant characters and conversion to a usable plain-text format.
This setup allows us to provide a clean textual basis for the subsequent evaluation process.

Our search engine evaluation pipeline consists of three primary stages: data preprocessing, passage and answer classification, and result aggregation.

In the first stage, we extract textual content from the retrieved webpages using lightweight web scraping based on the BeautifulSoup library.
4
4

4

https://pypi.org/project/beautifulsoup4/

We remove navigation elements, advertisements, and scripts to obtain clean page text.
When a page cannot be parsed—for example due to dynamic rendering or missing HTML—we rely on Firecrawl
5
5

5

https://www.firecrawl.dev/
as a fallback tool.
Firecrawl provides a plain-text version of the page, which we further clean by removing automatically extracted URLs.
This ensures a consistent textual representation for downstream classification.

In the second stage, we leverage the
Qwen-Plus

6
6

6

https://help.aliyun.com/zh/model-studio/what-is-qwen-llm
model’s reading comprehension capabilities to determine whether each retrieved passage addresses the given question.
We designed a two-category classification prompt that tasks the model with categorizing each passage as: (A) answering the question, (B) contradicting or providing content inconsistent with the question.
The prompt template used was (see Appendix
A.5
for details):

•

“You are a fact-checking assistant. Your task is to determine the answer tendency for a given question based on the webpage content. Question: query Webpage content: web Criteria: Choose “Yes” if the webpage content clearly supports, confirms, or affirms the answer to the question. Choose “No” if the webpage content clearly opposes, denies, or refutes the answer to the question. Note: Make your judgment solely based on the provided webpage content; do not use your background knowledge. Please choose one of the following options and reply with the corresponding word only: Yes No”.

Finally, we aggregate the classified passages using a simple majority vote over the “Yes” and “No” labels. For each query–engine pair, the label that appears most frequently is assigned as the engine’s tendency.

To evaluate correctness, we compare this engine-level tendency with the ground truth Yes/No label derived from the dataset.

3.2.4.
Baidu AI Overview

Baidu, China’s largest search engine, also offers an AI-generated overview feature similar to Google’s AI Overview.
We collected these summaries using the same set of factual Yes/No queries.
Because Baidu AI Overview responses often contain explanatory text rather than explicit binary answers, we use the DeepSeek model to infer the overall answer.
The model is prompted to classify each summary as “Yes,” “No,” based on its dominant stance toward the query.

This procedure allows us to evaluate how AI-powered search features respond to factual questions in a consistent and comparable way.

3.3.
Estimating Potential Exposure to Misinformation via Baidu Index

To contextualize user information needs and estimate potential exposure to misinformation, we use Baidu Index as a proxy for public search interest across health-related domains.
Baidu Index is a public analytics service that reports temporal and regional search-volume statistics for user-defined keywords on the Baidu search engine.
9
9

9

https://index.baidu.com
We collect province-level Baidu Index data from 2020-09-19 to 2025-09-19.
Using a multi-year window provides a stable measure of baseline health-information demand,
smoothing out short-term fluctuations and seasonal cycles, while remaining recent enough to capture
contemporary search patterns relevant to our analysis.
Our goal is to characterize long-run differences in provincial information demand—not to reconstruct
search behavior for a specific year—making a five-year window appropriate for the exposure metric.

For each of the ten top-level topics identified in our dataset, we constructed a set of representative keywords grounded in the fact-checking queries and collected their daily Baidu Index values using an open-source crawler.
10
10

10

https://github.com/longxiaofei/spider-BaiduIndex

The resulting dataset includes province-level time series for all
34
, enabling an examination of geographic variation in public attention.

Health-related search queries represent a high-stakes information domain: inaccurate, misleading, or low-quality online health information can influence medical decision-making and, in some cases, expose users to harmful recommendations
(
29
;
4
;
24
;
11
)
.
For each province
p
, we therefore compute an aggregate measure of health-related attention, denoted
I_{p}
, by averaging its daily Baidu Index values.

To connect public information demand with the factual reliability of different retrieval and summarization tools, we derive a simple province-level exposure metric:

R_{p,m}=I_{p}\times A_{m},

where
I_{p}
captures average health-related search interest in province
p
, and
A_{m}
reflects the factual accuracy of system
m
(e.g., search engines or AI-generated overviews) on health queries.
This metric provides an interpretable indicator of how strongly public demand for health information interacts with the reliability of each system, highlighting where high user interest coincides with lower-accuracy tools and thus greater potential exposure to misinformation.

Potential Exposure to Misinformation Across Chinese Provinces

To assess how factual reliability interacts with real-world information demand, and thus estimate the potential impact of misinformation, we compute a province-level exposure index in the Health domain by combining Baidu Search Index signals with system accuracy.

We focus on health because it represents nearly half of all factual queries in our dataset (Figure

Although absolute index values differ across systems due to variation in accuracy, the spatial structure of the exposure distribution remains highly consistent across paradigms. Mid-ranked provinces exhibit only minor differences across systems, and the overall provincial ordering is largely preserved.

Because the exposure index jointly reflects (i) the intensity of health-related search demand and (ii) the accuracy of system responses, these results indicate that potential misinformation exposure is not geographically uniform. Instead, informational risks disproportionately concentrate in high-demand regions, highlighting how structural inequalities in information access can amplify the societal impact of factual errors.

5.
Discussion

This work provides an empirical characterization of how major Chinese information-access systems handle real factual queries.
Using
12\,161
Yes/No questions derived from authentic search logs, we show that factual information needs on the Chinese web concentrate heavily in a small number of domains—especially health—and that errors are systematic rather than random.
Systems exhibit clear blind spots across both topics and answer polarities (“Yes” vs. “No”), highlighting structural weaknesses in current web-based information services.

Our unified evaluation of retrieval-based search engines, standalone LLMs, and Baidu’s AI Overview reveals distinct error profiles across paradigms.
Traditional search engines display strong biases, most notably Bing’s tendency toward negative answers, while LLaMA is limited primarily by a high refusal rate.
Even the strongest-performing systems, including Qwen, DeepSeek, and AI Overview, reach only moderate factual percentage of correct predictions (60–70%) and show substantial topic-level variation.
These results indicate that systems occupy different regions of the accuracy–bias–refusal space and should not be treated as interchangeable tools for factual verification within the web ecosystem.

By combining system accuracy with Baidu Index signals in the Health domain, we further link factual reliability to real-world patterns of information demand.
The resulting exposure metric reveals pronounced spatial inequalities: eastern coastal provinces have substantially higher potential exposure to incorrect answers, while many central and western regions exhibit lower exposure levels.
These findings demonstrate that informational risks are unevenly distributed across geographic and socioeconomic contexts, and illustrate how web-mediated information systems can amplify existing regional disparities.

The implications of these results span users, platforms, and research.
For users, the wide variability in accuracy across systems and topics underscores that neither traditional search nor AI-generated answers can be assumed to be uniformly reliable.
Users may be particularly vulnerable when systems present confident but systematically biased responses.
For platforms, the modest accuracy of all systems—even those integrating retrieval and generation—highlights persistent limitations in web-scale factual reasoning and the need for monitoring topic-level variability.
For research, our study emphasizes the importance of evaluating factual reliability on real-world, non-English queries and within specific national ecosystems.
The dataset and pipeline introduced here provide a foundation for future work on Chinese-language fact-checking and cross-platform comparisons, supporting responsible deployment of GenAI and web agents in high-stakes contexts.

This study has several limitations.
While part of the dataset was manually validated, full human annotation was infeasible, and reliance on LLM-assisted labeling in the preprocessing phase may introduce (circular) noise.
Retrieval gaps produced uneven system coverage, limiting fully parallel comparisons.
The exposure metric captures search activity rather than demographic composition, meaning that Baidu Index values may reflect population size as well as information demand.
Finally, despite preprocessing efforts, residual webpage boilerplate or noise may still influence model judgments. Moreover, the Bing results obtained through our self-hosted SearXNG instance are not fully reliable, as it occasionally failed to forward complete Chinese queries, producing incomplete or mismatched retrieval.

Future work should compare Chinese-language factual reliability across international platforms such as Google Search and Google AI Overview, enabling a broader understanding of how domestic and global information-access ecosystems differ in their handling of Chinese factual queries.
Beyond cross-platform comparisons, future research should explore richer forms of factual reasoning that extend beyond binary verification, including multi-hop inference, time-sensitive claims, ambiguous or partially answerable questions, and scenarios where retrieval and generation must be jointly evaluated.
Another important direction is to incorporate larger-scale human evaluation, particularly in high-stakes domains such as health, finance, and public safety where the societal consequences of misinformation are most severe.
It would also be valuable to study how users interact with AI-mediated search results in real settings, and how interface design, ranking, and explanation mechanisms influence trust, attention, and susceptibility to factual errors.

6.
Ethical Use of Data

All data used in this study are publicly available and contain no personally identifiable information.

The T2Ranking dataset includes anonymized search queries and passages, and our additional collection of search engine results relies solely on publicly accessible interfaces without bypassing platform restrictions. Web pages were retrieved only for research purposes and were used in compliance with the respective terms of service. Our work does not involve human subjects or sensitive user data. The small amount of manual annotation performed in the dataset construction relied exclusively on de-identified text. In line with ACM’s policy on research involving human participants, this study poses no privacy or ethical risks.

Case 1: Evaluation of Online Search Engines

Example 3: Evaluation of Online Search Engines

Query (zh):

碘伏可以用酒精代替吗？

Query (en):
“Can alcohol be used as a substitute for povidone-iodine?”

Ground Truth:
No.

Result:
For this query, Qwen and DeepSeek answer
No
, and LLaMA gives an uncertain response. A qualitative check shows that the retrieved search result focuses on a different question—“

碘伏和酒精一起用可以吗？” (“Can povidone-iodine and alcohol be used together?”). Because that page stresses that the two should not be used simultaneously, the models are biased toward
No
, despite the fact that this does not address the substitution question. This illustrates how search retrieval can induce semantic drift, causing LLMs to follow the retrieved content rather than the user’s query.

Case 2: Evaluation of LLMs

Query (zh):

Result:
All three LLMs (LLaMA, DeepSeek, and Qwen) incorrectly answer

. This consistent misconception suggests that the models may be constrained by their knowledge boundaries: while dogs do not possess human-like nasal hair, their nasal cavity does contain small vibrissae-like structures, which may cause the models to overgeneralize from human anatomy or related species. As a result, the LLMs misinterpret the query and produce an incorrect answer.
