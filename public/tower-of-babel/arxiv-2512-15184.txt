Continuous data assimilation for 2D stochastic Navier–Stokes equations
Hakima Bessaih, Benedetta Ferrario, Oussama Landoulsi and Margherita Zanella

Abstract
. Continuous data assimilation methods, such as the nudging algorithm introduced by Azouani, Olson, and Titi (AOT) [ 2 ] , are known to be highly effective in deterministic settings for asymptotically synchronizing approximate solutions with observed dynamics. In this work, we extend this framework to a stochastic regime by considering the two-dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes equations subject to either additive or multiplicative noise. We establish sufficient conditions on the nudging parameter and the spatial observation scale that guarantee convergence of the nudged solution to the true stochastic flow. In the case of multiplicative noise, convergence holds in expectation, with exponential or polynomial rates depending on the growth of the noise covariance. For additive noise, we obtain the exponential convergence both in expectation and pathwise. These results yield a stochastic generalization of the AOT theory, demonstrating how the interplay between random forcing, viscous dissipation and feedback control governs synchronization in stochastic fluid systems.

1.
Introduction

Accurate computational prediction of nonlinear dynamical systems, such as fluid flows governed by the Navier-Stokes equations, faces two fundamental challenges: the initialization problem and the maintenance of long-time accuracy. The initialization problem arises because the complete initial state of the system is typically unknown or only partially observed with limited precision. The long-time accuracy problem concerns the accumulation of modeling and numerical errors during the systems evolution, which may lead to divergence from the true state.

Data assimilation provides a rigorous framework to address these challenges by combining observational measurements with the governing dynamical equations to produce reliable approximations of the true system state. The classical method of continuous data assimilation (CDA), as described by Daley
[
12
]
, consists of directly incorporating observational measurements into a model as it is being integrated in time.
A more recent approach, introduced by Azouani, Olson, and Titi (AOT) in
[
2
]
, reformulates CDA as a feedback-control (or nudging) mechanism, inspired by ideas from control theory
[
3
]
. In this formulation, an additional relaxation term is introduced to nudge the model solution toward the observed data. The resulting system generates an approximate trajectory that converges asymptotically to the reference (true) solution. Under suitable assumptions on the data and model regularity, convergence can be shown to occur at an exponential rate. This algorithm offers a practical and efficient approach to estimating the current and future states of a dynamical system when only sparse observational data are available.

The AOT algorithm has since been rigorously analyzed for a wide range of physical models, confirming its effectiveness in diverse settings. Examples include the Navier-Stokes equations with noisy data
[
5
]
and
[
7
]
, discrete data assimilation schemes
[
20
]
, miscible displacement in porous media
[
4
]
, the 3D Navier-Stokes-
\alpha
model
[
1
]
, the 2D Rayleigh–Bénard convection problem
[
14
,
16
,
15
]
, the surface quasi-geostrophic equation
[
26
,
25
]
, the Korteweg de Vries equation
[
27
]
, the 2D magnetohydrodynamic system
[
6
]
, the 3D Brinkman–Forchheimer–extended Darcy model
[

]
, the 3D primitive equations
[

]
, the 3D Leray–

[

]
, the Voigt relaxation of the 2D Navier–Stokes equations
[

]
and the 3D Ladyzhenskaya model
[

]
.

Let us briefly recall the general structure of the continuous data assimilation algorithm. Let

u=u(t)
denote the unknown true state of a dynamical system governed by the equation

\frac{{\rm d}u(t)}{{\rm d}t}=F(u(t)),\qquad t>0

with an unknown initial condition
u(0)
.
Observations are available only at a coarse spatial scale through an interpolant operator
R_{h}(u(t))
, where
h>0

denotes the observation resolution. The CDA algorithm constructs an approximate solution of the modified equation

\frac{{\rm d}U(t)}{{\rm d}t}=F(U(t))+\mu R_{h}(u(t)-U(t)),

where
\mu>0
is the nudging parameter. Under suitable conditions on
\mu
and
h
it can be shown that

U(t)
converges to
u(t)
as
t\to+\infty
, for any initial data
u(0)
and
U(0)
.
The estimates controlling the difference between
U
and
u
are often referred to as Foias–Prodi estimates, and they typically guarantee exponential decay in time provided
h
and
\mu
satisfy appropriate bounds. The choice of
R_{h}
is flexible and may correspond to projections onto low Fourier modes, local spatial averages, or nodal values. A standard example is the projection onto Fourier modes with wavenumbers
|k|\leq 1/h
.
A physically relevant example is given by spatial volume averages over partitions of the domain, as studied in
[
28
,
29
,
21
]
.

Most rigorous results on continuous data assimilation have been developed in the deterministic setting (see
[
2
,
12
]
and references therein). The stochastic setting, where either the model dynamics or the observations are affected by random perturbations, has received comparatively less attention (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
,
[
7
]
); for the numerical results see
[
24
,
10
]
and the references therein. Nevertheless, stochastic modeling is essential for realistic applications, as measurement noise and unresolved fluctuations are intrinsic features of physical systems.

In the stochastic framework, the system evolution is described by a stochastic differential equation (SDE) of the form

{\rm d}u(t)=F(u(t))\,{\rm d}t+G(u(t))\,{\rm d}W(t),

or

{\rm d}u(t)=F(u(t))\,{\rm d}t+G\,{\rm d}W(t).

Here
W(t)
is a cylindrical Wiener process and
G
a suitable operator representing the noise structure, which may depend on the solution or not, thus distinguishing between multiplicative and additive noise.

The corresponding data assimilation equations take, respectively, the forms

{\rm d}U(t)=\left[F(U(t))+\mu R_{h}(u(t)-U(t))\right]\,{\rm d}t+G(u(t))\,{\rm d}W(t),

or

{\rm d}U(t)=\left[F(U(t))+\mu R_{h}(u(t)-U(t))\right]\,{\rm d}t+G\,{\rm d}W(t).

In this context, convergence analysis becomes more subtle due to the stochastic forcing. One may study convergence either pathwise (almost surely, for each realization of the noise) or in expectation (in terms of statistical averages). When the noise is additive, the stochastic terms cancel in the evolution of the difference

u(t)-U(t)
, which allows one to establish pathwise exponential convergence as
t\to+\infty
, for any initial data
u(0)
and
U(0)
.
For multiplicative noise, this cancellation no longer occurs, and convergence is typically obtained in expectation, with explicit polynomial or exponential rates depending on the noise growth.

To illustrate these ideas, we focus on the two-dimensional incompressible stochastic Navier-Stokes equations, which provide a canonical testbed: they are analytically tractable yet retain the essential nonlinear features of realistic fluid models. The stochastic formulation offers a natural way to incorporate random perturbations representing unresolved scales, measurement noise, or uncertain forcing. Specifically, we consider a velocity field
u

evolving on a bounded two-dimensional domain
D

with no-slip Dirichlet boundary conditions, that satisfies an equation driven by a multiplicative noise:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm d}u(t)-\nu\Delta u(t)\,{\rm d}t+(u(t)\cdot\nabla)u(t)\,{\rm d}t+\nabla p\,{\rm d}t=f\,{\rm d}t+G(u(t))\,{\rm d}W(t),\\
\text{div }u(t)=0,\\
u(0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

or an equation perturbed by an additive noise

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{\rm d}u(t)-\nu\Delta u(t)\,{\rm d}t+(u(t)\cdot\nabla)u(t)\,{\rm d}t+\nabla p\,{\rm d}t=f\,{\rm d}t+G\,{\rm d}W(t),\\
\text{div }u(t)=0,\\
u(0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

Here the unknowns are the velocity
u
and the pressure
p
.
The data are the kinematic viscosity
\nu>0
, the forcing terms (
f
is the deterministic one) and the initial velocity.
The stochastic term

W(t)
represents random external influences, such as fluctuating wind or temperature variations, and will be modeled as a cylindrical Wiener process. The precise assumptions on
f
,
G
, and
W

are specified in the following sections.

1.1.
Summary of contributions, challenges and main results

In this work, we first review the deterministic data assimilation results given in
[
2
]
and then present new results for the stochastic setting, distinguishing between additive and multiplicative noise.
The stochastic setting introduces additional challenges, due to the presence of noise and the inherent uncertainty in the system’s evolution.
We will investigate the convergence of the solution
U
of the nudging equation towards the solution
u
of the true stochastic Navier–Stokes equations, as time goes to infinity.

We begin with the case of multiplicative noise, which represents the most general and technically demanding scenario. In this setting, we establish convergence in expectation of
U
to
u
, with explicit rates that depend crucially on the growth properties of the noise coefficient
G(u)
. The proof relies on uniform-in-time a priori estimates for the solution. The convergence behavior of the assimilation algorithm is shown to depend sensitively on the growth conditions imposed on

G(u)
. When
G
is bounded, we obtain exponential convergence in expectation as time goes to infinity. If

G
exhibits sublinear growth, convergence in expectation still holds, though with a polynomial decay rate of arbitrary order

p>0
. In the case of linear growth of
G
, convergence persists but only with a polynomial rate, and for

p
restricted to a certain range that depends explicitly on the viscosity
\nu
and the intensity of the additive component of the noise. In this regime, ensuring convergence requires a structural condition relating the dissipativity of the system to the strength of the stochastic forcing.

These results highlight the greater mathematical complexity of the multiplicative noise framework, where one must balance the destabilizing influence of the stochastic forcing against the stabilizing mechanisms induced by nudging and viscous dissipation. In this case, since the noise term depends explicitly on the state, when taking the difference between
U
and
u
, the stochastic contributions do not vanish pathwise. Instead, the noise disappears only after taking the expectation, which leads to convergence results in expected value.

We then turn to the case of additive noise, where the situation is considerably simpler. The estimates in expectation follow as a direct corollary of the bounded multiplicative noise case. Moreover, when considering the difference between
U
and
u
, the additive noise terms cancel out almost surely, allowing for a fully pathwise analysis. This enables us to establish a pathwise exponential convergence result.

These fundamental differences between the multiplicative and additive noise settings reflect the distinct mathematical challenges posed by the two noise structures, and influence the choice of analytical tools and assumptions required to obtain rigorous data assimilation results.
Taken together, these findings can be viewed as a stochastic generalization of the classical results by
[
2
]
. In particular, the conditions on the nudging parameters
\mu
and the spatial resolution
h
now explicitly depend on the intensity of the stochastic forcing. When the noise intensity is set to zero, one recovers, modulo a constant, the deterministic convergence conditions of the original framework
[
2
]
.

We emphasize that the techniques employed in this work to analyze stochastic data assimilation are closely related to those developed for the study of invariant measures, particularly in establishing uniqueness and asymptotic stability. Indeed, both contexts share the fundamental objective of understanding the long-time behavior of stochastic dynamical systems. In this article, we show that the same analytical tools used in the study of invariant measures, especially in the hypoelliptic setting (see e.g.
[
23
]
,
[
31
]
,
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
), can be effectively adapted to address challenges arising in stochastic data assimilation.

We can summarize our main contributions as follows.

(i)

In the case of multiplicative noise,

–

if the noise coefficient
G(u)
is bounded, under suitable assumptions on the nudging parameter
\mu
and the spatial resolution
h
(depending on the viscosity
\nu
, as well as on the deterministic and stochastic forcings), we prove that (see Theorem
4.2
(i))

\mathbb{E}\,\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}^{2}\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{exponentially fast as }t\to+\infty.

–

In the case of multiplicative noise, if the noise coefficient
G(u)
exhibits sublinear growth, then, under appropriate conditions on
\mu
and
h
(depending on the viscosity
\nu
, as well as on the deterministic and stochastic forcings), we obtain (see Theorem
4.2
(ii))

\mathbb{E}\,\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}^{2}\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{$p$-polynomially fast for any }p\in(0,+\infty)\text{ as }t\to+\infty.

–

In the case of multiplicative noise, if the noise coefficient
G(u)
exhibits linear growth, and again under suitable conditions on
\mu
and
h
(depending on the viscosity
\nu
, as well as on the deterministic and stochastic forcings), we show that (see Theorem
4.2
(iii))

\mathbb{E}\,\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}^{2}\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{$p$-polynomially fast as }t\to+\infty,

for exponents
p
lying in a range that depends explicitly on the viscosity
\nu
and on the intensity of the stochastic forcing.

(ii)

In the case of additive noise, we prove that the solution
U
of the nudging system converges to the true solution
u

both in expectation and pathwise, exponentially fast as
t\to+\infty
, provided that
\mu
and
h
satisfy suitable conditions (depending on the viscosity
\nu
, as well as on the deterministic and stochastic forcings). See Theorems
5.2
and
5.3
.

We specify that exponential convergence means that the limit is dominated by a term of the kind
\tilde{C}e^{-Ct}
for suitable positive constants
C
and
\tilde{C}
; similarly,
p
-polynomial decay means that we have a bound in the limit with a
term of the kind
\frac{C}{t^{p}}
for suitable positive constant
C
.

2.3.
Assumptions on the stochastic term

Eventually, let us deal with the stochastic term.
On the normal filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0},\mathbb{P})
, we consider a cylindrical Wiener process
W
with values in some separable Hilbert space
U
.
For convenience, we fix once and for all a complete orthonormal system

\{u_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
on
U
.
We recall that, as a cylindrical process on
U
,
W
admits the following formal representation

(2.3)

W=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\beta_{k}u_{k},

where
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a family of real and independent Brownian motions. It is well known that (
2.3
) does not converge in

. Nonetheless, there always exists a larger Hilbert space

, such that

with Hilbert–Schmidt embedding

as a

-Wiener process on

, with
Q^{0}=\iota\circ\iota^{*}
being trace-class on
U_{0}
(see
[
33
, §2.5.1]
).
In the following, we may implicitly assume this extension by simply saying that
W
is a cylindrical process on
U
. Similarly, one deals with the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}G(s)\,{\rm d}W(s)
(see
[
33
, §2.5.2]
).

2.3.1.
The multiplicative noise case

When dealing with a multiplicative type noise, that is a noise that depends on the solution, we impose the following conditions on the covariance operator, i.e. on the operator
G
appearing in equation (
1.1
).

Assumption 2.1
.

G:{H}\to L_{HS}(U,H)
is a Lipschitz continuous operator, i.e.

(2.4)

\exists\ L>0:\quad\|G(u)-G(v)\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\leq L\|u-v\|^{2}_{H}\qquad\forall u,v\in H.

Assumption 2.2
.

One of the following conditions is in force.

(B)

[Bounded noise]
There exists a positive constant
K_{B}
such that

\|G(u)\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\leq K_{B},\quad\forall u\in H.

(S)

[Sublinear noise]
There exist non negative constants
K_{S},\widetilde{K_{S}}
and
\gamma\in(0,1)
such that

\|G(u)\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\leq K_{S}+\widetilde{K_{S}}\|u\|_{H}^{2\gamma},\quad\forall u\in H.

(L)

[Linear noise]

There exist non negative constants
K_{L},\widetilde{K_{L}}
such that

\|G(u)\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\leq K_{L}+\widetilde{K_{L}}\|u\|^{2}_{H},\quad\forall u\in H.

In the sequel, when we say that Assumption
2.2
holds we mean that
one of the three assumptions (B),(S) or (L) holds.
Concrete examples of operators
G
satisfying the above conditions can be found in
[
18
, Example 2.4]
.
Notice that

(\bf L)\Longrightarrow(\bf S)\Longrightarrow(\bf B)
; so we could consider only part
(\bf L)
in Assumption

2.2
. However the convergence results are different in the three cases and with assumption

(\bf L)
we obtain the most restrictive result.

2.3.2.
The additive noise case

When dealing with an additive type noise, that is a noise that does not depend on the solution, we

is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator from

into

2.4.
Interpolant operators

In the absence of the knowledge of the initial velocity

, the data assimilation method for

which interpolates its input function at a length scale

\left\|\phi-R_{h}(\phi)\right\|_{[L^{2}(D)]}^{2}\leq c_{0}h^{2}\left\|\phi\right\|_{[H^{1}(D)]^{2}}^{2},

A physically relevant example of an interpolant which satisfies (

Specifically, let

R_{h}(\varphi)(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{h}}\overline{\varphi}_{j}\,\boldsymbol{1}_{Q_{j}}(x),

where

\overline{\varphi}_{j}=\frac{1}{|Q_{j}|}\int_{Q_{j}}\varphi(x)dx

The orthogonal projection onto the Fourier modes, with wave numbers k
such that

is an another example of an interpolant operator which satisfies the approximation property

3.
Data assimilation in the deterministic framework

Rigorous analysis of the nudging algorithm for partial differential equations in fluid mechanics began with
the work of Azouani, Olson and Titi
[
2
]
, where the authors estimate threshold values for the relaxation parameter
\mu
and data resolution

for the two dimensional incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.

More in details, in
[
2

the authors consider the Navier-Stokes equations

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\nu\Delta u+\left(u\cdot\nabla\right)u+\nabla p\,=f\\
{\rm{div}}\;u=0,\\
u(0)=u_{0},\end{cases}

on an open, bounded and connected set
D
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with

-boundary, endowed with no-slip Dirichlet boundary conditions.
They introduce the nudging equation as

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}U-\nu\Delta U+\left(U\cdot\nabla\right)U+\nabla p\,=f-\mu R_{h}(U-u)\\
{\rm{div}}\;U=0,\\
U(0)=U_{0},\end{cases}

and formulate their main result as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
f\in L^{\infty}(0,+\infty;H)
and let
R_{h}
satisfy assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
be the solution to the equation (
3.1
) with initial condition
u_{0}\in H
and
U
the solution to the equation (
3.2
) with initial condition
U_{0}\in H
. Then, if

and

satisfy

(3.3)

c\mathcal{G}^{2}\nu\lambda_{1}\leq\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}},

where

is a suitable positive constant,

is the constant appearing in (

denotes the Grashof number

\mathcal{G}:=\frac{1}{\nu^{2}\lambda_{1}}\limsup_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\left\|f(t)\right\|_{H},

then

exponentially fast as

.

4.
Data assimilation in the stochastic framework: the multiplicative noise case

We start with the study of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations driven by a multiplicative noise. Projecting equation (
1.1
) onto
H
, we get rid of the pressure term and we obtain the following abstract
formulation for the equations (

)

(4.1)

\begin{cases}{\rm d}u(t)+\left[\nu Au(t)+B(u(t),u(t))\right]\,{\rm d}t=f\,{\rm d}t+G(u(t))\,{\rm d}W(t),\qquad\qquad t>0\\
u(0)=u_{0}\in H\end{cases}

We assume
\nu>0

and

independent of time.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
be in force,
let

and
u_{0}\in H
.
Then there exists a unique strong solution

to equation (

) with
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. paths in

that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. satisfies

\left(u(t),\phi\right)_{H}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(A^{1/2}u(s),A^{1/2}\phi\right)_{H}\,{\rm d}s-\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle B(u(s),\phi),u(s)\big\rangle\,{\rm d}s\\
=\left(u_{0},\phi\right)_{H}+\langle f,\phi\rangle t+\int_{0}^{t}\langle G(u(s))\,{\rm d}W(s),\phi\rangle,

for every
t\geq 0
and every

.

Proof.

See
[
18
, Theorem 3.6]
.
∎

The solution given in the previous theorem is a strong solution in the probabilistic sense, and it is an adapted process.
Since we are working in the intersection of analysis and probability, the terminology concerning the notion of solutions can cause some confusion.
When we talk of a strong solution we understand it in a probabilistic sense, that is we mean that the underlying probability space is given in advance.
On the other hand, the term weak solution is used as a synonym of martingale solution: we mean that the stochastic basis is constructed as part of the solution. In both cases solutions are weak in the PDE sense.

Mean square estimates for the
H
and
V
-norm of the solution
u
are given in Appendix
A
.

From now on we assume
u_{0}\in H
and
f\in V^{*}
. We will not repeat these assumptions in the next statements. Only the assumptions on the covariance of the noise will be specified in our next results.

We introduce the data assimilation equation as follows. We consider the solution
u
of the Navier-Stokes equation (
4.1
) and define the process
U
solving the following equation, which we call the data assimilation equation

(4.2)

\begin{cases}{\rm d}U(t)+\left(\nu AU(t)+B(U(t),U(t))\right){\rm d}t=G(U(t)){\rm d}W(t)+f{\rm d}t-\mu\Pi R_{h}\left(U(t)-u(t)\right){\rm d}t,\\
U(0)=U_{0}\in H,\end{cases}

where
u
is the solution of the Navier-Stokes equation (
4.1
) and
R_{h}
, defined in Section
2.4
, fulfills (
2.5
).

Notice that when
R_{h}
fulfills condition (
2.5
), equation (
4.2
) admits a unique strong solution with the same regularity as the solution to equation (
4.1
). This can be easily proved by noticing that the additional term
\mu\Pi R_{h}(U(t)-u(t))
does not crucially impact the well-posedness estimates.

4.1.
Convergence in expectation

Dealing with a multiplicative noise, the convergence analysis is conducted in expectation, meaning that convergence results hold in expected value. Provided the observational resolution
h
and the nudging strength
\mu
satisfy suitable conditions, we show that
\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(t)-U(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right]

vanishes as
t\to+\infty
.
The convergence rate depends on the growth of
G
as specified in Assumption
2.2
.
In the context of data assimilation the estimate
\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(t)-U(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right]
is often referred to as Foias-Prodi estimate in expected value.
This estimate shows that the control term
\mu\Pi R_{h}(U(t)-u(t))
, when the parameters
\mu
and
h
are chosen in a proper way, allows to synchronize (in expectation) the solution
u
of the Navier-Stokes equation (
4.1
) and
the solution
U
of the data assimilation equation (
4.2
) in the limit as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

The main result of this Section reads as follows.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let Assumption
2.1
be satified and assume that
R_{h}
satisfies assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
and
U
be, respectively, the solution to the Navier-Stokes equation (

) and

(i)

Assume assumption

.

(ii)

Assume asssumption
2.2
(S) holds.
If

and

\left(L+\frac{C_{S}}{\nu^{2}}\right)<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}},

p

(iii)

Assume assumption
2.2
(L) holds. If

(4.7)

\nu>\frac{6\widetilde{K}_{L}}{\lambda_{1}}

and

and

To prove the above result, we need some estimates, both for the difference
U-u

and for the solution

of the Navier-Stokes equation (

).
Indeed, as we will see in the next proof, the difference process

fulfills a stochastic equation with noise (unless the noise is additive, see the next section), and the basic estimates on the

-norm of

depend on the term

, see (

).
We emphasize once more that, due to the presence of a multiplicative noise, we obtain estimates in the mean square sense.
Only in the next section, when we consider an additive noise, also pathwise estimates can be obtained.

We begin with a result involving a generic stopping time; later on, we will choose it in a suitable way.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
satisfy Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, and
R_{h}
satisfy assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
be the solution to the Navier-Stokes equation (
4.1
) and
U
be the solution to the data assimilation equation (
4.2
).

If

0<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}},

where
c_{0}
is the constant that appears in estimate (
2.5
),
then the following estimate holds for any
\delta\geq 0
, any stopping time
\tau
and any
t\geq 0
:

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\left(\frac{\mu}{1+\delta}-L\right)(t\wedge\tau)-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{t\wedge\tau}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s}\left\|u(t\wedge\tau)-U(t\wedge\tau)\right\|_{H}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u_{0}-U_{0}\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Proof.

Given
u
and
U
satisfying the equations (
4.1
) and (
4.2
), respectively, we obtain the evolution of the difference
r(t)=U(t)-u(t)

\begin{cases}{\rm d}r(t)+\left[\nu Ar(t)+\left(B(U(t),U(t))-B(u(t),u(t))\right)\right]\,{\rm d}t=-\mu\Pi R_{h}(r(t))+(G(U(t))-G(u(t)))\,{\rm d}W(t)\\
r(0)=U_{0}-u_{0}\in H.\end{cases}

We apply the Itô formula to the functional
\|\cdot\|^{2}_{H}
.
Using the fact that
B(U,U)-B(u,u)=B(U,r)+B(r,u)
and bearing in mind (
2.2
), we obtain for every
t\geq 0
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.,

\displaystyle{\rm d}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle+2\nu\|r(t)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\left[-2\langle r(t),B(r(t),u(t))\rangle-2\mu\langle\Pi R_{h}(r(t)),r(t)\rangle+\|G(U(t))-G(u(t))\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\right]\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle+\langle r(t),[G(U(t))-G(u(t))]\,{\rm d}W(t)\rangle.

Thanks to the Gagliardo-Nirenberg, Cauchy-Schwartz and Young inequalities we estimate

\displaystyle-2

\displaystyle\langle r(t),B(r(t),u(t))\rangle-2\mu\langle\Pi R_{h}(r(t)),r(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\langle r(t),r(t)\cdot\nabla u(t)\rangle\right|-2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}-2\mu\langle\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t),r(t)\rangle

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|r(t)\right\|_{[L^{4}(D)]^{2}}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{H}-2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}\left\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}\left\|\nabla r(t)\right\|_{H}\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{H}-2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}\left\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{H}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\nu}{2}\left\|\nabla r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{2\nu}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\right)-2\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+2\mu\left(\frac{1}{2}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\nu\left\|\nabla r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\left\|\nabla u(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}-\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\mu\left\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}.

Thus we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle{\rm d}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}

\displaystyle+\left[\nu\|r(t)\|^{2}_{V}+\mu\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right]\,{\rm d}t\leq\langle r(t),[G(U(t))-G(u(t))]\,{\rm d}W(t)\rangle

\displaystyle+\left[\frac{1}{\nu}\|u(t)\|_{V}^{2}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}+\mu\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t))\|^{2}_{H}+\|G(U(t))-G(u(t))\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}\right]\,{\rm d}t.

Exploiting the fact that
R_{h}
satisfies (
2.5
) we have

\left\|\Pi R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}=\left\|\Pi(R_{h}(r(t))-r(t))\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq\left\|R_{h}(r(t))-r(t)\right\|_{[L^{2}(D)]^{2}}^{2}\leq c_{0}h^{2}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}.

Therefore, exploiting Assumption
2.1
we obtain the estimate

{\rm d}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left(\nu-c_{0}\mu h^{2}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}+\left(\mu-L-\frac{1}{\nu}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{V}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}dt\leq\langle r(t),[G(U(t))-G(u(t))]\,{\rm d}W(t)\rangle.

If we assume
\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}}
, we thus obtain

(4.11)

{\rm d}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left(\mu-L-\frac{1}{\nu}\|u(t)\|^{2}_{V}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}dt\leq\langle r(t),[G(U(t))-G(u(t))]\,{\rm d}W(t)\rangle.

Let us set

M(t):=\int_{0}^{t}\langle r(s),[G(U(s))-G(u(s))]\,{\rm d}W(s)\rangle,\quad t\geq 0.

Fix
\delta\geq 0
and define

\Gamma(t):=\left(\frac{\mu}{1+\delta}-L\right)t-\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s,\quad t\geq 0.

We rewrite (
4.11
) as

{\rm d}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}+\left(\frac{\delta\mu}{1+\delta}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}+\Gamma^{\prime}(t)\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right){\rm d}t\leq{\rm d}M(t).

Multiplying both members of the above expression by
e^{\Gamma(t)}
and noticing that

{\rm d}(e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H})=e^{\Gamma(t)}{\rm d}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}+\Gamma^{\prime}(t)e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}{\rm d}t
,
we get

(4.12)

{\rm d}\left(e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right)+\frac{\delta\mu}{1+\delta}e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}{\rm d}t\leq e^{\Gamma(t)}{\rm d}M(t)

i.e.

e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}+\frac{\delta\mu}{1+\delta}\int_{0}^{t}e^{\Gamma(s)}\|r(s)\|^{2}_{H}{\rm d}s\leq\|r_{0}\|^{2}_{H}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\Gamma(s)}{\rm d}M(s).

In particular

e^{\Gamma(t)}\|r(t)\|^{2}_{H}\leq\|r_{0}\|^{2}_{H}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{\Gamma(s)}{\rm d}M(s).

Using usual strategies for local martingales, considering a stopping time
\tau
and taking the expected value, we infer

(4.13)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\Gamma(t\wedge\tau)}\|r(t\wedge\tau)\|_{H}^{2}\right]\leq\|r_{0}\|_{H}^{2},

that is (
4.10
).
∎

In order to control the integrating factor that appears in (
4.10
), we will make a suitable choice of the stopping time. For
R,\beta,\delta,\mu>0
, let

(4.14)

\tau_{R,\beta,\delta\,\mu}:=\inf\left\{r\geq 0:\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{r}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s+\left(L-\frac{\mu}{(1+\delta)^{2}}\right)r-\beta\geq R\right\}

and
\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty
if the set is empty, i.e. if

(4.15)

\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s+\left(L-\frac{\mu}{(1+\delta)^{2}}\right)t-\beta<R,\qquad\forall\ t\geq 0.

The parameter
\beta
will be useful to track the dependence on the initial velocities
and on the forcing terms in subsequent estimates of
\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}
; see Proposition
4.5
below.

From the definition (
4.14
) of

we immediately get the following corollary of Lemma

.

Corollary 4.4
.

Under the same conditions as in Lemma
4.3
, for any

and any

Proof.

From (

\frac{\delta\mu}{(1+\delta)^{2}}t-\beta-R\leq\Gamma(t)
for any

, when

\mathbb{E}\left({\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)}e^{\frac{\delta\mu}{(1+\delta)^{2}}t-\beta-R}\|u(t)-U(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left({\boldsymbol{1}}_{(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)}e^{\Gamma(t)}\|u(t)-U(t)\|^{2}_{H}\right)\leq\|u_{0}-U_{0}\|^{2}_{H}

∎

for the explicit dependence according to which case (B), (S) or (L) in Assumption
2.2
is considered).

We now make a suitable choice of the parameters
R,\beta,\delta
and

and use the previous bounds on
\tau_{R,\beta,\delta\mu}
. From the lower bounds on

), (

\frac{C\left(1+\|u_{0}\|^{2(p+1)}_{H}\right)}{R^{\frac{p}{2}}}&\text{ for any $p\in(0,+\infty)$, under Assumption \ref{assumption-stochastic2} (S)},\\

4.2.
Some partial results for pathwise convergence

Since
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|^{2}_{H}=0
, there exists a subsequence converging
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.

We now show that given any subsequence with
t_{n}\to+\infty
as
n\to+\infty
we have

\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|^{2}_{H}\to 0
,
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s.
Notice that this is not enough to prove that there is pathwise convergence
\|u(t)-U(t)\|^{2}_{H}\to 0
as
t\to+\infty
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Under the same assumption of Theorem
4.2
, for any diverging increasing sequence of times
(t_{n})_{n}
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|^{2}_{H}=0\right)=1.

Proof.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we introduce the events

B_{n}:=\left\{\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|^{2}_{H}>\frac{1}{t_{n}^{2}}\right\}

and we set

B:=\bigcap_{m=1}^{+\infty}\bigcup_{n=m}^{+\infty}B_{n}.

We consider the stopping time
\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}
introduced in (
4.14
) and write

\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}<+\infty)\right).

We now observe that
\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)\right)=0
for any
R,\beta,\delta>0
and
0<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}}
. In fact, thanks to the Markov inequality and Corollary
4.4
, for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(B_{n}\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)\right)

\displaystyle\leq t_{n}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{1}_{\left(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty\right)}\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|_{H}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{R+\beta}\|u_{0}-U_{0}\|^{2}_{H}t_{n}^{2}e^{-\frac{\delta\mu}{(\delta+1)^{2}}t_{n}}.

Thus,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}\left(B_{n}\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)\right)<+\infty.

Hence, by the Borel-Cantelli Lemma it follows
\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}=+\infty)\right)=0
. We infer

\mathbb{P}(B)=\mathbb{P}\left(B\cap(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}<+\infty)\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}<+\infty\right).

We now make a suitable choice of the parameters
R,\beta,\delta
and
\mu
and use the bounds on
\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}
. From the lower bounds on
\mu
(
4.3
), (
4.5
) and (
4.8
) we infer the existence of
\delta>0
such that the lower bounds (
4.17
), (
4.19
) and (
4.20
), respectively, in Proposition
4.5
hold true.
By also choosing
\beta
as in Proposition
4.5
, for any
R>0
, we infer

\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{R,\beta,\delta,\mu}<+\infty\right)\leq\begin{cases}e^{-CR}&\text{under Assumption \ref{assumption-stochastic2} (B)},\\
\frac{C}{R^{p}}&\text{ for any $p\in(0,+\infty)$, under Assumption \ref{assumption-stochastic2} (S)},\\
\frac{C}{R^{p}}&\text{ for any $p\in\left(0,\frac{\nu\lambda_{1}}{4\widetilde{K}_{L}}-\frac{1}{2}\right)$, \ under Assumption \ref{assumption-stochastic2} (L)},\end{cases},

where the constant
C
does not depend on
R
(and depends on the structural parameters of the equation; see the proof of Theorem
4.2
for more details).
Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
we can fix
\overline{R}=\overline{R}(\varepsilon)>0
such that
\mathbb{P}(B)<\varepsilon
.
With such choice of
\overline{R}
,
from the continuity from below we can find
m^{*}>0
sufficiently large such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=m^{*}}^{+\infty}B_{n}^{c}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

Now we observe that

\left\{\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|_{H}^{2}=0\right\}\supseteq\bigcap_{n=m^{*}}^{+\infty}B_{n}^{c},

hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|u(t_{n})-U(t_{n})\|_{H}^{2}=0\right)\geq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{n=m^{*}}^{+\infty}B_{n}^{c}\right)\geq 1-\varepsilon.

By the arbitrariness of
\varepsilon
we conclude the proof.
∎

5.
Data assimilation in the stochastic framework: the additive noise case

Let us now turn to the study of the 2D Navier-Stokes equations driven by an additive noise.
This is the particular case when the covariance of the noise does not depend on the velocity
u
, i.e.

(5.1)

\begin{cases}{\rm d}u(t)+\left[\nu Au(t)+B(u(t),u(t))\right]\,{\rm d}t=f\,{\rm d}t+G\,{\rm d}W(t),\qquad\qquad t>0\\
u(0)=u_{0}\in H\end{cases}

As before, we assume
\nu>0
and
f\in V^{*}
independent of time, and we do not repeat these assumptions in the next statements.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
G\in L_{HS}(U;H)
.
Then, there exists a unique strong solution
u
to equation (
5.1
) with
{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. paths in

\mathcal{C}([0,+\infty),H)\cap L_{loc}^{2}(0,+\infty;V),

that
\mathbb{P}
-a.s. satisfies

\displaystyle\left(u(t),\phi\right)_{H}+\int_{0}^{t}\left(A^{1/2}u(s),A^{1/2}\phi\right)_{H}\,{\rm d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\big\langle B(u(s),\phi),u(s)\big\rangle\,{\rm d}s=\left(u_{0},\phi\right)_{H}+\langle f,\phi\rangle t+\big\langle GW(t),\phi\rangle,

for every
t\geq 0
and every
\phi\in V
.

Mean square estimates for the
H
and
V
-norm of the solution
u
are given in Appendix
B
.

The data assimilation equation in the additive case is now

(5.2)

\begin{cases}{\rm d}U(t)+\left(\nu AU(t)+B(U(t),U(t))\right){\rm d}t=G{\rm d}W(t)+f{\rm d}t-\mu\Pi R_{h}(U(t)-u(t)){\rm d}t,\\
U(0)=U_{0}\in H\end{cases}

where
u
is the solution of the Navier-Stokes equation (
5.1
) and
R_{h}
, defined in Section
2.4
, fulfills (
2.5
).
Notice that equation (
5.2
) admits a unique strong solution with the same regularity as the solution to equation (
5.1
).

In the previous section, we obtained convergence in expectation
when the multiplicative coefficient is bounded.
In contrast, in the presence of additive noise, the pathwise behavior can be investigated in a more straightforward manner.
We first establish the convergence result in expectation,
subsequently, we turn to the pathwise analysis.

5.1.
Convergence in expectation

The convergence in the mean square sense is obtained exactly as in the multiplicative noise setting. The next result can indeed be seen as a particular case of part i) of Theorem
5.1
, considering
L=0
and
K_{B}=\|G(u)\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
G\in L_{HS}(U;H)
and
R_{h}
satisfy assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
be the solution to the Navier-Stokes equation (
5.1
) and
U
the solution to the data assimilation equation (
5.2
). If
\mu
and
h
satisfy

(5.3)

\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}},

then

\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}^{2}\right]\rightarrow 0
exponentially fast as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

5.2.
Pathwise convergence

Let us now focus on the pathwise analysis, meaning that convergence results hold
\mathbb{P}
-almost surely.
Our main result reads as follows.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
G\in L_{HS}(U;H)
and let
R_{h}
satisfy assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
be the solution to the Navier-Stokes equation (
5.1
) and
U
the solution to the data assimilation equation (
5.2
). If
\mu
and
h
satisfy

(5.4)

\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}},

then

\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}\rightarrow 0
exponentially fast as
t\rightarrow+\infty
,
\mathbb{P}
-a.s.

The proof of the above result is based on the following preliminary lemma.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
G\in L_{HS}(U;H)
and let
R_{h}
satisfy assumption (
2.5
).
Let
u
be the solution to the Navier-Stokes equation (
5.1
) and
U
the solution to the data assimilation equation (
5.2
). If
\mu
and
h
satisfy

(5.5)

0<\mu\leq\frac{\nu}{c_{0}h^{2}}

where
c_{0}
is the constant that appears in estimate (
2.5
),

{\mathbb{P}}
-a.s. we have

(5.6)

\displaystyle\left\|u(t)-U(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|u_{0}-U_{0}\right\|_{H}^{2}\quad\exp\left(-\mu t+\frac{1}{\nu}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u(s)\right\|_{V}^{2}ds\right),\qquad\text{ for any }t>0.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of Lemma
4.3
. But now the difference
r=U-u

fulfills an equation without noise and with random coefficients.

Given
u
and
U
satisfying the equations (
5.1
) and (
5.2
), respectively, we obtain the evolution of the difference
r(t)=U(t)-u(t)

\begin{cases}\frac{{\rm d}}{dt}r(t)+\nu Ar(t)+B(U(t),U(t))-B(u(t),u(t))=-\mu\Pi R_{h}(r(t))\\
r(0)=U_{0}-u_{0}\in H\end{cases}

There is no longer the noise term;
the pathwise estimate is easily obtained. Hence, from now on we work pathwise, i.e. the path is fixed.
Taking the scalar product with
r(t)
we obtain the equality

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+2\nu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}

\displaystyle=-2\langle r(t),B(r(t),u(t))\rangle-2\mu\langle R_{h}(r(t)),r(t)\rangle,

which holds for every
t\geq 0
. Proceeding similarly to the proof of Lemma
4.3
we infer

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\nu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}+\mu\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mu c_{0}h^{2}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}+\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|r(t)\right\|^{2}_{H}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}

which yields

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left(\nu-\mu c_{0}h^{2}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}+\left(\mu-\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq 0.

By (
5.5
) we have

\nu-\mu c_{0}h^{2}\geq 0
; hence

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}+\left(\mu-\frac{1}{\nu}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{V}^{2}\right)\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq 0.

Using the Gronwall inequality, we infer

\left\|r(t)\right\|_{H}^{2}\leq\left\|r(0)\right\|_{H}^{2}\ \exp\left(-\mu t+\frac{1}{\nu}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u(s)\right\|_{V}^{2}\,{\rm d}s\right),

for any time
t>0
.
∎

We have now all the ingredients to prove Theorem
5.3
.

Proof of Theorem
5.3
.

We want to consider the limit as
t\to+\infty
in the right hand side of (
5.6
).

Let
G
satisfy the Assumptions of Theorem
5.1
.
From (
B.4
) we know that for any initial velocity
u_{0}\in H
,

(5.7)

\limsup_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{1}{\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s\leq\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)

with probability one. From now on we work pathwise.
From assumption (
5.4
) there exists a positive constant
\varepsilon
such that

\mu-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)-\varepsilon>0.

Now we write the exponent in the right hand side of (
5.6
) as follows

-t\mu+\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{V}^{2}{\rm d}s=-t\left[\mu-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)-\varepsilon\right]\\
+t\left[\frac{1}{\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u(s)\right\|_{V}^{2}ds-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)-\varepsilon\right].

Thanks to (
5.7
) we get that

\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{t\left[\frac{1}{\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u(s)\right\|_{V}^{2}ds-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)-\varepsilon\right]}=0

for any
\varepsilon>0
. Moreover

\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-t\left[\mu-\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)-\varepsilon\right]}=0.

Therefore

\lim_{t\to+\infty}e^{-t\mu+\frac{1}{\nu}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{V}^{2}{\rm d}s}=0

and by (
5.6
) also
u-U
vanishes for large times.
In particular, we have obtained that there exists a time
\tau>0
such that for any
t>\tau
we have

\|u(t)-U(t)\|_{H}^{2}\leq\|u_{0}-U_{0}\|^{2}_{H}\exp\left(-\left(\mu-\tfrac{2}{\nu^{2}}\big(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\tfrac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\big)-\varepsilon\right)t\right)

showing the exponential convergence rate.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

Assuming that the Navier–Stokes equation (
5.1
) admits a unique (and hence ergodic) invariant measure, one can obtain an alternative proof of Theorem
5.3
by applying the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem. We point out, however, that although this approach leads to the same conclusion as Theorem
5.3
, it requires more restrictive assumptions on the operator
G
, since they must guarantee the existence and uniqueness of an invariant measure rather than merely the well-posedness of the equation. For completeness, we present this argument in Appendix
C
.

Remark 5.6
.

Notice that, by taking
G=0
in equation (
5.1
), we recover (modulo a constant) the same condition on the nudging parameter
\mu
given in
[
2
]
(see Theorem
3.1
) for the deterministic setting, in the case of a time independent forcing term
f
(compare condition (
3.3
) with condition (
5.3
)).

Thus, in the presence of additive stochastic noise, our analysis shows that convergence still holds almost surely, with an exponential rate, provided that the nudging parameter
\mu
is chosen large enough to balance both the intensity of the deterministic forcing and that of the stochastic perturbation.
This demonstrates that our result can be viewed as a natural generalization of the deterministic result in
[
2
]
, extending it to the case of stochastic forcing.

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\geq T}\left[\frac{\nu}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s-\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H}-\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)t\right]\geq R\right)\leq e^{-\frac{\nu\lambda_{1}}{8\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}}R},

equivalently

\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s\leq\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\right)+\frac{2}{\nu^{2}t}(\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H}+R)\text{ for all }t\geq T\right)\geq 1-e^{-\frac{\nu\lambda_{1}}{8\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}}R}.

Therefore

(B.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\nu t}\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}s\leq\frac{2}{\nu^{2}}\Big(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}}{\nu}\Big)\right)=1.

Appendix C
A different proof of Theorem
5.3

In this appendix we present an alternative proof of Theorem
5.3
. The argument relies on the existence of a unique ergodic invariant measure and on suitable moment bounds with respect to it.
Notice that the assumptions on
G
in the previous sections are not enough to obtain the uniqueness of the invariant measure. For simplicity, here we simply assume that such a unique invariant measure exists and refer the reader to the existing literature for the precise conditions ensuring its existence, uniqueness, and ergodicity (see, for instance,
[
19
]
and the references therein).
The next step consists in getting some moment bounds with respect to the unique invariant measure.

We denote by
u(t;x)
the solution to equation (
5.1
) evaluated at time
t>0
, started at time
0
from
x
.
We introduce the operator
P_{t}
for each fixed time
t>0
as

(C.1)

(P_{t}\phi)(x)=\mathbb{E}[\phi(u(t;x))],\qquad\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(H),

where
\mathcal{B}_{b}(H)
is the space of all bounded Borel functions
\phi:H\to\mathbb{R}
.
This defines a Markov semigroup acting on
\mathcal{B}_{b}(H)
.
We recall that a probability measure
\gamma
, defined on the Borel subsets of
H
, is
invariant
if
for any
t\geq 0
,

(C.2)

\int_{H}P_{t}\phi\>{\rm d}\gamma=\int_{H}\phi\>{\rm d}\gamma\hskip 28.45274pt\forall\phi\in\mathcal{B}_{b}(H).

Lemma C.1
.

Assume that equation (
5.1
) admits a unique ergodic invariant measure
\gamma
.
Then

(C.3)

\int_{H}\|x\|^{2}_{V}\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\leq\frac{2}{\nu}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right).

Proof.

To prove estimate (
C.3
) we proceed in a classical way.
We consider the identity (
C.2
) and thanks to the definition (
C.1
) of the operator
P_{t}
, we use the previous mean estimates on
\|u(t)\|_{H}^{2}
and
\int_{0}^{t}\|u(s)\|_{V}^{2}{\rm d}s
.

We start by proving that the function
\Psi:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
,
\Psi(x):=\|x\|^{2}_{H}
belongs to
L^{1}(\gamma)
and

(C.4)

\int_{H}\|x\|^{2}_{H}\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\leq\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right).

We consider the bounded map
\Psi_{k}:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as

\Psi_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\|x\|^{2}_{H}&\text{if}\ \|x\|^{2}_{H}\leq k,\\
k&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By the invariance of the measure
\gamma
and the boundedness of
\Psi_{k}
we get

\int_{H}\Psi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)=\int_{H}P_{s}\Psi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x),\qquad\forall\ s\geq 0.

Moreover,

P_{s}\Psi_{k}(x):=\mathbb{E}\left[\Psi_{k}(u(s;x))\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(s;x)\|^{2}_{H}\right].

From Lemma
B.1
we get an estimate for
\mathbb{E}\left[\|u(s;x)\|^{2}_{H}\right]
(see (
B.2
)). Letting
s\rightarrow+\infty
the exponential term in the r.h.s. of (
B.2
) vanishes, so we obtain the estimate

\limsup_{s\rightarrow+\infty}P_{s}\Psi_{k}(x)\leq\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right).

As a consequence,

\int_{H}\Psi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\leq\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right).

Since
\Psi_{k}
converges pointwise and monotonically from below to
\Psi
, from the Monotone Convergence Theorem we infer (
C.4
).

Let us now come to the proof of (
C.3
). We consider the bounded map

\phi_{k}:H\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
defined as

\phi_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\|x\|^{2}_{V}&\text{if}\ \|x\|^{2}_{V}\leq k,\\
k&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This is a truncation, approximating the function
\phi:H\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}
, defined as

\phi(x)=\|x\|^{2}_{V}
.
It obviously holds

\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)=\nu\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\,{\rm d}s.

On the other hand, the invariance of
\gamma
and the boundedness of
\phi_{k}
yield

\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)=\int_{H}P_{s}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x),\qquad\forall\ s\geq 0.

Thus, by the Fubini-Tonelli Theorem, since

\phi_{k}(x)=\|x\|^{2}_{V}\wedge k\leq\|x\|^{2}_{V},

thanks to Lemma
B.1
(see (
B.1
)) we get

\displaystyle\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)

\displaystyle=\nu\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\,{\rm d}s=\nu\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}}\int_{H}P_{s}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\nu\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}}\int_{H}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi_{k}(u(s;x))\right]\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\,{\rm d}s=\nu\lambda_{1}\int_{H}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}}\phi_{k}(u(s;x))\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}\int_{H}\nu\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{{\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}}}\left[\|u(s;x)\|^{2}_{V}\right]\,{\rm d}s\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}\left(\frac{1}{\nu\lambda_{1}}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right)+\int_{H}\|x\|_{H}^{2}\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\right).

Estimate (
C.4
) thus yields

\int_{H}\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}\gamma(x)\leq\frac{2}{\nu}\left(\|G\|^{2}_{L_{HS}(U,H)}+\frac{1}{\nu}\|f\|^{2}_{V^{*}}\right).

Since
\phi_{k}
converges pointwise and monotonically from below to
\Phi
,
estimate (
C.3
) follows. This concludes the proof.
∎

Alternative proof of Theorem
5.3
(under more restrictive assumptions on
G
).

We work under the assumptions on
G
ensuring the existence of a unique ergodic invariant measure.
The proof relies on the Birkhoff Ergodic Theorem (see, e.g.,
[
11
]
). It states that, if
\gamma
is an ergodic invariant measure for the Navier-Stokes equation (
5.1
)), then for any
\phi\in L^{1}(\gamma)
and
for
\gamma
-a.e. initial velocity, it holds

(C.5)

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\phi(u(s))\,{\rm d}s=\int_{H}\phi\,{\rm d}\gamma\qquad\mathbb{P}-a.s.

On the other hand, from Lemma
C.1

\phi(x)=\|x\|^{2}_{V}\in L^{1}(\gamma)

From now on we proceed as in the proof of Theorem
