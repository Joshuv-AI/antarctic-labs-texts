Association between alcohol consumption per capita and suicide mortality rate across 30 European countries
Authors: Katherine Guo, Huan Jiang, Kevin David Shield, Sheryl Spithoff, Shannon Lange
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide was the cause of 86 000 deaths in Europe in 2021. Accordingly, suicide prevention has been designated as a serious public health concern by the World Health Organization (WHO). Alcohol use is a causal risk factor for suicide and may be a cost-effective target for prevention efforts. Globally, the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goals have set a target of a 20% reduction in alcohol consumption per capita (APC). However, evidence on the utility of APC as a metric in relation to the prevention of alcohol-attributable suicides is currently limited.ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between APC and suicide mortality rate across 30 European countries.MethodsSuicide mortality rates were obtained for 27 European Union countries as well as the UK, Switzerland and Norway for 1991–2021 from the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation. APC data was obtained from the WHO Global Information System on Alcohol and Health. Linear mixed effects models with random intercepts for country and first-order autoregressive errors were used to test the association between APC and suicide mortality rate. Tests of interaction were conducted to examine the impact of sex, country-level drinking patterns and the 2008 global economic recession. All models were adjusted for relevant population-level confounders.FindingsOn average, a 1 L increase in total APC was associated with a 0.39% increase in the suicide mortality rate (95% CI 0.03% to 0.76%; p value=0.03) among males, and a 0.59% increase in the suicide mortality rate (95% CI 0.12% to 1.06%; p value=0.01) among females. There was no evidence of a significant sex difference, nor was there evidence of a modifying effect of country-level drinking patterns or 2008 global economic recession on the respective association.ConclusionsNational suicide prevention strategies should consider incorporating APC as an indicator for monitoring and assessing the impact of interventions targeting alcohol use on suicide.

Introduction

The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated that over 86 000 deaths by suicide occurred within Europe in 2021, with eastern Europe having one of the highest regional age-standardised suicide mortality rates for that year (19.2 per 100 000 population).1 Suicide has been identified as a serious public health concern by multiple international organisations, including the WHO2 and the United Nations (UN).3

Alcohol consumption is a well-established causal risk factor for suicide,4 5 with both acute effects (via reduced inhibition, impaired judgement, increased impulsivity and worsened mood) and long-term effects (via psychiatric comorbidity and adverse life events, among other vulnerabilities). Although sex differences are well-documented with respect to both suicide and alcohol consumption, with males having higher rates of suicide and a higher prevalence of heavy chronic and episodic drinking compared with females,4 6 emerging evidence suggests sex differences may be absent in the risk relationship.7 Regardless, given the relationship between alcohol and suicide, interventions targeting consumption on both the individual level and population level may be effective in reducing the suicide mortality rate.8 9 However, the monitoring and assessment of population-level health outcomes often requires population-level indicators. Measuring alcohol consumption at the population level is challenging, with surveys under-reporting the actual consumption within a population by as much as 50%.10 In a recent meta-analysis by Guo et al,11 a proxy measure of consumption which relies on economic data, referred to as alcohol consumption per capita (APC), was found to be associated with the suicide mortality rate. Specifically, it was estimated that the suicide mortality rate increased by 3.6% (95% CI 2.4% to 4.8%) for every 1 L increase in APC. Thus, APC may be a useful indicator to consider within suicide prevention strategies.

Although there are a number of studies available on the association between APC and suicide, the pool of literature is plagued by limitations,11 which inherently limit the understanding of the relationship between APC and suicide. Existing studies have largely relied on traditional time-series methods, despite their limited number of data points owed to the annual reporting of APC. Related to this, many studies do not adjust for confounding, likely due to concerns with limited power. Further, prior studies do not tend to formally test for sex differences, regardless of noting observed differences.

Recent meta-analyses by Lange et al found that as average daily consumption increases, the risk of suicide increases proportionally,12 and that alcohol use disorder, an indicator of problematic alcohol use marked by frequent or heavy consumption, is associated with greater than twofold higher odds of suicide.7 Taken together, it is likely that the relationship between alcohol and suicide differs by drinking pattern, which could also be present at the population level. Further, patterns of alcohol consumption vary across differing sociocultural contexts13 and are often impacted by periods of crisis, such as economic recession.14 However, few studies exist which have explored the modifying effect of country-level drinking pattern on the relationship between APC and suicide, and no such studies exist for the potential modifying effect of economic crisis.

The current study aims to address the aforementioned limitations of the literature. First, by evaluating the association between APC and suicide mortality rate across 30 European countries using an alternative modelling method. Second, by formally testing whether the association differs by sex or country-level drinking pattern, and by testing the impact of the 2008 global economic crisis. Our hypotheses were twofold: (1) APC and suicide mortality rate are positively related, and (2) there are no sex differences in the respective relationship. Although we surmised that the risk relationship would differ by country-level drinking pattern and the occurrence of the economic recession, we did not have formal hypotheses for these modifying effects given the lack of prior studies.

Methods

This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Data sources

Annual overall and sex-specific suicide mortality rates for 1991–2021 were obtained from the Institute of Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)15 for European Union (EU) member states (Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, Republic of Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Ireland, Italy, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Poland, Portugal, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain and Sweden), as well as the UK, Switzerland and Norway. Suicide was defined as death caused by self-directed injurious behaviour with any intent to die as a result of the behaviour and/or the appropriate International Classification of Diseases codes (ICD-9: E950-E959; ICD-10: X60-X64.9, X66-X84.9, Y870).1 Suicide mortality rate (per 100 000 population) was quantified as the number of deaths by suicide for those that were 10+ years of age within a given year, divided by the total population (10+ years) and multiplied by 100 000. Total APC was defined as the amount of pure alcohol consumed per person (10+ years) per year, measured in litres (L).

Data on total APC, measured in litres of pure alcohol consumed per adult (≥15 years) annually, was obtained from the WHO Global Information System on Alcohol and Health16 for the respective years and countries. APC is calculated as the sum of recorded (ie, alcohol consumption captured through taxation and sales databases), and unrecorded (ie, homemade or informally produced alcohol, smuggled alcohol and surrogate alcohol) consumption in pure litres of ethanol, and is then divided by the total adult population (≥15 years).5 Furthermore, APC estimates are corrected for the consumption of both inbound and outbound travellers for each country. As APC data from the WHO are not age-adjusted, unadjusted suicide mortality rates were used to ensure the underlying population metrics were in line with one another.

In order to align with the age range defined for the suicide mortality rate, APC data were converted to represent those 10+ years of age using UN total population estimates, as APC was originally reported for the population 15+ years of age (see online supplemental text S1 for conversion formula). Total APC was used, rather than sex-specific APC, as alcohol sales data do not typically record information related to sex, making true measures of sex-specific APC impossible to obtain. Existing sex-specific APC metrics, such as those from the WHO, represent modelled data rather than true sex specific APC and could, thus, introduce excess uncertainty in our estimates.

The following country-varying and year-varying covariates were taken into consideration: proportion of the population who identified as Muslim or Buddhist, the unemployment rate, gross domestic product based on purchasing power parity, Human Development Index score, Gini index score, infant (<5 years) mortality rate, educational attainment (completion of postsecondary or equivalent), literacy rate, divorce rate, WHO 2010 pattern of drinking score (ie, country-specific drinking pattern), population density, female labour force participation rate, migration, proportion of the population living in urban areas, alcohol-attributable liver cirrhosis mortality rate, homicide rate, and proportion of the population aged 65+ years.1518 The WHO 2010 pattern of drinking score is calculated by combining various drinking attributes (eg, quantity of alcohol consumed per occasion, proportion of daily drinkers, context of drinking such as with meals or in public spaces) to produce a score from 1 to 5, where 1 represents the ‘least risky’ pattern of drinking and 5 represents the ‘most risky’. Additionally, three dummy variables were created to represent the 2008 global economic recession (coded as ‘1’ for 2008 and 2009, ‘0’ otherwise), changes from ICD-9 to ICD-10 (coded as ‘1’ for 1993 onwards, ‘0’ otherwise) and the COVID-19 pandemic (coded as ‘1’ for 2020 and 2021, ‘0’ otherwise).

Statistical analyses

Linear mixed effects models with random intercepts for country were constructed to examine the association between APC and suicide mortality rate over time. Linear mixed effect models allowed for both country-level variability (variation between countries) and subject-level variability (variation within countries) to be accounted for. Suicide mortality rates were log-transformed due to concerns of skewness. Post-transformation coefficients of skewness for the complete case data had a magnitude less than 1.0 (males=−0.29; females=−0.81), indicating successful transformation. The models included first-order autoregressive errors, which allowed us to account for serial autocorrelation present within our data. Covariates with a missingness >30% were excluded from our analysis (ie, literacy rate, Human Development Index, Gini index and educational attainment). Religious composition was also excluded as there were few countries which had a large proportion (>25%) of their population identifying as either Muslim or Buddhist. In 2020, the proportion of the population identifying as Muslim ranged from <1% (multiple countries) to 24.4% (Cyprus), and the proportion of the population identifying as Buddhist was less than 1% for all countries of interest. Correlation matrices were then generated using Pearson’s correlation coefficients to check for potential redundancy between the remaining variables (ie, all variables listed above, excluding literacy rate, Human Development Index, Gini index, educational attainment and religious compositions). It was prespecified that if high correlation was detected (ie, r≥0.80), the covariate with the lowest p value would be retained; however, there were no highly correlated covariates detected.

The main models relied on a complete case analysis approach based on the remaining covariates and a full model was fitted. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to check for multicollinearity within the models, with a threshold of 5.0 used as an indicator of problematic multicollinearity. Problematic variables were removed from the model, starting with those with the highest VIF, until the VIF for each individual variable was below the prespecified threshold. To further refine the models, non-significant terms were removed and a likelihood ratio test (LRT) was conducted comparing full and reduced models. The final sex-specific models were attained using the following model selection approach: if the p value of the LRT was ≥0.3, the simpler model (ie, the reduced model) would be retained; if the p value was <0.3, covariates would be added back into the model in the order of smallest to largest p value until the LRT p value was ≥0.3. In addition to the main sex-specific analyses, models with an interaction term for (1) APC and sex, (2) APC and global recession and (3) APC and country-level drinking pattern were used to test whether the association between APC and suicide mortality rate was impacted by sex, the 2008 global economic recession and/or country-level drinking pattern, respectively. Model assumptions were verified visually using residual QQ plots and residual versus predicted plots (see online supplemental figures S1 and S2). It should also be noted that mixed models are typically fairly robust to violations of distributional assumptions.19

Statistical significance was set at p≤0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (V.4.4.3). Results have been reported on the per cent-change scale for interpretability and were converted according to formulas found in online supplemental text S2.

Sensitivity analysis

As complete case analysis may result in biased estimates when data is not missing completely at random, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by performing the previously-described analyses on data containing imputed values for missing data. Little’s missing completely at random test20 was conducted to assess whether data was missing completely at random. For data which were not missing completely at random, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was conducted with 35 replicates.

Results

There were 715 observations each for males and females for the 30 countries included. The average suicide mortality rate across all years and countries was 26.41 per 100 000 males (SD=14.32) and 7.55 per 100 000 females (SD=3.58). In 2021, the suicide mortality rate ranged from 8.06 (Malta) to 49.96 (Lithuania) per 100 000 males, and 1.85 (Malta) to 9.36 (Belgium) per 100 000 females (see figure 1 for the annual suicide mortality rates by country). The average annual APC across all years and countries was 10.07 L of pure alcohol consumed per adult (SD=2.38).

Sensitivity analysis

Homicide rate had the highest percentage of missing data at 19%, while all other covariates had less than 10% missingness. Missing data was determined to not be missing completely at random in all instances (p<0.001). Following imputation, there were 930 observations each for males and females for the 30 countries included. The descriptive statistics for the data used for the sensitivity analysis, which includes information on missingness, are available in online supplemental table S4; results of Little’s missing completely at random test are available in online supplemental table S5.

Analyses of imputed data yielded similar results for females; however, results for males differed, with the association between APC and suicide mortality lacking statistical significance (APC effect estimate=0.25%; 95% CI −0.10% to 0.59%; p=0.16). Results for the modifying effects of sex and the 2008 recession were similar to complete case models. Full results from imputed data can be found in online supplemental tables S6–S8.

Discussion

Our analyses found that, on average, a 1 L increase in APC was associated with approximately half a per cent increase in the suicide mortality rate across both sexes. In absolute terms, using data from 2021, if the total APC across countries was reduced by 20% (the UN Sustainable Development Goals target for APC),3 our results suggest that approximately 615 deaths by suicide would be averted across the analysed countries.

The positive association found in this study is in line with individual-level findings related to alcohol consumption and suicide risk,4 5 7 12 as well as existing literature on APC and suicide mortality.11 The point estimates reported in our study are smaller than those estimated in the recent meta-analysis by Guo et al,11 which found that a 1 L change in APC was associated with a 3.6% (95% CI 2.4% to 4.8%) change in the suicide mortality rate. This discrepancy is possibly due to the lack of adjustment for confounding by the studies included in the meta-analysis resulting in an overestimation of the association. This difference in magnitude may also reflect the consequences of averaging estimates across multiple countries. The majority of studies included in the meta-analysis11 reflect country-specific estimates; it is possible that the relationship is stronger in certain countries, which subsequently becomes diluted when averaged across countries. However, despite the potential for such ‘dilution’, a cross-country average is still a useful metric in the context of international policies and guidelines, such as those presented in the UN Sustainable Development Goals.3

Our study did not find evidence of a sex difference in the association, which is in line with a meta-analysis by Lange et al,7 who found no evidence of a sex difference in the relationship between alcohol use disorder and suicide. In fact, the authors concluded that any apparent sex differences were a function of bias introduced by study design and did not reflect causal impact. Our results also align with the meta-analysis by Guo et al,11 which also did not find evidence of a sex difference. It should be noted that, similar to the studies included in that meta-analysis, the current results rely on total APC as opposed to sex-specific APC due to data limitations. The absence of reliable sex-specific exposure data may be responsible for the lack of sex-specific findings. Thus, it would be valuable for future research to assess a reliable measure of sex-specific consumption and its relation to sex-specific suicide. Regardless, although the association between APC and suicide mortality rate appears to be similar for males and females, policies and intervention targeting alcohol use may not result in similar net effects across the sexes depending on the nature of the intervention. The risk per drink may also differ across sexes. On average, females typically achieve higher blood alcohol concentrations at equivalent consumption levels to males,21 which may influence their risk of suicide. Further research is needed to determine whether there exists some sex-specific difference in risk per drink. Additionally, we were unable to assess the impact of gender, as suicide mortality statistics rely on official death report data, which typically only record biological sex. However, gender has a complex and distinct impact on both alcohol consumption and risk of suicide due to sociocultural factors related to gender as well as the impact of oppression and discrimination on gender-diverse populations.22 Lack of data collection may further perpetuate health inequities within these populations;23 thus, additional efforts need to be made to collect gender-based data and subsequently research the impact of gender on the relationship between APC and suicide mortality.

The absence of a modifying effect of the global economic recession and country-level drinking pattern was unexpected. With respect to the global recession, existing literature suggests less alcohol was purchased during this period24 despite there being an increase in deaths related to problematic alcohol consumption in the EU.25 These observations are in line with the polarisation hypothesis, which suggests light to moderate drinkers either decrease their consumption or consume as per usual, while heavy drinkers increase their consumption.26 The lack of modification may, instead, be related to APC and its inability to capture changes in individual-level drinking patterns. With respect to country-level drinking patterns, a study by Roche et al,27 which examined the modifying effect of country-level drinking pattern on APC and suicide mortality using the WHO 2010 pattern of drinking score, found evidence of an association at the ‘most risky’ pattern of drinking level only. Thus, the absence of a modifying effect of country-level drinking patterns in our study may be related to the countries included in our analysis, as they only ranged from ‘least risky’ pattern of drinking to ‘medium risky’ pattern of drinking. The chosen variable, the WHO 2010 pattern of drinking score, may also not be an adequate measure of drinking pattern. The use of this variable was due to a lack of alternative data; however, as it is cross-sectional, it also required us to make the assumption that drinking patterns have not substantially changed within countries over time, which may not be the case and may introduce variable measurement bias within the full models as a result.28 However, as this variable was removed in the final reduced models, concerns associated with its use may be attenuated within the final results.

Limitations

Our study had a few notable limitations. First, this is an ecological study, which precludes our ability to make causal inferences and statements about individual-level risk, due to concerns with ecological fallacy. However, this does not negate APC’s use as a proxy measure of consumption for the purposes of population-level suicide prevention strategies; this is further highlighted by our alignment with studies at the individual level.7 12 Second, as mentioned prior, our analyses use sex-specific suicide mortality and total APC, as opposed to sex-specific APC, due to limitations related to sex-specific APC data. While this is not necessarily unusual within the current literature on APC and suicide mortality,11 it does make the interpretation of our sex-specific analyses somewhat less intuitive as it is related to total APC as opposed to sex-specific consumption. Third, missingness within our original data was likely missing not at random, meaning complete case analyses may be biased, and traditional MICE methods are not generally able to fully resolve such bias.29 Future studies should consider alternative methods to handle missingness such as Monte Carlo simulations and Bayesian bias analysis, among others.29 Fourth, data on suicide mortality rates from IHME are modelled. While this is not necessarily unusual on its own, as mortality data from sources such as the WHO also represent modelled data, our analyses may still risk representing a ‘decoding’ of their model. That is, we may be unintentionally reproducing the model which was used to generate the suicide mortality rate data provided by IHME. Fifth, while IHME attempts to correct for misclassification of deaths involving self-harm with unclear intent when producing their estimates,1 the resulting rates likely underestimate the true suicide mortality rate. This may, in turn, impact the estimated association between APC and suicide mortality rate. The degree of misclassification may also vary by country. Sixth, APC was transformed to align with the age range of suicide mortality, as data availability and reliability concerns limited our ability to transform suicide mortality rates to align with the age range of APC. Further, APC does not capture illegal consumption nor consumption within subpopulations which may be at greater risk for suicide. The limitations of APC may also be of particular concern when considering younger populations. While youth may contribute a relatively small amount to total APC and the suicide mortality rate, the association between alcohol consumption and suicide may be stronger within those of younger age.30 Thus, while APC may still be useful as a metric within overarching population-level strategies, care should be taken to monitor consumption within specific high-risk subpopulations through alternative measures, despite known challenges with data capture. To address this, future studies may wish to consider binge drinking prevalence as an alternative measure of population-level alcohol consumption. For youth in particular, alcohol use or ‘drunkenness’ within the past 30 days (or lifetime prevalence) may also be potential alternatives. Seventh, due to concerns with overfitting, multicollinearity, as well as consistency of variable measurement across countries, there were other potential covariates which were not included in the current analyses, but may be relevant, such as prevalence of depression, illicit drug use, mental healthcare access and tobacco use. Future studies may wish to take these variables into consideration when conducting similar analyses if they are relevant to the study population of interest. Finally, our use of linear mixed models may not be appropriate when examining other countries. While non-linearity was not a concern in this study, based on residual versus predicted plots (see online supplemental figure S2), some countries may possess a different suicide mortality profile which requires greater consideration of non-linearity. Future studies should consider generalised additive mixed models as a potential alternative to linear mixed models.

Despite the noted limitations, there are a number of strengths worth mentioning. First, our analyses adjusted for confounders identified a priori from the literature. Second, our choice of modelling methods may be more appropriate for the current state of data availability compared with traditional time series methods which are currently common in the literature.11 Third, these methods also allow us to assess the association between APC and suicide mortality rate using panel data from multiple countries. Multi-country results may be more useful for overarching international strategies than country-specific results, which are also more common within the current literature.11 Fourth, our alignment with individual-level5 7 12 and population-level11 findings on the presence and overall direction of association reassures us of the robustness of our results.

In light of the finding of an association between APC and suicide mortality rate at the population level, which is similar across the sexes, national suicide prevention strategies should consider APC as an indicator. Further, implementing interventions such as alcohol pricing and availability policies which would target APC, a metric that is relatively easy to ascertain and could be closely monitored, may be reflected in the suicide mortality rate. However, the effectiveness of such population-level interventions on suicide may vary across countries due to differences in implementation as well as relevant sociocultural factors.
