Familial hereditary cardiac myxoma: report of three cases and literature review
Authors: Qian Yao, Bin Zhan, Huimin Liu, Liu Luo, Lin Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac myxoma, the most common primary benign cardiac tumor, carries a risk of embolization and can be life-threatening. It typically arises in the left atrium but may involve any cardiac chamber. Myxomas may occur sporadically, have familial origins, or present as part of Carney’s syndrome.Case presentationThree cases of cardiac myxoma are presented; two involved a father-daughter pair with familial myxoma, while one patient presented with acute exacerbation and was diagnosed postoperatively. Echocardiography and Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) revealed intracardiac space-occupying lesions suggestive of myxoma. Elective atrial resection was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass; histopathology confirmed the diagnosis. Genetic testing identified a Protein Kinase cAMP-Dependent Type I Regulatory Subunit Alpha (PRKAR1A) gene mutation.ConclusionsPaternal-to-daughter transmission of PRKAR1A mutations is uncommon compared with typical familial cardiac myxomas. All three cases in this series represent this transmission pattern. Surgical resection resulted in a favorable recovery without sequelae. Timely diagnosis and intervention are essential to prevent complications, and regular follow-up is necessary to detect recurrence. This study reports three cases of familial cardiac myxoma and includes a literature review.Keywords: Myxoma, Familial inheritance, Cardiac myxoma, Carney's syndrome, Cardiac tumor

Introduction

Although cardiac myxomas are biologically benign, they demonstrate “functional” malignancy due to their embolic potential, which can result in life-threatening events [1]. They may mimic connective tissue or inflammatory disorders, underscoring the need for timely diagnosis and surgical resection. Familial hereditary cardiac myxoma is a rare genetic disorder associated with specific gene mutations. Clinicians should remain vigilant to enable early recognition and definitive surgical management. This report presents three cases of familial hereditary cardiac myxoma and reviews current research to provide a reference for diagnosis and treatment.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 70-year-old man presented with “cough, chest distress, and discomfort” at an external hospital.The patient had a history of previous thoracic wall surgery and left upper limb dysfunction caused by cerebral infarction. After hospitalization, the patient improved and has been taking “aspirin enteric tablets” regularly.Cardiac ultrasound revealed a slightly hyperechoic mass in the left atrium and mitral valve, suggestive of myxoma. The patient was admitted to our hospital on November 25, 2024, for further diagnosis and treatment. Comprehensive assessment, including medical history, clinical manifestations, family history (Table 1), and imaging examinations— Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA; Table 1, Figure A) and echocardiography (Table 2)—led to the following diagnoses: ① Left atrial space-occupying lesion: myxoma; ② Valvular heart disease - mitral, aortic, and tricuspid regurgitation with cardiac dilatation and cardiac function class II; ③ Old cerebral infarction. After preoperative preparation, the patient underwent cardiac tumor resection with tricuspid valvuloplasty under general anesthesia with extracorporeal circulation on November 30, 2024. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 20 with good recovery and has remained complication-free.

Case 2

A 42-year-old female patient was admitted on December 6, 2024, after a “left atrial mass” was detected on cardiac ultrasound during a routine health checkup, one day following her father’s (Case 1) diagnosis of cardiac myxoma and surgical resection. Relevant medical history, clinical findings, and family history are provided in Table 1. CTA (Table 1, Fig. 1E) and echocardiography (Table 2, Fig. 1F) confirmed a diagnosis of “giant left atrial myxoma.” On December 7, 2024, she underwent elective cardiac tumor resection with mitral valvoplasty, tricuspid valvuloplasty, and closure of the coronary artery-right atrial fistula under general anesthesia with extracorporeal circulation. Intraoperative findings are documented in Tables 2 and Fig. 1G. Postoperative histopathology confirmed myxoma (Fig. 1H), and genetic testing identified a PRKAR1A mutation (Table 2). The patient was discharged on postoperative day 12 with complete recovery and has remained free of complications during follow-up.

Discussion

Cardiac myxoma is a benign cardiac tumor that may cause severe “functional” impairment [1, 2]. Approximately 90% are solitary [3], representing nearly 90% of cardiac surgery cases and 50% of cardiovascular medicine cases [4]. Female patients account for approximately 67%, and 75% of myxomas occur in the left atrium near the fossa ovalis [5, 6], while 15%–20% arise in the right atrium [3]. Familial cardiac myxoma represents approximately 10% of cases, frequently presenting as multiple tumors at atypical sites [7]. Although surgical resection is the mainstay of treatment, recurrence remains a concern. Sporadic myxomas recur in 4%–7% of cases, with secondary recurrence rare, whereas familial myxomas recur in 10%–21% [8], frequently with secondary recurrence. Secondary recurrence frequently develops approximately 10 years after the initial operation [9, 10]. Possible mechanisms include [11]: ① incomplete primary excision, following regrowth; ② familial predisposition; ③ spontaneous or surgical implantation of tumor fragments into myocardium; ④ pre-tumor lesions in other myocardial sites. Even with complete excision, recurrence occurs in approximately 20%, due to residual or new lesions [12]. Accordingly, patients in this study require echocardiographic follow-up every 6–12 months.Case 1 developed left upper limb dysfunction following cerebral infarction. Although standardized treatment led to functional recovery and improvement in the left arm, secondary symptoms caused by myxoma embolism could not be ruled out. Therefore, three patients in this study underwent regular stroke and myxoma recurrence risk screenings at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months post-surgery. In the second year after surgery, we conducted 12-month periodic screenings based on existing literature references [13, 14].

Cardiac myxoma, the most common cardiac tumor [8], is typically pedunculated and attached to the interatrial septum of the left atrium [11]. Less commonly, it arises from the posterior left atrium, right atrium, ventricles, or valves [15]. Familial myxomas exhibit autosomal incomplete dominant inheritance [9], frequently affecting younger individuals. They are more atypical, recur more regularly, and may present with Carney syndrome, neurofibromatosis (NAME) syndrome, or as non-syndromic familial myxoma [8, 11].Although genetic variations may be more likely to cause thrombotic myxomas, the data are not yet sufficient to draw a conclusion.None of the three patients in this study showed Carney syndrome; instead, all had paternal histories of myxoma and were incidentally diagnosed during examinations or admissions for unrelated conditions. The classic triad of myxoma includes intracardiac obstruction, embolism, and systemic symptoms [1]. Depending on location, size, morphology, and histopathological profile, patients may be asymptomatic, present with typical features, or develop life-threatening emergencies such as systemic inflammation, multiple emboli, or sudden cardiac death [16–18]. Up to 50% of cases are incidentally detected [19, 20]. Approximately one-third of patients develop mucocutaneous myxomas, affecting eyelids, external auditory canal, breasts, or oropharynx [3]. Cardiac tumors occur in 67% of such patients, and 75% display cutaneous pigmentation, including blue nevi, café-au-lait spots, or hypopigmented macules[21–24].

Additional features include freckles (melasma) on the lips, conjunctiva, canthi, and genital mucosa during prepuberty. Endocrine abnormalities may manifest as nodular cortical adenomas, growth hormone- or prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas, or large-cell calcifying Sertoli cell tumors [3]. Histopathology typically reveals polygonal multipotent mesenchymal cells [25–27], predominantly located in the subendocardial layer of the atria, especially the interatrial septum. Pathologically, myxoma cells are negative for endothelial markers such as keratin but positive for vimentin [26]. Some may also originate from neuroendocrine tissue [28]. These cells can differentiate into fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, and neuroendocrine cells [29, 30], and secrete an acid mucopolysaccharide matrix [25]. Differential diagnoses include organized thrombus, papillary fibroelastoma, myxoid sarcoma, angiosarcoma, epithelioid hemangioendothelioma, intimal sarcoma, and myxofibrosarcoma [31].

The diagnosis of familial cardiac myxoma associated with Carney syndrome follows Carney’s criteria, which include 12 clinical features and two supplementary items [3, 8, 12, 32–34]. A diagnosis is established when two clinical symptoms, or two features plus one supplementary item, are present [32]. Consequently, first-degree relatives should undergo screening and close follow-up. However, most cases lack specific manifestations and are incidentally detected during routine health examinations or while evaluating unrelated conditions. Patients with confirmed Carney syndrome require comprehensive screening, including urinary free cortisol measurement, testicular ultrasonography, and assessment of potential myxoma sites [3]. Routine echocardiography of first-degree relatives is the most effective strategy for detecting familial cardiac myxomas. Echocardiography remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, with computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) serving as useful adjuncts. Conversely, chest X-ray and electrocardiography have limited diagnostic value, while histopathology remains definitive. Approximately 75% of familial cardiac myxomas follow an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, with the remaining 25% arising from de novo mutations [35]. Most are caused by inactivating mutations or large deletions of the PRKAR1A gene on 17q22-24. Genetic testing confirmed a mutation at this locus in the present case, underscoring its critical role in evaluating familial myxoma cases.

Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for cardiac myxoma and significantly reduces the risk of tumor embolism [1, 36]. The procedure entails excision of the tumor’s pedicle at its base along with the full thickness of the adjacent interatrial septum, followed by closure of the resulting atrial septal defect [1]. Postoperative recurrence occurs in approximately 22% of patients, accounting for over 50% of related mortality [11]. However, data from recent decades indicate that surgical mortality, long-term survival, and recurrence rates remain relatively low [34, 37–53]. In recent years, adopting minimally invasive and robotic-assisted techniques has further improved postoperative outcomes and quality of life [54–57]. During operation, strict vigilance is required to prevent intraoperative embolization from tumor fragment dislodgement. No standardized treatment exists for embolism; however, therapeutic strategies such as open surgery, endovascular coil occlusion, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and conservative management have been reported [31, 58–60].

Conclusion

Although cardiac myxoma is among the most common cardiac tumors, it occurs sporadically in most cases. Familial inherited cardiac myxoma is extremely rare and is frequently associated with Carney syndrome. Despite its benign histology, cardiac myxoma can lead to severe complications, such as intracardiac obstruction and systemic embolism. Echocardiography is invaluable for diagnosis, yet the condition generally lacks specific clinical symptoms, delaying recognition. Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice, followed by long-term surveillance to detect recurrence. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion and apply appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to ensure timely intervention, thereby minimizing the morbidity and mortality rates in affected patients.
