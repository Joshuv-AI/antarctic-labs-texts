Classical and Quantum Beam Dynamics Simulation of the RF Photoinjector Test Bench at JINR Preprint: APS/123-QED
A.S. Dyatlov, A.V. Afanasyev, V.V. Kobets, A.E. Levichev, D.A. Nikiforov, M.A. Nozdrin, K.A. Sibiryakova, K.E. Yunenko, D.V. Karlovets

Abstract
We present simulation of the classical and quantum beam dynamics of an RF photoinjector test bench under development at Joint Institute for Nuclear Research. A 0.63 pC electron bunch is driven from a copper photocathode by a 262 nm ultraviolet laser and accelerated to energies of a few MeV in a 2856 MHz, 1.5-cell RF gun. Using realistic electromagnetic field maps and the ASTRA particle-tracking code, we optimize a Carlsten-style emittance-compensation solenoid to achieve a normalized transverse emittance of 3.2 ​ π ⋅ mm ⋅ mrad 3.2\ \pi\cdot\mathrm{mm}\cdot\mathrm{mrad} downstream of the gun. We also simulate the dynamic of a single-electron Laguerre–Gaussian wave packet carrying ℓ = ℏ \ell=\hbar orbital angular momuntum projection in the same RF fields: continuous acceleration reduces its de Broglie wavelength and suppresses transverse spreading by a factor of six compared to free-space propagation over 30 cm. These results confirm the injector design, demonstrate the feasibility of the proposed emittance-compensation scheme, and pave the way to the first experimental demonstration of relativistic vortex electrons generation in the few MeV energy range.



I
Introduction

At the Laboratory of Nuclear Problems, Joint Institute for Nuclear Research (JINR), an RF photoinjector test bench is under development. The system is based on an S-band RF photogun
Nikiforov et al. 2018
and a high-power ultraviolet (UV) drive laser
Gacheva et al. 2014
.

RF photoinjectors are the standard for producing high-brightness electron beams in modern accelerator facilities, such as LCLS
Alley et al. 1999
, European XFEL
Ferrario et al. 2001
, and SuperKEKB
Cahill et al. 2014
. Their advantage lies in generating ultrashort, high-current bunches with low transverse emittance, enabled by rapid acceleration to relativistic energies using high gradient RF cavities
Giribono et al. 2023
. This minimizes space charge effects, particularly critical at low energies
Maltseva et al. 2023
.

Such beams are essential for the
\mathrm{4^{th}}
generation light sources, free-electron lasers, and future linear colliders. The pursuit of better beam quality and control drives continued development of photoinjectors, supported by advanced beam dynamics simulations
Winick 1997
;
He et al. 2024
, which are vital for design optimization and understanding collective effects.

The existing JINR LINAC uses a DC thermionic gun following an RF bunching system consisting of the chopper, prebuncher and buncher
Nozdrin et al. 2021
. The bunch length after bunching system is
\sim
1–2 ps. Replacing the DC gun an RF photoinjector will enable direct production of
\sim
10 ps bunches. Although the bunch length is somewhat longer, the photoinjector provides a significant improvement in beam quality and offers precise control over the generated bunch charge and transverse size, thereby broadening opportunities for time-resolved experiments and advanced beam shaping.

These improvements also create the conditions to tackle another important scientific task – the generation of relativistic vortex electron beams, the massive counterparts of the twisted photons
Knyazev and Serbo 2018
. These beams represent quantum states with a helical phase and quantized orbital angular momentum (OAM)
Bliokh et al. 2017
;
Floettmann and Karlovets 2020
;
Karlovets 2021
;
Ivanov 2022
. While demonstrated at energies of about 300 keV using phase masks
Uchida and Tonomura 2010
;
Verbeeck et al. 2010
;
Blackburn and Loudon 2014
, relativistic vortex beams remain experimentally elusive. However they are promising for nuclear and hadronic physics, including spin structure studies and as alternatives to spin-polarized beams
Ivanov 2022
. Theoretically, OAM can be transferred from twisted light
Knyazev and Serbo 2018
to electrons via photoemission under specific conditions
Karlovets 2021
;
Pavlov et al. 2024
;
Kazinski et al. 2025
. Combining this approach with high gradient RF acceleration, the test bench aims to produce vortex beams in the multi-MeV range, with a path toward
\sim
200 MeV enabled by modernization of the LINAC accelerator injector.

We present beam dynamics simulations of bunch generation and acceleration in the JINR photoinjector, demonstrating implementation of the Carlsten scheme. Under optimized solenoid focusing and injection phase, normalized emittance can be reduced by over an order of magnitude compared to the uncompensated case. These results confirm the viability of the design and inform further development of high brightness and structured beams at JINR.

This study also considers the single electron quantum regime: the electron is modeled as a fully coherent Laguerre–Gaussian packet
Floettmann and Karlovets 2020
;
Karlovets 2021
with fixed transverse size and OAM. Collective effects, such as Coulomb interactions or space-charge effect and decoherence, are not included. Thus, the results represent an upper bound for coherence and structure preservation. Future work will extend this to multi-electron systems with realistic beam statistics.

II
Test Bench Design

The RF photoinjector test bench under development at JINR is a system featuring an S-band RF photogun — the source of high-quality electron beams for injection into the accelerator line. The overall layout, shown in Fig.
1
, includes the RF gun, laser beamline, magnetic focusing elements, and vacuum infrastructure.

Figure 1
:
Schematic of the RF photoinjector test bench. Key components: S-band RF photogun, UV drive laser, solenoid lenses, vacuum laser beamline, and laser injection mirror.

A copper photocathode is mounted inside the cathode assembly and illuminated by UV laser pulses synchronized with the RF phase at a wavelength of
\lambda=262\ \mathrm{nm}
chosen to match its work function for efficient emission
Xiang and Teichert 2015
. The high accelerating field quickly brings electrons to relativistic energies, preserving beam quality at the earliest stage.

Two solenoids are used for beam formation and control: a cathode solenoid close to the photocathode and a focusing solenoid. They regulate transverse phase space and beam size. The beam then passes through a vacuum transport section at
\sim 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{Torr}
, providing space for diagnostics and future components. Ion vacuum pumps along the line maintain necessary pressure levels for photocathode stability and minimizing beam scattering. The total line length is about 1.5 m.

Such compact acceleration is critical for maintaining low transverse emittance and mitigating space charge effects. The internal geometry of the gun and the placement of the photocathode, depicted in Fig.

The laser is injected at an angle through the coaxial tube, which simultaneously serves for RF cavity–waveguide impedance matching, laser delivery, and electron beam extraction, enabling direct illumination of the photocathode.

II.2
Ultraviolet Drive Laser

The UV laser system is based on a mode-locked ytterbium-doped fiber oscillator at
\lambda=1047\ \mathrm{nm}
, employing a semiconductor saturable absorber mirror (SESAM) for pulse compression to 10 ps. Amplification is achieved by two lamp-pumped Nd:YLF crystal amplifiers.

The
\mathrm{4^{th}}
harmonic is generated sequentially: second harmonic in a KTP crystal, followed by fourth harmonic in a BBO crystal. A recent upgrade
Dyatlov et al. 2024
increased the oscillator repetition rate to the
\mathrm{71^{st}}
subharmonic of the RF frequency (40.225 MHz) and introduced synchronized control of the lamp amplifiers, improving UV pulse energy and stability (3–4
\mathrm{\mu J}
).

The laser beamline is divided into two sections: the first transports the laser beam through air from the drive laser to the vacuum chamber, while the second delivers the beam within the vacuum chamber, redirecting it by 45
∘
using a specialized metallic mirror. The beam is focused onto the cathode surface by a lens with a focal length of 2500 mm, resulting in a laser spot size on the photocathode of approximately 2 mm.

III
Beam Dynamics Simulation

All beam dynamics simulations were carried out with ASTRA
Floettmann 2017
, which couples a 3D particle-in-cell space-charge solver (in cylindrical symmetry) to high-order Runge–Kutta tracking through RF-cavity and magnetic fields. The key inputs are the bunch charge (from photocathode quantum efficiency and laser energy), mean initial energy and its spread, initial normalized emittance and spatial/momentum distributions, and the RF phase at photoemission.

IV
Quantum Beam Dynamics

IV.1
Vortex Electron Beams

In general, a vortex electron beam represents a free-electron state whose wave function can be described in terms of a Laguerre–Gaussian mode, which is an exact non-stationary solution to the Schroedinger equation and is well-known, for instance, in optics
Allen et al. 1992
. Its transverse wave function is expressed as
Karlovets 2021

\begin{split}\psi_{\ell,n}(\bm{\rho},t)&=N\frac{\rho^{|\ell|}}{(\sigma_{\perp}(t))^{|\ell|+1}}L_{n}^{|\ell|}\left(\frac{\rho^{2}}{(\sigma_{\perp}(t))^{2}}\right)\\
&\quad\times\exp\Biggl[i\ell\phi_{r}-i(2n+|\ell|+1)\arctan\left(\frac{t}{t_{d}}\right)\\
&\qquad-\frac{\rho^{2}}{2(\sigma_{\perp}(t))^{2}}\left(1-i\frac{t}{t_{d}}\right)\Biggr]\end{split}

(9)

where the normalization constant
N
is chosen such that

\int d^{3}x\,|\psi_{\ell,n}(\bm{r},t)|^{2}=1.

(10)

Here,
\ell=0,\pm 1,\pm 2,...,
is the OAM in units of
\hbar
,
n=0,1,2,...,
is the radial quantum number,
L_{n}^{\ell}
denotes the generalized Laguerre polynomial,
\sigma_{\perp}(t)
is the transverse beam size, and
t_{d}
is the characteristic diffraction time. It is important to note that the azimuthal phase factor
\exp(i \ell\phi_r)
, which makes the state an eigenfunction of the OAM operator
Karlovets 2021

\hat{L}_{z}\psi_{\ell,n}=\ell\hbar\,\psi_{\ell,n},

(11)

is a defining feature of vortex beams, as it gives rise to the helical phase-front structure and underlies the presence of OAM
\ell\hbar
per particle.

Among the various approaches, the most viable method for generating vortex electron beams in RF photoinjectors is via the photoeffect induced by light-driven atomic ionization. Theoretical studies have shown that when an atom is positioned on the axis of a light vortex, the photoemitted electron acquires a well-defined projection of OAM; this behavior persists for ensembles of atoms
Pavlov et al. 2024
;
Kazinski et al. 2025
. Alternative schemes based on electron diffraction
Uchida and Tonomura 2010
;
Verbeeck et al. 2010
;
Blackburn and Loudon 2014
are impractical in such setups. For electrons with relativistic energies of approximately 2 MeV (Fig.
7
), the Bragg–Wolf condition requires diffraction gratings with sub-picometer periods, which are currently beyond fabrication capabilities. Moreover, integrating a grating with a larger, feasible period into the cavity of an RF gun is precluded by the resonator geometry. Therefore, photoemission remains the method of choice for producing vortex electron beams in RF photoinjectors.

IV.2
Quantum Evolution of a Single-Electron Wave Packet in the RF Field

To generate relativistic vortex electrons with OAM, we must track the quantum evolution of individual wave packets during acceleration. Here, we examine the transverse expectation value
\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(\langle z\rangle)
, where the braces denote quantum averaging, for a single-electron state under two scenarios:

1.

Free-space propagation over the RF photogun length;

2.

Acceleration in the RF field of the photoinjector.

The general law of wave packet propagation is given by:
Karlovets 2021

\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(\langle z\rangle)=\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)\left(1+\frac{\langle z\rangle^{2}}{z_{R}^{2}}\right)

(12)

where
z_{R}
is the Rayleigh length, given as

z_{R}=\frac{2\pi}{M}\frac{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}{\lambda_{dB}},

(13)

where
M=2n+|\ell|+1
is a quality factor of the packet and
\lambda_{dB}
is the De Broglie wavelength. In the far field (
\langle z\rangle\gg z_{R}
), from Eq. (
12
), follows a simple relationship, dubbed van Cittert-Zernike theorem
Karlovets 2021
, between the measured packet width at the detector and the initial packet width near the source

\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}=\frac{\langle z\rangle\lambda_{dB}}{2\pi\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(\langle z\rangle)}}M,

(14)

The propagation law, which holds in the intermediate (Fresnel) zone, is given by

\begin{split}\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(\langle z\rangle)}={}&\frac{\langle z\rangle\lambda_{dB}}{2\pi\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}}M\\
&\times\left(1+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{2\pi\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}{\langle z\rangle\lambda_{dB}}\frac{1}{M}\right)^{2}\right)\end{split}

(15)

for an initial size
\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}=1\ \mathrm{nm}
, which is a typical rms radius of an electron packet nearby the generation region
Karlovets 2021
, mode numbers
n=3
and
\ell=1
(OAM
=\ell\hbar
), and a 30 cm photogun length, this expression predicts free‐space spreading to an RMS width of about 12 mm, which corresponds to an electron with an initial energy of about 5 eV(Fig.
15
).

Next, we include acceleration. The de Broglie wavelength varies as

\lambda_{\mathrm{dB}}(\langle z\rangle)=\frac{hc}{p_{z}(\langle z\rangle)}.

(16)

The longitudinal momentum
p_{z}(\langle z\rangle)
is determined by integrating the energy gain acquired by an electron passing through the RF cavity. The field profile was obtained previous in this simulation and shown in Fig.
3
. This field acting on a particle moving along the
z
-axis with velocity
v
is given by

E_{z}(z)=E_{z0}\sin\left(\omega\frac{z}{v}+\phi\right),

(17)

where
E_{z0}
is a peak field amplitude,
\omega
is the RF angular frequency and
\phi
is the injection phase. The corresponding gain in kinetic energy over accelerating gap of length

\Delta E_{\rm kin}=eE_{z0}\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}\cos\!\bigl(\omega\,\tfrac{z}{v}+\phi\bigr)\ dz

(18)

This expression accounts for the phase-dependent acceleration experienced by particles with finite longitudinal extent and underlies the development of a velocity–energy correlation during bunching. At larger distances, once the beam is fully accelerated, its transverse spread enters the far-field regime, where the RMS radius evolves as described by Eq. (
14
). This yields a nonlinear, reduced spreading (Fig.
15
), with the final RMS width about
six times smaller than in free-space
, due to continuous momentum gain and de Broglie‐wavelength shortening.

Figure 15
:
Evolution of the transverse width of a single-electron Laguerre–Gaussian wave packet with initial size
\sqrt{\langle\rho^{2}\rangle(0)}=1\ \mathrm{nm}
, OAM
=\ell\hbar
, and
n=3
over the 30 cm photogun length in free space (blue) and in an RF field (red). In free space, the packet expands to
\sim
12 mm due to quantum spreading. Accounting for the change in longitudinal momentum

under RF acceleration slows the spreading by about a factor of six due to the shortening of the de Broglie wavelength. For clarity, the transverse width in the
p_{z}
-varying case is scaled in the plot.

These propagation laws, derived for single-particle wave packets without accounting for collective effects, can also be applied to structured vortex beams. This extension provides valuable guidance for preserving spatial coherence and OAM content during beam transport and acceleration. By understanding and controlling these fundamental behavior, photoinjector designs can be optimized to maintain the quality and characteristic properties of vortex beams throughout the early acceleration stages.

V
Conclusions

We have demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed emittance-compensation scheme in a practical photoinjector, using particle tracking with realistic boundaries and field maps. Under optimized conditions, space-charge effects are substantially mitigated, and a solenoidal lens suppresses nonlinear phase-space distortions, stabilizing the beam envelope and normalized transverse emittance.

While operationally viable, the scheme yields a final emittance of
\approx 3.2\ \pi\cdot\mathrm{mm\cdot mrad}
, about twice the theoretical minimum without a compensating solenoid, due to unavoidable magnetization near the photocathode from fringe fields. Potential improvements include an extended solenoid for smoother gradients and a bucking coil to nullify residual cathode fields.

The RF photoinjector is assembled and in commissioning, with initial photoemission signals detected. Efforts to increase laser intensity and cathode quantum efficiency are underway to achieve robust bunches. An emittance-diagnostics module, developed in collaboration with Tomsk Polytechnic University and validated via ASTRA simulations, will enable precise experimental benchmarking.

The present theory treats single-electron quantum wave packets in RF fields, neglecting space-charge and many-body decoherence, whose inclusion is planned for a full description of relativistic vortex beams. In parallel, significant progress has been made on an ultraviolet vortex photon source, with phase-structured optics and diagnostics assembled and tested. However, experiments require a stable Gaussian electron beam to verify the predicted suppression of transverse spreading for OAM-carrying packets in RF acceleration.
