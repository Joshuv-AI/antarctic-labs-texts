Risk factors for the development of acral melanoma: A multi-institutional, retrospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcral melanoma (AM) is a rare but aggressive melanoma subtype. Understanding demographic and clinical risk factors is essential for advancing prevention and early detection.ObjectivesTo identify demographic and clinical risk factors for AM compared with nonmelanoma and cutaneous melanoma (CM) controls.MethodsThis multi-institutional retrospective cohort study of patients evaluated at the Mass General Brigham and Dana-Farber Cancer Institute. Three cohorts were created via manual curation of pathology reports: AM cases, nonmelanoma controls, and CM controls. AM patients were matched 1:4 to controls based on pre/postindex follow-up time and dermatologic care history. Multivariable logistic regression and structural equation modeling assessed direct and indirect pathways linking sex, actinic keratoses, and melanoma subtype.ResultsA total of 2115 patients were analyzed. AM patients were more often female and disproportionately Asian or Black compared with both controls. Relative to non-melanoma controls, AM was associated with female sex, Asian, or Black race, leukemia/lymphoma, nonmelanoma skin cancer, and actinic keratoses. Similar findings were present against CM controls.ConclusionsAM risk is associated with female sex, Asian or Black race, and select prior malignancies. Findings demonstrate an association between markers of UV exposure (actinic keratoses) and AM risk, though significantly weaker than in CM. These insights may guide prevention, risk stratification, and mechanistic research.

Introduction

Acral melanoma (AM) is a rare but aggressive subtype of cutaneous melanoma arising on the palms, soles, and nail unit.1 In contrast to cutaneous melanoma (CM), which is strongly associated with ultraviolet (UV) radiation, AM is generally regarded as UV-independent.2 Epidemiologic studies consistently demonstrate that AM represents a disproportionately high share of melanoma cases among Asian, Black, and Hispanic/Latino populations.3 Alarmingly, AM is often diagnosed at more advanced stages, a disparity largely attributed to delayed detection and limited public awareness.4

While the risk factors for CM, such as UV exposure, fair skin, and high naevus counts, are well established, the etiological drivers of AM remain incompletely defined. Improved understanding of demographic and clinical risk factors for AM is needed to guide early recognition, prevention strategies, and surveillance, particularly in high-risk groups. In this multi-institutional retrospective cohort study, we compared patients with AM to both nonmelanoma and CM controls. We aimed to delineate demographic and clinical predictors of AM, and to evaluate the contribution of UV exposure, proxied by actinic keratoses, using mediation analysis.

Materials and methods

Study design

This multi-institutional retrospective study was conducted at the Mass General Brigham Healthcare System and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, 2 major academic dermato-oncology centers. After approval by the institutional review board, data were collected from electronic patient records. Patients were included if they had a confirmed diagnosis of melanoma between January 1, 1980, and December 31, 2020. The study assembled 3 distinct cohorts for analysis: (1) AM cases, with diagnoses confirmed by manual review of the medical record and dermatopathology histologic reports; (2) nonmelanoma controls; and (3) CM controls.

Data collection

Pseudonymized, patient-related data were extracted in a standardized manner. The index date for AM and CM cohorts was the date of melanoma diagnosis; for nonmelanoma controls, it was the closest clinical appointment in the same calendar year as their matched case. Collected data included demographics (sex, age at index date), clinical variables (body mass index, smoking status), and relevant medical history. Comorbidities, including prior actinic keratosis (AK) and nonmelanoma skin cancer (NMSC), were identified using dermatologist-curated ICD-9 and ICD-10 code sets. The duration of pre-index follow-up time and years of prior dermatologic care were also recorded for each patient.

Statistical methods

To minimize surveillance bias, AM patients were matched 1:4 to controls using a nearest-neighbor algorithm based on the duration of preindex follow-up and prior years of dermatologic care. Multivariable conditional logistic regression, an appropriate method for matched data, was used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A final parsimonious model was selected via a bidirectional stepwise variable selection procedure based on the Akaike Information Criterion. To evaluate a hypothesized mediation pathway, Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) was used to estimate the direct effect of biological sex on melanoma subtype risk, as well as the indirect effect mediated through a history of AK (as a proxy for cumulative UV-mediated damage). All statistical analyses were performed using R Core Team (v4.21, R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing, R Foundation for Statistical Computing; 2022), with the MatchIt and lavaan packages.

Mediation analysis of UV exposure

SEM mediation analyses revealed important differences in the magnitude, but not the direction, of UV-mediated risk between AM and CM subtypes. While cutaneous melanoma demonstrated robust complete mediation through prior AK history (indirect effect: β = −0.035, P < .001; direct effect: β = −0.011, P = .136), AM showed a marginally significant indirect effect (β = −0.007, P = .086) with a nonsignificant direct pathway (β = 0.048, P = .164). Critically, the positive association between prior AK and acral melanoma risk (β = 0.107, P = .034) was statistically significant, albeit substantially attenuated compared to cutaneous melanoma (β = 0.481, P < .001), indicating that UV exposure does contribute to acral melanoma pathogenesis, though to a lesser degree. Both subtypes demonstrated comparable sex-based differences in AK prevalence (acral: β = −0.068, P = .005; cutaneous: β = −0.073, P < .001, Tables III and IV), but the downstream melanoma risk attributable to UV damage diverged in magnitude. These findings challenge the traditional paradigm that AM is entirely UV-independent and suggest instead that chronic UV exposure may serve as a contributory, if not predominant, risk factor even for melanomas arising on sun-protected sites. Discussion

In this large, multi-institutional retrospective cohort study, we identified several demographic and clinical factors associated with increased risk of AM compared with both non-melanoma and CM controls.

Race and ethnicity

Our results demonstrated that Asian and Black patients had markedly higher odds of AM compared to both nonmelanoma and CM controls. This is consistent with established epidemiology: although melanoma incidence overall is lower in patients of color, AM constitutes a much higher proportion of melanomas in these groups. In U.S. registry data, acral lentiginous melanoma accounts for nearly 19% of melanomas in Black patients and 23% in Asian/Pacific Islander patients, compared to <1% in non-Hispanic White patients.5 Similarly, studies of Asian populations have consistently shown acral lentiginous melanoma to be the most common subtype.6 Diagnostic delays in skin of color have been widely reported, with AM often presenting at more advanced stages. The enrichment of AM in these populations underscores the importance of culturally tailored public health campaigns, community education, and increased vigilance during skin examinations in non-White patients.

Sex differences

Female sex emerged as a significant risk factor for AM compared to both nonmelanoma and CM controls. This observation aligns with prior institutional series and registry data that reported a modest female predominance among AM cases.7, 8, 9, 10 Several mechanisms may underlie this sex disparity, such as discussed in our SEM results. From a behavioral perspective, women are more likely to engage in activities that subject acral sites to repetitive trauma, such as the use of constrictive footwear and cosmetic nail practices.11,12 These exposures are consistent with the “mechanical stress” hypothesis, which posits that chronic microtrauma contributes to melanocytic proliferation and malignant transformation in acral sites.13 We also considered the possibility of detection bias. However, because our matching addressed dermatologic utilization, we evaluated outpatient visit frequency and observed only a small absolute difference between men and women (0.56 vs 0.64 visits respectively, P = .23). This suggests that the observed female predominance in AM risk is likely biological or environmental rather than an artifact of surveillance.

Hematologic malignancy

We also observed a strong association between prior leukemia or lymphoma and AM, with more than a twofold increased risk compared to nonmelanoma controls. This finding likely reflects impaired immune surveillance, a known contributor to melanoma risk.14 Patients with hematologic malignancies, particularly chronic lymphocytic leukemia, have consistently been shown to carry elevated risks of melanoma and other skin cancers, attributable to both disease-related immune dysfunction and treatment-related immunosuppression.15, 16, 17, 18, 19 Our study extends this association specifically to AM, suggesting that immune compromise may play a role in enabling acral melanomagenesis, even in sites with limited UV exposure. This finding supports the need for enhanced dermatologic surveillance in patients with hematologic cancers, with careful attention to acral sites.

Actinic keratoses and UV exposure

The differential association of AK history across control groups is particularly noteworthy. CM patients were more likely than AM patients to have a history of AK, consistent with the well-established link between CM and cumulative photodamage. Notably, AM patients were more likely than non-melanoma controls to have a history of AKs. This finding is intriguing given that AM is historically considered UV-independent. Genomic analyses demonstrate that most AMs lack UV-induced mutational signatures and instead arise through complex structural rearrangements and amplifications of genes such as TERT, CCND1, CDK4, and MDM2.20 In the minority of AMs where SBS7 UV signatures are detected, they are typically confined to the in situ stage and largely absent from invasive and metastatic stages, suggesting that UV may not be an inciting factor but rather a modifier of tumor evolution.20 However, acral site may warrant further investigation. Previous research has demonstrated that dorsal AMs differ from volar and subungual/interdigital tumors, with the latter exhibiting fewer BRAF mutations, greater mutational heterogeneity (eg, PIK3CA, STK11, EGFR, FGFR3, PTPN11), and more frequent CDK4/cyclin D1 copy number aberrations.21 We aimed to delineate demographic and clinical predictors of AM and employed SEM—a novel methodological approach in this context—to evaluate the contribution of UV exposure, proxied by actinic keratoses, using mediation analysis. Our mediation analysis supports this nuance, showing that while the association between AK history and AM is statistically significant, it is substantially weaker than that observed for CM. It is important to note that while AKs reflect cumulative UV dose to sun-exposed skin, their association with AM likely reflects systemic factors—such as UV-induced immunosuppression or inherent DNA repair defects—rather than direct UV mutagenesis at the acral site. Thus, while UV exposure is not the primary driver of AM, it may play a contributory role via these systemic pathways.

Nonmelanoma skin cancer

A history of NMSC was also associated with increased AM risk compared to non-melanoma controls. This finding is consistent with large population-based studies showing that prior NMSC significantly increases the risk of subsequent melanoma.22, 23, 24, 25 The mechanisms underlying this association are multifactorial: shared host susceptibility, field cancerization, chronic cutaneous injury, and immune dysfunction all likely contribute.26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 Additionally, patients with prior NMSC undergo more frequent dermatologic surveillance, increasing the likelihood of detecting AM. While detection bias cannot be excluded, the strong and consistent association suggests that NMSC history is an important marker of elevated risk for AM and should prompt careful examination of acral sites during follow-up visits.

Limitations

This retrospective, multi-institutional study is subject to several limitations. Reliance on electronic health records cannot fully account for diagnostic delays and raises the possibility of misclassification. A total of 423 AM patients were enrolled, and the sample size is relatively small compared with that of other large-sample studies on AM. Although AK diagnoses were histologically verified, the retrospective nature of the data precluded quantification of total body surface area affected. Furthermore, the prevalence of AKs in our nonmelanoma control group (2.8%) is lower than population estimates for this age group. This likely reflects surveillance bias and under-coding in routine dermatologic care, in contrast to melanoma patients who undergo rigorous full-body skin examinations. Consequently, the magnitude of the association between AKs and AM in our study may be inflated by differential ascertainment and should be interpreted as a marker of health care phenotype as well as biological risk. While we matched cases and controls on dermatologic healthcare utilization as a proxy for access to care, this approach does not directly capture or mitigate diagnostic delays, particularly those related to the acral site, where lesions are more likely to be overlooked. At present, no validated EHR-based measures exist to directly adjust for diagnostic delay in AM, and this remains an important limitation of retrospective registry-based analyses. Use of AKs as a proxy for UV exposure may misrepresent true sun damage, potentially under- or overestimating its contribution to AM. Residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, socioeconomic status, trauma, or behavioral exposures) is possible, and subgroup analyses remain limited by small sample sizes. Finally, as this cohort was drawn from tertiary referral centers, findings may not be fully generalizable.

Conclusion

In this multi-institutional retrospective cohort study, we compared patients with AM to both nonmelanoma and CM controls and identified demographic and clinical factors associated with AM risk, including female sex, Asian or Black race, prior hematologic malignancy, and a history of nonmelanoma skin cancer. Unlike prior studies that primarily compare melanoma subtypes, this design allows for the identification of risk factors relative to the general patient population. These results extend prior observations by delineating distinct risk profiles for AM relative to both nonmelanoma and cutaneous melanoma controls and highlight the need for heightened clinical vigilance, particularly among patients of color and those with immune compromise or prior skin cancer. Future studies integrating genomic, environmental, and behavioral data are warranted to clarify mechanisms of acral melanomagenesis and to prospectively validate these risk factors in diverse populations.
