A general method for synthesizing heteropore covalent organic framework membranes to rapidly enrich uranyl ions
Authors: Yue Zheng, Wanying Chen, Cheng Zhang, Jiarui Cao, Sirui Li, Xinbo Li, Yingbo Song, Lu Luo, Liping Wen, Xiangyu Kong, Hongjuan Ma, Ye Yuan, Guangshan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heteropore covalent organic framework membranes are predicted to exhibit excellent uranium extraction capabilities due to their combination of rapid transport and strong affinity. However, the growth orientation of covalent organic framework networks is susceptible to disturbance when a supporting substrate is introduced, posing a major challenge to the fabrication of covalent organic framework membranes with well-defined hierarchical pores. Here, a general synthetic method is introduced for the preparation of heteropore covalent organic framework membranes. By pre-coating covalent organic framework nanoparticles onto a nylon substrate, the subsequent covalent organic framework network grows following the orientation of the nanoparticles under solvothermal conditions. This method demonstrates remarkable versatility across a broad range of covalent organic framework systems, including single-pore (with sql net) and dual-pore (with kgm net) networks. Several heteropore covalent organic framework membranes are fabricated with pore sizes ranging from 7.1/26.9 Å to 11.2/34.3 Å and 14.0/42.1 Å. The covalent organic framework membrane with 7.1/26.9 Å dual-pores exhibits the fastest uranyl ion transport rate among all Schiff-base porous membranes, which is 4.3 times faster than that of typical covalent organic framework membranes with single-pore channels. After irradiation treatment, the covalent organic framework membrane enables the conversion of uranium (VI) ions from seawater into insoluble precipitates, which suggests a versatile design paradigm for heteropore covalent organic framework membranes with synthetically engineered functionalities.

Introduction

Seawater harbors the largest known reserve of uranium (U), primarily in the form of uranyl ions, estimated at 450 million tons—sufficient to sustain global nuclear industries for nearly 1000 years1–9. However, extracting uranium from seawater remains a formidable challenge due to its extremely low concentration (~3.3 mg per ton) and the presence of abundant interfering ions, such as Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, Fe3+, and VO2+5,10–13. Membrane separation technology (MST) has emerged as a promising solution, offering high selectivity, low energy consumption, and environmental friendliness14–17. Recent advancements have led to the development of innovative membranes, including metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)18, hydrogels19, and poly(amidoxime) fibers20. Despite improvements in adsorption capacity and recyclability, the large kinetic diameters (0.59–1.15 nm) of uranyl-based complexes, such as UO2(H2O)n2+ and UO2(CO3)34− in seawater21 hinder their diffusion through membrane pores, resulting in relatively low adsorption efficiency.

Hierarchically porous membranes (HPMs) are materials characterized by pore structures with two or more distinct length scales. The large apertures in HPMs enable rapid and unimpeded diffusion of uranyl-containing species, while the interconnected small cavities enhance host-guest affinity22. Due to their reduced diffusion barriers and high mass transport efficiency, HPMs have garnered significant attention for applications in substance transport and separation23–26. Remarkably, Wen et al. demonstrated a 20-fold increase in uranium (U) adsorption capacity by incorporating polymers of intrinsic microporosity (PIMs) into HPMs, surpassing the performance of conventional PIM membranes27. Although high mass transport has been realized, most HPMs are constructed from amorphous solids. Inside the irregular channels, uranyl-based complexes must undergo kinetically unfavorable transformations, such as shedding bound water or carbonate ions in narrow cavities and rebinding with water molecules in larger apertures. These processes result in low ion migration rates and prolonged extraction times28,29.

Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop HPMs with ordered structures to accelerate mass transport rates and enhance the uranium enrichment efficiency. Heteropore covalent organic frameworks (COFs) were first proposed by Zhao et al.30, combining the core characteristics of homopore COFs while broadening their potential applications across fields like multiphase catalysis, energy storage, gas adsorption, chemical sensing, and environmental remediation31–34. Their unique multicompartment framework and diverse pore distribution provide them with structural benefits that homopore COFs cannot achieve35. Heteropore covalent organic frameworks (COFs) have emerged as a novel HPM material. The well-ordered structures, hierarchical porosity, and tunable compositions of heteropore COFs could provide diverse pathways and reduce the motion resistance of guest ions, thereby increasing their transport rate36,37. However, the synthesis of COF-based HPMs is challenging because their ordered heteropore structure is highly susceptible to being disturbed. Slight changes in factors such as solvent, temperature, and impurities will lead to variations in the topology and crystallinity of the resultant heteropore structure38,39. In this paper, a general synthetic method was introduced for the preparation of high-quality hierarchically porous COF membranes. After uniformly distributing the COF nanoparticles on a nylon substrate, the COF-covered nylon was placed in a solvothermal system to obtain the COF membrane. Adjusting the length of the linking blocks, a series of COF membranes was successfully fabricated with different dual-pore (with kgm net) sizes of 7.1/26.9, 11.2/34.3, and 14.0/42.1 Å. Compared with other membranes, the 7.1/26.9 Å COF with kgm net membrane enabled the fastest transport rates for uranyl ions, which were 5.3 times that of single-pore (Bd-COF) materials. Under external light exposure, the COF-TPA (with kgm net) material converted U(VI) into insoluble precipitates, achieving a U extraction capacity of 16.9 mg g−1 in actual seawater.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of heteropore COF membranes

The dual-pore (kgm topology) COF-TPA network was selected as an object for illustrating the assembly process of COF-based HPMs (Fig. 1). First, COF-TPA with pore sizes of 7.1 and 26.9 Å was prepared via a solvothermal reaction of tetrakis(4-aminophenyl)ethene (ETTA) and 1,4-phthalaldehyde (TPA) in a 9:1 (v:v) mixture of 1,4-dioxane and mesitylene30. After sonication for 60 min, the COF-TPA nanoparticles with an external diameter of ~200 nm were obtained [determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fig. 2a]. As depicted in Fourier transform infrared (FT–IR) spectroscopy, the C=N stretching vibration was observed at 1619 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 1), verifying the formation of imine linkages. Based on theoretical simulations performed with the Forcite module in a modeling program, where geometry optimization was conducted via the Quasi-Newton algorithm at the ultra-fine quality level30, COF-TPA was found to possess two possible 2D structures corresponding to kgm and sql topologies. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis indicated that the COF-TPA nanoparticles displayed distinct peaks at 2.78°, 5.43°, and 8.14°, assignable to the (100), (200), and (300) facets, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 2), which were identical to those of COF-TPA with a kgm topology. After the COF-TPA nanoparticles were successfully synthesized, a turbid suspension of COF-TPA nanoparticles and ethanol was uniformly cast onto the nylon substrate (Supplementary Fig. 3). The dioxane solution of ETTA and TPA was then uniformly coated on the nylon substrate, followed by spraying 6 M acetic acid. Finally, the substrate was put in a flask and heated to 120 °C under a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere for 48 h. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, FT-IR spectroscopy confirmed the formation of the imine linkages initially, as evidenced by the C=N characteristic band at 1618 cm−1. The solid-state cross-polarization magic angle spinning carbon-13 nuclear magnetic resonance (CP-MAS 13C-NMR) spectrum confirmed the C=N bonds with a resonance signal at ~157 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 5).

SEM characterization revealed that irregular aggregates composed of short lumps with rough surfaces were immobilized on the nylon substrate during the initial 0–3 h of polymerization (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 6). As the polymerization was prolonged, the surface of the COF membrane gradually flattened. After 48 h, the top-view SEM image illustrated the uniform coating of the COF-TPA membrane, and the side-view showed that the COF layer tightly adhered to the nylon substrate with a ~23-µm thickness (Fig. 2b). Moreover, the monomer concentrations were adjusted, and COF membranes were prepared with different thicknesses of ~31, ~23, and ~14 µm.

PXRD analysis of the COF-TPA membrane showed obvious peaks at 2.78°, 5.43°, and 8.14°, corresponding to the (100), (200), and (300) planes, respectively (Supplementary Figs. 7, 13, and 14). These results confirmed the successful preparation of the COF-TPA membrane with kgm net30.

To verify the function of this method, several factors, including nanoparticle crystallinity and concentration, were investigated to probe the mechanism by which the nanoparticles guide the growth process of COF membranes. With the total area (A2θ) of the main PXRD diffraction peak as a reference, a normalization parameter A2θrel can be obtained (Supplementary Fig. 8)40, and this parameter is used to quantitatively analyze the crystallinity of the membrane.

The COF monomers were placed in solutions with stir bars rotating at different speeds (0, 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 10, and 30 rpm), which gave COF nanoparticles with crystallinity (A2θrel) ranging from 0.37 to 0. Using the COF membrane synthesis method above, but with different COF nanoparticles, a series of COF membranes was prepared. As shown in Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 9, and Supplementary Table 1, the crystallinity of the synthesized membranes (A2θrel) was found to be linearly correlated with nanoparticle crystallinity. A series of nanoparticle concentrations (the crystallinity, A2θrel, was 0.37), including 0.01, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, and 0.20 mg mL–1 was employed to prepare the COF membranes. As shown in Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 10, and Supplementary Table 2, the crystallinity of the COF membranes (A2θrel) increased with increasing nanoparticle concentration in the range of 0.01-0.10. When the concentration exceeded 0.10 mg mL–1, the crystallinity of the COF membranes remained essentially unchanged. All of these results demonstrated that the concentration and crystallinity of the nanoparticles have a significant impact on the crystallinity of COF membranes.

To investigate the validity of this method, a COF-TPA membrane was synthesized directly using the same synthetic procedure described above, but without COF-TPA nanoparticles. The wide angle X-ray scattering (WAXS) 1D patterns of the resultant COF-TPA membrane exhibited considerably lower COF diffraction peak intensities at 0.19, 0.39, and 0.58 Å–1 [corresponding to the (100), (200), and (300) planes], demonstrating that the COF membrane prepared without COF-TPA nanoparticles had lower crystallinity than the counterpart prepared using our method (Fig. 2e). In addition, 2D WAXS patterns also exhibited stronger diffraction ring intensities using this method compared to without nanoparticles (inset of Fig. 2e and Supplementary Figs. 11 and 12).

This method was applied to several different kgm COFs, including COF-BPDA synthesized by ETTA and 4,4’-biphenyldicarbaldehyde (BPDA), and COF-TPDA synthesized by ETTA and (1,1’:4’,1”-terphenyl)−4,4”-dicarboxaldehyde (TPDA) (Figs. 1c and 3b–c). PXRD analysis of COF-BPDA showed diffraction peaks at 2.19°, 4.41°, and 6.65°, corresponding to the (100), (200), and (300) planes, respectively; the pattern of COF-TPDA showed peaks at 1.86°, 3.75°, and 5.62° corresponding to the (100), (200), and (300) planes, respectively (Figs. 3b and 3c). These patterns were consistent with the kgm topology predicted from the simulations, confirming the kgm structures with AA stacking of both COF-BPDA and COF-TPDA membranes (Supplementary Figs. 15–19)41. N2 adsorption-desorption analysis was performed to determine the porosity and pore size distribution of the COF powder, which was scraped off the top of the COF membranes (Supplementary Figs. 20–21). The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas of COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, and COF-TPDA were 1760, 1395, and 320 m2 g−1, respectively. The pore size distribution analysis by non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) was consistent with the PXRD results that all COF membranes possessed a dual-pore (kgm topology) structure42. This method was applicable to the fabrication of COF membranes with both kgm and sql structures. The COF-DAB with an identical component to COF-TPA but a sql structure was synthesized as nanoparticles43. After that, the COF-DAB membrane was synthesized using the same synthetic procedure as the kgm COF-TPA membrane. The PXRD pattern of the COF-DAB membrane displayed peaks at 5.01°, 10.24°, and 15.27° corresponding to the (100), (200), and (300) planes, respectively, indicating the sql topology (Fig. 3d)43. Uranium uptake in COF membranes

Uranium adsorption studies were conducted at 25 °C by circulating 300 mL of a 10 ppm U-spiked solution (initial pH ~5.5) through the COF membrane (~1.5 mg COF membrane covered on the commercial nylon substrate) at a flow rate of 30 mL min−1. The adsorption kinetic curves for all COFs displayed a rapid increase in adsorption capacity during the initial 4 h, reaching equilibrium after 12 h of contact (Fig. 3e). After 14 h of adsorption, the saturated adsorption capacities of COF membranes (COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, COF-TPDA, and COF-DAB) for uranyl ions were measured to be 57.0, 25.5, 9.7, and 14.2 mg g−1, respectively. The adsorption kinetics of COF-TPA were analyzed through the Weber-Morris intraparticle diffusion model27. The linear fitting of the qt–t0.5 plots revealed three distinct linear segments with varying slopes (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating that the mass transfer process occurs in three stages: mesopore transfer, ion migration from the mesopore to the micropore, and micropore diffusion (Supplementary Fig. 24). In the first stage (0–4 h), the adsorption capacity increased sharply within the initial 4 h, which was attributed to the rapid transfer of uranium from the bulk solution to the mesopore surfaces. In the second stage (4–6 h), the uranyl adsorption capacity exhibited a distinct plateau, which was mainly attributed to the diffusion of uranyl ions from mesopores to micropores within the COF membrane via intrinsic framework defects. In the third stage (6–14 h), uranyl ions diffused further into the intrinsic micropores of the COFs, and this micropore diffusion step proceeded much more slowly compared to that of the external mass transfer process. These distinctive adsorption kinetic behaviors highlight the unique advantages of heteroporous COF membranes, which enable rapid ion diffusion and permeation through the membrane matrix before their stable immobilization within the micropores (Supplementary Figs. 25–27).

The U 4 f X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra of the U-adsorbed COF-TPA and COF-DAB products, namely U@COF-TPA (kgm) and U@COF-DAB (sql), respectively, exhibited characteristic peaks at binding energies of 382.4 (4f7/2) and 393.4 eV (4f5/2). There were lower energy shifts of ~0.4 (4f7/2) and ~0.3 eV (4f5/2) compared to UO2(NO3)2·6H2O (382.8 and 393.7 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 28)44. These variations were attributed to the fact that nitrogen (N) atoms along the COF channels attract the electron cloud from the U atom, which increased the shielding effect and thus decreased the binding energy45. The identical binding energy shifts observed for U@COF-TPA and U@COF-DAB demonstrated that uranyl ions were coordinated to COF-TPA and COF-DAB in the same coordination mode (Supplementary Fig. 29).

To evaluate the uranium adsorption rate of COF-TPA, a series of Schiff-based polymer membranes (~1.5 mg polymer membrane immobilized on a commercial nylon substrate) was synthesized using the method above. All membranes had C=N binding sites for uranyl ion adsorption, although with different structures, including PI (linear polymers), TPETA-TPEDH (1D COF, 8.1 Å pore size), POP-E (2D porous organic polymer, 14.0 Å pore size), TAPB-PDA-COF (2D COF, 34.0 Å pore size), Im-TFPPy-PDA-COF (2D COF, 21.9 Å pore size), COF-TAPB-BTCA (2D COF, 12.0 Å pore size), Azine-TFPPy-COF (2D COF, 16.7 Å pore size), Bd-COF (2D COF, 26.0 Å pore size), TPTF-COF (2D COF, 14.0 Å pore size), COF-LZU-1 (2D COF, 20.0 Å pore size), N0-COF (2D COF, 24.0 Å pore size), TFPB-DATP-COF (2D COF, 41.0 Å pore size), TAEB-COF (2D COF, 38.0 Å pore size), COF-923 (2D COF, 33.2 Å pore size), HFPB-TABPB (2D COF, 12.0 Å pore size), COF-DAB (2D COF, 13.4 Å pore size), HPB-COF (2D COF, 12.0 Å pore size), and COF-300 (3D COF, 7.2 Å pore size) (please see Supplementary Information for details, Supplementary Table 4). COF-TPA exhibited the fastest adsorption rate (10.9 mg g−1 h−1) in the first 4 h, ~2.4 times higher than the classical COF-LZU-1 (3.2 mg g−1 h−1) (Fig. 3f). In particular, Bd-COF (26.0 Å) and COF-300 (7.2 Å) each had an sql channel similar to one of the kgm structures in the COF-TPA material (7.1 and 26.9 Å). The adsorption rate of COF-TPA was ~4.3 times higher than Bd-COF (2.1 mg g−1 h−1) and ~2.5 times higher than that of COF-300 (3.2 mg g−1 h−1), respectively. This rapid adsorption rate of COF-TPA was ascribed to the kgm structure.

In heteroporous COF-TPA with kgm topology, mesopores served as efficient pathways for ion migration. This was supported by the significantly higher diffusion coefficient of uranyl in mesopores (D = 1.3×10−7 m2 s−1), which was 2.1 times higher than that in micropores (D = 4.2×10−8 m2 s−1), allowing for faster ion transport through the mesopores (Fig. 3g, h). In contrast, micropores provide a highly confined environment that enhances the adsorption capacity. DFT calculations revealed that the uranyl ion binds strongly to the pore structure in micropores, with a binding energy of −61.3 kcal mol−1, which was significantly lower than that in mesopores (−20.6 kcal mol−1), indicating a high affinity of micropores for uranyl ions (Fig. 3i, j). This cooperative effect of the two pore types ensured that the mesopores accelerated the migration of uranyl ions, while the micropores enhanced the adsorption capacity, leading to a balance between efficient transport and high uptake of uranyl ions in the membrane (Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31)22,32,46–48.

Owing to the extended conjugated structure and enhanced π-electron delocalization of the tetraphenylethylene (TPE) unit49, all COF solids were expected to exhibit promising photocatalytic properties for the photocatalytic conversion of U(VI). DFT calculations demonstrated that the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels of COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, COF-TPDA, and COF-DAB were −4.73/−2.89, −4.63/−2.75, −4.45/−2.46, and −4.88/−2.39 eV, respectively (Fig. 4a). The near-complete spatial separation of the HOMO and LUMO in COF-TPA, combined with its narrowest HOMO–LUMO gap (1.84 eV), facilitates the highly efficient directional transport of photogenerated electrons50. Ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy confirmed that all COFs exhibited strong absorption within the 300–800 nm range (Supplementary Fig. 32), indicating good visible-light harvesting capability53. Using the Kubelka-Munk function54, the optical band gaps were calculated as 2.17, 2.21, 2.25, and 2.57 eV for COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, COF-TPDA, and COF-DAB, respectively (inset of Supplementary Fig. 32). Mott-Schottky plots (C−2 vs. E) demonstrated a positive slope, indicating n-type semiconductors with flat band potentials of −0.76, −0.94, −1.00, and −0.93 V (vs. NHE) for COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, COF-TPDA, and COF-DAB, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 33)55. Their band structures (valence/conduction band) have been calculated as of −0.86/1.31 (COF-TPA), −1.04/1.17 (COF-BPDA), −1.10/1.15 (COF-TPDA), and −1.03/1.54 eV (COF-DAB) (Supplementary Fig. 34). These results indicated that all the COF solids could reduce U(VI) to U(IV) (0.41 V vs. NHE) thermodynamically (Supplementary Figs. 35−37)56.

Uranium photocatalytic extraction experiments were conducted under 300 W xenon lamp irradiation (Beijing Perfectlight, PLS-SXE300) by circulating 300 mL of a 10 ppm U-spiked solution (pH ~5.5, 25 °C) through the COF membrane (~1.5 mg COF membrane covered on the commercial nylon substrate) at a flow rate of 30 mL min−1. The resulting adsorption isotherms showed an initial steep rise, indicating a rapid extraction of U, followed by a gradual plateau that was reached within 18 h. The U extraction capacities of the COF-TPA, COF-BPDA, COF-TPDA, and COF-DAB membranes were 483.7, 367.5, 69.2, and 202.1 mg g−1, respectively (Fig. 4b).

Because vanadium (V) is considered the strongest element competing with uranium in seawater, the U/V selectivity was investigated under the aforementioned conditions, but at a concentration of 10 ppm for each element. The COF-TPA membrane exhibited the highest selectivity for U over V with a U/V selectivity coefficient of 11.8 among all COF membranes in this work (Supplementary Fig. 38), which was much higher than that of classical adsorbents, including PCN-222-AO and HCP-PAO (U/V selectivity coefficients of 8.0 and 8.4, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 39 and Supplementary Table 5)57,58.FT-IR confirmed the presence of the U = O asymmetric stretching vibration peak (Supplementary Fig. 40).

XPS characterization of COF-TPA after irradiation revealed a U 4 f peak with binding energies of ~393.3 and 382.3 eV, which differed from those of physicochemically adsorbed U@COF-TPA. This shift demonstrated that after irradiation, the bonding structure of U atoms changed and became identical to that of UO2O2·2H2O (Supplementary Fig. 41). The phase composition of the uranium product bound to the COF-TPA membrane was analyzed at different time intervals during irradiation using PXRD analysis. In the initial stage, no substantial diffraction peaks were observed in the 2θ range of 10.0° − 40.0°. After 1 h of exposure, the PXRD pattern featured new diffraction peaks at 15.0° and 26.3°, attributed to UO2O2·4H2O (PDF#49-1821). After irradiation for 4 h, a series of broad diffraction peaks centered at 17.0°, 20.3°, 23.5°, 25.1°, 27.7°, 32.5°, 33.5°, 36.4°, and 37.5° appeared, which were assigned to UO2O2·2H2O (PDF#35-0571, Fig. 4c). These observations suggested that under light irradiation, soluble uranyl ions are stepwise converted into insoluble uranyl peroxide hydrates. The process of U deposition on COF membranes was investigated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The TEM images revealed that in the first 4 h of irradiation, a small amount of U precipitates with particle sizes of 50–200 nm were observed (Fig. 4d). With an increase in irradiation time (18 h), the particle size of the U precipitates increased considerably, reaching 300–500 nm.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) analysis indicated that no free radicals were observed when the COF-TPA powder was placed in a dark environment (Fig. 4e). After light irradiation, singlet oxygen (1O2) and superoxide radical (·O2⁻) signals were detected, confirming that both species were generated by the COF-TPA framework under light irradiation (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 42). The total relative free energy of COF-TPA interacting with UO22+ decreased from 0 to −99.14 kcal mol−1, indicating that the UO22+ ion could be spontaneously adsorbed onto the COF-TPA framework. After irradiation, photogenerated electrons were transferred from the COF-TPA skeleton to UO22⁺ ion, thus reducing U(VI) to U(IV) and further lowering the relative free energy to −404.73 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Fig. 43). This suggested that the reduction of uranyl ions by COF-TPA is spontaneous under light irradiation, forming insoluble UO2O2·4H2O precipitates, and ultimately converting to UO2O2·2H2O59. Based on the above results, a synergistic effect of heteroporous COF-TPA in photocatalytic uranium reduction is proposed: mesopores of COF-TPA transport uranyl ions to micropores, the micropores enrich the uranyl ions; the micropores exhibit enhanced carrier transport and separation efficiency, thus enabling the efficient photocatalytic reduction of adsorbed uranyl ions (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45).

The U(VI) uptake data were collected at 25 °C for the COF-TPA membrane exposed to U(VI) solution concentrations ranging from 5 to 100 ppm (Supplementary Figs. 46–48). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 47, at a U(VI) concentration of 100 ppm, COF-TPA exhibited a high U(VI) uptake of 3659.5 mg g−1, ~77.5 times that of COF-300 (47.2 mg g−1), a Schiff-base COF architecture with 3D open channels for rapid ion transport (Supplementary Fig. 49). The selectivity of the COF-TPA membrane for U(VI) was examined in an aqueous solution containing 10 ppm of UO22+ ions and 10 ppm of competing metal ions (Li+, Mg2+, Ni2+, Zn2+, Na+, Ca2+, Mn2+, K+, and VO2+). As shown in Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 50, COF-TPA maintained a high U capacity of 377.7 mg g−1, and outperformed other metal ions (e.g., V capacity of 35.3 mg g−1), reaching a selectivity coefficient (KU/V) of 10.7. Furthermore, the effects of easily redox-active metal ions (Fe3+, Co2+, and Cu2+) on the photoreduction of uranium were investigated. The removal efficiency of UO22+ ions by the COF-TPA membrane was measured (Fig. 5b). After 72 h of treatment, the U concentration decreased to 11.8 ppb, producing a removal efficiency of 99.7%, which was below the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (UPA) limit of 30 ppb. Correspondingly, the distribution coefficient (Kd) reached 1.69 × 107 mL g−1, which was greater than that of the standard adsorbent [NH4]+[COF-SO3−] (9.80 × 106 mL g−1)60, confirming the high affinity of COF-TPA for U extraction. The COF-TPA membrane could be regenerated by immersing it in a nitric acid solution, with a < 15% decrease in uptake capacity after 10 cycles relative to the initial value (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 52).

Real-world application performance was tested in natural seawater (purchased from Bohai, China, with a uranium concentration of ~3.3 ppb). Within the first 48 h, an ultrahigh U uptake of 9.4 mg g−1 was achieved (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 53), and the capacity further increased continuously to 16.9 mg g−1 within 12 d, far exceeding the uranium extraction from seawater (UES)9,61,62 of 6 mg g−1. Notably, the uranium adsorption rate of COF-TPA achieved the highest value in the first 2 d (4.7 mg g−1 d−1) among all membranes and other materials with application potential (e.g., fibers63 and gels64) (Fig. 5e, for further references and details, please see Supplementary Table 6).

Discussion

A general synthetic method was developed to fabricate a series of 2D heteropore COF membranes. The method is applicable not only for the preparation of dual-pore (kgm topology) COF membranes but also for other COF membranes. The synthesized kgm COF membranes accelerated the transport kinetics of guest molecules and provided strong host-guest interaction. The resulting COF-TPA membrane realized a high uranium uptake capacity of 4.7 mg g−1 d−1 under light irradiation within the first 2 d, placing it among the top-performing industrial application potential materials in natural seawater. This study establishes a promising platform for the future development of COF-based membranes, particularly for uranium extraction from seawater, and provides valuable insights for the design of advanced adsorbent materials for broader environmental and resource recovery applications.

Methods

Synthesis of COF-TPA powder

4,4’,4”,4”’-(Ethene-1, 1, 2, 2-tetrayl)tetraaniline (30 mg, 0.075 mmol) and terephthalaldehyde (20.5 mg, 0.15 mmol) were dissolved in a mixture of dioxane (0.9 mL), mesitylene (0.1 mL), and acetic acid (6 M, 0.1 mL) in a sealed glass tube. After degassing in a liquid nitrogen bath for 20 min, the tube was sealed and kept at 120 °C for 3 d to yield a yellow solid. After cooling to room temperature, the solvent was decanted, and the solid was washed with anhydrous dioxane 3 times, and then dried under vacuum at 150 °C for 2 h to afford an orange-yellow powder (41.4 mg, 91.8%). For C54H36N4: calcd (%) C 85.69, H 4.79, N 9.52; found (%) C 82.63, H 4.92, N 9.11.

Synthesis of COF-TPA membrane

1 mg of the COF powder was ultrasonically treated in 10 mL of ethanol for 1 h to obtain the nanoparticles, which were activated under vacuum at 120 °C for 4 h. A piece of nylon substrate was placed on a clean glass flask, and then a turbid suspension of COF-TPA nanoparticles (0.5 mg) and ethanol (1 mL) was coated on the nylon substrate. TPA (13.4 mg, 0.1 mmol) and ETTA (19.7 mg, 0.05 mmol) were dissolved in 1,4-dioxane (10 mL), and then the dioxane mixture (2 mL) was uniformly coated on the nylon substrate, followed by the spraying of 6 M acetic acid (0.25 mL). The flask was heated to 120 °C under N2 atmosphere for 48 h. After that, the resulting membranes that had been brought to room temperature were washed with dichloromethane, tetrahydrofuran, and acetone, respectively. Finally, the residual solvent was removed by treating the COF membranes (~1.5 mg COF membrane covered on the commercial nylon substrate) under vacuum at 60 °C. See Supplementary Information for the synthesis of other COF membranes.

Uranium capacity in the irradiation environment

The uranium capacity in the irradiation of the COF membranes was evaluated under visible light irradiation from a 300 W xenon lamp (Beijing Perfectlight, PLS-SXE300UV) with an optical powder density of 1 kW m−2, and cooled by circulating water (25 ± 2 °C). In a quartz flask, the uranium (VI) solution was prepared by dissolving uranyl nitrate in 300 mL with a range of concentration from 5 to 100 ppm (pH ~ 5.5). This solution was continuously passed through the COF membranes (~1.5 mg) using a circulating device (30 mL min−1). At various time intervals, an aliquot was removed from the mixture; after filtration, the filtrate was analyzed by ICP-AES to determine the residual ion concentration.

Computational methods

The HOMO and LUMO calculations for all units were conducted using the Dmol3 module built into the modeling program. For COF, the COF fragments (a linker of an aldehyde monomer and an amino monomer) were chosen as the computational targets; for the monomers, their reaction groups were removed. After geometric optimization of the object in the Dmol3 module, the HOMO and LUMO were calculated. The single-point calculations were carried out with the Gaussian 16 program. The geometry structures were optimized in the gas phase using the B3LYP functional and def2TZVP+SDD basis (please see Supplementary Information for details). Forcite calculations used the Universal Force Field (PES energy), 5 × 10−4 e charge tolerance, Ewald summation (10−5 kcal mol−1 accuracy), and 298 K geometry optimization (full atomic relaxation, given criteria), followed by 250 ps NVT-MD (1 fs step) (please see Supplementary Information for details).
