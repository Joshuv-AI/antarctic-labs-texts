Clinical Outcomes After Percutaneous Coronary Intervention in Patients With Cirrhosis: A Multicentre National Study
Authors: Dhir Gala, Sameer Rao, Manas Gunani, Kajol Shah, Anand V Kulkarni, Karn Wijarnpreecha, Daniel Q Huang, Pojsakorn Danpanichkul, Ahmed Al‐Khazraji, Kaveh Hajifathalian, Vivek Lingiah, Paul Gaglio, Nikki Duong, Christine Gerula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) is common in cirrhosis patients because of shared risk factors, including metabolic syndrome and alcohol use; however, outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) remain poorly defined.AimsTo evaluate outcomes after PCI in patients with cirrhosis.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using TriNetX US Collaborative Network data from 2015 to 2024. Adults undergoing PCI were stratified by cirrhosis status, with outcomes including gastrointestinal bleeding, ischaemic events, and all‐cause mortality. Within the cirrhosis cohort, we evaluated effect modification by aetiology, disease severity, and antiplatelet strategy at 30 days after PCI. One‐to‐one propensity score matching adjusted for demographics and comorbidities, with results reported as relative risks and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsAmong 3351 patients with cirrhosis undergoing PCI matched 1:1 to controls, cirrhosis was associated with higher 1‐year gastrointestinal bleeding (16.14% vs. 8.12%; p < 0.0001) and mortality (13.97% vs. 10.41%; p < 0.0001). Decompensated cirrhosis had higher gastrointestinal bleeding and mortality than compensated disease, while alcohol‐associated cirrhosis had higher gastrointestinal bleeding than metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) cirrhosis. In a 30‐day landmark analysis, antiplatelet monotherapy (aspirin or P2Y12 inhibitors) had lower 1‐year gastrointestinal bleeding (10.31% vs. 20.77%; p < 0.0001) and mortality (9.46% vs. 12.89%; p = 0.04) without increased stent restenosis compared with dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT).ConclusionCirrhosis confers substantially higher bleeding and mortality after PCI, particularly in decompensated disease. Antiplatelet monotherapy after 30 days was associated with less bleeding and improved survival, supporting abbreviated DAPT in this high‐risk group.Keywords: antiplatelet therapy, cirrhosis, coronary artery disease, percutaneous coronary intervention

Introduction

Cirrhosis has emerged as a growing challenge in cardiovascular medicine, as the increasing prevalence of both chronic liver disease and coronary artery disease has created a complex patient population requiring coronary revascularisation [1]. In the United States, cirrhosis is projected to affect approximately 2.5 million individuals by 2030, primarily driven by the growing burden of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [2, 3, 4, 5]. MASLD currently affects nearly one‐third of the adult USA population and has become the second leading indication for liver transplantation nationally, as well as a significant global cause of cirrhosis‐related mortality [6, 7, 8, 9].

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is estimated to affect approximately one in five patients with cirrhosis [10, 11]. Although cirrhosis itself may not be an independent risk factor for CAD, the cardiovascular burden in this population appears to be primarily driven by shared cardiometabolic risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia [10, 12, 13]. Notably, CAD prevalence varies by cirrhosis aetiology, with the highest rates observed in MASLD‐related cirrhosis (21%) and alcohol‐associated cirrhosis (17%), compared with substantially lower rates in primary biliary cholangitis (4%) and viral hepatitis–related cirrhosis (5%) [10]. In addition, CAD is also a major cause of both early and late post‐liver transplant complications, including perioperative myocardial infarction, heart failure, and mortality. Therefore, the accurate identification of CAD is an integral part of the patient work‐up before liver transplant [14, 15].

The high prevalence of CAD in cirrhosis patients has led to an increase in patients with cirrhosis requiring revascularisation, either in the setting of acute ischemia or chronic CAD, especially during pre‐transplant workup [16, 17]. Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has become the preferred revascularisation strategy for patients with cirrhosis and CAD because coronary artery bypass grafting is associated with high mortality in this population, particularly in patients with portal hypertension [18, 19]. However, the coexistence of PCI and cirrhosis presents a clinical dilemma. Currently, data and guideline recommendations regarding PCI outcomes in patients with cirrhosis remain limited, as most landmark PCI trials have excluded individuals with cirrhosis or hepatic dysfunction, and existing studies are constrained by small sample sizes or single‐centre designs [20]. Observational studies using the National Inpatient Sample have demonstrated high post‐PCI in‐hospital mortality among patients with cirrhosis, with 90‐day mortality reaching 10.3% compared with 2.5% in patients without liver disease [21, 22]. Despite these findings, important knowledge gaps persist regarding long‐term outcomes beyond the index hospitalisation, the optimal duration and composition of antiplatelet therapy, and whether outcomes differ by the underlying aetiology of cirrhosis.

A major challenge in post‐PCI management among patients with cirrhosis is determining the most appropriate dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) strategy. Although DAPT with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor is the standard of care after coronary stenting and reduces recurrent ischaemic events in the general population, patients with cirrhosis face a substantially higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding due to coagulopathy and manifestations of portal hypertension including portal gastropathy, oesophageal and gastric varices, thrombocytopenia, and platelet dysfunction [23, 24]. Multiple randomised controlled trials support shorter durations of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) of 1–3 months followed by P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy to reduce bleeding without compromising ischaemic protection, particularly in high bleeding risk populations [25, 26]. However, patients with cirrhosis have been excluded from these studies, leaving no comparative data to guide DAPT duration, long‐term antiplatelet strategy, or selection of monotherapy in this population. As a result, clinicians must empirically balance the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding associated with prolonged DAPT against ischaemic complications such as stent thrombosis or recurrent myocardial infarction with abbreviated DAPT regimens. To address these gaps, we conducted a retrospective multicentre cohort study of adults undergoing PCI to compare bleeding events, ischaemic complications, and mortality in patients with and without cirrhosis.

Methods

Database

TriNetX is a federated research platform that provides access to deidentified electronic health record data from healthcare organisations in the United States and internationally. This study utilised data from the United States Collaborative Network, comprising 72 participating sites within the United States. The database contains longitudinal information on patient demographics, clinical diagnoses, medical procedures, prescription medications, laboratory test results, and mortality outcomes. Data elements are standardised using established clinical coding systems, including the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes (LOINC), and RxNorm standardised nomenclature for clinical drugs (RxNorm). Analyses are conducted within secure institutional environments, with only aggregated, nonidentifiable results returned to investigators [27].

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adults in the United States using the TriNetX research network between 1 January 2015, and 31 December 2024. Institutional review board approval was not required because the study used deidentified data.

We identified patients who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) using ICD‐10 codes for stent placement, atherectomy, or both; procedures limited to balloon angioplasty were excluded to avoid heterogeneity in post‐procedural management and antiplatelet therapy (Table S1). Patients with prior coronary artery bypass grafting or liver transplantation were excluded to avoid confounding related to advanced cardiovascular or liver disease states. Cohorts were defined by the presence or absence of cirrhosis and matched 1:1 using propensity scores based on demographics, comorbidities, and potential indications for PCI. Covariates were captured during the 1‐year period before and including the date of PCI.

Outcomes were assessed at 30 days and 1 year after PCI and included cardiovascular, Haemorrhagic, thrombotic, neurological, renal, healthcare utilisation outcomes, and all‐cause mortality. Recurrent myocardial infarction and repeat PCI were not reported as outcomes because prior coding limited reliable identification of incident events.

We also performed an exploratory analysis within the cirrhosis cohort to identify clinical factors associated with 1‐year all‐cause mortality after PCI. The variables evaluated included demographics, cirrhosis aetiology, hepatic decompensation events, PCI indication, and laboratory measures reflecting hepatic and renal function.

Sub‐Group Analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed within the cirrhosis cohort to explore effect modification by cirrhosis aetiology, disease severity, and post‐PCI antiplatelet strategy. For each subgroup, cohorts were compared after propensity score matching using the same clinically relevant variables as in the primary analysis. Cirrhosis aetiology was additionally included in the propensity model for all subgroup analyses except the comparison between alcohol‐associated cirrhosis and MASLD cirrhosis. Laboratory parameters reflecting liver disease severity were also incorporated, except in the comparison of compensated versus decompensated cirrhosis.

For aetiology‐based comparisons, patients with chronic hepatitis B or C were excluded. MASLD cirrhosis was defined as cirrhosis with at least one metabolic risk factor and no alcohol‐associated liver disease, whereas alcohol‐associated cirrhosis was defined as cirrhosis attributed to alcohol use without concurrent metabolic dysfunction; patients with metabolic dysfunction–associated alcohol‐related liver disease (MetALD) were excluded.

Cirrhosis severity was categorised based on clinical decompensation, with decompensated cirrhosis defined by a history of ascites, portal hypertension, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, or hepatorenal syndrome and compensated cirrhosis defined by the absence of these features.

For antiplatelet strategy analyses, a 30‐day landmark approach was used among patients who survived beyond 30 days and had identifiable antiplatelet therapy. Monotherapy was defined as aspirin or a single P2Y12 inhibitor, while dual therapy consisted of aspirin plus a P2Y12 inhibitor at any time between 30 days and 1 year after PCI. Within the monotherapy cohort, aspirin and clopidogrel were compared, excluding patients who switched agents during follow‐up.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses were performed using the analytic functionality within the TriNetX platform. One‐to‐one propensity score matching was conducted using nearest‐neighbour matching with a calliper of 0.1 times the pooled standard deviation. After matching, categorical variables were compared using chi‐square tests and continuous variables using independent‐samples t‐test. Binary outcomes are reported as relative risks with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and two‐sided p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate clinical factors associated with 1‐year all‐cause mortality after PCI in patients with cirrhosis, with results reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

Among adults undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in the US Collaborative Network within TriNetX, 3351 patients with cirrhosis were identified (mean age 66.3 ± SD 9.2 years; 67.9% male) and compared with 324,274 patients without cirrhosis (mean age 66.1 ± SD 11.9 years; 68.4% male) (Figure 1, Table 1). Before matching, patients with cirrhosis had a higher prevalence of cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities and greater use of anticoagulants and other cardiovascular medications. After 1:1 propensity score matching, 3351 patients with cirrhosis were well balanced with 3351 non‐cirrhosis controls across demographics and comorbidities (Table 1).

At 1 year, cirrhosis was associated with significantly higher rates of gastrointestinal bleeding (16.14% vs. 8.12%; RR 1.99), blood transfusion (12.12% vs. 6.74%; RR 1.80), and all‐cause mortality (13.97% vs. 10.41%; RR 1.34) (all p < 0.0001). Stent restenosis was numerically higher but not statistically different, while coronary artery bypass grafting was less frequent in patients with cirrhosis (Table 2).

In multivariable Cox analysis among patients with cirrhosis undergoing PCI, older age was associated with higher 1‐year mortality (HR 1.03, p < 0.0001). Among decompensation events, ascites (HR 1.31, p = 0.0045) and variceal bleeding (HR 1.25, p = 0.0306) were independently associated with mortality, while hepatorenal syndrome (HR 1.54, p = 0.0515), hepatic encephalopathy (HR 1.02, p = 0.8659), and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (HR 1.11, p = 0.5806) showed positive but nonsignificant associations. PCI performed for acute coronary syndrome was associated with higher mortality, including NSTEMI (HR 1.25, p = 0.0283) and STEMI (HR 1.43, p < 0.0001). Higher creatinine, INR, and total bilirubin were associated with increased mortality, whereas higher albumin and sodium were protective (Figure 2).

Aetiology‐Based Comparison (Alcohol‐Associated Vs. MASLD‐Related Cirrhosis)

Before propensity score matching, patients with alcohol‐associated cirrhosis had a higher prevalence of portal hypertension and oesophageal varices compared with those with MASLD‐related cirrhosis. These differences, along with other baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, were balanced after propensity score matching (Table S2).

After propensity matching (472 per group), alcohol‐associated cirrhosis was associated with higher 1‐year GI bleeding (19.92% vs. 15.04%; RR 1.34; p = 0.04) and blood transfusion (16.31% vs. 11.02%; RR 1.48; p = 0.02) compared to MASLD‐related cirrhosis. One‐year stent restenosis, cerebral infarction, and mortality were similar between groups (Table 3).

Cirrhosis Severity‐Based Comparison (Decompensated Vs. Compensated)

Before propensity score matching, patients with decompensated cirrhosis were younger and more often had heart failure, chronic kidney disease, dialysis dependence, alcohol‐associated cirrhosis, whereas compensated cirrhosis was more commonly associated with chronic viral hepatitis; these characteristics were well balanced after matching (Table S3).

After propensity score matching (980 per group), decompensated cirrhosis was associated with higher 1‐year gastrointestinal bleeding (23.78% vs. 11.12%; RR 2.14), blood transfusion (17.91% vs. 9.09%; RR 1.97), and mortality (19.18% vs. 11.22%; RR 1.71) compared with compensated cirrhosis (all p < 0.0001), as well as higher stent restenosis (9.49% vs. 6.73%; RR 1.41; p = 0.03) (Table 3).

Antiplatelet Strategy‐Based Comparison (Monotherapy Vs. Dual Antiplatelet Therapy After 30 Days)

Before propensity score matching, patients later managed with dual antiplatelet therapy more often presented with acute coronary syndromes and cardiovascular comorbidities, whereas those managed with antiplatelet monotherapy more frequently had atrial fibrillation and oral anticoagulant use. After matching, baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (Table S4).

In the 30‐day landmark analysis (698 per group), antiplatelet monotherapy was associated with lower rates of gastrointestinal bleeding (10.31% vs. 20.77%; RR 0.50) and blood transfusion (8.17% vs. 14.76%; RR 0.55) compared with dual therapy (both p ≤ 0.0001). One‐year mortality was also lower with monotherapy (9.46% vs. 12.89%; RR 0.73; p = 0.04), while rates of stent restenosis and cerebral infarction were similar (Table 3).

Aspirin Versus Clopidogrel Monotherapy‐Based Comparison

Before propensity score matching, patients later managed with clopidogrel monotherapy were older and more frequently had atrial fibrillation and heart failure, whereas aspirin monotherapy was more common among patients presenting with ST‐segment elevation myocardial infarction at PCI; these differences were well balanced after matching (Table S5).

Among matched monotherapy recipients, gastrointestinal bleeding, transfusion, and mortality were numerically higher with aspirin compared with clopidogrel, although these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 3).

Discussion

In this propensity‐matched study, patients with cirrhosis undergoing PCI had a twofold higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and a 34% higher risk of mortality within 1 year. Subgroup analyses revealed important risk stratification patterns, with alcohol‐associated cirrhosis patients demonstrating a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and a nonsignificant trend toward higher 1‐year mortality compared with those with MASLD‐related cirrhosis. In addition, patients with decompensated cirrhosis had more than a twofold higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and a 71% higher risk of mortality within 1 year compared with those with compensated cirrhosis. Furthermore, antiplatelet monotherapy after 30 days post‐PCI was associated with significantly lower risks of bleeding and 1‐year mortality compared with dual antiplatelet therapy, without an increased rate of thrombotic events. These findings have important implications for risk stratification, shared decision‐making, and antiplatelet management in patients with cirrhosis undergoing PCI.

The increased risks of gastrointestinal bleeding and mortality observed in our cirrhotic cohort are consistent with findings from prior meta‐analyses demonstrating higher rates of gastrointestinal bleeding and in‐hospital mortality following PCI in patients with cirrhosis [24]. The mechanisms underlying this elevated bleeding risk are multifactorial and include portal hypertension, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, and platelet dysfunction. Among these, portal hypertension is a key contributor, promoting bleeding through several pathways such as the development of oesophageal and gastric varices, portal hypertensive gastropathy, and ectatic vessels throughout the gastrointestinal tract [28]. According to the Academic Research Consortium for High Bleeding Risk (ARC‐HBR), cirrhosis patients are classified as Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) type 3 or 5 bleeding risk. BARC type 3 bleeding represents major bleeding requiring transfusion or medical intervention, whereas type 5 bleeding represents fatal bleeding [29]. The additional exposure to antiplatelet therapy in this already high‐risk population likely explains the nearly twofold increase in gastrointestinal bleeding observed at both 30 days and 1 year in our study.

The increased mortality observed in cirrhosis patients post‐PCI likely represents a complex interaction of bleeding complications, underlying cardiac dysfunction, and reduced physiologic reserve. Cirrhotic cardiomyopathy, present in up to 50% of patients with advanced liver disease, is characterised by QT interval prolongation, impaired contractile response to stress, and beta‐adrenergic receptor desensitisation [28, 30, 31, 32]. In addition, acute kidney injury occurs in approximately 28.4% of PCI procedures among patients with cirrhosis compared with 10.9% in the general STEMI‐PCI population, with dialysis required in 4.9% versus 0.8%, respectively [21, 33]. Importantly, cardiovascular disease has emerged as the second leading cause of death among patients with cirrhosis and the leading cause of mortality in those with MASLD‐related cirrhosis [34, 35].

In our aetiology‐driven subgroup analysis, patients with alcohol‐associated cirrhosis had a higher risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and blood transfusion compared with those with MASLD‐related cirrhosis, along with a nonsignificant trend toward higher mortality. These findings may reflect differences in portal hypertension severity, ongoing alcohol exposure, or other unmeasured clinical factors [36, 37]. Notably, recent meta‐analyses demonstrate that alcohol abstinence reduces the risk of portal hypertensive complications and confers a clear survival benefit in patients with alcohol‐associated cirrhosis, underscoring the modifiable nature of this risk and the importance of counselling regarding alcohol abstinence in patients with alcohol‐associated cirrhosis undergoing PCI [38, 39].

Decompensated cirrhosis was associated with significantly higher risks of gastrointestinal bleeding, transfusion, all‐cause mortality, and stent restenosis, highlighting the uniquely elevated procedural risk in this population. This relationship was further supported by our Cox analysis, in which ascites and variceal bleeding were independently associated with mortality after PCI, while hepatorenal syndrome, hepatic encephalopathy, and spontaneous bacterial peritonitis showed positive but nonsignificant associations. The marked differences in outcomes between compensated and decompensated cirrhosis underscore liver disease severity as an important determinant in PCI decision‐making and support viewing cirrhosis as a continuous rather than categorical condition. Incorporation of Child–Pugh, Vocal‐Penn, and MELD scores into pre‐procedural assessment, along with multidisciplinary discussion, modulation of portal hypertension, and consideration of noninvasive alternatives, may help guide patient selection.

In addition to liver disease severity, the indication for PCI should also be considered, as PCI for acute coronary syndromes, including NSTEMI and STEMI, was independently associated with higher mortality.

The most clinically actionable findings from our study relate to antiplatelet management. In a 30‐day landmark analysis, antiplatelet monotherapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) was associated with a 50% lower risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, a 45% lower risk of transfusion, and a 27% lower risk of mortality compared with dual antiplatelet therapy, without higher rates of stent restenosis or cerebral infarction at 1 year. These findings align with prior evidence and current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association and European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommending 1–3 months of dual antiplatelet therapy in high bleeding risk patients followed by P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy [40, 41]. Although no statistically significant differences were observed between aspirin and clopidogrel monotherapy, likely due to limited sample size, aspirin monotherapy showed a consistent numerical trend toward higher risks of gastrointestinal bleeding, transfusion, and mortality, consistent with prior literature suggesting clopidogrel may be preferable in high bleeding risk patients [42, 43, 44]. Additional strategies to mitigate bleeding risk include proton pump inhibitor co‐therapy and preferential radial access during PCI [45, 46, 47, 48].

This study has several important strengths, including a large real‐world cohort drawn from 72 healthcare institutions across the United States, the use of propensity score matching to reduce confounding, evaluation of clinically relevant outcomes, and prespecified subgroup analyses by cirrhosis aetiology, disease severity, and antiplatelet strategy. However, several limitations should be considered. As a retrospective observational study, residual confounding from unmeasured or imbalanced variables may persist despite propensity matching. Although patients with prior liver transplantation were excluded, we could not reliably censor patients who underwent liver transplantation after PCI during follow‐up. Therefore, 1‐year mortality was analysed as all‐cause mortality without censoring for interval transplantation. Cause‐specific mortality was also unavailable, limiting our ability to distinguish cardiac, liver‐related, and other causes of death. Key procedural details, including stent type and vascular access site, were unavailable, and cirrhosis‐specific measures such as hepatic venous pressure gradient and the grade of varices could not be assessed, limiting more granular risk stratification. We attempted to account for liver disease severity using Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease–related laboratory parameters when relevant; however, direct matching on Model for End‐Stage Liver Disease score is not feasible within the TriNetX platform. The landmark analysis of antiplatelet strategies was restricted to patients identifiable as receiving specific regimens, which may introduce selection bias, and comparisons between aspirin and clopidogrel monotherapy were likely underpowered. In addition, administrative data did not permit assessment of medication adherence, dose adjustments, or clinical rationale for treatment selection. Finally, as with other database studies, our findings may be subject to surveillance and immortal time bias.

In conclusion, this large propensity‐matched cohort study demonstrates that cirrhosis is associated with substantially elevated risks of bleeding and mortality following PCI, with outcomes strongly influenced by disease severity and aetiology. Decompensated cirrhosis confers a particularly poor prognosis, underscoring the importance of careful risk stratification in clinical decision‐making. Our findings support abbreviated dual antiplatelet therapy strategies in patients with cirrhosis, with antiplatelet monotherapy after 1 month associated with significant reductions in bleeding and mortality without an increased risk of thrombotic events. As the prevalence of cirrhosis continues to rise and cardiovascular disease emerges as a leading cause of death in this population, these findings provide evidence‐based guidance for managing the complex intersection of coronary artery disease and advanced liver disease. Future prospective randomised trials are needed to definitively establish optimal antiplatelet regimens, the duration of dual therapy, and the comparative effectiveness of medical versus invasive management strategies in patients with cirrhosis and coronary artery disease.
