Biomimetic nanotherapy for P. gingivalis-infected oral squamous cell carcinoma: Photothermal therapy and immunoactivation via precise intracellular bacterial elimination
Authors: Zihan Dai, Qiaoxin Li, Longxuan Guo, Kaijing Zhong, Jing Cheng, Xiuhong Weng, Bo Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorphyromonas gingivalis (P. gingivalis) infection in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) undermines patient responses to standard therapies by driving chemoresistance, tumor progression, and immune suppression. Mounting research evidence—including our staining of clinical OSCC biopsies—confirms intratumoral P. gingivalis colonization and CXCL2 overexpression as risk factors for poor prognosis. Therefore, precisely eliminating tumor-promoting microorganisms and alleviating immune suppression are crucial for improving the treatment efficacy. Inspired by validated observations, we have developed a unique clinically oriented nanoparticle platform (MC-MM@MPDA) that integrated precise intracellular antibiotic delivery, photothermal tumor ablation, photothermal bactericidal and immune activation. Despite growing interest in OSCC photothermal ablation, this platform is the first to utilize the dual anti-tumor and antibacterial functions of photothermal therapy aiming to achieve targeted therapy tailored to P. gingivalis-infected OSCC. Minocycline (MC) was loaded into mesoporous polydopamine (MPDA) nanoparticles and encapsulated with macrophage membranes, enabling selective homing to infected tumor sites and efficient uptake by cancer cells. Subsequently, the nanoplatform utilized photothermal effects to ablate tumor tissue, eliminate intracellular bacteria and induce immunogenic cell death (ICD). pH-triggered antibiotic release eradicated residual bacteria and unleashed bacterial tumor associated antigens. Alongside damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) generated by ICD, these signals reprogrammed the immunosuppressive microenvironment and established a synergistic antitumor network. In P. gingivalis infected OSCC xenograft models, this platform dramatically suppressed tumor growth, cleared pathogen burden, and overcame bacteria-mediated therapy resistance. By leveraging membrane-mimetic targeting, and synergistic photothermal-immunotherapy, MC-MM@MPDA offered a scalable, biocompatible, and readily translatable strategy to address pathogen-driven barriers in OSCC therapy.Keywords: Porphyromonas gingivalis, OSCC, Intracellular bacterial elimination, Photothermal therapy, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC) is a devastating malignancy arising from the mucosal epithelium of the oral cavity accounting for approximately 90 % of oral malignancies with an overall five-year survival rate of below 50 %. Beyond high mortality, OSCC severely impacts appearance, speech, swallowing, and taste perception, profoundly reducing patient quality of life (Li et al., 2022; Bray et al., 2024). At present, main therapeutical interventions include chemotherapy and surgery but the effectiveness of which is hindered by the occurrence of drug resistance (Sui et al., 2024) and destruction of oral function (Alsahafi et al., 2019; Kang et al., 2024). The human oral cavity harbors a diverse and highly differentiated microbial community that engages in bidirectional signaling with host tissues and immune cells (Liu et al., 2024; Rajasekaran et al., 2024). It has been reported that local periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis (P.gingivalis) in the oral cavity was significantly enriched in OSCC patients (Guo et al., 2023). Importantly, P. gingivalis infection upregulated CXCL2, recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and establishing an immunosuppressive niche that accelerated malignancy (Lv et al., 2025). Studies have also shown that P. gingivalis contributed to the progression of other tumors and enhanced resistance to chemotherapy (Lamont et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2024; Zang et al., 2025). Therefore, the precise elimination of tumor-promoting microorganisms is crucial for improving treatment efficacy and the prognosis of OSCC patients. However, conventional systemic antibiotic administration always lacks specificity in clinical treatment, which may disrupt the oral microbiota and has limited effectiveness in eliminating intracellular bacteria.

Photothermal therapy (PTT) has the characteristics of minimally invasive, long-lasting, and can effectively eliminate tumors while protecting normal tissue and reducing adverse reactions, making it ideal for the treatment of exposed superficial tumors such as oral cancer (Cronshaw et al., 2023; Fang et al., 2025a). PTT has been proven antibacterial effects, reducing pathogen loads at infection sites and alleviating excessive inflammation, demonstrating effective anti-oral pathogen activity against P.gingivalis in the treatment of periodontitis (Guo et al., 2024). However, conventional PTT relies mainly on local ablation (Sayar et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025), an integrated strategy of PTT with dual antibacterial and antitumor functions for infection-associated OSCC remains unexplored. Immunotherapy, which fights tumors by activating specific immune responses, has shown promising clinical outcomes (Filin et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2024; Cheng et al., 2024). However, single mechanism approaches often face tumor resistance, resulting in low response rates and limited long-term benefits for most patients (Emens et al., 2024). For microorganisms invaded tumor, the clearance of intracellular bacteria can release tumor-associated antigens related to bacteria, promoting the transition of the immune system and promoting synergistic antitumor effects (Nejman et al., 2020). Furthermore, PTT not only induces thermal damage to tumor tissue but also triggers immunogenic cell death (ICD), leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as calreticulin (CRT) and high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). These molecules can activate dendritic cells, thereby enhancing intrinsic immune responses. A multi-mechanism immunotherapy strategy is widely recognized as more effective for tumor treatment.

The concept of cell membrane–coated, tumor microenvironment–responsive nanoplatform has provided valuable inspiration and research guidance for synergistic multimodal therapy (Wang et al., 2023a). Tumor tissues infiltrated by bacteria exhibited elevated expression of inflammatory factors (Zhang et al., 2025), while macrophage membranes possessed intrinsic the capability to target both tumor cells and inflammatory mediators (Wang et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2024). Mesoporous dopamine nanoparticles (MPDA) could efficiently absorb near-infrared light and convert it into thermal energy for tumor ablation. While its mesoporous structure supported efficient drug loading, with drug release triggered by acidic microenvironments (Zhu et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2025). Recent studies on the combined application of MPDA and MM have mainly focused on tumor models such as prostate cancer (Li et al., 2024), and glioblastoma (Cao et al., 2023), or inflammatory diseases like acute lung injury (Zhao et al., 2025), no studies employing this strategy have been identified for bacterial infected OSCC. Given the malignant growth, inflammatory microenvironments and immunosuppression characteristics of this cancer, and macrophage-derived membrane-coated MPDA may serve as a preferred carrier to simultaneously fulfil the requirements for precise intracellular bacterial clearance, tumor ablation and tumor immune activation. Therefore, a minocycline (MC) loaded MPDA coated with macrophage membrane (MC-MM@MPDA) was constructed. Clearance of intracellular bacteria downregulated the CXCL2–MDSC axis and liberated tumor-associated antigens, while PTT-induced ICD generated DAMPs, these signals activated endogenous immune responses, promoted dendritic cell maturation, enhanced antigen presentation, and increased immune cell infiltration, and remodeled the tumor microenvironment from suppressive to stimulatory, achieving a synergistic therapeutic effect (Scheme 1). Given the association between intracellular bacterial clearance and tumor immune activation, the MC-MM@MPDA nanoplatform holds promise to transcend conventional photothermal-immunotherapy and monotherapy antibacterial treatments by modulating the unique interplay between bacterial infection and tumor immunity, offering novel insights for treating infection-driven malignancies.

Materials and methods

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH)

The OSCC tumor tissues from clinic OSCC patients were collected from the previously established biorepository of the research group and fabricated into tissue paraffin section to evaluate the distribution of P. gingivalis in tumor tissue. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. No.2022095 K and was approved by Zhongnan Hospital of Wuhan University Medical Ethics Committee. The P. gingivalis RNA probe was 3′-FITC-labeled oligonucleotides (CAATACTCGTATCGCCCGTTATTC-FITC). After dewaxing and rehydration, the sections were detected using Enhanced Sensitive ISH Detection KitV (FITC) (MK1034, Boster, China) staining method following the instructions. Thereafter, tissue chips were observed under the fluorescence microscope (Keyence, Japan).

Immunofluorescence for paraffin sections

Preparation for Immunofluorescence was mentioned in Supporting Information S3. Paraffin-embedded tumor tissue (Human tumor tissue or tumor-bearing mice tissue) in sections was dewaxed and rehydrated, and antigen retrieval was done in boiling water with citrate buffer (CXCL2, CRT and HMGB1) or proteinase K (Tunel) for 20 min. Then sections were washed and blocked with 3 % BSA for 60 min, incubated with Tunel detection solution for 60 min at room temperature in the dark, and incubated with CXCL2, CRT or HMGB1 antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody and observed by fluorescence microscope.

Co-culturing P.gingivalis infected tumor cells

Detailed methods for cell and bacteria culture were provided in Information S2. The P.gingivalis was resuspended in an appropriate volume of cell culture medium DMEM with concentration 109/CFU (OD600 = 1), MOC1 or Cal27 cells was co-cultured with P.gingivalis suspension at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0, 100 and 200 for 24 h to achieve P.gingivalis infected MOC1 or Cal27 cells with the optimal bacteria-cell ratio. All operations in the cell modelling process involving P. gingivalis infection were conducted using antibiotic-free culture medium. To detect the enrichment of P.gingivalis in MOC1 or Cal27 cells, the BCECF (2′,7’-Bis-(2-carboxyethyl)-5-(and-6)-carboxyfluorescein, Solarbio 40701ES50) labeled P.gingivalis was co-cultured with MOC1 or Cal27 cells for 24 h at MOI of 100. For image observation, the culture media were removed, and the cells in 6-well plates were washed three times with PBS. In addition, DAPI (Beyotime, C1006) was used to stain the nucleus. Thereafter, all the cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope.

P. gingivalis within infected MOC1 cells was cultured using blood agar plates. Cells from different treatment groups were washed twice with sterile PBS and incubated in fresh medium containing gentamicin (300 μg/mL) and metronidazole (200 μg/mL; Sigma) for 90 min to eliminate remaining extracellular bacteria. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and lysed with sterile distilled water for 30 min to release intracellular bacteria. The released bacteria were plated on blood agar and cultured under anaerobic conditions.

Establishment of P. gingivalis infected and uninfected OSCC tumor model

All animal experiments conducted in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Department of Laboratory Animal Science at Wuhan University (Approval No. AUP 20241147). Eight-week-old female C57BL/6 mice, maintained under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions, were obtained from Beinert Animal Co., Ltd. The animals were housed in individually ventilated cages within a barrier facility at the Wuhan University Laboratory Animal Center, with unrestricted access to food, water, and space for movement. To establish P. gingivalis infiltrated or uninfected OSCC orthotopic tumor-bearing mice, 100 μL (2 × 106) of P. gingivalis-infected or uninfected MOC1 cells was subcutaneously injected into the left flank of the mice. Ten days later, a distant tumor was established by subcutaneous injection of 100 μL (2 × 106) P. gingivalis-infected MOC1 cells into the contralateral flank. Tumor volume was calculated using the following formula: Tumor volume (mm3) = 0.5 × length × width2.

Preparation of MC-MM@MPDA

To prepare MPDA, pluronic F127 (120 mg) was dissolved in a 1:1 mixture of water (7.5 mL) and ethanol (7.5 mL) at room temperature and stirred until the solution turned clear. Dopamine hydrochloride (100 mg) was dissolved in 1 mL of water and added dropwise into the reactants, followed by stirring for 30 min. Subsequently, trimethylbenzene (TMB) (160 μL) was added slowly, and the mixture was stirred for another 30 min. The solution was observed to turn into a white turbid liquid, followed by the very slow dropwise addition of 500 μL NH3·H2O (25 %–28 %) in order to form self-polymerization of dopamine polymer nanosphere. After continuous reaction for 24 h, the mesoporous dopamine nanoparticles (MPDA) were collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm for 20 min). MPDA were washed with ethanol for 3 times to remove the unpolymerized dopamine and soft template. To form minocycline (MC) loaded MPDA (MC-MPDA), 10 mg MPDA and 4 mg MC (mass ratio of 5:2) were added to 10 mL Tris-HCl (pH 8.5, 10 mM) solutions, and further stirred for 24 h. Then MC-MPDA were centrifuged (10,000 rpm for 20 min) and collected for use. To prepare the macrophages cell membrane fragments encapsulated MC-MPDA MC-MM@MPDA, Raw 264.7 cell membrane was obtained following as Supporting information S2. The membrane extracted from 5 × 107 macrophages (1 mL) was mixed with 1 mg of MM-MPDA (1 mL), and the mixture was sonicated at 40 kHz and 50 W for 5 min using an ultrasonic instrument (Suzhou Nason Ultrasonic Technology Co., Ltd.) to obtain MC-MM@MPDA.

Characterization of MC-MM@MPDA

Dispersed the sample in deionized water to prepare a 100 μg/mL solution (n = 3), then monitored the size and zeta potential using Dynamic light scattering (DLS, Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, UK). The morphology of MPDA and MC-MM@MPDA was determined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-6700F, JEOL, Japan) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7700, Hitachi, Japan), with detailed methods outlined in Supporting Information S4. The absorbance of MC at 347 nm was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-800, Shimadzu, Japan) to calculate the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) and loading efficiency (LE%) according to the following formulas in Supporting information S4. To verify successful loading of MC onto the MPDA carrier, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis was performed on MC, MDPA, MC-MPDA and MC-MM@MPDA using an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode (Supporting information S4). To confirm the macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles retained the characteristic proteins CD11b and CCR2, the flow cytometry analysis for CD11b and western blot analysis for CCR2 were conducted (Supporting information S5). The MC-MM@MPDA of 5 mg/mL was kept in PBS at 4 °C and then the particle stability was assessed over the 7 days to evaluate its stability. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms of MPDA were measured using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method and Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method (Autosorb iQ Station 1, Quantachrome Instruments, USA), followed by analysis of its specific surface area, pore size, and pore volume. To assess the release behavior of MC from MC-MM@MPDA, 3 mg of nanocomposites were suspended in 10 mL PBS at different pH (pH 5.5 or 7.4, 0.1 M) and incubated in an incubator shaker (150 rpm) at 37 °C for 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h. Then the sample with laser groups were stimulated in a 50 °C water bath for 10 min to mimic the photothermal process at 0.5, 2, and 6 h. At each time point, the supernatant was collected, and the released MC was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer and the cumulative release of MC was calculated (Supporting Information S4). The photothermal conversion capability of MC-MM@MPDA was investigated. The temperature changes of MPDA, MC-MPDA, and MC-MM@MPDA solutions (250 μg/mL) under NIR irradiation (808 nm, 2 W.cm−2) for 300 s were recorded every 30 s using an infrared thermal imager (E 86, FLIR, USA). Additionally, the effects of different MC-MM@MPDA concentrations (125, 250, and 500 μg/mL) and laser power densities (1, 1.5, and 2 W.cm−2) on temperature elevation were investigated. Finally, the photothermal stability of MC-MM@MPDA (250 μg/mL) was assessed by subjecting the solution to four on/off laser cycles (808 nm, 2 W/ cm2), and its photothermal conversion efficiency was calculated.

In vitro cell biocompatibility and viability

The biocompatibility of MM@MDPA, the cytotoxicity of CDDP solution and MM@MDPA on MOC1 cells or HOK1 cells were determined by CCK8 method. Briefly, MOC1/ HOK1 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (1× 104 cells/well) and cultured with fresh medium or MM@MDPA (MDPA concentration with 1, 4, 63, 125, 250, 500 μg/mL) for 24 h, cells were incubated with 10 μL of CCK-8 solutions for another 30 min, and the absorbance at 450 nm of each well was detected under a microplate reader (Thermo MutliscanMK3, USA). The best concentration of MM@MDPA was determined at 250 μg/mL. Moreover, the uninfected and infected cells (MOI = 100) were incubated with CDDP solution (concentration of CDDP with 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10 μg/mL) for 24 h, the cell viability of MOC1 cells were evaluated using method similar to the described above. Antitumor activity of MC-MM@MDPA in vitro was evaluated using infected MOC1 or Cal27 cells. The corresponding samples (control, MC, MM@MDPA, MC-MM@MDPA, MM@MDPA+laser, MC-MM@MDPA+laser) were added to cells and incubation for 3 h, the laser group was irradiated with 808 nm near-infrared laser (2 W/cm2, 3 min), then the cell viability was calculated by (OD450Sample − OD450Blank) / (OD450Control − OD450Blank) × 100 %. Moreover, the cell viability and apoptosis were also monitored by calcein AM/PI staining kit, γ-H2AX staining and JC-1 staining, which were carried out according to the operation manual. Fluorescence was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

In vitro cellular uptake

To detect the cellular uptake by tumor cells, the fluorescent dye 1,1’-Dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3’-Tetramethylindocarbocyanine Perchlorate (Dil) was used as a probe to be loaded into MM@MPDA (Dil-MM@MPDA). MOC1 cells at a density of 4 × 105 per well were seeded in 6-well plate and cultured overnight, followed by incubating with Dil-MPDA and Dil-MM@MPDA (equivalent to the Dil concentration of 2 μM) for 4 h, 12 h, 24 h. For image observation, the culture media were removed, and the cells in 6-well plates were washed three times with PBS. In addition, DAPI was used to stain the nucleus. Thereafter, all of the cells were observed under the fluorescence microscope.

Hemolysis assay

Heparinized rabbit blood (Hongquan Bio, China) was co-incubated with PBS, H2O, different concentrations of MPDA and MM@MPDA. Following a 6-h incubation at 37 °C, the mixture was subjected to centrifugation at 600 ×g for 5 min. To eliminate potential interference from nanocomposites, the resulting supernatant was further centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 10 min. An equal volume of the final supernatant was then transferred into a 96-well plate, and absorbance was measured at 540 nm, hemolysis rate followed as the formula: Hemolysis % = [(Asample − Anegative) / (Apositive − Anegative)] × 100 %.

Determination of ICD and mature DCs in vitro

Immunofluorescence staining was employed to evaluate immunogenic cell death (ICD) induced by MC-MM@MPDA, with or without laser irradiation. MOC1 and Cal27 cells were treated under the same conditions as in the cell viability assay. After treatment, cells were fixed with pre-chilled methanol (−20 °C) for 10 min, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100 (for HMGB1 detection) for 20 min, and subsequently blocked with 5 % goat serum (100 μL) at room temperature for 2 h. Following removal of the blocking buffer, cells were incubated with either a CRT primary antibody (1:500 dilution) or an HMGB1 primary antibody (1:100 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. After washing, cells were incubated with a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (1:200 dilution; BOSTER, China) for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with 50 μM DAPI for 15 min, and fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Flow cytometry was employed to assess the maturation status of immature dendritic cells (iDCs). The extraction method of iDC was shown in Supporting Information S6. MOC1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and co-cultured for 24 h with various treatment groups: MC, MM@MPDA (two groups), and MC-MM@MPDA (two groups). Subsequently, the designated groups were exposed to near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation at 808 nm (2 W/cm2) for 3 min, followed by a 6-h incubation. Afterward, the treated tumor cells were co-cultured with iDCs for an additional 24 h. The dendritic cells were then harvested and stained with PE-conjugated anti-mouse I-A/I-E and PerCP-conjugated anti-mouse CD86 antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min. After washing with PBS, samples were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Cytek, USA).

In vivo biodistribution

To evaluate the distribution of MM@MPDA in vivo, Dil was selected as a model probe to prepare Dil-MM@MPDA. The P.gingivalis-infected tumor-bearing mice were divided into 2 groups and injected with Dil solution and Dil-MPDA@MM (5 mg/kg based on concentration of Dil) via the tail vein, respectively. The mice were sacrificed at 4 h, 8 h and 12 h after injection, then major organs including heart, kidney, spleen, lung, liver and tumors were harvested. Ex vivo imaging was acquired using In Vivo Imaging System(Thermal， USA). To evaluate the in vivo photothermal performance, the primary tumor in the MC-MM@MPDA+ Laser were irradiated with a near-infrared laser (2 W/cm2, 3 min) after injection the formulation, and the temperature changes were monitored every 1 min using an infrared thermal imager (Flir, USA).

In vivo antitumor and distal tumor inhibitory effects

When the primary tumor of the P.gingivalis-infected tumor-bearing mice reached a volume of 100 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5) and subjected to different treatments: (1) Control, (2) Cisplatin, (3) Minocycline, (4) MC-MM@MPDA, (5) MM@MPDA+ Laser, and (6) MC-MM@MPDA+ Laser. All groups were administered via tail vein injection at a volume of 100 μL, containing MC at a concentration of 450 μg/mL and CDDP at 10 μg/mL. The administered concentration for both the MM@MPDA and MC-MM@MPDA group (with or without laser irritation) was calculated based on the MPDA concentration, amounting to 2.5 mg/mL. Given the loading efficiency of MC, the MC concentration in the MC-MM@MPDA formulation was approximately 450 μg/mL, consistent with the concentration in the free MC group. After injection 8 h, the primary tumor was irradiated with NIR light at 2 W/cm2 for 3 min. The schedule of injection and laser irradiation treatment in the bilateral tumor-bearing C57BL/6 mice was every other day with 4 total dosages, the volumes of both primary and distal tumors, and the body weight of mice were recorded every other day to monitor changes, and the treatment was terminated on the 22nd day. After treatment, mice were euthanized, and both primary, distal tumors were collected for histological analysis, including H&E staining, TUNEL staining, and immunofluorescence staining.

Analysis of immune responses in vivo

The single-cell suspension from tumor tissue was prepared following Supporting Information S7. Fluorescent labeled antibodies (Table. S1) used for cell identification: Treg cells: FOXP3-PE, CD25-APC, CD4-PerCP/Cyanine5.5, CD8-Pacific Blue™; Dendritic cells (DCs): MHC-II-APC, CD86-FITC; T cells and NKT cells: CD3-FITC, CD4-PerCP/Cyanine5.5, CD8-Pacific Blue™, NK1.1-PE; Effector memory T cells and central memory T cells: CD3-FITC, CD8-Pacific Blue™, CD44-PE/Cyanine7, CD62L-PE; Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs): Gr-1-PE, CD11b-FITC. The immune cells were monitored by flow cytometry using Dxp Athena (Cytek, USA), the flow data were analyzed by Flowjo-V10 software. Using the tumor single-cell suspensions, cytokine levels of TNF-α, IFN-γ, IL-1, and IL-6 were measured using the ELISA kit, following the manufacturer's instructions.

In vivo biosafety

To monitor the effects of treatment on mouse health, whole blood was collected from the tail into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes and left to stand at 4 °C for 10 min, then centrifuged at 400 ×g for 5 min. The serum was aspirated out and analyzed using ELISA kits(Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) to measure levels of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CR), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). The main internal organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney) of tumor bearing mice were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and then dehydrated, transparent, paraffin-embedded, embedded, sectioned, and displayed for H&E staining to evaluate biological safety.

Statistical analysis

Experimental results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with n = 3 for cell experiments and n = 5 for animal experiments. A two-tailed Student's t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, while one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett's multiple comparison test was applied for statistical analysis among multiple groups. Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software.

Result and discussion

Enrichment of P.gingivalis in human OSCC and establishment of P.gingivalis infected model

To validate the association between P. gingivalis infection and CXCL2 overexpression, H&E, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), and CXCL2 immunofluorescence staining were conducted on OSCC tissues well-differentiated and poorly differentiated OSCC patients (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1A). The results showed that a higher presence of P.gingivalis was observed in poorly-differentiated OSCC, suggesting a possible association between the malignancy of OSCC and the abundance of P.gingivalis (Fig. 1A, B). It's reported that CXCL2 was related to a poor prognosis in OSCC patient, it could bind to its receptor CXCR2 and activate signaling pathways such as ERK/MAPK, enhancing cancer cell proliferation and migration (Lv et al., 2025). Additionally, CXCL2 could attract neutrophils into the tumor microenvironment, leading to immune suppression, making tumor cells more capable of evading immune system attacks. The high expression level of CXCL2 in the poorly-differentiated group (Fig. 1A, C and Fig. S1A) might be positively related to the abundance of P.gingivalis in tumor (Guo et al., 2021). Subsequently, green fluorescence (BCECF probe) labeled P.gingivalis (Fig. 1D) and Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Fig. 1E) confirmed the presence of P.gingivalis inside tumor cells (indicated by red arrows). When infected cells were lysed, the released P.gingivalis formed black colonies on blood agar plates, demonstrating that the bacteria remained viable after intracellular invasion (Fig. 1F). Based on the optimal multiplicity of infection (MOI) and calculation from the colony count on the blood agar plate, a bacterial invasion rate yielded of 0.15 % ± 0.012 % (n = 3), and the optimal cell model condition was MOI 100 with co-culture duration of 24 h (Pang et al., 2024; Ha et al., 2016). Furthermore, P.gingivalis infection could promote tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 1G), and bacterial infected tumors exhibited a certain level of resistance to cisplatin (CDDP), a conventional chemotherapy drug (Fig. 1H). The IC50 value for CDDP in non-infected MOC1 cells was 5.81 ± 0.35 μg/mL (n = 4), whereas in infected MOC1 cells, it increased to 9.25 ± 0.55 μg/mL (n = 4). To further validate these findings, P. gingivalis infected mice model (Fig. 1I) was established to assess the anti-tumor effect of CDDP in vivo. Based on tumor size and tumor inhibition rate results (Fig. 1J, K, L, S1B), the infected group exhibited faster tumor growth, significantly larger tumors, and marked resistance to CDDP, which was aligned with cisplatin resistance in vitro (Fig. 1G, H). CXCL2 expression was also significantly higher in infected tumor tissues compared to non-infected ones (Figure1M, 1O). Flow cytometry further revealed greater infiltration of MDSCs in the infected group (Figure1N, 1P), contributing to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. There findings aligned with the observation in poorly differentiated OSCC samples. Therefore, we speculated that infection with P.gingivalis in tumors might upregulate CXCL2 expression, thereby promoting the infiltration of MDSCs and establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which in turn facilitated tumor progression and metastasis. Targeting the clearance of intracellular bacteria should constitute an effective approach for improving therapeutic outcomes in bacterial-infected OSCC (Zhou et al., 2025).

Synthesis and characterization of MC-MM@MPDA

As shown in Fig. 2A, the MPDA was synthesized under alkaline conditions using Pluronic F-127 as a soft template and TMB as a porogen via the self-polymerization of dopamine. Subsequently, MC was loaded onto the MPDA through molecular group interactions, and macrophage membranes (MM) were coated onto MC-MPDA via ultrasonication. DLS measurements revealed that the particle size of MPDA was 186.40 ± 2.03 nm, with negligible change after MC loading (189.27 ± 1.86 nm). After MM coating, the particle size increased to 197.73 ± 0.95 nm, reflecting an approximately 8 nm increase, consistent with previous reports (Fig. 2B) (Cao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023b). The zeta potential of MPDA was −17.77 ± 0.33 mV, which shifted to −20.40 ± 0.37 mV after MC loading. After coating MM onto MC-MPDA, the zeta potential decreased to −25.47 ± 1.24 mV, closely matching that of MM (Fig. 2C). The SEM and TEM images showed that MPDA had an obvious mesoporous structure with an average diameter of about 182.70 nm, and the pore size of its mesopores was about 5.24 nm (Fan et al., 2024). In contrast, the mesoporous structure on the surface of MC-MM@MPDA became blurred or even disappeared after loading MC and coating MM (Fig. 2D). Flow cytometry and western blot analysis confirmed that the macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles retained the characteristic proteins CD11b (Fig. 2E) and CCR2 (Fig. S2A). CD11b mediates macrophage adhesion, migration, and phagocytosis, while CCR2 is the receptor for CCL2 that drives macrophage chemotaxis into tumor and inflammatory sites (Khatoon et al., 2022). The presence of these proteins on the nanoparticle surface suggested that the biomimetic coating might endow the particles with macrophage-like adhesive and migratory properties, thereby providing a molecular basis for their preferential accumulation in tumors. Taken together, these results indicated that the MM had been successfully attached to the MPDA.

The stability of nanoparticles might influence their therapeutic efficacy, the particle size and PDI of MC-MM@MPDA were assessed its stability. Over the 7 days, the particle size of MC-MM@MPDA consistently remained around 197 nm and PDI also showed no significant changes, indicating satisfactory dispersion uniformity and good stability (Fig. 2F). In the N2 adsorption-desorption experiments, the adsorption-desorption isotherms were typical Type IV isotherms with a distinct hysteresis loop, indicating the presence of pore structures in MPDA (Fig. 2G). MPDA exhibited a Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area of 37.886 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.1028 cm3/g, and a pore size of 3.260 nm, consistent with TEM observations. These mesoporous structural properties endowed MPDA with an expanded specific surface area, thereby significantly enhancing its drug-loading capacity. It was found that MPDA had a higher drug loading capacity, with a loading efficiency (LE%) of 16.89 ± 0.43 % and encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of 47.55 % ± 1.20 %, which was significantly higher than that of ordinary PDA (Liu et al., 2020a). To verify the successful loading of MC onto the MPDA carrier, the result of Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) revealed characteristic coupled doublet peaks at 1650 cm−1 and 1580 cm−1 in the spectrum of MC-MPDA and MC-MM@MPDA respectively, corresponding to the conjugated enone-enol system of MC, confirming its successful loading onto the MPDA carrier (Fig. S2B). The release behavior of MC from MC-MM@MPDA was evaluated in PBS at pH 7.4 and 5.5, under condition with and without laser irradiation (808 nm, 2 W/cm2). MC-MM@MPDA remained stable under neutral pH conditions but degraded and released MC under acidic conditions and photothermal effects. Notably, the release rate of MC was faster under the simulated acidic environment (pH 5.5) than in neutral media, and photothermal stimulation resulted in a cumulative release rate of 56.62 % within 12 h (Fig. 2H). The photothermal properties of MC-MM@MPDA were systematically evaluated, as shown in Fig. 2I and S2C, the temperature of the MC-MM@MPDA solution increased significantly from 29.1 °C to 55.0 °C after 5 min of irradiation at 2.0 W/cm2, with no significant difference compared to MPDA alone (56.6 °C), indicating that the loading MC and coating MC had little impact on the photothermal performance of MPDA. The solution temperature rose significantly with increasing MPDA concentration and laser power (Fig. 2J and S2D). When the MPDA concentration was 250 μg/mL and the laser power was 2.0 W/cm2, the solution temperature reached 55.0 °C. Additionally, the photothermal conversion efficiency of MC-MM@MPDA was determined to be approximately 23.92 % based on the photothermal heating-cooling curves (Fig. 2K), with good photothermal stability (Fig. S2E). Although MC-MM@MPDA has the potential to serve as an effective PTT therapeutic agent its excellent properties, large-scale manufacturing of the membrane-coated nanoparticles also faces technical challenges, such as the extraction and purification of macrophage membranes, alongside the uniformity and reproducibility of membrane coating, which all require further validation.

In vitro inhibitory effect and precise targeting of MC-MM@MPDA

The biosafety of nanomedicine is a crucial prerequisite for its clinical translation. The cell viability of HOK1 cells (Fig. 3A) and infected MOC1 (Fig. 3B) were compared after co-culturing for 24 h under different concentrations of MPDA, the biocompatibility remained excellent even at high concentrations (500 μg/mL) after macrophage membrane encapsulation. According to the photothermal effect, biosafety and the MIC of MC against P. gingivalis, the working concentration of MC-MM@MPDA was ultimately determined to be 250 μg/mL based on MPDA concentration. Hemolysis experiments (Fig. 3C) further revealed that nanocomposites encapsulated with macrophage membranes hardly induced hemolysis. These findings confirmed that macrophage membrane encapsulation significantly enhanced biosafety of MPDA nanoparticles.

Nanomedicine possesses biological targeting capabilities, which can increase drug accumulation at specific sites, improve drug utilization efficiency, and reduce side effects during treatment. The uptake of the nanocomposites was positively correlated with time in both MOC1 (Fig. 3D and E) and CAL27cell lines (Fig. S1D and S1E). Notably, the macrophage membrane encapsulated material showed significantly higher accumulation in MOC1 and CAL27 cells, but significantly lower drug uptake in RAW264.7 cell (Fig. 3D, F), compared to unencapsulated nanocomposites. The results of the targeting efficacy of the nanomedicine on P.gingivalis infected and non-infected MOC1 and CAL27 (Fig. S1C) demonstrated that the targeting specificity for infected tumor cells was significantly enhanced when the nanocomposites were coated with macrophage membranes. This enhanced targeting was primarily attributable to the inherent chemotactic capability of macrophages and their strong affinity for inflamed and lesioned sites (Koh and DiPietro, 2011). Under physiological conditions, macrophages respond to inflammatory factors via various chemokine receptors (such as CCR2) expressed on their membranes, thereby directing their migration toward inflammatory regions (Gu et al., 2024; Song et al., 2025). Macrophage membrane encapsulation provided enhanced biosafety and high-precision targeting. In cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 3G), the MC-MM@MPDA co-culture group exhibited significantly reduced cell viability after light exposure on MOC1, and the MM@MPDA group also showed a notable photothermal inhibitory effect. Similar results were also observed in the CAL27 cell line (Fig. S3A). After treatment, the cells from different groups were lysed and resuspended in liquid culture medium or blood agar plates. On blood-agar plates, MC-MM@MPDA+L and MM@MPDA+L yielded virtually no bacterial colonies, while MC-MM@MPDA alone showed visible but markedly fewer colonies than the control. This demonstrated that both intracellular delivery of minocycline and standalone photothermal therapy exerted antibacterial effects. In liquid culture, MC-MM@MPDA+L yielded markedly fewer bacteria after lysis than MM@MPDA+L which indicated together they synergized for superior bacterial clearance. In contrast, the free-MC group still produced abundant black colonies, revealing that minocycline alone was ineffective against intracellular bacteria (Fig. 3H, I). Similar results were showed in Live/dead cell staining (Fig. 4A, S4A), γ-H2AX staining (Fig. 4B), and JC-1 staining (Fig. S3B). Live/dead staining showed that almost no dead cells were observed in the four groups without light exposure, indicating that minocycline alone had no inhibitory effect on tumor cells. In contrast, the two light-exposed groups had almost no live cells, demonstrating the superior tumor-suppressing ability of the nanomedicine. γ-H2AX staining revealed significant DNA damage in the two laser irradiated groups, indicating tumor cell apoptosis. JC-1 staining showed a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential in the light-exposed groups, further supporting tumor cell apoptosis.

Photothermal ablation efficiency and dendritic cell maturation in vitro

Following photothermal therapy, tumor cells subjected to thermal stress undergo immunogenic cell death (ICD), leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) such as calreticulin (CRT) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). These signals play a crucial role in initiating and enhancing both innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses (Fang et al., 2025b; Dou et al., 2024; Lackner et al., 2025). The photothermal ablation effect of MC-MM@MPDA in MOC1 or CAL27 cells was evaluated to assess the ICD-inducing effect of the nanomedicine. HMGB1 (Fig. 4C and Fig. S4B) was released from the cell nucleus, resulting in difficult to detect fluorescence in both laser-exposed groups, while CRT (Fig. 4C and Fig. S4C) expression significantly increased under laser stimulation, indicating that the high temperature induced by photothermal treatment caused ICD in tumor cells. As DAMPs are released, dendritic cells (DCs) should be theoretically activated. Here, immature DCs (iDCs) co-culturing with tumor cells had subjected to different interventions, the two groups exposed to light showed a markedly higher number of mature DCs (Fig. 4D, S6A), although no significant difference was observed between MM@MPDA +L and MC-MM@MPDA +L groups. This might be attributed to the potent efficacy of photothermal therapy at the cellular level, which effectively eradicated tumor cells and eliminated P. gingivalis within, thereby resulting in no significant difference between the two groups in terms of dendritic cell induction. Taken together, the combined results from γ-H2AX, JC-1, CRT exposure, HMGB1 release, and DC maturation provided a coherent and multi-level line of evidence that the treatment indeed induced bona fide ICD, thereby initiating downstream immune activation.

In vivo biodistribution and anti-tumor effect of MC-MM@MPDA

The treatment schedule for mouse model of OSCC with bilateral tumor infection of P.gingivalis was shown in the schematic diagram (Fig. 5A). Primary tumor growth curve (Fig. 5B) within the treatment cycle was shown that the MC-MM@MPDA+L treatment exhibited superior tumor inhibition compared to the cisplatin-positive control group (p < 0.0001) and the MM@MPDA+Laser group (p < 0.0001). In the primary tumor (Fig. 5C and D), the tumor inhibition of MC-MM@MPDA+L group was 89.9 % and superior compared to both the cisplatin-positive control group and the MM@MPDA+L group, with statistically significant differences (p < 0.01), underscoring the pronounced anti-tumor therapeutic efficacy of the MC-MM@MPDA+L formulation. This enhanced efficacy might be attributed to the clearance of intracellular bacteria by MC from nanocomposites and tumor cells ablation caused by photothermal effect, which in turn slowed tumor progression. Furthermore, the tumor-associated antigens derived from the lysed intracellular bacteria might contribute to the reprogramming of the tumor microenvironment from immunosuppressive to immunoactive states (Wang et al., 2023b; Fan et al., 2024), thereby facilitating synergistic antitumor effects through the combination of photothermal therapy and immunotherapy. In contrast, neither the MC group nor the non-irradiated MC-MM@MPDA group exhibited significant tumor suppression, highlighting the necessity of both precise intratumoral delivery of MC and laser irradiation to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. As shown in Fig. S5A, S5B and S5C, the MC-MM@MPDA+L group exhibited moderate inhibition in the distant tumor, with efficacy significantly superior to the MM@MPDA+L group (p < 0.01). However，compared with the CDDP treatment group, the MC-MM@MPDA+L group showed limited efficacy in controlling distant tumors volume (p < 0.05), indicating that this therapy could only moderately activate systemic immune. Based on these results, it is further proposed that combining this nanomedicine with cisplatin should more effectively eradicate metastatic tumors in clinical practice. As the thymus is a key organ for T cell development and maturation, its enlargement may indicate increased T cell production and differentiation (Khatoon et al., 2022). The changes in thymus volume/weight (Fig. 5F) also served as indirect evidence of enhanced immune activity.

Infrared thermal imaging was used to record temperature changes in the tumor site during treatment under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. As shown in the Fig. 5E, the group treated with MC-MM@MPDA led to an increase in local temperature upon NIR irradiation compared to PBS group, the temperature gradually rose with prolonged irradiation, reaching 59.9 °C after 3 min. This indicated that the nanomedicine efficiently converted light energy into heat in vivo, also indirectly confirming its accumulation at the tumor site. As shown in the Fig. 5G and H, compared with mice treated with free Dil, the mice in the Dil-MM@MPDA treatment group exhibited significantly higher signal intensity in the tumor region than those in the Dil treatment group (p > 0.0001), with the signal gradually increasing over time and reaching a peak at 8 h post-injection. In contrast, the fluorescence signal in Dil-treated mice primarily accumulated in the liver, demonstrating that macrophage membrane-coated nanomedicine could effectively deliver therapeutic agents to infected tumor tissues and prolong their circulation time in vivo.

FISH staining (Fig. 6A) and blood agar plate culture (Fig. 6B) further validated the presence of bacteria within tumor cells and their impact on tumor development and treatment. The results of bacterial release from tumor tissue in blood agar plate culture were highly consistent with the FISH experimental results. In the MC-MM@MPDA+L group, P.gingivalis was virtually undetectable while the MC-MM@MPDA and MM@MPDA+L groups exhibited a reduction in intracellular bacterial. In contrast, free minocycline showed no significant inhibition effect on intracellular bacteria. These results indicated that intracellular delivery of minocycline exhibited potent bactericidal activity against intracellular pathogens, while photothermal therapy exerted complementary antibacterial effects through thermally induced damage. This combination strategy achieved effective suppression of both intracellular bacteria and tumor progression.

H&E and TUNEL staining (Fig. 6C) of tumor tissues revealed the tumor tissue in the control group exhibited a malignant tumor or highly proliferative pathological state. In contrast, the MC-MM@MPDA+L group showed extensive necrosis, nuclear fragmentation, and dissolution, with an increase in eosinophils in necrotic areas and significantly enhanced green fluorescence signals around the necrotic regions. Ki-67 is a cell cycle-associated protein expressed in the G1, S, G2, and M phases, and serves as a marker of cell proliferation. Ki-67 staining showed weak proliferative activity in the MM@MPDA +L and MC-MM@MPDA +L groups, suggesting that these treatments effectively suppressed tumor cell proliferation，further confirming the potential inhibition on tumor growth and invasion.

Antitumor immunoregulatory mechanism of MC-MM@MPDA

To better understand the mechanism by which MC-MM@MPDA effectively inhibited primary and distant tumors, the immune modulation induced by treatment were explored. In human clinical samples, Fig. 1A observed that increased tumor malignancy was correlated with higher abundance of intracellular P.gingivalis and elevated CXCL2 expression. CXCL2 recruits neutrophils and other immune cells via its receptor CXCR2, playing a crucial role in modulating the tumor microenvironment (Lv et al., 2025; Taki et al., 2018; Lepsenyi et al., 2021). Herein, immunofluorescence staining for CXCL2 (Fig. 7A, Fig. S5D) revealed that the fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced in both two groups subjected to near-infrared irradiation, with a more pronounced decreased in CXCL2 expression in the MC-MM@MPDA group, while the CDDP and MC groups exhibited mild inhibition of CXCL2 expression. The trend in CXCL2 expression levels (Fig. S5D) was consistent with the intracellular clearance of P. gingivalis presented in Fig. 6A and B. Both the CDDP group and the photothermal therapy groups (MM@MPDA+L and MC-MM@MPDA+L) exhibited release of the DAMPs proteins including HMGB1 and CRT (Fig. 7A, Fig. S5E, Fig. S5F) and significantly increased the number of mature DCs (Fig. 7B), thereby activating more NK cells and T cells in tumor tissues (Fig. 7C and D) (Li et al., 2023; Jhunjhunwala et al., 2021). NK cells bridge the innate and adaptive immune systems by rapidly producing cytokines such as IFN-γ. High expression of both CD44 and CD62L generally indicated that the cells exhibited a central memory T cell (Tcm) phenotype and could rapidly response upon antigen re-encounter (Liu et al., 2020b; Mittra et al., 2023; Herda et al., 2019). In the MC-MM@MPDA+L group, the number of CD44+ and CD62L+ cells (Fig. 7E) was significantly increased compared to the other treatment groups (p < 0.0001), reflecting effective immune activation and long-lasting immunological memory. This was consistent with the marked reduction in distal tumor size observed in the MC-MM@MPDA+L group shown in Fig. S5A.

Previous studies had collectively shown that tumor-infiltrating Tregs played a pivotal role in creating an immunosuppressive microenvironment (Chen et al., 2022; Kumagai et al., 2024; Nie et al., 2024). As shown in the Fig. 7G, the MC-MM@MPDA+L group exhibited a markedly lower number of Treg cells compared to the control group. Low Treg cell expression indicated that the immunosuppression microenvironment has been alleviated. High expression of CXCL2 caused by bacterial infection in tumors was closely linked to the recruitment and functional modulation of MDSCs (Shi et al., 2018), correlating with more aggressive tumor behavior and poorer patient prognosis (Zhang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 7F, the MC-MM@MPDA+L group exhibited a markedly lower number of MDSCs compared to the control group (p < 0.0001), indicating that clearing intracellular bacteria could reduce CXCL2 expression, thereby diminishing MDSC infiltration in the tumor microenvironment. By contrast, MC-MM@MPDA under non-irradiated conditions exhibited only limited inhibitory effects on both tumor growth and MDSC accumulation. Both the MM@MPDA and MC-MM@MPDA groups showed a significant increase in TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion after near-infrared irradiation, while the MC-MM@MPDA group without irradiation also exhibited a slight rise in these cytokines (Fig. 7H and I). As key pro-inflammatory cytokines, IL-1β and IL-6 can promote immune cell recruitment, angiogenesis, and even tumor invasion and metastasis (Mohamed et al., 2024; Hirano, 2021). The expression levels of IL-1β and IL-6 were markedly higher in bacteria-infected tumors compared to non-infected tumors (Fig. 7J and K), indicating a heightened inflammatory response in the bacteria-infected tumor microenvironment. Following the clearance of intracellular bacteria in the treatment group using MC and MC-MM@MPDA, IL-1β and IL-6 expression were significantly reduced. Furthermore, IL-1β and IL-6 levels remained relatively high in the groups received near-infrared irradiation due to thermal damage trigger cellular stress (Ma et al., 2020). These findings supported a potential cascade effect: bacterial clearance leaded to reduced CXCL2 levels, which in turn diminished MDSC/Treg recruitment. Concurrently, CXCL2 downregulation was not an isolated effect but acted as a critical early step synergising with photothermal therapy-induced ICD effects. By amplifying immune cascade responses in immune cells such as NK cells and CD8+ cells, it promoted cytokine secretion, regulated the establishment of an immunosuppressive environment through positive feedback loops, and ultimately achieved tumor eradication.

Biosafety of MC-MM@MPDA for antitumor therapy in vivo

A more detailed evaluation of the nanodrug's biosafety was conducted. Heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney tissues were collected to investigate whether MC-MM@MPDA combined with NIR irradiation caused damage to major organs. As shown in the S7A, H&E staining indicated that the morphology of these organs was normal in all groups, with no apparent damage observed. Furthermore, serum levels of blood alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA) confirmed that neither the liver nor the kidney sustained significant injury (S7B, 7C, 7D, 7E), demonstrating good biosafety in vivo. Despite achieving some encouraging findings, current efficacy and safety data remain confined to in vitro and small animal xenograft models. Achieving clinical translation hinges on establishing bridges to clinically relevant models and demonstrating reproducibility across different biological systems.

Conclusion

In summary, the presence of P.gingivalis and overexpression of CXCL2 have been confirmed in clinical OSCC specimens, speculating that it was associated with poor response to conventional chemotherapy and poor prognosis in OSCC patients. Results from cellular and animal experiments confirmed that P.gingivalis infection in tumors could upregulate CXCL2 expression, leading to increased infiltration of MDSCs and the immunosuppressive microenvironment, in turn facilitated tumor progression and metastasis. Inspired by these validated observations, precise elimination of tumor-promoting microorganisms and alleviation of immune suppression should enhance the clinical treatment outcomes. The macrophage-biomimetic nanoplatform (MC-MM@MPDA) integrated intracellular P.gingivalis clearance, photothermal antitumor and bactericidal effects, and immune activation was designed to achieve targeted therapy tailored to P. gingivalis-infected OSCC. The synergistic activation of the immune system by thermal damage-induced ICD and tumor-associated antigens released during intracellular bacteria clearance could achieve near-complete eradication of the bacterial infected tumors. The innovation of this work lied in providing an efficient solution to infection-associated cancers and elucidating the mechanistic link between bacterial clearance and immune reconstitution. Although this approach still faces some challenges in its clinical translation process, it holds promise for overcoming treatment bottlenecks in clinically infection-associated OSCC. For example, the platform may also serve as an adjuvant therapy to integrate chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immune checkpoint inhibitors for OSCC treatment in future clinical translation (Chen et al., 2024). Another important future direction is the integration of oral microbiome profiling into therapeutic design and patient selection.
