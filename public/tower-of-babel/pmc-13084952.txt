Surgical patients’ awareness about their diagnosis and management plans in Ethiopia: cross-sectional study
Authors: Kaletsidk Dessalegn Mossie, Meseret Tadesse Zerihun, Habtie Bantider Wubet, Temesgen Birlie Asmare, Kumlachew Geta Belete, Negesse Zurbachew Gobezie, Biruk Demissie, Getachew Mekete Deress
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundPatient awareness of their diagnosis and management plan is crucial for improving compliance, empowering patients and enhancing outcomes. We aimed to assess surgical patients’ awareness of their diagnosis, management plans and associated factors.MethodA cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to March 2025 on 400 adult surgical inpatients who had undergone surgery in the general surgery, gynaecology and obstetrics, and orthopaedic wards at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Ethiopia. Data were collected using a structured written questionnaire and analysed using the SPSS V.25. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with patients’ awareness of their diagnosis and care plan, with significance determined using adjusted ORs and 95% CIs.ResultOverall, 52% of respondents had global awareness of their clinical conditions and management plans. Awareness was highest for clinical diagnosis (78.9%), necessity of admission (78.9%) and operations performed (72.0%). However, more than 50% of respondents did not seek information on the diagnosis, possible cause and investigation related to their condition. In multivariable analysis, patients with tertiary education were 7.12 times more likely to have global awareness than those without formal education (adjusted OR, AOR=7.12; 95% CI 1.95 to 25.95), and patients living in urban areas were 3.15 times more likely to have global awareness than those in rural areas (AOR=3.15; 95% CI 1.63 to 6.10; p<0.05).ConclusionsAwareness of various aspects of healthcare ranged from 35.5% to 78.9%, with about half of respondents demonstrating global awareness of their diagnosis and management plans. Implementing shared decision-making models may improve patients’ understanding of their care plans.Keywords: Inpatients, Ethiopia, Awareness

Introduction

Nations all over the world have ratified patient rights charters that aim to address patients’ awareness of their health status as well as their comprehensive medical care.1 While each charter has outlined patients’ rights to access information, each institution is responsible for the process of bringing this ideal to life.2 One of the main goals of the Ethiopian Health Service Transformation Plan is to provide patient-centred treatment.3 By guaranteeing that patients have access to information regarding their diagnosis and course of treatment, patient-centred care is thought to maximise patients’ health literacy. Patients who have higher levels of health literacy will be better able to comprehend, evaluate and use health information to make decisions that are pertinent to their conditions.4

Patients’ health literacy and self-efficacy to manage their disease are highly dependent on patient–provider communication and patient involvement in decision-making about their treatment plan.5 6 Patient participation and engagement in healthcare decision-making have been associated with improved health outcomes, better treatment adherence, enhanced quality of life, greater patient satisfaction and more cost-effective healthcare services.6 7

Effective communication between medical staff and patients is essential to understanding and delivering quality care.8 9 Patient satisfaction, compliance, understanding of the diagnosis and treatment plan, and the desire for a speedy recovery are all correlated with communication between the physician and the patient.10 11 For improved care and patient outcomes, it is crucial to ascertain whether patients can understand their diagnosis and treatment plan.12

The healthcare provider has an obligation to inform, and the patient has an obligation to ask for information to improve patient awareness. Any patient whose clinical condition warrants admission should ideally be informed of the clinical diagnosis, the rationale behind the admission and the risks associated with not being admitted. Once in the ward, the patient must be continuously informed on the management plan, which includes the anticipated duration of hospitalisation, investigation, medication and surgical procedures.2 When patients are discharged from the hospital, physicians often overestimate their understanding of the treatment plan and may fail to identify individuals with low health literacy.13

A study done in a South African academic hospital found that about 51.5% of inpatients had global awareness of their clinical conditions and management plans.2 Despite inpatient awareness of the reason for their admission and the planned management enhancing patient compliance and empowering patients to be resourceful in subsequent consultations, there is limited evidence of patients’ awareness about their clinical condition and management plans for their disease in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aimed to assess patients’ awareness of their reason for admission, their management plan and the factors influencing overall awareness of their clinical condition at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Methods

Study design and study area

A cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to March 2025 on 400 adult surgical inpatients who had undergone surgery in the general surgery, gynaecology and obstetrics, and orthopaedic wards at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Amhara region, Ethiopia. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines, and the completed STROBE checklist is provided as online supplemental material. Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital provides patient care and also serves as a facility for teaching and clinical training in various medical disciplines. All patients who underwent surgery were included in the study. Patients with cognitive impairments or psychiatric conditions that hinder their ability to comprehend or accurately respond to the questionnaire, as well as critically ill patients and outpatients, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The calculated sample size was 403, based on a single population proportion of p=50%, a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error and a 5% adjustment for non-response. Systematic random sampling was used to select study participants by using a skip interval of K=N/n. During the 3-month period, a total of 800 patients were seen. Therefore, K=800/403=2 (skip interval), and the first study participant (randomly started) was selected by the lottery method. Every second case from the daily surgeries was included in the study.

Eligible participants were identified from the daily surgical admission and operation lists during the study period. Selected patients were approached in the surgical ward after surgery when they were clinically stable and able to communicate. The purpose of the study was explained to each participant, and informed consent was obtained before administering the questionnaire. Patients who declined participation or met the exclusion criteria were skipped, and the next eligible patient was recruited according to the sampling interval. These procedures helped minimise selection bias and ensured that the sample was representative of the surgical inpatient population.

Data collection procedures and instruments

Data were collected from selected study participants from 15 December 2024 to 15 March 2025, by four trained anaesthetists using a structured questionnaire (online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was made by reviewing different studies.214,16 It was translated from English into Amharic (the predominant language spoken at the research setting) and then translated back into English to ensure the accuracy of the translation. The questionnaire contains patients’ demographic data and awareness of the patient’s clinical condition and planned management. The main outcome variable was patients’ global awareness of their clinical diagnosis and management plan. Independent variables included age, sex, marital status, educational level, occupation, residence, department of admission and first encounter with a clinician.

It also included data on whether the patient sought information and clarity on information provided by the healthcare providers. Information related to the outcome variables, such as patients’ diagnosis, admission reasons and planned management, was obtained from both the patient and the patient’s medical record. Agreement between the two sources of information was examined. Patients were classified as aware when their verbal reports were consistent with the corresponding information recorded in their medical records.14,16

The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of the sample size at Felege Hiwot Specialized Hospital to ensure the questions were balanced, correctly constructed and able to obtain crucial information. It was also evaluated by experienced researchers. The questionnaire was internally consistent with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (α=0.89). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant after providing detailed information about the study and its objectives. The questionnaire was then administered to patients in the surgical ward after surgery, once they were clinically stable and able to communicate. The principal investigator and supervisor checked the completeness of data every day.

Data processing and analysis

After collection, data were summarised and coded. Data were entered into a SPSS Windows V.25 for data cleanup and analysis. Age, the only quantitative variable, was recorded in years and categorised for logistic regression. Data cleanup was performed by checking for frequencies and missed values and variables. There were no missing data for any of the variables, so all 400 participants were included in the analysis. A descriptive statistical analysis was used to show the characteristics of the participants and the proportion of patients who were aware of the reasons for admission, diagnosis and planned management. We used logistic regression models to assess factors associated with patients’ global awareness. The cut point used during bivariate analysis was a p value of less than 0.2 and was considered fit for multivariable analysis to control for potential confounding. The crude and adjusted ORs with their corresponding 95% CIs were computed. P values of less than 0.05 were considered significant. The analysis result was presented as frequencies and percentages using tables and bar graphs.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct and reporting of this study.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of surgical patients

A total of 403 eligible patients were approached to participate in the study. Three patients declined to participate for personal reasons. Consequently, 400 patients were included in the final analysis, resulting in a response rate of 99.2%. From these, the majority of participants were females, 207 (51.7%). 253 (63.2%) of them were married, and 155 (38.8%) were not formally educated. About 215 (53.8%) lived in rural areas, and the most common occupation of the participants was farming 168 (42.0%). About 188 (47.0%) of patients undergo general surgery, and the majority (66.8%) encounter the clinician for the first time (table 1).

Discussion

Our study assesses inpatients’ awareness about the admission reason and management plan of their clinical condition. Global awareness of healthcare among patients comprises awareness of the clinical diagnosis, reasons for admission, planned management, possible causes of the condition that necessitated admission, estimated duration of admission, planned investigations and operative procedures done. The study revealed that only 52% of patients reported being globally aware of various aspects of the healthcare they received, indicating that nearly half of the patients were unaware of important elements of their care. The finding was consistent with the study done in South Africa.2

While the majority of respondents indicated awareness of their clinical diagnosis (78%) and the reason for admission (76.9%), only 51.5% reported having a comprehensive understanding of their overall healthcare. This disparity indicates that patients may have knowledge of specific details but lack a holistic view of their clinical condition and management plan, signalling a need for more thorough communication from healthcare providers to ensure complete patient awareness. The result was similar to a study done in Nigeria (86.8%),16 South Africa (78%2 and Nepal (57.7%),12 in which the majority of the respondents were aware of their diagnosis.

In our study, only 56.8% of respondents were aware of the management plan despite a high percentage of patients being aware of their clinical diagnosis. This might be due to patients being more immediately informed about what condition they have, whereas understanding and retaining the management plan involves multiple steps, which requires more communication, time and repeated interactions with healthcare providers. This makes it difficult to understand the management process.15 The result is similar to the study done in Nigeria,16 but lower than the study done in South Africa2 and the USA.17 This might be because economically developed countries have more resources for public awareness campaigns and health literacy.

In our study, the majority (64.5%) of the study participants were unaware of the possible cause of their clinical condition, which is lower than in the study done in South Africa.2 This might arise from information gaps, low health literacy and limited healthcare provider and patient communication.18,20 Only 40% of respondents were aware of the estimated duration of their hospital stay. Knowing the anticipated length of stay helps patients prepare mentally and emotionally, alleviating anxiety and enabling them to feel more in control of their circumstances. It also facilitates improved planning for patients and their families regarding work commitments and helps manage healthcare expenses.

This study revealed that 28% of patients were unaware of the surgical procedure performed, suggesting they either lacked the opportunity to seek clarification or did not fully understand the procedure. This raises ethical concerns in patient care, highlighting the necessity for patients to receive clear and comprehensible information before consenting to any procedures, including surgeries.1 Furthermore, increasing patient involvement marks a shift from traditional paternalistic models, where clinicians dictate care, to a collaborative approach in which clinicians’ partner with patients. This encourages shared decision-making, ultimately aimed at enhancing the quality of medical decisions made.7

In our study, most respondents did not seek clarification on their diagnosis, potential causes or investigations related to their clinical condition unless the healthcare provider voluntarily offered this information. In the context of healthcare, the responsibility of seeking information about a patient’s health condition is primarily shared between the patient and the healthcare provider. Patients should inquire about their clinical conditions to obtain the necessary information.21

Patients often hesitate to seek information from their healthcare providers as they perceive healthcare practitioners to be busy, aloof or poor communicators and sometimes uncertain about patients’ conditions, which may discourage questions.22 Although patient hesitancy was not formally assessed, this factor may partly explain why some patients did not seek information regarding their diagnosis and management plans. This underscores the need to empower patients to ask questions about their health during a clinical encounter. Empowering patients can enhance involvement and engagement in their care. Encouraging patients to take an active role can also improve their adherence to the care pathway.23

According to this study, 60% of respondents participated in decision-making, and 55% of patients knew they could choose their clinical management plan. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Nigeria.16 Patients involved in the shared decision-making process are more compliant with treatments and have a reduced risk of complications related to the pathology.24 Although formal shared decision-making scales were not used, patient involvement was assessed using a structured questionnaire.

Level of education and residency predicted global awareness of patients’ clinical condition. The finding is consistent with a study done in Norway, Iran and the USA, which showed that higher education levels increase patients’ understanding about their disease and treatment.17 25 26 As the educational level increased, health literacy also increased because education is associated with a greater ability to access, understand and utilise health information. While our study did not formally assess health literacy, such capacity may facilitate more effective communication with the healthcare providers, better understanding of diagnosis and treatment plans, and greater confidence in navigating the healthcare system.27

Patients residing in urban areas had higher global awareness about their clinical condition. This might be because urban areas offer easier access to medical facilities and services, giving people more chances to interact with medical professionals and get clear information about their diagnoses and treatment options.15 Additionally, urban residents benefit from health communication strategies and patient-centred care initiatives.28

Study limitations

This study has some methodological limitations. The cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between the identified factors and patients’ awareness of their clinical condition and management plan. The study relied partly on patient-reported data, which has inherent subjectivity, and participants were asked to recall information about their clinical condition retrospectively, which may have introduced recall bias. In addition, the study was conducted in a single healthcare institution, so caution should be exercised when generalising the findings to other settings. Key unmeasured confounders, including health literacy and patient hesitancy to engage clinicians, further limit causal inferences, as these variables were not directly measured using validated assessment tools in this study. Furthermore, the level of patient awareness may vary across ward specialties; however, this study assessed the global awareness of surgical inpatients at the institution.

Conclusions

This study revealed that the levels of awareness among inpatients varied based on the specific aspects of healthcare received. While many inpatients reported awareness of certain care components, only half were globally aware of the reasons for their admission and their management plans. To enhance patients’ understanding of their care plans, efforts should focus on implementing a shared decision-making model. Future research should investigate the reasons why inpatients do not seek information about their clinical conditions and management plans.
