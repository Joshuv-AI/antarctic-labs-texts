Patient Experiences and Safety Concerns Regarding GLP-1 Receptor Agonists for Obesity: A Cross-Sectional Survey in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Mansour A Tobaiqy, Sulafa T. Alqutub, Mohammed A. Alzahrani, Renad H. Alazwari, Faisal A. Asiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjective:To evaluate the perceptions, experiences, and safety concerns of patients regarding the glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. Obesity is a significant public health issue. The newer anti-obesity medications: GLP-1 receptor agonists, with an increase in therapeutic options. However, little is known about the perceived benefits, safety, cost, and accessibility.Methods:Cross-sectional survey included adults who previously used at least 1 anti-obesity medication and conducted in 3 general hospitals in Jeddah, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (KSA) during June 2025 to February 2026. The questionnaire was created after reviewing the current evidence and validated the contents. It included questions on demographic and clinical information, medication use patterns, perceptions, and safety experiences. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics.Results:A total of 365 participants (median age: 41 years; 55.3% female) included, with a median body mass index (BMI) of 29.59 kg/m2 and 48.6% were obese. Current medication use was 63.3%, associated with diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, family obesity history, and higher BMI. Duration of use was statistically significant (p = 0.001), with tirzepatide used for longer durations and semaglutide for shorter durations. Perceived safety was significantly associated with the duration of use (p = 0.001). Despite adverse effects reporting, many questioned effectiveness, information adequacy and safety.Conclusion:Substantial gaps in perceived effectiveness, safety confidence, affordability, and therapy continuity of GLP-1 receptor. This highlights need for structured education, improved access, and system interventions to improve anti-obesity pharmacotherapy adherence.

Introduction

Obesity is a complex, long-term, and recurrent metabolic disorder and a leading determinant of cardiometabolic morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. In KSA, obesity constitutes a major public health challenge. The Saudi Arabian national data from the General Authority for Statistics (2024) report that 23.1% and 45.1% of individuals aged 15 years and older are obese and overweight [2]. The current international consensus recognizes obesity as a disease requiring long-term, structured management rather than short-term weight loss attempt. Clinical guidelines recommend pharmacotherapy for individuals with obesity-related comorbidities who fail to achieve clinically meaningful weight loss with lifestyle intervention alone [3].

Glucagon-like peptide-1(GLP-1) receptor agonists were first licensed in 2005 for the management of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) based on their capacity to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion and suppress glucagon release [3,4]. Subsequent evidence demonstrating central appetite regulation, increased satiety, delayed gastric emptying, and clinically meaningful weight reduction resulted in the approval of liraglutide 3.0 milligrams (mg) for chronic weight management in 2015 [4]. This was followed by the approval of semaglutide 2.4mg and, more recently, tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and GLP-1 receptor agonist, both of which showed substantial and sustained weight loss in phase III randomized controlled trials [3,5,6]. Emerging long-acting incretin-based agents with enhanced receptor selectivity are currently in advanced clinical development, further broadening the pharmacological options for obesity management. Recently, an oral formulation of semaglutide has been approved, expanding access to anti-obesity pharmacotherapy and facilitating broader global utilization of GLP-1-based treatment [7].

In addition to glycemic control and weight reduction, large cardiovascular outcome trials and high-quality meta-analyses have demonstrated that GLP-1-based therapies reduce major adverse cardiovascular events and improve cardiometabolic risk profiles [8,9,10]. Additional evidence supports benefits in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, including delays in progression to type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), reductions in systolic blood pressure and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and potential therapeutic effects in chronic kidney disease and other obesity-related medical conditions [8,9,10].

However, GLP-1 receptor agonists exhibit a predictable, dose-dependent safety profile, with gastrointestinal adverse effects such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and constipation being the most common and potentially limiting dose escalation and long-term adherence [5,6,11,12]. Meta-analyses of randomized trials report a modest but significant increase in gallbladder-related events compared with placebo, while regulatory labeling notes rare risks, including pancreatitis, and contraindications in patients with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma [12,13,14].

Globally, real-world evidence indicates a rapid expansion in the use of semaglutide and other anti-obesity medications, with Saudi tertiary care data confirming their effectiveness and acceptable short-term safety in routine practice [15,16]. However, other studies highlight substantial barriers to initiation and continuation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, including high cost, limited insurance coverage, supply shortages, and adverse effects, with 42.7% of users reporting interruption of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy due to shortages, cost, or adverse effects [16,17].

Given the high prevalence of obesity in KSA and the accelerating uptake of incretin-based therapies worldwide, further evaluation of patient-centered outcomes is critical. Assessing perceived effectiveness, tolerability, safety concerns, accessibility, and reasons for discontinuation is essential to improve adherence, guide policy decisions, and ensure sustainable integration of modern anti-obesity therapy [16,17].

This aim of this study is to evaluate the perceptions, experiences, and safety concerns of patients regarding the use of licensed glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists for obesity management in routine clinical practice.

Methods

This investigation employed a cross-sectional survey design and was conducted at 3 major public hospitals in Jeddah, KSA: King Fahad Hospital, East Jeddah Hospital, and King Abdullah Medical Complex. During the period between June 2025 to February 2026. This cross-sectional observational study was conducted and reported in accordance with the statement “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).” All applicable items from the STROBE checklist were addressed to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting.

Arabic speaking adults with prior exposure to at least 1 licensed anti-obesity medication were considered eligible to be included in the study. Potential participants were identified in outpatient clinics, provided with study information, and enrolled after obtaining informed consent. Non-Arabic speakers and special needs groups were excluded to ensure that participants could adequately understand and respond to the study items and questions.

The questionnaire was developed after reviewing relevant scientific literature and receiving expert input from clinical pharmacology and obesity treatment specialists. The questionnaire included structured Likert scale items, ranking questions, and open-ended responses to capture demographic and clinical data, treatment patterns, perceived effectiveness, adverse events, and safety concerns. The research team evaluated content validity, and a pilot assessment involving 10 participants was conducted to ensure clarity and practicality.

Data analysis. Descriptive statistical techniques were applied to summarize demographic variables, treatment utilization patterns, and reported perceptions and safety-related experiences.

Ethical clearance was granted by the University of Jeddah Committee of Scientific and Medical Research (Registration No. HAP-02-J-094). This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant demographics. The questionnaire was completed by 365 participants from 3 general hospitals in Jeddah, KSA. The participants had a median age of 41 years (interquartile range (IQR) = 29); 55.3% were female and 44.7% were male. Most were Saudi nationals (72.6%, n = 231) living in the Western region (79.7%, n = 291). The sample had a median body mass index (BMI) of 29.59 kg/m2, and (59.7%, n = 218) reported a family history of obesity (Table 1). Overall, the participant group was predominantly middle-aged, female, Saudi, and resided in the Western region, with high rates of overweight and a family history of obesity.

Clinical profile, dispensing and use patterns of anti-obesity medications. The current use of anti-obesity medications was reported by 231 participants (63.3%). A history of type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in (52.3%, n = 191), hypertension in (41.6%, n = 152), hypercholesterolemia in (34.2%, n = 125), and polycystic ovarian disease in (20.3%, n = 74).

Doctor prescription was the most frequently used method of accessing anti-obesity medication, accounting for (59.7%, n = 218,) participants. Pharmacist dispensing was the second-most frequently listed method, accounting for (28.8%, n = 105) cases. Online orders accounted for approximately 8.2% of the methods reported by the users. Those who did not disclose the method or source of anti-obesity medication purchase accounted for 3.2%.

More than half of the participants strongly disagreed that anti-obesity medications were effective for weight loss (52.9%, n = 193,). Disagreement regarding improvement in overall health like blood glucose and blood pressure was reported by (38.1%, n = 139) participants. Improvements in body image and self-esteem were strongly agreed upon by the participants (24.7%, n = 90).

Only 23% participants reported uncertainty regarding medication safety. A substantial proportion (44.7%, n = 163) strongly disagreed that they received adequate information from reliable sources. Additionally, nearly half (49%, n = 179) of the respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed that the cost of medication was reasonable.

Social perceptions and motivating factors associated with the use of anti-obesity medication. Positive social and personal perceptions influenced the participants’ decision regarding medication use. Most participants reported positive perceptions regarding anti-obesity medications. Over half agreed or strongly agreed that their family and community have positive perceptions of weight loss and related medications (52.4%, n = 12), while (22.5%, n = 88) were undecided. Similarly, (57.1%, n = 245) agreed or strongly agreed that anti-obesity medications align with their personal values, whereas smaller proportions expressed disagreement (18.6%, n = 68) or uncertainty (14.2%, n = 52).

The top motivating factor for medication use was existing health conditions (34.2%, n = 125), which were significantly associated with current use (current users: 69.6%, n = 87, p = 0.022). Other factors were personal satisfaction with self-image (24.9%, n = 91) and physician recommendation (21.4%, n = 78). Finally, partner requests and expectations were the least reported factor cited by participants (3.3%, n = 12).

Effectiveness of anti-obesity medications was prioritized in influencing participants’ decision to use therapy, reported by 277 participants (75.9%). This was followed by concerns about adverse effects (42.5%), physician recommendation (35.3%), and medication availability (27.9%). Cost of anti-obesity medications was also considered, though less frequently, reported by 88 participants (24.1%) (multiple responses allowed).

Discussion

This multicenter investigation provides contemporary real-world evidence demonstrating that GLP-1 receptor agonist-based pharmacological therapy for obesity has been widely implemented in KSA, as all 365 participants reported current or prior exposure to at least one licensed anti-obesity medication. The findings reveal several important patterns. Medication use was strongly linked to cardiometabolic burden, including diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, higher BMI class, and family history of obesity, indicating that pharmacotherapy is primarily adopted as a metabolic risk reduction strategy rather than for cosmetic reasons, consistent with current therapeutic guidelines positioning obesity as a chronic cardiometabolic disease requiring medical management [18,19]. A significant discordance was observed between measured and perceived BMI, with objective obesity prevalence substantially exceeding self-perception, a phenomenon previously described in epidemiological studies of weight misclassification [20,21]. Although incretin-based agents, such as tirzepatide and semaglutide, were frequently used, perceptions of effectiveness and safety were mixed, and early duration of therapy was associated with lower safety confidence. Concerns regarding affordability and insufficient information were also noted. Together, these results illustrate the interaction between clinical risk, perception, treatment experience, and structural factors in shaping pharmacotherapy for real-world obesity [22].

The strong association between medication use and cardiometabolic comorbidity supports the contemporary therapeutic paradigm of treating obesity as a chronic metabolic disease requiring risk-oriented intervention [18, 19]. Large-scale randomized trials have demonstrated that semaglutide and tirzepatide produce clinically meaningful weight reduction and cardiometabolic improvements, reinforcing their role in high-risk populations [5,6]. The pattern observed in this study, particularly among individuals with severe obesity, aligns with this evolving evidence base and suggests that prescribing practices reflect medical indication rather than aesthetic motivation [23].

The discrepancy between actual and perceived obesity is a clinically significant concern. Misperception of weight status has been associated with delayed preventive action and reduced engagement in sustained behavioral change [20,21]. Under-recognition of obesity severity may weaken motivation for adherence and long-term lifestyle modification even within a treated population [20,24]. Therefore, improving patients’ understanding of BMI classification and its associated health risks may be essential to optimize pharmacological outcomes [20,24].

Despite robust clinical trial data demonstrating the efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonists, more than half of the participants expressed skepticism regarding their effectiveness [5,6]. This divergence between trial efficacy and patient perception may reflect unrealistic expectations, insufficient counseling regarding expected weight trajectory, early discontinuation, or inadequate dose escalation [22,24]. Real-world persistence rates for anti-obesity medications are frequently lower than those observed in controlled studies, emphasizing the importance of aligning expectations and providing structured follow-up [22].

Perceived medication safety was significantly associated with therapy duration in this study. Individuals with less than 1 month of exposure reported lower safety confidence than those treated for longer periods. Gastrointestinal symptoms are common during the initiation of incretin-based therapy and may shape early impressions of tolerability [25]. Transient adverse effects may be interpreted as major safety concerns without adequate anticipatory guidance, contributing to premature discontinuation [17,25,26]. With regard to the considerable proportion of adverse effects, namely, depressed mood and suicidal thoughts reported by more than 27%, this proportion should be treated with caution. Evidence from previous studies and from post-marketing databases shows that suicidal events are rare but serious [12,27]. Our findings could be confounded by the preexisting mental health burden of obesity and diabetes or could be related to selection bias, which is common in observational studies. Furthermore, the findings underscore the need for systematic counseling and pharmacovigilance.

Affordability has emerged as a major barrier. Given that pharmacotherapy for obesity requires sustained administration to maintain benefit, economic considerations are central to treatment continuity. Notably, cost was a low-priority consideration for participants when selecting anti-obesity pharmacotherapy and was reported to be unreasonable by approximately half of the participants. However, a study conducted in Tabuk City showed that more than 50% of GLP-1 receptor agonist discontinuation was attributed to cost and/or shortage of supply [17]. International analyses have identified cost as a key determinant of long-term adherence to metabolic therapies for chronic diseases [28,29]. Motivational patterns further contextualize treatment engagement. In this study, existing health conditions were identified as the dominant driver of medication use. These findings are consistent with the evidence that individuals with cardiometabolic disease are more likely to pursue pharmacological intervention [13,20,30].

Study strengths and limitations. This study has several strengths. Recruitment from multiple hospital settings enhances contextual validity and reflects contemporary prescribing practice. The integration of clinical characteristics, medication access patterns, perceptual variables, safety attitudes, and motivational determinants provides a multidimensional understanding of anti-obesity pharmacotherapy. The direct comparison between measured and perceived BMI adds clinically relevant insight into weight awareness. Evaluation of safety perception by therapy duration provides novel evidence of temporal influences on patient confidence. Certain limitations must be acknowledged. The study findings reflect subjective patient-reported experiences rather than clinically validated outcomes, which may introduce reporting bias and limit causal interpretation. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation [30]. Self-reported responses may introduce recall or social desirability bias. Furthermore, hospital-based recruitment enriched for individuals with established cardiometabolic disease, constraining external validity; safety perceptions, tolerability, and treatment responses may not be generalizable to lifestyle users in primary or private care settings. Detailed information regarding dosage, adherence behavior, reasons for discontinuation, and socioeconomic status was not collected. Adverse effects and longitudinal metabolic outcomes were not independently verified.

A more appropriate interpretation is an expectation–adherence gap rather than a certain therapeutic failure. Although dissatisfaction with the effectiveness of GLP-1 receptor agonists was common, continued use of tirzepatide beyond 6 months (23.9%, n = 26) indicates sustained clinical engagement. This suggests that perceived ineffectiveness may reflect unmet expectations regarding the pace or magnitude of weight loss; however, it may also partly reflect variable individual responses, tolerability, and cost [31]. Real-world evidence indicates that persistence is multifactorial rather than based solely on perceived efficacy [31]. Accordingly, perceived ineffectiveness should be interpreted cautiously within the broader context of chronic treatment adherence.

Although depressive symptoms and suicidal thoughts were reported in a notable proportion of participants, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of a confirmed causal psychiatric effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists. Pharmacovigilance data from EudraVigilance Tobaiqy and Elkout [32], 2024 identified 372 (1.18%) psychiatric reports among 31,444 total reports, including rare severe outcomes; however, these are signal-generating and do not establish causality.

An apparently high prevalence of adverse effects in the present study may partly reflect the method of ascertainment. Unlike clinical trials and regulatory evaluations, which assess treatment-emergent and medically adjudicated adverse events, our study captured patient-reported symptoms in a cross-sectional survey. Patient-reported methods are known to detect more frequent and more severe subjective symptoms than clinician-based reporting [33, 34]. In addition, prompted survey responses may increase attribution of nonspecific symptoms to treatment, while public, media, and social-media attention may amplify expectation-driven reporting [33,34]. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting patient-perceived symptom burden rather than confirmed drug-attributable toxicity.

Future research should include prospective longitudinal studies to determine whether early safety perception predicts sustained adherence and weight reduction. Regional pharmacovigilance initiatives are necessary to characterize the real-world safety outcomes of incretin-based therapies in the Middle Eastern population. Qualitative investigations exploring cultural beliefs and treatment expectations may clarify effectiveness skepticism. Interventional studies evaluating structured counseling at the initiation of therapy could determine whether improved alignment of expectations enhances persistence. Comprehensive cost-effectiveness analyses are required to inform reimbursement strategies and ensure sustainable access in countries with a high obesity burden.

In conclusion, GLP-1 receptor agonist–based anti-obesity therapy was widely utilized across 3 general hospitals in Jeddah, KSA, with greater use observed in higher BMI categories and among individuals with cardiometabolic comorbidities, indicating appropriately targeted prescribing. Adverse effects were frequently reported, with evidence of active reporting to healthcare providers. However, these reported events should be interpreted with caution, as causality was not established, and the findings reflect patient-reported symptoms rather than confirmed drug-related adverse events. Safety perceptions remained variable and appeared to evolve with duration of use, reflecting ongoing uncertainty in patient experience. Perceived effectiveness was limited, accompanied by notable deficiencies in access to reliable information and persistent concerns regarding treatment affordability. Nevertheless, perceived ineffectiveness should be interpreted cautiously within the broader context of chronic treatment adherence.

Overall, while prescribing aligns with clinical need, real-world effectiveness and sustainability are limited by gaps in patient understanding, variable risk perception, and structural barriers. Addressing these is essential to optimize adherence, compliance and outcomes. Importantly, beyond weight reduction, GLP-1 receptor agonists provide established cardiovascular benefits, including reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events, supporting their role as disease-modifying therapies.
