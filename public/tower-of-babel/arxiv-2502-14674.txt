Abstract
. The construction of permutation trinomials of the form X r ​ ( X α ⁡ ( 2 m − 1 ) + X β ⁡ ( 2 m − 1 ) + 1 ) X^{r}(X^{\alpha(2^{m}-1)}+X^{\beta(2^{m}-1)}+1) over 𝔽 2 2 ​ m \mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}} , where m , r ​ and ​ α > β m,~r\text{ and }\alpha>\beta are positive integers, is an active area of research. Several classes of permutation trinomials with fixed values of α \alpha , β \beta and r r have been studied. Here, we construct three new classes of permutation trinomials with ( α , β , r ) ∈ { ( 7 , 5 , 7 ) , ( 8 , 6 , 9 ) , ( 10 , 4 , 11 ) } (\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(7,5,7),(8,6,9),(10,4,11)\} over 𝔽 2 2 ​ m \mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}} . We also analyze the quasi-multiplicative equivalence of the newly obtained classes of permutation trinomials to both the existing ones and to each other. Additionally, we prove the nonexistence of a class of permutation trinomials over 𝔽 2 2 ​ m \mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}} of the same type for r = 9 r=9 , α = 7 \alpha=7 , and β = 3 \beta=3 when m > 3 m>3 . Furthermore, we provide a proof for a conjecture on the quasi-multiplicative equivalence of two classes of permutation trinomials, as proposed by Yadav, Gupta, Singh, and Yadav (2024).

1.
Introduction

Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field with
q
elements. Throughout this paper, we assume
q=2^{m}
, where
m
is a positive integer, unless stated otherwise. We denote by
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
the multiplicative cyclic group of non-zero elements of the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and by
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
the ring of polynomials in one variable
X
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A polynomial
f(X)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
is called a permutation polynomial of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if its associated mapping
X\mapsto f(X)
is a permutation of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Permutation polynomials over finite fields have been a significant area of research for many years due to their broad applications in various fields of engineering and mathematics, such as cryptography, coding theory, and combinatorial design theory. For a survey of recent developments and contributions in this area, the reader is referred to
[
7
,
15
,
21
]
and the references therein.

There has been significant interest in permutation polynomials, particularly those with nice algebraic forms and additional desirable properties. While a random permutation polynomial can be readily constructed using Lagrange interpolation, such permutation polynomials rarely exhibit a concise or structured form. Among permutation polynomials, monomials are the simplest and can be easily classified. In contrast, the classification of permutation binomials and trinomials presents a greater challenge. For explicit constructions of permutation binomials and trinomials, the reader is referred to
[
5
,
6
,
8
,
25
,
22
,
13
,
7
,
14
,
11
,
12
,
16
,
26
]
.

It is well known that permutation binomials with identity coefficients do not exist over finite fields of even characteristic. Motivated by this, several researchers have investigated classes of permutation trinomials of the form
X^{r}(X^{\alpha(q-1)}+X^{\beta(q-1)}+1)
over finite fields of even characteristic; see, for example,
[
6
,
23
,
14
,
25
]
and the references therein. In 2016, Gupta and Sharma
[
6
]
introduced four new classes of such permutation trinomials with
(\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(3,2,2),(3,1,4),(4,3,3),(4,1,5)\}
. Moroever, additional classes of permutation trinomials of the same form were proposed in
[
14
,
25
]
for
(\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(3,1,3),(3,2,3),(3,1,4),(4,3,5),(5,1,4),(5,2,5),(5,4,5),(6,2,5)\}
. Subsequently, Yadav, Gupta, Singh, and Yadav
[
23
]
identified three new classes of permutation trinomials of this type with
(\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(6,5,5),(6,1,7),(6,4,7)\}
. In this paper, we extend this investigation by exploring additional classes of permutation trinomials of this form with
(\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(7,5,7),(8,6,9),(10,4,11)\}
.

In this work, the problem of analyzing the permutation behavior of the proposed trinomial is first converted into studying a polynomial in two variables over the unit circle. Then, an algorithmic approach is applied to factorize this polynomial, as its factors play a key role in determining whether the proposed trinomials exhibit permutation behavior. On the other hand, to establish the nonexistence of a certain class of permutation trinomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, we leverage the connection between permutation polynomials and algebraic curves over finite fields. Specifically, it turns out that the Hasse-Weil bound serves as a powerful tool for proving the nonexistence of a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
when
q
is sufficiently large. However, a significant technical challenge in applying the Hasse-Weil bound lies in proving the absolute irreducibility of the algebraic curve involved. Several researchers
[
3
,
4
,
9
,
10
]
have demonstrated the nonexistence of permutation polynomials by showing that the corresponding curve is absolutely irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Here, we demonstrate the absolute irreducibility of a specific curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and, using the Hasse-Weil bound, identify a class of trinomials that does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

A permutation polynomial
f(X)
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
remains a permutation polynomial when transformed into
af(bX+c)+d
for
a,b\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
and
c,d\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. While this transformation preserves the degree of the polynomial, it may alter the number of monomials. To overcome this limitation, Wu, Yuan, Ding, and Ma
[
22
]
introduced the concept of Quasi-Multiplicative (QM) equivalence, which ensures that the number of monomials remains unchanged. Identifying QM equivalence between two classes of permutation polynomials is a challenging problem. In this paper, we verify that the proposed trinomials are QM inequivalent both among themselves and to those previously known in the literature. In 2024, Yadav, Gupta, Singh, and Yadav
[
23
]
proposed a conjecture regarding the QM equivalence of two classes of permutation trinomials over quadratic extensions of finite fields with arbitrary characteristic. We provide a different yet elementary proof of their conjecture, which appears to have been resolved by Yadav, Singh, and Gupta
[
24
]
.

We shall now give the structure of the paper. We first recall some results in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we present three new classes of permutation trinomials over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Section
4
deals with the QM equivalence of the permutation trinomials introduced in this paper to known ones, confirming that the classes we obtain are indeed new. Additionally, in Section
5
, we show that a family of trinomials
X^{9}(X^{7(q-1)}+X^{3(q-1)}+1)
does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}
for
m>3
. Furthermore, in Section
6
, we give a proof of the conjecture on the QM equivalence of trinomials stated in
[
23
]
. Finally, we conclude the paper in Section
7
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some results to be used later in subsequent sections. Throughout the paper, we denote by

\mbox{Tr}_{m}^{n}
the (relative) trace function from
\mathbb{F}_{p^{n}}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{p^{m}}
, i.e.,
\mbox{Tr}_{m}^{n}(X)=\sum^{\frac{n-m}{m}}_{i=0}X^{p^{mi}},
where
m
and
n
are positive integers with
m\mid n
, and
p
is a prime number.

Note that the results stated below in this section are true for an arbitrary prime power
q=p^{n}
, where
n
is an integer and
p
is a prime number. We now present a well-known criterion studied in various forms by several authors; for instance, Wan-Lidl
[
19
]
, Park-Lee
[
17
]
, Akbary-Wang
[
1
]
, Wang
[
20
]
, and Zieve
[
27
]
. This method is particularly helpful for determining whether a polynomial of the form
X^{r}h(X^{s})
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The criterion is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
and
r,s
are positive integers such that
s\mid(q-1)
. Then
f(X)=X^{r}h(X^{s})
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if the following two conditions

(1)

\gcd(r,s)=1
,

(2)

X^{r}({h(X)})^{s}
permutes
\mu_{\frac{q-1}{s}}
,

are satisfied, where
\mu_{\frac{q-1}{s}}=\{a\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}\mid a^{\frac{q-1}{s}}=1\}
is the unit circle of order
\frac{q-1}{s}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

The special case of the above lemma for the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
and
s=q-1
gives the following result, which we use to determine whether the new families of trinomials considered in this paper permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
h(X)\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[X]
,
f(X)=X^{r}h(X^{q-1})
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if the following two conditions hold

(1)

\gcd(r,q-1)=1
,

(2)

g(X)=X^{r}(h(X))^{q-1}
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
.

The following lemma presents a result by Yadav, Gupta, Singh, and Yadav
[
23
]
, which we use throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
23
, Lemma 2.2]

Let
q
be any prime power co-prime to 3 and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
\alpha>\beta
. Then, the polynomials
X^{\alpha}+X^{\beta}+1
and
X^{\alpha}+X^{(\alpha-\beta)}+1
have no roots in
\mu_{q+1}
if any one of the following conditions holds

(1)

\gcd(\alpha+\beta,q+1)=1
,

(2)

\gcd(|\alpha-2\beta|,q+1)=1
,

(3)

\gcd(2\alpha-\beta,q+1)=1
,

(4)

\gcd(3,q+1)=1
,

where
|\cdot|
denotes the usual absolute value.

We also recall the Chinese Remainder Theorem, which will be needed later.

Lemma 2.4

(Chinese Remainder Theorem (CRT))
.

Let
a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{t}
be any
t
integers, and let
m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{t}
be pairwise coprime positive integers. Then, there exists an integer
X
such that

X\equiv a_{i}\pmod{m_{i}},\quad\text{for }i=1,2,\dots,t.

Moreover, the solution
X
is unique modulo
M=m_{1}m_{2}\cdots m_{t}
.

3.
Three new classes of permutation trinomials

In this section, we introduce three new classes of permutation trinomials over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. We begin with the following lemma, which is used to determine our first family of permutation trinomials.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m
be a positive integer such that
\gcd(5,m)=1
. Let
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{10m}}
be such that

b^{10}+b^{6}+b^{5}+b^{3}+b^{2}+b+1=0.
Then, we have

\operatorname{Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(b)=\operatorname{Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(b^{33})=0.

Proof.

Since
\gcd(5,m)=1
and
b
satisfies the irreducible polynomial
X^{10}+X^{6}+X^{5}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X+1
over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, it follows that
b\not\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}
and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{10}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2^{10m}}
. In addition, we can write
m=5k+r
for some integers
k\geq 1
and
0<r<5
. This implies that

(3.1)

{}\operatorname{Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(b)=b+b^{2^{2r}}+b^{2^{4r}}+b^{2^{{6r}}}+b^{2^{{8r}}}=b+b^{2^{2}}+b^{2^{4}}+b^{2^{6}}+b^{2^{8}}.

Now, using the relations
b^{10}+b^{6}+b^{5}+b^{3}+b^{2}+b+1=0
and
b^{2^{10}}=b
, we compute

\begin{array}[]{l}b^{2^{4}}=b+b^{5}+b^{7}+b^{9},\\
b^{2^{6}}=b^{2}+b^{4}+b^{5}+b^{6}+b^{7}+b^{9},\\
b^{2^{8}}=b^{2}+b^{6}.\end{array}

Substituting the obtained values of
b^{2^{i}}
for
i\in\{4,6,8\}
in Equation (
3.1
), we get

\operatorname{Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(b)=b+b^{2^{2}}+b^{2^{4}}+b^{2^{6}}+b^{2^{8}}=0.

Consequently, we get

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(b^{33})

\displaystyle={bb^{2^{5}}+{(bb^{2^{5}})}^{2^{2}}+{(bb^{2^{5}})}^{2^{4}}+{(bb^{2^{5}})}^{2^{6}}+{(bb^{2^{5}})}^{2^{8}}}

\displaystyle=bb^{2^{5}}+b^{2^{2}}b^{2^{7}}+b^{2^{4}}b^{2^{9}}+b^{2^{6}}b^{2}+b^{2^{8}}b^{2^{3}}=0.

∎

We now introduce our first class of permutation trinomials, where
r=11
,
\alpha=10
and
\beta=4
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
q=2^{m}
. Then, the trinomial
F_{1}(X)=X^{11}\left(X^{10(q-1)}+X^{4(q-1)}+1\right)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{5}
.

Proof.

We first assume that
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{5}
. From Lemma
2.2
, for
F_{1}
to be a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, it is sufficient to show that
\gcd(11,q-1)=1
and
G_{1}(X)=X^{11}(X^{10}+X^{4}+1)^{q-1}
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
. Since
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{5}
,
it is straightforward to observe that
\gcd(11,q-1)=1
.
Additionally, by Lemma
2.3
, the polynomial
X^{10}+X^{4}+1
has no roots in
\mu_{q+1}
. Therefore, we can write
G_{1}(X)=\dfrac{X^{11}+X^{7}+X}{X^{10}+X^{4}+1}
over
\mu_{q+1}
. Now, to prove that
G_{1}(X)
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
, we show that
G_{1}(X)
is injective on
\mu_{q+1}
. Suppose that
G_{1}(X)=G_{1}(Y)
for some
X,Y\in\mu_{q+1}
. This leads us to the following equation

(X^{11}+X^{7}+X)(Y^{10}+Y^{4}+1)+(Y^{11}+Y^{7}+Y)(X^{10}+X^{4}+1)=0.

We denote the left-hand side of the above equation as
f(X,Y)
. By using SageMath, we get that
f(X,Y)
can be factored into the product of six polynomials over the quintic extension
\mathbb{F}_{(q^{2})^{5}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
as following

f(X,Y)=(X+Y)f_{1}(X,Y)f_{2}(X,Y)f_{3}(X,Y)f_{4}(X,Y)f_{5}(X,Y),\mbox{~~where}

\displaystyle f_{1}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{33}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{528}XY+1,

\displaystyle f_{2}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{66}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{33}XY+1,

\displaystyle f_{3}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{132}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{66}XY+1,

\displaystyle f_{4}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{264}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{132}XY+1,

\displaystyle f_{5}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{528}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{264}XY+1,

and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{10}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q^{10}}
is a
(2^{10}-1)
th primitive root of unity satisfying
X^{10}+X^{6}+X^{5}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X+1
. Since
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{5}
, it follows that
b\not\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
but
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{10}}
.

Clearly,
f(X,Y)=0
if and only if either
X=Y
or
f_{i}(X,Y)=0
for some
1\leq i\leq 5
in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{10}}
. First, suppose that
f_{1}(X,Y)=0
, which implies
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(f_{1}(X,Y))=0
. From Lemma
3.1
, we obtain
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{10m}(f_{1}(X,Y))=X^{2}Y^{2}+1
. Thus, we have
X^{2}Y^{2}+1=(XY+1)^{2}=0
i.e.,
XY=1
. Substituting
XY=1
or
X=\frac{1}{Y}
into the expression for
f_{1}(X,Y)
, we get
Y=X^{q}
and this will further imply that
b^{495}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}.
However, since the multiplicative order of
b^{495}
in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{10}}^{*}
is
31
, and
5
is the least positive integer such that
q^{5}\equiv 1\pmod{31}
, we get that
b^{495}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{5}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
, a contradiction. This disproves our assumption that
f_{1}(X,Y)=0
for every
X,Y\in\mu_{q+1}
. Similarly, one can verify that
f_{i}(X,Y)\neq 0
for
X,Y\in\mu_{q+1}
where
i\in\{2,3,4,5\}
. Therefore, the only possibility for
f(X,Y)=0
is when
X+Y=0
, which in turn implies that
G_{1}(X)
is injective on
\mu_{q+1}
.

Conversely, let
F_{1}(X)
permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
for some
m\equiv 0\pmod{5}
, or equivalently, for
m=5k
where
k
is a positive integer. If
k
is even, then
\gcd(11,2^{m}-1)\neq 1
. By Lemma
2.2
this contradiction the assumption that
F_{1}(X)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Next, if
k
is odd, we have
q+1\equiv 0\pmod{11}
and
q+1\equiv 0\pmod{3}
. In this case, we can choose an element
\alpha\in\mu_{11}\subset\mu_{q+1}
and then verify that
G_{1}(\alpha)\in\mu_{3}\subset\mu_{q+1}
for each
\alpha\in\mu_{11}
. Therefore, we can conclude that
G_{1}(X)
does not permute
\mu_{q+1}
. Consequently, by Lemma
2.2
, we get a contradiction to the assumption that
F_{1}(X)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
∎

Next, we present another class of permutation trinomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
with
r=9,\alpha=8
and
\beta=6
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
q=2^{m}
, then the trinomial
F_{2}(X)=X^{9}(X^{8(q-1)}+X^{6(q-1)}+1)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
m
is odd.

Proof.

It is known from Lemma
2.2
that
F_{2}(X)
permutes the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
\gcd(9,q-1)=1
and
G_{2}(X)=X^{9}(X^{8}+X^{6}+1)^{q-1}
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
. We first assume that
m
is odd which ensures
\gcd(9,q-1)=1
. Thus, it suffices to show that
G_{2}(X)
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
. It is straightforward to verify that if
X\in\mu_{q+1}
, then
G_{2}(X)
also belongs to the unit circle
\mu_{q+1}
. Since, from Lemma
2.3
,
X^{8}+X^{6}+1
has no roots in
\mu_{q+1}
, we can express
G_{2}(X)
as
G_{2}(X)=\dfrac{X^{9}+X^{3}+X}{X^{8}+X^{6}+1}
over
\mu_{q+1}
. Now, we proceed as in Theorem
3.2
to establish the injectivity of
G_{2}(X)
on
\mu_{q+1}
.
Let
G_{2}(X)=G_{2}(Y)
for some
X,Y\in\mu_{q+1}
. Then, we obtain the following equation

\displaystyle X^{9}Y^{8}+X^{8}Y^{9}+X^{9}Y^{6}+X^{6}Y^{9}+X^{3}Y^{8}+X^{8}Y^{3}+X^{3}Y^{6}+X^{6}Y^{3}

\displaystyle\quad+XY^{8}+X^{8}Y+XY^{6}+X^{6}Y+X^{9}+Y^{9}+X^{3}+Y^{3}+X+Y=0.

If
f(X,Y)
denotes the left-hand side of the above equation, then using SageMath,
f(X,Y)
can be factored as the product of five polynomials over quadratic extension
\mathbb{F}_{(q^{2})^{2}}
of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
as follows

\displaystyle f(X,Y)=(X+Y)f_{1}(X,Y)f_{2}(X,Y)f_{3}(X,Y)f_{4}(X,Y),

where
f_{i}(X,Y)=X^{2}Y^{2}+b^{2^{i-1}}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+b^{2^{i+2}}XY+1\mbox{~for~}1\leq i\leq 4,
and
b\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{4}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q^{4}}
is a fifteenth primitive root of unity satisfying
b^{4}+b^{3}+1=0
. Since
m
is odd, we have
b\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{4}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

It is evident that
f(X,Y)=0
if and only if either
X=Y
or
f_{i}(X,Y)=0
for any
1\leq i\leq 4

\text{ in }\mathbb{F}_{q^{4}}
. Let us first consider
f_{1}(X,Y)=0
. This implies that

{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{1}(X,Y))={\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(b)(X^{2}+Y^{2})+{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(b^{8})XY=0.

Since
m
is odd and
2^{2m}\equiv 4\pmod{15}
, we get
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(b)=b^{5}
. Substituting the value of
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(b)
in the expression for
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{1}(X,Y))
, we obtain
X^{2}+Y^{2}+b^{5}(XY)=0
. Raising
X^{2}+Y^{2}+b^{5}(XY)=0
to the powers
q+1
and
2
, we get the equations
(X^{2}+Y^{2})^{2}+b^{5q+5}(XY)^{2}=0
and
(X^{2}+Y^{2})^{2}+b^{10}(XY)^{2}=0
, respectively. This further renders
b^{5q+5}=b^{10}
, or equivalently,
b^{5}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. However, since
b
is the fifteenth primitive root of unity, the multiplicative order of
b^{5}
is three.
As
m
is odd, it follows that
b^{5}=1
, which contradicts the fact that
b
is a primitive fifteenth root of unity. This, in turn, contradicts the assumption that
f_{1}(X,Y)=0
for every
X,Y\in\mu_{q+1}
. A similar approach can be applied to
f_{3}(X,Y)
since
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{1}(X,Y))={\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{3}(X,Y))
. Moreover, upon computing
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{2}(X,Y))=0
(or, respectively,
{\rm Tr}_{2m}^{4m}(f_{4}(X,Y))=0
), we obtain
b^{5}(X^{2}+Y^{2})+XY=0
. Hence, using similar arguments as those applied for
f_{1}(X,Y)
, we can show that
f_{2}(X,Y)\neq 0
and
f_{4}(X,Y)\neq 0
. Therefore, we conclude that
X=Y
, or equivalently,
G_{2}(X)
is injective on
\mu_{q+1}
.

For the converse part, suppose
m
is an even positive integer. In this case, we get
2^{m}\equiv 1\pmod{3}
, and therefore
\gcd(9,2^{m}-1)\geq 3
. Thus, from Lemma
2.2
, we get that
F_{2}(X)
does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

∎

In the following theorem, we provide our third class of permutation trinomials over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
q=2^{m}
, then the trinomial
F_{3}(X)=X^{7}(X^{7(q-1)}+X^{5(q-1)}+1)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
m
is even and
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{3}
.

A natural question when studying permutation polynomials is whether the proposed classes of permutation polynomials are new or not, and QM equivalence plays an important role in addressing this question. However, determining QM equivalence between two permutation polynomials is not so easy, since there is no general method for this evaluation. We recall the following definition of QM equivalence introduced by Wu, Yuan, Ding and Ma
[
22
]
.

Definition 4.1
.

Two permutation polynomials
f(X)
and
g(X)
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[X]
are called quasi-multiplicative (QM) equivalent if there exists an integer

1\leq d<q-1
with
\gcd(d,q-1)=1
and
f(X)=\alpha g(\beta X^{d})
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
.

In this section, we show that the classes of permutation trinomials proposed in this paper are not QM equivalent to each other. Furthermore, we verify that our proposed classes of permutation trinomials are not QM equivalent to the known permutation trinomials mentioned in Table
1
. Now, we recall the strategy introduced in
[
18
]
to show the QM inequivalence between two permutation trinomials, which we also used in this paper.
Let
q=2^{m}
and
F(X)=X^{r}(X^{\alpha(q-1)}+X^{\beta(q-1)}+1)
be a permutation trinomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Suppose
G(X)=aX^{A}+bX^{B}+cX^{C}
is any other permutation trinomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Then, to show that
F(X)
and
G(X)
are not QM equivalent over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, we proceed by the following two-steps approach.

Step 1.
Show that there does not exist any positive integer
1\leq d<q^{2}-1
such that
\gcd(d,q^{2}-1)=1
and
\{r,r+\alpha(q-1),r+\beta(q-1)\}_{\pmod{q^{2}-1}}=\{Ad,Bd,Cd\}_{\pmod{q^{2}-1}}
, where
A_{\pmod{q^{2}-1}}=\{a\pmod{q^{2}-1}\mid a\in A\}
.

Step 2.
If the condition in Step 1 does not hold, then compare the coefficients of
F(X)
with
A_{1}G(A_{2}X^{d})
and show that there does not exist any
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
such that

.

If both steps fail, we conclude that
F(X)
and

are QM equivalent, denoted as

5.
Nonexistence of a class of permutation trinomials

In this section, we give a class of trinomials that does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
. Let
F(X,Y)
define an algebraic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. A rational point
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a point on this curve such that
F(X,Y)=0
. The collection of all rational points of
F(X,Y)
is given by the following set

V_{\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}}(F)=\{(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}\mid F(X,Y)=0\}.

We denote by
\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
, the projective plane over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. For a homogeneous polynomial

f(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y,Z]
, the set of zeros of
f
in
\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
is defined as

V_{\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}(f)=\{(X,Y,Z)\in\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})\mid f(X,Y,Z)=0\}.

In addition, a polynomial
F(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
is said to be absolutely irreducible if it is irreducible in
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[X,Y]
, where
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
denotes the algebraic closure of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
We use the following version of the Hasse-Weil theorem by Aubry and Perret in our result.

Lemma 5.1
.

(Aubry-Perret bound)
[
2
, Corollary 2.5]

Let
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
be an absolutely irreducible curve of degree
d
. Then

|\#V_{\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}(\mathcal{C})-(q+1)|\leq(d-1)(d-2)q^{1/2},

where
|\cdot|
denotes the usual absolute value.

In the following lemma, we show the absolute irreducibility of a curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which, together with Lemma
5.1
, give us a class of trinomials that does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
H(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
be the polynomial
X^{16}+AX^{8}+(B+1)X^{4}+AX^{2}+BX+A
, where
A=Y^{8}+Y^{4}+Y^{3}+Y^{2}+Y
and
B=Y^{8}+Y^{7}+Y^{4}+Y^{2}
. Then,
H(X,Y)
is absolutely irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.

Proof.

Notice that
H(X,Y)
can be considered as a polynomial in variable
X
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
. Moreover, it is primitive as the gcd of the coefficients is
1
. Hence, it is sufficient to show that
H(X,Y)
is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)
or equivalently,
\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(X,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)\right]=16
.

Let
Z=X^{4}+X
, then we can write
H(X,Y)
as
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A=0
, a polynomial in
Z
over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
. Therefore, in order to show
\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(X,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)\right]=16
, we need to prove that,
\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)\right]=4
and
\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(X,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y)\right]=4
. Furthermore, suppose that
Z=X^{4}+X=X^{4}+X^{2}+X^{2}+X
and
Z_{1}=X^{2}+X
.

\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y)
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z_{1},Y)
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(X,Y)
4
2
2

Thus, it is sufficient to show the following three claims.

(1)

\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)\right]=4
, that is,
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A
is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Y)
.

(2)

\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z_{1},Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y)\right]=2
, that is,
Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{1}+Z
is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z,Y)
.

(3)

\left[\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(X,Y):\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z_{1},Y)\right]=2
, that is,
X^{2}+X+Z_{1}
is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}(Z_{1},Y)
.

Claim 1.
Since gcd of coefficients of
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A
is one, it is primitive over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
, and hence it is enough to show that it is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
. Assume that
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A
factors as the product of two quadratics over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
, say

(5.1)

Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A=(Z^{2}+rZ+s)(Z^{2}+rZ+t).

Equating coefficients in the above expression, we get
A=r^{2}+s+t=st
and
B=r(s+t).
Therefore,
B=r(s+t)=r(A+r^{2})
and comparing degree in terms of
Y
on the both side, we get
8=\max\{\text{degree}(r)+8,3\text{degree}(r)\}
, we obtain a contradiction.

As a consequence, we conclude that
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A
can not be expressed as the product of two quadratic polynomials over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
. Similarly, it can be shown that
Z^{4}+AZ^{2}+BZ+A
can not be factored into a product of a degree-three irreducible polynomial and a linear factor over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Y]
.

Claim 2.
Using the fact that the gcd of coefficients of
Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{1}+Z\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Z,Y]
is one (
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Z,Y]
is an integral domain and indeed a UFD), it is primitive over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Z,Y]
. Hence, it is sufficient to show that it is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Z,Y]
. It is straightforward to verify that
Z_{1}^{2}+Z_{1}+Z
is irreducible over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[Z,Y]
.

Claim 3.
It follows along the same lines as Claim 2.

Therefore, we conclude that
H(X,Y)
is absolutely irreducible over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. ∎

Next, we present a class of trinomials

F(X)=X^{9}(X^{7(q-1)}+X^{3(q-1)}+1)

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
q=2^{m}
. Then, the trinomial
F(X)=X^{9}(X^{7(q-1)}+X^{3(q-1)}+1)
is not a permutation trinomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
for
m>3
.

Proof.

It is known from Lemma
2.2
that
F(X)
is a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
\gcd(9,q-1)=1
and
G(X)=X^{9}(X^{7}+X^{3}+1)^{q-1}
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
. Moreover, from Lemma
2.3
,
G(X)
can be written as
G(X)=\dfrac{X^{9}+X^{6}+X^{2}}{X^{7}+X^{3}+1}
over
\mu_{q+1}
. Thus,
F(X)
is a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if and only if
m
is odd and
G(X)=\dfrac{X^{9}+X^{6}+X^{2}}{X^{7}+X^{3}+1}
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
.

To prove that
F(X)
is not a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, we show that
G(X)
does not permute
\mu_{q+1}
. Define
\phi(X):=\dfrac{X+\omega^{2}}{X+\omega}
, a bijection from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mu_{q+1}\setminus\{1\}
, where
\omega\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
. Clearly,
G
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
if and only if
G\circ\phi
is a bijection from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mu_{q+1}\setminus\{1\}
. Alternatively,
G
permutes
\mu_{q+1}
if and only if
\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi
is a bijection from
\mathbb{F}_{q}
to
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Therefore, to show that
G
is not a bijection over
\mu_{q+1}
, we prove that
\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi
is not a bijection on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The map
G\circ\phi:\mathbb{F}_{q}\rightarrow\mu_{q+1}
is given by

G(\phi(X))=\dfrac{X^{9}+\omega^{2}X^{8}+X^{5}+\omega X^{4}+X^{3}+\omega X^{2}+\omega^{2}X+\omega^{2}}{X^{9}+\omega X^{8}+X^{5}+\omega^{2}X^{4}+X^{3}+\omega^{2}X^{2}+\omega X+\omega}.

Since
\phi^{-1}(X)=\dfrac{\omega X+\omega^{2}}{X+1}
, we get that

\phi^{-1}(G(\phi(X)))=\dfrac{X^{9}+X^{5}+X^{4}+X^{3}+X^{2}}{X^{8}+X^{4}+X^{2}+X+1}.

It is easy to see that the numerator of
\phi^{-1}(G(\phi(X))=X^{2}(X^{7}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X+1)
vanishes on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
when either
X=0
or
X^{7}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X+1
splits over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Since
X^{7}+X^{3}+X^{2}+X+1
is an irreducible polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, it splits in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{7t}}
for a positive integer
t
. Therefore,
\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi
does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{q}
when
q=2^{7t}
, where
t
is a positive integer.

Now, to prove that
\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi
is not a permutation on
\mathbb{F}_{q}
when
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{7}
, we show that there exist
X\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
Y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{*}
such that
\phi^{-1}\left(G(\phi(X+Y))\right)+\phi^{-1}\left(G(\phi(X))\right)=0
. The expression for
\phi^{-1}(G(\phi(X+Y)))+\phi^{-1}(G(\phi(X)))
is given by
\dfrac{YH(X,Y)}{D(X,Y)}
, where

\displaystyle H(X,Y)=~

\displaystyle X^{16}+X^{8}Y^{8}+X^{8}Y^{4}+X^{4}Y^{8}+X^{8}Y^{3}+X^{4}Y^{7}+X^{8}Y^{2}+X^{2}Y^{8}+X^{8}Y

\displaystyle\quad+XY^{8}+X^{4}Y^{4}+XY^{7}+Y^{8}+X^{4}Y^{2}+X^{2}Y^{4}+X^{2}Y^{3}+XY^{4}+X^{4}

\displaystyle\quad+X^{2}Y^{2}+Y^{4}+X^{2}Y+XY^{2}+Y^{3}+Y^{2}+Y,

\displaystyle D(X,Y)=~

\displaystyle(X^{8}+X^{4}+X^{2}+X+1)(X^{8}+Y^{8}+X^{4}+Y^{4}+X^{2}+Y^{2}+X+Y+1).

We can simplify
H(X,Y)
as
X^{16}+AX^{8}+(B+1)X^{4}+AX^{2}+BX+A\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[X,Y]
, where
A=Y^{8}+Y^{4}+Y^{3}+Y^{2}+Y
and
B=Y^{8}+Y^{7}+Y^{4}+Y^{2}
. Note that
\phi^{-1}\circ G\circ\phi
is not a permutation over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if
H(X,Y)
has a rational point
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
Y\neq 0
. Since
H(X,Y)
is absolutely irreducible by Lemma
5.2
, we can use Lemma
5.1
to compute a bound on the number of rational points for
H(X,Y)
. Let
{V_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}}(H)=\{(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2}:H(X,Y)=0\}}
be the set of all rational points on
H(X,Y)
. We observe that the degree of
H(X,Y)
is
16
and the number of zeros of
H(X,Y)
at infinity are two. This follows because if
h(X,Y,Z)
is homogenized form of
H(X,Y)
then setting
Z=0
in the homogenized polynomial, we get exactly two solutions
(0:1:0)
and
(1:1:0)
of
h(X,Y,0)=0
in the projective space
\mathbb{P}_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Thus, by applying Lemma
5.1
, we obtain the bound on number of rational points of
H(X,Y)
as follows

\displaystyle|\#V_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}}(H)|

\displaystyle\geq 2^{m}+1-2^{8+\frac{m}{2}}-2\geq 2^{8+\frac{m}{2}}(2^{\frac{m}{2}-8}-1)-1.

Clearly when
m\geq 18
, we have
2^{\frac{m}{2}-8}-1\geq 1
. Therefore, for
m\geq 18

|\#V_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}}(H)|\geq 2^{8+\frac{m}{2}}-1\geq 2^{17}-1.

Additionally, the number of zeros of
H(X,0)=X^{16}+X^{4}
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is
2
which is strictly less than
|\#V_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}}(H)|

for
m\geq 18
. Hence,
H(X,Y)
must have a zero
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
Y\neq 0
, implying that
F(X)
is not a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
for
m\geq 18
. Furthermore, using SageMath, for
m\not\equiv 0\pmod{7}
and
m
odd, we confirm that this result holds for smaller values of
m
, such as
m=5,9,11,13,15,17
. This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Resolution of a conjecture

In this section, we provide a proof of the conjecture proposed in
[
23
]
. As part of the proof, we begin with the following lemma, which is used in our argument.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
q=p^{m}
and
F(X)=X^{r}(X^{\alpha(q-1)}+X^{\beta(q-1)}+1)
, where
m,\alpha,\beta
and
r
are positive integers. If
\gcd(\alpha,\beta,r,q+1)\neq 1
, then
F(X)
is not a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}.

Proof.

Suppose that
F(X)
is a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
and
\gcd(\alpha,\beta,r,q+1)\neq 1
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{*}=<\omega>
and define
Y=\omega^{\frac{q^{2}-1}{\gamma}}
, where
\gamma=\gcd(\alpha,\beta,r,q+1)
. Then, we have

F(Y)=\omega^{\frac{r}{\gamma}(q^{2}-1)}\Big(\omega^{\frac{\alpha(q-1)}{\gamma}(q^{2}-1)}+\omega^{\frac{\beta(q-1)}{\gamma}(q^{2}-1)}+1\Big)=F(1).

This contradicts the assumption that
F(X)
is a permutation over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
∎

Theorem 6.2
.

[
23
, Conjecture 1]

Let
r,m
and
\alpha>\beta
be positive integers and
q
be any prime power. If
F(X)=X^{r}(X^{\alpha(q-1)}+X^{\beta(q-1)}+1)
and
G(X)=X^{2\alpha-r}(X^{\alpha(q-1)}+X^{(\alpha-\beta)(q-1)}+1)
permutes
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, then they are QM equivalent.

Proof.

We know that the permutation trinomials
F(X)
and
G(X)
are QM equivalent over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
if the sets of exponents of
F(X)
and
G(X)
, namely
A
and
B
, satisfy the following relation

\displaystyle A_{\text{mod~}(q^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\{rd+\alpha(q-1)d,rd+\beta(q-1)d,rd\}_{\text{mod~}(q^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\{2\alpha-r+\alpha(q-1),2\alpha-r+(\alpha-\beta)(q-1),2\alpha-r\}=B_{\text{mod~}(q^{2}-1)}.

Obviously, on comparing the sets
A_{\text{mod~}(q^{2}-1)}
and
B_{\text{mod~}(q^{2}-1)}
, we have six possible cases. However, only one among the six cases hold true, stated as follows

(6.1)

\begin{cases}&rd+\alpha(q-1)d\equiv 2\alpha-r\pmod{q^{2}-1},\\
&rd+\beta(q-1)d\equiv\alpha-r+(\alpha-\beta)(q-1)\pmod{q^{2}-1},\\
&rd\equiv 2\alpha-r+\alpha(q-1)\pmod{q^{2}-1}.\end{cases}

This is because we can discard the remaining five possible cases, using the argument that
q+1
does not divide any of the elements in the set
\{2\alpha\beta-\alpha r,\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}-\alpha\beta,\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2},\beta^{2}-2\alpha\beta\}
for large
q
.

We now split our analysis into two cases depending on whether
q
is even or odd. First, suppose that
q
is even. We simplify System (
6.1
) to obtain the following system

(6.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(d+1)\eta&\equiv 0\pmod{q+1},~\mbox{where }\eta\in\{\alpha,\beta,r\},\\
(d+1)r&\equiv\alpha(q+1)\pmod{q-1}.\end{cases}

Next, we consider the following two cases

Case 1.
Let
\gcd(\alpha,q+1)=1
, or
\gcd(\beta,q+1)=1
, or
\gcd(r,q+1)=1
. WLOG assume
\gcd(\alpha,q+1)=1
. Then, from System (
6.2
), we can write

\begin{cases}d&\equiv-1\pmod{q+1},\\
d&\equiv(\alpha(q+1))r^{-1}-1\pmod{q-1}.\end{cases}

We apply Chinese Remainder Theorem
2.4
on the above system, as
\gcd(q-1,q+1)=1
. Let
n_{1}=q+1,n_{2}=q-1
and
N=q^{2}-1
. Then, we have
N_{1}=\frac{N}{n_{1}}=q-1
and
N_{2}=\frac{N}{n_{2}}=q+1
. Moreover, it is easy to see that the multiplicative inverse of
N_{1}
modulo
n_{1}
and the multiplicative inverse of
N_{2}
modulo
n_{2}
are both
2^{m-1}
. Therefore, applying the proof of CRT, we obtain

d\equiv-q^{2}+2^{m-1}\alpha r^{-1}(q+1)^{2}\pmod{q^{2}-1}.

Similarly, when
\gcd(\beta,q+1)=1
or
\gcd(r,q+1)=1
, we reach at the same value of
d
.

Case 2.
Let
\gcd(\alpha,q+1)=\delta_{1}\neq 1
,
\gcd(\beta,q+1)=\delta_{2}\neq 1
and
\gcd(r,q+1)=\delta_{3}\neq 1
, then we have,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}d&\equiv-1\pmod{(q+1)/\delta_{i}}\text{ for }i\in\{1,2,3\},\\
d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)r^{-1}-1\pmod{q-1}.\end{cases}

Since,
\gcd(q-1,(q+1)/\delta_{i})=1
for
1\leq i\leq 3
, we apply the CRT to the above system independently for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, yielding

(6.3)

d\equiv-q^{2}+2^{m-1}\alpha r^{-1}(q+1)^{2}\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/\delta_{i}},\text{ for }i\in\{1,2,3\}.

We further consider the following two subcases depending upon
\gcd(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})
.

Subcase 2(a).
Suppose that
\gcd(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})=1
. From Equation (
6.3
), we get

d\equiv-q^{2}+2^{m-1}\alpha r^{-1}(q+1)^{2}\pmod{\ell}
,
\ell=\lcm((q^{2}-1)/\delta_{1},(q^{2}-1)/\delta_{2},(q^{2}-1)/\delta_{3})
. Through some elementary calculations, we obtain
\ell=q^{2}-1
. Thus, we can express
{d\equiv-q^{2}+2^{m-1}\alpha r^{-1}(q+1)^{2}\pmod{q^{2}-1}}
.

Subcase 2(b).
Let
\gcd(\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\delta_{3})\neq 1
. From Lemma
6.1
, it follows that
F(X)
is not a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, a contradiction.

Summarizing the above discussion, we get
d\equiv-q^{2}+2^{m-1}\alpha r^{-1}(q+1)^{2}\pmod{q^{2}-1}
or more precisely,
d=\alpha(q+1)r^{-1}-1
. Moreover, note that
\gcd(d,q^{2}-1)=1
. This completes the proof for
q
as an even prime power.

Next, we consider the scenario where
q
is an odd prime power. Let
n
be a positive integer such that
2^{n}\mid(q+1)
and
2^{n+1}\nmid(q+1)
. Given that
\gcd(r,q-1)=1
, there exists a positive integer
s
such that
rs\equiv 1\pmod{2^{n}(q-1)}
. We deduce the following equations from System (
6.1
)

(6.4)

\displaystyle{}\begin{cases}(d+1)\eta&\equiv 0\pmod{q+1},~\mbox{where }\eta\in\{\alpha,\beta,r\},\\
d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{2^{n}(q-1)}.\end{cases}

Now, we analyze the following two possible cases.

Case 1.
Let
\gcd(\alpha,q+1)=1
, or
\gcd(\beta,q+1)=1
, or
\gcd(r,q+1)=1
. WLOG assume
\gcd(\alpha,q+1)=1
. Then, from System (
6.4
), we have

\begin{cases}d&\equiv-1\pmod{(q+1)/2},\\
d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q-1)/2}.\end{cases}

Since
\gcd((q+1)/2,(q-1)/2)=1
, we apply CRT to the above system of equations and obtain that
d\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/4}
.

Case 2.
Suppose that
\lambda_{1}=\gcd(\alpha,q+1)\neq 1
,
\lambda_{2}=\gcd(\beta,q+1)\neq 1
and
\lambda_{3}=\gcd(r,q+1)\neq 1
.
Now, we need to examine the following two subcases

Subcase 2(a).
Assume that
\gcd(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})=1
. Then, one can write
\lambda_{i}=2^{k}\delta_{i}
with
k\in\{0,1\}
, depending on whether
\lambda
is odd or even, respectively. This leads to the following system

\begin{cases}d&\equiv-1\pmod{(q+1)/2\delta_{i}}\text{ for }i\in\{1,2,3\},\\
d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q-1)/2}.\end{cases}

By applying CRT to the system of equations provided above, we can derive

d\equiv\alpha{(q+1)}s-1\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/4\delta_{i}},\text{ where }i\in\{1,2,3\}.
This further results in
d\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{\ell},
where
\ell
denotes
\lcm((q^{2}-1)/4\delta_{1},(q^{2}-1)/4\delta_{2},(q^{2}-1)/4\delta_{3})
.
In the similar manner as the case of even characteristic, one can compute
\ell
and show that

(6.5)

d\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/4}.

Subcase 2(b).
Let
\gcd(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})\neq 1
. Then, from Lemma
6.1
, we observe that
F(X)
is not a permutation polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, a contradiction.

Notice that
d\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/4}
holds in Case 1 and Case 2. From last equation of the System (
6.4
) and Equation (
6.5
), we have the following system of equations

\begin{cases}d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{(q^{2}-1)/4},\\
d&\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{2^{n}(q-1)}.\end{cases}

One can easily see that
\lcm((q^{2}-1)/4,2^{n}(q-1))=q^{2}-1.
Therefore, we have

d\equiv\alpha(q+1)s-1\pmod{q^{2}-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we construct three new classes of permutation trinomials for
(\alpha,\beta,r)\in\{(7,5,7),(8,6,9),(10,4,11)\}
over the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}
. Moreover, we analyze the QM equivalence among the newly determined permutation trinomials and the previously known ones. Finally, using some algebraic geometric techniques, we show that a specific class of trinomials does not permute
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2m}}
.
