Development, Validity, and Test Reliability and Reproducibility of Facial Palsy-Associated Facial Movement: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Devang Deshpande, Kumar Chowdary Yaramati, Charan Teja Bobba, Meena Priya, Sai Charan Pasupuleti, Sajan Anand Gubbala, Nandisha Thakkar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Facial palsy impairs facial muscle function, impacting both physical and psychosocial well-being. Existing assessment tools often lack sensitivity or functional comprehensiveness. This study aimed to develop and validate a novel facial palsy functional assessment scale (FPFAS) to address these limitations.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted in two phases. Phase 1 involved item generation through literature review, expert interviews, and patient input to identify key functional domains. In Phase 2, content validation was performed using the Delphi technique with a panel of eight clinical experts, evaluating item relevance and clarity. The finalized scale was then subjected to test–retest reliability analysis in 48 patients with facial palsy over a three-day interval.Results:The FPFAS demonstrated excellent content validity, with a scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) of 0.97. Test–retest reliability was high, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.893, indicating strong consistency. Cronbach’s alpha of 0.999 further supported the scale’s internal reliability.Conclusion:The FPFAS is a valid and reliable tool for assessing functional impairments in facial palsy. Its comprehensive, patient-centered approach offers a significant improvement over existing grading systems. Further research is recommended to evaluate construct validity and clinical responsiveness.

INTRODUCTION

Facial palsy is a neurological condition characterized by the weakness or paralysis of the muscles of facial expression, typically due to dysfunction of the seventh cranial nerve (facial nerve). It can result from a variety of causes, including idiopathic origins (as in Bell’s palsy), infections, trauma, tumors, and stroke. Bell’s palsy is the most common form, accounting for approximately 60–75% of all unilateral facial paralysis cases.[1] The global incidence of Bell’s palsy is estimated to be 11–40 cases per 100,000 people annually,[2] making it a relatively frequent neurological condition encountered in both primary and specialist care.

The World Health Organization has recognized facial palsy as a significant cause of physical and psychosocial disability, particularly due to its visible effects on appearance and communication. Facial paralysis often leads to asymmetry, impaired eye closure, difficulties in speaking, eating, and expressing emotions, which significantly reduce the quality of life.[34] Studies report that up to 30% of patients with facial palsy do not fully recover, resulting in long-term functional and aesthetic impairments.[5]

METHODOLOGY

This cross-sectional content validation study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital from August 2024 to March 2025. The study was carried out in two phases. In the first phase, an initial pool of assessment items was developed through a comprehensive review of existing literature and in-depth interviews with patients affected by facial palsy. The preliminary items addressed key domains related to facial function and patient experience, including symmetry at rest, voluntary facial movements (e.g., eyebrow elevation, eye closure, smiling, lip pucker), synkinesis, speech clarity, eye protection, oral competence, emotional expression, facial muscle strength, pain or discomfort, dryness or tearing, eating and drinking ability, social confidence, and psychological impact.

In the second phase, the Delphi technique was used to validate the content of the proposed assessment items.

CONTENT VALIDITY

Phase 1: Domain and item development

In this phase, key domains relevant to facial palsy were identified and used to develop preliminary items for the facial palsy functional assessment scale (FPFAS). The domains included: symmetry at rest, voluntary facial movements (e.g., eyebrow raise, eye closure, smiling, lip pucker), synkinesis, speech clarity, eye protection, oral competence, emotional expression, facial muscle strength, eating and drinking ability, tearing or dryness, pain or discomfort, social interaction, psychological impact, and self-confidence.

Phase 2: Content validation via Delphi method

To validate the content of the FPFAS, the Delphi technique was used with eight clinical experts. The experts, including neurologists, an otolaryngologist, and physiotherapists with over eight years of experience, rated each item on a four-point scale. The final scale-level content validity index (S-CVI/Ave) was 0.97, surpassing the recommended 0.90 threshold. This high score indicated excellent content validity, eliminating the need for a second Delphi round [Table 1].

TEST–RETEST RELIABILITY

A total of 48 individuals with facial palsy were recruited from tertiary care hospitals and outpatient rehabilitation centers [Table 2]. Each participant completed an assessment using the facial palsy functional assessment scale (FPFAS) under standardized conditions. After a fixed interval of three days, participants were reassessed using the same protocol to minimize clinical changes while ensuring temporal separation.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Content validity analysis

Content validity was assessed by analyzing expert ratings using the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) for individual items and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) for the entire scale. Both the universal agreement method and average method were employed. Following Lynn’s guidelines, an S-CVI of ≥0.78 was considered acceptable for a panel of 6–10 experts, and ≥0.90 was regarded as excellent. The FPFAS achieved an S-CVI/Ave of 0.97, confirming strong content validity.

Test–retest reliability analysis

Responses were analyzed only for participants who completed both baseline and follow-up assessments. Inconsistent or unrealistic responses were excluded. Reliability was determined using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The FPFAS demonstrated high test–retest reliability, with an ICC of 0.893, indicating strong consistency across repeated measures and supporting the scale’s dependability for clinical use.

DISCUSSION

The findings from the Delphi survey and reliability testing demonstrate that the scale possesses strong content validity and test–retest reliability, supporting its use as a practical clinical tool.

A structured Delphi process with expert input confirmed the strong content validity of the facial palsy functional assessment scale (FPFAS). The scale demonstrated excellent agreement among experts, with most items scoring a perfect 1.00 on the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and a scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) of 0.97, well above the recommended 0.90 threshold. The scale’s test–retest reliability was also excellent, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.893, indicating consistent results over time. Compared to existing tools like the House–Brackmann[6] and Sunnybrook grading systems,[7] the FPFAS provides a more comprehensive, function-focused assessment, incorporating psychosocial aspects, such as emotional expression and self-confidence.[8]

CONCLUSION

The FPFAS is a valid and reliable tool for evaluating functional deficits in individuals with facial palsy. Its strong psychometric properties and comprehensive scope make it a valuable addition to the clinical assessment toolkit. Future research should focus on broader implementation, longitudinal responsiveness, and integration into rehabilitation protocols.
