Reversing the gap: Do gendered food preferences explain a historic shift in obesity from women to men?
Authors: Xiaoyun Quan, Ruihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough overweight and obesity (OWOB) rates in China were historically higher among women than men, a reversal occurred around 2003, such that men now exhibit higher OWOB rates than women. This trend aligns with the global transition observed across developing and developed nations. However, the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain underexplored. We investigate how food preferences shape dietary intake and whether this explains the reversing gender gap. First, we examine the socio-cultural and physiologically driven mechanisms that shape these preferences. We then quantify whether preferences translate into measurable gaps in food consumption. A clear divergence is identified: adult males demonstrate a stronger preference for animal-sourced foods, fried snacks, sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) and alcohol, whereas adult females show a greater preference for dairy and plant-based alternatives. These preferences explain a significant portion of the observed gender gap in consumption patterns. Furthermore, dietary patterns interact with income. At lower income levels, females' higher starchy food preference elevates their OWOB risk. With rising incomes, males' specific food preferences are increasingly fulfilled, leading to higher energy intake and OWOB rates that ultimately exceed those of females. We further show that these gendered preferences are moderated by residential context, with urbanization strengthening females' inclination toward healthy foods and rural settings reinforcing males’ preference for energy-dense items. Finally, while general dietary knowledge has limited impact, targeted health communication linking diet to disease, especially within households, can reduce OWOB odds and mitigate inequalities. Income growth alone does not guarantee healthier choices, particularly among high-income males, underscoring the need for interventions that address both economic and behavioral drivers of dietary risk.Keywords: Food preferences, Economic development, Overweight, Obesity, Gender inequality

Introduction

Since the 1990s, the global prevalence of overweight and obesity (OWOB) has increased annually (WHO, 2024). By 2022, approximately 2.5 billion adults exceeded the standard healthy weight, accounting for 43% of the global adult population - a 72% rise from 1990. OWOB are major contributors to numerous noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, neurological disorders, chronic respiratory diseases, and digestive disorders (WHO, 2024). The latest findings on the global burden of disease indicate that in 2019, NCDs linked to OWOB resulted in at least five million deaths (Murray et al., 2020).

The increasing rates of OWOB are a phenomenon resulting from the combined influence of physiological and socioeconomic factors (Frühbeck et al., 2019; Lin & Li, 2021; Pan et al., 2021; Swinburn et al., 2011). Key drivers of this public health issue include profound changes in the dietary and physical activity patterns. With improvements in economic conditions, Chinese diets have been increasingly Westernized (Howard et al., 2021), characterized by higher intakes of animal products, vegetable oils, and sugar. Furthermore, the dominant occupations have shifted from physically demanding roles to those characterized by lower caloric expenditure (Du et al., 2002). These transitions have contributed to a continuous increase in OWOB rates within the Chinese population (see Fig. 1), with the rate of growth accelerating over the past two decades. By 2022, the prevalence of OWOB was approximately 43% among adult males and 32% among adult females in China.

It is worth noting that Fig. 1 also displays a marked disparity between males and females, with a significant reversal occurring around 2003. The data from WHO and NCD-RisC (2016) both indicate that, before 1995, the prevalence of OWOB and its rate of increase for were higher among females than among males. However, the growth rate among males surpassing that of females since 1995. This led to a crossover in 2003, when males' OWOB rates exceeded females’, and the gap has continued to widen since.

This distinct sex reversal pattern in OWOB also appears across LMICs (low- and middle-income countries) and HICs (high-income countries) (Ford et al., 2017; GBD 2021Adult BMI Collaborators, 2025). In many LMICs, obesity being more prevalent among wealthier groups and female groups (Wang et al., 2025). This pattern often linked to cultural associations of weight with prosperity and more restricted physical activity for women (Dinsa et al., 2012). In contrast, in most HICs, the socioeconomic gradient of obesity has reversed, concentrating the burden in lower-income groups and male groups. Research attributes the rising male burden in HICs to intersecting factors: a steeper decline in occupational physical activity for men during deindustrialization (Maruyama & Nakamura, 2018), and gendered sociocultural norms (Kanter & Caballero, 2012). However, due to data constraints, the existing literature typically examines countries or regions of different income levels separately. The distinct socio-cultural contexts across these settings make direct comparison of findings difficult and hinder the extraction of generalizable mechanistic explanations. China, with its internal disparities in economic development across regions and its position of having undergone rapid nutritional transitions within a shared cultural and policy environment (Ren et al., 2021; Zhai et al., 2009), provides an ideal context to address this gap. By examining whether and how gender-specific food preferences shape OWOB patterns across different developmental stages within China, we can isolate the role of social and cultural determinants from cross-country confounding factors, offering a rigorous test of the drivers behind the sex reversal phenomenon.

This analytical advantage is further amplified by China's distinctive historical trajectory. Its unprecedented pace of socioeconomic transformation over the past three decades has condensed health transitions into a remarkably short period. This accelerated timeline enables a quasi-experimental assessment of the interplay between changing food environments and enduring cultural norms in driving changes in population health. Furthermore, China's unique cultural fabric, including a collective, family-oriented social structure, a distinctive culinary tradition with strong gender symbolism (e.g., the central role of meat in masculine identity and alcohol in social bonding), and historically defined gender roles within the household, creates a specific vulnerability to OWOB (Swinburn et al., 2011; Yuan, 2021). These factors make the Chinese context not merely an example of a global trend, but a powerful locus for testing and refining theories about the social determinants of OWOB.

While the public sector has recognized the risks of NCDs associated with obesity, less attention has been paid to significant and evolving gender disparities in these conditions. China has introduced a range of policies to tackle NCDs and promote healthy diets, beginning with early initiatives such as the “prevention-first” healthcare strategy and continuing into recent efforts like the Healthy China 2030 initiative and various obesity and nutrition guidelines (e.g., Chinese Dietary Guidelines). Although these measures have helped draw attention to diet-related NCDs and encouraged gradual shifts in dietary behavior (Zhai et al., 2002), they have not curbed the persistent rise in OWOB and have also failed to narrow the growing gender gap.

Many studies quantified gender disparities in OWOB (Garawi et al., 2014; Ismail et al., 2002; Maruf et al., 2013; Saha et al., 2023; Santiago et al., 2013; Wilkosz et al., 2010; Yang et al., 2020), some of them have examined the underlying causes. A recent study categorizes causal factors into behavioral (food consumption, physical activity and alcohol drink), material (educational and income level), and psychological influences (feel stressful or not), applying the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition method to analyze gender differences in OWOB (Yusuf et al., 2023). Their results attribute 39% of the disparity to behavioral factors, among which food consumption has the greatest impact. In addition, several empirical studies have concluded that significant gender differences in food consumption are importantly associated with heterogeneous risks of OWOB. For instance, there are significant gender differences in the consumption of ultra-processed foods (McKenzie et al., 2024), and high intake of such foods is significantly associated with increased obesity risk among men (Lee & Lee, 2025). This underscores the significant influence of gender-specific food preferences on OWOB patterns.

Building on existing studies, disparities in food consumption can be attributed to two primary categories of determinants. The first pathway involves socioeconomic mediation, such as unequal intra-household resource allocation. For instance, the favoring of primary male earners in food distribution is a function of labor market inequalities and household bargaining power (Harris-Fry et al., 2017). As a direct economic manifestation of this power disparity, men are often granted priority access to high-cost, nutrient-dense foods, such as animal-sourced products, as well as discretionary goods like alcoholic beverages (Duflo & Udry, 2004; Sarker et al., 2022). While critically important, this pathway is ultimately contingent on external economic structures, and the scholarly conclusions regarding it are relatively well-established and consistent.

In contrast, a second line of research focuses on the pathway of internalized gender identity or gender-based preferences to explain the impact of food choices on OWOB. However, the findings are less consistent. Some studies suggest that women, being generally more concerned with body shape, are more likely than men to control their weight and consume healthier foods (Hartmann et al., 2013; Kanter & Caballero, 2012; Wardle et al., 2004). Conversely, other studies indicate that women may have a higher tendency to choose sugary snacks (Kanter & Caballero, 2012; Wansink et al., 2003). These conflicting findings may be related to the specific types of foods studied and the different regional contexts considered (Rodrigues et al., 2020). Furthermore, comparative analyses of such disparities across different stages of economic development remain scarce, resulting in an insufficient understanding of the mechanisms behind the reversal phenomenon in OWOB.

Therefore, this study aims to isolate and examine the latter pathway. Theoretically, sociocultural and physiologically driven factors lead to gender-based preferences in food choices, which in turn drive gender disparities in OWOB. First, in sociocultural contexts, the “food-gender stereotype” theory suggests that food choices and consumption behaviors convey gender signals (Feraco et al., 2024; Vandello et al., 2024; Vartanian, 2015; Zhu et al., 2015). Societal preferences for body shape also influence diet. The general preference for a slender, healthy female figure (Bouvier & Chen, 2021) encourages women to eat more vegetables and fruits and fewer high-fat foods. In China, this body shape consciousness is intensified by the rise of a “beauty economy” that promotes youthful slenderness as a defining feature of femininity (Luo, 2013; Pan et al., 2021; Yang, 2011), coupled with collectivist value orientations that heighten sensitivity to others’ judgments (Forbes & Jung, 2008; Jackson & Chen, 2014; Luo et al., 2021). This culturally specific context offers a valuable lens for understanding how gendered body image concerns shape health behaviors and disparities more broadly. Second, sex-based physiological differences influence food preferences. Males generally possess greater muscle mass and higher metabolic rates than females (Garn et al., 1953; Hasson et al., 2011; Janssen et al., 2000), leading them to favor energy-dense foods such as fatty, protein-rich animal products (Johnston et al., 2008). Moreover, adult females generally have greater access to health information due to regular medical check-ups, preventive health services associated with menstruation and reproductive health and higher health and nutrition awareness (Bertakis, 2009; Bidmon & Terlutter, 2015; Verbrugge, 1982). This access encourages adult females to make healthier food choices.

The differences in food preferences may lead to significant gender disparity variations in OWOB across socioeconomic development process. According to Bennett's Law, in lower-income economies, residents primarily consume starchy foods, which are relatively inexpensive, to meet their energy needs, with limited consumption of high-cost animal-sourced foods (Bennett, 1941; Gouel & Guimbard, 2019). Consequently, women who prefer plant-based foods are more prone to excessive carbohydrate intake from refined carbohydrates1 (e.g., white rice, refined wheat products), increasing their risk of OWOB. Conversely, as an economy advances and household incomes rise, people gain increased access to certain energy-dense animal-based and high-cost foods, such as fatty red meats, processed meats, and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs) (Popkin et al., 2020).2 This shift enables men to more fully indulge their preferences for such energy-dense options, significantly increasing their risk of excessive energy intake and ultimately leading to higher OWOB rates than women.

Building on the above, this study utilizes data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) (1997-2011) to address the following questions: Is there a difference in food preferences among adult males and females in China? Whether and to what extent gender-specific food preferences can account for the changing disparity of OWOB rates between sexes across different stages of economic development? How does residential context (urban vs. rural) moderate the expression of these gendered preferences? Given the rising trend of OWOB rates, particularly the more severe challenges faced by males, can enhancing the food and nutrition literacy help alleviate this issue? Does the effect of improving health literacy differ across income groups?

This study makes three key contributions to the literature. First, it advances the mechanistic understanding of the shifting sex disparities in OWOB, a phenomenon increasingly observed globally. Second, it examines how dietary preferences shape these disparities across varying levels of economic development within China, offering insights applicable to countries at different developmental stages. Third, it introduces gendered food stereotypes as a key psychological factor that shape dietary behavior, thereby extending the analytical framework for understanding health gaps among adult.

The remainder of this research is structured as follows. Section 2 is the theoretical mechanism analysis. The empirical methodology, data, variables and descriptive statistics are presented in Section 3. Section 4 discusses the empirical results. Finally, we present the conclusions and policy implications in Section 5.

Theoretical mechanism analysis

A framework of determinants: Gendered food consumption and its drivers

Individual food intake quantities and dietary structures are influenced by several factors. To analytically disentangle the mechanisms behind gender disparities in food consumption and subsequent OWOB risk, we propose a conceptual framework that categorizes these determinants into two parts: socioeconomically-mediated disparities and internalized gender preferences. This distinction is crucial for clarifying the scope of this study and pinpointing the fundamental drivers of the observed gender gap reversal in OWOB.

The pathway of socioeconomically-mediated disparities refer to differences in food consumption arising from unequal resource distribution within the household and society. This dynamic is driven by two interconnected forces. First, men have historically held an advantageous position in the labor market, leading to a greater contribution to household income (Folke & Rickne, 2022; Sawhill, 1973). This economic advantage translates into greater bargaining power (Harris-Fry et al., 2017; Wheeler, 1991), favoring men in the distribution of high-cost or hedonic foods. Such items are associated with superior taste and immediate satisfaction (Maehle et al., 2015), including animal products, fried foods, and alcohol. Second, traditional gender roles compound this effect. Women, as the primary food preparers in most households, often exhibit altruistic behavior during meal allocation (Kabeer, 1999; Simmons & Emanuele, 2007). This role leads them to prioritize the nutritional needs and preferences of other family members, particularly male spouses and children, over their own, thereby systematically directing a larger share of nutrient-dense and desirable foods away from themselves. This dual dynamic results in men consistently consuming a larger proportion of animal-based foods, fried items, alcohol, and other hedonic foods. In other words, this phenomenon is fundamentally a function of labor market inequalities and intra-household bargaining dynamics, reinforced by gendered social norms.

The second pathway originates from internalized gender identity and gender-based food preferences. This refers to the deep-seated, culturally constructed, and physiologically reinforced associations between specific foods and concepts of masculinity or femininity (Vartanian, 2015). These associations become internalized as intrinsic tastes that guide food choices independently, to a large degree, from short-term socioeconomic constraints. For instance, the symbolic link between meat and masculinity or between light, plant-based foods and femininity, operates as a powerful motivator for food selection. The relationship between these pathways and their link to OWOB disparities are described in detail in the following section.

In summary, the first pathway mainly explains disparities through economic structures, whereas the second pathway locates the cause in internalized gender-specific preferences. The latter mechanism is the core investigative objective of this paper. By focusing here, we aim to disentangle the complex etiology of OWOB and provide a more nuanced explanation for its shifting gender pattern.

The formation of food-gender preferences: Sociocultural and physiological mechanisms

Individual diets are influenced by multiple factors across social, household, and individual levels. At the social level, dietary cultures associated with gender symbols, specific regional dietary practices shaped by natural conditions (such as Western and Asian dietary cultures), and food prices can all affect long-term food consumption patterns. At the household level, factors such as demographic structure, wealth levels, and the dietary preferences of the household head can impact each family member's food choices. At the individual level, knowledge related to diet and health, income levels, education, age, and physical activity levels all play a role in food selection and consumption (Blaylock et al., 1999; Cai et al., 2024; Cavazza et al., 2015; Deaton & Paxson, 1998; Kuri et al., 2024). An unbalanced diet is one of the primary causes of OWOB, thus, these multidimensional factors strongly influence an individual's weight status.

Among the various factors influencing individual diet, social cultural factors and physiological drivers contribute to the formation of systematic gender preferences in food, which are insensitive to changes in individual income or educational attainment. At the social-cultural level, food plays a role in expressing identity and gender roles (Vartanian, 2015). The theory of “food-gender stereotypes” posits that there is a widespread perception associating meat with masculinity, fruits and vegetables with femininity (Vartanian, 2015). Campbell and Campbell (2023, p. 117) also note that existing culture equate meat with physical strength, masculinity, and gender identity. Consequently, the differing perceptions of food based on gender ultimately affect the quantity and structure of food consumption. While the food consumption patterns exhibited by different genders are influenced by a complex array of factors, a robust body of evidence confirms that, after controlling for socioeconomic covariates, systematic differences persist. This well-documented pattern shows that women, aiming to express their femininity, tend to consume more vegetables, fruits, grains (such as oats and other whole grains), and dairy products. In contrast, men, in an effort to exhibit masculinity, are more inclined to choose meat and fried foods (Feraco et al., 2024; Vandello et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2015).

Gendered societal pressures exert distinct influences on the dietary behaviors of men and women, shaping their respective risks for OWOB. Women are generally more responsive to social ideals of appearance, with contemporary norms emphasizing a slim and healthy female image. This pressure fosters a stronger inclination toward health-conscious eating among women (Bouvier & Chen, 2021). In contrast, men face a different set of expectations rooted in performative masculinity. In China, alcohol culture is deeply embedded in business and social rituals, where a high tolerance for spirits like baijiu is perceived as a masculine trait (Yang et al., 2019; Yuan, 2021). This social imperative to consume alcohol functions as a significant external pathway to excessive energy intake, substantially increasing men's caloric surplus and OWOB risk.

From a physiology-driven perspective, differences in energy requirements and specific nutrient needs during certain life stages may lead to gender differences in food choices. Due to variations in muscle mass and metabolic rate (Hasson et al., 2011; Janssen et al., 2000), males generally have higher caloric needs, which leads them to prefer more energy-dense foods (Gorini et al., 2025; Johnston et al., 2008; Kimura et al., 2014), such as fatty animal-sourced products, fried foods, and SSBs. For females, various life stages post-adulthood, including pregnancy, breastfeeding, menopause, and perimenopause, require careful dietary considerations. During pregnancy and breastfeeding, a female's diet not only affects her health but also her offspring's early and long-term health (Ashman et al., 2016; Nguyen et al., 2013; Rodríguez-Bernal et al., 2010). Therefore, it is essential for females in these life stages to adhere to a nutritionally balanced diet that includes an appropriate amount of animal products while also increasing their intake of vegetables, fruits, and dairy.3

Beyond these physiological drivers, the distinct physiological stages women experience also predispose them to greater exposure to health information, which further steers their dietary preferences toward healthier options. Furthermore, in terms of disease prevention, women are suggested to undergo regular reproductive health screenings (such as gynecological exams and breast cancer screenings). Their interactions with the healthcare system are often more frequent than men (Bertakis, 2009; Verbrugge, 1982), resulting in a deeper understanding and heightened sensitivity to the importance of health (Shin & Mattila, 2019). This greater awareness unconsciously influences women to select more healthful foods and to avoid unhealthy options.

Shaped by this combination of socio-cultural and physiologically driven factors, men tend to prefer animal-sourced food (e.g., meat), alcoholic beverages, fried foods, and SSBs, while women generally favor plant-based foods (e.g., starchy vegetables, other vegetables, fruits, cereals) and dairy products. Consequently, under similar conditions, men appear more likely than women to consume excess energy, resulting in a higher risk of OWOB. Given this, one may wonder why, in lower levels of economic development, women exhibit higher rates of OWOB than men, as shown in Fig. 1. The next section will provide a theoretical analysis of this phenomenon.

From preferences to disparities: The reversal of OWOB gaps across development levels

The shifting gender gap of OWOB in China is not an automatic outcome of economic growth, but a consequence of how rising incomes interact with and amplify pre-existing food preferences. This section theorizes the mechanisms through which these preferences reshape energy intake and OWOB risk across the socioeconomic spectrum.

At lower levels of economic development, dietary patterns are constrained by resource scarcity. As Bennett's Law posits, diets in low-income settings are dominated by inexpensive, carbohydrate-rich staples (Bennett, 1941; Gouel & Guimbard, 2019). In this context, although gendered food preferences exist, their expression is limited by purchasing power and intra-household allocation logic. Women, responsible for meal preparation, tend to consume starchy staples themselves while reserving scarce meat or eggs for working males, a practice rooted in the perceived need to maintain male labor productivity (Harris-Fry et al., 2017; Luo et al., 2001). Research on intrahousehold food allocation in China has consistently documented working males receive preferential access to nutrient-rich foods, a pattern attributed to their role as primary income earners (Huang et al., 2024). This pattern is rooted in patriarchal household structure, where men have traditionally been primary income earners. Consequently, women's preference for plant-based foods translates into disproportionately high consumption of refined cereals and tubers, elevating their OWOB risk. Men's preferred items, by contrast, remains largely unfulfilled, as household resources prioritize their basic nutritional needs rather than preference satisfaction.

As incomes rise, the binding constraint of resource scarcity loosens. However, the translation of increased purchasing power into dietary change is not gender-neutral. Two interconnected mechanisms disproportionately amplify male energy intake. The first is household resource allocation. With greater resources now available, the same intra-household logic that operated under scarcity enables greater expression of male dietary preferences. Consequently, rising household wealth is channeled into the kinds of energy-dense foods that men are predisposed to prefer, directly translating into their higher caloric intake. The second mechanism is ritualistic alcohol consumption. In China, the consumption of baijiu (distilled liquor) and other alcoholic beverages is not merely a dietary choice but a performative ritual central to masculine identity and business sociality (Zhang et al., 2012). Unlike routine eating, drinking is embedded in social obligations: building trust, cementing business deals, and maintaining guanxi (social networks) often require male participation in heavy drinking occasions (Cochrane et al., 2003; Lemle & Mishkind, 1989). As economic activity intensifies with development, so does the frequency and cultural expectation of these ritualistic drinking events. This creates a powerful, socially mandated pathway for excessive energy intake that is predominantly available to men, substantially contributing to their caloric surplus.

Concurrently, women's dietary trajectory diverges. Their inherent preference shifts away from staple carbohydrates towards more diverse and often healthier plant-based foods and dairy. In the Chinese context, this shift is reinforced by two forces that, while culturally specific in their manifestation, reflect broader patterns. First, women's greater health literacy, stemming from their role in reproductive health and food preparation, enhances their awareness of diet-disease links. Research from rural China substantiates this pattern, showing that women's primary role in household food preparation makes them more responsive than men to nutritional information (Aunan et al., 2019; Ma, 2015; Ma et al., 2022). Second, societal pressure to maintain a slender figure, amplified by China's “beauty economy” (Jackson et al., 2021; Luo, 2013), exemplifies the universal phenomenon of gendered body ideals shaping consumption (Swami et al., 2010). These mechanisms, while locally inflected, offer general insights into how gender norms mediate the relationship between economic development and nutritional outcomes more broadly.

In summary, the changing disparity in OWOB rates is propelled by a gendered amplification of dietary risk: rising incomes selectively activate the male-preferred, high-energy diet through household allocation and socially mandated drinking rituals, while simultaneously enabling the realization of a healthier, female-preferred dietary pattern reinforced by health literacy and body image norms. It is this asymmetric fulfillment of gendered preferences, embedded within these socioeconomic and cultural structures, that ultimately drives the historic shift in OWOB from females to males.

Model and variable

Empirical models

The gender gap in actual food intake may stem from differences in individual, household, and community characteristics, or from the effect of gender itself. To explore whether there are gender preferences in food choices, we construct a counterfactual scenario: we estimate what women's food intake would be if they had the same observable characteristics as men. If there are no gendered differences in food preferences, this counterfactual group should not show significant differences from women's actual food consumption levels. Conversely, if a significant difference exists, it indicates the presence of food gender preferences. Since we cannot observe such “counterfactual” data in reality, statistical methods must be employed to construct it.

The advantage of the Oaxaca-Blinder method lies in its ability to construct a counterfactual group using a straightforward approach and to decompose the differences between groups into an “explained part” and an “unexplained part” (Jann, 2008). When studying income discrimination in the labor market, the “explained part” corresponds to income gaps related to differences in productivity conditions, while the “unexplained part” represents income gaps unrelated to productivity conditions, often interpreted empirically as discrimination. The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method, proposed by (Blinder, 1973; Oaxaca, 1973), is a classic technique widely used to analyze income inequality (Dang & Viet, 2021; Hanewald et al., 2021). To date, this method has been widely applied in studies examining health inequalities (Eriksson et al., 2014; Sharma & Smieliauskas, 2022) and inequalities related to obesity issues (Sen, 2014; Taber et al., 2016).

In simple terms, this method can be thought of as performing a “fair comparison”. For example, when comparing food intake between males and females, we cannot simply attribute all the differences to gender preferences because there may be systematic disparities between males and females in factors such as income, education level, and region of residence, all of which can also influence diet. The role of the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method is, first, to isolate the effects of observable “background differences” (such as income and education). These effects constitute the “explained component”. The remaining portion that “cannot be explained by background differences”, the “unexplained component” can then be more reasonably attributed to more underlying factors, such as the “gender preferences” driven by socio-cultural and physiological factors that are the focus of this study. We chose this method precisely because it allows for such effective separation. It should be noted that the “unexplained component” is a statistical residual. While it provides an important perspective for measuring potential preferences, it may also encompass other factors not captured in the model. To mitigate this limitation, we include a comprehensive set of control variables at the individual, household, and community levels, as well as provincial and rural-urban dummy variables, to account for as many observable confounding factors as possible.

Therefore, we first utilize this decomposition method to assess whether there are differences in food gender preferences among Chinese adults. The core idea of this approach is to break down the overall difference in food intake between males and females into two parts: one that can be explained by observable characteristics, such as income, education, regional factors, or physiological factors like estimated energy requirements (EER),4 and another “unexplained” part that this study interprets as reflecting gender preferences stemming from socio-cultural factors and physiology-driven factors. Data, variables and descriptive statistics

Data source

We utilize data from the China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), an international collaborative project between the Carolina Population Center and the National Institute for Nutrition and Health at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CCDC). The survey began in 1989 and has conducted eleven rounds. It is the only publicly available database that includes detailed individual food intake data in China. The database employs a 24-h dietary recall method to collect dietary information from rural and urban households across more than ten provinces in China over three consecutive days. As the CHNS team currently only publicly releases dietary survey data from 1989 to 2011, and the surveys conducted before 1997 involve the unreleased 1981 version of the Chinese Food Composition Table (FCT), we utilize data from the years 1997, 2000, 2002, 2004, and 2011. The dataset covers a wide range of regions with significant socioeconomic disparities, thus demonstrating strong representativeness. Specifically, the 1997 survey included eight provinces: Heilongjiang, Jiangsu, Shandong, Henan, Hubei, Hunan, Guangxi, and Guizhou. From 2000 to 2009, the survey expanded to include Liaoning Province. In 2011, Shanghai, Beijing, and Chongqing province were added to the coverage.

To answer the research question whether there exists a gender preference in food among Chinese adults, we employ empirical analysis using five waves of CHNS data from 1997 to 2011. The sample includes individuals aged over 17 years.

Moreover, to investigate whether the shifting OWOB gap is linked to food gender preferences, we use the earliest and latest available data from the CHNS for comparison. A larger gap in socio-economic development levels provides a better basis for examining the role of food gender preferences in this reversal phenomenon. Therefore, we ultimately conduct Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition using data from 1997 and 2011 separately. China's per capita real GDP grew from 3251 (constant 2021 international $) in 1997 to 11,187 (constant 2021 international $) in 2011 (World Bank, 2024), with an average annual growth rate of 9%. This substantial growth suggests significant changes in residents' diet. As shown in Fig. 1, in 1997 women's OWOB rates surpassed those of men's, while in 2011 the opposite was observed. Thus, the data from these years fulfill the study's second research objective.

Variables

(1)Dependent Variables. This study includes two types of dependent variables: food intake quantity (g/day) and whether an individual is classified as OWOB. Food intake encompasses fourteen categories, including the ten food groups from the “Chinese Balanced Diet Pyramid”: cereals, tubers, vegetables, fruits, meats, eggs, aquatic products, dairy, soy products, and nuts, as well as an additional four energy-dense items: alcoholic beverages, SSBs, sugary snacks, and fried foods. Additionally, given the substantial differences in caloric content between refined grains and whole grains, lean meat and fatty meat,5 these four categories are also incorporated into the analytical framework, resulting in a total of 14 food groups.

These univariate comparisons reveal notable gender disparities in dietary patterns. However, these differences alone cannot be attributed to gender-based food preferences, as they may be confounded by other factors. For instance, the higher food intake among men may partly reflect their generally higher energy requirements. To move beyond these bivariate associations and rigorously quantify the role of gender-specific food preferences in the observed consumption gaps, we employ the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method in the next section. This approach will formally decompose the gender gaps into portions attributable to differences in observable characteristics and an unexplained portion that serves as a proxy for gender-based preferences. Establishing this unexplained component is a critical prerequisite for ultimately explaining its contribution to the reversal of gender inequality in OWOB across different economic development levels.

Empirical results

Differences in food preferences between males and females

First, The Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition method as shown in Model (1) is applied to investigate whether there exists a gender preference difference for food among Chinese adults. The “Difference” shown in Table 2 (see Table A2 for detailed results) represents the average differential in food intake between males and females, with males defined as the reference group. The “Explained” represents the difference in food intake can be attributed to characteristic variables. The “Unexplained” is the primary interest of this study regarding food gender preference. With males as the reference group, a positive unexplained component indicates a stronger preference for that food among males, whereas a negative value denotes a stronger preference among females. The contribution values for some food categories exceed 100% in absolute terms. This occurs because the contribution is calculated as follows: Contribution value = (Difference attributable to the component/Overall group difference) × 100%. When the overall group difference (Difference) is small, even a modest absolute difference in a component can yield a proportionally large contribution value. For example, females show a significantly stronger preference for fruits and dairy than males, resulting in a large negative value for the unexplained portion in these categories, which in turn leads to contribution values numerically exceeding 100%. This is a common occurrence in decomposition analysis and does not undermine the logical validity of the decomposition. On the contrary, this actually highlights the significant differences in gender preferences for food intake.

The results in Table 2 7 indicate that a significant gender preference difference exists for most food. Among plant-based food groups, the unexplained difference results indicate that females show a stronger preference for whole grains, tubers, vegetables, and fruits compared to males, whereas an opposite preference is observed for soy products. Specifically, the observed sex difference in tuber consumption (2.69 g, p < 0.001) can primarily be explained by sex-based factors. The unexplained component of −5.57 g accounts for −207% of the raw gap observed between males and females. The preference differences for cereals and nuts are not significant. The unexpected result for cereals and refined cereals (unexplained difference>0) may be due to rising resident incomes from 1997 to 2011 and males’ preference toward meats, which leads to an increase in the consumption of staple foods (especially refined cereals) that are typically consumed alongside meats. In terms of animal-sourced foods, males have a greater preference for meats (including fat meats and lean meats), eggs, and aquatics compared to females, while the opposite holds for dairy products. For instance, the coefficient for the unexplained difference (33.81 g) of meat is 1.82 times the magnitude of the sex-specific raw gap (18.56 g), indicating that the gender-specific effect accounts for the primary component of the observed disparity. Toward the remaining four high-energy density foods and beverages, males show a significantly stronger preference for alcoholic drinks, SSBs, and fried foods than females, with no notable difference in sugary snacks. Overall, males tend to prefer animal-based foods, high-energy density snacks and beverages, whereas females show a greater preference for plant-based foods and dairy products.

A key explanation for the evident gendered food preference derived from the theoretical framework is that: on one hand, males' generally higher caloric requirements lead them to consume more high-calorie foods. It should be noted that the model (1) and (2) already incorporate the EER variable to account for sex-based differences in food intake arising from variations in energy requirements. Consequently, a portion of the unexplained difference can be attributed to dietary habits shaped by males' relatively higher EER, particularly their tendency to consume more energy-dense foods, which further contributes to the disparity in overall food intake. This physiology-driven behavior is compounded by masculinity performance in dietary choices and China's particular liquor culture, resulting in a preference for energy-dense food and alcohol. On the other hand, females' greater intake of dairy and plant-based foods is influenced by greater receptivity to health information, social body image expectations and requirements for expressing femininity.

Income variables play a systematic role in explaining dietary gaps (shown in Table A2). In the explained component, household income is negatively associated with cereal consumption (−0.16∗∗∗) but positively associated with fruits (0.19∗∗∗), dairy (0.12∗∗∗), meats (0.27∗∗∗), and other nutrient-dense foods, suggesting that economic resources facilitate a shift away from staple grains toward more diverse diet. These results imply that a portion of the observed gender gap in food consumption is economically structured, particularly through males’ generally higher income levels, which enable greater consumption of animal-based and energy-dense foods.

To assess the reliability of the results presented in Table 2, we conduct robustness checks utilizing two methods. First, following (Li et al., 2004), outliers were removed. Measurement errors in food intake records may lead to biased estimates of group differences. Therefore, observations with daily caloric intake exceeding four standard deviations were excluded for robustness test. Second, the age range of the sample was modified. Since age data is derived from the survey year minus the birth year from CHNS, the exact age in years cannot be confirmed. The baseline regression included samples that were nominal 18 years old based on the aforementioned method. To test the robustness of this approach, we re-estimate the results with the sample restricted to individuals older than 18.

The detailed robustness check results (shown in Tables A3–A4) strongly support the existence of food gender preferences among Chinese adults and confirm the reliability of our baseline estimates. Specifically, after removing extreme outliers and redefining the age variable into alternative categorical groupings, the size and statistical significance of the key decomposed components remained largely consistent with those in the baseline regression. The positive unexplained gap in the consumption of animal-based foods, alcoholic and SSBs, and fried snacks, which serves as a proxy for male preference, not only retained its statistical significance but also exhibited stable effect sizes across robustness checks. Similarly, the stronger female preference for dairy and plant-based foods remained robust across both alternative specifications. These results indicate that the identified gender-based dietary patterns are not driven by outlier influences or arbitrary age categorization, enhancing confidence in the conclusion that food gender preferences are persistent among Chinese adults.

Gender preferences in food and OWOB across different levels of economic development

Income gradients in gendered food preferences: Decomposition evidence and robustness tests

This section will empirically analyze how food gender preferences lead to shifts in gender inequality regarding OWOB issues under different socio-economic development conditions, based on Model (1). A greater gap in socio-economic development levels allows for a more robust examination of how food gender preferences contribute to the changing disparity. In the CHNS data, the earliest and latest years available for our analysis are 1997 and 2011.

It is worth noting that the unexplained difference value for cereals and refined grains shifted from negative in 1997 to positive in 2011, which suggests a transition in preference for these foods from females to males. The underlying reason may be the increased consumption of staple foods paired with meats, driven by rising meat intake among males as economic conditions improved. Overall, as income rises, females tend to intake more healthful foods, whereas males do the opposite. Males' preference has been more significantly satisfied with increasing income, thus greatly increasing the risk of OWOB. The growth in wealth has also better fulfilled females' preferences for plant-based foods such as whole grains, fruits and vegetables, as well as dairy products, which are largely under-consumed in China. Therefore, females’ dietary changes are relatively more balanced, and their risk of OWOB is lower than males.

The mechanism behind the reversal of the income-OWOB relationship, can be explained by the detailed Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition results as well. As shown in Table A2, the coefficient for individual income in the unexplained part is 16 (statistically significant at the 5% level). This implies that, ceteris paribus, males have a significantly higher marginal return to income for meat consumption than females. In the other words, an identical increase in individual income leads to a larger rise in meat intake for males compared to females. Meanwhile, a contrasting pattern emerges for cereals, fruits, and dairy products. In the unexplained component, the coefficients for individual income are significantly negative for these food categories. This indicates that, holding other characteristics constant, the promotive effect of individual income growth on the consumption of these foods is significantly stronger among women than among men. These results provide additional evidence for the robustness of our conclusions.

To further verify that gender-normative eating patterns constitute a key mechanism behind the divergence in OWOB between males and females, we employed a Seemingly Unrelated Regression (SUR) model for testing. First, we performed separate OLS estimates for female and male subsamples. Given that the error terms across gender groups may be correlated, joint estimation of the two subsamples is more efficient (Greene, 2018). Therefore, we then conducted tests for between-group coefficient differences based on SUR estimation, focusing on two key variables: household income and individual income share. These two variables capture the expression of gendered preferences from distinct dimensions. Household income reflects how each gender allocates additional economic resources toward various food categories as overall resources increase, thereby revealing the dynamic change in their preference structures with rising income. Individual income share, on the other hand, captures the influence a member gains in household consumption decisions due to his or her relative economic contribution, illustrating how gendered preferences are realized through intra-household resource allocation. By testing the statistical significance of the differences in these coefficients between genders, we can identify the existence and specific manifestations of gendered food preferences, thereby providing mechanistic evidence for understanding the gender divergence in obesity risk.

The SUR estimation results are presented in Table 5. The results first indicate that as household income rises, both males and females significantly reduce their intake of cereals and whole grains (negative coefficients), while increasing consumption of animal-based foods, fruits, snacks, and alcohol.

(4) For animal-based foods (e.g., meat), alcohol, and fried foods, the effect of individual income share is mostly non-significant for both genders, and the between-group differences are also non-significant.

A noteworthy question is why males' preferences for meats and alcohol are not significantly reflected in their “individual income share” coefficients. One plausible explanation is that, given males' persistent relative advantage in the labor market, their baseline economic contribution to the household is already high, and household food allocation is already biased toward satisfying their preferences. Thus, a marginal increase in males' income contribution may not significantly alter the consumption allocation of these foods. Conversely, women's preferences (e.g., for vegetables and dairy) may be more fully realized in household consumption only when they possess greater economic bargaining power. Furthermore, the lack of significant gender differences in the consumption of fried foods, SSBs and sweet snacks may stem from consumption contexts (e.g., shared family meals, eating out) that blur gender boundaries based on individual preferences.

Taken together, the SUR analysis corroborates and extends the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition findings. Females exhibit stronger preferences for plant-based foods (e.g., cereals, vegetables) and dairy products, while males show stronger preferences for animal-based foods (especially meat) and alcohol. This finding is consistent with the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition results. Although gender differences are not fully significant for some food categories, the SUR test provides systematic evidence supporting the gendered dietary preference hypothesis from two dimensions: the allocation differential associated with household income growth and the decision-making weight linked to individual economic contribution. This preference structure leads males and females onto distinct dietary upgrading pathways during economic development, ultimately contributing to the observed gender divergence in OWOB risks.

To further test the reliability of these conclusions, we conducted robustness checks by removing outliers and redefining the sample age range, as described in the previous section. The results, detailed in Tables A7–A10, confirm that our core findings are highly robust. The magnitude, sign and statistical significance of all decomposition parts exhibit remarkable stability.

Moderating effects of health literacy

Providing residents with information on balanced diets, nutrition, and diseases can theoretically enhance awareness of the importance of healthy weight and help alleviate rising OWOB rates (Upton et al., 2025). This section will first empirically examine the moderating effects of three proxy variables for information exposure: the presence of elderly individuals in the household, awareness of the dietary pyramid and awareness of the benefit of intaking less fat. It will then empirically test whether health literacy plays a differential role for males, assessing its capacity to alleviate gender-based health disparities. Furthermore, based on the possibility that individuals with higher incomes may have greater access to health information and more resources to apply health knowledge, whereas lower-income groups may prioritize immediate survival needs over long-term health behaviors, we proceed to empirically examine the heterogeneity of income in the effects of information exposure.

The main results are shown in Table 7, with detailed results available in Table A11. Specifically, as shown in column (1), the odds ratio for the old variable is 0.86.8 This suggests that individuals living in households with elderly members have approximately 14% lower odds of being OWOB compared to those in households without elderly members. Column (2) shows that for men: the presence of elderly family members is associated with a substantial 22%9 reduction in the odds of being OWOB. For females: The odds ratio of 0.9310 indicates a much weaker association, corresponding to only a 7% reduction in the odds of being OWOB. It suggests that exposure to health and disease information from elderly individuals and the increased emphasis on health influence the dietary structure of the household, leading to a more balanced diet among family members, particularly males. A potential explanation may stem from the fact that men tend to be less aware of dietary knowledge (due to physiology-driven factors and traditional gender roles that women responsible for food preparation), disease risks, and their interrelationships compared to women. As a result, co-residing with elderly family members may help compensate for this informational gap, thereby exerting a stronger influence on males’ health behaviors and outcomes.

We further examine the moderating effect using a more specific indicator of health literacy. The health literacy variable is constructed based on the level of agreement with the statement: “Eating less fatty meat and animal fat is beneficial to health.” A higher level of agreement corresponds to a higher value of the variable, reflecting greater health literacy. This indicates that higher health literacy is associated with a lower likelihood of OWOB among females, whereas no comparable effect is observed among males.

The weaker link between the last two health literacy and OWOB risk among males may stem from several factors. Socio-cultural norms that discourage males from changing masculine eating habits (e.g., high meat intake), coupled with their tendency not to prioritize health knowledge even when acquired, may explain the weaker link between health literacy and OWOB risk among males. In contrast, females generally exhibit greater concern and sensitivity toward information related to body weight, nutrition, and health. As a result, when they become aware of the Dietary Pagoda or recognize the health risk of high-fat diets, they are more likely to translate this knowledge into tangible changes in their eating behavior.

More direct and compelling information on the diet-disease link may be more effective for males, as shown in Column (2). A possible reason is that men often exhibit distinct patterns in health information processing and health behavior decision-making (Courtenay, 2000). Compared to more abstract or general nutritional knowledge, clear information that directly articulates the link between diet and disease may align better with the cognitive style of men, who tend to be more outcome-focused, efficiency-driven, and oriented toward solving concrete problems (Robertson et al., 2013). Additionally, communication within the family is typically more contextual, interactive, and emotionally supportive, which may deliver health information in a more natural and less threatening manner (Berkman et al., 2000). This approach may be more readily accepted by men and translated into behavioral change, making it potentially more effective than mere knowledge dissemination. Thus, family-based communication not only conveys information but may also more directly influence men's dietary environment and behavioral motivation by reshaping social norms and providing both supervision and support.

Finally, this paper examines whether income leads to heterogeneous effects in the influence of information exposure (see Column 3). Within households with elderly co-residents, the net marginal effect of males’ personal income is 0.25 (statistically significant).11 This indicates that although the health-information environment brought by the elderly constrains the OWOB-promoting effect of income among males (−0.07∗), such constraint is only partial, and OWOB risk among high-income males still rises significantly with income. For females living with the elderly, a one-unit increase in personal income (log) raises the log-odds of being OWOB by 0.14,12 meaning that income growth also elevates OWOB risk. However, compared with males, the effect size for females (0.14) is lower than that for males (0.25), and the difference is statistically significant (p = 0.02). These results suggest that while the health information provided by elderly co-residents can moderate the consumption choices of both males and females, it cannot fully offset the OWOB-promoting effect associated with higher income. The risk-enhancing effect is most pronounced among high-income males.

Conclusions and policy implications

Conclusions

The issue of OWOB is becoming increasingly severe worldwide. During this process, a differentiated growth trend has emerged in China. OWOB rates have reversed from higher among adult females to now exceeding among adult males. This trend aligns with the global transition observed across developing and developed nations. However, public sector and existing literature pay little attention to the reasons behind the gender differences and the reversal trend. OWOB increases the risk of various NCDs, making the gender disparity in OWOB an urgent problem.

We focus on food gender preferences in relation to OWOB. First, we theoretically analyze the mechanisms behind the formation of food gender preferences, clarifying their role in the reversal of OWOB gender disparities under varying development levels. Second, the study utilizes the Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition method to assess the existence of food gender preferences among Chinese adults and to analyze how these preferences shift food intakes under varying socio-economic conditions. Finally, we offer policy recommendations aimed at improving the increasingly severe OWOB problems, particularly for male populations, through an analysis of the moderating effects of improving health literacy.

Our empirical analysis reveals the following findings:

First, Gendered food preferences are evident among Chinese adults. Socio-cultural and physiologically driven factors contribute to gender differences in food preferences. Consistent with theories linking food to gender food stereotypes, societal preferences for body shape (Bouvier & Chen, 2021; Ekebas-Turedi et al., 2021; Feraco et al., 2024; Vartanian, 2015), adult males tend to prefer animal-based foods (especially meats) and specific types of high-energy-dense items (including SSBs, alcoholic drinks, and fried foods), while adult females show a stronger preference for plant-based foods (such as starchy foods, vegetables, and fruits) and dairy products.

Second, these food preferences dynamically explain the shift of the disparities in OWOB between males and females. In low-income contexts, females' preferences lead to higher consumption of starchy foods, resulting in excessive carbohydrate intake and thus elevating their OWOB risk. Males, by contrast, have limited purchasing power to fulfill their preference for energy-dense foods. Consequently, females bear a greater burden of OWOB at this stage. For instance, a typical low-income family might prioritize inexpensive staples like rice and noodles. Women, often responsible for meal preparation, tend to consume these starchy foods themselves while reserving small portions of meat or eggs for working males. This intra-household allocation means women's plant-based preference translates into excessive starchy food intake, while men's energy-dense preference remains largely unfulfilled. As income rises, females' diet shift towards increased consumption of fruits, vegetables, and dairy products, while their starchy foods intake declines. Conversely, males' preferences lead to greater consumption of energy-dense options, increasing their OWOB risk and ultimately surpassing that of females. At the household level, this asymmetry plays out as women, drawing on health information (accessed through reproductive services and their role as food gatekeepers) and responding to pressure to maintain body shape, increasingly incorporate healthier options into their meals. Men, meanwhile, finally fulfill suppressed preferences as households allocate more resources to meat and as their participation in social drinking is with economic activity.

Third, the expression of gendered food preferences is significantly shaped by the residential environment. The urbanization process amplifies females' preference for healthy foods through enhanced market accessibility, greater exposure to health information, and the modernization of gender roles. In contrast, males’ preference for traditional energy-dense foods is more pronounced in rural settings, highlighting the role of environmental context in shaping dietary behaviors (Swinburn et al., 2011).

Finally, enhancing knowledge about the relationship between diet and diseases can help mitigate the rising trend of OWOB and reduce the recent growing inequality in OWOB. While general knowledge of a balanced diet alone shows limited impact, specific literacy linking food choices to disease outcomes is associated with a stronger protective effect for males than for females, thereby reducing inequality. This aligns with evidence on the importance of actionable health literacy (Sanders et al., 2021). Furthermore, the analysis reveals that higher income does not automatically translate into healthier choices, even in the presence of health information from co-residing elders. For high-income males especially, the OWOB-promoting effect of increased income remains strong and only partially mitigated, suggesting that economic resources alone are insufficient to counteract obesogenic dietary preferences without targeted behavioral interventions (Marteau et al., 2012).

Discussion

Our findings on the moderating effect of diet-disease knowledge invite further reflection on the role of health information in shaping gendered OWOB disparities. In contemporary societies characterized by information saturation and complex food environments, health literacy plays a critical role in enabling individuals to filter health information, resist unhealthy dietary temptations, and maintain a healthy weight (Krause et al., 2018; Silva et al., 2023). However, access to such information is unevenly distributed. Adult females generally have greater exposure to nutrition knowledge due to regular medical check-ups associated with reproductive health (Bertakis, 2009) and their primary role in household food preparation (Aunan et al., 2019). This gendered pattern of information access may partly explain why women, despite facing similar obesogenic environments, have not experienced the same surge in OWOB as men in recent decades.

This interpretation aligns with our core finding that enhancing diet-disease knowledge can mitigate OWOB risk, particularly among males. Our analysis shows that while health literacy can partly curb the OWOB-promoting effect of income for men, its impact is not uniform across all groups. Among high-income males who do have access to such information, its effectiveness remains limited. This disconnect underscores that information alone is not sufficient; its effectiveness is moderated by both economic resources and the broader food environment (Dover & Belon, 2019). Higher-income groups may have more resources to act on health knowledge, but they also have greater means to afford energy-dense discretionary foods. This tension highlights the need for interventions that address both informational and structural drivers of dietary risk, a theme we develop in the policy implications below.

Policy implications

Accordingly, the study offers the following policy recommendations:

(1)Policy interventions should be differentiated based on the local level of economic development and the corresponding stage of transition in OWOB disparities. In high-income regions where adult males' OWOB rates significantly exceed adult females' rates, official dietary guidelines should incorporate evidence-based, male-specific recommendations. Rather than generic messages, concrete, behaviorally-focused advice should be provided. For instance, encouraging men to “replace SSBs with locally prevalent and culturally accepted healthy beverages (e.g., traditional herb tea, unsweetened barley tea, mint water, water), or “include vegetables in at least two meals per day”. These recommendations should be promoted through male-tailored communication channels and role models (e.g., sports figures), a strategy supported by research on gendered health communication (Kanter & Caballero, 2012). In low-income regions where females' OWOB rates remain relatively high, policies should pay close attention to the specific dietary constraints and nutritional needs without reinforcing harmful stereotypes, recognizing the complex socio-cultural determinants at play (Harris-Fry et al., 2015). Across all contexts, offline or online components should include gender-sensitive counseling protocols in primary healthcare settings.

(2)Develop and disseminate short-form video content on dominant platforms, co-designed with nutrition experts and behavioral scientists, to promote health literacy about the relationship between diet and disease (Chou et al., 2020). For male audiences, feature relatable influencers demonstrating quick, healthy recipes, while credible nutrition experts explain the health consequences of dietary choices (Silva et al., 2023). Short-form video platforms such as Douyin and Kuaishou have achieved high levels of penetration in China. By December 2025, the country's internet user base had reached 1.125 billion, representing over 80% of the population (CNNIC, 2026). Their algorithmic precision enables targeted messaging to specific segments including rural or high-income urban males, making them a powerful, low-cost tool for delivering gender-tailored health campaigns at scale. In less developed regions, campaigns should be gender-aware but may prioritize enhancing women's health literacy and delivering family-based nutrition education, while engaging men as supportive participants, akin to successful community-based models (Folkvord et al., 2020).

Urban policies can leverage females' health-oriented preferences to improve household dietary patterns, whereas rural interventions should focus on encouraging males to reduce consumption of fried foods and alcohol, while also enhancing overall access to healthy foods. This place-based approach aligns with the principles of creating healthier food environments (Swinburn et al., 2011). It also responds to China's rapid urbanization and urban-rural divides in food environments. In cities, widespread supermarkets and retail provide infrastructure that policies can build on to reinforce healthy choices. These recommendations are designed to provide concrete and actionable pathways for policymakers and health practitioners to address the escalating OWOB epidemic through gender-specific approaches. Such strategies contribute to more sustainable health outcomes, stronger public health systems, and reduced long-term socioeconomic and environmental burdens associated with obesity.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study is subject to three limitations. Firstly, the use of a binary variable for dietary pyramid awareness offers a clear but simplified measure of nutritional knowledge, failing to capture its depth or practical application. This may lead to non-differential misclassification bias, likely attenuating effect estimates. Secondly, our reliance on pre-2011 CHNS data represents a significant limitation for assessing contemporary dietary patterns. China has undergone profound socioeconomic and nutritional transitions over the past decades, including rapid urbanization, substantial increases in disposable income, and the widespread emergence of modern food retail environments. These changes have likely altered both food access and consumption norms in ways that our data cannot capture. Consequently, while our estimates provide a valuable historical benchmark, they may not fully reflect the current magnitude or nature of gender-based dietary disparities. As noted, higher incomes and evolving food environments could further enable the expression of gender-specific preferences, such as increased male-oriented consumption of energy-dense foods and alcohol, suggesting that actual disparities today are potentially larger than those reported here. Future studies should prioritize the use of recent nationally representative data and adopt more nuanced assessments to validate and extend these findings in the context of China's ongoing nutritional transition. Finally, it should be noted that the Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition is based on observational data, and its findings constitute associational evidence that does not support rigorous causal inference. The “unexplained difference” attributed to gender-specific preferences in this study still requires future confirmation of its causal effect through more rigorous research designs, such as Instrumental Variable (IV) approaches.

Notwithstanding these limitations, this study provides compelling evidence on the gendered nature of dietary preferences and their implications for the escalating obesity epidemic in China, offering evidence for more targeted and effective public health interventions.
