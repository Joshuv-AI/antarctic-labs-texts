Large Colonic Perineurioma Presenting During Evaluation of Chronic Abdominal Pain and Constipation
Authors: Srikaran Bojja, Michael Bebawy, Sameer Kandhi, Danial Shaikh, Ahmed Al-Khazraji, Harish K. Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Colonic perineuriomas (PNs), also known as fibroblastic polyps, are rare benign mesenchymal lesions. They are typically found in the distal colon, particularly in the rectosigmoid region. These lesions are usually asymptomatic and incidentally detected during routine screening colonoscopy. We present a 44-year-old woman with long-standing lower abdominal pain and constipation. Colonoscopy revealed a large (5 cm), multilobulated polyp, diagnosed as a colonic PN based on histopathologic and immunohistochemical findings.

INTRODUCTION

Perineurioma (PN) is an uncommon benign peripheral nerve sheath tumor originating from the perineurial cell of mesenchymal origin. Colorectal PNs, also known as fibroblastic polyps, are soft tissue PNs arising from the gastrointestinal tract. They are usually asymptomatic and are mostly discovered incidentally in patients undergoing routine screening colonoscopy.1 In this study, we present an interesting case of a 44-year-old woman with long-standing lower abdominal pain and constipation not responding to conventional laxative therapy. A colonoscopic examination revealed the presence of multilobulated polypoid lesions within the sigmoid colon. Histologic and immunohistochemical examination of the lesion confirmed the presence of PN. This represents one of only a few reported case reports of a large colonic PN (5 cm) presenting during evaluation of chronic abdominal pain and constipation, substantially exceeding the typical reported lesion size (<1 cm).

CASE REPORT

We report a case of a 44-year-old Hispanic immigrant woman from Spain who was seen in our Gastroenterology (GI) clinic for concerns of chronic persistent lower abdominal pain and constipation for about a year. The patient's medical comorbidities included remote history of poliomyelitis, hypertension, and uterine fibroids. She was referred to the GI clinic for evaluation of chronic lower abdominal pain and constipation unresponsive to over-the-counter laxatives.

During the initial clinic visit, the patient complained of chronic lower abdominal pain that was colicky in nature, intermittent with occasional radiation to the back. She reported bowel movements every 2 days, often requiring straining. However, she denied any overt bleeding episodes with bowel movements, weight loss, or familial history of colon cancers. She had been using over-the-counter stool softeners for some time with no significant improvement in her symptoms. Physical examination revealed a soft and nondistended abdomen with mild tenderness on palpation in the right lower, left lower, and suprapubic quadrants. No abdominal masses or organomegaly were appreciable on palpation. Auscultation of the abdomen revealed normal bowel sounds. A digital rectal examination revealed normal sphincter tone and brown stools in the rectal vault. Rest of the physical examination was within normal limits. Laboratory results showed hemoglobin concentration of 13.6 g/dL, mean corpuscular volume of 90.2 fL, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration of 33.6 g/L, all of which were within normal limits along with normal liver and thyroid function tests. The review of the patient's tumor markers showed a persistently elevated cancer antigen 125, with normal GI cancer antigen 19-9 and alpha-fetoprotein levels. She denied prior endoscopy or colonoscopy. She was started on bulk forming and osmotic laxatives, was advised to maintain a high-fiber diet, and return to the clinic in one month.

The patient returned to the GI clinic with minimal to no improvement of her symptoms, despite the laxative use, and was scheduled for a colonoscopy, which showed a large polypoid multilobulated nonobstructing lesion in the sigmoid measuring 5 cm in size (Paris classification type Is; Figures 1 and 2). No bleeding sites were appreciable during the endoscopic examination. Multiple biopsies were taken using cold forceps. Specimens were sent to pathology for further analysis.

Histologically, it was identified as bland mesenchymal lesion of the sigmoid colonic wall consistent with PN with overlying changes of adenoma. The lesion was also characterized by spindle cell to epithelioid cell proliferation in the lamina propria layer. Patchy positive staining was observed for smooth muscle actin, and strong positive staining was seen for glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1). Staining for cluster of differentiation 117 (CD117), CD1a, CD34, epithelial membrane antigen, pan cytokeratin, Desmin, vimentin, S100, CD68, CD45, CD10, human melanoma black 45, and Ki-67 were all negative. Additional stains for sex-determining region Y-related high-mobility group-box 10 (SOX10) protein, Mucin 4 glycoprotein, and Melan-A were negative.

Given the lesion's size, both advanced endoscopic resections such as endoscopic mucosal resection and endoscopic submucosal dissection were considered and surgical resection was discussed. Given the possibility of incomplete endoscopic resection requiring repeated endoscopic intervention after shared decision making, she elected for definitive surgical resection. The patient underwent sigmoid colectomy with good postoperative recovery. The patient's lower abdominal pain and constipation resolved on follow-up, 6 months after surgery.

DISCUSSION

Colorectal PNs are rare mesenchymal polyps, initially described as “benign fibroblastic polyps of the colon” in a 2004 case series of 14 patients by Eslami-Varzaneh reflecting the initial challenges recognizing their perineurial differentiation on histologic evaluation.2 With advancements in immunohistochemical techniques, these lesions were subsequently reclassified into “intestinal PNs” due to confirmed perineural features.3

The average age at diagnosis ranges from 56 to 64 years. Although no definitive gender predilection has been established, there might be a slight female predominance as reported in a case series.4

Patients with colonic PN are commonly asymptomatic and are often recognized incidentally on routine screening colonoscopy given their benign nature.1 Presenting with colonic obstruction is exceptionally rare, as these lesions are typically small (<1 cm) and noninfiltrative.3–5 Published reports of giant symptomatic colonic PNs remain exceedingly limited. This is contrasted to intestinal PN, which has been reported to cause intestinal obstruction and some instances of intussusception, especially with ileal lesions.6,7

Most of these lesions are detected on diagnostic and surveillance colonoscopy in patients with previous colonic polyps, colorectal carcinoma, gastrointestinal bleeding or occult blood in the stools, abdominal pain, persistent diarrhea, change of bowel habit, and thickening of large bowel wall on computed tomography scan. Although PN has been directly responsible for some of these presenting symptoms and signs, the associations might have been purely coincidental.4,8,9 Although causality cannot be definitively established, extensive evaluation including magnetic resonance imaging and biliary imaging failed to identify an alternative explanation for her symptoms. Furthermore, complete resolution following resection raises the possibility that the unusually large lesion contributed to her presentation. Elevated cancer antigen 125 has not been associated with colonic PN. Additional evaluation failed to identify an alternative pelvic or intra-abdominal malignancy and the elevation was considered nonspecific.

Endoscopically, PN typically appears as solitary, sessile, well-circumscribed mucosal lesions; however, reports of flat/sessile or rounded/pedunculated variants have also been described in the literature.3,10,11 Almost 75% of the reported lesions were found to have occurred in the sigmoid colonic wall or rectum. Less than 15% of cases were located proximal to the splenic flexure. The average size was 4.1 mm (range 1–30 mm).4 No evidence of recurrences or metastases has been reported to date; however, it is pretty common to find concurrent hyperplastic or adenomatous polyps elsewhere in the gastrointestinal tract of these patients. Our patient was found to have a large multilobulated polyp on endoscopic examination, which exceeded the mean size and was accompanied by a few hyperplastic polyps reported in other areas of the lower GI tract.

Histologic diagnosis of mesenchymal polyps such as PNs can be challenging as they are difficult to classify due to their low incidence and overlapping histologic features with other mesenchymal polyps.12 These lesions usually arise from the mucosa and superficial submucosa. The characteristic histologic appearance of the colonic perineurioma includes uniform spindled cells of perineurial origin (mesenchymal), with pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and indistinct cell borders, which proliferate and fill up the lamina propria, causing separation, distortion, and entrapment of adjacent colonic crypts. These cells often have a concentric arrangement around crypts or glands. In many cases, a thin zone of uninvolved, mildly inflamed lamina propria separated the perineurial proliferation from the overlying surface epithelium.9

Colonic PNs are often known to be associated with serrated lesions. No clear explanation has been established to explain this association; however, it was suggested that perineurioma might induce epithelial changes or that serrated lesions might promote perineurial proliferation in the stroma, or their existence could be purely coincidental. More recent theories suggested that these lesions were true mixed epithelial-stromal polyps involving epithelial-mesenchymal transition. Identification of B-Raf proto-oncogene (BRAF) V600E mutation through molecular studies exclusively in perineuriomas with serrated architecture and their absence in nonserrated variants further strengthened the notion that the epithelial components are actual serrated lesions. However, the exact nature (neoplastic or reactive) and origin of the perineurial stromal component remain controversial to date.13,14 No serrated architecture in the perineurioma was reported in our case. Interestingly, our patient's lesion demonstrated adenomatous changes rather than serrated epithelial architecture. This finding may represent a coincidental collision lesion or less common epithelial–stromal interaction, although the exact relationship remains uncertain. Regardless, the presence of adenomatous epithelium has important clinical implications, as postresection surveillance should follow standard adenoma surveillance recommendations rather than those for an isolated perineurioma.

Immunohistochemical analysis is another tool that commonly aids in the histologic diagnosis of these lesions. GLUT1 and claudin 1 showed strong and diffuse immunoreactivity in up to 90% of reported cases of colonic PNs. Although present in three-fourths of the reported cases, epithelial membrane antigen expression was often found to be focal and weak and could be missed. Collagen IV and vimentin expression demonstrated a major proportion of cases but are not pathognomonic for perineurioma. CD34 expression was reported to be limited and focal. Desmin and C-Kit (CD117) expressions were consistently negative, helping to exclude inflammatory myoglandular polyp and gastrointestinal stromal tumor, respectively. The presence of S100 protein expression should usually raise suspicion of a benign nerve sheath tumor over a perineurioma. Other negative immunohistochemical stains included broad-spectrum cytokeratins, h-caldesmon, CD31, B-cell lymphoma 2, cyclin D1, CD21, CD23, and CD35. Ki-67 proliferation index was less than 1% in these lesions.3,10,15,16 Our patient had strong immunoreactivity to GLUT1 expression establishing the diagnosis of perineurioma.

Colonic PNs are usually managed by endoscopic polypectomy if the lesions are small in size < 2–3 cm. However, extensive laterally spreading lesions, as reported in our case, might benefit from surgical evaluation for the removal of the polyps. It is essential to understand the risk of malignancy potential in BRAF-positive serrated variants of colorectal perineurioma. Such patients would benefit from surveillance colonoscopy at regular intervals as recommended for regular serrated lesions.1

Colonic PNs are rare, benign mesenchymal polyps that are usually asymptomatic and detected incidentally. However, as demonstrated in our case, large perineuriomas, although rare, may present with symptoms and sometimes require surgery. The pathognomonic histologic feature of the lesion consists of the proliferation of spindle cells within lamina propria that distort the adjacent intestinal crypts. Expression of GLUT1 and Claudin markers within the lesion confirms the diagnosis of colonic perineurioma. The presence of BRAF-positive serrated variants confers malignancy potential to these lesions and would therefore require periodic surveillance at regular intervals.
