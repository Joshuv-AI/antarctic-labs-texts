Surgical Treatment of Lateral Skull Base Tumors Extending to the Craniovertebral Junction
Authors: Songbo Xue, Yang Zhao, Huiying Sun, Hanlin Chen, Yong Zhao, Xin Ding, Wanru Zheng, Fei Ren, Yiwei Zhao, Jiajing Tong, Yu Chen, Zhiqiang Gao, Xu Tian, Siyi Cai, Guodong Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo evaluate the clinical manifestation, surgical strategies, and long‐term outcomes of lateral skull base tumors extending to the craniovertebral junction (CVJ), with a focus on the infratemporal fossa approach (IFA) and individualized treatment options for cervical‐occipital stability.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 12 patients with lateral skull base tumors extending to the CVJ who underwent surgical treatment between 2018 and 2024. Surgical resection via IFA (Type A, Type B, or Type A + Type B), with OCF performed when indicated by the extent of occipital condyle and C1–C2 resection.ResultsPrimary presenting symptoms included hearing loss (42%), headache (25%), and neck mass (17%). Imaging demonstrated that the original lateral skull base primary tumors often involved the temporal bone (92%), foramen magnum (92%), and jugular foramen (83%). Eight patients (67%) needed staged resections, while four (33%) patients underwent one‐stage surgery. Occipitocervical fusion (OCF) was performed in five patients (42%), and the others received rigid collar or Halo‐vest immobilization. Gross‐total resection was accomplished in 11 patients (92%); one patient (8%) received palliative treatment. No recurrence was observed in these cases during a median follow‐up of 45 months (range 6–78). Persistent posterior cranial nerve deficits occurred in 83% of the patients, and 33% developed transient aspiration pneumonia, both of which resolved with treatment.ConclusionIn a multidisciplinary context, the IFA, when combined with selective OCF, allows for complete resection and long‐term tumor control of complex lateral skull base–CVJ tumors. This approach achieves both oncologic radicality and biomechanical stability.Keywords: craniovertebral junction, infratemporal fossa approach, lateral skull base, occipitocervical fusion

Introduction

Lateral skull base tumors that extend to the craniovertebral junction (CVJ) pose one of the most formidable challenges in skull base surgery. These tumors are characterized by a deep anatomical location and close proximity to critical neurovascular structures, including the internal carotid arteries (ICA) and vertebral arteries (VA), posterior cranial nerves, brainstem, and atlanto‐occipital (AO) joint complex. Given these anatomical relationships, complete removal of the lesion is extremely challenging and exposes patients to the risk of both neurological and biomechanical complications [1, 2]. Preserving or restoring CVJ stability is of utmost importance; otherwise, it may lead to severe pain, limited mobility, and even life‐threatening brainstem compression [3, 4].

Traditional midline approaches, including the posterior suboccipital or transoral route, can offer access to specific regions of CVJ. However, they are restricted when dealing with tumors that have extensive lateral extension. The approaches often require significant neural retraction yet provide limited control over lesions involving the internal carotid artery or the jugular foramen. Endonasal endoscopic techniques can extend anterior access; however, they are laterally restricted. Moreover, when substantial bony resections are required, these techniques may undermine the stability of the cervical spine. Posterolateral and far‐lateral approaches are methods that enhance lateral exposure. Nevertheless, they increase the risk of vertebral artery injury and are less advantageous for cases involving simultaneous lateral skull base involvement [5, 6].

Since Fisch's original description, the infratemporal fossa approach (IFA) and its modifications have become a cornerstone for accessing the lateral and ventral skull base [7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. By redirecting the facial nerve and mobilizing the petrous internal carotid artery, the IFA allows wide extradural exposure of the jugular foramen, foramen magnum, and upper cervical spine with minimal brain retraction [12, 13]. Nevertheless, when tumors extensively invade the occipital condyle and axis‐atlas complex—the foundation of craniovertebral stability, a single surgical approach rarely allows the simultaneous attainment of radical tumor resection and functional preservation.

When the tumor invades the occipital condyle, C1, or C2, radical resection may lead to instability of the CVJ [14, 15]. Some researchers suggest that unilateral occipital condyle resection exceeding 50%–70% or combined bilateral loss ≥ 50% are indications for occipitocervical fusion (OCF) [5, 16]. The optimal integration of tumor resection, approach selection, and stabilization strategy has not been systematically explored [17].

This study represents our experience in managing patients with lateral skull base–CVJ tumors employing individualized IFA, with selective OCF based on preoperative and intraoperative assessments. This study aims to characterize the clinical and imaging features of these tumors, describe our surgical strategy integrating IFA with individualized stabilization, and evaluate long‐term oncologic and functional outcomes within a multidisciplinary treatment.

Methods

Study Design

A retrospective cohort study was conducted to evaluate the clinical characteristics, surgical approaches, and treatment outcomes of patients diagnosed with lateral skull base–CVJ tumors at Peking Union Medical College Hospital, from December 2018 to August 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the hospital (Approval No. 1‐25P1782).

Patients

The patients were enrolled in the study if they met the following criteria were as follows: (1) patients had lateral skull base tumors extending to the CVJ as confirmed by preoperative imaging, including high‐resolution/contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT), contrast‐enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and/or computed tomography angiography (CTA); (2) patients with tumors confined to either the lateral skull base or the CVJ without cross‐regional invasion. Patients were excluded if there was inadequate clinical, imaging, or follow‐up data. Clinical data were retrospectively retrieved from medical records, encompassing demographic information (age, sex, and side of lesion), primary presenting symptoms such as hearing loss, headache, or neck mass, and relevant medical history, including comorbidities and previous interventions.

Imaging and Diagnosis

All patients underwent a series of imaging examinations, namely high‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT), gadolinium‐enhanced MRI, and CTA. HRCT scans were utilized to assess the extent of bony involvement of critical structures, such as the mastoid, jugular foramen, foramen magnum, atlas, axis, vertebral artery, and other significant structures. MRI scans were employed to evaluate the soft tissue involvements and their relationship with adjacent structures. CTA could display the blood supply of the tumor, mainly involving the ICA and VA, and accurately indicate the relationship between the tumors and the surrounding blood vessels.

Surgical Approaches

The Surgical strategy was determined based on tumor's location, extent, and involvement of critical neurovascular structures. Fisch's IFAs, namely type A (IFA type A), type B (IFA type B), and type C (IFA type C), or a combination thereof, were chosen to achieve optimal exposure according to the tumor's location. Specifically, IFA type A were selected for lesions involving the jugular foramen, IFA type B for clival and petrous apex tumors, and IFA type C for ventral brainstem, cavernous sinus (CS), and eustachian tube lesions. When lateral skull base tumors involve the CVJ but have limited involvement of the dura, ICA, vertebral artery (VA) and CS, a single‐stage surgical procedure is employed. In another condition, if the patient is at high risk of cerebrospinal fluid leakage (CFL), such as in cases of the tumor intradural severe damage to the dura, this might lead to life‐threatening complication, most notably intracranial infection. Therefore, staged surgery is necessary to imply: Stage I for extradural tumor removal and ICA/VA control, Stage II for intradural dissection with brainstem/CS sinus management and watertight dural closure, and Stage III for OCF.

OCF was indicated when vertebral instability met any of the following criteria: resection involving more than 75% of unilateral AO or/and AA joints, or resection exceeding 50% bilaterally in the AO or/and AA joints. Patients who met these criteria were treated with OCF if cervical instability occurred during the same period or after follow‐up. In cases where OCF was not performed, postoperative management involved immobilization with a cervical collar, along with imaging follow‐up at 3, 6, and 12 months. Based on postoperative cervical CT scans, flexion and extension views were obtained to assess alignment, and patients were examined for neck pain or neurologic signs to determine if OCF would be performed. All surgeries were carried out by a multidisciplinary team (MDT), with otolaryngology–skull base surgeons responsible for tumor resection and orthopedic/neurosurgical teams performing CVJ stabilization when required. Margin status was primarily assessed intraoperatively using frozen‐section analysis and correlated with permanent pathology and postoperative imaging.

Outcome Measurements

Tumor control was assessed by the extent of resection and recurrence on follow‐up imaging. The primary outcome was recurrence‐free survival (RFS), which was defined as the absence of tumor regrowth on serial imaging. All patients were scheduled to receive postoperative clinical examinations and gadolinium‐enhanced MRI at 3 months, followed by imaging per 6–12 months. The median follow‐up duration was 45 months (ranging from 6 to 78 months).

The stability of CVJ was evaluated radiologically and clinically. Clinical evaluation included the presence of pain, the sensory and motor function of the limbs, and the range of motion of the cervical spine. The functions of cranial nerves were evaluated as follows: the function of the facial nerve was graded by the House–Brackmann (HB) scale, and deficits in the lower cranial nerves were assessed based on swallowing ability, phonatory function, and other complications (e.g., aspiration, pneumonia).

Result

Patient Demographics and Symptoms

A total of 12 patients (9 female and 3 male) with lateral skull base tumors involving the CVJ were enrolled in this study (Table 1). The mean age was 38 years (range 20–58 years). Clinical symptoms varied by tumor location. The most common initial complaints were hearing loss in five patients (42%), followed by headache in three patients (25%), and a palpable neck mass in two patients (17%). Other symptoms noted in individual cases included tinnitus, hoarseness, dysphagia, and facial paralysis. The tumors exhibited diverse histopathological features. The most common diagnoses were paraganglioma (3 cases, 25%) and meningioma (3 cases, 25%), followed by chondrosarcoma (2 cases, 17%). Less common pathologies (each accounting for 8% with one case) included schwannoma, endolymphatic sac tumor (ELST), intravascular myofibroblastoma, and a chronic fibrous inflammatory pseudotumor. Histopathological grading was further reviewed in the revised analysis. Both chondrosarcomas were low‐grade (grade I), and all three meningiomas were WHO grade I.

Critical skull base structures were frequently affected: the foramen magnum in 12 patients (100%), the temporal bone in 11 patients (92%), and the jugular foramen region in 10 cases (83%). Surgical Outcomes

Eight patients (67%) underwent pre‐planned multi‐stage resections, typically in two or three stages. This approach was adopted to ensure adequate tumor removal while effectively managing the risk to critical structures (e.g., ICA, VA, dura, cerebellum, and brainstem). One patient (Case 5) had only a subtotal (palliative) resection due to the extent of disease and patient factors, with the goal of alleviating symptoms rather than complete excision. An IFA served as the primary approach for all tumors. IFAs were used either independently or in combination at various stages, providing extensive lateral skull base exposure. OCF for CVJ stabilization was required in five patients (42%) because of significant condylar or cervical spine involvement (Table 2).

In three patients (25%), invasion of the skull base by the tumor necessitated resection of more than 75% of the unilateral occipital condyle. Three patients underwent partial or complete removal of the lateral mass of the atlas (C1), and one patient required partial resection of the axis (C2) to achieve clear surgical margins. Dural invasion was confirmed in nine cases (75%). In those with intradural extension, the dura was incised, and gross‐total resection of the intradural tumor component was performed during stage II surgery. In patients with intradural extension, the dura was incised for tumor removal and subsequently reconstructed to achieve watertight closure. Dural repair was performed using fascia lata in five patients, temporalis fascia in one patient, and artificial dura in one patient, depending on the size and location of the dural defect. In the remaining cases with only minor intraoperative CFL, direct suture closure was sufficient.

Major blood vessels were frequently involved by the tumor but were successfully managed in all cases. ICA encasement by tumor (~270°–360°) was observed in six patients (50%). In five of these cases, the ICA was carefully dissected free from the tumor without injury. In one case, a segment of tumor‐encased ICA required resection followed by vascular graft reconstruction (a surgical video of this reconstruction is provided in the Supporting Information). VA was encircled (~180°) by tumor in three patients (25%); all VAs were preserved through meticulous microsurgical dissection, and none required sacrifice or bypass.

Tumors invasion of the jugular foramen region was associated with significant involvement of the posterior cranial nerve. In 10 patients (83%), posterior cranial nerves had to be sacrificed or were rendered nonfunctional during tumor removal. Anatomical preservation was achieved through meticulous dissection in only two cases (17%). Despite the high rate of lower cranial nerve sacrifice, swallowing‐related complications did occur in the early postoperative period. Ten patients required early nutritional support via nasogastric tube because of severe dysphagia, and four patients developed aspiration pneumonia. These acute postoperative complications resolved after appropriate treatment and did not result in chronic pulmonary sequelae at the last follow‐up.

Follow‐Up and Recurrence

In this study, 12 patients were followed up for 6–78 months postoperatively (median follow‐up of 45 months). The tumor control rate was 100% (11/11): One patient (Case 5, palliative treatment) underwent regular surveillance and showed no tumor progression throughout follow‐up, while the remaining 11 cases showed no recurrence. The longest‐surviving patient (Case 3) has remained recurrence‐free for 78 months.

Regarding functional outcomes, 10 cases (83.3%) had long‐term posterior cranial nerve dysfunction (e.g., dysphagia and hoarseness), primarily attributable to posterior cranial nerve resection. Nasogastric feeding was not used routinely at the time of surgery. Instead, it was instituted selectively in the early postoperative period in patients with severe dysphagia or high aspiration risk. In addition to lower cranial nerve dysfunction, the decision was influenced by swallowing safety, cough effectiveness, adequacy of oral intake, and overall nutritional status. Ten patients with severe dysphagia received early nutritional support via nasogastric feeding tube. Due to impaired swallowing, four patients (33%) developed aspiration pneumonia in the early postoperative period. Each case was managed promptly with antibiotics and supportive care, resulting in full recovery without chronic pulmonary complications.

With regard to survival outcomes, all patients except Case 5—who received palliative treatment—survived without evidence of recurrence. Overall, aside from anticipated cranial nerve deficits related to tumor invasion and resection, postoperative complications remained manageable. Adjuvant therapy was considered on an individual basis according to final pathology, extent of resection, and multidisciplinary discussion. In this series, only one patient with chondrosarcoma received postoperative radiotherapy, while no additional adjuvant treatment was administered in the remaining cases. All patients attained spinal stability by the last follow‐up.

Discussion

In this series of 12 patients with lateral skull base tumors involving the CVJ, complete tumor resection was achieved in 11 cases. Despite anatomic complexity, stability of the CVJ was preserved or restored in all patients—including five who underwent fusion—and no tumor recurrences were observed over a median follow‐up of 45 months. However, posterior cranial nerve deficits were common, reflecting the difficulty of tumor removal in this region. These outcomes preliminarily suggest that the combined surgical approach used in this study is both safe and effective for managing such complex lesions (Figure 3).

One of the major challenges in CVJ surgery is choosing the optimal surgical approach. Traditional midline approaches, such as the transoral route, provide direct anterior access to the odontoid and clivus but carry a high infection risk and can be limited for lateral disease [18]. Endoscopic endonasal techniques offer a less invasive midline corridor to the CVJ, yet may not reach far lateral extensions and still risk CSF leak or instability in extensive cases [14, 19]. The posterior approach allows for the management of various posterior located lesions, including meningiomas, schwannomas, and intraspinal tumors, but carries a risk of neurovascular injury during dissection process [20].

The far‐lateral or extreme‐lateral approaches enhance the surgical angle of attack to the CVJ. However, they carry an increased risk of vertebral artery injury and provide no distinct advantage in cases with significant lateral skull base involvement (e.g., ICA encasement) is present [21]. In contrast, the lateral IFA provides extensive exposure to both the ventral and lateral aspects of the CVJ through an extradural route. By rerouting the facial nerve and mobilizing the internal carotid artery as needed, this approach offers excellent visualization of the jugular foramen, foramen magnum, and upper cervical vertebrae simultaneously. It minimizes brain retraction compared to posterior approaches and avoids transoral contamination risk. The findings of this study support these advantages: using the infratemporal approach, we achieved gross‐total resection in all cases with acceptable morbidity [9, 22].

Another critical consideration in these cases is the timing and indication for the CVJ stabilization through fusion. Determine the optimal timing and criteria for OCF is critical, with the primary goal being the prevention or correction of postoperative craniovertebral instability. Prior studies suggest that craniovertebral stability is at risk when > 50% of a unilateral occipital condyle is resected (or ~50% combined bilateral condyle loss) [5]. In such scenarios, immediate OCF is advised to prevent late instability. Similarly, if the tumor resection involves critical stabilizers like the C1 anterior arch or odontoid process, fusion is generally recommended [4, 23, 24].

In our series, a relatively conservative threshold for fusion was adopted, tailored to individual stability and symptomatic presentation. OCF was indicated when vertebral instability met any of the following criteria: resection involving more than 75% of a unilateral atlanto‐occipital or atlanto‐axial joint (AA), or resection exceeding 50% bilaterally in the AO or AA joints. This criterion was informed by multidisciplinary discussion and aligns with Kooshkabadi et al.'s experience of managing CVJ stability after resection [25].

Instead, management involved rigid cervical collar immobilization and close monitoring. Postoperative assessment included cervical CT scans and flexion and extension views of the cervical spine to evaluate alignment, alongside clinical evaluation for neck pain or neurologic signs. Asymptomatic patient with radiologic stability underwent continued surveillance with serial imaging at 3, 6, and 12 months. None of these patients developed delayed instability under this protocol, supporting the deferral of fusion in selected borderline cases with structured follow‐up.

Furthermore, it is emphasized that when combining skull base tumor resection with cervical fusion, complete tumor removal should precede spinal instrumentation. In case 3, initial performance of cervical fusion resulted in a constrained surgical corridor, substantially complicating subsequent tumor resection. The presented findings underscore the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in managing CVJ tumors. This collaborative strategy allowed us to simultaneously accomplish radical resection and structural reconstruction—a combination necessary for optimizing patient outcomes [26, 27]. The results indicate that lateral skull base approaches can achieve outcomes comparable to or better than traditional midline approaches for appropriately selected CVJ tumors. By utilizing the IFA, excellent oncologic control was achieved (no recurrences) while addressing stability issues with selective fusion. This combined approach expands the surgeon's armamentarium for CVJ tumors that extend laterally, offering a safe and effective alternative route.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although it reflects the rarity and anatomical complexity of lateral skull base–CVJ tumors treated with IFA, the cohort size remains modest (n = 12). There was no contemporaneous control group using far‐lateral corridors or other approaches. Second, as a single‐institution series, surgical expertise and outcomes may differ at other centers. Third, although postoperative complications were documented, prospective administration of validated instruments to quantify posterior cranial nerve function or occipitocervical outcomes (e.g., quality of life rating scale) was not performed. Lastly, the follow‐up duration, while median 45 months, may be insufficient to detect late recurrences or long‐term complications. In addition, the cohort was histopathologically heterogeneous, including both benign and malignant lesions with distinct biological behaviors, surgical goals, and recurrence risks, which may limit the generalizability of the oncologic and functional outcomes.

Conclusion

For lateral skull base tumors extending to the CVJ, a combined treatment approach utilizing a temporal fossa approach and an individualized OCF strategy, implemented within a MDT framework, represents an effective therapeutic option that balances radical tumor resection with functional reconstruction.
