Surgical management and outcomes analysis of lower lumbar (L3–L5) burst fractures: A single-center experience and meta-analysis
Authors: Sandeep Mishra, Nidhisha Sadhwani, Abhishek Kumar, Kanwaljeet Garg, Deepak Agrawal, Pankaj Kumar Singh, G. D. Satyarthee, Deepak Gupta, P. Sarat Chandra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Lower lumbar (L3–L5) burst fractures are uncommon injuries with distinct clinical and biomechanical characteristics. We evaluated injury patterns, surgical management, and outcomes in patients treated at a single center and performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to assess neurological and functional outcomes reported in the literature.Materials and Methods:We retrospectively reviewed surgically treated L3–L5 burst fractures between January 2015 and July 2022. A Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses-compliant systematic review of studies published from January 1993 to January 2026 was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library.Results:Eighteen patients (mean age: 28.1 ± 9.1 years; 77.8% male) were included. Falls from height were the most common mechanism (61.1%), with L4 most frequently involved (61.1%). All fractures were AO Spine Type A (61.1% A3; 38.9% A4); 50% had posterior column injury, and 61.1% had Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score ≥5. Preoperatively, 55.5% had neurological deficits and 27.8% had bowel/bladder dysfunction. All underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fixation; 66.7% received long-segment fixation, 33.3% short-segment fixation, and 27.8% corpectomy with anterior column reconstruction. Surgery significantly improved vertebral body height, sagittal alignment, and canal diameter. At a mean follow-up of 3.8 years, 66.7% achieved ASIA Grade E, with no neurological deterioration. Complications occurred in 11.1% and 94.4% reported minimal disability (Oswestry Disability Index). Meta-analysis of 16 studies (248 patients) demonstrated pooled neurological improvement of 53% (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.25–0.79) and return-to-work (RTW) of 85% (95% CI, 0.76–0.91).Conclusion:Surgical treatment produced favorable radiological and functional outcomes, with neurological improvement in 53% of patients and an 85% RTW rate.

INTRODUCTION

Lower lumbar (L3–L5) burst fractures (LBFs) are uncommon injuries with distinct anatomical and biomechanical characteristics. Although burst fractures account for 10%–20% of all spinal fractures, involvement of the lower lumbar spine is rare.[1] Its larger vertebral bodies, physiological lordosis, robust paraspinal musculature, and pelvic stabilization create distinct injury patterns and management challenges compared with thoracolumbar junction fractures.

These injuries usually result from high-energy trauma, such as falls from height or motor vehicle accidents, and are characterized by vertebral comminution, canal compromise, and neurological deficits. Despite the relatively spacious lower lumbar canal, severe canal narrowing or posterior column injury can cause radiculopathy, cauda equina syndrome, and bowel or bladder dysfunction. Surgical decision-making depends on fracture morphology, posterior ligamentous complex integrity, canal compromise, neurological status, and stability as assessed by the AO Spine classification and Thoracolumbar Injury Classification and Severity Score (TLICS).

Because lower lumbar burst fractures are rare, the available evidence is limited and largely comprises small retrospective series. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating neurological and functional outcomes after surgical treatment of L3–L5 burst fractures. We also present a retrospective series of 18 surgically treated patients to further define injury patterns, surgical management, and clinical outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case series

A retrospective review was conducted of patients who underwent surgical treatment for L3–L5 burst fractures at a Level I trauma center between January 2015 and July 2022. Patients with additional spinal fractures or those managed nonoperatively were excluded. Demographic, clinical, radiological, operative, and follow-up data were retrieved from the institutional database. Clinical variables included age, sex, mechanism and level of injury, posterior column involvement, neurological status (ASIA grade), and associated injuries. Radiological assessment included pre- and postoperative vertebral body height, canal diameter, fusion status, and implant integrity.

Surgical strategy was based on AO Spine fracture classification, posterior column integrity, and the degree of canal compromise. All patients underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fixation. The choice between short- and long-segment fixation depended on fracture morphology and stability, while corpectomy with anterior column reconstruction was reserved for patients with retropulsed fragments not amenable to indirect decompression. Functional outcome was assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), and neurological recovery was evaluated by changes in ASIA grade between presentation and final follow-up. Surgical details and postoperative complications were also recorded.

Literature review

Search strategy and screening

The systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines [Figure 1]. A systematic search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and the Cochrane Library was performed for studies published between January 1993 and January 2026 using the terms lower lumbar burst fracture, L4 burst fracture, L5 burst fracture, lumbar decompression, fixation, fusion, posterior spinal instrumentation, neurological outcome, radiological outcome, and functional outcome. The literature search was performed independently by two reviewers.

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses flowchart showing the systematic search and study selection process

Titles, abstracts, and full-text articles were independently screened by two reviewers (SM and KG). Disagreements were resolved by the consensus. Reference lists of included studies were screened for additional eligible articles, and authors were contacted when clarification or missing data were required. No language restrictions were applied.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible studies were original articles (prospective, retrospective cohort, or case–control) published between January 1993 and January 2026 that included adult patients undergoing surgical treatment for L3–L5 burst fractures. Case reports, review articles, commentaries, studies including additional spinal injuries, and conservatively managed patients were excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted the data on study characteristics, patient demographics, injury patterns, surgical techniques, follow-up duration, neurological, radiological, and functional outcomes, and postoperative complications. Neurological recovery was categorized as complete, partial, or unchanged, and return-to-work (RTW) status was recorded when available. Subgroup analyses were performed according to surgical technique (PSIF, AC + PSIF, and PC + PSIF). Variables unavailable in the original studies were recorded as not reported (NR).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the meta, metafor, and dmetar packages. Single-arm random-effects meta-analyses were conducted to estimate pooled neurological improvement and RTW rates using the DerSimonian–Laird method after logit transformation of proportions. Studies with event rates of 0% or 100% were excluded from quantitative synthesis. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic, with the values of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. Forest plots and funnel plots were generated to assess pooled outcomes and publication bias, respectively, and sensitivity analyses evaluated the influence of individual studies. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Case series

A total of 18 patients met the inclusion criteria. The mean age was 28.1 ± 9.1 years (range, 16–52 years), with a male predominance (77.8%). Falls from height (61.1%) were the most common mechanism of injury, followed by road traffic accidents (38.9%). L4 was the most frequently affected level (61.1%), followed by L3 (22.2%) and L5 (16.7%). Associated injuries were present in 38.9% of patients, predominantly long bone fractures, with less frequent thoracic and abdominal injuries [Table 1]. Posterior column involvement was present in 50% of patients. All fractures were classified as AO Spine Type A (61.1% Type A3 and 38.9% Type A4), with TLICS scores ranging from 4 to 8; 61.1% of patients had a TLICS score ≥5, indicating surgical instability [Table 1].

At a mean follow-up of 3.8 years (range: 3 months–7 years), neurological status improved or remained stable in all patients. Twelve patients (66.7%) achieved ASIA E, while persistent deficits remained in six patients (ASIA A, n = 2; ASIA B, n = 2; ASIA C, n = 2); no patient experienced postoperative neurological deterioration [Table 2]. Two patients (11.1%) developed postoperative complications, including one implant-related infection requiring hardware removal and one case of persistent postoperative pain. Functional outcomes were favorable, with 94.4% of patients reporting minimal disability on the ODI.

Meta-analysis

A total of 5049 articles were identified through database searches [Figure 1]. After removing duplicates, 820 records underwent title and abstract screening, of which 58 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Sixteen studies (Mick 1993–Mallepally 2022), comprising 248 patients, met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [Table 3]. Most were retrospective studies (9 studies, 56.25%), followed by case series (4, 25%), prospective studies (2, 12,5%), and one comparative cohort.

Clinical characteristics

The mean age of patients across the included studies was 33.77 ± 5.67 years (one study, Kim 2010, did not report age). Mean follow-up duration was 23.3 ± 16.4 months (range: 10–70 months), with the maximum follow-up of 70 months reported by Butler et al.[15] Motor vehicle accidents were the most common mechanism of injury (70.8%), followed by falls (29.2%). Multi-level L3–L5 fractures were the predominant injury pattern (51.6%), followed by isolated L5 fractures (45.2%), while L4–L5 fractures were uncommon (3.2%) [Table 3].

Surgical approach and complications

Posterior spinal instrumentation and fixation (PSIF) alone was the most common surgical technique, performed in 203 of 248 patients (81.9%), followed by anterior corpectomy with PSIF (13.3%), posterior corpectomy with PSIF (3.6%), and standalone anterior corpectomy (1.2%). Overall, 36 complications occurred in 248 patients (14.5%). The complication rate was highest following PSIF alone (16.7%) and lowest after anterior corpectomy with PSIF (3.0%), while no complications were reported after standalone anterior corpectomy, although this group was very small.

Neurological and functional outcome

Nearly half of the patients (49.7%) experienced loss of lordosis, whereas 38.0% showed improvement. Vertebral body height loss occurred in 57.6% of patients, with improvement in 24.8%. Among 159 patients with neurological follow-up, 54.1% demonstrated neurological improvement, including complete recovery in 40.9% and partial recovery in 13.2%, while 45.9% remained unchanged. RTW data, reported in eight study groups, showed that 79.6% of patients RTW (median 83.2%; range, 52.6%–100%).

Pooled analysis

A single-arm random-effects meta-analysis of 14 study arms demonstrated a pooled neurological improvement rate of 0.53 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.25–0.79), with moderate heterogeneity (Q = 21.91, P = 0.057; I2 = 40.7%) indicating that approximately 53% of patients showed neurological recovery after treatment [Figure 3a]. Eight study arms reported RTW outcomes, yielding a pooled rate of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.76-0.91) with no significant heterogeneity (Q = 3.75, P = 0.808; I2 = 0%) indicating that about 85% of patients returned to work after treatment [Figure 3b].

DISCUSSION

Lower lumbar burst fractures (L3–L5) are uncommon and differ biomechanically and clinically from thoracolumbar junction injuries, which are more frequently associated with neurological deficits and sagittal malalignment.[2]

Demographics and injury mechanism

The young mean age (28.1 years) and male predominance (77.8%) in our cohort reflect the typical epidemiology of high-energy spinal trauma in developing countries.[3] Falls from height (61.1%) were the leading injury mechanism, followed by road traffic accidents (38.9%), consistent with Motten et al., who also reported falls as the predominant cause (61.8%).[3] This pattern likely reflects greater occupational and recreational exposure of young males to high-energy trauma.

Associated injuries

Associated injuries occurred in 38.9% of patients, most commonly long bone fractures (27.8%), followed by thoracic and abdominal injuries (11.1% each). Although lower than the 68.5% reported by Dai,[4] these findings emphasize the need for comprehensive trauma evaluation. The lower rate of laparotomy for visceral injuries compared with previous studies may be attributable to improved trauma imaging, increased nonoperative management of solid organ injuries, or selection bias in retrospective surgical series.[4] The frequent association of lower LBFs with calcaneal and lower-extremity fractures, consistent with the findings of Motten et al., supports an axial-loading mechanism.[3] Green et al. observed abdominal injuries in 50% of flexion-distraction fractures caused by seatbelt trauma, highlighting the importance of mechanism-specific injury patterns.[5]

Fracture distribution and morphology

L4 was the most commonly affected level, followed by L3–L4 and L5, consistent with the caudal decline in burst fracture incidence reported by Motten et al. and reflecting the biomechanical transition from the rigid thoracolumbar junction to the more mobile, load-sharing lower lumbar spine.[3] All fractures were AO Spine Type A, with Type A3 predominating. However, the high proportion of Type A4 fractures and posterior column involvement (50%) indicate high-energy trauma and mechanical instability, correlating with the marked canal compromise and neurological deficits observed in our cohort. These findings support surgical stabilization even in selected Type A3 fractures with posterior column injury.

Neurological deficits

Neurological deficits occurred in 55.5% of patients, challenging the traditional view that lower lumbar burst fractures carry a low neurological risk because of the wider spinal canal. Severe canal compromise (4-6 mm) explains the high rate of neural injury. Radiculopathy (22.2%), bowel/bladder dysfunction (27.8%), and complete ASIA A injuries (11.1%) reflected significant cauda equina involvement. These findings are consistent with previous reports. Ramieri et al. demonstrated that L5 burst fractures can cause substantial neural compromise despite anatomical differences from the thoracolumbar junction.[6] Similarly, Cavagnaro et al., Motten et al., and Seybold et al. reported neurological deficits in 50%, 35.3%, and 42.8% of patients, respectively.[378] These results indicate that lower LBFs, particularly Type A4 injuries with marked canal compromise, carry a substantial risk of neurological injury.

Surgical management strategy

Surgical indications generally include neurological deficit with significant canal compromise, marked kyphosis, or substantial vertebral body height loss.[9] The primary goals are neural decompression, restoration of spinal alignment, and stable fixation while preserving as many motion segments as possible. Although anterior and combined approaches allow direct decompression and anterior column reconstruction, they are associated with greater morbidity.[10] Consequently, current practice favors a single posterior approach with transpedicular decompression and pedicle screw fixation, which provides effective decompression and stabilization while avoiding the morbidity of staged procedures.[111213]

All patients in our series underwent posterior spinal instrumentation and fixation, with long-segment fixation (66.7%) used more frequently than short-segment fixation (33.3%). This likely reflects the high prevalence of posterior column injury (50%), Type A4 fractures (38.9%), and neurological deficits (55.5%), which favored more stable constructs. Only 27.8% of patients required corpectomy with anterior column reconstruction for severe vertebral body comminution, persistent canal compromise, or major anterior column deficiency. The favorable outcomes in the remaining patients suggest that posterior fixation with indirect decompression is adequate for most lower LBFs with incomplete neurological injury, consistent with previous evidence.[2] Although short-segment fixation preserves lumbar motion, long-segment fixation may provide superior biomechanical stability in highly unstable lower lumbar fractures and was therefore preferred in our cohort.[14]

Radiological and neurological outcomes

Postoperative imaging demonstrated effective deformity correction and indirect neural decompression, with improved vertebral body height, restoration of sagittal alignment, and increased canal diameter. Although previous studies have reported variable improvement in lumbar lordosis following posterior fixation, most agree that posterior instrumentation effectively restores vertebral body height and achieves adequate canal decompression.[15161718]

Neurological outcomes were favorable, with 66.7% of patients achieving ASIA Grade E at final follow-up and no postoperative neurological deterioration. Persistent deficits in 33.3% of patients, including two with ASIA A injuries, likely reflected the severity of the initial trauma rather than inadequate surgical management. These findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating neurological improvement without postoperative deterioration following surgical treatment.[37] Our meta-analysis demonstrated a pooled neurological improvement rate of 53%, supporting surgery as an effective treatment for unstable lower LBFs with neurological compromise. Despite moderate heterogeneity among studies, more than half of patients experienced neurological recovery, highlighting the significance of timely decompression and stabilization while also emphasizing the need for standardized outcome reporting.[1519]

Complications and functional outcomes

Complications occurred in 11.1% of patients, including implant-related infection requiring hardware removal (5.6%) and persistent postoperative pain (5.6%). The infection rate was comparable to previous reports and likely related to prolonged operative time, extensive exposure for long-segment fixation, and polytrauma.[7] No implant failure, pseudoarthrosis, or loss of correction was observed, suggesting adequate biomechanical stability with long-segment fixation. Although Cavagnaro et al. reported a higher complication rate (37.5%), including infection, pseudoarthrosis, and hardware failure, our findings support the safety and durability of this approach.[7]

Functional outcomes were favorable, with 94.4% of patients reporting minimal disability at a mean follow-up of 3.8 years, consistent with the good-to-excellent outcomes reported by Motten et al. demonstrating that surgical stabilization achieves durable functional restoration despite severe initial injury.[3] Our meta-analysis of eight study arms demonstrated a pooled RTW rate of 85%. RTW rates varied across studies, ranging from 52.6% to 100%, highlighting the heterogeneity among the included studies.[820] The higher pooled RTW rate (85%) compared with the neurological improvement rate (53%) should be interpreted cautiously. RTW was reported in fewer studies (8 vs. 14 study arms) and reflects the entire surgical cohort rather than only patients with neurological deficits. Moreover, RTW is influenced by occupational demands, rehabilitation, and socioeconomic factors, and patients may resume work despite incomplete neurological recovery. Therefore, RTW should not be considered a surrogate marker of neurological outcome.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The case series was limited by its small sample size, retrospective single-center design, absence of a nonoperative comparison group, and incomplete radiological data. Variability in referral times precluded the analysis of surgical timing. In addition, surgeon preference and institutional protocols may have also influenced treatment decisions, limiting generalizability. The meta-analysis predominantly included small retrospective studies with heterogeneous injury patterns, surgical techniques, and outcome reporting. Inconsistent reporting of neurological and functional outcomes limited quantitative analysis, and publication bias could not be formally assessed.

CONCLUSION

Lower LBFs (L3–L5) are uncommon but severe injuries that predominantly affect young adults following high-energy trauma and are frequently associated with neurological deficits. Posterior spinal instrumentation and fixation achieved effective deformity correction, favorable neurological and functional outcomes, with an acceptable complication rate. Our meta-analysis demonstrated pooled neurological improvement and RTW rates of 53% and 85%, respectively. These findings support early surgical stabilization for unstable lower lumbar burst fractures, with anterior column reconstruction reserved for selected cases with severe vertebral body comminution or anterior column deficiency.
