Computing functions of A − 1 ​ B A^{-1}B where A A and B B are Hermitian matrices
Dario A. Bini, Massimiliano Fasi, Bruno Iannazzo

Abstract
We consider the numerical evaluation of the quantity A ​ f ​ ( A − 1 ​ B ) Af(A^{-1}B) , where A A is Hermitian positive definite, B B is Hermitian, and f f is a function defined on the spectrum of A − 1 ​ B A^{-1}B . We study the conditioning of the problem, and we introduce several algorithms that combine the Schur decomposition with either the matrix square root or the Cholesky factorization. We study the numerical behavior of these algorithms in floating-point arithmetic, assess their computational costs, and compare their numerical performance. Our analysis suggests that the algorithms based on the Cholesky factorization will be more accurate and efficient than those based on the matrix square root. This is confirmed by our numerical experiments. Keywords. primary matrix function; positive definite matrix; Cholesky factorization; Schur decomposition; matrix square root; Kubo–Ando mean; matrix geometric mean

Introduction

A function
f:\mathcal{I}\subset\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, can be extended to diagonalizable matrices with eigenvalues in
\mathcal{I}
as a primary matrix function.
The concept is thoroughly discussed in the monograph by Higham
[
14
]
, to which we refer the reader.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian positive definite and let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian. The matrix
A^{-1}B
is diagonalizable and has eigenvalues, and one can leveraging the concept of primary matrix function to give meaning to the expression

\varphi(A,B):={}Af(A^{-1}B),

(1.1)

where the function
f
is defined on the spectrum of
A^{-1}B
.

It is often convenient to consider the formulation

\varphi(A,B)=A^{1/2}f(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})A^{1/2},

(1.2)

where
A^{1/2}
is the principal square root of
A
, which is equivalent to (
1.1
) but has a symmetric form.

Here, we consider the problem of computing
\varphi(A,B)
in the most efficient way, taking into account both the numerical stability and the computational cost of the computation.

In the literature, expressions like (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) appear frequently in applied mathematics. Kubo and Ando
[
18
]
use the formulation (
1.2
), for
B
positive definite, to define means of positive definite operators (and in particular matrices) in a systematic way.
Notable cases are the arithmetic, harmonic, and geometric means, which are obtained for

f(x)=\frac{1+x}{2},\quad f(x)=\frac{2x}{1+x},\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)=\sqrt{x},

respectively.
Setting
f(x)=x^{t}
, for
t\in[0,1]
, yields the weighted geometric mean

A\#_{t}B:=A(A^{-1}B)^{t},

and the weighted power mean of two matrices is obtained with
f(x)=\bigl((1-t)+tx^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}
for
p\neq 0
. We refer the reader to the recent survey
[
7
]
for a discussion of geometric means of two matrices.

Looking at the problem from a slightly different viewpoint, Parlett
[
22
, sect. 2]
considers
A
and
B
with no structure and discusses methods to compute
f(A^{-1}B)
without forming
A^{-1}
. The application considered by Parlett is the solution of the system of ordinary differential equations

Bu^{\prime}(t)=Au(t),\qquad u(0)=u_{0},

whose explicit solution is
u(t)=\exp(A^{-1}Bt)u_{0}
. Similar functions arise in the numerical solution of differential-algebraic equations
[
19
]
.

Functions like
f(A^{-1}B)
or
\varphi(A,B)
are related to the matrix pencil
B-\lambda A
which appears in the generalized eigenvalue problem
(B-\lambda A)x=0
. When
A
is nonsingular, the generalized eigenvalue problem is equivalent to the standard eigenvalue problem
(A^{-1}B-\lambda I)x=0
. For this reason Parlett
[
22
]
refers to the expression
f(A^{-1}B)
as a function of the pencil
B-\lambda A
.

Two pencils are spectrally equivalent if they have the same sets of eigenvalues.
The pencil
\widetilde{B}-\lambda\widetilde{A}
is spectrally equivalent to
B-\lambda A
if there exist two invertible matrices
P
and
Q
such that
\widetilde{B}=PBQ
and
\widetilde{A}=PAQ
.
The function
\varphi
is compatible with pencil equivalence, as shown by property
(b)
of the following
Theorem

1
, which summarizes the properties of
\varphi

Theorem 1
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be a nonsingular Hermitian matrix, let
B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
be Hermitian, and let

\varphi(A,B):={}Af(A^{-1}B)
.
Then, for any nonsingular
P,Q\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, we have:

(a)

Af(A^{-1}B)=f(BA^{-1})A
;

(b)

P\varphi(A,B)Q=\varphi(PAQ,PBQ)
(compatibility with pencil similarity); and

(c)

\varphi(\alpha A,\alpha B)=\alpha\varphi(A,B)
, for
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
(homogeneity).

In the analysis of (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), it is often useful to swap the position of

and

\widehat{f}(x):={}xf(x^{-1})

The dual of
\widehat{f}
is

2
Preliminaries

For a complex function
f
, we define the divided difference

f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]:={}\begin{cases}\dfrac{f(\lambda_{j})-f(\lambda_{i})}{\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i}},&\mbox{ if }\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda_{j},\\
f^{\prime}(\lambda_{i}),&\mbox{ if }\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}.\end{cases}

Given
A\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
,
we denote by
\opvec(A)
the vector of length
mn
that stacks the columns of
A

from left to right, and by
A\otimes B
the Kronecker product of
A
and
B
. We will use the property
\opvec(AXB)=(B^{T}\otimes A)\opvec(X)
, which holds whenever the product
AXB
is defined.

A linear map
L:\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{p\times q}
, can be represented by a matrix
M\in\mathbb{C}^{(pq)\times(mn)}
such that
\opvec(L[H])=M\opvec(H)
for any
H\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}.

In this case, we say that
M
represents
L
in the vec basis—or that
M
is the Kronecker form of the map
L
.

This form will be used for the Fréchet derivatives of primary matrix functions.
A primary matrix function obtained from the complex function
f
is continuously differentiable at
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
if it is sufficiently smooth, for instance analytic, at the eigenvalues of
A
; see
[
14
, Thm. 3.8]
for the precise statement. We denote by
Df(A):\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
the Fréchet derivative of
f
at
A
. Thus
Df(A)[H]
is the directional derivative of
f
at
A
in the direction
H\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, namely

Df(A)[H]=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\bigl(f(A+\varepsilon H)-f(A)\bigr).

(2.1)

We recall two properties of the Fréchet derivative of primary matrix functions. First, (
2.1
) implies the commutativity with similarities, that is,

M^{-1}Df(A)[H]M=Df(M^{-1}AM)[M^{-1}HM],

(2.2)

where
f
is differentiable at
A
and
M
is invertible. Second, the Daleckiĭ–Kreĭn theorem
[
14
, Thm. 3.11]
,

Df\bigl(\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\bigr)[H]=F\circ H,\qquad F_{ij}=f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}],

(2.3)

holds, where
A\circ B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
denotes the component-wise (Hadamard) product of
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.

Throughout this work, we use the Frobenius norm, denoted by
\|\cdot\|_{F}
, and the 2-norm, denoted by
\|\cdot\|
, with its condition number
\cond(A)=\|A\|\|A^{-1}\|
.
As is customary, we denote by
u
the unit roundoff of the working floating-point arithmetic.
We use a dot to signify that a relation only holds to the first order, ignoring higher terms in
u
. Therefore,
\doteq
and
~\dot{\leq}~
denote first-order equivalence and inequality, respectively.

3
Conditioning

If
f
is differentiable in a neighborhood of
A^{-1}B
, then the function
\varphi(A,B)=Af(A^{-1}B)
is differentiable in a neighborhood of
(A,B)
. We can measure the sensitivity of
\varphi(A,B)
with respect to perturbations using the relative Frobenius-norm condition number
[
23
]
of
\varphi(A,B)
, which is

\hbox{cond}(\varphi;A,B):={}\frac{\|D\varphi(A,B)\|_{F}\|[A\ B]\|_{F}}{\|\varphi(A,B)\|_{F}},

(3.1)

where
D\varphi(A,B)
is the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
at
(A,B)
and

\|D\varphi(A,B)\|_{F}:={}\max_{[H\ L]\neq 0}\frac{\|D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]\|_{F}}{\|[H\ L]\|_{F}}.

The following result provides an explicit expression for the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
, whereby an explicit expression for the condition number can be obtained.
This will allow us to derive useful bounds.

Lemma 2
.

Let
A,B\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, with
A
invertible, and let
f
be a function differentiable at
A^{-1}B
. Then the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi(A,B):=Af(A^{-1}B)
in the direction
[H\ L]
, with
H,L\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is

D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]=Hf(A^{-1}B)+ADf(A^{-1}B)[-A^{-1}HA^{-1}B+A^{-1}L].

(3.2)

If
B
is invertible, then the function
\widehat{f}(x):={}xf(x^{-1})
is differentiable at
B^{-1}A
and

D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]=BD\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)[B^{-1}H]+ADf(A^{-1}B)[A^{-1}L].

(3.3)

Proof.

Let
\zeta(A,B):={}A^{-1}B
. Using the chain rule and the product rule yields

\begin{split}D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]&=Hf(A^{-1}B)+ADf(A^{-1}B)[D\zeta(A,B)[H,L]]\\
&=Hf(A^{-1}B)+ADf(A^{-1}B)[-A^{-1}HA^{-1}B+A^{-1}L].\end{split}

(3.4)

If
B
is invertible, then the eigenvalues of
B^{-1}A
are the reciprocals of those of
A^{-1}B
. Thus,
\widehat{f}
is differentiable at
B^{-1}A
, and applying the identity (
3.4
) to the function
\psi(B,A):={}B\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)=\varphi(A,B)
,
we obtain

D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]=D\psi(B,A)[L,H]=L\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)+BD\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)[-B^{-1}LB^{-1}A+B^{-1}H].

(3.5)

Finally,

\displaystyle D\varphi(A,B)[H,L]

\displaystyle=D\varphi(A,B)[H,0]+D\varphi(A,B)[0,L]

\displaystyle=BD\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)[B^{-1}H]+ADf(A^{-1}B)[A^{-1}L],

where we have used (
3.5
) for the first summand and (
3.4
) for the second.
∎

If we work in the vec basis and assume that
A
and
B
are positive definite, then a more explicit expression can be given for the derivative of
\varphi
.

Lemma 3
.

In the notation of
Lemma

2
, let
A
and
B
be positive definite, and let
Z_{1}
and
Z_{2}
be such that
Z_{1}^{-1}A^{-1}BZ_{1}=\diag(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})=:{}\Lambda
and
Z_{2}^{-1}B^{-1}AZ_{2}=\diag(1/\lambda_{1},\ldots,1/\lambda_{n})=\Lambda^{-1}
. Then,

\opvec(D\varphi(A,B)[H,L])=[M_{2}\ M_{1}]\opvec([H\ L]),

where

M_{1}=(Z_{1}^{-T}\otimes AZ_{1})\varDelta^{(1)}(Z_{1}^{T}\otimes(AZ_{1})^{-1})

and

M_{2}=(Z_{2}^{-T}\otimes BZ_{2})\varDelta^{(2)}(Z_{2}^{T}\otimes(BZ_{2})^{-1})
.
The diagonal matrices
\varDelta^{(1)}
and
\varDelta^{(2)}
satisfy

\varDelta^{(1)}_{(i-1)\ell+j,(i-1)\ell+j}=f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}],

and

\varDelta^{(2)}_{(i-1)\ell+j,(i-1)\ell+j}=\widehat{f}[1/\lambda_{i},1/\lambda_{j}],

for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.
In particular, with

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}_{1}

\displaystyle=A^{-1/2}Q_{1},\qquad Q_{1}^{*}A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}Q_{1}=\Lambda,

\displaystyle\widehat{Z}_{2}

\displaystyle=B^{-1/2}Q_{2},\qquad Q_{2}^{*}B^{-1/2}AB^{-1/2}Q_{2}=\Lambda^{-1},

where
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
are unitary, we obtain

M_{p}=(\widehat{Z}_{p}^{-T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{p}^{-*})\varDelta^{(p)}(\widehat{Z}_{p}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{p}^{*}),\qquad p=1,2.

(3.6)

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to take the vec of (
3.3
) and manipulate its right-hand side, starting from
ADf(A^{-1}B)[A^{-1}L]
.
By (
2.2
), we get

\displaystyle ADf(A^{-1}B)[A^{-1}L]

\displaystyle=AZ_{1}Df(Z_{1}^{-1}A^{-1}BZ_{1})[Z_{1}^{-1}A^{-1}LZ_{1}]Z_{1}^{-1}

\displaystyle=AZ_{1}Df(\Lambda)[(AZ_{1})^{-1}LZ_{1}]Z_{1}^{-1},

and the Daleckiĭ–Kreĭn theorem (
2.3
) implies that

Df(\Lambda)[(AZ_{1})^{-1}LZ_{1}]=F\circ\bigl((AZ_{1})^{-1}LZ_{1}\bigr),\qquad F_{ij}=f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}].

Using the properties of the vec operator, we can write

\begin{split}\opvec(ADf(A^{-1}B)[A^{-1}L])&=(Z_{1}^{-T}\otimes(AZ_{1}))\opvec\bigl(F\circ((AZ_{1})^{-1}LZ_{1})\bigr)\\
&=(Z_{1}^{-T}\otimes(AZ_{1}))\diag(\opvec(F))(Z_{1}^{T}\otimes(AZ_{1})^{-1})\opvec(L)\\
&=M_{1}\opvec(L),\end{split}

(3.7)

where we have used the fact that
\varDelta^{(1)}=\diag(\opvec(F))
.
Repeating the same argument for
\opvec(BD\widehat{f}(B^{-1}A)[B^{-1}H])
and using
Lemma

2
completes the first part of the proof.

For the second part, we use the first part and observe that: if
\widehat{Z}_{1}:={}A^{-1/2}Q_{1}
, then
\widehat{Z}_{1}
diagonalizes
A^{-1}B
and
A\widehat{Z}_{1}=\widehat{Z}_{1}^{-*}
; and if
\widehat{Z}_{2}:={}B^{-1/2}Q_{2}
, then
\widehat{Z}_{2}
diagonalizes
B^{-1}A
and
B\widehat{Z}_{2}=\widehat{Z}_{2}^{-*}
.
∎

In order to bound the condition number, we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 4
.

In the notation of
Lemma

3
, the following bounds hold

\|M_{1}\|\leq\mu(A)\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]|,\qquad\|M_{2}\|\leq\mu(B)\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|\widehat{f}[1/\lambda_{i},1/\lambda_{j}]|.

Proof.

Using equation (
3.6
), we obtain

\|M_{p}\|\leq\mu(\widehat{Z}_{p}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{p}^{*})\|\varDelta^{(p)}\|,

for
p=1,2
.
We have that

\|\widehat{Z}_{1}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{1}^{*}\|=\|\widehat{Z}_{1}^{T}\|\|\widehat{Z}_{1}^{*}\|=\|\widehat{Z}_{1}\|^{2}=\|A^{1/2}\|^{2}=\|A\|,

and analogous identities hold for
\|\widehat{Z}_{1}^{-T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{1}^{-*}\|
,
\|\widehat{Z}_{2}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{2}^{*}\|
, and
\|\widehat{Z}_{2}^{-T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{2}^{-*}\|
. Therefore, we have that

\mu(\widehat{Z}_{1}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{1}^{*})=\mu(A)

and

\mu(\widehat{Z}_{2}^{T}\otimes\widehat{Z}_{2}^{*})=\mu(B).

Noting that the 2-norm of a diagonal matrix is the maximum modulus of its diagonal elements concludes the proof.
∎

Using
Lemma

4
, we can easily bound the Frobenius norm of the Fréchet derivative of
\varphi
, since

\begin{split}\|D\varphi(A,B)\|_{F}&=\|[M_{2}\ M_{1}]\|\leq\sqrt{\|M_{1}\|^{2}+\|M_{2}\|^{2}}\\
&\leq\sqrt{\mu(A)^{2}\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]|^{2}+\mu(B)^{2}\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|\widehat{f}[1/\lambda_{i},1/\lambda_{j}]|^{2}}.\end{split}

Similar bounds involving only
\mu(A)
or
\mu(B)
can be obtained. An example is

\|D\varphi(A,B)\|_{F}\leq\mu(A)\sqrt{\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]|^{2}+\max_{i,j=1,\ldots,n}|f(\lambda_{j})-f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]\lambda_{j}|^{2}}.

(3.8)

If
f
and
\widehat{f}
are convex and monotone, then the bounds simplify, since

\max|f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}]|=f^{\prime}(\lambda_{M})\text{ and }\max|\widehat{f}[1/\lambda_{i},1/\lambda_{j}]|={\widehat{f}}^{\prime}(1/\lambda_{m}),

where
\lambda_{m}
and
\lambda_{M}
are the smallest and the largest eigenvalues of
A^{-1}B
, respectively.
For instance, using (
3.8
) with

yields the bound
[
7
, eq. 8]
on the condition number of the matrix geometric mean.

4.1
Background results

We recall some standard results concerning rounding error analysis of basic matrix operations. We refer the reader to the monographs by Higham
[
13
,
14
]
for more background and context on these results.

By definition,
\||A|\|_{F}=\|A\|_{F}
, and one can show that
\|A\|_{F}\leq\sqrt{n}\|A\|
, that
\|\hbox{vec}(A)\|=\|A\|_{F}
, and that
\|I\otimes A\|=\|A\otimes I\|=\|A\|
.
If
C=AB
, then taking the 2-norm of both sides of the identity
\opvec(C)=(I\otimes A)\opvec(B)=(B^{T}\otimes I)\opvec(A)
gives

\|C\|_{F}\leq\|A\|\|B\|_{F},\quad\|C\|_{F}\leq\|A\|_{F}\|B\|.

(4.1)

Now consider the square root
X=A^{1/2}

of a positive definite matrix
A
,
and denote by
\widetilde{X}=\hbox{fl}(A^{1/2})
the matrix computed in
floating-point arithmetic by means of the
Schur algorithm. The error analysis on

[
13
, p. 139]
shows that

\widetilde{X}^{2}=A+\varDelta,\quad\|\varDelta\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,c\,n^{3}\|X\|_{F}^{2},

(4.2)

where
c
is a small positive constant and
u
is the unit roundoff of the working precision. In other words, the computed matrix
\widetilde{X}
is the exact square root of a slightly perturbed matrix
A+\varDelta
.

For the Cholesky factorization
A=R_{A}^{*}R_{A}
of a positive definite matrix
A
, a direct inspection shows that

U:={}R_{A}^{-*}A^{1/2}
and

U^{-1}=A^{-1/2}R_{A}^{*}
are both unitary. Since
R_{A}^{-*}=UA^{-1/2}
, this fact implies that

\|R_{A}^{-*}\|=\|A^{-1/2}\|,\quad\|R_{A}^{*}\|=\|A^{1/2}\|,\quad\cond(R_{A}^{*})=\cond(A^{1/2}).

(4.3)

Moreover, if we denote by
\widetilde{R}_{A}

the matrix obtained by computing the Cholesky factorization in floating-point arithmetic, from
[
13
, Thm. 10.5]
, we obtain

\widetilde{R}_{A}^{*}\widetilde{R}_{A}=A+H,\quad|H|~\dot{\leq}~u(n+1)dd^{*},\quad d_{i}=a_{ii}^{1/2}.

(4.4)

Therefore, the factor
\widetilde{R}_{A}
is the exact Cholesky factor of a slightly perturbed matrix
A+H
.
Noting that
\|dd^{*}\|_{F}=\|dd^{*}\|=d^{*}d=\hbox{trace}(A)=\|A^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}
, that
\|A^{1/2}\|^{2}=\|A\|
, and that
\|A^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}\leq n\|A\|
, we can use (
4.4
) to obtain the bound

\|H\|_{F}=\|\widetilde{R}_{A}^{*}\widetilde{R}_{A}-A\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u(n+1)\|A^{1/2}\|_{F}^{2}\leq un(n+1)\|A\|.

(4.5)

For the matrix multiplication
X=AB
, if we denote by
\widetilde{X}=\hbox{fl}(AB)
the product computed in floating-point arithmetic, from
[
13
, eq. (3.13)]
we have

|\widetilde{X}-X|~\dot{\leq}~u\,n|A|\cdot|B|,

(4.6)

whence
we get

\|\widetilde{X}-X\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,n\|A\|_{F}\|B\|_{F}\leq u\,n^{\frac{3}{2}}\|A\|_{*}\|B\|_{*}
, where one of the two norms
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is the Frobenius norm and the other is the Euclidean norm.
A straightforward computation based on (
4.1
) yields

\|\hbox{fl}((AB)C)-ABC\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~2u\,n^{\frac{3}{2}}\|A\|_{*}\|B\|_{*}\|C\|_{*},

(4.7)

where one of the three
\|\cdot\|_{*}
above is
\|\cdot\|_{F}
and the remaining two are Euclidean.

We can compute
X=T^{-1}B
, where
T
is triangular, by substitution. Let
\hbox{fl}(T^{-1}B)
denote the value obtained by running the back substitution algorithm in floating point arithmetic, from
[
13
, Thm. 8.10]
we have

\hbox{fl}(T^{-1}B)=T^{-1}B+E_{1},\quad|E_{1}|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(T)^{-1}|B|,\quad\sigma_{n}=n^{2}+n+1,

(4.8)

where
M(T)
is the M-matrix whose entries have the same moduli as the corresponding entries of
T
.
From (
4.1
), we deduce that

\|E_{1}\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}\|M(T)^{-1}\|\cdot\|B\|_{F}\leq\sigma_{n}\sqrt{n}\|M(T)^{-1}\|\cdot\|B\|
.
For
CT^{-*}
we analogously obtain
\hbox{fl}(CT^{-*})=\hbox{fl}(T^{-1}C^{*})^{*}=(T^{-1}C^{*}+E_{2})^{*}
, where

|E_{2}|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(T)^{-1}|C^{*}|
.

Setting
C=T^{-1}B
, we can bound the error in the computation of
(T^{-1}B)T^{-*}=CT^{-*}
. We have

\begin{split}&\hbox{fl}\bigl((T^{-1}B)T^{-*}\bigr)\doteq(T^{-1}B+E_{1})T^{-*}+E_{2}^{*}=T^{-1}BT^{-*}+E_{1}T^{-*}+E_{2}^{*},\\
&|E_{1}|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(T)^{-1}|B|,\qquad|E_{2}|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(T)^{-1}|T^{-1}B|^{*}.\end{split}

In other words,

\hbox{fl}\bigl((T^{-1}B)T^{-*}\bigr)=T^{-1}BT^{-*}+E
, where

E=E_{1}T^{-*}+E_{2}^{*}
.
Using (
4.1
), we get

\|\hbox{fl}\bigl((T^{-1}B)T^{-*}\bigr)-T^{-1}BT^{-*}\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~2u\,\sigma_{n}\|T^{-1}\|\cdot\|M(T)^{-1}\|\cdot\|B\|_{F}.

(4.9)

For
B=I
, equation (
4.8
) becomes

|\hbox{fl}(T^{-1})-T^{-1}|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(T)^{-1},

(4.10)

and taking norms yields

\|\hbox{fl}(T^{-1})-T^{-1}\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}\|M(T)^{-1}\|_{F}\leq u\,\sqrt{n}\sigma_{n}\|M(T)^{-1}\|.

(4.11)

Consider the inversion of a positive definite matrix
A
using the identity
A^{-1}=R_{A}^{-1}R_{A}^{-*}
, where
R_{A}
is the Cholesky factor of
A
. Let
\widetilde{R}_{A}
denote the computed Cholesky factor that satisfies
\widetilde{R}_{A}^{*}\widetilde{R}_{A}=A+H
in (

) and (
4.5
). If
\widetilde{V}:={}\hbox{fl}(\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1})
, then by (
4.10
) we have
\widetilde{V}=\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1}+E
, where

|E|~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}M(R_{A})^{-1}
. Moreover, denote
\fl(A^{-1})=\fl(\widetilde{V}\widetilde{V}^{*})
.
Relying on (
4.6
), we find that

\hbox{fl}(A^{-1})=\hbox{fl}(\widetilde{V}\widetilde{V}^{*})=(\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1}+E)(\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-*}+E^{*})+W,\quad|W|~\dot{\leq}~u\,n|\widetilde{V}|\cdot|\widetilde{V}^{*}|\doteq u\,n|\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1}|\cdot|\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-*}|,

so that

K:={}\fl(A^{-1})-A^{-1}\doteq R_{A}^{-1}E^{*}+ER_{A}^{-*}-A^{-1}HA^{-1}+W
.
Taking norms, we get

\|K\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~2\|R_{A}^{-1}\|\cdot\|E\|_{F}+\|A^{-1}\|^{2}\cdot\|H\|_{F}+\|W\|_{F}.

Since
\|E\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,\sigma_{n}\|M(R_{A})^{-1}\|_{F}
,
\|W\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,n\|\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1}\|_{F}^{2}
, and
\|R_{A}^{-1}\|^{2}=\|A^{-1}\|
,
from (

) we obtain

\begin{split}&\|K\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~u\,\|A^{-1}\|\bigl(n(n+1)\cond(A)+2\sigma_{n}\psi(R_{A})+n^{2}\zeta\bigr),\\
&\psi(R_{A}):=\frac{\|M(R_{A})^{-1}\|_{F}}{\|R_{A}^{-1}\|},\quad\zeta=\|\widetilde{R}_{A}^{-1}\|^{2}/\|A^{-1}\|.\end{split}

(4.12)

Observe that, since
\widetilde{R}_{A}
is an approximation of
R_{A}
and
\|R_{A}^{-1}\|^{2}=\|A\|
, one expects that
\zeta\approx 1
.

the last two terms in (
4.21
) can be written as

\widehat{Q}f(\widehat{\Lambda})\widehat{Q}^{*}-S_{4}=f(\widetilde{S}_{3})-f(S_{3})\doteq Df(S_{3})[F_{3}],

where
Df(S_{3})[F_{3}]

is the Fréchet derivative of
f(X)
at
X=S_{3}
with increment
F_{3}
. Thus, we get

\begin{split}F_{4}&\doteq E_{4}+E_{Q}f(\Lambda)\widehat{Q}^{*}+\widehat{Q}f(\Lambda)E_{Q}^{*}+\widehat{Q}\left(f^{\prime}(\Lambda)E_{\Lambda}+E_{f}\right)\widehat{Q}^{*}+Df(S_{3})[F_{3}].\end{split}

(4.22)

Finally,
for
F_{5}=\widetilde{S}_{5}-S_{5}
we get

\begin{split}F_{5}&=E_{5}+S_{1}(S_{4}+F_{4})S_{1}^{*}-S_{5}=E_{5}+S_{1}F_{4}S_{1}^{*}.\end{split}

(4.23)

Taking the Frobenius norm on both sides, and using (
4.1
) and

\|S_{1}\|\cdot\|S_{1}^{*}\|=\|A\|
, we get the inequality
\|F_{5}\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~\|E_{5}\|_{F}+\|A\|\|F_{4}\|_{F}
. Using (
4.22
) and the fact that

\|\widehat{Q}\|=\|\widehat{Q}^{*}\|=1
, we obtain

\begin{split}\|F_{5}\|_{F}&~\dot{\leq}~\|E_{5}\|_{F}+\|A\|\bigl(\|E_{4}\|_{F}+2\|E_{Q}\|\cdot\|f(\Lambda)\|_{F}\\
&\qquad+\|f^{\prime}(\Lambda)E_{\Lambda}+E_{f}\|_{F}\bigr)\\
&\qquad+\|A\|\cdot\|Df(S_{3})[F_{3}]\|_{F}.\end{split}

(4.24)

Using the bounds (
4.14
) in the above expression together with (
4.16
) yields

\begin{split}&\|F_{5}\|_{F}~\dot{\leq}~\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2},\\
&\mathcal{E}_{1}=u\,\|A\|\bigl(2(\theta_{2}+\theta_{4})\|f(\Lambda)\|_{F}+(\theta_{3}+\theta_{4})\|f^{\prime}(\Lambda)\|_{F}\bigr),\\
&\mathcal{E}_{2}=u\|A\|\cdot\|F\|_{F}\cdot\|F_{3}\|_{F},\end{split}

(4.25)

where
F=(f[\lambda_{i},\lambda_{j}])_{ij}
,
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2}
and the quantity
\|F_{3}\|_{F}
is bounded in (
4.19
).
To show that
\mathcal{E}_{2}
bounds
\|A\|\cdot\|Df(S_{3})[F_{3}]\|_{F}
, note that the Daleckiĭ–Kreĭn theorem implies that
Df(S_{3})[F_{3}]=Q(F\circ(Q^{*}F_{3}Q))Q^{*}
, where
Q^{*}Q=I
, and thus

\begin{split}\|Df(S_{3})[F_{3}]\|_{F}=\|Q(F\circ(Q^{*}F_{3}Q))Q^{*}\|_{F}=\|F\circ(Q^{*}F_{3}Q)\|_{F}\leq\|F\|_{F}\cdot\|F_{3}\|_{F}.\end{split}

(4.26)

Here, we have used the property
\|F\circ(Q^{*}F_{3}Q)\|\leq\|F\|\cdot\|Q^{*}F_{3}Q\|=\|F\|\cdot\|F_{3}\|
,
which holds for any unitarily invariant norm
[
5
, Fact
11.10.96]
.
From (
4.26
) and (
4.19
), we obtain the desired bound on
\|F_{5}\|_{F}
.

Therefore, for the overall error, we get the bound

\mathcal{D}_{1}+\|F_{5}\|_{F}\leq\mathcal{E}_{0}+\mathcal{E}_{1}+\mathcal{E}_{2},

(4.27)

where
\mathcal{E}_{0}
is defined in (
4.17
) and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are defined in (
4.27
).

6
Conclusions

We studied the problem of computing
Af(A^{-1}B)
when
A
and
B
are Hermitian matrices of moderate size with
A
positive definite. We studied the conditioning of the problem and we considered two methods. The first approach computes the square root of
A
and uses the equivalent expression
A^{1/2}f(A^{-1/2}BA^{-1/2})A^{1/2}
. The other approach is the Cholesky–Schur algorithm, which computes the Cholesky factor
R_{A}
of
A
and relies on the equivalent expression
R_{A}^{*}f(R_{A}^{-*}BR_{A}^{-1})R_{A}
. We also proposed a variant of the Cholesky–Schur algorithm that computes the Cholesky factorization of
B
, if the latter is positive definite.

We analyzed the computational cost of the algorithms in terms of the floating-point operations required, and we carried out an error analysis to assess their numerical stability in finite arithmetic. We confirmed our analysis by means on numerical experiments. We found that the Cholesky–Schur algorithm is well suited to this problem and should be the algorithm of choice for it, as it outperforms the other approaches (including the naive computation of
Af(A^{-1}B)
by forming
A^{-1}B
).

This work does not address the related problem of computing
Af(A^{-1}B)v
, where
A
and
B
are large and sparse matrices and
v
is a vector. The algorithms developed here would not be appropriate in that case, because the matrix
Af(A^{-1}B)
is typically dense, too large to store in memory, and not needed in the application. These problems require the development of different techniques, such as those based on the Krylov subspace and on quadrature, which will be the topic of future work.
