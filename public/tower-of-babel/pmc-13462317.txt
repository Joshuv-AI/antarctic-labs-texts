Dose-dependent effects of radiotherapy on body composition, nutritional status, and clinical symptoms in head and neck cancer
Authors: Meltem Öztürk İyilikci, Derya Hopancı Bıçaklı, Özlem Özkaya Akagündüz, Beril Balcı Topuz, Mustafa Esassolak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Radiotherapy (RT) and chemoradiotherapy (CRT) for head and neck cancer (HNC) frequently result in significant malnutrition, muscle wasting, and systemic inflammation, which can compromise treatment tolerance and clinical outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the dose–volume effects of RT, symptom burden, and treatment modality on nutritional status, body composition, and inflammatory markers in HNC patients. Eighty-five patients with HNC undergoing adjuvant or definitive RT/CRT were prospectively evaluated at baseline, at the end of treatment, and 6–12 weeks post-treatment. Nutritional status was assessed using the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA), anthropometric measurements, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), handgrip strength, and laboratory parameters (albumin, prealbumin, transferrin, CRP, hemoglobin, and lymphocytes). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed to identify RT dose–volume thresholds associated with clinically significant weight and muscle loss. The prevalence of malnutrition increased from 33.3% at baseline to 65.3% at the end of treatment and remained high at 55.6% during follow-up. 42% of patients experienced ≥ 4.6% weight loss, and 49% had ≥ 4.8% muscle mass loss. Higher RT doses (> 65 Gy), larger high-dose volumes (> 48 cc), and greater symptom burden were significantly associated with clinically relevant deterioration in body composition. Patients requiring nutritional support showed greater weight and muscle loss; however, this likely reflects confounding by indication, as these patients had more advanced disease and higher baseline symptom burden. CRP levels increased significantly during treatment, while hemoglobin and lymphocyte counts decreased. RT for head and neck cancer causes substantial and persistent deterioration in nutritional status, muscle mass, and inflammatory markers. RT dose–volume parameters and symptom burden are important predictors of nutritional decline. Early, individualized nutritional intervention combined with effective symptom management is essential to improve treatment outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51384-3.

Introduction

Head and neck cancers (HNCs) are among the most challenging malignancies worldwide, often leading to severe impairments in swallowing, chewing, speech, and nutrition. At diagnosis, a substantial proportion of patients already present with malnutrition, sarcopenia, and systemic inflammation due to the disease itself, poor dietary intake, and associated risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption1,2.

Radiotherapy (RT), with or without concurrent chemotherapy (CRT), remains a cornerstone of curative treatment for HNC. While modern intensity-modulated radiotherapy techniques have improved target conformity and reduced toxicity to surrounding tissues, treatment-related adverse effects—including mucositis, dysphagia, xerostomia, dysgeusia, and anorexia—frequently result in profound nutritional deterioration. Malnutrition and sarcopenia are observed in up to 80% of HNC patients during treatment and are independently associated with reduced treatment tolerance, increased complications, unplanned treatment interruptions, and poorer survival3–5.

Progressive weight loss and muscle wasting during RT can also alter planned dose distribution, potentially compromising tumor control and necessitating adaptive replanning. Despite these well-recognized challenges, the quantitative relationship between radiotherapy dose–volume parameters and the severity of nutritional decline remains insufficiently characterized. Most previous studies have focused on clinical factors or qualitative toxicity, with limited attention to objective dose–volume thresholds6,7.

The present prospective study was designed to systematically evaluate the trajectory of nutritional status, body composition, muscle function, and inflammatory markers in HNC patients undergoing RT/CRT. Specifically, we aimed to1 determine the prevalence and severity of malnutrition and sarcopenia before, during, and after treatment2, identify critical radiotherapy dose and volume thresholds associated with clinically significant weight and muscle loss, and3 assess the interplay between symptom burden, treatment modality, and nutritional outcomes. By integrating detailed anthropometric, bioelectrical impedance, and laboratory assessments, this study seeks to provide actionable insights for risk stratification and timely nutritional intervention in this vulnerable population.

Methods

Study design and patients

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Radiation Oncology, Ege University Faculty of Medicine, between June 2021 and June 2023. Patients aged ≥ 18 years with a histologically confirmed diagnosis of head and neck cancer who underwent adjuvant or definitive radiotherapy (RT) or chemoradiotherapy (CRT) were eligible. Patients with recurrent disease, distant metastasis, or a history of previous head and neck cancer treatment were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee of Ege University Faculty of Medicine (Decision No: 24-11T/45) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessments were performed at three time points:1 baseline (before the start of RT)2, at the end of RT, and3 during post-treatment follow-up (6–12 weeks after completion of RT).

Patient and treatment characteristics

Demographic data (age, sex, height, weight), tumor characteristics (primary site, histological type, TNM stage), and treatment details (surgery status, RT technique and dose, fraction number, concurrent chemotherapy regimen, and clinical target volume) were prospectively recorded.

Anthropometric and body composition measurements

Body weight and height were measured using a calibrated digital scale and stadiometer. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). Body composition parameters, including fat mass, fat-free mass, and skeletal muscle mass, were assessed using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) with a standardized device under fasting conditions and according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. Phase angle was automatically calculated as an indicator of cellular integrity.

Muscle function and circumference assessments

Handgrip strength was measured using a calibrated digital hand dynamometer. The maximum value of three trials from the dominant hand was recorded. Mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) and calf circumference were measured using a non-elastic tape following standard protocols.

Nutritional and functional assessment

Nutritional status was evaluated by a trained dietitian using the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA). Patients were classified as well-nourished (Category A), moderately malnourished/suspected malnutrition (Category B), or severely malnourished (Category C). Performance status was assessed using the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) scale.

Laboratory parameters

Fasting blood samples were analyzed for serum albumin, prealbumin, transferrin, C-reactive protein (CRP), hemoglobin, and lymphocyte count using standard laboratory methods.

Radiotherapy dose-volume parameters

All patients were treated with intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT). High-dose and low-dose clinical target volumes (CTV) and corresponding doses were recorded. High-dose volume was defined as the volume receiving ≥ 65 Gy, and low-dose volume as the volume receiving ≥ 57 Gy. Dose-volume histograms were used to extract relevant parameters.

Nutritional support

Nutritional support during treatment included oral nutritional supplements (ONS), enteral tube feeding, or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy/jejunostomy (PEG/PEJ) when indicated. Decisions were made according to institutional nutritional protocols and ESPEN guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (range), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Changes over time were assessed using paired-samples t-tests and repeated-measures ANOVA. Comparisons between subgroups were performed with independent-samples t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests as appropriate. Correlations were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine optimal cut-off values for RT dose and volume parameters associated with clinically significant weight loss (≥ 5%) and muscle mass loss. The area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding nutritional support, only 34% of patients maintained adequate oral intake throughout treatment. 54% required oral nutritional supplements (ONS), 5% needed nasogastric tube feeding, and 7% required PEG/PEJ placement. Patients who received nutritional support experienced numerically greater weight loss (–4.7 kg, p = 0.008) and muscle mass loss (–4.06 kg, p = 0.037) compared with those who maintained normal oral intake. This difference is likely attributable to confounding by indication, as patients requiring support had more advanced disease and higher baseline symptom burden.

Nutritional status deteriorated markedly during treatment. The proportion of well-nourished patients (PG-SGA Category A) decreased from 41% at baseline to 33% at the end of RT, while severe malnutrition (Category C) increased from 19% to 43%. The percentage of patients with PG-SGA score ≥ 9 rose from 33% to 65% (Table 4). Although partial improvement was observed during follow-up, malnutrition remained prevalent at 55.6%.

The number and severity of nutrition-impact symptoms increased significantly during RT (p < 0.001), with partial recovery at follow-up. The most common symptoms at the end of treatment were impaired food intake (91.8%), dysphagia (51.8%), oral ulcers, dysgeusia, and xerostomia. Patients with three or more symptoms showed significantly greater declines in body composition and muscle function (Supplement 1).

Partial recovery was observed at follow-up, but values remained significantly different from baseline (Table 5). Discussion

The present study demonstrates the profound and multifactorial impact of head and neck cancer treatment on nutritional status, body composition, and symptom burden. Our findings highlight that radiotherapy, particularly at higher fraction numbers and volumes, is strongly associated with significant weight loss, muscle depletion, and persistent nutrition-related symptoms, effects that are further exacerbated by chemotherapy and prior surgical interventions.

The anthropometric changes, malnutrition, and muscle loss observed during treatment underscore their clinical importance in head and neck cancer patients. In our study, 42% of patients experienced ≥ 4.6% weight loss compared to baseline, while 49% had muscle mass loss exceeding 4.8%. Anthropometric alterations during treatment have been widely investigated in the literature. A systematic review in 2016 reported significant muscle mass depletion in this patient population during treatment, which negatively affected treatment tolerance and 30-day mortality rates6. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated substantial reductions in fat-free mass during therapy, with these losses persisting for 8–12 months post-treatment. Our findings are consistent with this evidence, further emphasizing that weight and muscle loss are not transient phenomena but represent clinically relevant events with long-term implications. Importantly, muscle depletion not only compromises functional capacity and quality of life but also impairs treatment compliance and may increase the risk of treatment-related toxicity. The persistence of body composition changes beyond the treatment period highlights the need for proactive, longitudinal nutritional and rehabilitative strategies7.

In our study, patients receiving nutritional support showed numerically greater weight and muscle loss with slower recovery, a finding seemingly at odds with previous reports on the benefits of ONS and enteral nutrition8,9. However, this difference most likely reflects confounding by indication rather than the inefficacy of nutritional interventions. Support is typically initiated in patients with advanced disease, severe symptoms, or multimodal treatments, placing them at higher baseline risk. Therefore, the poorer outcomes observed in this group likely reflect underlying vulnerability rather than a failure of nutritional support. Moreover, the nutritional support provided may have been insufficient to fully meet the elevated requirements during treatment. These results emphasize the importance of early initiation, individualized adjustment, and close monitoring of nutritional care. Consistent with the literature, timely and structured nutritional interventions remain essential for improving treatment tolerance, preventing complications, and enhancing quality of life in head and neck cancer patients8,10.

In our study, no significant changes were observed in PA values before, during, or after treatment, suggesting that cellular integrity was largely preserved. However, concomitant reductions in muscle mass and other anthropometric measures more clearly reflected the adverse impact of malnutrition and treatment on body composition. In contrast, the literature consistently associates low PA with poor prognosis and reduced quality of life in head and neck cancer patients11. These discrepancies may be explained by differences in patient demographics, measurement techniques, or treatment protocols across studies.

Treatment modalities such as surgery, chemotherapy (CT), and RT can induce significant alterations in body composition. In our study, patients receiving CT experienced more pronounced losses in body weight and muscle mass, likely due to its association with increased metabolic stress and anorexia8. By contrast, patients with a history of surgery demonstrated less pronounced muscle depletion, which may be explained by the protective effect of early postoperative nutritional support in preserving body composition, as also reported in the literature7. RT fractionation further exerted a critical impact, with patients receiving higher numbers of fractions exhibiting more substantial deterioration. Collectively, these findings underscore that individual risk assessment and timely nutritional intervention during CT and RT are essential to prevent body composition deterioration and to enhance treatment compliance12.

Our study emphasizes the impact of RT dose and volume on treatment-related body composition changes. Weight loss at treatment completion was strongly associated with RT dose, with ROC analyses identifying critical thresholds—high-dose RT > 65 Gy, low-dose RT > 57 Gy, high-dose volume > 48 cc, and low-dose volume > 122 cc—beyond which both weight and muscle loss were significantly more likely. Interestingly, muscle loss did not exhibit the same clear dose–response relationship, suggesting that weight change may be a more sensitive early indicator of radiation-induced nutritional deterioration. These findings are in line with previous literature indicating that higher RT doses and larger volumes contribute to clinically relevant body composition changes, including skeletal muscle atrophy. Collectively, these data underscore the importance of incorporating RT dose–volume parameters into patient risk stratification and supportive care planning to mitigate treatment-related nutritional compromise13–15.

In our study, malnutrition was assessed using the PG-SGA, a validated tool for both inpatients and outpatients in oncology settings. At baseline, 33.3% of patients were at risk of malnutrition (PG-SGA ≥ 9), increasing to 65.3% post-treatment and remaining at 55.6% during follow-up. These findings highlight a substantial rise in malnutrition prevalence associated with treatment and underscore the high vulnerability of head and neck cancer patients. Consistent with previous literature, treatment-related side effects and elevated metabolic demands play a critical role in this process16,17. Overall, our results confirm PG-SGA as a sensitive and practical instrument for monitoring nutritional deterioration, symptom management, and guiding individualized nutritional support, strongly supporting its routine clinical use18.

In head and neck cancer patients, symptom burden is strongly linked to malnutrition and adverse body composition changes. In our study, malnutrition prevalence increased in parallel with symptom frequency and severity, with more than three symptoms reported by 75% of patients post-treatment (vs. 41% at baseline). Dysphagia, anorexia, swallowing difficulties, and xerostomia were the most common, in line with prior studies19,20. These findings highlight that greater symptom burden directly elevates PG-SGA scores and worsens nutritional status. Importantly, effective symptom management can reduce malnutrition by up to 20%, underscoring its pivotal role in preserving nutritional status and body composition19–21.

During treatment of head and neck cancer, nutritional and inflammatory parameters are significantly altered. In our study, CRP levels increased while hemoglobin and lymphocyte counts markedly decreased, reflecting heightened inflammation, treatment-related anemia, and immunosuppression. Consistent with the literature, an inverse correlation between CRP and albumin confirmed the direct impact of inflammation on nutritional status22,23. These alterations were most pronounced in malnourished patients with high symptom burden, showing significant differences in albumin, transferrin, CRP, and lymphocyte levels. Moreover, the associations between CRP, prealbumin, albumin, and muscle mass underscore the detrimental effect of inflammation on muscle preservation. Given that hypoalbuminemia is linked to sarcopenia and poor survival, our findings highlight the critical importance of integrated nutritional and inflammation management during treatment24.

Despite its valuable findings, our study has several limitations. The relatively small sample size and single-center design limit generalizability, while the observational nature precludes causal inference. Potential confounders, such as variations in treatment protocols and adherence to nutritional interventions, could not be fully controlled. In addition, formal grading of dysphagia using standardized tools (e.g., CTCAE) and specific dose-volume analysis of dysphagia/aspiration-related structures (DARS) were not performed25,26. Furthermore, long-term outcomes, including overall and progression-free survival, were not assessed, restricting evaluation of the prognostic impact of malnutrition and sarcopenia. A major strength, however, is that all anthropometric and nutritional assessments were performed by a single experienced dietitian, ensuring measurement consistency and minimizing inter-observer variability. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and standardized protocols are needed to address these limitations.

Conclusion

This prospective study demonstrates that radiotherapy for head and neck cancer causes clinically significant and persistent deterioration in nutritional status, body composition, and systemic inflammation. Higher radiotherapy doses, larger irradiated volumes, and greater symptom burden were identified as important predictors of weight loss and muscle depletion. Nutritional decline was most pronounced in patients with advanced disease and those requiring nutritional support, largely due to confounding by indication.

These findings underscore the need for early risk stratification using radiotherapy dose–volume parameters and symptom burden. Timely, individualized, and adequately intensive nutritional interventions, combined with effective symptom management, should be integral components of multidisciplinary care to improve treatment tolerance and quality of life in this vulnerable population.
