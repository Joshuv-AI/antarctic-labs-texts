Factors associated with work-life conflict among pharmacists: a cross-sectional study in Jordan
Authors: Mohamad Alameddine, Karem H. Alzoubi, Nivine Hanach, Sayer Al-Azzam, Reema Karasneh, Dalia M. Shakhatreh, Maryam K. El-Zubi, Sawsan Abuhammad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Work-life conflict affects workforce stability, quality of care, and health-system performance. Pharmacists are essential healthcare providers in community, hospital, clinical, and primary care settings, but they frequently experience workload-related pressures. Limited evidence is available regarding work-family and family-work conflict among pharmacists in Jordan. This cross-sectional study examined work-life conflict among pharmacists in Jordan and its associated demographic, work-related, and psychological factors. Between January and July 2023, an anonymous online survey link was circulated by convenience sampling through professional pharmacists’ messaging groups and investigator networks. The questionnaire collected sociodemographic and work-related data and included the two five-item Netemeyer work-family conflict and family-work conflict subscales. Factors associated with high conflict were examined using simple and multiple logistic regression. Among 405 pharmacists with valid aggregate outcome data, 122 (30.1%) met the exploratory high-conflict definition; the adjusted model included 369 complete cases and 120 high-conflict events. In the model, pharmacists with an employed spouse had higher odds of high work-life conflict than those without an employed spouse or those who were not married (aOR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.21–7.37, p = 0.018). Higher meaningful work score (aOR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.12–1.95, p = 0.005), DASS stress score (aOR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.02–1.14, p = 0.007), and burnout score (aOR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.04–1.12, p < 0.001) were independently associated with higher odds of high work-life conflict. Female gender (aOR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.90, p = 0.022) and part-time staff-pharmacist status (aOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.91, p = 0.030) showed lower adjusted odds. The inverse DASS depression coefficient (aOR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p = 0.021) emerged only after adjustment and was treated as model-dependent, not as evidence of a protective effect. Within this convenience sample, work-life conflict was common and several demographic, occupational, and psychological variables were associated with the exploratory high-conflict outcome. These cross-sectional findings are hypothesis-generating and support prospective evaluation of staffing, scheduling, workload, and organizational-support strategies; they do not establish national prevalence or intervention effects.

Introduction

Work-life conflict has received increasing attention because it can affect individuals’ ability to meet the demands of work, family, and other areas of personal life1,2. Although the concept is most commonly associated with work and family roles and is frequently discussed as the work-family interface, it also includes conflict between work and other life domains1–5. The broader term work-life conflict is therefore used in this study. Work-life conflict occurs when the demands of work interfere with responsibilities or participation in family, personal, social, or health-related domains1,2. Work-family conflict theory similarly explains how incompatible work and family demands can produce role strain2,3. Research in the health sector suggests that assessments of work-life conflict should consider professional, personal, family, and health-related dimensions1,2. Work demands include working hours, workload intensity, staffing pressure, and the time devoted to job-related tasks6,7. Longer working hours reduce the time available for personal and family life. Heavy workloads, high job pressure, and insufficient recovery may also cause fatigue, stress, poor health, and greater interference between work and non-work responsibilities6–9. Studies involving pharmacists have linked workload, staffing conditions, work-life balance, and professional satisfaction with health, burnout, and service-performance outcomes4,5,7–9.

In Jordan, the pharmacy profession has expanded substantially, and pharmacists have assumed wider responsibilities in healthcare, primary care, public health, and professional development10,11. Evidence concerning pharmacists’ working lives in Jordan remains limited. Recent research from Amman found that many pharmacy certificate holders reported job dissatisfaction associated with low pay, limited promotion opportunities, and heavy workloads12. In Jordanian private hospitals, work-life balance practices were associated with job satisfaction and organizational commitment through work-life conflict. Managerial support was also identified as important in addressing employees’ work-life needs13. A qualitative study conducted across 12 Arab countries, including Jordan, found that pharmacists’ dissatisfaction was associated with the undervaluation of their role, inadequate salaries, low motivation, and heavy workloads. Professional commitment and workplace culture contributed to greater satisfaction14. A study has also linked work-life balance, job satisfaction, and burnout among Jordanian nurses, indicating that conflict between occupational and personal responsibilities is relevant across the country’s health workforce15. Another Jordanian study described changing pharmacy-practice models and technology-related challenges that may influence pharmacists’ work demands16. Although stress among health professionals has been widely studied, work-life conflict among pharmacists has received limited attention, especially in Arab countries and Jordan4,5,8,14,16. Recent regional nursing studies, for example, reported high stress, difficulties with coping, and serious intentions to leave the profession among nurses in Jordan and Saudi Arabia17,18. A cross-sectional study in the United Arab Emirates also found psychological stress among frontline healthcare providers during the COVID-19 pandemic19. These findings demonstrate the extent of occupational stress within the region’s health professions, but they do not adequately explain work-life conflict among pharmacists or how Jordanian pharmacy regulations and practice models shape pharmacists’ daily working lives10,11,16,20,21.

Research on work-life balance and work-family conflict among Jordanian health professionals remains limited and provides an incomplete account of pharmacists’ experiences12,13,15. Existing studies have generally been conducted in a single setting, restricted to Amman, or focused mainly on job satisfaction without examining how occupational demands interfere with pharmacists’ personal and family lives12,13. Further research is needed because the Jordanian pharmacy sector is developing rapidly, pharmacists face increasing workloads, and the long-term retention of the health workforce remains an important concern10,11,14,16. The present cross-sectional study therefore has two purposes: (1) to assess the level of work-life conflict among pharmacists working across multiple practice settings in Jordan and (2) to examine the associations between sociodemographic and work-related characteristics and work-life conflict. The study examines associations and does not seek to establish causal pathways, with particular attention given to potentially modifiable workplace factors. Few studies have examined work-life conflict among pharmacists across multiple practice settings in Jordan.

Methodology

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional survey study was conducted in Jordan between January and August 2023 to evaluate work-life conflict among pharmacists practicing in community, hospital, clinical, and primary care settings.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.722. The original calculation was conducted using the z-test family for logistic regression, with alpha = 0.05, power = 0.95, and a medium effect-size assumption, resulting in a minimum required sample of 371 participants. Because the main inferential analysis was logistic regression, the number of outcome events was also reviewed after data cleaning. Overall, 405 participants had valid work-life conflict outcome data, and 122 participants (30.1%) were classified as having high work-life conflict. These values were considered when interpreting model stability and confidence intervals.

Participants, sampling strategy, and data collection procedures

A convenience sampling strategy was used because no complete, accessible sampling frame of all practicing pharmacists was available to the research team and because online professional groups enabled recruitment across multiple pharmacy sectors and geographic areas. However, this approach does not ensure national representativeness. Data were collected via an anonymous self-administered online survey hosted on Google Forms. Recruitment links were circulated through professional pharmacists’ messaging groups and investigator networks. Eligibility was screened at the beginning of the questionnaire. Because the link was distributed through open professional groups, the exact number of pharmacists who received or opened the survey could not be determined and a formal response rate could not be calculated. To reduce duplicate responses, the survey settings restricted multiple submissions from the same Google account when respondents were signed in. The retained study records do not identify a formal Jordan Pharmacists Association mailing list or a single social-media platform as the sampling frame; therefore, recruitment is reported only through the documented professional messaging groups and investigator networks. Google Forms recorded submitted questionnaires rather than unsubmitted partial attempts, so the number of incomplete starts could not be quantified. complete cases.

Study instruments

Netemeyer and colleagues developed and validated two related but distinct five-item subscales: work-family conflict and family-work conflict23,24. Together, the two subscales comprise five work-family conflict items and five family-work conflict items. In the present survey, the English items were administered with a 5-point Likert response format. The two subscales were summed for an exploratory aggregate score ranging from 10 to 50. The items are negatively worded and were not reverse-coded; therefore, higher total scores indicate greater work-life conflict rather than better work-life balance. An example item for the work-family conflict section is “The demands of my work interfere with my home and family life”, whereas a representative item for family-work conflict is “Family-related stress interferes with my ability to perform job-related duties”. In the current sample, internal consistency was good for the total 10-item scale (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.862), work-family conflict subscale (alpha = 0.841), and family-work conflict subscale (alpha = 0.760). The original validation supports interpretation of the two directional subscales; it does not establish the 10-item aggregate as a unidimensional clinical scale or validate a diagnostic cut-off. The total-score alpha reported here describes internal consistency in this sample and does not, by itself, establish unidimensionality or threshold validity.

Variables and definitions

For the exploratory logistic analysis, the dependent variable was a high aggregate conflict group, defined as a 10-item score of 35 or more. The value 35 corresponded to the sample’s upper-quartile boundary (75th percentile); it was not taken from a previously validated clinical or population threshold, and observations tied at 35 produced a high-conflict group larger than 25% of the sample. Independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics and work-related variables such as pharmacy type, sector, geographic location, position, working hours, overtime compensation, shift schedule, days off, staffing levels, prescription volume, and intention to leave.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25. Continuous variables are summarized as means +/- standard deviations (SDs), whereas categorical variables are reported as frequencies and valid percentages. For the exploratory aggregate assessment, the 10 Netemeyer items were summed without reverse coding. The sample’s upper-quartile boundary was 35; participants scoring 35 or more were classified in the high-conflict group, whereas those scoring below 35 formed the comparison group. This threshold was derived from the present sample and has not been externally or clinically validated. The approach was retained to describe a high-burden group, while recognizing that combining the directional subscales can mask work-to-family and family-to-work differences and that dichotomization discards information and may reduce statistical power23,25. Simple and multiple logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with high work-life conflict. Variables with p < 0.20 in simple logistic regression and variables considered conceptually relevant were entered into the multiple logistic regression model. Results are expressed as odds ratios (ORs) or adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Complete-case analysis was used for the model. Model adequacy was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Linearity in the logit for age was checked using an age-by-log(age) term, and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated acceptable fit (p = 0.883), the age-by-log(age) term did not indicate violation of linearity in the logit (p = 0.175), and the maximum VIF was 5.84. This value does not indicate extreme collinearity under the frequently cited VIF < 10 convention, but it exceeds the more conservative value of 5 and was therefore treated as evidence of moderate overlap among predictors rather than as proof that collinearity was absent26.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Jordan University of Science and Technology (JUST-IRB; approval no. 32/149/2022, dated June 13, 2022). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Before entering the questionnaire, all participants provided electronic informed consent to participate and for publication of anonymized, aggregate data. Participation was voluntary; respondents could skip questions or leave the survey before submission without penalty. No personal identifiers were collected, and confidentiality was maintained throughout the study.

Patient and public involvement

The study did not involve patients. Study findings were made publicly available to participants and the general public through the production of study reports and open access journal articles.

Table 1 shows the participants’ sociodemographic and work-related characteristics after data cleaning (N = 405). The majority of participants were female (n = 242, 59.8%). The average age was 32.08 years (SD = 7.62). Most participants were married (55.3%), and 52.1% had at least one child. The largest group held a Bachelor of Pharmacy degree (n = 311, 76.8%), followed by Doctor of Pharmacy (n = 69, 17.0%) and Master of Pharmacy (n = 25, 6.2%).

Work experience varied among participants: 26.4% had 1–2 years, 29.9% had 3–5 years, 23.5% had 6–10 years, and 20.2% had 11 years or more. Most pharmacists were employed in independent community pharmacies (n = 260, 64.2%) and in the private sector (n = 359, 88.6%). A substantial proportion worked outside Amman, including other cities (n = 262, 64.7%) and rural areas (n = 103, 25.4%). More than half were full-time staff pharmacists (n = 212, 52.3%).

Among respondents with valid working-hour data, 47.4% typically worked 40–49 h per week and 35.9% worked 50 h or more. Preferred working hours were most commonly 40–49 h per week (45.8%). Evening/night shifts were reported by 40.0%, morning/afternoon shifts by 33.8%, and variable shifts by 26.2%. Most respondents with valid data were entitled to one day off per week (79.6%). Most shifts were staffed by one pharmacist (53.7%) and no technicians (62.1%). Daily prescription volume was reported as 1–9 prescriptions by 36.7%, 10–14 by 25.7%, 15–29 by 24.7%, and 30 or more by 13.0%.

Multiple logistic regression analyses are shown in Table 3. Simple logistic regression results are presented for the full set of demographic, work-related, and psychological variables shown in the table. In simple logistic regression, several variables were associated with high work-life conflict, including gender, marital status, spouse employment status, number of children, sector, position, contractual entitlement to days off per week, meaningful work, DASS stress, and burnout score.

In the multiple logistic regression model (complete-case N = 369; high work-life conflict events = 120), pharmacists whose spouse was employed had higher adjusted odds of high work-life conflict than those without an employed spouse or those who were not married (aOR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.21–7.37, p = 0.018). Female gender was associated with lower adjusted odds than male gender (aOR = 0.46, 95% CI: 0.23–0.90, p = 0.022), and part-time staff pharmacists had lower adjusted odds compared with full-time staff pharmacists (aOR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.91, p = 0.030). Meaningful work, DASS stress, and burnout total score were independently associated with higher adjusted odds. DASS depression had a null simple association (OR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.99–1.03, p = 0.505) but an inverse adjusted coefficient (aOR = 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.99, p = 0.021), indicating a model-dependent sign change rather than a straightforward protective association. Marital-status estimates were elevated but not statistically significant after adjustment. Some estimates had wide confidence intervals, especially in smaller categories, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to investigate work-life conflict and its associated factors among pharmacists in Jordan. Within this convenience sample, many participants reported that work interfered with personal and family responsibilities. In the multiple logistic regression model, the exploratory high-conflict outcome was associated with gender, spouse employment status, meaningful work, stress, burnout, depression score, and employment position. The gender estimate and inverse adjusted depression coefficient require cautious, model-specific interpretation. These results describe conditional associations rather than causal effects, and several estimates were imprecise. Conflict may arise when work and family demands are incompatible; under the job demands-resources model, strain is expected when demands exceed available resources and organizational support3,6. The descriptive response patterns and the adjusted associations with DASS stress and burnout are broadly compatible with these frameworks. However, the present data do not test the proposed mechanisms and cannot establish that work demands caused psychological strain or work-life conflict.

The inverse adjusted coefficient for DASS depression should not be interpreted as evidence that depression protects against work-life conflict. The simple association was null (and became inverse only after simultaneous adjustment for DASS stress, burnout, and other covariates. This sign change is compatible with suppression, over-adjustment, or shared variance among conceptually related psychological constructs27. The maximum VIF of 5.84 indicates moderate, although not extreme, collinearity. Even when a conventional VIF threshold is not exceeded, predictor overlap can inflate standard errors, widen confidence intervals, and make individual coefficients and their signs sensitive to model specification26,27. Accordingly, the depression, stress, and burnout coefficients are interpreted as conditional associations within this model; the depression result requires replication and should not be assigned a biological or psychological protective meaning.

Family and household variables were also relevant in the adjusted model. Pharmacists with an employed spouse had higher adjusted odds of high work-life conflict than those without an employed spouse or those who were not married. Marital-status estimates were elevated but were not statistically significant after adjustment. This pattern may reflect family obligations, work-family pressures, or the demands of dual-income households. However, domestic workload, caregiving burden, financial strain, and support from a spouse were not measured. The spouse-employment finding should therefore be interpreted as an association that may indicate family-work dynamics, not as evidence of a causal mechanism. Female sex was significantly associated with reduced odds in the unadjusted analysis and in the adjusted analysis. The effect goes against what has been observed in the pharmacy literature, where higher work–family conflict has been reported for females, or the females have been identified as the group who carries the heavier work-family burden28,29. Because the effect occurred before adjustment for other variables in the model, it was not created solely by the adjusted analysis. Nevertheless, the finding can be due to self-selection into the convenience sample, occupational role differences, residual confounding, cultural background, or uncontrolled household/caregiving responsibilities. The model also suggested that employment arrangement was relevant. Part-time staff pharmacists had lower adjusted odds compared with full-time staff pharmacists, while the association for government/public-sector employment was lower but did not reach statistical significance in the full adjusted model.

These findings may reflect differences in scheduling stability, workload intensity, employment expectations, staffing structure, or organizational policies. Conversely, pharmacists in full-time and private-sector roles may experience greater exposure to long hours, customer-facing workload, overtime, and staffing pressures. Because the sample included unequal group sizes across sectors and positions, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and examined further in larger studies that can separate the effects of sector, job role, working hours, and staffing. It is noteworthy that highly meaningful work was found to be a significant and independent factor for high WLC as well. This does not suggest that meaningful work is detrimental; rather, pharmacists perceiving their jobs as highly meaningful may be more engaged, committed, or emotionally invested in their work.

In such an interpretation, it can be observed that professional commitment may coexist with stress when adequate resources are unavailable, whether personal or organizational. Future research should focus on whether engagement in meaningful work protects against or exacerbates work-life conflict. As previously discussed, some factors that were significant in simple logistic regression analysis lost statistical significance after adjustment. These factors include having children and the pharmacist-in-charge designation. These findings suggest that the effects of some demographic and work-related characteristics may be partly explained by related work and family factors, including marital status, spouse employment, working hours, role type, and stress. Similarly, variables such as pharmacy type, shift schedule, overtime compensation, staffing numbers, and intention to leave were not statistically significant in the model.

The relationship between work-life conflict and turnover intention remains theoretically and practically important, as previous studies have linked work-family conflict, burnout, job stress, organizational support, and work climate with turnover-related outcomes30–33. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the temporal relationships among work-life conflict, burnout, job satisfaction, and intention to leave among pharmacists. Although reducing work-life conflict is a reasonable workforce objective, this study did not evaluate any intervention and cannot show that changing staffing, schedules, overtime, or workload will reduce conflict. Prior pharmacy and health-workforce literature provides a rationale for prospectively evaluating sustainable staffing, predictable scheduling, reasonable overtime, workload management, and manager support33–36. These measures should therefore be treated as candidates for evaluation rather than as effects demonstrated by the present analysis.

Study limitations

Several limitations should guide interpretation. First, the cross-sectional design establishes associations but not temporal ordering or causation, particularly for work-life conflict, stress, burnout, and depression. Second, convenience recruitment through online professional networks may have introduced selection and coverage bias and does not support nationally representative prevalence estimates. The recruitment denominator was unavailable, so a response rate could not be calculated, and unsubmitted partial questionnaires were not captured. Third, all measures were self-reported and may be affected by recall, social-desirability, and common-method bias. Fourth, the exploratory outcome combined two directional Netemeyer subscales and dichotomized a sample-derived upper-quartile threshold of 35. This threshold is neither clinical nor externally validated, may obscure work-to-family and family-to-work differences, and loses information contained in the continuous scores. Fifth, the adjusted analysis used complete cases (369 of 405 participants; 120 high-conflict events), so missing data could introduce bias if the complete cases differed systematically from excluded participants. Sixth, numerous and partly correlated predictors were evaluated relative to the number of events. The maximum VIF of 5.84, wide confidence intervals, small category counts, and the adjusted sign reversal for depression indicate that some coefficients may be unstable or sensitive to model specification26,27. Residual confounding is also possible because caregiving responsibilities, domestic workload, household income, workplace culture, and organizational support were not measured. Finally, the predominance of community and private-sector pharmacists, together with small hospital, primary-care, and public-sector subgroups, limits generalizability across pharmacy settings and to pharmacists in Jordan as a whole.

The results should therefore be considered hypothesis-generating and require replication using probability-based sampling, continuous directional subscale scores, and longitudinal designs. Policy and Practice Implications Because the study was observational and used nonprobability sampling, its findings should not be used to prescribe a single policy or to infer effects on retention, service quality, or patient safety. For policymakers, pharmacy councils, health-system leaders, hospital leaders, community pharmacy owners, and pharmacy managers, the results identify priorities for prospective evaluation: minimum staffing during shifts, limits on excessive consecutive working hours, transparent overtime compensation, protected breaks, and support for pharmacists with substantial personal or financial obligations36,37. Telepharmacy and hybrid practice models should likewise be considered only as candidates for evaluation where they may reduce workload or improve access, because telepharmacy was not measured in this study16. Future intervention studies should assess effects on work-life conflict, pharmacist retention, dispensing accuracy, patient safety, and continuity of care7,38–42.

Conclusion

Within this convenience sample of pharmacists in Jordan, work-life conflict was common, and the exploratory high-conflict outcome was associated after adjustment with spouse employment status, gender, employment position, meaningful work, stress, burnout, and a model-dependent inverse depression coefficient. Higher Netemeyer scores represented greater conflict because the items were not reverse-coded. The inverse depression coefficient should not be interpreted as protective, and the marital-status estimates were not statistically significant in the adjusted model. Because the study was cross-sectional, used convenience sampling, and applied a sample-derived threshold, the findings do not establish causation, national prevalence, or the effectiveness of any workplace intervention. They support further longitudinal and intervention research on staffing, scheduling, workload, and organizational support.
