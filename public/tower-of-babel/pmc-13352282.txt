Crystallite Rotation Drives Strain Softening in Semicrystalline Polyethylene
Authors: Kyle Wm. Hall, Timothy W. Sirk, Wataru Shinoda, Michael L. Klein, K. Michael Salerno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This work examines the response of semicrystalline polyethylene under triaxial strain. The Shinoda-DeVane-Klein (SDK) model of polyethylene (PE) was used in coarse-grained (CG) molecular dynamics (MD) simulations of more than 2 × 106 particles, representing more than 2 × 107 atoms and a volume of greater than 105 nm3. For strains in the range of ϵ = 0.25–0.5, the stress was related to the overall orientation of the polymer backbone with respect to the extension axis. However, at intermediate strains (ϵ = 0.05–0.3), where strain softening typically occurs, there were large variations in mechanical response as a result of the initial distribution of crystallite orientations. At intermediate strains, both the compressive and tensile stress also depended strongly on the initial distribution of crystallite orientations. The orientation distribution also determined the onset of the strain-hardening regime. We found that, due to the inextensible nature of the crystallites, compressive stresses drove rotation for crystallites oriented away from the extension axis, which in turn strongly decreased the tensile stress. Results from MD simulations suggested that, while spatial averaging smooths the overall macroscopic response of semicrystalline samples, intermediate scale or local stress imbalances due to crystallite rotation have a key role in driving strong local strain and potentially leading to plastic deformation like banding or cavitation.

Introduction

Semicrystalline polymers, such as polyethylene (PE), have a molecular structure that is a mix of ordered crystalline regions and disordered amorphous regions. This unique structure allows for a versatile and tunable set of properties, making them suitable for a wide span of uses ranging from everyday consumer items to highly specialized, high-performance materials. While these polymers are useful in their base forms, they can be processed further to create exceptional properties. High performance PE materials undergo processing to create a high crystallinity structure where long polymer chains are drawn into extended conformations. Such materials are valued for their excellent mechanics, particularly the combination of high strength and low density.

The response of semicrystalline polymers to applied stress or strain is relevant to both the performance of the final product in structural and energy-dissipation applications as well as efficient processing and manufacturing. In particular, several studies have tracked the polymer response during cold drawing in order to understand how alignment, stress and plastic strain develop as PE samples are processed. − Experimental techniques like small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) have been used to quantify orientation and alignment in semicrystalline samples. − Previous experiments suggest that cavitation occurs in semicrystalline polymers − concurrently with yielding, and that the thickness or size of crystals and overall crystallinity have a role in driving cavitation. Overall, studies have probed the motion of crystalline and amorphous regions in strained semicrystalline polymers but a complete picture of polymer chain structure and evolution remains elusive.

Modeling semicrystalline polymers is an outstanding computational problem due to the inherent multiscale nature of the material. The prevalent spherulite and lamellae structures are typically too large to simulate with all-atom MD simulations. Conversely, structural details such as polymer chain orientation, spacing and folding as well as mechanical response depend on details of the force-field or model, often down to the all-atom level. , The high molecular weight of long PE molecules leads to slow dynamics in both semicrystalline and melt samples, as entanglements introduce strong constraints on motion. Entanglements can also influence the solid-state structure, and the crystallization process itself, with significant efforts being taken to reduce entanglement density through the use of solvents.

Despite these scale-bridging challenges, recent advances from computational studies of semicrystalline polymers have shed light in several areas, such as capturing the growth of crystallinity with CG models − and mechanics of semicrystalline structures for PE and poly vinyl alcohol (PVA). − We note that, although extensive work has leveraged classic bead–spring polymer models to understand the fundamentals of glasses and melts, − few studies exist for bead–spring or CG semicrystalline polymer models. Other computational work has studied the interfacial properties between crystalline and amorphous PE using a united atom model. − Such models are more detailed representations, ultimately better capturing structural details like chain tilt, lamellae fold, and interface features. They also indicated that the primary or driving deformation mechanism can change with temperature. Here, we build on these earlier works by considering the stress–strain behavior of semicrystalline PE using CG molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, with a focus on the distinct behavior of crystalline and amorphous regions.

Methods

Simulation Details

Starting configurations for strain simulations were taken from the final configuration of a large-scale nucleation simulation at 280 K. The semicrystalline sample generated in the nucleation simulations is comprised of 10,800 separate coarse-grained n-C720H1442 chains with each chain consisting of 240 CG beads. Triaxial strain simulations were then performed at 280 K, with temperature controlled by a chain of four coupled Nose-Hoover thermostats with a time constant 200 timesteps. Three independent strain simulations were run with the tensile deformation aligned along a different coordinate axis. All three strain simulations were initialized from a single molecular configuration.

Each of the three systems was strained at a true strain rate of ϵ̇T=2×106 s–1 in the extension direction under constant volume conditions, resulting in the axial length of the simulation growing as LT(t)=LT(0)eϵ̇Tt . Each simulation was run for 108 timesteps with a 5 fs time step. Rather than impose the zero-lateral-stress condition, which mimics a macroscopic experimental setup with free boundaries, triaxial strain was applied as a representative volume embedded in a larger macroscopic sample. We note that previous simulation studies of semicrystalline polymers found only small volume changes during plastic deformation and support an incompressible treatment. Additional tests were carried out with constant pressure conditions in the lateral directions, resulting in qualitatively similar evolution of the structures. All simulations used the LAMMPS code.

The Shinoda-DeVane-Klein (SDK) model was used for the CG interactions of the polymer beads. , The SDK model features interaction potentials similar to those derived from all-atom simulations at the level of 3 CH2 units per CG bead. The SDK angle interaction does not contain multiple minima, but does exhibit backfolding. The intrinsic stiffness of a PE chain with the SDK model, as measured by the bond–bond correlation, is similar to the AA-derived PE model without a dihedral interaction.

The SDK model allows extended length and time scales to be captured in the simulations. We note that the time acceleration of a CG model relative to an atomistic reference is a state-dependent feature of the model, and is difficult to quantify at low temperature. , Further, the CG model speedup has been shown to increase with decreasing temperature for similar CG PE models. From these earlier results, we estimate a speedup of more than two orders of magnitude was achieved compared with all-atom simulations, resulting in strain rates of less than 104s–1.

Results

Alignment, Local Order, Segment Classification

First, we examine the evolution of polymer structure during axial deformation. Two metrics were computed to describe the degree of crystallinity and polymer orientation. We take the quantity P2(i)=12⟨3⁡cos2(θij)−1⟩j as a metric of local order, where the angle θ ij is computed between the bond vector from the (i – 1) → (i + 1) beads and the surrounding (j – 1) → (j + 1) bond vectors for particles separated by |r⃗ij|<0.6 nm. Beads were then classified as amorphous (P2(i)<Pc) or crystalline (P2(i)>Pc) with P c chosen as 0.85. As further characterization, we find the bond orientation relative to the extension (tension) axis, computed as PT(i)=12(3⁡cos2(θi)−1) ; The orientation is found for each bead i from the angle θ i formed by the vector between the adjacent backbone beads (i – 1) → (i + 1) and the extension axis.

The results of this characterization are shown in Figure for crystalline (top) and amorphous (bottom) beads with increasing axial strains of ϵ = 0, 0.25, 0.5. The orientation of beads is captured with the bead color, ranging from unaligned (blue), partially aligned (green), to diffusely aligned regions (red) at large strain. There is a strong visual contrast between the larger regions of similar alignment in the crystalline beads and the disordered amorphous regions. With increasing strain, the crystallites rapidly align with the extension axis at intermediate strain, and achieve a highly uniform alignment at the large strains.

Importantly, the apparent heterogeneity in alignment of the crystalline regions at ϵ = 0 belies the fact that the average initial alignment with any axis is approximately zero for both the amorphous and crystalline regions. Despite having only a minimal net alignment, the distribution of individual crystallite orientations influences the mechanical response of the sample and has strong heterogeneity at this scale, which is described in more detail below.

The results below show that the response to strain of crystalline vs amorphous regions is dramatically different. We track the evolution of the polymer backbone alignment with the extension axis and outline how the intermediate, strain-softening stress response of semicrystalline samples depends on the distribution of the crystallite orientations.

During the simulations, there is a qualitatively different behavior for beads in highly aligned, crystalline regions, and we make a distinction between beads with local P2(i)>0.85 , classified as crystalline or ordered, and those with P2(i)<0.85 . Single-bead order, strain or length values were found to be noisy, complicating analysis. Thus, we average over a length of bead segments to reduce noise and simplify analysis. We found that values became noisy for short lengths, while the presence of chain folds became important for longer length. Ten-bead segments were chosen as an intermediate length. Similar choices longer or shorter than ten led to similar results. Therefore, the classification of the ten-bead segments is as follows: Segments with high local orientation (P2(i)>0.85) for all beads are classified as crystalline. Segments with low local orientation (P2(i)<0.85) for all beads are amorphous. Segments are classified as mixed when they contain both high and low local orientation for beads in the same segment. Any segment that backfolds in a crystallite is labeled as mixed, since the local P2(i) value decreases substantially near the chain fold.

P T and Strain Hardening

Classifying each segment as crystalline, amorphous or mixed is required to correlate the orientation of different local environments with the imposed strain. Based on the distinct crystalline and amorphous behavior observed in l(t) and l T (t), it appears the natural response of the crystalline segments to an imposed strain is rotation, while the amorphous and mixed segments undergo both rotation and extension. Here, the orientation of the crystalline polymer segments is quantified over time and the orientation is linked to the overall stress response. The orientation with respect to the extension direction is measured from the cosine of the angle θ between ten-bead end-end vector and the extension direction. We note the quantity P T is related to PT(i) shown above in Figure , but corresponds to ten-bead segments rather than a single bead, and is averaged over different segment types.

In the intermediate strain range ϵ = 0.2–0.25, Figure shows that both the tensile-x and -z systems have a considerable fraction of crystalline segments with θ > 45°. This contrasts with the tensile-y system, where essentially no crystalline segments remain with θ > 45° by ϵ = 0.2. As illustrated in Figure above, the rotation of crystallites with θ > 45° leads to a strong decrease in the tensile stress. Once crystalline segments have rotated past ≈50° there is no further decrease in tensile stress. This occurs at strain ϵ = 0.3 for the tensile-x simulation, and at a larger value of ϵ = 0.35 for the tensile-z simulation. For each different tensile direction, the minimum of the tensile stress loosely corresponds to last crystallite rotating past 50°.

After samples are largely aligned, P T > 0.5 for all segments, there are more mechanisms involved than simple crystallite rotation. The rate of decrease of the crystalline fraction does not change (see Figure ), but there is a strong increase in conversion from amorphous to mixed segments. The conversion between segment types as a function of angle (not shown) indicates that amorphous segments that tend to align strongly do convert to “mixed” segment types, while crystalline segments with large alignment (small θ) can experience forces orthogonal to the extension axis, driving them to convert to “mixed.” At this large orientation there is also a strong effect from the nonbonded interactions in the compressive direction, as measured by the interchain radial distribution function, after alignment of crystalline and amorphous segments. An interesting future research direction would be to extend the analysis here into the strain hardening regime and connect with previous work in that regime.

Summary

This work presents large coarse-grained molecular dynamics simulations of semicrystalline polyethylene modeled using the SDK model. The results classify polymer segments as crystalline, amorphous or mixed according to the local P2(i) order parameter. These three polymer segment types undergo different motion during sample deformation. Measures of segment length l(t)=|r⃗(t)| and orientation lT(t)=r⃗(t)·x̂T show that, while amorphous segments are extensible, crystalline segments are inextensible and relieve stress through rotation. Consistent with these results, mixed type segments were found to be extensible, but less so than amorphous segments.

The stress response taken across the sample was connected to polymer segment motion. For different sample orientations, the overall alignment with the tensile direction P T follows a single trajectory for amorphous and mixed segments after accounting for differences in the initial orientation. The sample-wide strain-hardening onset and crystalline orientation were also collapsed with some variation due to different crystallite orientation distributions in different directions. Crystalline segment motion is driven by the compressive stress over a large range of orientation angles, and the local compressive strain is related to the compressive stress on the inextensible crystalline segments.

Our work here posits the simple mechanism of crystallite rotation as the origin of the stress strain behavior observed at intermediate strains. This rotation is a natural consequence of the polymer backbone connectivity and inextensibility of the crystallites. That is, polymer connectivity enforces a single crystallographic direction to align with the extension direction while crystallite inextensibility requires that the motion is purely rotational and not length-changing.

Future Directions

Notably, further increases in strain, elevated temperatures or lower strain rates have potential to activate or emphasize the importance of other mechanisms. For example, previous work focusing on tie-chains and chain-length effects has observed larger strain-hardening modulus for longer chains with more ties between crystallites. Through visual analysis, we also find that tie-chains influence crystallite rotation, particularly when crystallites become strongly aligned (θ < 20°) with the tension axis. Do longer chains with interpenetrating tie-chains tend to pull out rather than rotate when deformed, or is their rotation somehow constrained or coupled? In the strain hardening regime, questions around the role of tie chains are key in future work.

In this vein, it will be meaningful to extend simulations to larger strains and examine crystal segment conversion as a measure of the crystallite breakup observed previously. The sample temperature, applied strain rate and crystal fraction could also have a strong influence on segment conversion. In particular, increased temperature or thermal annealing would likely cause highly oriented chains to find ordered, local crystalline configurations. In the current simulations, the low temperature inhibits recrystallization of highly oriented “mixed” chain backbone segments, leading to the decrease in crystalline fraction. The crystal fraction influences strain-hardening modulus, thus capturing variations in crystal fraction and distribution in the intermediate strain regime, and at higher temperatures, has strong potential for future concepts in materials-by-design. Although these approaches are necessarily at the expense of atom-level detail and one-to-one comparisons with macro-level mechanics, designing on the “intermediate” scale is a promising path forward for semicrystalline materials.
