Targeting caveolin-1: dual challenges in tumor immunity and drug therapy strategies
Authors: Xiyue Ge, Tianyi Song, Xiangyan Guo, Jiajie Li, Zenghua Wang, Yaning Shi, Li Qin, Chanjuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTumor immunity is a crucial defense mechanism that suppresses tumor initiation and delays tumor progression. The interplay between tumor metabolic reprogramming and immune evasion is a putative determinant in the tumor progression. Caveolin-1 (Cav-1) is a major biomarker of caveolae and is involved in cell signaling, lipid metabolic reprogramming, and antitumor immune responses. Cav-1 is widely expressed in immune cells and tumor cells, with frequent upregulation in breast cancer, liver cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, glioma, and melanoma. However, Cav-1 expression is context-dependent and varies across cancer subtypes, including gastric cancer. This review discusses the structure and functions of Cav-1, emphasizing its role in tumor-associated immune cells. We summarized Cav-1-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming regulates antitumor immunity and highlighted challenges in developing Cav-1-targeting drugs. Notably, tumor cell metabolic reprogramming not only supports cancer cell progression but also drives immunosuppression via inhibitory cytokines like transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) and interleukin-4 (IL-4), fostering immune evasion and therapy resistance. While Cav-1 represents a potential biomarker and therapeutic target, its heterogeneous expression and context-specific functions necessitate further research to develop precise therapies. Future investigations into the mechanisms of Cav-1 in tumor immunity may pave the way for more effective cancer treatments.Keywords: Caveolin-1, Tumor, Immunity, Lipid metabolic reprogramming, Therapeutic strategies

Introduction

The immune system specifically recognizes antigens on the tumor cells membrane and eliminates tumor cells, thereby inhibiting the occurrence and development of tumors [1]. A central concept in tumor immunology is the cancer immunoediting hypothesis, which frames the interaction between the immune system and cancer as a dynamic process of identification and selection. The processes that the innate and adaptive immune systems control tumor growth and shape tumor immunogenicity are mainly divided into three stages: elimination, equilibrium, and escape [2]. The “non-self” protein peptides derived from tumor cells are captured by antigen-presenting cells (APCs)as part of the innate immune recognition process. These APCs then prime adaptive immune responses by presenting the antigens to T cells, which subsequently become activated and secrete cytokines to suppress tumor development [3]. The abnormalities in this process lead to immune evasion of tumor cells, thus forming a dynamic balance between elimination, equilibrium, and escape. Therefore, maintaining tumor immune homeostasis plays a key role in tumor development and treatment. On the one hand, tumor immunity enhances the resistance to tumors; on the other hand, tumor cells maintain growth by disrupting or evading immune surveillance [4]. Therefore, exploring the mechanisms of tumor immune regulation and finding strategies to enhance antitumor immune responses are important directions in the field of oncology.

Caveolae, a flask-shaped invaginations of the cell membrane, rich in cholesterol and lipid rafts, is important component of membrane proteins [5].Cav-1 is one of the earliest discovered caveolae components and has been identified as one of the main biomarkers of cancer immunity, endocytic pathways and plasma membrane microdomains, involved in cellular processes such as endocytosis, signal transduction, cholesterol homeostasis, and lipid transportation [6–12]. As a vital structural protein on the cell membrane, the role of Cav-1 is context-dependent, functioning as a dual regulator in immune cells such as macrophages, T lymphocytes (T cells), dendritic cells (DC), where it can either suppress or promote tumor progression depending on the specific cellular and molecular environment such as tumor microenvironment (TME) [13–15]. Given the importance of both Cav-1 and the immune system in tumor progression, a large number of studies have focused on revealing their interactions.

Lipid metabolic reprogramming refers to the process that cells or organisms regulate lipid synthesis, degradation, storage, and transport to adapt to different physiological or pathological states. Lipid metabolic reprogramming usually involves changes in the expression of key enzymes and transport proteins in lipid metabolic pathways, as well as the control of the composition and function of lipid metabolic products. In tumor cells, lipid metabolic reprogramming provides the energy and biomembrane materials needed for tumor cell proliferation. Cav-1 regulates lipid metabolic reprogramming to activate immune responses and inhibit tumor progression [16]. In addition, the roles of Cav-1 in lipid transport and vesicle transport, signal transduction, lipid metabolic enzymes, and the regulation of immune responses through lipid metabolic reprogramming are discussed [17–18]. It has been found that a variety of antitumor drugs indirectly regulate the activity of immune cells by either upregulating or downregulating Cav-1 expression in a context-dependent manner, thereby exerting antitumor effects, providing guidance and ideas for the development of new strategies for tumor immunotherapy [19].

Structure and function of caveolae and Cav-1

Caveolae is inward invaginations formed by the interaction between the cell membrane and lipids, appearing flask-shaped under transmission electron microscopy, with a diameter of approximately 50–100 nm and accounting for about 20% of the cell membrane area [20–21]. The composition of caveolae includes caveolin, cavin (polymerase I and transcription release factor), lipids, EH-domain containing protein 2 (EHD2), and ion channel proteins (Fig. 1).

Caveolins consist of three subtypes: Cav-1 is one of the most important components of caveolae structure, with a molecular weight of 22–24 kDa. Cav-2, approximately 20 kDa, is expressed in most cells except muscle cells [3]. Cav-3 is expressed in cardiomyocytes, vascular smooth muscle cells, and skeletal muscle cells [19]. Initially, caveolae were thought to be vesicles for transporting macromolecules because they are abundant in adipocytes, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and primary fibroblasts. Studies showed Caveolae has multiple functions, such as signal transduction, cell metabolism, cholesterol homeostasis, endocytosis, tumor promotion, and tumor suppression [20]. By enriching cholesterol and sphingolipids, integrating lipid raft structures, and mediating endocytosis/exocytosis, caveolae dynamically regulate lipid transport in the cell membrane, thereby affecting the localization and activity of signaling molecules. Based on the special microenvironment formed by lipid transport, caveolae spatially organize molecules (such as kinases), regulate the activity of lipid second messengers (such as phosphatidylinositol-derived lipids and sphingolipid-derived mediators) and ultimately affect downstream signaling pathways [21]. Moreover, the flattening of caveolae alters the structure and lipid environment of membrane microdomains, which is necessary for the signaling of the H-Ras subtype [22]. The absence or flattening of Cav-1 leads to the disintegration of lipid raft structures, the diffusion of H-Ras from lipid rafts to non-raft areas, and the loss of interaction with raft-associated signaling molecules (such as Raf-1), inhibiting the activation of its classical extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (ERK) pathway (Raf/MEK/ERK pathway) [23]. H-Ras translocation activates non-classical pathways, such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/Protein Kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway, and promotes cell survival. In the process of cell metabolism, caveolae maintain the integrity of the cell membrane by preserving membrane tension, especially during cell expansion [24]. As cholesterol-enriched areas on the cell membrane, caveolae significantly affects the lipid composition of the cell membrane and plays an important role in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis. In addition, as endocytic carriers, caveolae buds from the cell membrane and fuse with early endosomes, participating in intracellular transport of substances. Thus, caveolae and Cav-1 play important roles in tumor development [25].

Cav-1, as a crucial structural protein of caveolae, is composed of 178 amino acid residues and contains four structural domains: the N-terminal region, signature motif (SM, 49–60), oligomerization domain (OD, 61–81), the scaffolding domain (SD, 82–101), the intramembrane domain (IMD, 102–134), and the C-terminal region (Fig. 1) [26]. Cav-1 induces membrane invagination through oligomerization, forming the unique “flask-shaped structure” with its hydrophobic structure directly binding cholesterol to maintain caveolar stability [27]. Cav-1 is involved in signal regulation, metabolic balance, and disease development [28]. In the process of signal regulation, Cav-1 directly binds and regulates various signaling molecules, such as endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), Src, Gαq, and H-Ras, through its scaffolding domain. On one hand, Cav-1 inhibits eNOS, EGFR, and Gαq, leading to downregulation of NO production, cell proliferation, and Ca²⁺ signaling. On the other hand, Cav-1 promotes Src and H-Ras signaling, modulating the ERK/PI3K balance. In addition, Cav-1 mediates receptor endocytosis and recycling, thereby controlling cellular signal transduction. In the field of metabolism, Cav-1 maintains glucose-lipid metabolic balance by regulating cholesterol transport, insulin receptor signaling, and glucose transporter 4 (GLUT4) membrane localization, such as promoting low-density lipoprotein endocytosis and ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux. Dysfunction of Cav-1 leads to metabolic diseases such as insulin resistance and atherosclerosis [29]. Cav-1 has a typical dual role in tumor development and its expression level also changes dynamically during tumor progression with downregulation or tumor-suppressive functions in early stages and upregulation with pro-oncogenic effects in the late stages. In the early stages of tumors, Cav-1 exerts anticancer effects through signal pathway like Transforming Growth Factor Beta/epithelial-mesenchymal transition (TGFβ/EMT) and Insulin-like Growth Factor (IGF)/Insulin pathway that inhibit and regulate metabolism regulation. However, these inhibitory effects are insufficient to eliminate tumor growth, as the TME undergoes dynamic remodeling over time, gradually shifting Cav-1 function from tumor-suppressive to tumor-promoting. In the late stages of tumors, Cav-1 promotes EMT (upregulating Twist1/Snail), reshapes the immunosuppressive microenvironment, including activating cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) to secrete interleukin-6 (IL-6), enhances angiogenesis like regulating vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR-2) endocytosis, and mediates the efflux of chemotherapeutic drugs, driving tumor metastasis and inducing treatment resistance [30–31].

The differential expression profile of Cav-1 in various tumors

Cav-1 has been widely studied in the occurrence, development, and metastasis of tumors [32]. The expression of Cav-1 in tumor-associated cells significantly affects tumor progression, such as tumor-associated fibroblasts and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes [33–36]. Studies have found that the expression of Cav-1 in tumor-associated fibroblasts is considerably higher in breast cancer than in normal breast tissue and ductal carcinoma in situ [37]. The expression levels and roles of Cav-1 in different types of tumors are dual-edged: in the late stages of aggressive tumors such as triple - negative breast cancer and pancreatic cancer, overexpression of Cav-1 promotes tumor development and metastasis; whereas in the early stages of epithelial-derived tumors such as breast cancer and prostate cancer, reduced expression of Cav-1 is associated with tumor malignancy [38].

Breast cancer

Breast cancer ranks among the most prevalent malignancies in women worldwide, and Cav-1 expression plays a critical role in modulating its progression [39]. Cav-1 is highly expressed in normal breast epithelial cells, but its expression level is reduced in breast cancer cells, which is associated with enhanced proliferation, migration, and invasion of breast cancer cells [17].

In breast cancer cells, overexpression of Cav-1 interacts with the p85 subunit of insulin/PI3K and activates the AKT signaling pathway, thereby increasing the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax and ultimately inhibiting the proliferation of breast cancer cells [40]. In MCF-7 cells, Cav-1 exhibits tumor-suppressing effects. Reducing the expression of Cav-1 increases the membrane localization of big conductance Calcium-activated potassium channels, promotes breast cancer cell proliferation [41]. Alawin et al. demonstrated that the accumulation of γ-tocotrienol in caveolae disrupts the integrity of caveolae, thereby interfering with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) dimerization and activating the phosphorylation of AKT and mitotic signaling in SKBR3 and BT474 human breast cancer cell lines. After inhibiting the HER2-Ras-ERK pathway, breast cancer cells maintain survival through the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, a compensatory mechanism commonly seen in drug-resistant breast cancer. AKT phosphorylation further activates mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1), promotes protein synthesis and cell cycle progression (such as G1/S transition) [42]. In contrast, Cav-1 induces G2/M cell cycle arrest by upregulating p21, p27, and cyclin B1 and downregulating cyclin D2 in MDA-MB-231 and MCF-7 cells, thereby exerting anti-proliferative effects. In late-stage, Cav-1 maintains the malignant morphology of tumor cells, regulates cytoskeletal remodeling through the FAK-Rho GTPases signaling axis, preserves tumor cell stemness, and promotes tumor growth and progression. (Fig. 2) [43]. The aforementioned effects can be exacerbated by the combination of docetaxel (DTX) and cyclophosphamide [44]. In addition, increased oxidative stress levels in the breast cancer stroma trigger catabolism, reduce the transfer of Cav-1 from cancer-associated stroma to tumor cells, thereby promoting proliferation [45–46].

Summary of cav-1 functions in different cancer types. The role of Cav-1 varies across different cancer types, primarily functioning as a tumor suppressor in breast cancer and gastric cancer, while acting as an oncogenic promoter in liver cancer, melanoma, leukemia, glioma, gastric cancer, and thyroid cancer

CAFs significantly affect the progression of breast cancer. The reduced mRNA and protein expression of Cav-1 in CAFs stimulates the secretion of stromal cell-derived factor-1 (SDF-1), epidermal growth factor (EGF), and fibroblast-specific protein-1 (FSP-1), all of which are regulated by immune cells and accelerate the proliferation of breast cancer cells [47]. Cav-1 regulates both intrinsic and extrinsic apoptotic pathways in breast cancer cells. For instance, 17β-estradiol (E2) upregulates Cav-1, enhances the expression of autophagy-related proteins (Beclin-1, LC3-II, and Atg12/5) in breast cancer BT474 cells, promotes autophagosome formation, and inhibits apoptosis [48]. Shi et al. have found that the absence of Cav-1 and disruption of lipid rafts promote the assembly of V-ATPase, activate lysosomal function, and autophagosome-lysosome fusion, thereby enhancing autophagy and inhibiting apoptosis [49]. The decreased Cav-1 expression in CAFs promotes TP53-induced glycolysis in breast cancer BT474 cells, upregulates the expression of apoptosis-regulating factor like TP53-induced glycolysis and apoptosis regulator (TIGAR), and inhibits the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and their mediated ROS-dependent apoptosis [47]. EMT plays a key role in tumor progression and metastasis. The main characteristics of EMT include downregulation of epithelial markvCCNB2 (E-cadherin, γ-catenin), upregulation of mesenchymal markers (vimentin, fibronectin, N-cadherin) and transcription factors (Snail, Slug), and enhanced invasiveness, migration, and stem-like properties [48–52]. Cav-1 is involved in all aspects of EMT to drive the development of breast cancer [53–56]. Studies have found that Cav-1 expression is negatively correlated with metastatic potential in breast cancer cell lines [57]. In high-glucose-induced matrix-specific EMT, inhibiting the fatty acid synthase (FASN)/estrogen receptor α (ERα) signaling pathway obviously upregulates Cav-1 mRNA and protein levels, increases Slug expression, and promotes the invasion of breast cancer MCF-7 and T47D cells [58]. In addition, lymph node metastasis in breast cancer is associated with methylation of Cav-1/CXCR4 in tumors and lymph nodes [59]. The differential expression of Cav-1 in breast cancer subtypes with varied genetic backgrounds may justify the diversified role of breast cancer in breast cancer.

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)

In HCC, Cav-1 exhibits a compartment-specific expression pattern. Cav-1 is markedly overexpressed in tumor parenchyma, where it promotes liver cirrhosis, local tissue invasion, and tumor thrombus formation [60–61]. Meanwhile, low expression of Cav-1 in the stroma of HCC promotes vascular invasion (Fig. 2) [62]. Cav-1 not only triggers EMT by activating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby enhancing the invasion and metastasis of HCC, but also promotes the migration of HCC cells by upregulating the expression of β-galactoside α2,6-sialyltransferase I mediated by nuclear receptor 4A2/retinoid X receptor α [63–64].

Melanoma

In melanoma, Cav-1 suppresses tumor cell proliferation by inhibiting the ERK signaling pathway [65–66]. Overexpression of E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF157 downregulates the expression of Cav-1, promotes the expression of phosphorylated ERK (p-ERK), activates the ERK pathway, and thus promotes the proliferation of melanoma cells (Fig. 2). Knockdown of Cav-1 partially reverses the inhibitory effect of Ring Finger Protein 157 (RNF157) knockdown on melanoma cell proliferation. This result indicates that RNF157 binds to Cav-1 and reduces its expression in melanoma cells [67]. The function of Cav-1 in melanoma is determined by its molecular state rather than its expression level alone. In late, upon bound to E-cadherin, Cav-1 suppresses tumor growth; however, when Cav-1 phosphorylated at tyrosine-14 (Y14), it promotes migration, invasion, and metastasis [68].

Leukemia

Leukemia is a group of malignant clonal diseases originating from hematopoietic stem cells. It is characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal white blood cells in the bone marrow, which inhibits normal hematopoietic function and infiltrates organs such as the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and lymph nodes [69]. Cav-1 inhibits the proliferation and invasion of leukemia cells by inhibiting the phosphorylation of VEGFR-2 [70]. VEGFR-2 binds to Cav-1 and is located in caveolae, indicating that caveolae/lipid rafts are platforms for negatively regulating the VEGF oxidative signaling cascade, and promote the proliferation of leukemia B1647 cells [71]. Cav-1 reduces leukemia cell proliferation, migration, and invasion by activating downstream mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/Erk and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways. (Fig. 2). Overexpression of Cav-1 led to a significant decrease in K562 cell viability, accompanied by a pronounced inhibition of cell proliferation [72]. Additionally, lectin Jacalin upregulates the mRNA level and protein expression of Cav-1 in leukemia K562 cells [73].

Glioma

Glioma is a primary brain tumor originating from neuroglial cells. The expression level of Cav-1 is associated with the grade of glioma [74]. The level of Cav-1-positive vascular endothelial cells in the blood vessels of high-grade glioma tissues is significantly higher than that in low-grade glioma tissues. Knockdown of Cav-1 notably increases the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, leads to the downregulation of tight-junction-related proteins of the blood-brain barrier, such as occludin, claudin-5, and ZO-1, thereby increasing the permeability of the blood-brain barrier [75–76]. Therefore, Cav-1 may be involved in the formation of the blood-brain barrier in high-grade gliomas [77]. Another study found that ER-α36 is highly expressed in glioma tissues. Low-concentration estrogen promotes the growth of glioma cells through the rapid non-genomic signaling pathway mediated by estrogen receptor ER-α36. ER-α36 may serve as a marker for the malignancy grade of gliomas [78]. Studies have shown that epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) colocalizes with Cav-1 in glioma cells and activates Cav-1 expression [79]. Cav-1 activates PI3K/AKT signaling pathway in glioma tissues [80]. Furthermore, ER-α36 functions as an upstream regulator of this signaling pathway [81]. Moreover, both Cav-1 and ER-α36 are associated with EGFR, suggesting their potential cooperative role in glioma progression (Fig. 2) [82–83].

Gastric cancer

Gastric cancer is a malignant tumor originating from the gastric mucosal epithelial cells and is closely related to factors such as diet and helicobacter pylori infection [84]. The receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)/RANK signaling pathway is a key pathway for regulating the maturation of gastric cancer cells, mainly dominated by RANKL [85]. In gastric cancer cells, RANK is associated with enhanced cell migration induced by RANKL, a process accompanied by the activation of AKT and ERK. Since there are a large number of RANKL-secreting infiltrating T cells in gastric cancer tissues, RANKL-induced cell migration may be an important mechanism for gastric cancer metastasis. Cav-1 is involved in RANKL-induced lipid raft aggregation and cell migration, promotes the migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells [86]. Additionally, lipid rafts play an important role in the migration of gastric cancer cells, and the formation of lipid rafts is regulated by the expression of Cav-1 [87–88]. Cav-1 is not only crucial for the proper localization of RANK in lipid rafts but also plays a key role in RANKL-induced lipid raft aggregation and tumor cell migration. Moreover, the expression of mTOR is upregulated in gastric cancer. mTOR activates Cav-1 through the AKT signaling pathway, thereby inhibiting gastric cancer cell apoptosis (Fig. 2) [89].

Prostate cancer (PCa) and thyroid cancer

In PCa, Cav-1 is overexpressed relative to normal prostate cells and its levels continue to rise with disease progression, promoting tumor growth [90]. Studies have revealed that Cav-1 promotes cancer stem cell phenotypes and facilitates EMT in PCa through the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 2) [91]. Furthermore, Cav-1 mediates exogenous sphingolipid scavenging in cancer cells, rendering PCa cells metabolically vulnerable [92].

Thyroid cancer is an endocrine system tumor originating from thyroid follicular epithelial cells. In thyroid cancer, the expression of Cav-1 is upregulated and combined with the BRAF V600E mutation, enhances the invasiveness of thyroid cancer cells [93].

The function of Cav-1 in regulating tumor immunity

Role of tumor-associated immune cells in tumor development

Tumor-associated immune cells types include tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), T cells, B lymphocytes (B cells), DC, tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), natural killer cells (NK cells), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and polymorphonuclear cells (PMNs). In primary tumors, TAMs promote tumor cells invasion, enhance the survival of tumor stem cells, and induce angiogenesis. At metastatic sites, metastasis-associated macrophages facilitate tumor cell extravasation and continued growth. Therefore, whether in primary or metastatic sites, TAMs suppress the activity of cytotoxic T cells and natural killer cells, which have potential antitumor effects [94]. TAMs promote tumor progression through various mechanisms, including inhibiting T cells, enhancing angiogenesis, and accelerating tumor cell proliferation [95]. In HCC, TAMs stimulated by hepatocyte growth factor (HGF) produce inducible nitric oxide synthase (NOS), thereby inhibiting tumor growth and metastasis [96]. Another preclinical study on non-small-cell lung cancer found that the response to anti-immune costimulatory molecule CD40 immunotherapy depends on an antitumor TAM subset with an interferon gene signature and increased secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) [97]. High-dimensional single-cell sequencing analysis shows that circulating neutrophils and tumor-infiltrating neutrophils exhibit heterogeneity at the transcriptional and protein expression levels, playing dual roles in tumor immunotherapy based on the differential expression of neutrophil surface-specific markers Sell and SiglecF [98]. In melanoma patients receiving immune checkpoint blockade therapy, the tumor tissues contain a large number of neutrophils. Activated neutrophils produce high levels of inducible nitric oxide synthase, clearing melanoma cells that escape T-cell recognition [99]. The absence of Cav-1 reduces the MAPK signaling transduction in Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)-activated MDSCs, thereby reducing the signaling pathways that suppress T-cell activity [100]. In Cav-1 knockout mice, the number of B lymphocytes is reduced [101]. The immune reactivity of NK1 and NK2 receptors is stronger in Cav-1-deficient mice [102]. In B-cell lymphoma, higher Cav-1 expression leads to more aggressive disease [103–104].

Dual role of Cav-1 in TAMs

The differentiation of monocytes into macrophages depends on the regulation of various transcription factors, such as SPI1, C/EBP family (C/EBPα, C/EBPβ, C/EBPδ), MAFB, KLF4, IRF8, NF-κB (p50/p65), STAT family (STAT1/STAT3/STAT6), and AP-1 (FOS/JUN) [105–111]. Transcription factors are mainly synthesized in free ribosomes and translocated to the nucleus to regulate macrophage differentiation. Cav-1 activates the phosphorylation of ERK and early growth response-1 factor (EGR-1), increases the expression of CD115 monocytes, promotes the expression of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), and is conducive to the differentiation of monocytes into macrophages, thereby affecting the formation of TAMs (Fig. 3; Table 1) [112]. Cav-1 activates the p38 MAPK pathway and inhibits macrophage apoptosis caused by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. ER stress upregulates the expression of the pro-apoptotic transcription factor C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) in macrophages, activates endoplasmic reticulum oxidase-1α (ERO1α), leading to cell apoptosis, which is not conducive to the formation of TAMs [113].

TAMs are mainly divided into M1 and M2 types, which are influenced by the TME [114]. M1 macrophages are generally considered to have tumor-killing ability, inhibiting tumor development and promoting immune responses while M2 macrophages exhibit immunosuppressive effects, promote tissue repair and tumor development [115]. M1 macrophages are mainly present in the early stages of tumors, while M2 macrophages dominate in the middle and late stages. An increase in the number of M2 macrophages is associated with poor prognosis. The role of Cav-1 in M1 macrophages is mainly to inhibit the secretion of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, iNOS, and CXCL10. Cav-1 deficiency enhances M1 activation induced by outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) by increasing the production of ROS, the secretion of TNF-α and IL-1β (Table 1) [116].

Cav-1 contributes to tissue repair in M2 macrophages by mediating the expression and secretion of tissue repair-related molecules. Exosome-mediated Cav-1 plays a key role in the transport of primary breast cancer and metastatic organ microenvironments. Cav-1 from breast cancer exosomes acts as a signaling molecule to upregulate the expression of PMNs marker genes and inflammatory chemokines in lung epithelial cells, promote the secretion of tenascin-C (TnC) in lung fibroblasts, lead to extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, inhibit the PTEN/CCL2/VEGF-A signaling pathway, thereby promoting M2 polarization and angiogenesis, mediating intercellular communication and regulating PMNs, and ultimately promoting breast cancer lung metastasis [19]. TGF-β signaling depends on Cav-1 to promote the phenotypic conversion of M2 macrophages. Cav-1 deficiency upregulates the mRNA levels of IL-4 and IL-17β, while anti- TGF-β treatment reverses the effects of Cav-1 and plays a key regulatory role in inducing M2 differentiation (Table 1) [117]. The CSD peptide in wild-type Cav-1 restores the activity of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and drives the M2 phenotype in alveolar macrophages by reducing the binding of Cav-1 to lipopolysaccharide (LPS), exerting an anti-inflammatory effect [118]. JQ1 effectively weakens tumor cell migration induced by M2 macrophage-derived exosomes and blocks Cav-1-related intercellular exosome exchange by inhibiting lncRNA H19 to upregulate miR-107 [10].

Cav-1 specifically inhibits vascular endothelial growth factor A/vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-1 (VEGF-A/VEGFR-1) and its downstream effector molecules matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and colony-stimulating factor-1 (CSF-1) in macrophages, thereby inhibiting angiogenesis and lung cancer metastasis (Table 1) [113]. Furthermore, Cav-1 in breast cancer-derived exosomes acts as a signaling molecule to mediate intercellular communication. Cav-1 increases the protein expression of CD63, CD9, and TSG101 in lung epithelial cells, promotes the formation of pre-metastatic microenvironments, induces the secretion of TnC by lung fibroblasts, causes ECM deposition, inhibits the PTEN/CCL2/VEGF-A signaling pathway in lung macrophages, thereby promoting M2 polarization and angiogenesis, and inducing breast cancer lung metastasis [19, 117, 118]. Therefore, Cav-1 has both promoting and inhibitory effects on TAMs, and TAMs act as intermediaries between Cav-1 and tumor progression.

Role of Cav-1 in tumor-associated T Cells and DC

T cells are broadly divided into two major lineages: CD4+helper T cells and CD8+cytotoxic T cells. A distinct population with immunosuppressive capacity is defined as CD4+regulatory T cells (Tregs). Although CD8+T cells are traditionally recognized for their cytolytic activity, accumulating evidence indicates that they can acquire regulatory properties under specific conditions, known as CD8+regulatory T cells [119–121]. CD4+ or CD8+ T cells play a key role in the immune response process of tumors or autoimmune diseases by expressing T cell receptors (TCRs) to recognize tumor antigens and self-antigens. The capacity of Cav-1-deficient T cells to transform into Treg cells is increased because TGF-β signaling is amplified in the absence of Cav-1, which is likely to negatively regulate TCR-induced proliferation and promote the production of Tregs (Table 2) [119]. In CD4+ T cells, Cav-1 activates the TGF-β/TCR/CD28 signaling pathway and synergistically promotes the generation of Tregs (Fig. 3) [122]. In CD8+ T cells, Cav-1 ablation selectively impairs TCR-induced NFAT-dependent NFATc1 and cytokine gene expression, while Cav-1 re-expression promotes NFATc1 gene expression [123].

Overexpression of tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 21 (TNFRSF21) (also known as death receptor-6, DR6) and recombinant cyclin B2 (CCNB2) further enhances Cav-1-mediated T cell stimulation and activation (Table 2) [124]. Cav-1 interacts with integrins, and integrin α1β1 binding/activation of T cell protein-tyrosine phosphatase (TCPTP) is homologous to tyrosine phosphatase PTP1B, which is known to de-phosphorylate Cav-1 [125]. Cav-1 activates Ras-related botulinum toxin substrate 1 (Rac1) and the formation of membrane protrusions, which are conducive to the transport of DCs to lymph nodes and the initiation of CD4+ T cell immune responses [126]. In addition, the expression of Cav-1 in mature DCs is increased, and it promotes the migration of DCs to lymph nodes, which is conducive to inducing CD8+ T cell immune responses (Table 2) [127]. Virus-like silica vaccine (V-scVLPs) effectively delivers HCC-specific neoantigens and Toll-like receptors to activate lymphoid DCs maturation through caveolin-mediated endocytosis, thereby promoting strong CD8+ T cells and central memory T cells responses and effectively preventing the growth of orthotopic HCC (Fig. 3; Table 2) [128].

Cav-1-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming in regulating tumor immunity

Lipid metabolism mainly includes lipid uptake, digestion, absorption, transport, excretion, mobilization, lipoprotein synthesis and metabolism, fatty acid oxidation (FAO) and synthesis, phospholipid synthesis and metabolism, cholesterol synthesis [129]. There are significant differences in lipid metabolism between tumor cells and normal cells, which promote the growth and survival of tumors. The main manifestations include increased fatty acid synthesis and lipid uptake, altered lipid storage, changes in the balance of oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis, changes in lipid desaturase activity, altered cholesterol metabolism, changes in lipid signaling, autophagy regulation, changes in lipid metabolic enzyme expression and activity, and the production of lipid mediators [130]. In addition, TME, tumor development stage and therapeutic intervention also affect the lipid metabolism of tumor cells [131].

Since lipid metabolic reprogramming affects multiple functions such as cell energy balance, signal transduction and membrane stability, it is closely related to the occurrence and development of tumors [132]. The TME is composed of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), immune cells, blood, and lymphatic vessels [133]. The TME is also closely related to lipid metabolic reprogramming. It is worth noting that the lipid metabolism of tumor cells is highly heterogeneous, and different types of tumor cells exhibit different lipid metabolic characteristics.

Cav-1-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming and immune-induced tumor progression

Lipid rafts are microdomains in the cell membrane, rich in cholesterol and sphingolipids. Lipid rafts have a significant impact on cell membrane stability, signal transduction, and lipid metabolism. Cav-1 affects the distribution and transport of lipids, thereby regulating the metabolic state of cells. For example, the cell surface marker CD133 is located in lipid rafts combined with Cav-1 and cholesterol [134]. By downregulating Cav-1, the mevalonic-acid pathway (MVAP) in the metabolic rearrangement pathway is inhibited, the lipid rafts are disrupted, and the anticancer effect is achieved [135]. In addition, low shear stress induces the aggregation of Cav-1 in lipid rafts and colocalizes with membrane-type 1 matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP) in breast cancer cells [136]. Breast cancer cells promote lipid metabolism by increasing the uptake of exogenous lipids and endogenous lipid synthesis, thereby supporting their proliferation and survival [137]. In contrast, CAFs and TAMs promote cholesterol accumulation and lipid peroxidation in HCC, weakening the antitumor effects of TAMs [138].

Cav-1 affects lipid metabolism by influencing fatty acid synthase and fatty ester synthesis pathways. Overexpression or absence of Cav-1 changes lipid accumulation and degradation, thereby affecting cell energy metabolism and survival status. In metastatic PCa cells containing androgen receptor (AR), Cav-1 induces the expression of acetyl-CoA carboxylase-1 (ACC1) and fatty acid synthase (FASN), increasing the phosphorylation of AR. The phosphorylation of AR at serine 81 is closely related to the growth and proliferation of PCa [139]. In the high-glucose-induced matrix-specific EMT process, silencing FASN or estrogen receptor α (ERα) triggers the upregulation of Cav-1 expression and enhances the invasiveness of tumor cells [140]. In addition, the upregulation of FASN, Cav-1, and P-glycoprotein (P-gp) in tumors promote the proliferation of melanoma cells in obese states, leading to poor prognosis [141].

The role of FAO in regulating immune cell function cannot be underestimated. Treg cells, M2 macrophages, and memory T cells mainly rely on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) derived from FAO for energy production [142]. Upregulation of FAO genes such as carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A) increases FAO, providing energy for the growth of Treg cells [143]. Cav-1 enhances the colocalization of TCR with lymphocyte-specific protein tyrosine kinase (Lck), promotes TCR phosphorylation and downstream signaling, thereby increasing the number of Treg cells. CAFs in pancreatic cancer promote tumor cell proliferation and metastasis through lipid metabolic reprogramming [144].

Lipid metabolic reprogramming plays an important role in T cells in pancreatic cancer, affecting their antitumor function [138]. Cav-1 induces the activation of its downstream effector molecule E2F1, which induces mitochondrial autophagy together with pro-apoptotic mitochondrial protein BNIP3 in CAFs, thereby selectively clearing dysfunctional mitochondria, avoiding excessive accumulation of ROS, and promoting cancer development [37].

Cav-1-mediated lipid metabolic reprogramming and immune-suppressed tumor progression

Studies have shown that cholesterol depletion downregulates the expression of Cav-1 in oral cancer [124]. Different concentrations of methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) induce cholesterol depletion, change the morphology of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) cells, and downregulate the expression of Cav-1, achieving an anticancer effect [145]. Changes in Cav-1 expression also affect cholesterol synthesis and transport, thereby weakening tumor cell proliferation. Cav-1 deficiency leads to the accumulation of cholesterol in the cell membrane, increases ROS, and causes tumor cell death [136]. Cav-1 binds to cholesterol to promote caveolae formation, while overexpression of Cav-1 promotes cholesterol efflux to caveolae, reducing intracellular high cholesterol levels. In OSCC, cholesterol depletion increases the fluidity of the primary tumor cell membrane, reduces the expression of Cav-1, thereby weakening the migration ability of tumor cells [126].

M2-like macrophages rely on FAO to meet bioenergetic needs to maintain antitumor effects, and inhibiting FAO promotes M1 polarization [146]. Compared with wild-type mice, the proportion of macrophages infiltrating adipose tissue in Cav-1-deficient mice is increased, which enhances the pro-tumor effects of adipocytes [147]. Cav-1 expression is negatively correlated with acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (ACADM). Upregulating Cav-1 promotes the nuclear translocation of sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1 (SREBP-1), inhibits ACADM activity, enhances FAO, thereby promoting the invasion of HCC cells [148]. The increase in fatty acid content in the TME is conducive to the production of Treg cells, which rely on exogenous fatty acid uptake to exert immunosuppressive functions. Acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1) is the main enzyme for cholesterol ester synthesis in CD8+ T cells. Inhibiting ACAT1 increases the cholesterol level in melanoma tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), thereby inducing an immune response. Inhibiting Cav-1 expression upregulates ACAT1 and promotes breast cancer lung metastasis [149]. Therefore, Cav-1 not only increases the number of Tregs to suppress immune responses and promotes tumor progression through lipid metabolic reprogramming, but also affects tumor development through lipid metabolic reprogramming (Fig. 4).

Targeting Cav-1 for tumor therapy: prospects and challenges

Based on a deep understanding of the functions and mechanisms of Cav-1, various therapeutic strategies have been developed. For example, combining Cav-1 intervention with chemotherapy or radiotherapy reduce drug resistance or enhance immune responses in the TME, or targeting Cav-1-regulated cell signaling pathways attenuate the uptake of pro-survival signals and increase drug delivery efficiency of cancer cells.

The anti-tumor treatment strategy of upregulating Cav-1

Methotrexate or etoposide intervention promotes the demethylation of the Cav-1 promoter, increases the transcriptional expression of Cav-1, and activates ERK and ROS generation. ROS promotes Src family kinase-dependent phosphorylation of Cav-1, activates Rac1 and metalloproteinases. These findings indicate that methotrexate or etoposide upregulating Cav-1 expression [150]. Cav-1 promotes the internalization of the humanized monoclonal antibody-drug conjugate trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1), thereby triggering apoptosis in HER2-positive breast cancer cells [151]. Metformin upregulates Cav-1 expression, enhances the internalization of T-DM1, and improves the efficacy of T-DM1 against breast cancer [152]. It has been reported that paclitaxel promotes the phosphorylation of Cav-1 at tyrosine 14 (Y14), down-regulates the B-cell lymphoma 2 (BCL2) gene, upregulates the expression of p21 and p53, and induces apoptosis in breast cancer MCF-7 cells [153].

Anti-Cav-1 based cancer therapies

Cav-1 activates the H-Ras/phosphatidylinositol-specific phospholipase Cε (PI-PLCε) signaling cascade in membrane caveolae, inducing the invasion and migration of prostate cancer cells. Simvastatin enhances the antitumor effects of androgen receptor antagonists by downregulating Cav-1 expression [154]. In addition, lovastatin reduces Cav-1 protein expression and prolongs the half-life of HER2, thereby enhancing the therapeutic effects of T-DM1 on HER2-positive tumors [155]. Therefore, using statins to lower cholesterol levels or downregulate Cav-1 expression is an effective strategy for the prevention and treatment of tumors. Additionally, a small number of anticancer drugs exert their effects by competing with Cav-1 for receptor binding. Bisphenol A (BPA), as an estrogen - like compound, competes with Cav-1 for binding to G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor in MDA-MB-231 cells in the hypoxic TME, triggers the release of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90), stabilizes and activates hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), upregulates the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and induces proliferation [156–158]. Celastrol (CeT) inhibits the Cav-1/LOX-1 pathway, reducing lipid accumulation and tumor stem cell-like properties in clear cell renal cell carcinoma, thereby inhibiting tumor development [157]. Besides, CeT also counteract the tumor-promoting effects induced by senescent cells by downregulating Cav-1 expression [159]. Astragaloside IV enhances the chemosensitivity of paclitaxel to breast cancer by inhibiting Cav-1 expression and activating the eNOS/NO/ONOO pathway [160]. In addition, the administration of Src family tyrosine kinase inhibitors (SFK inhibitors) inhibits the phosphorylation of Cav-1 in melanoma B16F10 cells, thereby inhibiting the early stage of lung metastasis [161].

In prostate cancer, colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, gastric adenocarcinoma, and clear-cell renal cell carcinoma, high Cav-1 expression exhibits pro-oncogenic effects. While Cav-1 plays a dual and opposing role in breast cancer progression: it acts as a tumor suppressor during the early stages by maintaining cellular homeostasis and inhibiting oncogenic signals, yet switches to a tumor promoter in advanced stages, where it facilitates cancer cell survival, metastasis, and drug resistance through the activation of pro-survival pathways (Table 3). Currently, drugs reported to exert antitumor effects by upregulating Cav-1 are mainly focused on anti-breast cancer therapy, while whether drugs targeting other types of tumors have similar effects requires further investigation. Most clinically used antitumor drugs are synthetic compounds, and whether active ingredients in traditional Chinese medicine exert antitumor effects by regulating Cav-1 requires further screening and validation.

Conclusion and outlook

This review explores the immune-regulatory roles of Cav-1 in different types of cancer and its impact on tumor-associated immune cells. It also summarizes the involvement of Cav-1 in lipid metabolic reprogramming and elaborates on its role in cancer therapy. The post-translational modifications of Cav-1 holds significant implications for the TME. Phosphorylation of Cav-1 inhibits the proliferation of CAFs in breast cancer [162]. The palmitoylation of Cav-1 may enhance membrane contact, thereby inducing cholesterol accumulation in the TME [163]. Conversely, palmitoylation-deficient Cav-1 (C133/143/156S mutants) reduces phosphorylation levels of Src and AKT, suppressing tumor progression [164]. Research on Cav-1-mediated post-translational modifications would benefit from further elucidation of how Cav-1-mediated epigenetic modifications influence the TME.

The impact of tumor heterogeneity on immunotherapy is not only attributed to the heterogeneity of antigen presentation but also to the diversity of the immune microenvironment. Different tumor subclones exhibit distinct neoantigen profiles, preventing the immune system from comprehensively recognizing all cancer cells. The tumor core often lacks T cell infiltration, while the periphery may be enriched with Tregs and MDSCs. Cav-1 may contribute to this heterogeneity by modulating lipid raft-dependent signaling and metabolic reprogramming. Cav-1 modulates TCR signaling via lipid rafts and indirectly participates in tumor-suppressive immune processes. However, high Cav-1 expression in aggressive tumor subclones promotes metastasis through integrin signaling. Additionally, Cav-1 regulates cholesterol metabolism, influencing immune synapse formation and conferring metabolic heterogeneity to tumor cells, thereby altering the cytotoxic efficiency of CD8+ T cells. Advances in multi-omics research will help clarify how differential Cav-1 expression affects therapeutic responses in tumors.

Whether pharmacological targeting of Cav-1, either alone or in combination with immunotherapy, can improve clinical outcomes appears to be closely associated with the TME where Cav-1 is expressed. Given its context-dependent roles, strategies for targeting Cav-1 should be tailored to specific cancer types: in breast cancer, upregulating Cav-1 may enhance sensitivity to antibody-drug conjugates such as T-DM1; in prostate, colorectal, and gastric cancers where Cav-1 exerts pro-oncogenic effects, inhibiting Cav-1 or disrupting caveolae integrity may represent a promising therapeutic strategy; in the context of immune regulation, blocking Cav-1 in TAMs may reprogram the immunosuppressive TME, while modulating Cav-1 in T cells could help restore antitumor immunity. The role of Cav-1 in Tregs is complex and may influence immune tolerance balance. Future investigations should focus on integrating Cav-1-targeted agents with immunotherapeutic approaches in clinical settings. Profiling therapy-resistant tumors may reveal key resistance mechanisms. Although various cell- and animal-based models have helped clarify the immune-regulatory functions of Cav-1, further studies are needed. Pharmacologically, Cav-1 expression may predict responses to chemotherapy/targeted therapy, and its modulation by traditional drugs warrants in-depth investigation. These findings lay the groundwork for future therapy development based on Cav-1.
