Botulinum Toxin Type A Reduces Pain and NF-κB Levels in a Rat Model of Complex Regional Pain Syndrome
Authors: Francis Sahngun Nahm, Byunghun Min, Joon Hee Lee, Minhye Chang, Sang-Soep Nahm, Pyung Bok Lee, Eun Joo Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a chronic pain disorder characterized by severe and disproportionate symptoms, with incompletely understood mechanisms. Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB), a key transcription factor in neuroinflammation and pain signaling, is upregulated in animal models of CRPS. Botulinum toxin type A (BTA) has analgesic efficacy in neuropathic pain; however, its effect on NF-κB in CRPS has not been evaluated. This study investigated whether BTA attenuates pain and suppresses NF-κB expression in a rat model of CRPS.MethodsA chronic post-ischemia pain (CPIP) model was induced in male Sprague-Dawley rats. After induction, rats were randomly assigned to receive either BTA or normal saline (NS) injection into the plantar surface of the ipsilateral hind paw. A separate sham group underwent anesthesia without CPIP induction or injection. Mechanical thresholds were assessed, and NF-κB expression in the foot and spinal cord was quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays.ResultsFollowing BTA injection, mechanical thresholds improved significantly compared with the NS group on days 14 and 21 (P = 0.039 and 0.010). NF-κB expression was significantly reduced in the ipsilateral foot (P = 0.009) and spinal cord (P = 0.025) of the BTA group compared with the NS group, whereas contralateral tissues showed no significant differences.ConclusionThese findings suggest an association between reduced NF-κB levels and the analgesic effects of BTA, supporting its potential therapeutic role in CRPS.Keywords: botulinum toxins, type A, complex regional pain syndrome, chronic pain, NF-κB, transcription factors

Introduction

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a challenging chronic pain condition that often develops after seemingly minor trauma yet results in severe, persistent pain.1,2 It is characterized by pain that exceeds the expected severity of the initial insult and may be accompanied by vasomotor changes, trophic alterations, and motor dysfunction.3 Although the exact pathophysiology of CRPS remains unclear, peripheral and central sensitization of the nociceptive system are considered key contributors, involving alterations at the peripheral, spinal, and cortical levels.4–6

Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) is a key transcription factor that regulates inflammation, immune responses, cell survival, and proliferation.7,8 Dysregulated NF-κB has been implicated in malignancies as well as in inflammatory and autoimmune disorders.9 NF-κB is also involved in long-term potentiation and neuronal plasticity, processes implicated in abnormal pain perception.10,11 A recent CRPS animal study demonstrated increased cerebral NF-κB expression, suggesting that peripheral injury can elevate cerebral NF-κB levels4 and activate pain-related cerebral processes even after minor trauma. The same model showed increased NF-κB expression in muscle and spinal cord, while systemic or intrathecal administration of NF-κB inhibitor pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate substantially alleviated mechanical and cold allodynia.12 Also, spinal nerve ligation has been shown to activate NF-κB signaling in the spinal dorsal horn, contributing to neuropathic pain pathogenesis. Consistent with these findings, a CRPS type I mouse study demonstrated that CXCL13/CXCR5–driven NF-κB activation in the dorsal horn mediates persistent pain, and blocking this axis attenuates nociception.13,14

Notably, a network analysis using text-mining tools identified NF-κB as a potential central regulator of CRPS initiation and progression, linking multiple pathogenic mechanisms—including neuropathic inflammation, classic inflammation, hypoxic changes, and sympathetic dysregulation—into a unified framework.15 This network also revealed associations between NF-κB and multiple CRPS-related mediators, including substance P, calcitonin gene-related peptide, bradykinin, vasoactive intestinal peptide, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, suggesting its role in integrating inflammatory, vascular, and neuronal pathways in CRPS pathogenesis. Although not yet validated experimentally, this hypothesis provides a compelling rationale to target NF-κB in therapeutic strategies.

Botulinum toxin type A (BTA), widely used for conditions involving excessive muscle activity, has recently emerged as a promising therapeutic option for neuropathic pain.16,17 Its analgesic effects possibly thought to involve inhibition of presynaptic release of pain-related neurotransmitters, including acetylcholine, calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), substance P, and glutamate.18–20 Recent mechanistic studies further highlight the multiple actions of BTA, including reduced glutamate/neuropeptide release and suppression of neuroinflammatory signaling.20 Despite these findings, no study has directly examined the effect of BTA on NF-κB expression in animal models of CRPS.

Given BTA’s demonstrated efficacy in neuropathic pain, the observed upregulation of NF-κB in CRPS models, and the hypothesis that NF-κB is a central integrator in CRPS pathogenesis, we aimed to test whether BTA attenuates pain by suppressing NF-κB expression. Specifically, we evaluated the effect of BTA on NF-κB activity and its potential role in modulating pain-related mechanisms.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Considerations

This study was designed as an experimental animal study and is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE (Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments) guidelines (Supplementary Material). All experimental procedures complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Research Council and with the institutional standards for animal research at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. All procedures for anesthesia and euthanasia were performed in strict accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital approved the study protocol (IACUC No. BA1206–106/043–01).

Animals

Male Sprague-Dawley rats (250–350 g, approximately 8–9 weeks of age) were purchased from Orient Bio Inc. (Seongnam, Korea) in this study. Animals were housed in groups of up to three per cage under a 12-h light/dark cycle in a temperature-controlled environment (approximately 21°C), with free access to food and water. Rats were acclimated for at least 1 week before model induction.

Chronic Post-Ischemia Perfusion (CPIP) Model Generation

The overall experimental procedure is presented in Figure 1. This CPIP model, a validated and widely used approach in CRPS-related research, was employed.21 After acclimation, the CPIP model was induced according to the procedure first described by Coderre et al.22 Briefly, anesthesia was induced by placing rats in a chamber with 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen (3 L/min) and maintained with 2–3% isoflurane via a nose mask. A Nitrile 70 Durometer O-ring (O-ring West Inc., Lynnwood, WA, USA) was placed around the left hind limb, just above the ankle, to induce ischemia. After 3 h, the O-ring was removed to allow hind limb reperfusion. Sham-operated animals underwent the same anesthesia protocol without O-ring application.

Schematic diagram of the experimental procedure. The sham group underwent no intervention. After chronic post-ischemia pain (CPIP) induction, rats received either normal saline (NS) or botulinum toxin type A (BTA) injection into the plantar surface on day 7. Tissue samples were collected on day 21 for enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay analysis (ELISA) of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB).

Behavioral Assessment and Group Allocation

Mechanical threshold (MT) testing was performed at baseline, 4 h, and on days 1, 2, 7, 14, and 21 after CPIP induction, as described previously.4,5 For testing, rats were placed individually in plastic cages with wire mesh floors. After a 20-min acclimation period, MT was measured by an independent investigator blinded to group allocation using von Frey filaments (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA) applied to the mid-plantar region of the hind paws with the up-down method.23 Testing began with the 2.0-g filament. A positive response (withdrawal or flinch) prompted testing with a lighter filament, whereas no response led to application of the next heavier filament. Blinding was maintained during both behavioral testing and NF-κB quantification in tissue samples.

Changes in MT (CMT, %) were calculated as follows, based on a previous study.4

Rats with a ≥50% reduction in CMT on day 7 were considered to have developed CRPS-like symptoms and were selected for further study. These rats were randomly allocated to either the normal saline (NS) group or the BTA group. In total, three groups were studied: an NS group, a BTA group, and a sham group that received no intervention and served as the control.

Drug Administration

All injections were performed under isoflurane anesthesia. In the BTA group, BTA (40 U/kg, diluted in 0.2 mL of NS, Allergan, Irvine, CA, USA) was injected into the superficial plantar layer. The NS group received 0.2 mL of NS into the same anatomical region. Sham animals were anesthetized with isoflurane but did not receive an injection.

Sample Preparation

On postoperative day 21, animals were deeply anesthetized with 2–3% isoflurane in oxygen (3 L/min). Under confirmed surgical anesthesia, rats were humanely euthanized by transcardial perfusion with 200 mL of ice-cold normal saline to remove residual blood for protein analysis. Muscle specimens were collected from the superficial plantar layer of the ipsilateral and contralateral paws. Spinal cord segments corresponding to the L5–L6 levels were excised, rapidly immersed in isopentane, transferred to dry ice, and subsequently stored at –80°C until further processing. Spinal cords were sectioned to isolate the dorsal region containing the dorsal horns, and tissues were bisected along the midline to separate the ipsilateral and contralateral sides.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) for NF-κB Expression

NF-κB levels were quantified with a commercially available sandwich ELISA kit (Cloud-Clone Corp., Houston, TX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, spinal cord specimens were washed in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.02 mol/L, pH 7.0–7.2) to remove residual blood, minced into small fragments, and homogenized in 5–10 mL PBS on ice. Homogenates were sonicated, centrifuged at 5,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected for assay. The lyophilized standard was reconstituted with 1.0 mL standard diluent to yield a 20 ng/mL stock solution, which was serially diluted twofold to generate a standard curve (0.312–20 ng/mL; lower limit of detection, 0.126 ng/mL). Standards and samples were loaded into a 96-well microplate pre-coated with an NF-κB capture antibody and incubated for 2 h at 37°C.

After incubation, a biotin-conjugated detection antibody was added and incubated for 1 h at 37°C, followed by five washes and incubation with avidin-conjugated horseradish peroxidase (HRP) for 30 minutes at 37°C. After seven additional washes, 90 μL substrate solution was added to each well, and plates were kept in the dark for 20 minutes at 37°C. The reaction was terminated with sulfuric acid, and absorbance was measured with a spectrophotometer. Protein concentrations were calculated from the standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with R software, version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Sample size was estimated with the pwr package in R, based on an effect size (ε2 = 0.455) derived from our previous three-group study of cerebral NF-κB expression. Assuming the Kruskal–Wallis test as analysis of variance on ranks for planning, a three-arm design with α = 0.05 and β = 0.20 required six animals per group (total N = 18). Because each group had fewer than 10 animals, nonparametric tests were used. Within-group temporal changes in CMT were analyzed with the Friedman test. Between-group differences in CMT at each time point and in NF-κB expression were first assessed with the Kruskal–Wallis test. When the omnibus test was significant, post hoc pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests compared the NS group with each of the other groups, with Bonferroni adjustment applied to control for multiple testing. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Behavioral Assessment

A total of 18 rats were enrolled, with six assigned to each group. Baseline CMT measurements did not differ among groups before CPIP induction. The Friedman test showed no significant temporal variation in the sham group (χ2 = 2.57, P = 0.861). In contrast, significant within-group effects were found in the NS group (χ2 = 19.60, P = 0.003) and the BTA group (χ2 = 31.65, P < 0.001).

On day 14, the Kruskal–Wallis test indicated a significant overall group difference in CMT% (χ2(2) = 13.178, P = 0.001). Post hoc Dunn’s test showed that CMT% was lower in the NS group than in the sham group (adjusted P < 0.001) and lower in the NS group than in the BTA group (adjusted P = 0.039). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the sham and BTA groups (adjusted P = 0.095).

On day 21, the overall group effect remained significant (χ2(2) = 15.01, P = 0.001). Post hoc analysis showed that CMT% was lower in the NS group than in both the sham group (adjusted P < 0.001) and the BTA group (adjusted P = 0.010). In contrast, no significant difference was found between the sham and BTA groups (adjusted P = 0.0954, Figure 2).

Time course of changes in mechanical threshold (CMT, %) following CPIP induction and treatment in the sham, normal saline (NS), and botulinum toxin type A (BTA) groups (n = 6 per group). Both the NS and BTA groups showed significant reductions in CMT on day 7 compared with the sham group, confirming the development of CRPS-like symptoms. After BTA injection, the BTA group demonstrated progressive recovery, with no significant difference from the sham group on days 14 and 21. In contrast, the NS group continued to show reduced CMT throughout the observation period. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Kruskal–Wallis test with Mann–Whitney test and Bonferroni correction. * P < 0.05 versus the NS group.

NF-κB Expression

NF-κB expression in the ipsilateral foot differed significantly among groups (χ2(2) = 12.431, P = 0.002). Post hoc analysis revealed that NF-κB expression was higher in the NS group than in both the sham group (adjusted P = 0.009) and the BTA group (adjusted P = 0.009; Figure 3A).

(A) In the ipsilateral foot, NF-κB expression was lower in the BTA group than in the NS group (P = 0.0091). In the contralateral foot, no significant differences were observed between the BTA and NS groups (P = 0.5025). (B) In the ipsilateral spinal cord, NF-κB expression was lower in the BTA group than in the NS group (P = 0.0249). In the contralateral spinal cord, no significant difference was found between the BTA and NS groups (P = 0.2389). Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Kruskal–Wallis test with Mann–Whitney test and Bonferroni correction. *P < 0.05 compared to the NS group.

In the contralateral foot, NF-κB expression also differed among groups (χ2(2) = 7.879, P = 0.020). The BTA group showed significantly higher NF-κB expression than the sham group (adjusted P = 0.0075); however, no significant differences were found between the NS group and either the sham group (adjusted P = 0.448) or the BTA group (adjusted P = 0.503; Figure 3A).

In the ipsilateral spinal cord, NF-κB expression differed significantly among groups (χ2(2) = 14.543, P < 0.001). NF-κB expression was higher in the NS group than in both the sham group (adjusted P = 0.009) and the BTA group (adjusted P = 0.025, Figure 3B).

In the contralateral spinal cord, a significant group effect was also observed (χ2(2) = 12.816, P = 0.002). NF-κB expression was significantly lower in the sham group than in the NS group (adjusted P = 0.009); however, no significant difference was found between the BTA and NS groups (adjusted P = 0.239, 1 3B).

Taken together, these findings suggest that BTA reduces NF-κB expression in the ipsilateral foot and spinal cord but has no significant effect on the contralateral side.

Discussion

In this study, BTA administration in a CPIP rat model alleviated mechanical allodynia, as reflected by restored MT. BTA also reduced NF-κB expression in the ipsilateral foot and spinal cord, but this effect did not extend to the contralateral side. These findings suggest that BTA exerts its analgesic effects in the CPIP model through both peripheral and central mechanisms, supporting its potential role in attenuating local neuroinflammatory processes via the NF-κB pathway.

In our study, no significant suppression of NF-κB was observed in the contralateral tissues. This pattern suggests that, within the limits of our assay, the administered BTA did not undergo appreciable systemic absorption or redistribution to the contralateral side. These findings are in line with the predominantly local action and limited systemic spread reported for BTA at therapeutic doses. Instead, the effect of BTA remained localized to the injection site and the associated primary sensory neurons. This is consistent with the known pharmacokinetic profile of BTA at therapeutic doses, where it primarily exerts its action locally and through ipsilateral axonal pathways without causing systemic or contralateral effects.19

Regarding the lateralized effect in the spinal cord, the absence of NF-κB suppression on the contralateral side is consistent with the mechanism of action of BTA and the characteristics of the CPIP model. Following local plantar injection, BTA is internalized by ipsilateral primary afferent terminals and undergoes retrograde axonal transport to the ipsilateral dorsal root ganglion and dorsal horn, where it exerts its molecular effects.19

In the CPIP model, nociceptive input is predominantly ipsilateral, and central sensitization within this observation period may not have extended sufficiently to produce bilateral spinal NF-κB upregulation. Importantly, the validity of our ELISA assay is supported by the detection of baseline NF-κB levels in the sham group and significantly elevated levels in the contralateral spinal cord of the NS group, supporting the interpretation that the absence of suppression in the BTA group is more likely to reflect biological specificity than a major technical limitation in detection sensitivity.

Several mechanisms may underlie these outcomes. BTA suppresses the presynaptic release of pain-related neurotransmitters, including glutamate, substance P, and CGRP, thereby dampening peripheral and central sensitization.20,24 BTA also modulates neuroimmune signaling by suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways, such as the TLR2/MyD88 and TLR4/NF-κB cascades, leading to reduced phosphorylation of IKKα/β and downstream transcriptional activity.25,26 Another proposed mechanism is the promotion of anti-inflammatory cytokine release and reduction of neuronal hyperexcitability.27 The NF-κB suppression observed in this study aligns with these mechanistic insights, highlighting a plausible molecular pathway through which BTA mitigates CRPS-associated pain.

These findings are consistent with previous preclinical research showing that BTA attenuates neuropathic pain and neuroinflammation via downregulation of NF-κB and related signaling networks.26,28 Clinical studies have also reported substantial pain relief in patients with CRPS treated with BTA, regardless of the route of administration, including lumbar sympathetic blocks29 or subcutaneous and intramuscular injections.30,31 Mechanistic studies further confirm that NF-κB activation is closely linked to the expression of pain-related neuropeptides, including CGRP, transient receptor potential vanilloid 1, and substance P, all of which can be modulated by BTA.32

However, not all evidence supports a direct link between BTA and NF-κB modulation in CRPS. Although several studies have documented NF-κB inhibition in neuropathic pain models,33,34 CRPS-specific molecular evidence remains limited. Some clinical reports describe analgesic benefits without accompanying molecular confirmation,35,36 and heterogeneity in dosing regimens, injection sites, and disease duration may contribute to inconsistent outcomes. A recent narrative review of intramuscular BTA for CRPS highlighted these heterogeneities and called for standardized protocols in both experimental and clinical settings.37

Beyond CRPS, BTA has been used clinically in various neuropathic pain conditions. In patients with postherpetic neuralgia (PHN), subcutaneous BTA injection produced significant reductions in pain severity and opioid consumption, demonstrating its efficacy in alleviating PHN-related pain.38,39 Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) has also shown favorable responses to BTA. A recent meta-analysis reported that BTA is an effective and safe option for patients with refractory TN unresponsive to pharmacological or surgical therapy, and it also produced statistically and clinically meaningful analgesic effects in diabetic neuropathy.40 Comparable findings were reported in spinal cord injury-related neuropathic pain, where BTA led to a higher proportion of patients achieving >20% pain reduction at 4 and 8 weeks, along with a trend toward improved physical quality-of-life scores.41 Furthermore, in peripheral neuropathic pain of various etiologies, repeated BTA injections provided significant and sustained pain relief for up to 24 weeks and had a favorable safety profile.42

Our results address an important gap in the literature, namely the paucity of experimental studies directly linking BTA therapy to NF-κB modulation in CRPS models. By demonstrating both functional recovery and NF-κB suppression in a validated CPIP model, our findings strengthen the biological plausibility of BTA as a dual-acting therapeutic agent. This integrative approach, combining behavioral and molecular endpoints, bridges mechanistic research in neuropathic pain with clinical findings in CRPS, thereby strengthening the foundation for targeted therapy development.

The strengths of this study include the use of a standardized CRPS animal model, integration of objective pain threshold measurements with molecular analysis, and consistency of the observed effects with established anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Nonetheless, a few limitations should be acknowledged. First, only a single BTA dose and post-treatment time point were evaluated, precluding assessment of dose–response relationships and long-term effects. Second, although NF-κB suppression was confirmed, functional activity was not directly assessed. As the study measured total NF-κB protein levels using ELISA rather than pathway activation or nuclear translocation, there is a limitation in directly linking NF-κB suppression to the observed analgesic effects. More specific assays—such as nuclear/cytoplasmic fractionation with Western blotting, electrophoretic mobility shift assays, or reporter gene assays—would be required. Third, the entire signaling cascade was not characterized. Downstream changes in cytokines, receptor trafficking, or shifts in the microglial phenotype. Therefore, the mechanistic interpretation of NF-κB modulation remains limited, and further studies are needed.

In conclusion, BTA treatment in a CPIP rat model produced antinociceptive effects and suppressed NF-κB expression, supporting a dual role in pain modulation and inflammation control. These findings provide novel mechanistic evidence in CRPS research and highlight BTA as a promising candidate for targeted therapy. Future investigations should explore optimal dosing strategies, the durability of molecular effects, and potential synergistic combinations with other treatments to enhance clinical efficacy in CRPS management.
