Delayed Diagnosis of Narcolepsy: Family History and Clinical Features as Predictive Factors
Authors: Eun Hye Lee, Tae-Won Kim, Young-Chan Kim, Su-Hyung Kim, Yoo Hyun Um, Ho Jun Seo, Jong-Hyun Jeong, Seung Chul Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective Narcolepsy often begins in adolescence, yet diagnosis is frequently delayed. This study aimed to identify factors associated with delayed diagnosis of narcolepsy, defined as more than 1 year between symptom onset and diagnosis, focusing on age at onset, clinical symptoms, and family history. Methods We recruited 235 patients with clinically diagnosed narcolepsy from St.Vincent’s Hospital in South Korea. Participants completed an online survey assessing age at symptom onset, diagnostic timing, clinical features, and family history of sleep disorders. Patients were classified into a non-delay group (diagnosis within 1 year, n=53) and a delay group (diagnosis after 1 year, n=182). Group differences were analyzed using independent t-tests and chi-square tests. Results The mean diagnostic delay was 0.49±0.50 years in the non-delay group and 10.96±8.37 years in the delay group. The delay group had a significantly younger age at symptom onset than the non-delay group (15.13±4.88 vs. 17.89±7.91 years, p=0.002). Diagnosis in the delay group typically occurred only after marked functional impairment due to excessive daytime sleepiness. Cataplexy showed a nonsignificant trend toward earlier diagnosis, while other narcolepsy symptoms and family history were not associated with diagnostic timing. Conclusion Earlier age at onset is the strongest risk factor for delayed diagnosis of narcolepsy. Neither symptom profile nor family history reliably facilitates early recognition. Proactive screening and improved awareness among clinicians and families are needed to reduce diagnostic delay and its psychosocial burden.

INTRODUCTION

Narcolepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) and other rapid eye movement (REM)-related phenomena, including cataplexy, sleep paralysis, and hypnagogic hallucinations [1,2]. The disorder is typically categorized into two types: narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), characterized by the presence of cataplexy and a deficiency of hypocretin-1, and narcolepsy type 2 (NT2), which is marked by EDS but does not include cataplexy [3].

Symptoms typically onset between the ages of 10 and 30, with a significant proportion of cases beginning in adolescence [4]. Onset during this developmental period can complicate early recognition due to overlapping symptoms with normative physiological changes. Consequently, diagnosis is often delayed by a decade or more [5]. This delay is especially evident in children and adolescents, who may struggle to articulate their symptoms clearly [6].

Youth may display atypical symptoms like hyperactivity, irritability, and restlessness as a compensatory response to the underlying sleepiness associated with narcolepsy. This can lead to a misdiagnosis of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder or other psychiatric conditions [7-9]. Atypical presentations of cataplexy, such as facial weakness or slight buckling of the knees, may be misidentified as tics, clumsiness, or even seizures [10]. Common sleep-related comorbidities often coexist with narcolepsy and share overlapping symptoms. Consequently, narcolepsy may be obscured by these comorbid conditions or their treatments, further delaying recognition [5,11].

Narcolepsy is a disorder with a strong genetic susceptibility, most notably associated with the HLA-DQB1*06:02 allele 1, which is present in up to 98% of NT1 patients. However, this allele is also found in a significant portion of the general population, where it confers a markedly increased risk for disease development [8].

This genetic susceptibility is not exclusive to narcolepsy; it is shared across a spectrum of sleep-wake disorders characterized by REM sleep dysregulation. As a result, families of patients with narcolepsy often show a prevalence not only of narcolepsy but also of other sleep disorders, such as hypersomnia and REM sleep-related phenomena [12].

Clinically, this broader pattern of familial sleep disturbance suggests that family history in narcolepsy should be viewed as a history of sleep disorders rather than narcolepsy alone. This perspective may influence both symptom interpretation and diagnostic timing [13,14]. Given these substantial consequences, identifying factors associated with earlier diagnosis represents a critical clinical priority [15].

Family history is a particularly compelling factor, as having affected family members may increase symptom awareness, lower the threshold for medical consultation, and accelerate diagnosis [16]. However, empirical evidence on whether family history serves as a protective factor against diagnostic delay remains limited and inconclusive. To date, no studies in Korea have specifically assessed whether family history reduces diagnostic delay in narcolepsy.

Delayed recognition of narcolepsy presents not only a diagnostic challenge but also significant clinical and psychosocial consequences [17]. Prolonged diagnostic latency is consistently linked to a lower quality of life, impaired academic and occupational functioning, increased psychiatric comorbidities, and reduced social integration. Adolescents and young adults with undiagnosed narcolepsy frequently face cumulative disadvantages during crucial developmental periods, such as interrupted education, stigmatization, and limited vocational opportunities [18]. From a public health perspective, reducing diagnostic delays is not just about clinical accuracy; it is a key determinant of long-term functional prognosis and societal participation.

This study builds on previous research that identified diagnostic delay as a significant issue for narcolepsy patients, particularly when symptoms first emerge during childhood or adolescence [19,20]. Our previous work demonstrated that the average diagnostic delay exceeded one year in a significant proportion of cases, with pediatric onset being a key predictor of delayed diagnosis [19]. Therefore, identifying predictive factors that enable early diagnosis—particularly within 1 year of symptom onset—is an urgent clinical priority. Since narcolepsy shows familial aggregation and has a strong genetic component, we hypothesized that individuals with a positive family history of sleep abnormalities are more likely to receive an earlier diagnosis due to heightened disease awareness within the family [21,22].

Furthermore, we explored symptom-based predictors of narcolepsy that could aid in earlier recognition. To investigate these hypotheses, we conducted an internet-based survey, gathering responses from narcolepsy patients about symptom onset, diagnostic timing, family history, and clinical features.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study utilized a cross-sectional design with an internetbased survey aimed at individuals clinically diagnosed with narcolepsy. A total of 235 participants were recruited from St. Vincent’s Hospital in Suwon, South Korea. All participants had previously received a narcolepsy diagnosis and were undergoing treatment at the time of the survey. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of St. Vincent’s Hospital (No.VC22QISE0225), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Diagnostic latency was defined as the interval (in years) between the age at symptom onset and the age at formal diagnosis, calculated by subtracting the age at symptom onset from the age at diagnosis. Diagnostic delay was operationally defined as a time interval exceeding 12 months between symptom onset and formal diagnosis. This threshold was based on prior epidemiological studies indicating that most narcolepsy patients are diagnosed more than 1 year after their symptoms begin. It represents a critical period for timely clinical recognition and has been established as a standard for differentiating prompt intervention from the extended latency typically seen in narcolepsy [19]. Specifically, previous study found that over 80% of patients experienced delays in diagnosis, with pediatric onset being a significant predictor of these delays. By adopting a cutoff of more than 1 year, our classification aligns with established literature and allows for comparison across studies [19]. Family history was defined as a record of major sleep abnormalities, such as narcolepsy, REM sleep-related disorders, hypersomnia, or insomnia, among first-degree relatives. Participants were classified post hoc into two groups based on diagnostic latency: a non-delay group (latency ≤1 year) and a delay group (latency >1 year). Data collection was conducted from September to December 2024.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp.). Continuous variables are reported as means±standard deviations, and categorical variables are shown as frequencies and percentages. The primary objective was to compare clinical and demographic characteristics between two groups based on diagnostic latency: the non-delay group (diagnostic latency of ≤1 year) and the delay group (diagnostic latency of >1 year). Multivariate logistic regression analysis was employed to identify independent predictors of diagnostic delay, while controlling for potential confounders.

For univariate comparisons, independent t-tests were used for continuous variables, including onset age, diagnosis age, and clinical scores (e.g., Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale). Chisquare tests, or Fisher’s exact tests when appropriate, were employed to compare categorical variables such as sex distribution, narcolepsy type, family history of sleep disorders, and the prevalence of clinical symptoms (e.g., EDS, cataplexy, hallucinations).

To identify independent predictors of diagnostic delay and calculate adjusted effect sizes, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted. To identify independent predictors of diagnostic delay, variables demonstrating a trend toward significance (p<0.10) in the univariate analyses (onset age and EDS) were included in the model. Additionally, sex and family history were included a priori as covariates, regardless of their univariate significance. This decision was based on their established clinical relevance in prior literature regarding sleep disorder presentation, family history, and healthcare-seeking behaviors [5,8,13]. The model was constructed using the enter method. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

RESULTS

A total of 235 patients with clinically confirmed narcolepsy were included in this study. To analyze the factors influencing the speed of diagnosis, the cohort was classified into two groups based on diagnostic latency: the non-delay group (latency ≤1 year; n=53, 22.6%) and the delay group (latency >1 year; n=182, 77.4%) (Table 1).

The overall cohort had a mean onset age of 15.75±5.81 years and a mean diagnostic latency of 8.60±8.57 years. The sex distribution was balanced, with 114 males (48.5%) and 121 females (51.5%). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of sex or narcolepsy type, indicating that these baseline demographics do not inherently influence the speed of diagnosis (Table 1).

Predictive factor analysis

Initial univariate comparisons revealed clear clinical differences between the two groups. The age at symptom onset was significantly lower in the delay group (15.13±4.88 years) compared to the non-delay group (17.89±7.91 years; p=0.002), indicating that younger patients are at a substantially higher risk for prolonged diagnostic latency (Table 2).

In terms of clinical symptoms, the prevalence of EDS was significantly higher in the delay group (98.9%) than in the nondelay group (90.6%; p=0.007). While EDS is a common symptom of narcolepsy, patients in the delayed diagnosis group more frequently reported extreme daytime sleepiness at symptom onset, suggesting that diagnosis often occurred only after the symptom burden became significant. Notably, there was no significant difference in family history of sleep disorders (p=0.241), implying that having an affected relative does not necessarily expedite the diagnostic process. Clinical trends approaching significance were observed in cataplexy prevalence (p=0.084), indicating a need for further multivariate investigation (Table 2).

Although the association between cataplexy and diagnostic delay did not reach statistical significance, a notable trend was observed (p=0.084) (Table 2). The prevalence of cataplexy was higher in the non-delay group (54.7%) than in the delay group (40.1%). This trend aligns with the distribution of narcolepsy subtypes shown in Table 1, where the proportion of patients with NT1—characterized by the presence of cataplexy—was higher in the non-delay group (71.7%) compared to the delay group (59.9%). These findings suggest that overt clinical markers like cataplexy may facilitate earlier diagnosis. Other REM-related symptoms, such as sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations, did not demonstrate an association with diagnostic latency (Table 2).

Independent predictors of delay: multivariate logistic regression

To identify independent predictors of diagnostic delay while controlling for confounding variables, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis. The model included onset age, EDS, family history, and sex as independent variables (Table 3).

The analysis revealed that onset age is a significant independent predictor of diagnostic delay (OR=0.91, 95% CI [0.85– 0.97], p=0.003). Specifically, patients who developed symptoms at a younger age were increasingly likely to experience diagnostic delays; for each year earlier the symptoms began, the likelihood of a delayed diagnosis increased by nearly 10%. Additionally, EDS remained a significant predictor (OR=9.21, 95% CI [1.68–50.45], p=0.011). This finding indicates that diagnoses often occur only after excessive sleepiness becomes severe enough to disrupt daily functioning, suggesting that milder or less obvious symptoms may not prompt timely clinical evaluation.

Objective sleep study findings in a subgroup

In a subgroup analysis of 31 patients, we compared objective MSLT findings between the non-delay (n=11) and delay (n=20) groups. Although the subgroup size was insufficient to detect statistically significant differences, this analysis aimed to objectively assess whether diagnostic delay was associated with variations in disease severity. The non-delay group had a higher proportion of NT1 (72.7%) compared to the delay group (45.0%), which supports the notion that overt symptoms like cataplexy may facilitate earlier diagnosis. Regarding objective sleep parameters, the non-delay group exhibited a numerically shorter mean sleep latency (3.01±2.61 minutes) and a slightly higher mean SOREM count (3.09±1.30) compared to the delay group (3.82±3.26 minutes and 2.85±1.57 minutes, respectively). Although these differences were not statistically significant, the results indicate that patients in the delay group demonstrated objective sleep pathology of severity comparable to that of patients diagnosed promptly (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the clinical factors related to the delayed diagnosis of narcolepsy, focusing on the age of symptom onset, key clinical features, and family history of sleep disorders. Three principal findings emerged. First, an earlier onset of symptoms was identified as the strongest independent predictor of diagnostic delay. Second, while cataplexy seemed to promote earlier diagnosis, its impact was limited and did not achieve statistical significance. Third, a family history of sleep disorders did not decrease the time to diagnosis.

Early symptom onset as a major risk factor for diagnostic delay

An earlier age at symptom onset was the strongest predictor of delayed diagnosis in this cohort. Patients who developed narcolepsy symptoms at a younger age were significantly more likely to experience prolonged diagnostic latency, with the risk of delay increasing progressively as the age of onset decreased. This finding aligns with previous reports indicating that narcolepsy starting in childhood or adolescence is especially prone to delayed recognition [19]. Delayed recognition may occur because the normal sleep phase delay in adolescents, combined with early school schedules, often leads to chronic sleep deprivation. This can result in pathological sleepiness being misattributed to insufficient sleep [23].

From a psychiatric perspective, this vulnerability may reflect the nonspecific and heterogeneous clinical presentation of narcolepsy during developmental periods. In children and adolescents, EDS may manifest as irritability, emotional lability, inattention, behavioral issues, or academic decline, rather than as overt sleep attacks [24].

Moreover, symptoms that emerge early in life can become integrated into an individual’s perceived baseline functioning. When sleepiness and fatigue gradually develop during childhood, they are often interpreted as personality traits or temperament rather than as signs of an underlying disorder [25]. This tendency is reinforced when similar patterns are observed among family members, contributing to the normalization of symptoms and deferral of clinical attention until functional impairment becomes pronounced in adolescence or adulthood. This contributes to a prolonged diagnostic delay [26].

Diagnostic impact of cataplexy

Cataplexy is a key characteristic of narcolepsy, yet this study found no significant difference between the delayed and nondelayed diagnosis groups regarding its presence. However, a descriptive trend emerged, indicating a higher proportion of NT1 in the non-delay group. This suggests that cataplexy might lower the threshold for clinical evaluation in some patients.

The lack of statistical significance implies that cataplexy alone cannot guarantee a timely diagnosis. One reason for this could be the limited awareness of cataplexy among patients, caregivers, and non-specialist clinicians. Episodes of sudden muscle weakness triggered by emotions may be misinterpreted as anxiety-related reactions, behavioral issues, or psychological phenomena, especially in pediatric and adolescent populations. Consistent with this observation, the delayed diagnosis group exhibited a higher proportion of NT2, while NT1 was more common in the non-delay group. This further suggests that the absence of overt cataplexy may contribute to delayed recognition. As a result, even the characteristic features of narcolepsy may fail to prompt appropriate diagnostic consideration, diminishing the potential diagnostic advantage of cataplexy.

Family history and misattribution to symptom recognition

Contrary to expectations, a family history of sleep disorders did not serve as a protective factor against diagnostic delays. This indicates that familial aggregation does not automatically lead to greater symptom awareness or prompt help-seeking behavior.

One possible explanation is that symptoms may be normalized within families. When excessive sleepiness or similar complaints are common among relatives, they may be viewed as familial traits instead of signs of a medical issue [27,28]. Such normalization may reduce the perceived severity of illness and delay psychiatric or medical consultation. In this context, a family history may paradoxically obscure the recognition of symptoms rather than facilitate diagnosis [29].

This finding is clinically important for clinics, as family history is often viewed as an indicator for increased vigilance. These findings suggest that relying solely on family-reported symptom history is insufficient; clinicians should systematically assess functional impairment and symptom burden independent of familial context [30]. Educational initiatives should be redesigned to specifically address families with a history of sleep issues, helping them differentiate between “familial traits” and clinical pathology. Ultimately, the early application of objective diagnostic tools, such as polysomnography and the Multiple Sleep Latency Test, in suspected juvenile cases remains the most reliable means of overcoming symptom normalization and ensuring timely intervention [31,32].

Implications for clinical practice

The burden of narcolepsy is twofold: it includes direct clinical disabilities, such as cataplexy-related injuries and severe daytime sleepiness, as well as long-term socioeconomic liabilities, which encompass impaired education, reduced employment opportunities, and decreased work productivity [33]. When diagnosis is delayed by over a decade, as observed in our juvenile-onset group, patients miss critical developmental windows. Early recognition is not just a clinical goal but a socioeconomic necessity; effective treatment during adolescence can preserve a patient’s academic trajectory and social integration, fundamentally improving their long-term quality of life [27,34].

Strengths and limitations

This study examines adolescent-onset narcolepsy, a clinically vulnerable and often overlooked population that experiences significant diagnostic delays. By analyzing symptom profiles and family history within a real-world clinical cohort, the research offers valuable insights into the factors that facilitate timely recognition of the condition. The use of a stratified design and a relatively large sample size enhances the clinical relevance of the findings. Additionally, subgroup analyses that incorporate objective sleep study data provide biological validation for the clinical observations. Notably, this research is the first in South Korea to investigate whether family history and clinical features can serve as predictive factors for delayed versus timely diagnosis of narcolepsy.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The timing of diagnosis and symptom onset relied on self-reported data, which may introduce recall bias; specifically, patients may systematically underestimate or overestimate the interval between symptom onset and formal diagnosis, potentially attenuating or inflating the magnitude of observed group differences. The crosssectional design limits causal inferences, and family history was assessed through binary self-report rather than through objective or genetic measures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, earlier age at symptom onset was the strongest independent predictor of diagnostic delay in narcolepsy within this cohort. The presence of cataplexy or a family history of sleep disorders did not consistently result in an earlier diagnosis. This indicates that even well-known symptoms and family background often do not prompt timely clinical recognition. These findings underscore the need for proactive consideration of narcolepsy in adolescents who present with EDS in psychiatric settings, as early diagnosis is crucial for minimizing delays and reducing long-term functional and psychosocial challenges.
