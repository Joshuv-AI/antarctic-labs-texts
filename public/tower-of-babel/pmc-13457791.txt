Comparison of postoperative survival after neoadjuvant treatment versus upfront surgery in resected pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma: a propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Wonjun Jang, Hye-Sol Jung, Youngmin Han, Yoonsoo Chae, Younsoo Seo, Inhyuck Lee, Young Jae Cho, Go-Won Choi, Joon Seong Park, Jin-Young Jang, Wooil Kwon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEvidence regarding long-term prognosis after surgery by pathological stage in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients following neoadjuvant treatment (NAT) remains limited.Methods757 patients who underwent PDAC resection between 2017 and 2021 were analyzed retrospectively. Among them, 262 received NAT followed by surgery (NAT group) and 415 underwent upfront surgery (UFS group). Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed for balanced comparison using the following variables: age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status class, and adjuvant treatment.ResultsAfter PSM, the UFS group showed higher postoperative CA 19-9, larger tumors, less advanced initial clinical stage, but more advanced pathological stage and fewer positive lymph nodes compared with the NAT group. Median overall survival (OS) was similar between groups (NAT vs. UFS: 35 vs. 34 months, P = 0.88). In stage IA, UFS patients had longer OS compared to NAT (not reached vs. 42 months, P = 0.037). No significant OS differences were observed at stages IB, IIA, IIB, and III.ConclusionPostoperative survival did not differ between NAT and UFS groups when stratified by pathological stage, except for stage IA where UFS showed better outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains one of the most challenging malignancies with a poor prognosis. Even after curative resection, the 5-year overall survival (OS) rate is reported to be 15%–20% [1], reflecting the aggressive nature of the tumor biology.

Neoadjuvant treatment (NAT) using a combination of chemotherapeutic agents such as fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin (FOLFIRINOX) or gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel has changed the paradigm of pancreatic cancer treatment, resulting in improved patient survival with increased resection rates, R0 resection rates, and negative lymph node (LN) involvement in patients with borderline resectable [2] or locally advanced PDAC [34].

However, there is a lack of understanding regarding the postoperative prognosis of patients who underwent NAT and reached curative resection. Patients with PDAC who are given NAT harbor a more aggressive tumor characteristic compared to those who undergo upfront resection. It remains unclear the prognosis of resected patients who received NAT in the real world. Moreover, the cancer staging system, which was established based on the data of patients undergoing upfront resection, is still applied to those who underwent NAT in pancreatic cancer.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate differences in postoperative survival outcomes in patients who underwent upfront surgery (UFS) vs. resection after NAT. Understanding actual prognosis after resection can be pivotal in predicting long-term outcomes and tailoring adjuvant treatment to enhance patient survival.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Seoul National University Hospital Clinical Trials Center (No. 2211-054-1377). It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Patient selection

We retrospectively reviewed 757 patients who underwent surgery for PDAC between January 2017 and December 2021 at Seoul National University Hospital. Patients who died within 90 days after surgery (90-day mortality, n = 14), underwent R2 resection (n = 45), had a complete response (n = 15), or had incomplete data (n = 6) were excluded. A total of 677 patients were included in the study. A 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using the following variables: age, sex, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA PS) class, and adjuvant treatment (Fig. 1).

Patient clinical information, pathological findings, and follow-up results were extracted from the department’s prospectively collected database. The initial resectability of each case was determined according to the 2021 National Comprehensive Cancer Network guideline, version 2. Abdominal CT or magnetic resonance imaging of the tumor-vein interface performed at diagnosis was reviewed for all patients. Patients were categorized into 2 groups: (1) NAT followed by surgery and (2) UFS.

Outcome measure

Age, sex, ASA PS class, serum CA 19-9 levels at initial and post-neoadjuvant therapy, postoperative CA 19-9 levels, tumor location and size, clinical stage on imaging, presence of vascular invasion, NAT regimen and duration, operation type, postoperative chemotherapy, recurrence and location, and survival duration were extracted from the database. OS was defined as the time from the date of surgery to death from any cause to compare the prognosis among patients with equivalent post-resection pathological stages.

Neoadjuvant therapy

NAT was defined as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or both, administered preoperatively. For patients who received neoadjuvant therapy, one or more of the following regimens were used according to clinical staging based on previous histologic or imaging studies: FOLFIRINOX, gemcitabine-based, and gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel. Radiotherapy, stereotactic body radiotherapy, or intensity-modulated radiotherapy was used.

Follow-up

Postoperative complications, CA 19-9 levels in the immediate postoperative period after surgery, chemotherapy or radiotherapy or not, recurrence and location of recurrence, and CA 19-9 values at the time of diagnosis of recurrence were assessed. Follow-up was performed every 3 months for 2 years postoperatively and every 4 months thereafter for 5 years. CT and tumor markers were evaluated, and recurrence was assessed by PET, if necessary.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp.) and R software for Windows ver. 4.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test and the Mann-Whitney test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. OS was compared using the Kaplan-Meier curve analysis and the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used for univariate and multivariate analyses of prognostic factors associated with OS. A P-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

In total, 262 and 415 patients underwent NAT followed by surgery (NAT group) and underwent UFS, respectively. After 1:1 PSM, there were 262 patients in each group. In the NAT group, the mean age was 62.9 ± 9.3 years and male patients were 124 (47.3%). The number of patients with initially resectable, borderline resectable, locally advanced, and metastatic disease was 57 (21.8%), 133 (50.8%), 49 (18.7%), and 23 (8.8%), respectively. Postoperative survival according to clinical stage before and after PSM is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

After PSM, patients in the UFS group had a higher median postoperative CA 19-9 level (153.1 U/mL vs. 55.9 U/mL, P = 0.026), greater tumor size (3.1 ± 1.2 cm vs. 2.5 ± 1.3 cm, P < 0.001), less advanced initial clinical staging (resectable: 95.0% vs. 21.8%, P < 0.001), more advanced pathological stage (T3 or above: 16.4% vs. 11.5%, P < 0.001; N1 or N2: 63.4% vs. 40.1%, P < 0.001), and less number of positive LNs (0.9 ± 1.6 vs. 2.0 ± 2.7, P < 0.001) compared to the NAT group, respectively.

Postoperative survival outcomes according to treatment

In the matched cohort, postoperative survival did not differ according to the treatment (NAT group vs. UFS group; median OS, 35 months vs. 34 months, P = 0.88) (Fig. 2). Additional Kaplan-Meier curves comparing postoperative survival according to treatment before and after PSM are presented in Supplementary Fig. 2.

Survival outcomes after surgery according to treatment in patients at the same stage were compared (Fig. 3). In the stage IA group, the postoperative survival was longer in patients who underwent UFS than in those who underwent NAT (not reached vs. 42 months, P = 0.037). The median OS was not significantly different between the 2 groups for stages IB, IIA, IIB, and III before and after PSM. Stage-stratified survival curves according to the AJCC 8th staging system in the NAT and UFS groups are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, and pairwise comparisons among stages are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Detailed comparisons of postoperative survival according to treatment within the same pathological stage are presented in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Prognostic factors

In the multivariate analysis, prognostic factors in the matched cohort were preop CA 19-9 (hazard ratio [HR], 1.308; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.064–1.608; P = 0.011), postoperative CA 19-9 ≥37 U/mL (HR, 1.445; 95% CI, 1.177–1.774; P < 0.001), R1 margin status (HR, 1.389; 95% CI, 1.124–1.716; P = 0.002), T3/4 classification (vs. T1/2: HR, 1.614; 95% CI, 1.201–2.169; P < 0.001), LN metastasis (vs. N0: HR, 1.915; 95% CI, 1.468–2.498; P < 0.001), adjuvant chemotherapy (HR, 0.723; 95% CI, 0.590–0.886; P = 0.002) (Table 1). Detailed results of univariate and multivariate analyses in the UFS group (before and after PSM) are provided in Supplementary Table 2 and Table 3, and additional subgroup analyses according to T/N combinations in the matched cohort are presented in Supplementary Fig. 5.

DISCUSSION

Systemic therapy for advanced PDAC has evolved considerably, with multi-agent regimens now serving as the backbone of treatment [5]. Reportedly, multi-agent neoadjuvant chemotherapy has a higher response rate than monotherapy (gemcitabine, <12%; gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel, 23%; and FOLFIRINOX, 31.6%) in advanced pancreatic cancer [67], and the number of patients undergoing surgery after neoadjuvant chemotherapy is increasing. Despite presenting with disease at a more advanced clinical stage at the time of diagnosis, patients who were able to undergo curative surgery mostly reached comparable pathological staging to patients receiving UFS. However, it remains unclear whether patients receiving NAT have different postoperative outcomes compared to pathological stage-matched patients undergoing upfront resection. If the outcomes differ, there may be a need for a separate pathological staging system for neoadjuvant-treated pancreatic cancer.

In this matched cohort, there was no significant difference in postoperative OS between the NAT and UFS group. Patients who underwent NAT followed by surgery were more likely to have borderline resectable disease or more advanced disease at initial diagnosis (Table 2). Nevertheless, these patients showed recurrence rates and postoperative survival outcomes similar to those who underwent UFS, which may be attributable to good disease control after NAT and careful patient selection.

In this study, patients who received NAT had higher R0 resection rates, lower median postoperative CA 19-9 levels, fewer positive LNs, and less advanced T and N classifications. This study confirmed the effectiveness of neoadjuvant therapy for tumor downstaging, systemic control, and R0 resection in advanced pancreatic cancer [289].

Despite the more favorable pathological features observed in the NAT group, these did not translate into superior OS compared with the UFS group. Several factors may account for this discrepancy. First, although patients in the NAT group achieved better local tumor characteristics (higher R0 resection rate, fewer positive LNs, and lower postoperative CA 19-9 levels), they initially presented with more advanced or biologically aggressive disease, which may not be fully captured by conventional pathological parameters. Second, patients selected for UFS may inherently have had more favorable tumor biology despite less intensive preoperative treatment, leading to comparable long-term outcomes even with slightly worse pathological surrogates. Third, neoadjuvant therapy may have reduced locoregional disease burden and delayed recurrence without completely eradicating occult micrometastatic disease, resulting in similar OS despite improved local control, as suggested in prior reports comparing NAT and UFS [1011]. Finally, the limited sample size and follow-up duration of our cohort may have reduced the statistical power to detect modest survival advantages associated with these pathological improvements. Taken together, these considerations suggest that currently used pathological markers, although improved after NAT, may be insufficient to fully reflect the underlying tumor biology and long-term prognosis on their own [1213].

The 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system for exocrine pancreatic neoplasms, revised in 2018, discriminates prognosis according to relevant stages for patients who had not undergone neoadjuvant therapy [14]. However, the application of the AJCC 8th edition to patients who underwent surgery after NAT is not fully understood. Marcinak et al. [15] reported that there was insufficient discrimination between all neighboring stage groups (all P > 0.5), and Zhu et al. [16] reported that AJCC stages II and III did not discriminate the survival of patients who underwent NAT. Several studies have reported that the ypT classification following NAT did not show prognostic differences [1718]. However, Lee et al. [19] showed that the current nodal staging of AJCC 8th edition demonstrated a solid predictive performance for patients who underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy (area under the curve [AUC], 0.629 for OS and 0.649 for recurrence-free survival).

Some studies have proposed new predictive models for postoperative survival in patients who undergo NAT followed by resection. Hank et al. [20] reported that the PANAMA-score model using tumor size, number of positive LNs, resection margin status, and preoperative CA 19-9 levels provided a better postresection survival discrimination compared with the AJCC staging system. Zhu et al. [16] proposed a new staging model using the simplified tumor size (tumor ≤2 cm or >2 cm), the number of positive LNs, and tumor differentiation with greater predictive ability compared with the current AJCC staging system (for 2 years: AUC, 0.682 [95% CI, 0.580–0.783] vs. 0.612 [95% CI, 0.507–0.717]). Taken together, these findings indicate that while the current AJCC 8th edition staging system can still provide meaningful prognostic stratification in selected neoadjuvant-treated patients, there is an ongoing need to refine or complement it with models specifically tailored to the NAT population [2122].

This study suggested that patients who underwent NAT had similar postoperative outcomes to those who underwent UFS when their post-resection pathological stages were the same. Accordingly, in our cohort, patients who underwent UFS or NAT followed by surgery were classified using the same staging system. Although further validation in larger, external datasets is warranted, controversy remains regarding the application of the T-classification, including the utilization of extrapancreatic extension [182324], and further research based on a large cohort is needed.

Interestingly, postoperative survival was better in patients who underwent UFS than in those who underwent NAT in the stage IA group (not reached vs. 42 months, P = 0.037). In early-stage PDAC, microscopic findings such as the presence of perineural invasion or patient factors such as long-standing diabetes are associated with prognosis [2526]. A large-scale, multicenter study is needed to investigate stage IA pancreatic cancer owing to its rare characteristics. The benefits of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with stage IA pancreatic cancer are controversial [2728]. This study showed that adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with longer postoperative survival (HR, 0.723) in the matched cohort. Even if a patient obtains a pathological result of T1N0, particularly after NAT, it is important not to overlook the initially advanced stage at diagnosis, and adjuvant treatment and close monitoring should be carefully considered, although these findings require confirmation in larger studies.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, and the number of patients in some stage-specific subgroups, such as stage IA, was limited, which may restrict the robustness and generalizability of subgroup analyses. Second, the retrospective design inherently carries a risk of selection bias, particularly because only patients who ultimately underwent resection after NAT were included in the analysis. Third, patients who achieved a pathological complete response, corresponding to pathological stage 0, were excluded from the primary analysis because there was no comparable stage 0 counterpart in the UFS group; as a result, the potential OS benefit of NAT at the cohort level may have been conservatively estimated, and our findings should be interpreted as applicable to patients with pathological stages that are shared between NAT and UFS. In the propensity score model, we included age, sex, ASA PS class, and adjuvant treatment as matching variables to balance major clinical factors related to both treatment selection and postoperative prognosis; however, we were not able to adjust for specific neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimens because the vast majority of patients received FOLFIRINOX, and the remaining regimen subgroups were too small for stable modeling. Finally, the relatively short follow-up period did not allow sufficient long-term outcome data to be obtained. Further multicenter studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up will be necessary to validate and refine our results.

Except for stage IA, postoperative survival did not differ between patients at the same pathological stage, even for those who underwent NAT because of an advanced clinical stage at initial diagnosis. Therefore, it is important to proceed with surgery after careful selection following NAT, and even if stage IA is achieved after surgery, active postoperative treatment and close follow-up may be warranted in light of the initially advanced disease status.
