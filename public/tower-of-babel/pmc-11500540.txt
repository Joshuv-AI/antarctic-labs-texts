Pharmacovigilance: the evolution of drug safety monitoring
Authors: Muhammad Akhtar Abbas Khan, Tehreem Sara, Zaheer-Ud-Din Babar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Pharmacovigilance system is aimed to promote and protect public health by ensuring the availability of essential medicines in the market and reducing the burden of ADRs. Pharmacovigilance is derived from two words; pharamakon rooted in the Greek word that means medicinal substance and vigilia rooted in the Latin word to keep watch. This concept evolved after Hannah Greener died in 1848 after having a tonsillectomy with chloroform. As a result of the Thalidomide tragedy, drug regulation in Europe has forever changed. From its earliest beginnings to its current state, pharmacovigilance has been shaped by several major milestones. The historical phases of pharmacovigilance can help us understand the value of pharmacovigilance and identify the challenges that lie ahead. Despite advancements in technology, it is imperative that we continue to strive for excellence in pharmacovigilance to ensure all individuals’ safety and health. Through collaboration between the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS), World Health Organization (WHO), and the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH), Pharmacovigilance has evolved into a regulatory activity.

Introduction

Pharmacovigilance is derived from two words; pharamakon rooted in the Greek word that means medicinal substance and vigilia rooted in the Latin word to keep watch (Fornasier et al., 2018). The World Health Organization defines Pharmacovigilance as ‘the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding and prevention of adverse effects or any other possible drug-related problems’ (World Health Organization, 2002). The European commission separately defines pharmacovigilance as ‘process and science of monitoring the safety of medicines and taking action to reduce the risks and increase the benefits of medicines’ (European Commission, 2022).

Hannah Greener died in 1848 after using chloroform before her tonsils were removed, which led to the concept of Pharmacovigilance. Many authors have attributed her death because of chloroform overdose, and some have referred to uncertain secondary adverse effects (Knight & Bacon, 2003). The first adverse drug reaction (ADR) related catastrophe in the United States of America (USA) occurred in 1937 after diethyl glycol was used as a solvent in sulfanilamide elixir. It is believed that diethyl glycol caused the death, and the company was unaware of its toxicity. In 1938, the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act was passed as a result of this tragedy. Under the new Act, drug manufacturers must ensure that their products are safe before they are marketed (Wax, 1996).

Drug regulation in Europe changed forever following the thalidomide tragedy (Figure 1). A letter from Australian Doctor Dr. McBride originally published in Lancet Journal in 1961 suggested link between newborn congenital malformation and use of thalidomide in pregnant women (Fornasier et al., 2018). The need for an effective Pharmacovigilance system was necessitated in the aftermath of the thalidomide tragedy. It was observed that the vigilance of a USFDA reviewer named Dr. Kelsey saved many lives by not approving thalidomide for the US market. This incident also marks the importance of an extensive review process and legislation (Rice, 2007). A thorough evaluation process was highlighted by this incident, which prompted many countries to introduce new regulations and strengthen existing drug safety laws and regulations (Beninger & Ibara, 2016; Khan, Hamid, Ur-Rehman, et al., 2022; Lembit & Santoso, 2010; Rice, 2007; World Health Organization, 2002). A 1962 amendment by the USFDA requires pre-marketing data on medicines’ safety and efficacy. The safety alert for thalidomide tragedy was first generated in Australia and this situation paved the path for the establishment of an Australian drug evaluation committee in 1963 by the national health department to investigate the safety of new medicines and a programme for spontaneous reporting of ADRs. In the next year, the Australian ADR reporting system was initiated with the sharing of reporting forms to prescribers (Khan et al., 2023). In 1964, the UK introduced the Yellow Card scheme to encourage healthcare professionals to report suspected ADRs. The yellow cards were made available to patients in 2005 (MHRA, 2022). The first European legislation was developed in 1965. Boston collaborative drug surveillance programme (1966) was established to conduct epidemiological studies to measure the ADRs using large hospital electronic databases.

The Pharmacovigilance system has undergone many changes due to the efforts of various actors and stakeholders. ‘During the 16th World Assembly, the 16.36 resolution called for a systematic collection of information on serious ADRs during the development and particularly after medicines have been made available for public use’. This resolution paved the path to the formation of the WHO, programme for international drug monitoring (PIDM) (Khan, Hamid, Khan, et al., 2022).

The WHO PIDM was introduced in the year 1968. Initially, 10 countries joined the PIDM. In contrast, 157 members have already received full membership status, and 23 associate members are currently awaiting it (World Health Organization, 2024). The first Pharmacovigilance centre started working in the city of Alexandria near Washington DC and after that, the programme was moved to Geneva. 40 years ago, the operational responsibility to run the WHO Programme for International Drug Monitoring was shifted to Uppsala, Sweden. To support the WHO's PIDM, UMC was founded in 1978. A central database called VigiBase collects ICSRs from all member states. Maintaining and managing VigiBase is the UMC's responsibility. Approximately more than 30 million safety reports are stored in this database. This centre was established primarily to collect adverse reaction reports from multiple sources worldwide. WHO has launched a new web application called VigiAccess. It allows anyone to access it and encourages reporting ADRs on medical products (Uppsala Monitoring Centre, 2018, 2022).

After the wake of the shocking thalidomide catastrophe, to provide signals of unexpected adverse reactions, spontaneous adverse reaction reporting schemes have been developed. The European Union enacted its first Community Directive (Council Directive 65/65/EEC) on medicines in 1965 (European Parliament: Directorate-General for Parliamentary Research Services & Scholz, 2015). In the early 1990s, EU member states began to cooperate more closely as proposals were made to build a more closely integrated regulatory system. Thus, the European Agency for Medication Product Evaluation was created in 1995. Later, it was named the European Medicine Agency (EMA). Through cooperation with the European medicines regulatory network, EMA has achieved tremendous success - a partnership unique among the EMA, European Commission, and the medicines regulatory authorities in the EEA (Bahri & Arlett, 2014; European Medicine Agency, 2022).

Eudravigilance was introduced by the EMA in 2001. Eudravigilance is the European Union's system for managing and analysing data related to suspected adverse reactions to medicines that have been approved for commercial use or are currently being studied in clinical trials. It enables the electronic transmission of ICSR between all stakeholders, early identification and evaluation of potential safety signals; and product information in EEA. Since May 2004, Eudravigilance has included clinical trial data alongside the case reports from around the world post-authorisation. This development conformed to the standards and formats of the International Council on Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Registration of Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (Banovac et al., 2017; European Medicine Agency, 2017, 2018).

The regulatory Pharmacovigilance in Europe has been enhanced two times in the new century. In 2004 the risk management approach was introduced through Regulation (EC) No. 726/2004 (EC, 2004) and in 2010 another legislation was introduced in the EU area (Bahri & Arlett, 2014).

After a successful audit, the Eudravigilance database was declared fully functional, and its system has met the functional specifications. On the recommendations of the pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC), an operational plan containing the key activities and developments for three years 2018–2020 was devised. EMA confirms that by the end of 2017, Eudravigilance contained a repository of more than 12.45 million ICSRs and 7.95 million cases along with information on 744,219 medicinal products. All ICSRs are also available to UMC (European Medicine Agency, 2018).

The Pharmacovigilance system is aimed to promote and protect public health by ensuring the availability of essential medicines in the market and reducing the burden of ADRs (Santoro et al., 2017). EU issued a directive MEMO/08/782 in 2008 emphasising the importance of the introduction of new measures in the field of Pharmacovigilance to reduce ADRs and their related deaths. It was estimated that almost 5% of hospital admissions were due to ADRs and it was ranked 5th common cause of hospital deaths in the EU. The number of deaths in a year was calculated at 1, 97,000 while 79 billion euros was the cost of ADRs to the public. After an assessment of the EU Pharmacovigilance system, necessary amendments were proposed in the legislation to strengthen the system. The proposed amendments outlined the clear roles and responsibilities of responsible parties, the introduction of risk management planning, the creation of a Pharmacovigilance risk assessment advisory committee, the Eudravigilance was declared the only point of collection of ADRs within the EU, introduction of a Pharmacovigilance system master file, the requirement of product risk management system for each new medicinal product by the applicants and provisions for ADR case reports, PSUR (EU, 2008; Sardela, 2019).

After the recall of ‘Rofecoxib,’ a survey titled consumer perceptions on drug safety was conducted in 2006 in the USA. The survey participants made FDA primary responsible for drug safety with 75% scores and US pharmaceutical firms & congress with scores of 41% and 70% respectively. The reports of the institute of medicines also suggest FDA adopt the principles of a culture of safety (Olsen & Whalen, 2009)

The stringent drug regulatory authorities have established user-friendly online reporting systems like USFDA MedWatch (FDA, 2022), and the yellow card scheme of the UK, to effectively identify and address serious drug-related problems. Studies show that these systems are underutilised due to their voluntary nature in reporting ADRs (Hazell & Shakir, 2006). In the US less than 10% of ADRs are reported through MedWatch (Lasser et al., 2002). A large number of countries including the USA, UK, Canada, Australia, India and Pakistan have developed mobile applications for ADR reporting.

Through collaboration between the Council for International Organizations of Medical Sciences (CIOMS), WHO, and the International Conference on Harmonization (ICH), Pharmacovigilance has evolved into a regulatory activity (Beninger & Ibara, 2016). Among other recommendations, Khan et al. propose that SAARC (South Asian Association for Regional Cooperation) form a technical group to explore ways to harmonise pharmaceutical regulatory standards and pharmacovigilance (Khan, Hamid, and Babar, 2022). There are three internationally recognised Pharmacovigilance systems: The European Union (EU) Pharmacovigilance system, WHO Uppsala Monitoring Center (UMC) system, and the ICH system. Pharmacovigilance characteristics vary across them, but they could all result in safe clinical drug use (Cui et al., 2021).
