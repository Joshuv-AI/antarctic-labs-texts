Pomegranate peel as a natural reducing agent in leaching of manganese concentrate
Authors: Meysam Naseri, Mehdi Irannajad, Akbar Mehdilo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study explores, for the first time, the potential of pomegranate peel as a reductant agent for the reductive leaching of manganese concentrate, where pyrolusite is the primary valuable mineral. Furthermore, the influence of several factors on the reductive leaching process, including agitation speed, sulfuric acid concentration, peel quantity, reaction temperature and time were investigated. Under optimized conditions of 1.5 M sulfuric acid, temperature of 75 °C, agitation speed of 450 rpm, reaction time of 4 h and pomegranate peel quantity of 5 g, the recovery of dissolved manganese reached 96.12%. Scanning electron microscopy of the leaching residue revealed the dissolution of pyrolusite. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy analysis was conducted on both the pomegranate peel and the leaching residue to identify changes in functional groups. Additionally, the kinetics of the reductive leaching process were examined, indicating that the reaction rate is controlled by a surface chemical reaction mechanism with an activation energy of 38.39 kJ/mol. The enthalpy and entropy changes of the reaction were determined to be 35.71 kJ/mol and 192.90 J/mol·K, respectively. Based on thermodynamic values, the Gibbs free energy at temperatures of 303, 318, 333 and 348 K was found to be 94.16, 97.05, 99.95 and 102.84 kJ/mol, respectively.Keywords: Pomegranate peel, Manganese, Leaching, Sulfuric acid, Plant reductant agentSubject terms: Chemistry, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Manganese finds broad applications across multiple sectors, including metallurgy, chemical manufacturing, battery technology, and electronic devices. As the demand for manganese-based materials continues to grow, and high-quality manganese ore reserves steadily decline, the use of lower-grade manganese ores is becoming increasingly essential1. In general, hydrometallurgical and pyrometallurgical methods are used to extract manganese from various resources. The pyrometallurgical method requires high temperature reactions and produces greenhouse gas emissions and environmental pollution. Compared to pyrometallurgy, the hydrometallurgical method offers lower pollution, reduced energy consumption, and simpler equipment1. In contrast to pyrometallurgical approach, the hydrometallurgical approach is associated with lower environmental impacts, decreased energy usage, and the use of more straightforward equipment2. Manganese oxides remain chemically stable in both acidic and alkaline media; as a result, their extraction requires a reducing environment. Recovery of manganese from such ores is typically achieved through roasting processes or by applying acid leaching in the presence of reductants1,3.

Figure 1 presents the Pourbaix diagram corresponding to the leaching system involving manganese at a temperature of 75°C. According to the diagram, manganese exists as ions within a pH range of 0 to 6. The stable range of manganese ions lies within an Eh range of − 1.2 to 1.2 V. Thus, to convert MnO2 to Mn2+, the leaching process must occur in an acidic environment (pH ) by adding a reductant to dissolve manganese into the solution phase.

The leaching behavior of manganese oxide ores is governed by multiple parameters, among which the choice of reductant is particularly significant1. To effectively extract manganese from its oxide ores, several reductants have been utilized, including sulfur dioxide4,5, pyrite6, ferrous sulfate7, metallic iron8, oxalic acid9, hydrogen peroxide10, microorganisms (such as Penicillium oxalicum11, Cladosporium sp. A and Cladosporium sp. B12, Aspergillus oryzae13, Trichothecium and Mycobacterium14 and Aspergillus species15 and ets16) and other reductants17,18. The reductant agents applied for manganese ore dissolution are typically classified into four main categories: chemical reductants19, organic9, plant-based reductants20,21 and microorganisms1. Employing plant-derived reducing agents in the manganese oxide ore leaching process provides several benefits, such as repurposing agricultural residues instead of relying on hazardous chemicals.

Ali et al. utilized corn cob as a reductant and applied sulfuric acid as the solvent for extracting manganese under acidic leaching conditions (1.9 mol/dm3). Their study investigated how the size of ore particles affects the process, reaction temperature and corn cob dosage, achieving a manganese recovery rate of 92.48% after 60 min of leaching at 90 °C with 1.9 mol/dm3 sulfuric acid and 4 g of corn cob22. In another study, manganese oxide ore was leached in a diluted sulfuric acid solution, with researchers examining the effects of tea waste quantity, temperature, the concentration of sulfuric acid employed, along with the proportion between liquid and solid phases, duration of the reaction were investigated. Under the best conditions (10:1 mass ratio of manganese oxide ore to tea waste, 2.5 mol/L sulfuric acid concentration, 7.5:1 liquid-to-solid ratio, 368 K temperature, and 8 h of leaching period), manganese recovery from Gabon manganese oxide ore reached 100%. Under similar conditions, but with a 1.7 mol/L acid concentration, the manganese recovery from Xiangxi ore reached 99.8%. According to the kinetic evaluation, the leaching process is primarily influenced by the transport of reactants within the solid matrix, with energy barriers calculated as 38.2 kJ/mol for the Gabon sample and 20.4 kJ/mol for the Xiangxi sample23. Further research focused on the reduction-based extraction of pyrolusite using sulfuric acid, where orange peel served as the reducing agent. A manganese recovery of 98.1% was achieved under optimal conditions: 90°C, 0.1 M sulfuric acid and a reducing agent to pyrolusite weight ratio of 1.5. The kinetic evaluation indicated that the Avrami model accurately described this leaching process, with an activation energy of only 5.5 kJ/mol24. Researchers have investigated banana peel for its potential role in reducing manganese during acidic leaching. Under optimal conditions (2 h reaction time, 120 °C, 4 g of banana peel, 5 g of ore, and 15% sulfuric acid by volume), 98% manganese dissolution was achieved. The mechanism was consistent with the shrinking core model, governed by a surface chemical reaction. The apparent activation energy was determined to be 40.19 kJ/mol25. Overall, these results suggest that plant-based reducing agents are a cost-effective, renewable, and environmentally friendly alternative for manganese leaching under acidic conditions (pH , outperforming many conventional reagents.

Around 45% of total waste is generated as by-products by the fruit and vegetable processing sector, making it one of the leading contributors to industrial organic residues. These processing by-products not only result in resource loss but may contribute to environmental contamination if not adequately handled. Pomegranate peels are increasing due to the increase in pomegranate production. Pomegranate peel is produced as a by-product in the pomegranate consumption process, which can cause many environmental problems if not appropriately managed26. Pomegranate peel is rich in bioactive constituents, including polyphenols, dietary fibers, essential vitamins, minerals and various compound. Hence, the bioactive constituents found in pomegranate peel have the potential to serve as functional raw ingredients, thus enabling effective utilization of the waste and creating added value for the pomegranate industry. Generally, the concentration of bioactive substances in pomegranate peel surpasses that of its edible portions27. Notably, the pomegranate peel contains a diverse array of complex bioactive substances that often occur in combination, and their interactions may contribute to a broad spectrum of physiological effects28.

Iran is one of the major producers of pomegranate peel in the world. Pomegranate peel is discarded as an agricultural by-product, which in the present study was used to perform the leaching process of manganese concentrate. This provides a sustainable way to manage and valorize agricultural waste. Extensive research and optimization of this process can enable the replacement of conventional hazardous chemicals with renewable, biodegradable, plant-based materials, thereby advancing green chemistry principles and promoting environmentally responsible mining practices.

The main objective of this work was to obtain a leach solution with minimal impurities via a green reductive pathway, thereby providing a suitable feed for future research on the eco-friendly synthesis of manganese oxide nanoparticles. According to the authors’ investigation, the use of pomegranate peel as a natural reductant for manganese concentrate leaching has not been previously reported. Consequently, the kinetic and thermodynamic aspects of this leaching process have not been systematically examined in earlier studies. This research thus contributes to the development of a sustainable reductive leaching route for manganese extraction. Thus, in this study, the leaching of manganese concentrate using sulfuric acid and pomegranate peel was investigated. Key parameters, including sulfuric acid concentration, agitation speed, pomegranate peel dosage, reaction time and temperature, were systematically optimized. The reaction kinetics were analyzed through model fitting, and the thermodynamic parameters (enthalpy, entropy, and Gibbs free energy) were calculated using the Eyring equation. The mechanism of the reductive leaching process in the presence of pomegranate peel was also examined.

Method

Materials characterization

The chemical composition of the solid samples was determined by X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis using a Philips X Unique II spectrometer (Rh 80 kV, Lif220 GeT1AP). The chemical composition of the feed ore, the obtained concentrate, and the leaching residue was also measured by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-OES) using an Optima 7300DV. Additionally, phase composition was performed via XRD with a Philips Expert Pro diffractometer, utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54058 Å) and a scanning range of 10–90° (2θ). Morphological and chemical studies of the concentrate, pomegranate peel, and leaching residue were performed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Philips XL 30 equipped with an Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector. EDX analysis was specifically conducted using quantitative analysis and elemental X-ray mapping techniques. The samples were coated with gold for EDX analysis; gold peaks were observed in the spectra, but gold was excluded from the elemental quantification. To confirm the presence of bioactive compounds and determine the amounts of phenolic content and tannins, Ultraviolet–Visible (UV–Vis) analysis was performed with a Shimadzu UV-1240 spectrophotometer.

Atomic absorption analysis

The dissolution recovery of manganese was quantitatively assessed using Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS). The manganese ion concentration in the solution was assessed by the flame atomic absorption method, using a model AA240FS instrument.

FTIR analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was utilized to detect the functional groups participating in the reductive leaching process of manganese concentrate. FT-IR spectra were acquired from 500 to 3000 cm−1 using a NICOLET MAGNA-IR 750 instrument.

Results

Effective parameters in leaching experiments

Reaction time

The leaching of manganese from manganese ore or concentrate using plant powder involves four distinct stages: first, monosaccharide hydrolysis; second, the diffusion of a reducing agent from the liquid phase to manganese particles across the boundary layer; third, the nucleation and hydration of products at active sites; and finally, these products diffuse into the solution phase. Consequently, the overall process is governed by time-dependent reactions25,30. Under conditions of 75 °C temperature, 1.5 M sulfuric acid concentration, 450 rpm stirring speed and a fixed 5 g of pomegranate peel, a series of experiments were conducted to investigate the effect of reaction time on manganese recovery. As shown in Fig. 6, increasing the reaction time led to higher manganese recovery, as the longer contact between the mineral and the leaching solution allows for greater extraction. The gradual release of reducing compounds from the pomegranate peel contributes to manganese dissolution. However, extending the time beyond 4 h shows minimal further effect, establishing 4 h as the optimal duration. Several studies have reported that the leaching duration using plant residues ranges from 2 to 8 h23,25.

Effect of reaction time on manganese dissolution in an acidic medium (pH using pomegranate peel as a natural reductant agent (experimental condition: 75 °C, 1.5 M H2SO4, 450 rpm agitation speed and 5 g of pomegranate peel).

Agitation speed

The influence of stirring speed on the leaching recovery of manganese from pyrolusite was investigated across a range of 150 to 600 rpm at 75 °C, 5 g of pomegranate peel and 1.5 M sulfuric acid concentration (Fig. 7). At lower stirring speeds, manganese recovery was limited due to inadequate mixing between the concentrate and pomegranate peel. This limitation arises from the presence of a boundary layer between the leaching agent, the reductant, and the mineral particles. Such a layer restricts the mass transfer of both sulfuric acid and the active compounds from the pomegranate peel to the mineral surface, as well as the subsequent diffusion of manganese ions into the solution. As the stirring speed increased, the effective contact between the leaching agents and the pyrolusite particles improved, enhancing manganese recovery. Interestingly, manganese recovery showed negligible variation between 450 and 600 rpm. This suggests that beyond 450 rpm, the reaction kinetics were no longer limited by mass transfer.

Effect of agitation speed on manganese dissolution in an acidic medium (pH with pomegranate peel as the reductant agent (experimental condition: 75 °C, 1.5 M H2SO4, 240 min and 5 g of pomegranate peel).

Furthermore, no significant improvement in recovery was observed at stirring speeds above 300 rpm. This indicates that the process was not considerably influenced by the fluid boundary layer surrounding the particles, implying that liquid film diffusion was not the rate-controlling step. However, the diffusion of reactants through a solid product layer could not be ruled out31. It should also be noted that excessively high stirring speeds increase energy consumption. The findings of this study are consistent with those reported in previous literature32. Consequently, a stirring speed of 450 rpm was selected as the optimum condition for the leaching process.

Sulfuric acid concentration

The sulfuric acid plays two critical roles in the leaching process: it establishes the necessary acidic and reductive conditions for effective leaching, and it facilitates the release of reducing compounds from the pomegranate peel. Higher sulfuric acid concentrations also significantly increase the hydrolysis of polysaccharides and cellulose33. Extracting active plant compounds, such as polyphenols and polysaccharides, using macromolecular substances is challenging. Therefore, acids are commonly employed to facilitate the isolation of these bioactive compounds34. Experiments were conducted at 75 °C, with a 4-h reaction time, 450 rpm stirring speed and 5 g of pomegranate peel, to investigate the influence of sulfuric acid concentration. As illustrated in Fig. 8, raising the sulfuric acid concentration to 1.5 M resulted in an improvement. This is because higher acid concentrations improve ore dissolution power and ion transport, accelerating the dissolution rate of MnO2 and releasing more manganese ions into the solution. Variation of sulfuric acid concentration within the range of 1.5 to 2 M did not result in a significant effect on the manganese leaching recovery. Therefore, 1.5 M was determined to be the optimal sulfuric acid concentration.

Effect of sulfuric acid concentration on manganese dissolution in an acidic medium (pH with pomegranate peel as the reductant agent (experimental condition: 75 °C, 450 rpm agitation speed, 240 min and 5 g of pomegranate peel).

Reaction temperature

During the reductive leaching of manganese concentrate, the process is fundamentally dependent on the hydrolysis of reducing plant compounds, including cellulose (polysaccharides), lactose (disaccharides), and monosaccharides. Given that hydrolysis is a temperature-dependent process, reaction rates are expected to increase proportionally with rising temperature24,25,35. An experimental investigation was therefore undertaken to evaluate the effect of temperature on the dissolution rate of manganese. The experimental parameters were set as follows: 1.5 M sulfuric acid concentration, 450 rpm stirring speed, 5 g of pomegranate peel and a 4-h leaching time. The temperature was varied across a range of 30 to 75 °C, with specific experiments conducted at 30, 45, 60 and 75 °C. The results, illustrated in Fig. 9, indicated that increasing temperature enhanced the dissolution of manganese in the presence of a reducing agent. This observation is consistent with the principle that elevated temperatures in reductive leaching processes lead to an increase in reaction rates, due to the enhanced kinetic energy of molecules and a higher probability of effective collisions. In effect, increased temperature provides the necessary activation energy to facilitate the leaching process. On the other hand, an increase in temperature enhances the solubility of metal ions and improves the mobility of protons or other reactive species involved in the reaction36. Furthermore, raising the temperature from 45 to 60 °C significantly enhances the manganese leaching recovery, indicating that the process is strongly temperature-dependent.

Effect of the Temperature on manganese dissolution in an acidic medium (pH with pomegranate peel as the reductant agent (experimental condition: 1.5 M H2SO4, 450 rpm agitation speed, 240 min and 5 g of pomegranate peel).

Reductant quantity

The role of pomegranate peel in the leaching process is due to its content of reducing compounds, the concentration of which is directly proportional to the quantity of pomegranate peel employed. To evaluate the influence of pomegranate peel quantity, a series of experiments were performed, holding the following parameters constant: temperature (75 °C), reaction time (4 h), sulfuric acid concentration (1.5 M) and stirring speed (450 rpm). The mass of pomegranate peel was varied across five levels: 0, 1.25, 2.5, 5 and 10 g.

Figure 10 shows that manganese dissolution under conditions without a reducing agent is less than 1%, highlighting the essential role of the reductant. As illustrated in Fig. 10, increasing the amount of pomegranate peel from 1.25 to 5 g increases manganese dissolution. The maximum manganese recovery is thus achieved with 5 g of pomegranate peel. Amounts exceeding 5 g do not show further improvements. The optimal amount of pomegranate peel depends on the manganese content present in the ore, particularly the concentration of Mn4⁺ ions. It is obvious that increasing the amount of pomegranate peel enhances the supply of reducing compounds, leading to improved manganese recovery.

Effect of amount of pomegranate peel on manganese dissolution in an acidic medium (pH with pomegranate peel as the reductant agent (experimental condition: 75 °C, 1.5 M H2SO4 acid, 450 rpm agitation speed and 240 min).

Optimal conditions

This study focused on the leaching of pyrolusite concentrate, utilizing sulfuric acid as the solvent and pomegranate peel as the reducing agent. The main objective was to optimize the process by assessing the impact of pomegranate peel weight, sulfuric acid concentration, agitation speed, temperature, and reaction time on the efficiency of manganese leaching. The optimized conditions for maximum manganese dissolution were determined to be: 5 g pomegranate peel, 240 min leaching time, 450 rpm stirring speed, 75 °C temperature, and 1.5 M sulfuric acid concentration. To validate these findings, three replicate leaching experiments were conducted under the optimized conditions, yielding a high manganese recovery with an average of 96.12%.

Since the studied ore is classified as a simple manganese ore, with the main mineral phases being pyrolusite, calcite, quartz, and barite. Under the applied acidic conditions, silica and barite remain insoluble, while calcite undergoes dissolution, which is separable in subsequent stages. Other impurities, such as iron, aluminum, and magnesium, are present in much smaller amounts (less than 1%), and their removal is feasible during downstream processing. The leach residue obtained under the optimal conditions was analyzed, and the results are summarized in Table 5. The low contents of CaO and MnO in the residue are attributed to the dissolution of calcite and pyrolusite during leaching process. The presence of Fe2O3 and Al2O3 indicates the incomplete dissolution of iron- and aluminum-bearing minerals. The high contents of BaO and SiO2 in the residue are due to the insoluble nature of barite and silica, which was expected under the applied acidic leaching conditions.

Scanning electron microscopy

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was utilized to examine the surface morphology and elemental composition of the concentrate, pomegranate peel and leaching residue. Figure 11 presents SEM images of the concentrate. Elemental distribution mapping revealed a concentration of manganese in the gray particles (Fig. 11c), confirming their identity as pyrolusite. A minor population of dark particles was also observed and identified as calcite due to the presence of calcium (Figs. 11b, d). Additionally, trace amounts of barite were detected as very light particles. Figure 11e shows the EDS analysis of the prepared concentrate (the area indicated in Fig. 11a), where the highest intensities correspond to manganese and oxygen. Minor peaks of silicon, barium, calcium, aluminum, and iron, related to impurities associated with pyrolusite, were also observed. The gold peaks are attributed to the sample coating.

Comparative analysis of SEM images revealed distinct morphological changes in the pomegranate peel following the leaching process. Prior to leaching, the pomegranate peel exhibited discrete particles with relatively smooth and clean surfaces. In contrast, the leached pomegranate peel displayed agglomerated particles, with a reduction in surface uniformity and smoothness. This change is attributed to the filling of pores and voids with undissolved mineral particles.

Across most studies, the optimal sulfuric acid concentration was consistently reported to be between 1.5 and 2.5 M, with this study selecting 1.5 M as the optimum. The amount of waste peel was typically examined as a weight ratio of peel to ore. Due to the lower grade of ore in those studies, reported ratios ranged from 0.25 to 0.5. However, this study utilized a higher-grade manganese concentrate, resulting in an optimal weight ratio of 1. It is evident that as the manganese content in the system increases, the required amount of reducing agent also increases. The optimal reaction time in this study is 4 h, aligning with previous reports that indicate optimal times ranging from 2 to 8 h, with shorter durations observed under specific conditions, such as a temperature of 120 °C. Reaction temperatures in different studies varied between 70 and 100 °C, while this study’s optimal temperature is 75 °C.

The reducing agent is a key factor in the reductive leaching of manganese concentrate. This study employed pomegranate peel, chosen for its abundance of highly reducing compounds. Some of the bioactive compounds of pomegranate are shown in Fig. 20. Pomegranate peel, as a beneficial by-product, is rich in bioactive constituents primarily phenolic compounds including tannins, flavonoids, and polyphenols that are known for their significant biological activity. The peel is rich in tannins (water-soluble phenolic compounds), most of which are hydrolyzable. Punicalagin (one of the four main groups of tannins) can undergo spontaneous hydrolysis from its hexahydroxybenzoic acid backbone, yielding ellagic acid. This ellagic acid can bind with sugar moieties and subsequently polymerize into structurally complex ellagitannins. In addition, the peels contain lignin, cellulose, uronic acid, and neutral sugars, with lignin being present in the highest amount. Cellulose, uronic acid, glucose, and fructose are also found in pomegranate peel26. In the presence of acid, cellulose in plants or different parts of plants is hydrolyzed into glucose, galactose, and mannose, while hemicellulose breaks down into xylose. These resulting sugars serve as the primary reducing agents for the reduction of Mn4+ to Mn2+21. However, the complex structure of biomass and the reaction mechanisms involved in its conversion to glucose and other sugar compounds remain largely unclear. Lignin, a complex polymer of phenylpropane units, possesses a stable three-dimensional structure. Its hydrolysis is typically achieved through chemical or biological degradation. Due to its intricate structure, lignin resists hydrolysis by water alone; thus, strong acids (e.g., sulfuric acid), bases, or oxidative methods are generally employed for its breakdown. This process cleaves ether and carbon–carbon bonds within lignin, yielding smaller phenolic compounds like phenol, guaiacol, and various other derivatives45. Tannins, complex phenolic compounds found in many plants, are another key component. Hydrolyzable tannins consist of phenolic units such as gallic acid or its oligomers, linked to a sugar (usually glucose) via ester bonds. In the presence of acids or specific enzymes (e.g., tannases), these bonds break down, leading to the decomposition of tannins into simpler components like gallic acid and sugars46. The reductive leaching of manganese concentrate using pomegranate peel is likely influenced by bioactive compounds such as tannins, lignin, cellulose, glucose, and fructose. Consequently, the plant-derived sugars present in pomegranate peel, alongside those produced during the hydrolysis of other compounds within the peel, act as reducing agents in the manganese leaching process. FTIR analysis confirms changes in functional groups, and UV analysis further verifies the significant presence of phenolic acids and tannins in the pomegranate peel.

Key bioactive compounds of pomegranate peels26.

Figure 21 illustrates the proposed mechanism of the reductive leaching process. Plant-based reductants promote the reduction of Mn(IV) to Mn(II) through the reaction of organic compounds such as glucose, sucrose, and polyphenols, which act as electron donors. During the leaching process, these compounds are hydrolyzed under acidic conditions, and the resulting species act as reducing agents, causing a slight effect on the solution pH. Although the initial leaching medium was highly acidic (pH < 1), a slight increase in pH was observed after the addition of the pomegranate peel reductant. This pH increase is attributed to the interaction of the organic compounds with the acidic solution. Challenges and future directions

Following solid–liquid separation, common routes for manganese recovery from sulfate leach solutions include precipitation, solvent extraction (SX), ion exchange (IX), and electrolysis47. Precipitation under controlled pH conditions is a fast and efficient method for recovering manganese; however, the management of co-dissolved impurities is required to ensure product purity48. The solvent extraction of Mn (II) from sulfate media using D2EHPA has been well documented and is particularly effective for producing high-purity MnSO4 solutions suitable for electrolytic manganese dioxide (EMD) or electrolytic manganese metal (EM) production49–51. Ion exchange using chelating or weak-acid resins can serve as a polishing step for impurity removal or selective Mn recovery at smaller scales. The production of EMD or EM from MnSO4 − H2SO4 electrolytes requires a highly purified feed solution; thus, prior solution purification (via precipitation, SX or IX) is essential to ensure stability and product quality52.

Future studies should focus on extending the reductive leaching of pyrolusite through detailed experimental, techno-economic, and environmental assessments before any consideration of scale-up. Research should also emphasize the optimization of process parameters, reproducibility, and solution conditioning to minimize residual organic matter derived from pomegranate peel. Further attention should be given to impurity control, particularly the management of Ca2⁺ originating from calcite. In addition, the recovery of manganese should be explored through precipitation, solvent extraction, or electrolytic processes. Future investigations may also target the potential use of other agricultural wastes and by-products as alternative or complementary green reductants to improve the sustainability and overall efficiency of the process.

Conclusion

The extraction of manganese from manganese concentrate using sulfuric acid and pomegranate peel was successfully demonstrated. This study establishes pomegranate peel, an agricultural byproduct abundant in natural reductants, as a sustainable and environmentally sound reducing agent for the reductive leaching of manganese from high-grade pyrolusite concentrate. These findings underscore the technical viability and ecological benefits of employing plant-based reductants in hydrometallurgical extraction, while also offering a valuable approach to waste valorization and sustainable resource recovery. By merging green chemistry principles with industrial waste management, this work advances environmentally conscious practices within the mining and metallurgical sectors. Comprehensive parameter analysis identified optimal conditions yielding near-complete manganese recovery, highlighting the potent reducing capabilities of compounds present in pomegranate peel—including tannins, lignin, and various sugars, as corroborated by FTIR analysis. Kinetic modeling revealed a surface chemical reaction mechanism with an activation energy of 38.39 kJ/mol, indicating that the rate-limiting step is governed by surface chemical reactions. Thermodynamic evaluation confirmed that the leaching process is endothermic, requiring external energy input. The results of the present study will be useful for extracting manganese from manganese sources using pomegranate peel. They can provide significant assistance regarding the use of biomass for the reductive leaching of manganese.
