Sexual dysfunction and related factors in adult males with familial Mediterranean fever
Authors: Sercan Gucenmez, Adil Emrah Sonbahar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sexual dysfunction is increasingly recognized in inflammatory diseases, yet data in Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) are limited. This study aimed to determine the ratio of erectile dysfunction (ED) in adult males with FMF and to identify demographic, clinical, and psychological correlates of sexual function. In this cross-sectional study, 50 adult males with FMF fulfilling Tel-Hashomer criteria were evaluated during attack-free periods. Sexual function was measured using the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF), emotional status with the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), and quality of life with the Familial Mediterranean Fever Quality of Life Questionnaire (FMF-QoL). Groups with and without ED (IIEF-Erectile Function Score ≤ 26) were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Correlations between IIEF domains and clinical or psychological variables were analyzed using Spearman coefficients. ED was present in 56% of the participants. Depression was higher in the ED group (7.5 [5.0–9.25] vs. 5.0 [2.5–7.0], p = 0.03). Age correlated positively with orgasmic function, intercourse satisfaction, overall satisfaction, and total IIEF (p < 0.05). Anxiety and depression correlated negatively with erectile function, orgasmic function, intercourse satisfaction, overall satisfaction, and total IIEF scores (p < 0.05). FMF-QoL correlated negatively with orgasmic function, desire, overall satisfaction, and total IIEF (p < 0.05). ED is common in adult males with FMF and is more closely related to psychological factors than to clinical disease variables. Incorporating routine screening for emotional distress and sexual health may support more comprehensive FMF management.

Introduction

Sexual function is influenced by a complex interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors such as previous sexual experiences, emotional relationships, medication use, and mood state1. The presence of sexual dysfunction can negatively affect not only intimate relationships but also general health and well-being, leading to a substantial decline in quality of life2. To evaluate this multifactorial and subjective health issue, standardized tools such as the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) have been developed and validated in multiple languages3,4.

Sexual dysfunction has been previously studied in rheumatic and immunological disorders, where pain, vascular changes, chronic inflammation, psychological stress, and medication often contribute to impaired sexual health. Pain, limited physical activity, and anti-inflammatory drugs have been identified as major contributors to sexual dysfunction in males with rheumatoid arthritis5. Similarly, in psoriatic arthritis, glucocorticoid exposure, musculoskeletal involvement, and longer disease duration have been associated with a higher prevalence of sexual dysfunction6. Approximately one in five patients with psoriatic arthritis perceive their disease as having a negative impact on sexual activity7. Elevated C-reactive protein levels and chronic back pain correlate with reduced sexual function in ankylosing spondylitis, while psychological distress and anxiety further exacerbate this impairment8. Collectively, these findings indicate that sexual dysfunction in rheumatic diseases arises from both physiological and psychosocial mechanisms.

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is the most common autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disease, prevalent among populations from the Mediterranean basin and the Middle East. The disease typically initiates in childhood and is characterized by recurrent attacks of fever, peritonitis, pleuritis, arthritis, or erysipelas-like skin lesions. Although patients are generally asymptomatic between attacks, subclinical inflammation often persists, contributing to chronic vascular and systemic effects that may impair daily functioning and overall quality of life9.

Data on sexual dysfunction in FMF is limited, but several studies suggest potential mechanisms linking the disease to erectile dysfunction (ED). Adult males with FMF have demonstrated impaired endothelial function, with significantly reduced flow-mediated dilation and increased serum endocan levels compared with healthy controls, suggesting endothelial damage as a possible underlying cause of ED9. Decreases in sperm concentration, total sperm count, and sperm motility have been observed in adolescents with FMF, particularly among those with frequent attacks, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and higher colchicine doses10. Rare but severe complications such as orchitis or testicular amyloidosis may further disrupt spermatogenesis. While colchicine may occasionally reduce sperm motility through its antimitotic effects, its overall impact on male fertility remains uncertain11.

Despite the high prevalence of FMF in Mediterranean basin and the Middle East, there is a lack of research directly examining sexual dysfunction in adult males with FMF. Considering the disease’s chronic inflammatory nature, endothelial involvement, and psychosocial burden, assessing sexual function in this population could reveal important but often overlooked health consequences. Understanding these relationships may contribute to improving patient comfort, sexual health, and overall quality of life through a more holistic approach to disease management. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to determine the ratio of erectile dysfunction (ED) in adult males with FMF and to identify demographic, clinical, and psychological correlates of sexual function.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the Rheumatology Clinic of Izmir Katip Celebi University Ataturk Training and Research Hospital. The study was approved by the Izmir Katip Celebi University Non-Interventional Clinical Studies Institutional Review Board (date: 23rd Feb 2023, approval no: 0091). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

All participants were evaluated during attack-free periods to minimize the acute effects of inflammation on sexual performance and psychosocial status. Fifty adult males diagnosed with Familial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) according to the Tel-Hashomer criteria12 who were in a stable clinical condition and under regular follow-up at the rheumatology outpatient clinic were included in the study. Exclusion criteria included the presence of diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, malignancy, neurological or psychiatric disorders, and the use of medications other than colchicine that could affect sexual function.

Procedures

All participants were evaluated during routine outpatient visits. Data collection was performed by a single trained researcher to ensure consistency and minimize measurement bias. Sociodemographic characteristics (age, marital status, education level, and smoking status) and disease-related variables (disease duration, attack frequency, colchicine dose, family history, symptoms on disease onset) were recorded from medical charts and patient interviews.

Each participant underwent a brief physical examination, including measurement of height and weight. Disease activity and attack characteristics were evaluated by a rheumatologist (S.G) based on clinical history and chart documentation. Laboratory markers of inflammation, including erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), were obtained from the most recent clinical assessments. Data consistency was checked twice by the same investigator to ensure accuracy, and incomplete entries were verified through patient contact or medical record review. Each participant completed the questionnaires in a private setting to ensure confidentiality and accuracy of self-reported data.

Data collection tools

Sexual function was assessed using the International Index of Erectile Function (IIEF) questionnaire, which evaluates five domains: erectile function (EF), orgasmic function (OF), sexual desire (SD), intercourse satisfaction (IS), and overall satisfaction (OS). The IIEF consists of 15 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 5 to 75. A score of 26 or lower in the erectile function domain was used to define erectile dysfunction (ED). The IIEF has demonstrated strong internal consistency and test–retest reliability, and its Turkish version was validated for use in clinical and research settings13.

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is a self-administered questionnaire designed to assess anxiety and depression in individuals with medical conditions. It consists of 14 items divided into two subscales: HADS-Anxiety (7 items) and HADS-Depression (7 items). Each item is scored from 0 to 3, resulting in a subscale score range of 0–21, with higher scores indicating worse emotional status. The HADS was validated in Turkish language14.

The Familial Mediterranean Fever Quality of Life Questionnaire (FMF-QoL) is a disease-specific, self-administered questionnaire designed to assess the impact of FMF on quality of life. It evaluates physical, emotional, social, and functional aspects of daily living affected by FMF. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life. The Turkish FMF-QoL was validated in adult FMF populations and is widely used in clinical and research settings15.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 15.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the data were not normally distributed, continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR, 25th − 75th percentile), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Mann-Whitney U test was used for group comparisons. The relationships between erectile function and clinical or demographic parameters were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Correlation strengths were interpreted as negligible (p < 0.30), fair (0.30–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.69), good (0.70–0.89), or excellent (p ≥ 0.90). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 50 adult males with FMF were included in the study. The main findings are summarized below, with detailed demographic, clinical, and patient-reported outcome data presented in Tables 1, 2 and 3. Participants’ median age was 33.0 years [24.0–45.0]. The median total IIEF score was 56.0 (38.75–66.0), and ED was present in 56% of participants.

Comparison of participants with and without ED

Demographic, anthropometric, and disease-related characteristics did not differ between groups (p > 0.05). Depression scores were higher in participants with ED (p = 0.030), while anxiety showed a non-significant trend towards higher values (p = 0.064). FMF-QoL scores were comparable (p = 0.355) (Table 2).

Correlations with sexual function

Age correlated positively with OF (rho = 0.340, p = 0.016), IS (rho = 0.315, p = 0.026), OS (rho = 0.366, p = 0.009), and total IIEF (rho = 0.318, p = 0.024). Symptom duration correlated positively with OF (rho = 0.362, p = 0.015) (Table 3).

Psychological variables showed the strongest associations. Anxiety was negatively associated with EF, OF, SD, IS, OS, and total IIEF (rho: − 0.338 to − 0.432; p < 0.05) (Table 3). Depression showed similar negative correlations with EF, OF, IS, OS, and total IIEF (rho from − 0.334 to − 0.438; all p ≤ 0.018) (Table 3). FMF-QoL correlated negatively with OF (rho=–0.351, p = 0.014) (Table 3).

No meaningful correlations were found for height, education, smoking, time since diagnosis, or colchicine dose.

Post-hoc power analysis

For the principal correlations between psychological variables and erectile function domains (Spearman’s rho ranging from − 0.423 to − 0.438; n = 50; α = 0.05, two-tailed), the achieved statistical power was estimated to be between 87% and 90%.

Discussion

In this study of adult males with FMF, we found that ED was a prevalent problem as more than half of participants had an IIEF erectile-function domain score below the threshold for ED. Those with ED had higher depression scores (median HADS-D 7.5 vs. 5.0) and a trend toward greater anxiety. On the other hand, sociodemographic and clinical variables (e.g. age, BMI, colchicine dose, attack history) did not differ significantly between the ED and non-ED groups. Additionally, higher HADS anxiety and depression scores were related to nearly all IIEF domains and total score, indicating that worse mood was associated with worse sexual function. It was also observed that older age, and longer disease duration showed fair positive correlations with some IIEF domains (e.g. orgasmic function, sexual desire, overall satisfaction), whereas poorer FMF-specific quality of life (higher FMF-QoL score) was modestly correlated with lower sexual function. In summary, sexual dysfunction was prevalent in our participants with FMF and appeared to be more strongly influenced by psychological factors than by disease-related factors. Best to our knowledge, this is the first study investigating ED and correlating factors in adult males with FMF.

Our findings are broadly consistent with data from other inflammatory diseases, which also show high rates of sexual dysfunction related to both physiological and psychosocial disease effects. Roughly one-third of patients with rheumatoid arthritis report sexual disorders, with pain, limited mobility, and active inflammation as key contributors5. As in our FMF cohort, studies on rheumatoid arthritis found a strong association between mood disturbance and sexual dysfunction5. Patients with psoriatic arthritis also have very high rates of sexual problems. Lobo et al. reported sexual dysfunction in 92% of men with psoriatic arthritis (predominantly mild ED), and one in five patients with psoriatic arthritis perceive their disease as exerting a large negative impact on their sexual activity6,7. Disease activity and joint involvement were identified as correlates of sexual problems7. Ankylosing spondylitis is similarly associated with impaired male sexual function. A recent meta-analysis found that men with ankylosing spondylitis score significantly lower on all IIEF domains than healthy controls8. Markers of active inflammation (e.g. elevated CRP) and chronic pain have been linked to worse sexual function, and psychological distress further exacerbates this effect in ankylosing spondylitis. The lack of significant association between FMF attack history or colchicine dose and ED in our data suggests that psychosocial factors play a dominant role in sexual problems in FMF.

Recent studies have found that people with FMF often report mild-to-moderate anxiety and depression16. Higher HADS-anxiety and HADS-depression scores correlated strongly with worse IIEF scores in the present study. This is in line with previous studies that explored the relationship between emotional factors and sexual function17,18. Previously, Liu et al. demonstrated in meta-analysis that depression significantly increases ED risk and, conversely, ED increases the risk of depression19. Similarly, Santos-Moreno et al. found that in rheumatoid arthritis psychological alterations were among the strongest predictors of sexual dysfunction5. In our cohort, every measure of sexual function declined markedly as HADS scores rose, suggesting that anxiety/depression may be the primary drivers of poor sexual health in FMF.

The positive correlations observed between age and several IIEF domains including orgasmic function, intercourse satisfaction, overall satisfaction, and total IIEF score were seemingly paradoxical, given that ED prevalence is known to increase with age in the general population. However, this finding can be explained through several convergent mechanisms. First, evidence suggests that the psychosocial factors are the primary drivers of ED in younger men, while organic etiologies become predominant only in older age groups. Saito et al. confirmed that psychosocial factors primarily influence ED in men younger than 40 years, whereas biological factors are more likely to be the underlying cause in older men20. In diseases like FMF, where psychological burden is high and our sample is relatively young (median age 33 years), younger participants may be disproportionately affected by performance anxiety and psychological distress despite having fewer physiological risk factors, a pattern well described previously20,21. Second, older participants in our cohort are more likely to be in stable long-term partnerships, and the presence of a stable partner has been identified as an important protective factor against sexual dysfunction in chronic inflammatory disease populations22. Third, participants who have lived with FMF longer may have developed better psychological adaptation and coping strategies over time. This finding should nonetheless be interpreted with caution given the sample size and the fact that age did not differ significantly between the ED and non-ED groups in the group comparison. Future studies with larger samples stratified by age group are needed to clarify this relationship.

Regarding disease-specific factors, no correlations were detected between colchicine dose, FMF-related clinical variables (attack frequency, initial symptoms, etc.) and ED in our participants. This suggests that the antimitotic effects of colchicine and the history of typical FMF inflammation do not translate into sexual impairment in this setting. This is consistent with the limited literature on FMF and male reproductive health. In adolescents with FMF, sperm mobility was lower in those with frequent attacks and higher colchicine dose10, but the impact on erectile function has not been clearly defined. The absence of a colchicine-ED link in our data aligns with other rheumatic diseases where medication type often plays a minor role in sexual function once disease control is achieved.

Our results underscore that sexual dysfunction in FMF likely arises from a complex interplay of chronic inflammation, endothelial health, and psychosocial factors. Subclinical inflammation in FMF can produce endothelial dysfunction even between attacks9, theoretically impairing penile blood flow. Özalper et al. found that adults with FMF have significantly higher serum endocan (an endothelial marker) and lower brachial flow-mediated dilation versus controls, implying early vascular damage9. While we did not measure endothelial function directly, our finding of widespread ED is somewhat compatible with this concept. However, the lack of correlation between ED and traditional FMF severity indices suggests that much of the sexual impairment in our sample was not simply a direct vascular effect of inflammation. Rather, the strong ties to mental health suggest that FMF’s psychological burden plays a central role in sexual impairment.

The high prevalence of sexual dysfunction in individuals with FMF – and its close link to depression and anxiety – carries important implications for patient care. Routine clinical assessments of FMF seldom address sexual health, yet our data suggest it as a possible quality-of-life issue. Rheumatologists and primary care providers should be aware that males with FMF may under-report erectile difficulties, even though these can impair life satisfaction and relationships. Screening tools like IIEF could be integrated into periodic evaluations, especially for patients exhibiting mood disturbance or quality-of-life complaints.

This study has several limitations. The sample size was modest (50 participants) and drawn from a single tertiary center, which may limit generalizability. We used a cross-sectional design, so causality cannot be inferred; longitudinal changes in sexual function over the course of FMF or in relation to flares were not assessed. All data were obtained during attack-free periods, which reduces the confounding effect of acute illness but may underestimate ED that occurs during flares. Self-reported questionnaires (IIEF, HADS, FMF-QoL) without objective physiological testing (e.g. Doppler ultrasound of penile vasculature, hormone levels, or nerve conduction) were employed for assessments. A healthy control group was not included, which represents the main limitation of this study; thus, we compared our findings mainly to published normative data and studies in other diseases. Future studies should incorporate age- and BMI-matched healthy controls to enable direct prevalence comparisons and strengthen causal inferences. Participants with overt diabetes or cardiovascular disease were excluded; the prevalence of ED may be even higher in individuals with FMF presenting more comorbidities. Cultural factors may have influenced self-reporting of sexual symptoms, although the questionnaires were completed in private to encourage candor. Finally, as with all cross-sectional studies, the directionality of the observed associations cannot be established. It remains unclear whether psychological distress drives erectile dysfunction or whether the burden of living with erectile dysfunction contributes to anxiety and depression. Longitudinal studies are needed to disentangle cause from consequence in this population.

Conclusion

Male sexual dysfunction is frequent in FMF, and more than half of participants had met erectile dysfunction criteria in our sample. Psychological distress was found to be a key correlation. These findings highlight the need for a holistic approach to FMF management that includes attention to sexual health and mental well-being. Future research should confirm these findings in larger, multicenter samples and explore whether interventions targeting mood, endothelial function, or disease activity can improve sexual outcomes. In the meantime, physicians treating FMF should proactively inquire about sexual symptoms and consider addressing anxiety and depression as part of comprehensive care.
