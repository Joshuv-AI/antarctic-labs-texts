Adult Acute Myeloid Leukaemia With DDX3X::MLLT10: A Rare Entity With Significant Unmet Clinical Needs
Authors: Jun Yen Ng, Arun SR, Maansi Joshi, Dipti Talaulikar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDDX3X::MLLT10¸ which arises from t(X;10)(p11.4;p12.31), is infrequently observed in adult acute myeloid leukaemia (AML). The clinical and pathological characteristics are also not well characterised, detailed in only two cases published to date. This third case reports the fusion gene in a 27‐year‐old female with acute myelomonocytic leukaemia (AMML) with refractoriness to multiple lines of chemotherapy. Collectively, these cases highlight the genomic heterogeneity of this rare entity, as well as significant unmet clinical needs due to the poor prognosis and chemoresistance observed. These findings may inform future iterations of AML classification and prognostication in adults.Keywords: acute myeloid leukaemia, DDX3X::MLLT10, prognostication

Introduction

DDX3X and MLLT10 genes are located on chromosomes X and 10, respectively. MLLT10 rearrangement occurs in 8%–10% of T‐acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (T‐ALL), 5.7% of acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), and 3.7% of B‐lymphoblastic leukaemia (B‐ALL). The fusion partners vary according to the disease, with PICALM most common in T‐ALL, whereas KMT2A is most common in AML and B‐ALL [1].

In a study of 2080 children and young adults between 0 and 30 years old with AML, MLLT10 rearrangements were reported in 6.1% of cases. KMT2A was the most common fusion partner, occurring in 81.9% of cases, followed by PICALM (10.2%) and X::MLLT10 (7.9%). The latter includes two cases of DDX3X::MLLT10 (approximately 0.1% of the entire cohort) [2]. There is limited literature reporting the prognosis, clinical and pathological characteristics of AML with DDX3X::MLLT10 in adults.

Case Presentation

A 27‐year‐old female presented with a 2‐week history of lethargy and bleeding gums. She was found to have significant leucocytosis with a white cell count (WCC) of 53.5 × 109/L and 50% blasts in the peripheral blood. Bone marrow biopsy confirmed acute myelomonocytic leukaemia (AMML; AML‐M4) and conventional karyotype testing revealed 46, X, t(X;10)(p10;q10)[2]/47, idem, +mar[18]. Next‐generation targeted sequencing (NGS) analysis of 37 genes with significance in myeloid conditions was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550, with libraries prepared using the Archer VariantPlex assay combined with the Archer Core Myeloid 37‐gene panel. This detected a KRAS c.38G>A p.(Gly13Asp) variant with a frequency of 27.89% without FLT3 and NPM1. She underwent induction chemotherapy with idarubicin and cytarabine (“7+3”) [3].

Bone marrow examination (BME) on Day 31 following induction therapy showed multilineage dysplastic features and 26% blasts (myeloblasts, monoblasts and promonocytes), as compared to 62% at diagnosis, indicating a lack of response to therapy (Figure 1). Other granulocytes include promyelocytes (9.9%), myelocytes (4.3%), metamyelocytes (5.8%), neutrophils or band forms (33.3%) and monocytes (9.9%). On immunophenotyping, the blasts expressed CD13, CD33, CD11b, CD11c, CD15 and CD64 with disparate HLA‐DR positive and negative populations. CD117 was expressed in a small subpopulation. They did not express CD34, CD14, CD4, CD7, surface CD3, CD19, CD20 and CD10 (Figure 2). Cytoplasmic 79a, CD3, TDT and myeloperoxidase (MPO) were negative at diagnosis and not repeated.

Conventional karyotyping was performed on the bone marrow aspirate sample with 24‐h unsynchronised and 48‐h synchronised cultures. The karyotype (Figure 3) was reported according to the International System for Human Cytogenetic Nomenclature (ISCN) 2020 [4]. There was an abnormal female karyotype, 46,X,t(X;10)(p10;p10) in most of the metaphases (27/30). Additionally, a marker chromosome of unknown origin was also noted (26/30 metaphases), suggesting additional chromosomal abnormalities. FISH was not performed due to unavailability of the specific probe.

Karyotype reporting 46, X, t(X;10)(p10;p10)[1]/47, sl, +mar[26]/46, XX[3] (GTL banding).

NGS analysis of 80 genes with clinical significance in haematological malignancy was performed. Libraries were prepared using a custom QIAGEN QIAseq single primer extension‐based panel (Peter MacCallum Cancer Centre AllHaem panel v4) and sequenced on an Illumina Novoseq 6000. This showed KRAS c.38G>A p.(Gly13Asp) and SMARCA4 c.3325A>G p.(Met1109Val) at variant read frequencies (VRF) of 43% and 39%, respectively.

Targeted RNA fusion sequencing performed using a custom QIAGEN QIAseq RNA fusion single primer extension‐based panel and sequenced on NextSeq500 reported DDX3X::MLLT10 exon5::exon9 (NM_001356.4::NM_001324297.1), in keeping with the known karyotypic abnormality. This fusion, as well as the KRAS and SMARCA4 variants identified, is not AML‐defining. According to the 2022 European Leukemia Net (ELN) risk stratification, the patient had intermediate‐risk disease [3, 5].

After discussing with the patient, she commenced salvage fludarabine, cytarabine, idarubicin and filgrastim (FLAG‐Ida) therapy. BME on Day 34 following salvage therapy showed hypocellular marrow with 1.6% blasts, along with neutrophils of 0.9 × 109/L and platelet count of 34 × 109/L, confirming AMML in morphologic leukaemia‐free state (MLFS) [5]. However, BME on Day 48 following salvage therapy showed 8.2% blasts, with neutrophils of 0.58 × 109/L and platelet count of 35 × 109/L, confirming AMML with refractory disease [5]. She commenced bridging venetoclax/azacitidine (Ven/Aza) therapy with a plan for allogeneic haematopoietic stem cell transplant (alloHCT). Repeat BME on Day 22 following Ven/Aza therapy demonstrated 11.9% blasts. NGS redemonstrated similar findings to those of the previous BM, including DDX3X::MLLT10, as well as KRAS and SMARCA4 at VRFs of 41% and 46%, respectively.

Treatment was complicated by neutropenic sepsis and respiratory failure of unclear aetiology requiring cessation of therapy and intensive care support. Subsequently, she developed multifocal intracranial haemorrhage, which was managed with platelets and cryoprecipitate transfusion. Following discussion with the family, she transitioned to comfort care and passed away the following day. The clinical course and investigation are summarised in Figure 4.

Discussion

DDX3X::MLLT10 arises from the translocation of DDX3X on chromosome Xp11.4 and MLLT10 on chromosome 10p12.31 [7]. The protein encoded by DDX3X is a member of the large DEAD‐box protein family and has ATP‐dependent RNA helicase activity. The gene is involved in important cellular processes including transcriptional regulation, pre‐mRNA splicing, mRNA export and cellular signalling. It also escapes X‐inactivation (in females), which allows the gene to be active in both X chromosomes. DDX3Y gene on Yq11.221 is a paralog to DDX3X [8]. The MLLT10 gene encodes a transcription factor and is involved in histone modification [9].

Only three cases of AML with DDX3X::MLLT10 have been detailed to date, including the present case, as summarised in Table 1 [9, 10]. They highlight important observations and unmet clinical needs in this rare entity. First, all cases occurred in younger adults. Second, there is significant heterogeneity in the pathological characteristics, particularly the genomic findings.

Most importantly, a poor prognosis, including resistance to multiple lines of therapy, is observed in these cases. According to the 2022 ELN risk stratification, MLLT10 rearrangements carry an intermediate risk, as it is neither favourable nor adverse [5]. However, the clinical course and treatment response seen in these cases are concordant with the poor outcomes reported in AML harbouring MLLT10 rearrangements. The adverse prognosis of DDX3X::MLLT10 observed in these cases parallels the poor outcomes associated with the more common fusions, namely KMT2A::MLLT10 (1.6%–4.7%) and PICALM::MLLT10 (0.6%–1.7%). Notably, an association with other adverse prognostic markers is observed, including FLT3‐ITD and complex karyotype [9, 10]. Given the poor outcomes, there may be an unmet need for more effective therapies.

Given the location of DDX3X and MLLT10 genes on chromosome Xp11.4 and 10p12.31, respectively, there is a slight discrepancy with the karyotypic finding of t(X;10)(p10;p10) in this case, which may be due to the resolution. In Li et al.’s study, t(X;10)(p11.2;p11.2) was reported [10]. Translocation t(X;10)(p10;p10) has been reported in a 2‐month‐old newborn with AMML. The patient attained CR after treatment with aracytine‐C and fludarabine, followed by aracytine‐C, amsacrine, rubidomycin and kidrolase. Genomic findings were not available in this case [11].

DDX3X::MLLT10 is not an AML‐defining genetic aberration in the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification [3]. The gene fusion has also been reported in 3% of adults with T‐ALL and in a 47‐year‐old male with triphenotypic acute leukaemia [12, 13]. The patient had a concurrent EZH2 mutation. He achieved complete remission after completing cycle 1B of cyclophosphamide, vincristine, doxorubicin, dexamethasone, cytarabine and high‐dose methotrexate (Hyper‐CVAD) chemotherapy [13].

In summary, this is the third case detailing AML with DDX3X::MLLT10 in adults, which collectively highlights significant unmet clinical needs. This case supports the poor prognosis and chemoresistance reported with this entity. The case also highlights the genomic heterogeneity and extends the co‐mutations to include KRAS and SMARCA4. However, more data are required for further characterisation. These findings may inform future iterations of AML classification and prognostication (e.g., WHO) in adult AML.
