Prescribed distinct-digit growth in countable alphabets
Ying Wai Lee

Abstract
. The number of distinct symbols appearing in digit expansions generated by full-branch affine countable iterated function systems is studied whose branch weights are regularly varying. The Hausdorff dimensions of the exceptional sets in which the distinct-digit count grows at a positive linear rate or at a prescribed sublinear rate are determined. The resulting dimension laws exhibit a sharp phase transition: imposing any positive linear rate forces the dimension to collapse to a value determined solely by the tail index, whereas under a broad class of sublinear growth rates, the exceptional sets retain full Hausdorff dimension.



1.
Introduction

Distinct-value statistics form a fundamental class of observables at the interface of probability, number theory, dynamical systems, and fractal geometry. Let
\Xi\coloneqq(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a symbolic process taking values in a countable alphabet, identified here with
\mathbb{N}
. A natural question is: how many
distinct
symbols have appeared up to time
n
? Define the
distinct-digit count

D_{n}
by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle D_{n}\coloneqq\#\{\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}\}.

This single quantity has several classical interpretations. In probability, it is the number of
occupied boxes
in an infinite-urn occupancy scheme after a given number of draws; in number-theoretic expansions, it is a measure of
digit diversity
; in dynamical systems, it is a non-additive observable derived from a symbolic coding; in fractal geometry, prescribing its growth naturally leads to exceptional sets whose size is quantified by Hausdorff dimension.

The present study focuses on affine full-branch countable iterated function systems on the unit interval
[0,1)
: the interval is partitioned into countably many subintervals, each branch maps its subinterval affinely onto the whole interval, and one digit is produced at each step. Under Lebesgue measure, the digit process is independent and identically distributed, so distinct-digit count is simultaneously identified with an infinite-urn occupancy count and with a natural dynamical statistic for the associated interval map with its coding cylinder metric. The classical Lüroth expansion is a distinguished example within this framework.

Under regularly varying (or heavy-tailed) digit weights, the Hausdorff dimensions of sets are determined where distinct-digit count grows atypically, both at positive linear growth and a broad class of prescribed sublinear growth. The resulting dimension law exhibits a sharp phase transition: sublinear prescriptions typically remain full-dimensional, while any positive linear rate forces a universal dimension drop governed solely by the tail index.

1.1.
Continued fractions

Continued fraction expansions provide a number-theoretic setting in which a digit process on a countable alphabet is generated by a well-understood dynamical system. Classical work has emphasised magnitude-related phenomena for partial quotients (such as extremes and growth rates), and more recently longest-run statistics have also been studied
[
14
,
17
,
20
]
. An equally natural, but less systematically explored, statistic concerns
diversity
: at any given cut-off, how many distinct integer values have occurred among the partial quotients. Each first appearance of a new partial quotient can be viewed as genuinely new symbolic information. Unlike the classical infinite-urn occupancy model, continued-fraction digits are not independent; nevertheless, under Gauss measure, their long-range statistics are sufficiently regular to support a refined Hausdorff-dimension theory for level sets of distinct-digit count.

For any
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, there exists a unique sequence of positive integers
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
x
admits the continued fraction expansion:

\displaystyle x=[a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},\ldots]\coloneqq\frac{1}{\displaystyle a_{1}+\frac{1}{\displaystyle a_{2}+\frac{1}{a_{3}+\cdots}}},

where for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n}\coloneqq a_{n}(x)\in\mathbb{N}
is referred to as
n
-th partial quotient of
x
. The sequence of range functions
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
,

\displaystyle R_{n}(x)\coloneqq\#\left\{a_{1}(x),a_{2}(x),\ldots,a_{n}(x)\right\},

which counts the number of distinct values among the first
n
partial quotients of
x
. For example, one obtains
R_{30}(\pi-3)=10
by observing the first 30 partial quotients of
\pi-3
:

\displaystyle\pi-3=[\dotuline{7},\dotuline{15},\dotuline{1},\dotuline{292},1,1,1,\dotuline{2},1,\dotuline{3},1,\dotuline{14},2,1,1,2,2,2,2,1,\dotuline{84},2,1,1,15,3,\dotuline{13},1,\dotuline{4},2,\ldots],

where the underlined terms represent the first occurrence of each distinct integer.

A classical consequence of the metric theory of continued fractions is that the sequence of partial quotients is unbounded for Lebesgue almost every irrational. Equivalently, for Lebesgue almost every
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
,
\lim_{n\to+\infty}R_{n}(x)=+\infty
. A natural quantitative question is therefore the typical growth rate of the range functions. Wu–Xie
[
21
]
initiated a systematic study of the range-renewal structure, and obtained an explicit almost-sure growth law with an effective convergence rate.

Theorem

(Wu–Xie
[
21
, Remark 2]
)
.

For Lebesgue almost every
x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}
, as
n\to+\infty
,

(1)

\displaystyle\frac{R_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{n}}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\log 2}}+O\!\left(n^{-\lambda}\right),

where the exponent is given by:

\displaystyle\lambda\coloneqq\frac{1}{6(1+2\log{2})}=0.06984\ldots.

The Hausdorff dimensions of exceptional sets associated with growth constraints on the range function were also studied by Wu–Xie
[
21
]
. Define, for any
\beta\geq 0
and
c>0
, the growth rate level set
E(\beta,c)
by:

\displaystyle E(\beta,c)\coloneqq\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{R_{n}(x)}{n^{\beta}}=c\right\}.

Theorem

(Wu–Xie
[
21
, Theorem 2 & Remark 3]
)
.

(1)

For any
0<\beta<1
and
c>0
,

\displaystyle\dim E(\beta,c)=1.

(2)

For any
0<c\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\dim E(1,c)=\frac{1}{2}.

(3)

For any non-decreasing and unbounded
\psi:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
, if
\lim_{n\to+\infty}\psi(n)/n=0
then:

\displaystyle\dim{\left\{x\in[0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}:\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{R_{n}(x)}{\psi(n)}=1\right\}}=1.

These results exhibit a sharp
dimension dichotomy
: a broad class of sublinear prescriptions yield full Hausdorff dimension, whereas any positive linear distinctness rate forces a universal drop to
1/2
. This naturally raises the question of how robust such a phase transition is for other digit systems, and which structural parameters determine the value of the dimension drop.

1.2.
Affine full-branch countable iterated function systems

More generally, digit sequences may be viewed as symbolic codings arising from countable iterated function systems. Attention is restricted to a canonical
affine, full-branch
subclass on the unit interval, for which the induced digit process is independent and identically distributed under Lebesgue measure. In this independent setting, the model may be regarded as a convenient prototype for distinct-value statistics.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a probability sequence, that is,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}p_{k}=1
and for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
p_{k}>0
. Let
(I_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a partition of
[0,1)
into pairwise disjoint half-open intervals. Suppose for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}{I_{k}}=p_{k}.

Define the associated family of affine contractions
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle\varphi_{k}(u)\coloneqq\inf I_{k}+p_{k}\,u

Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

\varphi_{k}([0,1))=I_{k}
and the images
(\varphi_{k}([0,1)))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are pairwise disjoint by the half-open convention. Define the full-branch map
T:[0,1)\to[0,1)
by, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in I_{k}

\displaystyle T(x)\coloneqq\frac{x-\inf I_{k}}{p_{k}}

Thus, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}

is the branch inverse, and

maps the interval

affinely onto
[0,1)

by, for any

and

\displaystyle d_{n}(x)\coloneqq k.

Then for any
x\in[0,1)

(d_{n}(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the coding (symbolic itinerary) of

with respect to
(\varphi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
; that is, for any
x\in[0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle x=\left(\varphi_{d_{1}(x)}\circ\varphi_{d_{2}(x)}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{d_{n}(x)}\right)\left(T^{n}x\right).

Define, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the distinct-digit count function
D_{n}:[0,1)\to\mathbb{N}
by, for any
x\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle D_{n}(x)\coloneqq\#{\left\{d_{1}(x),d_{2}(x),\ldots,d_{n}(x)\right\}}.

A key simplification of the affine full-branch setting (in contrast with continued fractions) is that under Lebesgue measure the digit process
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is independent and identically distributed
[
5
,
11
]
. In particular, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(d_{n}=k)=\operatorname{diam}{I_{k}}=p_{k}.

Consequently, the count of distinct digits is equal to the number of occupied boxes in an infinite-urn occupancy scheme, with box probabilities determined by the branch weights. Sharp asymptotic results from occupancy theory are thus applicable, and serve as probabilistic input for the dimension results stated later. Within this framework, the classical Lüroth expansion is taken as a guiding case.

1.4.
Classical infinite-urn occupancy theory

Although the digit sequence is generated by a deterministic full-branch map, an independent and identically distributed description of the digit process is valid under the reference measure. The digit process is thereby represented as an infinite-urn occupancy scheme, with box probabilities given by the branch weights, and the count of distinct digits is equal to the number of boxes that have been occupied after a given number of draws.

Since the coding under Lebesgue measure has the same distribution as independent and identically distributed draws, statements that hold with probability one in classical occupancy theory are transferred into Lebesgue-almost-everywhere statements on the unit interval. Through this identification, sharp asymptotic results from occupancy theory are imported to describe the typical growth of distinct-digit count, and these estimates are used as the probabilistic backbone for the Hausdorff dimension results established later.

The typical growth of the distinct-digit count is well understood in broad heavy-tailed regimes. Suppose, for the moment, there exist
C>0
and
\rho>1
such that:

(2)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{k^{-\rho}}=C.

Since
(D_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
coincides with the number of occupied boxes in the associated infinite-urn scheme, results from classical occupancy theory
[
9
,
12
]
yield the
n^{1/\rho}
law: for Lebesgue almost every
x\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{D_{n}(x)}{n^{1/\rho}}=\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho}\right)C^{1/\rho},

where
\Gamma
is the gamma function. Moreover, law-of-the-iterated-logarithm type refinements provide essentially optimal almost-sure fluctuation scales around this leading term; for instance, one deduces from
[
3
]
that for Lebesgue almost every
x\in[0,1)
, as
n\to+\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{D_{n}(x)}{n^{1/\rho}}=\Gamma\!\left(1-\frac{1}{\rho}\right)C^{1/\rho}+O\!\left(n^{-1/(2\rho)}\sqrt{\log\log n}\right).

In the classical Lüroth setting, one deduces that for Lebesgue almost every
x\in(0,1]
, as
n\to+\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{D_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{n}}=\sqrt{\pi}+O\!\left(n^{-1/4}\sqrt{\log\log n}\right).

These almost-everywhere laws identify the typical scale of distinct-digit count, but they do not address the geometric size of the sets where the growth of the distinct-digit count is
atypical
. Motivated by the continued-fraction results of Wu–Xie
[
21
]
and by the occupancy interpretation above, the Hausdorff dimensions of exceptional sets specified by asymptotic prescribed growth constraints are determined in the sequel.

2.
Main Results

Building on the typical growth discussed previously, attention now turns to exceptional sets defined by prescribed asymptotic growth of the distinct-digit count. The analysis is carried out under a regularly varying tail assumption, which is more general than the pure power-law condition (
2
).

Definition
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a probability sequence and
\rho\geq 1
.
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to be
regularly varying
with tail index
\rho

L:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that

(3)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to+\infty}\frac{p_{k}}{k^{-\rho}L(k)}=1.

Theorem
1
concerns linear distinctness rates.
Define, for any
0<\theta\leq 1
, the growth rate level set
E_{\theta}
at linear scale
\theta
by:

\displaystyle E_{\theta}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{x\in[0,1):\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{D_{n}(x)}{n}=\theta\right\}.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a probability sequence and
\rho\geq 1
.
Suppose
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is regularly varying with tail index
\rho
.
Then for any
0<\theta\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\dim{E_{\theta}}=\frac{1}{\rho}.

Thus, any positive linear rate forces a strict drop from full dimension (unless the tail index is 1), and the value is universal at the linear scale and determined solely by the tail index. In the classical Lüroth setting, one obtains that for any
0<\theta\leq 1
,
\dim{E_{\theta}}=1/2
, mirroring the continued-fraction phenomenon of Wu–Xie but in an independent and identically distributed digit setting.

The lower bound in Theorem
1
is proved by constructing a probability measure supported on a suitable subset and applying the mass distribution principle, with cylinder estimates derived from regular variation. The upper bound follows from a Hausdorff–Cantelli type covering argument combined with an appropriate change-of-measure (tilting) device for regularly varying tails.

Theorem
2
concerns a broad class of prescribed sublinear distinctness rates.

\displaystyle E_{f}\coloneqq\left\{x\in[0,1):\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{D_{n}(x)}{f(n)}=1\right\}.

.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a probability sequence and
\rho\geq 1
.
Suppose

3.1.
Block concatenation construction

The lower bound is obtained by constructing a subset via a block concatenation scheme, defining a probability measure supported on it, and then applying the mass distribution principle.

3.2.
Hausdorff–Cantelli and tilting

It remains to prove the upper bound
\dim E_{\geq\theta,+}\leq 1/\rho
. For
\rho=1
, the desired bound is immediate as
E_{\geq\theta,+}\subset[0,1)
. Thus, one may assume
\rho>1
and apply a Hausdorff–Cantelli argument combined with tilting.

The following lemma is a standard consequence in combinatorics.

Lemma 2
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Suppose
\#\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}\geq m
. Then,

\displaystyle\#\left\{1\leq i\leq n:x_{i}\geq\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil\right\}\geq\left\lceil\frac{m}{2}\right\rceil.

Proof.

Let
r\coloneqq\lceil m/2\rceil
and
S\coloneqq\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
. Since

\displaystyle\#\left(S\cap\{1,2,\ldots,r-1\}\right)\leq r-1,

one obtains:

\displaystyle\#\left\{1\leq i\leq n:x_{i}\geq r\right\}\geq\#\left(S\cap\{r,r+1,\ldots\}\right)\geq\#S-(r-1)\geq m-(r-1)=\left\lfloor\frac{m}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

∎

The following lemma is a standard consequences regarding tails. Proofs may also be found in references on regular variation.

Lemma 3
.

Let
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a probability sequence and
\rho>1
.
Suppose
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is regularly varying with tail index
\rho
.
Then there exists a slowly varying function
L:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
such that for any

, there exists
C_{s}>0
and
M_{s}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
M\in\mathbb{N}
, if
M\geq M_{s}
then:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M}^{+\infty}{p_{k}}^{s}\leq C_{s}M^{1-\rho s}L(M)^{s}.

Proof.

Pick any
s\in(1/\rho,1)
. By regular variation (
3
), there exists a slowly varying function
L:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if
k\geq k_{0}
then
p_{k}\leq 2k^{-\rho}L(k)
. By taking
\varepsilon\coloneqq(\rho-1/s)/2>0
and the Potter bound for slowly varying function
[
2
, Theorem 1.5.6]
, there exist
M_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
D_{\varepsilon}>0
such that for any

, if

then:

\displaystyle\frac{L(k)}{L(M)}\leq D_{\varepsilon}\left(\frac{k}{M}\right)^{\varepsilon}.

Define

. For any

then:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=M}^{+\infty}{p_{k}}^{s}\leq 2^{s}{D_{\varepsilon}}^{s}M^{-\varepsilon s}L(M)^{s}\sum_{k=M}^{+\infty}{k}^{-(\rho-\varepsilon)s}\leq\frac{2^{s+1}{D_{\varepsilon}}^{s}}{\beta-1}M^{1-\rho s}L(M)^{s}.

∎

(s_{K})_{K\in\mathbb{N}}

K_{t}^{*}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, if
K\geq K_{t}^{*}
then (
18
) is satisfied. Define, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle K_{n}\coloneqq\max{\left\{K_{t}^{*},\left\lfloor\sqrt{f(n)}\right\rfloor\right\}}.

Since
f
is non-decreasing and
\lim_{n\to+\infty}f(n)=+\infty
, the sequence
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing and unbounded. Define

\displaystyle n_{t}\coloneqq\min{\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:K_{t}^{*}\leq\sqrt{f(n)}\right\}}.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq n_{t}
then (
19
) is satisfied.
∎

Define the new-digit times set
\mathcal{T}
by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:f(n)=f(n-1)+1\right\},\quad

with the convention
f(0)\coloneqq 0
.
Define the forced-digit pools
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\{K_{n}+f(n)\},&n\in\mathcal{T},\\
\emptyset,&n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{T}.\end{cases}

Note that
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is pairwise disjoint.
By (
19
), one obtains that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq n_{t}
then:

(20)

\displaystyle\max{\mathcal{P}_{n}}\leq 2f(n),

with the convention
\max{\emptyset}=0
. Define the free alphabets
(\mathcal{A}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{n}\coloneqq\{1,2,\ldots,K_{n}\}.

Define the class of admissible sequences
(\mathcal{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}_{n},&n\in\mathcal{T},\\
\mathcal{A}_{n},&n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{T}.\end{cases}

Let
F_{f,t}
be the set of all real numbers in the unit interval whose digit sequence is a concatenation of digits from
(\mathcal{B}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
; that is,

\displaystyle F_{f,t}\coloneqq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\{x\in[0,1):d_{n}(x)\in\mathcal{B}_{n}\right\},

Proposition 7
.

For any
0<t<1
and admissible function
f
,
F_{f,t}\subset E_{f}
.

Proof.

Pick any
x\in F_{f,t}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
. One has the disjoint union:

\displaystyle\{1,\ldots,n\}=\left(\mathcal{T}\cap\{1,\ldots,n\}\right)\cup\left(\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\mathcal{T}\right).

Pick any
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\cap\mathcal{T}
. By the construction of
F_{f,t}
, the
j
-th digit of
x
is forced to be:

\displaystyle d_{j}(x)=b_{j}\coloneqq K_{j}+f(j)\in\mathcal{P}_{j}.

The value taken is genuinely new. Since
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing, for any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,j-1\}\setminus\mathcal{T}
,
b_{j}>K_{j}\geq K_{i}\geq d_{i}(x)
.
Since
(\mathcal{P}_{n})_{n\in\mathcal{T}}
is pairwise disjoint, one obtains that for any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,j-1\}\cap\mathcal{T}
,
b_{j}\neq b_{i}
.
Thus,

\displaystyle D_{n}(x)\geq\#\left(\mathcal{T}\cap\{1,\ldots,n\}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(f(i)-f(i-1)\right)=f(n).

On the other hand, for any
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}\setminus\mathcal{T}
, then the
i
-th digit of
x
is free to choose from:

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)\in\mathcal{A}_{i}=\{1,2,\ldots,K_{i}\}.

Since
(K_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-decreasing, one obtains:

\displaystyle\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{n}=\{1,\ldots,K_{n}\}.

Thus, the first
n
-th digits take values from
\{b_{i}:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\cap\mathcal{T}\}\cup\mathcal{A}_{n}
and:

\displaystyle D_{n}(x)\leq\#\{b_{i}:i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\cap\mathcal{T}\}+\#{\mathcal{A}_{n}}=\#\left(\mathcal{T}\cap\{1,\ldots,n\}\right)+\#{\mathcal{A}_{n}}=f(n)+K_{n}.

By combining the last inequalities of both previous paragraphs, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle f(n)\leq D_{n}(x)\leq f(n)+K_{n}.

By (
19
), one obtains that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq n_{t}
then:

\displaystyle 1\leq\frac{D_{n}(x)}{f(n)}\leq 1+\frac{K_{n}}{f(n)}\leq 1+\frac{1}{\sqrt{f(n)}}.

Since
f
is unbounded,
\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{n}(x)/f(n)=1
follows and
x\in E_{f}
.
∎

Define the coding space
\Omega
by:

\displaystyle\Omega\coloneqq\prod_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{B}_{n},

and the coding map
\Phi:\Omega\to[0,1)
by, for any
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\Phi\left((d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\right)\coloneqq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}(d_{1},d_{2},\ldots,d_{n}).

Define, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
s_{n}\coloneqq s_{K_{n}}
be the unique solution of (
17
) with
K=K_{n}
. Define, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a probability measure
\nu_{t,n}
on
\mathcal{B}_{n}
by:

\displaystyle\nu_{t,n}\coloneqq\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K_{n}}{p_{k}}^{s_{n}}\delta_{k},&n\in\mathbb{N}\setminus{\mathcal{T}},\\
\delta_{b_{n}},&n\in\mathcal{T},\end{cases}

where
\delta
denotes the Dirac probability measure, and
b_{n}\coloneqq K_{n}+f(n)
denotes the unique forced digit in
\mathcal{B}_{n}
. Define
\nu_{t}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\nu_{t,n}
and a Borel probability measure
\mu_{t}
supported on
F_{f,t}
by the push-forward:

\displaystyle\mu_{t}\coloneqq\Phi_{*}\nu_{t}.

Proposition 8
.

For any
0<t<1
, there exist
A_{t}>0
and
c_{t}>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in F_{f,t}}\frac{\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))}{\left({\operatorname{diam}{C_{n}(x)}}\right)^{t}}\leq{A_{t}}{e^{-c_{t}n}},

where for any
x\in[0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
C_{n}(x)
is that rank-
n
cylinder containing
x
; that is:

\displaystyle C_{n}(x)\coloneqq C\left(d_{1}(x),d_{2}(x),\ldots,d_{n}(x)\right)

Proof.

Define, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
U_{n}\coloneqq\{1\leq i\leq n:i\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\mathcal{T}\}
to be the set of free positions up to
n
, and
V_{n}\coloneqq\{1\leq i\leq n:i\in\mathcal{T}\}
to be the set of the forced positions up to
n
. Pick any
x\in F_{f,t}
.
By the construction of
\mu_{t}
, one obtains for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))=\prod_{i\in U_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{\,s_{i}},

\displaystyle\operatorname{diam}{C_{n}(x)}=\left(\prod_{i\in U_{n}}p_{d_{i}(x)}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in V_{n}}p_{d_{i}(x)}\right);

hence,

(21)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))}{\left(\operatorname{diam}{C_{n}(x)}\right)^{t}}=\left(\prod_{i\in U_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{s_{i}-t}\right)\left(\prod_{i\in V_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{-t}\right).

By (
18
), for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
s_{i}-t\geq(1-t)/2
. Since
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
i\in U_{n}
and
x\in F_{f,t}
,

\displaystyle{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{s_{i}-t}\leq{p_{1}}^{s_{i}-t}\leq{p_{1}}^{(1-t)/2}.

By multiplying over
i\in U_{n}
, one obtains:

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in U_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{s_{i}-t}\leq\prod_{i\in U_{n}}{p_{1}}^{(1-t)/2}={p_{1}}^{\,(1-t)\#U_{n}/2}=\exp{\left(\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{2}\#U_{n}\right)}.

Note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\#U_{n}=n-\#V_{n}=n-f(n)
and:

(22)

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in U_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{s_{i}-t}\leq\exp{\left(\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{2}(n-f(n))\right)}.

Note that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
i\in V_{n}
,
d_{i}(x)\in\mathcal{P}
is a forced digit. By (
20
) and
f
being non-decreasing, for any
n\geq n_{t}
and
i\in V_{n}
,

\displaystyle d_{i}(x)\leq 2f(i)\leq 2f(n).

Since
(p_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, one obtains:

\displaystyle-\log p_{d_{i}(x)}\leq-\log p_{2f(n)}.

By summing over
i\in V_{n}
and
\#V_{n}=f(n)
,

(23)

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in V_{n}}\log p_{d_{i}(x)}\leq-\#V_{n}\log p_{2f(n)}=-f(n)\log{p_{2f(n)}},

Pick any
\alpha>0
. By regular variation (
3
) and the Potter bound for the slowly varying function
[
2
, Theorem 1.5.6]
, there exists
K_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
k\geq K_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle p_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}k^{-\rho}L(k)\geq\frac{1}{2}k^{-(\rho+\alpha)};

hence, for any
k\geq K_{\alpha}
,

(24)

\displaystyle-\log p_{k}\leq(\rho+\alpha)\log k+\log 2.

Since
f
is non-decreasing and unbounded, there exists
N_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq N_{\alpha}
then
2f(n)\geq\max{\{K_{\alpha},2\}}
. By applying (
24
) with
k=2f(n)
, one obtains that for any
n\geq N_{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle-\log{p_{2f(n)}}\leq(\rho+\alpha)\log{(2f(n))}+\log 2\leq 2(\rho+\alpha)\log{f(n)}+\log 2.

From (
23
), one obtains that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq N_{\alpha}
then:

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in V_{n}}\log p_{d_{i}(x)}\leq f(n)\left(2(\rho+\alpha)\log{f(n)}+\log{2}\right).

By the admissibility of
f
, there exists
n_{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq n_{\alpha}
then:

\displaystyle f(n)\left(2(\rho+\alpha)\log{f(n)}+\log{2}\right)\leq-\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{3t}n.

By combining the two inequalities above, one obtains that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq\max{\{N_{\alpha},n_{\alpha}\}}
then:

\displaystyle-\sum_{i\in V_{n}}\log p_{d_{i}(x)}\leq-\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{3t}n;

therefore,

(25)

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in V_{n}}{p_{d_{i}(x)}}^{-t}=\exp{\left(-t\sum_{i\in V_{n}}\log p_{d_{i}(x)}\right)}\leq\exp\!\left(-\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{3}\,n\right).

By combining (
21
), (
22
), and (
25
), for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq\max{\{N_{\alpha},n_{\alpha}\}}
then:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))}{(\operatorname{diam}C_{n}(x))^{t}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp{\left(\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{2}(n-f(n))-\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{3}n\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp{\left((1-t)\log p_{1}\left(\frac{n}{6}-\frac{f(n)}{2}\right)\right)},

By the admissibility of
f
, there exists
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
n\geq n_{1}
then
f(n)\leq n/4
. Therefore, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in F_{f,t}
, if
n\geq n^{*}\coloneqq\max{\{N_{\alpha},n_{\alpha},n_{1}\}}
then:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))}{(\operatorname{diam}C_{n}(x))^{t}}\leq\exp{\left(\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{24}\,n\right)}.

The proof is completed by taking:

\displaystyle c_{t}\coloneqq-\frac{(1-t)\log p_{1}}{24}>0,

\displaystyle A_{t}\coloneqq\max{\left\{1,\max_{1\leq n\leq n^{*}}{\sup_{x\in F_{f,t}}{\frac{e^{c_{t}n}\mu_{t}(C_{n}(x))}{(\operatorname{diam}C_{n}(x))^{t}}}}\right\}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Since
E_{f}\subset[0,1)
,
\dim E_{f}\leq 1
; hence, it remains to prove
\dim E_{f}\geq 1
.

Pick any
0<t<1
. Define, for any interval
I\subset[0,1)
, the stopping time
\tau_{I}:I\to\mathbb{N}
by, for any
x\in I
:

\displaystyle\tau_{I}(x)\coloneqq\min{\{n\in\mathbb{N}:C_{n}(x)\subset I\}};

and a sequence of covers of pairwise disjoint maximal cylinders
(\mathcal{C}_{n}(I))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)\coloneqq\left\{C_{\tau_{I}(x)}(x)\in[0,1):x\in I\cap F_{f,t}\text{ and }\tau_{I}(x)=n\right\}.

Note that
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)
is a cover of
I\cap F_{f,t}
. By Proposition
8
, for any interval
I\subset[0,1)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(I)\leq\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)}\mu_{t}(C)\leq A_{t}\,(\operatorname{diam}{I})^{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\#\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)}{e^{c_{t}n}}.

By maximality, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
C\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)
,
C
can only arise as children at time
n
of the two rank
n-1
boundary cylinders determined by the endpoints of
I
. For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the
n
-digit alphabet in the construction has size most
K_{n}+1
; hence
\#\mathcal{C}_{n}(I)\leq 2(K_{n}+1)
and

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(I)\leq 2A_{t}\,(\operatorname{diam}{I})^{t}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{K_{n}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}.

By (
19
) and the admissibility of
f
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{K_{n}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{n_{t}-1}\frac{K_{n}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}+\sum_{n=n_{t}}^{+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{f(n)}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{n_{t}-1}\frac{K_{n}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}+\sum_{n=n_{t}}^{+\infty}\frac{\sqrt{n}+1}{e^{c_{t}n}}<+\infty.

By applying
[
6
, Mass distribution principle 4.2]
, one obtains that for any
t\in(0,1)
,
\dim F_{f,t}\geq t
.
By Proposition
7
and monotonicity of dimension, one obtains that
\dim E_{f}\geq 1
.
∎
