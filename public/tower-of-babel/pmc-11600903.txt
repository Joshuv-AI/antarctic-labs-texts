Background

Madagascar is a low-income country situated in the Indian Ocean. It is the largest island country in Africa and the world’s second-largest, after Indonesia [1]. The country has a population of 29,718,249 [2], a gross domestic product of Int$ 56.479 billion (US$ 16.063 billion), and a GDP per capita of Int$ 1,897.40 (US$ 539.635) [3].

On 20 March 2020, the first three cases of coronavirus disease (COVID-19) were confirmed in the capital city of Antananarivo in Madagascar [4]. As of 3 March 2023, Madagascar had conducted 525,461 COVID-19 tests, which is the equivalent of 18,009 tests per million population [5]. The tests revealed a total of 67,865 cases, which included 66,433 recovered cases, 10 active cases, and 1,422 deaths [5].

Madagascar received its first doses of the COVID-19 vaccine on 8 May 2021. As of 25 February 2023, the country had administered 2,675,087 vaccine doses [6]. Madagascar's total vaccine doses administered were 9.66 per 100 population, with 8.14 persons fully vaccinated with the last dose of the primary series and 0.46 persons boosted per 100 population. This is in comparison to the global averages of 169.72, 65.1, and 30.8, respectively [7].

The availability of COVID-19 tests, the number of administered vaccine doses, and promoting of good hygiene practices rely on the performance of a country's national health system (NHS), integrated disease surveillance system (IDSS), and other systems that address social determinants of health (SDH). In terms of the NHS, in 2019, Madagascar's universal health coverage (UHC) service coverage index (UHCSCI) was 35 out of 100, indicating that 65% of the population in need lacked access to essential health services. The UHCSCI had sub-indices of 55 for reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health (RMNCH), 27 for infectious diseases (IDs), 61 for noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), and 16 for service capacity and access (SCA) [8]. These component sub-indices suggest coverage deficits of 45% in RMNCH, 73% in IDs, 39% in NCDs, and 84% in SCA. Madagascar's UHCSCI was lower than Africa’s average of 46, as well as Africa’s sub-indices of 47 in IDs, 69 in NCDs, and 29 in SCA. However, Madagascar’s UHCSCI sub-index for RMNCH was equal to Africa’s average of 55.

Regarding IDSS, in 2020, Madagascar had an average International Health Regulations (IHR) core capacities score of 36 out of 100, which was lower than Africa’s average IHR score of 49 [9]. Concerning SDH, in 2020, only 21% of the population in Madagascar had access to safely managed drinking water services, and only 10% had access to safely managed sanitation services. Additionally, just 27% had access to a handwashing facility with soap and water [10]. This means that 79%, 90%, and 73% of the population did not have access to safely managed drinking water, sanitation services, and handwashing facilities, respectively. The lack of these essential services contributes to the spread of infectious diseases, such as COVID-19 [11, 12].

The existing gaps in the coverage of essential health services, IHR capacities, and safely managed water and sanitation services, may be attributed to inadequate health development spending. For example, in 2020, Madagascar's current health expenditure per capita was US$18, with 38% coming from out-of-pocket payments. This amount is four times lower than the recommended minimum of US$74 for low-income countries to have a chance of achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3, which focuses on ensuring healthy lives and promoting well-being for all people at all ages [13–15].

Rice and Cooper [16] stated that "the value of human life, expressed in terms of lifetime earnings, is a fundamental tool for economists, program planners, government administrators, and others who are interested in measuring the social benefits of investments in specific programs" (p.1954). Studies on the valuation of human lives lost due to COVID-19 have been conducted in various countries, including Brazil [17], Canada [18], China [19], France [20], Germany [21], India [22], Iran [23], Italy [24], Japan [25], Kenya [26], Mauritius [27], South Africa [28], Spain [29], Turkey [30], the United Kingdom (UK) [31], and the United States of America (USA) [32], to support health development advocacy. However, to date, no such studies have been conducted in Madagascar.

A study is needed to estimate the present value of human life losses caused by COVID-19 and to project potential indirect cost savings from COVID-19 vaccination in Madagascar. This economic evidence is crucial for advocating increased domestic and international investments in the NHS, IDSS, and SDH to address existing gaps in IHR, UHC, and water and sanitation. This will help mitigate health risks and productivity losses associated with public health emergencies. The study discussed in this paper aimed to address the current knowledge gap.

Analytical framework

Model for estimating the total present value of human life losses from COVID-19

There are three alternative methods for estimating the monetary value of a statistical (unknown) life. The first method is the human capital method (HCM), which values unknown human life lost in terms of potential total future earnings foregone by society due to premature mortality from any cause, such as COVID-19. According to Mooney [33], HCM values a person based on their productive output, which is equated with their earnings, discounted through time, and adjusted to allow for expected participation in the labour force (p.65).

The main advantages of HCM include ease of application, allowing valuation of relevant market output, enabling comparisons across categories (e.g., gender, age) of people, and availability of objective hard data, such as GDP per capita, number of deaths by cause and age, and average life expectancy. However, the standard HCM values the lives of pensioners, homemakers (e.g., housewives), and the severely handicapped at zero, ignores individual preferences, and overlooks the fact that the provision of health care has other objectives apart from enabling the sick to return to productive employment [33, 34]. For example, apart from the impact of health on labour productivity, good health is also valued for its own sake and enables people to enjoy leisure [33].

Second, the implied-values method (IVM) investigates implicit values placed on saving lives and preventing illness through past public sector decisions. It reveals the political preferences for reducing morbidity and mortality from various public sector actions [34–36]. However, the IVM has some methodological issues, including decisions to invest in specific projects not being based on popular vote, arbitrary and erratic valuations of political outcomes, and non-homogeneity in the lives saved and morbidity prevented [35, 36].

Third, the contingent valuation method (CVM) aims to determine the maximum amount of money each person affected by a public health program would be willing to pay to reduce their risk of morbidity or death from a specific cause, such as COVID-19 [34–36]. For instance, Zhou et al. [37] used CVM to assess the willingness-to-pay (WTP) of 543 participants in China for the COVID-19 vaccine booster dose. Similarly, Cerda and García [38] conducted a double-bounded dichotomous choice questionnaire survey among 531 participants to estimate the WTP for a COVID-19 vaccine in Chile. Additionally, Ayifah and Ayifah [39] utilized CVM to assess the WTP for COVID-19 testing and vaccines in Ghana and South Africa.

Advantages of the CVM include its ability to value the reduction in the probability of statistical death or morbidity, capture consumer preferences, consider various benefit categories, and allow the valuation of non-economic aspects of intervention [33–36]. However, the method is criticized for assuming that individual preferences are static, being inequitable, having strategic bias among respondents, and assuming consumer sovereignty applies to healthcare consumers [33–36].

In our current study, we decided to use the HCM because it has been successfully applied in several other countries to estimate the value of lives lost due to COVID-19 [17–32]. We also chose it because there is relevant data available on GDP per capita, current health expenditure per capita, average life expectancy, and COVID-19 cases in international databases.

When someone dies prematurely from COVID-19 in Madagascar, the society experiences a loss in years of life (YLL) equivalent to the gap between the average life expectancy and the age at death [40]. In our study, we used Madagascar's national average life expectancy at birth as the potential life limit. Therefore, YLL is calculated as Madagascar's average life expectancy at birth minus the age at death for a particular age group.

Weisbrod [41], the World Health Organization (WHO) [42], and Chisholm et al. [43] explain that the value of YLL (years of life lost) is determined by the discounted expected market earnings forgone by society due to premature death, after deducting claims on future healthcare consumption. Therefore, the value of each YLL lost is translated into monetary terms using Madagascar’s GDP per capita minus current health expenditure per person. Our study replicates the HCM (human capital method) used to assess the value of human life lost due to COVID-19 in the WHO Regions of Africa [26, 27], Americas [17, 18, 32], Eastern Mediterranean [23], Europe [20, 21, 24, 29–31], South-East Asia [22], and Western Pacific [25].

Second, utilizing a discount rate of 10% [17–32], while holding Madagascar’s average life expectancy and reported COVID-19 deaths constant. Third, applying the highest average life expectancy in the WHO African Region of 78.76 years (Algeria females life expectancy) [44], while holding the reported number of COVID-19 deaths and discount rate 3% constant. Fourth, applying the world’s highest mean life expectancy of 88.17 years (for Hong Kong Females) [44], while holding constant the reported number of COVID-19 deaths and a discount rate of 3%. Fifth, using the projected excess COVID-19 mortality of 11,419, instead of the reported mortality of 1,422, holding other variables constant. The excess COVID-19 mortality for Madagascar was projected using a ratio estimated by Msemburi et al. [45] to be 8.03 for Africa. Since as of 3 March 2023, the reported mortality was 1422, the projected excess mortality in 2023 equals 11,418.66, i.e., 1422 × 8.03.

Discussion

Major findings

Our study aims to fill in the existing knowledge gaps regarding the following: the total present value of human life losses associated with COVID-19, the total productivity losses associated with COVID-19, and the potential total productivity losses prevented due to COVID-19 vaccination at four target population coverage levels in Madagascar. The study made seven main findings. First, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR of the 1,422 human lives lost to COVID-19 was Int$ 46,331,412 (0.082% of GDP), with an average value of Int$ 32,582 per human life lost. Second, around 76% (Int$ 35,166,033) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR was borne by five regions: Analamanga, Haute Matsiatra, Boeny, Vakinankaratra, and Diana.

Third, when the HCM was recalculated using discount rates of 5% and 10%, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR decreased by approximately 23% and 53% respectively. Fourth, when the HCM was re-run with life expectancies of 78.76 years and 88.17 years, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR increased by 23.7% and 39.5% respectively.

Fifth, a re-run of the HCM with projected excess COVID-19 mortality of 11,419 increased the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR by 703%. Sixth, the 1,337 COVID-19 deaths among individuals aged 15 years and above resulted in a total productivity loss of Int$ 36,892,713, with an average total productivity loss per death of Int$ 27,600.3. Seventh, if 70% of the population aged 15 years and above were vaccinated against COVID-19, it could potentially avert 39,706 deaths, leading to a total indirect cost saving of Int$ 1.1 billion (i.e., 1.94% of GDP).

Implications of the findings

The average monetary value for each person who dies from COVID-19 is significant, approximately 17 times the GDP per capita of Madagascar. A COVID-19 death among individuals aged 15 years and older, who are in the workforce, has a high cost for Madagascar (about 14 times the GDP per capita). Providing universal coverage of the target population with the COVID-19 vaccine is likely to prevent thousands of premature deaths and lead to significant savings in economic productivity losses (or indirect costs).

The policymakers in Madagascar could use this information to strengthen their evidence-based advocacy with the Minister of Finance and development partners. This could help them secure more domestic and external resources to strengthen the national health system's capability to handle future infectious disease emergencies [56–58]. More resources would help to fully develop and maintain the IHR core capacities across sectors to effectively prevent, detect, respond to, and recover from public health emergencies [59]. Additionally, increased resource allocations should be directed towards improving the performance of other systems that address broader social determinants of health, such as WASH [60, 61].

Besides being useful for policymakers' public health advocacy, the average monetary value of human life and average indirect cost estimates can also potentially be used to inform the parameters used in cost–benefit analysis models of COVID-19 intervention strategies. Furthermore, the methodology used in this study could be adapted for application in other low-income countries.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, in the current study, GDP per capita and current health expenditure per capita were used to calculate non-health GDP per capita. However, this approach overlooks inequities in income and wealth distribution, quality of life, air pollution (including climate change), and the depletion of natural resources due to economic production processes [71]. As a result, since these factors are typically not accounted for in GDP per capita calculations, our study may have underestimated the indirect costs, and therefore, potential savings from COVID-19 vaccination.

Second, in Eq. 5, the GDP per capita was used to calculate the present value of human lives lost (PVHL) and the productivity losses (PL). However, the GDP per capita is calculated using the total population, while the PL estimation only considers those above the age of 15, as the minimum working age is typically set at 15 years old. As a result, there might be an underestimation of productivity loss.

Third, in 2023 the CHEPC was calculated using the CAGR, which reflects a smoothed growth in CHEPC over the 17-year period from 2005 to 2021. The CAGR shows that the growth rate is constant because it calculates the growth rate based only on the initial (2005) and final (2021) years for which CHEPC data was available [50].

Fourth, according to the WHO [72], many countries still struggle with limited capacity in their civil registration and vital statistics systems, which affects their ability to provide accurate, complete, and timely data on births, deaths, and causes of death. Msemburi et al. [45] estimated that the reported number of COVID-19 deaths in Africa may have been underestimated by 8.03 times. The sensitivity analysis demonstrated that this underestimation of COVID-19 deaths may have led to a significant underestimation of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${TPVHL}_{MADAGASCAR}$$\end{document}TPVHLMADAGASCAR

Fifth, in this study the HCM was used to monetarily value human life losses linked to COVID-19. However, the HCM approach has weaknesses. It does not account for the fact that healthcare objectives are not solely focused on enhancing economic productivity (GDP). People place value on preventing deaths. The HCM assesses livelihood rather than health itself, it overlooks the preferences of potential beneficiaries, and it discriminates against retired individuals, the unemployed, children, and full-time homemakers [34–36]. In the current study, to mitigate the bias against these groups, all years of life lost to COVID-19 were valued using the same non-health GDP per capita. However, the productivity loss (indirect cost) model only included people aged 15 years and above, i.e., those legally eligible to work.

Sixth, the current study relied on data from a case–control vaccine effectiveness study conducted in England [54] to assess the risk of death among vaccinated and unvaccinated individuals, as there was a lack of relevant data in Madagascar. It's important to note that the differences in epidemiological and socio-economic contexts between England and Madagascar could potentially impact the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines. This may lead to varying COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness due to the differing contexts. Since there is limited data available on the impact of COVID-19 vaccines on mortality in Madagascar, it's challenging to accurately determine how vaccine effectiveness may differ due to these varying contexts.

Finally, it is important to note that the costs associated with COVID-19 in Madagascar may be even higher than what we have calculated. This is because we did not have relevant data for certain expenses. Here are four examples of costs that we did not include: 1. Direct costs for activities like disinfecting, enforcing lockdowns, contact tracing, diagnosis, quarantine, and transportation, as well as the management and treatment of COVID-19, WASH facilities, and community resources [73–76]. 2. Intangible costs related to the physical and psychological pain caused by stigma [77]. 3. Losses in the quality of life for people who were not infected with COVID-19 and those with different levels of the disease [78]. 4. The impact on people's life expectancy and health-related quality of life due to difficulties accessing diagnostics and treatments for other health issues, like infectious diseases and non-communicable diseases, because of fear of getting COVID-19 in health facilities that are already dealing with a high number of COVID cases [79–81].

The value placed on each human life lost to COVID-19 is Int$32,582, which is approximately 17 times Madagascar's GDP per capita. The reported deaths from COVID-19 led to total indirect costs (or productivity losses) of Int$36,892,713 and an average of Int$27,600 per person.

By 3rd March 2023, Madagascar had vaccinated 8.266% of its target population with COVID-19 vaccines. This vaccination effort may have prevented a productivity loss of Int$129,410,049. If the country can achieve the global target of vaccinating 70% of the target population, it has the potential to save Int$1,095,899,276, which is 1.94% of GDP. Therefore, expanding COVID-19 vaccination coverage is likely to significantly reduce productivity loss in Madagascar.

The negative health and economic impact of COVID-19 underscores the need for increased investments to address gaps in the coverage of essential health services and other services that target health risks associated with various determinants of health, especially environmental factors. In addition to the economic rationale for investing in COVID-19 prevention and management, the primary justification is the adherence to the United Nations Declaration of Human Rights, which guarantees the rights to life, health, medical services, education, food, shelter, water, and sanitation [91].

The findings of our study are intended for evidence-based advocacy to raise awareness among both the public and policymakers about the economic productivity impact of COVID-19. Our study only presents the potential benefits of eradicating COVID-19 and does not assess the costs and consequences of alternative public health interventions. Therefore, it should not be used as a guide for decision-making, and the results should not be interpreted as such. Further economic research is needed to understand the costs and consequences of alternative intervention options to ensure universal access to WASH services and NHS services, including COVID-19 prevention, diagnosis, management, and rehabilitation, to aid decision-making.
