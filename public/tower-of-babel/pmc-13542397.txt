Auditory gamma‐band synchronization and attentional performance in juvenile myoclonic epilepsy

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCognitive difficulties in juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) are part of the clinical phenotype alongside epileptic seizures; however, the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. Auditory steady‐state responses (ASSRs) to repetitive 40‐ and 80‐Hz stimuli are widely used measures of gamma‐band oscillations (GBOs) relevant to cognition, but have not yet been investigated in JME. To address this gap, we examined 40‐ and 80‐Hz ASSRs using magnetoencephalography and their relationship with cognitive function in JME.MethodsIn this cross‐sectional case–control study, we recorded ASSRs in 16 patients with JME and 16 age‐ and sex‐matched healthy controls (HC). Evoked power and phase‐locking factor (PLF) were quantified from magnetic resonance imaging‐based source estimates in the bilateral primary auditory cortices and compared between groups using linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs). Cognitive function was assessed with the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS). Where applicable, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.ResultsLMMs showed lower evoked power and PLF in JME than in HC (both group effects p < 0.001). INTRODUCTION

Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME) is a common idiopathic generalized epilepsy syndrome, accounting for 5–10% of patients with epilepsy worldwide. 1 Patients are typically diagnosed during adolescence, presenting with myoclonic seizures or myoclonic–tonic–clonic seizures as initial manifestations. Although 65–92% of patients are responsive to antiseizure medications (ASMs) and many achieve good seizure control, a high quality of life (QoL) is not necessarily guaranteed. For example, some patients have uncontrolled seizures, whereas others face the burden associated with ongoing treatment, including adverse effects of ASMs and psychosocial stress. 2

In addition, cognitive difficulties have been identified as another factor affecting QoL in patients with JME. Since Janz and Christian's classic description of “impulsive petit mal”, JME has been discussed not only as a seizure disorder but also in the context of behavioral and neuropsychological features, including emotional instability, impulsivity, and reduced perseverance in goal‐directed activities. 3 The clinical importance of this broader perspective is underscored by long‐term studies reporting psychosocial difficulties in a substantial proportion of patients with JME, including depression, unemployment, and social isolation. 4 , 5 Importantly, these features may not simply represent secondary psychosocial consequences but may reflect structural and functional alterations in fronto‐thalamo‐cortical circuitry. 6 Previous studies have further suggested that cognitive difficulties may predate seizure onset and can also be observed in unaffected siblings. 7 , 8 Together, these findings suggest that the characteristic neurocognitive profile of JME is largely independent of seizure expression and may constitute an endophenotype reflecting the disorder's neurobiological substrates. However, the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying these cognitive difficulties remain poorly understood despite their clinical importance.

Efficient cognitive processing is supported by the precise temporal organization of neural oscillations. 9 Among these rhythms, gamma‐band (30–100 Hz) oscillations (GBOs) are considered to play a particularly important role in cognition. GBOs emerge from the balance between excitation and inhibition (E/I balance) in cortical microcircuits, particularly interactions between pyramidal cells and fast‐spiking interneurons, and are widely regarded as a physiological marker of local circuit function. 10 , 11 The gamma‐band auditory steady‐state response (ASSR), elicited by repetitive auditory stimulation at 40 or 80 Hz, serves as a reliable probe of GBOs. Assessed using electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG), gamma‐band ASSRs may be quantified with measures including evoked power and phase‐locking factor (PLF), which capture the strength and synchrony of auditory‐evoked cortical GBOs. 12 In psychiatric disorders, 13 , 14 , 15 decreases in evoked power and PLF have repeatedly been reported and provide important insights into the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying cognition‐related symptomatology, such as auditory hallucinations, working memory deficits, and attentional dyscontrol. 16 , 17 , 18

Collectively, these findings have motivated investigations of gamma‐band ASSRs in JME to elucidate the relationship between GBO dynamics and its characteristic neurocognitive profile. However, evidence on GBOs in JME remains limited, 19 and gamma‐band ASSRs have not been systematically examined. To address this knowledge gap, we used MEG with source modeling constrained by individual structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to characterize 40‐ and 80‐Hz ASSRs in patients with JME and matched healthy controls (HC). This approach affords high spatiotemporal precision and is well suited to the cortical source localization of ASSR signals. 20 We quantified evoked power and PLF at each stimulus frequency for both groups and examined their associations with neuropsychological performance within the JME group.

METHODS

Study design and setting

The present study was a cross‐sectional case–control study conducted from September 2020 to February 2024, to examine 40‐ and 80‐Hz ASSRs in the HC and JME groups. All assessments were conducted at Kyushu University Hospital (Fukuoka, Japan) following a standardized procedure across both groups. The sample size was determined by feasibility: we aimed to enroll all eligible patients with JME during the study period and to recruit an equal number of HC. The reporting of this study is in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. 21

Participants

We recruited 16 HC from the local community in the Fukuoka metropolitan area and 16 patients with JME from the outpatient clinics of Kyushu University Hospital. The HC and JME groups were matched for age and sex (HC: mean age 31.81 ± 8.82 years, range 21–47 years, 14 females; JME: mean age 33.25 ± 10.43 years, range 22–54 years, 14 females). Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) younger than 20 years or older than 60 years; (2) left‐handedness; (3) history of major psychiatric disorders, including psychotic disorders and severe mood disorders; (4) history of metabolic disorders; (5) history of substance abuse; (6) history of a major head injury; (7) verbal IQ lower than 75; or (8) hearing impairments that could compromise the perception of auditory stimuli. Medical histories relevant to these exclusion criteria were assessed through a review of available medical records and clinical interviews. In addition, HC with any history of epilepsy or other neurologic disorders were excluded, whereas patients were excluded if they had neurologic disorders other than epilepsy. The diagnosis of JME followed the International League Against Epilepsy diagnostic criteria 1 : (1) the onset of myoclonic seizures in late childhood or adolescence, with or without absence seizures or generalized tonic–clonic seizures; (2) interictal, bilaterally synchronous 3–5.5 Hz generalized polyspike–wave or spike–wave discharges with normal background EEG activity; (3) no apparent structural abnormalities on brain imaging; and (4) a normal developmental history. Diagnoses and key clinical features were confirmed by RM, a board‐certified psychiatrist and epileptologist, through a review of previous medical records. All patients continued their regular ASM regimen during the study, except for one who was not receiving medication at the time.

Cognitive function was assessed using the Japanese version of the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), 22 a brief cognitive battery previously used in epilepsy studies. 23 , 24 The five RBANS index scores (Immediate Memory, Visuospatial/Constructional, Language, Attention, and Delayed Memory) are summarized in Table 1 together with their task domains and corresponding subtests. The RBANS was administered by a trained neuropsychologist (TM) in a quiet room under standardized conditions on the same day as the MEG recording.

Auditory stimuli and MEG/MRI acquisition

The 40‐ and 80‐Hz chirp trains were used to elicit gamma‐band ASSRs. Each train lasted 0.5 s with a 0.6‐s interstimulus interval, and each frequency condition included 150 repetitions. Stimuli were presented binaurally via insert earphones at 80 dB sound pressure level. The 40‐ and 80‐Hz blocks were conducted in separate runs, and their order was randomized across participants to minimize potential order effects.

Neuromagnetic data were acquired using a 306‐channel whole‐head MEG system (Neuromag, Elekta) consisting of 102 identical sensor triplets, each with two planar gradiometers and one magnetometer. MEG data were sampled at 1 kHz with an online band‐pass filter of 0.01–330 Hz. Participants lay supine inside a sound‐attenuated and magnetically shielded room with their head supported by the MEG helmet and were instructed to remain still with their eyes gently closed. Participants were continuously monitored via video throughout the recording to assess movement and ensure safety. None of the patients had a seizure during the MEG sessions.

Structural T1‐weighted MRI scans were obtained from all participants within one year of the MEG session. Cortical surfaces and head shape were reconstructed from each participant's MRI using FreeSurfer 7.4.1 for MEG‐MRI coregistration and generation of a MEG forward model. Further details of auditory stimuli and data acquisition are provided in the Supporting Information.

Preprocessing and source‐level time–frequency analysis

To minimize analysis bias, preprocessing and source‐level time–frequency analyses were conducted with group labels masked. Only signals from planar gradiometers were analyzed due to their reduced susceptibility to distant magnetic interference. Raw MEG data were initially processed using MaxFilter software (version 2.2; Elekta) with temporal signal‐space separation and head‐movement compensation. 25 All subsequent preprocessing and analyses were performed with MNE‐Python. 26 MEG signals were filtered offline with a band‐pass filter of 1–100 Hz and notch filter of 60 Hz. Ocular and cardiac artifacts were removed using independent component analysis before epoching. Data were then segmented into epochs lasting 1.1 s, starting 0.4 s prior to stimulus onset (referred to as “−0.4 s”) and continuing for 0.7 s after stimulus onset. Epochs with peak‐to‐peak amplitudes exceeding 5000 fT/cm on any channel were rejected. Sensor‐level traces were visually inspected by RM; no epileptiform activity was detected in any participant across all epochs.

Regarding source estimation, forward solutions between MEG sensors and source locations were computed using each participant's single‐compartment boundary element model. 27 The individual source space was defined on each participant's reconstructed cortical surface and decimated to a mesh‐patterned 20 484 vertices. A noise covariance matrix was estimated from a 4‐min empty‐room MEG recording data acquired prior to each participant's session. Inverse solutions were computed with the minimum norm estimation, and noise‐normalized source activity was obtained using dynamic statistical parametric mapping. 28

Time–frequency analysis of source activity was performed at each vertex to calculate evoked power and PLF separately for the 40‐ and 80‐Hz conditions. Time–frequency representations at each vertex (−0.4 to 0.7 s; 1–100 Hz in 1‐Hz steps) were obtained by applying a Morlet wavelet transform to the averaged event‐related field and each epoch for calculation of evoked power and PLF, respectively. Evoked power, representing the spectro‐temporal power of stimulus phase‐locked activity, was computed as the squared magnitude of the complex representation of the averaged event‐related field. To compute PLF, the complex representations of each epoch were normalized to have a norm of 1 for each time–frequency bin. The PLF, quantifying the degree of phase synchronization with the stimulus input across epochs (ranging from 0 for random phase distribution to 1 for perfect synchronization), was then obtained as the norm of averaged normalized complex vectors across epochs.

The bilateral primary auditory cortices were designated as regions of interest (ROIs) based on consistent findings showing that these regions are the dominant cortical generators of the ASSR. 29 , 30 Specifically, the primary auditory cortex was defined as the anterior transverse temporal gyrus (G_temp_sup‐G_T_transv) in the Destrieux cortical atlas. 31 Regional time–frequency evoked power and PLF were obtained by averaging their values across vertices within the ROI in each hemisphere.

Statistical analyses

Age and RBANS indices were compared between groups using Welch's t‐tests after visual inspection of quantile–quantile (Q–Q) plots and formal assessment of normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. When a variable deviated from normality, a Mann–Whitney U test was additionally performed as a sensitivity analysis. RBANS analyses were conducted using available data (missing data: 2 HC and 1 patient with JME).

For statistical analyses, evoked power and PLF at each ROI were averaged over 0.2–0.5 s to focus on the steady‐state portion of the response, and within ±2 Hz of the stimulus frequency (40 or 80 Hz) to quantify the narrowband, stimulus‐locked component. 12 These values were then analyzed using separate linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) fitted with restricted maximum likelihood to test for group differences in each ASSR measure and to determine whether these differences depended on stimulus frequency or hemisphere. Prior to model fitting, scale‐appropriate transformations were applied to each outcome measure to reduce skewness and improve the distributional properties of the model residuals: evoked power, a positive continuous measure, was natural‐log transformed, whereas PLF, a bounded measure on the 0–1 scale, was logit‐transformed. Each LMM included group (HC or JME), frequency (40 or 80 Hz), and hemisphere (left or right) as fixed effects, together with all two‐way interactions and the three‐way interaction among these factors. Age and sex were also entered as covariates without interaction terms. To account for repeated measurements within individuals, participant was specified as a random intercept.

Fixed effects were evaluated using Type III tests, with denominator degrees of freedom estimated using the Kenward–Roger approximation. When a significant group‐related interaction (group‐by‐frequency, group‐by‐hemisphere, or group‐by‐frequency‐by‐hemisphere) was detected, follow‐up simple‐effects analyses were conducted using model‐based estimated marginal means and pairwise contrasts to examine group differences at the relevant levels of the interacting factor or factors.

Within the JME group, associations between ASSR measures and the five RBANS index scores were examined as secondary analyses. For these analyses, participant‐level transformed ASSR values were derived according to the significant group‐related effects identified in the LMMs. When a main effect of group was significant, values were averaged over stimulus frequency and hemisphere; when a group‐related interaction was significant, values corresponding to the relevant simple‐effect contrast were used. Separate linear regression models were fitted for each ASSR measure and each RBANS index score, with the RBANS index score entered as the dependent variable, the ASSR value as the predictor, and age and sex as covariates. All p values subject to multiple testing were adjusted within each relevant family of tests using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure, including comparisons across the five RBANS indices, follow‐up LMM contrasts when applicable, and association analyses across the five RBANS index scores separately for each ASSR measure.

Associations between gamma‐band ASSR measures and cognitive performance in patients with JME

Based on the LMM results, associations with the five RBANS index scores were assessed in the JME group using participant‐level ASSR measures averaged over stimulus frequency and hemisphere. Age‐ and sex‐adjusted linear regression analyses indicated that the Attention Index was positively associated with both evoked power and PLF (evoked power: B = 16.65; 95% CI, 5.81 to 27.49; FDR‐adjusted p = 0.03; PLF: B = 38.82; 95% CI, 13.82 to 63.83; FDR‐adjusted p = 0.03; Figure 3). No significant associations were observed for the other four RBANS indices (detailed statistics are shown in the Supporting Information).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, the present study provides the first characterization of gamma‐band ASSRs in JME and yielded two principal findings. First, patients with JME showed lower evoked power and PLF than matched HC. Second, among the five RBANS indices in patients with JME, only the Attention Index showed positive associations with ASSR measures. These findings offer initial evidence linking gamma‐band auditory synchronization to attentional performance in JME and may help refine our understanding of the disorder's pathophysiology in terms of neural oscillatory dynamics.

Gamma‐band ASSR deficits in patients with JME

The LMM analyses of evoked power and PLF revealed that gamma‐band ASSRs were overall reduced in patients with JME relative to HC, providing initial evidence for impaired auditory‐driven gamma synchronization in this disorder. Furthermore, the absence of significant group‐related interactions with stimulus frequency or hemisphere for both measures suggests that these reductions were not restricted to a specific stimulus frequency or hemisphere. At the same time, our results provided no clear evidence that the typical right‐hemisphere dominance of gamma‐band ASSRs was disrupted in patients with JME relative to HC. 32 This pattern contrasts with the disruption of hemispheric asymmetry reported in lesional epilepsy, such as mesial temporal lobe epilepsy. 33 Previous neuroimaging studies have suggested that JME is characterized not by uniformly diffuse dysfunction, as might be expected from its classification as a “generalized” epilepsy, but rather by spatially selective abnormalities within specific brain networks. 34 , 35 In this context, the absence of a clear disruption of right‐hemisphere dominance in JME may indicate that network abnormalities in this disorder, unlike those observed in lesional epilepsy, are not sufficiently lateralized to markedly alter typical hemispheric asymmetry.

Pathophysiologically, the gamma‐band ASSR deficits observed in this study may reflect disruptions in the E/I balance within cerebral networks in JME. Numerous neurophysiological and neuroimaging studies on JME reported increased neuronal excitability and changes in inhibitory function across cortical and subcortical regions, suggesting that the disruption of the E/I balance is a characteristic feature of the disorder. 36 , 37 , 38 Such abnormalities in the E/I balance are considered to arise from multiple factors, including mutations in genes encoding gamma‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor subunits and calcium channels, as well as subtle abnormalities in neuronal migration and circuit formation involving cortical interneurons. 39 , 40

Among these factors, given that the ASSR critically relies on connectivity between the thalamus and auditory cortex, abnormalities in thalamocortical circuits may be the most directly related to the present results. These circuits, which consist of recurrent connections between the thalamus, thalamic reticular nucleus, and cerebral cortex, provide a key substrate for bidirectional information transfer and synchronization between the thalamus and distributed cortical networks. 41 In JME, structural and functional abnormalities within thalamocortical circuits, including reduced thalamic volume and changes in thalamocortical connectivity, have repeatedly been reported and are regarded as a major pathophysiological basis of the disorder. 35 , 39 These circuits regulate the timing of the local E/I balance in cortical microcircuits through rhythmic thalamic excitation and coordinated GABAergic inhibition from the thalamic reticular nucleus and cortical interneurons, thereby supporting the generation and stabilization of cortical rhythms, including GBOs. 42 Therefore, thalamocortical dysfunction may compromise the temporal coordination of the E/I balance, which, in turn, may manifest as impaired GBO synchronization with auditory input.

Associations between attentional performance and gamma‐band ASSRs in JME

Among the cognitive domains examined in JME, attention has been one of the more frequently implicated domains in previous neuropsychological studies. 43 , 44 In the present study, only the Attention Index showed a significant between‐group difference among the five RBANS indices, with lower scores in the JME group. However, the mean Attention Index score was within the average range in the JME group, whereas the HC group showed relatively high performance. Therefore, this difference alone should not be considered evidence of attentional impairment in the JME group but may be better understood as a relative between‐group difference in attentional performance.

Within the JME group, the Attention Index was also the only RBANS index that showed significant positive associations with the ASSR measures. Notably, these associations were observed in the same direction for both evoked power and PLF. Evoked power and PLF differ in their analytical properties and capture different aspects of stimulus‐locked gamma responses: phase‐locked oscillatory strength and inter‐trial phase consistency, respectively. Taken together, the consistency of these associations across both ASSR measures suggests that the relationship between attentional performance and gamma‐band synchronization in JME may represent a consistent pattern that extends beyond measure‐specific characteristics.

These associations are in line with the view that attention is one of the cognitive domains most consistently associated with GBOs. 45 Beyond the functional role of GBOs in cognitive processing, the observed relationship may also be considered from a network perspective, particularly in relation to thalamocortical circuit dysfunction in JME. Within these circuits, the thalamus serves as a central node for the real‐time control of selective attention by filtering goal‐relevant sensory input and coordinating large‐scale cortical networks that support attentional control. 46 As discussed in Section 4.1, these thalamocortical circuits contribute to the generation and stabilization of GBOs. The present results are not sufficient to clarify the causal relationships among GBOs, thalamocortical circuits, and attentional performance in JME; however, they suggest that gamma‐band ASSRs offer a useful approach for investigating pathophysiological mechanisms associated with potential vulnerability in attentional functioning in this disorder.

LIMITATIONS

The present results need to be interpreted in light of several limitations. The modest sample size and female predominance in our cohort may have reduced statistical power and limited the generalizability of our results. Sex differences have been reported in brain structures and functions, including those relevant to neural oscillatory activity. 47 Thus, although sex was included as a covariate in the statistical models, sex‐related effects on ASSR measures cannot be fully excluded. To investigate whether gamma‐band ASSR abnormalities are broadly characteristic of JME, future studies need to recruit larger, sex‐balanced cohorts.

Furthermore, we confined the MEG source analysis to the primary auditory cortex in the present study. This approach was selected because MEG has inherently limited sensitivity to subcortical structures due to signal attenuation and depth constraints. 48 Consequently, we cannot make firm conclusions about subcortical contributions, including thalamocortical circuits, to ASSR abnormalities in JME. To more directly evaluate thalamocortical involvement in gamma‐band ASSR abnormalities, it will be useful to employ multimodal imaging techniques (e.g., EEG‐functional MRI) that assess cortical and subcortical activities in parallel.

Moreover, most patients were taking ASMs of different types and doses; therefore, the effects of medication on the present results cannot be fully ruled out. ASMs exert their effects mainly by modulating neurotransmitter systems, including GABAergic enhancement and glutamatergic inhibition, and these pharmacological actions may affect cortical gamma activity. 49 Studies involving ASM‐naive patients or samples with more homogeneous medication profiles will help to clarify the intrinsic neural oscillatory changes associated with JME.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

In patients with JME, MEG recordings allowed us to detect gamma‐band ASSR deficits and to identify a consistent association between cortical gamma synchronization and attentional performance across both power‐ and phase‐based measures. These exploratory findings are consistent with pathological network alterations in JME, potentially involving thalamocortical dysfunction, and provide preliminary evidence for the role of altered GBOs in its pathophysiology. Further studies addressing the limitations of the present study are needed to validate these findings and establish their clinical relevance.
