Investigation of the operational and combustion performance of in-service gas cooktops fueled by hydrogen-enriched natural gas
Authors: Mingyuan Yuan, Xiaomei Huang, Yitong Xie, Mengxiao Sun, Mingxiao Tang, Haidong Xu, Jinzhi Wang, Fengwen Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInjecting hydrogen into natural gas pipelines is regarded as a cost-effective and practical pathway toward a low-carbon energy future. As the most widely used domestic gas appliance, the operational performance and safety of cooktops are essential. In this study, over 500 in-service cooktops with different service lives are tested under hydrogen ratios ranging from 0 to 25%vol to investigate the effects of hydrogen addition on gas flow rate, heat load, flame structure, ignition performance, and flame stability. The influence of service life on heat load, ignition performance, and flame stability is also evaluated. The results show that with increasing hydrogen ratio, the gas flow rate increases while the heat load decreases, and the additional heat load reduction exhibits a strong correlation with service life. Ignition instability and flashback are mainly observed in long-service-life burners with structural deformation or improper air shutter adjustment. No flashback occurs below 15% hydrogen, whereas limited flashback appears at 20–25%vol. In contrast, yellow flame and lifting are mitigated with hydrogen addition. Finally, practical approaches for compensating heat load variation and mitigating unstable combustion are proposed, providing technical support for hydrogen injection into natural gas pipeline projects.Keywords: Hydrogen, Natural gas, In-service cooktop burner, Flame stabilitySubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

The continuous increase in greenhouse gas emissions, such as carbon dioxide and methane, can be mitigated by adopting renewable and low-carbon energy sources1–3, with a global goal to achieve net-zero emissions by 20504. However, the large-scale use of renewable energy such as wind and solar is limited by inherent intermittency and fluctuating generation5. In contrast, hydrogen has emerged as a promising solution due to its carbon–neutral characteristics and ease of storage6. Many countries have carried out research and development on hydrogen and regard it as a part of national energy strategies. According to the Hydrogen Council, hydrogen will contribute ~ 20% of global carbon dioxide reduction by 20407 and account for more than 15% of total energy consumption by 20508.

The hydrogen industry chain includes production, transportation, and utilization. Renewable-power hydrogen, generated using wind9, solar4, and nuclear energy8, represents the ultimate goal of hydrogen production development. Hydrogen can be transported by high-pressure tankers, hydrogen pipelines, or by blending it into existing natural gas pipelines10,11. Considering the limited transmission capacity and distance range of tankers12 and the high initial investment required for hydrogen pipelines13, injecting hydrogen into natural gas pipelines is regarded as the most cost-effective and practical method14, which can also enhance the resilience and flexibility of both gas and electricity systems15.

The strong differences between hydrogen and natural gas properties, such as flame speed, relative density, and heating value, make it challenging to determine how much hydrogen can be added into natural gas pipelines without increasing transmission and utilization risks. For utilization, it must be ensured that end-use appliances can operate safely without incident and maintain their performance within an acceptable range after hydrogen addition. For domestic gas appliances such as cooktops and water heaters, operational performance mainly focuses on heat load. Because the heating value of hydrogen is much lower than that of natural gas, hydrogen addition will reduce heat load, increase operating time, and thereby affect operating performance. In addition, the flame speed of hydrogen is much higher than that of natural gas, which increases the tendency for flashback and can damage the burner16,17. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate the operational and combustion performance of gas appliances fueled by hydrogen-enriched natural gas.

Many countries have conducted pilot projects to evaluate the effects of hydrogen addition to natural gas pipelines on operational performance and safety risks, including the Netherlands18, Denmark19, Italy20, Canada21, and Australia22. The results show that domestic gas appliances can operate safely and stably at hydrogen ratios up to 20%vol. However, the number of gas appliances tested in these projects is limited, and most appliances were either newly installed or adjusted by professionals. In contrast, the service life of most in-service gas appliances is long, and many are of relatively low quality. After extended use, structural degradation and unstable operation are common. Therefore, extensive and targeted testing is essential for evaluating the operational performance and stability of in-service appliances.

Several studies have evaluated the performance of gas appliances fueled by hydrogen-natural gas blends, such as cooktop burners23, engines24, room furnaces25, ovens26, and industrial burners27. The results show that most burners can operate stably with hydrogen addition exceeding 25%vol. Research on cooktop burners mainly focuses on flame structure, heat load variation, emissions, thermal efficiency, ignition performance, and flame stability. The results indicate that with increasing hydrogen ratio, heat load and CO emissions decrease28, while noise, flashback, and lifting may occur for some burners29. However, combustion stability in a specific gas appliance is governed by multiple factors30, including structure design, on-site installation and customers’ adjustment and maintenance. Experiments conducted on a limited number of burners cannot represent the large-scale population of in-service gas appliances.

Previous research has mainly concentrated on evaluating the influence of adding hydrogen into natural gas on the gas appliance operational performances through experiments conducted on a limited number of new burners. However, in practical applications, gas appliances often have long service lives and may be of low quality or inadequately adjusted, which can significantly affect flame stability and determine how much hydrogen can be added to natural gas pipelines. Among various types of burners, cooktops are the most numerous, have the highest usage frequency, and are strongly affected by user operating habits.

Therefore, in this study, a large number of in-service cooktops are collected based on service life and tested under hydrogen ratios ranging from 0 to 25%vol. The effects of hydrogen addition on cooktop gas flow rate, heat load, flame structure, ignition performance, and flame stability are assessed. In addition, the influence of service life on heat load, ignition performance, and flame stability is evaluated. Finally, approaches for compensating heat load variation and mitigating unstable combustion are proposed, which provide technical support for hydrogen injection into natural gas pipeline projects.

Materials and methodology

Gas properties

The natural gas composition used in this study is dominated by methane (94.32% vol), with minor components including ethane (3.61% vol), propane (1.32% vol), n-butane (0.29% vol), iso-butane (0.35% vol), n-pentane (0.02% vol), iso-pentane (0.01% vol), and neo-pentane (0.003% vol). In this paper, hydrogen and natural gas ratios are expressed on a volumetric basis, and ‘%’ is used to denote ‘%vol’.

It is essential to compare the property differences of hydrogen and methane to evaluate the influence of adding hydrogen into natural gas on gas appliance performance. Both the heating value and density of hydrogen are lower than those of methane31,32, which means less heat release per unit flow rate and a lager flow rate for a constant pressure drop according to Bernoulli’s principle33. Thus, the Wobbe Index is used to evaluate the heat load variation under constant gas pressure, which is calculated as 34,35, where s is relative density of the gas and Hl is the lower heating value of the gas. The Wobbe Indexes of methane and hydrogen are very close which indicates that the heat load change on gas cooktops is quite small and acceptable, showing a positive aspect of blending hydrogen into natural gas pipelines. The flame speed of hydrogen is about five times higher than that of methane31, which can disturb the balance between flame speed and the velocity of unburned gas at the flame surface and increase the possibility of flashback.

General description of the experimental samples

The sampling area of this study is Weifang, a northern city in China. This city has a strong industrial foundation and a high natural gas penetration rate. Natural gas is transmitted and distributed through pipelines in this region. The demand for natural gas among local residents is quite high due to common cooking habits such as stir-frying and stewing, which can represent the natural gas usage patterns of most regions in China.

The samples are classified into two categories according to their service life, namely 0–8 years and over 8 years, and the collected cooktops encompass most major manufacturers and types. The samples from each burner type were selected using a simple random sampling method. In total, more than 500 samples were collected, covering the predominant in-service cooktop types, thereby providing a representative distribution of cooktop service life.

In this study, a total of 462 cooktops were selected and tested in the laboratory. Some cooktops with long service life were damaged during disassembly and transportation. Ultimately, 312 cooktops had two functional burners, 92 cooktops had only one functional burner, and 58 cooktops were completely damaged. In total, 716 burners were tested, of which 249 burners with clearly identified service life are summarized in Fig. 1. According to the Chinese standard, the scrapping age of domestic cooktops is 8 years, but only 50.2% of the collected burners meet this requirement. Many cooktops have exceeded their deemed service life, resulting in issues such as structural damage and ignition electrode failure. Some cooktops cannot operate stably under natural gas, which poses a significant safety risk under hydrogen-enriched natural gas. Therefore, it can be concluded that testing in-service cooktops before hydrogen injection into natural gas pipeline projects is essential.

Test rig

The test rig comprises two sub-systems: a continuous gas-blending sub-system and a cooktop performance testing sub-system, as shown in Fig. 2a. In the continuous gas-blending sub-system, mass flow controllers (MFCs) are installed downstream of both the hydrogen cylinder and the natural gas pipeline and are controlled via software to regulate the composition and total flow rate of the gas mixture.

Experimental setup and test procedure: (a) Schematic of continuous gas-blending and cooktop performance testing system; (b) Typical configurations of cooktop burners: (i) circular-port burner and (ii) slit-port burner; (c) Experimental procedures for (i) hydrogen limit test, (ii) flame stability test, and (iii) combustion characteristic test; (d) Typical unstable combustion phenomena observed during the experiments: (i) flashback and (ii) lifting.

The cooktop performance testing sub-system includes a U-tube manometer, a cooktop, and a pot. The U-tube manometer measures the pressure at the cooktop inlet to adjust the flow rate set in the MFCs. Typical cooktop configurations are shown in Fig. 2b, where Fig. 2b(i) and Fig. 2b(ii) show circular-port and slit-port burners, respectively. For these atmospheric burners, gas enters from the inlet and injects air at the injector. The amount of injected air can be adjusted by the air shutter. The gas-air mixture then flows into the inner and outer rings for combustion. The size of the tested pot is selected according to the Chinese standard (GB 16410 - 2020), with a diameter of 260–300 mm, and is filled with a specified amount of water.

Test procedure

To reflect the real operating conditions of in-service cooktops, no adjustments were made to the collected cooktops, including the air shutters. Hydrogen percentages of 0–25% were selected for the flame stability test for all burners, including flashback, lifting, yellow flame, and ignition performance. The experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 2c(i). Flame stability is first tested for each burner under a 25% hydrogen ratio. If no unstable combustion phenomenon occurs, the burner is considered hydrogen-adaptive. If instability occurs, the burner is then tested under natural gas to evaluate whether the instability is caused by the cooktop condition. If no unstable combustion phenomenon occurs under natural gas, the burner is subsequently tested at 20%, 15%, 10%, and 5% hydrogen ratios to determine the highest acceptable hydrogen percentage. In addition, 25 burners were selected for combustion characteristic tests at 0%, 10%, and 20% hydrogen ratios, including measurements of gas flow rate and heat load.

The experimental procedure for flame stability is shown in Fig. 2c(ii). The cooktop inlet pressure is stabilized at 2000 Pa by adjusting the flow rate in the MFCs. After recording the flow rate of mixture, the flame is visually observed to identify unstable combustion. The flame structure is captured by a camera. Then the cooktop is turned off and allowed to cool down. After that, ten ignition trials are conducted, and the number of successful ignitions and flame propagations is recorded. The unstable combustion phenomena include flashback, lifting, and yellow flame. Flashback is identified when the flame tilts at the burner port (Fig. 2d(i)), propagates into the burner, or is extinguished. Lifting is identified when more than half of the flame lifts off from the burner port (Fig. 2d(ii)) or is extinguished. A yellow flame is characterized by a distinct yellow or orange color and a large, unsteady, flickering shape.

The experimental procedure for combustion characteristics is shown in Fig. 2c(iii). The initial procedures are the same as those of the flame stability test. After the cooktop inlet pressure stabilizes at 2000 Pa, the corresponding flow rates of hydrogen and natural gas are recorded.

Flame structure

The flame height, defined as the height difference between the flame root and the flame tip, varies with different hydrogen ratios as shown in Fig. 4a. The variation in flame height under natural gas conditions is mainly related to differences in the mixture velocity at the burner ports, which are affected by factors such as heat load, number of ports, and port geometry. With increasing hydrogen ratio, the flame height decreases, with reductions of 12.44% and 23.06% under 10% and 20% hydrogen ratios, respectively. When the flame stabilizes at the burner port, it propagates inward along the inner cone surface, while the unburned gas-air mixture flows outward. At this interface, the flame speed equals the normal velocity of the mixture. When the primary air supplied is less than the stoichiometric requirement, partial combustion occurs in the inner cone, and intermediate combustion products flow outward, diffuse, and react with secondary air to form the outer cone. Therefore, the flame structure is jointly influenced by flame speed, primary air supply, and secondary air supply. Blending hydrogen into natural gas increases both the flame speed and the primary air coefficient, resulting in a lower flame height.

Effect of hydrogen blending on flame structure of cooktop burners: (a) Variation of flame height with hydrogen blending ratio; (b) Flame morphology at different hydrogen blending ratios under constant inlet pressure.

The variation of flame structure with different hydrogen ratios under constant cooktop inlet pressure is shown in Fig. 4b. Consistent with the results above, the flame height decreases with increasing hydrogen ratio. In addition, the distance between the flame and the burner port becomes shorter. The tested burner exhibits a yellow flame under natural gas conditions, and the yellow flame remains but shows some improvement at low hydrogen ratios. When the hydrogen ratio increases to 25%, the yellow flame is significantly reduced. It can be concluded that for burners with incomplete combustion, hydrogen addition can mitigate this issue.

Ignition performance

Under a hydrogen ratio of 25%, more than 30 burners among the 716 tested burners could not be ignited stably. Most of these burners also failed to ignite stably under natural gas conditions. The main reason is that aging or misalignment of the ignition electrode leads to delayed ignition, and after several unsuccessful attempts, the accumulated unburned gas reaches the lower flammability limit, which can trigger a deflagration during the next ignition attempt. In addition, structural deformation of the burner, such as damage or warping of the burner head, creates gaps between the burner head and the distribution plate, which disrupts the stability of the ignition process.

Burners that can ignite successfully under natural gas conditions but fail under a 25% hydrogen ratio total 11 units, accounting for 1.5%. Hydrogen addition increases the flame speed, which can cause flashback before the flame fully stabilizes at the burner port, leading the flame to propagate backward into the burner head. When the gas-air mixture inside the burner head falls within the flammability range, ignition leads to outward flame propagation through the burner port, accompanied by a loud noise, followed by flame extinction. It is observed that the service life of all 11 burners with unstable ignition exceeds 6 years. Therefore, it can be concluded that long-term use of cooktops affects burner structure and increases ignition instability, which requires greater consideration during the implementation of hydrogen injection into natural gas pipeline projects.

Flame stability

Most domestic cooktops adopt an injector-type atmospheric burner. Gas issuing from the nozzle entrains primary air into the injector, and the resulting gas-air mixture enters into the burner head for combustion. The air ratio in the mixture is lower than the theoretical requirement for complete combustion. A stable flame front near the burner port, known as the ‘ignition ring’ serves as the ignition source for the mixture and represents the interface between the unburned mixture and the combustion products. When the flame intensity increases, the gas flow rate of the mixture rises, which pushes the flame away from the burner port, causing the ignition ring to narrow and eventually disappear, leading to flame lifting. Conversely, when flame intensity decreases, the mixture velocity is reduced and the inner cone shortens and eventually retracts into the burner ports, resulting in flashback. When hydrogen is blended into natural gas, the increased flame speed shortens the flame and pushes it closer to the port, increasing the tendency for flashback. In addition, the hydrogen-enriched natural gas flame is closer to the burner wall, which enhances the preheating effect on the mixture and further increases the flame speed, thereby promoting flashback.

To evaluate the influence of hydrogen on flame stability, burners that exhibit flashback under natural gas conditions are not considered. No flashback occurs when the hydrogen ratio is below 15%. However, when the hydrogen ratio increases to 20% and 25%, four burners experience flashback, accounting for 0.6%. The information for these four burners is shown in Table 2. Burner #1 is a circular-port burner with a service life of 10 years. The burner structure is deformed, creating a gap between the burner head and the distribution plate. The larger effective port openings disrupt the balance between flame speed and mixture velocity, increasing the tendency for flashback. Burner #2 is also a circular-port burner, and the flame structure indicates improper adjustment of the inner-ring air shutter. The flame is short and hard, with almost no outer flame, indicating that the primary air coefficient is too high. Under such conditions, the flame speed becomes very high, increasing the flashback tendency when hydrogen is added. Burners #3 and #4 are both slit-port burners with relatively large port areas. The flame structure shows an increase in the effective single-port opening. When hydrogen is blended into natural gas, the higher flame speed can easily exceed the mixture velocity, leading to flashback.

In incomplete combustion, carbon particles are generated and become incandescent in the flame, resulting in a yellow flame. When hydrogen is blended into natural gas, the carbon concentration in the gas decreases. In addition, hydrogen has a lower stoichiometric air requirement than methane; therefore, under similar air entrainment conditions, the primary air coefficient increases. This leads to more complete combustion and suppression of yellow flame. Some collected cooktops exhibit yellow flame under natural gas conditions due to structural deformation or improper air shutter adjustment, and the yellow flame in these cooktops decreases with increasing hydrogen ratio. Therefore, yellow flame does not need to be considered as a limiting factor for stable operation when hydrogen is added to natural gas.

As discussed earlier, flame stability depends on the balance between flame speed and mixture velocity. When hydrogen is added, although the mixture flow rate increases, the significant rise in flame speed suppresses the lifting phenomenon. For cooktops that exhibit lifting under natural gas conditions, the tendency for lifting decreases with increasing hydrogen ratio. Therefore, lifting also does not need to be considered as a limiting factor.

In summary, flashback is the main factor affecting stable operation under hydrogen-enriched natural gas, and it is primarily influenced by cooktop structure and user habits such as air shutter adjustment. During the implementation of hydrogen injection into natural gas pipeline projects, greater attention needs to be paid to flashback. This issue can, to some extent, be predicted through burner structural inspection and test results under natural gas conditions.

Strategies for improvement

Heat load compensation

As discussed earlier, considering the combined effects of heating value and density, hydrogen addition leads to a reduction in heat load, which affects the thermal performance of cooktops and user experience. In addition, the reduced heat load increases cooktop operating time, thereby increasing the simultaneity factor of appliance usage in the gas network and influencing the hydraulic performance of gas transmission and distribution systems. Therefore, it is necessary to take measures to compensate for the reduced heat load of gas cooktops under hydrogen-enriched natural gas conditions.

According to Eq. (2), the heat load can be regulated by modifying the inlet pressure or through design adjustments such as changing the nozzle diameter to compensate for the effect of hydrogen addition. The influence of hydrogen ratio on heat load is shown in Fig. 5a. Taking a hydrogen ratio of 20% as an example, under the Original condition, the heat load under natural gas is Qini. The heat load decreases with increasing hydrogen ratio. Based on the theoretical calculation derived from the Wobbe Index under constant pressure conditions, the heat load reductions are 2.6% and 5.3% under 10% and 20% hydrogen ratios, respectively. If the operating condition is adjusted to Condition 1 by enlarging the nozzle size or increasing the inlet pressure, the cooktop can maintain the original heat load Qini at a 20% hydrogen ratio. However, under Condition 1, if the hydrogen ratio decreases, the heat load increases significantly, as shown in part 1 and part 3. For instance, the heat load under natural gas will increase by 5.6% compared with the Original condition, which will increase the tendency for flame lifting.

Influence of hydrogen blending on burner heat load and compensation strategies: (a) Variation of heat load with hydrogen blending ratio under different operating conditions; (b) Required adjustments of inlet pressure and nozzle diameter to compensate for heat load reduction.

In hydrogen-natural gas blended pipeline systems, fluctuations in the hydrogen content are inevitable. Therefore, a new approach is proposed to address this issue by adjusting the cooktop operating condition to Condition 2. Under a 10% hydrogen ratio, the cooktop maintains the original heat load Qini. When the hydrogen ratio varies from 0 to 20%, the maximum heat load variation is 2.7%, as shown in part 1 and part 2, which falls within the normal fluctuation range of gas heating values in natural gas networks. This method has minimal impact on the safe and stable operation of cooktops and is considered a reasonable and effective solution.

According to Eq. (2), the heat load is proportional to the Wobbe Index, the square of the nozzle diameter, and the square root of the inlet pressure. The required changes in nozzle diameter and inlet pressure to compensate for the Wobbe Index variation and maintain a constant heat load are shown in Fig. 5b. The change in nozzle diameter is small, increasing by 1.3% and 2.8% under 10% and 20% hydrogen ratios, respectively. In contrast, the required change in inlet pressure is much larger, increasing by 5.5% and 11.5% under 10% and 20% hydrogen ratios.

Residential buildings in China are equipped with regulators to control the gas inlet pressure of household gas appliances. In some countries, in addition to a building-level regulator, an individual regulator is also installed for each unit. Therefore, compensating for the reduction in Wobbe Index by adjusting the inlet pressure to maintain a constant heat load is highly feasible and serves as the primary method to avoid large-scale appliance replacement. However, when the operating pressure of the gas pipeline is increased, the safety of the transmission and distribution system must be verified to prevent significant leakage resulting from the pressure increase.

Flame stability improvement

As analyzed earlier, blending hydrogen into natural gas increases the flame speed, which raises the flashback tendency while mitigating lifting and yellow flame tendencies. Therefore, this section focuses on flashback analysis to reduce the risk of unstable combustion through design optimization and operating condition adjustments.

The analysis of flame stability is based on the critical boundary velocity gradient proposed in Ref.35. The variation of the stable operating region for a typical burner under 0% and 20% hydrogen ratios is shown in Fig. 6a. As hydrogen is added to natural gas, the flashback and lifting limits both increase, resulting in the division of the burner operating region into five distinct regions. In Part 1, the burner exhibits persistent flashback, while in Part 5 it experiences persistent lifting, so the burner cannot operate in these regions. Part 3 is the eternal stability region, where flame stability is maintained both before and after hydrogen addition. Part 4 is a newly formed stable region created by hydrogen addition, whereas Part 2 represents the reduced stable region. Thus, for burners with improper design, operating points near the flashback limit, improper initial adjustment during installation, or heat intensity deviations caused by structural deformation, the operating point will shift into Part 2 after hydrogen addition, leading to unstable combustion phenomena. This also confirms the earlier assertion that determining the acceptable hydrogen ratio based solely on a limited number of professionally adjusted gas appliances is not rigorous. To avoid these unstable combustion phenomena, burners operating in Part 2 need to be shifted into Part 3. There are two main approaches to achieve this: increasing the heat intensity or reducing the primary air coefficient.

Effect of hydrogen addition on flame stability and mitigation of flashback: (a) Variation of the stable operating region of a typical burner with hydrogen blending; (b) Adjustment procedure for mitigating flashback through operating condition optimization.

For in-service cooktops that cannot be replaced, the heat intensity can be increased by raising the inlet gas pressure, as shown in method ① in Fig. 6a. When adopting this method, the effects of inlet gas pressure on both flashback and lifting need to be considered. The other approach is to reduce the primary air supply by adjusting the air shutter, as shown in method ② in Fig. 6a. For this approach, only flashback needs to be considered, because increasing the equivalence ratio shifts the burner operating point away from the lifting limit. This method is validated by experiments that for a burner exhibiting flashback, the phenomenon is eliminated by reducing the primary air supply through air shutter adjustment, as shown in Fig. 6b. The equivalence ratio increases from 1.54 to 1.88. In addition, if the equivalence ratio increases sufficiently, the gas concentration in the gas-air mixture exceeds the upper flammability limit, and flashback will not occur regardless of heat intensity, thereby ensuring flame stability. However, excessive increases in the equivalence ratio may lead to incomplete combustion and increased pollutant emissions. Therefore, this strategy should be applied within an appropriate range.

For newly designed cooktops, the heat intensity can be increased by reducing the port size and enlarging the nozzle size. Reducing the port size increases heat intensity with minimal impact on other parameters, as indicated by method ① in Fig. 6a. Enlarging the nozzle size increases the cooktop heat load, thereby enhancing the heat intensity. This approach can improve flame stability while also compensating for the reduction in heat load. Reducing the primary air supply (as indicated by method ② in Fig. 6a) during the design stage can be achieved by modifying the injector structure for injector-type burners or by resetting the primary air amount for burners equipped with proportional valves. In addition, flame-stabilizing ports can be incorporated into the burner design. The port diameter can be determined based on the critical boundary velocity gradient theory. When the port diameter is smaller than the quenching distance, the cooling effect of the port wall prevents flashback regardless of flow velocity, thereby ensuring flame stability.

Conclusions

In this study, more than 500 in-service burners were tested under hydrogen ratios ranging from 0 to 25% to evaluate the influence of hydrogen addition on the operational and combustion performance of gas cooktops. The findings of this work provide valuable technical support for the large-scale implementation of hydrogen injection into natural gas pipelines. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

With increasing hydrogen ratio, the gas flow rate increases while the heat load decreases. The additional reduction in burner heat load shows an obvious correlation with service life, as long-term use affects burner structure and increases pressure loss, leading to greater deviation from the designed operating condition.

Flame stability in existing cooktops can be enhanced by increasing inlet pressure or the equivalence ratio, while design optimizations such as reducing burner port size, enlarging nozzle diameter, or incorporating flame-stabilizing ports are effective for newly designed cooktops.

It should be noted that this study has certain limitations. Although more than 500 in-service cooktops were assessed, some analyses were conducted using a limited subset of samples to ensure comparable burner characteristics. Further studies with larger datasets are needed to validate and generalize the findings.
