Comparative evaluation of reinforcing effect of three different intra-orifice barriers on pericervical dentin of endodontically treated teeth: An in vitro study
Authors: Keerthi Sri Thota, K. Lakshmi Bhavya, K. Sreeha, Sudhendra Deshpande, Jahnavi Javvadi, Arjampudi Avina
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aims:The purpose of this in vitro study was to evaluate and compare the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth using three different intra-orifice barriers.Settings and Design:This study was conducted at the department of conservative dentistry and endodontics, an in vitro study.Subjects and Methods:Forty freshly extracted single-rooted mandibular bicuspids (n = 40) were decoronated to obtain a 14-mm standard root length and all the canals were enlarged with ProTaper up to size F3 and they were obturated with gutta-percha using AH Plus sealer and randomly divided into 4 groups (n = 10). Group I: Control group obturated with gutta-percha only. Groups II, III, and IV had placement of intra-orifice barriers after the removal of 3-mm coronal gutta-percha such that Group II: Ever X posterior; Group III: Beautifil II; and Group IV: Tetric N-Ceram. Mounting of specimens was done in acrylic resin to expose coronal 3 mm and tested using the universal testing machine.Statistical Analysis Used:Data were analyzed for significance by ANOVA and Tukey’s post hoc test.Results:Ever X posterior group showed better fracture resistance and the control group is the least among the groups.Conclusions:Placement of intra-orifice barriers significantly improves the fracture resistance compared with the control group.

INTRODUCTION

Endodontically treated teeth are more prone to fracture than vital teeth due to excessive tooth structure loss, dentin dehydration, and pressure during obturation procedures. Previous clinical studies have shown that vertical root fractures are associated with 11%–13% of extracted teeth with endodontic treatment, making it the second most common identifiable reason for root-filled tooth loss.[12]

The success of root canal therapy is dependent on the tooth being restored with a definitive, permanent postendodontic restoration. A well-executed restoration with a good coronal seal prevents microleakage, keeps the tooth stable, and protects it from cuspal flexure and future fracture.[3] To overcome the shortcomings of current endodontic filling materials such as gutta-percha or resilon to reinforce roots, different materials and/or techniques are required. Among those, the intra-orifice barrier is a credible alternative method for reducing coronal leakage in endodontically treated teeth.[4] Since bonded composite restorations are essential in enhancing the fracture resistance of teeth that have undergone endodontic treatment, they should be the first option for coronal restorations.[5]

According to Swartz P et al., the failure rate of endodontically treated teeth was nearly double in cases lacking adequate postendodontic restoration. In this regard, pericervical dentin (PCD), which extends 4 mm above and 4 mm apical to the crestal level of bone, is of interest.[6] The occlusal stress is transferred along the root long axis by PCD. According to research, long-term tooth retention and fracture resistance are directly related to the amount of PCD remaining.

Adhesive restorative materials used as intra-orifice barriers and intracoronal restorations have been shown to increase the fracture resistance of endodontically treated teeth.[7] Because of their adhesive properties, composite resin restorations require minimal cavity preparation, preserving tooth structure and providing intracoronal reinforcement.[17] Moreover, it has been demonstrated that endodontically treated teeth’s fracture resistance can be considerably increased by direct and indirect cusp coverage adhesive restorations.[8]

Previous studies have not compared the efficacy of these materials as root reinforcing materials, which offer greater ease of placement and superior properties than conventional materials. Thus, the aim of the present study is to compare and evaluate the reinforcing effect of three different intra-orifice barriers on PCD of endodontically treated teeth.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

Forty permanent single-rooted mandibular premolars with a single canal were chosen. Radiographs were taken to ensure there was just one canal. Teeth with more than one canal, curved roots, internal or external resorption, or fracture lines were not included in this study. Teeth were sterilized with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) and kept in distilled water until used. The samples were standardized to 14 mm by decoronating at the cementoenamel junction using a bur in a high-speed handpiece and water.

A #10 K file was advanced into the root canals of the sample until it was visible at the apical foramen. The working length was established to be 1 mm shorter. Up to #20 K, the root canals were meticulously instrumented using a hand file. Biomechanical preparation was done with an endo motor (E-connect Pro, Orikam Healthcare India Pvt. Ltd., India) and a rotary ProTaper Universal system (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) until F3 in the crown-down technique. During instrumentation, 5 mL of 5.25% NaOCl was used to irrigate the canals following each file change. Final irrigation was done by rinsing the canals with 5 mL of 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). The root canals were finally flushed with 10 mL of distilled water.

Gutta-percha (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and AH Plus sealer (De Trey Dentsply, Konstanz, Germany) were used for obturation after the canals had been dried with paper points. The excess gutta-percha was seared to the orifice level using a hot instrument. The samples were then kept for 8 h at 37°C in an incubator (Alcor, India) to allow the sealer to set fully. A hot plugger was used to remove the coronal 3 mm of root canal obturation for all experimental groups other than the control group. A cotton pellet soaked in 70% ethanol was used to remove any sealant or gutta-percha remains from the prepared area.

Group I: Control group (n = 10): No intra-orifice barrier was placed, and the coronal 3 mm gutta-percha remained in place.

In all the experimental groups, intra-orifice barriers were placed. Acid etching was done with 37% phosphoric acid for 15 s, followed by a 10-s rinsing with distilled water. Finally, the moist cotton was used to remove any remaining moisture from the cavity. A small layer of the bonding agent (Adper Single Bond 2, 3M ESPE, USA) was applied and light-cured for 10 s.

Group II: Ever X posterior, a fiber-reinforced composite (Turku, Southwest Finland, Finland) (n = 10): The fiber-reinforced composite was placed incrementally in the prepared space and was light-cured for 40 s.

Group III: Beautifil II (Giomer) (n = 10) Giomer was placed incrementally into the cavity, and each increment was light-cured for 20 s.

Group IV – Tetric N-ceram, 3M ESPE (Nanohybrid Composite) (n = 10). The prepared intra-orifice space was restored with a nanohybrid composite in two increments of 1.5 mm thickness and light-cured.

After the intra-orifice barrier materials were placed, all specimens were kept at 100% humidity and 37°C to allow the complete set of the restorative materials. The apical root end of each sample was positioned vertically along its long axis in self-curing acrylic (Quick-Ashvin, Delhi, India) filled in stainless steel blocks of 10 mm × 10 mm × 20 mm (length, width, and height), leaving 3 mm exposed. Using light body elastomeric impression material (Express, 3M ESPA, USA), the periodontal ligament was simulated. A 1 mm/min compressive force was applied to the specimens until fracture occurred using a universal testing machine (MICRONIX, South Korea). The force needed to fracture each specimen in newtons (N) was noted.

Statistical analysis

The data were assessed with IBM SPSS version 20 software (IBM SPSS, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The data were assessed using descriptive statistics, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey’s post hoc tests. A box plot was employed to illustrate the data.

DISCUSSION

For the success of postendodontic restorative treatment, the optimal restorative approach must be employed to compensate for the loss of coronal tooth structure.[8] Calcium-phosphate mineral particles are used to reinforce the mechanical demands of the oral cavity, which are well-suited for the essential elements of dentin, and it has been found that dentin loss eventually jeopardizes the mechanical integrity of the remaining tooth structure.[9] Based on a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms causing stress formation, the cervical region of the tooth is the most susceptible location for stress concentration in endodontically treated teeth.[10] It has been noted that compared to other regions of the dentinoenamel junction (DEJ), the mechanical interlocking between dentin and enamel in the cervical region of the DEJ is weaker.[11]

Dentin in the tooth’s cervical region is referred to as PCD. It serves as the neck of the tooth.[5] Restoring PCD with resin composite generated a change in microstrain which directs away from the apical root dentin, resulting in reduced apical microstrain, less cervical root dentin flexing, and aiding in transferring functional loads away from the apical region.[12] The likelihood of vertical root fracture ought to decrease due to the shift of the apical microstrain. In light of the preceding outcomes, the current study was centered on evaluating the reinforcement of the PCD, a critical zone.

According to Weiger et al., 5.25% NaOCl followed by 17% EDTA was used as a final irrigant to eliminate the smear layer, during the root canal cleaning and shaping procedure in this in vitro study.[13] In this investigation, an epoxy resin-based sealer (AH Plus sealer) was also utilized because of its innate capacity for volumetric expansion, which improves bond strength.[14]

Zandbiglari et al. noted that obturation with the AH Plus sealer could not improve the fracture resistance and that using larger taper instruments resulted in a considerable weakening of the roots.[15] Research has demonstrated that bonded restorations provide coronal strengthening of the tooth. Thus, the current investigation compares several types of bonded restorative materials.

The results of this current study demonstrated a statistically significant difference (P = 0.05) when the mean fracture resistance of Groups I, II, III, and IV were compared.

The samples demonstrated that teeth with intra-orifice barriers are more fracture-resistant than roots that have been obturated with gutta-percha and do not have an intra-orifice barrier placed. The results were in agreement with the previous findings by Aboobaker et al.,[16] Gupta et al.,[17] and Chauhan et al.[18]

The tetric N-Ceram Bulk Fill (Ivoclar Vivadent) is a novel addition to the field of dentistry. It is a high-viscosity, universal bulk restorative composite resin with a high filler content. This nano-hybrid composite was introduced to replace both bulk-fill flowable and traditional nonflowable composites, offering a promising alternative in postendodontic restorations.[19]

With the promise of improved mechanical qualities, Beautifil II is a second-generation Giomer released into the market. Giomer’s cross-linked polymer matrices and resin-based prereacted glass fillers provide greater compressive strength.[9]

Ever X posterior is a short fiber reinforced composite (SFC). The current study showed substantially more excellent fracture resistance of SFC among the experimental groups. The semi-interpenetrating polymer network, which is formed by cross-linked bisphenol A-glycidyl methacrylate, triethylene glycol dimethacrylate, and linear polymethyl methacrylate, is the resin matrix of the ever X posterior.[20] It strengthens the composite and improves bonding.[21] In addition, it has been demonstrated that the micromechanical interlocking between the dentin and the short fibers of ever X posterior enhances the capacity to bind to the dentin surface underneath.[22]

Compared to all other adhesive composite resins employed in the current investigation, the ever X posterior has a higher elastic modulus of 12.3 GPa. Studies have demonstrated that SFC can halt the spread of cracks and offer the maximum reinforcement efficiency in a multidirectional isotropic reinforcement effect, increasing the composite resin’s resistance to fracture.[12] Each of the above factors could have impacted the results of the study.

With its limitations, the results of this in vitro study suggest that endodontically treated teeth have considerably greater fracture resistance when PCD is restored using adhesive composite resins than when the control group does. Among the experimental groups, short-fiber reinforced composite resin showed noticeably more significant reinforcing of the PCD, a finding that could potentially revolutionize postendodontic restorations.

CONCLUSIONS

Within the limitations of the present in vitro study, it is concluded that the placement of intra-orifice barriers enhanced the fracture resistance and reinforcement of obturated roots. Ever X posterior and tetric N-Ceram can be used as intra-orifice barriers as they exhibited good fracture resistance in endodontically treated teeth.
