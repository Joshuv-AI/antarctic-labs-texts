Explicit Generators for the Unit Group of the Burnside ring
Ziad Ghanem

Abstract
. To the best of our knowledge, there is no explicit, constructive description of the generating set for the unit group A ​ ( G ) × A(G)^{\times} of the Burnside ring associated with a finite group G G . We resolve this long-standing open question, proving that A ​ ( G ) × A(G)^{\times} is generated by the set of basic degrees —canonical Burnside ring elements arising from the G G -equivariant degree of the identity map on irreducible G G -representations. In particular, we demonstrate that every unit in A ⁡ ( G ) A(G) is realized as the equivariant degree of a linear G G -isomorphism on a suitable orthogonal G G -representation which, in turn, can be described as the Burnside ring product of a finite number of basic degrees, establishing a concrete link between the multiplicative structure of the Burnside ring and the field of equivariant topology.

1.
Introduction

The Burnside ring
A(G)
of a finite group
G
encodes the algebraic structure of
G
-actions on finite sets, providing one of the most fundamental invariants in equivariant mathematics. As the universal example of a Mackey functor, it underpins induction theorems and characterizes relationships between subgroups and their fixed-point data while the identification of
A(G)
with the zeroth equivariant stable homotopy group of spheres establishes it as a cornerstone of equivariant homotopy theory
[
7
]
. This dual algebraic-topological nature makes the Burnside ring both a powerful computational tool and a testing ground for exploring structural phenomena across representation theory and equivariant topology

The multiplicative structure of
A(G)
reflects deep group-theoretic properties. For example, Dress established that a group
G
is solvable if and only if its Burnside ring has no non-trivial idempotents
[
8
]
while Gluck gave an explicit Möbius-inversion formula for the primitive idempotents in the
rational Burnside algebra
[
9
]
. Yoshida later generalized these results to localized rings, identifying idempotents corresponding to
\pi
-perfect subgroups and deriving Dress’s induction theorem as a corollary
[
12
]
. Together, these results show how the Burnside ring mediates between algebraic invariants and the combinatorics of the subgroup lattice.

Much of the Burnside ring’s multiplicative structure is characterized by its units. The unit group
A(G)^{\times}
is known to be an elementary abelian
2
-group, and for groups of odd order, the Feit–Thompson theorem implies that it is simply
\{\pm 1\}

[
10
]
. For even-order groups, particularly
2
-groups, the structure is far more complex. Yoshida provided a precise characterization of units: a function mapping subgroups to
\pm 1
represents a unit if and only if it satisfies a linear character condition on the Weyl group of every subgroup
[
13
]
. Matsuda and Miyata investigated tom Dieck’s homomorphism from the real representation ring of
G
into the unit group of the Burnside ring, showing that the surjectivity of this map for a group
G
could be determined by studying its homomorphic images of
G
and extensions by odd-order groups
[
10
]
. Focusing specifically on
2
-groups, Yalçın proved that
A(G)^{\times}
is generated by elements induced from subquotients that are either trivial or dihedral groups
D_{2^{n}}
with
n\geq 4

[
11
]
. More recently, these inductive methods have been formalized using the language of biset functors, allowing Bouc to determine the complete structure of
A(G)^{\times}
for all
p
-groups
[
5
]
and Barsotti to extend these results to certain classes of solvable groups
[
4
]
.

Despite these advances, a fundamental problem persists:
Can the generating set for
A(G)^{\times}
be explicitly constructed for an arbitrary finite group
G
?

In this paper, we resolve this question affirmatively, proving that the unit group is generated by a canonical family of Burnside ring elements called
basic degrees
, uniquely associated with the irreducible representations of
G
. These basic degrees emerge naturally from the
G
-equivariant degree theory—a topological framework that classifies solutions to
G
-symmetric equations by their isotropy subgroup in
G
(for a comprehensive overview of equivariant degree theory we refer the reader to the two manuscripts
[
1
,
2
]
and also to the survey article
[
3
]
). Our construction is fully explicit: we demonstrate that every unit in
A(G)^{\times}
can be realized as the
G
-equivariant degree of a linear
G
-isomorphism on the unit ball in an appropriate orthogonal
G
-representation. This correspondence establishes a precise connection between the topological invariants of equivariant maps and the algebraic structure of the Burnside ring, providing both a complete answer to the generation problem and a new perspective on the interplay between equivariant topology and representation theory.

2.2.
The Burnside Ring and its Multiplicative Structure

We denote by
A(G)=\mathbb{Z}[\Phi(G)]
the free
\mathbb{Z}
-module generated by the set of subgroup conjugacy classes
\Phi(G)
. Every element
a\in A(G)
can be uniquely expressed as a formal sum

\displaystyle a=a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N})

over the generators
(H_{i})\in\Phi(G)
, specified by a unique sequence of integer coefficients
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. This correspondence yields a natural isomorphism

\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}:A(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{N}
defined by

(2)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}(a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N}))=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N}).

The module
A(G)
inherits an abelian group structure with componentwise addition: for elements
a=a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N})
and
b=b_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+b_{N}(H_{N})
, we define

\displaystyle a+b:=(a_{1}+b_{1})(H_{1})+\ldots+(a_{N}+b_{N})(H_{N}).

To endow
A(G)
with a ring structure, we define multiplication on generators by

(3)

\displaystyle(H_{i})\cdot(H_{j}):=\sum_{k=1}^{N}n_{ij}^{k}(H_{k}),

where the coefficient
n_{ij}^{k}
counts the number of orbits of type
(H_{k})
in the
G
-set
G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j}
. This product extends bilinearly to all of
A(G)
, making the free
{\mathbb{Z}}
-module an abelian ring with multiplicative identity
(H_{N})=(G)
. We call
A(G)
the
Burnside ring
of
G
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to the derivation of a recurrence formula for practical computation of the coefficients
\{n_{ij}^{k}\}_{i,j,k=1}^{N}
. To begin, notice that the number of
(H_{k})
-type orbits in
G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j}
coincides with the cardinality of the
(H_{k})
-orbit space
(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{(H_{k})}/G
which, in turn, is isomorphic to the
H_{k}
-orbit space
(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}/W(H_{k})
(cf. Lemma (
A.1
), Proposition (
A.2
)). Therefore, the multiplication coefficient
n_{ij}^{k}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
can be expressed in terms of the
H_{k}
-orbit space and the mark
m(H_{k},H_{k})=|W(H_{k})|
(see Appendix
B
) as follows,

\displaystyle n_{ij}^{k}=|(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}/W(H_{k})|=\frac{|(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}|}{m(H_{k},H_{k})}

Next, we can make use of the
H_{k}
-fixed point space decomposition

\displaystyle(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}=\bigcup_{H_{l}>H_{k}}(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{l}},

to obtain

\displaystyle(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}=(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}\setminus\bigcup_{H_{l}>H_{k}}(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{l}}.

On the one hand, from the equality
(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}=(G/H_{i})^{H_{k}}\times(G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}
, one has

\displaystyle|(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}|

\displaystyle=|(G/H_{i})^{H_{k}}|(G/H_{j})^{H_{k}}|

\displaystyle=m(H_{k},H_{i})m(H_{k},H_{j}),

On the other hand, the cardinality of each
H_{l}
-isotropic set
(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{l}}
can be expressed, in terms of the multiplication coefficients, as follows

\displaystyle(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{l}}=n_{ij}^{l}|W(H_{l})|

At this point, we can present a preliminary recurrence relation for the cardinality of the
H_{k}
-isotropic set:

\displaystyle|(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}|=m(H_{k},H_{i})m(H_{k},H_{j})-\sum_{H_{l}>H_{k}}n_{ij}^{l}|W(H_{l})|

Leveraging Proposition (
A.1
) which establishes that there are exactly
\frac{m(H_{k},H_{l})}{|W(H_{l})|}
subgroups,
H\in(H_{l})
with
H_{k}\leq H
, we can rephrase the above relation in terms of conjugacy classes,

\displaystyle|(G/H_{i}\times G/H_{j})_{H_{k}}|=m(H_{k},H_{i})m(H_{k},H_{j})-\sum_{(H_{l})>(H_{k})}n_{ij}^{l}m(H_{k},H_{l})

In this way, we have obtained the following recurrence formula:

(4)

\displaystyle n_{ij}^{k}=\frac{m(H_{k},H_{i})m(H_{k},H_{j})-\sum\limits_{l>k}n_{ij}^{l}m(H_{k},H_{l})}{m(H_{k},H_{k})}

In terms of the coefficient isomorphism
\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}
, each generator
(H_{i})\in\Phi(G)
corresponds to the standard basis vector
\mathbf{e}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
. The multiplication rule (
3
) therefore induces a product structure on
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
given by

(5)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{e}_{j}=(n_{ij}^{1},\ldots,n_{ij}^{N}),

where the components are computed using the recurrence formula (
4
).

2.2.1.
The Burnside Algebra

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a field (in practice,
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{R}
). The
\mathbb{K}
-Burnside algebra

A_{\mathbb{K}}(G)
is the
\mathbb{K}
-vector space
\mathbb{K}[\Phi(G)]
with basis
\{(H_{1}),\ldots,(H_{N})\}
. Elements of
A_{\mathbb{K}}(G)
are linear combinations

a=a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N}),\quad a_{i}\in\mathbb{K}.

The vector space isomorphism
\rho_{\mathbb{K}}:A_{\mathbb{K}}(G)\to\mathbb{K}^{N}
maps each element to its coefficient vector

(6)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbb{R}}(a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N}))=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})

The algebra structure on
A_{\mathbb{K}}(G)
is defined by extending the Burnside ring multiplication
\mathbb{K}
-bilinearly. Specifically, for basis elements
(H_{i}),(H_{j})\in\Phi(G)
, we define

(7)

\displaystyle(H_{i})\cdot(H_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}n_{ij}^{k}(H_{k}),

where the structure coefficients
n_{ij}^{k}
coincide with those of the Burnside ring, such that the multiplication induced by (
7
) is the natural and unique extension of the multiplication defined on the Burnside ring.

Lemma 2.1
.

The Burnside algebra is associative, commutative, and unital with multiplicative identity
(H_{N})=(G)
.

Proof.

These properties follow immediately from the corresponding properties of the Burnside ring
A(G)
, since the algebra multiplication is defined by extending the ring multiplication
\mathbb{K}
-linearly.

\square

2.3.
The Mark Homomorphism and the Ghost ring

The complexity of the Burnside product motivates the study of ring homomorphisms from the Burnside ring to rings with more tractable multiplicative structures. In Appendix
B
, we established that the table of marks (TOM)
\Psi
is nonsingular and upper triangular. We now show that
\Psi
defines a ring homomorphism to a particularly simple ring structure.

We call the vector space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
equipped with the Hadamard product
\odot:\mathbb{R}^{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}
the
Ghost algebra
, where the Hadamard product is defined by componentwise multiplication:
(x\odot y)_{i}=x_{i}y_{i}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The following result establishes the fundamental relationship between the Burnside and Ghost algebras.

Proposition 2.1
.

The table of marks
\Psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{N}
defines an algebra isomorphism between the Burnside algebra
(A_{\mathbb{R}}(G),\cdot)
and the Ghost algebra
(\mathbb{R}^{N},\odot)
.

Proof.

We already know that the table of marks is a linear isomorphism between the underlying vector spaces of the Burnside and Ghost algebras, what remains to be shown is that it preserves the multiplicative structure between the two algebras as well. In fact, it will be sufficient to demonstrate that the table of marks respects the multiplicative structure on the basis elements of the Burnside algebra, that is for any two basis elements,
\textbf{e}_{i},\textbf{e}_{j}\in A_{\mathbb{R}}(G)
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi(\textbf{e}_{i}\cdot\textbf{e}_{j})=\Psi(\textbf{e}_{i})\odot\Psi(\textbf{e}_{j})

We have on the right hand side the vector
(m(H_{1},H_{i})m(H_{1},H_{j}),\ldots,m(H_{N},H_{i})m(H_{N},H_{j}))
. On the other hand, the Burnside product,
\textbf{e}_{i}\cdot\textbf{e}_{j}=(n_{ij}^{1},\ldots,n_{ij}^{N})
, is defined with respect to the structure coefficients of the Burnside algebra. Therefore,
(n_{ij}^{1},\ldots,n_{ij}^{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
is the solution of an upper triangular matrix equation such that, using back-substitution, each structure coefficient
n_{ij}^{k}
can be expressed,

\displaystyle n_{ij}^{k}=\frac{m(H_{k},H_{i})m(H_{k},H_{j})-\sum\limits_{l>k}n_{ij}^{l}m(H_{k},H_{l})}{m(H_{k},H_{k})}

which coincides exactly with the recurrence relation (
4
) derived in Section
2.2

\square

The Ghost algebra naturally contains a subring with integer coefficients. We define the
Ghost ring
as
(\mathbb{Z}^{N},\odot)
, where
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
has componentwise addition and Hadamard multiplication. Since
\Psi
is an algebra isomorphism that maps
A(G)\subseteq A_{\mathbb{R}}(G)
into
\mathbb{Z}^{N}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, its restriction
\Psi_{\mathbb{Z}}:A(G)\to\mathbb{Z}^{N}
defines a ring homomorphism. Moreover,
since the restriction of the table of marks to the Burnside ring
\Psi_{\mathbb{Z}}:\mathbb{Z}^{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{N}
is injective, it is a ring isomorphism between
(A(G),\cdot)
and its image
(\Psi_{\mathbb{Z}}(A(G)),\odot)
. These relationships are summarized in the following commutative diagram:
(A_{\mathbb{R}}(G),\cdot)
\quad(\mathbb{R}^{N},\odot)\quad
(A(G),\cdot)
(\Psi_{\mathbb{Z}}(A(G)),\odot)

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Psi_{\mathbb{Z}}}

The simplicity of the Hadamard product allows us to derive structural results about the Burnside ring through its image in the Ghost ring. As an illustration, we characterize the unit groups of these rings.

Lemma 2.2
.

An element of the Ghost algebra
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
has a multiplicative inverse if and only if
x_{i}\neq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq N
.

Proof.

Let
x
and
y
be elements of of the Ghost algebra. Notice that
x\odot y=\mathbb{1}_{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if for every
x_{i}y_{i}=1
for every
1\leq i\leq N
. In other words,
x
and
y
are multiplicative inverses if and only if for all
i=1
to
N
,
x_{i}=\frac{1}{y_{i}}
. Consequently, an element
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
possesses a multiplicative inverse if and only if each of its components has a well-defined reciprocal, that is to say if and only if each of its components is nonzero.

\square

The situation for the Ghost ring is more restrictive:

Lemma 2.3
.

An element
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
is a unit in the Ghost ring if and only if
x_{i}\in\{\pm 1\}
for all
1\leq i\leq N
.

Corollary 2.1
.

An element of the Ghost ring
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
has a multiplicative inverse if and only if it is an involution.

Corollary 2.2
.

An element of the Burnside ring
a\in A(G)
has a multiplicative inverse if and only if it is an involution.

Corollary 2.3
.

An element
a\in A(G)
is invertible if and only if its image
\Psi(a)
in the Ghost ring has components that are all
\pm 1
.

3.1.
Computational Formula of the
G
-Equivariant Degree for Linear
G
-isomorphisms.

Our main result relies on computing the degree for
G
-equivariant linear isomorphisms. The following strategy, adapted from Section
3.6
in
[
1
]
, reduces this problem to its fundamental components.

Let
T:V\rightarrow V
be a
G
-equivariant linear isomorphism on an orthogonal
G
-representation
V
.
Assuming that a complete list
\{\mathcal{V}_{i}\}_{i=0}^{r}
of of the irreducible
G
-representations is made available,
V
admits an
G
-isotypic decomposition
of the form

(10)

\displaystyle V=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{r},

where the
j
-th
G
-isotypic component
V_{j}
is modeled on the corresponding
G
-irreducible representation
\mathcal{V}_{j}
. By Schur’s Lemma,
T
preserves (
10
) allowing us to write

T=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{r}T_{k},\quad T_{k}:=T|_{V_{k}}:V_{k}\rightarrow V_{k}.

The Multiplicativity property
(G5)
of the
G
-equivariant degree, allows us to decompose the degree of
T
on the unit ball
B(V)
into a Burnside ring product over these components

(11)

\displaystyle G\text{\rm-deg}(T,B(V))=\prod_{k=0}^{r}G\text{\rm-deg}(T_{k},B(V_{k})).

Since
T_{k}
is
G
-equivariant for all
k=0,\ldots,r
, the generalized eigenspace
E(\beta)
for any eigenvalue
\beta\in\sigma(T_{k})
constitutes a
G
-invariant subspace such that every isotypic component
V_{k}
admits a spectral decomposition
V_{k}=\bigoplus_{\beta\in\sigma(T_{k})}E(\beta)
which is preserved by
T_{k}
. Applying multiplicativity again yields

G\text{\rm-deg}(T_{k},B(V_{k}))=\prod_{\beta\in\sigma(T_{k})}G\text{\rm-deg}(T_{k}|_{E(\beta)},B(E(\beta))).

Notice that the degree of the restriction
T_{k}|_{E(\beta)}
depends on the sign of
\beta
. Indeed, if
\beta>0
or is complex,
T_{k}|_{E(\beta)}
is homotopic to the identity, and its degree is the multiplicative identity in
A(G)
. Thus, only negative eigenvalues contribute non-trivially. For
\beta<0
,
T_{k}|_{E(\beta)}
is homotopic to
-\mathrm{Id}
such that its degree is
G\text{\rm-deg}(-\mathrm{Id},B(E(\beta)))
. Now, each eigenspace
E(\beta)
is a sub-representation of
V_{k}
and must therefore be a direct sum of some number of copies of the irreducible representation
\mathcal{V}_{k}
. Let this number be
\mu_{k}(\beta):=\dim E(\beta)/\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}
. Applying the multiplicativity property a third and final time, one has

G\text{\rm-deg}(-\mathrm{Id},B(E(\beta)))=G\text{\rm-deg}(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathcal{V}_{k}))^{\mu_{k}(\beta)}.

To formalize the above construction, we put
\mu_{k}:=\sum_{\beta\in\sigma_{-}(T)}\mu_{k}(\beta)
and
associate to each irreducible
G
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{k}
the corresponding
k
-th basic degree

\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}:=G\text{\rm-deg}(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathcal{V}_{k})),

such that
(
11
) becomes

\displaystyle G\text{\rm-deg}(T,B(V))=\prod_{\beta\in\sigma_{-}(T)}\prod_{k=0}^{r}(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})^{\mu_{k}(\beta)}=\prod_{k=0}^{r}(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})^{\mu_{k}}.

Notice that the coefficients
n_{H}:=\operatorname{coeff}^{H}(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})
specifying each of the basic degrees
\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}\in A(G)

can be practically computed, using the recurrence formula (
9
), as follows

\displaystyle n_{H}=\frac{(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H}}-\sum_{(H)\preceq(K)}{n_{K}\,n(H,K)\,\left|W(K)\right|}}{\left|W(H)\right|}.

3.2.
The
G
-Equivariant Ghost Degree

Motivated by the mark homomorphism, we propose in this section an equivalent
G
-equivariant degree taking values in the Ghost ring. Specifically, we define the
G
-equivariant Ghost Degree
of an admissible
G
-pair
(f,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}^{G}
, denoted
\Phi(G)\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
, as the image of the corresponding
G
-equivariant degree
\deg(f,\Omega)
under the mark homomorphism
\Psi
, i.e.

\Phi(G)\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega):=\Psi G\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega).

Proposition 3.1
.

The
G
-equivariant Ghost degree of an admissible
G
-pair
(f,\Omega)\in\mathcal{M}^{G}
is specified by the sequence of integers

\Phi(G)\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)=\{\deg(f^{H_{i}},\Omega^{H_{i}}\}_{(H_{i})\in\Phi(G)}.

Proof.

Let the
G
-equivariant degree of
(f,\Omega)
be written in the Burnside ring basis as
G\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}n_{H_{j}}(H_{j})
.
The
i
-th component of its image in the Ghost ring is given by the matrix-vector product

(\Psi\cdot G\text{\rm-deg}(f,\Omega))_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Psi_{ij}n_{H_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m(H_{i},H_{j})n_{H_{j}}.

Recall that the coefficients
n_{H_{j}}
are given by the recurrence formula

n_{H_{i}}=\frac{\deg(f^{H_{i}},\Omega^{H_{i}})-\sum_{(H_{k})\succ(H_{i})}n_{H_{k}}m(H_{i},H_{k})}{|W(H_{i})|}.

Multiplying both sides by
|W(H_{i})|=m(H_{i},H_{i})
and rearranging terms, we solve for the local Brouwer degree degree on the corresponding fixed-point set to obtain

\deg(f^{H_{i}},\Omega^{H_{i}})=n_{H_{i}}m(H_{i},H_{i})+\sum_{(H_{k})\succ(H_{i})}n_{H_{k}}m(H_{i},H_{k}).

Since the Table of Marks
\Psi
is upper-triangular for our chosen ordering, we know that
m(H_{i},H_{k})=0
for any
k
such that
(H_{k})\prec(H_{i})
. Therefore, we can write the right-hand side as a sum over the
G
-isotypic indices
j=1,\dots,N
as follows

\deg(f^{H_{i}},\Omega^{H_{i}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}m(H_{i},H_{j})n_{H_{j}}.

\square

Notice that the image of each basic degree
\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}\in A(G)
under the mark homomorphism is specified by the fixed point dimensions of the corresponding irreducible
G
-representation
\mathcal{V}_{k}
as follows

\displaystyle(\Psi\cdot\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})_{i}=\deg(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}))=(-1)^{\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}}.

It follows that the
G
-equivariant ghost degree of an admissible
G
-pair
(-\id,B(\mathcal{V}_{k}))\in\mathcal{M}^{G}
is a vector in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
whose components are all either
+1
or
-1
.

Corollary 3.1
.

The basic degree
\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}}
associated with any irreducible
G
-representation is an involutory element of the Burnside ring.

4.
Main Result: Generators of the Unit Group

We are now prepared to prove the main result: every invertible element in the Burnside ring can be realized as the degree of a linear map, and is therefore generated by the basic degrees. The key insight is that we can construct appropriate orthogonal
G
-representations and
G
-equivariant linear maps to realize any necessary pattern of signs in the ghost ring.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
a\in A(G)
is an invertible element, then there exists a
G
-representation
V
and a
G
-equivariant linear isomorphism
A\in GL^{G}(V)
such that
a=G\text{\rm-deg}(A,B(V))
.

Proof.

Since
a
is invertible,
its image under the mark homomorphism
\Psi(a)
can be uniquely identified with a vector of parities

\delta=(\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{N})\in({\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}})^{N}
, satisfying

(-1)^{\delta_{i}}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{j}m(H_{i},H_{j}),\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq N.

Let
\mu=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
be a vector of non-negative integers and consider the orthogonal
G
-representation
V=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\oplus\mu_{k}}
. Notice that the
G
-equivariant ghost degree of
the
G
-equivariant linear isomorphism
A:=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}(-\id|_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})^{\oplus\mu_{k}}:V\rightarrow V
on the open unit ball
B(V)
can be calculated component-wise as follows

\displaystyle\Phi(G)\text{\rm-deg}(A,B(V))_{i}

\displaystyle=\Psi\left(\prod_{k=1}^{r}(\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})^{\mu_{k}}\right)_{i}=\prod_{k=1}^{r}(\Psi\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{k}})^{\odot\mu_{k}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{r}(-1)^{\mu_{k}\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}}=(-1)^{\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mu_{k}\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}}.

To realize
a
, it suffices to demonstrate the solvability of the congruence system

(12)

\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mu_{k}\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}\equiv\delta_{i}\pmod{2},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

Notice that, if we indicate by
D
the
N\times r
matrix with
D_{ik}=\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}
, then
(
12
) becomes

D\mu\equiv\delta\pmod{2}.

Let
{\mathbb{C}}[G/H_{i}]
be the permutation representation of the coset
G/H_{i}
, which admits a decomposition as a direct sum of irreducible
G
-representations of the form

{\mathbb{C}}[G/H_{i}]=\bigoplus_{k=1}^{r}\mathcal{V}_{k}^{\oplus c_{ik}},\quad c_{ik}\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\},

such that for each pair of subgroup conjugacy representatives
H_{i},H_{j}\leq G
, one has

\displaystyle m(H_{i},H_{j})

\displaystyle=\dim({\mathbb{C}}[G/H_{i}])^{H_{j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{jk}\dim\mathcal{V}_{k}^{H_{i}}.

It follows that the table of marks admits the factorization
\Psi=DC
, where
C
is the
r\times N
matrix
C_{kj}=c_{jk}
. In particular if
\mathbf{a}
is the coefficient vector of
a
modulus
2
, then

\delta\equiv\Psi\mathbf{a}=(DC)\mathbf{a}\pmod{2}.

Thus
\delta
lies in the column space of
DC
, which is contained in the column space of
D
. Therefore, the system (
12
) is solvable over
{\mathbb{Z}}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
, and we may take
\mu_{k}\in\{0,1\}
, ensuring
\mu_{k}\geq 0
.

\square

Corollary 4.1
.

The unit group
A(G)^{\times}
is generated by the set of basic degrees
\{\deg_{\mathcal{V}_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{r}
associated with the irreducible representations of
G
.

Appendix A
The Isotropic Sets
X^{H}
,
X_{H}
and
X_{(H)}

Let
X
be a
G
-space and
H\leq G
be a subgroup. Recall from Section
2.1
the definitions of the isotropy sets

\displaystyle\begin{cases}X^{H}:=\{x\in X\mid G_{x}\geq H\};\\

In general, the sets
X^{H}
and
X_{H}
are not
G
-invariant. However, the set
X_{(H)}
, which consists of all points whose isotropy groups are conjugate to
H
, is a
G
-set. To see this, let
x\in X_{(H)}
and choose any
g\in G
. By definition, one has
(G_{x})=(H)
. On the other hand, since the isotropy group
G_{gx}=gG_{x}g^{-1}
of

is conjugate to
G_{x}
, one has
gx\in X_{(H)}
for all
g\in G
. With

as an established
G
-set, we can form the orbit space
X_{(H)}/G=\{G(x)\mid x\in X_{(H)}\}
. Notice that the following lemma is a direct consequence of this definition.

Lemma A.1
.

The cardinality of the orbit space
X_{(H)}/G
is equal to the number of orbits in
X
of orbit type
(H)
.

Although the sets
X^{H}
and

are not
G
-invariant, one can easily demonstrate that they are
N(H)
-sets. Since
H
is a normal subgroup of
N(H)
and acts trivially on
X^{H}
(and thus on
X_{H}
), there is an induced action of the Weyl group
W(H)=N(H)/H
on both
X^{H}
and
X_{H}
, given by

(nH)\cdot x=nx,\quad\text{for all }n\in N(H)\text{ and }x\in X^{H}\text{ or }x\in X_{H}.

Proposition A.1
.

W(H)
acts freely on the set
X_{H}
.

Proof.

Recall that
W(H)
is said to act freely on

if the isotropy
W(H)_{x}
of any point
x\in X_{H}
coincides with the neutral element
H\in W(H)
. Since the condition
nx=x
for an element

implies that
n\in G_{x}=H
, the isotropy of any
x\in X_{H}
is given by

W(H)_{x}=\{nH\mid n\in H\}=\{H\}.

\square

Since
X_{H}
is an established

-set, we can consider its orbit space
X_{H}/W(H)=\{W(H)(x)\mid x\in X_{H}\}
. Notice that for any
x\in X_{H}
, the
W(H)
-orbit
W(H)(x)
is identical to the
N(H)
-orbit
N(H)(x)
. The remainder of this section is dedicated to proving that the orbit space of points of type
(H)
under the
G

H
under the

-action. Before proving the main result, we establish a necessary lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

For any
x\in X_{(H)}
, its orbit
G(x)
has a non-empty intersection with

.

Proof.

Let
x\in X_{(H)}
. By definition, one has
(G_{x})=(H)
, meaning
G_{x}
and
H
are conjugate. Thus, there exists an element
g\in G
such that
gG_{x}g^{-1}=H
. Since
G_{gx}=gG_{x}g^{-1}
, we have
G_{gx}=H
, which shows that

.

\square

Proposition A.2
.

X_{(H)}/G\simeq X_{H}/W(H)

Proof.

Consider the map
\varphi:X_{(H)}/G\rightarrow X_{H}/W(H)
which associates each
G
-orbit in the

-isotropic set,

, with the
N(H)
-orbit,

, where
gx\in X_{H}
. Let’s first show that this map is well-defined by demonstrating that its definition is independent of the choice of representative
x\in G(x)
and the choice of the group element

. Suppose
x\in X_{(H)}
. By Lemma

, there exists at least one

. Moreover, suppose there exists another group element,

, with

. Clearly,
G_{gx}=G_{g^{\prime}x}\iff g^{-1}g^{\prime}G_{x}{g^{\prime}}^{-1}g=G_{x}
such that
g^{-1}g^{\prime}\in N(G_{x})
, and in particular, since
G_{x}=H
, we have
g^{-1}g^{\prime}\in N(H)
and hence,
N(H)(gx)=N(H)(g^{\prime}x)
.

Surjectivity of
\varphi
also follows immediately from Lemma
A.2
so what remains is to demonstrate injectivity. Suppose

are such that
\varphi(G(x))=\varphi(G(x^{\prime}))
, but then,
\exists_{g,g^{\prime}\in G}
with

such that

and therefore,

.

\square

Appendix B
The Table of Marks

In his book
Theory of Groups of Finite Order
, William Burnside defined the
mark
of a subgroup
H\leq G
in the
G
-set
X
as the number of points in
X
which are fixed by every element of
H

[
6
]
. For us, the
mark
of a pair

(H,K)
consisting of two subgroups
H,K\leq G
, denoted
m(H,K)
, is the number of elements in the set of left cosets of
K
in
G
fixed by
H
, i.e.

m(H,K):=|(G/K)^{H}|.

The mark of a subgroup pair
(H,K)
has another interpretation in terms of the quantities
n(H,K)
and
|W(H)|
.

Proposition B.1
.

For any pair of subgroups
H,K\leq G
, one has

\displaystyle m(H,K)=n(H,K)|W(K)|.

Proof.

We begin with the observation that any
H
-invariant coset,
gK\in(G/K)^{H}
is specified by a group element
g\in G
with
H\leq gKg^{-1}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle(G/K)^{H}

\displaystyle=\{gK\mid hgK=gK\;\forall_{h\in H}\}

\displaystyle=\{gK\mid g^{-1}hgK=K\;\forall_{h\in H}\}

\displaystyle=\{gK\mid g^{-1}hg\in K\;\forall_{h\in H}\}

\displaystyle=\{gK\mid g^{-1}Hg\leq K\}

\displaystyle=\{gK\mid H\leq gKg^{-1}\}

Let
N_{(H,K)}
denote the set of subgroups
K^{\prime}\in(K)
containing
H
, so that
|N_{(H,K)}|=n(H,K)
. Notice that the
W(K)
-action,
W(K)\times(G/K)^{H}\rightarrow(G/K)^{H}
, defined for any
nK\in W(K)
and for any
gK\in(G/K)^{H}
as follows,
(nK,gK)=ngK
, is free such that the
W(K)
-orbit of each coset has cardinality
|W(K)|
. Finally we define the
W(K)
-invariant map,
\varphi:(G/K)^{H}\rightarrow N_{(H,K)}
, which associates the
H
-invariant
K
-coset,
gK\in(G/K)^{H}
, with the
K
-conjugate subgroup
\varphi(gK)=gKg^{-1}
. Therefore, each coset in the orbit of
gK\in(G/K)^{H}
shares the same image in
N_{(H,K)}
such that
|\varphi^{-1}(K^{\prime})|=|W(K)|
for any
K^{\prime}\in N_{(H,K)}
and in particular,
|N_{(H,K)}|=\frac{|(G/K)^{H}|}{|W(K)|}
.

\square

Since both
n(H,K)
and
|W(K)|
depend only on the conjugacy classes
(H)
and
(K)
, the mark descends to a well-defined function
m:\Phi(G)\times\Phi(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
, although we will continue to use write ‘
m(H,K)
’ for representatives
H\in(H)
and
K\in(K)
. Using our indexing
\Phi(G)=\{(H_{1}),\ldots,(H_{N})\}
where
(H_{i})\prec(H_{j})
for
i<j
, we define the
table of marks
as the
N\times N
matrix

\displaystyle\Psi_{ij}=m(H_{i},H_{j}).

Proposition B.2
.

The table of marks
\Psi
is upper triangular with positive diagonal entries. Specifically, one has

(a)

If
i>j
, then
m(H_{i},H_{j})=0
.

(b)

For all
i
, we have
m(H_{i},H_{i})=|W(H_{i})|
.

Proof.

For our first claim
(a)
, let
i>j
such that there does not exists any
g\in G
with
H_{i}\leq gH_{j}g^{-1}
, then

\displaystyle(G/H_{j})^{H_{i}}

\displaystyle=\{gH_{j}\mid H_{i}\leq G_{gH_{j}}\}

\displaystyle=\{gH_{j}\mid\exists_{g\in G}\;H_{i}\leq gH_{j}g^{-1}\}

\displaystyle=\{\emptyset\}

Here we use the fact that
G/H_{j}
is a transitive
G
-set, meaning that the
H_{j}
-cosets in
G
all share a single orbit, which in turn implies that the isotropies of the cosets share the same subgroup conjugacy class. In addition, we use the fact that the shared conjugacy class of the coset isotropies is exactly the conjugacy class of
H_{j}
, to see this notice,

\displaystyle G_{gH_{j}}

\displaystyle=\{h\in G\mid hgH_{j}=gH_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\{h\in G\mid g^{-1}hgH_{j}=H_{j}\}

\displaystyle=\{h\in G\mid g^{-1}hg\in H_{j}\}

\displaystyle=g^{-1}H_{j}g.

For our second claim
(b)
, notice that one has

\displaystyle(G/H_{i})^{H_{i}}

\displaystyle=\{gH_{i}\mid\forall_{h\in H_{i}}\;hgH_{i}=gH_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\{gH_{i}\mid g^{-1}H_{i}g=H_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\{gH_{i}\mid g\in N(H_{i})\}

\displaystyle=N(H_{i})/H_{i}.

\square

Corollary B.1
.

The table of marks
\Psi
is nonsingular.

Appendix C
A Burnside Multiplication Table

A rule for multiplication on the Burnside ring (resp. the Burnside algebra) is completely determined by the rule for the multiplication of any two members of its generating set (resp. basis). Indeed, given any two Burnside ring elements
a,b\in A(G)
with coefficients,
\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}(a)=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
and
\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}(b)=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})
, we can use the multiplication rule (
3
) to find their product in the Burnside ring
a\cdot b\in A(G)
as follows,

\displaystyle a\cdot b

\displaystyle=\left(a_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+a_{N}(H_{N})\right)\cdot\left(b_{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+b_{N}(H_{N})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}(H_{i})\cdot(H_{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{k}(H_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{1}(H_{1})+\cdots+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{N}(H_{N})

Moreover, if the multiplication coefficients,
n_{ij}^{k}
, for all
1\leq i,j,k\leq N
are made available, and if we use
c=a\cdot b
to denote the product of any two Burnside ring elements
a,b\in A(G)
, then the integer coefficient,
c_{k}
, of the symbol,
(H_{k})\in\Phi(G)
, can be obtained with the double sum,
c_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{k}
. With respect to the the isomorphism, (
2
), we can now reformulate the product in the Burnside ring as a product of any two integer vectors,
\textbf{a},\textbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, with
\textbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
and
\textbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})
, in the following way,

(13)

\displaystyle\textbf{a}\cdot\textbf{b}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{1},\ldots,\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}n_{ij}^{N}\right)

Importantly, the necessary
3N
multiplication coefficients for the computation of any particular product in the Burnside ring cannot be obtained
a la carte
, as it were; instead, a certain order to their calculation must be adhered to. Indeed, the nature of the recurrence formula (
4
) is such that, for any
1\leq i,j,k\leq N
, the coefficient
n_{ij}^{k}
can only be obtained if
n_{ij}^{l}
has already been calculated for all
k<l\leq N
.

With this in mind, we define the multiplication table,
M
, for the Burnside ring as the
N\times N\times N
tensor such that
\textbf{M}[i,j,\cdot]=(n_{ij}^{1},\ldots,n_{ij}^{N})
is the vector of integer coefficients from the Burnside ring product
(H_{i})\cdot(H_{j})\in A(G)
. Denoting by
\Psi
the
N\times N
-table of marks with
\Psi[i,j]=m(H_{i},H_{j})
, the multiplication table,
M
, can be constructed algorithmically with the pseudo-code (
1
).

Proposition C.1
.

Given any two Burnside ring elements
a,b\in A(G)
with coefficients
\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}(a)=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{N})
and
\rho_{\mathbb{Z}}(b)=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{N})

\displaystyle c_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_{i}b_{j}\textbf{M}[i,j,k]
