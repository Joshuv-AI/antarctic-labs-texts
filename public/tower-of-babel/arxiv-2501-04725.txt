Abstract.

In this note, we give an alternative and explicit construction of the
G_{2}(2)
-hexagon from a
U_{3}(2)
-geometry.

Keywords:

generalized hexagon; unitary geometry

1
Introduction

The paper
[
10
]
has given an alternative approach to construct the Chevalley group
G_{2}(2)
with a certain 2-transitive permutation representation of degree 28 as well as a concrete tool to study the structure of it. In this note, using the quite elementary description, we shall give an alternative construction of the
G_{2}(2)
-hexagon (i.e., the split Cayley hexagon of order 2), which yields an alternative approach of
G_{2}(2)
with two non-equivalent permutation representations of degree 63.

2
Preliminaries

A
partial linear space
of
order

(s,t)
is an incidence structure (
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{L}
) where
\mathcal{P}
is a set of
points
and
\mathcal{L}
is a set of
(s+1)
-subsets of
\mathcal{P}
(called
lines
) such that each point is contained in exactly
t+1
lines and any two distinct points are contained in at most one line (and necessarily any two distinct lines meet in at most one point). If
s=t
, then we say
order

s
instead of order
(s,s)
. Let
\bm{S}:=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
be a partial linear space.
The
dual
of
\bm{S}
is the partial linear space
\bm{S}^{D}:=(\mathcal{P}^{D},\mathcal{L}^{D})
of order
(t,s)
where
\mathcal{P}^{D}:=\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{D}:=\left\{\{l\in\mathcal{L}\mid p\in l\}\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\right\}
. Two lines of
\bm{S}
are
concurrent
if they meet in a point. The
concurrency graph
of
\bm{S}
is the graph whose vertex-set is
\mathcal{L}
, where two vertices are adjacent whenever they are concurrent.

In order to describe generalized hexagons, we use graph-theoretic terminology as follows. A
graph
is an incidence structure
(V,E)
where
V
is a set of
vertices
and
E
is a set of
2
-subsets of
V
(called
edges
). Take two vertices
x
and
y
(possibly
x=y
). We say that
x
and
y
are
adjacent
for an edge
\{x,y\}
. A
path
from
x
to
y
is a sequence of distinct vertices
(x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m}=y)
such that for
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m\}
,
x_{i-1}
is adjacent to
x_{i}
, and its
length
is the number of occurrences of edges. If
x
and
y
are connected by a path, the
distance
from
x
to
y
is the length of the shortest path from
x
to
y
, and denoted by
\textrm{d}(x,y)
. A graph is
connected
if there is a path connecting each pair of vertices. The
diameter
of connected graph is the maximum value of all the distances. A
cycle
of
length

m
is a closed path
(x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{m}=x_{0})
, and the
girth
of a graph is the minimum length of all the cycles.

A partial linear space
\bm{S}:=(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
of order
(s,t)
is called a
generalized hexagon
of
order

(s,t)
if the incidence graph of
\bm{S}
(i.e., the graph whose vertex-set is
\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}
and edges are pairs
\{p,l\}
such that
p\in\mathcal{P},l\in\mathcal{L}
and
p\in l
) has diameter 6 and girth 12, and we abbreviate it as a
\textrm{GH}(s,t)
. Note that the dual of a
\textrm{GH}(s,t)
is a
\textrm{GH}(t,s)
.
For two generalized hexagons
\bm{S}
and
\bm{T},
we define an
isomorphism

\sigma

from
\bm{S}
onto
\bm{T}
to be a one-to-one mapping from the points of
\bm{S}
onto the points of

\bm{T}
and the lines of
\bm{S}
onto the lines of
\bm{T}
such that
p
is in
l
if and only if
p^{\sigma}
is in
l^{\sigma}
for each point
p
and each line
l
of
\bm{S},
and then say that
\bm{S}
and
\bm{T}
are
isomorphic
. An
automorphism
of
\bm{S}
is an isomorphism from
\bm{S}
to itself. The set of all automorphisms of
\bm{S}
forms a group, which is called the
automorphism group
of
\bm{S}
and denoted by
{\rm Aut}\bm{S}
.
A general introduction for generalized hexagons is van Maldeghem
[
14
]
. See also Brouwer, Cohen and Neumaier
[
2
]
, Brouwer and van Maldeghem
[
3
]
and Thas
[
13
]
.

3
The
G_{2}(2)
-hexagon

In this only section, suppose temporarily that
V
is a 6-dimensional vector space over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
with a non-degenerate alternating form, which we call an
S_{6}(2)
-
geometry
. It follows from Taylor
[
12
, Exercise 8.1]
that the projective space
PG(V)
contains 63 isotropic points (or vectors), 315 totally isotropic lines (abbreviated as
t.i. lines
), 135 totally isotropic planes (abbreviated as
t.i. planes
). Each t.i. lines
l
contains 3 vectors, each t.i. plane
M
contains 7 vectors and
|l\cap M|=0,1
or
3
. An elementary counting argument shows that each t.i. line is exactly contained 3 t.i. planes. It is well known that the isotropic vectors and some of the t.i. lines form a GH(2,2) with the automorphism group
G_{2}(2)
. For more details on the construction, see
[
14
, Section 2.4]
. Although there are various ways to call this hexagon
[
14
]
, we call it the
G_{2}(2)
-
hexagon
(or the
Split Cayley hexagon
of
order
2). For the other constructions, see
[
3
, Section 4.1]
, van Maldeghem
[
15
]
, Wilson
[
16
, Section 4.3]
, Payne
[
11
]
, Cameron and Kantor
[
4
]
, Cossidente and King
[
8
]
and Bamberg and Durante
[
1
]
. It is also known that there is a unique GH(2,2) up to isomorphism and duality. This result is due to Cohen and Tits
[
6
]
.

4
The Construction

Let
V
be a 3-dimensional vector space over the finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{4}
, and
h
a non-degenerate hermitian form. Then we consider the
U_{3}(2)
-geometry
(V,h)
instead of the
S_{6}(2)
-geometry, as
V
is considered as a 6-dimensional vector space over the field
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
with the non-degenerate alternating form
s
, where
s(x,y):=h(x,y)+h(y,x)
for all
x,y\in V
(see
[
12
, Exercise 10.14]
), and in turn give an alternative and explicit construction of the
G_{2}(2)
-hexagon, which is not appearing elsewhere in the above references. The definitions and the notations of
[
10
, Preliminaries]
will be freely used.

Since
H_{0}\cap\tilde{H_{0}}=\emptyset
and
|H_{0}|=|\tilde{H_{0}}|=6
, the set
V_{1}:=\{x\in V\mid h(x,x)=1\}
is divided into

\displaystyle W:=\{x\in V_{1}\mid[x]\in H_{0}\}

and

\displaystyle\tilde{W}:=\{x\in V_{1}\mid[x]\in\tilde{H}_{0}\},

each consisting of 18 vectors.

Definition 4.1
.

Define the design
\bm{S}
with the point-set
\mathcal{P}:=V\setminus\{0\}
of which the lines consist of three types as follows:

(i)

[a]
;

(ii)

L(a):=\left\{x\in W\mid h(a,x)=0\ \text{and}\ a+x\in W\right\}\cup\{a\}
;

(iii)

\tilde{L}(a)=\left\{x\in\tilde{W}\mid h(a,x)=0\ \text{and}\ a+x\in\tilde{W}\right\}\cup\{a\}
;

where
a\in V_{0}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

\bm{S}
is a partial linear space of order
(2,2)
.

Proof.

For
a,b\in V_{0}
,
a=b
if and only if
L(a)=L(b)
or
\tilde{L}(a)=\tilde{L}(b)
, and so the number of lines of
\bm{S}
is
63(=9+27+27)
. It follows from
|[a]^{\perp}\cap H_{0}|=|[a]^{\perp}\cap\tilde{H_{0}}|=2
that
L(a)
is the form
\{a,u,a+u\}
for some
u\in W
and
\tilde{L}(a)
is the form
\{a,v,a+v\}
for some
v\in\tilde{W}
. Therefore two vectors of
L(a)
are orthogonal with respect to
s
. By symmetry, the same is true of
\tilde{L}(a)
. These imply that any line of
\bm{S}
is a t.i. line in
S_{6}(2)
-geometry. Thus two distinct lines of
\bm{S}
meet at most one point. Also, each
a\in V_{0}
is contained in
[a],L(a)
and
\tilde{L}(a)
. For each
u\in W
, the set
\{x\in V_{0}\mid h(x,u)=0\ \text{and}\ x+u\in W\}
has three vectors, say
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
. Then
u
is contained in the three lines
L(a_{i})
, for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Similarly, for each
v\in\tilde{W}
, put
\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}=\{x\in V_{0}\mid h(x,v)=0\ \text{and}\ x+v\in\tilde{W}\}
, and
v
is contained in the three lines
\tilde{L}(a_{i})
, for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Hence the lemma follows.
∎

Suppose that
{\mathbb{F}}_{4}={\mathbb{F}}_{2}[\omega]
, where
\omega^{2}+\omega+1=0
.

Lemma 4.3
.

For each
x\in\mathcal{P}
, the union of the three lines containing
x
is a t.i. plane.

Proof.

(i) For each
a\in V_{0}
, the three lines containing
a
are
[a],L(a)
and
\tilde{L}(a)
. Putting
L(a):=\{a,u,v\}(v:=a+u)
, we have
\tilde{L}(a)=\{a,\omega a+u,\bar{\omega}a+u\}
. Since
a,\omega a
and
u
are
{\mathbb{F}}_{2}
-linearly independent and
h(a,u)=0
, it is easy to see that
[a]\cup L(a)\cup\tilde{L}(a)
is a t.i. plane.

(ii) For each
u\in W
, the set
\{x\in V_{0}\mid h(x,u)=0\ \text{and}\ x+u\in W\}
has the three vectors
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
, we see that the three lines containing
u
are
L(a_{i})
for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Since
a_{1}+u,a_{2}+u
and
a_{3}+u
are mutually orthogonal with respect to
h
,
h(a_{i},a_{j})=0
for all
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
, and so
s(a_{i},a_{j})=0
for all
i,j
. Therefore the set
\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\}
is a t.i. line and
a_{3}=a_{1}+a_{2}
. An argument similar to the case (i) shows that
L(a_{1})\cup L(a_{2})\cup L(a_{3})
is a t.i. plane.

(iii) For each
u\in\tilde{W}
, in a similar way to the case (ii), we see that the three lines containing
u
are
\tilde{L}(b_{1}),\tilde{L}(b_{2})
and
\tilde{L}(b_{3})
, where
\{b_{1},b_{2},b_{3}\}:=\{x\in V_{0}\mid h(x,u)=0\ \text{and}\ x+u\in\tilde{W}\}
, and
\tilde{L}(b_{1})\cup\tilde{L}(b_{2})\cup\tilde{L}(b_{3})
is a t.i. plane.
This proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

Take
a,b\in V_{0}
with
h(a,b)=1
. If
|[a,b]\cap H_{0}|=2
, then there exists
u\in W
such that
\{[u+a],[u+b]\}\subset H_{0}
.

Proof.

Putting
\langle u\rangle:=\langle a,b\rangle^{\perp}
, we have from
|[a,b]\cap H_{0}|=2
that
[u]\in H_{0}
. By
|[u,a]\cap H_{0}|=2
, there is
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{\times}
such that
[\alpha u+a]\in H_{0}
, and
h(\alpha u+a,\alpha u+b)=1+1=0
. Therefore
[\alpha u+b]\in H_{0}
. Hence the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

The concurrency graph of
\bm{S}
is connected.

Proof.

By symmetry of
H_{0}
and
\tilde{H}_{0}
, it is enough to check the connectivity of two lines of the following four types:

i)

[a]
and
[b]
;

ii)

[a]
and
L(b)
;

iii)

L(a)
and
\tilde{L}(b)
;

iv)

L(a)
and
L(b)
;

where
a,b\in V_{0}
.

i) By a scalar multiple of
a
, we may choose
a
so that
h(a,b)=1
, and put
\langle u\rangle:=\langle a,b\rangle^{\perp}
.

(i)

If
[u]\in H_{0}
, then by Lemma
4.4
we can choose
u
so that
\left\{[u+a],[u+b]\right\}\subset H_{0}
, so
u\in L(a)\cap L(b)
. Therefore there is the path
\left([a],L(a),L(b),[b]\right)
from
[a]
to
[b]
.

(ii)

If
[u]\in\tilde{H}_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
\{[u+a],[u+b]\}\subset\tilde{H}_{0}
as in Lemma
4.4
, so
u\in\tilde{L}(a)\cap\tilde{L}(b)
. Therefore there is the path
([a],\tilde{L}(a),\tilde{L}(b),[b])
from
[a]
to
[b]
.

ii) If
b\in[a]
, then
|[a]\cap L(b)|=1
, so we may assume
b\notin[a]
and choose
a
so that
h(a,b)=1
. Put
\langle u\rangle:=\langle a,b\rangle^{\perp}
.

(i)

If
[u]\in H_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
u\in L(a)\cap L(b)
as in the statement i)(i). Therefore there is the path
([a],L(a),L(b))
from
[a]
to
L(b)
.

(ii)

If
[u]\in\tilde{H}_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
u\in\tilde{L}(a)\cap\tilde{L}(b)
as in the statement i)(ii). Also,
\tilde{L}(b)\cap L(b)=\{b\}
, and therefore there is the path
([a],\tilde{L}(a),\tilde{L}(b),L(b))
from
[a]
to
L(b)
.

iii) If
a=b
, then
L(a)\cap\tilde{L}(b)=\{a\}
. If
a\neq b
and
b\in[a]
, then there is the path
(L(a),[a],\tilde{L}(b))
from
L(a)
to
\tilde{L}(b)
. Therefore we may assume that
b\notin[a]
. Then there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{\times}
such that
h(\alpha a,b)=1
. Put
\langle u\rangle:=\langle a^{\prime},b\rangle^{\perp}
, where
a^{\prime}:=\alpha a
.

(i)

If
[u]\in H_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
[u+a^{\prime}]\in H_{0}
, so
L(a^{\prime})=\{a^{\prime},u,a^{\prime}+u\}
. Since
h(b+u,a^{\prime}+u)=0
, we have
L(b)=\{b,u,b+u\}
. Therefore there is the path
(L(a),[a],L(a^{\prime}),L(b),\tilde{L}(b))
from
L(a)
to
\tilde{L}(b)
.

(ii)

If
[u]\in\tilde{H}_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
[u+b]\in\tilde{H}_{0}
, so
\tilde{L}(b)=\{b,u,b+u\}
. Since
h(a^{\prime}+u,b+u)=0
, we have
\tilde{L}(a^{\prime})=\{a^{\prime},u,a^{\prime}+u\}
. Therefore there is the path
(L(a),[a],\tilde{L}(a),\tilde{L}(b))
from
L(a)
to
\tilde{L}(b)
.

iv) If
b\in[a]
, then there is the path
(L(a),[a],L(b))
from
L(a)
to
L(b)
, so we may assume
b\notin[a]
. Then there exists
\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{4}^{\times}
such that
h(\alpha a,b)=1
. Put
\langle u\rangle:=\langle a^{\prime},b\rangle^{\perp}
, where
a^{\prime}:=\alpha a
.

(i)

If
[u]\in H_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
u\in L(a^{\prime})\cap L(b)
as in the statement i)(i). Therefore there is the path
(L(a),[a],L(a^{\prime}),L(b))
from
L(a)
to
L(b)
.

(ii)

If
[u]\in\tilde{H}_{0}
, then we can choose
u
so that
u\in\tilde{L}(a^{\prime})\cap\tilde{L}(b)
as in the statement i)(ii). Therefore there is the path
(L(a),[a],\tilde{L}(a^{\prime}),\tilde{L}(b),L(b))
from
L(a)
to
L(b)
.

This completes the proof.
