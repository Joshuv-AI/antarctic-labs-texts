Circular RNA functions in HIV-1 infection
Authors: Gabriela Berger, Y. Grace Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCircular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed RNA molecules with increasingly recognized roles in post-transcriptional gene regulation, immune signalling, and host–pathogen interactions. Although circRNAs have been characterized across diverse biological contexts, their roles in viral infection, particularly HIV-1, remain an underexplored area of RNA biology. Here, we review current knowledge of circRNA function in HIV-1 infection, organized into three major themes. First, we examine host-derived circRNAs, which are broadly dysregulated during infection and correlate with viral load, immune status, and antiretroviral therapy response. Second, we discuss viral-derived circRNAs, highlighting emerging evidence that HIV-1 encodes functional circRNA isoforms capable of modulating viral replication and transcription. Finally, we review synthetic circRNA-based approaches that exploit key viral RNA–protein interactions as potential antiviral tools. Collectively, these findings underscore the expanding functional roles of circRNAs in HIV-1 infection and highlight their potential as both therapeutic targets and molecular tools for future research.

Introduction

Viral infection extensively remodels host RNA regulatory networks, affecting transcription, alternative splicing, RNA stability, and post-transcriptional gene regulation, to facilitate viral replication and persistence. While decades of research have focused primarily on viral proteins and linear RNA transcripts, increasing evidence suggests that circular RNAs (circRNAs) represent an additional and previously underappreciated layer of regulation in host–virus interactions capable of influencing viral replication, immune signalling, and disease progression.

CircRNAs are a class of endogenous RNA molecules characterized by covalently closed-loop structures lacking 5′ and 3′ ends and polyadenylated tails [1]. Initially identified in plant viroids in the 1970s and subsequently observed in eukaryotic cells, hepatitis delta virus, and Sendai virus [2–4], circRNAs were long considered rare splicing artefacts or transcriptional noise arising from aberrant RNA processing [5]. This perception was fundamentally revised in the 2010s with advances in high-throughput RNA sequencing, declining sequencing costs that enabled deeper transcriptome profiling, and the development of circRNA-specific bioinformatic approaches, which together revealed that circRNAs are abundant, evolutionarily conserved, and functionally active regulatory RNA species [6,7]. Subsequent studies revealed that circRNAs often exhibit highly tissue-, developmental stage-, and cell type-specific expression patterns across the mammalian transcriptome [7–9].

CircRNAs are generated from pre-mRNAs through backsplicing, a splicing mechanism in which a downstream splice donor is joined to an upstream splice acceptor, producing a covalently closed RNA molecule [10,11] (Figure 1). Unlike forward splicing, backsplicing generates transcripts without free termini. This circular structure protects circRNAs from exonuclease-mediated degradation, making them considerably more stable than their linear counterparts [12]. Consequently, circRNAs can accumulate to high cellular levels, in some cases exceeding the abundance of their corresponding linear mRNA transcripts [12]. These distinctive structural properties enable circRNAs to perform diverse regulatory functions and have contributed to growing interest in their roles as modulators of gene expression and as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets (Figure 1). Mechanistically, circRNAs influence gene expression through multiple modalities, including interactions with microRNAs (miRNAs) and RNA-binding proteins (RBPs), and regulation of transcription and splicing [13–16]. Through these functions, circRNAs participate in diverse physiological and pathological processes, and dysregulated circRNA expression has been implicated in numerous human diseases, including cancer and cardiovascular and neurological disorders [17–19]. Beyond their roles in endogenous cellular regulation, circRNAs have emerged as important mediators of host-pathogen interactions and antiviral immunity. Host circRNA expression is frequently altered following infection, and circRNAs have been implicated in regulating antiviral signalling pathways, viral replication, and disease pathogenesis [20–22]. In parallel, many viruses have been shown to encode their own circRNAs, several of which promote immune evasion, enhance viral replication, and contribute to tumorigenesis [23–26].

Human immunodeficiency virus-1 (HIV-1) provides a particularly compelling context in which to investigate these roles, given its extensive RNA processing, integration into the host genome, establishment of latent reservoirs, and rapid evolution within hosts [27]. HIV-1 remains a major global public health challenge, with approximately 41.0 million people living with the virus worldwide in 2024 [28]. Progressive infection can lead to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), characterized by severe immunodeficiency, opportunistic infections, and malignancies [29]. Although antiretroviral therapy (ART) effectively suppresses plasma viraemia, it is not curative because the integrated HIV-1 genome persists indefinitely within long-lived CD4+ T cells and other cellular reservoirs in a transcriptionally silent but replication-competent state [30,31]. As a result, interruption of therapy leads to rapid viral rebound and disease progression [32], and persistent low-level viral transcription from these reservoirs contributes to chronic immune activation despite virologic suppression [30,31]. Additionally, the high mutational capacity of HIV-1 further enables immune escape and can contribute to antiretroviral therapy failure [33].

At the molecular level, HIV-1 gene expression depends heavily on host RNA-processing machinery, particularly through extensive alternative splicing that generates multiple viral RNA isoforms from a single primary transcript [34]. Given the central importance of RNA regulation throughout the HIV-1 life cycle, circRNAs are poised to influence multiple aspects of infection, including viral replication, host immune responses, and responses to ART. Yet, research into the roles of circRNAs in HIV-1 infection remains relatively limited. In this review, we summarize current knowledge of circRNA function in the context of HIV-1 infection, focusing on three major areas: (i) Host-derived circRNAs, (ii) Viral-derived circRNAs, and (iii) Synthetic circRNAs (Table 1). Host-derived circRNAs

Host-derived circRNAs as biomarkers of HIV-1 infection and treatment response

Increasing evidence indicates that HIV-1 infection broadly reshapes host-derived circRNA expression across immune cells, including CD4+ T cells and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), with distinct expression patterns observed between viraemic and ART-suppressed individuals [35–37]. These alterations are closely associated with viral replication, immune dysfunction, and ART-mediated viral suppression, supporting the view that host circRNAs may serve as stable, minimally invasive biomarkers of HIV-1 disease activity and host immune status.

Early transcriptome-wide evidence for this biomarker potential was provided by analyses of PBMCs from viraemic individuals. Zhang et al. identified 1,365 host-derived circRNAs that were differentially expressed relative to healthy controls [35]. Among these, circRNA_chr16:68155890–68,160,513+ and circRNA_chr17:59853762–59,857,762- showed stage-specific upregulation during early but not chronic infection, suggesting that circRNA expression profiles may reflect distinct phases of disease progression.

This stage-specific pattern was further examined in the context of ART-mediated viral suppression by Zucko et al., who analyzed publicly available RNA-seq datasets from 19 HIV-1-positive individuals sampled during both viremia and viral suppression. Using a bioinformatic pipeline to compare circRNA profiles between these states, the authors identified a subset of circRNAs that were detectable during active viraemia but absent following ART. In particular, seven circRNAs: circANKRD17, circATXN1, circFAM13B, circFBXW7, circHIPK3, circPHC3, and circZNF609, were detected in all viraemic patients but not after viral suppression, supporting an association between circRNA expression patterns and HIV-1 replication status [36].

The relationship between circRNA expression and treatment status was subsequently assessed in a larger clinical cohort by Fatima et al., who analyzed whole-blood samples from 195 HIV-infected individuals across viraemic, ART-suppressed, and healthy control groups [37]. The study profiled six circulating circRNAs: circZNF292, circMTO1, circBANP, circABCB10, circACP6, and circFAM114A2, and found that all were significantly upregulated in viraemic individuals relative to healthy controls. In ART-suppressed individuals, distinct expression patterns emerged: circZNF292 and circMTO1 exhibited lower expression than in those with active viraemia, whereas circBANP, circACP6, circABCB10, and circFAM114A2 showed further increases and were expressed at higher levels in ART-suppressed individuals than in either viraemic patients or healthy controls. In addition, circMTO1 expression negatively correlated with CD4+ T-cell counts, suggesting a potential association with immune dysfunction and HIV-1 disease progression [37].

Collectively, these findings support the potential utility of circulating host circRNAs as stable biomarkers of HIV-1 disease activity, immune status, and treatment-associated host responses during ART.

Host circRNAs in ceRNA regulatory networks

One of the most widely described functions of circRNAs is their role as competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), in which they regulate gene expression by modulating microRNA (miRNA) availability [14,44] (Figure 2(A)). In this model, circRNAs contain miRNA response elements (MREs) that enable circRNAs to bind and sequester miRNAs. This interaction reduces miRNA-mediated repression of target mRNAs, which can occur through both translational inhibition and mRNA destabilization, thereby indirectly increasing mRNA expression [45]. To investigate potential ceRNA regulatory interactions in HIV-1 infection, Zhang et al. integrated RNA-seq, miRNA-seq, and mRNA expression data from PBMCs of ART-naïve individuals with early infection to construct a computational circRNA–miRNA–mRNA network based on predicted MRE interactions [35]. This approach identified differentially expressed circRNAs, miRNAs, and mRNAs, and inferred potential regulatory relationships by predicting shared miRNA binding sites. Functional enrichment analyses of these predicted networks implicated potential associations with immune signalling, inflammatory responses, antiviral defence, and transcriptional regulation. Notably, 67 circRNAs were predicted to be associated with HIV-relevant host genes, including CCNK, CDKN1A, and IL-15, which are implicated in viral transcription and immune activation [35].

In a follow-up study, Zucko et al. reported that predicted circRNA-associated regulatory networks differed between viraemic and ART-suppressed individuals, indicating context-specific remodelling of predicted ceRNA interactions [36]. Specifically, circRNAs predominant during viraemia were enriched in epigenetic regulation–related processes, whereas those enriched following ART initiation were associated with macroautophagy-related pathways. These findings suggest that circRNA-associated regulatory networks may be shaped by distinct host regulatory states influenced by viral replication and treatment status. Extending this analysis, Zucko et al. identified shared MREs between circRNAs and their corresponding linear mRNAs, some of which were also found in HIV-1 mRNAs. In addition, mRNAs associated with these shared MREs were enriched for RNA polymerase II–related processes, suggesting that these computationally inferred circRNA–miRNA–mRNA relationships may be coupled to broader transcriptional regulatory programmes across both viraemic and ART-suppressed conditions. The authors further identified two host-derived circRNAs, circSATB1 and circHIPK3, that share predicted MREs for antiviral miRNAs, including miR-125b and miR-150 [36]. These miRNAs have been found to be downregulated during HIV-1 infection and associated with reduced viral replication [46], suggesting that circRNA-mediated regulation of miRNA availability may influence antiviral responses.

Extending these broader network analyses, Fatima et al. focused on two individual circRNAs, circZNF292 and circMTO1, which were selected based on their significant upregulation in viraemic HIV-1 patients. Using circRNA interaction databases (circBank, circInteractome), miRNA target prediction tools (miRDB, TargetScan), and Cytoscape for network construction, they reconstructed circRNA–miRNA–mRNA ceRNA networks and performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses to infer functional implications of putative target genes. The predicted targets of circZNF292 included immune-related genes, such as IL23R, FOS, and CCL22, and were enriched in pathways associated with HIV-1 infection, T-cell receptor signalling, and MAPK signalling. GO analysis further implicated RNA polymerase II–mediated transcriptional regulation and protein binding and suggested that the encoded gene products were predominantly associated with nuclear compartments. In contrast, circMTO1-associated targets included genes involved in viral infection and immune regulation, including IL6R, JAK2, and CD4, and were enriched in pathways related to autophagy and cellular stress responses, including mTOR, FoxO, and MAPK signalling. GO analysis likewise implicated transcriptional regulation and protein binding but suggested that the corresponding gene products were predominantly associated with cytoplasmic compartments [37].

These findings suggest that host circRNAs may contribute to HIV-1-associated post-transcriptional regulation by modulating miRNA availability and shaping circRNA–miRNA–mRNA regulatory networks across distinct stages of viral replication and treatment.

Host circRNAs as positive regulators of HIV-1 transcription

Among the best-characterized host circRNAs implicated in HIV-1 pathogenesis is ciTRAN (also known as circSMARCA5), which functions as a positive regulator of HIV-1 transcription [38,39] (Figure 2(B)). During HIV-1 infection, the viral accessory protein viral protein R (Vpr) activates host stress-response and transcriptional pathways, promoting ciTRAN biogenesis through the TGF-β/SMAD signalling axis and subsequent upregulation of the RNA-binding protein Quaking (QKI), a key regulator of ciTRAN formation [39]. As a result, ciTRAN expression is strongly induced during infection and has been found to enhance viral transcription and replication in Jurkat T cells, PBMCs, primary CD4+ T cells, and monocytes [38].

HIV-1 transcription is initiated at the viral long terminal repeat (LTR) by host RNA polymerase II and requires Tat-mediated transcriptional elongation for efficient viral gene expression. ciTRAN promotes this process by binding and sequestering serine/arginine-rich splicing factor 1 (SRSF1), a host restriction factor that suppresses HIV-1 transcription through interference with the viral transcriptional machinery [38]. By reducing SRSF1-mediated repression at the LTR, ciTRAN enhances formation and activity of the Tat–RNA polymerase II elongation complex, thereby increasing viral transcriptional output [38].

The functional importance of this pathway is supported by loss-of-function studies demonstrating that disruption of the ciTRAN–SRSF1 interaction reduces HIV-1 transcription and replication [38]. Similarly, inhibition of TGF-β signalling decreases ciTRAN expression and limits viral replication, further supporting the role of this regulatory axis in HIV-1 pathogenesis [39].

These observations position ciTRAN as a host-derived enhancer of HIV-1 transcription and illustrate how HIV-1 can exploit circRNA-dependent regulatory pathways to optimize viral gene expression. The characterization of ciTRAN further expands the functional repertoire of circRNAs beyond post-transcriptional regulation, highlighting their capacity to directly modulate host–virus interactions and influence viral pathogenesis.

Viral-derived circRNAs

Identification and biogenesis of viral-derived circRNAs

CircRNAs of viral origin have been extensively characterized in several DNA viruses, including Kaposi sarcoma-associated herpesvirus, Epstein–Barr virus, and human papillomavirus [24, 47–50]. In contrast, viral circRNAs remain comparatively understudied in retroviruses, including HIV-1. Recent studies by Mauer et al. [40] and Obi et al. [41] have begun to define the HIV-1 circRNA landscape, providing complementary evidence that HIV-1 generates circRNA isoforms through backsplicing of viral RNA transcripts and establishing some of the first well-characterized examples of viral circRNAs in a retrovirus (Figure 3). Mauer et al. [40] developed a systematic framework for identifying HIV-1-derived circRNAs based on the established viral splice-site landscape. By considering combinations of three major splice donor and six major splice acceptor sites, the authors predicted 15 circRNA isoforms and experimentally evaluated these candidates in HIV-1-infected RevCEM-D4 cells. RT-PCR and sequencing confirmed the predicted back-splice junctions, while isoform-specific qPCR and RNase R resistance further supported the identity and circular nature of these transcripts. Importantly, HIV-derived circRNAs were also detected in primary CD4+ T cells infected ex vivo with HIV-1, extending evidence for their production beyond transformed cell lines [40].

In parallel, Obi et al. [41] used circRNA-enriched RNA sequencing of HIV-1-infected Jurkat-derived clones to independently identify viral circRNAs. Sequencing reads were mapped to the HIV-1 NL4-3 genome, and candidate circRNAs were identified computationally based on back-splice junctions, yielding nine putative viral circRNAs. Among these candidates, circHIV: 5,390–6,044 (hereafter referred to as circHIV) was prioritized based on its abundance, RNase R enrichment, and detection in both infected clones. Notably, circHIV was defined by a back-splice junction between splice acceptor 2 (A2) and splice donor 4 (D4), corresponding to one of the 15 HIV-derived circRNA isoforms independently predicted and experimentally validated by Mauer et al. [40]. The identification of the same circRNA through two distinct approaches provided convergent evidence for circHIV as a bona fide HIV-1-derived circRNA. Further characterization supported its circular structure through RT-qPCR, Sanger sequencing of the back-splice junction, and RNase R-resistant Northern blotting. Beyond molecular validation, Obi et al. detected circHIV across diverse HIV-1 infection models, including Jurkat and J-Lat T-cell lines, ex vivo infected primary CD4+ T cells, and several non-T-cell lines infected with pseudotyped HIV-1. Notably, circHIV was also detected in purified virions and in plasma from people living with HIV-1, demonstrating that circHIV is produced during natural infection and can be detected in both viral particles and clinical samples [41].

HIV-derived circRNAs promote viral replication through miRNA sequestration

Functional characterization of HIV-derived circRNAs has revealed their potential to regulate viral replication through interactions with host regulatory RNAs. Mauer et al. [40] proposed that HIV-derived circRNAs may promote viral replication by sequestering cellular miRNAs with antiviral activity, thereby reducing their ability to suppress viral gene expression. Using bioinformatic prediction, the authors identified 11 cellular miRNAs predicted to interact with HIV-derived circRNAs, seven of which had previously been implicated in cellular proliferation and/or HIV-1 replication. Among these miRNAs, miR-6727-3p and miR-4722-3p were selected for experimental validation based on their predicted binding to a shared sequence present in eight of the 15 HIV-derived circRNA isoforms. Both miRNAs were upregulated following HIV-1 infection of primary CD4+ T cells, consistent with their potential role in the antiviral host response [40].

Luciferase reporter assays confirmed that the predicted interactions were sequence-specific, as miRNA-mediated repression occurred only when the seed-matching region was intact and was abolished following mutation of the binding site. The functional consequences of these interactions were then examined in the context of HIV-1 replication. Expression of miR-6727-3p or miR-4722-3p mimics in HEK293T cells containing the HIV-1 molecular clone pNL4-3 reduced both viral RNA released into the supernatant and infectious viral titres measured. Conversely, expression of a circularized HIV-derived RNA containing the shared miRNA-binding sequence increased viral RNA production and infectious virion output, whereas mutation of the miRNA-binding sequence largely abolished this effect. These findings indicate that the intact miRNA-binding sequence is required for the observed enhancement of viral production. Biotinylated antisense oligonucleotide pull-down assays further supported direct association between the circRNA and both miRNAs, with enrichment observed for the wild-type but not mutant RNA [40].

Overall, these results support a model in which HIV-derived circRNAs enhance viral replication by functionally inhibiting antiviral miRNAs, thereby antagonizing their antiviral activity.

HIV-derived circRNA enhances viral transcription

The functional repertoire of HIV-derived circRNAs extends beyond their proposed roles in miRNA-mediated regulation. Emerging evidence suggests that these transcripts can also influence HIV-1 transcription, with Obi et al. [41] identifying the HIV-1-derived circRNA circHIV as a positive regulator of viral gene expression.

CircHIV enhances HIV-1 gene expression by promoting transcription from the viral LTR promoter. In Jurkat cells, increasing circHIV abundance enhanced LTR-driven reporter activity and viral gene expression, whereas depletion of endogenous circHIV using junction-targeting shRNA or the RNA-targeting enzyme Cas13 reduced both readouts, supporting a positive regulatory role for circHIV in HIV-1 transcription. Consistent with a direct effect on transcription, circHIV overexpression had minimal effects on viral entry, reverse transcription, or integration but increased newly transcribed viral RNA and protein expression. To investigate the mechanism underlying circHIV-mediated transcriptional enhancement, the authors examined whether circHIV interacts with the HIV-1 transactivator protein Tat. RNA immunoprecipitation demonstrated that circHIV was specifically enriched in Tat-containing complexes, whereas linear HIV RNA and control RNAs showed substantially weaker association. This interaction was further supported by in vitro streptavidin pulldown assays using biotin-labelled RNAs, which demonstrated specific binding of Tat to circHIV. Mutational mapping identified nucleotides 93–188 of circHIV as the key region required for Tat binding, defining the RNA segment responsible for this interaction. Competition assays further demonstrated that circHIV does not disrupt the canonical interaction between Tat and the trans-activation response (TAR), a structured RNA element at the 5′ end of nascent HIV-1 transcripts that binds Tat and promotes efficient transcriptional elongation [41].

The functional significance of this interaction was further examined using LTR reporter assays, in which luciferase activity serves as a readout of HIV-1 promoter activity. CircHIV expression increased reporter activity, an effect that was further enhanced by co-expression with Tat in HEK293T cells, indicating that circHIV potentiates Tat-mediated transactivation [41]. Tat normally binds the TAR element within nascent HIV-1 transcripts and recruits host factors that promote RNA polymerase II elongation and synthesis of full-length viral transcripts [51,52]. The ability of circHIV to enhance Tat-dependent LTR activity without disrupting Tat–TAR binding therefore suggests that circHIV may provide an additional RNA-mediated layer of regulation within the transcriptional elongation pathway, potentially by facilitating or stabilizing Tat interactions with the elongation machinery [41]. CircHIV therefore represents a distinct example of an HIV-derived circRNA that positively regulates viral gene expression, revealing a previously unrecognized role for viral circRNAs in the regulation of HIV-1 transcription.

Synthetic circRNAs

CircRNAs as decoy inhibitors of HIV-1 Tat-mediated transcription

The covalently closed structure of circRNAs confers increased stability, making these molecules attractive as durable mimics of native RNA elements or as decoys that engage specific RNA-binding proteins and regulatory factors. These properties have motivated the development of synthetic circRNAs as engineered tools for investigating and modulating RNA–protein interactions, including regulatory pathways relevant to HIV-1 infection. Early proof-of-concept studies in the mid-1990s provided evidence that engineered circRNAs could disrupt viral RNA–protein interactions and modulate HIV-1 transcription [42,43].

This strategy was first explored in HIV-1 by targeting the TAR element, given its critical role in Tat-dependent transcriptional elongation and its defined Tat-binding activity, making it a logical target for a competitive RNA decoy. Bohjanen et al. [42] showed that circularization of TAR preserved its Tat-binding activity while converting the element into a stable RNA decoy capable of inhibiting Tat-mediated transcription. Using a self-splicing group I permuted intron–exon system, the authors engineered a 48-nucleotide circular TAR RNA (‘TAR Circle’) that preserved the native stem–bulge–loop structure required for Tat binding while exhibiting substantially greater stability than its linear counterpart in HeLa nuclear extracts. The TAR Circle specifically bound the TAR-binding domain on Tat and a Tat-associated peptide, confirming retention of RNA–protein interaction specificity. Functionally, the TAR Circle strongly inhibited Tat-dependent transactivation of the HIV-1 LTR in vitro, whereas linear TAR RNA displayed weaker inhibitory activity. Mutations in the TAR bulge or loop significantly reduced inhibitory activity, indicating that inhibition depended on preservation of the native TAR structure [42].

In a follow-up study, the same group investigated the mechanism underlying this inhibition. They found that excess Tat alone could not overcome the inhibitory effects of TAR Circle, whereas addition of Tat together with nuclear extract partially restored transcriptional activity, suggesting that TAR Circle interacts with a Tat-containing complex that includes additional cellular cofactors rather than simply sequestering Tat. Furthermore, TAR Circle did not affect transcription preinitiation complex assembly but instead inhibited transcription at a later stage, selectively disrupting Tat-dependent elongation [43].

Although largely unexplored since these initial studies, this work established that synthetic circRNAs can be designed to modulate HIV-1 transcription through defined RNA–protein interactions. Advances in circRNA engineering now provide new opportunities to build on this foundation and investigate synthetic circRNAs as programmable tools for regulating viral transcription and developing novel antiviral approaches.

Conclusions and future directions

Emerging evidence positions circRNAs as an important regulatory layer in HIV-1 biology, operating across both host and viral systems (Figure 4, Table 1). Host-derived circRNAs are widely dysregulated during infection and have been detected in patient-derived samples, including PBMCs, CD4+ T cells, and whole blood, where their expression is associated with viral load, immune status, and ART response. Beyond their potential as biomarkers of infection and treatment response, individual host circRNAs have been implicated in ceRNA networks and transcriptional regulation, suggesting that they may contribute to the modulation of host gene expression during HIV-1 infection. The identification of functional HIV-1-derived circRNAs further expands this regulatory landscape. Evidence suggests that viral circRNAs can promote viral replication through RNA-mediated regulatory networks and interactions with the viral transcriptional machinery. Complementing these endogenous systems, synthetic circRNA approaches demonstrate that engineered circRNAs can disrupt essential viral RNA–protein interactions within HIV-1 transcriptional regulatory pathways, opening avenues for targeted modulation of viral gene expression. Collectively, these findings establish circRNAs as an emerging and previously underappreciated layer of HIV-1–host interactions, with potential relevance to viral replication, host responses, disease monitoring, and therapeutic development. Building upon these recent discoveries, new questions arise regarding the regulation and functional significance of circRNAs in HIV-1 infection. Although numerous circRNA–miRNA–mRNA networks have been proposed from transcriptomic analyses and database-derived interaction mapping, many remain to be experimentally validated. Further studies are needed to assess whether these predicted interactions reflect bona fide regulatory relationships. For example, targeted circRNA depletion using CRISPR- or antisense-mediated strategies can provide a direct test of whether a candidate circRNA has a regulatory role by determining whether its loss alters the expression of predicted miRNA target mRNAs. Reporter assays incorporating mutated miRNA seed sites can also test whether predicted binding sites are functionally required for miRNA-mediated repression. Argonaute-2 RNA immunoprecipitation (AGO2-RIP) followed by sequencing can further determine whether candidate circRNAs are enriched in AGO2-containing RNA-induced silencing complexes, providing evidence for their association with miRNA-mediated regulation. Together, these approaches will be important for distinguishing causal regulatory relationships from computationally predicted associations.

For host-derived circRNAs, elucidating the contributions of individual circRNAs to HIV-1 latency, reservoir persistence, immune modulation, and disease progression will require physiologically relevant models, including primary cells, tissue-resident reservoirs, and in vivo or humanized systems. At the clinical level, establishing the biomarker potential of host-derived circRNAs depends on larger, longitudinally characterized cohorts with detailed clinical annotation and standardized detection and quantification methods to determine how ART regimen and duration, disease stage, and interindividual heterogeneity influence circRNA expression and diagnostic performance. Extending these analyses across diverse HIV-1 isolates and longitudinal samples spanning acute infection, chronic disease, and ART-mediated viral suppression will be critical for determining the robustness and clinical utility of circRNA biomarkers and for defining how circRNA profiles change over time in relation to virologic and clinical outcomes.

The recent identification of HIV-1-encoded circRNAs raises related questions about the prevalence and functional significance of viral circRNA production. Determining how widely these circRNAs are produced across HIV-1 strains and whether their expression varies with viral subtype or treatment status will help determine whether viral circRNA production represents a conserved feature of HIV-1 infection. Functional studies in primary-cell latency models and clinical isolates, together with more precise approaches to circRNA manipulation, will be important for establishing their physiological relevance and distinguishing genuine biological effects from those associated with circRNA overexpression or nonspecific targeting of back-splice junctions. Understanding the broader significance of HIV-1 circRNAs will also require determining how their biogenesis is integrated with other pathways governing viral RNA fate. Because backsplicing converts pre-mRNA transcripts into circRNAs rather than linear RNAs, circRNA production could alter the availability of viral RNAs needed for other stages of the HIV-1 life cycle. This may be particularly important because circularization occurs in the nucleus, where viral transcripts undergo processing and export before supporting downstream functions such as translation or genome packaging. Determining whether backsplicing reduces the pool of linear viral RNAs available for these functions could clarify whether circRNA production competes with other pathways of HIV-1 RNA utilization. A related question is whether viral factors such as Rev, which promotes nuclear export through binding the Rev response element, influence the balance between circRNA formation and export of linear viral transcripts. Together, these studies could provide insight into how circRNA biogenesis is integrated into HIV-1 RNA processing and the broader viral life cycle.

Advances in RNA engineering provide another opportunity to investigate circRNAs as potential tools for HIV-1 research and therapeutic development. Recent circRNA engineering platforms have expanded the ability to generate synthetic circRNAs with improved circularization efficiency, sequence control, and functional versatility. These approaches include intron-based strategies that use engineered intronic or splice elements to promote circularization of a desired RNA sequence; enzymatic ligation methods that directly join RNA ends to form a covalently closed circle; and intracellular circularization systems that use ribozyme-mediated processing and endogenous RNA ligases to generate circRNAs within cells [53]. These platforms could enable circRNAs to be designed to regulate viral or host regulatory pathways, for example by acting as molecular decoys that alter Tat–TAR interactions, modulating miRNA activity, or supporting sustained production of antiviral proteins. Although these applications have yet to be extensively explored in HIV-1, engineered circRNAs could provide a means of achieving sustained and targeted modulation of viral or host pathways. Translating these technologies into effective therapeutic strategies, however, will require overcoming challenges in delivery, manufacturing, purification, and immunogenicity. Efficient delivery to relevant HIV-1 target and reservoir cells, including CD4+ T cells and macrophages, will be necessary to achieve sufficient intracellular circRNA abundance and activity. At the same time, scalable manufacturing and purification methods will need to generate consistent circRNA preparations while minimizing residual linear RNA and other impurities that could trigger unwanted innate immune responses. Chromatography-based purification and incorporation of modified nucleosides represent potential strategies for improving circRNA purity and reducing immunogenicity. Continued advances in circRNA engineering, delivery, and purification will help translate these platforms from experimental concepts into practical approaches for sustained and targeted modulation of HIV-1 infection.

Expanding research on circRNAs in HIV-1 infection has revealed a previously underappreciated layer of viral–host RNA regulation. By integrating mechanistic studies of host- and viral-derived circRNAs with advances in synthetic circRNA technologies, the field has the potential to reshape our understanding of HIV-1 biology and open new avenues for biomarker discovery and therapeutic development.
