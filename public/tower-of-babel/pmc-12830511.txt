Laparoscopic vs open gastrectomy after neoadjuvant chemotherapy for locally advanced gastric cancer: comparative short- and long-term outcomes
Authors: Mingtao Liang, Jianan Tan, Shengning Zhou, Pengliang Wang, Yequan Xie, Guangyu Zhong, Dejiang Zhou, Fanghai Han, Bin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLaparoscopic gastrectomy (LG) represents a technically challenging procedure for locally advanced gastric cancer (LAGC) after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC). The safety and efficacy profiles of LG following NAC remain incompletely defined. This study aimed to compare short- and long-term outcomes between LG and open gastrectomy (OG) in NAC-treated LAGC patients.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed consecutive LAGC patients undergoing LG or OG following NAC at our institution (Jan 2010-Dec 2019). Data from the prospectively maintained gastric surgery database were evaluated for demographic characteristics, operative parameters, perioperative morbidity, and oncological outcomes.ResultsAmong 85 patients (46 LG, 39 OG), LG showed reduced blood loss, shorter hospitalization, faster bowel recovery, and earlier oral intake versus OG. Operative time was similar (LG 224 ± 64 min vs. OG 183 ± 52 min, P = 0.212). Complication rates were 19.6% (LG) vs. 25.6% (OG) (P = 0.683). At median 52-month follow-up, recurrence rates were 32.6% vs. 41.0% (P = 0.309). Five-year overall survival (51.8% vs. 56.7%, P = 0.492) and disease-free survival (45.3% vs. 52.4%, P = 0.443) showed no significant differences. The analysis involving multiple variables validated that the type of surgical method was not a standalone prognostic indicator for overall survival (P = 0.116) or disease-free survival (P = 0.108).ConclusionsLaparoscopic gastrectomy provides non-inferior technical safety and oncologic outcomes compared to open gastrectomy for LAGC after NAC. Given its benefits of being minimally invasive, such as attenuated surgical trauma and faster postoperative recovery, LG should be considered a standard therapeutic option for appropriately selected patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-04280-7.Keywords: Locally advanced gastric cancer, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Laparoscopic, Long-term outcome

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth globally in incidence and third in mortality, posing a significant public health burden [1, 2]. In China, locally advanced GC(LAGC) comprises a high proportion of total GC cases due to nonspecific symptoms and suboptimal screening, presenting a major clinical challenge [3, 4]. Although radical gastrectomy remains the sole potentially curative approach, long-term outcomes for patients with LAGC remain disappointing. Rapidly accumulating evidences demonstrate that neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) improves oncological outcomes compared to surgery alone in LAGC patients [5–7].

Over recent decades, laparoscopic gastrectomy (LG) has gained significant popularity as a method for treating early-stage gastric cancer (GC) across the globe. This approach has proven to be technical and oncological safety, offering improved short-term postoperative outcomes compared to open gastrectomy (OG) [8, 9]. With advancements in minimally invasive surgery, laparoscopic techniques have been extended to LAGC treatment. Three pivotal multicenter RCTs have demonstrated that laparoscopic distal gastrectomy (LDG) is a non-inferior alternative to open surgery for LAGC patients [10–12]. However, these trials excluded NAC-treated patients. Chemotherapy-induced profibrotic and cytotoxic alterations compromise surgical tissue interfaces, potentially increasing the surgical difficulty and major morbidity risks. Despite the inclusion of NAC-treated patients in certain randomized trials and retrospective studies, current evidence remains limited regarding the safety and efficacy of LG for NAC-treated LAGC [14, 15, 27, 37].Consequently, the applicability of minimally invasive approaches in this setting requires further validation [13–16].

This retrospective cohort study compares the oncological feasibility and technical safety of LG versus OG for LAGC after NAC at a tertiary referral center.

Materials and methods

Patients

During the period from January 2010 to December 2019, 98 consecutive patients diagnosed with histologically confirmed gastric adenocarcinoma who underwent NAC followed by gastrectomy for LAGC at the Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery, Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital (Guangzhou, China) were included in this retrospective analysis. Medical records were consecutively extracted from the prospectively maintained electronic database containing patient demographics, clinical characteristics, and oncologic outcomes.

The eligibility criteria for NAC were clinical stage of T3-4aN + M0 by preoperative evaluation. Patients with emergency surgery, distant metastasis, previous or co-existing other malignancies and incomplete clinicopathological data were excluded. Thus, a total of 85 patients were enrolled in the final analytical cohort. The patients were categorized into two surgical cohorts: LG (n = 46) and OG (n = 39).

Preoperative tumor staging integrated gastroscope with biopsies, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and computed tomography (CT) scan. Patients needing additional diagnostic assessment underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and/or positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT). Standardized NAC protocols predominantly comprised TS-1 + oxaliplatin (SOX), capecitabine + oxaliplatin (CAPOX) or fluorouracil/leucovorin + oxaliplatin (FOLFOX). HER2-positive patients received trastuzumab plus chemotherapy. The evaluation of tumor responses was conducted utilizing the Response Evaluation Criteria In Solid Tumors (RECIST, version 1.1) by analyzing pre-/post-NAC CT scans [17]. Radical gastrectomy was performed 4–6 weeks following the final NAC cycle. The study received approval from the ethics committee and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was given by all participants.

Operative techniques

Both laparoscopic and open surgeries were performed by specialized surgical teams. The choice of surgical approach (open or laparoscopic) depended on the patient’s comorbidities and surgeon’s discretion.More specifically, minimally invasive approaches are generally contraindicated in patients with major cardiopulmonary comorbidities, severe obesity, a history of multiple prior abdominal surgeries (which suggests significant adhesions), or large tumors involving adjacent critical organs that may necessitate multivisceral resection. Conversely, such patients are typically reserved for open surgery.The procedural sequence was identical for both groups. A standard radical gastrectomy accompanied by D2 lymphadenectomy, following the Japanese Gastric Cancer Treatment Guidelines, was carried out in both groups [18]. Diagnostic peritoneal lavage for cytology (DPLC) was not routinely performed in this enrolled patient cohort. En bloc resection of involved adjacent organs was undertaken upon confirmation of macroscopic tumor invasion.Gastrectomy extent (total/subtotal) was dictated by tumor characteristics (size and location). The splenic-hilar lymph nodes(LNs) dissection was performed for LAGCs measuring ≥ 6 cm with primary tumor involvement of the proximal two-thirds of the greater curvature, with splenectomy when necessary. The superior mesenteric venous LNs for distal LAGC were dissected when metastasis to the infrapyloric LNs was suspected.Intra-abdominal drains were routinely placed adjacent to the anastomosis and pancreatic tail to facilitate postoperative monitoring. Reconstruction strategies in both groups were individualized based on residual gastric anatomy and patient comorbidities, including standardized procedures of Billroth I/II or Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy after distal gastrectomy, overlap side-to-side or functional end-to-end esophageal-jejunal anastomosis subsequent to total gastrectomy and esophagogastrostomy after proximal gastrectomy. All anastomoses were created using predefined surgical protocols with either a linear or circular stapling device, ensuring a tension-free, well-vascularized anastomosis.

Perioperative management and follow-up evaluation

All patients in both groups received standardized postoperative care. Intra-abdominal drains were routinely placed, whereas nasogastric tubes were selectively omitted. Oral liquid diet was initiated upon documented flatus. Early mobilization was encouraged based on pain control with oral analgesics and patient tolerance. Patients were discharged once they met the criteria of hemodynamic stability, adequate pain control, independent ambulation and tolerance to oral intake, per ERAS protocols.

Patient staging according to the 8th edition AJCC-TNM gastric cancer criteria was performed to assess pathological findings [19]. Preoperative status was classified using the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status system [20]. Tumor regression grade (TRG) was conducted to evaluate the pathological response to NAC. Complications within 30 days postoperatively were graded per Clavien-Dindo classification (≥ grade II recorded) [21]. Postoperative mortality encompassed all deaths occurring within 30 days after surgery.

Adjuvant chemotherapy was recommended to all the eligible patients within 4–6 weeks postoperatively. The adjuvant chemotherapy regimens were selected based on the preoperative NAC regimen and patient tolerance, primarily continuing with the SOX, CAPOX, or FOLFOX protocols.The most common reasons for incomplete adjuvant chemotherapy were treatment-related adverse events and patient refusal due to compromised general condition following major surgery.Follow-up occurred quarterly for the first two years, then semiannually until five years, and annually thereafter. The surveillance protocol for recurrence included physical examination, serum tumor biomarkers, gastroscopy, abdominal ultrasound, and chest/abdominal CT/MRI. PET-CT scans were performed when clinically indicated for recurrence diagnosis. Recurrence patterns were classified as locoregional (gastric/regional nodes), peritoneal, or nonperitoneal distant. From the time of surgery until death or October 2022, patients were monitored. Long-term oncological outcomes such as overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) were compared between laparoscopic and open gastrectomy cohorts.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 24.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation; categorical variables as frequency (%). Group comparisons employed Mann-Whitney U tests (continuous) and Chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests (categorical). Survival analysis used Kaplan-Meier curves with log-rank testing. Significance threshold was P < 0.05.

Results

The utilization rate of LAGC treated by laparoscopic approach increased annually, approximating 90% after 2019. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Surgical characteristics and perioperative outcomes

Table 2 details intraoperative characteristics and postoperative outcomes of the two groups. Both groups successfully completed all procedures, with the LG cohort experiencing no conversions to open surgery. Four (8.7%) patients in the LG group and 5(12.8%) in the OG group underwent gastrectomy combined with splenectomy, including one total gastrectomy with pancreaticosplenectomy for pancreatic invasion in each group. Despite longer operative time (224 ± 64 vs. 183 ± 52 min; P = 0.212), the laparoscopic gastrectomy group demonstrated significantly reduced surgical blood loss versus the open gastrectomy group (102 ± 72 mL versus 189 ± 94 mL; P < 0.001). The laparoscopic gastrectomy group demonstrated earlier liquid diet intake and shorter postoperative hospital stay in comparison to the OG group (both P < 0.05). No surgery-related mortality occurred in either group. The overall postoperative complication rate of LG group was lower than that of the OG group, but the difference was not statistically significantly (19.6% vs. 25.6%; P = 0.683). Major surgical complications (Clavien-Dindo ≥ III) were observed in 5 individuals (10.9%) from the LG group and in 4 patients (10.3%) from the OG group, with no notable difference between the two (P = 0.927).One patient in each group developed massive intraperitoneal hemorrhage requiring reoperation. A patient from the LG group experienced a leakage at the esophagojejunostomy site following a total gastrectomy, and he was treated effectively by endoscopic stent. One patient in the OG group developed an esophagojejunostomy stricture and was also treated by balloon dilation under endoscope. One patient in the OG group received ultrasonically guided percutaneous drainage due to abdominal lymphatic leakage. Adjuvant chemotherapy completion rates did not differ between groups (P = 0.776).

Oncological outcomes

Following a median follow-up duration of 52 months (with a range of 10 to 129 months) and no loss to follow-up, recurrence developed in 15 laparoscopic gastrectomy patients (32.6%) and 16 open gastrectomy patients (41.0%) (P = 0.309). The sites of recurrence included peritoneal (6 [13.0%] vs. 7 [17.9%]), locoregional (7 [15.2%] vs. 6 [15.4%]), and nonperitoneal distant (5 [10.9%] vs. 5 [12.8%]) in the LG and OG groups, respectively.The recurrence rate was similar between groups (P = 0.309). The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate was 51.8% for the LG group compared to 56.7% for the OG group (P = 0.492; Fig. 2a). In terms of the 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) rate, the figures were 45.3% for LG and 52.4% for OG (P = 0.443; Fig. 2b), demonstrating comparable long-term outcomes between groups.

Discussion

In recent years, LG has gained broad acceptance as a minimally invasive approach and is widely recommended for treating early GC [7, 8]. Meanwhile, the efficacy of LG for LAGC has also been demonstrated by several pivotal RCTs [9–11].While emerging evidence from studies such as the LOGICA trial and other retrospective cohorts [14, 15, 27, 34, 37] has addressed NAC-treated patients, there remains a relative scarcity of data, especially on long-term outcomes in Asian patients, regarding the laparoscopic procedure for LAGC after NAC.The present study found that both LG and OG with curative intent after NAC are safe and feasible for patients with LAGC, yielding similar perioperative and oncological outcomes. As expected, LG for LAGC after NAC demonstrated reduced estimated blood loss, decreased surgical trauma, and accelerated postoperative recovery, while maintaining comparable oncological outcomes compared with OG.

Current management of LAGC involves radical surgery combined with adjuvant and/or neoadjuvant chemotherapy/chemoradiotherapy. Substantial evidences have confirmed that NAC for LAGC induces pathological downstaging of tumor, enhancing R0 resection rate while reducing recurrence rate and metastasis, ultimately improving survival [22–24]. In this research, we exclusively enrolled patients with clinically staged T3-4 N + M0 of LAGC, confirmed by preoperative CT and EUS per Chinese gastric cancer diagnosis and treatment guidelines [6]. The optimal NAC regimen for LAGC has yet to be defined. In our practice, SOX, CAPOX, and FOLFOX were the most frequently administered regimens, with documented clinical response rates of 46.3–61.0% in prior studies [13, 15, 25]. The overall response rate to NAC was 56.5% in both groups, with only 8.2% of patients developing severe chemotherapy-related toxicities (grade III/Ⅳ), aligning with prior reported data [25, 26]. Accurate preoperative staging is essential to optimize patient selection for NAC, maximizing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing surgical morbidity risks.

LG after NAC for LAGC represents a technically demanding procedure. Chemotherapy-induced tissue edema and fibrosis obscure normal anatomical planes, significantly increasing surgical difficulty for safe resection [13, 15]. This high-acuity procedure requires surgeons with advanced expertise at our institution. As a minimally invasive modality, laparoscopy minimizes tissue traction while offering magnified visualization for enhanced exposure. This facilitates reliable identification of critical anatomical structures and surgical planes, enabling elaborate dissection. LG demonstrated significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss compared to OG (P < 0.01), aligning with established literature and substantiating its hematologic benefit [27, 28]. This comparative study further revealed attenuated surgical injury and accelerated postoperative recovery following the laparoscopic approach. Patients in LG group experienced faster gastrointestinal function restoration and shortened hospitalization. Notably, prior NAC research reported a 50% decrease in postoperative complications following LG, partially attributable to the modality’s inherent technical advantages: enhanced visualization, superior exposure, and refined surgical manipulation [15]. This trial observed comparable postoperative complication rates between LG and OG (19.6% vs. 25.6%, P = 0.683), aligning with published morbidity ranges (10.5−30.7%) for post-NAC gastrectomy [15, 27, 29, 30]. With evolving laparoscopic proficiency and instrumentation, LG-associated complication rates for NAC-treated LAGC are projected to decline progressively. These findings establish LG as a safe therapeutic option for NAC-treated LAGC, with safety profiles comparable to OG.

Accurate nodal staging mitigates stage migration and predicts prognosis. and recent evidences confirm ≥ 30 LNs dissected optimizes survival benefit in curatively resected LAGC [31, 32]. With the widespread use of NAC in LAGC, its impact on LN yield remains contentious: some report reduced retrieval due to treatment-induced nodal regression/fibrosis after NAC [27], while other studies show no chemotherapy effect on evaluable LNs [33]. Our present study demonstrated sufficient lymphadenectomy (median yield > 30 nodes in both groups), with comparable total nodal counts and metastatic nodal burden between groups.

The findings regarding long-term outcomes revealed that the laparoscopic gastrectomy and open gastrectomy groups exhibited OS rates at 5 years of 51.8% and 56.7%, alongside DFS rates of 45.3% and 52.4%, respectively, without significant difference between groups. According to the retrospective study conducted by Fujisaki et al. [15], similar OS at 5 years and DFS at 5 years outcomes were reported for laparoscopic gastrectomy versus open gastrectomy in NAC-treated LAGC patients. Additional clinical trials on NAC for LAGC documented 5-year OS ranging from 47% to 58% [22–26, 28–36]. Direct comparison with prior reports is limited by differences in study design and patient selection criteria.Nevertheless, the individuals participating in this research do coincide with those mentioned in previous studies, indicating that the long-term oncologic results of LG are noninferior to OG for NAC-treated LAGC patients. In addition, multivariate analysis further identified survival predictors in NAC-treated LAGC patients. The present research suggested that the surgical method did not exert an independent effect on overall survival or on disease-free survival, supporting LG as a safe and effective alternative to OG in NAC-treated LAGC patients.

However, our study faced several limitations. Firstly, the investigation employed a monocentric retrospective design with restricted total analyzed cases, and randomization was not implemented. Although most demographic and clinicopathologic characteristics were balanced between groups, potential confounding from unmeasured variables may affect result accuracy of the results. Second, incomplete adjuvant chemotherapy courses occurred in a subset of patients, potentially compromising long-term oncological outcomes due to suboptimal therapeutic exposure.Owing to the small sample size, the NAC-immunotherapy combination regimen was not evaluated in the current analysis.

The clear temporal trend, wherein LG was predominantly performed in the later phase of the study (Fig. 1), introduces a potential confounder. Although the core NAC regimens and perioperative care principles remained stable throughout the study period, it must be acknowledged that the decade-long span inherently encompasses broader advancements in multidisciplinary care and institutional expertise. Notably, the LG group achieved non-inferior operative and long-term oncological outcomes compared to the OG group, even during its technical learning curve. Our study provides robust, practical evidence from an evolving clinical setting to support the integration of LG for NAC-treated LAGC. Future multicenter RCTs are warranted to establish definitive conclusions on the optimal surgical strategy. Further well-designed multicenter RCTs are imperative to establish robust conclusions regarding optimal surgical approaches for this patient population.

In conclusion, this retrospective study clarified the technical safety and feasible of LG for NAC-treated LAGC when performed by experienced laparoscopic surgeons. LG demonstrated non-inferior long-term survival outcomes compared to OG. Given the inherent advantages of minimally invasive approaches—including attenuated surgical trauma and faster postoperative recovery, LG represents a viable therapeutic alternative for LAGC patients following NAC.
