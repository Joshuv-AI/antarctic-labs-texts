Coupled Wasserstein Gradient Flows for Min-Max and Cooperative Games
Lauren Conger a,, Franca Hoffmann b,, Eric Mazumdar c,, Lillian J. Ratliff d,

Abstract
We propose a framework for two-player infinite-dimensional games with cooperative or competitive structure. These games take the form of coupled partial differential equations in which players optimize over a space of measures, driven by either a gradient descent or gradient descent-ascent in Wasserstein-2 space. We characterize the properties of the Nash equilibrium of the system, and relate it to the steady state of the dynamics. In the min-max setting, we show, under sufficient convexity conditions, that solutions converge exponentially fast and with explicit rate to the unique Nash equilibrium. Similar results are obtained for the cooperative setting. We apply this framework to distribution shift induced by interactions among a strategic population of agents and an algorithm, proving additional convergence results in the timescale-separated setting. We illustrate the performance of our model on (i) real data from an economics study on Colombia census data, (ii) feature modification in loan applications, and (iii) performative prediction. The numerical experiments demonstrate the importance of distribution-level, rather than moment-level, modeling.

Introduction

Gradient flows generalize gradient descent to infinite dimensional spaces, for instance when probability distributions evolve in the direction of steepest descent for a given functional and metric. Recent years have seen a surge in the application of gradient flow theory including work in Monte Carlo sampling

This interdependence results in a system of coupled PDEs that globally can no longer be viewed as a gradient flow on a single functional. Due to the loss of the gradient flow structure, this class of PDEs can exhibit complex dynamics like cycling or chaos. Such systems model many types of phenomena, ranging from chemotaxis

In such settings, natural questions that arise include the existence and characterization of solutions and steady states, and convergence of the coupled PDE system to these steady states (if they exist). Current results focus on proving existence of solutions for some of these systems

In this work we study multispecies systems that arise from competitive and cooperative games. In these games the two species either seek to maximize the same functional, or compete with one maximizing and the other minimizing the functional—i.e., they are infinite dimensional players in a cooperative or min-max game. We show that the steady states of the resulting system of partial differential equations (PDEs) coincide with the equilibria of the underlying games and characterize their rates of convergence under natural structural assumptions on the corresponding functionals. The convergence of Wasserstein-2 gradient flows for min-max problems over spaces of measures was recently posed as an open question in
[
82

We use these results to investigate the long-term effects of strategic interactions in driving distribution shift in real-world machine learning contexts.
In many machine learning systems, agents whose data is analyzed by the system are incentivized to manipulate their data to achieve a desired output. Additionally, distribution shift can occur naturally, or agents share information that causes other players to evolve. This behavior is not well-understood and has become a subject of recent interest; see for instance

In settings where the objective of the learning algorithm opposes that of the agents, the update process can be modeled as a min-max problem over a large number of agents, which in a mean-field limit can be analyzed as an optimization problem over measures. In particular, we incorporate intra-agent interactions in the model via an interaction potential, exogenous shifts, and strategic responses to the algorithm. We illustrate how these model components capture rich distributional behavior (see Section

In this work, we consider two-species systems with an energy functional containing potential terms, self-interaction kernels, diffusion, and a coupling term which is linearly dependent on both species. Each species evolves according to a Wasserstein-2 gradient flow with respect to its own energy differential, in the direction of steepest ascent or descent. The resulting dynamical system is a joint gradient flow in the setting where both species descend, or a gradient descent-ascent flow, in the setting of opposing dynamics. In line with intuitive notions from game theory, we call the joint gradient flow setting the
cooperative
setting, because one can view the resulting dynamics as a game in which both players aim to achieve the same objective of minimizing the same energy. We name the gradient descent-ascent case the
competitive
setting, due to the zero-sum game structure in which one player aims to maximize a function which the other player aims to minimize.

In order to show uniform boundedness of the second moments for both species, we show that they converge exponentially fast to a ball, and then remain inside that ball for all time.
Finally, we show that the steady state is in fact a critical point of
F_{c}
, and the unique Nash equilibrium.
While the convexity and smoothness assumptions can be generalized, even mild relaxations on the lower-bounds in finite dimensions do not give the same guarantees. For example, in Euclidean space, assuming that the energy satisfies a Polyak Łojasiewicz (PŁ) condition instead of convexity results in non-uniqueness of Nash equilibria.
With respect to convexity, our results mirror the state-of-the-art guarantees existing for finite-dimensional games. However, relaxing the regularity assumptions on the functionals is likely possible, and an interesting direction of future research.

Related Literature

The existence and convergence results utilize tools from long-time behavior analysis of PDEs and game theory, and apply to multispecies PDEs with a cooperative or competitive structure. Our results extend what is known in min-max problems with applications in machine learning, DRO, and strategic distribution shifts.

Game Theory

Although the dynamical system we study is a system of PDEs, the steady state of the system can be analyzed through the lens of game theory and optimization. In game theory, existence of equilibria in min-max problems has been studied over spaces of (1) deterministic strategies or (2) probabilistic strategies over compact sets

Recent progress in developing algorithms to solve infinite-dimensional min-max optimization problems includes

1.2
Contributions

Our contributions in this work are twofold; first on the theoretical side, our theorems extend what is known in the PDE literature and optimization literature. Secondly, we apply our framework to illustrate the importance of modeling distribution shift in strategic populations in machine learning.

Theory

This framework sits at the intersection of PDE analysis and optimization, providing contributions to each of these fields. From an optimization perspective, the existence of a Nash equilibrium over measures on unbounded sets has been an open question. Since existence is unknown, there are no systematic tools for computing equilibria and in particular, convergence of gradient descent-ascent to an equilibrium is an open problem
[
82
]
. We expand this area of game theory in two key ways.

1.

Classical proofs for existence of Nash equilibria assume optimization over compact spaces of measures; we prove results without this assumption by showing contraction in
\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

2.

By analyzing distributions over action spaces rather than deterministic actions, the achieved equilibrium is not restricted to a pure Nash equilibrium; it can be a mixed Nash equilibrium. Outside of specific games, such as ones with a finite number of actions or structure that allows direct computation via calculus of variations, computing mixed Nash equilibria over continuous action spaces is difficult to solve in the general setting. Our results suggest that the gradient ascent-descent structure in Wasserstein-2 offers a solution.

From a PDE perspective, this setting opens the door
for using techniques from calculus of variations and gradient flows in metric spaces.
In particular, it can be framed in the language of multi-species systems, a field for which only very few and recent results exist on asymptotics via entropy methods. We show the existence of a unique steady state and exponential convergence to it with explicit rates in four different two-species settings. This extends what is known about long-time asymptotics for systems of coupled PDEs; in particular, the technical contributions include the following.

1.

In the cooperative setting, classical functional inequalities are extended to the case of multiple species.

2.

In the competitive setting, convergence is proven without the use of timescale separation; this requires entirely different proof techniques both for existence of the steady state and convergence. Direct differentiation of
\mathscr{W}_{2}
results in convergence, and existence of a unique Nash equilibrium is shown via contraction. A dynamical systems-type argument is used for uniform estimates of the second moments.

3.

We demonstrate in a particular application setting how a Danskin-type result (also known as an envelope theorem in analysis) can be obtained from basic assumptions using a
\Gamma
-convergence argument (see Proposition
B.5
). This removes a key assumption in
[
25
]
on the differentiability of the best response (see
[
25
, Lemma 29]
). Such a
\Gamma
-convergence approach is expected to generalize to other choices of functionals.

Application

One particular setting in which models of type (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) appear is when machine learning algorithms interact with strategic populations
[
25
]
. In many real-world settings, populations dynamically adapt their strategy based on algorithm behavior. Optimization methods for algorithms do not usually account for this data manipulation, and we provide examples illustrating why modeling distribution shift in the face of learning is critical for improved performance.

1.

We illustrate our model on real data from an economics study (see Section
5.1
), a setting in which agents manipulated data in response to the action of an algorithm, showing that our model is able to accurately capture such behavior.

2.

We show the importance of modeling distribution shift in detail. A state-of-the-art performative prediction method, based on mean shift, is outperformed when the classifier follows a simple gradient descent scheme. We also illustrate how modeling population interactions can be overlooked when looking at classifier accuracy, but these interaction terms matter when considering classifier performance on certain subpopulations.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=-\nabla_{\mathscr{W}_{2}}G(\rho)=\mathrm{div}\left(\rho\nabla\delta_{\rho}G[\rho]\right)\,.

(2.1)

All the coupled gradient flows considered in this work have this Wasserstein-2 structure.

The main goal in our theoretical analysis is to characterize the asymptotic behavior for the models (

The steady states of these equations are the natural candidates to be asymptotic profiles for the corresponding dynamics. Thanks to the gradient flow structure, we expect to be able to make a connection between critical points of the energy functionals and the steady states of the corresponding gradient ascent/descent dynamics. More precisely, any minimizer or maximizer is in particular a critical point of the energy, and therefore satisfies that the first variation is constant on disconnected components of its support. If this ground state also has enough regularity (weak differentiability) to be a solution to the equation, it immediately follows that it is in fact a steady state. Main Results

The convergence analysis of these systems allows us to understand and predict the long-time behavior of the dynamics.
The asymptotics are given by the ground and saddle states of the energy functionals
F_{a}
and
F_{c}
respectively. We prove existence and uniqueness of the critical points of the functionals and, under sufficient convexity criteria, convergence with explicit rates.

Remark 3.1

(Cauchy-Problem)

To execute the arguments on convergence to equilibrium, we require sufficient regularity of solutions to the PDEs under consideration.
In fact, it is sufficient if we can show that equations (

1.4

[

are expected to apply to the coupled gradient flows considered here to guarantee existence of smooth solutions with fast enough decay at infinity. In this work, we do not focus on the existence and regularity of solutions.

3.1

We use subsets of the following assumptions in the cooperative and competitive cases.

4
Application: Distribution Shift in Machine Learning

Machine learning algorithms in real-world settings often update their parameters over time to improve performance; as more data is collected, it is natural for the algorithm to update based on more recent information. However, in common applications the distribution of data on which the algorithm is training may not be stationary over time. This phenomenon is known as
distribution shift
, and is induced from a variety of causes, including mis-aligned incentives, interactions with other agents, and natural causes.

The model in
[
25
]
proposes an energy functional which the algorithm seeks to minimize and which the population aims to maximize; some terms have dependence on both distributions while others model energies specific to the evolution of agents and algorithms respectively. The model in
[
25
]
can be seen as a special case of the competitive setting considered here by choosing

\displaystyle f(z,x)

\displaystyle=f_{1}(z,x)\,,\quad W_{1}(z)=W(z)\,,\quad\alpha>0\,,\quad\beta=0\,,

\displaystyle V_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=-\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\,,\quad V_{2}(x)=\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\,,

with all other terms set to zero and
d_{1}=d_{2}=d
. Here,
\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are fixed reference measures.
Let us denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\rho,\mu)=\iint f_{1}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\iint f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2}\int W\ast\rho(z)\,\rho(z)+\alpha KL(\rho\,|\,\tilde{\rho})\,,\quad\mathcal{Q}(\mu)=\frac{\kappa}{2}\int\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,.

The functional
\mathcal{V}(\rho,\mu)
models the cost which the algorithm seeks to minimize, and the population minimizes or maximizes depending on the setting. For example, when
\mu
represents a binary classifier, the distribution
\pi
models individuals carrying the true label 1, and the distribution
\rho(t)
model individuals carrying a true label 0, where 0 and 1 denote the labels of two classes of interest. The term
\int f_{1}(z,x)\mu(t,\mathrm{d}x)
represents a penalty for incorrectly classifying an individual with features
z
with true label 0 when using the classifier
\mu(t,x)
. Analogously, the term
\int f_{2}(z,x)\pi(\mathrm{d}z)
is large if
x
incorrectly classifies the population
\pi
that carries the true label 1. The functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
can be chosen according to the application; a standard choice for classification problems is the logarithmic loss
[
68
]
.

The functional
\mathcal{Q}(\mu)
is a regularizer for the algorithm; this penalizes the classifier for selecting extreme learning parameters, and provides convexity for the loss function. The coefficient
\kappa>0
parameterizes the strength of the regularizer.

The functional
\mathcal{R}(\rho)
contains two terms, the Kulbeck-Leibler divergence (denoted
KL
), also called the relative entropy, and the interaction term driven by the potential
W
. The term
\alpha KL(\rho|\tilde{\rho})
forces the evolution of
\rho(t)
to approach
\tilde{\rho}
. In other words, it penalizes (in energy) deviations from a given reference measure
\tilde{\rho}
. In many application settings, we take
\tilde{\rho}
to be the initial distribution
\rho(t=0)
. The solution
\rho(t)
then evolves away from
\tilde{\rho}
over time due to the other forces that are present. Therefore, the term
KL(\rho\,|\,\tilde{\rho})
in the energy both provides smoothing of the flow and a penalization for deviations away from the reference measure
\tilde{\rho}
.

The self-interaction term
W\ast\rho
introduces non-locality into the dynamics, as the decision for any given individual to move in a certain direction is influenced by the behavior of all other individuals in the population. The choice of
W
is application dependent. Very often, the interaction between two individuals only depends on the distance between them. This suggests a choice of
W
as a radial function, i.e.
W(z)=\omega(|z|)
. A choice of
\omega:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\omega^{\prime}(r)>0
corresponds to an
attractive
force between individuals, whereas
\omega^{\prime}(r)<0
corresponds to a
repulsive
force.

However, in many real-world applications of this model, either the population or the algorithm may update much faster than the other. For example, many government policies are updated on a much slower timescale compared with how quickly individuals can adjust their response. In settings like advertising algorithms, companies can update their algorithms faster than users will adjust search strategies or viewing patterns. We provide convergence results for these two settings for competitive objectives, taking the timescale separations to be large enough that one entity instantly minimizes or maximizes its objective while the other entity evolves over time. Here we consider the case
\mu(t)=\delta_{x(t)}
where
x(t)
solves
\dot{x}(t)=-\nabla_{x}L(\rho,x)
with loss function
L(\rho,x)=\int f_{1}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+V_{2}(x)
. The energy in these two setting is denoted
G(\rho,x)
.
The energy functional is given by

\displaystyle G(\rho,x)\coloneqq\int f_{1}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\mathcal{R}(\rho)\,.

When the algorithm updates quickly relative to the population, we consider dynamics given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\nabla_{\mathscr{W}_{2},\rho}G(\rho,x)|_{x=b(\rho)}\,,\qquad b(\rho)\coloneqq\amin_{\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(\rho,\bar{x})\,.

(4.2)

In the opposite setting where the population is fast relative the algorithm, we can consider the population immediately responding to the algorithm, which results in the dynamics

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}x=-\nabla_{x}G(\rho,x)|_{\rho=r(x)}\,,\qquad r(x)\coloneqq\amax_{\hat{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}}G(\hat{\rho},x)\,.

(4.3)

In this time-scale separated setting, model (
4.2
) is a dynamic maximization of
G
with respect to
\rho
in Wasserstein-2 space, and an instantaneous minimization of
G
with respect to the algorithm parameters

. Model (
4.3

G

\rho

G

5
Insights from Numerical Experiments

In this section, we provide examples of numerical experiments that illustrate how our framework models real-world data and implications of using various algorithm learning strategies while interacting with a dynamic, strategic population. First, we model data from an economic study of how local government officials are incentivized to misreport census data. Then we show how individuals modify loan application data to achieve a more desirable outcome from a classifier algorithm, comparing different interaction models among agents and showing how subpopulations are affected differently. Finally, we illustrate our strategic population model under a state-of-the-art performative prediction algorithm, showing that it is critical to consider distribution shift beyond only mean shift when optimizing performance. The numerical experiments are implemented using the finite volume method from
[
19
]
.

5.2
Loan Applications: Feature Modification

In settings such as loan applications, agents not eligible for a loan (label 0) aim to be misclassified to receive a more desirable outcome, such as qualifying for a loan (algorithm predicts label 1). In this numerical experiment, we consider real loan application data from

Cooperative Setting (Proof of Theorem

Denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\rho,\mu)

\displaystyle=\iint f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\int V_{1}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int V_{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,,

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}(\rho,\mu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int(W_{1}\ast\rho)(z)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int(W_{2}\ast\mu)(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\,,

so that the functional

is given by

\displaystyle F_{a}(\rho,\mu)=\mathcal{V}(\rho,\mu)+\alpha H(\rho)+\beta H(\mu)+\mathcal{W}(\rho,\mu)\,.

In order to prove the existence of a unique ground state for

, a natural approach is to consider the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equations

\displaystyle\alpha\log\rho(z)+\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+V_{1}(z)+(W_{1}\ast\rho)(z)

\displaystyle=c_{1}[\rho,\mu]\quad\text{ for all }z\in\supp(\rho)\,,

(6.1a)

\displaystyle\beta\log\mu(x)+\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+V_{2}(x)+(W_{2}\ast\mu)(x)

\displaystyle=c_{2}[\rho,\mu]\quad\text{ for all }x\in\supp(\mu)\,,

(6.1b)

where

are constants that may differ on different connected components of

and

-convexity properties for the functional

(Lemma

and Proposition

), where

essential properties that will allow us to deduce existence and uniqueness of ground states using the direct method in the calculus of variations (Proposition

). Using the Euler-Lagrange equation (
6.1
), we then prove properties on the support of the ground state (Corollary

).
To obtain convergence results, we apply the HWI method: we first show a general ’interpolation’ inequality among the energy, the energy dissipation and the metric (Proposition

Proposition 6.2

respectively, we have
F_{a}\geq 0
by Pinsker’s inequality and using
W_{i}\geq 0
.
∎

Proposition 6.5
.

(Ground state)
The functional
F_{a}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})\to[0,\infty]
admits a unique minimizer
\gamma_{*}=(\rho_{*},\mu_{*})
which satisfies
\gamma_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
. Moreover, if
\alpha>0
it satisfies
\rho_{*}\in L^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
, and if
\beta>0
it satisfies
\mu_{*}\in L^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
.

Proof.

We show existence of a minimizer of
F_{a}
using the direct method in the calculus of variations. Denote by
\gamma=(\rho,\mu)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}
a pair of probability measures as a point in the product space of Radon measures. Since
F_{a}\geq 0
on
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
by Lemma
6.4
and not identically
+\infty
everywhere, there exists a minimizing sequence
(\gamma_{n})\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
. Note that
(\gamma_{n})
is in the closed unit ball of the dual space of continuous functions vanishing at infinity
(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}))^{*}
endowed with the dual norm
\left\lVert\gamma_{n}\right\rVert_{*}=\sup\frac{|\int f\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}+\int g\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}|}{\left\lVert(f,g)\right\rVert_{\infty}}
over
f,g\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}})
with
\|(f,g)\|_{\infty}:=\|f\|_{\infty}+\|g\|_{\infty}\neq 0
.
By the Banach-Alaoglu theorem
[
73
, Thm 3.15]
there exists a limit
\gamma_{*}=(\rho_{*},\mu_{*})\in\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}=(C_{0}\times C_{0})^{*}
and a convergent subsequence (not relabelled) such that
\gamma_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\gamma_{*}
.

It remains to show that
\int\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}=\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}=1
to conclude that
\gamma_{*}\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
. To this aim, it is sufficient to show tightness of
(\rho_{n})
and
(\mu_{n})
, preventing the escape of mass to infinity as we have
\int\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}=\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}=1
for all
n\geq 1
. Tightness follows from Markov’s inequality
[
45
]
if we can establish uniform bounds on
the second moments, i.e. we want to show that there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
n
such that

\int\|z\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\int\|x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)<C\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

(6.4)

To establish (
6.4
), observe that thanks to Assumption
1
, there exist a constant
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and vector
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
such that
f(z,x)\geq c_{0}\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}z,x\end{bmatrix}+c_{1}\right\rVert^{2}
for all
[z,x]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}+d_{2}}
.
Additionally, from Lemma
C.3
applied with
\varepsilon=c_{0}/\left(2\max\{\alpha,\beta\}(\left\lVert c_{1}\right\rVert+1)\right)
, we have that
\mathcal{H}(\rho)\geq-\varepsilon\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)-c_{\varepsilon}
for some
c_{\varepsilon}\geq 0
, with the analogous bound for
\mathcal{H}(\mu)
. Then

\displaystyle c_{0}\iint

\displaystyle\left\lVert[z,x]+c_{1}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}(z,x)\leq\iint f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\gamma_{n}(z,x)

\displaystyle\leq F_{a}(\gamma_{n})+\varepsilon\left(\alpha\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\beta\int\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\right)+(\alpha+\beta)c_{\varepsilon}\,.

Since
\frac{\left\lVert[z,x]\right\rVert^{2}}{\left\lVert c_{1}\right\rVert+1}-\left\lVert c_{1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert[z,x]+c_{1}\right\rVert^{2}
, the estimate can be rearranged to

\displaystyle\frac{c_{0}}{2(\left\lVert c_{1}\right\rVert+1)}\left(\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\int\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(x)\right)\leq F_{a}(\gamma_{n})+\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}\leq F_{a}(\gamma_{1})+\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}<\infty\,,

where
\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq c_{0}\left\lVert c_{1}\right\rVert+(\alpha+\beta)c_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, the second moment is uniformly bounded.
This concludes the proof that the limit
\gamma_{*}
satisfies
\gamma_{*}\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}
, and indeed
\rho_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}),\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
as well.
Further, note that the above second moment bound implies that
(\gamma_{n})
also converges weakly according to Definition
2.1
. Finally,
\gamma_{*}
is a minimizer of
F_{a}
thanks to weak lower-semicontinuity of
F_{a}
following Lemma
6.1
.

If
\alpha>0
, the ground state
\rho_{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\rho_{*}(z)=c_{3}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)+(W_{1}\ast\rho_{*})(z)+V_{1}(z)\right)\right)\quad\text{on }\supp\rho_{*}\,.

and so
\rho_{*}\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
.
Similarly, if
\beta>0
, the ground state
\mu_{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mu_{*}(x)=c_{4}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(z)+(W_{2}\ast\mu_{*})(x)+V_{2}(x)\right)\right)\quad\text{on }\supp\mu_{*}\,,

and we have that
\mu_{*}\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
.

Next we show uniqueness using a contradiction argument.
Suppose
\gamma_{*}=(\rho_{*},\mu_{*})
and
\gamma_{*}^{\prime}=(\rho_{*}^{\prime},\mu_{*}^{\prime})
are minimizers of
F_{a}
. For
s\in[0,1]
, define

\gamma_{s}:=((1-s)\id+sT,(1-s)\id+sS)_{\#}\gamma_{*}
, where
T,S:\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
are the optimal transport maps such that
\rho_{*}^{\prime}=T_{\#}\rho_{*}
and
\mu_{*}^{\prime}=S_{\#}\mu_{*}
. By Proposition
6.2
the energy
F_{a}
is uniformly displacement convex, and so we have

\displaystyle F_{a}(\gamma_{s})\leq(1-s)F_{a}(\gamma_{*})+sF_{a}(\gamma_{*}^{\prime})=F_{a}(\gamma_{*}).

If
\gamma_{*}\neq\gamma_{*}^{\prime}
and
s\in(0,1)
, then strict inequality holds by applying similar arguments as in
[
64
, Proposition 1.2]
.
However, the strict inequality
F_{a}(\gamma_{s})<F_{a}(\gamma_{*})
for
\gamma_{*}\neq\gamma_{*}^{\prime}
is a contradiction to the minimality of
\gamma_{*}
. Hence, the minimizer is unique.
∎

Corollary 6.6
.

Any minimizer
\gamma_{*}=(\rho_{*},\mu_{*})
of
F_{a}
is a steady state for equation (
1.3
) according to Definition
2.2a
. If
\alpha>0
, then
\supp(\rho_{*})=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
and
\rho_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
. If
\beta>0
, then
\supp(\mu_{*})=\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
\mu_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
.

Proof.

By Proposition
6.5
, we have
\rho_{*},\mu_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
. As
\gamma^{*}
is a minimizer, it is in particular a critical point, and therefore satisfies equations (
6.1
). In order to show that
\gamma_{*}
is a steady state for equation (
1.3
), we need to show that
\nabla W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}\in L_{loc}^{1}
and
\nabla W_{2}\ast\mu_{*}\in L_{loc}^{1}
, and that if
\alpha>0
, then
\rho_{*}\in W^{1,2}_{loc}\cap L^{1}_{+}\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}
and if
\beta>0
, then
\mu_{*}\in W^{1,2}_{loc}\cap L^{1}_{+}\cap L^{\infty}_{loc}
.

We claim that
\int f(z,\tilde{x})\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(\tilde{x})<\infty
,
\int f(\tilde{z},x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(\tilde{z})<\infty
,
W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}(z)<\infty
and
W_{2}\ast\mu_{*}(x)<\infty
for any fixed
(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Indeed, each term in the energy without the interaction and coupling potentials can be lower-bounded by a constant separately using positivity of the potentials and Lemma
C.3
together with the second moment bound for
\gamma_{*}
. Hence,
\iint f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(z)<\infty
,
\int(W_{1}\ast\rho_{*})(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(z)<\infty
and
\int(W_{2}\ast\mu_{*})(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)<\infty
as
\gamma_{*}
is a minimizer. This concludes the bounds, and so we also obtain
\nabla W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}\in L_{loc}^{1}
and
\nabla W_{2}\ast\mu_{*}\in L_{loc}^{1}
since
W_{1},W_{2}\in C^{2}
.

If
\alpha=\beta=0
, we can differentiate (
6.1
) directly to obtain (
2.2
).
Now, consider the case
\alpha>0,\beta>0
. Rearranging (
6.1
), we obtain (for possibly different constants
c_{1}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}],c_{2}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}]\neq 0
) that

\displaystyle\rho_{*}(z)

\displaystyle=c_{1}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}]\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)+W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}(z)+V_{1}(z)\right)\right]}\quad\text{ on }\supp(\rho_{*})\,,

(6.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{*}(x)

\displaystyle=c_{2}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}]\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(z)+W_{2}\ast\mu_{*}(x)+V_{2}(x)\right)\right]}\quad\text{ on }\supp(\mu_{*})\,,

and so
\rho_{*},\mu_{*}\in L^{1}_{+}
.
Then for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in K}\rho_{*}(z)\leq c_{1}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}]\sup_{z\in K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)+V_{1}(z)\right)\right)\sup_{z\in K}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}\right).

As
f,V_{1},W_{1}\geq 0
, the exponential terms on the right-hand side are finite. Therefore
\rho_{*}\in L_{loc}^{\infty}
.
To show that
\rho_{*}\in W_{loc}^{1,2}
, note that for any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
, we have
\int_{K}|\rho_{*}(z)|^{2}\mathrm{d}z<\infty
as a consequence of
\rho_{*}\in L_{loc}^{\infty}
.
Moreover, defining
T[\gamma](z)\coloneqq-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+W_{1}\ast\rho(z)+V_{1}(z)\right)\leq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{K}|\nabla\rho_{*}|^{2}\mathrm{d}z=c_{1}[\rho_{*},\mu_{*}]^{2}\int_{K}|\nabla T[\gamma_{*}]|^{2}\exp(2T[\gamma_{*}])\mathrm{d}z\,,

which is bounded noting that
\exp(2T[\gamma_{*}])\leq 1
and that
\nabla T[\gamma_{*}](\cdot)
is in
L^{\infty}_{loc}
, since
f(\cdot,x),W_{1}(\cdot),V_{1}(\cdot)\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
by Assumptions
1
-
3
. We conclude that
\rho_{*}\in W^{1,2}_{loc}
, and use an identical argument for the case when
\beta>0
, and indeed
(\rho_{*},\mu_{*})
solves (
2.2a
) in the sense of distributions as a consequence of (
6.1
).

Next, we show that if
\alpha>0
then
\supp(\rho_{*})=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
using again the relation (
6.5
).
Indeed,
\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)+W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}(z)+V_{1}(z)\right)\right]}>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
since
\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)<\infty
,
W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}(z)<\infty
and
V_{1}(z)<\infty
.
Then, thanks to continuity of
f\in C^{2}
,
V_{1}\in C^{2}
, and
W_{1}\ast\rho_{*}(z)\in C^{2}
, we conclude

\rho_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
. The same argument is applied to
\mu_{*}
when
\beta>0
to obtain
\supp\mu_{*}=\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
and
\mu_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
.
∎

6.2
Functional Inequalities

The following inequality is referred to as HWI inequality and represents the key result to obtain convergence to equilibrium.

Proposition 6.7

(HWI inequality)
.

Define the dissipation functional

\displaystyle D_{a}(\gamma):=\iint\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{a}[\rho,\mu](z)\\
\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{a}[\rho,\mu](x)\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(z,x)\,.

Let
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
{F_{a}(\gamma_{0}),F_{a}(\gamma_{1})\allowbreak,D_{a}(\gamma_{0})<\infty}
, and let Assumptions
1
(i),
2
and
3
hold with
\lambda_{a}>0
. Then

\displaystyle F_{a}(\gamma_{0})-F_{a}(\gamma_{1})\leq\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma_{0})}-\frac{\lambda_{a}}{2}\,\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})^{2}\,.

(6.6)

Proof.

For simplicity, consider
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
that have smooth Lebesgue densities of compact support. The general case can be recovered using approximation arguments. Let
(\gamma_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
denote a
\overline{\mathscr{W}}
-geodesic between
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1}
. Following similar arguments as in
[
21
]
and
[
69
, Section 5]
and making use of the calculations in the proof of Proposition
6.2
, we have

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{a}(\gamma_{s})\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle=\iint\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}(z)\\
\xi_{2}(x)\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla\phi(z)-z)\\
(\nabla\psi(x)-x)\end{bmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}(z,x)\,,

where

\displaystyle\xi_{1}[\gamma_{0}](z)

\displaystyle:=\int\nabla_{z}f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}(x)+\alpha\nabla_{z}\log\rho_{0}(z)+\nabla W_{1}\ast\rho_{0}(z)+\nabla_{z}V_{1}(z)=\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{a}[\gamma_{0}](z)\,,

\displaystyle\xi_{2}[\gamma_{0}](x)

\displaystyle:=\int\nabla_{x}f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(z)+\beta\nabla_{x}\log\mu_{0}(x)+\nabla W_{2}\ast\mu_{0}(x)+\nabla_{x}V_{2}(x)=\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{a}[\gamma_{0}](x)\,.

Note that the dissipation functional can then be written as

D_{a}(\gamma_{0})=\iint\left(\left\lVert\xi_{1}(z)\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\xi_{2}(x)\right\rVert^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}(z,x)\,.

Using the double integral Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
[
77
]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{a}(\gamma_{s})\right|_{s=0}

\displaystyle\geq-\left(\sqrt{\iint\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}\\
\xi_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}\right)\left(\sqrt{\iint\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\phi(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma_{0})}\,\sqrt{\int\|\nabla\phi(z)-z\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\|\nabla\psi(x)-x\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{0}}

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma_{0})}\,\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\,.

Next, we compute a Taylor expansion of
F_{a}(\gamma_{s})
when considered as a function in
s
and use the bound on
\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}F_{a}
from (
6.2
):

\displaystyle F_{a}(\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle=F_{a}(\gamma_{0})+\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}F_{a}(\gamma_{s})\right|_{s=0}+\int_{0}^{1}(1-t)\left.\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}F_{a}(\gamma_{s})\right)\right|_{s=t}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq F_{a}(\gamma_{0})-\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma_{0})}\,\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})+\frac{\lambda_{a}}{2}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})^{2}\,.

∎

Remark 6.8
.

The HWI inequality in Proposition
6.7
immediately implies uniqueness of minimizers for
F_{a}
in the set
\left\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\,:\,D_{a}(\gamma)<+\infty\right\}
. Indeed, if
\gamma_{0}
is such that
D_{a}(\gamma_{0})=0
, then for any other minimizer
\gamma_{1}
in the above set we have
F_{a}(\gamma_{0})\leq F_{a}(\gamma_{1})
with equality if and only if
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})=0
.

Corollary 6.9

(Generalized Log-Sobolev inequality)
.

Denote by
\gamma_{*}
the unique minimizer of
F_{a}
. With Assumptions
1
(i),
2
and
3
, any
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
F_{a}(\gamma),D_{a}(\gamma)<\infty
satisfies

D_{a}(\gamma)\geq 2\lambda_{a}\,F_{a}(\gamma\,|\,\gamma_{*})\,.

(6.7)

Proof.

This statement follows immediately from Proposition
6.7
. Indeed, let
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{*}
and
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
in (
6.6
). Then

\displaystyle F_{a}(\gamma\,|\,\gamma_{*})

\displaystyle\leq\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma,\gamma_{*})\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma)}-\frac{\lambda_{a}}{2}\,\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma,\gamma_{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{t\geq 0}\left(\sqrt{D_{a}(\gamma)}t-\frac{\lambda_{a}}{2}\,t^{2}\right)=\frac{D_{a}(\gamma)}{2\lambda_{a}}\,.

∎

Corollary 6.10

(Talagrand inequality)
.

Denote by
\gamma_{*}
the unique minimizer of
F_{a}
. With Assumptions
1
(i),
2
and
3
and
\lambda_{a}>0
, it holds

\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma,\gamma_{*})^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{a}}F_{a}(\gamma\,|\,\gamma_{*})

for any
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
F_{a}(\gamma)<\infty
.

Proof.

This is also a direct consequence of Proposition
6.7
by setting
\gamma_{0}=\gamma_{*}
and
\gamma_{1}=\gamma
. Then
F_{a}(\gamma_{*})<\infty
and
D_{a}(\gamma_{*})=0
, and the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

The entropy terms
\mathcal{H}(\rho)
and
\mathcal{H}(\mu)
produce diffusion in
\rho
and
\mu
for the corresponding PDEs in (
1.3
). As a consequence, if
\alpha>0
(resp.
\beta>0
) a solution
\rho_{t}
(resp.
\mu_{t}
) to (
1.3
) and minimizer
\rho^{*}
(resp.
\mu^{*}
) for
F_{a}
has to be an
L^{1}
function.
Result (a) corresponds to the statements in Proposition
6.5
and Corollary
6.6
. To obtain (b), we differentiate the energy
F_{a}
along solutions
\gamma_{t}
to the equation (
1.3
):

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{a}(\gamma_{t})

\displaystyle=\int\delta_{\rho}F_{a}[\gamma_{t}](z)\partial_{t}\rho_{t}\mathrm{d}z+\int\delta_{\mu}F_{a}[\gamma_{t}](x)\partial_{t}\mu_{t}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\left\|\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{a}[\gamma_{t}](z)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)-\int\left\|\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{a}[\gamma_{t}](x)\right\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-D_{a}(\gamma_{t})\leq-2\lambda_{a}F_{a}(\gamma_{t}\,|\,\gamma_{*})\,,

where the last bound follows from Corollary
6.9
. Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we immediately obtain decay in energy,

F_{a}(\gamma_{t}\,|\,\gamma_{*})\leq e^{-2\lambda_{a}t}F_{a}(\gamma_{0}\,|\,\gamma_{*})\,.

Finally, applying Talagrand’s inequality (Corollary
6.10
), the decay in energy implies decay in the product Wasserstein metric,

\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{*})\leq ce^{-\lambda_{a}t}\,,

where
c>0
is a constant only depending on
\gamma_{0}
,
\gamma_{*}
and the parameter
\lambda_{a}
. It follows immediately that
\gamma_{*}
is a steady state; see Corollary
6.6
.
∎

7
Competitive Setting (Proof of Theorem
3.4
)

In the competitive setting, the dynamics are parameterized by the energy functional

\displaystyle F_{c}(\rho,\mu)=\iint f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)-\mathcal{R}(\rho)+\mathcal{U}(\mu)

where
\mathcal{R}(\rho)=\alpha\mathcal{H}(\rho)+\frac{1}{2}\int(W_{1}\ast\rho)(z)\,\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int V_{1}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)
and
\mathcal{U}(\mu)=\beta\mathcal{H}(\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\int(W_{2}\ast\mu)(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)+\int V_{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)
. For all results in this section, let Assumptions
1
(ii),
2
, and
3
hold. We define two convexity coefficients,

\lambda_{c,1}\coloneqq\lambda_{f,1}+\lambda_{V,1}\,,\qquad\lambda_{c,2}\coloneqq\lambda_{f,2}+\lambda_{V,2}

where
\lambda_{c,1}
is the displacement concavity coefficient of
F_{c}
with respect to
\rho
, and
\lambda_{c,2}
is the displacement convexity coefficient of
F_{c}
with respect to
\mu
. We assume that
\lambda_{c,1},\lambda_{c,2}>0
in order to obtain convergence. The rate of convergence depends on the species with weaker convexity; the convergence rate is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{c}=\min\{\lambda_{c,1},\lambda_{c,2}\}>0\,.

Lemma 7.1

(Concavity-Convexity of
F_{c}
)
.

The functional
F_{c}
is uniformly displacement
\lambda_{c,2}
-convex in
\mu
for any fixed
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and uniformly displacement
\lambda_{c,1}
-concave in
\rho
for any fixed
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
.

Proof.

For a fixed
\mu
, the
\lambda_{c,1}
concavity of the functional
F_{c}(\rho,\mu)
can be computed as in Proposition
6.2
.
For a fixed
\rho
, the
\lambda_{c,2}
convexity of the functional
F_{c}(\rho,\mu)
can be computed as in Proposition
6.2
.
∎

To show contraction, we apply
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
, which provides an expression for the time derivative of
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}
. This theorem requires that the velocities of the trajectories are in
L^{2}
, which we show in the following lemma using the dissipation functional
D_{c}:\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
,

\displaystyle D_{c}(\gamma):=\iint\left\lVert\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma](z)\\
\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma](x)\end{bmatrix}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma(z,x)\,.

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
\gamma_{t}
be a solution of the dynamics (
1.4
), with initial condition
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
such that
D_{c}(\gamma_{0})<\infty
.
Then

\displaystyle D_{c}(\gamma_{t})\leq e^{-2\lambda_{c}t}D_{c}(\gamma_{0})\quad\forall\ t\geq 0\,.

Proof.

Denote

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle:=\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+\int\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=D_{c}(\gamma_{t})\,.

By direct differentiation, we can write
h(t)
as the difference of two dissipations,

\displaystyle h(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}F_{c}[\rho_{\tau},\mu_{t}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}\,.

We use that the coupling term cancels when differentiating
h(t)
so that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}h(t)=\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}-\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}\,.

(7.1)

To show (
7.1
), recall from the definition of the energy that

-\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)=\nabla_{z}\left(-\int f(z,x)\mu_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x+\alpha\log\rho_{t}(z)+V_{1}(z)+W_{1}\ast\rho_{t}(z)\right).

Computing the time derivative of the weighted
L^{2}
norm of this velocity gives

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)=\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\qquad-2\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})+\alpha\nabla(\partial_{t}\rho_{t}/\rho_{t})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla_{z}f(z,x)\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}x\,.

For the diffusion term,

\displaystyle-2\alpha\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\alpha\nabla(\partial_{t}\rho_{t}/\rho_{t})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\int\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right)\partial_{t}\rho(z)

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\int\left|\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}z\,.

Considering each of the three remaining terms individually, we start with

\displaystyle\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\partial_{t}\rho_{t}\mathrm{d}z=\int\nabla_{z}\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=2\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla^{2}_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\cdot\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\,.

For the second term, we obtain

\displaystyle-2\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\partial_{t}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}

\displaystyle=-2\iint\big\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z),\nabla^{2}_{zz^{\prime}}W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z^{\prime})\big\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})\,.

The third term is

\displaystyle\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla_{z}f(z,x)\partial_{t}\mu_{t}(x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad=-\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla_{zx}^{2}f(z,x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,.

Likewise, we compute the time derivative of the term
\int\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)
which is nearly identical to that of the
\rho_{t}
velocity term, where the diffusion term again can be bounded above by zero. Further, note that the expression for the coupling term is exactly the same as for
\rho_{t}
, just with the opposite sign. Due to the zero-sum structure, when both velocities are summed, this term cancels. Thus, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}h(t)=2\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla^{2}_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\cdot\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)

\displaystyle\quad-2\int\left\langle\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](x),\nabla_{x}^{2}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\cdot\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\quad-2\iint\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\,\mu_{t}](z),\nabla^{2}_{zz^{\prime}}W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z^{\prime})\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad-2\iint\big\langle\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x),\nabla^{2}_{xx^{\prime}}W_{2}(x-x^{\prime})\cdot\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x^{\prime})\big\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad-2\alpha\int\left|\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}z-2\beta\int\left|\mathrm{div}\left(\mu_{t}\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x\,.

(7.2)

This expression is equivalent to

\displaystyle\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}\int\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\tau}(x)\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}\,,

which proves (
7.1
).

By Lemma
7.1
, we have
F_{c}[\rho,\mu]
with fixed
\mu
is
\lambda_{c,1}
-concave in
\rho
and
F_{c}[\rho,\mu]
with fixed
\rho
is
\lambda_{c,2}
-displacement convex in
\mu
. Therefore, we have for all
t,\tau\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\leq-2\lambda_{c,1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\,,\qquad\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\geq-2\lambda_{c,2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\,.

This allows us to use a Bakry-Emry type approach for deriving a decay estimate for
h(t)
. In particular, using (
7.1
), the fact that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\geq 0
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\leq 0
and
\lambda_{c}:=\min\{\lambda_{c,1},\lambda_{c,2}\}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}h(t)

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}-\left[\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}\tau^{2}}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}

\displaystyle\leq\left[-2\lambda_{c,1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]+2\lambda_{c,2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\tau}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{\tau}]\right]\bigg|_{\tau=t}\leq-2\lambda_{c}h(t)\,.

We conclude using Grönwall’s estimate.
∎

Proposition 7.3

(Contraction)
.

Fix
T>0
.
Let
\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
be any two solutions of the dynamics (
1.4
), with initial conditions
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
such that
D_{c}(\gamma_{0})<\infty
and
D_{c}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime})<\infty
. Assume
\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
for all
t\in[0,T)
and
\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z),\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z),\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](x),\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](x)
are locally Lipschitz in
z,x
for all
t\in[0,T)
.
Then
\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
satisfy

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})\leq e^{-\lambda_{c}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime})\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T)\,.

Remark 7.4
.

Although the contraction theorem is stated for measures that are absolutely continuous, the result
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
on the time derivative of
\overline{\mathscr{W}}
can be generalized to the case where
\rho_{t}=\delta_{z(t)},\rho_{t}^{\prime}=\delta_{z^{\prime}(t)}
or
\mu_{t}=\delta_{x(t)},\mu_{t}^{\prime}=\delta_{x^{\prime}(t)}
for all times
t\geq 0
. For details, see Lemma
C.2
for the setting in which
\beta=0
with
\mu_{0}=\delta_{x(0)}
, and
\alpha>0
with
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
.

Proof.

Define
\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)
and
\nabla\psi_{t}(x)
so that
\rho_{t}={\nabla\varphi_{t}}_{\#}\rho_{t}^{\prime}
and
\mu_{t}={\nabla\psi_{t}}_{\#}\mu_{t}^{\prime}
. Due
to Lemma
7.2
,

\displaystyle\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+\int\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)<\infty\quad\forall\ t\geq 0\,,

with the same holding for
\gamma^{\prime}
.
From
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
, the time derivative of the joint metric along solutions to (
1.4
) can be controlled by

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\iint\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi_{t}^{-1}(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi_{t}^{-1}(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z)\\
\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}(z,x)

\displaystyle\quad+2\iint\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi_{t}(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z)\\
\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}^{\prime}(z,x)

\displaystyle=2\iint\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi_{t}(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](\nabla\varphi_{t}(z))-\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z)\\
\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](x)-\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](\nabla\psi(x))\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}^{\prime}(z,x)

\displaystyle\leq-2\lambda_{c}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}\quad\forall\ t\in(0,T)\,,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
C.1
. By Grönwall’s lemma, exponential convergence follows. For time
t=0
, the inequality holds by definition since
e^{-\lambda_{c}t}|_{t=0}=1
.
∎

Next, our goal is to show that the semigroup for the dynamics (
1.4
) maps to
\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
. The key ingredient is control of second moments along the evolution. More precisely, we will show that the second moments converge exponentially to a ball and remain in that ball for all time. This result will then allow us to apply a contractive inequality to prove the existence of a steady state for the dynamics (
1.4
).

Proposition 7.5

(Uniformly Bounded Second Moments)
.

Let
\gamma_{t}
be a solution to (
1.4
) with
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
D_{c}(\gamma_{0})<\infty
. If
\alpha=0
, assume
\rho_{0}=\delta_{z_{0}}
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
. If
\beta=0
, assume
\mu_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. Then
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}\leq-\lambda_{c}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}+2\hat{c}\,,

for some
\hat{c}\geq 0
, where
\bar{\delta}(z,x)=\delta_{(0,0)}(z,x)
.
For any time
t\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+\int\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\leq K\coloneqq\max\left\{\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\bar{\delta}),\frac{2\hat{c}}{\lambda_{c}}\right\}\,.

Proof.

If
\alpha=0
(
\beta=0
), then
\rho_{t}
(
\mu_{t}
) remains a Dirac Delta for all times, and so its second moment vanishes. Let us assume
\alpha,\beta>0
. Thanks to the diffusion,
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}\times\mathcal{P}^{ac}
for all
t>0
, and thanks to Lemma
7.2
we have
D_{c}(\gamma_{t})<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
.
The sum of the squared second moments can be written as
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}
.
Directly differentiating the second moments along solutions to (
1.4
),
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}\leq 2\iint\begin{bmatrix}z\\
x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z)\\
-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\,.

(7.3)

Our goal is to upper-bound the right-hand side in terms of
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}
. We will compute the terms in
F_{c}
separately, starting with the entropy terms. We have

\displaystyle-\int z\cdot\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}\mathcal{H}(\rho_{t})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=-\int z\cdot\nabla\log\rho_{t}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)=-\int z\cdot\nabla_{z}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}z=d_{1}\int\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)=d_{1}\,,

\displaystyle-\int x\cdot\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t})\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\int x\cdot\nabla\log\mu_{t}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=-\int x\cdot\nabla_{x}\mu_{t}(x)\mathrm{d}x=d_{2}\int\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)=d_{2}.

For the remaining terms, we will use the convexity inequality

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(y^{\prime})+\nabla f(y^{\prime})\cdot(y-y^{\prime})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\lVert y-y^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall y,y^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

Applying this to
W_{1}
, we use a change of variables on half of the integral and use the symmetry of
W_{1}
, in particular that
\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})=-\nabla W_{1}(z^{\prime}-z)
, to rewrite

\displaystyle-\iint z\cdot\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int(z-z^{\prime})\cdot\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})\,.

Selecting
y^{\prime}=z-z^{\prime}
and
y=0
results in

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\iint(z-z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\cdot\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})\leq\iint-\frac{\lambda_{W,1}}{4}\left\lVert z-z^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\iint[W_{1}(0)-W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})]\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z^{\prime})\leq 0\,,

since
W_{1}(0)\leq W_{1}(z)
for all
z
by symmetry and convexity of
W_{1}
. A similar estimate can be computed for
W_{2}
. For the cross-term and potential terms, we will use the convexity inequality for each species, with
y=0
and
y^{\prime}=z
for
z\cdot\nabla_{z}f(z,x)
and
y=0
and
y^{\prime}=x
for
-x\cdot\nabla_{x}f(z,x)
,

\displaystyle\iint

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}z\\
x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}(f(z,x)-V_{1}(z))\\
-\nabla_{x}(f(z,x)-V_{2}(x))\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\leq-\int\frac{\lambda_{c,1}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)-\int\frac{\lambda_{c,2}}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)

\displaystyle+\iint(-f(0,x)+f(z,0)+V_{1}(0)-V_{1}(z)+f(z,x)-f(z,x)+V_{2}(0)-V_{2}(x))\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}(z,x)\,.

Next, we use that
f(z,0)-V_{1}(z)
is
\lambda_{c,1}
concave in
z
,
f(0,x)+V_{2}(x)
is
\lambda_{c,2}
convex in
x
, and continuity to define

\displaystyle c:=V_{1}(0)+V_{2}(0)+\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}f(z,0)-V_{1}(z)+\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}-f(0,x)-V_{2}(x)<\infty\,,

which gives an upper-bound for the cross term

\displaystyle\iint\begin{bmatrix}z\\
x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}f(z,x)\\
-\nabla_{x}f(z,x)\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)\leq-\int\frac{\Lambda_{f,1}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)-\int\frac{\lambda_{f,2}}{2}\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}(x)+c\,.

Note that
c\geq 0
because
\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}}f(z,0)-V_{1}(z)\geq f(0,0)-V_{1}(0)
and
\max_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}}-f(0,x)-V_{2}(x)\geq-f(0,0)-V_{2}(0)
.
Combining all terms gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}\leq-\frac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}+\hat{c}\,,

where
\hat{c}=c+\alpha d_{1}+\beta d_{2}
. It holds that
\hat{c}\geq 0
because all terms are non-negative.
The solution to this ODE satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\bar{\delta})^{2}\leq\max\left\{\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\bar{\delta})^{2},\frac{2\hat{c}}{\lambda_{c}}\right\}\quad\forall t\geq 0\,.

∎

Proposition 7.6

(Existence and Uniqueness of Steady States)
.

There exists a unique steady state
\gamma_{\infty}=(\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\infty})\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
of equation (
1.4
) according to Definition
2.6
with
\int\rho_{\infty}=1,\int\mu_{\infty}=1
. This steady state is a Nash equilibrium for
F_{c}
. Additionally,

•

if
\alpha>0
, the steady state
\gamma_{\infty}
satisfies
\rho_{\infty}\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
with
\left\lVert\rho_{\infty}\right\rVert_{1}=1
and
\supp(\rho_{\infty})=\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
;

•

if
\beta>0
, the steady state
\gamma_{\infty}
satisfies
\mu_{\infty}\in L_{+}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})\cap C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
with
\left\lVert\mu_{\infty}\right\rVert_{1}=1
and
\supp(\mu_{\infty})=\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.

Proof.

We split the proof into three steps.
First, (i) we prove there exists
\gamma_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
satisfying
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{\infty},\gamma(t))=0
for all
t\geq 0
with
\gamma(t)
the solution to (
1.4
) with initial condition
\gamma_{\infty}
. Then (ii) we will show that
\gamma_{\infty}
is also a critical point; specifically it is a Nash equilibrium.
Lastly, (iii) we show
\gamma_{\infty}
has the required regularity properties to satisfy Definition
2.6
.

(i) Fix
\gamma_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
D_{c}(\gamma_{0})<\infty
. If
\alpha=0
, assume
\rho_{0}=\delta_{z_{0}}
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}
. If
\beta=0
, assume
\mu_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}}
for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
. By Proposition
7.5
, we know that
\gamma_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
for all
t\geq 0
. If
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have
\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{2}
thanks to the diffusion.
Contraction in
\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}^{ac}_{2}
follows from Proposition
7.3
. If
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
, contraction is in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
instead of
\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
(see Remark
7.4
).
Let
T(t):\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}\to\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
be the semigroup for (
1.4
). Using a contraction result,
[
22
, Lemma 7.3]
, there exists a unique steady state
\gamma_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
(or
\mathcal{P}_{2}\times\mathcal{P}_{2}
resp.), that is,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{\infty},T(t)\gamma_{\infty})=0\,.

(ii) To show that
\gamma_{\infty}
is a critical point, we consider the optimization problems:

\displaystyle c_{1}=\sup_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}F_{c}(\rho,\mu_{\infty})\,,\qquad c_{2}=\inf_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}F_{c}(\rho_{\infty},\mu)\,.

First, note that the supremum
c_{1}
and infimum
c_{2}
are in fact attained by a unique maximizer
\rho_{\dagger}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and unique minimizer
\mu_{\dagger}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
. This can be shown using similar arguments as those in Proposition
6.5
; concavity of
F_{c}(\cdot,\mu_{\infty})
and convexity of
F_{c}(\rho_{\infty},\cdot)
follow from Lemma
7.1
. The functional
F_{c}(\cdot,\mu_{\infty})
has an upper-bound due to concavity via a similar argument as in Lemma
6.4
and Lemma

, and

has a lower-bound using the same argument with convexity. Upper and lower-semicontinuity complete the set of required ingredients for the proof of Proposition

. We can then write the well-defined optimization problem

\displaystyle\rho_{\dagger}=\amax_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}F_{c}(\rho,\mu_{\infty})\,,\qquad\mu_{\dagger}=\amin_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}}F_{c}(\rho_{\infty},\mu)\,.

(7.4)

As a maximizer and minimizer respectively, they satisfy the Euler-Lagrange (EL) conditions

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\dagger},\mu_{\infty}](z)=c_{1}\,\forall z\in\supp\rho_{\dagger}\,,\quad\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\dagger}](x)=c_{2}\,\forall x\in\supp\mu_{\dagger}\,.

The EL condition for
\rho_{\dagger}
is

\displaystyle\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\dagger},\mu_{\infty}](z)=-\alpha\log\rho_{\dagger}(z)+\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}(x)-V_{1}(z)-(W_{1}\ast\rho_{\dagger})(z)=\tilde{c}_{1}\,\,\forall z\in\supp\rho_{\dagger}\,.

Note that the left-hand side of the EL condition is finite for any fixed
z
.
Rearranging, in the case when
\alpha>0
, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{\dagger}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[-\tilde{c}_{1}+\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}(x)-W_{1}\ast\rho_{\dagger}(z)-V_{1}(z)\right]\right)\,.

(7.5)

We have
W_{1}\ast\rho_{\dagger}\in C^{2}
due to
W_{1}\in C^{2}
,
\int f(z,x)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}(x)\in C^{2}
because
f(\cdot,x)\in C^{2}
, and
V_{1}\in C^{2}
from Assumptions
1
(ii),
2
, and
3
. Therefore
\rho_{\dagger}\in C^{2}
. If
\alpha=0
, then
\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\dagger},\mu_{\infty}](\cdot)\in C^{2}
follows immediately. We conclude that
\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\dagger},\mu_{\infty}](\cdot)\in C^{2}
and
\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{\dagger}\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\dagger},\mu_{\infty}](z)\right)=0
pointwise a.e. Similarly,
\mathrm{div}\left(\mu_{\dagger}\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\dagger}](x)\right)=0
pointwise a.e., and if
\beta>0
, then
\mu_{\dagger}\in C^{2}
.

Define
S_{1}(t):\mathcal{P}_{2}\to\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
S_{2}(t):\mathcal{P}_{2}\to\mathcal{P}_{2}
as the semigroups for the uncoupled dynamics given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{\rho}=-\mathrm{div}\left(\hat{\rho}\nabla\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\hat{\rho},\mu_{\infty}](z)\right)\,,\quad\partial_{t}\hat{\mu}=\mathrm{div}\left(\hat{\mu}\nabla\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\hat{\mu}](x)\right)\,,

(7.6)

respectively. Note that
S_{i}(t)
maps to
\mathcal{P}_{2}
by the same argument as in Proposition
7.5
. Due to the regularity of
\rho_{\dagger}
and
\mu_{\dagger}
and the fact that they are critical points, they are also steady states of (
7.6
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{\dagger},S_{1}(t)\rho_{\dagger})=0\,,\qquad\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu_{\dagger},S_{2}(t)\mu_{\dagger})=0\,.

Since
\gamma_{\infty}
is a steady state of (
1.4
),

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{\infty},S_{1}(t)\rho_{\infty})=0\,,\qquad\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu_{\infty},S_{2}(t)\mu_{\infty})=0\,.

Next, we again apply the contraction theorem
[
22
, Lemma 7.3]
to (
7.6
), which states that there is a unique steady state for
S_{1}(t)
and for
S_{2}(t)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{\dagger},\gamma_{\infty})=0\,.

(iii) If
\alpha,\beta>0
, then
\gamma_{\dagger}\in C^{2}\times C^{2}
. Since equality of
\gamma_{\dagger}
with
\gamma_{\infty}
holds almost everywhere with respect to the Lebesgue measure, it holds that

\gamma_{\infty}\in(W_{loc}^{1,2}\cap L_{loc}^{\infty})\times(W_{loc}^{1,2}\cap L_{loc}^{\infty})

When

∎

Proposition 7.7

(Uniqueness of Nash Equilibrium)
.

There exists at most one Nash equilibrium in
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
for the energy functional
F_{c}
.

Proof.

Suppose there exists two equilibria,
\gamma_{*}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
and
\gamma_{\infty}\in\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
such that
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{\infty},\gamma_{*})>0
. In the weak sense, the following hold:

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{\infty}](z)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall\ z\in\supp\rho_{\infty}\,,\qquad\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](z)=0\quad\,\forall z\in\supp\rho_{*}\,,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{\infty}](x)

\displaystyle=0\quad\forall\ x\in\supp\mu_{\infty}\,,\qquad\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](x)=0\quad\forall\,x\in\supp\mu_{*}\,.

We plug
\gamma_{*}
and
\gamma_{\infty}
into the inequality proved in Lemma
C.1
, with
\rho_{*}=\nabla\varphi_{\#}\rho_{\infty}
and
\mu_{*}=\nabla\psi_{\#}\mu_{\infty}
. Note that both
\nabla\varphi
and
\nabla\psi
exist and are invertible (on the support of
\rho_{\infty}
and
\mu_{\infty}
resp.) because the measures are either absolutely continuous or propagating as Diracs, and so the left-hand side in Lemma
C.1
is well-defined. Hence,

\displaystyle\iint

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi(z)\\
x-\nabla\psi(x)\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](\nabla\varphi(z))\\
-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](\nabla\psi(x))\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\rho_{\infty}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}(x)

\displaystyle=\iint\begin{bmatrix}(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}(z)-z\\
(\nabla\psi)^{-1}(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](z)\\
-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{*}](x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\rho_{*}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{*}(x)=0\geq\lambda_{c}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{\infty},\gamma_{*})^{2}\,,

which is a contradiction and therefore the Nash equilibrium is unique.
∎

Now we present the proof of Theorem
3.4
.

Proof of Theorem
3.4
.

From Proposition
7.6
we have existence and uniqueness of steady states of (
1.4
) in
\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}\times\tilde{\mathcal{P}}_{2}
,
and that the steady state is a Nash equilibrium for
F_{c}
. This Nash equilibrium is unique from Proposition
7.7
. This concludes the proof of (a).

For (b), the uniform bound on the second moment follows from Proposition
7.5
. Further, let
(\rho_{0},\mu_{0})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})\times\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}})
,
\rho_{\infty}=\nabla\varphi_{\#}\rho_{t}
and
\mu_{\infty}=\nabla\psi_{\#}\mu_{t}
. We compute the time derivative of the joint Wasserstein-2 metric using
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
.
We have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{\infty})^{2}=2\iint\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\\
\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}(z,x)

\displaystyle=2\iint\begin{bmatrix}\nabla\varphi(z)-z\\
\nabla\psi(x)-x\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}](\nabla\varphi(z))-\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](z)\\
\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{t},\mu_{t}](x)-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}](\nabla\psi(x))\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}(z,x)\,,

where we can add the second term because
\gamma_{\infty}
is a steady state, so

\int\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\rho_{\infty},\mu_{\infty}](\nabla\varphi(z))\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)=0\,.

This can be shown by rewriting the support of the steady state using the pushforward from
\rho_{\infty}
to
\rho_{t}
. A similar argument applies to the first variation in
\mu
, and in the setting in which the measure propagating is a Dirac.
Now we can apply Lemma
C.1

to obtain
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{\infty})^{2}\leq-2\lambda_{c}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{\infty})^{2}
which gives exponential convergence with rate
\lambda_{c}
using Grönwall’s Lemma, completing the proof of (b).
∎

Lemma A.1

Lemma A.2

(First variation of the best response)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}^{2}G(\rho,x)

Lemma A.3

Proposition A.5

(Displacement concavity)
.

\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

, we have for all
s\in[0,1]

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}G_{b}(\rho_{s})\leq-\lambda_{b}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}\,,\qquad\lambda_{b}:=\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1}\,.

(A.8)

As a result, the functional
G_{b}:\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is uniformly displacement concave with constant
\lambda_{b}>0
.

Proof.

Consider any
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then any
\mathscr{W}_{2}
-geodesic
(\rho_{s})_{s\in[0,1]}
connecting
\rho_{0}
with
\rho_{1}
solves the following system of geodesic equations:

\begin{cases}\partial_{s}\rho_{s}+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{s}v_{s}\right)=0\,,\\
\partial_{s}(\rho_{s}v_{s})+\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{s}v_{s}\otimes v_{s}\right)=0\,,\end{cases}

(A.9)

where
\rho_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
v_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The first derivative of
G_{b}
along geodesics can be computed explicitly as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}G_{b}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{z}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(z)\rho_{s}(z)\mathrm{d}z-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{R}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle+\left\langle\bigg[\int\nabla_{x}f_{1}(z,\,x)\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(z)+\int\nabla_{x}f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\kappa(x-x_{0})\bigg]\bigg|_{x=b(\rho_{s})},\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}b(\rho_{s})\right\rangle.

For the last term, the left-hand side of the inner product is zero by definition of the best response
b(\rho_{s})
to
\rho_{s}
, see (
A.7
). Therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}G_{b}(\rho_{s})=\int\nabla_{z}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(z)\rho_{s}(z)\mathrm{d}z-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathcal{R}(\rho_{s})\,.

Differentiating a second time, using (
A.9
) and integration by parts, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}G_{b}(\rho_{s})=L_{1}(\rho_{s})+L_{2}(\rho_{s})-\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}\mathcal{R}(\rho_{s})\,,

where

\displaystyle L_{1}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle:=\int\nabla_{z}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot(v_{s}\otimes v_{s})\,\rho_{s}\mathrm{d}z=\int\left\langle v_{s},\,\nabla_{z}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}\right\rangle\,\rho_{s}\mathrm{d}z\,,

\displaystyle L_{2}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle:=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}b(\rho_{s})\cdot\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(z)\,\rho_{s}(z)\mathrm{d}z\,.

From (
6.3
), we have that

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}\mathcal{R}(\rho_{s})\geq\alpha\tilde{\lambda}\,\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}\,,

and thanks to Assumption
1
(ii) it follows that

\displaystyle L_{1}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\Lambda_{1}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}.

This leaves
L_{2}
to bound; we first consider the term
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}b(\rho_{s})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}b(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle=\int\delta_{\rho}b[\rho_{s}](\tilde{z})\partial_{s}\rho_{s}(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}=-\int\delta_{\rho}b[\rho_{s}](\tilde{z})\mathrm{div}\left(\rho_{s}v_{s}\right)(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}

\displaystyle=\int\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}b[\rho_{s}](\tilde{z})\cdot v_{s}(\tilde{z})\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(\tilde{z}).

Defining
u(\rho_{s})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\displaystyle u(\rho_{s})\coloneqq\int\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(z)\,,

using the results from Lemma
A.2
for
\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}b[\rho_{s}]

and the fact that
Q(\rho)
is constant in
z
and
x
, we have

\displaystyle L_{2}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle=-\iint\left[Q(\rho_{s})^{-1}\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(\tilde{z},b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(\tilde{z})\right]\cdot\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\cdot v_{s}(z)\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(\tilde{z})

\displaystyle=-\left\langle u(\rho_{s}),\,Q(\rho_{s})^{-1}u(\rho_{s})\right\rangle\leq 0

Combining all terms together, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}}{\mathrm{d}s^{2}}G_{b}(\rho_{s})\leq-\left(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1}\right)\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}\,.

∎

Remark A.6
.

Under some additional assumptions on the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, we can obtain an improved convergence rate. In particular, assume that for all
z,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

•

there exists a constant
\Lambda_{1u}\geq\lambda_{1}
such that
\nabla_{x}^{2}f_{1}(z,x)\preceq\Lambda_{1u}\Id_{d}
;

•

there exists a constant
\Lambda_{2u}\geq\lambda_{2}
such that
\nabla^{2}_{x}f_{2}(z,x)\preceq\Lambda_{2u}\Id_{d}
;

•

there exists a constant
\sigma\geq 0
such that

\left\lVert\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(z,x)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\sigma
.

Then we have

-Q(\rho_{s})^{-1}\preceq-1/(\kappa+\Lambda_{1u}+\Lambda_{2u})\Id_{d}
.
Using Lemma
A.2
, we then obtain a stronger bound on
L_{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle L_{2}(\rho_{s})

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\kappa+\Lambda_{1u}+\Lambda_{2u}}\left\lVert u(\rho_{s})\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{\kappa+\Lambda_{1u}+\Lambda_{2u}}\int\left\lVert\nabla_{xz}^{2}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{s}))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(z)\int\left\lVert v_{s}(z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{s}(z)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa+\Lambda_{1u}+\Lambda_{2u}}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}.

This means we can improve the convergence rate in (
A.8
) to
\lambda_{b}:=\alpha\tilde{\lambda}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\kappa+\Lambda_{1u}+\Lambda_{2u}}-\Lambda_{1}
.

Lemma A.7

(Uniform boundedness of the best response)
.

Let Assumption
7
hold. Then
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have

\left\lVert b(\rho)\right\rVert^{2}\leq\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{2(a_{1}+a_{2})}{\kappa}\,.

Proof.

The bound trivially holds if
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is such that
G(\rho,x)=\pm\infty
for some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
since then we have
b(\rho)=0
. Else,
by definition of the best response
b(\rho)
, we have

\displaystyle\int\nabla_{x}f_{1}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int\nabla_{x}f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\kappa(b(\rho)-x_{0})=0\,.

To show that that
b(\rho)
is uniformly bounded, we take the inner product of the above expression with
b(\rho)
itself

\displaystyle\kappa\|b(\rho)\|^{2}=\kappa x_{0}\cdot b(\rho)-\int\nabla_{x}f_{1}(z,b(\rho))\cdot b(\rho)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)-\int\nabla_{x}f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\cdot b(\rho)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)\,.

Using Assumption
7
to bound the two integrals, together with using Young’s inequality to bound the first term on the right-hand side, we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa\|b(\rho)\|^{2}\leq\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert b(\rho)\right\rVert^{2}+a_{1}+a_{2}\,.

∎

Lemma A.8

(Upper semi-continuity)
.

Let Assumption

7
hold. The functional
G:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is upper semi-continuous when
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is endowed with the product topology of the weak topology and the Euclidean topology. Moreover, the functional
G_{b}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is upper semi-continuous with respect to the weak topology.

Proof.

The functional
G:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
is continuous in the second variable thanks to Assumption
1
(ii). Similarly,
\int f_{1}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)
is continuous in
\rho
thanks to
[
75
, Proposition 7.1]
using the continuity of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Further,
-\mathcal{R}
is upper semi-continuous using Lemma
C.4
and
[
75
, Proposition 7.2]
thanks to Assumptions
2
and
3
. This concludes the continuity properties for
G
.

The upper semi-continuity of
G_{b}
then follows from a direct application of a version of Berge’s maximum theorem
[
4
, Lemma 16.30]
. Let
R:=\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{2(a_{1}+a_{2})}{\kappa}>0
. We define
\varphi:(\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathscr{W}_{2})\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the correspondence that maps any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the closed ball
\overline{B_{R}(0)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the graph of
\varphi
is
\text{Gr}\,\varphi=\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\{\overline{B_{R}(0)}\}
. With this definition of
\varphi
, the range of
\varphi
is compact and
\varphi
is continuous with respect to weak convergence, and so it is in particular upper hemicontinuous. Thanks to Lemma
A.7
, the best response function
b(\rho)
is always contained in
\overline{B_{R}(0)}
for any choice of
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. As a result, maximizing
-G(\rho,x)
in
x
over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for a fixed
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
reduces to maximizing it over
\overline{B_{R}(0)}
. Using the notation introduced above, we can restrict
G
to
G:\text{Gr}\,\varphi\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and write

\displaystyle G_{b}(\rho)\coloneqq\max_{\hat{x}\in\varphi(\rho)}-G(\rho,\hat{x}).

Because
G(\rho,x)
is upper semi-continuous when
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
is endowed with the product topology of the weak topology and the Euclidean topology,
[
4
, Lemma 16.30]
guarantees that
G_{b}(\cdot)
is upper semi-continuous in the weak topology.
∎

Proposition A.9

(Ground state)
.

Let Assumption
7
hold.
There exists a unique maximizer
\rho_{*}
for the functional
G_{b}
over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, it satisfies
\rho_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\rho}
.

Proof.

Uniqueness of the maximizer (if it exists) is guaranteed by the uniform concavity provided by Lemma
A.5
.
To show existence of a maximizer, we use the direct method in the calculus of variations, requiring the following key properties for
G_{b}
: (1) boundedness from above, (2) upper semi-continuity, and (3) tightness of any maximizing sequence.
To show (1), note that
\nabla_{z}^{2}(f_{1}(z,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z))\preceq-(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1})\Id_{d}
for all
z,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
by Assumptions
1
(ii) and
2
, and so

f_{1}(z,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\leq c_{0}(x)-\frac{(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1})}{4}\|z\|^{2}\qquad\forall(z,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(A.10)

with
c_{0}(x):=f_{1}(0,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(0)+\frac{1}{\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1}}\|\nabla_{z}\left[f_{1}(0,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(0)\right]\|^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle G_{b}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int\left[f_{1}(z,b(\rho))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\right]\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|b(\rho)-x_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad-\alpha\int\rho\log\rho-\frac{1}{2}\int\rho W\ast\rho

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}(b(\rho))+\int f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\|b(\rho)-x_{0}\|^{2}\,.

To estimate each of the remaining terms on the right-hand side, denote
R:=\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{2(a_{1}+a_{2})}{\kappa}
and recall that
\|b(\rho)\|\leq R
for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
thanks to Lemma
A.7
. By continuity of
f_{1}
and
\log\tilde{\rho}
, there exists a constant
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{R}(0)}c_{0}(x)=\sup_{x\in B_{R}(0)}\left[f_{1}(0,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(0)+\frac{1}{\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1}}\left\|\nabla_{z}\left(f_{1}(0,x)+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(0)\right)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq c_{1}\,.

(A.11)

The term
\int f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\pi(z)
is controlled by
c_{2}
thanks to
(
4.4
).
The third term can be bounded directly to obtain

\displaystyle G_{b}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}+c_{2}+\kappa(R^{2}+\|x_{0}\|^{2})\,.

This concludes the proof of (1). Statement (2) was shown in Lemma
A.8
using Assumption
7
. Then we obtain a maximizing sequence
(\rho_{n})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which is in the closed unit ball of
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
and so the Banach-Anaoglu theorem
[
73
, Theorem 3.15]
there exists a limit
\rho_{*}
in the Radon measures and a subsequence (not relabeled) such that
\rho_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\rho_{*}
. In fact,
\rho_{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\rho}
as otherwise
G_{b}(\rho_{*})=-\infty
, which contradicts that
G_{b}(\cdot)>-\infty
somewhere. We conclude that
\rho_{*}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
\tilde{\rho}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. To ensure
\rho_{*}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we require (3) tightness of the minimizing sequence
(\rho_{n})
. By Markov’s inequality
[
45
]
it is sufficient to establish a uniform bound on the second moments:

\int\|z\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)<C\qquad\forall n\in\mathrm{N}\,.

(A.12)

To see this we proceed in a similar way as in the proof of Proposition
6.5
. Defining

\displaystyle K(\rho):=-\int\left[f_{1}(z,b(\rho))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\right]\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\alpha\int\rho\log\rho\,\mathrm{d}z+\frac{1}{2}\int\rho W\ast\rho\,\mathrm{d}z\,,

we have
K(\rho)=-G_{b}(\rho)+\int f_{2}(z,b(\rho))\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert b(\rho)-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
. Then using again the bound on
b(\rho)
from Lemma
A.7
,

\displaystyle K(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq-G_{b}(\rho)+\sup_{x\in B_{R}(0)}\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\kappa\left(R^{2}+\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-G_{b}(\rho)+c_{2}+\kappa\left(R^{2}+\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\right)\,,

where the last inequality is thanks to (
4.4
).
Hence, using the estimates (
A.10
) and (
A.11
) from above,

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1})}{4}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}-\int\left[f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{n}))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\right]\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)\,.

Applying Lemma
C.3
with
\varepsilon=\frac{\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1}}{8\alpha}
in the same fashion as in the proof of Proposition
6.5
and noting that the sequence
(\rho_{n})
is minimizing
(-G_{b})
,

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1})}{4}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}+K(\rho_{n})+\alpha\varepsilon\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\alpha c_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\frac{(\alpha\tilde{\lambda}-\Lambda_{1})}{8}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}-G_{b}(\rho_{n})+c_{2}+\kappa\left(R^{2}+\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\right)+\alpha c_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leq c_{1}-G_{b}(\rho_{1})+c_{2}+\kappa\left(R^{2}+\left\lVert x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\right)+\alpha c_{\varepsilon}<\infty\,,

which uniformly bounds the second moments of
(\rho_{n})
. This concludes the proof for the estimate (
A.12
) and also ensures that
\rho_{*}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Corollary A.10
.

Any maximizer
\rho_{*}
of
G_{b}
is a steady state for equation (
4.2
), and satisfies
\supp(\rho_{*})=\supp(\tilde{\rho})=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\rho_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

To show that
\rho_{*}
is a steady state according to Lemma
A.1
we can follow exactly the same argument as in the proof of Proposition
7.6
, just replacing
\int f_{1}(z,x)\,\mu_{*}(x)
with
f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{*}))
. It remains to show that
\supp(\rho_{*})=\supp(\tilde{\rho})
.
As
\rho^{*}
is a maximizer, it is in particular a critical point, and therefore satisfies that
\delta_{\rho}G_{b}[\rho_{*}](z)
is constant on all connected components of
\supp(\rho_{*})
. Thanks to Proposition
A.4
, this means there exists a constant
c[\rho_{*}]
(which may be different on different components of
\supp(\rho_{*})
) such that

\displaystyle f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{*}))-\alpha\log\left(\frac{\rho_{*}(z)}{\tilde{\rho}(z)}\right)-W\ast\rho_{*}(z)=c[\rho_{*}]\qquad\text{ on }\supp(\rho_{*})\,.

Rearranging, we obtain (for a possibly different constant
c[\rho_{*}]\neq 0
)

\displaystyle\rho_{*}(z)=c[\rho_{*}]\tilde{\rho}(z)\exp{\left[\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{*}))-W\ast\rho_{*}(z)\right)\right]}\qquad\text{ on }\supp(\rho_{*})\,.

(A.13)

Firstly,
\supp(\rho_{*})\subset\supp(\tilde{\rho})
since
\rho_{*}
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\tilde{\rho}
. Secondly, note that
\exp{\frac{1}{\alpha}f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{*}))}>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
since
f_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Finally, we claim that
\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}W\ast\rho_{*}(z)\right)}>0
for
\rho_{*}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In other words, we claim that
W\ast\rho_{*}(z)<\infty
for
\rho_{*}
-a.e.
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Else, if
W\ast\rho_{*}(z)=\infty
on a set of non-zero measure, then
\int\rho_{*}W\ast\rho_{*}=+\infty
. Since all other terms are upper bounded due to the
x
-player minimization, this implies that
G_{b}(\rho_{*})=-\infty
. Since
\rho^{*}
is a maximizer of
G_{b}
, this would mean
G_{b}\equiv-\infty
, which is a contradiction.
We conclude that
\supp(\rho_{*})=\supp(\tilde{\rho})
.
Finally,
\rho_{*}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
thanks to
\tilde{\rho},f_{1},W\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
∎

Remark A.11
.

Note that
\tilde{\rho}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by Assumption
2
.
If we have in addition that
f_{1}(\cdot,x)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the maximizer
\rho_{*}
of
G_{b}
is in
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
as well. This follows directly by bounding the right-hand side of (
A.13
).

With the notion of steady state given in Lemma
A.1
, we can obtain improved regularity for any steady state
\rho_{\infty}
under mild additional assumptions.

Lemma A.12
.

Any steady state
\rho_{\infty}
for equation (
4.2
) has continuous Lebesgue density,
\rho_{\infty}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

Thanks to our assumptions, we have
f_{1}(\cdot,b(\rho_{\infty}))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(\cdot)\in C^{1}
, which implies that
\nabla_{z}(f_{1}(\cdot,b(\rho_{\infty}))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(\cdot))\in L_{loc}^{\infty}
. By the definition of a steady state,
\rho_{\infty}\in L^{1}\cap L_{loc}^{\infty}
and
\nabla W\ast\rho_{\infty}\in L_{loc}^{\infty}
.
Let

\displaystyle h(z)\coloneqq\rho_{\infty}(z)\nabla_{z}\left[f_{1}(z,b(\rho_{\infty}))+\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)-(W\ast\rho_{\infty})(z)\right]\,.

Then by the aforementioned regularity, we obtain
h\in L_{loc}^{1}\cap L_{loc}^{\infty}
.
By interpolation, it follows that
h\in L_{loc}^{p}
for all
1<p<\infty
. This implies that
\mathrm{div}\left(h\right)\in W_{loc}^{-1,p}
. Since
\rho_{\infty}
is a weak
W_{loc}^{1,2}
-solution of (
A.2
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta\rho_{\infty}=\mathrm{div}\left(h\right)\,,

and so by classic elliptic regularity theory we conclude
\rho_{\infty}\in W_{loc}^{1,p}
. Finally, applying Morrey’s inequality, we have
\rho_{\infty}\in C^{0,k}
where
k=\frac{p-d}{p}
for any
d<p<\infty
. Therefore
\rho_{\infty}\in C(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(after possibly being redefined on a set of measure zero).
∎

With the above preliminary results, we can now show the HWI inequality, which implies again a Talagrand-type inequality and a generalized logarithmic Sobolev inequality.

Proposition A.13

(HWI inequalities)
.

Define the dissipation functional

\displaystyle D_{b}(\gamma):=\iint\|\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}G_{b}[\rho](z)\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\,.

Let Assumption
7
hold.
Denote by
\rho_{*}
the unique maximizer of
G_{b}
.

Let
\rho_{0},\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
G_{b}(\rho_{0}),G_{b}(\rho_{1}),D_{b}(\rho_{0})<\infty
. Then

\displaystyle G_{b}(\rho_{0})-G_{b}(\rho_{1})\leq\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\sqrt{D_{b}(\rho_{0})}-\frac{\lambda_{b}}{2}\,\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})^{2}

(A.14)

Any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
G_{b}(\rho),D_{b}(\rho)<\infty
satisfies

D_{b}(\rho)\geq 2\lambda_{b}\,G_{b}(\rho\,|\,\rho_{*})\,.

(A.15)

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
G_{b}(\rho)<\infty
, we have

\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho,\rho_{*})^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\lambda_{b}}G_{b}(\rho\,|\,\rho_{*})\,.

(A.16)

Proof.

The proof for this result follows analogously to the arguments presented in the proofs of Proposition
6.7
, Corollary
6.9
and Corollary
6.10
, using the preliminary results established in Proposition
A.5
and Proposition
A.9
.
∎

Competitive Objective, Fast Population (Proof of Theorem
4.2
)

In this section, let Assumptions
1
(ii),
2
and
3
hold throughout with
\alpha\tilde{\lambda}>\Lambda_{1}
. The proof for Theorem
4.2
uses similar strategies as that of Theorem
4.1
, but considers the evolution of an ODE rather than a PDE.
Recall that for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the best response
r(x)(\cdot)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in (
4.3
) is defined as

r(x)\coloneqq\amax_{\hat{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}}G(\hat{\rho},x)\,,

where the energy
G(\rho,x):\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-\infty,\infty]
is given as in Appendix
A
by

\displaystyle G(\rho,x)

\displaystyle=\int f_{1}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\alpha KL(\rho\,|\,\tilde{\rho})-\frac{1}{2}\int(W\ast\rho)(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\,.

We start by showing that the best response
r(x)
exists and is uniquely defined for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(Proposition
B.1
). In order to show convergence to equilibrium for the algorithm dynamics
x(t)
(Theorem
4.2
), we need to differentiate the energy

G_{d}(x):=\left.G(\rho,x)\right|_{\rho=r(x)}
in
x
, which is a non-trivial task given that it is not obvious that the best response
r(x)
is even differentiable in
x
. Our goal is therefore to achieve a Danskin-like result of the form

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}G_{d}(x)=\left.\left(\nabla_{x}G(\rho,x)\right)\right|_{\rho=r(x)}

(Proposition
B.10
). Danskin’s theorem, also known as the envelope theorem, is a classical result in (Euclidean) game theory. In zero-sum games, the derivative of a cost function through the best response of the other player is equal to the derivative with the best response plugged in after differentiating, and is used to prove convexity through the implicitly-defined best response function; see
[
9
]
for details.
To achieve this result, we will need to use that the best response
r(x)
is continuous in
x
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
(Corollary
B.8
). For the rest of this section, we use the following notation: denote
m(z,x):=-f_{1}(z,x)-\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)
and

\displaystyle F(\rho,x):=\alpha\int\rho(z)\log\rho(z)\,\mathrm{d}z+\int m(z,x)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)+\frac{1}{2}\int(W\ast\rho)(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\,.

Then
G(\rho,x)=\int f_{2}(z,x)\mathrm{d}\pi(z)+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-F(\rho,x)
, and so maximizing
G(\rho,x)
in
\rho
corresponds to minimizing
F(\rho,x)
in
\rho
. Showing that the best response
r(x)
is continuous means that for any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
converging to
\bar{x}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we have
\mathscr{W}_{2}(r(x_{n}),r(\bar{x}))\to 0
.
For a sequence
x_{n}\to\bar{x}
, define

\displaystyle F_{n}(\rho)\coloneqq F(\rho,x_{n})\,,\quad\bar{F}(\rho)\coloneqq F(\rho,\bar{x})\,,\quad\rho_{n}\coloneqq\amin_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}F_{n}(\rho)\,,\quad m_{n}(z)\coloneqq m(z,x_{n})\,.

(B.1)

To obtain the continuity of
r(x)
, we first show that the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has uniformly bounded second moments (obtained from convergence of the second moments Proposition
B.7
) and that
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}\bar{F}
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
(Proposition
B.5
). For these results, we will need an extension of Fatou’s Lemma (Theorem
B.3
) which gives conditions under which limits and integrals can be exchanged when not only the integrand but also the measure of integration depend on
n\to\infty
. The notion of
asymptotically uniformly integrable (a.u.i)
(Definition
B.2
) relaxes the notion of lower-boundedness of the integrand, appears as a condition in the extension of Fatou’s Lemma (Theorem
B.3
), and is a key ingredient to show the
\Gamma
-convergence result in Proposition
B.5
.

Proposition B.1
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists a unique maximizer
\rho_{*}:=r(x)
solving
\amax_{\hat{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}}G(\hat{\rho},x)
. Further,
r(x)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\supp(r(x))=\supp(\tilde{\rho})
, and there exists a function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that the best response
\rho_{*}(z)=r(x)(z)
solves the Euler-Lagrange equation

\delta_{\rho}G[\rho_{*},x](z):=\alpha\log\rho_{*}(z)+m(z,x)+(W\ast\rho_{*})(z)=c(x)

(B.2)

for all
(z,x)\in\supp(\tilde{\rho})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Equivalently, consider the minimization problem for
F(\rho,x)
for some fixed
x
.
Note that
m(z,x)
is strictly convex in
z
for fixed
x
by Assumptions
1
(ii) and
2
. Together with Assumption
3
, we can directly apply the uniqueness and existence result from
[
21
, Theorem 2.1 (i)]
.

The result on the support of
r(x)
and the expression for the Euler-Lagrange equation follows by the same arguments as in Corollary
6.6
and Corollary
A.10
, using Assumption
1
(ii) and that
\alpha\tilde{\lambda}>\Lambda_{1}
. The proof that
r(x)\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
uses the same arguments as those in Proposition
A.9
, where instead of using
x=b(\rho)
,
x
is some fixed value.

Definition B.2

(asymptotically uniformly integrable,
[
40
]
)
.

A sequence of measurable
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\})
-valued functions
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called
asymptotically uniformly integrable (a.u.i) with respect to a sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if

\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow+\infty}\limsup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|f_{n}(z)|\mathbb{I}\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ :\ |f_{n}(z)|\geq R\}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)=0\,.

Theorem B.3

(Theorem 2.4 in
[
40
]
, Fatou’s Lemma for Weakly Converging Measures)
.

Let
\mathbf{S}
be a metric space,
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measures on
\mathbf{S}
that converges narrowly to
\mu\in\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{S})
, and let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of measurable
(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\})
-valued functions on
\mathbf{S}
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty,s^{\prime}\rightarrow s}f_{n}(s^{\prime})
exists for
\mu
-a.e.
s\in\mathbf{S}
. Denote
f_{n}^{-}(z)=-\min\{f_{n}(z),0\}
. Let
(f_{n}^{-})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a.u.i with respect to
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\mathbf{S}}f_{n}(s)\mu_{n}(s)(\mathrm{d}s)\leq\int_{\mathbf{S}}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty,s^{\prime}\rightarrow s}f_{n}(s^{\prime})\mu(\mathrm{d}s)\,.

Remark B.4
.

If instead the condition on
f_{n}
is strengthened to
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
being a.u.i, then the limit bound holds with equality
[
40
, Corollary 2.8]
; this result will also be utilized.

Proposition B.5

(
\Gamma
-Convergence of Energy)
.

Let Assumption
4
(b)
hold.
For any sequence
(x_{n})
converging to some limit
\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}\bar{F}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
for sequences
(\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
.

Proof.

We write

\displaystyle F_{n}(\rho)=H_{0}(\rho)+H_{n}(\rho)

where we define

\displaystyle H_{0}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\alpha\int\rho\log\rho+\frac{1}{2}\int\rho W\ast\rho\,,\qquad H_{n}(\rho)=\int m_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\,.

where
m_{n}(z)=m(z,x_{n})
. Convergence in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
is equivalent to narrow convergence
\mu_{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\bar{\mu}
in
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
together with convergence of second moments
\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)\rightarrow\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(z)
. This is equivalent to
\int\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)\rightarrow\int\varphi(z)\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(z)
for all
\varphi
with at most quadratic growth, and implies that
\mu_{n}
also converges weakly. First, we will show that for every

(\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
converging to
\bar{\mu}
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
,
we have

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\bar{\mu})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\mu_{n})\,.

We accomplish this by considering individual terms, noting that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\mu_{n})\geq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{0}(\mu_{n})+\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{n}(\mu_{n})\,,

see
[
14
, Chapter 1, (1.2)]
, and thus lower bounds for each term sufficiently lower-bound
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\mu_{n})
. By Lemma
C.4
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\mu_{n}\log\mu_{n}\geq\int\bar{\mu}\log\bar{\mu}\,.

The interaction kernel term can be written as
\iint W(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n})(z,z^{\prime})
. Since
W\geq 0
and is lower-semicontinuous due to Assumption
3
, applying
[
78
, Portmanteau Theorem 1.3.4 (iv)]
gives that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int W(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n})(z,z^{\prime})\geq\int W(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}(\bar{\mu}\times\bar{\mu})(z,z^{\prime}).

This concludes the proof that for any sequence
(\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
such that
\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu_{n},\bar{\mu})\rightarrow 0
,
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{0}(\mu_{n})\geq H_{0}(\bar{\mu})\,.

For the second term
H_{n}(\mu_{n})
, define

\displaystyle\overline{H}(\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\int m(z,\bar{x})\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(z)\,.

We now show that
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{n}(\mu_{n})\geq\overline{H}(\bar{\mu})
via Fatou’s Lemma for narrowly converging measures, Theorem
B.3
. Note that
\nabla_{z}^{2}m(z,x)\succeq\lambda_{b}\Id_{d}
for all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which implies
\left\lVert\nabla_{z}^{2}m(z,x)\right\rVert_{2}\geq\lambda_{b}
for all
x,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We also have from Assumption
4
(b) that
\left\lVert\nabla_{xz}^{2}m(z,x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq L
. Hence it follows from Lemma
C.6
that
\{m_{n}^{-}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is asymptotically uniformly integrable with respect to
(\mu_{n})
.
This allows us to apply Theorem
B.3
, which gives us that

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}H_{n}(\mu_{n})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int m_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\geq\int\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty,z^{\prime}\rightarrow z}m_{n}(z^{\prime})\bar{\mu}(z)\mathrm{d}z=\overline{H}(\bar{\mu})\,.

Secondly, we must show that for any measure
\bar{\mu}
there exists a recovery sequence
(\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
with
\mu_{n}
converging in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
to
\bar{\mu}
, such that

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\bar{\mu})\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\mu_{n})\,.

We select the constant sequence
\mu_{n}=\bar{\mu}
, and evaluate

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\bar{\mu})

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(H_{0}(\bar{\mu})+H_{n}(\bar{\mu})\right)\leq H_{0}(\bar{\mu})+\int(\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}m_{n}(z))\mathrm{d}\bar{\mu}(z)

using Fatou’s Lemma with the uniform upper bound on
m_{n}(z)
from Lemma
C.5
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\bar{\mu})\leq H_{0}(\bar{\mu})+\overline{H}(\bar{\mu})\,.

∎

Proposition B.6

(Uniform Second Moment Bound)
.

Given a sequence
x_{n}\rightarrow\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the sequence
(\rho_{n})
as defined in (
B.1
) has uniformly bounded second moments.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
C.5
(1) with
m(z,x):=-f_{1}(z,x)-\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)
, we have
m_{n}(z):=m(z,x_{n})\geq-\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
\hat{c}_{2}>0
since
\lambda_{b}>0
.
Integrating the inequality with respect to
\rho_{n}
results in

\displaystyle\hat{c}_{2}\int

\displaystyle\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)\leq\hat{c}_{1}+\int m_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq\hat{c}_{1}+\int m_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\alpha\int\rho_{n}\log\rho_{n}+\frac{\hat{c}_{2}}{2}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\hat{c}_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\int\rho_{n}W\ast\rho_{n}

using Lemma
C.3
with
\varepsilon=\hat{c}_{2}/(2\alpha)
, denoting
\hat{c}_{3}=\alpha c_{\varepsilon}
.
Using (
4.4
),
there exists
N>0
and
c_{2}>0

such that
\int f_{2}(z,x_{n})\mathrm{d}\pi(z)\leq c_{2}
for all
n\geq N
.
Then for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{c}_{2}}{2}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C+\int m_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)+\alpha\int\rho_{n}\log\rho_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\int\rho_{n}W\ast\rho_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x_{n}-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}-\int f_{2}(z,x_{n})\mathrm{d}\pi(z)

\displaystyle=C-G(\rho_{n},x_{n})

where
C\coloneqq\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{3}+c_{2}+\max_{n\geq 1}\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert x_{n}-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}
.
Since
\rho_{n}
maximizes
G
, we have
G(\rho_{n},x_{n})\geq G(\tilde{\rho},x_{n})
for any choice of
n\geq 0
. Then for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle\frac{\hat{c}_{2}}{2}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)

\displaystyle\leq C-G(\tilde{\rho},x_{n})\leq C+\max_{x}(-G(\tilde{\rho},x))\,.

The right-hand side is finite and bounded by some constant independent of
n
since
G(\tilde{\rho},\cdot)
is twice continuously differentiable and strongly
\lambda_{d}
-convex over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see proof of Lemma
A.2
).
∎

Proposition B.7

(Convergence of Second Moments)
.

Let Assumptions

4
-
6
hold. If
x_{n}\rightarrow\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the sequence
(\rho_{n})
as defined in (
B.1

\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)\rightarrow\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\bar{\rho}(z)

Proof.

Note that
(\rho_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by Proposition
B.1
. We will show convergence of the second moments by showing that
(\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\rho_{n}(z))

\displaystyle\lim_{K\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{n}\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\rho_{n}(z)\mathbbm{1}\{z:\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\rho_{n}(z)\geq K\}\,\mathrm{d}z=0\,.

Since
\rho_{n}
is a minimizer of

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(z)=\tilde{c}(x_{n})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}m(z,x_{n})-\frac{1}{\alpha}W\ast\rho_{n}\right)\leq\tilde{c}(x_{n})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\alpha}m(z,x_{n})\right)\,,

where we used that

since

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(z)\leq\tilde{c}(x_{n})\exp\left(\frac{\hat{c}_{1}-\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}}{\alpha}\right)

In order to obtain an upper-bound for

The upper-bound on
\rho_{n}
is therefore

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(z)\leq\tilde{c}_{0}\exp\left(\hat{c}_{1}-\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\right)\quad\text{for a.e. }z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,

(B.3)

which shows that

Corollary B.8
.

Let Assumptions
4
-
6
hold. The best response
r(x)\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
\mathscr{W}_{2}
-continuous in

, and

is continuous in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Given any sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
as
n\to\infty
, we have that
F_{n}\xrightarrow{\Gamma}\bar{F}
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
for sequences such that
(\mu_{n})\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by Proposition
B.5
.
From Lemma

exists and is unique for every
x_{n}
; similarly,

is the unique minimizer of
\bar{F}
. Uniform boundedness of second moments for
(r(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}})
follows from Proposition

,
and tightness of
(r(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

. Then precompactness of

. Together with convergence of second moments (Proposition

converges to

in
\mathscr{W}_{2}

[
13
, Theorem 2.10]
. In other words, the best response
r(x)
is continuous in
x
in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
.

Recall that
G_{d}(x)\coloneqq\max_{\rho\in\mathcal{P}}G(\rho,x)
. For any fixed
\rho
,

is continuous in

due to our assumptions. Because the maximum over continuous functions is lower-semicontinuous,

. From Proposition

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\mu_{n})\geq\bar{F}(\bar{\mu})\,.

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}G_{d}(x_{n})=-\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}F_{n}(\rho_{n})+\frac{\kappa}{2}\left\lVert\bar{x}-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}+\int f_{2}(z,\bar{x})\mathrm{d}\pi(z)\leq G_{d}(\bar{x})\,,

which means that

is upper-semicontinuous in

with respect to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Since both lower- and upper-semicontinuity have been shown,

is continuous in

.

Lemma B.9

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\iint\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi(z)\\
x-\nabla\psi(x)\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}-\nabla_{1}^{2}f(y_{s})&0\\
0&\nabla_{2}^{2}f(y_{s})\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi(z)\\
x-\nabla\psi(x)\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma(z,x)\mathrm{d}s\,.

By Assumption
1
(ii), we use that
-\nabla_{z}^{2}f(z,x)\succeq\lambda_{f,1}\Id_{d_{1}}
and
\nabla_{x}^{2}f(z,x)\succeq\lambda_{f,2}\Id_{d_{2}}
to obtain

\displaystyle C_{2}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})\geq\lambda_{f,1}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{2}+\lambda_{f,2}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})^{2}\,.

Next, we show a lower-bound for
C_{3}
. We show the exact Taylor expansion calculation explicitly for the first term:

\displaystyle(\nabla W_{1}\ast\tilde{\rho})(\nabla\varphi(z))-(\nabla W_{1}\ast\rho)(z)

\displaystyle=\int\nabla W_{1}(\nabla\varphi(z)-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(z^{\prime})-\int\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int[\nabla W_{1}(\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}))-\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})]\mathrm{d}\rho(z^{\prime})\,.

Define
z_{s}\coloneqq(1-s)(z-z^{\prime})+s(\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}))
. The expansion of
\nabla W_{1}
around
z-z^{\prime}
is

\displaystyle\nabla W_{1}(\nabla\varphi(z)-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime}))=\nabla W_{1}(z-z^{\prime})+\int\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})(\nabla\varphi(z)-z)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})(\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime})-z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}s\,,

which, after integrating against
(z-\nabla\varphi(z))\rho(z^{\prime})\rho(z)
and
relabeling, results in a quadratic and can be bound via Assumption
3
using that
\nabla^{2}W_{1}
is even:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\iint\int_{0}^{1}\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi(z)\\
z^{\prime}-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\begin{bmatrix}\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})&-\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})\\
-\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})&\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z_{s})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi(z)\\
z^{\prime}-\nabla\varphi(z^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}\rho(z^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho(z)\geq 0\,,

since the eigenvalues of
\begin{bmatrix}\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z)&-\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z)\\
-\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z)&\nabla^{2}W_{1}(z)\end{bmatrix}
have a tight lower-bound of zero
(\lambda_{W,1}\geq 0)
.
Computing a similar bound for the term dependent on
\mu
, we have

\displaystyle C_{3}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})\geq 0\,.

Lastly, we show the bound for
C_{4}
. Computing for just the
\rho
-dependent term, we have

\displaystyle\int(z-\nabla\varphi(z))

\displaystyle\cdot(\nabla V_{1}(\nabla\varphi(z))-\nabla V_{1}(z))\mathrm{d}\rho(z)

\displaystyle=-\int\int_{0}^{1}(z-\nabla\varphi(z))\nabla^{2}V_{1}((1-s)z+s\nabla\varphi(z))(z-\nabla\varphi(z))\mathrm{d}\rho(z)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda_{V,1}\int\left\lVert z-\nabla\varphi(z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)=-\lambda_{V,1}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{2}\,,

using Assumption
2
.
Computing similarly for the term dependent on
\mu
, we have
C_{4}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})\geq\lambda_{V,1}\mathscr{W}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})^{2}+\lambda_{V,2}\mathscr{W}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})^{2}
.
We now have shown that
C_{1}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})+C_{2}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})+C_{3}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})+C_{4}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})\geq\lambda_{c}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma,\tilde{\gamma})^{2}
, concluding the proof.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let Assumptions
1
(ii),
2
, and
3
hold with
\lambda_{c}>0
. Fix
T>0
. Let
\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
be any two solutions of the dynamics (
1.4
), with initial conditions
\rho_{0},\rho_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\mu_{0}=\delta_{x_{0}},\mu_{0}^{\prime}=\delta_{x_{0}^{\prime}}
, with
\alpha>0
and
\beta=0
such that
\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}(\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}})
and
\mu_{t}=\delta_{x_{t}},\mu_{t}^{\prime}=\delta_{x_{t}^{\prime}}
for all
t\in(0,T)
. Assume
D_{c}(\gamma_{0})<\infty
,
D_{c}(\gamma_{0}^{\prime})<\infty
and
\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z),\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z)
are locally Lipschitz in
z
for all
t\in[0,T)
.
Then
\gamma_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
satisfy

\displaystyle\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})\leq e^{-\lambda_{c}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{0}^{\prime})\quad\text{ for all }t\in[0,T)\,.

Proof.

Define
\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)
and
T_{t}(x)
such that
\rho_{t}=\nabla\varphi_{t\#}\rho_{t}^{\prime}
and
x_{t}=T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime})
. Note that
\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)
is invertible because
\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime}
are absolutely continuous. The time derivative of
\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\lVert x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}\,.

To compute the time derivative of the Wasserstein-2 squared distance via
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
, we use that

\displaystyle\int\left\lVert\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z)\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+\left\lVert\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](x_{t})\right\rVert^{2}<\infty\,,

thanks to Lemma
7.2
,
with the same holding true for
\gamma_{t}^{\prime}
. Then by
[
79
, Theorem 23.9]
, the time derivative of
\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{2}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z),\,z-\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](z),\,z-(\nabla\varphi_{t})^{-1}(z)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=2\int\left\langle\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z)-\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)),\,z-\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{\prime}(z)

Since
\mu
evolves as a Dirac for all time, the dynamics for
x_{t}
are

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{t}=v(\rho_{t},x_{t})\coloneqq-\nabla_{x}\left(\int f(z,x_{t})\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(z)+V_{2}(x_{t})\right)\,,

Note that the interaction term does not appear in the dynamics because

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\int\delta_{x_{t}}W_{2}\ast\delta_{x_{t}}=\nabla_{x}W_{2}(x_{t}-x_{t})=\nabla_{x}W_{2}(0)=0\,.

The time derivative of
\left\lVert x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}
is therefore

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}=2\left\langle x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime},\,v(\rho_{t},x_{t})-v(\rho_{t}^{\prime},x_{t}^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left\langle x_{t}^{\prime}-T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime}),\,v(\rho_{t}^{\prime},x_{t}^{\prime})-v(\rho_{t},T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime}))\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\left\langle x_{t}^{\prime}-T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime}),\,\nabla_{x}\left(\int(f(z,x_{t}^{\prime})-f(\nabla\varphi_{t}(z),T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime})))\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}^{\prime}(z)+V_{2}(x_{t}^{\prime})-V_{2}(T_{t}(x_{t}^{\prime}))\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\int\left\langle x-T_{t}(x),\,\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](x)-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](T_{t}(x))\right\rangle\mathrm{d}\mu_{t}^{\prime}(x)\,,

where
\mu_{t}^{\prime}=\delta_{x_{t}^{\prime}}
.
Summing
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\lVert x_{t}-x_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathscr{W}_{2}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{2}
results in

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\overline{\mathscr{W}}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t}^{\prime})^{2}=-2\iint\begin{bmatrix}z-\nabla\varphi_{t}(z)\\
x_{t}-T_{t}(x)\end{bmatrix}\cdot\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](\nabla\varphi_{t}(z))-\nabla_{z}\delta_{\rho}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](z)\\
\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}^{\prime}](x)-\nabla_{x}\delta_{\mu}F_{c}[\gamma_{t}](T_{t}(x))\end{bmatrix}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{t}^{\prime}(z,x)\,,

which is the same expression given in the proof of contraction in the setting where both
\mu_{t},\rho_{t}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}^{ac}
(Proposition
7.3
) by choosing
\psi_{t}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{t}^{\prime}+x_{t}\right\rVert^{2}
resulting in
T_{t}=\nabla\psi_{t}
on
\supp\mu_{t}^{\prime}
. Contraction, boundedness of the second moments, and convergence to the steady state follow similarly.
∎

Lemma C.3
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that
\int\rho\log\rho\geq-\varepsilon\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)-c_{\varepsilon}
, for some
c_{\varepsilon}\in[0,\infty)
.

Proof.

Consider the function
a(x):[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined as
a(x)=x\log x
. The Legendre dual, given by

a^{*}(y)=\sup_{x\geq 0}\left[x\cdot y-a(x)\right]\,,

is
a^{*}(y)=e^{y-1}
. By definition, for all
x,y\in[0,\infty)
, it holds that
a(x)+a^{*}(y)\geq x\cdot y
. Selecting
x=\rho(z)
and

y=-\varepsilon\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}
for any
\varepsilon>0
, for any value of
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\rho(z)\log\rho(z)+e^{-\varepsilon\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}-1}\geq-\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\varepsilon\rho(z)\,,

and integrating this function over all
z
results in

\displaystyle\int\rho\log\rho\,\mathrm{d}z\geq-\varepsilon\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho(z)-c_{\varepsilon}\,,

where
c_{\varepsilon}:=\int e^{-\varepsilon\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}-1}\mathrm{d}z\in[0,\infty)
.
∎

Lemma C.4

(Lower Semicontinuity of Entropy)
.

For every sequence
\rho_{n}\rightharpoonup\rho
converging weakly according to Definition
2.1
, we have
\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\rho_{n}\log\rho_{n}\geq\int\rho\log\rho
.

Proof.

A uniform bound on the second moment implies a uniform bound on the first moment:

\displaystyle\int\left\lVert z\right\rVert\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)\leq\int\left(\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(z)\leq C+\frac{1}{4}\,,

then the result follows from
[
74
, Proposition 2.1]
.
∎

Lemma C.5

(Upper and Lower Bounds on
m
)
.

Let
m(z,x)\coloneqq-f_{1}(z,x)-\alpha\log\tilde{\rho}(z)\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x_{n}\rightarrow\bar{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

(1)

Under Assumptions
1
(ii) and
2
, there exist constants
\hat{c}_{1},\hat{c}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle m(z,x_{n})\geq-\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Further, if
\lambda_{b}>0
, then one can choose
\hat{c}_{2}>0
.

(2)

Under Assumptions
4
and
5
, there exists constants
c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
c_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle m(z,x_{n})\leq c_{1}+c_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,,n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Proof.

Taylor expanding
m(z,x)
first in
z
and then the second term in
x
, we have

\displaystyle m(z,x)

\displaystyle=m(0,x)+z^{\top}\nabla_{z}m(0,x)+\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}\nabla_{z}^{2}m(\xi_{2},x)z

\displaystyle=m(0,x)+z^{\top}\nabla_{z}m(0,0)+z^{\top}\nabla_{xz}^{2}m(0,\xi_{1})x+\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}\nabla_{z}^{2}m(\xi_{2},x)z\,,

with
\xi_{1}=sx
for some
s\in[0,1]
,
\xi_{2}=\tau z
for some
\tau\in[0,1]
.

Proof of (1): By Assumptions
1
(ii) and
2
, we have for some continuous function
\hat{c}(x)>0

\displaystyle m(z,x)\geq m(0,x)+z^{\top}\nabla_{z}m(0,0)-L\|z\|\|x\|+\frac{\lambda_{b}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\geq\hat{c}(x)+\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\,,

with
\hat{c}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
and
\hat{c}_{2}>0
if
\lambda_{b}>0
.
Substituting
x=x_{n}
in the above estimate and using that
x_{n}
converges to
\bar{x}
, there exists a constant
\hat{c}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle m(z,x_{n})\geq-\hat{c}_{1}+\hat{c}_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\,.

(C.1)

Proof of (2): Similarly for the upper bound,

\displaystyle m(z,x)

\displaystyle\leq m(0,x)+z^{\top}\nabla_{z}m(0,0)+L\|z\|\|x\|+\frac{-\ell_{1}+\alpha\tilde{\Lambda}}{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\leq c(x)+c_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\,,

where
c_{2}>0

and
c(x)
is a continuous function. The upper bounds
-\ell_{1}
and

come from Assumption
4

and
\tilde{\Lambda}
from Assumption
5
. Again using that
x_{n}
converges to
\bar{x}
, there exists a constant
c_{1}
such that

\displaystyle m(z,x_{n})\leq c_{1}+c_{2}\left\lVert z\right\rVert^{2}\,.

∎

Lemma C.6
.

Let
m\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\|\nabla^{2}_{z}m(z,x)\|_{2}\geq\lambda
and
\|\nabla^{2}_{xz}m(z,x)\|_{2}\leq L
for all
z,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for some
\lambda,L>0
.
Consider a sequence of vectors
x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
converging to some limit
\bar{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any sequence of measures
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
narrowly converging to some limit
\bar{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Then there exists a constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that
m_{n}(z)\coloneqq m(z,x_{n})\geq c
for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for all
n\geq 0
. In particular,
(m_{n}^{-})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is asymptotically uniformly integrable with respect to
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
m_{n}^{-}(z)=-\min\{m_{n}(z),0\}
.

Proof.

Let
z^{*}(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
m^{*}(x):\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, and
\bar{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be given by

\displaystyle m^{*}(x)\coloneqq\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(z,x)\,,\quad z^{*}(x)\coloneqq\amin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(z,x)\,,\quad\bar{z}=\amin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}m(z,\bar{x})\,.

Showing that
(m^{*}(x_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
has a uniform lower bound is sufficient to achieve the desired a.u.i result for
(m_{n}^{-})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We will show this uniform lower bound
using the implicit function theorem
[
38
, Theorem C.7]
. By definition of the best response,

\displaystyle\nabla_{z}m(z,x)\big|_{z=z*(x)}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad\nabla_{z}^{2}m(z^{*}(x),x)\nabla_{x}z^{*}(x)+\nabla_{zx}^{2}m(z^{*}(x),x)=0\,,

and since
\nabla_{z}^{2}m(z^{*}(x),x)
is invertible due to strong convexity,

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}z^{*}(x)

\displaystyle=-\left(\nabla_{z}^{2}m(z,x)^{-1}\nabla_{zx}^{2}m(z,x)\right)\big|_{z=z^{*}(x)}\,.

Using the matrix bounds results in the following bound for
\nabla_{x}z^{*}(x)

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{x}z^{*}(x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L}{\lambda}\quad\forall\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.

This gradient bound provides a bound on the distance of
z^{*}(x_{n})
from
z^{*}(\bar{x})
as

\displaystyle\left\lVert z^{*}(x_{n})-z^{*}(\bar{x})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{L}{\lambda}\left\lVert x_{n}-\bar{x}\right\rVert\,.

In particular,
z^{*}(x)
is continuous and therefore
m^{*}(x)\coloneqq m(z^{*}(x),x)
is continuous in
x
.
Hence
m(z,x_{n})\geq m^{*}(x_{n})\geq c
for all
n>0
for some
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
c\geq 0
, then the a.u.i condition is immediately satisfied because
m_{n}^{-}(z)=0
for all
n
. If
c<0
, then for all
K>|c|
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int|m_{n}^{-}(z)|\mathbbm{1}\{z:|m_{n}^{-}(z)|\geq K\}\mathrm{d}\mu_{n}(z)=0

and
m_{n}^{-}
is therefore asymptotically uniformly integrable.
∎
