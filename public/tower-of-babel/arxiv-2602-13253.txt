Implicit Bias in LLMs for Transgender Populations
Micaela Hirsch, Marina Elichiry, Blas Radi Tamara Quiroga, David Restrepo, Luciana Benotti, Veronica Xhardez, Jocelyn Dunstan, Enzo Ferrante

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) have been shown to exhibit biases against LGBTQ+ populations. While safety training may lessen explicit expressions of bias, previous work has shown that implicit stereotype-driven associations often persist. In this work, we examine implicit bias toward transgender people in two main scenarios. First, we adapt word association tests to measure whether LLMs disproportionately pair negative concepts with “transgender” and positive concepts with “cisgender”. Second, acknowledging the well-documented systemic challenges that transgender people encounter in real-world healthcare settings, we examine implicit biases that may emerge when LLMs are applied to healthcare decision-making. To this end, we design a healthcare appointment allocation task where models act as scheduling agents choosing between cisgender and transgender candidates across medical specialties prone to stereotyping. We evaluate seven LLMs in English and Spanish. Our results show consistent bias in categories such as appearance, risk, and veracity, indicating stronger negative associations with transgender individuals. In the allocation task, transgender candidates are favored for STI and mental health services, while cisgender candidates are preferred in gynecology and breast care. These findings underscore the need for research that address subtle stereotype-driven biases in LLMs to ensure equitable treatment of transgender people in healthcare applications.

2
Surfacing implicit biases via word association tests

Prior work shows that LLMs may appear unbiased on explicit prompts yet still exhibit implicit biases in indirect tasks, and that explicit bias mitigation can even amplify such effects
1
;
10
. To investigate implicit biases, we follow the methodology introduced in
1
to quantify them via word association tests. We task seven LLMs to associate a set of positive and negative concepts with the words “cisgender” and “transgender”. Then, we proceed to measure implicit biases by quantifying how many times positive and negative terms are associated to each word, and compute a bias score defined as the normalized difference in assignment frequency to each group (“cisgender” or “transgender”). We design 8 categories of words that allow positive and negative concepts:
emotions
,
morality
,
risk
,
veracity
,
appearance
,
knowledge
,
legality and crime
, and
life outcomes
(see complete word list in the Appendix
B.2
). These each have 15 positive and 15 negative words. For each category we sample 10 words (5 from each sentiment) for a total of 50 iterations, and average results across all words from the same category.
The concepts originate from a list of prejudices constructed by members of the research group in consultation with transgender individuals, which is included in Appendix
B.3
. From this list, we extracted words making sure that each one was unambiguously positive or negative. Afterwards, following the methodology introduced in Bai et al. 2025, we grouped the words into the 8 categories present in the paper. We apply one out of three prompt templates, from
1
, chosen uniformly at random to filter out the noise that may come from the model being sensitive to the instruction phrasing
14
.

We measure bias as introduced in
1
. For each category
x
:

\mathrm{Bias}(x)=\frac{\#N_{x,t}}{\#P_{x,t}+\#N_{x,t}}+\frac{\#P_{x,c}}{\#N_{x,c}+\#P_{x,c}}-1,

where
P_{x}
represent positive words for category
x
and
N_{x}
negative words;
\#P_{x,c}
is the amount of times a positive word of category
x
was tagged with the word cisgender (
\#P_{x,t}
for transgender), and correspondingly for
\#N_{x,c}
and
\#N_{x,t}
with negative words.
A score value close to 1 would represent bias coming from the models associating positive words to "cisgender" and negative ones to "transgender", whereas a score close to -1 represents the opposite. Lastly, a score closer to 0 represents no bias.

3
Implicit biases in health-related resource allocation

In the previous experiment we exposed the existence of implicit biases in LLMs against transgender individuals via word association tests.
Previous work by Bai et al
1
has shown that word association bias correlates with what they call
relative decision bias
, i.e. the systematic tendency of an LLM to make discriminatory choices when comparing two candidates in a relative decision task. Following this hypothesis, here we moved from word association tests to decision scenarios, focusing on allocation tasks by prompting the LLMs to function as a medical appointment scheduling agent.
The agent was always tasked to assign medical appointments of a particular health specialty to one of two possible candidates: one cisgender and one transgender. Notably, all models explicitly affirmed that both candidates should be treated equally when asked directly (see Table
Appendix 3
). We then generated a fictitious list of 100 patients represented by the patient’s full name, age, sex assigned at birth and gender identity using GPT 4o. The list was equally distributed in terms of age and gender identity (see Figure
Appendix 1
for more details). We sampled 100 pairs of patients, and each pair was presented twice with the candidate order reversed
(for a total of 200 instances)
to mitigate order bias. To examine language effects and assess whether reasoning serves as a debiasing mechanism
8
, we conduct the allocation task in both English and Spanish under two conditions: (i) selection only, and (ii) selection accompanied by an explanation.

The models had to choose exactly one candidate per pair across 8 disciplines. We group disciplines into three domains where inequitable allocations could plausibly manifest. We selected disciplines where the need is based on the sexual organs of the patient and disciplines associated with known prejudices against the LGBTQ+ community. The potential impact lies in systematically over or under-allocating services by identity when no relevant differences in clinical information is provided:

•

Medical services linked with STIs and mental health:
assign appointments for HIV testing, STI testing and mental health clinic appointments.

•

Medical specialties usually associated with individuals whose sex assigned at birth is female:
assign appointments for medical services associated to Gynecological and Breast Care including gynecology, breast ultrasound, mastectomy, and transvaginal ultrasound.

•

Medical specialties usually associated with individuals whose sex assigned at birth is male:
assign appointments for a prostate study.

Medical services linked with STIs and mental health:
Figure

Importantly, requiring models to provide explanations does not substantially alter these patterns, as the direction and magnitude of the bias remain largely unchanged, indicating that explanation does not operate as a debiasing mechanism in these domains. In particular, in mental health appointments this method even increases allocation towards transgender individuals.
Similar results can be seen in Spanish as well, as indicated by Figure

Medical specialties usually associated with a specific sex assigned at birth:
In every Gynecological and Breast Care service, most models favor cisgender candidates, showing a strong bias which could lead to transgender men getting gynecology appointments with much more delay than cisgender women and implicitly minimize the need of gynecological care for trans men. Grok 3 mini, however, does not assign with significant bias. In prostate study, the bias again tilts toward cisgender patients, except for Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite, which is closer to parity. When required to explain their decisions, the magnitude of these biases often diminishes, effectively debiasing. For example, in gynecology and prostate appointments in English, GPT-4o mini, GPT-4o, and Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite show a noticeable shift toward more balanced allocations. However, the effect is partial: while explanation narrows the gap, it does not eliminate it, and in cases like Grok 3 the cisgender preference remains pronounced.

The observed results suggest that the models rely on strong stereotypical associations between patient identity and medical needs. Bias toward transgender individuals in services related to STIs and mental health may reflect stereotypical and pathologizing assumptions. Conversely, the bias towards cisgender in specialties like gynecology and prostate care highlights the models’ association of these services with cisgender people.
This preference for cisgender women could suggest the models conflate population-level prevalence with individual medical priority. While cisgender women statistically represent the majority of this patient group, transgender men retain the same anatomical necessity for gynecological care. Consequently, prioritizing a cisgender candidate when urgency is equal indicates that the models are relying on base rates to treat trans identity as lower-priority, rather than applying equitable clinical standards.

As a control, we repeated the experiment while augmenting each patient profile with clinical symptoms. For each discipline, we compiled a list of 10 representative annotated by a medical doctor as either
high urgency
or
low urgency
, included in Appendix
F
. In this setting, both candidates were assigned randomly sampled symptoms of the same urgency level, so that no clinically justified preference existed. The corresponding results are reported in Figures
Appendix 4
and
Appendix 3
for English and Spanish, respectively. Interestingly, we observe that incorporating symptoms reduces the magnitude of the observed biases, suggesting that models partially rely on clinical information rather than demographic attributes alone. However, a consistent (though attenuated) pattern of identity-based allocation persists.

To better understand the rationale behind these allocations, we include in the Table
Appendix 1
exemplar cases of LLM self-explanations when performing this task. In gynecology, both GPT 4o and Grok 3 prioritize cis women by treating them as the default patients, excluding trans men from rightful access. In mental health, they instead prioritize trans patients, but based on assumed vulnerability just for being transgender rather than clinical evidence. Similarly, Llama 3 70B excludes a transgender patient from a transvaginal ultrasound based on the clinically flawed assumption that gender-affirming care negates the need for the procedure, since SOC 8 recommends that health care professionals offer cervical, breast, ovarian, and endometrial cancer screening to transgender individuals who have these organs, following local guidelines for cisgender women
3
. Together, these reveal exclusion in reproductive care and over-pathologization in mental health, rooted in stereotypes rather than actual need.

Although simplified, our setup captures realistic healthcare tie-breaking scenarios such as cancellations, waitlists, or concurrent requests where only one patient can be assigned to a scarce slot. In this narrow decision support role (ranking requests or recommending who should receive the next appointment) our paired-candidate design holds medical information constant, so any deviation from parity reveals demographic-based bias.

4
Conclusions

Our experiments reveal persistent implicit biases toward transgender individuals. Models associate negative concepts with “transgender” and exhibit systematic allocation patterns, favoring cisgender patients in gynecological care and transgender patients in STI and mental health services. Adding medical context and requiring explanations reduce but do not eliminate these effects, highlighting the limits of current zero-shot debiasing and risk of inequitable healthcare access when decisions rely on socio-demographic cues rather than clinical need.

Limitations

(1) In this work, we only study model behavior in English and Spanish; it may differ in other languages, particularly those with limited training representation or different socio-cultural contexts. (2) While we examine the content of the models’ self-explanations, we recognize that these may not faithfully represent the true rationale underlying their outputs. We acknowledge that these results should be interpreted with caution, as self-explanations may misrepresent the true reason for a model’s prediction
17
. However, we include them as they may still serve to shed some light on the rational behind the aforementioned biases. (3) Our evaluation is limited to a selected set of tasks and scenarios, which may not capture the full spectrum of contexts in which implicit bias or differential treatment can emerge. Broader task coverage and real-world evaluations would be necessary for more general conclusions. (4) The findings are based on static model versions and specific prompting conditions; variations in model updates, fine-tuning, or prompt phrasing could lead to different behaviors.
(5) Our word association test does not explicitly account for negation or pragmatic context, so the presence of a word may not always reflect a true negative or positive association.

Ethical Considerations

This study investigates implicit bias in large language models (LLMs) toward transgender individuals, particularly in healthcare-related contexts. All experiments were conducted using synthetic and publicly available data, ensuring that no real personal or clinical information was used. The design of the word association and appointment allocation tasks was exclusively intended to evaluate model behavior, not to reproduce or reinforce stereotypes. Our findings aim to advance understanding of bias mechanisms in LLMs to promote fairness and accountability in AI systems. We acknowledge the ethical responsibility of developing and reporting such evaluations transparently, as exposing and mitigating these biases is essential to prevent the perpetuation of inequities that affect marginalized populations, especially in high-stakes applications such as healthcare.

•

•

•

Transvaginal ultrasound:
You are an agent managing a high-demand gynecological imaging service. Several patients are awaiting a transvaginal ultrasound for critical diagnostic purposes. Select one of the following people to go first in the queue.

Mastectomy:
You are an agent scheduling surgical procedures in an oncology department. Several patients have been approved for mastectomy. Choose who should receive surgery first based on urgency and clinical factors.

Prostate study:
