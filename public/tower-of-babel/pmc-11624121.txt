We present a case where fiberoptic intubation was unsuccessful because of equipment error following continuous suction pressure application.

Introduction

Flexible fibreoptic intubation (FFI) is a strategy of anticipated and unanticipated difficult airway management. It is a cornerstone during difficult airway management in patients who are awake, sedated, or under general anesthesia. Fiberoptic intubation involves using a flexible endoscope, with a tracheal tube mounted along its length, to navigate through the glottis. Identifying the location of the vocal cord, insertion of the fibrescope into the trachea, and insertion of a tube over the fibrescope into the trachea are crucial steps of FFI. Difficulties can be encountered at any of these steps, leading to failure. We faced a situation of failed FFI in an unanticipated difficult airway. Because of continuous suction application, the airway collapsed and obscured the view. The suction port of the fibrescope lacked an opening in the valve attached to the fiberscope, which created continuous pressure.

Case History

A 49‐year‐old gentleman, 165 cm tall and weighing 78 kg (kg), was planned for laparoscopic right inguinal hernia repair. Preoperative airway examination showed a short, thick neck with a circumference of 40 cm and a full mouth opening with adequate neck extension with Mallampati Class III.

The patient was kept in a sniffing position under standard monitoring. After preoxygenation for 3 min, anesthesia induction was done with 2% lidocaine 40 mg (mg), fentanyl (1 μg/kg), and propofol (2 mg/kg). Manual breathing was adequately maintained by mask ventilation following induction agents, and vecuronium 8 mg was used for muscle relaxation. Direct laryngoscopic intubation with a macintosh number 4 blade was attempted but was unsuccessful because of Cormack and Lahane's grade 3 view.

Methods

Fiberoptic intubation was planned as an institutional protocol. After ensuring a properly functioning light source, the anesthesia assistant attached the camera to the eyepiece, the lens was focused by visualizing fine print, and the camera was oriented at the 12 o'clock position. A 7‐mm tracheal tube was railroaded into the fiberscope. The suction apparatus was attached to the fiberscope by an anesthesia assistant. The fiberscope was negotiated through the oral cavity but could not obtain the optimum view of airway structures, leading to the failure of intubation.

We inserted a supraglottic airway device i‐gel, and adequate ventilation was confirmed with a capnograph. The third attempt of fiberoptic intubation through the i‐gel was done, but we still couldn't obtain the optimum view as oropharyngeal structures were obscuring our laryngeal view. The tip of the scope was lying against the mucosal surface pink out. Then, the tip was withdrawn slightly, but the view was still not re‐established. We rechecked all the equipment parts and found that the suction apparatus was continuously creating negative pressure and the airway soft tissues were collapsing, which caused procedural difficulty. The suction apparatus was disconnected, which led to the release of the oropharyngeal structures, and optimum view could be attained, successful tracheal fiberoptic intubation was performed, and the airway was secured.

Outcome

As we found later, the suction adapter was misplaced, and it was replaced with an adapter used in laparoscopic sets, which did not have the suction valve with an opening. This caused the suction to function continuously even when the suction button was not pressed, leading to the high negative pressure inside the oropharyngeal cavity and causing the airway structure's collapse, leading to failed fibreoptic intubation (Figure 1).

Discussion

The anesthesiology practice management guidelines define a difficult airway as a clinical situation in which a trained anesthesiologist experiences difficulty with face mask ventilation of the upper airway, difficulty with tracheal intubation, or both [1] can be both anticipated and unanticipated airway problem which is faced on a day to day practice and fiber optic intubation is an effective technique of management of difficult airways in various clinical scenarios.

Under general anesthesia, the loss of muscle tone in the pharyngeal and laryngeal structures and apposition of the tongue base to the posterior pharyngeal wall may obscure the operator's view of the laryngeal aperture during fiberoptic intubation (FOI) [2]. To achieve a clear laryngeal view, several tips are recommended, such as jaw thrust, oral airway placement, and neck extension [3], which were all performed in our case.

The operation room has a centralized vacuum system comprising a pump, receiver, and filter to which the suction apparatus is connected. A vacuum pump creates a negative pressure that pulls any blood, secretions, mucus, or saliva to clear the patients' airway. Each pump creates a vacuum pressure of at least 67 kilopascal. Aspiration of secretions requires accurate timing in initiating and discontinuing negative pressure. Attempts at suctioning fail if the tip is 1 to 2 mm away from secretions [4]. A suction pressure of ∼120 mm Hg was able to displace the tongue anteriorly [5], which helped improve the visualization of the epiglottis. Excessive application of suction results in the apposition of mucosa against the bronchoscope end and further obscuring the view, so suctioning must be terminated rapidly to prevent complete collapse to distal tip of the instrument.

Conclusion

Our case report demonstrates the difficulty of fiber optic intubation, with unusual equipment errors, such as excessive suction pressure leading to failure to secure the airway. Proper vigilance is required regarding the rearranging of equipment.
