One- and two-particle spectral gap identities for the symmetric inclusion process and related models
Seonwoo Kim, Federico Sau

Abstract
. The symmetric inclusion process (SIP) models particles diffusing on a graph with mutual attraction. We recently showed [ KS24 ] that, in the log-concave regime (where diffusivity dominates interaction), the spectral gap of the conservative SIP matches that of a single particle. In this paper, our main result demonstrates that this identity generally fails outside this regime, but always holds for the non-conservative SIP, regardless of the interaction strength. When this one-particle spectral gap identity breaks down, we derive sharp bounds for the gap in terms of diffusivity, and reveal a two-particle spectral gap identity in the vanishing diffusivity limit. Our approach leverages the rigid eigenstructure of SIP, refined comparisons of Dirichlet forms for arbitrary diffusivity and particle numbers, and techniques from slow-fast system analysis. These findings extend to the dual interacting diffusion known as Brownian energy process, and shed some light on the spectral gap behavior for related Dirichlet-reversible systems on general, non-mean-field, geometries.

Primary 60K35; secondary 60J27, 05C50.

1.
Introduction

The
symmetric inclusion process
(
{\rm SIP}
) is a system of interacting particles hopping on the sites
x,y,z,\ldots\in V
of a finite graph
G
. In its conservative version, each particle jumps from a site
x
to another one
y
at rate
c_{xy}\left(\alpha_{y}+\eta_{y}\right)
, where:

•

c_{xy}=c_{yx}\geq 0
is a symmetric weight attached to the edge
xy
;

•

\alpha_{y}>0
is a weight attached to the arrival site
y
;

•

\eta_{y}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
denotes the number of particles sitting on
y
.

While the rate
c_{xy}\,\alpha_{y}
accounts for the free motion (or diffusion) of each particle,
c_{xy}\,\eta_{y}
introduces an interaction among them by favoring jumps on top of already occupied sites. Moreover, this dynamics is conservative (i.e., it preserves the total number of particles), reversible with respect to a discrete Dirichlet distribution, and irreducible as soon as the underlying graph is connected.
We refer to Section
2
below for the detailed definition of the process and its properties.

For now, let us just remark that
{\rm SIP}
arises within different contexts (e.g., as a discrete model of heat conduction
[
GKR07
]
, and as a multi-type Moran model from population genetics with non-mean-field interactions), and comes with closely related models from statistical physics. For instance, by setting
\alpha_{y}=1
and replacing the plus sign with a minus sign in the rates, one obtains the renowned symmetric exclusion process
[
Spi70
]
. Furthermore, SIP admits a continuous-spin counterpart, referred to as the Brownian energy process
[
GKRV09
]
(see also Section
8.1
below). This continuous-spin model, roughly speaking, describes the energies of an instance of Kac’s walk
[
Kac56
]
, in which velocities evolve as interacting diffusions, rather than being instantaneously updated at random “collision” times.

In all these models, a central question is that of determining precise convergence rates to equilibrium, and, in the context of reversible processes, one of the most investigated quantities for this purpose is the so-called
spectral gap
.

1.1.
One-particle spectral gap identity

For
{\rm SIP}
on a graph
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
endowed with site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
,

(1.1)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)>0

denotes the corresponding spectral gap, i.e., the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of (the negative of) the infinitesimal generator (see Section
2
for the precise definition). In a previous work
[
KS24
]
, we established the following bounds on
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
in terms of
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)
, the spectral gap of the random walk (
{\rm RW}
) on
G
which jumps from
x
to
y
with rate
c_{xy}\,\alpha_{y}
: for all graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
,

(1.2)

\left(1\wedge\alpha_{\rm min}\right){\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\;\text{.}

Here and all throughout,
a\wedge b\coloneqq\min\{a,b\}
and
\alpha_{\rm min}\coloneqq\min_{x\in V}\alpha_{x}
.

Notably, as soon as
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1
, the inequalities in (
1.2
) saturate to an identity:

(1.3)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1\;\text{.}

This one-particle reduction corresponds to a
{\rm SIP}
-version of the celebrated Aldous’ spectral gap conjecture, originally formulated for interchange and symmetric exclusion processes in the early 90s, and settled two decades later in
[
CLR10
]
. In fact, the identity in (
1.3
) is extremely powerful, as it reduces, for every underlying graph
G
, the spectral gap of
{\rm SIP}
— an infinite system, as it may consist of arbitrarily many particles — to that of
{\rm RW}
, a much simpler Markov chain with finite state space
V
, for which several techniques to bound the spectral gap in terms of simple features of the underlying graph
G
are known (see, e.g.,
[
SC97
,
MT06
,
LP17
,
Her23
]
). For completeness, let us emphasize that an identity like (
1.3
), while trivial for a system of independent particles, is not at all expected to hold for truly interacting systems. Indeed, apart from the processes treated in
[
CLR10
]
, only a handful of other models were discovered to satisfy, on any geometry, a spectral gap identity involving the corresponding random walk. These models are:

•

the symmetric exclusion process in contact with reservoirs in
[
Sal23
,
Sal24
]
;

•

the Binomial splitting process in
[
QS23
,
BC24
]
.

The identity in (
1.3
) adds
{\rm SIP}
to the short list above, provided that
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1
(we shall also refer to this condition as “log-concave regime”, see Remark
2.1
below).

One of our main results (
Theorem
2.4
) states that, without this condition on
\alpha_{\rm min}
, this identity, in general, fails: for some graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
,

(1.4)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\lvertneqq{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\;\text{.}

In other words, the factor
1\wedge\alpha_{\rm min}
in (
1.2
) cannot be generally neglected when

(1.5)

\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)\;\text{.}

This naturally leads us to further investigate this regime, with the twofold goal of:

(1)

deriving an alternative lower bound for
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
, better capturing its dependence on
\alpha_{\rm min}
;

(2)

obtaining a two-particle reduction of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
, in the asymptotic regime of vanishing diffusivity
\alpha\to 0
.

We discuss these two steps in Sections
1.2
and
1.3
, respectively.

1.2.
Sharp dependence on
\alpha_{\rm min}

In order to isolate the role of
\alpha_{\rm min}
, let us introduce new site weights
\hat{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{\rm min}}
.
With this notation, a simple scaling argument implies

(1.6)

{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)=\alpha_{\rm min}\,{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})\;\text{.}

Due to the particle interaction, the same argument does not directly apply to
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
. Instead,
if combined with the inequalities in (
1.2
), the above identity yields

(1.7)

\alpha_{\rm min}^{2}\,{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\leq\alpha_{\rm min}\,{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)\;\text{.}

Hence, if we encode the dependence on
(G,\hat{\alpha})
through
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
, the lower bound in (
1.7
) above would predict
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
to depend on the square of

\alpha_{\rm min}
.

Our second main result (
Theorem
2.2
) proves this guess to be wrong, showing that, in general,
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
depends linearly, rather than quadratically as in (
1.7
), on
\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)
: for all graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
,

(1.8)

C\,\alpha_{\rm min}\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)\;\text{,}\;\;

where
C>0
is a constant depending only on
G
and
\hat{\alpha}=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha_{\rm min}}
. Because of the second inequality in (
1.7
), the order-one dependence on
\alpha_{\rm min}
for
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
in (
1.8
) is sharp. As we will see, this sharpened result crucially requires to express the dependence on
G
not through
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
, but other features of the underlying geometry.

1.3.
Two-particle spectral gap identity

All considerations made so far leave the following question unanswered:
given that the spectral gap of
{\rm SIP}
does not always coincide with that of a single particle (namely,
{\rm RW}
), does it instead coincide with the spectral gap of the
k
-particle
{\rm SIP}
, for some integer
k\geq 2
, independent of the underlying graph
G
and site weights
\alpha
?

Asking the same question for
{\rm SIP}
’s “instantaneously thermalized” variant also known as Beta-Binomial splitting process (see, e.g.,
[
PR23
]
or Section
8.2
) — Pietro Caputo conjectured (personal communication) this spectral gap reduction to hold true with
k=2
. In words, the Beta-Binomial splitting dynamics consists of, first selecting an edge
xy
with rate
c_{xy}
(regardless of the particle configuration), and then letting particles sitting on
x
and
y
redistribute themselves according to the
{\rm SIP}
-equilibrium, restricted to that edge. Besides being clearly related to
{\rm SIP}
,
the Beta-Binomial splitting process is the particle analogue of a model which shows up in the literature under various names, e.g., the Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti model
[
KMP82
]
, a random walk on the simplex
[
CLL20
]
, or (the energies of) the renowned Kac’s walk
[
Kac56
,
CCL03
]
. Spectral gap estimates for this model are known only on two specific geometric settings: the complete graph
[
CCL03
,
Cap08
]
, and the segment with
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1

[
CLL20
]
. In both cases, a spectral gap identity with
k=2
is indeed verified.

The aforementioned conjecture translates to the context of
{\rm SIP}
as follows: for all graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
,

(1.9)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{2}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)
,
k\geq 1
, stands for the spectral gap of the
k
-particle
{\rm SIP}
. Clearly, we have
{\rm gap}_{1}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)
. Moreover, in view of (
1.3
) and
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{2}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{1}(G,\alpha)
in (
2.8
), the two-particle spectral gap identity in (
1.9
) remains to be verified only off the log-concave regime, i.e., when (
1.5
) holds.

We provide a partial answer in the affirmative to this conjecture, by determining spectral gap asymptotics as
\alpha\to 0
. The precise form of the asymptotic validity of (
1.9
) is the content of
Theorem
2.3
below. We refer to Section
2.3
for more details, but let us briefly remark that our asymptotic analysis highlights the key role that the two-particle dynamics plays for both results in Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
. We believe this point of view to be fruitful also for other related interacting systems with Gamma-like reversible measures. For more details, see Section
8.2
.

1.4.
Non-conservative case

So far, we discussed only closed (or conservative) systems in which inclusion particles neither get created nor annihilated. Open (or non-conservative) systems allow for this possibility, usually modeled as systems in contact with reservoirs, see, e.g.,
[
Spo83
,
DEHP93
,
CGGR13
]
.
For
{\rm SIP}
, the standard choice consists of creating a particle in
x
with rate
\omega_{x}\,\theta_{x}\left(\alpha_{x}+\eta_{x}\right)
, and
annihilating each particle therein (if any) with rate
\omega_{x}\left(1+\theta_{x}\right)
, where
\omega_{x}
and
\theta_{x}
form a set of non-negative site parameters added to the model. For precise definitions, see Section
6
.

In essence, the open
{\rm SIP}
presents two main features: first, due to the lack of particle conservation, the configuration space now consists of a unique, countably infinite, irreducible component; second, the unique ergodic measure is reversible if the
\theta
-parameters do not depend on
x
, and non-reversible if they do depend.
This drastic change from reversibility to non-reversibility goes together with the emergence of a number of remarkable phenomena (e.g., currents, long-range correlations), which distinguish equilibrium from non-equilibrium statistical mechanics, see, e.g.,
[
Spo91
,
DMP91
,
KL99
,
Sch01
]
.

Nevertheless, as already observed in the physics literature around thirty years ago for a large class of exactly solvable models
[
ADHR94
]
(see also
[
FGK20
]
), the “spectrum” does not depend on the
\theta
-parameters, and, thus, is the same for both equilibrium and non-equilibrium systems. The quotation marks above are required when dealing with infinite configuration spaces and corresponding generators, because the full spectrum,
a priori
, may include also non-eigenvalues and may depend on the underlying functional space on which the matrix/operators act. That said, this
\theta
-independence of the spectrum becomes rigorous for systems having a finite configuration space as, for instance, the symmetric exclusion process in contact with reservoirs (see Remark
6.6
below for more details).

In this article, we establish the following results for the non-conservative
{\rm SIP}
, for any underlying graph
G
and site weights
\alpha
,
\omega
, and
\theta
:

(1)

Eigenfunctions of the purely absorbing system (corresponding to
\theta\equiv 0
) “lift” to (generalized) eigenfunctions of
{\rm SIP}
corresponding to the same eigenvalues.

(2)

Remarkably, the spectral gap of the particle system with
\theta\equiv 0
coincides with

(1.10)

{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)>0\;\text{,}\;\;

the spectral gap of the one-particle system, namely, of the random walk evolving on
G
, and killed with rate
\omega_{x}
when sitting on
x\in V
(
Theorem
6.1
).

(3)

In the reversible case (i.e., when
\theta\equiv{\rm const.}
), the eigenfunctions obtained in
1
provide an orthonormal basis in a natural
L^{2}
-space.

Let us emphasize that step
1
holds in both reversible and non-reversible settings, while step
2
is a statement concerning the case
\theta\equiv 0
only; establishing the claim in
3
is where we need to restrict to the reversible
{\rm SIP}
.

Putting these steps together, our main result in this non-conservative setting (
Corollary
6.4
) may be summarized as follows: for all graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
and
\omega
,

(1.11)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\varrho)={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{whenever}\ \theta\equiv\varrho>0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\varrho)
denotes the first gap in the spectrum of the reversible
{\rm SIP}
with
\theta\equiv\varrho>0
, compatibly with the functional setting of step
3
.

When comparing the conservative and non-conservative settings, we remark a striking different behavior of
{\rm SIP}
’s spectral gap: while a system of
two particles
sharply captures the spectral gap of the many-particle
closed

{\rm SIP}
, the spectral gap of the
open

{\rm SIP}
is always governed (at least when
\theta\equiv{\rm const.}
) by that of a
single random walk with killing
— with no constraint on the value of
\alpha_{\rm min}>0
. We believe this dichotomy to hold true not just for
{\rm SIP}
, but also for a larger class of interacting systems in statistical mechanics having Dirichlet-like steady states.

2.
Conservative SIP. Setting and main results

Unless stated otherwise, all throughout the article, our underlying geometry consists of a weighted finite graph
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
, with symmetric conductances
c_{xy}=c_{yx}\geq 0
(conventionally,
c_{xx}=0
). We always assume the undirected graph
G
to be connected, i.e., for all
x,y\in V
, there exists a sequence
x_{0}=x,x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell-1},x_{\ell}=y
in
V
such that
\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}c_{x_{j-1}x_{j}}>0
. The length
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
of the shortest sequence connecting two distinct sites
x,y\in V
corresponds to their graph distance,
{\rm dist}_{G}(x,y)
. As usual, we define the diameter,
{\rm diam}(G)\in\{1,\ldots,|V|\}
, as the largest of all pairwise distances.

2.1.
Model

For a graph
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
, some positive site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
denotes the Markov chain evolving on the configuration space

(2.1)

\Xi_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\eta\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{V}:|\eta|=k\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ |\eta|\coloneqq\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{x}\;\text{,}\;\;

with infinitesimal generator given, for all
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, as

(2.2)

L_{G,\alpha,k}f(\eta)=\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\left(\alpha_{y}+\eta_{y}\right)\left(f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta)\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\eta\in\Xi_{k}\;\text{.}

In this formula,
\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\in\Xi_{k}
denotes the configuration obtained from
\eta
by removing a particle from
x
(if any) and placing it on
y
.
Since all particle configurations in
\Xi_{k}
are accessible,
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
is irreducible. As a simple detailed balance computation shows,
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
admits a unique reversible measure
\mu_{\alpha,k}
, given, for all
\eta\in\Xi_{k}
, by

(2.3)

\mu_{\alpha,k}(\eta)=\frac{1}{Z_{\alpha,k}}\prod_{x\in V}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{x}+\eta_{x})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{x})\,\eta_{x}!}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ Z_{\alpha,k}\coloneqq\frac{\Gamma(|\alpha|+k)}{\Gamma(|\alpha|)\,k!}\;\text{.}

Here,
\Gamma
denotes the usual gamma function satisfying, for all
a>0
,
\Gamma(a+1)=a\,\Gamma(a)
.

Remark 2.1

(Log-concavity)
.

The measure
\mu_{\alpha,k}
in (
2.3
) is known as Dirichlet-Multinomial distribution of parameters
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
, and is the discrete analogue of the Dirichlet distribution on the simplex
\varSigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{V}
of probability measures, see (
8.1
) and (
8.3
) below. The Dirichlet distribution is well known to be log-concave (in the sense that its probability density is of the form
\exp(-U)
, for some convex
U:\mathbb{R}^{V}\to\mathbb{R}
) if and only if
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1
. Hence,
\mu_{\alpha,k}
is the discrete counterpart of a log-concave measure if and only if
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1
.
More directly,
\mu_{\alpha,k}
is a “discrete log-concave measure” also in the following sense:
\mu_{\alpha,k}
is the canonical measure (i.e., conditional on
|\eta|=k
) of the product measure
\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}
in (
6.3
) below, whose Negative-Binomial marginal measures on
\mathbb{N}_{0}
are log-concave (i.e.,
\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}(\eta_{x}=\ell)^{2}\geq\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}(\eta_{x}=\ell-1)\,\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}(\eta_{x}=\ell+1)
, for all
\ell\geq 1
and
x\in V
) if and only if
\alpha_{\rm min}\geq 1
.

2.2.
A non-asymptotic lower bound for the spectral gap

Because of irreducibility and reversibility, all
|\Xi_{k}|
eigenvalues
of the (negative) generator
-L_{G,\alpha,k}
in (
2.2
) are real and non-negative, the smallest one being equal to zero, whereas the second one — referred to as spectral gap of
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
and shortened as
{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)
— being strictly positive. Confronting with the notation from Section
1
, we have

(2.4)

{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{1}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;

because
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
with
k=1
corresponds to a single, thus, non-interacting particle, abbreviated as
{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)
. All cases
k\geq 2
describe a truly interacting system; hence, we define

(2.5)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)=\inf_{k\geq 2}{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)\;\text{.}

We are now ready to state the precise statement of the result in (
1.8
), concerned with the sharp dependence of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
on
\alpha_{\rm min}=\min_{x\in V}\alpha_{x}
, with a special focus on the regime
\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

For all graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
, we have

(2.6)

\alpha_{\rm min}\left\{\frac{1}{21}\,\frac{\alpha_{\rm ratio}}{6^{\alpha_{\rm min}/\alpha_{\rm ratio}}}\,\frac{c_{\rm min}}{|V|^{2}\,{\rm diam}(G)}\right\}\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;

where

(2.7)

\alpha_{\rm ratio}\coloneqq\frac{\alpha_{\rm min}}{\alpha_{\rm max}}=\frac{\min_{x\in V}\alpha_{x}}{\max_{y\in V}\alpha_{y}}\leq 1\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad c_{\rm min}\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in V\\
c_{xy}>0\end{subarray}}c_{xy}>0\;\text{.}

As already discussed in Section
1.2
, this lower bound captures the correct dependence of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
on
\alpha_{\rm min}
for every fixed
G
, provided that
\alpha_{\rm ratio}
remains bounded away from zero. Moreover, the estimate is non-asymptotic, with a rather explicit constant depending on some relevant features of the graph, such as its size and diameter. Nevertheless, we do not claim this factor to be sharp, and certainly there is plenty of room for improving our proof arguments when passing to concrete examples such as
d
-dimensional discrete boxes and tori, complete bipartite graphs, trees, etc.

Instead of pursuing this plan, we stick to general geometries, but pass to the regime of vanishing weights
\alpha\to 0
. In this setting, we provide an asymptotic characterization of this factor not in terms of the spectral gap of
{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)
, but of the “second simplest system” on this geometry:
{\rm SIP}_{2}(G,\alpha)
, namely, the symmetric inclusion process with just two particles.

2.3.
Spectral gap’s asymptotics

As already proved in
[
KS24
]
(see also Section
3.1
below), we have, for all graphs
G
and site weights
\alpha
,

(2.8)

{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{k-1}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\geq 2\;\text{.}

The next result shows that, in the limit
\alpha\to 0
, we can reverse the above inequality for
k\geq 3
.
In what follows, for simplicity, instead of
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}\to 0
, we consider

(2.9)

\alpha=\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ 0<\varepsilon\ll 1\ \text{and}\ \hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{x})_{x\in V}\ \text{fixed}\;\text{.}

Theorem 2.3
.

For all graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and site weights
\hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{x})_{x\in V}
, we have

(2.10)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}{{\rm gap}_{2}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}=1\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\geq 3\;\text{.}

In view of the definition of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
in (
2.5
) and of the inequalities in (
2.8
),
the above limit is an asymptotic version of the two-particle spectral gap identity in (
1.9
).
Let us stress that this result holds true for any graph
G
and integer
k\geq 3
. However,
the case
k=2
is excluded for a good reason, as shown in the following result.

Theorem 2.4
.

For some graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and site weights
\hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{x})_{x\in V}
, we have

(2.11)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\rm gap}_{2}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}{{\rm gap}_{1}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}<1\;\text{.}

This inequality provides, if combined with (
2.8
) and the definitions in (
2.4
)–(
2.5
), an instance of (
1.4
), namely, of the failure of the one-particle spectral gap identity in (
1.3
).
The graphs
G
and site weights
\hat{\alpha}
which we adopt to prove this result are far from being intricate. In fact, we decide to take
G
to be the most standard
d
-dimensional discrete torus, with
d\geq 2
and sufficiently large size, and
\hat{\alpha}\equiv 1
. These are just some of the possible examples that one could exhibit for proving (
2.11
). Indeed, as it will become apparent from the proof, essentially any other sparse geometry for which the random walk’s relaxation time (i.e.,
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)^{-1}
) is much smaller than the expected meeting time of two independent random walks initialized at equilibrium, will equally work.
We refer to the subsequent section for further explanations.

2.4.
Proofs outline

Recall that an inclusion particle jumps from
x
to a nearest neighbor
y
at rate proportional to
\alpha_{y}+\eta_{y}
. On the one hand, when non-zero, the term
\eta_{y}\geq 1
stands for the interaction between particles on adjacent sites, which mutually attract each other, tending to stick together. On the other hand,
\alpha_{y}>0
represents an independent mechanism of particle diffusion. In this sense, the regime
\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)
depicts the situation in which, at least in some portions of the graph, particle stickiness dominates over diffusion.

This regime becomes particularly significant when considering the limit
\alpha\to 0
. In this setting,
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
exhibits a metastable behavior, as thoroughly studied in the last decade
[
GRV13
,
BDG17
,
KS21
,
Kim21
,
Kim23
]
. These works describe the following qualitative picture, when
\alpha=\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}
as in (
2.9
). Roughly speaking, as long as particles are far from each other (i.e., at graph distance
\geq 2
), they perform jumps on the timescale
\varepsilon^{-1}
. As soon as they are at distance one, they stick together in a much shorter time, roughly of order one. These two mechanisms bring particles to meet and pile up together in large stacks (or, condensates) in a time of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
. Concurrently, some particles sitting on a stack would still attempt to jump to empty nearest neighboring vertices at rate
\asymp\varepsilon
. While some of the attempts fail, that is, the “courageous” particle is instantaneously sucked back into a neighboring stack, some of these will succeed to attract sufficiently many particles, managing to move the whole stack. Due to the rates’ symmetry encoded in the condition
c_{xy}=c_{yx}
, it is then part of the results in
[
GRV13
,
BDG17
]
to show that relevant jumps of entire stacks occur at times of order
\varepsilon^{-1}
, and that, on this timescale, stacks are effectively approximated by independent random walks on
G
, each evolving at rate proportional to
\varepsilon
, and capable of coalescing (possibly in a very complicated way if three or more stacks are involved) when getting at distance one from each other. Hence, the metastable relevant part of the dynamics takes place, according to this qualitative picture, on the timescale
\varepsilon^{-1}
.

Although this metastable picture provides a first correct intuition that
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})\asymp\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, our quantitative analysis must take into account also features not captured by usual metastability limit theorems.
For instance, while metastability describes macroscopic features of the system when initialized from macroscopically relevant configurations, global functional inequalities as those determining spectral gaps require bounds which must be uniform over the initial conditions.
Moreover, we seek for lower bounds for
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
being independent of the system size.
However, a crucial ingredient in the metastability picture of
{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
is that
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the number of particles in the system is not too large; more precisely,

(2.12)

\log k\ll\varepsilon^{-1}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{as}\ \varepsilon\ll 1\;\text{.}

This condition, always assumed in previous works (see, e.g.,
[
GRV13
,
BDG17
,
Kim21
,
Kim23
]
) is not just technical, but strictly required for the condensation mechanism to take place: (
2.12
) is a sufficient and necessary condition for the steady state of the system to charge configurations consisting of a single stack of particles only.

2.4.1.
Outline of the proof of Theorem
2.2

Our proof for determining the order-one dependence on
\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{\rm min}
of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
combines three main ingredients:

•

the nested eigenstructure of
{\rm SIP}
(valid for any underlying graph
G
) as already exploited in
[
KS24
]
;

•

the full knowledge of the eigendecomposition in mean-field geometries and, more specifically, the fact that eigenvalues grow quadratically (neglecting multiplicities) with the particle total number;

•

comparison inequalities of Dirichlet forms associated to the particle system on a graph
G
, against that on the complete graph.

All three steps are presented and combined to yield the proof of Theorem
2.2
in Section
3
, but the actual proof of the comparison inequality, longer and more technical, is presented in Section
4
.

In the metastable regime (
2.12
), our comparisons build on the classical distinguishing paths method as applied, e.g., in the seminal work
[
DSC93
]
to the symmetric exclusion process. Most of the care in our context lies in devising paths in which the total “cost” becomes not larger than
\varepsilon^{-1}\,k^{2}
, up to constants depending only on
G
and
\hat{\alpha}
. Here, the cost of each move heavily depends on whether relocating a single particle augments, keeps constant, or lowers the total number of stacks, and whether stacks do have or not neighboring stacks. Hence, following the metastable behavior of the system offers us a guideline to construct efficient distinguishing paths, e.g., taking care of never creating more than one extra occupied site along the path. We refer to Sections
4.2
and
4.3
for the details.

When (
2.12
) does not hold, as already mentioned, the metastability picture breaks down, and so does this approach via distinguishing paths. In order to overcome this, we replace paths by more sophisticated two-dimensional ones, as particular instances of flows. In presence of stacks consisting of a huge number of particles, these two-dimensional paths allow us to rigorously implement the idea of moving only a smaller portion of the stack at the time according to some suitably chosen probability, and leaving the rest untouched. While this strategy of moving only some (and not all) particles of a stack produces an extra factor
\varepsilon^{-1}
, which we could not afford in the regime (
2.12
), this is now not problematic, as we can control this factor with a suitable function of the number of particles of the stack. For more details, see Section
4.4
.

As we just sketched, these estimates involving Dirichlet forms yield comparison constants which unavoidably degenerate like
1/k^{2}
as the total number of particles
k\in\mathbb{N}
grows. Here is where we crucially exploit the first two ingredients of the proof, in particular, the quadratic growth of eigenvalues for the mean-field system, which removes this degeneracy.

2.4.2.
Outline of the proof of Theorems
2.3
and
2.4

In order to establish the strict inequality between
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
and
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
as in (
1.4
), comparison arguments as those we just described turn out to be too loose (and, actually, too elaborated) to capture the precise pre-factors. Instead, we follow a more basic approach, namely, turning the aforementioned metastability picture into some quantitative spectral information.

More specifically, for a fixed graph
G
and a fixed number of particles
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we employ and sharpen classical limit theorems for slow-fast systems from
[
Kur73
]
(see also
[
GRV13
]
for another application in the context of
{\rm SIP}
). These theorems rigorously describe the metastable dynamics for the slow macroscopic variables of
{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. The limit of the slow variables after a thermalization of the fast ones identifies the low-lying spectrum. This allows us to capture the behavior of
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
, at least for
\varepsilon
small enough. These intuitive ideas are stated and proved rigorously in Section
5.1
.

We are then left with identifying the spectral gap of the limiting metastable dynamics. In contrast to
{\rm SIP}
, which is irreducible, this metastable chain may consist of both recurrent and transient states, as we detail in Section
5.2
. Recurrent regions correspond to those configurations in which all particles sit together, and the resulting single stack moves like
{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
. Transient states are those configurations in which there are stacks at graph distance at least two from each other. Hence, the spectral gap of the metastable process is just the smallest among the random walk’s gap and the eigenvalues associated to any part of the (sub-stochastic) transient dynamics.

Most of our work is devoted to analyzing the hierarchy of transient states. Indeed, even the decomposition into irreducible components and the jump rates of the transient dynamics are, in general, highly complicated and non-trivially dependent on the underlying geometry and the number of particles. Nevertheless, by exploiting a form of consistency for the metastable limiting dynamics, we are able to deduce that the lowest-lying eigenvalue of the transient dynamics is attained by the system with just
two
particles, as adding more particles — thus, potentially, stacks — does not cause any slowdown in the system. More in detail, in Section
5.3
, we verify that one only needs to focus on the number of stacks (and not on the precise allocation of particles in each stack): for each
m\geq 2
, the smallest eigenvalue of all transient sub-systems with
m
piles comes exactly from the sub-system with
m
isolated particles. As a next step, in Section
5.4
, we prove that the smallest eigenvalue of all sub-systems with
m\geq 2
isolated particles is attained at
m=2
, i.e., the sub-system with two isolated particles. Collecting these observations, we prove Theorem
2.3
in Section
5.5
.
Theorem
2.4
is also proved in this section: by exploiting this rigorous metastable description of eigenvalues’ asymptotics, our proof boils down to estimates of relaxation and mean meeting times of independent particles on a well studied geometry, corresponding, respectively, to (the inverse of) the spectral gap of the recurrent and transient dynamics of
{\rm SIP}
’s metastable process.

3.
Proof of Theorem
2.2

We follow the outline in Section
2.4.1
.

3.1.
General eigenstructure of SIP

We start by recalling from
[
KS24
]
some general facts on the eigenstructure of
{\rm SIP}
functional to our analysis.
First of all,
{\rm SIP}
is consistent, in the sense that the system with
k-1
particles is recovered (in the sense of finite-dimensional distributions) from the system with
k
particles, provided that one particle is removed
uniformly at random
. In formula, this means that

(3.1)

L_{G,\alpha,k}\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{a}_{k}\,L_{G,\alpha,k-1}

holds true for all graphs
G
, site weights
\alpha
, and
k\geq 2
, where
\mathfrak{a}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
is the
annihilation operator
defined as

(3.2)

\mathfrak{a}_{k}g(\eta)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{x}\,g(\eta-\delta_{x})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad g\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k-1}}\;\text{,}\;\;\eta\in\Xi_{k}\;\text{.}

As proved in
[
KS24
, Appendix A]
,
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
is one-to-one. This readily implies that the (real) spectrum of
L_{G,\alpha,k-1}
is contained (with multiplicities) in that of
L_{G,\alpha,k}
(and, in particular, that (
2.8
) holds). More precisely, if
(\lambda,f)
is an eigenvalue-eigenfunction pair for
-L_{G,\alpha,k-1}
, then
(\lambda,\mathfrak{a}_{k}f)
is one for
-L_{G,\alpha,k}
. As a consequence of this fact and reversibility, the rest of the spectrum of
-L_{G,\alpha,k}
that does not come from
-L_{G,\alpha,k-1}
must be found in the orthogonal complement of those eigenfunctions “lifted” from those of
-L_{G,\alpha,k-1}
.
This observation leads us to introduce the
creation operator

\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}:\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k-1}}
:

(3.3)

\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}f(\xi)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in V}(\xi_{x}+\alpha_{x})\,f(\xi+\delta_{x})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\;\text{,}\;\;\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}\;\text{.}

As demonstrated in
[
KS24
, Proposition 3.1]
, the two operators
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
and
\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
are adjoint one to each other in the following sense:

(3.4)

\langle\mathfrak{a}_{k}g\,|\,f\rangle_{\alpha,k}=\frac{k}{|\alpha|+k-1}\,\langle g\,|\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}f\rangle_{\alpha,k-1}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\;\text{,}\;\;g\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k-1}}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\langle\,\cdot\,\,|\,\,\cdot\,\rangle_{\alpha,k}
denotes the inner product in
\Xi_{k}
with respect to
\mu_{\alpha,k}
given in (
2.3
). In turn,
\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
is onto and the following orthogonal decomposition holds:

(3.5)

L^{2}(\mu_{\alpha,k})={\rm Im}\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}\oplus_{\perp}{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}\;\text{.}

Next, let us recall a well-known variational characterization of
{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)
: for each
k\geq 2
,

(3.6)

{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\\
f\neq\text{const.}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)}{{\rm Var}_{\alpha,k}(f)}\;\text{.}

Here,
\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)=\left\langle f\,\middle|\,-L_{G,\alpha,k}f\right\rangle_{\alpha,k}
denotes the Dirichlet form evaluated at
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, namely,

(3.7)

\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\sum_{x,y\in V}\mu_{\alpha,k}(\eta)\,c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\left(\alpha_{y}+\eta_{y}\right)\left(f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta)\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

whereas
{\rm Var}_{\alpha,k}(f)
stands for the variance of
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
with respect to
\mu_{\alpha,k}
. It is immediate to check that, if
f\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
, then
\langle f\rangle_{\alpha,k}=\langle f\,|\,1\rangle_{\alpha,k}=0
and, thus,

(3.8)

{\rm Var}_{\alpha,k}(f)=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\mu_{\alpha,k}(\eta)\,f(\eta)^{2}\;\text{.}

Taking advantage of the lifting property of
\mathfrak{a}_{k}
, the self-adjointness of
L_{G,\alpha,k}
on
L^{2}(\mu_{\alpha,k})
, and the orthogonal decomposition in (
3.5
), (
3.6
) simplifies as follows:

(3.9)

{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{k-1}(G,\alpha)\wedge\bigg(\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}\\
f\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}}\bigg)\;\text{.}

Hence, by an iterative argument on
k\geq 2
, we may focus on comparing
\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)
and
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}
, only for functions
f\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
.

3.2.
Dirichlet forms and comparisons

The Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)
depends on the underlying graph structure, whereas the orthogonal decomposition in (
3.5
) and
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}
do not. This simple observation motivates us to compare
\mathcal{E}_{G,\alpha,k}(f)
with its complete graph analogue, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{K,\alpha,k}(f)
, where
K=K_{V}
denotes the complete graph on the sites of
V
with unitary conductances
c_{xy}\equiv 1
.

It turns out that, in this complete graph case, we can obtain the full eigendecomposition of
{\rm SIP}
. Remark that, although the spectrum and a set of eigenfunctions of
{\rm SIP}_{k}(K,\alpha)
are known
[
Shi77
]
(see also
[
Cor23
, Theorem 1.4]
or
[
WZ19
]
and references therein), the next proposition, together with the identity in (
3.9
), provides a simple and complete description of eigenvalues and eigenspaces when
G=K
, which we shall exploit later.

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
k\geq 1
and
f\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
, we have

(3.10)

\mathcal{E}_{K,\alpha,k}(f)=k\left(|\alpha|+k-1\right)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}\;\text{.}

Proof.

The result is well known for
k=1
, the random walk case. Indeed,
{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,0}^{\dagger}
coincides with the subspace of functions
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{1}}
having mean zero with respect to
\mu_{\alpha,1}
, which equals
\mu_{\alpha,1}(\delta_{x})=\frac{\alpha_{x}}{|\alpha|}
,
x\in V
.

Now, fix
k\geq 2
and
f\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,k-1}^{\dagger}
. By
[
KS24
, Eq. (3.10)]
, we have

(3.11)

\mathcal{E}_{K,\alpha,k}(f)=k\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{k}}\mu_{\alpha,k-1}(\xi)\,\mathcal{E}_{K,\alpha+\xi,1}(f_{\xi})\;\text{,}\;\;

with
f_{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{1}}
being defined as
f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})\coloneqq f(\xi+\delta_{x})
. Remark that one easily deduces (
3.11
) from the following identity (
[
KS24
, Eq. (3.9)]
): for all
x\in V
and
\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}

(3.12)

\mu_{\alpha,k}(\xi+\delta_{x})\left(\xi_{x}+1\right)=\frac{Z_{\alpha,k-1}}{Z_{\alpha,k}}\,\mu_{\alpha,k-1}(\xi)\left(\alpha_{x}+\xi_{x}\right)=k\,\mu_{\alpha,k-1}(\xi)\,\frac{\alpha_{x}+\xi_{x}}{|\alpha|+k-1}\;\text{,}\;\;

where the second step used (cf. (
2.3

(3.13)

\frac{Z_{\alpha,k-1}}{Z_{\alpha,k}}=\frac{k}{|\alpha|+k-1}\;\text{.}

Analogously, we get

(3.14)

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}=\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}}\mu_{\alpha,k-1}(\xi)\left\lVert f_{\xi}\right\rVert_{\alpha+\xi,1}^{2}\;\text{.}

By (
3.11
), the identity in (
3.10
) for
k=1
(note that

ensures that
f_{\xi}\in{\rm Ker}\,\mathfrak{a}_{\alpha,0}^{\dagger}
) and

(3.15)

\mathcal{E}_{K,\alpha,k}(f)=k\left(|\alpha|+k-1\right)\sum_{\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}}\mu_{\alpha,k-1}(\xi)\left\lVert f_{\xi}\right\rVert_{\alpha+\xi,1}^{2}=k\left(|\alpha|+k-1\right)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\alpha,k}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

namely, the desired result.
∎

For all

We postpone the proof of this estimate to Section

Remark 3.3

While all previous steps relied on the fact that we chose the test function

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\ell,m,\sigma}\coloneqq\mu_{\alpha,k}(\sigma_{m,\ell-m}^{xy})\,m\left(\alpha_{y}+\ell-m\right)(f(\sigma_{m-1,\ell-m+1}^{xy})-f(\sigma_{m,\ell-m}^{xy}))^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

so that

(4.8)

\mathcal{E}_{K}^{x,y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell}\sum_{\sigma\in\Xi_{k-\ell}^{V\setminus\{x,y\}}}\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\ell,m,\sigma}\;\text{.}

Fix a shortest sequence

(4.9)

x=x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}=y

in

such that
c_{x_{s-1}x_{s}}>0
for all
s\in\llbracket 1,t\rrbracket
, where
t\in\llbracket 1,{\rm diam}(G)\rrbracket
.
For
\eta,\zeta\in\Xi_{k}
, write

(4.10)

\mathfrak{c}(\eta,\zeta)\coloneqq\mu_{\alpha,k}(\eta)\,r_{\alpha,k}(\eta,\zeta)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^{2}f(\eta,\zeta)\coloneqq\mathfrak{c}(\eta,\zeta)\left(f(\zeta)-f(\eta)\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
r_{\alpha,k}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the transition rate function of
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
. Then, we have

(4.11)

\mathcal{E}=\sum_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z<w\\
c_{zw}>0\end{subarray}}\nabla^{2}f(\eta,\eta-\delta_{z}+\delta_{w})\;\text{.}

A sequence of configurations
\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{M}
in
\Xi_{k}
is a path if the transition rates
r_{\alpha,k}(\cdot,\cdot)
are positive along the sequence, i.e.,
r_{\alpha,k}(\eta_{s-1},\eta_{s})>0
for all
s\in\llbracket 1,M\rrbracket
.

The idea to prove (
4.3
) is as follows. First, we decompose
\mathcal{E}_{K}^{x,y}
according to (
4.8
). Then, for each triple
(\ell,m,\sigma)
, we upper bound
\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\ell,m,\sigma}
with a certain collection of terms
\nabla^{2}f(\eta,\eta-\delta_{z}+\delta_{w})
that appear in the right-hand side of (
4.11
). Finally, we count the number of overlaps for each term
\nabla^{2}f(\eta,\eta-\delta_{z}+\delta_{w})
for all such triple
(\ell,m,\sigma)
, which gives an upper bound of
\mathcal{E}_{K}^{x.y}
in terms of
\mathcal{E}
.

The procedure of upper bounding each
\mathcal{E}_{K}^{\ell,m,\sigma}
depends on the detailed distribution of particles along the sequence (
4.9
). In the following four subsections, Sections
4.1
,
4.2
,
4.3
and
4.4
, we demonstrate each simple cases, and finally in Section

we deal with the general case and conclude the proof of (
4.3
).

5.
Proofs of Theorems
2.3
and
2.4

In this section, we prove Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
, following the outline of Section
2.4.2
. The basic starting idea is to split the infinitesimal generator
L_{G,\alpha,k}
into two parts: a slow part and a fast part. In view of this decomposition, standard limit theorems for slow-fast systems ensure that, in the small-diffusivity limit,
\rm SIP
is well approximated by the slow dynamics, after a suitable projection/thermalization according to the fast one. Understanding how and when this projection affects
{\rm SIP}
’s spectral gap is the main non-trivial task of this section.

Recall from (
2.9
) that we assume
\alpha=\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}
, where
\hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{x})_{x\in V}
are fixed site weights and
\varepsilon
tends to
0
. Finally, since the graph
G
is fixed all throughout the section, we drop it from the notation, and simply write, e.g.,
{\rm SIP}_{k}(\alpha)={\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
and
L_{\alpha,k}=L_{G,\alpha,k}
.

5.1.
Slow and fast dynamics

Fix
k\geq 2
, and consider
{\rm SIP}_{k}(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
with time sped up by a factor
\varepsilon^{-1}
. Recalling (
2.2
), the corresponding
generator is then simply given by
\varepsilon^{-1}L_{\alpha,k}=\varepsilon^{-1}L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}
.
By separating the
\varepsilon
-dependent terms from the rest, we obtain

(5.1)

\varepsilon^{-1}L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}=A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k}\;\text{,}\;\;

where, for all
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
and
\eta\in\Xi_{k}
,

(5.2)

\displaystyle A_{\hat{\alpha},k}f(\eta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left({f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta)}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.3)

\displaystyle B_{k}f(\eta)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\left(f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta)\right)\;\text{.}

Let
(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
and
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
denote the Markov chains in
\Xi_{k}
generated by
A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
and
B_{k}
, respectively. More in detail,
(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
describes
k
independent particles on
G
, while
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a particle system with absorbing set

(5.4)

\Omega_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\eta\in\Xi_{k}:c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\eta_{y}=0\ \text{for all}\ x,y\in V\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

and transient set
\Delta_{k}\coloneqq\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k}
, i.e.,

(5.5)

\Delta_{k}\coloneqq\left\{\eta\in\Xi_{k}:c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\eta_{y}>0\ \text{for some}\ x,y\in V\right\}\;\text{.}

Let
\Pi_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
denote the
B_{k}
-harmonic projection operator, i.e.,

(5.6)

\Pi_{k}f\coloneqq\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{tB_{k}}f\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}\;\text{.}

Remark that the range of
\Pi_{k}
, written as
\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, is a
|\Omega_{k}|
-dimensional subspace.

The following result builds on a powerful theorem
[
Kur73
, Theorem 2.1]
(see also
[
EK86
, §1, Theorem 7.6]
) on limit theorems for Markovian slow-fast systems.

Proposition 5.1
.

The operator
\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
is an infinitesimal generator on
\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})
. Moreover, for all
t>0
and
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, we have the following semigroups convergence:

(5.7)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\max_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\left|e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})}f(\eta)-e^{t\,\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}}\Pi_{k}f(\eta)\right|=0\;\text{.}

Proof.

In this proof, we write
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}=e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})}
. The first claim and a convergence as in (
5.7
) with
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}f(\eta)
replaced by
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\Pi_{k}f(\eta)
are the content of
[
EK86
, §1, Theorem 7.6]
. In order to obtain the desired claim (i.e., remove the projection operator
\Pi_{k}
), it suffices to prove that

(5.8)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\max_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\left\lvert P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\Pi_{k}f(\eta)-P_{t}^{\varepsilon}f(\eta)\right\rvert=0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t>0\;\text{.}

For this purpose, fix
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, and observe that

(5.9)

\Pi_{k}f(\eta)=f(\eta)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\eta\in\Omega_{k}\;\text{,}\;\;

because
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
has
\Omega_{k}
as absorbing states. Hence, since
\Pi_{k}
is a contraction, we get

(5.10)

\left\lvert P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\Pi_{k}f(\eta)-P_{t}^{\varepsilon}f(\eta)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{\xi\in\Delta_{k}}p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\xi)\left\lvert\Pi_{k}f(\xi)-f(\xi)\right\rvert\leq\big(2\max_{\xi\in\Xi_{k}}\left\lvert f(\xi)\right\rvert\big)\,p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\Delta_{k})\;\text{,}\;\;

where
p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
denotes the transition kernel of the
\varepsilon^{-1}
-sped up
{\rm SIP}_{k}(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
at time
t
.
Since

(5.11)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\eta,\Delta_{k})=0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\eta\in\Xi_{k}\;\text{,}\;\;t>0\;\text{,}\;\;

(see Proposition
A.1
below for the full statement and proof of this fact), the proof of the proposition is concluded.
∎

Fix
t>0
, and recall that
e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})}
has all real eigenvalues, just one equal to
1
, while all the others lying in the interval
(0,1)
. Further, Proposition
5.1
ensures that all eigenvalues (with multiplicity) of
e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})}
converge to those of
e^{t\,\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}}
. In particular, the spectrum of
e^{t\,\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}}
entirely lies in
[0,1]
:
|\Omega_{k}|-1
of these eigenvalues are strictly positive, while the eigenvalue
0
has multiplicity
|\Xi_{k}|-|\Omega_{k}|+1=|\Delta_{k}|+1
.

As a consequence, the second-largest eigenvalue of
e^{t(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})}
converges to the second-largest eigenvalue of
e^{t\,\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}}
, which is given by
e^{-t\,w_{k}(\hat{\alpha})}
, where

(5.12)

w_{k}(\hat{\alpha})\coloneqq\ \text{the second-smallest eigenvalue of}\ -\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}:\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})\to\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})\;\text{.}

Thus, we proved the following claim. (Recall that
{\rm gap}_{k}(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})={\rm gap}_{k}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
.)

Corollary 5.2
.

For all integers
k\geq 2
, we have

(5.13)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\rm gap}_{k}(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}{\varepsilon}=w_{k}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{.}

In the next subsection, we analyze more in detail the limiting generator
\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
on
\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})\subset\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
and its block-triangular structure.

5.2.
Recurrent and transient states

As already proved in Proposition
5.1
,

\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}:\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})\to\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})
is the infinitesimal generator of a Markov chain on
\Omega_{k}
, hereafter referred to as
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
. For notational simplicity, instead of dealing with
\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
on the
|\Omega_{k}|
-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{R}(\Pi_{k})
of
\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
, we shall rather work with the corresponding rate matrix

(5.14)

M_{\hat{\alpha},k}=\left({\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\xi)\right)_{\eta,\xi\in\Omega_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega_{k}|\times|\Omega_{k}|}\;\text{,}\;\;

whose non-diagonal entries read as

(5.15)

{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\xi)=\sum_{\zeta\in\Xi_{k}}{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{A}}(\eta,\zeta)\,{\bf P}_{\zeta}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{\xi}=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\;\text{,}\;\;

encoding the jump rate from
\eta\in\Omega_{k}
to
\xi\in\Omega_{k}
. In the above formula,
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{A}}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
denotes the transition rate function of the process
(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, whereas
{\bf P}^{\mathcal{B}}_{\cdot}
stands for the law of the process
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, and

(5.16)

\tau_{\Omega_{k}}\coloneqq\min_{\xi\in\Omega_{k}}\tau_{\xi}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ \tau_{\xi}\coloneqq\inf\{t\geq 0:\mathcal{B}_{k}(t)=\xi\}\;\text{.}

From the form of these rates, we immediately derive a classification of recurrent and transient states for this Markov chain; this will lead to a further characterization of the spectral gap
w_{k}(\hat{\alpha})
of
\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
. We collect this classification in the following proposition; its direct consequence on the spectral gap is the content of the subsequent corollary. Before that, let us introduce, for every integer
m\geq 1
,

(5.17)

\Omega_{k,m}\coloneqq\left\{\eta\in\Omega_{k}:|\{x\in V:\eta_{x}>0\}|=m\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

that is, the set of configurations in
\Omega_{k}
with
m
separated stacks of particles (cf. (
5.4
)).
Clearly,
\Omega_{k,m}=\varnothing
implies
\Omega_{k,m+\ell}=\varnothing
for all
\ell\geq 1
. Further, we have
\Omega_{k,1}\neq\varnothing
,
\Omega_{k,k+1}=\varnothing
, and, thus,
\Omega_{k}=\sqcup_{m=1}^{\infty}\Omega_{k,m}=\Omega_{k,1}\sqcup\Omega_{k,2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{k,k}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

For the chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, the following two claims hold true:

(1)

For every
m,m^{\prime}\geq 1
with
\Omega_{k,m}\neq\varnothing\neq\Omega_{k,m^{\prime}}
and
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}
, we have

(5.18)

{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\xi)=0\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{for all}\ \xi\in\Omega_{k,m^{\prime}},\ m^{\prime}>m\;\text{,}\;\;

while, when
m\geq 2
, there exist
\eta^{1},\ldots,\eta^{\ell}\in\Omega_{k,m}
satisfying

(5.19)

{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\eta^{1})\left(\prod_{h=1}^{\ell-1}{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta^{h},\eta^{h+1})\right){\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta^{\ell},\xi)>0\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{for some}\ \xi\in\Omega_{k,m^{\prime}},\ m^{\prime}<m\;\text{.}

As a consequence,
\Omega_{k,m}
,
m\geq 2
, consists of transient states only.

(2)

The chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible on
\Omega_{k,1}\neq\varnothing
, and the position of the single stack evolves as
{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})
.

Proof.

1
The desired claim follows from the connectedness of the graph
G
, and the observation that the rates
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
in (
5.15
) describe the following two subsequent mechanisms: a step of the chain
(\mathcal{A}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
increases the number of stacks at most by one, creating, in this case, at least two neighboring stacks; the chain
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
forces all neighboring particles to eventually merge into isolated (i.e., non-neighboring) stacks.

2
Observe that, for all
x,y,z\in V
with
c_{xz}>0
, we have

(5.20)

{\bf P}^{\mathcal{B}}_{(k-1)\delta_{x}+\delta_{z}}\,[\tau_{k\delta_{y}}=\tau_{\Omega_{k,1}}]=\frac{\mathds{1}_{y=z}}{k}+\frac{(k-1)\,\mathds{1}_{y=x}}{k}\;\text{,}\;\;

because the trajectories of
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
started from
(k-1)\delta_{x}+\delta_{z}\in\Delta_{k}
coincide with those of the symmetric random walk on
\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
started from
k-1
. Plugging this expression into (
5.15
), we obtain, for
x\neq y
,

(5.21)

{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(k\delta_{x},k\delta_{y})\\
=\sum_{z\in V}{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{A}}(k\delta_{x},(k-1)\delta_{x}+\delta_{z})\,{\bf P}_{(k-1)\delta_{x}+\delta_{z}}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{k\delta_{y}}=\tau_{\Omega_{k,1}}]=k\,c_{xy}\,\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,\frac{1}{k}=c_{xy}\,\hat{\alpha}_{y}\;\text{,}\;\;

namely, the transition rates of
{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})
. Irreducibility is then an obvious consequence of that of
{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})
.
∎

2

Remark 5.5
.

For

, the dynamics on
\Omega_{k,m}\neq\varnothing

goes as follows. All stacks evolve as independent

as long as their mutual distances are at least three. When two or more stacks reach mutual distance two, then there is a positive rate for them to merge. These rates — as well as the new positions of the stacks and their new compositions — depend on

, but also non-trivially on

and, when

The state-classification in Proposition

may be equivalently restated as follows: after a suitable conjugation with a permutation matrix,
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
is turned into a block lower triangular matrix with
M_{\hat{\alpha},k,1},\ldots,M_{\hat{\alpha},k,k}
as diagonal blocks, where,
for all
m\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
,

(5.22)

M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega_{k,m}|\times|\Omega_{k,m}|}

is the submatrix of
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
obtained from the restriction on configurations in
\Omega_{k,m}
(see Figure

). Since the eigenvalues of block triangular matrices are the union of those of the diagonal blocks, it suffices to focus on the spectrum of the blocks

,
m\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
.
As already showed in the previous section, the eigenvalues of
-M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
,
m\in\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
, are all real and non-negative. Remark that:

•

for
m=1
, the spectrum of

coincides with that of the generator of
{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})
;

•

for

is the rate matrix of a sub-Markovian chain killed upon exiting

; thus, all its eigenvalues are strictly negative.

In view of these simple observations, we register the following useful characterization of the spectral gap

defined in (

).

Corollary 5.6
.

For all
m\geq 2
such that
\Omega_{k,m}\neq\varnothing
, define

(5.23)

\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})\coloneqq\ \text{smallest eigenvalue of the matrix}\ -M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\Omega_{k,m}|\times|\Omega_{k,m}|}\;\text{,}\;\;

whereas
\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})\coloneqq+\infty
if
\Omega_{k,m}=\varnothing
. Then, we have

(5.24)

w_{k}(\hat{\alpha})={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})\wedge\min_{m\in\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket}\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{.}

The natural next step is to estimate the eigenvalue
\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})
for each
k\geq 2
and
m\in\llbracket 2,k\rrbracket
, by bounding it from below by
\lambda_{2,2}(\hat{\alpha})
. This task is carried out in the following two subsections. In the first one, we prove
\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})=\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})
; in the second one, we show
\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})\geq\lambda_{2,2}(\hat{\alpha})
.

We conclude this subsection by observing that each submatrix
M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
in
\Omega_{k,m}
is self-adjoint with respect to a positive measure
\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
given in (
5.25

L^{2}
-theory for the corresponding continuum model. On the other hand, in our storyline, neither this property nor the precise form of the symmetrizing measure are of primary importance. Thus, we only state the full statement here, and postpone its proof to Appendix
B
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
m\leq k
,
M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
is self-adjoint as an operator on
L^{2}(\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k,m})
, where

(5.25)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}(\eta)\coloneqq\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V\\
\eta_{x}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{x}}{\eta_{x}}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}\;\text{.}

5.3.
Comparing
\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})
with
\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})

The main goal of this subsection is to prove the following result:
for all integers
m\geq 2
with
\Omega_{m,m}\neq\varnothing
, we have

(5.26)

\lambda_{k,m}(\hat{\alpha})=\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\geq m\;\text{.}

We divide the proof of this identity into a few steps, and fix
m\geq 2
so as to satisfy
\Omega_{m,m}\neq\varnothing
all throughout.

5.3.1.
Consistency

Recall the consistency property (
3.1
) satisfied by
{\rm SIP}
, as well as the corresponding annihilation operator
\mathfrak{a}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
in (
3.2
). Since
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
arises as a small-diffusivity limit of
{\rm SIP}
, it is natural to expect that an analogous property should hold also for
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
. This is the content of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.8
.

Define, for all integers
k\geq 1
, the following “restricted” annihilation operator
\hat{\mathfrak{a}}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{k-1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{k}}
as

(5.27)

\hat{\mathfrak{a}}_{k}g(\eta)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in V}\eta_{x}\,g(\eta-\delta_{x})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad g\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{k-1}}\;\text{,}\;\;\eta\in\Omega_{k}\;\text{.}

Then, we have

(5.28)

M_{\hat{\alpha},k}\,\hat{\mathfrak{a}}_{k}=\hat{\mathfrak{a}}_{k}\,M_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}\;\text{.}

Proof.

First of all, observe that, whenever
\eta\in\Omega_{k}
with
\eta_{x}\geq 1
, then necessarily we have
\eta-\delta_{x}\in\Omega_{k-1}
; thus, the definition of
\hat{\mathfrak{a}}_{k}
makes sense.
Hence, in view of the relation between
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
and
\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
, it suffices to show that

(5.29)

\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k}\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{a}_{k}\,\Pi_{k}A_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}\;\text{.}

It is well known that a system of
k
independent random walks is consistent, i.e.,
A_{\hat{\alpha},k}\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{a}_{k}\,A_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}
. Moreover, by rewriting the consistency property of
{\rm SIP}(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})
in (
3.1
) with the notation in (
5.1
), we have
(A_{\hat{\alpha},k}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k})\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{a}_{k}\,(A_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}+\varepsilon^{-1}B_{k-1})
. Thus, by linearity, we must also have
B_{k}\,\mathfrak{a}_{k}=\mathfrak{a}_{k}\,B_{k-1}
, and a similar relation for the harmonic projection
\Pi_{k}
of
B_{k}
. This concludes the proof. ∎

5.3.2.
Irreducible decompositions

The chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
is not necessarily irreducible on
\Omega_{k,m}
. For instance, when
G=\mathbb{T}_{2m}
is the one-dimensional discrete torus of size
2m
,
\Omega_{m,m}=\{\eta,\tau_{1}\eta\}
consists of exactly two configurations (one with particles/stacks on the odd numbers, the other one on the even numbers), and
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\tau_{1}\eta)={\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\tau_{1}\eta,\eta)=0
.
Moreover, the structure of the irreducible components of the chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
on
\Omega_{k,m}
is always
finer
than the corresponding structure on
\Omega_{m,m}
, in the sense of the following proposition.

Proposition 5.9
.

Decompose
\Omega_{m,m}
into irreducible components for the chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},m}(t))_{t\geq 0}
: for some integer
\mathfrak{n}_{m}\geq 1
(depending only on
m\geq 2
and
G
),

(5.30)

\Omega_{m,m}=\Omega_{m,m}^{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{m,m}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}\;\text{.}

For all
k>m
and
i\in\llbracket 1,\mathfrak{n}_{m}\rrbracket
, define, iteratively,

(5.31)

\Omega_{k,m}^{i}=\{\eta+\delta_{x}\in\Omega_{k,m}:\eta\in\Omega_{k-1,m}^{i},\ x\in V\ \text{such that}\ \eta_{x}\geq 1\}\;\text{.}

Then, there exist partitions
\Omega_{k,m}^{i}=\Omega_{k,m}^{i,1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{k,m}^{i,\mathfrak{N}^{k}_{i}}
for all
i\in\llbracket 1,\mathfrak{n}_{m}\rrbracket
such that

(5.32)

\Omega_{k,m}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}\bigsqcup_{j=1}^{\mathfrak{N}_{i}^{k}}\Omega_{k,m}^{i,j}

is an
irreducible
decomposition of
\Omega_{k,m}
for the chain
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

Proof.

First, the fact that
\Omega_{k,m}
, for each
k\geq m\geq 2
, can be decomposed into irreducible components follows at once from Lemma
5.7
. Thus, to prove the validity of Proposition
5.9
, it suffices to check the following two properties:

(1)

\Omega_{k,m}=\Omega_{k,m}^{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{k,m}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}
;

(2)

if
i\neq i^{\prime}
,
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}^{i}
, and
\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}^{i^{\prime}}
, then
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
belong to different irreducible components of
\Omega_{k,m}
for
(\mathcal{M}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
.

To verify item
1
, take
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}
and define a new configuration
\eta^{*}\in\Omega_{m,m}
as

(5.33)

\eta^{*}\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V\\
\eta_{x}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\delta_{x}\;\text{.}

Since
\eta
has
m
separated stacks of particles, indeed
\eta^{*}
belongs to
\Omega_{m,m}
. Then, according to (
5.30
),
\eta^{*}\in\Omega_{m,m}^{i}
for some
i
, thus
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}^{i}
by (
5.31
). This proves that
\Omega_{k,m}=\Omega_{k,m}^{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Omega_{k,m}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}
.

We move on to item
2
. It suffices to prove that, for any
\eta,\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}
with
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta,\xi)>0
, the corresponding configurations
\eta^{*},\xi^{*}\in\Omega_{m,m}
(cf. (
5.33
)) satisfy either
\eta^{*}=\xi^{*}
or
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},m}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta^{*},\xi^{*})>0
.

In view of (
5.15
), there exists
\zeta\in\Xi_{k}
such that
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{A}}(\eta,\zeta)>0
and
{\bf P}_{\zeta}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{\xi}=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]>0
. In turn, there exist
x,y\in V
with
c_{xy}>0
such that
\zeta=\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}
. First, suppose that
\zeta\in\Omega_{k}
, i.e.,
\zeta=\xi
. Then, it is straightforward that
\zeta^{*}=\xi^{*}\in\Omega_{m}
and
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},m}^{\mathcal{A}}(\eta^{*},\zeta^{*})>0
, thus we have
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},m}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta^{*},\xi^{*})>0
by (
5.15
). On the contrary, suppose that
\zeta\in\Delta_{k}
. This means that
y
has at least one neighboring site (possibly
x
) on which
\zeta
has a particle. We divide into several cases.

1.

If
\zeta_{x}\geq 1
and
\zeta_{z}=0
for all
z\neq x
with
c_{yz}>0
, then the dynamics
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
can only move particles between sites
x
and
y
. Thus, by
{\bf P}_{\zeta}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{\xi}=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]>0
it necessarily holds that
\xi=\eta-\eta_{x}\delta_{x}+\eta_{x}\delta_{y}\in\Omega_{k}
. This implies that
\xi^{*}=\eta^{*}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\in\Omega_{m}
, thus
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},m}^{\mathcal{M}}(\eta^{*},\xi^{*})>0
.

2.

Suppose that
\zeta_{x}\geq 1
and there exists
z\neq x
with
c_{yz}>0
such that
\zeta_{z}\geq 1
. Then, the dynamics
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
gets absorbed exactly when
y
becomes empty. For
\xi
to have the same number of stacks,
m
, as
\eta
, it follows that
\xi^{*}=\eta^{*}
.

3.

Suppose that
\zeta_{x}=0
. Then, since the absorption of
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
triggers at least one additional loss of a stack of particles, the absorbed configuration
\xi
has strictly less number of stacks than
\eta
, which contradicts the assumption that
\eta,\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}
.

The above three cases conclude the proof of Proposition
5.9
.

Conclusion of the proof of (

Recall the definition of the matrix
M_{\hat{\alpha},k}
and its submatrices
M_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
from (

) and (

), respectively.

First, we deal with the case
k=m
. We split
M_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}
into
\mathfrak{n}_{m}
diagonal blocks
M_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{1},\ldots,M_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}
, each corresponding to an irreducible sub-Markovian dynamics. Adopting an analogous notation, we further write the eigenvalue
\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})
in (
5.23
) as

(5.36)

\lambda_{m,m}(\hat{\alpha})=\lambda_{m,m}^{1}(\hat{\alpha})\wedge\cdots\wedge\lambda_{m,m}^{\mathfrak{n}_{m}}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\lambda_{m,m}^{i}(\hat{\alpha})
stands for the smallest eigenvalue of
-M_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}
. By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, in view of sub-Markovianity, this eigenvalue is strictly positive. Furthermore, because of irreducibility, this is the only eigenvalue admitting a non-negative (actually, strictly positive) eigenfunction, which we refer to as

(5.37)

\psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{m,m}^{i}}\;\text{.}

Let, for all

,

(5.38)

\Psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}(\eta)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}(\eta)&\text{if }\ \eta\in\Omega_{m,m}^{i}\\
0&\text{if }\ \eta\in\Omega_{m}\setminus\Omega_{m,m}^{i}\;\text{,}\end{cases}

be the extension of the eigenfunction
\psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{m,m}^{i}}
to
\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{m}}
. Then, due to the block lower triangular structure of

, we have

(5.39)

M_{\hat{\alpha},m}\Psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}=-\lambda_{m,m}^{i}(\hat{\alpha})\,\Psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}\;\text{.}

Since
\Psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega_{m}}\neq 0
, this shows that a straightforward extension of
\psi_{\hat{\alpha},m,m}^{i}
produces an eigenfunction for
M_{\hat{\alpha},m}
.

Remark 5.11

For

5.4.
\lambda_{k,k}(\hat{\alpha})
and
k
coalescing particles

In this subsection, we prove

(5.47)

\lambda_{k,k}(\hat{\alpha})\geq\lambda_{2,2}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\geq 2\;\text{.}

Assuming that
\Omega_{k,k}\neq\varnothing
, and recalling the definition of Dirichlet forms and norms from Section
3.1
, our first observation is the following variational formula for
\lambda_{k,k}(\hat{\alpha})
:

(5.48)

\lambda_{k,k}(\hat{\alpha})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\Xi_{k}}\\
f=0\ \text{on}\ \Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}\end{subarray}}\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(f)}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{2}}\;\text{.}

Indeed, as a straightforward inspection of the rates
{\bf r}_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{\mathcal{M}}(\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,)
in (
5.15
) shows, the matrix
M_{\hat{\alpha},k,k}
describes the dynamics of
k
indistinguishable particles, all evolving as independent
{\rm RW}(\hat{\alpha})
as long as their mutual graph-distances are at least two, which get instantaneously killed as soon as at least one of them attempts a jump to a site neighboring another particle. The right-hand side of (
5.48
) is then readily obtained because
{\rm SIP}
particles evolve as independent random walks as long as they are not neighbors, and
\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
plays the role of a cemetery state.
In light of this reformulation, the inequality in (
5.47
) is, at least intuitively, expected: in a system of
k\geq 2
independent particles, the rate for the first touch of at least two of them cannot decrease as
k
grows.

The remainder of this subsection is devoted to the rigorous proof of this fact, whose key step is based on the following comparisons.

Lemma 5.12
.

For all integers
k\geq 3
and any function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
which vanishes on
\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
, define (
f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})\coloneqq f(\xi+\delta_{x})
)

(5.49)

g(\xi)=g_{f}(\xi)\coloneqq\left\lVert f_{\xi}\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},2}=\sqrt{\sum_{x\in V}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{x}}{|\hat{\alpha}|}\,f(\xi+\delta_{x})^{2}}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}\;\text{.}

Then,
g
vanishes on
\Xi_{k-1}\setminus\Omega_{k-1,k-1}
, and we have

(5.50)

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}^{2}=\frac{{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}}{|\hat{\alpha}|}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(g)\leq\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{|\hat{\alpha}|}\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(f)\;\text{.}

By applying Lemma
5.12
to the function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi_{k}}
attaining the infimum in the variational formula in (
5.48
), we obtain

(5.51)

\lambda_{k,k}(\hat{\alpha})=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(f)}{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{2}}\geq\frac{\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(g_{f})}{\left\lVert g_{f}\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}^{2}}\geq\lambda_{k-1,k-1}(\hat{\alpha})\;\text{,}\;\;

which, by iteration over
k\geq 3
, yields (
5.47
).

Proof of Lemma
5.12
.

Since
f=0
on
\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
, we similarly have
g=0
on
\Xi_{k-1}\setminus\Omega_{k-1,k-1}
, because
\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}\setminus\Omega_{k-1,k-1}
implies
\xi+\delta_{x}\in\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
for all
x\in V
. Moreover,

(5.52)

\displaystyle|\hat{\alpha}|\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{x\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(\xi)\,\hat{\alpha}_{x}\,f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})^{2}

(5.53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{x\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(\xi)\left(\hat{\alpha}_{x}+\xi_{x}\right)f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})^{2}

(5.54)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{k}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{x\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi+\delta_{x})\left(\xi_{x}+1\right)f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})^{2}

(5.55)

\displaystyle=\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{k}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{x\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi+\delta_{x})\,f_{\xi}(\delta_{x})^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

where for the second and fourth identities we used that

on
\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
, whereas for the third one we applied (
3.12
). Since each
\eta=\xi+\delta_{x}\in\Omega_{k,k}
is counted exactly
k
times in the above double summation, we deduce

(5.56)

|\hat{\alpha}|\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}^{2}=\left(|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1\right)\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{k,k}}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\,f(\eta)^{2}=\left(|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1\right)\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\hat{\alpha},k}^{2}\;\text{.}

This proves the identity in (
5.50
).
As for the inequality therein, recall

(5.57)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(g)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(\xi)\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\xi_{x}\,{\hat{\alpha}_{y}}\left(g(\xi)-g(\xi-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}\;\text{.}

By applying the triangle inequality

(5.58)

\left(g(\xi)-g(\xi-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{z\in V}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{|\hat{\alpha}|}\left(f_{\xi}(\delta_{z})-f_{\xi}(\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

for any
\xi\in\Xi_{k-1}
satisfying
\xi_{x}\geq 1
,
we get that
|\hat{\alpha}|\,\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(g)
is bounded above by

(5.59)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k-1}(\xi)\sum_{x,y,z\in V}c_{xy}\,\xi_{x}\,\hat{\alpha}_{y}\hat{\alpha}_{z}\left(f_{\xi}(\delta_{z})-f_{\xi}(\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}\;\text{.}

Now, fix
\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}
and
x,y,z\in V
such that
c_{xy}>0
. If
\xi_{z}\geq 1
, then both
\xi+\delta_{z}
and
\xi+\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}
belong to
\Xi_{k}\setminus\Omega_{k,k}
, the subset on which
f=0
. Hence, by (
3.12
), the expression above reads as

(5.60)

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{2k}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{z\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi+\delta_{z})\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\xi_{x}\,\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left(f_{\xi}(\delta_{z})-f_{\xi}(\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}

(5.61)

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{2k}\sum_{\xi\in\Omega_{k-1,k-1}}\sum_{z\in V}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi+\delta_{z})\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\left(\xi+\delta_{z}\right)_{x}\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left(f_{\xi}(\delta_{z})-f_{\xi}(\delta_{z}-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}

(5.62)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1}{2}\sum_{\eta\in\Omega_{k,k}}\mu_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\,\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left(f(\eta)-f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})\right)^{2}

(5.63)

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\left(|\hat{\alpha}|+k-1\right)\mathcal{E}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(f)\;\text{,}\;\;

where for the third line we used again the change of variables
\eta=\xi+\delta_{z}\in\Omega_{k,k}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Recall that, for all graphs
G
and site weights
\hat{\alpha}
, we have
{{\rm gap}_{1}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}=\varepsilon\,{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
,
\varepsilon>0
; moreover, by (
5.64
) for

, we have

(5.66)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{{\rm gap}_{2}(G,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha})}{\varepsilon}={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})\wedge\lambda_{2,2}(G,\hat{\alpha})\;\text{.}

Thus, it suffices to verify the existence of
G
and
\hat{\alpha}
such that

(5.67)

\lambda_{2,2}(G,\hat{\alpha})<{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})\;\text{.}

In particular, let us recall from the discussion in Section
5.4
, that
\lambda_{2,2}
is the smallest eigenvalue of (the negative of) the generator describing two independent
{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
, having
\hat{\alpha}=(\hat{\alpha}_{x})_{x\in V}
as a (non-normalized) reversible measure, and which get instantaneously killed when attempting to get to a mutual graph-distance strictly smaller than two.

In view of this, it is not surprising that the standard discrete torus
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
, with dimension
d\geq 2
, size
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and homogeneous site weights, satisfies (
5.67
), provided that
N
is large enough. From now on, we fix
\hat{\alpha}\equiv 1
and

(5.68)

G=\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\;\text{,}\;\;\text{with}\ c_{xy}\equiv 1\ \text{for all neighboring sites}\ x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\;\text{,}\;\;

and drop them from the notation, simply writing, e.g.,
\lambda_{2,2}
and
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}
instead of
\lambda_{2,2}(G,\hat{\alpha})
and
{\rm RW}(G,\hat{\alpha})
.

Consider the positions
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
of two independent particles on
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
, each one jumping with unit rate to any of its neighbors.
Observe that
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is reversible with respect to the uniform measure
\kappa=\kappa_{N,d}
on
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
. In what follows, let
\mathsf{P}_{(x,y)}
and
\mathsf{E}_{(x,y)}
denote the law and corresponding expectation for
(X_{t},Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
when starting from
(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}\times\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
. It is well known (see, e.g.,
[
LP17
, Sections 12.3.1 & 12.4]
) that, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough and all
d\geq 1
, we have

(5.69)

{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4N^{2}}\;\text{.}

Letting

(5.70)

\mathcal{W}\coloneqq\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N}:{\rm dist}_{\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}(x,y)\geq 2\}\;\text{,}\;\;

by the second inequality in
[
Her23
, Eq. (1.7)]
, we have

(5.71)

\lambda_{2,2}\leq\frac{1}{\max_{\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{E}_{\kappa(\,\cdot\,|\mathcal{U})}[\tau_{\mathcal{U}^{c}}]}\leq\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}_{\kappa(\,\cdot\,|\mathcal{W})}[\tau_{\mathcal{W}^{c}}]}\;\text{,}\;\;

where in the second step we substituted
\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{W}
.
The last denominator in (
5.71
) is almost the mean meeting time of two independent random walks, both being uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}
(and independently) at time
t=0
. This latter quantity is known
[
Cox89
, Theorem 4]
to asymptotically diverge, as
N\to\infty
, like
s_{N}
, where

(5.72)

s_{N}\coloneqq\begin{cases}N^{2}\log N&\text{if}\ d=2\;\text{,}\\
N^{d}&\text{if}\ d\geq 3\;\text{.}\end{cases}

In the remainder of this proof, we show that the denominator in (
5.71
) follows the same asymptotic behavior.

Since
\kappa(\mathcal{W})=1-\frac{2d+1}{N^{d}}
, we calculate

(5.73)

\mathsf{E}_{\kappa(\,\cdot\,\mid\Omega)}[\tau_{\mathcal{W}^{c}}]=\left(1-\frac{2d+1}{N^{d}}\right)^{-1}\frac{1}{N^{2d}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{E}_{(x,y)}[\tau_{\mathcal{W}^{c}}]\;\text{.}

Moreover, since
Z_{t}\coloneqq X_{t}-Y_{t}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N}
is a simple random walk (jumping to a nearest neighbor at rate
2
), we have

(5.74)

\mathsf{E}_{(x,y)}[\tau_{\mathcal{W}^{c}}]=\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad(x,y)\in\mathcal{W}\;\text{,}\;\;

where we use the hat-notation to refer to quantities related to
Z_{t}
, while
\hat{\tau}_{\ell}
,
\ell\in\llbracket 0,\lfloor N/2\rfloor\rrbracket
, denotes the first hitting time of the set

(5.75)

\{z\in\mathbb{T}^{d}_{N}:{\rm dist}_{\mathbb{T}_{N}^{d}}(z,0)=\ell\}\;\text{.}

By the strong Markov property and translation invariance of the dynamics, we have

(5.76)

\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{0}]=\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]+\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{e_{1}}[\hat{\tau}_{0}]=\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]+\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}[\hat{\tau}_{0}^{+}]-\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\hat{\tau}_{0}^{+}
denotes the first return time to
0
for the walk

started in
0
, i.e.,

(5.77)

\hat{\tau}_{0}^{+}\coloneqq\inf\{t>\hat{\tau}_{1}:Z_{t}=0\}\;\text{.}

Clearly,
\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}\,[\hat{\tau}_{1}]=\frac{1}{4d}
, while, by Kac’s formula for return times (see, e.g.,
[
AF02
, p. 34, Lemma 2.25]
), we obtain

(5.78)

\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}[\hat{\tau}_{0}^{+}]=\frac{N^{d}}{4d}\;\text{.}

Hence, we get

(5.79)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N^{2d}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{W}}\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N^{2d}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{W}}\left(\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{0}]-\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}[\hat{\tau}_{0}^{+}]+\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{0}[\hat{\tau}_{1}]\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left\{\frac{1}{N^{2d}}\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{W}}\widehat{\mathsf{E}}_{x-y}[\hat{\tau}_{0}]\right\}-\frac{N^{d}}{4d}\;\text{.}

By
[
Cox89
, Theorem 4]
(see also
[
Dur24
, Lemma 7.3.2]
for a more recent textbook version), the expression between curly brackets is, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough, bounded below by
\mathfrak{g}_{d}\,s_{N}
, for some positive constant
\mathfrak{g}_{d}
(which, for
d\geq 3
, is strictly larger than
\frac{1}{4d}
, cf.
[
Cox89
, Eq. (1.2)]
). Hence, collecting (
5.73
), (
5.74
) and (
5.79
), we obtain, for some
C=C_{d}>0
and for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough,

(5.80)

{\mathsf{E}}_{\kappa(\,\cdot\,|\mathcal{W})}[\tau_{\mathcal{W}^{c}}]\geq Cs_{N}\;\text{,}\;\;

which, along with (
5.71
), implies

(5.81)

\lambda_{2,2}\leq\frac{1}{Cs_{N}}\;\text{.}

Comparing this inequality with (
5.69
) (cf. (
5.72
)) completes the proof of the theorem.

6.
Non-conservative SIP. Setting and main results

In this section, we attach particle reservoirs to some sites of the graph. We describe this by introducing two sets of non-negative site parameters,
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in V}
and
\theta=(\theta_{x})_{x\in V}
. The former one represents the rates of interaction between a site and its reservoir. The latter one prescribes the reservoirs’ particle densities. Next to the inclusion particles’ motion considered so far, we now let particles be created and annihilated with rates resembling those of
{\rm SIP}
.

6.1.
Model

Let
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
be as in Section
2
, and introduce some non-negative site weights
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in V}
and
\theta=(\theta_{x})_{x\in V}
. Then, we write
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
for the Markov process on
2
2

2

\Xi_{0}\coloneqq\{\emptyset\}
consists of the empty configuration
\emptyset
with no particles.

\Xi\coloneqq\cup_{k=0}^{\infty}\,\Xi_{k}
with infinitesimal generator
L_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}
given, for a bounded function
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\Xi}
and a configuration
\eta\in\Xi
, by

(6.1)

\displaystyle L_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}f(\eta)=\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\left(\alpha_{y}+\eta_{y}\right)\left(f(\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-f(\eta)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{x\in V}\omega_{x}\,\big\{\eta_{x}\left(1+\theta_{x}\right)\left(f(\eta-\delta_{x})-f(\eta)\right)+\theta_{x}\left(\alpha_{x}+\eta_{x}\right)\left(f(\eta+\delta_{x})-f(\eta)\right)\big\}\;\text{.}

Here, the first summation on the right-hand side corresponds with the conservative dynamics described by
L_{G,\alpha,k}
in (
2.2
), and is referred to as bulk dynamics. The second summation concerns the particle creation-annihilation mechanism, and may be interpreted as a bulk-boundary dynamics. More in detail, particles at
x\in V
get killed independently, each at rate
\omega_{x}\left(1+\theta_{x}\right)
, while a new particle is created at
x\in V
with a configuration-dependent rate
\omega_{x}\,\theta_{x}\left(\alpha_{x}+\eta_{x}\right)
.
Furthermore, albeit the configuration space
\Xi
is countably infinite,
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
does not explode in finite time. Indeed, the total number of particles is stochastically dominated by a pure birth process with linearly growing rates (see, e.g.,
[
FGS22
, Proposition 2.1]
).

The long-time behavior of
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
clearly depends on the values of
\omega
and
\theta
. To start with, we mention that
\omega_{x}=0
means that site
x\in V
does not directly exchange particles with any reservoirs, whereas

\theta_{x}=0
corresponds to having a purely absorbing reservoir at
x\in V
. As a consequence, if
\omega\equiv 0
, we recover the conservative case having, for any total number of particles
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\mu_{\alpha,k}
in (
2.3
) as a unique reversible measure. If
\theta\equiv 0
and
\omega\not\equiv 0
, the system eventually empties out. In other words, if
\theta
vanishes on the support of
\omega
, that is,

(6.2)

{\rm supp}(\omega)\coloneqq\{x\in V:\omega_{x}>0\}\;\text{,}\;\;

then
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
has the empty configuration
\emptyset\in\Xi_{0}
as its unique stationary state, which, in turn, is absorbing.
More generally, for
\omega\not\equiv 0
, there exists a unique stationary measure for
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
, referred to as
\mu_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}
.
In this situation, one typically distinguishes between two scenarios, commonly referred to as equilibrium and non-equilibrium, respectively.

More in detail,
equilibrium
occurs if
\theta
is constant on

{\rm supp}(\omega)
. In this case, it is well-known that
\mu_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}
is reversible, in product form, and explicit: assuming that
\theta\equiv\varrho>0
, then
\mu_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}=\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}
, where, for all
\eta\in\Xi
,

(6.3)

\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}(\eta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}_{\alpha,\varrho}}\left(\frac{\varrho}{1+\varrho}\right)^{|\eta|}\prod_{x\in V}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_{x}+\eta_{x})}{\Gamma(\alpha_{x})\,\eta_{x}!}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ \mathcal{Z}_{\alpha,\varrho}\coloneqq(1+\varrho)^{|\alpha|}\;\text{.}

Note that
\mu_{\alpha,k}
in (
2.3
) is the canonical measure associated to the grand-canonical
\nu_{\alpha,\varrho}
.
One speaks of
non-equilibrium
if
\theta
is non-constant on
{\rm supp}(\omega)
. Then,
\mu_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}
is, in general, neither reversible, nor in product form, nor explicit. We will not need to know more on the structure of this steady state; for further details, see, e.g.,
[
FRS22
]
and references therein.

6.2.
Spectral gap identity when
\theta\equiv 0

We start with the analysis of the purely absorbing
{\rm SIP}
. As already anticipated in Section
1.4
, we shall see that understanding the case
\theta\equiv 0
is one of the key ingredients for the spectral analysis of the setting with general
\theta
.

Observe that substituting
\theta\equiv 0
in (
6.1
) gives rise to a system in which all particles are never created, and get annihilated one at the time, until the system eventually gets empty. In particular,
L_{G,\alpha,\omega,0}
, interpreted as an infinite matrix, is similar to a block lower triangular one. Moreover, the blocks on the diagonal — each for any total number of particles
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
in the bulk of the system — are of finite size and describe the following finite-state Markov chains.
The case
k=1
corresponds to a process with just one particle in the bulk, which moves as
{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)
defined in Section
2
, additionally killed at rate
\omega_{x}\geq 0
when sitting on
x\in V
. We refer to this process as
{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)
. The case
k\geq 2
is similar, with
k
interacting particles evolving in the bulk as
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
, each independently killed with rate
\omega_{x}\geq 0
when sitting at
x\in V
. Each of these Markov chains, say
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha,\omega)
, are sub-stochastic. Moreover, since the conservative
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha)
admits
\mu_{\alpha,k}
in (
2.3
) as its unique reversible measure, all eigenvalues of (the negative of) the generator of
{\rm SIP}_{k}(G,\alpha,\omega)
(see (
7.1
)) are real and strictly positive.
Letting
{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha,\omega)
,
k\geq 1
, be the lowest of such eigenvalues, we write (cf. (
2.5
))

(6.4)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega)=\inf_{k\geq 2}{\rm gap}_{k}(G,\alpha,\omega)\;\text{.}

Similarly, we introduce the spectral gap of
{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)
as

(6.5)

{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)={\rm gap}_{1}(G,\alpha,\omega)\;\text{.}

Our main result of this section establishes that, as soon as the killing rate
\omega
is not identically zero, the spectral gaps in (
6.4
) and (
6.5
) coincide, regardless of the underlying geometry and of the site weights — even when
\alpha_{\rm min}\in(0,1)
. This spectral gap identity stands in stark contrast to the behavior observed in the conservative setting, where restrictions are imposed on the value of
\alpha_{\rm min}
.

Theorem 6.1

(
\theta\equiv 0
)
.

For all graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
, and site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
,
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in V}
with
\omega\neq 0
, we have

(6.6)

{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega)={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)\;\text{.}

Remarkably, the proof of the above identity (deferred to Section
7.1
) is quite elementary.

6.3.
General setting

When dealing with the general case
\theta\neq 0

Theorem 6.3

(1)

Given

(2)

Letting

(

For all graphs

Remark 6.5

(Non-reversible setting)
.

In view of Theorem

Remark 6.6

(Symmetric exclusion processes & Co.)

The proof of Theorem

8.
Extensions and related models

In this last section, we extend our results to the Brownian energy process, and briefly discuss their connections with what is known for some closely related models.

8.1.
Brownian energy process

Given a graph
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
, the corresponding Brownian energy process — referred to as
{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)
— is the diffusion on the probability simplex

(8.1)

\varSigma\coloneqq\left\{\zeta\in[0,1]^{V}:\sum_{x\in V}\zeta_{x}=1\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

with infinitesimal generator

(8.2)

\mathcal{L}_{G,\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\left\{-\left(\alpha_{y}\zeta_{x}-\alpha_{x}\zeta_{y}\right)\left(\partial_{\zeta_{x}}-\partial_{\zeta_{y}}\right)+\zeta_{x}\zeta_{y}\left(\partial_{\zeta_{x}}-\partial_{\zeta_{y}}\right)^{2}\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

and the following Dirichlet distribution on
\varSigma
as its unique reversible measure

(8.3)

\gamma_{\alpha}({\rm d}\zeta)=\left(\frac{1}{{\rm B}(\alpha)}\prod_{x\in V}\zeta_{x}^{\alpha_{x}-1}\right){\rm d}\zeta\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ {\rm B}(\alpha)\coloneqq\frac{\prod_{x\in V}\Gamma(\alpha_{x})}{\Gamma(|\alpha|)}\;\text{.}

Here,
{\rm d}\zeta
stands for the uniform measure on
\varSigma
. We refer to Section
1
,
[
KS24
]
and references therein, for more information and history on this model. Here, we just mention that its mean field version (i.e., when
c_{xy}\equiv{\rm const.}
) coincides with the renowned multi-type Wright-Fisher diffusion with parent independent mutations (see, e.g.,
[
Shi77
,
WZ19
]
).

As shown in
[
KS24
]
,
{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)
inherits its eigenstructure from that of
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
. More precisely, the Poisson intertwining relation
[
KS24
, Eq. (4.1)]
between the generators
\mathcal{L}_{G,\alpha}
and
L_{G,\alpha,k}
allows to lift eigenfunctions of
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
to eigenfunctions of
{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)
, both corresponding to the same eigenvalue. Using this intertwining relation and the fact
\mathcal{L}_{G,\alpha}
is a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\gamma_{\alpha})
, one concludes that this lifting saturates all the info on the spectrum and, in particular, the corresponding spectral gaps coincide

(8.4)

{\rm gap}_{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)={\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)\;\text{.}

Combining the findings in
[
KS24
]
with our Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
on
{\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha)
, we readily deduce the following analogues of (
1.4
) and (
1.8
).

Corollary 8.1
.

For all graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
and site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
, we have

(8.5)

\alpha_{\rm min}\left\{\frac{1}{21}\,\frac{\alpha_{\rm ratio}}{6^{\alpha_{\rm min}/\alpha_{\rm ratio}}}\,\frac{c_{\rm min}}{|V|^{2}\,{\rm diam}(G)}\right\}\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)\leq{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\;\text{,}\;\;

and, in some cases, we have

(8.6)

{\rm gap}_{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha)\lneqq{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha)\;\text{.}

Interestingly, also our results on the spectrum of the non-conservative
{\rm SIP}
, via similar arguments, translate to the context of the
{\rm BEP}
. Indeed, the aforementioned Poisson intertwining is effective also when one adds the interaction with external reservoirs. As shown, e.g., in
[
GRT24
, Section 4.1]
, using this operator, the generator
L_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}
in (
6.1
) of
{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
satisfies, for all
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in V}
and
\theta=(\theta_{x})_{x\in V}
, an analogous intertwining relation with

(8.7)

\mathcal{L}_{G,\alpha,\omega,\theta}=\mathcal{L}_{G,\alpha}+\sum_{x\in V}\omega_{x}\left\{\left(\alpha_{x}\theta_{x}-\zeta_{x}\right)\partial_{\zeta_{x}}+\theta_{x}\zeta_{x}\,\partial_{\zeta_{x}}^{2}\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

the generator of what we refer to as
{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\theta)
.

When
\theta\equiv\varrho>0
, this non-conservative diffusion on
[0,\infty)^{V}
is well known to have the following product measure
\gamma_{\alpha,\varrho}=\otimes_{x\in V}\,{\rm Gamma}(\alpha_{x},\varrho)
as its unique reversible measure. Hence, arguing as above, we derive the corresponding spectral gap identity

(8.8)

{\rm gap}_{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\varrho)={\rm gap}_{\rm SIP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\varrho)\;\text{,}\;\;

and, as a consequence, the following analogue of Corollary
6.4
.

Corollary 8.2
.

For all graphs
G=(V,(c_{xy})_{x,y\in V})
, and site weights
\alpha=(\alpha_{x})_{x\in V}
,
\omega=(\omega_{x})_{x\in V}
with
\omega\neq 0
, we have

(8.9)

{\rm gap}_{\rm BEP}(G,\alpha,\omega,\varrho)={\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\varrho>0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\rm gap}_{\rm RW}(G,\alpha,\omega)
is defined in (
6.5
).

8.2.
Other models

As already anticipated in Section
1
,
{\rm SIP}
and
{\rm BEP}
come with several closely related models, having a different dynamics, but exactly the same steady states. The models we have in mind fall into the class of mass redistribution models. Here, we shall focus only on those models with configuration-independent edge updates (see, e.g.,
[
Sas15
]
and references therein for configuration-dependent mass redistribution models with Dirichlet-type steady states): after an edge, say
xy
, is selected with rate
c_{xy}\geq 0
, particles/energies sitting on
x
and
y
get randomly redistributed therein. Among them, let us mention:

(1)

the Beta-Binomial splitting process and its continuous version, also known as Beta splitting process or (generalized) KMP model (see, e.g.,
[
PR23
,
DMFG24
,
GRT24
]
);

(2)

the discrete and continuous harmonic processes (see, e.g.,
[
FFG23
,
GRT24
]
).

All models in (a) and (b) have been studied in their conservative and non-conservative variants (see the aforementioned references for the precise definitions of the redistribution rules), and, just like
{\rm SIP}
and
{\rm BEP}
, discrete and corresponding continuous models are isospectral (when reversible), enjoying completely analogous Poisson intertwining relations.

To the best of our knowledge, the only sharp quantitative results on their convergence to equilibrium are confined to a handful of results, see
[
CCL03
,
Smi14
,
CLL20
,
PR23
]
and references therein. For what concerns the spectral gap, the only sharp results that we are aware of concern the Beta and Beta-Binomial splitting processes in two specific settings, both conservative:

(1)

the complete graph with
\alpha\equiv{\rm const.}

[
CCL03
]
(see also
[
CLL20
, Remark 4]
);

(2)

the segment with
\alpha\equiv{\rm const.}\geq 1

[
CLL20
]
.

In particular, in (i), the gap is attained by the system with
k=2
particles (which is strictly smaller than
{\rm gap}_{1}
), while in (ii) it coincides with that of the corresponding single particle.

In order to obtain spectral gap asymptotics for small diffusivity, the proof ideas of Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
apply also to these models (actually, with considerable simplifications due to the presence of edge updates only, and edges are a.s. never updated simultaneously). For instance, it is not difficult to show that, on graphs like that in Section
5.5
and for
\alpha
small enough, the spectral gap of the two particles must be strictly smaller than that of the single random walk.
Similarly, the non-conservative (reversible) models satisfy a one-particle spectral gap identity, analogous to the one contained in Corollary
6.4
. Indeed, the key step in that proof (Section
7.1
) carries over to these other models in contact with reservoirs without much changes, because of the analogous intertwining and consistency/duality relations available also for them, see, e.g.,
[
GRT24
]
. We leave the verification of these claims to the interested reader. What remains largely open is the problem of obtaining spectral gap’s sharp lower bounds — and, possibly, identities in terms of simpler processes — for the conservative variants of these mass redistribution models on general geometries.

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathds{E}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
denote the law and corresponding expectation of
(\eta^{\varepsilon}(t))_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\eta\in\Xi_{k}
.

The first lemma states that the time to reach the absorbing set
\Omega_{k}
from the transient collection
\Delta_{k}
(defined in (
5.5
)) is uniformly negligible. Given a subset
A
of
\Xi_{k}
, let
\tau_{A}
be its (random) first hitting time. Moreover, define

(A.3)

c_{\rm min}=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in V\\
c_{xy}>0\end{subarray}}c_{xy}>0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad c_{\rm max}=\max_{x,y\in V}c_{xy}\;\text{.}

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
\varepsilon|\hat{\alpha}|\leq\frac{c_{\rm min}}{4\,c_{\rm max}\,k}
. Then, for some
C=C(G)>0
and for all
\varepsilon>0
,
k\geq 2
,

(A.4)

\max_{\zeta\in\Delta_{k}}\mathds{E}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\leq\frac{C\,\varepsilon}{c_{\rm min}}\,\log k\;\text{.}

Proof.

Define a test function
\phi:\Xi_{k}\to\mathbb{R}
as

(A.5)

\phi(\eta)\coloneqq-\sum_{x\in V}\left(1+\frac{1}{2}+\cdots+\frac{1}{\eta_{x}}\right)\;\text{.}

Then, there exists
C=C(G)>0
such that

(A.6)

\max_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\phi(\eta)-\min_{\eta\in\Xi_{k}}\phi(\eta)\leq\frac{C}{4}\log k\;\text{.}

For each
\zeta\in\Delta_{k}
, we may express
L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}\phi(\zeta)
as

(A.7)

\sum_{\{x,y\}\subseteq V}c_{xy}\left[\zeta_{x}\,(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}+\zeta_{y})\,(\phi(\zeta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y})-\phi(\zeta))+\zeta_{y}\,(\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{x}+\eta_{x})\,(\phi(\zeta-\delta_{y}+\delta_{x})-\phi(\zeta))\right]\;\text{.}

If
\zeta_{x},\zeta_{y}\geq 1
, then the term inside the bracket in (
A.7
) becomes

(A.8)

\frac{\zeta_{x}}{\zeta_{y}+1}+\frac{\zeta_{y}}{\zeta_{x}+1}+\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left(1-\frac{\zeta_{x}}{\zeta_{y}+1}\right)+\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{x}\left(1-\frac{\zeta_{y}}{\zeta_{x}+1}\right)\geq\frac{(1-\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y})\,\zeta_{x}}{\zeta_{y}+1}+\frac{(1-\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{x})\,\zeta_{y}}{\zeta_{x}+1}\;\text{.}

By the assumption, we have
\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}\leq\varepsilon|\hat{\alpha}|\leq\frac{c_{\rm min}}{4\,c_{\rm max}\,k}<\frac{1}{2}
. Thus, the right-hand side is bounded from below by

(A.9)

\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\zeta_{x}}{\zeta_{y}+1}+\frac{\zeta_{y}}{\zeta_{x}+1}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

where the inequality holds because
\zeta_{x},\zeta_{y}\geq 1
. Next, if
\zeta_{x}\geq 1
and
\zeta_{y}=0
, then the term inside the bracket in (
A.7
) equals

(A.10)

\zeta_{x}\,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\left(\frac{1}{\zeta_{x}}-1\right)=-\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,(\zeta_{x}-1)\geq-\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,\zeta_{x}\;\text{.}

Similarly, if
\zeta_{x}=0
and
\zeta_{y}\geq 1
, then the term is at least
-\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{x}\,\zeta_{y}
. As
\zeta\in\Delta_{k}
, there exists at least one
\{x^{*},y^{*}\}
such that
c_{x^{*}y^{*}}>0
and
\zeta_{x^{*}},\zeta_{y^{*}}\geq 1
. Thus, by (
A.3
), (
A.7
), (
A.9
), and (
A.10
),

(A.11)

L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}\phi(\zeta)\geq\frac{c_{x^{*}y^{*}}}{2}-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in V\\
\zeta_{x}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in V\\
\zeta_{y}=0\end{subarray}}c_{xy}\,\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,\zeta_{x}\geq\frac{c_{\rm min}}{2}-c_{\rm max}\,k\,\varepsilon|\hat{\alpha}|\geq\frac{c_{\rm min}}{4}\;\text{,}\;\;

where the last inequality holds since
\varepsilon|\hat{\alpha}|\leq\frac{c_{\rm min}}{4\,c_{\rm max}\,k}
.

Now, it is well known that

(A.12)

\phi(\eta^{\varepsilon}(t))-\phi(\eta^{\varepsilon}(0))-\int_{0}^{t}(\varepsilon^{-1}L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}\phi)(\eta^{\varepsilon}(s))\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;\quad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;

is a
\mathds{P}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}
-martingale. Setting
t=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}\wedge r
, applying (
A.6
) and (
A.11
), taking expectations with respect to
\mathds{E}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}
, and then sending
r\to\infty
, we obtain

(A.13)

\frac{c_{\rm min}\,\varepsilon^{-1}}{4}\,\mathds{E}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\leq\mathds{E}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{\Omega_{k}}}(\varepsilon^{-1}L_{\varepsilon\hat{\alpha},k}\phi)(\eta^{\varepsilon}(s))\,{\rm d}s\right]\leq\frac{C}{4}\log k\;\text{.}

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

For each
t\geq 0
, let
T_{\varepsilon}(t)
be the local time spent by
(\eta^{\varepsilon}(s))_{s\geq 0}
in
\Omega_{k}
up to time
t
:

(A.14)

T_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}{\bf 1}\{\eta^{\varepsilon}(s)\in\Omega_{k}\}\,{\rm d}s\;\text{.}

Then, let
S_{\varepsilon}(s)
,
s\geq 0
, be its generalized inverse:

(A.15)

S_{\varepsilon}(s)\coloneqq\sup\,\{t\geq 0:T_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq s\}\;\text{.}

By definition,
T_{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon}(s))=s
and
T_{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon}(s)+u)>s
for any
s\geq 0
and
u>0
. In turn, we have

(A.16)

\eta^{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon}(s))\in\Omega_{k}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad s\geq 0\;\text{.}

Note also that
\{S_{\varepsilon}(s)\geq s+u\}=\{T_{\varepsilon}(s+u)\leq s\}
for
s\geq 0
and
u>0
.

Lemma A.3
.

For all
\eta\in\Xi_{k}
,
t\geq 0
and
\delta>0
, we have

(A.17)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[S_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq t+\delta]=0\;\text{.}

Proof.

First, suppose that
\eta\in\Delta_{k}
. We may bound

(A.18)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[S_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq t+\delta]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t]

(A.19)

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t,\ \tau_{\Omega_{k}}<\delta/2]+\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}\geq\delta/2]\;\text{.}

By Lemma
A.2
and the Markov inequality, we obtain

(A.20)

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}\geq\delta/2]\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\,\mathds{E}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\leq\frac{2}{\delta}\,\frac{C\,\varepsilon}{c_{\rm min}}\,\log k\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\;\text{.}

Thus, by the strong Markov property of
(\eta^{\varepsilon}(s))_{s\geq 0}
and the fact that
t\mapsto T_{\varepsilon}(t)
is non-decreasing, we deduce

(A.21)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[S_{\varepsilon}(t)\geq t+\delta]\leq\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{\xi\in\Omega_{k}}\mathds{P}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta/2)\leq t]\;\text{.}

Thus, it suffices to prove Lemma
A.3
for
\eta\in\Omega_{k}
.

It remains to prove that, for
\eta\in\Omega_{k}
,
t\geq 0
and
\delta>0
,

(A.22)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t]=0\;\text{.}

Let
0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{2}<\cdots
be the consecutive jump times from
\Omega_{k}
to
\Delta_{k}
. Then, for any integer
m\geq 1
, we get

(A.23)

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t]\leq\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t,\ \tau_{m+1}>t+\delta]+\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{m+1}\leq t+\delta]\;\text{.}

Conditioned on the first event in the right-hand side of (
A.23
), there occurs at most
m
jumps from
\Omega_{k}
to
\Delta_{k}
up to time
t+\delta
. Let
\tau_{1}^{\prime}<\tau_{2}^{\prime}<\cdots
denote the consecutive return times to
\Omega_{k}
, such that we have

(A.24)

0<\tau_{1}<\tau_{1}^{\prime}<\tau_{2}<\tau_{2}^{\prime}<\cdots\;\text{.}

Then, by (
A.14
), we may bound the first probability in the right-hand side of (
A.23
) by

(A.25)

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[(\tau_{1}^{\prime}-\tau_{1})+(\tau_{2}^{\prime}-\tau_{2})+\cdots+(\tau_{m}^{\prime}-\tau_{m})\geq\delta]\;\text{.}

We may further bound this by

(A.26)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{i}^{\prime}-\tau_{i}\geq\delta/m]

\displaystyle\leq m\sup_{\zeta\in\Delta_{k}}\mathds{P}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}\geq\delta/m]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m^{2}}{\delta}\sup_{\zeta\in\Delta_{k}}\mathds{E}_{\zeta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\leq\frac{m^{2}\,C\,\varepsilon\,\log k}{\delta\,c_{\rm min}}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\;\text{.}

Here, the first inequality is due to the strong Markov property at
\tau_{i}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,m\rrbracket
, the second inequality is due to the Markov inequality, and the third inequality is due to Lemma
A.2
.
Further, for
(\eta^{\varepsilon}(s))_{s\geq 0}
, any direct jump from
\Omega_{k}
to
\Delta_{k}
has rate bounded above by

(A.27)

\sup_{\eta\in\Omega_{k}}\sup_{x,y\in V}\varepsilon^{-1}\,c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\varepsilon\hat{\alpha}_{y}\leq c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}\;\text{,}\;\;

where
\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}\coloneqq\max_{x\in V}\hat{\alpha}_{x}
. Hence, each of the random variables
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}-\tau_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\tau_{m+1}-\tau_{m}^{\prime}
stochastically dominates an exponential random variable with rate
c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}
. Thus, letting
\tau_{1}^{*},\tau_{2}^{*},\dots
be some i.i.d. exponentials of rate
c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}
, by Markovianity we get

(A.28)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{m+1}\leq t+\delta]

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\,[\tau_{1}^{*}+\cdots+\tau_{m+1}^{*}\leq t+\delta]

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\,[{\rm Gamma}\,(m+1,c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max})\leq t+\delta]\xrightarrow{m\to\infty}0\;\text{,}\;\;

where
{\rm Gamma}\,(u,\beta)
denotes the Gamma distribution with shape
u>0
and rate
\beta>0
. Therefore, by (
A.23
), (
A.26
) and (
A.28
), we conclude that

(A.29)

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[T_{\varepsilon}(t+\delta)\leq t]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{m\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{m^{2}\,C\,\varepsilon\,\log k}{\delta\,c_{\rm min}}

(A.30)

\displaystyle+\limsup_{m\to\infty}\mathds{P}\,[{\rm Gamma}\,(m+1,c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max})\leq t+\delta]=0\;\text{,}\;\;

as desired.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

We have

(A.31)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\sup_{\xi\in\Omega_{k}}\mathds{P}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Delta_{k}}<3\delta]=0\;\text{.}

Proof.

As explained in the proof of Lemma
A.3
, starting from any
\xi\in\Omega_{k}
,
\tau_{\Delta_{k}}
stochastically dominates an exponential random variable of rate
c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}
. Thus, we get

(A.32)

\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,1)}\mathds{P}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Delta_{k}}<3\delta]\leq\limsup_{\delta\to 0}\left(1-e^{-c_{\rm max}\,k\,\hat{\alpha}_{\rm max}\,3\delta}\right)=0\;\text{.}

This concludes the proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove Proposition
A.1
.

Proof of Proposition
A.1
.

We prove

(A.33)

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\eta^{\varepsilon}(t)\in\Omega_{k}]=1\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t>0\;\text{,}\;\;\eta\in\Xi_{k}\;\text{.}

We follow the proof ideas of
[
LLM18
, Lemma 3.1]
. By (
A.16
), for
\delta\in(0,t/3)
, we estimate

(A.34)

\displaystyle 1=\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\eta^{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon}(t-3\delta))\in\Omega_{k}]\leq

\displaystyle\ \mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\left[\eta^{\varepsilon}(S_{\varepsilon}(t-3\delta))\in\Omega_{k},\ S_{\varepsilon}(t-3\delta)\in[t-3\delta,t-2\delta]\right]

(A.35)

\displaystyle+\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[S_{\varepsilon}(t-3\delta)>t-2\delta]\;\text{.}

By Lemma
A.3
, the second probability in the right-hand side vanishes as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Moreover, by (
A.16
), the first probability in the right-hand side is bounded by

(A.36)

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\,\exists s\in[t-3\delta,t-2\delta],\ \eta^{\varepsilon}(s)\in\Omega_{k}\,]\;\text{.}

We can further divide this into two events and bound the last probability as

(A.37)

\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\eta^{\varepsilon}(t)\in\Omega_{k}]+\mathds{P}_{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\,[\,\exists s\in[t-3\delta,t-2\delta],\ \eta^{\varepsilon}(s)\in\Omega_{k},\ \eta^{\varepsilon}(t)\in\Delta_{k}\,]\;\text{.}

Then, by the Markov property, the second probability above is bounded by

(A.38)

\sup_{\xi\in\Omega_{k}}\mathds{P}_{\xi}^{\varepsilon}\,[\tau_{\Delta_{k}}<3\delta]\;\text{,}\;\;

which, by Lemma
A.4
, vanishes by first taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, and then
\delta\to 0
.
Collecting all displayed inequalities, we conclude that (
A.33
) holds.
∎

Appendix B
Reversibility of the limit process

In this appendix, we prove Lemma
5.7
and simply write
\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k,m}
,
k\geq m
.

Proof of Lemma
5.7
.

We start by introducing an auxiliary continuous-time Markov chain on
\Xi_{k}
with rate function (cf. (
5.1
)–(
5.5
))

(B.1)

{\bf s}(\eta,\,\cdot\,)\coloneqq\begin{cases}{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta,\,\cdot\,)&\text{if}\ \eta\in\Omega_{k}\;\text{,}\\
{\bf r}^{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(\eta,\,\cdot\,)&\text{if}\ \eta\in\Delta_{k}\;\text{.}\end{cases}

In words, the above dynamics follows the rule of
A_{\hat{\alpha},k}
in
\Omega_{k}
, while that of
B_{k}
in
\Delta_{k}
. (Remark that this is not a
{\rm SIP}
-dynamics.)
Now, recall the definition of
\Omega_{k,m}
from (
5.17
) and, similarly, introduce the set

(B.2)

\Delta_{k,m}\coloneqq\left\{\zeta\in\Delta_{k}:|\{x\in V:\zeta_{x}>0\}|=m\right\}\;\text{.}

Observe that this auxiliary chain can jump from
\Omega_{k,m}
to either
\Delta_{k,m}
or
\Delta_{k,m+1}
(if
m\leq k-1
), but a jump from
\Omega_{k,m}
to
\Delta_{k,m}
has rate zero to backtrack.

Now, fix
m\leq k-1
. We claim that this dynamics, restricted to
\Omega_{k,m}\cup\Delta_{k,m+1}
, is reversible with respect to
\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}
.
For this purpose, it suffices to consider pairs
\eta,\xi=\eta-\delta_{x}+\delta_{y}\in\Omega_{k,m}\cup\Delta_{k,m+1}
(hence,
\eta_{x}\geq 1
); rates are zero otherwise. If
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}
and
\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}
(such that
\eta_{x}=1
), then

(B.3)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta,\xi)=c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\eta_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\eta_{z}}=c_{xy}\,\hat{\alpha}_{x}\xi_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\xi_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\xi_{z}}=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi,\eta)\;\text{.}

If
\eta\in\Omega_{k,m}
and
\xi\in\Delta_{k,m+1}
(such that
\eta_{x}\geq 2
), we have

(B.4)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta,\xi)=c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\hat{\alpha}_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\eta_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\eta_{z}}=c_{xy}\,\xi_{x}\xi_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\xi_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\xi_{z}}=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(\xi,\eta)\;\text{.}

If
\eta,\xi\in\Delta_{k,m+1}
, we have

(B.5)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(\eta,\xi)=c_{xy}\,\eta_{x}\eta_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\eta_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\eta_{z}}=c_{xy}\,\xi_{x}\xi_{y}\,\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in V\\
\xi_{z}>0\end{subarray}}\frac{\hat{\alpha}_{z}}{\xi_{z}}=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{B}}_{k}(\xi,\eta)\;\text{.}

This shows that
\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}
is reversible for the Markov chain described in (
B.1
), restricted to
\Omega_{k,m}\cup\Delta_{k,m+1}
.
Furthermore, by (
5.15
), we have, for all distinct
\eta,\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}
,

(B.6)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta,\xi)=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta)\sum_{\zeta\in\Delta_{k,m+1}}{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\eta,\zeta)\,{\bf P}_{\zeta}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{\xi}=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]\;\text{.}

Following
(\mathcal{B}_{k}(t))_{t\geq 0}
, from
\zeta\in\Delta_{k,m+1}
to arrive at
\xi\in\Omega_{k,m}
, the trajectory must stay in
\Delta_{k,m+1}
. Thus, by reversibility, the right-hand side is equal to

(B.7)

\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi)\sum_{\zeta\in\Delta_{k,m+1}}{\bf r}^{\mathcal{A}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi,\zeta)\,{\bf P}_{\zeta}^{\mathcal{B}}\,[\tau_{\eta}=\tau_{\Omega_{k}}]=\varsigma_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi)\,{\bf r}^{\mathcal{M}}_{\hat{\alpha},k}(\xi,\eta)\;\text{.}

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎
