Impaired left ventricular global longitudinal strain is associated with diastolic dysfunction in obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Authors: Wei Li, Wei Liu, Xiaopei Lin, Xiaoying Mi, Tingting Sun, Di Wang, Fan Zhang, Ceng Wang, Jing Wang, Jian Zhang, Zhenzhen Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesLeft ventricular global longitudinal strain (LV-GLS) provides additional insight into subtle myocardial contractile impairment in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). However, its clinical value in evaluating diastolic dysfunction remains insufficiently characterized. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the association between LV-GLS and the severity of diastolic dysfunction in symptomatic hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) patients with preserved LVEF, and to assess the potential utility of LV-GLS for grading diastolic dysfunction in this patient cohort.MethodsA total of 145 consecutive symptomatic HOCM patients were enrolled and classified into diastolic dysfunction grade I or grade II according to the ASE-recommended algorithm. Multivariate regression analysis was performed to identify independent factors associated with the severity of diastolic dysfunction.ResultsAmong the 145 patients, 109 (75.2%) had grade II and 36 (24.8%) had grade I diastolic dysfunction. Grade II dysfunction was more common among females. Greater myocardial wall thickness, elevated BNP levels, and reduced LV-GLS were independently associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction, irrespective of loading conditions and preserved LVEF. Receiver operating characteristic analysis demonstrated that LV-GLS effectively discriminated diastolic dysfunction grade, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.728 (p = 0.001).ConclusionsReduced LV-GLS was independently associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction in symptomatic HOCM patients with preserved LVEF. LV-GLS provides incremental value for grading diastolic dysfunction and could improve risk stratification for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in this population.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, Diastolic dysfunction, Global longitudinal strain, Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction

Introduction

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is one of the most common inherited cardiovascular diseases, with an estimated prevalence of 1:500 to 1:200 [1]. It is characterized by left ventricular (LV) hypertrophy with heterogeneous phenotypic expressions in both the pattern and extent of wall thickening, which may differentially affect myocardial stiffness and LV diastolic performance [2]. LV diastolic dysfunction is a key determinant of symptoms and adverse outcomes in patients with cardiomyopathies and preserved LV ejection fraction (LVEF). Accordingly, beyond the correction of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO), growing attention has been directed toward the evaluation and management of diastolic dysfunction in patients with obstructive HCM (HOCM) [2].

Recent evidence suggests that global longitudinal strain (GLS), although traditionally regarded as a systolic parameter, also provides important insights into diastolic physiology by reflecting myocardial stiffness, relaxation abnormalities, and systolic–diastolic coupling [3]. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), where myocardial dysfunction often originates from—or is predominantly driven by—diastolic impairment, reduced GLS correlates with markers of elevated left ventricular (LV) filling pressures and more advanced diastolic dysfunction, even in the presence of preserved LVEF. Unlike conventional indices that primarily reflect filling pressures, LV-GLS quantifies intrinsic myocardial damage underlying diastolic dysfunction, rather than merely capturing its hemodynamic manifestations. Impaired LV-GLS is observed across various myocardial diseases with heart failure and preserved LVEF and has been associated with worse clinical outcomes in HCM [3–5]. Previous studies have shown that LV-GLS is significantly reduced in HCM, with the lowest absolute strain values typically occurring in regions of maximal hypertrophy and fibrosis [6, 7]. Given the presence of myocardial hypertrophy and interstitial fibrosis [8], incorporating GLS into the assessment framework may offer incremental value in detecting subclinical myocardial impairment, refining the evaluation of LV diastolic dysfunction (LVDD), and improving risk stratification in patients with obstructive HCM, particularly when conventional diastolic parameters are inconclusive or pseudonormal. We hypothesize that GLS may aid in identifying patients at higher risk of progressive diastolic dysfunction or heart failure dysfunction rather than as a surrogate for LVEF.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 145 consecutive patients with symptomatic HOCM who had not responded adequately to medical therapy and were referred to our cardiac center for evaluation of septal reduction surgery between November 30, 2021, and July 31, 2023. All patients routinely underwent comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography and two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography as part of their diagnostic assessment. According to the 2020 AHA/ACC guideline for the diagnosis and management of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, HOCM is defined by a maximal end-diastolic LV wall thickness ≥ 15 mm in the absence of alternative causes of hypertrophy, or a septal thickness ≥ 13 mm when supported by a positive family history or pathogenic genetic findings, together with a resting or provocable left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) pressure gradient > 30 mmHg [9]. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Provincial People’s Hospital, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the investigation.

Clinical data

Baseline clinical data were retrieved from the institutional electronic medical record system and included medical history, physical examination findings, 12-lead electrocardiography, comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography, 24- or 48-hour Holter monitoring, laboratory test results, and New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification.

Echocardiography

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography and two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography (2D-STE) were performed according to the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography recommendations [10, 11]. All studies were acquired with a GE Vivid E95 system (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI, USA). For offline analysis, three consecutive cardiac cycles were recorded in cine-loop format from standard apical four-, three-, and two-chamber views. Left ventricular (LV) and left atrial (LA) dimensions were measured per guideline recommendations. LV hypertrophy was quantified by measuring wall thickness in 16 segments at three short-axis levels (basal, mid-cavity, apical) (Fig. 1); the mean wall thickness was calculated from these 16 segments. LVEF was derived by the biplane Simpson method. Maximal LA volume was measured using the biplane area-length method and indexed to body surface area.

Measurement of 16-segment thickness of the left ventricular wall at three short-axis levels (basal, mid-cavity, and apical)

Transmitral inflow velocities (E and A waves) were obtained by pulsed-wave Doppler, and mitral annular velocities (septal e′ and lateral e′) by tissue Doppler imaging. Continuous-wave Doppler of LV outflow tract (LVOT) flow was recorded from the apical five-chamber view to assess resting and provoked LVOT gradients. For patients with a resting gradient < 30 mmHg, provocation maneuvers comprised the Valsalva maneuver and repetitive squat-to-stand maneuvers (10–15 repetitions) to unmask latent obstruction. The peak instantaneous LVOT gradient was calculated using the modified Bernoulli equation (ΔP = 4 V²), and the highest value measured during rest or provocation was used for analysis [12, 13].

Patients were classified into two groups (grade II and grade I) following the 2016 ASE/EACVI diastolic function algorithm [10]. LV diastolic dysfunction was graded using mitral inflow, tissue Doppler, and left atrial indices. Grade I (impaired relaxation) was defined as reduced mitral annular e′ velocity (septal e′ < 7 cm/s, lateral e′ < 10 cm/s) with normal or low LV filling pressure, indicated by E/e′ ≤ 14, TR velocity < 2.8 m/s, and left atrial volume index (LAVI) ≤ 34 mL/m² (≥ 2 parameters within normal range). Grade II (pseudonormal pattern) required evidence of elevated filling pressure, characterized by ≥ 2 of the following abnormal findings: average E/e′ > 14, TR velocity > 2.8 m/s, and LAVI > 34 mL/m².

Strain analysis was performed offline using EchoPAC software (GE Healthcare) by investigators blinded to all clinical and echocardiographic diastolic function data. Optimal images were acquired during breath-hold for at least three consecutive cardiac cycles in standard apical three-, four-, and two-chamber views, with a frame rate of 60–80 frames/s [14]. LV global longitudinal strain (LV-GLS) was calculated as the mean peak longitudinal strain of all 17 LV segments (Fig. 2A). For statistical analysis, GLS values were expressed as absolute values.

Laboratory tests

Laboratory data were extracted from the institutional electronic medical record system, with particular emphasis on plasma brain-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and cardiac troponin I (cTnI). BNP, a well-validated biomarker of myocardial wall stress, is routinely assessed at our center. In accordance with established clinical cutoffs, BNP levels ≥ 119 pg/mL were categorized as elevated, whereas cTnI concentrations > 0.05 µg/L were interpreted as indicative of myocardial injury.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies with corresponding percentages. BNP and cTnI values were log-transformed to approximate normal distribution. Between-group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. To determine whether LV-GLS independently predicted diastolic dysfunction grade in HOCM, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, followed by univariate and multivariable logistic regression, was conducted. Correlations between LV-GLS and Doppler-derived echocardiographic indices of diastolic function were evaluated and illustrated using scatter plots. In addition, multivariable regression analyses were performed to examine the independent associations of LV-GLS with diastolic parameters, BNP, and cTnI after adjusting for loading conditions. All candidate variables were assessed using correlation matrices and variance inflation factors (VIFs). Variables demonstrating high collinearity (e.g., strong pairwise correlations or VIF > 5) were not entered simultaneously into the final model in order to avoid instability and overfitting. Instead, we selected the most clinically relevant and statistically robust representative variables for multivariable adjustment. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical data of the study population

A total of 223 patients who had not responded adequately to medical therapy and were referred to our cardiac center for evaluation of septal reduction surgery during the study period. After excluding individuals with a restrictive mitral inflow pattern (Grade III diastolic dysfunction), 198 patients were confirmed to have obstructive HCM based on a comprehensive echocardiographic assessment. Twenty-eight patients with persistent atrial fibrillation were excluded due to inadequate image quality for strain analysis. An additional 25 were excluded because of incomplete laboratory data, missing echocardiographic parameters required for diastolic evaluation (e’, E/e’, LAVI, or TR velocity), or lack of hospitalization records.

The final study cohort comprised 145 symptomatic patients who underwent standardized echocardiographic and Doppler-based diastolic function assessment according to the 2016 ASE guidelines. Of these, 109 patients were classified as having grade II diastolic dysfunction and 36 as grade I. Patients with grade II diastolic dysfunction were generally older, although the age difference did not reach statistical significance. Female sex was more common in the higher-grade diastolic dysfunction group. No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to physical examination findings, comorbid conditions, or ongoing medications (Table 1).

Echocardiographic characteristics and their associations with diastolic dysfunction

Patterns of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) differed significantly by diastolic dysfunction grade. Neutral-curve and diffuse hypertrophic morphologies were more frequently observed in patients with grade II diastolic dysfunction, whereas the sigmoid pattern predominated in grade I. Greater maximal and mean LV wall thickness, more severe mitral regurgitation (MR), reduced LV-GLS (Fig. 2B), and elevated BNP were all associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction. In contrast, loading conditions, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and cTnI showed no significant association with diastolic dysfunction severity (Table 2).

In stepwise forward multivariable regression analysis, female sex, septal e′, mean E/e′, LAVI, and LV-GLS emerged as independent predictors of diastolic dysfunction severity. Importantly, absolute LV-GLS remained independently and inversely associated with higher diastolic dysfunction grade (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.82–0.85, p = 0.018), underscoring its potential role as a sensitive marker of impaired diastolic performance.

Clinical and echocardiographic determinants of LV-GLS

In this cohort, age was not associated with LV-GLS, whereas female patients demonstrated relatively higher absolute GLS values (p = 0.017). LV-GLS showed no significant relationship with loading conditions—including LV end-diastolic and end-systolic volume indices, LAVI, or LVEF—nor with tricuspid regurgitation velocity or the vena contracta width of mitral regurgitation. In contrast, LV-GLS was negatively correlated with maximal and mean LV wall thickness, peak resting LVOT gradient, E/e′ ratio, BNP, and cTnI, and positively correlated with both septal and lateral e′ velocities (Table 3; Fig. 4).

Discussion

The main findings of this study include: GLS serves as a sensitive, load-independent biomarker for early myocardial dysfunction in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), showing the strongest correlation with the degree of diastolic dysfunction, rather than as a surrogate for LVEF. Secondly, GLS was significantly negatively correlated with surrogate markers of myocardial stiffness (left ventricular wall thickness and BNP), supporting its role as a non-invasive phenotypic indicator of the pathophysiology of potential fibrotic remodeling and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in HOCM. Thirdly, we found that a |GLS| threshold < 14.7% may help identify patients with severe diastolic dysfunction and may provide a clinically meaningful indicator for refined HFpEF assessment, risk stratification, and individualized management of symptomatic HOCM.

Generally, in patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and diastolic dysfunction are major determinants of symptoms and long-term cardiovascular outcomes. Accordingly, accurate grading of diastolic dysfunction is essential for risk stratification and individualized management. In this study of symptomatic HOCM patients with preserved LVEF, reduced left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LV-GLS) was independently associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction, even after adjustment for sex, loading conditions, and established echocardiographic indices of diastolic function. In addition, mean LV wall thickness and BNP levels emerged as independent correlates of LV-GLS. Given that both parameters reflect increased myocardial stiffness and elevated filling pressures [15, 16], our findings support the use of LV-GLS as a noninvasive, quantitative, and highly reproducible marker for evaluating diastolic dysfunction in HOCM.

Ito et al. reported that in patients with HFpEF, LV-GLS derived from cine cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) was the only independent predictor of the abnormal left ventricular relaxation index, Tau [17]. These results suggest that GLS may provide incremental value in the noninvasive assessment of LV diastolic properties. Similarly, LV-GLS measured by two-dimensional speckle-tracking echocardiography has been shown to independently predict adverse cardiovascular outcomes in HCM patients [5], particularly by reflecting impaired LV contractility. Notably, in HCM, diastolic stiffening often precedes overt systolic dysfunction. Therefore, early detection of increased myocardial stiffness using LV-GLS may facilitate risk stratification and guide targeted therapy in patients with HFpEF [18].

In the current cohort, absolute LV-GLS was significantly lower in patients with grade II diastolic dysfunction compared with grade I (12.9 ± 3.6% vs. 15.5 ± 3.4%, p = 0.001), with both values falling below the lower limit of normal (18%) [19]. In addition to septal e′, mean E/e′, and LAVI, female sex, maximal and mean LV wall thickness, ≥ moderate mitral regurgitation, and BNP were associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction. However, in stepwise forward multivariable regression analysis, only female sex, reduced septal e′, elevated mean E/e′, increased LAVI, and reduced LV-GLS remained independent predictors of higher diastolic dysfunction grades. Collectively, these findings suggest that LV-GLS provides incremental information for evaluating the severity of diastolic dysfunction, independent of sex, LV wall thickness, and conventional echocardiographic parameters (e′, E/e′, and LAVI). Receiver operating characteristic analysis further indicated that an absolute LV-GLS below 14.7% may identify patients with more severe diastolic dysfunction, supporting its utility in comprehensive diastolic assessment.

We further explored the relationships between LV-GLS and echocardiographic indices of diastolic function, along with serum biomarkers reflecting myocardial stress and injury. BNP, a natriuretic peptide released by cardiomyocytes, is a sensitive indicator of increased myocardial wall stress and stiffness—often outperforming conventional hemodynamic parameters in detecting these abnormalities [20, 21]. Elevated BNP levels have been linked to left ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial fibrosis, and a higher risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes in HCM patients [22, 23]. Cardiac troponin I (cTnI), a structural component of the sarcomeric contractile apparatus, serves as a highly sensitive marker of myocardial injury and predicts cardiovascular events and mortality [24]. Accordingly, we examined the associations of BNP and cTnI with myocardial diastolic performance in HOCM patients and assessed their relationship with LV-GLS.

In this cohort, LV-GLS was not associated with age, whereas higher absolute LV-GLS values were more frequently observed in female patients. LV-GLS correlated negatively with LV wall thickness, peak LVOT gradient, E/e′, BNP, and cTnI, and positively with septal and lateral e′ velocities. Stepwise multivariable regression analysis revealed that only mean LV wall thickness and BNP remained independently and inversely associated with LV-GLS, indicating that LV-GLS predominantly reflects intrinsic myocardial stiffness rather than loading conditions or other conventional diastolic indices. Given that both wall thickness and BNP are well-established markers of myocardial stiffness and fibrosis linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes [15, 22, 23], LV-GLS may function as a robust, noninvasive quantitative surrogate of myocardial stiffness and diastolic dysfunction in patients with HOCM. This concept is further supported by MRI evidence [25], which demonstrated that impaired GLS closely reflects the burden of myocardial fibrosis and predicts subsequent cardiac events in HCM patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. It was a retrospective, single-center study including only symptomatic obstructive HCM patients; the role of LV-GLS in non-obstructive HCM remains unclear. Patients with grade III diastolic dysfunction were excluded due to small numbers, limiting the evaluation of severe diastolic impairment. Diastolic function was assessed noninvasively via echocardiography and Doppler rather than catheterization, which may have affected classification accuracy. Additionally, the clinical utility of LV-GLS for risk stratification or outcome prediction was not assessed. Prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the incremental value of LV-GLS in HOCM.

Conclusion

In symptomatic HOCM patients with preserved LVEF, impaired LV-GLS was independently associated with more advanced diastolic dysfunction, beyond the contributions of wall thickness and conventional echocardiographic parameters. These findings suggest that LV-GLS captures subclinical myocardial impairment that is not fully reflected by standard indices and may therefore aid comprehensive assessment of diastolic function and risk stratification in patients with HFpEF due to HOCM. Incorporating LV-GLS into routine evaluation could help identify patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes and guide more individualized management. Future prospective studies are warranted to investigate the combined utility of LV-GLS and conventional diastolic parameters for optimizing risk prediction and therapeutic decision-making in this population.
