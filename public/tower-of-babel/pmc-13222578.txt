Management of HER2+ Breast Cancer with and without Brain Metastases in Saudi Arabia: Literature Insights and an Expert Survey of Current Practices
Authors: Sharif Kullab, Ahmed Refae, Abdullah Altwairgi, Atlal Abusanad, Shereef Elsamany, Nafisa A Abdelhafiez, Bassam Basulaiman, Amer Zahralliyali, Abdulraheem Ahmed Alshangiti, Mustafa Al-mubarak, Ahmed Alwbari, Aisha Alharbi, Ahmed Osman, Hesham Kiwan, Sabah Alaklabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeNew therapies can improve outcomes in patients with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+) metastatic breast cancer (mBC), including those with brain metastases (BMs). To our knowledge this is the first review focusing on HER2+ mBC, with and without BMs, in the Saudi Arabian context. This review evaluated the epidemiology of HER2+ mBC with and without BMs, treatment and monitoring practices in Saudi Arabia.MethodsTwo key strategies have been pursued: a comprehensive literature review of international recommendations to complement a focused, nationally-representative cross-sectional survey involving BC experts in Saudi Arabia using an 18-item questionnaire.ResultsTwelve expert BC physicians from tertiary treatment centers in Saudi Arabia completed the survey. It was estimated that 15–35% of mBC patients present with HER2+ disease, with 5–25% developing BMs. Internationally, brain imaging of asymptomatic patients is currently a subject of controversial debate; only 17% of surveyed physicians conducted brain imaging in asymptomatic patients. Treatment of HER2+ mBC with and without BMs in Saudi Arabia largely aligned with international recommendations identified in our literature analysis, although access to lapatinib, tucatinib and neratinib can be limited and may lead to use of alternative regimens. In third-line or later settings, tucatinib combinations were favored by physicians.ConclusionThe management of HER2+ mBC with and without BMs among expert physicians in Saudi Arabia is aligned with international guidelines. The tucatinib-combination may be considered the standard of care for adult patients with HER2+ locally advanced or mBC who have received at least two prior anti-HER2+ treatment regimens, including patients with BMs. Ensuring access to innovative HER2+ mBC therapies across Saudi Arabia is crucial to supporting best practice.

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most commonly diagnosed cancer globally and is estimated to affect more than 2.3 million women and account for 670,000 deaths annually.1 In Saudi Arabia, BC accounts for 30.9% of all cancers affecting women, with over 2,400 cases diagnosed annually and over 1,100 deaths attributed to BC annually.2,3 Multiple factors influence the prognosis of BC, including the stage at diagnosis and the molecular profile of the tumor.4 For instance, metastatic BC (mBC) is associated with a lower survival rate than earlier stages of the disease.5 The molecular profile of mBC is also an important factor that influences prognosis and treatment options.5

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive disease (HER2+) is seen in approximately 15–20% of cases of mBC globally,6 with a report from Saudi Arabia suggesting HER2+ disease may be seen in 29.9% of BC cases.7 This subtype of mBC is characterized by overexpression of HER2 and has historically been associated with chemotherapy resistance, aggressive disease profile and poor prognosis, with high rates of mortality and recurrence.5 The introduction of HER2-targeted therapies, such trastuzumab in combination with chemotherapy, has significantly improved survival in this group of patients.8 Additional HER2-targeted therapies have shown survival benefits in HER2+ mBC, revolutionizing the treatment paradigm.8 However, treatment resistance is a recognized challenge in applying HER2-targeted therapies. In addition, the optimal sequencing of these therapies is yet to be determined as new clinical trial data and treatment options emerge, complicating treatment decision-making.9–12

Despite improvement in survival for patients with HER2+ mBC with targeted therapies, brain metastases (BMs) are a common complication of the disease and pose a significant risk of morbidity and mortality.13 The management of patients with HER2+ mBC and BMs is a challenge in practice and their prevention represents an important unmet medical need. Many trials with HER2-targeted agents in patients with HER2+ mBC excluded patients with BMs, although emerging data suggest that these agents may have a certain level of efficacy in this patient group.14–17 Therefore, key challenges remain in overcoming therapeutic resistance, optimizing treatment sequencing as well as treating and ultimately preventing BMs in patients with HER2+ mBC. To address these key challenges there is an important need to consider the published literature and emerging trials, as well as the local context of care.

The focus of the present review was on clinical care of HER2+ mBC in Saudi Arabia. The amount of literature on HER2+ mBC management, with and without BMs, in Saudi Arabia is limited, which may have implications for applying international guidelines and recommendations to the local setting. This paper provides insights into contemporary studies and guidelines from the international literature and explores local experience and clinical challenges seen in HER2+ mBC management in the Saudi Arabian context.

Materials and Methods

This paper combined a comprehensive literature review with a national survey of BC experts in Saudi Arabia to provide insights into local experience in the management of HER2+ mBC, with and without BMs. The literature review serves to complement and contextualize expert‑reported local experience.

A literature search was completed using online databases (Cochrane Library, PubMed and Scopus) to identify studies focused on the management of HER2+ mBC with and without BMs. The search strategy focused on primary and secondary peer-reviewed articles published in English from December 2019 to December 2024, including human trial data and guidelines on HER2+ mBC management.

The literature review served as the foundation for the survey design, ensuring that the questions were aligned with evidence-based themes and relevant to local clinical realities and to assess local clinical practice of physicians in Saudi Arabia. Two key objectives were defined as priorities in the Saudi Arabian setting: (1) To document local experience in treatment of HER2+ mBC in patients with and without brain metastases in Saudi Arabia and (2) To understand treatment sequencing strategies in Saudi Arabia in the context of international guidelines for these patient groups.

An 18-item survey comprised three key sections, each derived from insights gathered during the literature analysis. The first explored physicians’ local experience managing HER2+ mBC, as well as challenges in its management. The second addressed monitoring practices, and the third focused on treatment history, capturing sequencing strategies and rationale for therapeutic choices (Supplementary Appendix A). To ensure relevance and robustness, the survey was validated by experts. The survey was developed with 18 items on the Survey Monkey platform, with a link provided to 12 expert participants to complete the survey online (Supplementary Appendix A). All participants were selected based on their expertise in BC care, currently practicing in Saudi Arabia, and were considered representative of BC experts across Saudi Arabia based on practice across geographic regions, healthcare institutions, and clinical practice settings. All experts who completed the survey are authors of this paper.

The following sections present the findings of the exploratory literature analysis and regional clinical insights from BC experts in Saudi Arabia, including available treatment options, monitoring practices, and local experience and challenges in the care of patients with HER2+ mBC.

Results

Epidemiology of HER2+ mBC

Survey Findings

Overall, 91.7% of respondents (n=11) reported that most patients with mBC had a performance status of ≤1. Among these, the majority (83.3%; n=10) indicated that a performance status of 1 was the most commonly observed. Survey responses indicated that approximately 60–75% of patients with mBC present with HR+ HER2-negative (HER2-) disease, 15–35% with HER2+ disease, and 5–20% with triple-negative BC (TNBC). The presence of BMs during the course of the disease was estimated at 5–25% (mean 9.6%) in patients presenting with mBC. Among patients with mBC and BMs at presentation, HR+ HER2- disease was estimated in 1–30% (mean 15.0%), HER2+ disease in 10–70% (mean 42.4%) and TNBC in 5–40% (mean 23.2%). Patients were reported to have upfront BMs in 1–30% (mean 11.7%) of cases at presentation of metastatic disease or BMs during the metastatic course in 50–95% (mean 66.5%) of cases.

Literature Analysis

International data on the epidemiology and presentation of HER2+ mBC highlight some key features of patients and disease characteristics. At the time of diagnosis, 30% of patients with mBC have a performance status of >1, which is associated with poorer prognosis.18 HER2+ mBC is reported to comprise 15–20% of cases of mBC,19 while BMs have been reported to occur in 20–40% of cases of mBC during the course of the disease.4 It is estimated that BMs are more common in HER2+ mBC disease and occur in approximately 30–50% of cases.4,20–22 The incidence of BMs associated with HER2+ mBC may be higher due to prolongation of survival time with the use of newer HER2-targeted agents.8

Published data on BC cases in Saudi Arabia suggest that mBC accounts for 27.7% of cases at the time of diagnosis.23 The same study found 6.8% of patients with mBC presented with BMs at time of diagnosis.23 In a separate study, HER2 overexpression was identified in 29.9% of all BC cases, and among patients with HER2+ disease, 13.1% were diagnosed with mBC.7

In patients with HER2+ mBC with BMs, first-line treatment preferences were consistent with those in patients without BMs (docetaxel + trastuzumab + pertuzumab) (Table 1).

Literature Analysis

Current international guidelines recommend that treatment of HER2+ mBC is guided by the wider molecular profiling of the tumor, including hormone receptor (HR) status, and the patient suitability for ChT.25

In HR+/HER2+ mBC, patients eligible for ChT should receive docetaxel (or paclitaxel) + trastuzumab-pertuzumab followed by trastuzumab-pertuzumab-endocrine therapy until disease progression.25 The addition of pertuzumab to docetaxel and trastuzumab is supported by a >6 months improvement in median progression-free survival (PFS) reported in the CLEOPATRA trial with the triple regimen;26 an 8-year follow-up reported a 16.3 month improvement in median overall survival (OS) when pertuzumab is added to docetaxel-trastuzumab.24 Where ChT is contraindicated, the use of trastuzumab (± pertuzumab) and endocrine therapy should be considered, which is supported by data from the PERTAIN trial where pertuzumab plus trastuzumab and aromatase inhibitor therapy was effective in patients with HR+/HER2+ mBC.25,27,28

In patients with HR-negative/HER2+ mBC, ChT may be considered in eligible patients in combination with pertuzumab-trastuzumab.25 Guidelines support the use of taxanes, including docetaxel in this combination.25 If docetaxel is used in the ChT regimen, dosing should be consistent with that reported in the CLEOPATRA trial,29 with docetaxel dosing at 75 mg/m2 every 3 weeks [Q3W] for at least six cycles (fewer cycles if disease progression or unmanageable toxic effects, and more cycles were allowed at the discretion of the investigator or patient), with dose escalation to 100 mg/m2 Q3W if well tolerated and dose reduction by 25% if myelosuppression, hepatic dysfunction, or other toxic effects occurred.29

Guideline recommendations do not differentiate between first-line treatment options for HER2+ mBC patients with or without BMs.25

Trastuzumab shows a low level of penetration across the blood-brain-barrier (BBB) but this may be increased in certain conditions impairing the integrity of the BBB, including following radiotherapy.30 Data suggest trastuzumab is associated with improved OS and disease control in patients with BMs, with no benefit to switching therapies.8,31 Trastuzumab monotherapy has shown some survival benefits in this population, while trastuzumab plus pertuzumab is also associated with survival benefits.32

Second-Line and Beyond Treatment of HER2+ mBC

Survey Findings

In patients without BMs, second-line treatment preference was T-DXd for 91.7% (n=11) of respondents and one instance of tucatinib + trastuzumab + capecitabine preference (Table 1).

Second-line treatment options in patients with BMs were largely consistent with those reported in patients without BMs, although a stronger preference for the tucatinib-combination was noted.

In patients without BMs, third-line and beyond treatment options included a range of systemic therapies, including the tucatinib-combination, T-DXd (if not previously given in second line), T-DM1 and other combination chemotherapy/anti-HER2 regimens.

In patients with BMs, third-line and beyond treatment preferences were dominated by T-DXd, T-DM1 and the tucatinib-combination.

Literature Analysis

In patients without BMs and those with unknown or stable BMs, the use of T-DXd is recommended.25,33 The DESTINY-Breast-03 trial supported the use of T-DXd in patients who have received a taxane and trastuzumab in an advanced disease setting, with an improvement in PFS (hazard ratio 0.28; P<0.001) compared with T-DM1.34

The presence of BMs is a significant driver of second-line treatment options in patients with HER2+ mBC. Active BMs should be managed with local interventions, where indicated. Local interventions include resection, stereotactic radiotherapy, whole brain radiotherapy, or combined surgery and radiotherapy depending on the number and size of metastases and presence of unfavorable prognostic factors.25 Where local intervention is not indicated, systemic therapy with tucatinib-capecitabine-trastuzumab or T-DXd is recommended.25 The HER2CLIMB trial supports the use of tucatinib-capecitabine-trastuzumab, demonstrating PFS and OS benefits in patients with active or stable BMs.35

In the third-line treatment context, in patients without BMs and those with unknown or stable BMs, the use of tucatinib-capecitabine-trastuzumab, T-DXd or T-DM1 may be considered.25 This may be followed by lapatinib-capecitabine, lapatinib-trastuzumab, trastuzumab-ChT, margetuximab-ChT or neratinib-capecitabine combinations.25 The optimal sequence of antibody-drug conjugates has not been completely established, although data from the DESTINY-Breast 01 trial showed that T-DXd was efficacious in patients with HER2+ mBC previously treated with T-DM1.36 In contrast, data on the alternative sequence of T-DXd followed by T-DM1 are limited.37 In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials, treatment choice should be based on prior second-line therapy, toxicity profile, patient characteristics and availability of agents.25 A summary of guidelines on overall treatment sequencing from first-line to later lines of therapy in patients with mBC (without BMs) is presented in Table 1.

Third-line and beyond treatment options for active BMs include local interventions, where indicated. Where local interventions are not indicated, systemic therapy with tucatinib-capecitabine-trastuzumab or T-DXd (if not previously used) is recommended. This may be followed by lapatinib-capecitabine, lapatinib-trastuzumab, trastuzumab-ChT, margetuximab-ChT or neratinib-capecitabine combinations. In the absence of head-to-head clinical trials, treatment choice should be based on prior second-line therapy, toxicity profile, patient characteristics and availability of agents.25

T-DM1 does not easily cross the BBB unless it is disrupted; T-DM1 is associated with prolonged OS in the third-line setting but is not superior to trastuzumab- or lapatinib-containing therapies.38 Lapatinib can penetrate the BBB and is associated with an objective response (BM volume reduction) of up to 66% when combined with capecitabine for first-line mBC BM therapy.39 However, a pooled meta-analysis of 12 studies (799 patients) found an ORR of 29.2% in patients HER2+ mBC with BMs treated with lapatinib + capecitabine, suggesting heterogeneity in the treatment response.40 Neratinib-capecitabine showed PFS benefits in one trial compared with ChT.41

Monitoring

Survey Findings

A summary of the survey responses on patient monitoring strategies is presented in Table 1. In patients with HER2+ mBC without BMs, 100% (n=12) of survey respondents perform local examination routinely. Commonly employed investigations included imaging with digital breast tomosynthesis (mammography) (83.3%; n=10), positron emission tomography (PET) imaging (58.33%; n=7) and echocardiogram (100%; n=12). The use of brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was less frequently reported (33.3%; n=4) and local imaging (eg mammography or ultrasound) was routine in only 8.3% (n=1) of respondents.

Regular brain imaging in asymptomatic patients without BMs at presentation was reported by 16.7% (n=2) of respondents, with the majority not regularly performing brain imaging in these patients (83.3%; n=10). Where routine brain imaging was performed, a computed tomography (CT) or MRI brain scan was preferred equally by the two respondents.

In patients with BMs at presentation, 100% (n=12) of respondents performed local examinations, brain imaging and echocardiography. Regular brain imaging was advocated in 58.3% (n=7) of respondents. Systematic CT imaging was performed routinely by 83.3% (n=10), PET imaging by 50.0% (n=6), multi-gated radionuclide angiography (MUGA) in 25.0% (n=3) and local mammography/ultrasound by 8.3% (n=1).

Literature Analysis

Guidelines42 advocate neurological testing and imaging in cancer patients with new symptoms suggestive of BMs. MRI is preferred to CT and PET due to higher sensitivity for BM detection.42

Symptomatic patients should receive brain imaging to rule out the presence of BMs, with a preference for brain MRI in European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) guidelines.22 Brain imaging is not routinely recommended in asymptomatic patients at initial mBC diagnosis or during disease monitoring to detect BMs.25,42 American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) guidelines suggest a low threshold for MRI use if any neurological symptoms develop.43 However, due to the high rate of BMs in patients with the HER2+ disease subtype, specific brain imaging may be considered in asymptomatic patients if detection of BMs would alter treatment decisions.42,44 Clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate the potential benefit of proactive screening in high-risk patients (NCT03881605, NCT03617341, NCT04030507).

In patients with BMs, monitoring is crucial to guide treatment decision-making, with guidelines advocating continuation of HER2-targeted therapy where CNS progression occurs with stable extra-CNS disease. However, switching to the next line of therapy if CNS and extra-CNS progression occurs is preferred.25,43 A recent consensus report supports the use of contrast-enhanced brain MRI for monitoring of patients with pre-existing BMs in preference to contrast-enhanced brain CT, consistent with current guidelines.25,44 Serial MRI imaging every 2–4 months has been recommended.43

Local Experiences and Challenges in Saudi Arabia

The survey findings highlighted some features of care provision in Saudi Arabia which are notable. Firstly, availability of imaging and drugs were considered important. The availability of brain imaging, including brain MRI, was high with 100% of respondents noting this was available in practice. However, logistical issues with imaging were seen among four respondents, with seven reporting no such issues; one respondent did not answer this question. Assessment of drug availability showed that 100% of respondents had access to trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1, T-DXd and capecitabine. Access was more limited for lapatinib and tucatinib with 81.8% (n=9) reporting availability of these drugs, while none of the respondents had access to neratinib at the time the survey was conducted.

Secondly, guidelines followed in practice included international guidelines for 100% of respondents. International guidelines followed included those published by the ASCO, the ESMO and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN). Discussion

This review provides an important insight into contemporary approaches to care of patients with HER2+ BC in Saudi Arabia, based on the results of a national survey. Overall, the findings suggest that HER2+ BC is seen in 15–35% of patients with BC in Saudi Arabia, which is broadly consistent with international data.19 The proportion of patients with mBC presenting with BMs is relatively low in Saudi Arabia, with a mean value of 9.6% noted in this survey. This is consistent with previous data from Saudi Arabia.4 However, it is anticipated that up to 40% of patients with HER2+ mBC may experience BMs during the course of their disease,22 which emphasizes the importance of specific treatment approaches in this population.23 Consistent with international data, survey findings from Saudi Arabia suggest that patients with HER2+ mBC frequently have a performance status ≤1 at the time of presentation.18

First-line treatment options reported in the survey largely aligned with international guidelines in patients with or without BMs (Table 3).25 Similarly, second-line, third-line and beyond treatment preferences also largely aligned with international guidelines in these patient groups (Table 3).25 However, some deviation from international guidelines was noted in Saudi Arabian practice, which potentially results from the limited availability of some agents for managing HER2+ mBC with and without BMs. The findings of this survey suggested that availability of HER2-targeted therapies was generally high among oncologists managing patients with mBC in different private and governmental settings in Saudi Arabia. However, access issues were noted for lapatinib, tucatinib and neratinib, suggesting unmet needs in this area, despite approval of these agents in Saudi Arabia (Figure 1). The survey respondents routinely used both T-DM1 and T-DXd in the management of HER2+ mBC. Lack of availability and access to HER2-targeted therapies for mBC remains an important barrier to treatment in many regions. A survey of 191 oncologists across nations conducted in 2021, highlighted that those in low- and middle-income nations have poor access to HER2 testing, which is often only available in the private sector.45 Trastuzumab was not available for 8% of those surveyed, while only 10% of providers could justify the cost of this treatment option.45 Guidelines stratified by resource availability may have value in guiding optimal treatment selection in instances of poor access to therapies.46 This may be considered in Saudi Arabia, pending approval or availability of new and emerging therapies.

Importantly, this survey has a number of limitations that may impact the application of the findings to practice. Firstly, the survey was completed using a small sample of physicians in Saudi Arabia and therefore may not reflect the experiences of physicians across the region. Secondly, the survey demonstrated variability in some of the responses, including drug availability across Saudi Arabia. Finally, the limited data on HER2+ mBC management in Saudi Arabia, including patients with or without BMs, limits the potential to compare these findings to other studies.

Additional areas for consideration include the potential for optimizing HER2+ mBC care in Saudi Arabia, including the adoption of new guidance and recommendations based on emerging research evidence. For instance, there is interest in the role of HER2-targeted therapies in HER2-low disease47 and this may be an area for expansion of HER2-targeted therapy use in the future. Identification of patients eligible for T-DXd may also be an important focus for optimizing deployment of available agents and could be based on repeated biopsies after therapy.47 There is a need to ensure that new and emerging therapies can be effectively integrated in the treatment paradigm, when available. For instance, the potential for bispecific antibodies to enhance outcomes compared to conventional monoclonal antibodies (building on combination therapy of trastuzumab and pertuzumab) and to prevent BMs is being realized and represents a potential paradigm shift.48,49 Physicians in Saudi Arabia should be prepared for such changes and be aware of the importance of maintaining guideline-based practice as new drugs and approaches are approved.

Conclusion

This study provides unique insights into expert practice in the management of HER2+ mBC with and without BMs in Saudi Arabia. HER2-targeted therapies have revolutionized the treatment paradigm in HER2+ mBC. While agents show benefits in improving survival, challenges remain in their optimal use including the potential for resistance, difficulty in defining optimal treatment sequencing and effective prevention and management of BMs. Several agents are recommended for the management of HER2+ mBC with BMs, reflecting positive trial data. However, experts note that not all agents are widely available in Saudi Arabia, with data suggesting lapatinib, tucatinib and neratinib may not be routinely used. The survey findings also suggest that imaging for the diagnosis and monitoring of BMs may also be limited by local resource constraints. Tucatinib, due to its demonstrated efficacy and its availability, should be considered as a standard of care in the third-line setting for HER2+ BC, particularly for patients with BMs. In Saudi Arabia, more needs to be done to increase the availability and effective use of tucatinib, which is a crucial drug for use in combination therapy to manage patients with HER2+ mBC with or without BMs. Similarly, efforts to increase drug availability and uptake in practice, to align care with international guidelines, is recommended for other new and emerging treatment options. The survey findings suggest that there is a need to improve access to effective imaging studies in Saudi Arabia to ensure BC care is consistent with international standards. Further studies exploring these issues across regional BC care would be valuable to provide further representative insights into specialist BC care in Saudi Arabia.
