Effect of intravenous alteplase before endovascular therapy for atherothrombotic stroke-related large vessel occlusion: subanalysis of the RESCUE AT-LVO registry
Authors: Hirotaka Hayashi, Satoshi Namitome, Seigo Shindo, Shinichi Yoshimura, Manabu Shirakawa, Mikiya Beppu, Nobuyuki Sakai, Hiroshi Yamagami, Kazutaka Uchida, Kazunori Toyoda, Yuji Matsumaru, Yasushi Matsumoto, Kenichi Todo, Mikito Hayakawa, Shinzo Ota, Masafumi Morimoto, Masataka Takeuchi, Hirotoshi Imamura, Hiroyuki Ikeda, Kanta Tanaka, Hideyuki Ishihara, Hiroto Kakita, Takanori Sano, Hayato Araki, Tatsufumi Nomura, Fumihiro Sakakibara, Mitsuharu Ueda, Makoto Nakajima
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) should be administered prior to endovascular therapy (EVT) in patients with atherothrombotic stroke-related large vessel occlusion (AT-LVO) remains unclear. This study aimed to assess the efficacy and safety of IVT administered before EVT in this patient population.MethodsWe analysed the data from a multicentre registry of patients who underwent EVT for AT-LVO. Patients were categorised based on presumed mechanism of occlusion: in situ occlusion (intracranial group) or embolism from cervical artery occlusion/stenosis (tandem group). We compared the efficacy and safety of IVT before EVT in patients who received IVT (IVT stratum) and those who did not (non-IVT stratum). The primary outcome was a modified Rankin Scale score of 0–2 at 90 days.ResultsAmong the 336 patients in the intracranial group, 99 patients underwent IVT. The rate of favourable functional outcomes did not differ between IVT and non-IVT strata (51.1% vs 47.6%; adjusted ORs (aORs) (95% CI), 1.18 (0.66 to 2.09)); whereas any intracranial haemorrhage (ICH) (10.1% vs 3.8%; aOR, 2.98 (1.01 to 9.26)) and mortality at 90 days (6.4% vs 1.3%; aOR, 4.66 (1.02 to 26.73)) were significantly higher in the IVT stratum. Among the 233 patients in the tandem group, 88 patients underwent IVT, with no significant differences in efficacy or safety outcomes between the strata.ConclusionIn patients with AT-LVO, IVT before EVT did not improve outcomes and was associated with increased risk of ICH and mortality in those with in situ intracranial occlusion. IVT before EVT may not be recommended in patients with atherosclerotic intracranial occlusions.

Introduction

Endovascular therapy (EVT) has become an established treatment for acute large vessel occlusion (LVO), and its indications have been expanding in recent years.13 Atherosclerosis is the second most common cause of LVO, and ischaemic stroke caused by atherosclerotic intracranial arterial stenosis is more prevalent in Asians compared with other groups.4 Approximately 15% of EVT are performed for atherothrombotic stroke-related LVO (AT-LVO) in Japan.5

Several studies have compared the effectiveness of EVT for LVO caused by intracranial atherosclerosis to that caused by other mechanisms. Some studies have reported comparable functional outcomes and rates of intracranial haemorrhage (ICH) between these groups.6 7 Meanwhile, other studies have shown that patients with intracranial atherosclerosis have poorer outcomes,8 and the optimal treatment strategy for AT-LVO remains unclear.

Intravenous thrombolysis (IVT) with alteplase is a well-established treatment for acute ischaemic stroke within 4.5 hour of onset.9 However, it remains unclear which patients with LVO should receive IVT before EVT and which may benefit from skipping it. Randomised clinical trials comparing EVT alone versus IVT followed by EVT have produced inconsistent findings. Some studies have shown EVT alone to be non-inferior,10 11 while others have failed to confirm this equivalence.1215 Additionally, these clinical trials did not stratify patients by stroke mechanism, such as cardioembolism or large artery atherosclerosis.

Although IVT alone has shown benefit in atherothrombotic stroke without EVT,16 few studies have examined the role of IVT before EVT in patients with AT-LVO. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no studies have examined the efficacy and safety of IVT prior to EVT in cases of in situ intracranial artery occlusion. Recently, we reported the result of a post hoc study using a nationwide registry of patients with AT-LVO who underwent EVT.17 The present study aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of IVT prior to EVT in these patients, with a focus on the underlying stroke mechanism.

Methods

Data collection and definitions

This was a subanalysis of the RESCUE AT-LVO (The Recovery by Endovascular Salvage for Cerebral Ultra-acute Embolic and Atherothrombotic Stroke with Large Vessel Occlusion) registry, a multicentre registry conducted at 51 hospitals across Japan.17 Patients enrolled between January 2017 and December 2019 had acute intracranial vascular occlusions due to intracranial or extracranial carotid artery sclerosis. AT-LVO was identified based on angiographic findings during EVT. These included ≥50% residual stenosis on final angiography or <50% residual stenosis in combination with a tapered occlusion or flow impairment. Patients were categorised into two groups based on the presumed mechanism of occlusion: (1) intracranial group–in situ intracranial artery occlusion and (2) tandem group–embolic intracranial occlusion originating from cervical artery occlusion or stenosis. The tandem group also included patients with in situ stenosis of the intracranial artery, which was occluded due to embolism from plaques of the carotid artery.

Patients were eligible if they fulfilled all of the following conditions: (1) diagnosis of acute ischaemic stroke due to LVO; (2) EVT performed for intracranial occlusion; (3) final diagnosis confirmed as atherosclerotic disease; (4) occlusion located in either the intracranial carotid artery or M1/M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery (intracranial group), or cervical artery occlusion or stenosis accompanied by ipsilateral intracranial artery occlusion (tandem group). Patients were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) arterial stenosis due to non-atherosclerotic aetiologies, such as arterial dissection, vasculitis or Moyamoya disease; (2) multiple acute infarcts involving different vascular territories, unless attributable to artery-to-artery embolism from cervical artery pathology; (3) EVT performed exclusively for cervical lesions without intracranial involvement; (4) posterior circulation occlusions; (5) a premorbid modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥3.

To evaluate the effect of IVT in both the intracranial and tandem groups, patients were categorised based on whether they received IVT.

Interventions

The EVT procedure was determined by the attending physicians and included the use of a stent retriever, aspiration catheter, balloon angioplasty, stenting, mechanical clot disruption and intracranial fibrinolysis. For patients who received intravenous alteplase, the dosage was 0.6 mg/kg of body weight, in accordance with national guidelines in Japan.18 Administration of IVT was also left to the clinical judgement of the treating physician.

Measurements

Demographic and clinical data collected included age, sex, medical history, smoking status, prior medications and pretreatment National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score. Imaging data and time metrics were also recorded. Use of IVT and EVT details were documented for all patients. Neuroimaging was performed using either CT or MRI. Ischaemic volumes and occluded vessels were evaluated at admission using the Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) on non-contrast CT or diffusion-weighted MRI. Reperfusion status following EVT was evaluated by a physician based on the modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) grading system.

Follow-up imaging (CT or MRI) was routinely conducted 24±8 hours after EVT. Any ICH was defined as any new ICH identified on follow-up imaging, regardless of clinical symptoms. Symptomatic ICH was defined as a neurological deterioration of ≥4 points on the NIHSS score. Reocclusion was evaluated based on digital subtraction angiography or magnetic resonance angiography.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was favourable functional status at 90 days after EVT, defined as an mRS score of 0–2. Secondary outcomes included the achievement of successful reperfusion, defined as mTICI score ≥2b, or excellent reperfusion (mTICI 2c/3) immediately after EVT. The incidence of reocclusion within 90 days after EVT was also recorded. Safety outcomes consisted of the occurrence of any ICH, symptomatic ICH and mortality within 90 days of EVT.

Statistical analysis

As this study used an existing database from a registry-based study, the sample size was determined using the available data. Therefore, a prior sample size calculation was not performed. The patient characteristics were compared and described based on whether they received IVT (IVT stratum) or not (non-IVT stratum), within both the intracranial and tandem groups. Continuous variables were reported as mean±SD or median with IQR, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

To examine the effect of IVT on clinical outcomes, logistic regression models were constructed to calculate the ORs and 95% CIs. Multivariable analyses were conducted to adjust for potential confounding factors, including age, sex, NIHSS score, ASPECTS and onset-to-puncture time. These variables were selected based on their known impact on clinical outcomes, as supported by previous studies.1923 All statistical analyses were performed with JMP V.10.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Reported p values were two-tailed and p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

No significant differences were observed in successful (mTICI≥2b) or excellent (mTICI 2c/3) reperfusion rates, or in reocclusion incidence (82.8% vs 81.9%, aOR (95% CI) 1.04 (0.53 to 2.08); 56.6% vs 62.5%, aOR (95% CI) 0.82 (0.48 to 1.40); 18.2% vs 16.0%, aOR (95% CI) 1.05 (0.53 to 2.06)). In contrast, the IVT stratum had higher rates of any ICH (10.1% vs 3.8%, aOR (95% CI), 2.98 (1.01 to 9.26)) and symptomatic ICH (4.0% vs 0.4%, crude OR (95% CI), 9.94 (1.01 to 90.06)). However, because of the limited number of events, the statistical power was insufficient to calculate the adjusted OR for symptomatic ICH. Mortality at 90 days was also significantly higher in the IVT stratum (6.4% vs 1.3%, aOR (95% CI), 4.66 (1.02 to 26.73)).

Tandem group

In the tandem group, the rate of favourable outcome (mRS 0–2 at 90 days) did not significantly differ between the IVT and non-IVT strata (41.7% vs 39.7%, aOR (95% CI), 1.15 (0.59 to 2.25)) (table 3).

Similarly, there were no significant differences in the rates of successful reperfusion (mTICI≥2b), excellent reperfusion (mTICI 2c/3) or reocclusion between the IVT and non-IVT strata (94.3% vs 93.1%, aOR (95% CI) 1.46 (0.43 to 5.34); 56.8% vs 55.9%, aOR (95% CI) 1.08 (0.60 to 1.94); 9.1% vs 3.5%, aOR (95% CI) 3.10 (0.84 to 13.22)). The occurrence of safety outcomes, including any ICH, symptomatic ICH and mortality at 90 days, was also comparable between the IVT and non-IVT strata (12.5% vs 11.0%, aOR (95% CI) 1.14 (0.47 to 2.70); 6.8% vs 5.5%, aOR (95% CI) 1.32 (0.40 to 4.28); 3.5% vs 4.4%, aOR (95% CI) 0.66 (0.12 to 3.08)).

Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical impact of administering IVT prior to EVT in patients with AT-LVO. The findings suggest that IVT before EVT did not improve favourable outcomes at 90 days in either the intracranial or tandem groups. Furthermore, in patients with in situ intracranial occlusion, IVT was associated with an increased risk of adverse events, including any ICH, symptomatic ICH and mortality at 90 days. These results indicated that IVT may not offer additional benefits in this subgroup.

IVT has the efficacy of dissolving thrombi, but it also carries an increased risk of haemorrhage, creating a trade-off between its potential benefits and risks. A meta-analysis of six randomised controlled trials comparing EVT alone versus bridging therapy with IVT has failed to demonstrate the non-inferiority of EVT monotherapy: functional outcomes were often similar between the two treatment strategies.24 Notably, patients receiving IVT were more likely to develop ICH. This risk appears to be heightened in those with a large ischaemic core, where the benefit of IVT is limited and the likelihood of symptomatic ICH may increase.25

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare EVT with and without IVT in patients with in situ intracranial artery occlusion. Our findings indicated that IVT before EVT does not confer a clinical advantage in this population and may instead elevate the risk of haemorrhagic complications. These findings suggest that IVT prior to EVT may not be beneficial for patients with atherosclerotic intracranial occlusion.

Several factors could explain these results. First, patients with LVO due to intracranial atherosclerosis require significantly longer procedure time and often have additional complexities, such as the need for rescue therapy.26 This is likely due to the high frequency of reocclusion in such cases. In LVO caused by intracranial atherosclerosis, arterial occlusion is primarily attributed to in-situ occlusion following the rupture of unstable plaque.27 The use of EVT devices in these cases may further damage inflamed plaques, leading to enhanced platelet activation, which in turn increases the risk of reocclusion due to platelet-rich thrombi or even arterial dissection.27 Notably, stent retrievers, which were frequently used in this study, have been reported to cause endothelial injury in the target artery,28 potentially exacerbating the risk of plaque damage. Moreover, repeated reocclusion may necessitate more complex interventions using multiple procedures such as percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) and stenting. These procedures tend to be performed more frequently in patients with in-situ occlusion than those with embolism from cervical arteries for the treatment of intracranial occlusions,17 which could lead to complications including ICH. Indeed, Beppu et al29 reported that the frequency of intraoperative complications was twice as high in the PTA group and three times as high in the stenting group as in the mechanical thrombectomy-only group.

Another possible reason for the higher incidence of ICH in the IVT stratum than in the other is the frequent concomitant use of antiplatelet agents. Although several reports have explored the safety of combining IVT and antiplatelet therapy,30 31 no definitive conclusion has been reached regarding whether this combination increases the risk of ICH. However, theoretically, the risk of ICH could be elevated because antiplatelet agents inhibit platelet aggregation and prevent thrombus formation, while IVT dissolves clots. In the intracranial group, patients who underwent PTA or stenting, which are associated with a higher risk of complications, were more likely to receive additional antiplatelet therapy.29 Particularly, in intracranial arteries, which lack an internal elastic lamina, the combination of IVT and antiplatelet therapy may further increase the risk of haemorrhagic complications in such cases.

In contrast, IVT was not associated with clinical outcomes in the tandem group. Previous studies which investigated the relationship between tandem occlusion and IVT have suggested that IVT was not associated with improvement of clinical outcomes.32 33 One study even found no association between IVT and symptomatic ICH or mortality at 90 days in cases where carotid artery stenting plus EVT was performed for intracranial occlusions.33 The findings of our study in the tandem group were consistent with these previous reports. The lack of effectiveness of IVT could be attributed to the fact that many tandem lesions are caused by atherothrombotic disease of the carotid artery with a significant thrombus burden, which is known to predict a poor recanalisation rate after IVT.34 35 Additionally, cervical internal carotid artery stenosis or occlusion can lead to reduced perfusion and impair the delivery of IVT to the thrombus in the anterior circulation. This may explain why IVT did not affect favourable or safe outcomes in the tandem group.36

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, there was no standardised EVT protocol in place that specified balloon and stent retriever sizes, lengths or perioperative antiplatelet therapy, leading to variability based on individual physicians’ judgement. Second, since this was a subanalysis of an observational and non-randomised registry study, it may not have provided strong evidence for the impact of IVT on AT-LVO. Additionally, as noted in the primary registry study, the sample size may not have been sufficient to evaluate low-incident outcomes, such as haemorrhagic complications and mortality. Third, the IVT dose of alteplase in Japan is 0.6 mg/kg, which is lower than the 0.9 mg/kg dose commonly used in western countries. However, previous reports have indicated that higher alteplase doses may increase the risk of ICH, which could further support our findings of an elevated risk of ICH following IVT in the intracranial group.37 Finally, although tenecteplase has been widely used in recent years, it is not yet commonly used in Japan. An integrated analysis comparing tenecteplase and alteplase for tandem occlusion showed that tenecteplase was associated with more favourable functional outcomes, lower mortality, earlier neurological improvement, shorter time to recanalisation and improved recanalisation.38 Therefore, if alteplase were substituted with tenecteplase, the results of this study might differ.

Conclusion

The subanalysis of the largest registry for AT-LVO with EVT indicated that, in terms of safety outcomes, it might be preferable to skip IVT before EVT in cases of in situ occlusion of the intracranial artery.
