Optimization of Extrusion Three-Dimensional Printing Liquid Silicone Rubber: Managing Thermal History and Modification for Improved Printability
Authors: Ernan Ju, Xinxin Li, Li Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Liquid silicone rubber (LSR) materials are often used in the manufacture of flexible circuit board substrates for wearable devices. However, traditional processing techniques limit the improvement of the molding precision and make it difficult to process complex structures. Three-dimensional (3D) printing is a novel manufacturing method for LSR wearable devices. The influence of the internal temperature field on LSR’s curing time was evaluated. The printing performance and mechanical properties were analyzed with three inks (SiO2/PEG/LSR, PEG/LSR, and LSR) with polyethylene glycol (PEG) and silicon dioxide (SiO2) modifying LSR. Furthermore, orthogonal experiments were conducted to optimize the material ratios, and the entropy weight method was applied to establish a comprehensive evaluation function that incorporates printable duration, tensile strength, and needle blockage. Under the optimal printing temperature (25 °C), it was clear that the performance of SiO2/PEG/LSR was the best. The forming accuracy of its line width was 98.5%, the tensile strength was 72.96% higher than pure LSR, and the impact strength remained at ∼6.1 MPa (equivalent to pure LSR). Orthogonal analysis confirmed the LSR dosage as the most significant influence on printability and mechanical properties, and the optimized material ratio was determined as LSR:PEG:SiO2 = 300:30:1 (by weight). The optimized materials combined with rheological experiments have demonstrated that integrating the thermal history with material modification to improve the printing performance of extrusion-based 3D printing is effective.

Introduction

Silicone rubber has gained significant attention in the field of flexible electronics due to its unique properties, such as flexibility, stretchability, and biocompatibility. , Silicone rubber has been the subject of numerous experiments employing it as a substrate. Examples of these studies include dielectric elastomers in flexible electronic goods and soft robotics, − as well as flexible electronic circuits. − For example, Goh et al. further explored the use of silicone rubber as a substrate for printing conductive ink using the aerosol jet process, highlighting its application in fabricating stretchable electronic circuits. Harada et al. demonstrated the development of a superhydrophobic and self-cleaning surface on SR through laser treatment, showcasing its potential for flexible and wearable electronics. Hu et al. prepared a PANI/CuPc/PDMS composite elastomer with a high dielectric constant and low modulus, showcasing the versatility of silicone rubber in dielectric elastomers for soft robots and flexible electronics. Additionally, silicone rubber was prepared with graphite-based materials to form a composite conductive material. On the one hand, it retains the soft and flexible characteristics of silicone rubber. On the other hand, due to the introduction of active materials to improve the electrical properties, the preparation of the electrical components was obtained with a high sensitivity. − An increasing number of scholars are devoted to the exploration of flexible conductive materials. Foremny et al. investigated the biocompatibility of liquid metal–silicone rubber composites for flexible implant technology, paving the way for the integration of stretchable devices in various applications. The research above demonstrates that flexible electronic devices must be assembled from flexible base materials by using flexible conductive materials. However, the majority of current research is focused on developing flexible conductive materials, and the substrate typically adopts a simple thin film structure, limiting the structure of the conductive part and making it impossible to realize complex structures in flexible electronic packaging.

Silicone material is a type of polymer elastomer mainly composed of polysiloxane. According to its physical state, it can be divided into solid silicone rubber and liquid silicone rubber (LSR). However, their main manufacturing methods still rely on mold-based manufacturing methods, such as injection molding, compression molding, and casting. Because of their great efficiency and adaptability for large-scale manufacturing, these conventional methods are preferred. , Injection molding demands a higher fluidity of the material but produces longer silicone rubber materials with superior stability. Conversely, compression molding is a straightforward and efficient manufacturing technique, but the process parameters frequently have an impact on the final product’s uniformity and surface quality. , However, all of these conventional methods require a high level of mold design and may be limited to the molding of complex geometries. As technology has developed, layer-by-layer (3D printing) production processes have become more and more popular as a molding technique. High design flexibility and quick production of intricate silicone rubber parts in response to particular requirements are made possible by 3D printing technology. , Furthermore, 3D printing has a lot to offer, in terms of shorter lead times and less material waste. Despite these benefits, several challenges remain in the 3D printing of silicone rubber, including relatively slow printing speeds, limited printing accuracy, and constraints in material compatibility. , These limitations hinder the widespread adoption of 3D printing for high-precision and functional silicone rubber components. Nevertheless, ongoing research and development efforts are actively addressing these issues, indicating strong potential for the future of silicone rubber molding via additive manufacturing. For example, recent advances in printhead design, rheology-modified silicone inks, and multimaterial printing platforms have shown promise in improving print resolution and mechanical performance.

Future research may concentrate on optimizing the 3D printing procedure and silicone rubber material formulations to enhance the printing performance and the quality of the final products. Key directions include the development of novel photocurable or shear thinning silicone composites tailored for additive manufacturing, as well as the integration of in situ curing and real-time monitoring systems to achieve high-dimensional stability and functional reliability. Moreover, combining computational modeling with machine learning approaches could facilitate the prediction and control of printing parameters, thereby minimizing defects and maximizing performance. − By fulfilling these objectives, 3D printing technology not only realizes the rapid manufacturing of complex geometries but also ensures the reliability and durability of the final products in practical applications. This progress provides important support for future smart manufacturing and innovation, particularly in fields such as soft robotics, wearable electronics, and biomedical devices, where customizability and structural complexity are critical.

This study intended to improve the printing performance and mechanical properties of LSR in order to enable direct molding of intricate wearable device structures. The fundamental problem with the current 3D printing of LSR material was addressed, which was that poor rheological properties result in unstable printing and inadequate mechanical strength, failing to meet wearable device requirements. First, ideal process parameters, such as printing temperature, speed, and layer thickness, were identified by examining the rheological behavior and curing properties of LSR during 3D printing. Subsequently, the LSR material was modified through polyethylene glycol (PEG) and silicon dioxide (SiO2), and the printing performance and mechanical properties of the modified material were tested by measuring the pressure change/material continuous extrusion printing time, line width change rate, tensile elasticity and impact strength. Finally, by combining orthogonal experimental design and the entropy method, the ratio of the modified LSR was optimized through testing printable duration, tensile strength, and needle blockage.

Experimental Section

Reagents and Equipment

The LSR material used in this study was a white translucent, two-part silicone elastomer that cures upon exposure to room temperature or could be accelerated by heat (E625, Shenzhen Hong Ye Jie Technology Co., Ltd., China). E625 was selected due to its biocompatible characteristics and safety. Its characteristics are displayed in Table S1. Materials and media were used as received, including polyethylene glycol (PEG-400, Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., China), silicon dioxide (SiO2, metal-based: 7–40 nm, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and all other reagents that were analytical reagents.

A 3D-Bioplotter (EnvisionTEC GmbH, Germany) was used for printing. A scanning electron microscope (SEM, JSM-6360LV, Japan) was employed to characterize the modified silica gel material. An LCTM-500 electric testing machine (Chatillon, USA) was combined with a force gauge to characterize the mechanical properties of 3D printed specimens. An electron microscope (AmScope, USA) was employed to characterize the dimensions of the printed specimens. A DSR 500 CP-4000 PLUS instrument (Lamy Rheology, France) was used to test the steady-state shear rheological behaviors.

Preparation of the Modified Material and the 3D Printing Process

The preparation method of SiO2 was with the following steps (Figure ). First, the SiO2 material and PEG material were mixed with a magnetic stirrer to evenly disperse and continuously stir for 10 min to fully mix. Furthermore, the mixed material was placed in a vacuum drying oven at 60 °C for 30 min. During this process, PEG was adsorbed into the pores of SiO2 to enhance its adhesion to other materials. Next, the mixture of SiO2 and PEG was allowed to naturally cool to room temperature after a sufficient reaction at high temperature. This allowed the system to stabilize and produce the modified SiO2. Then, it was introduced to the silicone monomer (component A), which made the modified material and component A evenly mixed. Finally, the curing agent (component B) was added and stirred for 5 min to ensure that all materials were evenly mixed.

Process of preparing modified materials and the 3D printing process.

The 3D printing process was as follows. The prepared ink was transferred into injection cartridges, and air bubbles were removed for 3D printing. The sample was extruded layer-by-layer through a 600 μm diameter nozzle (d 0) using a pressure of 0.2–5 bar. Considering that there might be delays in curing during the printing process of this material, the distance (h) was set to 480 μm. The scaffold architecture was constructed by plotting filaments with 0° and 90° angle steps between each layer (for printing performance testing). Another dense membrane structure was also prepared by narrowing the interfilament distance to 800 μm and solidified after printing (for mechanical property testing).

Print Duration

This was measured by recording the time (min) that they were able to continuously print and ensuring that two neighboring print lines were clearly visible, while the material could be discharged during the printing process under the corresponding set conditions. Timing started when the above requirements were met, and no adjustment was made until a continuous print line could not be formed or the corresponding vulcanization time period was exceeded; then, the timing was stopped. The time error caused by the operation was controlled within ±30 s, with an average of 3 times for each sample.

Pressure Change/Material Continuous Extrusion Printing Time

For a new printing material, while ensuring stable and continuous extrusion of the material by the printing needle during the printing process, it was hoped that the printing time could be as long as possible, the printing pressure change would be as small as possible, and the thickness of the printing lines would be less varied. Regarding the printing materials used in this article, considering the differences in operation time during each stage, the initial measurement point for the printing time period was 15 min after the completion of component B addition and the start of the vulcanization reaction timing. The second measurement point was 20 min after the vulcanization reaction started. The third measurement point was 30 min after the vulcanization reaction started, and the subsequent measurement points were sequentially increased by 10 min compared to the previous measurement point. The end point of the timing was when the working set pressure reached 4.5 bar and the machine cylinder emitted a prompt sound. All samples were tested in triplicate.

The sample was stretched upward at a speed of 200 ± 10 mm/min to 200% of the original length, and the maximum tensile force was measured during the stretching process. A cone head was used to impact the sample downward at a speed of 200 ± 10 mm/min until the sample was completely damaged. Characterization

The steady-state shear rheological behaviors of LSR, PEG/LSR, and SiO2/PEG/LSR, three types of inks, were tested using a parallel plate rheometer (DSR 500 CP-4000 PLUS, Lamy Rheology), with the strain rate between 1.2 and 100 s–1. The step strain (three-interval thixotropy) tests were conducted after 15 min of SiO2/PEG/LSR vulcanization and then tested at 55 °C. First, a 5% small strain was applied for testing to establish the linear viscoelastic region. Second, an 800% large strain was applied to disrupt the structure. Finally, it was returned to small strain to observe recovery of the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″). Each test lasted for 120 s.

Results and Discussion

Effect of Thermal History on Printing Ink

The materials utilized in extrusion-based 3D printing are flowable and can be in various forms, such as solutions, colloidal dispersions, and molten solids. Given the inherent challenge of directly 3D printing silicone materials, we aim to achieve their formability by precisely controlling the thermal history during printing. This thermal history primarily involves two parameters: the cartridge temperature and the external temperature (i.e., the heated build plate). The curing process of the printed structure is layer-by-layer and cumulative. As illustrated in Figure a, when the first layer (L1) is deposited onto the heated plate, it begins to solidify rapidly. A waiting time is incorporated after printing each layer to ensure that L1 reaches a sufficiently cured state before the next layer (L2) is deposited. During the printing of L2, the heat transferred from the heated plate continues to promote the curing of L1, while L2 itself also starts to cure upon contact with the hot surface and the underlying warm layer. This process repeats for subsequent layers, with the heat from the build plate and the exothermic reaction of previously deposited layers contributing to the progressive curing of the entire structure. To ensure proper curing of the LSR, an 8 s interval was set between the printing of successive layers when investigating the effects of external heating. The external temperature of the build plate was set to 55 °C for studying the outfield printing effect. This temperature was carefully selected because the printing nozzle is in proximity to the build platform; an excessively high plate temperature would cause premature curing and clogging at the nozzle tip, rendering printing impossible. Further details can be found in Supporting Information Section 2.1.

Study on internal and external temperature-assisted curing. (a) Schematic illustration of external temperature-assisted curing extrusion-based 3D printing. (b) Effect of cartridge temperature and vulcanization time on print duration.

When the temperature exceeded 30 °C, the inhomogeneity became highly pronounced. Although the liquid silicone rubber in the central region still maintained fluidity, the overall high viscosity rendered it unprintable for direct extrusion-based fabrication. Given that the LSR investigated in this study is a room-temperature vulcanizing silicone rubber, whose extrudate exhibits poor forming quality at temperatures below 20 °C, the entire experimental testing range was thus defined as 20–30 °C. By studying the cartridge temperature and curing time, the optimal printing effect is evaluated based on the print duration. The influence of the temperature on viscosity is shown in Figure S3. The LSR was measured at 20, 25, and 30 °C with curing for 10, 25, 40, 55, and 70 min. As can be seen from Figure b, regardless of the temperature, the printing time decreases significantly when the curing time is longer than 60 min. The print duration at 20 °C was longer than at the other temperatures, but the line widths at this temperature were too large and, therefore, did not matter. When the temperature was 30 °C, with the increase in curing time, the print time showed a significant reduction in the trend because the viscosity of the material increased as the LSR material vulcanized more thoroughly. This process continued until the final force that prevented the material from flowing out was significantly larger than the vertical downward force, which caused printing to stop.

LSR Modification Index Analysis

The effects of SiO2 and PEG on the properties of LSR were first qualitatively analyzed. The modified material SiO2 was used in this experiment, which is poorly bonded when directly added to LSR as a blended material. Therefore, PEG was used as a surface activator to improve the surface activity of the modified materials. Printed line effects obtained using SiO2/PEG/LSR, PEG/LSR, and LSR as printing inks are shown sequentially in Figure a–c. Both Figure a and Figure b are three-dimensional, continuous, smooth, and uniformly distributed lines. As shown in Figure c, due to the high fluidity of printing materials and poor forming stability, the thickness of the lines varies greatly. It was obviously apparent that lines printed directly with silicone exhibit a flattened appearance with line widths significantly exceeding the diameter of the printing nozzle. When printing with the LSR modified solely by PEG, the lines contracted, resulting in an actual line width smaller than the nozzle diameter. It indicated that PEG regulates the thixotropic recovery behavior of the system through hydrogen bonding interactions, which was conducive to improving the printing performance. Meanwhile, SiO2 functions not merely as a filler but, crucially, provides yield stress by forming a network structure. However, the printing material obtained through SiO2/PEG/LSR composite modification yielded superior results, with line widths approximating the nozzle diameter (forming accuracy of 98.5%) and producing three-dimensional, well-defined printed lines.

In Figure d, it can be seen that a small amount of evenly distributed white particles is nondetected gas-phase SiO2 particles, and the small circular holes are the pores left after the silicon dioxide falls off. Overall, the silicone rubber material is in a continuous state with a large number of solid particles embedded inside (Figure S4), which improves the various properties of the silicone rubber material.

In terms of pressure changes/continuous extrusion printing time of materials, it can be seen from Figure e that the printing extrusion pressure of all the samples gradually increases with time. This is mainly because the addition of modified fillers changes the viscosity and solidification rates of LSR materials. The printing extrusion pressure values for materials containing PEG additives were similar, and they all increased quickly in the early stage. However, the termination pressure of the SiO2-modified composite material in SiO2/PEG/LSR was relatively large, and the sustainable extrusion time was longer. During the early printing stage of LSR, the extrusion pressure increased slowly, but when the pressure was below 0.5 bar, it was almost impossible to print uniform and continuous lines. After 40 min, the pressure change rate rapidly increased, and the printing lines were unstable beyond 80 min. The effective printing stage of the entire printing process was between 35 and 80 min. According to eq , it is calculated that the TP values of SiO2/PEG/LSR, PEG/LSR, and LSR are 0.074, 0.080, and 0.037, respectively. It can be concluded that the pressure change per unit time of LSR is small and the duration is long, which shows that the SiO2/PEG/LSR material has the advantages of long continuous printing time and low average change pressure, followed by SiO2/PEG/LSR, and the continuous printing time of PEG/LSR is shorter.

Due to the combined force of material extrusion, self-gravity, and extrusion caused by a layer height slightly smaller than the diameter of the needle during printing, the printed lines may deform to a certain extent. Generally speaking, printing with a line change rate of less than 10% can be considered to have good printing results. In terms of the dynamic printing line width change rate, we measured the variation of the line width of each group of samples over time during the printing process, as shown in Figure f. It can be seen that the line width fluctuation of SR is relatively large and the overall printing process is in an unstable state. In terms of the dynamic printing line width change rate, the average error rate of SiO2/PEG/LSR is 7.296%, and PEG/LSR is 9.072%; they both have good forming effects. At the beginning of the LSR, the rate of change in printing was very large, gradually decreasing over time. After 20 min, the printed lines showed significant shrinkage, gradually decreasing to 10% around 60–80 min. It can be seen that the line width fluctuation of the LSR is relatively large, and the overall printing process is in an unstable state. It is therefore clear that the use of SiO2/PEG/LSR as ink materials yields superior printing performance.

LSR itself is an elastic material with toughness and high tensile strength. According to Figure g, it is found that the tensile force required per unit mass of the LSR material is 5.49 N/g, which is at a disadvantage compared to other modified materials. The tensile properties of SiO2/PEG/LSR and PEG/LSR were significantly improved; the most significant improvement in tensile properties was achieved by SiO2-modified composite materials, with an improvement of the average tensile performance of the LSR by 72.96%. The enhanced bonding between the modified mineral materials and SR resulted from a higher adsorption between their surfaces and LSR as the surface activity of the materials increased. As a result, the combination of chemical bonding, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces between the added mineral material and the LSR material enhanced the tensile strength of the material. , In terms of impact strength, the impact strength of the SiO2/PEG/LSR and PEG/LSR materials was almost equal to that of LSR without adding any material, and the strength remained at about 6.1 MPa. This indicates that the introduction of SiO2 and PEG does not adversely affect the material’s impact strength.

Optimization of Material Ratios Based on the Orthogonal Experiment

Orthogonal experiments were employed to optimize material ratios, with the experimental evaluation criteria comprising printable duration, tensile strength, and needle blockage. As the experimental assessment involved multiple factors, a comprehensive analysis of the results across all indicators was required. During this multifactor evaluation, the weighting of each factor within the overall assessment needed to be determined. The entropy method was used to determine the weight coefficients for printable duration, tensile strength, and needle blockage. The entropy method’s objective weighting mechanism avoids the subjective bias of empirical weighting methods that are common in polymer processing optimization, which is critical for ensuring the objectivity of the ‘optimal’ formulation. This is the primary application-driven justification for entropy method selection, consistent with the engineering demand for reproducible and objective process optimization in the additive manufacturing of silicone rubber. According to the process shown in Supporting Information Section 2.3, the following were calculated sequentially: the total contribution of each test factor (sum­(p j )), the total contribution of all factors (E j ), the degree of consistency in the contribution across experiments (d j ), and the weighting of each measurement indicator (w j ). Rheological Mechanisms Governing Printability

For extrusion-based 3D printing, printability is fundamentally governed by shear thinning behavior (complex viscosity vs shear rate), yield stress, and thixotropic recovery (structure breakdown and rebuilding after shear). , Therefore, steady-state shear flow measurements were conducted to compare the rheological behavior of the LSR, PEG/LSR, and SiO2/PEG/LSR systems. As shown in Figure a, the LSR system exhibits nearly Newtonian fluid characteristics within the tested shear rate range, with the apparent viscosity remaining basically constant and showing no significant shear thinning behavior. In contrast, the PEG/LSR system began to exhibit weak shear thinning behavior, where the viscosity decreases steadily with an increasing shear rate, suggesting that PEG effectively introduces reversible structural interactions that facilitate ink flow under high shear conditions. The SiO2/PEG/LSR composite system exhibits typical characteristics of non-Newtonian pseudoplastic fluids. The apparent viscosity shows a significant decrease with the increase in shear rate, demonstrating strong shear thinning behavior: at high shear rates (simulating the extrusion process of a 3D printing nozzle), the viscosity of the system drops significantly, enabling smooth continuous extrusion through micro nozzles; at low shear rates (simulating the postprinting deposition state), the viscosity of the system rapidly recovers, providing the foundation for the self-supporting nature of the printed structure.

Rheological properties. (a) Representative apparent viscosity of printing ink, SiO2/PEG/LSR, PEG/LSR, and LSR; (b) G′ and G″ of SiO2/PEG/LSR printing ink as a function of a three-interval thixotropy (low–high–low: 5%–800%–5%) at a fixed frequency of 1 Hz, demonstrating the ink’s thixotropic behavior.

In the first step, G′ was higher than G″, indicating that the material exhibited dominant solid-like elastic behavior, which corresponded to the stationary state of the ink in the barrel before printing. At a high strain of 800%, the strain increased instantaneously to simulate the high shear effect during printing extrusion. G′ decreased, and G″ exceeded G′, indicating that the physical structure composed of a SiO2 network and PEG-mediated hydrogen bond interactions was completely destroyed, and the material transformed into a liquid-like flow state, thereby achieving smooth extrusion. When the strain is reduced back to 5%, it can be observed that both G′ and G″ rapidly recovered over time, with G′ always being higher than G′, marking the reconstruction of the solid-like structure. This test clearly confirmed that the SiO2/PEG/LSR ink has excellent reversible thixotropic properties. The rapid increase in modulus during the recovery stage is mainly due to the reorganization of physical interactions (such as hydrogen bonds) rather than irreversible chemical curing. This is supported by the final plateau reached on the recovery curve rather than a continuous upward trend. This rapid and reversible structural recovery characteristic ensures that the enhanced printability arises from reversible microstructural rebuilding of the SiO2–PEG network, rather than irreversible LSR cross-linking, which is critical for maintaining consistent extrusion and shape retention in extrusion-based 3D printing.

The improved printability of the SiO2/PEG/LSR ink is mechanistically linked to its rheological properties. A sufficiently high static yield stress ensures filament shape retention after extrusion, reflected in a low line width change rate of 7.296%. Extrusion stability, reflected by a low pressure change rate, was governed by pronounced shear thinning behavior (for smooth flow). Most critically, the three-interval thixotropy test reveals a G′ recovery of high-strain disruption; the line width change rate showed a strong negative correlation with the G′ recovery percentage from step strain tests. This directly evidences that rapid, reversible thixotropic recovery of the physically cross-linked networkenhanced by PEG and SiO2is the rheological origin for precise shape fidelity after deposition.

Conclusions

This study aims to establish a foundation for the 3D printing of flexible electronic devices by investigating the effects of internal temperature fields and material modifications on the printing performance and mechanical properties of LSR. The research focuses on optimizing printability and material strength through thermal field-assisted processes and composite material design. The internal temperature field (20, 25, and 30 °C) was analyzed systematically in combination with the curing time (10, 35, and 60 min) to evaluate its impact on the printing time. To improve the printing performance (line width, pressure change, continuous extrusion time, and dynamic line width change rate) of LSR materials and to maximize their mechanical properties (tensile/impact strength), a modification method using PEG and SiO2 was proposed. Adding modified material particles to evenly disperse into the LSR system can change the viscosity of silica gel and improve the mechanical properties of LSR materials by fully combining the filled particles with LSR. The interlayer curing of the hot plate auxiliary can effectively enhance the printing performance of LSR. The best printing is achieved at 25 °C with a vulcanization time of 35 min. PEG and SiO2 can effectively enhance the printability and mechanical properties of LSR simultaneously, and the resulting 300:30:1 (by weight) formula provides a nonclogging, ready-to-use ink for high-resolution, stretchable 3D printing technology. The significant shear thinning phenomenon and the rapid recovery of G′ in the three-interval thixotropy experiment once again prove the effectiveness of using thermal history combined with material modification in improving the printing performance of extrusion-based 3D printing. In the future, internal and external temperature-assisted precuring modification of silicone ink for 3D printing will offer a viable approach for the one-step manufacture of flexible electronic devices.
