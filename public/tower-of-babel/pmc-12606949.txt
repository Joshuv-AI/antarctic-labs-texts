Prediction of ultrafiltration volume in maintenance hemodialysis patients using an artificial neural network model based on body composition information
Authors: Jiaoyan Chen, Jurong Yang, Xianqiong Lu, Jingrong Peng, Liangji He, Wei Tan, Qing Yu, Yunyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate and rapid assessment of fluid status of maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) patients and maintaining fluid balance is essential to ensure the quality of dialysis treatment. Currently, clinical methods for assessing ultrafiltration volume are still insufficient, and reliable tools that are more accurate and rapid are needed. The objective of this study was to construct a model for predicting ultrafiltration volume (UF) in MHD patients based on artificial neural network (ANN) algorithms, to validate and evaluate this model, and to investigate the impact of body composition prior to dialysis on UF in MHD patients.MethodsA total of 319 patients undergoing MHD treatment at our center were enrolled. Basic demographic and clinical characteristics were collected and evaluated using the hemodialysis information system. Body composition was measured on ≥ 3 separate days before dialysis treatment using an Inbody bioimpedance instrument. The target ultrafiltration volume was determined by nephrologists based on the integration of body composition measurements and clinical characteristics, yielding a dataset of 1,205 entries. Heat maps were used to demonstrate the correlation between body composition and UF in MHD patients, and LASSO regression and multifactorial linear regression were used to screen the relevant indicator factors for final inclusion in the model, and Backpropagation Neural Network model (BPNN) was developed using the MATLAB (R2022a) neural network toolbox to establish the projected relationship between UF and pre-dialysis body composition. The effectiveness of the model was assessed based on the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE) of the calculated regression.ResultsThe artificial neural network model demonstrated an optimal predictive performance metric of R2 = 0.965 for forecasting ultrafiltration volume in MHD patients. With an average difference of 0.182 L between observed and predicted values, and highlighted the significant influence of certain body composition indicators on UF in MHD patients.ConclusionThis study effectively demonstrates the predictive role of an artificial neural network model based on pre-dialysis body composition information in estimating ultrafiltration providing a valuable predictive tool to optimize assessment volume for MHD patients, of ultrafiltration volume in MHD patients.Keywords: Ultrafiltration volume, Artificial neural network, Body composition, MHD

Background

The rising prevalence of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) has led to a significant healthcare burden, posing a serious public health challenge [1]. Maintenance hemodialysis (MHD) is the main treatment for end-stage renal disease, which can largely improve the life expectancy of ESRD patients [2], and cardiovascular-related complications are the most important cause of death in MHD patients [3], and MHD patients with fluid overload present a higher risk of death [4], and long-term volume overload abnormality is also one of the major risk factors for the development of cardiovascular-related complications including high blood pressure, pulmonary high blood pressure, and heart failure [5, 6]. In addition, if the patient’s body fluids are removed excessively during dialysis, insufficient effective circulating blood volume can also cause the occurrence of adverse events such as hypotension, myocardial ischemia, and residual renal failure, which can affect the patient’s prognosis [7, 8]. Therefore, rapid and accurate assessment of the volume loading status of dialysis patients and maintaining the balance of volume loading are the keys to ensure the quality of dialysis treatment.

The methods of assessing the volume loading status of MHD patients include blood volume monitoring, isotope dilution method, clinical assessment method, and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) [9–11]. The BIA method is to estimate the body composition by measuring the body’s electrical resistance and impedance to electric current, including total body water (TBW), extracellular water (ECW) and intracellular water (ICW), etc., and is able to make accurate and objective measurements of the distribution of fluids and hydration It can accurately and objectively measure the fluid distribution and hydration status [12]. In clinical work, the volume loading status of MHD patients is mainly monitored and judged by the clinical assessment method combined with the BIA method. Although the BIA method of monitoring volume status has been proved to have a high degree of consistency with the gold standard [13], it is not sensitive to intracavernous fluid accumulation, and it is easy to confuse excessive fluid volume with excessive water loading caused by malnutrition, anemia, etc., which may lead to a certain deviation between the measured data and the actual volume loading value [14]. In addition, when a patient’s UF undergoes significant changes and dialysis status deteriorates, clinicians must re-evaluate the patient’s UF. Given the complex clinical characteristics of end-stage renal disease patients, this demands significant clinical expertise on the part of physicians, and accurate assessment requires substantial time and effort and labor-intensive.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms can safely and efficiently evaluate large amounts of data [15], which provides an opportunity to develop and utilize models for real-time personalized prediction of dynamic ultrafiltration volumes in patients with MHD and the ability to use these data to predict hemodynamic responses during dialysis has been demonstrated [16], and real-time models that take into account a wider range of factors may increase the performance of predictive models. There have been many studies using machine learning algorithms to develop and validate predictive models related to hemodialysis, such as predictive models for risk of cardiovascular events and predictive models for high blood pressure and hypotension [17–19]. In this study, we collected prospectively gathered data from dialysis patients, including basic patient characteristics, clinical status, and pre-dialysis body composition, and developed a neural network prediction model for ultrafiltration volume in MHD patients based on artificial intelligence and machine learning, and validate and evaluate the model, and investigate the prediction of ultrafiltration volume by pre-dialysis body composition in MHD patients.

Methods

Research design

This study aimed to develop a predictive model for ultrafiltration volume (UF) in patients undergoing maintenance haemodialysis (MHD). The study adheres to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Fangda Hospital). Blood pressure data and patient demographics were extracted from the centre’s MHD patient medical record system, with data collection conducted between December 2024 and January 2025.

Participants

The participants in this study were MHD patients from our center, totaling 319 cases. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) stable condition, regular dialysis with a dialysis duration > 3 months; (3) intact limbs capable of maintaining the body composition measurement posture. Exclusion criteria: (1) Presence of factors affecting body water composition, such as fever or dehydration; (2) Presence of a pacemaker or other metallic implants that interfere with body composition measurement; (3) Active tumors or pregnancy; (4) Refusal to participate in the study. All participants provided and signed written informed consent.

Data preparation

Using a portable whole-body bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody S10), body composition was measured in patients at least three times on different days prior to dialysis treatment. Measurements were conducted approximately 30 min before hemodialysis by a trained dialysis nurse and a nephrologist. All patients underwent routine dialysis treatment in the dialysis unit of this center. When MHD patients were in the supine position, electrodes were placed on the thumb and middle finger of both upper limbs and the ankle side of both lower limbs for measurement. A total of 1,205 measurements were performed on all patients. Final measurements were collected: total body water (TBW), intracellular water (ICW), extracellular water (ECW), protein (Protein), bone mineral content (BMC), basal metabolic rate (BMR), inorganic salts (Mineral), fat free weight (FFM), visceral fat area (VFA), muscle mass (SLM), skeletal muscle mass (SMM), body fat (BFM), percent body fat (PBF), body mass index (BMI), body cell content (BCM), upper arm circumference (AC), upper arm muscle circumference (AMC). The target ultrafiltration volume is determined by the nephrologist based on the patient’s pre-dialysis weight and estimated dry weight, which is derived from bioimpedance parameters (the ECW/TBW ratio) and clinical assessment.The method used to calculate the UF value based on dry weight is to subtract the dry weight from the pre-dialysis weight.

Variable selection

In addition to incorporating body composition information, we also focused on patient groups with high blood pressure (HBP), diabetes mellitus (DM), and anemia, as these conditions are common among patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD). HBP is closely associated with volume overload during dialysis [20], DM plays a significant role in promoting the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [21], and anemia, as a common complication of CKD, anemia is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality in CKD patients [22]. Through correlation analysis and using LASSO regression and multiple linear regression analysis, we selected the eight variables most strongly correlated with UF as the optimal parameter set for the predictive model: DM, HBP, gender, age, BFM, BMI, AC, and AMC.

Data preprocessing

The mean value of multiple measurements was taken as the final indicator; all continuous variables underwent standardisation; data consistency was verified, and obvious outliers were excluded. The data included in the study were complete with no missing values.

Modeling neural networks

MATLAB (MATrix LABoratory, R2022a) is a multi-paradigm numerical computing environment and a fourth-generation programming language that allows algorithmic implementations, functions and data plotting, and connectivity with programs written in other languages, including C++, Java, and Python, among others, and has gained widespread use in different areas of science [23]. In this study, we used the Newff function in the MATLAB to create a neural network model (ANN) with a network structure consisting of eight input layers, five hidden layers, and one output layer. The Trainlm function (Levenberg-Marquardt algorithm) was used for training, setting the maximum number of training times to 1000, the data was randomly split into a training set (95%) and a test set (5%). During training, an internal validation subset was employed for early stopping to prevent overfitting.The model performance was evaluated using the test set root mean square error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R2).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 and R (4.3.3) software packages. Measurement information was expressed using mean and standard deviation, and count information was expressed using percentages. Using an independent samples t-test to compare UF between groups in terms of gender, anemia, DM, and HBP, and to assess multicollinearity, all data were analyzed using correlation analysis and screened for the best set of parameters to be included in the predictive model using LASSO regression and multiple linear regression analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To avoid the problem of multicollinearity, we screened the data features using the Lasso regression model, Fig. 2A, with coefficients for a total of 25 indicators. By plotting vertical dashed lines using the minimum criterion and one standard error of the minimum criterion (1-SE criterion), the 10-fold cross-validation method was applied to the iterative analysis, and λ for the 1-SE criterion in the range was selected as the best model (λ = 0.002548), and we subsequently left the 8 variables with non-zero λ including DM, HBP, Gender, Age, BFM, BMI, AC, and AMC (Fig. 2B). In addition, we built multiple linear regression models with UF as the dependent variable (Table 2), after adjusting, more variables were excluded and based on the multiple linear regression coefficients, we selected the 8 variables that were most correlated with UF, Regression coefficients showed that upper arm circumference had the strongest correlation with UF (AC, β=-1.271, P < 0.001), and as expected, the LASSO convergence and the variables screened out by the multiple linear regression model were almost the same. Therefore, the final data features included in the neural network model were DM, HBP, gender, age, BFM, BMI, AC, and AMC. (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Accurate assessment of UF is crucial for safeguarding the quality of life and survival of chronic kidney disease patients undergoing dialysis treatment. This evaluation remains challenging given the complex interplay of comorbidities, weight fluctuations, and patient subjective perceptions. We employed an artificial neural network (ANN) model to address this issue, incorporating individualised dynamic changes in fluid load over time in haemodialysis patients. Whilst simple equations based on pre-dialysis weight, dry weight, and treatment duration remain clinically prevalent, our model may offer additional value by integrating body composition and comorbidity data to account for individual variations in fluid tolerance. The model demonstrated optimal UF prediction performance for haemodialysis at 96.5% (R²=0.965), indicating that continuous real-time UF prediction during haemodialysis is theoretically feasible.

MATLAB is a high-performance and powerful language for technical computing that integrates computation, visualization, and programming into one easy-to-use environment [24]. MATLAB-based ANN models have demonstrated efficacy in many studies to improve the effectiveness and ease of use of clinical pathways [25, 26]. The self-learning capability of ANN enables mapping functions from input to output. In this study, we input the obtained eight variables into the input layer and obtain the desired predictions from the output layer. Five nodes in the designed hidden layer are used to analyze the linear relationship between the data. The core idea of ANN algorithm utilizes the training error to react back on the five hidden layer neurons, thus adjusting the connection weights and thresholds between the neurons and continuously updating them to achieve the minimum training error (0.195). In addition, it has been reported that the network model also includes functionality to implement a large number of other complex nonlinear mappings, which gives it credible decision-making capabilities in the face of problems with more complex mechanisms [27, 28].

In this study, we selected a total of 8 possible variables of patients’ clinical characteristics (DM, HBP, Gender, Age) and pre-dialysis body composition (BFM, BMI, AC, AMC) as the input parameters of the model through LASSO regression and multiple linear regression modeling. ANN can automatically screen variables internally, assigning small weights to variables with smaller contributions and large weights to variables with larger contributions [29]. Based on the regression model, the screening of feature variables may be more conducive to the neural network’s accurate assignment of feature weights to these data, and after repeated training of features on larger samples, the selection of variables, the choice of parameters, and the standardization of the data would have minimal impact on the final output results.

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is an important method for assessing volume loading in patients with MHD, and its body composition measurements are commissioned based on age, gender, and weight parameters [30]. In fact, differences in gender, age, and body weight are also important factors for clinicians to consider when assessing UF in patients with MHD [31], and BASILE et al. [32] reported the correlation of UF with age, body mass index, and predialysis blood pressure. Hypertension is a major feature of end-stage renal disease (ESRD), and high blood pressure in patients before and after dialysis can reflect extracellular volume overload to a certain extent [33, 34], and Enass et al. reported that bioimpedance-derived indices of hypervolemia were independently associated with higher postdialysis systolic blood pressure and the risk of high blood pressure during dialysis [35], whereas the interconnectedness of high blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, and obesity would further increase the renal disease burden [36–38], which may lead to lower glomerular filtration and generate a higher volume load. A study of peritoneal dialysis patients noted a higher fluid overload in diabetic patients than in non-diabetic patients [39], and the effect of diabetes on decreasing renal function and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) may lead to more severe volume loading in MHD patients [40].

Based on the results of linear regression analysis, human body composition (BFM, BMI, AC, AMC) has varying degrees of influence on UF. Previous research has reported that BMI plays a certain role in monitoring the hydration status of the human body [41], Chang et al. [42]also noted that adults with insufficient water intake tend to have higher BMIs. Given the potential association between BFM, AC, AMC, and BMI, it appears that these body composition indices may exhibit a consistent response to UF. However, there currently seems to be no research confirming a direct link between these body composition indices and UF; they primarily reflect overall nutritional status. Additionally, the widely recognized method of assessing patient hydration status based on body composition analysis appears to support the rationality and feasibility of the methodology employed in this study. In clinical practice, ECW/TBW and the ECW-to-weight ratio are commonly used to measure fluid overload in patients and assess dry weight, as they are most directly related to hydration status [43]. Although dry weight derived from bioimpedance serves as a key input for clinical ultrafiltration prescriptions, our model incorporates additional patient-specific factors to capture complex interactions. This may enhance individualised ultrafiltration planning, particularly in patients with fluctuating volume status or multiple comorbidities. In summary, the neural network model demonstrated good predictive performance for UF in this study. In the future, we can continue to expand the sample data for training to improve model performance and explore the potential value of more body composition indicators in assessing the ultrafiltration volume of MHD patients and the clinical treatment process.

It is noteworthy that poor model prediction is more commonly observed in patients with substantial inter-dialytic weight gain (IDWG) or clinically unstable conditions. In fact, dialysis patients with high ultrafiltration volumes in our center are not regular in controlling their water intake in their daily life, it is necessary to strengthen the health education for this group of patients to improve their compliance, it indicates that real-time adjustment factors may need to be incorporated in future model iterations. Furthermore, Our model predicts the prescribed ultrafiltration volume rather than the actual ultrafiltration achieved. Considering treatment interruptions or complications, future models should incorporate dialysis data to predict the effective ultrafiltration volume. Currently, we lack patient samples with zero ultrafiltration, and more data from patients with zero single ultrafiltration is still needed for training to improve future prediction performance. Considering that changes in ultrafiltration within a single patient are much smaller than changes between patients, training the model using multiple measurements from the same patient may inflate the model’s accuracy. This study was conducted at a single centre with a limited sample size, which may impact the model’s generalisability and robustness. Despite internal validation, the risk of overfitting persists due to the small size of the test set. Future work should incorporate multi-centre data and a larger independent test set to enhance the model’s robustness and clinical applicability. Even though different dialysis methods, medications used in dialysis treatment and other factors may have a small effect on the patient’s personalized ultrafiltration volume, these factors must be considered, and to reduce inter-subject bias, we carefully screened and consistency-checked our subjects prior to the experiment in order to form a uniform criterion for inclusion.

A meaningful question worth discussing is how nephrologists should deal with the problem when patients do not tolerate the final UF predicted by the model during dialysis? We believe that clinicians must incorporate patients’ subjective symptoms, and need to take into account their physical conditions (e.g., malnutrition, colds, etc.) at the time of a single dialysis session, and combine artificial neural network predictions with personalized medical care, because artificial neural network models are only our tools, not our only criteria for judging disease conditions.

Conclusion

This study effectively demonstrates the predictive role of an artificial neural network model based on pre-dialysis body composition information in estimating ultrafiltration volume for MHD patients. Notably, mean prediction error was minimal (0.182 L), providing clinicians with valuable insights and predictive tools for assessing ultrafiltration volume in MHD patients, thereby further enhancing clinical decision-making efficiency. Additionally, correlation analysis and regression analysis further emphasize the significant influence of DM, HBP, gender, age, BFM, BMI, AC, and AMC on UF in MHD patients.
