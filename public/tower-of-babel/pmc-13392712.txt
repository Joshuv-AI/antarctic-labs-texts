Decoding α‐MoC1− x Nanoparticle Formation in Continuous Flow via Machine Learning
Authors: Bin Pan, Allison P Forsberg, Ricki Chairil, Majed S Madani, Susan E Habas, Frederick G Baddour, Richard L Brutchey, Noah Malmstadt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMolybdenum carbide nanoparticles (α‐MoC1− x NPs) are promising catalysts that offer noble‐metal‐like performance at lower cost. We report a mild continuous‐flow synthesis of α‐MoC1− x NPs from Mo(CO)6, coupled with in‐line spectroscopic monitoring and machine learning (ML)‐based analysis to quantify precursor conversion and product formation in real time. A multilayer perceptron ML model was found to accurately deconvolute complex, nonlinear spectral patterns, enabling identification of a two‐step reaction pathway, involving precursor conversion to an amorphous intermediate followed by intraparticle crystallization to α‐MoC1− x NPs, with the first step being rate limiting. Ex situ small angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) and X‐ray diffraction (XRD) validation confirm the predicted concentration profiles and crystallization behavior. This integrated approach showcases how ML can empower insights into NP nucleation and growth, paving the way for self‐driving, flow‐based platforms for NP synthesis.Keywords: flow chemistry, machine learning, nanoparticle synthesis, nucleation and growth mechanisms, reaction kinetics, transition metal carbides

Introduction

Transition metal carbides are emerging as cost‐effective alternatives to noble metals in heterogeneous catalysis owing to their comparable electronic structures, high thermal and chemical stability, and high catalytic activity [1, 2, 3, 4]. Among them, molybdenum carbides display outstanding catalytic performance across diverse reactions, inducing hydrogen evolution [5], CO2 hydrogenation [6], hydrodeoxygenation [7], and reforming processes [8]. In particular, the face‐centered cubic (fcc) α‐MoC1− x phase exhibits high intrinsic catalytic activity, good corrosion resistance, and the ability to achieve large specific surface areas when prepared as nanoparticles (NPs) [9]. These features make α‐MoC1‐ x NPs an attractive class of materials for both fundamental mechanistic studies and practical catalyst development.

Although several synthetic strategies have been explored, including temperature‐controlled carburization, obtaining α‐MoC1− x NPs under mild conditions remains challenging. Conventional high‐temperature carburization approaches often lead to mixed β‐Mo2C/α‐MoC1− x phases, large crystallite sizes, and limited control over active surface area [10, 11, 12]. Recently, a mild, solution‐phase batch synthesis using Mo(CO)6 and long‐chain organic ligands was reported to yield colloidal α‐MoC1− x NPs below 320°C [13]. Continuous‐flow reactors have also emerged as a promising route for colloidal NP synthesis due to their uniform heat and mass transfer, tunable residence times, reproducible kinetic control, and facile integration of in‐line analytical tools [14, 15, 16, 17]. We previously demonstrated that the thermal decomposition of Mo(CO)6 into α‐MoC1− x NPs can be translated to high‐throughput, continuous‐flow systems, where reaction conditions were optimized to increase yield using design of experiments (DoE) [18].

However, the mechanistic understanding of α‐MoC1− x NP nucleation and growth remains unexplored. Addressing this gap is critical for enabling predictive control over NP properties, including size and crystal phase, which influence catalytic activity and selectivity [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. In general, crystalline NPs form through the conversion of molecular precursors into nuclei, followed by particle growth and crystallization [24, 25, 26]. In certain systems, this process occurs in two distinct stages, where the precursor first forms a stable amorphous intermediate, which subsequently crystallizes into the final NPs [27].

Constructing these mechanistic and kinetic models of reactions requires precise monitoring of component concentrations and compositions throughout the reaction [28]. In‐line monitoring enabled by continuous‐flow systems is particularly advantageous because it captures reaction outcomes with minimal off‐line handling, which might otherwise lead to the degradation or loss of product information [29, 30]. Among these techniques, in‐line UV‐vis spectroscopy and in situ small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) are widely used to probe NP nucleation and growth [31, 32, 33]. SAXS provides detailed information about NP size, size distribution, and shape, and the use of synchrotron‐based SAXS has greatly expanded nanoscale characterization capabilities [34]. Despite these merits, adapting cuvette‐ or tube‐based reactor setups typically used for in situ SAXS to full continuous‐flow chemistry remains challenging, as customizable configurations are only available on a case‐by‐case basis [35]. Moreover, laborious data processing and complex model interpretation can hinder exploratory studies [36].

UV‐vis spectroscopy offers a complementary and efficient approach by measuring changes in the optical density of a sample to obtain compositional information [37]. By quantifying transmitted or extinction intensity [38], concentration changes can be tracked for reactions with characteristic spectral signatures [39]. For example, some spherical Au NPs exhibit a single absorption peak at 525 nm due to the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), while the [AuBr4]− precursor shows an absorption band at 400 nm; tracking peak shifts and intensity changes allow real‐time monitoring of reaction trajectories [40]. In contrast, in the synthesis of MoC1− x NPs from a Mo(CO)6 and oleylamine (OAm) precursor solution, precursor conversion and product yield cannot be directly obtained via conventional Beer–Lambert analysis. This is caused by two main factors: (1) the nonlinear relationship between the precursor solution's characteristic absorption at ca. 395 nm and its real‐time concentration in the reaction caused by NP scattering across the entire wavelength range; and (2) the absence of a distinct spectral signature for crystalline MoC1− x NPs that could serve as an indicator of NP concentrations. Consequently, advanced analytical approaches are required to extract quantitative concentration and composition data from the UV‐vis spectra.

Machine learning (ML) provides a powerful tool to bridge these challenges, as it can link complex spectral data to corresponding component concentrations and reaction progression [41]. Since its early development in the 1940s [42], ML has evolved into a transformative methodology capable of recognizing patterns and generating predictions without explicitly defined analytical solutions [43]. While ML has been applied to numerous chemical problems, including spectrum interpretation [44], molecular structure generation [45], reaction optimization [46], phase identification [47], and flow control [48], its interpretability and application to the specific problem of spectral data interpretation remain underexplored [49].

In this work, we present a rapid and precise approach that modularizes the pipeline for investigating NP synthesis mechanisms and kinetics. α‐MoC1− x NPs were synthesized via the thermolytic decomposition of a Mo(CO)6 and OAm precursor solution in a continuous‐flow reactor. Preliminary studies revealed that the reaction proceeds through two steps: isolable amorphous intermediate particles first form upon precursor solution decomposition, which subsequently crystallize into the final NPs via intraparticle crystallization. A library of ML models was screened to interpret the convoluted UV‐vis spectra of the reaction mixture over the course of the reaction. Among them, the multilayer perceptron (MLP) provided the most accurate results—as validated ex situ by SAXS and powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD)—for determining the real‐time concentrations of all components and the product composition (i.e., the ratio of amorphous to crystalline NPs). Kinetic modeling of the reaction data further indicated that the initial step of precursor conversion to the amorphous intermediate constitutes the rate‐determining step.

Results and Discussion

To develop mechanistic insights, we implemented a modular workflow that incorporates synthesizing α‐MoC1− x NPs in continuous flow, in‐line monitoring of spectral data, training various ML models, and ML‐based predictions of reaction component concentrations in silico (Figure 1). First, the precursor solution containing Mo(CO)6 in OAm was loaded into a programmable syringe pump for automatic infusion into a millifluidic reactor. The solution was delivered to a glass reactor tube embedded within a custom‐machined copper plate with a ceramic heat‐insulation layer. The copper plate temperature was precisely maintained at 300 ± 1°C for the synthesis reaction. Depending on residence time, the product of this reaction was either the amorphous intermediate or crystalline α‐MoC1− x NPs. The reaction could also be monitored visually by a color transition from orange precursor solution to black product in the flow, as well as the production of slugs separated by gas, which is a byproduct of the reaction. The reaction mixture exiting this heated region was instantly thermally quenched in a room‐temperature water bath. The quenched reaction solution then passed through a flow sensor that measured the liquid:gas ratio of the flow. Based on the measured ratio, the residence time of the reaction flow was calculated using a previously reported protocol [18]. Subsequently, the reaction stream was directed either to a waste container (before steady state was reached; i.e., after 1.5× the reactor volume of precursor had been infused), or to a collection container (after steady state was established) via a three‐way T‐junction valve. The entire reaction process was maintained at 20 psig using an inert gas back‐pressure regulation setup to stabilize the flow (Figure S1).

Modular workflow for this ML‐powered reaction kinetic study. The precursor solution is infused into the reactor at 300°C at a rate set by the syringe pump, and the reaction mixture is quenched before flow sensing to measure residence time. The product stream is then collected in a downstream container while the entire reaction process is maintained at 20 psig. For ML training purposes, the flow‐synthesized products are washed manually (see experimental section) and resuspended at known concentrations. These solutions (precursor, amorphous, and crystalline products) are diluted and combined in flow at different concentrations, and the spectra are measured in‐line. For kinetic measurements, the flow‐synthesized products are diluted 100× with neat OAm by hand in tubes, and the dilutions are fed directly into the in‐line spectral flow cell by a syringe pump. The spectra captured are input into the trained ML model. Further information about preprocessing can be found in the experimental section.

In the ML‐based architecture to measure reactant and product concentrations, the input is the UV–vis spectrum of the reaction sample collected at different residence times, and the output (label) is the corresponding concentrations of species. Each wavelength can be considered as a dimension; that is, each spectrum is a vector in a multidimensional space. Each wavelength point is an independent variable [50]. As inputs to the ML models, the order of intensities at each wavelength is not randomized and strictly follows the sequence of the wavelengths; therefore, the order is preserved by the fixed wavelength arrangement.

Before training the untrained ML models from randomly initialized parameters, a series of solution mixtures with varied concentrations were prepared to simulate real reaction samples, consisting of the reaction components that contribute most to the spectral extinction signal: Mo(CO)6 and OAm precursor solution, amorphous intermediate, and crystalline MoC1− x NPs, with the amorphous intermediate and crystalline NPs synthesized in flow and confirmed by XRD (Figure S5). Every component was diluted by hand in neat OAm to provide stock solutions with known mass concentrations. The concentrations of the stock solutions were tuned to keep the resulting spectra within the detectable range to ensure a good signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) while avoiding oversaturation of the detector. Verification is provided in a pilot study in the Supporting Information (Figures S3 and S4).

For training the ML algorithm, an automated continuous‐flow process was employed to prepare mixtures of different concentrations of the three components in which the infusion flow rates determined concentrations of each component upon mixing (Figure 1). Thorough mixing was ensured by two microfluidic lamination mixers placed on a hot plate maintained at 45°C to keep the OAm in the liquid phase and preserve the fluidity of the mixture (Figure S2) [51]. After mixing, the stream entered an in‐line detection block for UV‐vis spectrum acquisition. The cycle of “infusion‐mixing‐acquisition” was executed automatically and iteratively, following a three‐factor full factorial matrix with 8 × 6 × 3 levels (crystalline × precursor×amorphous), derived using DoE [44]. The crystalline MoC1− x NP stream was sampled at the most levels to obtain higher resolution in this product of greatest investigative interest. The second‐most sampled component was the precursor solution. A total of 144 spectra, each labeled with its corresponding compositional concentrations, were collected to form the training dataset (Table S2).

During model training, a ten‐fold cross‐validation was employed to obtain a robust estimate of model performance on unseen data and to maximize the use of the limited dataset [52]. The acquired spectral dataset and concentration labels were preprocessed and standardized. The dataset was categorized into two schemes for individual model training: (1) “three components” that separated all three components and (2) “two components” that summed the mass concentrations of amorphous and crystalline products as a “total particle” single value alongside the precursor solution. That is, in the three‐component process, the model reported concentrations for the three separate components, while in the two‐component process, the model reported concentrations only for precursor solution and total particles, even though the input spectra were the same.

To tackle this regression‐type problem, we screened six different kinds of supervised‐learning ML models (Figure 2; Figure S6), each of which possesses distinct characteristics and algorithms: (1) multilayer perceptron (MLP); linear regression with ridge regulation (LRR); (3) k‐nearest neighbors (KNN); (4) support vector machine (SVM); (5) extreme gradient boosting (XGB); and (6) Bayesian linear regression (BLR). MLP is a type of feedforward artificial neural network (ANN) that consists of three types of layers of neurons (input layer, hidden layer, and output layer) [53]. LRR introduces a penalty term to mitigate overfitting when modeling the linear relationship between target labels and one or more independent features (i.e., spectral intensities here) [54]. Ridge (L2) regularization is applicable here over Lasso (L1) because it retains all features while reducing their influence, whereas Lasso sets some coefficients to zero [55], which is undesirable since all spectral features are relevant. KNN regression predicts the value of a new data point by finding its k nearest neighbors (e.g., Euclidean distance) in the training set and averaging their target values [56]. SVM regression predicts a continuous value by finding a function that fits the training data within a margin of tolerance while maximizing flatness and allowing some errors controlled by a regularization parameter [57]. XGB regression is a tree‐based model that predicts a continuous value by building an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects the errors of the previous ones using gradient boosting with regularization for improved accuracy and generalization [58]. BLR predicts outcomes by treating the model parameters as probability distributions and updating these distributions with Bayes’ theorem based on the observed data, providing both predictions and uncertainty estimates [59]. Direct fitting using the Beer–Lambert law for precursor molecular absorption and the scattering law for particles was also conducted for comparison and labeled as “non‐ML method” (Figure 2).

As shown in Figure 3, all ML models showed excellent fits for predicting precursor solution concentrations with validation R 2 > 0.8 and mean square error (MSE) < 0.01. This is to be expected given the distinct absorption features of the precursor solution that benefit the ML models in navigating its concentration. In contrast, the non‐ML method failed to capture this relationship (validation R 2 = 0.1 and MSE > 10) even with such characteristic features due to interference of particle scattering. The prediction of total particle concentrations posed greater challenges to the algorithms due to the featureless nature of the scattering patterns. MLP and BLR still delivered satisfactory predictions (validation R 2 > 0.7 and MSE < 0.01), while other ML models began to degrade in performance (validation R 2 < 0.5 and MSE > 0.015), though all ML models still outperformed the non‐ML method (validation R 2 = 0.27 and MSE = 0.036). Furthermore, it became increasingly challenging for the ML models to differentiate between amorphous and crystalline phases based solely on optical density. Specifically, the low R 2 in LRR emphasized the strong nonlinearity between crystalline and amorphous concentrations and their spectral patterns. With the non‐ML method, it produced negative R 2 values, indicating performance worse than simply predicting the mean. Of all the models tested, MLP achieved the best overall predictive performance (highest R 2 and lowest MSE) across all components, and as shown Table 1, the gaps between the mean training R 2 and mean validation R 2 for all four outputs are small, indicating that overfitting is not noticeable in the MLP method. It was therefore utilized to monitor flow synthesis reactions (Figure 3).

Next, the trained MLP model was used to determine the unknown concentrations of precursor solution and products in the reaction mixtures. Reactions with varying residence times were performed using the above‐described continuous‐flow process (Figure 1). Aliquots of the reaction mixture were diluted by hand 100× in neat OAm to keep the spectral signal below the upper detection limit. The dilutions were injected into the in‐line detection flow cell for spectral acquisition. Figure 4 presents the original UV‐vis spectra and the corresponding MLP‐predicted concentrations over the entire span of observed residence times. Unsurprisingly, the results show that the concentration of precursor solution decreased as the reaction proceeded, visualized by the diminishing peak at ca. 395 nm in the UV‐vis spectra corresponding to the precursor solution (Figure 4a). Over time, the precursor concentration plateaued at ca. 200 s, reflecting that the precursor conversion reached ca. 49% by 300 s (Figure 4b). This is accompanied by a rise in the total particle yield, visualized by the increasing extinction intensity across the entire wavelength range (Figure 4b). Looking deeper at the particle yield, the fraction of amorphous intermediates initially increased and then diminished to a minimal level, indicating the formation and then incorporation of this amorphous intermediate into the final crystalline NP product. The crystalline MoC1− x NP fraction exhibited a slow rise during the first 50 s, followed by a steep increase to a plateau consistent with the total particle yield. It is important to note that the model training revealed uncertainty and only moderate confidence in measuring the concentrations of the amorphous and crystalline fractions as distinct species (relatively low R 2 and high MSE, Figure 3). The model was still used to obtain the time‐series profiles of these two components, and its applicability was verified in the ex situ validation below. The overall trend shows that the precursor solution is consumed concurrently with the phase transition, indicating that no level of supersaturation of precursor must be reached before crystallization occurs.

To validate the accuracy of the MLP model and to assure that it was not suffering from overfitting [60], external (ex situ) data validations were performed. Nine samples randomly selected from those enumerated in Figure 4 were washed and analyzed by SAXS. SAXS was used to determine volume fraction of solid in each sample, thereby providing a measurement of particle concentration at the various residence times. Aliquots of each sample were placed in capillaries; each sample was irradiated for 15 min on a laboratory SAXS instrument, producing a scattering pattern for each sample (Figure 5a). Using the Porod invariant, the particle concentration of each sample was calculated. Porod invariant was chosen to fit the data because it estimates the mass concentration of the sample on a size‐independent framework, and since these MoC1– x NPs are highly anisotropic, size‐independent analysis is preferred. In comparing the SAXS‐validated concentration with ML‐predicted concentrations for total particles, MLP predictions showed a satisfactory mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 9% (Figure 5b). From Figure 5b, we calculated the standard deviation of the prediction errors (SAXS measurements ‐MLP measurements), or residuals, which represents the uncertainty of the MLP predictions for total particle concentration measurements, and the resulting uncertainty is ±2.75 mg/mL.

While MLP was chosen and utilized as the best ML model for this dataset, other ML models were put through validation just to compare the results and variances between ML models. While the BLR model was the second‐best performing model during training, it produced a large MAPE (542%) during ex situ validation (Figure S8). This discrepancy likely arises from model misspecification and distributional differences between the samples in training and samples in real reactions that contained byproducts omitted in the washed training samples. First, BLR assumes a linear mapping between spectral inputs and concentrations, which may not adequately capture the nonlinear extinction effects present in the synthesis reaction. Second, the limited training dataset may have led to overfitting, even within a Bayesian framework, particularly if the chosen priors were weakly informative. In short, the training and real reaction datasets may not come from the same distribution, and this distributional shift magnified the overfitting issue. In contrast, the MLP demonstrates strong generalizability and resilience to data shifts. These potential variations from different batches of datasets highlight why it is crucial to test a variety of ML models on the experimental datasets to achieve better accuracy.

The MLP‐predicted concentrations of the amorphous and crystalline fractions were further validated by ex situ XRD. As shown in Figure 5c, the diffraction patterns for samples at early residence times indicate an amorphous material; because all samples were washed prior to the ex situ analysis, this was not simply a transient soluble species but a stable intermediate leading to crystalline MoC1− x NPs. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was also carried out on the collected samples, and the mass loss difference between the early and longer residence time samples was only 20%, suggesting that the collected amorphous material is not simply crosslinked organic material but a distinct inorganic material that has a different mass loss profile compared to pure Mo(CO)6 or OAm (Figure S9). Typically, in XRD, Rietveld refinement is used to quantify crystalline vs. amorphous content from powder patterns; however, since these particles are small, leading to significant Scherrer broadening, reliable refinement is not possible for MoC1− x NPs. The validated MLP‐measured concentration profiles then allowed us to investigate the reaction mechanism and kinetics for the precursor decomposition of Mo(CO)6 into α‐MoC1− x . In nanoparticle formation, typical behavior following nucleation can be described as first‐order intraparticle conversion or second‐order interparticle coalescence. Autocatalytic growth is another pathway in which the formed particles catalyze further particle growth [27]. Regardless of the type of particle growth, it is also crucial to determine whether the process is controlled by surface reaction or diffusion. We used two approaches to solve for k 1 and k 2: (1) the classic kinetic rate law and (2) the kinetic Monte Carlo (kMC) method as cross‐validation. The classic rate law translates the mechanism into first‐order differential rate equations (Equation (3)), where k 1 and k 2 are either solved analytically or obtained via best fitting of the concentration profiles [62].

Compared to the macroscopic rate law, the Gillespie‐driven kMC method is a microscopic algorithm that simulates the temporal stochastic evolution of species (i.e., number concentrations) in a chemical system [63]. Normally, the kMC method outputs time‐series particle number profiles with known microscopic rate coefficients. Here, we used the reverse process to find the best‐fit k 1 and k 2 that minimized the error between the kMC‐simulated concentrations and the MLP‐measured concentrations (see Experimental Procedures for details). The classic rate law showed slightly better fitting in terms of MAPE than the kMC method (Table 2 and Figure 4; Figure S10), and both methods concluded that k 2 is approximately twice as large as k 1, suggesting that precursor conversion to the amorphous intermediate is slower and therefore the rate‐determining step.

With the overall step mechanism determined, an in‐depth investigation of nucleation and growth was performed. At early residence times (<160 s), the precursor conversion increased steadily, accompanied by a steep increase in both total particle yield and particle size, as determined by SAXS in the ex situ validation (Figure 6). This indicates rapid nucleation and growth of small amorphous intermediate particles that quickly incorporate precursor. At longer residence times (>180 s), when precursor conversion approached completion (Figure 6a) and the total particle yield began to plateau (Figure 6b), the particle size correspondingly also plateaued. At this point, the amorphous‐to‐crystalline phase transition was still actively ongoing, as evidenced by the increasing ML‐informed CD and the increasing intensity ratio of the first two reflections in the powder XRD patterns (Figure 5c,d). These results imply that the phase transition to final crystalline MoC1− x NPs proceeds through internal crystallization rather than through continuous monomer incorporation or consumption. Moreover, over the entirety of the reaction, the Porod slope increased from −2 to −4 (Figure 5a), reflecting a transformation from diffuse, rough particles to more compact crystals [64, 65].

Additionally, the second step in Equation (2) was modeled as a second‐order process, representing a coalescence‐driven nucleation mechanism, i.e., two Amorph. Int. to two Cryst. NP (Figure S13). The larger MAPE (0.268 from the rate law) and poorer fit for the second‐order step further suggest that rather than interparticle coalescence, crystalline NPs are transformed from the amorphous intermediate via intraparticle transition. Taken together, these observations support a final intraparticle crystallization step in which initially loosely packed, partially ordered NPs collapse into compact crystalline MoC1− x NPs.

To determine whether the NP formation process is diffusion‐ or reaction‐controlled, a Damköhler number (Da, Equation (5)) and the Avrami equation were used to quantify the dominant process. For the precursor conversion, a Da value of 10−12 was calculated, where any value less than one indicates a strongly reaction‐controlled regime [66]. For the intraparticle crystallization, we fitted the Avrami equation to the particle size data obtained from the ex situ SAXS analysis (Figure 7) [67]. The fitted Avrami exponent n was found to be 3.18 (R 2 = 0.89), indicating also a reaction‐controlled process with three‐dimensional growth [68].

Conclusion

We implemented a continuous‐flow reaction system to synthesize catalytically relevant α‐MoC1‐ x NPs under mild, solution‐based conditions, where downstream in‐line spectroscopic monitoring enabled the capture of time‐resolved reaction profiles. While translating the convoluted spectral patterns into reaction mechanisms and kinetics can be mathematically intensive due to their nonlinearity to the corresponding concentration profiles, ML algorithms effectively establish the connections to deconvolute the problem. We evaluated a panel of ML models for predicting compositional concentrations during NP synthesis, and the MLP algorithm stood out with the best accuracy, as validated by external SAXS and XRD analyses.

Based on the MLP‐measured conversion and yield data, kinetic models were developed, indicating that the MoC1− x formation proceeds through a two‐step consecutive reaction mechanism, with the conversion of Mo(CO)6 and OAm precursor solution to an amorphous intermediate constituting the rate‐determining step, followed by the phase transition to crystalline NPs. Analyses of the ex situ SAXS and XRD patterns and resulting particle sizes corroborate this two‐step mechanism, in which the precursor first converts into an amorphous intermediate that subsequently crystallizes into MoC1− x nanoparticles via intraparticle crystallization and reorganization. Each step of the reaction was found to follow a reaction‐controlled behavior. These mechanistic insights provide a foundation for understanding the formation of transition metal carbide NPs and enable further tailoring of product quality for catalytic applications, such as control over particle size.

Importantly, ML has transformed modern chemistry by providing accurate and informative reaction details in a cost‐ and time‐effective manner, serving as an alternative to traditional instrumental approaches that require trained operators, synchrotron sources, and/or complex data interpretation. Moving toward fully automated and autonomous chemistry, self‐driving laboratories integrated with ML offer a path to optimize reaction outcomes and accelerate scientific discovery [69].

Experimental Section

Materials

Mo(CO)6 (99.9%) and oleylamine (OAm, technical grade 70%) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich and used as received. Hexanes and acetone were purchased from VWR and used as received.

Reagent Preparation

Mo(CO)6 (2.48 g, 9.35 mmol) is added to a glass vial charged with a stir bar and 15 mL of OAm. The solution is placed in a sand bath with the lid loosely sitting on the vial and heated to 100°C for 30 min. After 30 min, the solution is a homogenous orange color with some Mo(CO)6 on the edges of the vial above the solvent level as Mo(CO)6 is known to sublime out of solution [70]. The solution is allowed to cool naturally and caution is used to extract the orange precursor solution into a syringe without incorporating the sublimed Mo(CO)6.

Continuous‐Flow Processes

The reagents were infused by programmable pumps (Legato OED, KD Scientific) into PTFE tubing (I.D. 1/32 in, Cole‐Parmer) for further processing. For the flow synthesis (Figure S1), the reactant mixture stream was delivered to a quartz tube (I.D. 2 mm) positioned on the channel of a straight‐path copper‐plate reactor heated to 300°C for the reaction to proceed. The quartz tube was extended through a water bath at room temperature to thermally quench the reaction stream. The outlet of the quartz tube was connected to a flow sensor (FLOW UNIT, Fluigent) to measure the residence time. A three‐way valve downstream directed the flow either to a waste container or a collection container. The entire system was pressurized to 20 psig under nitrogen. For the flow dilution preparation used in ML training, the reagents were delivered to two 3D‐printed microfluidic mixers (GR‐10 Resin, Pro3dure) set in series (Figure S2). For in‐line detection, the stream was directed to a borosilicate capillary (I.D. 1.12 mm, WPI) housed in a 3D‐printed block (Black F1+ Resin, FunToDo) connected to a xenon light source (PX‐2, Ocean Insight) and a miniature spectrophotometer (Flame‐S‐UV‐VIS‐ES, Ocean Insight). To minimize the effect of random fluctuations on the reaction system, 30 consecutive spectra were captured at each data point, and the final resulting spectrum was the average of the 30 spectra. The flow process was controlled by custom Python scripts.

Product Workup

After the desired amount of product is collected (typically 1 mL), the black product is resuspended in <5 mL of hexanes and sonicated to help solubilize the product. The hexanes product mixture is split between two centrifuge tubes and allowed to bath sonicate for 2 min. Acetone (∼40 mL) is added to each centrifuge tube to facilitate precipitation of the amorphous and crystalline products. The centrifuge tubes are vortex mixed and then sonicated for 3 min before being centrifuged (6000 rpm, 5 min). The supernatant was discarded, and the resulting NP pellet was resuspended in a known amount of hexanes (typically 1 mL).

ML Model Hyperparameters

In MLP, the model consisted of two hidden layers (512 and 256 neurons) with ReLU activation and a 0.2 dropout after the first layer. The Adam optimizer (learning rate = 0.0001) and MSE loss were used for backpropagation, trained for up to 1000 epochs with early stopping after 400 epochs of no improvement. In LRR, ridge regularization was applied. In KNN, principal component analysis (PCA) was first conducted to reduce the high‐dimensional spectral data to five principal components (PCs) to mitigate the curse of dimensionality, and five neighbors were used for regression. In SVM, the kernel, regularization parameter (C), and epsilon were set to “rbf”, 10, and 0.1, respectively. In XGB, the number of estimators, learning rate, and max depth were 100, 0.25, and 3, respectively. In BLR, a Bayesian ridge model was used. The hyperparameters were identified with the assistance of a grid search strategy.

ML Model Training

The training set was first shuffled and then split into ten folds for cross‐validation. The spectral data and concentration labels were preprocessed using a MinMaxScaler for normalization. To prevent data leakage and ensure that validation sets remained unseen by the model during training, the scaler was fitted to the training set before being used to normalize the validation sets. To ensure independence, scalers were recalculated for each fold of the ten‐fold cross‐validation. After cross‐validation, the models were retrained on the entire training set. The algorithm outputs were inverse‐transformed to concentrations in their original units using the same MinMaxScaler. The MLP model was implemented in PyTorch. LRR, KNN, SVM, and BLR were implemented using Scikit‐learn, with SVM and BLR wrapped by a MultiOutputRegressor for multiple outputs. The XGB model was implemented using XGBoost.

Non‐ML Method

The extinction intensity (I ext) at a single wavelength (395 nm for the precursor solution and 600 nm for the total particles, amorphous, and crystalline particles) was selected for all samples in the training set to fit the equation of I ext, i = KiCi , where Ki is the coefficient representing the Beer‐Lambert law or scattering law for component i. Once Ki was obtained, the equation was used to calculate concentrations from the measured I ext, i .

Small‐Angle X‐Ray Scattering (SAXS)

The washed NP samples were all resuspended in 1 mL of hexanes and then 0.1 mL of this suspension was loaded into a precision quartz capillary with a needle and syringe (Charles Supper, 1.5 mm OD, 80 mm length, 0.01 mm wall thickness). The samples were placed in a capillary holder and irradiated for 15 min in a Xenocs Xeuss 3.0 SAXS instrument with a Genix 3D Cu source and a Dectris EIGER2 R 1M detector. The instrument configuration was set using the option “MAXS standard” to give a q range of ∼0.017‐0.9 Å−1. The sample‐to‐detector distance was calibrated using a silver behenate standard and was determined to be 0.371 m. All samples had a blank hexanes background in the same quartz capillary subtracted. All data reduction and background subtraction were carried out in XSACT software from Xenocs. Absolute intensity was calibrated by inputting the wall thickness of the glass capillary when subtracting the background. The 2D small‐angle scattering patterns were reduced to 1D spectra using IgorPro with the Nika package from the Advanced Photon Source. The resulting 1D spectra were analyzed using SasView 6.1.0. Concentration was calculated from the Porod invariant within the concentration function in XACT. To determine particle size from SAXS data, the size distribution function within SasView was utilized with the following parameters held constant: ellipsoid shape with 1.2 aspect ratio, weight factor 8, q range: 0.0113‐0.72, contrast 61.1e−6 Å−2 (calculated from SasView SLD calculator) [71, 72].

Powder X‐ray Diffraction (XRD)

Powder X‐ray diffraction was carried out by a Rigaku Miniflex diffractometer using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.541 Å). Samples were resuspended in 1 mL of hexanes, sonicated, and then 2 drops of solution were dropcast on a zero‐diffraction silicon substrate and scanned at a rate of 3° min−1.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

The samples were resuspended in 1 mL of hexanes and sonicated before loading 0.25 mL into an alumina crucible, which was then placed within a TGA Q50 instrument and heated to 500°C with a ramping rate of 10°C min−1.

Concentration Measurements by ML

Spectral data of the reaction mixture were preprocessed using the same MinMaxScaler before being fed into the trained ML models. The model outputs were then inverse‐transformed by the scaler to obtain actual concentrations, which were subsequently corrected using the predicted concentrations of the blank solvent sample from the same model. The corrected concentrations were multiplied by a factor of 100 to represent the true values in the reaction.

Kinetic Modeling With Classic Rate Law

Ordinary differential equations (ODEs) were solved analytically for Equation (2), and the resulting solutions were fitted to the time‐series MLP‐measured concentrations to obtain the rate coefficient k. For Equation (2) paired with Equation (4) and Equation S1, the ODEs were fitted numerically to the time‐series concentrations to determine k using the solve_ivp module in SciPy in Python.

Kinetic Modeling With kMC Method and Optimization

A combination of k 1, k 2, and k 3 (optional) within the range [10−9, 1] was selected to perform the kMC simulation (100 000 particles assumed), following a previously reported method [63], to generate the simulated time‐series concentrations of precursor, amorphous intermediate, and crystalline NPs. The sum of squared errors (SSE) between the MLP‐measured and kMC‐simulated concentrations was then calculated. The differential_evolution module in SciPy (maxiter = 100, popsize = 15) was used to identify the optimal combination of k within the defined range that minimized the SSE globally.
