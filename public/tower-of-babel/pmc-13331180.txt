Case of spontaneous regression of DISH-associated retro-odontoid pannus following occipto-cervical fusion
Authors: André Luiz Girotto, Guilherme Afonso Ludwig, Camila Pereira de Souza, Guilherme da Rosa Raya, Alisson Roberto Teles
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Retro-odontoid pannus is a reactive fibrocartilaginous mass that develops posterior to the odontoid process, causing ventral compression of the cervicomedullary junction. Although classically associated with rheumatoid arthritis, it is increasingly recognized in non-inflammatory conditions (e.g., diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis [DISH]) driven by chronic atlantoaxial instability. Over the past two decades, posterior stabilization alone has emerged as the preferred first-line approach, inducing spontaneous pannus regression by eliminating pathological motion.Case Description: An 85-year-old male with DISH and extensive subaxial cervical ankylosis presented with severe occipital/cervical pain. The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a retro-odontoid soft-tissue mass that peripherally enhanced with gadolinium, resulting in cervicomedullary compression. Following an occipitocervical decompression/fusion (C0–C2), MRI 6 months later demonstrated spontaneous regression of the retro-odontoid mass.Conclusion: Posterior occipitocervical stabilization readily addresses degenerative retro-odontoid pannus formation attributed to DISH and may resorb following C0-C2 fusion.

INTRODUCTION

Retro-odontoid pannus is a fibrocartilaginous mass located posterior to the odontoid process that causes ventral spinal cord compression at the craniocervical junction.[11] As a reactive lesion rather than a true neoplasm, it is often referred to as retro-odontoid pseudotumor.[4]

Historically, treatment involved anterior resection followed by posterior stabilization, a strategy associated with significant morbidity.[1] More recently, posterior fusion alone has been shown to promote pannus regression and neurological improvement, becoming the preferred approach in most cases.[5,10,12]

Here, we report an 85-year-old male with a retro-odontoid pannus causing spinal cord compression that was successfully treated with posterior C0-C2 fixation, along with a short literature review.

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 85-year-old male was admitted for the evaluation of severe cervical and occipital pain, but was neurologically intact. The cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrated a retro-odontoid soft tissue mass extending into the atlantoaxial joint and foramen magnum; the peripheral gadolinium enhancement was consistent with a retro-odontoid pannus causing ventral cord compression at the cervicomedullary junction [Figure 1].

(C) Axial gadolinium-enhanced T1-weighted MRI showing peripheral enhancement of the retro-odontoid mass extending into the atlantoaxial joint. (D) Axial T2-weighted MRI at the C1–C2 level. The cervical computed tomography (CT) additionally demonstrated extensive anterior osteophytes/ankylosis in the subaxial spine consistent with diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH) [Figure 2]. The patient underwent a C0–C2 fixation with a C1–C2 decompressive laminectomy accompanied by bilateral C1 lateral mass and C2 pars-pedicle screw placement; an occipital plate was secured to the occipital bone, followed by the application of rods. An autologous bone graft was placed to promote arthrodesis.

Preoperative computed tomography of the cervical spine demonstrating extensive anterior osteophytes, consistent with diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis, resulting in marked cervical ankylosis.

Postoperatively, the patient’s clinical course was uneventful (i.e., occipital/cervical pain markedly improved, and the patient was discharged from the intensive care unit on postoperative day 1 and from the hospital on postoperative day 4). The postoperative cervical CT showed satisfactory positioning of the instrumentation and adequate bony decompression, including the foramen magnum enlargement provided by the C1–C2 laminectomy [Figure 3]. The MRI also confirmed adequate decompression [Figure 4]. Six months later, the MRI revealed continued reduction of the retro-odontoid pannus, with complete resolution of spinal cord compression [Figure 5].

DISCUSSION

Retro-odontoid pseudotumors are a reactive fibrocartilaginous proliferation posterior to the odontoid process driven by chronic atlantoaxial instability/altered cervical biomechanics. To contextualize our findings, we performed a brief review of the literature on retro-odontoid pannus, which is summarized in Table 1. The central pathophysiological mechanism involves repetitive microtrauma and abnormal motion at C1–C2, leading to hypertrophy of the transverse ligament and adjacent soft tissues with subsequent fibrocartilaginous metaplasia.[6,11]

Notably, multiple studies demonstrate that posterior atlantoaxial or occipitocervical fixation alone can induce spontaneous pannus regression by eliminating pathological motion.[3,8] Clinical and imaging studies indicate that patients with DISH are at increased risk for atlantoaxial instability and retro-odontoid pannus formation.[7,9] Posterior atlantoaxial stabilization eliminates pathological motion, thereby removing the mechanical stimulus responsible for reactive fibrocartilaginous proliferation. Here, we opted to include the occiput to increase construct strength for the C0-C2 fusion. Six months later, the pannus spontaneously regressed along with the cord compression. Previous studies have demonstrated that such regression may require up to several months to years following fusion.[2,12]

CONCLUSION

Posterior C0–C2 fixation was safely achieved in an 85-year-old male with DISH demonstrating significant retro-odontoid cord compression attributed to pannus formation; 6 months postoperatively, the MRI demonstrated spontaneous regression of the pannus with resolution of cord intrusion.
