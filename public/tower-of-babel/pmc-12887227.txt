Noninvasive Myocardial Work Predicts Long‐Term Postinterventional Outcomes in Patients With Primary Mitral Regurgitation
Authors: Yun Yang, Lingyun Fang, Yuji Xie, Wenqian Wu, He Li, Lin He, Huan Wang, Tezhi Alimu, Xin Zhang, Tan Liu, Jiatong Li, Ruize Zhang, Linyue Zhang, Yuting Tan, Shukun He, Yuman Li, Li Zhang, Jing Wang, Mingxing Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate and early identification of impaired left ventricular (LV) function is essential to the optimal timing of intervention for primary mitral regurgitation. Myocardial work derived from noninvasive pressure‐strain loop is a novel and promising afterload‐independent approach to evaluate LV performance. We hypothesized that it may provide diagnostic and prognostic utility in these patients. This study aimed to evaluate myocardial work parameters in patients with significant primary mitral regurgitation, and explore their association with postinterventional LV ejection fraction and clinical composite events.MethodsThe study prospectively enrolled 180 patients with severe primary mitral regurgitation at baseline and patients were followed up for postinterventional LV function (>12 months) and clinical events (24.0 [23.3–24.6] months). Logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed as appropriate.ResultsCompared with patients exhibiting postinterventional LV ejection fraction ≥50%, individuals with LV ejection fraction <50% demonstrated lower LV global longitudinal strain, global work index (GWI), global constructive work (GCW), and global work efficiency (P<0.001 for all) and higher global wasted work (P=0.001). Preinterventional LV global longitudinal strain, GWI, GCW, and global work efficiency were independent predictors of postinterventional LV dysfunction (P<0.05 for all). The predictive power of logistic regression models comprising LV global longitudinal strain, GWI, and GCW were similar but stronger than the model comprising LV ejection fraction. Preinterventional LV global longitudinal strain, GWI, GCW, and global work efficiency were independently associated with the risk of composite clinical events during follow‐up (P<0.001 for all).ConclusionsMyocardial work parameters, especially GWI, GCW, and global work efficiency, are independent predictors of postinterventional LV dysfunction and are associated with the occurrence of postinterventional clinical composite events.Keywords: echocardiography, mitral valve regurgitation, myocardial work, prognostic valueSubject Categories: Echocardiography, Imaging, Prognosis

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is one of the most common valvular heart diseases, and primary mitral regurgitation (PMR) accounts for 61% to 67%. 1 , 2 Chronic PMR can lead to progressive left ventricular (LV) remodeling and dysfunction and, subsequently, heart failure or other adverse cardiac events. 3 Accurate identification of impaired LV function at the early stage of PMR is essential for the prediction of follow‐up outcomes, including postinterventional LV impairment, 4 complications, and death, 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 so as to help choose the optimal timing of therapies. 10 Currently, in the clinical setting, evaluation of LV function in the population with MR primarily relies on LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and LV end‐systolic diameter (LVESD) as recommended by the latest guideline. 11 , 12 However, these parameters are dependent on LV geometry, heart rate, and volume status 13 and can overestimate the LV systolic function in patients with MR. 4 , 14 LV global longitudinal strain (LV GLS) has shown greater sensitivity to detect subclinical dysfunction than LVEF and LVESD. 15 , 16 However, it is important to note that LV GLS can be influenced by afterload, 4 , 17 , 18 potentially leading to misinterpretations of cardiac performance in conditions characterized by abnormal afterload including PMR. 17 , 19

Myocardial work derived from a noninvasive pressure‐strain loop is a novel afterload‐independent approach to evaluate LV performance 20 , 21 , 22 and can reflect myocardial glucose metabolism. 22 It may have the potential to evaluate the LV performance and predict the outcomes more concisely, and therefore, improve the identification of the intervention timing. This study aimed to evaluate LV myocardial work in patients with PMR and to explore its prognostic value for postinterventional outcomes.

METHODS

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for cohort studies. 23 The research was approved by the Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology Ethical Committee (UHCT‐IEC‐SOP‐016‐03‐01), and the requirement for informed consent from the patients was waived. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patients

Two hundred thirty‐six patients diagnosed with severe PMR and scheduled to undergo mitral valve intervention, encompassing both surgical and transcatheter interventions, were prospectively enrolled from June 2021 to April 2023. PMR refers to the disease condition that involves the pathological mitral valve apparatus (annulus, leaflets, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscles). 12 MR severity was graded according to the recommendation from the American Society of Echocardiography in Collaboration with the Society for Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance. 24 Patients who had not undergone an intervention, diagnosed with acute infective endocarditis, had concomitant atrial septal defect, had a history of previous mitral valve intervention, or presented with poor images that were difficult to analyze were excluded from the study. Finally, 180 patients were included at baseline. Participant flow chart was presented in Figure 1. Prospective collection of demographic information, clinical information, surgical data, and laboratory examination data was conducted through direct patient inquiry or electronic medical record retrieval.

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Echocardiographic examinations were performed using a GE Vivid E95 machine (GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway) equipped with an M5Sc‐D transducer (1.4–4.6 MHz). Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed based on the recommendations of the American Society of Echocardiography. 25 Blood pressure was collected by an electronic sphygmomanometer (Panasonic, EW3106, Newark, NJ, USA) at the same time as the echocardiographic examinations.

Conventional echocardiographic parameters were measured according to American and European recommendations for cardiac chamber quantification by echocardiography in adults, 26 including LV end‐diastolic diameter, LVESD, and left atrial diameter (LAD). LV end‐diastolic volume (LVEDV), LV end‐systolic volume, LVEF, and left atrial volume were measured by biplane Simpson’s method. The indexed echocardiographic parameters were calculated as the ratio of the parameters to the body surface area. Tricuspid annular peak velocity in systole was measured by pulsed‐wave tissue Doppler echocardiography from the lateral annulus of the tricuspid valve. Systolic pulmonary artery pressure (SPAP) was determined by the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient plus right atrial pressure.

Strain and Myocardial Work Analysis

The longitudinal strain was analyzed offline using an EchoPAC workstation (Version: 204, GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway). Apical 3‐chamber, 4‐chamber, and 2‐chamber view data sets were used for strain analysis. It should be noted that the analysis of the longitudinal strain was started from an apical 3‐chamber view to determine the onset of aortic valve closing. LV GLS was the average peak systolic longitudinal strain that occurred before the closure of the aortic valve. LV GLS in this study is reported as absolute values (% absolute) reflecting the magnitude of longitudinal shortening, with statistical models interpreting higher magnitudes as protective. Conventionally, raw strain values are inherently negative to denote myocardial shortening. Absolute values herein represent the numerical magnitude without directional sign (eg, an absolute value of 18% corresponds to a raw strain of −18%).

Myocardial work parameters were automatically calculated using a segmental speckle‐tracking strain approach through the EchoPAC workstation. Upon completion of longitudinal strain analysis, the “Myocardial Work” model was activated. Subsequently, the timing of mitral valve closure, aortic valve opening, aortic valve closure, and mitral valve opening were determined. These strains and timings were then combined with the corresponding blood pressure readings. Finally, by clicking the “approve” button, the global work index (GWI), global constructive work (GCW), global wasted work (GWW), global work efficiency (GWE), and LV pressure‐strain curve were obtained. Representative examples of cases are presented in Figure 2. In order to measure the LV GLS and myocardial work parameters in patients with atrial fibrillation, the beats with represented (ie, approximately average) heart rate were selected just as established study. 27

A reproducibility analysis was conducted on LV GLS and myocardial work parameters (GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE) in a cohort comprising 40 patients selected at random. Intraobserver reproducibility was assessed by using repeated analyses of the same echocardiographer (Y.Y.) at an interval of 1 month. Interobserver reproducibility was evaluated by comparing the analyses performed by a second echocardiographer (Z.Y.) blinded to the analysis results of the first observer.

Follow‐Up Data

The primary outcome of this study was postinterventional LV dysfunction more than 12 months after intervention. Postinterventional LV dysfunction was defined as postinterventional LVEF <50%. 28 , 29 LVEF was obtained from follow‐up echocardiogram. The secondary outcome was the composite clinical events including death, rehospitalization for heart failure, stroke, and reoperation. 15 , 16 , 30 , 31 Clinical outcomes were evaluated by direct patient inquiry, review of medical records and telephone interviews. Follow‐up was censored on April 30, 2024, and mean overall duration of follow‐up was 24 months.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as the means±SDs (x¯±s) or mean (lower quartile–upper quartile) (M [Q1–Q3]) as appropriate, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages). Histograms and Q–Q plots were used to determine whether the data were normally distributed. To compare the results between the 2 groups (LVEF ≥50% and LVEF <50%), Welch’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. Chi‐square test or Fisher’s exact test were used for categorical variables. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to identify parameters that possess predictive capabilities for the occurrence of LV dysfunction. Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to evaluate the prognostic value of preinterventional myocardial work parameters for composite clinical events after intervention. Before the modeling, linearity assumption for the relationships between the continuous predictors and each outcome were tested using spline smoothing plots (locally estimated scatterplot smoothing) (Figure S1), the likelihood ratio test (Table S1), restricted cubic spline (Figure 3), and partial residual plots (Figure S2). Variables satisfying the linearity assumption were retained as continuous linear terms in the final models. An exception was made for GWE, which demonstrated a marginally nonlinear relationship with LV dysfunction. Although modeled with restricted cubic splines for visualization (Figure 3), it was categorized into 3 clinically interpretable groups (≤90%, >90% to ≤95%, and >95%) for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression model. All continuous variables in the Cox regression models satisfied the linearity assumption and were thus entered as linear terms. The proportional hazards assumption was verified via Schoenfeld residuals for all Cox models. Visual inspection of scaled Schoenfeld residual plots for all covariates included in the models (Figure S3) further tested the validity of the assumption. Survival curves were constructed by Kaplan–Meier method and compared by log‐rank test to verify whether myocardial work parameters were associated with event‐free survival. During the modeling, guidelines‐recommended parameters (LVEF, LVESD, LAD, indexed left atrial volume [LAVI], and SPAP) were introduced as dichotomous variables based on cutoff values indicated (60%, 40 mm, 55 mm, 60 mL/m2, and 50 mm Hg, respectively). 11 , 12 Left atrium dilation was defined as LAD ≥55 mm or LAVI ≥60 mL/m2. 11 , 12 Multivariate regression models were constructed using a hybrid selection strategy. Variables with established clinical relevance, as defined by current guidelines 11 , 12 and expert consensus, were forced into the models. Additional exploratory variables that demonstrated univariable association with the outcome (P<0.10) were subsequently added. Model refinement was achieved through forward stepwise selection (Forward: Likelihood Ratio) and the parameters finally retained in the models were selected based on Akaike information criterion (AIC), ensuring both statistical rigor and clinical plausibility. AIC (AIC=−2LL+2k) and C‐index were used for the comparison among different models. In addition, both the logistic and Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were repeated when guidelines‐recommended parameters (LVEF, LVESD, LAD, LAVI, and SPAP) were retained as continuous variables, and in the subgroup with preinterventional LVEF ≥50%. Subgroup analyses were performed, with stratification by intervention types. The optimal cutoff values of LV GLS, GWI, and GCW were defined by receiver operating characteristic curve analysis. Given the minimal amounts of missing values (Table S2), missing data were addressed through listwise deletion. To verify both intraobserver and interobserver variability, the interclass correlation coefficient and Bland–Altman analysis were employed.

RESULTS

Patient Population

Baseline population clinical and intervention‐related characteristics of the 175 patients who underwent follow‐up echocardiogram (median age, 63 years; 64.6% male) were summarized in Table 1. Among these patients, the majority were in New York Heart Association functional class II or III (140, 80.0%), and presented with symptoms (116, 79.4%). Regarding the comorbidities, atrial fibrillation (49, 28.2%), hypertension (66, 37.7%), coronary artery disease (38, 21.7%), and renal insufficiency (19, 10.9%) were present. Preinterventional brain natriuretic peptide and N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide were 136.0 and 212.0 pg/mL. All patients underwent mitral valve intervention, including mitral valve replacement (85, 48.6%), mitral valve repair (43, 24.6%), and transcatheter mitral valve treatment (47, 26.9%). Concomitant surgeries included coronary artery bypass grafting (23, 13.1%), tricuspid valve repair (40, 23.0%) and surgical aortic valve replacement (12, 6.9%). The total mean duration of intervention was 246.0 (209.0–300.0) minutes. The durations of extracorporeal circulation, aortic clamping, and circulatory support were 120.0 (95.5–164.8) minutes, 79.0 (58.0–103.3) minutes, and 29.5 (24.0–42.0) minutes, respectively. The duration of intensive care unit stay and postinterventional hospitalization was 2.0 (2.0–3.0) days and 12.0 (8.0–17.0) days, respectively.

Comparison of Echocardiographic Parameters According to Postinterventional LVEF

Out of 146 patients, 22 (15.1%) had postinterventional LV dysfunction >12 months after intervention. Accordingly, the population were stratified into 2 groups (LVEF ≥50% and <50%). As shown in Figure 3, the novel parameters reflecting LV performance differed significantly between groups. Compared with patients with postinterventional LVEF ≥50%, the patients with postinterventional LVEF <50% had lower preinterventional LV GLS (13.4%±3.3% versus 21.0±4.2, P<0.001), lower preinterventional GWI (1233.0±332.3 mm Hg% versus 1924.9±444.1 mm Hg%, P<0.001), lower preinterventional GCW (1536.2±429.9 versus 2316.8±510.0 mm Hg%, P<0.001), and lower preinterventional GWE [90.0 (86.0, 94.0)% versus 96.0 (94.0, 97.0)%, P<0.001] with a higher preinterventional GWW [106.0 (78.0, 198.5) mm Hg% versus 75.0 (49.0, 109.0) mm Hg%, P=0.001].

Traditional echocardiographic parameters between the 2 groups also differed significantly (Table 2). Compared with patients with postinterventional LVEF ≥50%, the patients with postinterventional LVEF <50% had lower LVEF at inclusion [48.9 (38.4, 53.3) % versus 64.5 (60.4, 69.3) %, P<0.001] and higher proportions of LVEF ≤60% (86.4% versus 24.2%, P<0.001). In addition, patients with postinterventional LVEF <50% had a larger preinterventional LV size (all P<0.05), including LV end‐diastolic diameter, LVESD, the proportion of LVESD ≥40 mm, LVEDV, and LV end‐systolic volume. In addition, the proportion of LA dilation was higher in patients with a postinterventional LVEF <50% (72.7% versus 43.5%, P=0.012). Tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion and tricuspid annular peak velocity in systole in the group with postinterventional LVEF <50% were relatively lower (both P<0.05), and resting SPAP was similar between these 2 groups (P>0.05).

Determinants of Postinterventional LV Dysfunction

Logistic regression analysis was performed to predict LV dysfunction 12 months after intervention (Table 3). Preinterventional LV GLS (odds ratio [OR], 0.624 [95% CI, 0.521–0.749] per 1% increment of the absolute value), GWI (OR, 0.995 [95% CI, 0.993–0.997] per 1 mm Hg% increment), GCW (OR, 0.996 [95% CI, 0.995–0.998] per 1 mm Hg% increment), and GWE (P<0.001) were significantly associated with postinterventional LV dysfunction on univariate logistic analysis. Further predictors of postinterventional LVEF <50% in univariate logistic analysis were preinterventional LVEF ≤60%, LVESD ≥40 mm, and LA dilation. Model 1 was constructed by incorporating all clinical characteristics and echocardiographic parameters that met 2 criteria: P<0.10 in univariate logistic analysis or recommended by the latest guidelines. 11 , 12 LVESD ≥40 mm (adjusted OR [adjOR], 7.575 [95% CI, 2.331–24.623], P=0.001) and LVEF ≤60% (adjOR, 11.489 [95% CI, 2.989–44.155], P<0.001) remained in the model and were significant predictors of postinterventional LV dysfunction. LVEF was then separately replaced by LV GLS, GWI, GCW, and GWE in the multivariable logistic analysis, and correspondingly, models 2 to 5 were constructed. LV GLS (adjOR, 0.649 [95% CI, 0.534–0.788] per 1% increment of the absolute value, P<0.001), GWI (adjOR, 0.995 [95% CI, 0.993–0.997] per 1% increment, P<0.001), GCW (adjOR, 0.996 [95% CI, 0.995–0.998] per 1% increment, P<0.001) and GWE (adjOR, 49.394 [95% CI, 5.302–460.196]) for ≤90% versus >95% could independently predict postinterventional LV dysfunction. As demonstrated in Table 3, compared with the guideline‐based model (Model 1, C‐index=0.874), models incorporating LV GLS, GWI, GCW, and GWE (Models 2–5) showed substantially improved discriminative capacity (ΔC‐index=+0.049 to +0.076) while reducing model complexity (ΔAIC=−20.423 to −17.797) except for GWE. Notably, GWI (Model 3) and LV GLS (Model 2) exhibited comparable performance, GCW (Model 4) provided slightly lower but still clinically meaningful improvement. Each 100 mm Hg% increase in GWI or GCW was associated with 33% to 40% risk reduction. GWE ≤90% was significantly associated with postinterventional LV dysfunction. Multivariate analysis adjusted for New York Heart Association classification, renal insufficiency, types of interventions, and concomitant procedures showed similar results (Table S3).

Association of Baseline Myocardial Work With Clinical Outcomes

During a median follow‐up of 24.0 (interquartile range, 23.3–24.6) months after intervention in the cohort of 175 patients (median age 62.5 years, 64.6% male), composite clinical events occurred in 28 patients, corresponding to a cumulative incidence of 16.0%. Cox proportional hazard regression models (Table 5) demonstrated that GWI (adjusted hazard ratio [adjHR], 0.999 [95% CI, 0.998–1.000] per 1 mm Hg% increment, P=0.012), GCW (adjHR, 0.999 [95% CI, 0.998–1.000] per 1 mm Hg% increment, P=0.002), and GWE (adjHR, 0.963 [95% CI, 0.931–0.995] per 1% increment, P=0.025) were associated with the increase of risk of composite clinical events during follow‐up. Myocardial work parameters enhanced prognostic stratification versus the conventional model (Model 1, C‐index=0.783). GCW (Model 4) and LV GLS (Model 2) showed the most substantial improvements (ΔC‐index=+0.039 to +0.049, ΔAIC=−3.742 to −3.140). Each 100 mm Hg% increase in GWI or GCW conferred 9.5% risk reduction. Kaplan–Meier survival curves analyses revealed that preinterventional myocardial work parameters and their optimal cutoff values were significantly associated with postinterventional event‐free survival (log‐rank P<0.001 for all comparisons; Figure 6).

Reproducibility

Intra‐ and interagreements in myocardial work measurements were good. The intraobserver interclass correlation coefficients for GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE were 0.99 (0.98–1.00), 0.99 (0.98–1.00), 0.83 (0.70–0.91) and 0.97 (0.95–0.99), respectively. The interobserver interclass correlation coefficients were 0.99 (0.97–1.00), 0.99 (0.98–1.00), 0.95 (0.90–0.97), and 0.98 (0.96–0.99), respectively. The intraobserver mean biases for GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE were 50 mm Hg%, 50 mm Hg%, 2 mm Hg%, and 0.3%, respectively. The interobserver mean biases for GWI, GCW, GWW, and GWE were 45 mm Hg%, 41 mm Hg%, 5 mm Hg%, and 0.0%, respectively (Table 6, Figure 7).

DISCUSSION

Main Findings

The main findings of this study can be summarized as follows: (1) myocardial work parameters (GWI, GCW, and GWE) are independent predictors of postinterventional LV dysfunction; (2) worse GWI, GCW, and GWE are associated with an increase of risk of clinical events during follow‐up; (3) each 100 mm Hg% increase in GWI/GCW conferred substantial protection against ventricular dysfunction (33%–40% risk reduction; OR, 0.995–0.996) and composite clinical events (9.5% risk reduction; HR, 0.999); and (4) these findings demonstrate remarkable consistency across validation subgroups.

Characteristics of Myocardial Work Parameters in Patients With PMR

Compared with healthy adults, 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 the GWI of patients with chronic PMR was lower. It has been demonstrated that increased oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte degeneration occur in patients with chronic isolated MR and LVEF >60%. 36 , 37 Additionally, in patients with chronic PMR, long‐term volume overload can lead to LV remodeling, and LV dilation, as observed in patients in our study. Dilated LV size increases LV wall stress according to Laplace’s law, 38 which ultimately results in myocardial damage. Therefore, GWI is reduced because of the impaired contractile performance of cardiomyocytes, demonstrating the insufficiency of LVEF measurements. 37 , 39 Moreover, compared with that of healthy adults, 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 GWW could be similar 35 or higher. 32 GWW is a measure of energy loss, 20 , 33 , 37 its increase may be mediated through LV enlargement, loss of mechanical synchrony, 20 , 37 and the development of myocardial fibrosis. 40 , 41 Furthermore, compared with that of healthy adults, 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 the GWE was lower in our study. GWE indicates the ratio of GCW to the sum of GCW and GWW, and it will decrease as GWW increases.

Myocardial Work and Prediction of Postinterventional Outcomes

Our study demonstrated that GWI, GCW, and GWE can independently predict postinterventional LV dysfunction and be assoiated with the occurrence of clinical events during follow‐up. The possible mechanisms of the predictive value of myocardial work parameters in patients with PMR may be as follows. Initially, noninvasive myocardial work parameters derived from a pressure‐strain loop can sensitively identify subtle myocardial impairment resulting from increased oxidative stress and cardiomyocyte degeneration 42 despite their LVEF remaining >60%. 36 , 37 Furthermore, myocardial work depends less on afterload because LV pressure was considered. 22 Third, the pressure‐strain loop area reflects regional glucose metabolism as assessed by 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography 22 and can be correlated well with stroke work or oxygen consumption. 43 , 44 The investigation of LV myocardial work parameters and their predictive values is ongoing. However, publications regarding the predictive value of myocardial work in patients with PMR are so limited, except for Althunayyan et al.’s study. 29 They found that GWI and GCW are significantly associated with postoperative LVEF <50% in univariate logistic analysis, which is consistent with our finding. However, GWI and GCW was not identified significant in the multivariable analysis. This may be attributed to the different way in which multivariable logistic regression models were constructed between the studies. Furthermore, the independent inverse associations between myocardial work parameters (GWI, GCW, GWE) and clinical composite events suggest that myocardial work may serve as predictors to postinterventional outcomes, but the predictive value was similar to that of LV GLS. This can partially be explained by the compensated state of patients included in our study. Most patients in our study had not reached the uncompensated state when the afterload could increase. 19 Thus, the advantages of afterload‐independent myocardial work indices were not demonstrated. Regression analysis retaining guideline‐recommended echocardiographic parameters as continuous variables, as well as the subgroup analyses, confirmed the predictive utility of myocardial work parameters for both postinterventional LV dysfunction and clinical composite outcomes. The mitral valve repair subgroup was not suitable to construct a model or exhibited nearly nonsignificant predictive performance, likely attributable to three interrelated limitations: (1) restricted sample size (n=43, 24.6% of cohort), (2) stringent clinical selection patterns, and (3) extreme outcome event sparsity (only 2 cases with postinterventional LVEF <50% and 1 case with clinical composite outcomes after intervention). Notably, directional consistency across all subgroups support GWI, GCW, and GWE as potential transinterventional risk stratification parameters. What is more, in patients with functional MR, Verbeke et al. 45 found that GWI was independently associated with cardiovascular events, but GWI did not outperform LV GLS, findings that are consistent with ours’. Moreover, Yedidya et al. 46 found that GWE was associated with forward flow reserve index improvement after intervention. Overall, myocardial work is promising, and further studies are desperately needed in the future to further research the value of myocardial work parameters in patients with PMR.

The reproducibility of myocardial work parameters was excellent, increasing its promise in clinical practice.

Clinical Implications

Although indications of treatments for PMR are recommended by current guidelines, 11 , 12 the optimal time of surgical intervention is still controversial. 47 The main challenge lies in assessing LV function sensitively and precisely. When evaluating the LV contractile function status of patients with PMR, myocardial work derived from the pressure‐strain loop is more sensitive (compared with LVESD and LVEF), less load dependent (compared with LVEF and LV GLS), and less invasive (compared with invasive myocardial work). Based on our findings, myocardial work parameters (particularly GWI, GCW, and GWE) may serve as promising indices to identify subclinical changes in LV function in the population with significant PMR and provide predictive value for postinterventional outcomes. With more evidence published in the near future, myocardial work parameters may help the clinical decision‐making and further improve the outcomes of patients with PMR.

Limitations

There were a few limitations in this study. First, we included transcatheter intervention patients (n=47, 126.9% of cohort) to enhance clinical generalizability, this introduces inherent heterogeneity in intervention approaches. However, subgroup analyses by intervention type (Tables S4 and S8) consistently demonstrated that myocardial work parameters (GWI, GCW, GWE) were predictive of postinterventional outcomes. Second, this was a single‐center study. The moderate sample size and event rates may increase susceptibility to overfitting. The findings need to be validated rigorously in subsequent research, including internal validation (expanded recruitment at our center with bootstrap validation), external validation (multicenter collaboration to assess generalizability), and prospective implementation. Third, the availability of noninvasive myocardial work analysis is contingent upon the quality of the image and precision of speckle tracking. 22 , 48 Last, myocardial work parameters derived from the pressure‐strain loop were not the actual measurement of LV work but just an index of global and regional work. For noninvasive myocardial work based on a pressure‐strain loop, LV pressure was regarded as a substitute for force, and LV pressure was considered to be equal to brachial artery pressure. 22 However, myocardial work and oxygen consumption can be presented by a force‐dimension loop or pressure‐volume loop. 49 LV force should be calculated as LV pressure × LV radius/(2 × wall thickness) according to Laplace’s law. The pressure‐strain loop does not take the LV radius and wall thickness into consideration, which may be improved when myocardial work can be measured by 3‐dimensional imaging in the future. 21

CONCLUSIONS

Myocardial work can assess the LV function of patients with PMR and provide additional value in the prediction of postinterventional outcomes.
