Leveraging Language Models and Bandit Algorithms to Drive Adoption of Battery-Electric Vehicles
Keiichi Namikoshi, David A. Shamma, Rumen Iliev, Jingchao Fang

Abstract
Behavior change interventions are important to coordinate societal action across a wide array of important applications, including the adoption of electrified vehicles to reduce emissions. The work of ( Harinen et al. 2022 ) has demonstrated that interventions for behavior must be personalized, and that the intervention that is most effective on average across a large group can result in a backlash effect that strengthens opposition among some subgroups. Thus, it is important to target interventions to different audiences, and to present them in a natural, conversational style. In this context, an important emerging application domain for large language models (LLMs) is conversational interventions for behavior change. In this work, we leverage prior work on understanding values motivating the adoption of battery electric vehicles. We leverage new advances in LLMs, combined with a contextual bandit, to develop conversational interventions that are personalized to the values of each study participant. We use a contextual bandit algorithm to learn to target values based on the demographics of each participant. To train our bandit algorithm in an offline manner, we leverage LLMs to play the role of study participants. We benchmark the persuasive effectiveness of our bandit-enhanced LLM against an unaided LLM generating conversational interventions without demographic-targeted values. We study the ability of LLMs to model attitudes in our target domain by reproducing a recent study on attitudes towards Battery Electric Vehicles (BEVs) ( Arechiga et al. 2022 ) , and find that LLMs are biased to prefer environmentally friendly BEVs more strongly than the typical human.



Introduction

Large language models (LLMs)
(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
unlock a wide array of applications in which machines have conversations with humans. One of the most explored applications is surfacing important information
(
OpenAI 2022
;
Singhal et al. 2023
;
Kasneci et al. 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023b
)
, but comparatively little work exists in leveraging LLMs to generate behavioral interventions with social impact. In this work, we consider the problem of conversational interventions for behavior change in the context of driving adoption of Battery-Electric Vehicles (BEVs).

The automotive industry is interested in shifting from internal combustion engine vehicles to BEVs for carbon neutrality
(
IEA 2023
)
.
Due to the significant differences between EVs and internal combustion engine vehicles, multiple technological and social concerns, including range, battery life, and charging infrastructure, can inhibit the shift
(
Bujold, Williamson, and Thulin 2020
;
Giansoldati, Monte, and Scorrano 2020
)
.
Therefore, it is important to verify what kind of information presentation is effective in addressing customer concerns toward BEVs to drive adoption of this impactful new technology. The work of
(
Harinen et al. 2022
)
has noted that interventions must be targeted in order to achieve maximum effectiveness due to differences in response among different subgroups. Different groups respond differently to the same intervention to such an extent that the intervention that is most effective on average across a large population may in fact induce a backlash effect in subgroups. In the presence of these
heterogeneous treatment effects
, it is possible to attain much higher intervention effectiveness by targeting different interventions to different subgroups. The work of
(
Arechiga et al. 2022
)
considers this problem, and uses short text interventions that are targeted at subgroups induced by gender and age.
In this work, we consider the use
of LLMs coupled with a contextual bandit algorithm to present conversational interventions to a prospective customer. The bandit algorithm learns to target high-level concepts to different demographic groups to target persuasive arguments. These high-level concepts are then used to prompt an LLM in conversation with the human customer.

Surveys with human subjects, however, can be time-consuming and costly. Scale and cost are disincentives for areas that require large amounts of data, such as large-scale surveys combined with machine learning. In this work, we build on recent work demonstrating the effectiveness of LLMs to display human-like behavioral characteristics
(
Safdari et al. 2023
)
as well as strong correlations with human preferences
(
Dubois et al. 2023
)
. We use LLMs prompted with demographic characteristics to serve as surrogate models for human survey participants. We evaluate the performance of this approach by replicating the survey scenario of
(
Arechiga et al. 2022
)
and comparing the most effective interventions. We consider rank correlation of overall intervention effectiveness, compare the skew of the distribution of preferences, and evaluate the KL-divergence of LLM preferences against real human preferences. Our contributions include:

1.

Development of a conversational intervention tool. We combine LLMs with a bandit algorithm that learns to match demographics to values, which can then be
used by the LLM to construct targeted conversational interventions. We train our intervention system on virtual survey participants, embodied by an LLM.

2.

To estimate the accuracy of the LLM’s embodiment of consumer attitudes towards BEVs, we replicate the survey in
(
Arechiga et al. 2022
)
to quantify the degree of agreement between real humans and virtual survey participants as embodied by LLMs.

We evaluate our numerical metrics against two LLMs and also investigate the role of sampling temperature.

Related work

Large language models for behavior change

Previous work proposed leveraging LLMs to produce attitude and behavior change. For example,
(
Sharma et al. 2023
)
used LLMs to generate interventions to assist people in overcoming negative thoughts.
(
Ashida and Komachi 2022
)
explored the potential of applying LLMs for counternarrative generation to combat online hate speech and microaggressions. In the medical domain,
(
Ali 2023
)
probed the possibility of leveraging LLMs to act as personalized virtual assistants and provide tailored healthcare suggestions to bariatric surgery patients.

(
Chen et al. 2022
)
contributed a framework that uses LLMs to support persuasive dialogue systems for social good. To the best of our knowledge, the role that LLMs can play in stimulating behavior change in the context of carbon neutrality has not been studied. In contrast with this related work, our system combines
LLMs with a contextual bandit that learns values relevant to
different survey participants, and is able to direct the LLM to generate relevant text.

Large language models as models of behavioral study participants

The possibilities of simulating humans and human behaviors using LLMs, or role-play
(
Shanahan, McDonell, and Reynolds 2023
;
Wu et al. 2023a
)
, have been discussed in recent years. By applying established psychometrics,
(
Safdari et al. 2023
)
demonstrated that LLMs can reliably simulate personalities and that LLM-generated personality traits can be shaped and controlled to imitate specific personality profiles.
(
Dillion et al. 2023
)
showed strong alignment between GPT-3.5 and humans in moral judgments, with a correlation of 0.95. In addition, LLM-based generative agents, when organized as a collective in an interactive sandbox environment, were found to be able to produce believable behaviors not only on an individual level but also on a social level
(
Park et al. 2023
)
.

LLMs’ ability to generate human-like personalities, judgments, and behaviors hints at the opportunity of constructing synthetic human participants in behavioral studies. Several recent works show initial attempts in this direction.
(
Aher, Arriaga, and Kalai 2023
)
applied LLMs to simulate human subjects and found that they can reproduce three out of four economic, psycholinguistic, and social psychology experiments and replicate findings from prior studies with real human participants.

(
Hämäläinen, Tavast, and
Kunnari 2023
)
evaluated LLMs’ potential of generating synthetic human-computer interaction research data in the form of open-ended questionnaire responses and revealed their capability of generating plausible, human-like self-report data regarding subjective experiences. Aligned with this line of work, our study harnesses LLMs’ power of role-playing human survey respondents on a large scale to train our contextual bandit algorithm.

Consumer preferences towards battery-electric vehicles

A body of literature evaluates the share of BEVs and factors influencing consumer intention. On an individual basis, the motivations of drivers’ EVs acceptance is influenced by perceived usefulness, satisfaction, and cost
(
Park, Lim, and Cho 2018
)
, and the barriers to a wider EVs diffusion include limited number of charging stations, range anxiety, purchasing cost, insufficient maintenance services, etc.
(
Giansoldati, Monte, and Scorrano 2020
)
. In market dimensions, the share of EVs is influenced by relative pricing of EVs as compared to conventional internal combustion engine vehicles, driving range, and variety of available models in the market
(
Kim, Lee, and Lee 2017
)
.
In this paper, we investigate how text-based interventions, generated by LLMs coupled with a contextual bandit algorithm, may be able to shape consumers’ preferences towards BEVs.

Contextual multi-armed bandit problem

The contextual multi-armed bandit problem is a decision problem in uncertain but stationary environments.
In this environment, the agent may observe context
x
and select one of
K
arms, where the set of possible arms is denoted by
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{K}\}
. Each context and arm has an associated
reward distribution

p(r|x,a)
, where
r
is a scalar.
At step
t\in\{0,\dots,T\}
, the agent chooses an arm
a
on context
x
and observes a reward
r_{t}\sim p(r|x,a)
.
The goal of the learning problem is to learn a distribution
\pi(a|x)
over the contexts and arms that maximize the cumulative reward experienced by the agent in a series of steps. This problem statement encounters the well known
exploration-exploitation dilemma
, in which the agent must trade off between exploring actions it has not tried (and which may yield high reward) against exploiting the best observed action.
A well-known algorithm to balance exploration and exploitation in context-free bandits is the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) algorithm
(
Auer 2000
)
and this algorithm can be extended to the contextual setting
(
Slivkins 2022
)
. The method
consists of selecting the action
a
that maximizes Equation
1
.

\mathrm{UCB}^{\rm{contextual}}_{t}(x,a)=\bar{r}_{t}(x,a)+\sqrt{\frac{2\log(t)}{n_{t}(x,a)}}.

(1)

Above,

n_{t}(x,a)
is the number of times that
a
has been selected on
x
prior to
t
, and
\bar{r}_{t}(x,a)
is the empirical average of the rewards that have been observed when
a
was selected on
x
prior to
t
.

Problem setting: Preferences for BEVs

Behavior change is expected to play an important role in combating environmental challenges
(
Bujold, Williamson, and Thulin 2020
)
. Behavioral interventions which were originally developed to improve physical health, mental well-being, educational achievements, and other outcomes are increasingly being applied to resource conservation and climate action
(
Williamson et al. 2018
)
. Attitudes are considered by many as the core component at which behavior interventions should be targeted
(
Petty and Cacioppo 2018
)
.

An important component in the global effort towards carbon neutrality is the electrification of consumer vehicles
(
Williamson et al. 2018
;
Shukla et al. 2022
)
.
Automotive manufacturers are developing a portfolio of different products which leverage batteries to reduce carbon emissions, including full BEVs but also including plugin-hybrid EVs, which combine a small battery, sufficient for full electric operation in short trips, with an internal combustion engine that can provide extended range.
In this work, we consider only attitudes towards BEVs due to the availability of human survey data
(
Arechiga et al. 2022
)
. In future work, we may consider extensions to other carbon-reducing behavioral interventions.

Existing work has found that although consumers are generally interested in BEVs, they still perceive challenges and barriers that preclude widespread adoption
(
Giansoldati, Monte, and Scorrano 2020
)
.

Our intervention system presents interventions in the form of text.
For example, the work of
(
Kihiczak and Ranney 2023
)
surveyed the effects of text interventions in the area of climate change, although we focus to intervene in preferences for BEVs.
We measure preference towards BEVs as a self-reported scalar metric on a scale from 0 to 100, with 0 indicating low preference for BEVs and 100 indicating high preference. Our experiments first measure the preference to a survey participant, and then measure it again after an intervention has been presented. The goal of the persuasion system is to produce a positive shift in preference.

Method

In this section, we demonstrate the implementation of our conversational intervention system, as well as our implementation of a virtual
survey participant agents using LLMs (referred to as virtual participants), a survey procedure for these agents, and an intervention system built from an
LLM together with a bandit algorithm. We call our system PeaR-RL, which stands for Personalized Rapport with Reinforcement Learning, since the goal of the
system is to personalize the conversational interventions and develop rapport with each survey participant.

In this paper, we assume that an effective way to craft effective conversational interventions is to address underlying values or beliefs that a person may hold.
In our context, we consider values to encompass consumer beliefs and attitudes towards BEVs.
The values to be considered in the paper are shown in Table
1
.
It includes concerns about cost and range, but also positive aspects,
such as reduced environmental impact and the status-enhancing effects of adopting a new technology.

Intervention system architecture

Figure
1
shows the intervention system architecture, which is composed of four modules.
Each module reflects a step of the intervention process.
First, we initialize the virtual participant, and measure the participant’s pre-intervention preference for BEVs.
The survey participant is initialized with demographics drawn from a target distribution, as detailed in next subsection.
This provides a baseline to understand the amount by which each intervention is able to shift the preference of the survey participant.
Second, PeaR-RL implements a contextual bandit algorithm, where the context is the demographic information of the virtual participant. The arm is selected from a list of BEV-relevant values that a virtual participant may hold.
Third, the LLM generates intervention text based on pre-intervention preference and the values chosen by PeaR-RL.
Finally, we survey the virtual participant for their post-intervention preference.
In the following sections, we describe implementation details.

Architecture of intervention system

Virtual participants implementation

The virtual participants are implemented by prompting an LLM to behave as a person with given demographic information.
For setting the virtual participant’s properties, the below system prompt is used.

[4]
I want you to act as the following character. Answer all of the following questions from the point of view of this character, do not break character. {properties}

Above, {properties} contains the name of a hypothetical human, together with a list of demographic characteristics. Our implementation
allows generating virtual participants with demographics drawn from any distribution, enabling targeting of behavioral interventions
to different populations of interest, including demographics that a company believes are likely to be customers for a specific product.

Measuring preferences for battery-electric vehicles

The following prompt is used to measure virtual participant preferences for BEVs. The measurement is carried out before and after
the intervention.

[4]
On a scale from 0 to 100, what is your current preference for battery electric vehicles (BEVs)? Please reply with just a single number rating and no additional words or explanations. Score:

PeaR-RL: Learning values of survey participants

PeaR-RL learns to select intervention parameters from virtual participant’s demographics that are useful for improving preferences.
The learning procedure is based on contextual multi-armed bandit method of Equation
1
.
The context
x
consists of demographic information, action
a\in A
is the estimated participant’s values, and reward
r\in R(a)
is a preference shift that is based on the difference in preference before and after intervention.
The reward function
R(a)
is stochastic because it depends on the output of the LLM, which is not deterministic.
In each step
t
and context
x
, action
a
that maximizes Equation
1
is selected and used to prompt the Wizard to generate a conversational intervention.

Wizard: LLM-based intervention generation

To generate a personalized intervention, we prompt an LLM with the target values as selected by PeaR-RL. First, we provide the LLM with the BEV preferences of the virtual survey participant with the following prompt with {initial pref.} replaced by the initial preference.

[4]
My preference for BEV is {initial pref.} out of 0 to 100.

Next, we generate the intervention text with the following system prompt with {values} replaced by PeaR-RL’s action.

[4]
Generate your response to improve the user’s preference for BEV. You are a Chatbot. The conversational partner’s values for BEV are inferred as follows: {values}. Please answer in 1 sentence concisely, with no additional explanation.

Discussion

Divergence between real humans and virtual participants

Comparisons with human survey data showed preference shifts that were close to those of real humans, but did not show low preferences or large shifts in preference.
This could be due to the different distribution of demographics and bias of LLMs.
Because the demographics were sampled independently for each attribute, extreme combinations of demographics and preference for BEVs were unlikely to occur.
Additionally, most LLMs have difficulties capturing specific people
(
Santurkar et al. 2023
)
, and the RLHF teaches the model to be helpful, honest, and harmless, and may generate responses that are unrealistically agreeable.
Therefore, applying the learning results to real humans may require fine-tuning to replicate real humans or adjusting interventions by experts.

Effectiveness of selecting values

Experimental results showed that Pear-RL is able to improve preference shift for BEVs compared to GPT-4 only, as demonstrated from the higher cumulative reward.
This result indicate that it is effective to use values to present specific information about BEVs.
However, when comparing the random policy and UCB policy, there was no significant difference in the second half of learning.
It is possible that longer training runs are required to more fully explore the action space, or that partial observability of the survey participant
makes it difficult to target values precisely.
In the experiment, only age and gender were observed among demographics, so there may be insufficient information to select optimal values (e.g., annual income and whether or not the respondent owns a car).
Possible solutions to tackle this problem are extensions such as extending the context to include more demographic information, as well as using multi-turn reinforcement to work with features inferred from longer conversational interactions, such as changing moods or concerns explicitly expressed
over the course of the conversation.

Conclusion and Future Work

In this paper, we proposed a conversational system to shift preferences for BEVs using a combination of LLMs and contextual bandit algorithms. We investigated the extent to which it is possible to shift preferences towards improving carbon neutrality. Our experiments show that LLM-based virtual study participants correspond somewhat closely to real humans in their preference for BEVs, both in pre-intervention surveys and in post-intervention preference shifts. We confirmed that our method of using values to inform the conversation is more effective than simply asking GPT-4 to be persuasive. Our contextual bandit has the benefit that it is an interpretable component that may be added to any existing LLM and can easily be adapted by domain experts to other applications. We demonstrated how the system can be trained with virtual survey participants. In future work, explore fine-tuning our bandit algorithm on interactions with real human subjects, as well as generalizing our bandit algorithm to a reinforcement learning algorithm that estimates additional features from a multi-turn conversation.
