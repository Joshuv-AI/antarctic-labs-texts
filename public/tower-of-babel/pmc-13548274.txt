Mapping longitudinal evidence on multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults: protocol for a scoping review
Authors: Mi-So Shim, Gaya Kim, Somyee Min
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMultidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults has emerged as an important issue in ageing research, given its close association with declines in health status, functional independence and overall quality of life. This scoping review aims to map longitudinal evidence on multidimensional frailty in community-dwelling older adults, including its measurement, prevalence, changes over time and associated risk factors. The findings will be used to generate insights for future research and inform the development of interventions targeting multidimensional frailty.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the methodological guidance of the Joanna Briggs Institute and the framework originally proposed by Arksey and O’Malley. The primary objective is to identify longitudinal evidence on risk factors associated with multidimensional frailty in community-dwelling older adults. A comprehensive literature search will be performed across six electronic databases (MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus), covering studies published from 1 January 2010 to the date of the final search (anticipated August 2026). Only peer-reviewed studies published in English and using longitudinal designs will be included. Study selection will be guided by the Population–Concept–Context framework. The review findings will be reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval is not required for this review as it is based solely on analysis of data from previously published studies. The results are expected to contribute to the development of interventions targeting multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications, academic conferences and scholarly forums.

Introduction

Frailty is widely recognised as a state of heightened vulnerability arising from cumulative declines in physiological reserves associated with ageing.1 Earlier conceptualisations of frailty have predominantly emphasised physical deterioration, as reflected in models that define frailty based on indicators such as unintentional weight loss, fatigue, reduced muscle strength, slowed gait and decreased physical activity.2 However, traditional approaches focusing primarily on physical frailty have been criticised for their limited scope, as they capture only part of the frailty construct.3 Furthermore, empirical studies have demonstrated that variations in frailty definitions and measurement approaches can lead to differences in prevalence estimates and characteristics of individuals classified as frail.4 In response to these challenges, the concept of multidimensional frailty has been proposed, encompassing not only physical deficits but also psychological and social dimensions.5 This expanded framework acknowledges that frailty is a complex and multifaceted condition shaped by interactions between diverse domains, thereby offering a more comprehensive understanding of vulnerability in later life.6 In addition, evidence from patient perspectives indicates that healthcare needs among older adults with frailty extend across physical, psychological and social domains, further supporting the relevance of a multidimensional approach.7

Multidimensional frailty is associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including falls, hospitalisation and readmission among older adults.8 It is also linked to an increased risk of mortality,9 10 lower life satisfaction and poorer quality of life.10 11 Importantly, frailty is not necessarily an irreversible condition, and timely identification and appropriate intervention may help to delay its onset or slow its progression.12 Consistent with this, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggests that multidomain interventions, often combining physical activity with components such as health education, nutritional supplementation, home visits, hormone therapy and counselling, can lead to improvements in frailty status or related indicators.13 In particular, identifying the factors that contribute to changes in multidimensional frailty over time may provide valuable insights for developing targeted and timely interventions, especially in community settings where early identification and intervention are feasible.

Although numerous studies have investigated factors associated with multidimensional frailty among older adults, much of the existing evidence is derived from cross-sectional study designs.14 15 While such studies are useful for identifying associations, they do not allow for the examination of temporal precedence or causal relationships. In contrast, longitudinal studies offer insights into how frailty develops and changes over time, as well as how various risk factors contribute to these changes.16 17 Despite their importance, longitudinal evidence on multidimensional frailty remains fragmented and has not been systematically mapped. This fragmented evidence base limits the ability to draw meaningful conclusions about frailty progression and domain-specific risk factors.

To date, no comprehensive efforts have been made to map longitudinal research on multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults. In particular, previous reviews have not specifically focused on multidimensional frailty,18 19 and most available evidence has not been restricted to longitudinal studies, with a predominant reliance on cross-sectional designs.18 20 21 Moreover, some systematic reviews have focused on specific risk factors rather than providing a comprehensive examination of multidimensional frailty across domains.22 23 One previous review focused on longitudinal studies of multidimensional frailty based on the Gobbens model; however, it did not include a broader range of multidimensional frailty conceptualisations and was not specifically focused on community-dwelling older adults.24 Thus, gaps remain in understanding its operationalisation, prevalence, trajectories and associated factors across multiple domains. Therefore, this scoping review aims to map longitudinal evidence on multidimensional frailty in community-dwelling older adults and identify evidence gaps to inform future research and intervention development.

Methods and analysis

This protocol was developed in accordance with the methodological guidance of the Joanna Briggs Institute25 and adopted the scoping review framework originally proposed by Arksey and O’Malley.26 The review process comprises five key stages: (1) defining the research questions; (2) identifying relevant studies; (3) selecting studies for inclusion; (4) charting the data; and (5) collating, summarising and presenting the findings. The reporting of this review will follow the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR).27

Stage 1: defining the research questions

This review aims to advance understanding of multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults by focusing on longitudinal evidence related to its risk factors. The review will address the following questions:

What are the characteristics of multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults, including its prevalence and patterns over time?

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

A comprehensive literature search will be performed across six electronic databases: MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus. Google Scholar will also be searched to identify potentially relevant grey literature. The search will cover studies published from 1 January 2010 to the date of the final search (anticipated August 2026). The year 2010 was selected as it marks an important development in frailty research, particularly with the introduction of a multidimensional conceptual framework that defines frailty as a dynamic state encompassing physical, psychological and social domains.4 This framework, proposed by Gobbens et al, addresses the limitations of earlier definitions by emphasising the multidimensional and dynamic nature of frailty, thereby providing a more suitable basis for identifying frailty in community-dwelling older adults.5 The search will target peer-reviewed articles published in English and using longitudinal designs.

Eligibility will be determined using the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework, with detailed inclusion criteria presented in box 1. In this review, multidimensional frailty is defined as a construct encompassing physical, psychological and social domains. Eligibility will also be restricted to studies with longitudinal designs. The search strategies are presented in online supplemental table 1. Terms related to risk factors are not included in the search strategy to maximise sensitivity, as these terms are used inconsistently across studies. Studies reporting risk factors will instead be identified during the screening process.

Stage 3: selecting studies for inclusion

All retrieved records will be imported into Covidence for the identification and removal of duplicates. The screening process will be conducted in two stages. First, two independent reviewers will assess titles and abstracts to determine potential eligibility based on the predefined inclusion criteria informed by the PCC framework. Subsequently, full-text articles of the selected records will be examined to confirm their eligibility for inclusion in the review. Any disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion and, where necessary, a third reviewer will be consulted to provide an independent judgement. Stage 4: charting the data

A standardised data charting form will be developed through iterative discussions within the research team to capture the key characteristics and findings of the included studies. Box 2 presents the anticipated data items to be extracted. These will include general study characteristics (eg, first author, year of publication, country, study design, study population and setting), as well as detailed information on multidimensional frailty. Specifically, data on frailty will encompass the domains assessed (eg, physical, psychological and social), measurement instruments, classification criteria, prevalence and changes in frailty status over time. In addition, data on longitudinal risk factors for multidimensional frailty will be extracted, including demographic characteristics, health-related factors and social and environmental factors.

Prior to the formal data charting process, the reviewers will be trained in the procedures to ensure consistency. The draft charting form will be pilot tested on a subset of studies and refined as necessary. Subsequently, two reviewers will independently chart the data from all included studies using the finalised form. Any discrepancies will be resolved through discussion, and if a consensus cannot be reached, a third reviewer will be consulted for adjudication.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and presenting the findings

In this stage, the charted data will be collated and presented to provide an overview of the existing evidence. The findings will describe the characteristics of multidimensional frailty among community-dwelling older adults, including the domains assessed, prevalence and patterns of frailty over time. In addition, the review will outline the measurement instruments used and identify longitudinal risk factors associated with multidimensional frailty. The results will be organised descriptively in tables and figures aligned with the research questions. Where appropriate, patterns and gaps in the literature will be highlighted.

The findings of this review are expected to reveal methodological variations, knowledge gaps and underexplored areas in the longitudinal evidence on multidimensional frailty. These findings may guide the design of future studies by informing the selection of multidimensional frailty measures, identifying priority research areas and providing a foundation for prediction models based on longitudinal risk factors. In community health practice, the review may support the early identification of older adults at high risk of multidimensional frailty and facilitate more comprehensive assessment across frailty domains. Furthermore, the review may inform the development of targeted, evidence-based interventions addressing the physical, psychological and social dimensions of frailty among community-dwelling older adults.

Ethics and dissemination

As this study is based on a review of previously published literature, ethical approval is not required. The findings are expected to contribute to the evidence base for future research and the development of strategies addressing multidimensional frailty. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journal publications and academic conference presentations.
