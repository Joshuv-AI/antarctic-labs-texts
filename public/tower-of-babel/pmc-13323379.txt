Correlation between swallowing and diaphragmatic functions in stroke patients with hemiplegia: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Xiaoman Liu, Jingrong Zhao, Yanyu Wu, Ying Yang, Xiang Zhu, Yu Wang, Chen Zhang, Mengxue Liu, Yanyu Song, Jie Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSwallowing and diaphragmatic functions share neural regulatory pathways and require synchronous assessment. Patients who have had a stroke are susceptible to many complications, of which dysphagia and diaphragmatic dysfunction are particularly common. To compare the distribution and severity of swallowing function in stroke patients with and without diaphragmatic dysfunction, and to explore the correlation between swallowing and diaphragmatic functions. This cross-sectional observational study among 102 Chinese stroke patients with hemiplegia was conducted in August 2022 to December 2024. Data collection was completed in the first 48 h following admission, including sex, age, post-stroke duration, stroke type, stroke region, hemiplegia side, nasogastric feeding, and pneumonia. The patients were stratified into two groups by the presence or absence of diaphragmatic dysfunction, which was assessed by diaphragmatic ultrasound with a threshold of diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) < 20%. We compared the distribution and severity of different swallowing functions using the Modified Barium Swallow Study Impairment Profile (MBSImP) and the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS) by Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study (VFSS) between the two groups. Significant differences were found between the two groups in the oral and pharyngeal phases of the MBSImP (p < 0.003), including hold position/tongue control, bolus preparation/mastication, bolus transport/lingual motion, oral residue, initiation of the pharyngeal swallow, anterior hyoid motion, pharyngeal stripping wave, and pharyngeal residue (p < 0.003). In contrast, there were no significant differences between the two groups in some components of the MBSImP including lip closure, soft palate elevation, laryngeal elevation, epiglottic movement, laryngeal closure, pharyngeal contraction, and tongue base retraction (p > 0.003). The severity of swallowing physiological impairment by MBSImP between the two groups, including the oral phase, pharyngeal phase and total MBSImP scores showed significant differences (p < 0.003). By contrast, the distribution and severity of penetration and aspiration risk by PAS showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.003). TFdi was negatively correlated with grades of Water Swallowing Test, the oral phase, pharyngeal phase and total MBSImP scores (rs = -0.327 to -0.300, p < 0.003). Whereas no significant correlations were found between TFdi and pneumonia, nasogastric feeding and the PAS scores (p > 0.003). Patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction exhibited a higher proportion of swallowing physiological impairment in the oral and pharyngeal phases, along with greater severity of such impairments. Introduction

It has been widely recognized that a tight neural coupling between the central control of swallowing and respiration1. Respiratory-swallowing coordination is essential for the safe ingestion of oral intake2. Dyscoordination of pharyngeal and diaphragmatic activity in humans may cause morbidity and mortality3. Under normal circumstances, post swallow expiration is a robust feature of breathing-swallowing coordination4. Recent studies indicate that relatively consistent post swallow exhalation protects against aspiration2. This coordinative pattern of expiration-swallowing-expiration is regarded as one of several mechanisms by which the airway is protected from aspiration during swallowing5,6. However, in most cases, swallowing occurs during inspiration and inspiration occurs immediately after a swallow in patients with stroke7,8.

Patients who have had a stroke are susceptible to numerous complications, among which pneumonia and dysphagia are particularly common9. Stroke disrupts multiple respiratory functions, and cortical damage results in decreased movement of the contralateral chest wall and diaphragm. The diaphragm is the primary respiratory muscle in the human body (accounting for approximately 70% of the respiratory pump function). The prevalence of diaphragmatic dysfunction in acute stroke is 51.7%10,11. Stroke can lead to varying degrees of oropharyngeal dysphagia12. Dysphagia affects 42% of stroke patients13, a condition that has been consistently associated with an elevated risk of pneumonia14, dehydration, and malnutrition15. Even severe dysphagia is estimated to lead to an 11-fold increase in this risk16. The reason can be briefly explained as the muscular dysfunction and the lack of coordination secondary to central nervous system damage may lead to the development of dysphagia after a stroke17. Therefore, a prompt and accurate diagnosis and assessment of diaphragmatic dysfunction and dysphagia in hemiplegic stroke patients are crucial12,18.

Recent studies proved ultrasonography can assess the mechanics, thickness19, and strength of the diaphragm20 to quantify their function, and it may be possible to provide valuable information in this context to complement clinical examination21,22. Diaphragmatic ultrasound can be used as a simple and effective tool for detecting pulmonary dysfunction in patients with stroke23. Diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) < 20% with inspiration by ultrasound was diagnostic of diaphragm paralysis in previous researches related to stroke24,25. The feasibility and reliability of ultrasound for assessing diaphragmatic muscle function have been well documented26,27.

The assessment of swallowing function is usually based on a clinical swallow examination and/or Videofluoroscopic Swallowing Study (VFSS) or Fiberoptic Endoscopic Evaluation of Swallowing (FEES)28,29. As a commonly used rapid screening method in clinical practice, the Water Swallow Test can preliminarily grade the choking risk30. VFSS provides dynamically visualizes a comprehensive assessment of swallowing which is considered the standard for diagnosing and characterizing the severity of oropharyngeal dysphagia31.

Previous studies have showed that the main muscles involved in the oropharyngeal phase of swallowing share many physiological features with the inspiratory muscles32,33. For the first time in humans, Anna et al.34 found specific diaphragmatic and abdominal muscle activity during swallowing, which promoted expiratory airflow after swallowing. Studies have confirmed the relationship between swallowing function and the maximum inspiratory pressure33, bilateral diaphragmatic movement of hemiplegic stroke patients35. However, bilateral diaphragmatic movements and swallowing assessment were evaluated simultaneously through fluoroscopy. The results of Geun-Young’s study showed that stroke patients with dysphagia have more pronounced weakness of the diaphragm as assessed by M-mode sonography, with a more restrictive lung dysfunction and weaker cough force36. In addition, stroke subjects at risk of silent aspiration showed reduced cough peak flow and more limited diaphragm excursion37. To date, limited research has evaluated post-stroke diaphragmatic function while subsequently using VFSS to examine the distribution and severity of dysphagia.

Based on the physiological relationship between diaphragmatic and swallowing functions in stroke patients, two research hypotheses are proposed as follows. First, do stroke patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction have a higher incidence and greater severity of dysphagia? Second, is the severity of diaphragmatic dysfunction positively correlated with the dysphagia? In these contexts, the first objective is to compare the distribution and severity of swallowing function in stroke patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction and without diaphragmatic dysfunction. The second is to explore the relationship between swallowing and diaphragmatic functions, thereby providing important guiding significance for clinical rehabilitation, especially for patients with stroke.

Methods

Participant

This cross-sectional observational study enrolled 102 patients with stroke from the Department Rehabilitation Medicine of Fudan University Huashan Hospital and the People’s Hospital of Suzhou New District from August 2022 to December 2024. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Fudan University Huashan Hospital (No. 2021/1004) and the People’s Hospital of Suzhou New District (No. [2022]033) and was prospectively registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Chi-CTR-2200056309, https://www.chictr.org.cn/bin/userProject, 03/02/2022). Research have been performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients’ data collection was completed in the first 48 h following admission. Among which, assessments including diaphragmatic ultrasound and VFSS performed at the same day. No interventions were administered to any patients prior to all the assessments. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participants were free to withdraw or discontinue from the study at any time. The research protocol was shown in Fig. 1. A previous study observed the incidence of diaphragmatic dysfunction in acute ischemic stroke, and the incidence was 51.7%11. Inclusion criteria: (a) confirmed diagnosis of stroke, both clinically and via imaging, within 1 to 6 months prior to enrollment; (b) age between 30 and 80 years; (c) presence of unilateral hemiplegia; (d) adequate auditory comprehension and ability to comply with test instructions. Exclusion criteria: (a) cognitive impairment (as indicated by a MoCA score < 26)38; (b) inability to complete evaluations due to hearing, visual, or language deficits; (c) history of obstructive or restrictive lung disease, or significant cardiac conditions; (d) history of previous thoracic and/or abdominal surgery; (e) history of excessive alcohol consumption39; (f) with prior neurological diagnoses other than stroke.

Assignment

All stroke subjects were divided into two groups according to whether they had diaphragmatic dysfunction or not.

Clinical variables

Data on the general characteristics of the participants were collected within 48 h of admission, including sex, age, post-stroke duration, stroke region, stroke type, hemiplegia side, nasogastric feeding, and pneumonia. Sex, age, post-stroke duration, hemiplegia side, and presence of nasogastric feeding were extracted from medical records; information regarding stroke region and stroke type was confirmed by cranial computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); and pneumonia was diagnosed by chest CT. In addition, all patients underwent the Water Swallowing Test within 48 h of admission before the assessments including diaphragmatic ultrasound and VFSS.

Assessment

Assessment of diaphragmatic function by ultrasonography

Diaphragm thickness: With the participants lying supine40, a 12-MHz linear array ultrasound transducer(General Electric Company, USA)41 operating in B-mode is employed to measure the diaphragm thickness at the zone of apposition during inspiration or expiration. The employed is placed roughly along the anterior axillary line, or just cephalad to the lower costal margin at the eighth and ninth intercostal spaces40–42. When oriented perpendicular to two adjacent ribs, the diaphragm can be visualized as a three-layered structure consisting of a relatively non-echogenic muscular layer bounded by echogenic membranes of the peritoneum and diaphragmatic pleura43. The diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) is calculated from measurements taken at end‑inspiration and end‑expiration using the following formula: TFdi = (end-inspiratory thickness − end-expiratory thickness) / end-expiratory thickness × 100%26. TFdi reflects on tractile activity that can be used to assess diaphragm function26,44. Diaphragmatic dysfunction was considered as diaphragm thickening fraction (TFdi) < 20% by ultrasound24,25,45.

Ultrasonography and data Acquisition: Ultrasonographic technique of diaphragm was previously reported to be reliable, with high intra-class correlation coefficient for intra-rater and inter-rater reliability25,46. To ensure the reliability and validity of the measurement values, the radiologist who performed diaphragmatic ultrasound was experienced in this procedure and was blinded to the diagnosis and hemiplegic side. GE Logiq V2 ultrasound machine (General Electric Company, USA) was used. For each participant, diaphragmatic excursion was recorded at a consistent position using M-mode ultrasonography over multiple consecutive respiratory cycles. At least three respiratory cycles were recorded both quiet and deep breathing. The distance between the echogenic lines was measured in frozen images, and measurements were averaged from three different cycles. All measurements were analyzed using imaging software, Image-J 1.8.0 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). Each parameter was measured in triplicate, and the average value was adopted for subsequent analysis.

Assessment of swallowing function by VFSS

In this study, X-ray photography system (Siemens, Germany) was utilized for capturing swallowing videos and images. Participants were examined in an upright seated position and instructed to swallow 3 ml, 5 ml, and 8 ml aliquots of a 60% barium sulfate suspension (National Medical Product Approval NumberH20163181, China). The suspension was thickened to a 2% concentration using a Softias-based thickener (NUTRI Co., Ltd., Japan). In accordance with a standardized protocol, the VFSS was digitally captured as video files in.avi format, with a frame rate of 30 frames/sec. Subsequent manual frame-by-frame review was conducted by a certificated therapist47. And the swallowing function assessors were unaware of the presence or absence of diaphragmatic dysfunction in the patients throughout the assessment. The results of VFSS were evaluated by the Modified Barium Swallow Study Impairment Profile (MBSImp©™)48 and the Penetration-Aspiration Scale (PAS)49.

The MBSImP, which results from combined rating of 17 components50, is a standardized tool with proven content, construct and external validity. It is physiologic vs. symptoms based, clinically practical and linked to clinical action. These 17 components assess the oral (5 items, 0–18 scores), pharyngeal (11 items, 0–33 scores), and esophageal phases (1 item, 0–4 scores) of swallowing, with higher scores indicating worse function. The PAS is a commonly used semi-quantitative assessment method in clinical practice. This scale is divided into 8 scales according to whether the contrast agent enters the airway, the depth it enters the airway and whether it can be discharged. Acceptable intra- and inter- judge reliability for the scale have been established sufficient enough to support its introduction into clinical practice49. In this study, two scales were used to evaluate the swallowing physiological impairment by MBSImP and penetration and aspiration risk by PAS. MBSImP was the primary outcome for swallowing function.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) or median and quartiles [M (Q1,Q3)], while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages [n (%)]. Normality of data distribution and linear regression residuals were evaluated using the Shapiro –Wilk normality test. Group comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test for normally distributed data. Non-parametric tests were conducted when the values did not follow a normal distribution: differences between conditions were analyzed with Mann-Whitney U test, and differences between groups were evaluated with non-paired tests. Categorical variables were analyzed with the Chi-square test. Logistic analysis on the correlation between swallowing function and key variables. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate associations between variables. There were 17 analysis items in total for the MBSImP subcomponents, so Bonferroni correction was applied. The significance level (α) was set at 0.003. The value of p < 0.003 was considered significant, with symbols presenting as * for p < 0.003.

Results

Clinical variables of diaphragmatic dysfunction and non-diaphragmatic dysfunction groups

All the recruited 102 hemiplegic patients successfully underwent the whole procedure of interview and assessment. They were divided into two groups based on the presence or absence of diaphragmatic dysfunction. Among them, 60 patients were in the diaphragmatic dysfunction group and 42 patients were in the non-diaphragmatic dysfunction group. There were no significant differences in sex, age, post-stroke duration, stroke region, stroke type, hemiplegic side, nasogastric feeding, and pneumonia between the two groups (p > 0.003). However, significant differences were shown in the MBSImp scores between the two groups (p < 0.003). The details of clinical variables were displayed in Table 1.

Correlation between swallowing function and diaphragmatic function

We analyzed the correlation between diaphragmatic function with pneumonia, nasogastric feeding, and swallowing function scales, as shown in Appendix T4 and Fig. 4. Spearman’s rank correlation analyses (n = 102) demonstrated that pneumonia was positively correlated with nasogastric feeding, grades of Water Swallowing Test, PAS scores, and oral, pharyngeal, and total scores of the MBSImP (rs = 0.307 to 0.413, p < 0.003). Nasogastric feeding was also positively correlated with the Grade of Water Swallowing Test, PAS scores, and scores of MBSImP (rs = 0.299 to 0.412, p < 0.003). Significant positive correlations were observed among the grades of Water Swallowing Test, PAS scores, and MBSImP scores (rs = 0.711 to 0.917, p < 0.003), especially between oral, pharyngeal, and total MBSImP scores (rs = 0.985 to 0.997, p < 0.003). Conversely, TFdi was negatively correlated with grades of Water Swallowing Test, the oral phase, pharyngeal phase and total MBSImP scores (rs = -0.327 to -0.300, p < 0.003). Discussion

This study examined swallowing function between post-stroke patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction and those without, and explored the correlation between diaphragmatic thickening fraction and pneumonia, nasogastric feeding, and swallowing function scales. The main results demonstrated that post-stroke patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction exhibited a higher proportion of swallowing physiological impairment in the oral and pharyngeal phases, along with greater severity of such impairments. Correlation analysis revealed that diaphragmatic function was negatively correlated with swallowing physiological impairment to a certain degree.

Our findings revealed that no significant sex-related differences were observed in diaphragmatic function among stroke patients. A systematic review has reported that the TFdi in healthy individuals is 39%±9% during forced inspiration, with diaphragmatic mobility of 59 ± 10 mm in females and 70 ± 11 mm in males during forced respiration22. Nevertheless, there was a paucity of comparative data on TFdi between male and female patients in stroke patient. Additionally, Although, there was no significant difference in stroke type between the two groups. the proportion of patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction was lower in those with ischemic stroke than hemorrhagic stroke. This observation might be attributable to the fact that hemorrhagic stroke is frequently accompanied by more severe cerebral edema and elevated intracranial pressure, which further suppresses respiratory function. No statistically significant differences were noted between the two groups in terms of age, post-stroke duration, stroke region, side of hemiplegia, pneumonia, and nasogastric feeding. On the contrary, the results identified that swallowing function may serve as influencing factors for diaphragmatic function.

A comparative analysis was performed on the distribution of swallowing physiological impairment between patients with and without diaphragmatic dysfunction. The results revealed that patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction exhibited a higher incidence of swallowing physiological impairment in both the oral and pharyngeal phases. Furthermore, comparisons stratified by the severity of swallowing physiological impairment by MBSImP scores, demonstrated that patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction presented with more severe swallowing physiological impairment. Collectively, these multi-dimensional comparisons have enabled a more in-depth understanding of the relationship between swallowing and diaphragmatic functions. Despite the fact that patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction showed a higher proportion of penetration (15.00% vs. 4.76%) and aspiration (16.67% vs. 9.52%) compared with those without diaphragmatic dysfunction in our study. Nevertheless, the distribution and severity of penetration and aspiration risk by PAS showed no significant difference. A plausible explanation was that the PAS primarily focuses on airway protection ability, whereas diaphragmatic function assessment measures the contractile strength of respiratory muscles. The potential relationship and anatomical mechanisms between them warrant further investigation, and whether temporal and movement-related factors exist remains to be elucidated.

Swallowing and diaphragmatic functions share neural regulatory pathways and require synchronous assessment. The regulatory centers are located in the brainstem network called the behavior control assemblies, meanwhile, both are regulated by the cerebral cortex51. Swallowing and breathing are closely intertwined, which is a close connection in terms of anatomical structure and function. And current research on the interactive relationship between respiration and swallowing has made some progress52. Previous studies have pointed out that there was a correlation between diaphragmatic function on the hemiplegic side and swallowing function in post-stroke patients35,53. Additionally, respiratory muscle training might lead to beneficial changes in swallowing54–56. Our results showed that TFdi was negatively correlated with the primary outcome for swallowing function, suggesting that higher TFdi indicated better swallowing function. However, the correlation was weak and of uncertain clinical significance. Whereas no significant correlations were found between TFdi and pneumonia, nasogastric feeding and PAS scores. The factors associated with post-stroke pneumonia were complex, and its exhibited certain temporal characteristics57. In addition, nasogastric feeding and the risk of penetration and aspiration also showed no correlation with diaphragmatic function. Dysphagia is a common complication after stroke and is associated with the development of pneumonia58. Consistent with the findings, our results showed that both pneumonia and nasogastric feeding were positively correlated with swallowing function.

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, it is a cross-sectional observational study, and its results do not allow causal inference between swallowing and diaphragmatic functions. It will be necessary to conduct long-term follow-up studies to verify the results. Since the research team had previously compared the swallowing diaphragmatic functions of post-stroke hemiplegic patients with that of healthy subjects, no matched healthy subjects were included in this study. Secondly, although clinical studies have demonstrated that diaphragmatic ultrasound exhibits high sensitivity and specificity to identify diaphragmatic dysfunction, no single assessment can completely replace comprehensive evaluation which may have resulted potential for misclassification. We performed a sensitivity analysis using TFdi < 25% as the threshold value, and the result of key value ( MBSImP ) also showed a significant difference (p < 0.003). Thirdly, VFSS is the gold standard for oropharyngeal dysphagia; however, there is no unified standard for the classification of components in dysphagia diagnosis. Additionally, the study only conducted clinical observations on the swallowing and respiratory functions of post-stroke hemiplegic patients, and existing research mainly focuses on the fields of clinical observation and rehabilitation. In the future, the underlying physiological mechanisms and neural regulation related to this topic can be explored as new research directions.

Conclusions

Patients with diaphragmatic dysfunction exhibited a higher proportion of swallowing physiological impairment in the oral and pharyngeal phases, along with greater severity of such impairments. Diaphragmatic function was correlated with swallowing function, but the correlation was weak and of uncertain clinical significance.
