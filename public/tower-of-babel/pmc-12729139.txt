Depression severity and neuropsychiatric symptoms among nursing home residents with late-life depression: a moderated mediation model of sleep quality and resilience
Authors: Ziping Zhu, Yuanjiao Yan, Danting Chen, Yanhong Shi, Chenshan Huang, Hong Li, Rong Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLate-life depression (LLD) is a significant global public health concern, with particularly high prevalence rates among individuals in nursing homes. The severity of LLD is a critical factor influencing the occurrence of neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) in patients. To our knowledge, no studies have investigated the mediating effects of sleep quality and resilience on the relationship between depression severity and NPS in nursing home residents with LLD. Accordingly, this study aims to assess the prevalence of NPS among this population and examine the relationship between depression severity and NPS, focusing on the mediating roles of sleep quality and resilience.MethodsUsing cross-sectional data from the baseline phase of a multicenter longitudinal study, we enrolled 414 patients with LLD from 42 nursing homes in Fujian Province, China. Standardized assessments included the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), the Mild Behavioral Impairment Checklist (MBI-C), the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10). Mediation and moderated mediation analysis were conducted with the PROCESS macro to examine the interactive relationships among depression severity, sleep quality, resilience, and NPS.ResultsThe prevalence of NPS among patients with LLD was 33.33%. Depression severity showed a significant positive correlation with NPS (β = 0.362, p < 0.001). Mediation analysis indicated that sleep quality partially mediated this relationship (β = 0.265, p < 0.001). Importantly, resilience significantly moderated both the direct pathway between depression severity and NPS (β = -0.102, p = 0.005) and the indirect pathway through sleep quality (β = -0.096, p = 0.016). Specifically, the effects of greater depression severity and poorer sleep quality on NPS were weaker among patients with higher resilience levels.ConclusionOur findings indicate that both sleep quality and resilience significantly influence the relationship between depression severity and NPS. These results underscore the importance of improving sleep quality and strengthening resilience as potential strategies to reduce the incidence of NPS in patients with LLD. Future intervention studies should specifically focus on enhancing sleep quality and resilience in this vulnerable population.

Background

Late-life depression (LLD), a depressive disorder occurring in older adults aged 60 years and above, is strongly associated with co-morbidities, increased mortality, and substantial family and social burdens [1, 2]. It is, therefore, recognized as an important public health concern in the context of global population aging. The prevalence of LLD in nursing homes has been reported at 36.8% [3]—notably higher than the prevalence in community settings (11.3%) [4] and the overall prevalence (20.0%) [5]. According to China’s Ministry of Civil Affairs, by the end of 2021, adults residing in nursing homes accounted for approximately 1.07% of the country’s population aged 60 and above—a proportion that continues to rise as China’s society ages [6]. These trends highlight the urgent need to address mental health issues among institutionalized older adults.

Unlike depression in younger adults, patients with LLD also experience neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) [7, 8]. NPS—often described as the “non-cognitive” symptoms of dementia, and referred to as behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia—include mood disturbances, anxiety, apathy, perceptual abnormalities, sleep and appetite problems, and agitation or aggressive behaviors [9]. The prevalence of NPS in older populations is estimated at approximately 17.0% [10], and its persistence reflects enduring changes in behavior and personality that are linked to several adverse clinical outcomes: accelerated dementia progression and increased mortality [11], higher rates of institutionalization [12], greater functional impairment [13], and reduced quality of life [14]. Therefore, targeted efforts to identify, monitor, and manage NPS in patients with LLD are essential.

There is a significant association between depression severity and NPS [15]. Indeed, studies indicate that 58% of individuals with current major depressive episodes experience severe NPS [16], and that depression severity is a significant predictor of NPS [17]. This relationship may be influenced by molecular-level alterations, changes in neural circuits, neurostructural modifications, and disruptions in brain networks [18, 19]. However, despite these findings, the precise nature of the relationship between depression severity and NPS remains underexplored, particularly regarding potential mediating and moderating mechanisms.

A key protective factor against NPS is sleep quality. Sleep disturbance is a core symptom of LLD, and numerous studies have confirmed a negative correlation between depression severity and sleep quality [20, 21]. The underlying mechanism may involve hyperactivity of the prefrontal cortex during acute depressive episodes, which disrupts sleep architecture and contributes to non-restorative sleep patterns through dysregulation of cholinergic-adrenergic neurotransmission. Clinically, this often manifests as excessive daytime sleepiness and disturbances in the sleep-wake circadian rhythm [22]. Poor sleep quality may also exacerbate NPS progression, as significant associations have been observed between poorer sleep and more severe NPS [23]. Mechanistically, this may be attributed to sleep deprivation-induced disruptions in synaptic homeostasis, neuroinflammatory processes, and circadian rhythm dysregulation [24]. Collectively, these findings suggest that sleep quality may mediate the relationship between depression severity and NPS.

Similarly, resilience has been recognized as an important protective factor against NPS, with numerous studies having observed a negative correlation between resilience and NPS [25, 26]. Conceptually, resilience is understood as a dynamic process defined as an individual’s ability to adapt and recover in the face of stress and adversity [27]. Furthermore, resilience may be linked to healthier circadian rhythms and sleep patterns, potentially acting as a buffer against the detrimental effects of circadian disruption on mental health [28]. Stress-coping theory underscores this factor as an effective coping mechanism that enables individuals to better adapt to and manage depressive symptoms [29]. Among patients with LLD, resilience has been negatively associated with depression and positively associated with quality of life [30]. However, little is known about the role of resilience as a direct or indirect buffer against the negative impact of depression severity on NPS.

Thus, depression severity, sleep quality, and resilience all appear to play important roles in NPS. However, their combined effects in patients with LLD residing in nursing homes remain unclear. Therefore, the present study aimed to (1) assess the prevalence of NPS in this population, (2) examine whether sleep quality mediates the association between depression severity and NPS, and (3) evaluate a moderated mediation model. In this model, we hypothesized that sleep quality would mediate the association between depression severity and NPS, while resilience would moderate both the direct pathway between depression severity and NPS (path a) and the indirect pathway via sleep quality (path b).

Materials and methods

Participants and ethical statement

This study represents the baseline phase of a multicenter longitudinal project. Using a cluster sampling method, we screened and enrolled patients with LLD who met the inclusion criteria from 42 nursing homes across nine cities in Fujian Province, China. The inclusion criteria were (1) age at first onset ≥ 60 years, (2) meeting the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria for depression, and (3) residing in a nursing home for more than two months. The exclusion criteria were having: (1) a major psychiatric or neurological disorder, 2) a history of alcoholism or drug dependence, and (3) language disorders or any condition that impaired verbal communication. All data were collected through standardized face-to-face interviews conducted by trained researchers.

The Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University approved all study procedures (approval no. 2023 − 177). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were informed of the study’s purpose. Data were collected between June and October 2023 (Fig. 1). A total of 419 questionnaires were distributed, of which 414 were completed and valid, yielding a response rate of 98.81%.

Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Study variables included age, gender, marital status, educational level, previous residence, economic status, chronic conditions, history of psychotropic medication use, smartphone use, intellectual activity, volunteer activity, antidepressant treatment status, and global cognitive function. Marital status was categorized as married or unmarried (including single, divorced, and widowed). Educational level was classified as primary school or below, middle or high school, and college or above; previous residence as city, town, or rural; and economic status as < 1000 RMB/month, 1000–3000 RMB/month, 3001–5000 RMB/month, and > 5000 RMB/month. Intellectual activities were defined as recreational activities that promoted cognition, such as reading and playing card games, whereas volunteer activities referred to philanthropic behaviors, such as community service. Both were classified as never, seldom (1–2 times per week), often (3–4 times per week), or usually (5–7 times per week). Global cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA).

Neuropsychiatric symptoms

The Mild Behavioral Impairment Checklist (MBI-C) is an observer-rated instrument used to assess NPS. In this study, it was administered by uniformly trained researchers based on information provided by family members or informants about symptoms persisting for more than six months. The checklist comprises 34 items under five domains: decreased motivation, affective dysregulation, impulse dyscontrol, social inappropriateness, and abnormal perception/thought content [31]. Scores range from 0 to 102, with higher values indicating greater NPS severity. A cutoff score of >7 was adopted to define MBI in this study [32, 33]. The scale has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Chinese populations [34]. In our sample, the MBI-C showed excellent internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.882.

Depression severity

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15) is a self-rated instrument, adapted from the original 30-item version, and is used to assess depression severity [35]. Each item requires a dichotomous yes/no response and is scored as 1 or 0. Scores range from 0 to 15, with higher values indicating greater depressive severity. The GDS-15 has been validated in Chinese populations [36].

Sleep quality

The Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) is a self-rated instrument used to assess sleep quality over the previous month. It consists of 19 items grouped into seven components: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of hypnotic medications, and daytime dysfunction. Scores range from 0 to 21, with higher values indicating poorer sleep quality [37]. Previous studies have shown that the PSQI has good reliability in Chinese populations [21]. In this study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.713.

Resilience

The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) is a self-rated instrument that measures an individual’s ability to cope with adversity and stress, serving as an indicator of psychological adaptability [38]. It consists of 10 items providing a rapid assessment of individual resilience. Scores range from 0 to 40, with higher values indicating greater resilience. The CD-RISC-10 has demonstrated good reliability in Chinese populations [39]. In our study, the Cronbach’s α was 0.948.

Statistical analyses

Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and proportions were reported for categorical variables. Independent-sample t-tests were used to examine group differences in continuous variables, and chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to explore associations between depression severity, sleep quality, resilience, and NPS among patients with LLD in nursing homes. Mediation and moderated mediation analyses were performed using the PROCESS macro [40], specifically Model 4 (mediation) and Model 15 (moderated mediation of direct or indirect pathways). Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was applied to generate 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the indirect effects of mediators. All analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27. A two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All models were adjusted for age and gender, and all study variables were standardized.

Results

Characteristics of the participants

This study included 414 patients with LLD residing in nursing homes, with a mean age of 78.85 ± 9.00 years. The prevalence of NPS (MBI-C > 7) was 33.33%. The majority of patients were unmarried (64.25%, including single, divorced, and widowed), female (64.25%), had completed middle or high school (41.50%), and had previously resided in urban areas (57.00%). Additionally, 32.61% reported a monthly income of 3001–5000 RMB, 68.36% had chronic conditions, 69.08% had no history of psychotropic medication use, 51.21% used smartphones, 49.28% never engaged in intellectual activities, and 49.76% never participated in volunteer activities.

Descriptive analyses showed that gender, marital status, previous residence, smartphone use, and intellectual activities were significantly associated with NPS (p < 0.05). By contrast, age, educational level, economic status, chronic conditions, history of psychotropic medication use, volunteer activities, antidepressant treatment, and global cognitive function (MoCA score) were not significantly associated (p > 0.05). Moderated analysis of moderation

As shown in Table 3, resilience moderated both the relationship between depression severity and NPS, and between sleep quality (PSQI total score) and NPS. The interaction between depression severity and resilience significantly predicted NPS (β = -0.102, p = 0.005), as did the interaction term between PSQI scores and resilience (β = -0.096, p = 0.016).

Further analyses using PROCESS model 15 and simple slope test (see Table 5; Fig. 2) indicated that depression severity was a significant positive predictor of NPS at low resilience levels (β = 0.281, p < 0.001), whereas the association was nonsignificant at high resilience levels (β = 0.077, p = 0.220).

Mediating pathway model

Figure 4 illustrates the moderated mediation model. The pathway coefficients indicate that the tested relationships include both significant positive and negative associations. The direct effect of depression severity on NPS remained significant after including PSQI scores (sleep quality, with higher values indicating poorer quality) as a mediator and resilience as a moderator. These findings suggest that the associations between depression severity and NPS in nursing home patients are partially mediated by sleep quality and further moderated by resilience.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of NPS among patients with LLD in nursing homes and examined how depression severity, sleep quality, and resilience are related to NPS, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for reducing its incidence. The findings showed that the association between depression severity and NPS was partially mediated by sleep quality, indicating that depression severity exerts both direct and indirect effects on NPS. In addition, higher levels of resilience buffered the adverse impact of depression severity and poor sleep quality on NPS. These findings have important clinical implications, suggesting that improving sleep quality and enhancing resilience may serve as effective strategies to reduce NPS in nursing home residents with LLD. Beyond enriching theoretical understanding of mental health in this population, these results also offer practical intervention pathways to improve psychological well-being and quality of life among older adults in nursing homes.

In the present study, the prevalence of NPS among patients with LLD in nursing homes was 33.33%, significantly higher than the prevalence reported among older adults in psychiatric outpatient clinics (3.50%) [41] and in community samples (10.00–15.00%) [42]. Differences in study settings may partly explain this discrepancy. The mean depression severity score in our sample (7.70 ± 2.62) was also higher than that reported in a Chinese study of community and hospital-based patients with LLD (7.07 ± 3.33) [43]. Similarly, the mean PSQI score of nursing home residents with LLD (10.41 ± 4.86) was higher than that reported among such patients in Yunnan hospitals (6.88 ± 2.45) [44], indicating poorer sleep quality. In contrast, the resilience score of LLD patients in nursing homes (20.24 ± 8.41) was higher than that of empty nesters in Huzhou, China (12.44 ± 3.63) [45]. One possible explanation is that nursing home residents with LLD are exposed to multiple psychological and environmental stressors that may contribute to poorer sleep quality and greater depression severity; however, these same challenges may also foster adaptive coping mechanisms, thereby elevating resilience levels [46, 47].

Our study demonstrated a significant positive correlation between depression severity and NPS among nursing home residents with LLD, consistent with previous findings [48, 49]. Prior research has shown that patients with LLD transitioning from a familiar home environment to a new and unfamiliar institutional setting tend to exhibit greater depressive symptom severity, which subsequently influences the manifestation of NPS. This supports the notion that environmental change can exacerbate depressive symptoms [3]. The psychological distress associated with adapting to new surroundings appears to be a key factor; this was why participants having resided in a nursing home for more than two months was an inclusion criteria. It was intended to ensure that participants had enough time to acclimate, thereby allowing a more accurate assessment of the relationship between depression severity and NPS. Neurophysiological studies further suggest that depression severity may influence NPS through changes in neurotransmitter systems, inflammatory processes, and functional brain connectivity [50]. Although this association has been well documented, the precise neural mechanisms remain unclear. Our findings reinforce earlier reports that better sleep quality and higher resilience levels act as protective factors against NPS in patients with LLD.

Furthermore, this study found that sleep quality mediates the relationship between depression severity and NPS. Depression severity not only directly and positively influences NPS but also indirectly exacerbates these symptoms by impairing sleep quality. This suggests that improving sleep quality may be an effective strategy for alleviating NPS. From a mechanistic perspective, the mediating effect of sleep involves multiple biological processes. Depression is frequently associated with circadian rhythm disruptions and reduced prefrontal cortex function, which lead to altered sleep architecture and diminished restorative sleep [22]. In turn, sleep deprivation further weakens the prefrontal cortex’s ability to regulate emotion and cognition, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle [51]. By contrast, improved sleep quality enhances neural plasticity, promotes brain function recovery, and increases emotional stability while reducing NPS [52]. Additionally, improving sleep significantly benefits mental health, although its effectiveness varies across specific psychological conditions, highlighting the complexity of the underlying mechanisms [53]. Therefore, further mechanistic research is warranted to provide a foundation for the targeted application of sleep-based interventions.

According to our moderator-mediator analyses, resilience moderated the relationship between depression severity and NPS, as well as the second stage of the mediated effect of sleep quality. Resilience functions as a dynamic moderating process in which adversity shapes an individual’s response to stress; this capacity can be adapted and learned throughout life [54, 55]. Recent studies suggest a potential bidirectional relationship between resilience and chronotype, indicating that greater resilience may foster healthier sleep-wake patterns, while circadian stability may, in turn, enhance an individual’s capacity for resilience, creating a positive feedback loop that protects against NPS [28, 56]. Our study examined NPS in patients with LLD residing in nursing homes and found that these patients exhibited more severe depression and poorer sleep quality. However, the impact of depression severity and sleep quality on NPS lessened as resilience increased. Therefore, strengthening resilience could serve as a critical strategy for improving NPS in patients with LLD [57, 58]. Targeted psychological interventions, supportive therapies, and rehabilitation programs that also address circadian health may enhance resilience and promote regular sleep, thereby reducing depressive symptoms and improving overall well-being. Future research and clinical practice should further investigate the effectiveness of integrated interventions, particularly those that combine resilience training with circadian rhythm stabilization.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, because the data are cross-sectional, it is difficult to draw causal inferences between the identified variables and NPS. Future longitudinal studies are therefore needed to clarify the causal links between depression severity and NPS and to examine more precise mediating relationships. Second, the study relied primarily on self-reports and subjective evaluations by participants, which may introduce bias. To mitigate this limitation, future research should incorporate in-depth interviews and behavioral observations. Third, although we controlled for some potential confounding variables (e.g., age, gender), other unmeasured variables (e.g., social support and functional limitations) may still influence the results. Further studies should account for these additional factors to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. Finally, because this study was conducted exclusively in nursing homes, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations or care contexts. Future research should therefore include diverse settings, including community and home-based care, to validate generalizability and explore the influence of different environments on the study variables.

Conclusion

This study is the first to investigate the relationship between depression severity and NPS among patients with LLD in nursing homes using a moderated mediation model. The prevalence of NPS in this population was 33.33%, and sleep quality partially moderated the relationship between depression severity and NPS. In addition, resilience moderated both the direct effect of depression severity on NPS and the latter stage of the mediating effect of sleep quality. These findings highlight the importance of developing interventions for LLD patients in nursing homes, particularly for those with poor sleep quality and low resilience. Such interventions may improve sleep quality, strengthen resilience, and ultimately reduce the occurrence of NPS. This study not only provides valuable guidance for optimizing care strategies in nursing homes but also offers practical directions for clinical interventions aimed at safeguarding the mental health and overall quality of life of older patients.
