Abstract
. We define Bregman variation of semimartingales. We give its pathwise representation, Itô-type isometry for martingales, and applications to harmonic analysis.

60H05, 60G44 (primary), 60H30 (secondary)

1.
Introduction and preliminaries

The quadratic variation of a càdlàg semimartingale is a limit of cumulated squared increments (for definitions and details see below).
It is a key concept in stochastic calculus, with many applications in mathematics, science, and economy. It yields a bridge between stochastic and deterministic integrations, especially for square integrable semimartingales; see, e.g., Protter
[
38
]
and Revuz-Yor
[
40
]
. In particular, the quadratic variation is of paramount importance in Itô calculus,
stochastic differential equations, finance and statistics.

In this work, for convex functions
\phi
satisfying mild growth and regularity conditions and càdlàg real-valued semimartingales
X_{t},\,t\!\in\![0,\infty)
, we study a similar, but more general concept, the
Bregman variation
, or
\phi
-variation
, defined by

(1.1)

V^{\phi}(X)_{t}:=\phi(X_{t})-\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(X_{s-})dX_{s},\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0.

As we shall see, Bregman variation is tailor-made for studying
\phi
-integrable semimartingales and problems of probability and analysis that involve convex functions more general than the quadratic function.
Our theory has wider scope than that of the quadratic variation, but also exhibits some similarities. In particular, the Bregman variation of a semimartingale can be approximated by cumulated
Bregman divergences
of the semimartingale. We propose a common framework that unifies observations and results in Probability and Analysis accumulated in recent years (see
[
9
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
12
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
and
[
35
]
). We also give applications to semigroups and harmonic functions of Markov processes.

Here is the structure of the paper. Below in this section we discuss convex functions on the line, the Bregman divergence, and semimartingales.
In Subsection
2.1
, we briefly address processes in discrete time. In Subsection
2.2
, we state precise assumptions for the definition of the Bregman divergence (
1.1
) for continuous-time (càdlàg) semimartingales.
In Theorem
2.5
, we give an Itô-type isometry (
\phi
-isometry) for martingales and in Theorem
2.10
, we provide a pathwise formula for
V^{\phi}(X)
involving the jumps and the continuous part of the quadratic variation of the semimartingale.
In Section
3
, we present applications and examples. In Appendix
A
, we give auxiliary estimates for
\phi
-integrable random variables and martingales and in Appendix
B
, we recall relevant notions and results on stochastic integration.

All the considered sets, functions, and measures are assumed to be Borel. The symbol
:=
indicates definition, e.g.,
\mathbb{R}_{+}:=[0,\infty)
,
a\wedge b:=\min\{a,b\}
. Random variables are (finite) real-valued. Unless stated otherwise, stochastic process, in particular continuous-time martingale or semimartingale, is a family of (real-valued) random variables
X(t)=X_{t}
indexed by
t\geq 0
, that is,
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, defined on the same probability space. Equality of random variables is understood almost surely (a.s.).
Constants
in
inequalities
involving functions mean numbers in
(0,\infty)
that do not depend on the arguments of the functions, see, e.g., (
1.2
).

1.1.
Convex functions

In this subsection, we briefly discuss convex functions; we refer to Krasnoselskii and Rutickii
[
32
]
, Rao and Ren
[
39
]
, Long
[
34
]
, or Alsmeyr and Rösler
[
1
]
for more information.
We say that
\phi
is a
Young function
if
\phi
is even, continuous, and convex,
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\phi(\lambda)/\lambda=0
, and
\lim_{\lambda\to+\infty}\phi(\lambda)/\lambda=+\infty
. In particular,
\phi(0)=0
.
Let
\phi^{*}
be the
Legendre transform of a Young function
\phi
:

\phi^{*}(\gamma):=\sup\left\{\ \!\gamma\lambda-\phi(\lambda):\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}\ \!\right\},\hskip 23.49976pt\gamma\in\mathbb{R}.

Then
\phi^{*}
is again a Young function and the definition immediately yields
Young’s inequality:

(YI)

\lambda\gamma\leq\phi(\lambda)+\phi^{*}(\gamma),\hskip 23.49976pt\lambda,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}.

We say that Young function
\phi
satisfies the (doubling) condition
\Delta_{2}
, and we write
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
, if there exists a constant
K>0
such that

(1.2)

\phi(2\lambda)\leq K\phi(\lambda),\hskip 11.74988pt\lambda>0.

Below,
K_{\phi}
will denote the smallest constant for which (
1.2
) holds.

Definition 1.1
.

A Young function
\phi
is called moderate if
\phi,\phi^{*}\in\Delta_{2}
.

Let
\phi^{\prime}
be the left derivative of
\phi
; see
[
34
, (3.1.1)]
. Define

(1.3)

D_{\phi}:=\sup_{\lambda>0}\frac{\lambda\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)}{\phi(\lambda)},\hskip 23.49976ptd_{\phi}:=\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\lambda\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)}{\phi(\lambda)}.

We call
D_{\phi}
(respectively,
d_{\phi}
) the Simonenko upper (respectively, lower) index of
\phi
. Due to convexity,
1\leq d_{\phi}\leq D_{\phi}\leq+\infty
.
By,
[
34
, Theorem 3.1.1]
, (
1.2
) is equivalent to
D_{\phi}<\infty

and the condition
\Delta_{2}
for
\phi^{*}
is tantamount to
d_{\phi}>1
; see also
[
32
, Theorem 4.1 and Theorem 4.3]
and
[
1
]
. In short,

moderate

means

1<d_{\phi}\leq D_{\phi}<\infty
.

1.2.
Luxemburg norm and Orlicz space

Given a Young function
\phi
and a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
Luxemburg norm
of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is

(1.4)

\|f\|_{\phi}:=\|f\|_{L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)}:=\inf\left\{K>0:\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\Big(|f(x)|/K\Big){\rm d}\mu(x)\leq 1\right\}.

We then define the
Orlicz space

L^{\phi}:=L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu):=\left\{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}:\;\|f\|_{\phi}<\infty\right\}.

If
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, we may write
L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}):=L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)
.
The Orlicz space
with the Luxemburg norm
is a Banach space, see
[
32
, Theorem 9.2 and p. 78]
.
For instance,
L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)=L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)
and
\|\cdot\|_{\phi}=\|\cdot\|_{p}
if
\phi(\lambda)=|\lambda|^{p}
with

p\in(1,\infty)
.
Furthermore,

(1.5)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f/\|f\|_{\phi})\,{\rm d}\mu(y)\leq 1;

we even have equality in (
1.5
) if
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
.
We note that
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)
does not necessarily imply
\phi\circ f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)
, however, it does when
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
.
Indeed, for the latter claim, we consider
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
that satisfies
2^{n}\geq\|f\|_{\phi}+1
, so by
\Delta_{2}
and (
1.4
), we obtain

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}\mu(y)\leq K_{\phi}^{n}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi\left(\frac{f(y)}{\|f\|_{\phi}+1}\right){\rm d}\mu(y)\leq K_{\phi}^{n}<\infty.

Thus for
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
,
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}},\mu)
is equivalent to the
\phi
-integrability
:
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f)d\mu<\infty
.

Proposition 1.2
.

(1)
For every Young function
\phi
,
L^{\phi}\subset L^{1}+L^{\infty}
.

(2)
If
\phi
is moderate, with Simonenko indices
1<d_{\phi}\leq D_{\phi}<\infty,
then
L^{\phi}\subset L^{d_{\phi}}+L^{D_{\phi}}
.

Proof.

Let
\|f\|_{\phi}>0
.
We note that there is
\lambda_{0}\in(0,\infty)
such that
\phi(\lambda)\geq\lambda
if
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
.
Then,

(1.6)

\phi(f/\|f\|_{\phi})\mathbf{1}_{|f|/\|f\|_{\phi}\geq\lambda_{0}}\geq(f/\|f\|_{\phi})\mathbf{1}_{|f|/\|f\|_{\phi}\geq\lambda_{0}}.

Of course,

f=f_{1}+f_{\infty}
,
where

f_{1}:=f\mathbf{1}_{|f|/\|f\|_{\phi}\geq\lambda_{0}}
and
f_{\infty}:=f\mathbf{1}_{|f|/\|f\|_{\phi}<\lambda_{0}}\in L^{\infty}.

By (
1.5
) and (
1.6
),

f_{1}\in L^{1}
. This proves (1).
In the setting of (2),
\phi(\lambda)/\lambda^{d_{\phi}}
is increasing and

\phi(\lambda)/\lambda^{D_{\phi}}
is decreasing on
[0,\infty)
by
[
34
, Theorem 3.1.1]
, so

\phi(1)\lambda^{d_{\phi}}\leq\phi(\lambda)\leq\phi(1)\lambda^{D_{\phi}},\hskip 11.74988pt\mbox{for }\lambda\geq 1,

\phi(1)\lambda^{D_{\phi}}\leq\phi(\lambda)\leq\phi(1)\lambda^{d_{\phi}},\hskip 11.74988pt\mbox{for }\lambda\leq 1.

By (
1.5
),

f_{d_{\phi}}:=f\mathbf{1}_{|f|\geq 1}\in L^{d_{\phi}}
and

f_{D_{\phi}}:=f\mathbf{1}_{|f|<1}\in L^{D_{\phi}}
. Since
f=f_{d_{\phi}}+f_{D_{\phi}}
, we get (2).
∎

1.3.
Bregman divergence

For a finite convex function
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we define the Bregman divergence by

(1.7)

F_{\phi}(x,y):=\phi(y)-\phi(x)-\phi^{\prime}(x)(y-x),\hskip 11.74988ptx,y\in\mathbb{R}.

Here
\phi^{\prime}
is the left derivative of
\phi
. By convexity,
F_{\phi}\geq 0
.
E.g., if
\phi(x)=|x|^{p}
,
p>1
, then

F_{\phi}(x,y)=F_{p}(x,y):=|y|^{p}-|x|^{p}-p|x|^{p-1}\sgn(x)(y-x),\hskip 11.74988ptx,y\in\mathbb{R},

in particular,
F_{2}(x,y)=(y-x)^{2}
.

Bregman divergence is commonly used in statistical learning and its
applications, see Amari
[
2
]
, Ay, Jost, Lê, and
Schwachhöfer
[
3
]
, Nielsen and Nock
[
36
]
,
or Frigyik, Srivastava and Gupta
[
23
]
. For applications to clustering, see A. Banerjee, S. Merugu,
I. S. Dhillon, and J. Ghosh

[
5
]
.
Moreover, it is important for the theory of entropy, see, e.g.,
Wang
[
45
]
. It is also used in PDEs in
Gagliardo–Nirenberg–Sobolev inequalities,
see Carrillo, Jüngel, Markowich, Toscani, and Unterreiter

[
18
, p. 71]
, Bonforte, Dolbeault, Nazaret, and Simonov

[
16
]
, and Borowski and Chlebicka

[
17
]
.

Our motivation stems from recent applications of Bregman divergences, chiefly
F_{p}
, in harmonic analysis and probabilistic potential theory, see Bogdan, Jakubowski, Lenczewska and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
13
]
, Bogdan, Grzywny, Pietruska-Pałuba and Rutkowski,
[
11
]
, Bogdan, Fafuła and Rutkowski
[
9
]
, Bogdan, Gutowski and Pietruska-Pałuba
[
12
]
, and in martingale inequalities, see Bogdan and Więcek
[
15
]
. For instance in
[
13
]
, the corresponding
Sobolev–Bregman forms
yield optimal estimates for certain Feynman–Kac semigroups on
L^{p}
; for comparison, we refer the reader to Farkas, Jacob and Schilling
[
22
, (2.4)]
and to Jacob
[
29
, (4.294)]
, where different integral forms are used to analyze semigroups in
L^{p}
. The results of the present paper indicate that Bregman variation of semimartingales, defined in Section
2
, is suitable for addressing similar issues in the generality of semimartingales.

As usual, we say that random variable
Y
is
\phi
-integrable
if the
\phi
-moment
\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)
is finite, and we say a process is
\phi
-integrable if each of its random variables is
\phi
-integrable.
The following lemma, inspired by
[
11
, Lemma 2.1]
, indicates that
F_{\phi}
captures the evolution of the
\phi
-moment
additively
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Let
\phi
be a Young function.
Consider a random variable
Y\in L^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{F}
. If
X=\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathcal{A}]
then

(1.8)

\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)=\mathbb{E}\phi(X)+\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y).

In particular, if
\mathbb{E}\phi(X)<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)=\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)=\infty
.

Formally, the difference between the two sides of
(
1.8
) is

\mathbb{E}[\phi^{\prime}(X)(Y-X)],

which would vanish upon conditioning on
\mathcal{A}
. Possible failures
of integrability, however, make this reasoning invalid as it stands:
the expectation above, as well as the conditional expectation used to
justify its vanishing, need not be well defined. The localization
argument below avoids imposing the additional integrability assumptions
that would justify this calculation.

Rather than proving the lemma separately, we establish the following
more general result, which also has a natural interpretation in
prediction theory.

Proposition 1.4
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a finite convex function. Let

(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space, let

\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra, and let

Y
be an integrable random variable. Put

X:=\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathcal{A}].

Then, for every real-valued
\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable

Z
,

(1.9)

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,Y)=\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,X)+\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y),

where the expectations are understood as extended expectations of
nonnegative random variables. Consequently,

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)\leq\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,Y).

If, in addition,
\phi
is strictly convex and

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)<\infty,

then
X
is the unique minimizer, up to almost sure equality. The
finiteness condition holds, in particular, if

\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)<\infty.

Proof.

From the definition of
F_{\phi}
, we get the pointwise identity

(1.10)

\displaystyle F_{\phi}(Z,Y)

\displaystyle=F_{\phi}(Z,X)+F_{\phi}(X,Y)+\bigl(\phi^{\prime}(X)-\phi^{\prime}(Z)\bigr)(Y-X).

We use a localization argument. For
n\geq 1
, let

A_{n}:=\{|X|\leq n,\ |Z|\leq n\}.

The left derivative of a finite convex function is locally
bounded so the random variable

\bigl(\phi^{\prime}(X)-\phi^{\prime}(Z)\bigr)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}

is bounded and
\mathcal{A}
-measurable.
Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left[\bigl(\phi^{\prime}(X)-\phi^{\prime}(Z)\bigr)(Y-X)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}\right]=0.

Since the mixed term is integrable and has expectation zero, the
pointwise identity (
1.10
) yields

\mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{\phi}(Z,Y)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{\phi}(Z,X)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\!\left[F_{\phi}(X,Y)\mathbf{1}_{A_{n}}\right].

Letting
n\to\infty
and applying the Monotone Convergence Theorem
gives (
1.9
). Since
F_{\phi}\geq 0
, this also
proves the minimizing property of
X
.

Now suppose that
\phi
is strictly convex

[
41
, p. 253]
and that

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)<\infty
. Then

F_{\phi}(z,x)>0

for all
z\neq x
.
If
\bar{X}
is another
\mathcal{A}
-measurable minimizer, then

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(\bar{X},Y)=\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)<\infty.

Hence, by (
1.9
),

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(\bar{X},X)=0.

Therefore
\bar{X}=X
almost surely.

Finally, if
\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)<\infty
, then

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(0,Y)=\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(Y)-\phi(0)-\phi^{\prime}(0)Y\right]<\infty.

Taking
Z=0
in (
1.9
) gives

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)\leq\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(0,Y)<\infty.

∎

Proof of Lemma
1.3
.

Taking
Z=0
in (
1.9
), we obtain

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(0,Y)=\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(0,X)+\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y).

Since

F_{\phi}(0,w)=\phi(w)-\phi(0)-\phi^{\prime}(0)w

and
\mathbb{E}X=\mathbb{E}Y
, the affine terms cancel, and we get
(
1.8
). The final assertion follows.
∎

Remark 1
.

In statistical terminology,
Y
is the observed outcome,

\mathcal{A}
represents the available information, and an

\mathcal{A}
-measurable random variable
Z
is a predictor. The
quantity
F_{\phi}(z,y)

is the Bregman divergence used as the loss
incurred when
z
is used to predict
y
.

The identity (
1.9
)
decomposes the expected loss
\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,Y)
into the excess loss

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,X)
,
resulting from the use of
Z
in place of
X
, and the minimal expected
loss

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y).

Accordingly,

X=\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathcal{A}]

is an optimal predictor.

The closest published antecedent of
Proposition
1.4
is the result of Banerjee,
Guo, and Wang

[
4
, Remark 1 and Theorem 1, p. 2665; equation (1), p. 2666]
. In their notation,

D_{\phi}(y,z)=F_{\phi}(z,y),

and their equation (1) has the same algebraic form as
(
1.9
). A closely related conditional
Pythagorean identity, under the compatibility condition stated there
as equation (37), appears in

[
25
, Appendix C, equation (36) and the display following
equation (37)]
.

Unconditional mean-minimization analogues are given for finitely
supported random vectors in

[
5
, Section 3.1, Proposition 1]
,
in the language of consistent scoring functions in

[
24
, Section 3.1, Theorem 3.1(b)–(c)]
, and for random
functions and distributions in

[
23
, Theorem 3.1]
. The additional point of
Proposition
1.4
is that
(
1.9
) is formulated as an identity of extended
nonnegative expectations and is justified by localization, without
presupposing integrability of the mixed term in
(
1.10
).

The same argument extends to vector-valued random variables. More
precisely, let
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be open and convex, let

\phi:C\to\mathbb{R}
be finite and convex, and fix a Borel map

s:C\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
s(x)\in\partial\phi(x)
, a selection of the subdifferential. Define

F_{\phi,s}(x,y):=\phi(y)-\phi(x)-\langle s(x),y-x\rangle.

If
Y
is integrable,
X=\mathbb{E}[Y|\mathcal{A}]
, and
X
,
Y
,
and
Z
are
C
-valued, with
Z
being
\mathcal{A}
-measurable, then
(
1.9
) remains valid with
F_{\phi}
replaced by

F_{\phi,s}
.
In the localization argument, one additionally bounds

s(X)
and
s(Z)
. If
\phi
is differentiable, one may take

s=\nabla\phi
, recovering the usual multivariate Bregman divergence.

Finally, if

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(X,Y)=\infty,

then (
1.9
) implies that

\mathbb{E}F_{\phi}(Z,Y)=\infty

for every
\mathcal{A}
-measurable
Z
. Thus, the minimization statement
remains formally valid, but uniqueness cannot be
concluded even when
\phi
is strictly convex.

1.4.
Semimartingales

In this subsection, we follow
[
38
]
, but we also like to mention inspirations coming from
[
21
, p. 334]
. We assume that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P},(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0})
is a filtered probability space and the filtration fulfills the usual conditions, i.e., it is complete and right continuous
[
38
, p. 3]
. Unless stated otherwise, all the notions below, especially adaptedness, refer to this filtration.
Recall that a stochastic process
X:=(X_{t},\,t\geq 0)
, is called càdlàg if (a.s.) it has right-continuous paths with left limits and it is called càglàd if it is left continuous with right limits.

Definition 1.5
.

Stochastic process

X
is called a semimartingale if
X_{t}=X_{0}+M_{t}+A_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, where
M
is a real-valued adapted càdlàg local martingale,
A
is a real-valued adapted càdlàg process of finite variation,
M_{0}=A_{0}=0
, and
X_{0}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}
-measurable.

From the definition, every semimartingale
X
is adapted and càdlàg. The same concerns martingales; in what follows we tacitly or explicitly assume they are adapted and càdlàg. For a semimartingale
X
, we define

(X_{-})_{s}:=X_{s-}:=\lim_{r\uparrow s}X_{r}:=\lim_{r\to s,\,0\leq r<s}X_{r}
for
s>0
,

(1.11)

X_{0-}:=0,

and

\Delta X_{s}:=X_{s}-X_{s-}
for
s\geq 0
. Furthermore, if
Y
is a
càglàd
adapted process, then the stochastic integral

(Y\cdot X)_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\,{\rm d}X_{s},\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0,

is well defined. Here

\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}\,{\rm d}X_{s}=Y_{0}X_{0}+\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\,{\rm d}X_{s},

where the notation
\int_{0+}^{t}
on the right-hand side is that of

[
38
, Chapter II]
; see also Appendix
B
. By
[
38
, Chapter II, Sections 4 and 5]
, the process

Y\cdot X
is a semimartingale. If
X
is a local martingale, then

Y\cdot X
is a local martingale as well; see

[
38
, Chapter III, Theorem 33]
.

For a stopping time
\tau
and a semimartingale
X
, we define the
stopped
semimartingale
X^{\tau}
:

X^{\tau}_{t}:=X_{t\wedge\tau},\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0.

By
[
38
, Chapter II, Theorem 12]
,

(1.12)

Y\cdot(X^{\tau})=(Y\cdot X)^{\tau}=(Y\mathbf{1}_{[0,\tau]})\cdot X.

Here is one more notational convention: If
X:=(X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(d)})
and
Y:=(Y^{(1)},\ldots,Y^{(d)})
are continuous-time processes with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

and, for each
i
,
X^{(i)}
is a semimartingale and
Y^{(i)}
is càglàd and adapted, then
Y\cdot X:=\int Y\,{\rm d}X
denotes the process

\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y^{(i)}\cdot X^{(i)}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int Y^{(i)}{\rm d}X^{(i)}.

For more details we refer to
[
38
]
; see also Appendix
B
.

2.
Bregman variation of stochastic processes

2.1.
Discrete time

As a preparation, we discuss the discrete-time Bregman variation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\phi
be a (finite) convex function on
\mathbb{R}
and let
\phi^{\prime}
be the left derivative of
\phi
.
The
\phi
-variation of a (real-valued) process
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots
is

(2.1)

\displaystyle V^{\phi}(X)_{n}

\displaystyle:=\phi(X_{n})-\sum_{0<k\leq n}\phi^{\prime}(X_{k-1})(X_{k}-X_{k-1}),\hskip 11.74988ptn=0,1,\ldots.

Of course, each
V^{\phi}(X)_{n}
is adapted to
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{X}:=\sigma(X_{0},\ldots,X_{n})
and

\displaystyle V^{\phi}(X)_{n}

\displaystyle=\phi(X_{0})+\sum_{0<k\leq n}(\phi(X_{k})-\phi(X_{k-1}))-\sum_{0<k\leq n}\phi^{\prime}(X_{k-1})(X_{k}-X_{k-1})

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\phi(X_{0})+\sum_{0<k\leq n}F_{\phi}(X_{k-1},X_{k}).

Since
F_{\phi}\geq 0
, the process
V^{\phi}(X)
is nondecreasing. If
\phi\geq 0
, in particular if
\phi
is a Young function, then
V^{\phi}(X)\geq 0
, too.
By Lemma
1.3
and (

), we get the following result.

Corollary 2.2
.

For a Young function
\phi
and a martingale
X_{0},\ldots,X_{n}
,
\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{n})=\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{n}
.

Clearly, the sum
\sum_{0\leq k<n}\phi^{\prime}(X_{k})(X_{k+1}-X_{k})
in (
2.1
) is a discrete analogue of the stochastic integral
\int_{0}^{t}\phi^{\prime}(X_{s-}){\rm d}X_{s}
in (
1.1
), which prepares the scene for the next subsection.

2.2.

Definition 2.3

Remark 2

Corollary 2.4

The Bregman variation is càdlàg, nondecreasing, nonnegative, and adapted.

Remark 3
.

For a general semimartingale, the approximation (

) shows
that

measures the cumulative change of

up
to time

, with the size of each change measured by

. For

, this reduces to the usual quadratic variation.

For martingales, the discussion following

and

gives an additional

X
is a

\phi
-integrable martingale, fix

Since

the quantity

is the expected Bregman loss when

is used
to predict

. Proposition

and the

-isometry give for

Thus, the expected increase of the Bregman variation over

equals the amount by which the expected loss decreases when the

X_{T}
is updated from
X_{s}
to
X_{t}
.

Note that (

) needs not hold when deterministic times

are replaced by stopping times

and the stopping time

) clearly holds for

with arbitrary

. So, if we make an additional integrability assumption

, we can pass to the limit

and obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.6
.

If
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function,
(X_{t},t\geq 0)

\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{\tau})=\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{\tau}.

By (

\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X^{\tau})_{T}=\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{T\wedge\tau}\leq\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{\tau}<\infty.

Hence, Theorem

is a

\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{T\wedge\tau})=\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{T\wedge\tau},\hskip 23.49976ptT\geq 0.

So, by Corollary

\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\geq 0}\phi(X_{t}^{\tau})=\sup_{T\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\sup_{t\leq T}\phi(X_{t}^{\tau})\leq C_{\phi}\sup_{T\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{T}^{\tau})=C_{\phi}\sup_{T\geq 0}\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{T\wedge\tau}\leq C_{\phi}\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{\tau}<\infty.

\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{\tau})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t\wedge\tau})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{t\wedge\tau}=\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}(X)_{\tau}.\qed

For (closed) martingales with finite time horizon, we proceed as follows.

.

.

If

We conclude this section with a result on the
\phi
-variation of a sum of independent martingales, which generalizes the case of quadratic variation. We recall that, given

,

is (optimal) such that
\phi(2x)\leq K_{\phi}\phi(x)
for every

.

Proposition 2.12
.

Assume that
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function and
X,Y
are two independent, càdlàg
\phi
-integrable martingales with
X_{0}=Y_{0}=0
. Then,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[V^{\phi}_{t}(X)+V^{\phi}_{t}(Y)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}_{t}(X+Y)\leq\frac{K_{\phi}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[V^{\phi}_{t}(X)+V^{\phi}_{t}(Y)\right],\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0.

If for some
s>0
,
\mathbb{E}V_{s}^{\phi}(X+Y)=\mathbb{E}[V_{s}^{\phi}(X)+V_{s}^{\phi}(Y)]
for all pairs
X,Y
of independent, bounded càdlàg martingales with
X_{0}=Y_{0}=0
,
then
\phi(x)=cx^{2}
for some
c>0
.

Proof.

By the
\phi-
isometry,
\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}_{t}(X)=\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t})
,
\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}_{t}(Y)=\mathbb{E}\phi(Y_{t})
and
\mathbb{E}V^{\phi}_{t}(X+Y)=\mathbb{E}\phi(X+Y)
. Hence, it suffices to prove

\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(X_{t})+\phi(Y_{t})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t}+Y_{t})\leq\frac{K_{\phi}}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\phi(X_{t})+\phi(Y_{t})\right],\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0.

Since
\phi
is convex and
X,Y
are independent and centered, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t}+Y_{t})=\int\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t}+y){\rm d}\mu_{Y_{t}}(y)\geq\int\phi(\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]+y){\rm d}\mu_{Y_{t}}(y)=\int\phi(y){\rm d}\mu_{Y_{t}}(y)=\mathbb{E}\phi(Y_{t}).

We proceed analogously with
\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{t})
and so the lower bound follows. On the other hand,

\phi(X_{t}+Y_{t})=\phi\left(\frac{2X_{t}}{2}+\frac{2Y_{t}}{2}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi(2X_{t})+\phi(2Y_{t})\right)\leq\frac{K_{\phi}}{2}\left(\phi(X_{t})+\phi(Y_{t})\right).

So, taking expectation of both sides, we get the upper bound.

Now, assume that
\mathbb{E}V_{s}^{\phi}(X+Y)=\mathbb{E}[V_{s}^{\phi}(X)+V_{s}^{\phi}(Y)]
holds for some
s>0
and all pairs
X,Y
of independent, bounded càdlàg martingales starting from
0
. Then
\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{s}+Y_{s})=\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{s})+\mathbb{E}\phi(Y_{s})
, too. Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and let
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
be independent Rademacher random variables. Define càdlàg martingales
X
,
Y
by the formula
X_{t}=0=Y_{t}
for
t<s
and
X_{t}=x\varepsilon_{1}
,
Y_{t}=y\varepsilon_{2}
for
t\geq s
. Then, by the symmetry of
\phi
,

\mathbb{E}\phi(X_{s}+Y_{s})=\mathbb{E}\phi(x\varepsilon_{1}+y\varepsilon_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\phi(x+y)+\phi(x-y)\right).

Of course,

\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{s})+\phi(Y_{s})]=\phi(x)+\phi(y).

Hence,
\phi
satisfies the functional equation

\phi(x+y)+\phi(x-y)=2\phi(x)+2\phi(y).

Its continuous solutions are
\phi(x)=cx^{2}
,
c\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\phi
is a Young function, necessarily
c>0
.
∎

. We expect that our method should extend to other Markovian semigroups; see Gutowski and Kwaśnicki
[
28
]
and Gutowski
[
27
]
for results in the case of
\phi(t)=t^{p}
.

3.1.1.
Assumptions and preliminaries

We consider a (Lévy) measure
\nu
on
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

satisfying

\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\left(|z|^{2}\wedge 1\right)\nu({\rm d}z)<\infty.

Assume that
\nu
is symmetric:
\nu(A)=\nu(-A)
,
A\subset{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. Let

Q
be a real symmetric nonnegative-definite
d\times d
matrix. Its symmetric, nonnegative-definite square root will be denoted
\mathcal{R}=(r_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{d}:=\sqrt{Q}
. Thus,
Q=\mathcal{R}^{2}
. Let

\psi(\xi):=\frac{1}{2}\langle Q\xi,\xi\rangle+\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\left(1-\cos\langle\xi,x\rangle\right)\nu({\rm d}x),\hskip 11.74988pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},

where
\langle\xi,x\rangle
is the Euclidean scalar product in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.
There exists an
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
-valued Lévy process
(Z_{t},\,t\geq 0)
satisfying the following Lévy-Khinchine formula:

\mathbb{E}_{x}{\rm e}^{i\langle\xi,Z_{t}\rangle}={\rm e}^{i\langle\xi,x\rangle-t\psi(\xi)},\hskip 11.74988pt\xi\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}},\,t\geq 0,\,x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

Here and below, we adopt the Markovian notation, with probabilities
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for the process

starting from

x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, the corresponding expectations
\mathbb{E}_{x}
, and the
usual filtration

\mathcal{F}^{Z}_{t}
,
t\geq 0
;
see
[
38
, Theorem I.31]
or
Sato
[
43
]
. Recall that
Z
is a Hunt process, that is,
Z
is càdlàg, quasi-left continuous, and strong Markov with right-continuous complete filtration, see
[
8
, IV.7]
or
[
19
, Chapter 3]
.
To simplify technical considerations, apart from the symmetry of
\nu
, in what follows, we also assume the Hartman-Wintner condition:

(HW)

\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}\frac{\psi(\xi)}{\log|\xi|}=\infty.

We let

(3.1)

p_{t}(x):=(2\pi)^{-d}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}{\rm e}^{-i\langle\xi,x\rangle}{\rm e}^{-t\psi(\xi)}\,{\rm d}\xi,\hskip 11.74988ptt>0,\ x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

We see that
p_{t}
is symmetric;
p_{t}(-x)=p_{t}(x)
. It is a convolution semigroup of probability densities, i.e.,
p_{t}*p_{s}=p_{t+s}
. By (
3.1
) and (
HW
),
\|p_{t}\|_{\infty}=p_{t}(0)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
. Furthermore,
p_{t}(x)
is smooth (i.e.,
C^{\infty}
) in
x
, with all the derivatives bounded and integrable; see
[
31
, Theorem 2.1]
.
We let
p_{t}(x,y):=p_{t}(y-x)
. Of course,
p_{t}(x,y)=p_{t}(y,x)

and

(3.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x){\rm d}x=1.

The function
(t,x,y)\mapsto p_{t}(x,y)
is a transition density of
Z,
the Hunt process associated with
\Psi
. In particular,
the semigroup of
Z
is given by

(3.3)

P_{t}f(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}f(Z_{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(y)p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y=f*p_{t}(x),\hskip 11.74988ptt>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

for nonnegative or integrable function
f
. Moreover, we let
P_{0}f:=f
.
If
f\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
P_{t}f\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in fact, the operators
P_{t}
are contractions on
L^{\phi}
. Indeed, for
t>0
,
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,

(3.4)

\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(f(Z_{t}))=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f(y))p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y\leq p_{t}(0)\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y<\infty,

hence, by Jensen’s inequality and (
3.3
),

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(P_{t}f(x)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\mathbb{E}_{x}f(Z_{t})){\rm d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(f(Z_{t})){\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y.

The last equality in (
3.5
) follows from Tonelli’s theorem and (
3.2
), which indeed give

(3.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(y))p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}x{\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y.

Of course, (
3.5
) remains true for
t=0
.
Considering
f/\|f\|_{\phi}
with
\|f\|_{\phi}>0
, by (
1.5
) we get

(3.7)

\|P_{t}f\|_{\phi}\leq\|f\|_{\phi},\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0.

We say that function
g:I\times U\to\mathbb{R}
is in class
C^{1,2}(I\times U)=C^{1,2}
(with
I\subset\mathbb{R},U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
open), if for every
t\in I
the function
U\ni x\mapsto g(t,x)
is
C^{2}
and for every
x\in U
the function
I\ni t\mapsto g(t,x)
is
C^{1}
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The map
(0,\infty)\times{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ni(t,x)\mapsto P_{t}f(x)
is
C^{1,2}
for
f\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Proof.

In view of Proposition
1.2
(2) and linearity of
P_{t}
, it suffices to consider
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
. In this case, the smoothness in the spatial variable was proved in
[
6
]
just above Example 1 therein, so we consider the time derivative.
Denote
q=p/(p-1)
. If
g\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
, then
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})\ni h\mapsto h*g(x)\in\mathbb{R}
is a continuous linear functional. In particular, this applies to
g:=p_{s}
for each
s>0
.
Of course,
P_{t}
is a bounded linear operator, in fact, a contraction on
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
.
Furthermore, the operator-valued map
(0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto P_{t}
extends to a sector in
\mathbb{C}
and is holomorphic there in the operator norm; see

[
44
, Theorem III.1, p. 67]
.
Moreover, any strongly holomorphic function is weakly holomorphic
[
42
, p. 80]
.
With this in mind, we let
t>s>0
and
n=0,1,\ldots
, and we see that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}t^{n}}\left(P_{t}f(x)\right)=\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}t^{n}}\left((P_{t-s}f)*p_{s}(x)\right)=\left(\left(\frac{{\rm d}^{n}}{{\rm d}t^{n}}P_{t-s}\right)f\right)*p_{s}(x)

exists and is finite. Thus,
(0,\infty)\ni t\mapsto P_{t}f(x)
is smooth.
∎

We note in passing that
p_{t}(x,\cdot)\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
t>0,

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and moderate Young function
\phi
. Indeed,
\phi(\lambda)/\lambda
is non-decreasing in
\lambda\geq 0
, so

(3.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(p_{t}(x,y)){\rm d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\phi(p_{t}(x,y))}{p_{t}(x,y)}p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y\leq\frac{\phi(p_{t}(0))}{p_{t}(0)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p_{t}(x,y){\rm d}y<\infty.

For later use, we recall that the process
Z
can be decomposed as

(3.9)

Z_{t}=D_{t}+J_{t},\hskip 11.74988ptt\geq 0,

where
D
is the diffusive part and
J
is the jump part.
Furthermore, the diffusive (continuous) part can be written as
D_{t}=\mathcal{R}W_{t}
, where
W
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion independent of
J
. For instance, if
\nu=0
and
Q={\rm I}_{d}
, the identity matrix, then
Z
is the Brownian motion.

3.1.2.
Parabolic martingale

The
Lévy
process

Z

is a semimartingale
[
38
, Section I.4]
.
We also consider the so-called
parabolic martingale

M
, defined as

(3.10)

M_{t}:=P_{T-t}f(Z_{t}),\hskip 11.74988pt0\leq t\leq T,

where
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
T\in(0,\infty)
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, then
M
is a
\phi-
integrable martingale under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
, for each
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
.

Proof.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. From Young’s inequality,
f\in L^{\phi}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and (
3.8
) with
\phi^{*}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{x}|f(Z_{T})|\leq\int\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y+\int\phi^{*}(p_{T}(x,y)){\rm d}y<\infty.

By the Markov property,

\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(Z_{T})|\mathcal{F}_{t}]=\mathbb{P}_{T-t}f(Z_{t})=M_{t},\hskip 11.74988ptt\in[0,T].

Thus the parabolic martingale is closed by
f(Z_{T})
.
Furthermore, by the conditional Jensen inequality and (
3.4
), for
t\in[0,T]
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(M_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(\mathbb{E}_{x}[f(Z_{T})|\mathcal{F}_{t}])\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}(\mathbb{E}_{x}[\phi(f(Z_{T}))|\mathcal{F}_{t}])=\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(f(Z_{T}))<\infty.

∎

Recall that for every (fixed)
t\geq 0
,
Z_{t}=Z_{t-}
a.s. Here is the limiting behavior of
M
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
,
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. we have
M_{t}\to M_{T}
as
t\to T
.

Proof.

This follows from the Martingale Convergence Theorem
[
38
, Chapter I, Theorem 9]
since
M
is closed by
f(Z_{T})
, which equals
f(Z_{T-})
a.s., so it is
measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{T-}^{Z}
, the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the union of
\mathcal{F}_{t}^{Z}
,
t\in[0,T)
; see Chung
[
19
, Theorem 10 of Section 1.3]
.
∎

The next result, concerning general symmetric Markov processes, was proved for
\phi(t)=t^{p}
,
p\in(1,\infty)
, by Kim
[
30
, Proposition 2.3]
. The proof applies verbatim
to moderate Young functions
\phi
, once we use an extension of Doob’s inequality
given in Corollary
A.3
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Assume that
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function with Simonenko indices
1<d_{\phi}\leq D_{\phi}<\infty
. Let
C_{\phi}=\left(d_{\phi}/(d_{\phi}-1)\right)^{D_{\phi}}
. Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f(x)|\right)\,{\rm d}x\leq C_{\phi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(x))\,{\rm d}x<\infty,\hskip 23.49976ptf\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}}).

We also have a Stein-type inequality for the Luxemburg norm,

(3.11)

\|\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}f|\|_{\phi}\leq C_{\phi}\|f\|_{\phi}.

Indeed, we can assume
\|f\|_{\phi}>0
and apply Proposition
3.4
with
\widetilde{f}:=f/\|f\|_{\phi}
, to obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}\widetilde{f}(x)|)\,{\rm d}x\leq C_{\phi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\widetilde{f}(x)){\rm d}x=C_{\phi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\left(f(x)/\|f\|_{\phi}\right){\rm d}x\leq C_{\phi},

by (
1.5
). Since
C_{\phi}\geq 1
, by the convexity of
\phi
and
\phi(0)=0,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi\left(\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}\widetilde{f}(x)|/C_{\phi}\right)\,{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(\sup_{t\geq 0}|P_{t}\widetilde{f}(x)|)\,{\rm d}x/C_{\phi}\leq 1,

so (
3.11
) follows the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\phi}
.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function and
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
, then

(3.12)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(P_{t}f(x)){\rm d}x=0.

Proof.

By Jensen’s inequality and (
3.4
), for
0<t\to\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\phi\left(P_{t}f(x)\right)\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(f(Z_{t}))\leq p_{t}(0)\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y\to 0.

The result follows from Proposition
3.4
and the Dominated Convergence Theorem.
∎

3.1.3.
The parabolic Hardy–Stein identity

As before, let
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the semigroup of the process
Z,
as given by (
3.3
). Here is the main result of Subsection
3.1
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\phi
be a moderate Young function with absolutely continuous derivative. Under the assumptions of Subsection
3.1.1
,
for every
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi(f(x)){\rm d}x=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(P_{t}f(y))\left\langle Q\nabla P_{t}f(y),\nabla P_{t}f(y)\right\rangle{\rm d}y{\rm d}t

(3.13)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}F_{\phi}(P_{t}f(y),P_{t}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

Proof.

Fix
f\in L^{\phi}({\mathbb{R}^{d}})
and
T\in(0,\infty)
, then consider the parabolic martingale
M=(M_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
, given by (
3.10
).
After some preparation,
we will apply Theorem
2.5

and Tonelli’s theorem to this martingale.
We first calculate the
\phi
-variation of
M.

Let

G(t,y):=P_{T-t}f(y),\hskip 11.74988pt0\leq t<T,\ y\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}.

Thus,
M_{t}=G(t,Z_{t})
when
0\leq t<T
.
By the discussion following (
HW
), in particular Lemma
3.1
,
G(t,x)
is
C^{1,2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so
Itô’s formula for semimartingales
[
20
, Corollary 14.2.9]
can be applied. It yields, for
t\in[0,T)
,

\displaystyle M_{t}-M_{0}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial G}{\partial s}(s,Z_{s-})\,{\rm d}s+\int_{0^{+}}^{t}\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-}){\rm d}Z_{s}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0^{+}}^{t}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\frac{\partial^{2}G}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(s,Z_{s-})\,{\rm d}[Z_{i},Z_{j}]_{s}^{c}

(3.14)

\displaystyle+\sum_{0<s\leq t,\,\Delta Z_{s}\neq 0}\!\!\!\!\!\!G(s,Z_{s})-G(s,Z_{s-})-\left\langle\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-}),(Z_{s}-Z_{s-})\right\rangle.

We now apply the formula (
2.13
).
By Lemma
3.3
, for every

x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
one has
M_{T-}=M_{T},

\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. Hence,

F_{\phi}(M_{T-},M_{T})=0
,
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s.
According to (
3.9
) and (
3.14
), the only term that can contribute to the continuous part of the quadratic variation of
M
is the stochastic integral

\displaystyle I_{t}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0+}^{t}\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-}){\rm d}Z_{s}=\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-}){\rm d}D_{s}+\int_{0+}^{t}\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-}){\rm d}J_{s}

\displaystyle=:I_{t}^{(1)}+I_{t}^{(2)},\hskip 11.74988pt0\leq t<T.

Since
J
is a pure jump process,
[I^{(2)}]^{c}=0
and
[I^{(1)},I^{(2)}]^{c}=0
, see (
2.9
). It follows that

[I]^{c}_{t}=\left[\int_{0}^{\cdot}\nabla_{x}G(s,Z_{s-})\,{\rm d}D_{s}\right]_{t}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{i}}(s,Z_{s-})\frac{\partial G}{\partial x_{j}}(s,Z_{s-})\,{\rm d}[D^{i},D^{j}]_{s}.

It remains to compute the quadratic covariations
[D^{i},D^{j}]
. Since
D_{t}=\mathcal{R}W_{t}
, it is elementary to see that

[D^{i},D^{j}]_{s}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{d}r_{ik}r_{kj}\right)s=q_{ij}s
.
Thus, for every
0\leq t<T,

\displaystyle[M]_{t}^{c}=[I]^{c}_{t}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\langle Q\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(Z_{s-}),\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(Z_{s-})\rangle{\rm d}s.

By Theorem
2.10
,

\displaystyle V^{\phi}(M)_{T}=\phi(M_{0})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{\prime\prime}(M_{s-})\langle Q\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(Z_{s-}),\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(Z_{s-})\rangle{\rm d}s

(3.15)

\displaystyle+\sum_{0<s<T,\Delta M_{s}\neq 0}F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s}).

We will integrate (
3.15
) first with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{x}
and then with respect to
{\rm d}x
, the Lebesgue measure.
For the left-hand side of (
3.15
), from Theorem
2.5
and Tonelli’s theorem,

(3.16)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{x}V^{\phi}(M)_{T}{\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\phi(M_{T}){\rm d}x=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(y)){\rm d}y.

We then consider the terms on the right-hand side of (
3.15
).
Since
p_{s}(x,y)
is the density of both
Z_{s}
and
Z_{s-}
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
, by (
3.2
),
we get

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{T}\phi^{\prime\prime}(M_{s-}){\rm d}[M]_{s}^{c}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(P_{T-s}f(y))\left\langle Q\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(y),\nabla_{x}P_{T-s}f(y)\right\rangle p_{s}(x,y){\rm d}y{\rm d}x{\rm d}s

(3.17)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(P_{t}f(y))\left\langle Q\nabla_{x}P_{t}f(y),\nabla_{x}P_{t}f(y)\right\rangle{\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

We then consider
\sum_{0<s<T,\,\Delta M_{s}\neq 0}F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s})
. Since the jumps of
M
can only occur at the jump times of
Z
,
it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{0<s<T,\,\Delta M_{s}\neq 0}F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s})=\sum_{0<s<T,\,\Delta Z_{s}\neq 0}F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s})=\sum_{0<s<\infty}H_{T}^{\phi}(s,Z_{s-},Z_{s}),

where

H_{T}^{\phi}(s,x,y):=\mathbf{1}_{[0,T)}(s)F_{\phi}(P_{T-s}f(x),P_{T-s}f(y)).

This is a nonnegative (Borel) function so by the Lévy system of
Z
, see, e.g., Bogdan, Rosiński, Serafin and Wojciechowski
[
14
, Lemma 4.1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\!\!\sum_{0<s<T,\,\Delta M_{s}\neq 0}\!\!\!F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}H_{T}^{\phi}(s,Z_{s},Z_{s}+z)\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}s

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{T-s}f(Z_{s}),P_{T-s}f(Z_{s}+z))\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}s

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{T-s}f(y),P_{T-s}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z)p_{s}(x,y){\rm d}y{\rm d}s.

Here
x\in{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is arbitrary. Integrating with respect to
{\rm d}x
and using Tonelli’s theorem and (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{x}\!\!\!\sum_{0<s<T,\,\Delta M_{s}\neq 0}\!\!\!F_{\phi}(M_{s-},M_{s}){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{T-s}f(y),P_{T-s}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z)p_{s}(x,y){\rm d}y{\rm d}s{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{T-s}f(y),P_{T-s}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}y{\rm d}s

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{t}f(y),P_{t}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}y{\rm d}t.

Considering (
3.15
), (
3.16
), (
3.17
) and (
3.18
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(f(x)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi^{\prime\prime}(P_{t}f(y))\left\langle Q\nabla_{x}P_{t}f(y),\nabla_{x}P_{t}f(y)\right\rangle{\rm d}y{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\phantom{ppp}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}F_{\phi}(P_{t}f(y),P_{t}f(y+z))\nu({\rm d}z){\rm d}y{\rm d}t+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(P_{T}f(x)){\rm d}x.

We let
T\to\infty
.
By
Lemma
3.5
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(P_{T}f(x)){\rm d}x\to 0
. All the integrands are nonnegative, so the Monotone Convergence Theorem yields (
3.13
).
∎

3.2.
Elliptic Hardy–Stein identity

In this subsection, we prove Hardy–Stein identities for harmonic functions of Lévy processes. The results generalize
[
11
, Section 3]
by allowing rather general convex functions
\phi
and more general processes.
Let
Z
be the Lévy process from Subsection
3.1.1
.
For technical reasons that will become apparent later on, we
additionally
assume that
the Lévy measure
\nu
is
unimodal

[
26
]
. More precisely, we require
\nu({\rm d}x)=\nu(|x|){\rm d}x
, with nonincreasing

Here is a Weyl-type lemma for unimodal Lévy operators, an extension of

Lemma 3.7
.

Assume that for every

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

\displaystyle\infty>u(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}u(x+y)P_{r}(dy)\geq\int_{|z|>r}u(x+z)P_{r}(z)dz

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}\tau_{B_{r}}\int_{|z|>r}u(x+z)\nu(|z|+r)dz.

Considering two such points

The class of moderate Young functions with absolutely continuous derivative contains
|\lambda|^{p}
and functions with the following growth at infinity:

|\lambda|^{p}\log^{\gamma_{1}}(e+|\lambda|),\hskip 23.49976pt|\lambda|^{p}\log^{\gamma_{1}}(e+|\lambda|)\big(\log\log(e^{e}+|\lambda|)\big)^{\gamma_{2}},\hskip 11.74988pt\text{etc.}

Here
p\in(1,\infty)
and
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\ldots\in\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, let
h
denote one of the displayed functions. Direct differentiation gives

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\lambda h^{\prime}(\lambda)}{h(\lambda)}=p,\hskip 23.49976pt\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\lambda^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)}{h(\lambda)}=p(p-1).

Hence, for some sufficiently large

, the function

is strictly increasing and strictly convex on
[R,\infty)
. We can modify

3.6
. For instance, increasing

\phi(\lambda):=\begin{cases}h(R)(|\lambda|/R)^{q},&|\lambda|\leq R,\\
h(|\lambda|),&|\lambda|>R.\end{cases}

The values and the first derivatives of the two expressions on the right-hand side agree at
\pm R

\phi
is a Young function with absolutely continuous derivative. Moreover,

\frac{\lambda\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)}{\phi(\lambda)}=q,\hskip 23.49976pt0<\lambda<R,

while

\frac{\lambda\phi^{\prime}(\lambda)}{\phi(\lambda)}\longrightarrow p\hskip 23.49976pt\text{as }\lambda\to\infty.

By choosing

Thus, by the discussion following (

[

and the references therein.

Appendix A
Random variables and martingales in
L^{\phi}

We collect here some properties of random variables and processes that are
\phi
-integrable. Since we did not find a comprehensive reference, we provide some proofs.

Lemma A.1
.

Assume that
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
is a Young function. Let
X,Y
be
\phi
-integrable random variables. Then,
\phi^{\prime}(X)Y\in L^{1}(\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

Since
\phi\in\Delta_{2}
, we get
|\phi^{\prime}(x)|\leq D_{\phi}\phi(x)/|x|,x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
\phi(x)/|x|
is interpreted as
0
if
x=0,

Moreover,
\phi(x)|y|/|x|\leq\phi(x)
for
|x|\geq|y|
and
\phi(x)|y|/|x|\leq\phi(y)
for
|y|\geq|x|
because
\phi(x)/x
is nondecreasing on
(0,\infty)
. Hence,

|\phi^{\prime}(x)y|\leq D_{\phi}\frac{\phi(x)}{|x|}|y|\leq D_{\phi}\big(\phi(x)+\phi(y)\big),\hskip 11.74988ptx,y\in\mathbb{R}.

The desired integrability follows from the
\phi
-integrability of
X
and
Y
.
∎

The following result is a generalization of Doob’s
L^{p}
-inequality, see Alsmeyer and Rösler
[
1
, Proposition 1.1]
see also Kühn and Schilling
[
33
]
for a survey and Osękowski
[
37
]
for specialized results.

Lemma A.2
.

If
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function, then for any martingale
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
,

(A.1)

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{k\leq n}\phi(X_{k})]\leq C_{\phi}\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{n})],\;n=0,1,2,\ldots,

where
C_{\phi}=\left(d_{\phi}/(d_{\phi}-1)\right)^{D_{\phi}}
and

and

are the Simonenko indices of
\phi
.

Here is a variant for continuous time martingales, with the same
C_{\phi}
.

Corollary A.3
.

If
\phi
is a
C^{1}
moderate Young function, then for every
càdlàg martingale
X=(X_{t})_{0\leq t<\infty}
,

(A.2)

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\phi(X_{s})]\leq C_{\phi}\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{t})],\hskip 11.74988pt0\leq t<\infty.

Proof.

Let
t\in[0,\infty)
. Let
\{D_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
be an ascending sequence of finite subsets of
[0,t]
containing

is dense in

. By the right continuity of

and Jensen’s inequality, we get

\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s\leq t}\phi(X_{s})]=\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s\in D}\phi(X_{s})]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[\sup_{s\in D_{n}}\phi(X_{s})]\leq C_{\phi}\mathbb{E}[\phi(X_{t})].\qed

We finish this subsection with a simple lemma concerning the continuous embedding of
(L^{\phi}(\Omega,\mathbb{P}),\|\cdot\|_{\phi})
into
(L^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{P}),\|\cdot\|_{1})
. For completeness, we give a proof.

Lemma A.4
.

Assume that
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
is a probability space and let
\phi
be a Young function.
Then there exists
A_{\phi}
such that

\|X\|_{1}\leq A_{\phi}\|X\|_{\phi}
for all
X\in L^{\phi}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
.

Proof.

Due to homogeneity, we may assume that
\|X\|_{\phi}=1
; the case
\|X\|_{\phi}=0
is trivial.
Since
\phi(\lambda)
is convex and non-zero for large

, there exist constants
a_{\phi},b_{\phi}>0
such that
\phi(\lambda)\geq a_{\phi}\lambda-b_{\phi}
for every

\|X\|_{1}=\int_{\Omega}|X|{\rm d}\mathbb{P}\leq\frac{1}{a_{\phi}}\int_{\Omega}\left(\phi(|X|)+b_{\phi}\right){\rm d}\mathbb{P}\leq\frac{1+b_{\phi}}{a_{\phi}}=\frac{1+b_{\phi}}{a_{\phi}}\|X\|_{\phi}.\qed

\Omega\times[0,\infty)

\mathcal{S}_{u}
.

By
\mathbb{D}
(respectively
\mathbb{L})
we denote the spaces of càdlàg, adapted processes (respectively, càglàd, adapted processes). For a process
Y
, let
Y^{*}_{t}=\sup_{s\leq t}|Y_{s}|
for
t\geq 0
. We consider the following notion of convergence.

Definition B.2
.

A sequence of càdlàg processes
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
converges to a process
X

uniformly on compacts in probability
(in short,
ucp
) if for every
t\in[0,\infty)
we have
(X^{(n)}-X)^{*}_{t}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}}0
. We then write
X^{(n)}\xrightarrow{ucp}X
.

By
\mathbb{D}_{ucp},\mathbb{L}_{ucp},\mathcal{S}_{ucp}
we denote the spaces
\mathbb{D},

\mathbb{L},

\mathcal{S}
(respectively) endowed with
ucp
topology. Let us recall that given a càdlàg, adapted process
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and stopping time
\tau
, the stopped process
X^{\tau}
, defined by
X^{\tau}_{t}=X_{\min\{t,\tau\}}
is also càdlàg and adapted.

Definition B.3
.

For a semimartingale
X
we define the mapping
J_{X}:\mathcal{S}_{ucp}\to\mathbb{D}_{ucp}
by

J_{X}(H)=H_{0}X_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}H_{k}(X^{T_{k+1}}-X^{T_{k}}),

for
H\in\mathcal{S}
of the form (
B.1
).

J_{X}
is a well-defined linear mapping. If
X
is càdlàg, then
J_{X}(H)\in\mathbb{D}
for every
H\in\mathcal{S}
, because
J_{X}(H)
is a sum of càdlàg processes.
As the space
\mathcal{S}_{ucp}
is dense in
\mathbb{L}_{ucp}
, and for
a semimartingale
X
, the mapping
J_{X}:\mathcal{S}_{ucp}\to\mathbb{D}_{ucp}
is continuous

[
38
, Chapter II, Theorems 10, 11]
, the mapping
J_{X}
extends from
\mathcal{S}_{ucp}
to
\mathbb{L}_{ucp}
. It is called the stochastic integral with respect to
X
.
Summarizing, the integral

J_{X}(Y)=(J_{X}(Y)_{t})_{t\geq 0}=\Big(\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}dX_{s}\Big)_{t\geq 0}

is well defined for every semimartingale
X
and every process
Y\in\mathbb{L}_{ucp}
. If
X
is a local martingale, then
J_{X}(Y)
is a local martingale as well
[
38
, Chapter III, Theorem 33]
. Of course,
J_{X}(Y)
is denoted
Y\cdot X
in our development above.

A sequence of random partitions
\pi^{(n)}=\{0=\tau_{0}^{(n)}\leq\tau_{1}^{(n)}<\ldots<\tau_{k_{n}}^{(n)}\},

n=1,2,\ldots
, is said to tend to the identity when
n\to\infty,
if
\diam(\pi^{(n)})=\max_{j<n}|\tau_{j+1}^{(n)}-t_{j}^{(n)}|
converge to
0
a.s. and
\tau_{k_{n}}^{(n)}\to\infty
a.s.
Here
\tau_{j}^{(n)}
are assumed to be stopping times.
The next theorem asserts that the stochastic integral above can be approximated by discrete integral sums (see
[
38
, Chapter II, Theorem 29]
).

Theorem B.4
.

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\in(0,\infty)}
be a semimartingale and let
Y\in\mathbb{L}
. If

\pi^{(n)}=\{0=t_{0}^{(n)}<t^{(n)}_{1}<\ldots<t_{k_{n}}^{(n)}=T\},\hskip 11.74988ptn=1,2,\ldots,

is a sequence of random partitions tending to identity,
then

\sum_{j=0}^{k_{n}-1}Y_{t_{j}^{(n)}}\big(X^{t_{j+1}^{(n)}}-X^{t_{j}^{(n)}}\big)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\text{ucp}}J_{X}(Y).
