Establishing Reporting Requirements for Decision‐Makers: A Cross‐Sectional Study From a Public Dental Service in New South Wales, Australia
Authors: Jared S Walters, Michelle J Irving, Glen C Livingston, Dileep Sharma, Janet P Wallace
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesPublic dental organizations require effective and evidence‐based support for clinical and business decision‐making. A pilot business intelligence system has been proposed for a public dental service in New South Wales, Australia, to aid with this decision‐making. To inform the design and development of this system, we aim to identify the key performance indicators and report features perceived to be the most valuable to the dental service decision‐makers.MethodA self‐administered survey employing best–worst scaling with balanced incomplete block designs was sent to all eligible decision‐makers in a Local Health District public dental service in New South Wales, Australia. Participants ranked seven areas of information and five report features. Quantitative rankings were complemented with free‐text comments.ResultsNine of the possible 12 participants (75%) completed the survey. The results reported “Patient demand” as the most important area of information for inclusion in the business intelligence system, with “patient demographics” ranked the lowest. For report features, the most important features were reported to be “readability” and “level of detail”. All participants reported that they were expected to use computerized reports as part of their role, with four (44%) participants reporting accessing computerized reports more than 100 times in their previous 30 working days.ConclusionThe novel pilot study applies best–worst scaling for prioritizing key performance indicators among public dental decision‐makers at a Local Health District within New South Wales and underscores the established role of computerized reports in decision‐making processes. These findings will directly guide the design and implementation of a pilot business intelligence reporting system, aiming to enhance data accessibility and accelerate evidence‐based decisions within a public dental service to ultimately enhance service efficiency. Future evaluation will be needed to assess the adoption rates of this system and its impact on operational efficiency and patient care outcomes.

Introduction

Effective decision‐making is crucial for health providers and organizations, requiring managers to navigate complex choices with significant consequences for patient care, financial performance, and operational efficiency. These decisions are often made by clinical managers, business operations managers, and policymakers. An ideal decision‐making process would involve a detailed analysis of options and their expected outcomes, and the selection of the choice that aligns most closely with organizational priorities [1]. To enable this, the development of a pilot on‐demand reporting system using business intelligence software for decision‐makers is needed. A co‐designed reporting system can aid in decision‐making processes to improve oral health outcomes for patients attending the public dental service.

Australia's public dental service plays a vital role in providing essential care, particularly for priority populations [2]. However, the service faces significant challenges, including limited resources and funding. As outlined in Australia's National Oral Health Plan 2015–2024, only approximately 20% of eligible individuals receive publicly funded dental care [3]. This constraint is particularly evident in New South Wales, where over 86,000 patients are currently on waiting lists for treatment, with comprehensive care often requiring waits exceeding a year [4]. This wait for dental care is not unique to NSW, as most states and territories in Australia also experience similar long waiting periods [2]. Long waiting lists and limited resources/funding can lead to inadequate care, poor health outcomes, and reduced patient satisfaction [5]. Despite these challenges, public dental services play a vital role in the provision of dental care, promoting oral health, preventing disease, and improving the quality of life for individuals and communities, especially for priority populations.

The dental service provided by the New South Wales public dental service involves 13 Local Health Districts [6] and is funded by varying levels of the Australian Government. To be eligible for these services, patients 18 years or older must be a recipient of an Australian Government concession card (Health Care Card, Pensioner Concession Card, or Commonwealth Seniors Health Card) and must be listed on an Australian Medicare card. Patients under the age of 18 are only required to be listed on a Medicare card to be eligible [7]. The number of patients waiting for dental care and the length of wait are examples of some of the key performance indicators measured within the public dental service of New South Wales.

Addressing these challenges requires improved decision‐making capabilities, driven by readily accessible and reliable data. To this end, a self‐service reporting system using business intelligence software would be critical for dental decision‐makers in New South Wales. This system will aim to connect to the existing electronic dental software database to provide up‐to‐date information on key performance indicators, trends, and forecasts.

Various studies have reported key performance indicators for dental care services [8, 9, 10]. However, a systematic review found that the majority of measures were developed by private sector providers and were not published in peer‐reviewed journals [9]. Further investigation is required to identify the indicators that can be evaluated by interrogating the existing data within the electronic dental services database and analyzing their influence on the decision‐making processes.

To inform the design of business intelligence software, this study aims to collate the existing performance indicators used by decision‐makers at the dental service and the perceived need for any additional performance indicators. Furthermore, the perceived importance of the performance indicators and the key features of automated reporting systems will be assessed. The results from this study will shape the development of the pilot on‐demand reporting system for a public dental service in New South Wales.

Method

Survey Design

A self‐administered survey was developed that included 21 questions on the areas of information important for service‐wide dental decision‐makers (“appointment utilization”, “patient outcomes”, “clinical treatment”, “clinical productivity”, “financial”, “patient demand” and “patient demographics”), the feature of computerized reports important to the decision maker's role (“easy to use” “readability”, “ability to filter information”, “real‐time information”, and “level of detail”), the frequency computerized reports are used in their role, the expectation to use computerized reports, performance indicators currently used in the dental service and performance indicators the participant would like added in future computerized reports. For this study, computerized reports refer to any reports generated by a computer system/software.

The survey utilized the best–worst scaling methodology, specifically Case 1, also known as Object Case. This methodology was used to determine the relative importance of the participant's response and establish a complete ranking [11]. Two sets of block questions utilizing the balanced incomplete block design from the best–worst scaling method [12] were also included in the survey.

The first set of balanced incomplete block design questions asked the participants about the importance of areas of information they utilize in their role. Each area of information was presented as an option to the participant four times within the block of seven questions. The full set of balanced incomplete block design questions was completed by nine participants with no errors.

One question utilized the Likert scale [13] with the question “Do you agree with the following statement: Computerized reporting assists my decision‐making in my role at Hunter New England Oral Health”. The available answers ranged from “strongly agree” (score: 5) to “strongly disagree” (score: 1). The remaining questions were made up of free‐text fields or multi‐select lists.

The balanced incomplete block design questions were constructed using the “support.BWS” package [14, 15] in R software [14, 15, 16]. Consultation occurred between the authors and a Health Economist and Senior Research Fellow at the Sydney School of Public Health, The University of Sydney, to review the application of the Best–worst scaling methodology to this research. The order of answers in the balanced incomplete block design questions was randomized utilizing Python code created by the lead author. This was required as the display order of the answers in the matrix‐style questionnaires could not be randomized in REDCap with the version in use at the time, version 13.8.1.

Study data, survey design, and survey distribution were completed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the Hunter New England Local Health District. REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) is a secure, web‐based application designed to support data capture for research studies, providing an intuitive interface for validated data entry, audit trails for tracking data manipulation and export procedures, automated export procedures for seamless data downloads to common statistical packages and procedures for importing data from external sources [17, 18]. A copy of the survey can be found in the appendix.

Ethics Approval

Hunter New England Local Health District in New South Wales, Australia, approved the study (AU202301‐04).

Recruitment of Participants

The targeted participants included the service‐wide management personnel employed within the public dental service in New South Wales, Australia. Clinic‐specific managers and higher‐level health executives were excluded as the proposed reporting system is intended for use by the organizational service‐wide decision‐makers employed within the dental service. The targeted participant roles included General Manager for Oral Health, Clinical Director for Oral Health, Clinical Lead for Oral Health, Data Systems Manager for Oral Health, Finance Manager for Oral Health, Operations Manager for Oral Health and Professional Team Leader for Oral Health.

An introductory email to the participants was sent by the Executive Assistant within the Local Health District. The information statement, consent form, and survey were then sent via email from the REDCap software. REDCap automated the reminder emails to participants who had not already completed the survey [17, 18].

A total of 12 decision‐makers (100% of eligible participants) were emailed invitations to participate in the survey. A flowchart of the recruitment process and method can be found in Figure 1.

Data Analysis

The responses from the survey were imported into a Microsoft SQL database. The import included the raw data and labels extracted from REDCap. Incomplete responses were filtered out, and the output was then analyzed using R. The Likert questions utilized the R package “Likert” [19], and the best–worst scaling questions were analyzed utilizing the R package “support. BWS” [14, 15].

Results

Of the 12 eligible participants, a total of 11 (92%) decision‐makers responded to the survey. Of the 11 respondents, two responses were partially completed. Of the nine participants who completed the survey in its entirety, five (56%) reported themselves as dental practitioners, with the remaining survey completed by four (44%) non‐dental practitioners. This resulted in nine (75%) completed surveys being included in the analysis, as partial responses were excluded.

Important Areas of Information for Decision‐Making

Survey participants indicated that “patient demand” was the most important area of information to the participant's role, with a best–worst mean of 1.56, while “clinical productivity” ranked second with a best–worst mean of 1.11. “Patient demographics” was ranked last with a best–worst mean of −2.56. For non‐dental practitioner participants (n = 4), “financial” was ranked first. Computerized Reports Usage

Regarding the frequency of computerized reports, participants were asked about the frequency of use in the last 30 working days. Four participants (44%) selected more than 100 times, two (22%) selected 61–80 times, one (11%) selected 41–60 times and two (22%) selected 21–40 times. No participants selected 0–20 times, indicating that all participants are currently using computerized reports in their role very frequently. Additionally, all participants (100%) reported that they were expected to use computerized reports as part of their role.

When asked, “Do you agree with the following statement: computerized reporting assists my decision‐making in my role,” seven (78%) of the responses were “strongly agree” and the remaining two (22%) responses were “agree”.

Discussion

This study was undertaken to establish a clear understanding of current performance indicators used by the dental service decision‐makers and to identify potential gaps in reporting capabilities. The overarching goal was to inform the development of a self‐service reporting system designed to improve data accessibility, analysis, and ultimately, decision‐making within the public dental service. Recognizing that effective decision‐making relies on readily available and relevant information, this study aimed to capture the specific needs and preferences of those responsible for guiding the direction of the dental service. The findings from this study can directly inform the design and functionality of the new self‐service reporting system, ensuring it aligns with practical requirements and contributes to improved oral health outcomes for patients.

This survey‐based study included a mix of participants, both dental practitioners and non‐dental practitioners, in service‐wide decision‐making roles. It is important to recognize that the nature of their roles may influence their perspectives and priorities regarding data‐driven decision‐making. Dental practitioners, with their clinical background, may prioritize more clinically focused areas, reflecting their direct involvement in patient care and treatment delivery. On the other hand, non‐dental practitioners, such as finance managers or operations managers, may focus more on financial performance and operational efficiency in their decision‐making process. Evidence of this can be found in the differences in the rankings for the areas of information important to the participant's role; however, the low sample size needs to be considered. Factoring in the variety of roles and responsibilities among the service‐wide decision‐makers is fundamental in designing effective decision‐support systems. Tailoring reporting systems to accommodate the specific needs and preferences of different roles can enhance user engagement, usability, and satisfaction. Providing role‐specific dashboards, customizable reporting templates, and interactive data visualization tools can empower decision‐makers to access relevant information, analyze data trends, and make informed decisions aligned with their respective areas of expertise and responsibilities.

The area of information “patient outcomes” was ranked low in the results of this survey; one possible explanation for this lower ranking could be that outcome data is often less accessible or not captured in a standardized way. This is in comparison to more productivity metrics such as “patient demand”, “appointment utilization” and “clinical productivity” which can be extracted from the electronic dental record database. Kalenderian et al. [9, 20, 22]. A low emphasis on patient outcomes carries service risk and may result in sub‐optimal treatment pathways or missed opportunities for quality improvement initiatives. NSW Health has implemented a patient‐reported outcome measurement platform; however, this is not implemented with the dental services at the time of writing [21]. In contrast, the areas of information, “patient demand” and “clinical productivity” were ranked the highest. These coincide with established measure groups in the literature [22] and can have their high rankings explained by the NSW public oral health services evaluated on the performance measures of dental waiting times and dental treatment activity [4] [23].

The survey findings demonstrate a high reliance on computerized reports among service‐wide decision‐makers, with a majority of participants using computerized reports extensively in their roles. This underscores the integration of technology in modern healthcare management and highlights the need for user‐friendly reporting systems to facilitate the decision‐making process.

The existing performance indicators provided by the participants suggest there is a current strong focus on operational efficiency, patient demand, financial management and clinical productivity. Regarding clinical productivity, the existing indicators show a focus on both clinical output, with indicators such as “item number utilizations”, as well as clinical effectiveness with indicators such as “retreatment rates and failed treatment”. Financial performance indicators such as “full‐time equivalent staff expenses”, “Child Dental Benefits Schedule compliance and revenue performance”, and “number of vouchers issued and associated costs” indicate that service‐wide decision‐makers are concerned with managing resources effectively and ensuring that services are financially sustainable.

The new performance indicators suggested by the participants indicate a growing need for both predictive data and trend data. The inclusion of “prediction of out‐of‐timeframe patients”, “past and present demand data”, and “waitlist movement tracking” suggests that service‐wide decision‐makers are interested in using data to predict patient demand and manage resources more effectively. If these new performance indicators can aid and support data‐driven decision‐making, the efficiency and effectiveness of the public dental service could improve, resulting in decreased waiting time.

The new performance indicators identified in this research follow closely with the themes of the existing performance indicators, such as waiting times and patient demand in use at the public dental service. Other themes and measures for improving efficiencies in public dental services have been established in the literature, with a systematic review identifying 215 quality measures [22] and another systematic review identifying 524 measures [9]. For example, an indicator of optimized dental workforce mix to achieve cost‐savings for the service [24, 25], while another indicator measures the number of and effectiveness of preventive dental treatments such as fluoride treatment and fissure sealants [22, 26]. Disease rate performance indicators, such as caries‐free clinical examinations, could also be established [27]. However, some of these indicators may not be preferred in public dental services due to various reasons, such as a preference to maintain the status quo, that is, the existing performance indicators themes have been in use for some time [28]. Another reason could be attributed to the fee‐for‐service funding model in the public dental service that does not incentivise preventive treatment, compared to a pay‐for‐performance model [29, 30].

The findings from this study should be interpreted with due consideration of the sample size limitation. The sample size (n = 9) is small; however, it represents a good proportion of the 12 eligible decision‐makers, with a high response rate (75%). This limits the generalizability of the results to other Local Health Districts or broader public dental services within New South Wales and Australia. The findings may not fully capture the diversity of perspectives among all potential users of the business intelligence system. Future research should aim to include a larger and more diverse sample size to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Expanding the scope of future surveys to encompass other Local Health District public dental services within New South Wales or nationally would provide valuable comparative data and inform broader implementation strategies. Eligible participants at individual Local Health Districts would remain small; however, performing this research across the Local Health Districts within NSW Health would provide a larger sample of participants, including multiple participants who have similar roles, for example, finance managers or clinical directors. With modifications to the survey, this process could be replicated in other areas of public health outside of dental services.

The participants in this study currently use paginated reports (Microsoft's SQL Server Reporting Services (SSRS)), which are suitable for generating static, pre‐defined reports intended for printing or distribution. The outcome of our study suggests that a self‐service reporting system using Microsoft's Power BI, allowing for a more interactive and dynamic solution, may be better suited. Power BI's strengths lie in its ability to provide users with greater flexibility to explore data, create custom visualizations, and drill down into details on demand [31]. This aligns directly with the identified need for decision‐makers to analyze trends, forecast future needs (e.g., out‐of‐timeframe patients), and tailor reports to their specific inquiries. Furthermore, Power BI's user‐friendly interface and data visualization tools promote greater adoption and engagement among users, fostering a more data‐driven culture within the organization. The ability to readily share dashboards and collaborate on insights further enhances its value, compared to the more static nature of SSRS reports [32].

While the survey provides valuable insights into the preferences and priorities of service‐wide decision‐makers of the targeted public dental service, challenges may arise in implementing the proposed reporting system. Addressing issues such as data integration, data protection, system usability, and training will be essential to ensure the successful adoption and utilization of the proposed reporting system.

For optimal data integration, the proposed reporting system will need to connect to the existing electronic dental record database. This will require a formal data dictionary generation and potentially require updates when the electronic record software is upgraded. Governance procedures will be required for data quality audits and corrections. Data protection and security will need to comply with relevant NSW Health policies and procedures as well as national requirements. This will include user access controls, auditing and secure data storage.

System usability and user training will require structured onboarding that covers navigation, filtering functions, and interpretation of visualizations. Training could be supplemented with video tutorials or creating a subject matter expert for the dental service. The reporting environment will need dedicated IT support to provide upgrades, new development and maintenance as required.

Further research will be needed to explore the effectiveness of different performance metrics and data‐driven decision‐making approaches in dental services. A mixed methods evaluation will track both utilization metrics and impact outcomes. Usage data can be collected during implementation and post‐implementation, and qualitative interviews with the decision‐makers can be performed and used to inform iterative refinements. Further research could also be conducted to investigate the usefulness and suitability of new and existing dental measures to determine their suitability and benefit in both a dental service context and the dental patient context.

Conclusion

The study underscores the established role of computerized reports in decision‐making processes within a public dental service. The gathered requirements and insights from dental decision‐makers from this research will shape the development of a pilot reporting system that leverages business intelligence to facilitate improved and more informed decision‐making. Further research will be required for the implementation of the self‐service reporting system and evaluation that will need to be performed after the introduction of the system to determine the frequency of use and if the system improves the decision‐making process of the public dental service. At the conclusion of the pilot implementation of the business intelligence system, this study can be conducted at other Local Health Districts in NSW to increase the number of eligible participants and strengthen the findings.
