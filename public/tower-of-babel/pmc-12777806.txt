Gender differences in post-traumatic symptomatology among refugees: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sofía Aragonés, Ana Maria Jiménez-García, Natalia Arias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Armed conflicts and persecution force millions of people to flee their countries, exposing them to severe pre-, peri-, and post-migration stressors that can negatively affect mental health. Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, and depression are common among forcibly displaced populations, and evidence suggests that gender may influence these outcomes.Objective: This systematic review and meta-analysis examined gender differences in mental health among refugees displaced by armed conflict, focusing on: (1) the odds and frequency of individuals presenting post-traumatic symptoms, (2) common comorbid conditions such as anxiety and depression, and (3) gender-based differences in exposure to traumatic events.Methods: Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Science Direct (December 2024). Two independent reviewers screened and extracted data according to PICOS criteria, and study quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Twenty-five studies met inclusion criteria, and 14 provided quantitative data for meta-analysis. Random-effects models were computed in R to estimate gender-based effect sizes for PTSD and related outcomes.Results: Women showed higher odds and frequency of PTSD symptoms, as well as increased levels of anxiety and depression, although differences in PTSD symptom severity were not consistently significant. Gender differences were also observed in exposure to traumatic events: women reported higher rates of sexual violence, whereas men were more likely to have been kidnapped or imprisoned. Longitudinal findings indicated a chronic trajectory of PTSD symptoms for both genders, likely shaped by post-migration stressors.Discussion: These findings highlight the relevance of gender in post-traumatic mental health among refugees. Higher symptom levels in women may reflect disproportionate exposure to gender-based violence and unequal access to support resources. Addressing these disparities is essential for designing gender-sensitive interventions and policies. Further research is needed to clarify long-term trajectories, standardize assessment tools, and identify effective strategies for diverse refugee populations.KEYWORDS: Post-traumatic stress disorder, gender differences, refugees, forced displacement, trauma experience

Introduction

Refugees are individuals compelled to flee their countries of origin due to a well-founded fear of persecution based on race, nationality, religion, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. In this paper, we use the term forcibly displaced persons in accordance with the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR, 2019) definition, encompassing refugees, asylum seekers, internally displaced persons (IDPs), and others in refugee-like situations. This broader term reflects the full scope of the global displacement crisis, while our analyses focus primarily on individuals with refugee status. According to recent UNHCR data, forced displacement continues to escalate: in 2023 alone, the number of forcibly displaced persons increased by 8.8 million, reaching 117.3 million. By April 2024, this figure had surpassed 120 million – an unprecedented high. Among them, 43.4 million were officially recognized as refugees, with nearly half being women (UNHCR, 2024). These figures likely underestimate the true scale of displacement, as legal criteria, cross-border movement requirements, administrative delays, and limited institutional capacity prevent many from obtaining formal refugee status. Moreover, while the 1951 Refugee Convention defines who qualifies as a refugee, recognition processes depend on national policies and registration systems, leaving many individuals unregistered. These numbers underscore not only the magnitude of the global displacement crisis but also the urgent need for rigorous, evidence-based research to inform interventions addressing its complex psychological and social consequences.

Armed conflict remains one of the primary drivers of forced displacement, exposing affected individuals to a wide range of traumatic experiences before, during, and after migration. War refugees frequently face not only the immediate dangers of violence and persecution but also chronic stressors such as family separation, uncertain legal status, detention, unemployment, and acculturative challenges in host countries (Disney & McPherson, 2020; Hajak et al., 2021). While acute traumatic events can trigger immediate stress responses, these chronic stressors may exacerbate or prolong psychological distress, highlighting the interplay between acute trauma and prolonged stress exposure.

The destruction of homes and livelihoods, coupled with the psychological toll of overnight displacement and separation from loved ones, often compels individuals to employ multiple coping mechanisms, including emotional suppression, but also other adaptive or maladaptive strategies such as cognitive appraisal and physiological regulation (Vukovic et al., 2024). Evidence indicates that these mechanisms interact with pre-existing vulnerabilities and environmental stressors, contributing to the onset of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, anxiety, and somatic symptoms – conditions widely recognized as trauma- and stress-related mental disorders (Jain et al., 2022). Vukovic et al. (2024) further emphasize that PTSD is associated with negative emotional states, distress, and cognitive biases (Byllesby et al., 2017; Eysenck & Fajkowska, 2018), which frequently overlap with major depression, its most common psychiatric comorbidity (Rytwinski et al., 2013). This overlapping symptomatology contributes to a heightened risk of mental health problems among refugees, including PTSD, anxiety, depression, and somatic complaints (Bogic et al., 2015; Gettler et al., 2024; Renner & Salem, 2009). PTSD is particularly prevalent in war-affected populations and is often associated with severe functional impairments in social integration, language learning, employment, and interpersonal relationships, ultimately reducing overall quality of life (Kivling-Bodén & Sundbom, 2002).

Emerging evidence suggests that men and women may differ in the types of traumatic events experienced, which may partially contribute to gender-specific symptom profiles. However, differences in trauma exposure alone do not fully account for observed gender differences, highlighting the role of additional biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors (Hanáková et al., 2024). Men are more often subjected to combat-related trauma, imprisonment, or torture, whereas women disproportionately experience sexual and gender-based violence and other forms of interpersonal victimization (Hanáková et al., 2024). These distinct patterns of trauma exposure are associated with different symptom profiles: women are more likely to present with heightened hyperarousal, anxiety, and avoidance, whereas men tend to exhibit emotional numbing and social withdrawal (Olff et al., 2007; Tolin & Foa, 2006). Such differences highlight the need to integrate gender-sensitive approaches into both research and clinical practice.

The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) provides a valuable framework for understanding the complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors that influence mental health outcomes in refugees. This model is particularly relevant for women, who may face additional sociocultural and structural vulnerabilities in host countries, including limited access to healthcare, economic instability, and restricted social support networks (Bogic et al., 2015). Studies with unaccompanied minors also reveal that girls report higher levels of depression and trauma-related symptoms compared to boys, suggesting that gender-related vulnerabilities emerge early (Mohwinkel et al., 2018).

Despite growing research on refugee mental health, important gaps remain regarding the influence of gender on the development and course of trauma-related disorders. Previous studies often treat refugee populations as homogeneous, overlooking critical sex-based differences in trauma exposure, symptom expression, and resilience mechanisms. Historically, women have been categorized alongside children and the elderly as ‘vulnerable populations’, limiting the nuanced analysis of their specific needs (Young & Chan, 2015). There is a pressing need for research that systematically explores gender differences in post-traumatic symptomatology, as well as the sociocultural factors that shape resilience, coping strategies, and access to support. This systematic review and meta-analysis aims to examine gender differences in mental health among refugee populations, focusing on three key areas: (1) the odds and frequency of post-traumatic symptoms, (2) the odds and frequency of other common mental health problems frequently comorbid in refugees, such as anxiety disorders and depressive disorders, and (3) the differential exposure to specific traumatic events (e.g. sexual violence, imprisonment, kidnapping). Although our primary focus was on psychological symptomatology and trauma exposure, we also report secondary findings from studies that examined biological correlates of mental health outcomes by gender, including evidence of differential neuroendocrine and inflammatory responses, such as variations in cortisol regulation and C-reactive protein levels observed among women with PTSD (Gettler et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2006). A better understanding of these dynamics is crucial to inform the design of effective, gender-responsive interventions and policies aimed at improving the mental health and integration outcomes of refugee populations.

Methods

Research strategy

A systematic bibliographic search was conducted following the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA; Page et al., 2021). Searches for original research articles on gender differences in post-traumatic symptomatology among refugees displaced by armed conflict were carried out across four electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Science Direct, and Scopus) on 15 December 2024, by two independent researchers (SA and NA). The search strategy combined the following terms: (‘gender differences’ OR ‘sex differences’) AND (‘post-traumatic stress disorder’ OR ‘PTSD’ OR ‘trauma symptoms’ OR ‘mental health’) AND (refugee* OR ‘asylum seeker*’ OR ‘displaced person*’ OR migrant*) AND (ethnic* OR ethnicity). No chronological or methodological filters were applied.

All records retrieved were managed in Zotero, with duplicates removed automatically. References were exported to Excel to facilitate systematic screening and documentation of eligibility decisions. This workflow ensured transparent and consistent tracking of study selection.

Study selection

After deduplication, two reviewers (SA and NA) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. A third reviewer (AJ) checked the selected studies for consistency, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Non-primary research (e.g. reviews, meta-analyses) and studies not addressing gender differences in post-traumatic symptomatology were excluded. Only articles published in English were considered.

Full texts of the remaining studies were then reviewed in detail according to the predefined criteria. Eligibility was structured following the PICOS framework: Population: refugees or asylum seekers displaced due to war or armed conflict. Both clinical and community samples were eligible. Intervention/exposure: traumatic events associated with war and forced migration. Comparison: gender (female vs. male participants). Outcomes: post-traumatic stress symptoms and other indicators of psychological distress (e.g. anxiety and depression), assessed either through formal clinical diagnosis (based on DSM/ICD criteria) or validated self-report instruments and screening scales (e.g. PCL, CAPS, HTQ, HSCL). Study design: quantitative studies, including cross-sectional, longitudinal, quasi-experimental, or randomized controlled trials with gender-disaggregated data.

Data extraction was independently conducted by two reviewers (SA and AJ) and organized in structured tables.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were eligible if they examined war refugees or asylum seekers exposed to trauma related to armed conflict. To be included, studies had to (1) involve individuals displaced due to war or armed violence, and (2) assess gender differences in post-traumatic or psychological symptoms. We excluded studies that focused on refugees displaced for economic, environmental, or political reasons not explicitly tied to war. Only quantitative studies providing gender-disaggregated results were included.

We excluded studies focused on displacement for non-war-related reasons (e.g. economic, environmental, or political), studies lacking gender-specific results, and articles not published in English. Gender-disaggregated data were required because gender (female vs. male participants) was the primary comparison of interest in this review. We also applied automated database filters to exclude non-primary research records such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, case reports, editorials, conference abstracts, and purely qualitative studies, as our focus was on primary quantitative data.

Methods quality

The methodological quality of the clinical research studies included in this review was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). This instrument, widely recommended for evaluating non-experimental research, offers a structured way to assess the methodological rigour of studies included in systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The scale focuses on three key domains: selection of study participants (up to 4 stars), comparability between groups (up to 2 stars), and outcome or exposure assessment (up to 3 stars). Each study can receive a minimum of 0 stars and a maximum of 9 stars.

Following the methodological guidelines proposed by Wells et al. (2000), studies scoring between 7 and 9 stars were considered to have high methodological quality, those scoring between 4 and 6 starts were rated as having moderate quality, and studies with fewer than 4 stars were classified as having low methodological quality. A detailed summary of the methodological quality assessment for all included studies is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Data analysis

A total of 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. Studies were excluded if they lacked a control or comparison group (n = 2), failed to report group-specific sample sizes or descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) (n = 7), or the use of non-equivalent measurement instruments (n = 2). Studies were included regardless of the statistical significance of their findings, allowing for a comprehensive and unbiased synthesis of the available evidence. The corresponding authors of all included studies were contacted to request access to the raw datasets or the specific summary statistics needed for the meta-analysis. In total, 15 corresponding authors were contacted to request access to raw data; however, for 14 studies, the requested datasets could not be obtained. The main reasons for inaccessibility included lack of response from the authors (n = 9), absence of valid contact information or undeliverable emails (n = 3), and unavailability of the original datasets due to the absence of detailed or control group data (n = 2).

Meta-analyses were conducted under random-effects models to account for between-study heterogeneity. For continuous outcomes, standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated and adjusted using Hedges’ g to correct for small-sample bias. For binary outcomes, including odds and frequency of exposure totraumatic events (e.g. being physically harmed in combat, exposure to combat, rape), effect sizes were computed as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals, following standard procedures for meta-analysis of dichotomous data (Borenstein et al., 2021). In accordance with methodological best practices, pooled analyses were carried out only when at least three studies (k ≥ 3) reported on the same outcome, ensuring greater robustness and interpretability of aggregated estimates (Borenstein et al., 2021; Harrer et al., 2021).

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using both Cochran’s Q test (with a significance threshold of p < .05) and the I2 statistic. I2 values of 0–30% were interpreted as low heterogeneity, 30–60% as moderate, 60–75% as substantial, and values above 75% as high (Higgins et al., 2003). Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out (LOO) method to examine the influence of each individual study on the overall pooled effect size and heterogeneity estimates. In this approach, each study was sequentially excluded from the meta-analysis, and the pooled effect was recalculated to assess the robustness of the findings. This procedure revealed some variability in the overall estimates, indicating that certain studies had a greater impact on the results than others. Such variability highlights the importance of methodological consistency and the potential influence of study-specific characteristics.

Publication bias was evaluated through Egger’s regression intercept test, and the Begg and Mazumdar rank correlation test was applied consistently across all analyses. In cases where more than ten studies were available, funnel plots were generated to visually inspect for asymmetry indicative of potential publication bias. Due to the limited number of studies in each meta-analysis, moderator analyses were not performed, as these would lack sufficient statistical power to yield reliable results.

Means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were extracted and entered into R Studio (version 4.3.1), using the packages ‘metafor’, ‘meta’, and ‘dmetar’. This software computed the SMDs, confidence intervals (CIs), heterogeneity indices, and overall effect sizes within a random-effects framework. Additionally, forest plots were generated using the package ‘forest’ to visually present individual and aggregated effect sizes, and odd ratios, along with their corresponding CIs. To prevent data duplication, each dataset was included only once in the analysis. When a study reported findings for multiple independent populations (e.g. by country of origin, gender, or time of assessment), these were treated as separate entries only if the samples were clearly non-overlapping and statistically independent. This approach ensured a more granular analysis of gender differences while preserving methodological rigour and avoiding artificial inflation of the total sample size. All decisions regarding the inclusion and disaggregation of data were based on explicit information provided in the original publications and were independently verified by both reviewers.

Results

The searches conducted in the electronic databases PubMed, Scopus, Science Direct, and Web of Science yielded 24, 65, 332, and 41 articles, respectively, totalling 462 publications. Of these, 22 were identified as duplicates and removed from the dataset, and 229 were excluded using automated tools from the databases. The titles and abstracts of the remaining 211 articles were assessed for eligibility, 17 publications were considered outside the scope of the systematic review and excluded because they were systematic reviews, meta-analyses, reviews, books, or conference proceedings, and 5 were excluded because they were not primary evidence. Of the remaining, 168 were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria for the research; refugee population and/or post-traumatic symptomatology. Additionally, four more articles were identified through citation searching and were found to meet the eligibility criteria. Thus, a total of 25 references were included in the systematic review (see Figure 1).

Methods quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), showed a mean score of 5.04 (SD = 1.53), with a median of 5 and a range of 2 to 9.

Most of the studies (n = 13) obtained a moderate quality score (ranging from 4 to 6 stars), some studies (n = 7) received high scores (ranging from 7 to 9 stars), and the remaining studies (n = 5) received a low score (ranging from 1 to 3 stars). One of the main limitations found in all the studies (n = 22) is the absence of an appropriate comparison group (or control group), which prevents clear comparisons from being made. Another common limitation in several of the studies is the lack of long-term follow-up, as many of them are cross-sectional studies. Among the strengths, the use of validated instruments in the majority of the articles for data collection and outcome assessment stands out. The score found in each study according to each criterion and its total score can be found in Supplementary Table 1 which provides a detailed breakdown of the methodological quality of each study and the specific criteria met or not met.

General characteristics of selected studies

The studies selected for inclusion in the systematic review were published between 1997 and 2024 (n = 25), with seven of them published in the most recent years (2023–2024). Among these articles, 7 longitudinal studies, 17 cross-sectional studies, and one randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) were identified. Within the cross-sectional studies, 3 were assessed through online questionnaires, 3 used observational methods, 2 performed factorial analyses, and 2 employed mixed approaches.

A wide range of validated instruments was employed to assess post-traumatic symptoms, most frequently the Harvard Trauma Questionnaire (HTQ, n = 11), the Hopkins Symptom Checklist (HSCL-25, n = 8), the PTSD Checklist (PCL, n = 3), and the Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS, n = 3). Less common tools included the IES-R, BSI, and HADS. The reliance on self-report measures across all studies may contribute to differences in symptom severity reporting and limits comparability with clinician-administered diagnostic assessments.

Gender differences in the post-traumatic symptomatology

Several studies reported significantly higher PTSD scores or pertentage among female refugees (Gettler et al., 2024; Halcón et al., 2004; Im & Swan, 2022; Kibler & Toukhly, 2023; Roth et al., 2006; Schubert & Punamäki, 2011). Others also noted elevated anxiety, depression, and somatization symptoms in women (Akgül et al., 2019; Im & Swan, 2022; Renner & Salem, 2009), highlighting greater psychological vulnerability among female refugees. A detailed summary of these findings can be found in Table 1.

Conversely, some studies did not find significant gender differences in PTSD prevalence (Bandeira et al., 2010; Çetrez et al., 2021; De Vries, 2001; Momartin et al., 2003; 2004; Ng et al., 2023; Sack et al., 1997). Yet even in these, women often reported higher symptom intensity or emotional impact related to specific traumas, such as the loss of loved ones (Momartin et al., 2003). Additionally, resilience scores were found to be lower in women (Çetrez et al., 2021), and while PTSD levels were similar, women had higher depressive symptoms (Momartin et al., 2004).

Several studies identified gender-specific trauma exposure patterns (Chung & Bemak, 2002; De Vries, 2001; Schubert & Punamäki, 2011). While some research found no differences (De Vries, 2001), others reported that women were more often victims of sexual violence, whereas men were more exposed to combat trauma (Schubert & Punamäki, 2011). In Vietnamese women, traumatic events were not significant predictors of distress, unlike in other groups (Chung & Bemak, 2002).

Factors associated with PTSD severity also varied by gender. Social isolation predicted higher symptom severity in women, while early symptoms were the best predictor for men (Kivling-Bodén & Sundbom, 2002). Perceived discrimination was linked to increased PTSD severity in Latina women (Vos et al., 2023), and low perceived social support worsened physical and mental health outcomes for both genders (Gettler et al., 2024). Differences in coping strategies were also reported. Women tended to engage in caregiving, family-centred support, and religious practices (Çetrez et al., 2021; Im & Swan, 2022), whereas men more often used distancing strategies, employment as distraction, and external support networks (Im & Swan, 2022; Renner & Salem, 2009). All included studies assessed post-traumatic symptoms through self-report questionnaires, with no clinician-administered diagnostic interviews reported. Consequently, no variation in assessment method was present across the included studies.

Socioeconomic stressors disproportionately affected female refugees: unemployment, financial hardship, and poor housing conditions were linked to greater distress (Handiso et al., 2024). In terms of symptom profile, female refugees showed higher intrusive thoughts, physiological reactivity, startle responses, and emotional numbness (Renner & Salem, 2009). Finally, studies on biological correlates found that women had lower cortisol levels in fingernails, and higher CRP and EBV antibody levels (Gettler et al., 2024). A longitudinal study also observed lower salivary cortisol among refugees diagnosed with PTSD (Roth et al., 2006).

Meta-analyses

Gender differences in trauma-related symptomatology

PTSD diagnosis

A meta-analysis of eleven studies (k = 11) examining the percentage of women diagnosed with PTSD yielded a moderate, significant effect (OR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.15–0.75; p = .0035; Figure 2(A)). The heterogeneity across studies was moderate (I2 = 56.28%; Q(10) = 15.82, p = .1049), indicating moderate variability in the outcomes, although this was not statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses using a Leave-One-Out (LOO) approach demonstrated that excluding any individual study did not substantially affect the overall effect. However, the study by Roth et al. (2006)1 had a considerable influence on the effect estimate (p < .0001) and significantly reduced heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) when excluded. This suggests that Roth et al. (2006)1 was a highly influential study in this meta-analysis. Regarding publication bias, funnel plot asymmetry tests showed no significant evidence of bias (Kendall's tau: p = .6481; regression test: p = .8804), suggesting that publication bias is unlikely to have influenced the results (Figure 2(B)).

Anxiety symptoms

A random-effects meta-analysis including three studies (k = 3) yielded a moderate effect size of g = 0.48 (SE = 0.07), indicating that women reported higher levels of anxiety compared to men (p = .0199; 95% CI: 0.18–0.77) (Figure 4(A)). Heterogeneity indicators suggested no relevant variability between studies (Q(2) = 1.25, p = .5352; I2 = 0.00%), indicating a high level of consistency among the included studies. Regarding publication bias, the funnel plot did not show significant asymmetry, and neither Egger’s test (p = .7902) nor Begg and Mazumdar’s test (p = 1.0000) indicated evidence of publication bias in the studies included in the meta-analysis.

Depression symptoms

A random-effects meta-analysis including four studies (k = 4) yielded a small-to-moderate effect size of g = 0.35 (SE = 0.10), indicating that women reported higher levels of depressive symptoms compared to men (p = .0415; 95% CI: 0.03–0.67) (Figure 4(B)). Heterogeneity indicators suggested low between-study variability (Q(3) = 4.18, p = .243; I2 = 16.47%), supporting the consistency of the results across the included studies. The leave-one-out analysis confirmed the stability of the effect size, although minor changes in heterogeneity were observed when individual studies were excluded. Funnel plot inspection did not reveal marked asymmetry, and neither Egger’s test (p = .1340) nor Begg and Mazumdar’s test (p = .3333) indicated significant publication bias.

Exposure to traumatic events

General exposure to combat situation

A meta-analysis of four studies (k = 4) examining combat exposure yielded no significant differences between men and women in terms of exposure (OR = 0.97; 95% CI: 0.25–3.78; p = .9607). The heterogeneity across studies was high (I2 = 87%; Q(3) = 16.82, p = .0008), indicating considerable variability in the outcomes. Sensitivity analyses using a LOO approach revealed that excluding any single study did not substantially change the overall result, with no study significantly affecting the outcome. Regarding publication bias, funnel plot asymmetry tests showed no significant evidence of bias (Kendall's tau = −0.33, p = .7500; regression test p = .8444). Therefore, the findings suggest that there is no significant difference in combat exposure between men and women.

Serious physical injury from combat

A meta-analysis of four studies (k = 4) examining physical injury from combat yielded a small, significant effect, with men being more exposed than women (OR = 1.42; 95% CI: 1.03–2.12; p = .0415) (Figure 5). The heterogeneity across studies was lowe (I2 = 16.47%; Q(3) = 4.18, p = .2426), suggesting some variability in the outcomes, though not statistically significant. Sensitivity analyses using a LOO approach showed that excluding individual studies did not substantially affect the overall result, with the OR remaining significant across most LOO tests. However, the study by Roth et al. (2006) had a large impact on the effect estimate, with a reduction in heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) when excluded. Regarding publication bias, funnel plot tests did not show any significant asymmetry, and both Egger's test (p = .2763) and the Begg and Mazumdar test (p = .3333) indicated no significant evidence of publication bias.

Rape

A meta-analysis of three studies (k = 3) examining the experience of being raped between men and women yielded a non-significant result with a small effect size (OR = 1.28; 95% CI: 0.36–2.93; p = .1252). The heterogeneity across studies was high (I2 = 76.45%; Q(2) = 9.80, p = .0074), indicating a substantial variability among the study outcomes. Sensitivity analyses using the LOO approach showed that excluding any individual study did not significantly affect the overall result, although the study by Roth et al. (2006) had a notable impact on the effect estimate (OR = 2.38, p = .0004), highlighting the influence of this particular study. Regarding publication bias, both the funnel plot and the asymmetry tests showed no significant evidence of bias (Kendall's tau p = 1.0000; Egger's regression test p = .5201). The interval prediction indicated that the true effect size could range from 0.21 to 63.65, which is very wide, further emphasizing the uncertainty in these results. Thus, the findings suggest that there is no strong evidence to support a gender difference in the experience of being raped based on the studies included in this meta-analysis.

Discussion

Despite growing academic and clinical interest in the mental health of refugee populations, important gaps remain in understanding how gender influences the manifestation and progression of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other psychological conditions in this vulnerable group. Gender-related factors, interacting with sociocultural variables such as coping strategies, economic adaptation, and access to social resources, play a critical role in shaping how individuals experience and respond to trauma (Araya et al., 2007; Handiso et al., 2024). Recent evidence indicates that the latent structure of PTSD, typically conceptualized in terms of re-experiencing, avoidance/numbing, and hyperarousal, is largely invariant across genders, as observed in war-exposed Bosnian adolescents (Armour et al., 2011). However, the same communities demonstrate gender-specific differences in symptom frecuency as girls reported significantly higher levels of intrusion and avoidance/numbing, while hyperarousal symptoms did not differ substantially by gender (Kaya et al., 2019). In a cross-sectional study of 352 Syrian refugees in Turkey, PTSD diagnosis rates were significantly higher in women (44.1%) than in men (18.1%) even though both genders faced similar traumatic environments. Women exhibited more frequent intrusion and avoidance/numbing symptoms, but no gender difference was found for hypervigilance (Sağaltıcı et al., 2022). These gender-based differences are not unique to refugee populations. Similar trends have been reported in studies conducted in the general population, which consistently show higher PTSD odds among women, especially following interpersonal or sexual violence (Olff et al., 2007; Tolin & Foa, 2006). In line with these findings, our meta-analysis identified a higher percentage of PTSD symptoms in refugee women, although no significant differences were found in symptom severity. Some studies did, however, reveal differences in the intensity of specific symptoms, with women reporting greater intrusion, hyperarousal, and startle responses (Birkeland et al., 2017; Chung & Bemak, 2002; Renner & Salem, 2009).

Importantly, longitudinal data indicate that post-traumatic symptoms tend to worsen over time, regardless of gender, suggesting that post-migration conditions may be a significant contributing factor to the chronicity of psychological distress. Several studies included in this review reported that low social support, socioeconomic hardship, and perceived discrimination in host countries were significantly associated with poorer psychological outcomes among refugees (Gettler et al., 2024; Handiso et al., 2024; Vos et al., 2023). Bogic et al. (2015) highlighted the impact of low socioeconomic status, unemployment, and cultural integration difficulties on the persistence of post-traumatic symptoms. Similarly, studies by Kivling-Bodén and Sundbom (2002), Handiso et al. (2024), and Roth et al. (2006) found that factors such as legal uncertainty, social isolation, and lack of structural support can intensify psychological distress years after resettlement. While the available literature supports the notion that symptom severity tends to increase over time in adverse post-migration contexts, one of the main methodological limitations of the present meta-analysis was the inability to assess this temporal effect. The studies included in the review exhibited considerable heterogeneity in both the timing of symptom assessment (ranging from a few months to over ten years post-displacement) and the contextual variables that influence psychological outcomes, such as legal status, country of residence, level of integration, and access to support systems. This variability made it methodologically unsound to conduct sub-analyses on the effect of time, as doing so would have introduced substantial bias and compromised the validity of the results.

In addition to PTSD, women consistently reported higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms, as measured by both the BSI and the HSCL. These results point to a greater overall psychological burden in refugee women, which may be influenced by the type of trauma experienced. Hanáková et al. (2024) found that women were more frequently exposed to interpersonal violence, particularly sexual violence, whereas men more often experienced combat, torture, or imprisonment. Among the included studies, the work by Roth et al. (2006) emerged as especially influential both methodologically and statistically. Interestingly, our meta-analysis suggests that both genders may have been similarly exposed to combat situations, possibly due to the cultural and geographical diversity of the included samples, particularly in contexts where civilian populations were directly involved in conflict. Although sexual violence was more frequent among women, only two studies reported this explicitly, and both showed methodological variability, limiting generalizability.

However, the greater psychological burden observed in refugee women may not be explained solely by differences in trauma type. Studies in general and clinical populations indicate that women are more likely to develop internalizing symptoms such as anxiety, sadness, and rumination, whereas men tend to exhibit more externalizing symptoms (Olff et al., 2007; Tolin & Foa, 2006). For example, rumination, a cognitive pattern associated with the persistence of negative affect and depression, has been found to be more prevalent among women (Johnson & Whisman, 2013). Moreover, structural and sociocultural factors, such as gender roles, stigma, and reduced access to resources, may shape not only the expression of symptoms but also help-seeking behaviours and symptom recognition (Christiansen & Hansen, 2015).

The higher percentage of post-traumatic symptomatology among women may be explained by sociocultural and contextual factors with differential gender impact. This perspective aligns with Engel’s biopsychosocial model (1977), which, although not trauma-specific, emphasizes the importance of considering biological, psychological, and social domains in understanding health. Traditionally, however, Western mental health treatment for survivors of torture and war trauma has largely relied on a biopsychomedical model, focusing on symptom reduction, particularly of PTSD, depression, and anxiety, through individually oriented strategies. Such approaches may overlook the influence of sociocultural context and gender-specific experiences, which are critical for a more nuanced understanding of trauma outcomes (Isakson & Jurkovic, 2013).

Olff et al. (2007) emphasize the influence of hormonal and neurobiological factors, gender-specific coping strategies, and social dynamics in the development of PTSD. Likewise, Maercker and Heim (2019) underscore the importance of cultural context in understanding how trauma is experienced and expressed. From this standpoint, social variables such as discrimination, poverty, substandard housing, and lack of psychosocial resources constitute barriers to recovery (Gillespie et al., 2023).

While there is extensive literature concerning PTSD among male veterans in conflict settings, epidemiological studies on trauma spectrum disorders have consistently shown higher symptom severity in women (Christiansen & Hansen, 2015; Chung & Breslau, 2008; O’Toole & Catts, 2008). In conflict-affected civilian populations, both men and women may be exposed to life-threatening experiences such as bombardments, detention, and forced displacement. However, women may also face additional forms of gender-based violence, including sexual assault, forced pregnancy, or sexual slavery during war (Hynes et al., 2004). It is important to note that such distinctions are not absolute, as men may also suffer from interpersonal trauma such as torture and imprisonment, which can have profound psychological impacts.

This is also congruent with Yehuda’s diathesis-stress model of PTSD (Yehuda, 2004), which asserts that the traumatic event serves as the primary stressor, and thus activator of PTSD symptoms, whereas pre- and post-war variables are likely to influence PTSD symptomatology and related comorbidity, by producing vulnerability, resilience, and recovery from adversities. This same conceptual model may be applied to major depression, the most common psychiatric comorbidity of PTSD, with more than half of all patients with PTSD also meeting diagnostic criteria for major depression (Rytwinski et al., 2013). From a clinical point of view, both disorders share a high presence of negative emotional states (Eysenck & Fajkowska, 2018), distress, and cognitive biases (Byllesby et al., 2017).

Multimorbidity with chronic physical impairment has been deeply investigated as well (Agrawal & Keshri, 2014; DiGiacomo et al., 2013; Jeon et al., 2013; Perkins et al., 2016; Tiedt et al., 2016; Zhou & Hearst, 2016). A previous study (Sarajlić Vuković et al., 2021) showed worsened physical health among women more than in men affected by PTSD, especially concerning hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, arthritis, and anaemia, and hypothesized a crucial link between mental and physical health impairment with loss of social support. Indeed, biological evidence also supports this integrative view. Several studies have identified gender-related physiological differences, such as lower cortisol levels and higher C-reactive protein concentrations in women with PTSD (Gettler et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2006). At the social level, perceived discrimination has been associated with elevated PTSD symptoms, particularly in refugee women (Gillespie et al., 2023; Vos et al., 2023). Precarious living conditions, including unemployment and residence in temporary accommodation, have also been linked to greater psychological distress (Handiso et al., 2024). While our results align with previous literature emphasizing the greater psychological vulnerability of refugee women (Hanáková et al., 2024; Mohwinkel et al., 2018), it is important to acknowledge that several studies included in the review did not report significant gender differences (Bandeira et al., 2010; Çetrez et al., 2021; De Vries, 2001; Momartin et al., 2004; Ng et al., 2023; Sack et al., 1997). This suggests that psychological distress is influenced by multiple factors beyond gender, including country of origin, host country context, and post-migration conditions. For instance, Kibler and Toukhly (2023) found that Syrian refugees in Jordan exhibited higher PTSD prevalence than those in the US, despite reporting fewer experiences of violence and discrimination – highlighting the decisive influence of host country conditions. Similarly, Vos et al. (2023) found that perceived discrimination was associated with more severe PTSD symptoms in Latin American women, especially those residing in the US, further supporting the role of the post-migration context as a risk factor.

Other studies reinforce the importance of structural determinants such as length of stay in the host country and legal status. Roth et al. (2006) showed that legal uncertainty and social precariousness contribute to the worsening of PTSD symptoms over time. Additionally, the lack of social support has been consistently associated with poorer physical and mental health outcomes (Çetrez et al., 2021; Gettler et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2006), underscoring its protective role in mitigating trauma.

Nevertheless, this study presents several limitations. First, we found heterogeneity among the studies included, as their designs varied. For this reason, it is not possible to draw meaningful conclusions regarding the adolescent population, due to the lack of uniformity in data presentation across studies. Additionally, most of the included studies were cross-sectional, which hinders the establishment of causal relationships or the adequate examination of symptom evolution over the long term. Another major limitation concerns the lack of studies including an appropriate non-refugee comparison group, making it difficult to compare the refugee population with the non-refugee population, and thus complicating a clearer understanding of how forced displacement impacts posttraumatic symptomatology. Furthermore, a major challenge encountered in the meta-analysis was the lack of complete statistical data in several studies. Notably, many articles reporting non-significant results did not include essential information such as means, standard deviations, or frequency data, which prevented their inclusion in the quantitative synthesis. This omission, although common in the literature, introduces an availability bias that ultimately limits the representativeness and generalizability of the findings.

Although our meta-analysis was limited to studies on refugee populations, findings from large-scale studies in general populations provide useful insights into gender differences in trauma responses. For instance, the WHO World Mental Health Surveys, a cross-national study involving over 68,000 adults from 24 countries, found that women were approximately twice as likely as men to develop PTSD, especially after exposure to interpersonal trauma such as sexual assault or partner violence (Kessler et al., 2017). Similarly, Tolin and Foa (2006) conducted a meta-analysis of gender differences in PTSD and concluded that women exhibit significantly greater PTSD symptomatology than men, even when controlling for the type of trauma experienced. A minor limitation of this review is that our inclusion criteria required explicit mention of war or armed conflict as the cause of displacement. Consequently, some potentially relevant studies may have been excluded if the reason for displacement was not clearly specified, even when related to armed conflict. In addition, the number of studies available for certain analyses was limited (k = 3–4). Such small sample sizes reduce the stability of pooled estimates and widen confidence intervals, which restricts the generalizability of these findings (Borenstein et al., 2021; Harrer et al., 2021). In addition, the limited number of eligible studies also prevented the use of moderator or meta-regression analyses, which require larger datasets to produce stable and interpretable estimates. Conducting these analyses with the available data would have been unreliable, constituting an additional limitation of the present meta-analysis.

Finally, although no age restrictions were applied during the selection process, none of the included studies focused specifically on children. Only two studies addressed adolescent populations (Akgül, 2019; Sack et al., 1997), limiting the generalizability of our findings to younger age groups. The findings of this study highlight the need for a broader and multidimensional perspective when addressing trauma-related mental health outcomes in refugee populations. While social and political determinants – such as legal insecurity, discrimination, and access to support services – play a critical role, our results also underscore the importance of gender-specific factors. These include the differential exposure to trauma types, greater vulnerability to internalizing symptoms (such as anxiety, rumination, and depressive affect), as well as the influence of sociocultural roles and coping styles. Addressing PTSD and comorbid conditions in refugees cannot rely solely on individual psychotherapeutic interventions; rather, responses must also incorporate structural and gender-sensitive approaches that promote psychological well-being at the community level.
