Diffusion Model’s Generalization Can Be Characterized by Inductive Biases toward a Data-Dependent Ridge Manifold
Ye He, Yitong Qiu, Molei Tao

Abstract
When a diffusion model is not memorizing the training data set, how does it generalize exactly? A quantitative understanding of the distribution it generates would be beneficial to, for example, an assessment of the model’s performance for downstream applications. We thus explicitly characterize what diffusion model generates, by proposing a log-density ridge manifold and quantifying how the generated data relate to this manifold as inference dynamics progresses. More precisely, inference undergoes a reach–align–slide process centered around the ridge manifold: trajectories first reach a neighborhood of the manifold, then align as being pushed toward or away from the manifold in normal directions, and finally slide along the manifold in tangent directions. Within the scope of this general behavior, different training errors will lead to different normal and tangent motions, which can be quantified, and these detailed motions characterize when inter-mode generations emerge. More detailed understanding of training dynamics will lead to more accurate quantification of the generation inductive bias, and an example of random feature model will be considered, for which we can explicitly illustrate how diffusion model’s inductive biases originate as a composition of architectural bias and training accuracy, and how they evolve with the inference dynamics. Experiments on synthetic multimodal distributions and MNIST latent diffusion support the predicted directional effects, in both low- and high-dimensions.

Introduction

Diffusion models
(
Sohl-Dickstein et al. 2015
,
Ho et al. 2020
,
Song et al. 2021
)
now deliver state-of-the-art sample quality across a wide range of generative tasks, which has made them a central tool for image, audio and video generation
(
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
,
Kong et al. 2021
,
Brooks et al. 2024
)
. At the same time, understanding how innovative these models can be (or in other words, what exactly do they generate) is becoming increasingly important. In fact, certain diffusion models can memorize the training data and act like a stochastic parrot that just randomly repeats training data, which means the generation quality may appear to be high but in fact no new knowledge is created; in addition, it can lead to safety and privacy concerns
(
Carlini et al. 2023
,
Somepalli et al. 2023
,
Duan et al. 2023
,
Liu et al. 2024
)
. Naturally, what triggers memorization of diffusion model has been extensively investigated
(
Kadkhodaie et al. 2023
,
Zhang et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. By now, it is fairly well accepted that it is the various sources of error inside diffusion model that give its non-memorization; in fact, criteria for (non-)memorization can already be made rather quantitative
(
Ye et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, and ‘generalization’ became a synonym of non-memorization in this literature.

One important question still being actively investigated though is, when diffusion model is not memorizing, how/where does the model produce new samples different from the training set, i.e. how does it innovate knowledge. A quantitative, explicit answer would help distinguish generalization from hallucination and assess the applicability of the generative model to specific downstream tasks. The powerful classical theory of statistical learning can help bound the gap between the generated distribution and the latent population distribution (e.g.,
Wang et al. 2024
), but in practice such a worst-case bound can be loose as it needs to account for all possible population distributions that are consistent with the finite dataset which the model trains on. This motivates an alternative, fully data-dependent perspective: compare the generated distribution directly to the empirical distribution of the training data, and regard this discrepancy as the model’s generalization.

Many recent progress along this line provide rich insights. For example,
Aithal et al. 2024
’s seminal empirical investigation suggests some geometric bias, in which generated data concentrate in structured regions that connect distinct modes of the data. More systematically speaking, generalization in diffusion models may arise from several mechanisms, and the literature studies it from complementary perspectives.
One perspective concerns the
target
, asking whether stochasticity in the finite training target is itself a driver of generalization
(
Vastola 2025
,
Bertrand et al. 2025
)
.
A second perspective examines
training inductive bias

(
Kamb and Ganguli 2024
,
Shah et al. 2025
,
Wu et al. 2025
,
Bonnaire et al. 2025
)
, characterizing how the learned reverse-time dynamics depend on the model class and learning procedure.
A third perspective focuses on
inference

(
Baptista et al. 2025
,
Chen 2025
,
Farghly et al. 2025
)
, understanding what reverse-time inference dynamics generate under manually assumed structured training error. While extremely insightful, the literature still lacks a rigorous and quantitative understanding of diffusion model’s generalization.

Motivated by this gap, we develop a purely data-driven (no population distribution involved) framework to explicitly quantify the inductive biases of diffusion model’s generation. Our work integrates the perspectives in the literature: On the
target
side, we treat the finite training dataset as the target and analyze how the generated distribution relates to the empirical distribution of training data, rather than attributing generalization to the stochasticity of sampling an unknown population distribution. On the
inference
side, we make the data-structure explicit by constructing a time-indexed family of log-density ridge sets from the smoothed empirical density, and we analyze how inference trajectories evolve relative to this geometry. This differs from the work
(
Farghly et al. 2025
)
that motivates log-density smoothing as a useful analytical lens but does not study the simulated inference dynamics or provides a ridge-manifold description based on the data. On the
training
side, building on random-feature analyses of diffusion training
(
George et al. 2025
,
Bonnaire et al. 2025
)
, we go beyond test/training error comparisons and show how limited-expressivity parametrization (due to finite width) and inaccurate optimization translate into
directional
geometric effects during inference. Our results confirm and explain when and why samples will populate structured intermediate regions beyond the training dataset, how this behavior depends on both the time of the sampling dynamics and what is learned during training. Compared to recent analytical explanations of interpolation bias that rely on stylized settings or imposed error ansatz (e.g., two-point data and smoothed empirical score)
(
Chen 2025
)
, our results are derived under verifiable regularity conditions and explicitly connect the resulting geometric bias to the dataset and to training parameters. We also complement the interesting work of
(
Li et al. 2025
)
, which investigates the transition between sampling uniform from some low-dimensional manifold and sampling a target distribution supported on the manifold, as our setting differs in two key ways: first, we study the full denoising diffusion, where noise level is time-dependent and vanishing as sampling approaches the data, as opposed to constant-noise level considered; second, rather than assuming there is some underlying manifold without spelling out what it is, we explicitly characterize the geometric object that governs generation in our framework and quantify how the learned score/mean estimator drives the inference dynamics around this object.

As illustrated in Figure
1
, our theoretical results (Section
3
) show that the inference dynamics follows a simple reach–align–slide progression, which can be summarized as follows:

1.

Reach:
after an initial transient, generated samples enter a neighborhood of the corresponding log-density ridge, so the log-density ridge becomes the natural reference geometry for the rest of the inference trajectory.

2.

Normal alignment:
generated samples move closer to the log-density ridge and effectively align with it. The closest distance, however, does not vanish in general and is controlled by the component of the training error in the normal directions.

3.

Tangent sliding:
generated samples slide along the log-density ridge toward training samples, which can populate structured intermediate regions between training samples. The tangent concentration around training samples is controlled by the component of the training error in the tangent directions.

Unlike test-error-based notions of generalization, the reach-align-slide view demonstrates that a generated samples are explicitly concentrated on a geometric structure terms as ‘ridge’ (Definition
3.1
), and our theory also characterizes how training error can result in different generated distribution along the ridge (Section
4.1
). The latter can be made very explicit when we consider random feature neural network (RFNN) trained by gradient descent (GD) in Section
4.2
, where the training error admits closed-form decompositions that map onto normal & tangent directions induced by ridges.

Preliminaries

SDE-based Diffusion Models.
To generate new samples in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
based on
n
-many existing data samples
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the variance-preserving (VP) SDE-based diffusion models consider forward process
(X_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfying
\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}
for all
t\in[0,T]
, with marginals
p_{t}=\text{Law}(X_{t})
and
p_{0}=p
being the data distribution. The reverse process
(Y_{t})_{0\leq t\leq T}
satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}Y_{t}=\big(Y_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(Y_{t})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\bar{B}_{t},\quad 0\leq t\leq T-\delta.

(1)

The term
\{s(t,x)=\nabla\log p_{t}(x)\}_{\delta\leq t\leq T}
in (
1
) is referred as the score function. The early stopping time
0<\delta\ll 1
is to make sure the score function is well-defined. In practice, (
1
) is simulated using Gaussian initial condition. As it is challenging to compute score directly, people usually learn
s(t,x)
through optimizing the denoising score matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DSM}}=\int_{\delta}^{T}w(t)\mathbb{E}\big[\|s_{\theta}(t,X_{t}(z))+\frac{z}{\sqrt{h_{t}}}\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

where
a_{t}\coloneqq e^{-t}
,
h_{t}=1-e^{-2t}
,
X_{t}(z)\coloneqq a_{t}X_{0}+\sqrt{h_{t}}z
and
z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
is independent to
X_{0}
.
w(t)
is the training weight and
s_{\theta}(t,x)
is the parametrized score.

Denosing Mean Matching Loss.
In this work, we consider an alternative training loss, which is motivated by the Tweedie’s formula: the score is determined by posterior mean of the clean data conditioned on noisy observation:
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=-\frac{x}{h_{t}}+\frac{1}{h_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{0|t}(\cdot|x)}[a_{t}x_{0}]
. This identity shows that learning the score
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
is essentially equivalent to learning the posterior mean

\displaystyle m(t,x)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{x_{0}\sim p_{0|t}(\cdot|x)}[a_{t}x_{0}],

(2)

Thus, we can view learning in diffusion model as learning either the score or the posterior mean. We adopt the latter as it is consistent with the viewpoint in
Li and He 2025
that data-prediction is a direct way to learn structure of clean data, especially when they concentrate near low-dim structure, which aligns with our geometric focus; analyzing the former would be analogous though.

The mean matching loss that controls the gap between the exact reverse process and the approximated reverse process can be expressed via the posterior mean
m(t,x)
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}=\int_{\delta}^{T}{w(t)}{h_{t}^{-2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m_{A}(t,X_{t})-m(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t,

(3)

where
m_{A}(t,x)
is parametrized by neural networks to approximate the posterior mean
m(t,x)
. Since
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}
is hard to evaluate directly using data samples, we instead minimize the denoising posterior mean matching loss (analogue to denoising score matching loss):

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}{w(t)}{h_{t}^{-2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+m_{A}(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t.

(4)

Crucially,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}
is fully computable from the training set by drawing
X_{0}
from data. For more details on the DMM loss, we refer the readers to Appendix
A
. In our paper, for the convenience of our analysis, we assume the expectations wrt. the noises in
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}
and time
t
are exactly evaluated and denote the learned posterior mean by
m_{A}
. The inference process, initialized at Gaussian, is characterized by the SDE

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{t}=\big(\tilde{Y}_{t}+2h_{T-t}^{-1}(m_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})-\tilde{Y}_{t})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t},\quad t\in[0,T-\delta].

(5)

Random Feature Neural Network and Gradient Descent.
We consider a simplified but analytical favored representation, random features neural networks (RFNN), for the posterior mean parametrization. Similar RFNN has been studied in
George et al. 2025
,
Bonnaire et al. 2025
, but whereas they study asymptotic regimes with dimension
d
, width
p
and data size
n
growing, we focus on a nonasymptotic setting where
d,p,n
are fixed and our results explicitly track their finite values. In particular, the parametrized posterior mean is represented by a two-layer neural network

m_{A}(t,x)\coloneqq\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma(\frac{W_{\mathrm{x}}x}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{W_{\mathrm{t}}\varphi_{t}}{\sqrt{2K_{t}+1}}+b)\coloneqq\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(x)

(6)

where
W_{\mathrm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
and
W_{\mathrm{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(2K_{\mathrm{t}}+1)}
are Gaussian random matrices with i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, also independent to
\xi
.
\varphi_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{2K_{\mathrm{t}}+1}
consists of
2K_{\mathrm{t}}+1
Fourier basis on
[0,T]
.
b\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{p})
is the bias feature, independent to
W_{\mathrm{x}},W_{\mathrm{t}}
.
\sigma
is the nonlinear activation function and
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
is the parameter matrix to learn. The DMM loss denoted as
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)
, is then given by
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)=\int_{\delta}^{T}\tfrac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+\tfrac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t
.
We optimize
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)
by gradient descent with constant learning rate
\eta
, with iterations given by

\displaystyle A_{k+1}-A_{k}=-{2\eta}A_{k}\tilde{U}+{2\eta}\tilde{V},

(7)

with
\tilde{U}=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(X_{t})\sigma_{t}(X_{t})^{\intercal}]}{p}\mathrm{d}t\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\tilde{V}=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{z}[a_{t}X_{0}\sigma_{t}(X_{t})^{\intercal}]}{\sqrt{p}}\mathrm{d}t\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
.

3
Geometric Dynamical Properties of the Inference Process

In this section, we introduce a family of data-dependent ridge manifolds across noise levels and study how the dynamic of
\{\tilde{Y}_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq T-\delta}
behaves relative to these sets. After stating mild assumptions on the data, we establish geometric properties needed for the analysis and prove dynamical results that formalize the reach–align–slide behavior.

Assumption 1

(Empirical Data Distribution)
.

Data points
\{x_{0}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
are well-separated and bounded, i.e.,
\Delta\coloneqq\min_{i\neq j}\|x_{0}^{(i)}-x_{0}^{(j)}\|>0
and
R\coloneqq\max_{i}\|x_{0}^{(i)}\|<\infty
.
The data distribution
p
is the empirical distribution of the data, i.e.,
p=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{0}^{(i)}}
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{T-\delta}^{(i)}\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(d\sqrt{\delta}+\sqrt{\delta}\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}h_{T-u}^{-\frac{3}{2}}{\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\parallel,i}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}]}\mathrm{d}u).

Remark 3.1

(Comparison between normal&tangent directions)
.

Our bounds in Theorems
3.2
,
3.3
show that geometry at the final inference stage is controlled by dimension-errors and the cumulated mean-errors, splitting along normal&tangent directions. The discrepancy on the coefficients in the two directions orients from different contraction mechanisms: normal contraction is due to the curvature threshold of the ridge, hence depending on the
\beta_{t}
-related parameter
c
; while tangential contraction is purely driven by the reverse-time SDE. While the discrepancy makes the normal dimension-error (
d\delta^{c}
) smaller than the tangential dimension-error (
d\delta^{1/2}
), its effect on the mean-errors is consistent in the two directions. Both reflect that the training weight
w(t)
sets how hard the objective pushes down the end-stage mean-errors: if the per-time error
w(t)h_{t}^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]=\mathcal{O}(1)
, the normal and tangential mean-errors scale like
\delta^{c}\int_{\delta}^{T-\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}h_{t}^{1-c}/{w(t)}\mathrm{d}t
and
\delta^{1/2}\int_{\delta}^{T-\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}h_{t}^{1/2}/{w(t)}\mathrm{d}t
respectively. In particular, under weights
w(t)=h_{t}^{2},h_{t},1
, the mean-errors along both directions yield
\mathcal{O}(1),\mathcal{O}(\delta),\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2})
respectively, hence inducing different inductive biases in generation.

4.3
RFNN for Two Points in 2D

Consider the two-point dataset in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Assume WLOG
x_{0}^{(1)}=(-\mu,0)
and
x_{0}^{(2)}=(\mu,0)
. Here
\mathcal{R}_{t}\equiv\{(x_{1},0)|x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}\}
and the posterior mean is
m(t,x)=(a_{t}\mu\tanh(\tfrac{a_{t}\mu}{h_{t}}x_{1}),0)\coloneqq(f_{t}(x_{1}),0)
. Moreover, the second row of
\tilde{V}
is zero, i.e.,
\tilde{V}=e_{1}\tilde{v}^{\intercal}
for some
\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Denoting the gradient descent initialization
A_{0}=(A_{0,1},A_{0,2})^{\intercal}
, the optimization-driven errors along tangent and normal directions are respectively given by
\mathrm{Err}_{train}^{\parallel}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(1-2\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}(A_{0,1}^{\intercal}u_{i}-\tilde{v}^{\intercal}u_{i}/\lambda_{i})^{2}
and
\mathrm{Err}_{train}^{\perp}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(1-2\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}(A_{0,2}^{\intercal}u_{i})^{2}
. Both errors decay to zero as
k\to\infty
, but their magnitudes at finite training time depend on how the initialization aligns with the kernel eigenvectors
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
. As a result, the error can be very different in normal versus tangent directions, hence leading to different geometric generation outcomes. For instance, normal-direction (vertical) generation behavior under finite width (
p
) and finite training time (
k
) differs sharply across initializations:

(1)

Zero initialization:
if
A_{0,2}=0
, then
\mathrm{Err}_{train}^{\perp}(k)\equiv 0
and hence
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}^{\perp}\equiv 0
. The theory therefore predicts essentially perfect normal alignment even with small
k
. So the samples are nearly on the horizontal line.

(2)

Slow-spectrum initialization
: if
A_{0,2}
is aligned with the smallest positive eigenvalue’s eigenspace and
A_{0,2}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{r}}u_{r}
. Then
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}^{\perp}=\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{r}}(1-2\eta\lambda_{r})^{2k}
. In this case, normal alignment can be poor when
k
is small. As
k
increases, the gap from samples to the horizontal ridge decreases at the slow geometric rate determined by
\lambda_{r}
.

In contrast, the architecture-driven error is purely tangential: the normal component
\mathrm{Err}_{arc}^{\perp}=0
, while the tangent component
\mathrm{Err}_{arc}^{\parallel}=\int_{\delta}^{T}\tfrac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{t}}[f_{t}(x_{1})^{2}]\mathrm{d}t-\tilde{v}^{\intercal}\tilde{U}^{+}\tilde{v}
which is strictly positive at finite
p
.
Consequently, the normal error is much smaller than the tangential error, leading to strong normal alignment to the ridge but persistent tangential spreading along it. The samples therefore form an edge-like interpolation between the two points. As
p
grows, the tangential architecture floor shrinks, and the slide stage increasingly concentrates samples near the two centers, yielding memorization-like behavior in this toy example.

Appendix A
Denoising Mean Matching Loss

In this section, we introduce the detailed derivation of the posterior mean-matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}
and the denoising posterior mean-matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}
. According to Tweedie’s formula that

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=-\frac{x}{h_{t}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}[a_{t}X_{0}|X_{t}=x]}{h_{t}}=-\frac{x}{h_{t}}+\frac{m(t,x)}{h_{t}},

to parametrize the score, it suffices to parametrize the posterior mean
m
. We denote the parametrization by
m_{A}
. Then the posterior mean matching loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}
defined in (
3
) is equivalent to the score matching loss:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m_{A}(t,X_{t})-m(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}w(t)\mathbb{E}\big[\|\frac{-X_{t}+m_{A}(t,X_{t})}{h_{t}}-\frac{-X_{t}+m(t,X_{t})}{h_{t}}\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}w(t)\mathbb{E}\big[\|s_{A}(t,X_{t})-\nabla\log p_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t.

However,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}
can’t be evaluated directly using data from
X_{0}
. We can apply the same denoising trick as what’s done for score matching loss.

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m_{A}(t,X_{t})-m(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m_{A}(t,X_{t})-a_{t}X_{0}+a_{t}X_{0}-m(t,X_{t})\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m_{A}(t,X_{t})-a_{t}X_{0}\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\|m(t,X_{t})-a_{t}X_{0}\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-\bcancel{2\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}\big[\langle m_{A}(t,X_{t})-a_{t}X_{0},m(t,X_{t})-a_{t}X_{0}\rangle\big]\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}+C,

where the last term in the third identity is canceled due to the definition of
m
and tower property. The second term in the third identity is a constant independent to the trained parameter
A
. Therefore, we can train to optimize
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}
directly. The DMM loss evolves two expectations and one integral:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}\mathbb{E}_{z}\big[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+m_{A}(t,a_{t}X_{0}+\sqrt{h_{t}}z)\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t.

Under data assumption
1
that
p=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{0}^{(i)}}
, the expectation
\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}}
can be exactly evaluated through empirical average over all training data, i.e.,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{z}\big[\|-a_{t}x_{0}^{(i)}+m_{A}(t,a_{t}x_{0}^{(i)}+\sqrt{h_{t}}z)\|^{2}\big]\mathrm{d}t.

(12)

For the convenience of analysis, we focus on analyzing the loss defined in (
12
), which corresponds to exact evaluations for
\mathbb{E}_{z}
and integral in
t
.

In practice, the loss in (
12
) is used after further numerical approximations for
\mathbb{E}_{z}
and integral in
t
. The practical DMM loss is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}^{m,N}=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n,m}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{t_{k}-t_{k-1}}{h_{t_{k}}^{2}}\|-a_{t_{k}}x_{0}^{(i)}+m_{A}(t_{k},a_{t_{k}}x_{0}^{(i)}+\sqrt{h_{t_{k}}}z^{(i,j)})\|^{2}

where
\{z^{(i,j)}\}_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq m}
is a sequence of i.i.d. standard Gaussian vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\delta=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}=T
are the time grids for numerical integration on
[\delta,T]
.

\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})
;

(2)

\forall x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})
,
n_{t}(x)\coloneqq x-\Pi_{t}(x)
is in the normal space at
\Pi_{t}(x)
, i.e,
n_{t}(x)\in N_{\Pi_{t}(x)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
;

(3)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})}\|\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)\|\leq\tfrac{1}{1-\rho_{t}/r_{t}}
;

(4)

the motion of
\Pi_{t}
is bounded: for any
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, there exists a velocity field
v_{t}\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
s.t.
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})}\|\partial_{t}\Pi_{t}\|\leq V_{t}+\frac{\rho_{t}}{1-\rho_{t}/r_{t}}W_{t}
where

V_{t}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}}\|v_{t}(z)\|,\quad W_{t}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t},\|u\|=1}\|P^{\parallel}(z)(\nabla v_{t}(z)u)\|.

Proof of Proposition
B.1
.

That
\Pi_{t}
is well-defined on
\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})
directly follows from Assumption
2
. The
C^{1}
smoothness of
\Pi_{t}
in space follows from
Leobacher and Steinicke 2021
. Property
(2)
follows from the optimality of the nearest-point:
z=\Pi_{t}(x)
minimizes
\|x-z^{\prime}\|^{2}
over
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Therefore, differentiating
z^{\prime}\mapsto\|x-z^{\prime}\|^{2}
along any tangent direction
u\in T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
yields

\displaystyle D\big(\|x-z^{\prime}\|^{2}\big)[u]|_{z^{\prime}=z}=-2\langle x-z,u\rangle=-2\langle n_{t}(x),u\rangle=0.

Therefore,
n_{t}(x)\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
.

To prove
(3)
, we first apply the explicit expression of
\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)
in
Leobacher and Steinicke 2021
:

\displaystyle\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)=\big(id_{T_{\Pi_{t}(x)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}-\|x-\Pi_{t}(x)\|S_{t,\Pi_{t}(x),v}\big)^{-1}P^{\parallel}(\Pi_{t}(x)),\quad\theta=\frac{x-\Pi_{t}(x)}{\|x-\Pi_{t}(x)\|},

where
S_{t,\Pi_{t}(x),\theta}
is the shape operator in the normal direction
\theta
. According to Lemma
B.1
and the linearity of
S_{t,\Pi_{t}(x),\theta}
in
\theta
, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Pi_{t}(x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\big(id_{T_{\Pi_{t}(x)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}-\|x-\Pi_{t}(x)\|S_{t,\Pi_{t}(x),v}\big)^{-1}P^{\parallel}(\Pi_{t}(x))\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\big(id_{T_{\Pi_{t}(x)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}-S_{t,\Pi_{t}(x),\|x-\Pi_{t}(x)\|v}\big)^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\|x-\Pi_{t}(x)\|/r_{t}}\leq\frac{1}{1-\rho_{t}/r_{t}}.

Last, to prove
(4)
, we first apply Lemma
B.2
to show existence of the normal velocity field
v_{t}
. Then the estimation of
\|\partial_{t}\Pi_{t}\|
follows from the definition of
V_{t}
and Lemma
B.3
.
∎

Definition B.1

(Shape Operator)
.

Let
\text{II}_{t,z}
be the second fundamental form of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
at
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
. For
\theta\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, the shape operator in the direction of
\theta
is defined as
S_{t,z,\theta}:T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\to T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
is then defined as

\displaystyle\langle S_{t,z,\theta}u,v\rangle=\langle\mathrm{II}_{t,z}(u,v),\theta\rangle,\quad\forall u,v\in T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t}).

Lemma B.1
.

Under Assumption
2
, for any
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
and
\theta\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, we have

(1)

the shape operator is bounded:
\|S_{t,z,\theta}\|\leq\|\theta\|/r_{t}
;

(2)

if
\|\theta\|\leq\rho_{t}<r_{t}
, the operator
L_{t,z,\theta}\coloneqq id_{T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}-S_{t,z,\theta}
is invertible and

\displaystyle\|L_{t,z,\theta}^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{1-\|\theta\|/r_{t}}.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

According to
Niyogi et al. 2008
, under Assumption
2
,
\|\mathrm{II}_{t,z}\|\leq 1/r_{t}
for all
t
. Therefore,
\|S_{t,z,\theta}\|\leq\|\theta\|/r_{t}
and hence
\|L_{t,z,\theta}^{-1}\|=\|(id_{T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}-S_{t,z,\theta})^{-1}\|\leq\tfrac{1}{1-\|\theta\|/r_{t}}
.
∎

Lemma B.2
.

Fix
t\in[\delta,T]
. Let
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
be open and
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
C^{1}
-family of
C^{2}
embeddings such that
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(U)\subset\mathcal{R}_{t}
and
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}
is a local parametrization of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
along a given
C^{1}
-curve
z(t)\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Assume that
z(t)=\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\tilde{u}(t))
for some
C^{1}
-curve
\tilde{u}(t)\in U
. Then there exists a
C^{1}
-family of local diffeomorphisms
\psi_{t}:U^{\prime}\to U
Then there exists a family of diffeomorphism
\psi_{t}:U^{\prime}\to U
such that

\displaystyle\Phi_{t}\coloneqq\tilde{\Phi}_{t}\circ\psi_{t}:U^{\prime}\to\mathcal{R}_{t},\quad\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(u)\perp T_{\Phi_{t}(u)}(\mathcal{R}_{t}),\ \forall(t,u)\in[\delta,T]\times U^{\prime}.

Consequently, the velocity field
v_{t}(z)\coloneqq\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(u)
for
z=\Phi_{t}(u)
is a well-defined
C^{0}
normal velocity field on
\Phi(U^{\prime})
, i.e.,
v_{t}(z)\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. Moreover,
v_{t}
is intrinsic in the sense that it equals to the normal component of
\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{t}
:
v_{t}(\Phi_{t}(u))=P_{N_{\Phi_{t}(u)}}(\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)))
, and is therefore independent of the tangent reparametrization of the chart.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Define
\tilde{v}_{t}(u)\coloneqq\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)
. Since
\tilde{\Phi}_{t}
is an embedding,
\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u):\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}\to T_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
is a linear isomorphism for each
(t,u)
. Let
P^{\parallel}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u))
denote orthogonal projection onto
T_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. Define a time-dependent vector field
a_{t}
on
U
by

\displaystyle\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)a_{t}(u)=-P^{\parallel}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u))\widetilde{v}_{t}(u)\in T_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)}(\mathcal{R}_{t}).

(14)

Since
\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)
is invertible on
T_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
,
a_{t}(u)
in (
14
) is uniquely defined. Furthermore, under our assumptions,
(t,u)\mapsto\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u)
and
(t,u)\mapsto P^{\parallel}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(u))\tilde{v}_{t}(u)
are continuous in
t
and smooth in
u
, hence
(t,u)\mapsto a_{t}(u)
is also continuous in
t
and smooth in
u
.

Pick an open set
U^{\prime}\subset U
that contains
\tilde{u}(t)
for all
t\in[\delta,T]
. Consider the ODE

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\psi_{t}(u)=a_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)),\quad\psi_{\delta}(u)=u\in U^{\prime}.

(15)

Due to the regularity of
a_{t}(\cdot)
, there exists a unique solution
\psi_{t}
for all
t\in[\delta,T]
and
\psi_{t}
is a diffeomorphism for each
t
.

Now apply chain rule and we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(u)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}\circ\psi_{t})(u)=\tilde{v}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))+\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))\partial_{t}\psi_{t}(u)

\displaystyle=\tilde{v}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))+\nabla\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))a_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))=\tilde{v}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))-P^{\parallel}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)))\widetilde{v}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))

\displaystyle=P^{\perp}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)))\widetilde{v}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)),

where the second last identity follows from (
14
) and
P^{\perp}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)))=I-P^{\parallel}(\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u)))
is the normal projection. Therefore,
\partial\Phi_{t}(u)\perp T_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. Last,
v_{t}(z)\coloneqq\partial\Phi_{t}(u)\in N_{\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
with

is well-defined on
\Phi_{t}(U^{\prime})
and we can check that
v_{t}(\Phi_{t}(u))
is exactly the normal component of
\partial_{t}\tilde{\Phi}_{t}(\psi_{t}(u))
, hence independent to the tangent reparametrization.
∎

Lemma B.3
.

For
t\in[\delta,T]
, let
x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})
and
z(t)\coloneqq\Pi_{t}(x)
. Under conditions in Lemma
B.2
and Assumption
2
, there exists a reparametrization chart
\Phi_{t}
in normal gauge and a
C^{1}
curve
u(t)\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
such that
z(t)=\Phi_{t}(u(t))
and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Pi_{t}(x)=v_{t}(z(t))+\tau_{t},\quad v_{t}(z(t))\in N_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\ \text{and }\tau_{t}\coloneqq\nabla_{u}\Phi_{t}(u(t))\partial_{t}u(t)\in T_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t}).

Furthermore,
\|\tau_{t}\|\leq\tfrac{\rho_{t}}{1-\rho_{t}/r_{t}}W_{t}
.

Proof of Lemma
B.3
.

The existence of
\Phi_{t}
follows from Lemma
B.2
. Next, differentiate
z(t)=\Phi_{t}(u(t))
and we get

\displaystyle\partial_{t}z(t)=\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(u(t))+\nabla_{u}\Phi_{t}(u(t))\partial_{t}u(t)=v_{t}(z)+\tau_{t}.

To prove the bound for
\|\tau_{t}\|
, we consider the local tangent frame
\{E_{i}(t)\}_{i=1}^{d^{*}}
along
z(t)
induced by the chart
u\mapsto\Phi_{t}(u)
. Since
n_{t}(x)=x-z(t)\perp T_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, we have
\langle n_{t}(x),E_{i}(t)\rangle=0
for all
1\leq i\leq d^{*}
. Differentiate wrt
t
on both sides and we get

\displaystyle 0=-\langle\partial_{t}z(t),E_{i}(t)\rangle+\langle n_{t}(x),\partial_{t}E_{i}(t)\rangle.

Decompose
\partial_{t}z(t)=v_{t}(z(t))+\tau_{t}
and use the fact that
v_{t}(z(t))\perp T_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, and we get

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{t},E_{i}(t)\rangle=\langle n_{t}(x),\partial_{t}E_{i}(t)\rangle.

(16)

Using the local frame we can compute
\partial_{t}E_{i}(t)
as follows. Since
E_{i}(t)=\partial_{u_{i}}\Phi_{t}(u(t))
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{u_{i}}\partial_{t}\Phi_{t}(u(t))+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{*}}\partial^{2}_{u_{i}u_{j}}\Phi_{t}(u(t))\partial_{j}u(t)

\displaystyle=\partial_{u_{i}}(v_{t}\circ\Phi_{t})(u(t))+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{*}}P^{\perp}(z)(\partial^{2}_{u_{i}u_{j}}\Phi_{t}(u(t)))\partial_{t}u_{j}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{*}}P^{\parallel}(z)(\partial^{2}_{u_{i}u_{j}}\Phi_{t}(u(t)))\partial_{t}u_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\nabla v_{t}(z)E_{i}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{*}}\text{II}_{t,z}(E_{i}(t),E_{j}(t))\partial_{t}u_{j}(t)+\sum_{j=1}^{d^{*}}P^{\parallel}(z)(\partial^{2}_{u_{i}u_{j}}\Phi_{t}(u(t)))\partial_{t}u_{j}(t).

Since
n_{t}(x)\in N_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
, we have

\displaystyle\langle n_{t}(x),\partial_{t}E_{i}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle n_{t}(x),\nabla v_{t}(z(t))E_{i}(t)\rangle+\langle n_{t}(x),\text{II}_{t,z}(E_{i}(t),\sum_{j}\partial_{t}u_{j}(t)E_{j}(t))\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle n_{t}(x),\nabla v_{t}(z(t))E_{i}(t)\rangle+\langle S_{t,z,n_{t}(x)}\tau_{t},E_{i}(t)\rangle,

where the last identity follows from the definition of shape operator and
\tau_{t}=\sum_{j}\partial_{t}u_{j}(t)E_{j}(t)
. Plug the above equation into (
16
) and we get that restricted to the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{z(t)}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
,

\displaystyle(I-S_{t,z,n_{t}(x)})\tau_{t}=P^{\parallel}(z(t))(\nabla v_{t}(z(t)))^{\intercal}n_{t}(x).

Therefore, according to Lemma
B.1
, the definition of
W_{t}
and the fact that
x\in\mathcal{T}_{t}(\rho_{t})
,

\displaystyle\|\tau_{t}\|\leq\|L_{t,z,n_{t}(x)}^{-1}\|\|\nabla v_{t}(z(t))\|\|n_{t}(x)\|\leq\frac{\rho_{t}}{1-\rho_{t}/r_{t}}W_{t}.

∎

\displaystyle v_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=-(E_{t}(z)\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)E_{t}(z))^{-1}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)

\displaystyle=-(E_{t}(z)\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)E_{t}(z))^{-1}\frac{1}{h_{t}}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z),

where the last identity follows from Lemma
C.6
. Therefore,

\displaystyle V_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}}\|v_{t}(z)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h_{t}}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}}\|(E_{t}(z)\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)E_{t}(z))^{-1}\|\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{t}h_{t}}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}}\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z)\|,

where the last inequality follows form the estimate in the proof of Lemma
C.5
. Hence according to Lemma
C.7
,
V_{t}=\mathcal{O}(\tfrac{R}{h_{t}\beta_{t}})
.

Last, to bound
W_{t}
, recall that
W_{t}=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t},\|u\|=1}\|P^{\parallel}(z)(\nabla v_{t}(z)u)\|
. Notice that
v_{t}(z)\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. Hence
P^{\perp}(z)v_{t}(z)=v_{t}(z)
. Differentiate both sides of the equation at
z
along direction
u
, we have

\displaystyle(\nabla_{u}P^{\perp}(z))v_{t}(z)+P^{\perp}(z)\nabla_{u}v_{t}(z)=\nabla_{u}v_{t}(z)

\displaystyle\quad\implies

\displaystyle(\nabla_{u}P^{\perp}(z))v_{t}(z)=(I-P^{\perp}(z))\nabla_{u}v_{t}(z)=P^{\parallel}(z)\nabla_{u}v_{t}(z).

Therefore, we immediately obtain

\displaystyle W_{t}\leq\big(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{R}_{t},\|u\|=1}\|\nabla_{u}P^{\perp}(z)\|\big)V_{t}

Next, we bound
\|\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)\|
. Notice that
P^{\parallel}(z)+P^{\perp}(z)=I_{d}
, hence
\|\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)\|=\|\nabla_{u}P^{\perp}(z)\|
. Now we start from
P^{\perp}(z)^{2}=P^{\perp}(z)
, taking the directional derivative on both side, left multiplying
P^{\parallel}(z)
and right multiplying
P^{\parallel}(z)
,

\displaystyle P^{\parallel}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\parallel}(z)+P^{\parallel}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\parallel}(z)=P^{\parallel}(z)\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)P^{\parallel}(z),

Hence
P^{\parallel}(z)\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)P^{\parallel}(z)=0
. Similarly, we start from
P^{\perp}(z)^{2}=P^{\perp}(z)
, taking the directional derivative on both side, left multiplying
P^{\perp}(z)
and right multiplying
P^{\perp}(z)
, and get

\displaystyle P^{\perp}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\parallel}(z)P^{\perp}(z)+P^{\perp}(z)P^{\parallel}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\perp}(z)=P^{\perp}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\perp}(z).

Since
P^{\parallel}(z)P^{\perp}(z)=P^{\perp}(z)P^{\parallel}(z)
, we have
P^{\perp}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\perp}(z)
. Therefore, we can express
\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)
on
T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\oplus N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
as

\displaystyle\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}0&B^{\intercal}\\
B&0\end{pmatrix},

with
B=P^{\perp}(z)(\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z))P^{\parallel}(z):T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\to N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{u}P^{\perp}(z)\|=\|\nabla_{u}P^{\parallel}(z)\|=\|B\|=\|\mathrm{II}_{t,z}(u,\cdot)\|_{T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})\to N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})}\lesssim\frac{1}{r_{t}}.

where the last inequality follows from
Aamari et al. 2019
.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle W_{t}\lesssim V_{t}/r_{t}.

Hence we proved Proposition
C.1
-(3).
∎

Lemma C.3

(Explicit formulas)
.

Under Assumption
1
, we have that for all
t\in[\delta,T]
,

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{x}{h_{t}}+\frac{m(t,x)}{h_{t}},\quad\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x)=-\frac{I_{d}}{h_{t}}+\frac{\Sigma(t,x)}{h_{t}^{2}},

\displaystyle\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}[(U(t,x)-m(t,x))^{\otimes 3}]}{h_{t}^{3}},

where
U(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a random vector taking values
\{a_{t}x_{0}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with probabilities
\{\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{t}x_{0}\|^{2}}{2h_{t}})_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and

\displaystyle m(t,x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[U(t,x)],\quad\Sigma(t,x)=\mathrm{Cov}(U(t,x)),

with
x_{0}=(x_{0}^{(1)},x_{0}^{(2)},\cdots,x_{0}^{(n)})
. Furthermore,
\nabla m(t,x)=\Sigma(t,x)/h_{t}
.

Lemma C.4

(Second Fundamental Form Bound)
.

For any
t\in[\delta,T]
and
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{II}_{t,z}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{t}}\|\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)\|.

Proof of Lemma
C.4
.

For any
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we consider
T\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d^{*}}
and
N\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-d^{*})}
to be the orthonormal basis spanning the spaces
T_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
and
N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t})
respectively. We use coordinates
(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-d^{*}}
via
x(u,v)=z+Tu+Nv
. We can define the function

\displaystyle F(u,v)\coloneqq N^{\intercal}\nabla\log p_{t}(x(u,v))\in\mathbb{R}^{d-d^{*}}.

Since
z\in\mathbb{R}_{t}
, the definition of ridge set
\mathcal{R}_{t}
includes the normal component of the score
\nabla\log p_{t}(x)
is zero, i.e.,
F(0,0)=0
. The partial derivatives along the normal direction is

\displaystyle\partial_{v}F(u,v)=N^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(x(u,v))N,\quad\partial_{v}F(0,0)=N^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)N.

Since
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}
is invertible, we have

\displaystyle\|(\partial_{v}F(0,0))^{-1}\|=\|(N^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)N)^{-1}\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{t}},

where the inequality follows from the second condition in the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
. Therefore, apply the implicit function theorem and we get: there exists a neighborhood
0\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
and a
C^{2}
map
\phi:U\to\mathbb{R}^{d-d^{*}}
with
\phi(0)=0
such that the local solution set to
F(u,v)=0
is the set
\{(u,v)\mid v=\phi(u)\}
. Therefore, a local parametrization of the manifold is

\displaystyle\gamma(u)=z+Tu+N\phi(u).

And differentiating
F(u,\phi(u))=0
at
u=0
implies
\nabla\phi(0)=0
. Now differentiate
F(u,\phi(u))=0
twice and evaluate at
u=0
and use the fact that
\nabla\phi(0)=0
, we get

\displaystyle\partial_{uu}F(0,0)+\partial_{v}F(0,0)\nabla^{2}\phi(0)=0.

Since
F(u,v)=N^{\intercal}\nabla\log p_{t}(x(u,v))
, we have that for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{*}}
,

\displaystyle\partial_{uu}F(0,0)[\xi,\xi]=N^{\intercal}(\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z))[T\xi,T\xi]\implies\|\partial_{uu}F(0,0)\|\leq\|\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)\|.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\phi(0)\|

\displaystyle=\|-(\partial_{v}F(0,0))^{-1}\partial_{uu}F(0,0)\|\leq\|-(\partial_{v}F(0,0))^{-1}\|\|\partial_{uu}F(0,0)\|\leq\frac{1}{\beta_{t}}\|\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)\|.

Last, along the local parametrization
\gamma(u)=z+Tu+N\phi(u)
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{ij}^{2}\gamma(0)=N\partial_{ij}^{2}\phi(0).

Therefore, apply the definition of
\mathrm{II}
and we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{II}_{t,z}(\nabla\gamma(0)\xi,\nabla\gamma(0)\theta)=P^{\perp}(z)\nabla^{2}\gamma(0)[\xi,\theta]=N\nabla^{2}\phi(0)[\xi,\theta].

Since
N
is isotropic (orthonormal), we prove the Lemma.
∎

Lemma C.5

(Normal velocity field expression)
.

For all fixed
t\in[\delta,T]
and
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, let
E_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-d^{*})}
be the normal eigen-matrix in the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
. The normal velocity field in Lemma
B.2
can be expressed as

\displaystyle v_{t}(z)=-(E_{t}(z)\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)E_{t}(z))^{-1}E_{t}(z)\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)\in N_{z}(\mathcal{R}_{t}).

(19)

Proof of Lemma
C.5
.

Fix
t_{0}\in(\delta,T)
and
z_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
, consider the normal gauge parametrization near
(t_{0},z_{0})
. we define
F(t,x)\coloneqq E_{t_{0}}(z_{0})^{\intercal}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d-d^{*}}
with
E_{t_{0}}
being the orthonormal basis of
N_{z_{0}}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}})
. Since
z_{0}\in\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}}
, we have
F(t_{0},z_{0})=0
. Taking partial derivatives of
F
and evaluating at
(t_{0},z_{0})

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F(t_{0},z_{0})

\displaystyle=E_{t_{0}}(z_{0})^{\intercal}\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z_{0})|_{t=t_{0}},

\displaystyle\nabla F(t_{0},z_{0})

\displaystyle=E_{t_{0}}(z_{0})^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t_{0}}(z_{0}).

Pick a normal gauge curve
t\mapsto z(t)\in\mathcal{R}_{t}

z(t_{0})=z_{0}
and
\partial_{t}z(t)|_{t=t_{0}}\in N_{z_{0}}(\mathcal{R}_{t_{0}})

F(z,z(t))=0
. Taking derivative wrt.
t
to both sides of the equation, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}F(t_{0},z_{0})+\nabla F(t_{0},z_{0})\partial_{t}z

\displaystyle=E_{t_{0}}(z_{0})^{\intercal}\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z_{0})|_{t=t_{0}}+E_{t_{0}}(z_{0})^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t_{0}}(z_{0})\partial_{t}z(t)|_{t=t_{0}}

Lemma C.6

(Expression of ridge motion)
.

Let
E_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-d^{*})}
be the normal eigen-matrix in the definition of
\mathcal{R}_{t}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{R}_{t}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid E_{t}(x)\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=0,,\lambda_{d^{*}+1}\leq-\beta_{t}\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)=\frac{1}{h_{t}}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z).

Proof of Lemma
C.6
.

According to Tweedie’s formula, we have

\displaystyle E_{t}(x)\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{t}}E_{t}(x)(m(t,x)-x),

\displaystyle\implies E_{t}(x)\partial_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{t}}E_{t}(x)\partial_{t}m(t,x)-\frac{\partial_{t}h_{t}}{h_{t}^{2}}E_{t}(x)(m(t,x)-x).

(20)

For
x=z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{t}(x)\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{h_{t}}E_{t}(x)(m(t,x)-x)=0.

Therefore, the last term in (
20
) cancels. The Lemma is proved.

Lemma C.7
.

For any
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, let
E_{t}(z)
be the eigen-matrix in Definition
3.1
. We have

\displaystyle\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z)\|\lesssim(1+\dot{h}_{t})a_{t}R.

Proof of Lemma
C.7
.

By Lemma
C.3
,

\displaystyle\quad\partial_{t}m(t,z)

\displaystyle=\partial_{t}(h_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}z)

\displaystyle=\dot{h}_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}\nabla(\frac{\partial_{t}p_{t}(z)}{p_{t}(z)})+\dot{h}_{t}z

\displaystyle=\dot{h}_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}\nabla(\frac{z\cdot\nabla p_{t}(z)+\Delta p_{t}(z)+dp_{t}(z)}{p_{t}(z)})+\dot{h}_{t}z

\displaystyle=\dot{h}_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)z+dh_{t}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}\nabla(\frac{\Delta p_{t}(z)}{p_{t}(z)})+\dot{h}_{t}z

\displaystyle=(\dot{h}_{t}+dh_{t})\nabla\log p_{t}(z)+h_{t}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)z+\dot{h}_{t}z

\displaystyle\quad+h_{t}(\mathrm{cont}_{23}\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)+2\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)\nabla\log p_{t}(z)).

Since
E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla\log p_{t}(x)=0
and
E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)\nabla\log p_{t}(z)=0
for all
z\in\mathcal{R}_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\partial_{t}m(t,z)

\displaystyle=h_{t}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)z+\dot{h}_{t}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}z+h_{t}E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\mathrm{cont}_{23}\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z).

Next we bound the three terms on the RHS. First, according to Lemma
C.3
and the fact that
E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla\log p_{t}(z)=0
,

\displaystyle\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}z\|=\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}m(t,z)\|\leq\|m(t,z)\|\leq a_{t}R.

Next, according to Lemma
C.3
,
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}=-\tfrac{1}{h_{t}}I_{d}+\tfrac{\Sigma(t,z)}{h_{t}^{2}}
with
\Sigma(t,z)\succeq 0
. Therefore, all negative eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}
are at least
-\tfrac{1}{h_{t}}
. On the other hand,
E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}
only preserve eigenvalues that are smaller than
-\beta_{t}
. Therefore,
\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)
only contributes eigenvalues that are smaller than
-\beta_{t}
, hence negative, in
E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)z
. We have

\displaystyle\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\nabla^{2}\log p_{t}(z)z\|\leq\frac{1}{h_{t}}\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}z\|\leq\frac{a_{t}R}{h_{t}}.

Regarding the last term, according to Lemma
C.2
,

\displaystyle\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}\mathrm{cont}_{23}\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)\|=\|E_{t}(z)^{\intercal}(\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z):I)\|\leq\sqrt{d}\|\nabla^{3}\log p_{t}(z)\|=\mathcal{O}(\frac{\sqrt{d}\theta_{t}R^{3}}{h_{t}^{3}}).

Since
\theta_{t}
can be arbitrarily chosen with order
\exp(-o(h_{t}^{-1}))
, the last term is negligible in the final order estimation. Hence we proved Lemma
C.7
.
∎

Appendix D
Analysis of Stage 1

In this section, we analyze the first stage: the reverse-time inference process enters the tube neighborhood of the log-density ridge with high probability. The formal version of Theorem
3.1
is derived from considering exactly the reverse OU process as the reference process:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\leftarrow}=\big((1-\frac{2}{h_{T-t}})X_{t}^{\leftarrow}+\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}m(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\big)\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\leftarrow},\quad X_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T},

and
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T-t}
for all
0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}
. We define the entering time of
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
:

\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{in}}^{\leftarrow}\coloneqq\inf\{0\leq t\leq T-\delta|X_{t}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{T-t}(\rho_{T-t})\}.

And we also analyze the reverse-time inference dynamics
Y_{t}
with

t_{\mathrm{in}}\coloneqq\inf\{0\leq t\leq T-\delta\mid Y_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{T-t}(\rho_{T-t})\}.

Then the formal Theorem that describing our stage 1 states as follows:

Theorem D.1
.

Under Assumption
1
, we have

(1)

\mathbb{P}(t_{\mathrm{in}}^{\leftarrow}\leq T-\delta)\geq 1-e_{\delta}
;

(2)

\mathbb{P}(t_{\mathrm{in}}\leq T-\delta)\geq 1-e_{\delta}-\varepsilon(T)
;

(3)

\mathbb{P}(\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}\leq T-\delta)\geq 1-e_{\delta}-\varepsilon(T)-\sqrt{\varepsilon_{A}(T,\delta)/8}
,

where
e_{\delta}=h_{\delta}^{\zeta}
for any
\zeta>0
and
e_{\delta}\to 0
at any polynomial order as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.
\varepsilon(T)=\frac{a_{T}}{2}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{h_{T}}}+R)\to 0
exponentially fast as
T\to\infty
.

Proof of Theorem
D.1
.

First, for the exact reverse OU process, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))=\mathbb{P}(X_{\delta}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))=\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(X_{\delta},\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\leq\rho_{\delta})\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{dist}(a_{\delta}X_{0},\mathcal{R}_{\delta})+\sqrt{h_{\delta}}\|z\|\leq\rho_{\delta}).

We will first show
\mathrm{dist}(a_{\delta}X_{0},\mathcal{R}_{\delta})
is very small when
\delta\ll 1
, and then apply the concentration of
d
-dimensional Gaussian to bound the probability.

According to Lemma
D.1
, if
\delta
is small enough such that
h_{\delta}^{-1}>\tfrac{2}{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\ln(\tfrac{4a_{\delta}^{2}R^{2}(n-1)}{\rho_{\delta}})
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(a_{\delta}X_{0},\mathcal{R}_{\delta})\leq\|z_{i}-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}\|\leq\rho_{\delta}/2.

Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))\geq\mathbb{P}(\sqrt{h_{\delta}}\|z\|\leq\rho_{\delta})=\mathbb{P}(\|z\|^{2}\leq\rho_{\delta}^{2}/h_{\delta})

According to the order estimation of
r_{t}
in Proposition
C.1
, we can pick
\delta
small such that
\rho_{\delta}^{2}/h_{\delta}\leq d+2\sqrt{d\zeta\log(1/h_{\delta})}+2\zeta\log(1/h_{\delta})
for any
\zeta>0
. Then according to the LMI inequality
(
Moshksar 2024
)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))\geq\mathbb{P}(\|z\|^{2}\leq d+2\sqrt{d\zeta\log(1/h_{\delta})}+2\zeta\log(1/h_{\delta}))\geq 1-h_{\delta}^{\zeta}.

Next, for the reverse-time inference process
Y_{t}
. Notice that
Y_{t}
and
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
have the same generator but with different initializations:
Y_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
while
X_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T}
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(Y_{T-\delta}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))-\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{TV}(\mathrm{Law}(Y_{T-\delta}),p_{\delta})\leq\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),p_{T})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(p_{T}|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}))}{2}}\leq\frac{a_{T}}{2}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{h_{T}}}+R),

where the second inequality follows from data processing inequality. The third inequality follows from Pinsker’s inequality. The last inequality follows from property of OU process, see
He et al. 2024
. Therefore, we proved

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y_{T-\delta}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))\geq\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))-\frac{a_{T}}{2}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{h_{T}}}+R)\geq 1-h_{\delta}^{\zeta}-\frac{a_{T}}{2}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{h_{T}}}+R).

Last, for the inference process
\tilde{Y}_{t}
, except for initialization error, there are extra trajectory error from
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
due to the approximate posterior mean
m_{A}
. We have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{T-\delta}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))-\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))|\leq\mathrm{TV}(\mathrm{Law}(\tilde{Y}_{T-\delta}),p_{\delta})\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}|\tilde{\mathbb{P}})}{2}},

where
\mathbb{P}^{\leftarrow}
is the path measure of
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
and
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
is the path measure of
\tilde{Y}_{t}
. Then we can apply the traditional analysis of the inference process via Girsanov’s Theorem. We get

\displaystyle\quad|\mathbb{P}(\tilde{Y}_{T-\delta}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))-\mathbb{P}(X_{T-\delta}^{\leftarrow}\in\mathcal{T}_{\delta}(\rho_{\delta}))|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(p_{T}|\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}))}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\int_{\delta}^{T}h_{t}^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|m(t,X_{t})-m_{A}(t,X_{t})\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a_{T}}{2}(\frac{\sqrt{d}}{\sqrt{h_{T}}}+R)+\sqrt{\frac{1}{8}\int_{\delta}^{T}h_{t}^{-2}\mathbb{E}[\|m(t,X_{t})-m_{A}(t,X_{t})\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t}.

The statements follows from transferring the probability to the defined stopping times.

Lemma D.1
.

Under Assumption
1
, when
\delta\ll 1
, for each
i\in[n]
, there exists a point
z_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}\|\leq 2a_{\delta}R(n-1)\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}}).

Proof of Lemma
D.1
.

According to Lemma
C.3
, we know

\displaystyle\nabla\log p_{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{x}{h_{\delta}}+\frac{1}{h_{\delta}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})_{i}a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p_{\delta}(x)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{h_{\delta}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{h_{\delta}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})_{i}(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}-m(\delta,x))(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}-m(\delta,x))^{\intercal}.

Next, we find critical points of
\nabla\log p_{\delta}(x)
and show that they are on the log-density ridge
\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
.

According to the expression of
\nabla\log p_{\delta}(x)
, the critical points are fixed points of

x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})_{i}a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}=m(\delta,x).

We claim: for each
i\in[n]
, within
B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
, there exists a unique fixed point, denoted as
z_{i}
. We prove the claim by the contraction mapping theorem. First,
B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a closed ball. Second, for all
x\in B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
, for all
j\neq i
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{j}}{\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{i}}=\exp(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(j)}\|}{2h_{\delta}}+\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}\|}{2h_{\delta}})\leq\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}}),

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{j}\leq(n-1)\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}}).

Hence, we can show
m(\delta,\cdot):B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)\to B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
, i.e., for all
x\in B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
,

\displaystyle\|m(\delta,x)-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{j}a_{\delta}\|x_{0}^{(j)}-x_{0}^{(i)}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2a_{\delta}R(n-1)\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})<a_{\delta}\Delta/4,

given the early stopping time is small enough such that
h_{\delta}^{-1}>\tfrac{2}{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\ln(\tfrac{8R(n-1)}{\Delta})
. Meanwhile, for
x\in B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
, similar to the proof of Lemma
C.2
,

\displaystyle\|\nabla m(\delta,x)\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{h_{\delta}}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|x-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{j}a_{\delta}^{2}\|x_{0}^{(j)}-x_{0}^{(i)}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}R^{2}(n-1)}{h_{\delta}}\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})<1,

given the early stopping time is small enough such that
h_{\delta}^{-1}>\tfrac{2}{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\ln(\tfrac{a_{\delta}^{2}R^{2}(n-1)}{h_{\delta}})
. Therefore, according to the contraction mapping theorem, there exists a unique fixed point
z_{i}\in B(a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)},a_{\delta}\Delta/4)
. At each
z_{i}
, according to Lemma
C.3
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p_{\delta}(z_{i})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{h_{\delta}}I_{d}+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(U(\delta,x))}{h_{\delta}^{2}}

\displaystyle\preceq-\frac{1}{h_{\delta}}I_{d}+\frac{1}{h_{\delta}^{2}}a_{\delta}^{2}R^{2}(n-1)\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}})I_{d}

\displaystyle\preceq-\frac{1}{2h_{\delta}}I_{d},

given the early stopping time is small enough such that
h_{\delta}^{-1}>\tfrac{2}{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\ln(\tfrac{a_{\delta}^{2}R^{2}(n-1)}{2h_{\delta}})
. Therefore, we proved that
z_{i}\in\mathcal{R}_{\delta}
with
\beta_{\delta}=\Theta(\tfrac{1}{h_{\delta}})
. Last, we estimate the distance from
z_{i}
to
a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}
.

\displaystyle\|z_{i}-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}\|

\displaystyle=\|m(t,z_{i})-x_{0}^{(i)}\|\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Softmax}(-\frac{\|z_{i}-a_{\delta}x_{0}\|}{2h_{\delta}})_{j}a_{\delta}\|x_{0}^{(j)}-x_{0}^{(i)}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2a_{\delta}R(n-1)\exp(-\frac{a_{\delta}^{2}\Delta^{2}}{2h_{\delta}}).

∎

Appendix E
Analysis of Stage 2

In this section, we analyze the stage 2 - align along normal directions. We start from a property of the square-normal distance, which simply follows from the log-density ridge property proved in Appendix

(2)

(3)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{a_{T-t}^{3}\beta_{T-t}}{2}D_{T-t}(Y_{t})\quad

. By Gronwall’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|u_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]\leq\exp\big(-2\int_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t}\frac{1}{h_{T-u}}-1\mathrm{d}u\big)\mathbb{E}[\|u_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{(i)}\|^{2}]+\int_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t}\exp\big(-2\int_{u}^{t}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-1\mathrm{d}s\big)\epsilon_{T-u}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}u.

When
t=T-\delta
and
\delta\ll 1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{t_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\exp\big(-2\int_{u}^{T-\delta}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-1\mathrm{d}s\big)\epsilon_{T-u}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}u=\mathcal{O}(d\delta\log(1/\delta)).

Hence we prove that as
t\to T-\delta
, with high probability (tends to
1
as
\delta\to 0^{+}
),
Y_{t}
will enter some region dominated by one of the center
\mathcal{B}_{T-t}^{(i)}(\theta_{T-t})
and then be pulled towards the center
a_{T-t}x_{0}^{(i)}
. Furthermore, quantitatively, the expected square-norm of
Y_{t-\delta}-a_{\delta}x_{0}^{(i)}
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\delta)
.

For the reverse-time inference process
\tilde{Y}_{t}
, we utilize the same idea. Define

\displaystyle\tilde{r}_{t}^{(i)}=\tilde{Y}_{t}-m_{T-t}^{(i)},\quad\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\coloneqq(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}(\tilde{Y}_{t}-m_{T-t}^{(i)}).

The following the same estimations, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]\leq-2(\frac{1}{h_{T-t}}-1)\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]-\frac{4}{h_{T-t}}\langle\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)},(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\rangle+\tilde{\epsilon}_{T-t}^{(i)},

where
\tilde{\epsilon}_{T-t}^{(i)}=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
t\to T^{-}
. Apply Young’s inequality, we have
\tfrac{4}{h_{T-t}}\langle\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)},(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\rangle\leq\tfrac{1}{h_{T-t}}\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}+\tfrac{4}{h_{T-t}}\|(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]\leq-(\frac{1}{h_{T-t}}-2)\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]+\frac{4}{h_{T-t}}\|(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}+\tilde{\epsilon}_{T-t}^{(i)}.

Apply Gronwall’s inequality and we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{t}^{(i)}\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\big(-\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t}\frac{1}{h_{T-u}}-2\mathrm{d}u\big)\mathbb{E}[\|\tilde{u}_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{(i)}\|^{2}]+\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t}\exp\big(-\int_{u}^{t}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-2\mathrm{d}s\big)\epsilon_{T-u}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{t}\exp\big(-\int_{u}^{t}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-2\mathrm{d}s\big)\frac{4}{h_{T-u}}\|(U_{T-u}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}u.

When
t=T-\delta
and
\delta\ll 1
, we have

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\exp\big(-\int_{u}^{T-\delta}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-2\mathrm{d}s\big)\epsilon_{T-u}^{(i)}\mathrm{d}u=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}),

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\exp\big(-\int_{u}^{T-\delta}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-2\mathrm{d}s\big)\frac{4}{h_{T-u}}\|(U_{T-u}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\delta}\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}h_{T-t}^{-\frac{3}{2}}\|(U_{T-u}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}u).

∎

Then the approximate SDE for

Appendix G
From Inference to Training

This section converts the error-bound terms in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, which are expectations along the simulated inference path
\tilde{Y}_{t}
, into quantities that depend only on the exact reverse-time OU path
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
. The key tool is a change of measure (Girsanov’s theorem). A minor technical issue is that
\tilde{Y}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
while
X_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T}
. To isolate the initialization error, we introduce an auxiliary process
\hat{Y}_{t}
that shares the transition kernel with
\tilde{Y}_{t}
but starts from the correct initialization
p_{T}
.

Reverse-time processes.
Let
e_{A}(t,x)\coloneqq m_{A}(t,x)-m(t,x)
. Consider the following reverse-time processes on
[0,T-\delta]
:

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}X_{t}^{\leftarrow}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(X_{t}^{\leftarrow}-\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}X_{t}^{\leftarrow}+\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}m(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow}))}_{b(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}^{\leftarrow},\quad X_{0}^{\leftarrow}\sim p_{T},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\hat{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\hat{Y}_{t}-\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}\hat{Y}_{t}+\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}m_{A}(T-t,\hat{Y}_{t}))}_{\coloneqq b_{A}(T-t,\hat{Y}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t},\quad\hat{Y}_{0}\sim p_{T},

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{Y}_{t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{(\tilde{Y}_{t}-\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}\tilde{Y}_{t}+\frac{2}{h_{T-t}}m_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t}))}_{\coloneqq b_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{B}_{t},\quad\tilde{Y}_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}).

We denote the path measures of
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
and
\hat{Y}_{t}
respectively by
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}
. Since
X_{0}\leftarrow
and
\hat{Y}_{0}
have the same initial distribution, we can apply Girsanov’s theorem without evolving extra initial-density ratio factor. The remaining gap between
\tilde{Y}_{t}
and
\hat{Y}_{t}
is purely from initialization, and it will be controlled since
p_{T}
is close to
\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
when
T
is large.

Assumption 3
.

We assume the following hold:

(0)

Novikov’s condition:
\exp\big(2\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\tfrac{\|e_{A}(T-s,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}}{h_{T-s}^{2}}\mathrm{d}s\big)<\infty
;

(1)

Bound
\chi^{2}
along trajectory:
\sup_{t\in[0,T-\delta]}\chi^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{t}|\mathbb{P}_{t})\leq C_{\chi}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)
;

(2)

Normal/tangent posterior mean error satisfies: for any
\dagger\in\{\perp,\parallel\}
,
\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\dagger}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{4}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C_{m}\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\dagger}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]
for some
C_{m}=\mathcal{O}(1)
;

(3)

Uniform boundedness of the weighted posterior mean error:

\sup_{t\in[0,T-\delta]}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{T}_{T-t}(\rho_{T-t})}\tfrac{w(T-t)\|e_{A}(T-t,x)\|^{2}}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\leq\tfrac{C_{u}}{T-\delta}

for some
C_{u}=\mathcal{O}(1)
and decreasing weight
t\mapsto w(t)
.

Theorem G.1
.

Under Assumption
3
, the normal/tangent-error floors in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
can be estimated by the projected posterior mean matching loss in normal/tangent direction, i.e.,

normal-error floor

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\delta}^{\perp}\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(t,X_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+C_{\delta}^{\perp}(\sqrt{d}+R)e^{-T}+d\delta^{c},

(22)

tangent-error floor

\displaystyle\lesssim C_{\delta}^{\parallel}\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{\mathbb{E}[w(t)\|e_{A}^{\parallel}(t,X_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+C_{\delta}^{\parallel}(\sqrt{d}+R)e^{-T}+d\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}.

(23)

where
C_{\delta}^{\perp}\coloneqq\delta^{c}\big(1\vee\frac{\delta^{1-c}}{w(\delta)}\big)
(
c=\lim_{t\to\delta^{+}}h_{t}\beta_{t}
) and
C_{\delta}^{\parallel}=\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)
.

Proof of Theorem
G.1
.

Applying Girsanov’s Theorem, define
\mathcal{L}_{t}\coloneqq\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\tfrac{e_{A}(T-s,X_{s}^{\leftarrow})}{h_{T-s}}\mathrm{d}B_{s}^{\leftarrow}
and according to Assumption
3
-(0), we have
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}\coloneqq\exp\big(\mathcal{L}_{t}-\frac{1}{2}[\mathcal{L}]_{t}\big)
is a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale and

\displaystyle t\mapsto B_{t}^{\leftarrow}-\sqrt{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{e_{A}(T-s,X_{s}^{\leftarrow})}{h_{T-s}}\mathrm{d}s

is a Brownina motion under
\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{T-\delta}\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{Q}=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{T-\delta}\mathbb{P}
. Therefore, we can change the path measure from
\mathbb{Q}
to
\mathbb{P}
, hence relating the inference bounds to the training error.

Recall that with
e_{A}(t,x)=m_{A}(t,x)-m(t,x)
and
e_{A}^{\perp}(t,x)=P^{\perp}_{t}(x)e_{A}(t,x)
. When
\beta_{t}=c/h_{t}
for some
c\in[1/2,1)
, the normal-error term related to training in Theorem
3.2
is

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\exp(-2\int_{u}^{T-\delta}(\beta_{T-s}-1)\mathrm{d}s)\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-u}^{2}\beta_{T-u}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{c}\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\frac{h_{T-t}^{1-c}}{w(T-t)}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{c}\big(1\vee\frac{\delta^{1-c}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t,

WLOG, assume that
\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}=0
. According to Girsanov’s theorem,

\displaystyle\quad\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{\mathbb{E}[w(T-t)\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]-\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\hat{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t.

Notice that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}-1)\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]+\mathbb{E}[(\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}-1)^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{4}]^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]+\sqrt{\chi^{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{t}|\mathbb{P}_{t})}\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{4}]^{\frac{1}{2}}

Under Assumption
3
-(1)(2), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{L})_{t}\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq(1+C_{\chi}C_{m})\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t.

Meanwhile, under Assumption
3
-(3), we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]-\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,\hat{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t\leq C_{u}\mathrm{TV}(\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d}),p_{T})\lesssim(\sqrt{d}+R)e^{-T}.

Therefore, the normal-error floor for aligning can be estimated as

\displaystyle\quad\delta^{c}\big(1\vee\frac{\delta^{1-c}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-u}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta^{c}\big(1\vee\frac{\delta^{1-c}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\perp}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+\delta^{c}\big(1\vee\frac{\delta^{1-c}}{w(\delta)}\big)(\sqrt{d}+R)e^{-T}.

Similarly, along the tangent direction, the tangent floor is of the form

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\exp\big(-\int_{u}^{T-\delta}\frac{1}{h_{T-s}}-2\mathrm{d}s\big)\frac{4}{h_{T-u}}\|(U_{T-u}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-u)\mathbb{E}[\|(U_{T-u}^{(i)})^{\intercal}e_{A}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-u}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u,

where the order estimation follows from the choice of
\beta_{t}=\Theta(1/h_{t})
. When
\tilde{Y}_{t}\in\mathcal{T}_{T-t}(\rho_{T-t})\cap\mathcal{B}^{(i)}(\theta_{T-t})
and
t\to T-\delta
, we have
U_{T-t}^{(i)}(U_{T-t}^{(i)})^{\intercal}\approx P^{\parallel}_{T-t}(m_{T-t}^{(i)})\approx P^{\parallel}_{T-t}(\tilde{Y}_{t})
. Hence we can use the following tangent error to reflect the tangent-floor of the sliding phase:

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|P^{\parallel}_{T-t}(\tilde{Y}_{t})e_{A}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{\tilde{t}_{\mathrm{in}}}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\parallel}(T-t,\tilde{Y}_{t})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t.

Then following the same change of measure discussion, the tangent-error floor for sliding can be estimated as

\displaystyle\quad\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-u)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\parallel}(T-u,\tilde{Y}_{u})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-u}^{2}}\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)\int_{0}^{T-\delta}\frac{w(T-t)\mathbb{E}[\|e_{A}^{\parallel}(T-t,X_{t}^{\leftarrow})\|^{2}]}{h_{T-t}^{2}}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{\delta}\big(1\vee\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{w(\delta)}\big)(\sqrt{d}+R)e^{-T}.

Last, the theorem follows from writing the reverse-time OU
X_{t}^{\leftarrow}
into the forward-time OU
X_{t}
and adding the contraction terms depending on
d,R
in Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
.
∎

Appendix H
Analysis of the Training Process

Recall from (
7
) that

\displaystyle A_{k+1}=A_{k}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U})+2\eta\tilde{V},

with
U_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))^{\intercal}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
V_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{z}[a_{t}X_{0}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))^{\intercal}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}

\displaystyle\tilde{U}

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))^{\intercal}]}{p}\mathrm{d}t,\quad\tilde{V}=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[a_{t}X_{0}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))^{\intercal}]}{\sqrt{p}}\mathrm{d}t.

\tilde{U}
is the RFNN kernel matrix in (
7
) with rank
r\leq p
and eigen-decomposition
\tilde{U}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{j}u_{j}u_{j}^{\intercal}
with
\{u_{j}\}_{j\in[r]}
being a set of orthonormal vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>\cdots>\lambda_{r}>0
.

We first provide initialization-dependent expressions for
A_{k}
and the equilibrium of gradient descent.

Apply the iteration recursively and we get

\displaystyle A_{k}=A_{0}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U})^{k}+2\eta\tilde{V}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U})^{j}.

If
2\eta{\|\tilde{U}\|}\leq 1
, the gradient descent is stable. As
k\to\infty
,

\displaystyle A_{k}\to A_{\infty}=A_{0}(I_{p}-\tilde{U}\tilde{U}^{+})+\tilde{V}\tilde{U}^{+},

where
\tilde{U}^{+}
is the pseudo-inverse of
\tilde{U}
.

Next, we present the error decomposition of
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}(A_{k})
into architecture-driven error and optimization-driven error.

Proposition H.1
.

Let
\{A_{k}\}
be the matrix iterates from gradient descent (
7
) with learning rate
\eta<2/\lambda_{1}
and initialization
A_{0}
, then the
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}(A_{k})=\mathrm{Err}_{arc}+\mathrm{Err}_{train}(k),\quad\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}(A_{\infty})=\mathrm{Err}_{arc},

with

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{arc}=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|m(t,X_{t}(z))-\frac{\tilde{V}\tilde{U}^{+}}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{train}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(1-2\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}\|a_{i}\|^{2},

and
a_{j}=(A_{0}-\tilde{V}\tilde{U}^{+})u_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for all
j\in[r]
.

Proof of Proposition
H.1
.

Based on the dynamics of
\{A_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, we can study the dynamics of the DMM loss
\{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A_{k})\}_{k\geq 0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t+\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad-{2\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\langle a_{t}X_{0},\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\rangle]\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=C+\mathrm{trace}(A\tilde{U}A^{\intercal})-2\mathrm{trace}(A\tilde{V}^{\intercal}),

(24)

where the constant
C=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|a_{t}X_{0}\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t
is independent to
A
. Notice that
\nabla_{A}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)=2(A\tilde{U}-\tilde{V})
. Therefore, the equilibrium satisfy
A^{*}
satisfy
A^{*}\tilde{U}=\tilde{V}
and they share the same DMM loss value
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})
. Next, we study the decay of DMM loss along gradient descent by tracking
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A_{k})-\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})
. Define
\Delta A_{k}=A_{k}-A^{*}
, according to (
24
), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A_{k})

\displaystyle=C+\mathrm{trace}\big((A^{*}+\Delta A_{k})\tilde{U}(A^{*}+\Delta A_{k})^{\intercal}\big)-2\mathrm{trace}\big((A^{*}+\Delta A_{k})\tilde{V}^{\intercal}\big)

\displaystyle=C+\mathrm{trace}(A^{*}\tilde{U}(A^{*})^{\intercal})-2\mathrm{trace}(A^{*}\tilde{V}^{\intercal})+\mathrm{trace}(\Delta A_{k}\tilde{U}\Delta A_{k}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle\quad+2\big(\bcancel{\mathrm{trace}(\Delta A_{k}\tilde{U}(A^{*})^{\intercal})-\mathrm{trace}(\Delta A_{k}\tilde{V}^{\intercal})}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})+\mathrm{trace}(\Delta A_{k}\tilde{U}\Delta A_{k}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})+\|\Delta A_{k}\tilde{U}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{F}^{2},

where the last part in the second equation cancel due to the property of the equilibrium
A^{*}
. Meanwhile, according to the iteration formula, we can easily get that

\displaystyle\Delta A_{k+1}

\displaystyle=A_{k+1}-{A^{*}}=\Delta A_{k}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U})+2\eta({A^{*}\tilde{U}}+\tilde{V})=\Delta A_{k}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U}),

where the last identity follows from the property of the equilibrium
A^{*}
. Combined with our equation of

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A_{k})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})+\|\Delta A_{k}\tilde{U}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{F}^{2}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})+\|\Delta A_{0}(I_{p}-2\eta\tilde{U})^{k}\tilde{U}^{\frac{1}{2}}\|_{F}^{2}.

Therefore, based on the spectral information of
\tilde{U}
and
A_{0}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A_{k})=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A^{*})+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}(1-2\eta\lambda_{i})^{2k}\|a_{i}\|^{2}.

(25)

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MM}}(A)=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-m(t,X_{t}(z))+\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DMM}}(A)

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-m(t,X_{t}(z))+\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+m(t,X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad+\bcancel{2\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\langle m(t,X_{t}(z))-a_{t}X_{0},\frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\sigma_{t}(X_{t}(z))\rangle]\mathrm{d}t}

and the tower property. Therefore, we can derive a decomposition of

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\delta}^{T}\frac{w(t)}{h_{t}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[\|-a_{t}X_{0}+m(t,X_{t}(z))\|^{2}]\mathrm{d}t

(27)

where the second last property follows from the tower property and the last identity follows from the property of
A^{*}
and the tower property. Therefore, Equation (

(1)

I.4
Derivation of the Newton Distance Estimator

First notice that the condition
E(x)E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)=0
in our definition is equivalent to
E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)=0
, as the columns of
E(x)
are orthonormal. Since
E(x)
has full column rank, the projection of the gradient vanishes if and only if its coordinates in the normal basis are all zero. Then we can only consider
F(x)=E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)=0
.

To measure the distance from a point
x
to
\mathcal{R}_{d^{*}}
, we perform a first-order Taylor expansion of the condition
F(x)=E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)=0
within the normal space
\text{span}\{E(x)\}
. By neglecting high-order variations of the eigenvector field
E(x)
, we obtain:

0=F(x+\delta)\approx F(x)+\left[E(x)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\log p(x)\right]\delta

Since the distance vector
\delta
lies in the normal space, we set
\delta=E(x)\eta
. Substituting the spectral decomposition
\nabla^{2}\log p(x)E(x)=E(x)\Lambda_{\perp}(x)
, the equation becomes:

0\approx E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)+E(x)^{\top}E(x)\Lambda_{\perp}(x)\eta

Given
E(x)^{\top}E(x)=I
, we solve for
\eta
:

\eta=-\Lambda_{\perp}(x)^{-1}E(x)^{\top}\nabla\log p(x)

The distance estimator
\hat{d}_{Newton}(x)
is the magnitude of this vector,
\|\eta\|_{2}
.
