Timing and prognosis of brain metastasis development in breast cancer by subtype
Authors: Narmeen S Rashid, Nayan Lamba, Ivy Ricca, Marciana Johnson, Paul J Catalano, Shyam K Tanguturi, Rifaquat Rahman, Daphne A Haas-Kogan, Patrick Y Wen, Sarah L Sammons, Nancy U Lin, Ayal A Aizer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited data on the timing of brain metastases development and subsequent prognosis by breast cancer subtype exist. We retrospectively evaluated a cohort of 671 patients diagnosed with brain metastases secondary to breast cancer. Patients were categorized by subtype: human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive (HER2+), hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−), and triple negative (TN). We found significant heterogeneity in the timing of brain metastases development and subsequent prognosis by subtype. Patients with HR+/HER2− disease developed brain metastases later in the disease course compared to patients with HER2+ and TN disease. Median overall survival (OS) following the diagnosis of brain metastases was shortest in patients with TN disease; however, among all subtypes, OS decreased with an increasing number of systemic therapies received before the diagnosis of brain metastases. Altogether, these findings may inform differential timing of brain-directed screening imaging in patients with breast cancer.

Brain metastases cause significant morbidity and mortality to patients with metastatic breast cancer.1 Patients with intracranial disease may experience neurologic symptoms including headaches, cognitive difficulties, seizures, focal deficits, and eventually, neurologic deterioration and death.2 Although breast cancer is the second most common primary cancer resulting in brain metastases, there are conflicting consensus guidelines regarding routine, imaging-based screening or surveillance of the brain in the absence of neurologic symptoms.3,4 Of note, the 2025 NCCN Guidelines recommend brain magnetic resonance imaging screening only “if suspicious (central nervous system) symptoms” or “as clinically indicated.”4,5

However, early identification of asymptomatic brain metastases has multiple potential advantages. First, the absence of neurologic symptoms at brain metastases diagnosis is associated with longer overall survival (OS).6 In addition, screening allows for the identification of smaller and fewer lesions.7 This may allow for the use of less aggressive or invasive measures such as systemic therapy alone or stereotactic radiosurgery, as opposed to whole brain radiation or neurosurgical resection. Also, studies suggest that radiotherapeutic treatment of smaller versus larger brain metastases is associated with decreased local recurrence and adverse radiation effects such as radiation necrosis.8,9 Importantly, patients are also highly interested in brain screening imaging. In a recent international cross-sectional online survey study, 85% of patients with breast cancer expressed interest in undergoing brain screening imaging even if the clinical benefit is uncertain.10

However, there are limited robust data on the timing and nature of extracranial disease at brain metastases diagnosis by subtype. We sought to describe the timing of brain metastases development in relation to extracranial disease status and number of prior systemic therapies used for management of metastatic disease, as well as subsequent OS, to identify when patients are at higher risk of intracranial involvement and provide perspective on times when a lower threshold to consider imaging may be of value.

We identified a cohort of 679 consecutive patients with newly diagnosed brain metastases, including newly diagnosed leptomeningeal disease, secondary to breast cancer diagnosed at Brigham and Women’s Hospital/Dana-Farber Cancer Institute between 2008 and 2022. Eight patients were excluded due to missing estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, or human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) receptor status, leaving 671 patients in the final cohort. Patients were categorized based on breast cancer subtype: HER2+ regardless of hormone receptor status (N = 213), hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) (N = 278), and triple negative (TN) (N = 180); the latter 2 subtypes are referred to as HER2− in this article. Intracranial pathology was used for molecular subtyping when available; otherwise, the tissue biopsy considered most representative of intracranial disease was used for subtype classification. For example, biopsies from metastatic sites were prioritized over biopsies from primary tumors. When multiple specimens were available, biopsies acquired closest to the time of intracranial disease development were favored. Extracranial disease status (progressive versus not) was determined via review of the most recent imaging of the extracranial space at the time of brain metastasis diagnosis. Baseline continuous and categorical covariates were compared with the Kruskal–Wallis test and chi-square test, respectively. The OS following the diagnosis of brain metastases was assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves, log-rank tests, and univariable Cox regression. Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS v9.4. The study was approved by the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute/Harvard Cancer Center Institutional Review Board; written informed consent was waived.

Initial intracranial presentation varied significantly between breast cancer subtypes (Table 1). The percentage of patients with central nervous system (CNS)-only metastatic disease was 16%, 9%, and 13% for HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN disease, respectively. Median OS following the diagnosis of metastatic disease was 61.6, 40.0, and 19.3 months for patients with HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN disease, respectively. The median maximal unidimensional size of an intracranial lesion at diagnosis of brain metastases was 21, 16, and 20 mm for HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN lesions, respectively. Patients with HER2+ disease tended to have fewer brain metastases at initial CNS presentation compared to patients with HER2− disease (median 2 and 5 brain metastases, respectively; P < .001). Patients with HER2+ breast cancer were also less likely to have progressive extracranial disease at the time of brain metastases diagnosis compared to patients with HER2− breast cancer (47% and 63% of patients, respectively; P < .001).

On average, patients with TN disease presented with brain metastases at the time of or within the first year after initial diagnosis of extracranial metastatic disease (56%, 100/180) and/or with receipt of 0-2 systemic therapies for metastatic disease (79%, 142/180). Patients with HR+/HER2− disease more commonly presented with brain metastases as a late event, >1 year after initial diagnosis of extracranial metastatic disease (65%, 180/278) and/or after ≥3 lines of systemic therapy for metastatic disease (53%, 146/278). Sites of synchronous extracranial disease at diagnosis of brain metastases varied by subtype. The most common site of synchronous metastatic disease was bone in HER2+ and HR+/HER2− disease, with 59% (126/213) and 74% (207/278) of patients having osseous metastatic involvement at diagnosis of brain metastases, respectively. The lungs were the most common site of synchronous metastatic disease in patients with TN disease, with 59% of patients (107/180) having pulmonary metastases at initial intracranial involvement. Visceral involvement, defined as adrenal, liver, lung, or soft tissue disease at the time of brain metastases diagnosis, was present in 64%, 74%, and 76% of patients with HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN disease, respectively. Brain metastases secondary to HER2+ disease were more likely to arise in the absence of visceral metastases compared with HER2− disease (P = .003).

Median OS following the diagnosis of brain metastases was 31.8, 10.6, and 8.4 months for HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN disease, respectively (Figure 1A). Among all subtypes, OS following the diagnosis of brain metastases varied significantly depending on the number of prior systemic therapy regimens for metastatic disease (0, 1-2, or ≥3; P < .001 for all subtypes) (Figure 1B-D). However, the magnitude of this effect varied numerically by breast cancer subtype. Among patients with HER2+ disease, median OS following the diagnosis of brain metastases was 42.2, 34.4, and 18.0 months for patients with 0, 1-2, or ≥3 prior systemic therapies, respectively. Respective median OS estimates were 23.3, 10.7, and 7.4 months for patients with HR+/HER2− disease and 16.0, 9.1, and 2.6 months for patients with TN disease.

Previous studies have described the relative propensity of HER2+, HR+/HER2−, and TN breast cancers to metastasize to the brain.11,12 These studies served as the foundation of contemporary prospective trials that assess screening magnetic resonance imaging of the brain in patients with breast cancer. In the current study, we report the timing, extracranial metastatic disease status, and prognosis at the time of brain metastases diagnosis by breast cancer subtype to provide insights into when patients are at greatest risk of intracranial dissemination.

Similar to previous studies, we observed that brain metastases in the absence of extracranial metastatic disease are uncommon in patients with breast cancer.11 Prior studies have demonstrated a greater interval from breast cancer diagnosis to initial intracranial presentation in patients with luminal versus non-luminal subtypes.13 Similarly, we found that patients with HR+/HER2− disease tended to develop brain metastases later in the disease course while patients with TN disease tended to develop brain metastases earlier in the disease course.

Together, these findings suggest that brain-directed imaging in TN disease may provide the greatest clinical benefit when performed early in the disease course. Identification of brain metastases might provide less actionable clinical benefit in patients with advanced TN breast cancer who have progressed on multiple prior lines of systemic therapy. Ongoing imaging following the diagnosis of extracranial metastatic disease may be needed to alter clinical outcomes in patients with HER2+ disease, who tend to have a more favorable prognosis but a sustained risk of developing brain metastases. In contrast, brain imaging in patients with HR+/HER2− breast cancer may be most impactful when obtained later in the disease course, given the generally more delayed intracranial presentation in this patient population.

Our study must be considered with several limitations. In particular, all patients in our cohort harbored brain metastases, so the incidence proportions could not be tabulated. This was also a retrospective, single-institution study. Despite efforts to most accurately characterize the subtype of intracranial disease, the specimen used for subtype classification may not have been representative of intracranial disease in all cases given the receptor discordance observed between primary and metastatic breast cancer in a minority of patients.14 In addition, complete data on the systemic therapies that our patients received were not available, precluding analyses correlating specific regimens with the identification of brain metastases. Ultimately, the clinical benefit conferred by screening for brain metastases in patients with breast cancer should be further evaluated in the context of prospective clinical trials.

In conclusion, we observed significant heterogeneity in the timing of intracranial involvement and subsequent prognosis by breast cancer subtype. These data could provide patients and clinicians with increased awareness of when neurologic symptomatology may develop or when such symptomatology carries the greatest risk of representing underlying brain metastases. In addition, should screening studies prove positive and change underlying guidelines, these data could inform the optimal time for screening imaging of the brain.
