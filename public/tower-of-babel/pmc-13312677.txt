Domain-general behavioral gains and neural correlates of cognitive training in healthy populations: a neuroimaging meta-analysis
Authors: Geng Li, Chengzhen Liu, Yang Liu, Yihan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preventive strategies to promote cognitive health are increasingly prioritized, yet the extent to which cognitive training yields domain-general behavioral gains and neural correlates remains unclear. Here we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 67 neuroimaging studies to quantify the domain-general effects of cognitive training on cognitive performance and task-related activation changes, and to test associations between behavioral gains and neural adaptations. Across training domains, task paradigms and age groups, cognitive training significantly improved cognitive task performance (Hedges’ g = 0.546, 95% CI = 0.414–0.678; t = 8.149; p < 0.0001), accompanied by increased activation in the right superior parietal lobule (R.SPL) and decreased activation in the bilateral anterior cingulate cortex (ACC). Regression analyses further indicated that cognitive improvements were closely associated with these activation changes, with training completion rate emerging as a key moderator. Collectively, these findings identify candidate domain-general neural signatures of cognitive training–induced plasticity and provide an initial empirical basis for the evidence-informed design and optimization of cognitive training interventions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12993-026-00338-w.

Introduction

Cognitive function underpins independent living [1], quality of life [2], and mental well-being [3]. Dementia, the most severe manifestation of cognitive decline, represents a major and growing public health challenge: an estimated 57 million people are currently living with dementia worldwide, and this figure is projected to reach 153 million by 2050, with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) accounting for approximately 115 million cases [4]. Although several disease-modifying therapies have shown preliminary efficacy in slowing early disease progression [5], their overall clinical benefits remain modest, and safety concerns have yet to be fully resolved [6]. Taken together, these challenges underscore the need to move upstream—prioritizing earlier preventive and health-promoting strategies to maintain cognitive function across the lifespan.

Within this preventive framework, cognitive training has attracted increasing interest as a scalable, behavioral, non-pharmacological approach that may support cognitive performance in healthy populations [7–9]. Neuroimaging evidence indicates that training can recruit plasticity-related processes and alter task-related functional activation [10, 11]. Yet existing studies differ markedly in training parameters, participant characteristics, and the cognitive tasks used to quantify outcomes [12–15]. A key open question is whether cognitive training produces a domain-general effect across variations in training parameters and assessment tasks, and which task-related activation changes most consistently covary with behavioral improvement.

Meta-analyses are essential for consolidating evidence and isolating reliable effects [16]. Although prior syntheses have examined cognitive-training effects on behavioral outcomes [17] and neuroimaging measures [18], several gaps remain. First, coordinate-based activation likelihood estimation (ALE) meta-analyses have typically evaluated activation increases and decreases separately [18], limiting the ability to detect concurrent, bidirectional changes. Second, heterogeneity in participant characteristics, training parameters and assessment tasks have not been systematically interrogated, complicating mechanistic interpretation and generalizability [18]. Third, brain–behavior coupling has rarely been quantified, leaving it unclear which task-related activation changes consistently track behavioral gains.

The present meta-analysis addresses these gaps by pursuing three objectives. First, we quantify the extent to which cognitive training improves cognitive task performance in healthy participants and characterize convergent training-related increases and decreases in task-related brain activation. Second, we test whether training-induced behavioral gains covary with changes in task-related activation. Third, we evaluate potential moderators of training effects. Together, these analyses delineate candidate neural signatures of cognitive training–related plasticity and provide an empirical basis for optimizing prevention-oriented intervention design across the lifespan.

Methods

Following established best-practice guidelines for neuroimaging meta-analyses [19], we conducted meta-analysis of task-related brain activation changes induced by cognitive training. The search and study selection procedures adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework, with detailed implementation provided in Supplementary Table S1. The study protocol was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024599205).

Information sources, search strategy, and study selection process

Searches were conducted in five electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, APA PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and Embase) from database inception to January 2026, with no restrictions on publication year. A comprehensive strategy incorporating medical subject headings and synonyms related to neuroimaging and cognitive training was applied (see Supplementary Table S2). Reference lists of eligible articles were also manually screened to identify additional studies.

Duplicates were removed using EndNote 21. Titles and abstracts were then screened against predefined eligibility criteria, followed by full-text review for final inclusion. To ensure reliability, two authors (GL and YL) independently performed all screening steps; disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, consultation with a senior author (YZ).

Eligibility criteria

Neuroimaging studies were eligible if they (1) reported stereotactic coordinates (Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) or Talairach space) for training-related changes in task-related brain activation following cognitive training, defined as structured practice of a cognitive task or task set with the explicit aim of improving targeted cognitive processes; (2) performed whole-brain analyses; (3) employed either a within-subject pre–post design (including single-group designs) or a between-group design with an active (e.g., mental leisure activities, educational programs, or low-challenge sham training) or passive (e.g., waitlist or no-contact) control condition; and (4) recruited healthy participants, defined as cognitively normal individuals without a clinical diagnosis of mild cognitive impairment or dementia and without a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders.

Study selection

To identify eligible studies, we conducted a systematic search across PubMed, Web of Science, APA PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and Embase using keywords related to neuroimaging and cognitive training. The initial search yielded 6,164 records. After removing 3,293 duplicates, 2,871 unique records remained. Title and abstract screening excluded 2,784 records, leaving 87 articles for full-text review. Of these, 67 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final neuroimaging meta-analysis (Fig. 1). Among the included studies, 60 reported sufficient behavioral outcome data and were therefore included in the behavioral meta-analysis.

Data extraction and summary of outcomes

Two authors (GL and YL) independently screened all search results for eligibility and extracted data using standardized forms. Extracted variables included participant characteristics, detailed descriptions of cognitive training protocols, control conditions, and study design features. For the behavioral meta-analysis, we prioritized extracting change scores (post–pre), their variability, and sample sizes; when unavailable, we used pre- and post-training means and standard deviations to derive effect sizes. For neuroimaging meta-analysis, we collected peak activation coordinates along with associated statistical indices (e.g., t values, Z scores, and P values). In studies involving multiple tasks, populations, or training groups, peak coordinates were extracted separately for each condition [20].

Meta-analysis

Cognitive improvement induced by cognitive training were synthesized using multivariate meta-analysis. Unlike univariate models, multivariate meta-analysis allows the inclusion of multiple effect sizes from the same study, accounting for intercorrelations among outcomes and producing more precise estimates [21]. Task-related brain activation changes were examined with the anisotropic effect size algorithm in seed-based d mapping (AES-SDM, hereafter referred to as SDM) for our analysis. Compared with ALE meta-analysis used by prior study [18], this technique integrates both increases and decreases in brain activation into a single statistical map, thereby avoiding duplication of activity data and allowing the inclusion of studies that did not report significant activation differences [20]. All results were thresholded at voxel level at an uncorrected p < 0.0001, consistent with prior meta-analytic practice and methodological studies [22–25].

Multivariate meta-analysis

This multivariate meta-analysis was conducted in RStudio (version 1.4.1106) using the metafor and clubSandwich packages. We adopted a three-level hierarchical structure: individual outcomes (Level 1), samples (Level 2), and studies (Level 3), with random effects specified at all levels to model nested dependencies. The within-study correlation among cognitive outcomes was fixed at 0.5, consistent with the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [26]. Effect sizes were calculated as standardized mean differences (SMDs) using Hedges’ g, which reduces small-sample bias and accounts for heterogeneity related to study paradigms, participant characteristics, and training protocols [27]. Estimates were derived from either pre–post comparisons or change scores and standardized by the pooled within-group standard deviation [28]. For outcomes such as reaction time or error rates, values were reverse-coded where necessary so that positive effects uniformly indicated cognitive improvement [29]. Following established practice [30], effect sizes were aggregated across both global cognition and specific cognitive subdomains to evaluate the overall impact of cognitive training. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (≤ 0.2), medium (0.2–0.8), or large (≥ 0.8) [31].

SDM meta-analysis

This study employed SDM analysis (version 5.15) to identify task-related activation changes induced by cognitive training [32]. Following established SDM guidelines [25], additional procedures were applied when statistical values or thresholds were unavailable. If coordinates were reported without test statistics, thresholds were inferred from estimated effect sizes; conversely, if only thresholds were provided, effect sizes were derived from those data. Reported Z or P values were converted into t statistics using the SDM online converter, with stereotactic space and statistical values automatically standardized by the software. Preprocessing followed SDM recommendations, using a 20-mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) isotropic Gaussian kernel and 500 randomizations. For studies reporting multiple contrasts, the “combine image” function was applied to generate composite maps reflecting the mean effect size and variance across contrasts [33, 34].

Brain–behavior association analysis

To examine the relationship between training-induced behavioral improvements and task-related brain activation changes, a regression analysis was performed using cognitive performance improvement (Hedges’ g) as the behavioral predictor. To ensure robustness and minimize false positives [25], mean effect-size values were extracted from the significant clusters identified in the overall SDM meta-analysis and regressed against the Hedges’ g to assess the association between cognitive gains and task-related brain activation changes [20].

Moderator analysis

To investigate potential factors influencing task-related brain activation associated with cognitive training, two complementary analytical approaches were employed. First, comparative analyses were conducted to examine binary moderators related to capturing training characteristics, study design features, participant characteristics, and outcome task features, including training domain (multi-domain vs. single-domain training), training paradigm (process-based vs. strategy-based training), training task type (working-memory vs. non–working-memory training), delivery mode (group-based vs. individual-based training), difficulty regulation (adaptive vs. non-adaptive training), age group (18–50 vs. <18 or > 50 years), design type (between-subjects vs. within-subjects), control condition (active vs. passive control) and assessment task type (working-memory vs. non–working-memory outcomes).

Second, regression analyses were performed to explore continuous moderators, including mean participant age, total training duration (minutes), sex (% female participants), and training completion rate (% of participants who completed the intervention). To minimize false positives, only clusters that remained significant in the overall SDM meta-analysis and demonstrated consistent effects across the extreme values of each moderator were retained [25]. For statistically significant regression results, influential observations were further excluded and the regression models were re-estimated to verify that the observed associations were not driven by single high-leverage studies. To facilitate visual inspection of potential moderating effects, scatter plots were additionally color-coded by training paradigm (process-based vs. strategy-based) and difficulty regulation.

Robustness analyses

The robustness of the SDM meta-analytic results was assessed using a jackknife sensitivity analysis, whereas the robustness of the multivariate meta-analytic findings was evaluated by re-estimating the models under alternative assumptions of within-study correlation (r = 0.3, 0.7, 1), in accordance with recent methodological recommendations [35]. Given the potential for small-sample effects in neuroimaging meta-analyses, studies with fewer than 10 participants were additionally excluded, and the overall SDM meta-analysis was repeated to evaluate the robustness of the main findings.

Quality assessment

To ensure the rigor of the systematic review, we evaluated the reporting quality of included neuroimaging studies using a 17-item checklist derived from prior research [36]. The checklist covered essential domains, including participant characteristics, study design, data acquisition, preprocessing, statistical analysis, and conclusion reporting. The full criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S3, and the quality assessment results are summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Results

Study characteristics

Across the 67 studies included in this meta-analysis [13–15, 37–100], a total of 2,410 participants (mean age = 33.62 years, range: 7.8–72.1) were analyzed. On average, cognitive training programs consisted of 19.68 sessions (range: 1–360), amounting to a total mean training duration of 902.03 min (range: 3–21,600), delivered at a frequency of 4.39 sessions per week (range: 1–8), with each session lasting approximately 42.33 min (range: 3–240). Detailed study characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Meta-analysis

Overall analysis

A multivariate meta-analysis of 60 studies demonstrated that cognitive training significantly improved cognitive task performance (Hedges’ g = 0.546, 95% CI = 0.414–0.678; t = 8.149; p < 0.0001). Furthermore, an overall SDM meta-analysis of 67 studies identified two significant clusters of task-related brain activation changes following cognitive training (Table 1; Fig. 2). These clusters showed increased activation primarily in the right superior parietal lobule (R.SPL), whereas one cluster exhibited significantly reduced activation in the bilateral anterior cingulate corte (ACC).

Brain–behavior associations and moderator analyses

Regression analyses revealed that improvements in cognitive task performance were significantly associated with training-induced changes in task-related activation in both the R.SPL (r = 0.233, p = 0.004) and the ACC (r = − 0.385, p < 0.001; Fig. 3a and c). Moreover, only training completion rate significantly moderated training-induced changes in task-related activation, both in the R.SPL (r = 0.214, p = 0.027) and the ACC (r = − 0.255, p = 0.009; Fig. 3b and d). To mitigate potential leverage effects, influential observations were excluded and the regression models were re-estimated; the findings were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S1). By contrast, no significant moderator effects were observed for total training duration or sex on task-related activation changes or cognitive improvement (Supplementary Table S5). Meanwhile, the comparative analyses did not identify any significant differences in task-related activation changes or cognitive improvement across training domain, training paradigm, assessment task type, delivery mode, difficulty regulation, age group, design type, or control condition (Supplementary Figs. S2–S3; Tables S6–S7).

Robustness analysis results

As shown in Supplementary Table S8, jackknife sensitivity analysis confirmed the high replicability of cognitive training–induced task-related brain activation changes, with results remaining consistent across all study combinations. Likewise, Supplementary Table S9 demonstrated the robustness of cognitive training–related improvements in cognitive performance, with findings stable across all assumed within-study correlation values. In addition, potential publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test and visual inspection of funnel plots (Supplementary Figs S4–S6). Egger’s test was not significant for R.SPL (β = 0.06, t = 0.39, p = 0.698) or for the behavior outcomes (β = 0.76, t = 1.64, p = 0.102), but indicated asymmetry for ACC (β = 0.85, t = 4.20, p < 0.001). After excluding studies with fewer than 10 participants, the overall results remained stable (Supplementary Table S10).

Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 67 neuroimaging studies to examine the domain-general effects of cognitive training on cognitive performance and task-related activation changes. Across diverse training domains, task paradigms, and age groups, cognitive training improved cognitive performance and elicited convergent modulation of task-related activity in the R.SPL and the ACC. Notably, training-related activation changes in the R.SPL and ACC were significantly associated with cognitive gains. Collectively, these findings identify candidate neural signatures of cognitive training–induced plasticity and provide an initial empirical basis for the development and optimization of evidence-based cognitive training programs.

This study demonstrates that cognitive training not only yields moderate improvements in task performance but is also accompanied by increased activation in the R.SPL and decreased activation in the ACC. While these behavioral improvements are consistent with previous findings [17], the present results further elucidate their underlying neural mechanisms. Specifically, greater R.SPL activation was associated with larger cognitive gains, whereas lower ACC activation was likewise associated with greater improvement. As a key hub of the frontoparietal attention network, increased R.SPL activation may reflect training-related enhancement of attentional control and cognitive resource allocation [101–103]. In contrast, reduced ACC activation may indicate suppression of task-irrelevant monitoring or introspective processing, thereby freeing resources for task execution [104, 105]. Together, these findings suggest that cognitive training improves performance through a dynamic optimization of neural resources—strengthening task-relevant activation while suppressing task-irrelevant activity to promote more efficient cross-network coordination.

This study did not detect significant moderating effects of training parameters, assessment task type, study design, or participant characteristics on training-induced neural changes, suggesting that cognitive training may elicit broadly consistent patterns of neural adaptation across diverse intervention formats, task contexts, and populations. By contrast, training completion rate emerged as a key moderator: studies with higher completion rates reported more pronounced task-related activation changes. Consistent with prior work [106, 107], these findings indicate that variability in training efficacy is less strongly driven by specific protocol features or age differences than by the extent of sustained engagement. On the one hand, continued participation is likely to increase repeated recruitment of task-relevant neural circuits, facilitating the consolidation and stabilization of functional adaptations and, consequently, stronger brain–behavior coupling [108]. On the other hand, higher completion rates may also index more stable samples and stricter study implementation, and may partly reflect selective retention, motivational differences, or attrition-related bias, all of which could influence effect estimates. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of prioritizing participant completion, alongside optimization of training content and delivery, and of strengthening the measurement and reporting of engagement quality and potential sources of bias.

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, we used a coordinate-based meta-analysis of study-level summary data; inference therefore relied on the spatial convergence of reported peak coordinates across studies, which may have limited power for moderator analyses. When relatively few effect sizes are available and effects are spatially diffuse, explicit multiple-comparisons correction can be overly conservative and further reduce sensitivity. Accordingly, consistent with common practice in the field [24, 109], we applied a stringent uncorrected voxel-wise threshold combined with whole-brain randomization-based inference to prioritize specificity and limit false positives; nevertheless, subtle but potentially meaningful moderator effects may have gone undetected. Future work should incorporate larger and more diverse samples and integrate individual-level imaging data to improve power, enable stricter multiplicity control and more systematically interrogate moderators. Second, the present meta-analysis was designed to characterize the domain-general neural signature of cognitive training. Although moderator analyses did not identify robust effects of training domain, assessment task type or age, the uneven distribution of studies across categories precluded formal tests of domain-specific effects. Future meta-analyses should therefore apply stratified designs to resolve training- and task-specific neural adaptations. Finally, funnel-plot asymmetry for ACC-related effects suggests potential publication bias. Study-level regression results may also be sensitive to high-leverage observations and non-linear relationships. Although multiple robustness and sensitivity analyses yielded consistent conclusions, future work should prioritize larger, well-powered datasets and implement influence diagnostics and non-linear modelling to further strengthen the stability and generalizability of these findings.
