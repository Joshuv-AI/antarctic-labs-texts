Communicator and collaborator – a Finnish national repeated cross-sectional survey of the two CanMEDS competencies by specialty group
Authors: Heli Halava, Piitu Parmanne, Minna Kaila, Teppo Heikkilä, Leila Niemi-Murola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe aim of this study was to find out if experience of two acquired competencies, communication skills and collaboration skills, differ by specialty group or graduation year in post-graduate medical training in Finland.MethodsA repeated cross-sectional survey conducted in 2018 and 2023. For this study a subsample of physicians who completed their specialist training after 2007 (n = 2 772) was extracted. Respondents were categorised into three graduation cohorts: 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2023 and asked to assess the perceived adequacy of teaching and guidance during specialization across two CanMEDS competency domains. Differences were examined in five specialty categories: operative specialties, conservative specialties, diagnostic specialties, psychiatric specialties, and general practice along with occupational health.ResultsOn average, 72% of respondents reported adequate teaching and supervision related to the Communicator role during specialist training. The cohort specialized in 2008–2012 reported insufficient teaching and supervision (OR 1.48; 95% CI 1.20 − 1.82) more frequently than those who completed their specialist training in 2013–2017 (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.83 − 1.21) across all communicator subdomains. Specialists in operative fields (OR 1.64; 95% CI 1.34-2.00) most frequently reported insufficient teaching and supervision within this role. Correspondingly, related to Collaborator role, 62% of respondents reported adequate teaching and supervision. Compared with the 2008–2012 cohort, the proportion reporting insufficient teaching and supervision in team leadership, consultation, and teamwork skills was significantly lower in the 2013–2017 cohort. Related to this role, child and adolescent psychiatrists less frequently experienced deficiencies in communication skills than adult psychiatrists.ConclusionsThe majority of the respondents were satisfied with the teaching they received during specialist training related to The CanMEDS competency domains Communicator and Collaborator. These skills play an important role in both the development of education and the quality of care across all specialties. However, it seems that within operative specialties the teaching of communication skills and within psychiatry the teaching of consultation skills could be increased.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09629-2.

Background

In many countries during this millennium, specialist training has been transformed from time-based to competence-based [1–3]. In Finland, a new statute regulating postgraduate medical education was launched in 2020, and this was the official starting point of the competency-based postgraduate education reform [4]. The reform was based on the European Union of Medical Specialists (UEMS) recommendations, and the CanMEDS competency framework developed in Canada [5] is used as the foundation in the European Training Requirements [6]. This framework has been used as a basis for continuous professional development in our country [7]. Postgraduate education is offered in 50 medical specialties and only 1% of young Finnish doctors do not intend to specialize [8].

Previously, learning outcomes were described at an abstract, high level without tools to monitor or assess achievement in practice. After the reform, skills should be systematically and repeatedly assessed through specialization training. Competency is multi-dimensional and changes with time, experience, and setting. Within the CanMEDS framework, physician competency is divided into seven areas (medical expertise, communication, collaboration, management, health advocacy, scholar, professionalism), all of which should be included in the curriculum with adequate and structured training and programmatic assessment. The relevance of various competencies varies in different specialties [9,10]. Specialties working in the operating room environment often share a precise and concise communication style, which differs from the more reflective discussion style commonly seen in conservative specialties. Diagnostic specialties, in turn, often communicate with other professionals mainly through written statements and reports. However, communication and collaboration skills are essential in every medical specialty and often integrated into clinical tasks.

Doctors with better communication and collaboration skills are able to detect problems earlier and provide better support to their patients [11,12]. Communicator role describes abilities related to a physician-patient encounter: physicians form relationships with patients and their families that facilitate the gathering and sharing of information for effective health care [13]. This may lead to better satisfaction, lower costs of care, greater patient understanding, and better adherence to the treatment process [14]. As for Collaborators, physicians work effectively with other health care professionals to provide safe, higher-quality outcomes, and patient-centred care [13].

Communicator and Collaborator roles may previously have been under-represented in learning outcomes. The role of these competencies in coping and well-being at work have been studied less than knowledge-based skills and so-called hard skills. We chose these two competencies, which, regardless of specialty, play an important role in the development of education, the quality of care, and well-being at work. Also, the relatively broad classification of specialty groups adopted in this study is sufficient for the present purposes.

Methods

The aim of this study was to find out if the perceived adequacy of teaching and supervision in specialist training across the CanMEDS competency domains Communicator and Collaborator differ by specialty group or graduation year. The main aim was to examine physicians as a unified professional group. For this reason, we did not conduct a highly detailed analysis of individual specialty groups. However, as substantial differences may exist between medical specialties, some degree of specialty-specific comparison is warranted. Additionally, we present means with confidence intervals for the ten largest specialty areas.

The present study is based on a repeated cross-sectional national survey conducted in 2018 and 2023 [8]. These two surveys are most recent part of a long-standing research program on Finnish medical education [15]. Surveys were initiated in 1988, to examine the relationship between medical training and working life at five-year intervals. These surveys compile information on social background, working conditions and career plans including doctors’ views of undergraduate and specialist training.

In both 2018 and 2023, the target population consisted of physicians aged under 70 and residing in Finland. Participants were randomly selected from the Finnish Medical Association’s registry, which covers over 90% of all physicians in Finland. Data were collected through postal and online questionnaires (Supplementary file 1). In total, 10 072 responses were obtained. The representativeness of the responses to the 2018 and 2023 surveys was assessed according to gender, age, and specialization status. Men, physicians aged 35–45 years, and non-specialized physicians were slightly underrepresented in the data. The data were not weighted. For the present study, a subsample of physicians aged under 65 years who completed their specialist qualification in Finland after 2007 (n = 2 772) was extracted (Table 1).

Since 2018, the survey has included items assessing the adequacy of teaching and supervision across the competency domains. These domains describe skills required for all physicians, regardless of specialty and were defined according to the seven categories of the CanMEDS framework (medical expert, communicator, collaborator, leader, health advocate, scholar and professional) [5]. This study examines how Finnish physicians perceive the teaching for Communicator and Collaborator domains and how these competencies differ between gender, medical specialties and graduation year on national-level. It was not feasible to compare these two competencies between individual hospitals or specific clinical environments.

Respondents were categorised into three graduation cohorts: 2008–2012, 2013–2017, and 2018–2023. Background variables included gender, age, and specialty. Finland has 50 medical specialties, which were grouped into five specialty groups: operative, conservative, diagnostic, psychiatric, and general practice along with occupational health (Table 2). We selected this classification because it had already been used in a previous Finnish study [16]. There are also other studies that have grouped specialties for analytical purposes [17,18].

The primary outcome measure was the perceived adequacy of teaching and supervision in specialist training across the CanMEDS competency items within the Communicator and Collaborator roles. The Communicator competency was assessed using four items and the Collaborator competency using five items (Table 3). Respondents were asked to assess the amount of teaching and guidance during specialist medical training on a five-step scale with the question: How much instruction and guidance on the following topics did you receive during your specialist education? (1 = far too little, 2 = too little, 3 = about the right amount, 4 = too much, 5 = far too much). Data were missing for only 18 − 26 respondents (at most 0,9% of participants) (Table 3).

We first examined the data using descriptive statistics. Group differences were tested using the chi-square test. Internal consistency of the Communicator and the Collaborator competency domains was assessed using correlation coefficients. Mean differences between subdomains by specialty group, focusing on the ten most highly represented specialties as mapped to the World Health Organization (WHO) international classification [19], were examined and 95% confidence intervals for means were calculated. Ordinal variables were compared across specialty groups with the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, and p-values were Bonferroni-adjusted. The proportions of respondents reporting insufficient teaching and guidance were calculated by specialty group and gender, and corresponding Wald 95% confidence intervals were obtained.

As a supplementary analysis, factors associated with the cumulative number of subdomains judged insufficient for teaching and supervision within the CanMEDS Communicator and Collaborator domains were examined (Figure 1). An ordinal outcome variable was constructed to indicate the number of subdomains in which respondents reported insufficient teaching and guidance, ranging from zero (adequate in all subdomains) to the maximum observed value (insufficient in all subdomains). Associations between this ordinal outcome and specialty group, gender, age, and graduation cohort were assessed using ordinal regression; models were adjusted for graduation cohort, gender, and physician age. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are reported. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05. The analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31.

Results

The subsample of this study was formed physicians who completed their specialist training between 2008 and 2023 (n = 2 772). Of these newly-qualified specialists, 87% were aged 35 − 54 years and 71% female (Table 2). The conservative specialties represented the largest proportion of specialists (30%) and among them the largest numbers were pediatrics (n = 148), geriatrics (n = 109), and neurology (n = 68). Among specialists in operative fields the largest groups were anaesthesiology and intensive care (n = 190), obstetrics and gynecology (n = 190), and ophthalmology (n = 85). Within the diagnostic specialties, radiology had the largest number of respondents (n = 94), and as for psychiatric specialties, adult psychiatry (n = 136).

CanMEDS communicator role

On average, 72% of respondents reported adequate teaching and supervision related to the Communicator role during their specialist training. The largest proportion (34%) of insufficient (far too little or too little) teaching concerned the competency of acknowledging and involving patients’ family (Table 3). Internal consistency of the communicator competency subdomains was high and correlation matrix is shown in Appendix Table 1.

The cohort specialized in 2008–2012 reported insufficient teaching and supervision (OR 1.48; 95% CI 1.20 − 1.82) more frequently than those who completed their specialist training in 2013–2017 (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.83 − 1.21) across all communicator subdomains (Table 4). Age was not significantly associated with perceived insufficiency (the effect of a one-year increase in age OR 1.01; 95% CI 1.00 − 1.02, P = 0,20).

Female gender was associated with higher odds of reporting insufficient teaching and supervision in the Communicator role (Table 4). The largest gender difference was observed in the competency of acknowledging and involving the patient’s family (35.5% vs. 30.1%; P < 0.01). When comparing specialty groups, specialists in operative fields most frequently reported insufficient teaching and supervision compared to other specialty groups (Table 4). Differences were statistically significant in patient interaction, patient‑centred care, and involving patients’ family. For written communication, statistically significant difference was observed in the psychiatric specialties (35.6%; 95% CI 29.7 − 41.6%) (Table 5). In diagnostic specialties, the proportion of respondents who assessed teaching as insufficient was the lowest across all four domains.

We also compared the teaching and supervision experienced between pediatric and adult specialties. Within psychiatry, it seems that specialists in child and adolescent psychiatry (n = 102) assessed teaching and supervision as insufficient statistically significantly (P < 0.01) less often than specialists in adult psychiatry (n = 145) in skills of patient interaction, patient-centered care, and acknowledging and involving patients’ family. Within conservative specialties, the only significant difference between specialists in pediatrics and pediatric neurology (n = 173) and other conservative specialties (n = 634) was in the domain of acknowledging and involving patients’ family (P < 0.01). Within operative specialties, differences between pediatric surgery (n = 16) and other operative specialties (n = 727) did not differ statistically significantly.

CanMEDS collaborator role

Related to the Collaborator role, 62% of respondents reported having received adequate teaching and supervision. The largest proportion of insufficient (far too little or too little) teaching concerned team leadership skills (60%) (Table 3). Internal consistency of the Collaborator competency subdomains was high and correlation matrix is shown in Appendix Table 2.

Specialists who graduated in 2008–2012 reported significantly higher odds of perceiving insufficient teaching and supervision (OR 1.58; 95% CI 1.30 − 1.93) within the Collaborator role than those who completed their specialist training in 2018–2023 (OR 1.00) and slightly higher than among those who graduated in the years 2013–2017 (OR 1.24; 95% CI 1.04 − 1.47) (Table 4). Age was not significantly associated with perceived insufficiency (the effect of a one-year increase in age OR 1.00; 95% CI 0.99 − 1.02, P = 0,55).

Female gender (Table 4) was associated with higher odds of reporting insufficient teaching and supervision within the Collaborator role. The largest gender difference was observed in the competency of presentation skills (55.6% vs. 43.9%; P < 0.01). Insufficient teaching and supervision in consultation skills was most often reported in psychiatric specialties (Table 6), and this proportion differed statistically significantly from all other specialty groups (P < 0.01).

Discussion

Our principal aim was to compare perceived competency gaps across specialty groups, with particular emphasis on identifying specialties exhibiting deficits across multiple subdomains. Focusing attention on identified insufficiencies and weaknesses facilitates their addressing and informs targeted improvements to specialist training and workforce planning.

Intrinsic (often mislabelled “soft”) competencies are integral to medical expertise and therefore merit equal prioritization within postgraduate curricula, since infrequent instruction may undermine their effective teaching and assessment [20,21]. While medical knowledge remains central, many clinical tasks (e.g., leading resuscitation, delivering bad news) also demand specific communication skills. For example, child and adolescent psychiatrists reported fewer communication deficits than adult psychiatrists, reflecting how such competencies are shaped by specialty, culture, and task profile [11,22] efforts to harmonize specialist training internationally [23].

Regarding the Collaborator role, inadequate training most often concerned team-leadership skills, despite general satisfaction with interprofessional education—interprofessional teaching rounds remain uncommon in Finland [24], although students frequently gain practical experience as locums [25].

Previous research has shown that competency skills are emphasized differently across specialties and shortage may vary by specialty. Grounds for dismissing residents from surgery or radiology are deficiencies in medical expertise and scholarship, whereas in general medicine the dismissal is deficiency in professionalism [9].

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design and reliance on respondents’ retrospective self-assessments do not allow causal inferences or evaluation of the actual impact of educational reforms. We only collected data with a CanMEDS-based questionnaire adapted for Finland [26]. It does not describe competencies extensively, thus the results are descriptive and individual follow-up was not feasible. There were no other variables measuring the same thing, so an information bias is possible. Also, the respondents were only asked their opinion on specialist training. Therefore, the answers do not necessarily reflect the true quality of the education.

The observed differences may also reflect generational or cultural changes, evolving expectations regarding training, or increased awareness of soft skills competencies. Additionally, there are personal differences in remembering events, and the answers reflect the interpretation of the moment of response. However, important-event recall can be reasonably reliable [27,28]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as the study identifies associations and subjective perceptions reported by the respondents rather than objective causal relationships.

The strengths are extensive, nationwide sample and internationally favorable response rate [29,30], and a well-defined study population.

Conclusions

Specialist training should provide competencies that correspond to the demands of clinical practice. The CanMEDS competency domains Communicator and Collaborator play an important role in both the development of education and the quality of care across all specialties. The majority of the respondents were satisfied with the teaching they received during specialist training in terms of these skills. Satisfaction can be related to changes in either changes in teaching content or by increased knowledge of the need for competency. It seems that within operative specialties the teaching of communication skills and within psychiatry the teaching of consultation skills could be increased.
