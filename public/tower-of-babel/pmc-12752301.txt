The prevalence and influence of EBV infection in patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome
Authors: Fangfang Geng, Xiaoxiang Hu, Yan Xu, Tingting Xiao, Meifang Yang, Xuan Zhang, Hong Zhao, Jianhua Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSevere fever with thrombocytopenia (SFTS) is a fatal emerging infectious disease. Co-infection, mainly with bacterial and fungal infections, in association with poorer outcomes in SFTS, but few studies have examined on viral infections. We aimed to investigate the prevalence of Epstein‒Barr virus (EBV) infection and its effects on the clinical features and mortality in patients with SFTS.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 104 patients with confirmed SFTS between 2011 and 2025, and analyzed their laboratory test results and clinical data.ResultsOf 104 SFTS patients, 48.10% had EBV infection. Compared to those without EBV infection, these patients were more likely to have bacterial infection in blood (p = 0.027), lower white blood cell (p < 0.001) and neutrophil counts (p = 0.019), lower serum calcium (p = 0.043), and higher alanine aminotransferase (p = 0.013), adenosine deaminase (p = 0.018), and urinary protein (p = 0.005). Mortality rates were higher in EBV-infected patients (7 [14%] vs. 4 [7.4%], p = 0.275), though not statistically significant. In this study, decreased WBC count and elevated ALT level were independently associated with EBV infection in SFTS patients (OR = 0.391 [95% CI, 0.233–0.657] and 1.006 [95% CI, 1.001–1.011], both p < 0.05).ConclusionsSFTS patients are at high risk for EBV infection. Those with EBV infection exhibit more severe clinical manifestations, although short-term survival does not differ significantly. Decreased WBC count and elevated ALT are risk factors for EBV infection in SFTS patients. The need for targeted treatment of EBV infection remains uncertain.Keywords: SFTS, EBV infection, Risk factor, Leukopenia

Introduction

Severe fever with thrombocytopenia (SFTS) is an acute emerging infectious disease caused by Dabie bandavirus, also known as severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome virus (SFTSV), a member of the family Phenuiviridae within the order Bunyavirales [1]. First identified in rural China in 2009, SFTS has since been reported in various regions of Asia and beyond. The incidence of SFTS continues to rise annually, with high fatality rates that present a significant public health concern. The primary clinical manifestations include unexplained severe fever, thrombocytopenia, and leukopenia, often accompanied by gastrointestinal symptoms such as diarrhea and abdominal pain [1]. Severe cases are characterized by neurological symptoms, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hemophagocytic syndrome, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and mortality [2].

SFTSV infection induces severe immune dysfunction and triggers inflammatory cytokine storms. Upon host entry, SFTSV impairs the antigen-presenting function of plasma blasts, leads to exhaustion of T lymphocytes and natural killer cells [3], exacerbates the interferon-1 (IFN-1) response, and activates the NF-κB signalling pathway [4]. These processes result in a markedly weakened humoral immune response. The resulting immune dysfunction and excessive inflammatory activation may increase the risk of secondary infections [5]. Recent studies indicate that secondary infections occur at a high rate in patients with SFTS, potentially worsening organ damage and leading to poor clinical outcomes, including death [6]. Secondary infections in SFTS patients are primarily bacterial and fungal, with common pathogens including Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Aspergillus fumigatus, and Aspergillus flavus [6]. However, limited data are available on viral infections in SFTS patients, particularly on opportunistic Epstein–Barr virus infection, which is particularly relevant in immunocompromised individuals.

Epstein–Barr virus (EBV) is a human gamma-herpesvirus with a high prevalence worldwide. It is known to cause infection and generally becomes latent in more than 90% of the global population. After entering the human body, EBV primarily infects B lymphocytes, epithelial cells, and muscle cells [7]. Most immunocompetent people have no clinical manifestations after infection. However, adolescent infection frequently results in infectious mononucleosis with major symptoms, including fever, fatigue, sore throat, hepatosplenomegaly, and lymphadenopathy [8]. EBV can establish host latency, potentially activating and proliferating in immunocompromised individuals, leading to several complications [9]. In addition, EBV activation is associated with other viral infections in the host. Certain viral infections can trigger EBV infection [10], worsening organ damage, increasing illness severity, and increasing the risk of poor outcomes.

However, evidence regarding the incidence of EBV infection in patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) is limited, and the impact of EBV infection on SFTS remains unclear. This retrospective study aims to determine the prevalence of EBV among SFTS patients and to assess whether EBV infection is associated with specific clinical features and poorer outcomes in this population.

Methods

Patient enrollment and data collection

We retrospectively reviewed patients with confirmed SFTS admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China, between Jan 1, 2011, and Feb 1, 2025. We collected demographic data, laboratory results, underlying conditions, clinical presentations, complications, ICU admissions, and mortality. The onset of the disease onset was defined as the date of the first symptom. We recorded the time from onset to admission, and the length of hospital stay from the medical records. The observation endpoints were death and discharge.

We determined EBV viral loads in plasma from SFTS patients quantitative real-time TaqMan polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the EBV nucleic acid amplification assay kit (Daan gene, China) and EBV specific primers. It was based on amplifying pathogen-specific regions of the genome using specific EBV primers. EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA)-immunoglobulin G (IgG) and VCA-IgM were measured using EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA)-immunoglobulin G (IgG)/immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody detection kit and chemiluminescent immunoassay (YHLO, Shenzhen). The laboratory department performed EBV DNA testing within 72 hours of admission. Blood samples were analyzed 12 hours after collection from all patients and stored at 4 °C. All laboratory tests were conducted by our hospital’s laboratory department.

Diagnostic criteria

The exclusion criteria for the control groups were included: (1) comorbidities involving severe disease of heart, brain, liver, kidney, hematopoietic system as well as other significant primary illnesses; (2) severe malnutrition, active infections, or substance addiction; (3) known immunodeficiency defined as a history of solid organ or stem cell transplantation, HIV infection, hematological malignancy, use of immunosuppressive medication, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy within the year before admission, or any documented humoral or cellular immune deficiency; and (4) pregnancy. All healthy participants were at least 18 years of age.

EBV deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) ≥500 copies/mL was diagnosed as EBV infection [11]. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) was defined according to the Berlin definition [12]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes definition [13]. MODS was defined as two or more systemic or organ dysfunctions simultaneously or sequentially [14]. DIC was diagnosed according to a scoring system proposed by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis in 2004 [15]. We defined clinically suspected myocarditis according to the European Society of Cardiology Working Group criteria in 2013 [16]. Liver dysfunction was defined if the serum levels of alanine aminotransferase and alanine aminotransferase were greater than normal.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed with SPSS for Windows version 26.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The Student t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the value difference between two groups. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables are reported as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Means for continuous variables were compared using independent-group student’s tests for normally distributed data and the Mann-Whitney test for nonnormally distributed data. The χ2 test analyzed categorical variables, although Fisher’s exact test was used when the data were sparse. A multivariate analysis was conducted to examine the impact of sex, age, corticosteroid treatment, intravenous immunoglobulin treatment, and variables with p < 0.05 from the univariate analysis. This was done using a binary logistic regression model, and the results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. We used Kaplan–Meier plots to analyze survival data. All tests of significance were two-tailed, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Basic characteristics of SFTS patients

From January 1, 2011, to February 1, 2025, 212 patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) were admitted to the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China. Of these, 108 patients were excluded because Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA quantitative testing was not performed. Therefore, 104 patients with SFTS were included in the study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Fig. 1. Among the included patients, 50 (48.1%) were diagnosed with EBV infection. Patients were categorized into two groups based on EBV infection status: the EBV group (n = 50) and the non-EBV group (n = 54).

The mean age of all patients was 64.13 ± 11.52 years. Although the EBV group was older than the non-EBV group (64.46 ± 13.33 years vs. 63.83 ± 9.66 years), the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.783). The most common comorbidities were hypertension (37 patients, 35.6%) and diabetes (12 patients, 11.5%). Baseline information of patients with SFTS enrolled in this study

Among the 104 patients in this cohort, fever (103 [99.0%]), fatigue (75 [72.1%]), and anorexia (68 [65.4%]) were the most frequently reported symptoms at illness onset. A minority of patients with severe SFTS also exhibited neurological symptoms. In the EBV infection group, initial symptoms included fever (50 [100%]), fatigue (39 [78%]), and anorexia (34 [68%]). The non-EBV group most commonly presented with fever (53 [98.1%]), fatigue (36 [66.7%]), and anorexia (34 [63.0%]). No significant differences in symptom presentation were observed between the EBV and non-EBV groups (Table 1).

Within the EBV group, 17 patients (34.0%) received corticosteroids and 10 patients (20.0%) received intravenous immunoglobulin prior to EBV infection. In comparison, 7 patients (13.0%) and 8 patients (14.8%) in the non-EBV group received corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, respectively. A significantly higher proportion of patients with EBV infection received corticosteroids compared to those without EBV infection (p = 0.011).

Of the 104 SFTS patients, 49 underwent serological testing, including 27 from the EBV group and 22 from the non-EBV group. VCA IgG levels were significantly higher in SFTS patients with EBV infection compared to those without EBV infection (p = 0.044), while all SFTS patients tested negative for VCA IgM.

Laboratory findings and clinical characteristics of SFTS patients with and without EBV infection

Although most SFTS patients underwent a single EBV DNA test at admission, two patients with EBV infection received two examinations. One 68-year-old immunocompetent man tested negative for EBV infection at admission but exhibited a high EBV DNA level of 2220 copies within one week. The first and second tests were conducted on days 7 and 14, corresponding to the fever and multiple organ injury phases. Another patient, a 67-year-old woman, had 3340 copies of EBV DNA at admission, which increased to 5440 copies the following day. These cases suggest that EBV may become increasingly activated during the course of SFTS.

Laboratory tests indicated that leukopenia (2.49 × 10^9/L, IQR: 1.59–3.78 × 10^9/L) and thrombocytopenia (42.50 × 10^9/L, IQR: 27.25–67 × 10^9/L) were prominent among patients with SFTS. The EBV group exhibited significantly lower white blood cell (WBC) counts (p < 0.001) and neutrophil counts (0.019) compared to the non-EBV group. Although the median platelet count was lower in the EBV group (36.50 × 10^9/L) than in the non-EBV group (44 × 10^9/L), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.096).

Abnormal liver function was identified in 96 patients, representing 93.2% of those with SFTS. SFTS patients with EBV infection demonstrated significantly increased serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) levels, with median values of 110 U/L and 236 U/L, respectively. ALT levels were significantly higher in the EBV group compared to the non-EBV group (p = 0.013). Elevated serum creatinine was observed in patients with SFTS, with levels of 76.50 mmol/L in the EBV group and 74.50 mmol/L in the non-EBV group; this difference was not significant (p = 0.891). Proteinuria was detected in 34 individuals (79.1%) with EBV infection and 24 individuals (47.1%) without EBV infection. Urine protein levels were higher in the EBV group than in the non-EBV group (1.00 U/L vs. 0.70 U/L, p = 0.005).

Elevated serum adenylate deaminase (ADA) levels were present in 71 individuals (78.9%) with SFTS. ADA levels were significantly higher in patients with EBV infection compared to those without EBV infection (33 U/L vs. 26 U/L, p = 0.018). Patients with EBV infection also exhibited lower serum calcium levels than non-EBV patients (p = 0.043), while no significant differences were observed in median serum potassium and sodium levels between the groups.

Ferritin levels were elevated above the normal range in most SFTS patients. Although ferritin levels were higher in the EBV group (9808.50 ng/mL) than in the non-EBV group (6924.50 ng/mL), this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.360). No significant differences were observed between the EBV and non-EBV groups in procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, international normalized ratio, D-dimer, creatine kinase, creatine kinase-MB, lactate dehydrogenase, troponin I, or lipid metabolism indicators such as triglyceride and low- or high-density lipoprotein.

Complications and outcomes of SFTS patients with and without EBV infection

Among the 104 patients with SFTS, the most common complications included MODS (74 [71.20%]), clinically suspected myocarditis (72 [69.20%]), acute respiratory distress syndrome (46 [44.20%]), central nervous syndrome (35 [33.70%]), DIC (22 [21.20%]), fungal infection (27 [26%]), and bacterial infection (18 [17.30%]) (Table 2). Although no significant differences in the frequency of bacterial or fungal infection were observed between the EBV and non-EBV groups (all p > 0.05), infection sites in SFTS were further analysed.

For bacterial infections, the blood (8 [7.70%]) and respiratory tract (10 [9.60%]) represented the primary sites in the EBV and non-EBV groups, respectively. Patients with EBV infection were more likely to develop blood borne bacterial infections compared to those without EBV infection (14% vs. 1.90%, p = 0.027). For fungal infection, the respiratory tract was the predominant site in both groups, with no insignificant difference observed (24% vs. 18.50%, p = 0.494). No significant differences in other complications were identified between SFTS patients with and without EBV infection.

Regarding clinical outcomes, invasive mechanical ventilation and intensive care unit (ICU) treatment were administered to 14 (13.50%) of the 104 patients with SFTS, including 9 (64.30%) with EBV infection and 5 (35.70%) without EBV infection. A total of 11 (10.60%) patients died in the ICU. Although more patients with EBV infection received invasive mechanical ventilation and ICU treatment, and mortality rates were higher in the EBV group than in the non-EBV group, these differences were not statistically significant (all p > 0.05).

The 28-day mortality rates for patients with and without EBV infection were 14% and 7.40%, respectively (p = 0.275, Table 2). Similarly, the 14-day mortality rate were 14% and 7.40% for patients with and without EBV infection, respectively (p = 0.275, Table 2). Univariate and multivariate binary logistic analyses of risk factors for the development of EBV infection in SFTS patients

A preliminary exploratory study was conducted to identify the risk factors for EBV infection in SFTS patients, including sex, age, corticosteroid treatment, intravenous immunoglobin treatment, and all variables with p values < 0.05 in Table 2. The results are presented in Table 3. Univariate logistic regression indicated that glucocorticoid treatment (OR = 0.289 [95% CI, 0.108–0.775], p = 0.014), increased alanine aminotransferase levels (OR = 1.004 [95% CI, 1.000–1.009], p = 0.044), decreased WBC (OR = 0.556 [95% CI, 0.406–0.761], p < 0.001) and decreased neutrophil count (OR = 0.771 [95% CI, 0.616–0.965], p = 0.023) were associated with the development of EBV infection in SFTS patients. Subsequently, the multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed. The multivariate analysis demonstrated that decreased WBC count (OR = 0.391 [95% CI, 0.233–0.657], p < 0.001) and increased alanine aminotransferase levels (OR = 1.006 [95% CI, 1.001–1.011], p = 0.024) were the independent risk factors for the occurrence of EBV infection in SFTS patients (Table 3).

Discussion

This study represents the largest cohort to date investigating the prevalence of Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection in hospitalized patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS). In contrast, a previous small cohort study of 22 SFTS patients identified 4 cases (18.2%) of EBV infection, which were associated with lower serum albumin and increased urine red blood cells; however, that study did not provide a detailed description of clinical features or further examine the impact of EBV infection on SFTS.

The present study systematically reviewed the clinical manifestations, laboratory findings, complications, and outcomes of SFTS patients with and without EBV infection. Additionally, risk factors for EBV infection in SFTS patients were explored. The findings indicate that hospitalized SFTS patients exhibited a higher incidence of EBV infection. Furthermore, SFTS patients with EBV infection were more likely to have lower white blood cell and neutrophil counts, lower serum calcium levels, and higher alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels, as well as higher urine protein levels.

A prevalence of up to 48.1% EBV infection was observed among patients hospitalized with SFTS, which exceeds rates reported in previous studies [17]. Various stressors, including immunosuppression, oxidative stress, infection, and psychological factors, can stimulate EBV infection [10, 18]. EBV may exploit host immunosuppression to reactivate as an opportunistic virus, similar to BK virus and human papillomavirus [19, 20]. Additionally, EBV DNA levels gradually increased over the course of SFTS. Immune suppression, including both cellular and humoral immune impairment caused by SFTSV, may contribute to EBV infection [3]. Research indicates that the production of reactive oxygen species induced by SFTSV infection significantly enhances EBV infection [21]. Thus, an imbalance in the immune network and the inflammatory cytokine storm induced by SFTSV infection may substantially promote EBV infection.

Although EBV infection was diagnosed based on the presence of EBV early antigen-diffuse (EA-D) IgG or EBV viral capsid antigen (VCA) IgM, it can also be determined by measuring circulating EBV DNA levels. [22] Among 27 patients with EBV infection who received serological testing admission, all were VCA IgM-negative. This finding may be attributable to several factors, such as impaired immunocompetence resulting from SFTSV infection and limitations in detection sensitivity. Serological tests for antibodies specific to EBV antigens are frequently used to assess infection status. However, interpretation can be challenging because VCA IgG may be present without VCA IgM or EBV nuclear antigen (EBNA)-1 IgG, and VCA IgM may disappear early or appear later in acute infections.[23, 24]

Primary finding of this study is that SFTS patients with EBV infection are more susceptible to neutropenia and blood stream bacterial infection. Neutropenia has been reported in clinical studies of SFTS, particularly in severe cases. [25] Pathogen infections are the most common causes of neutropenia, and most viruses induce a transient reduction in neutrophil count. In contrast, viruses, including EBV and parvovirus B19 can cause persistent neutropenia. [26] Additionally, EBV induces neutrophil apoptosis by increasing the expression of Fas and Fas ligand(L) neutrophil membrane surface. [27] The combination of neutropenia and immunosuppression in SFTS patients with EBV infection may increase bloodstream bacterial infections.

Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection exacerbates liver damage in patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS). Approximately 96.0% of individuals with EBV infection exhibit varying degrees of liver function impairment, and some severe cases progress to liver failure [28]. While most SFTS patients present with liver enzyme abnormalities, those with concurrent EBV infection demonstrate more pronounced liver enzyme derangements compared to those without EBV infection. Consequently, monitoring liver function in SFTS patients with EBV infection is essential to prevent further deterioration.

The present study identified an association between EBV infection and elevated serum adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels in SFTS patients. ADA, a key enzyme in the adenosine metabolic pathway, is widely distributed in various cell types and is particularly abundant in lymphoid tissues, serving as a marker of T-lymphocyte-mediated immunity [29]. ADA is closely linked to diseases caused by EBV infection. Previous research has shown that ADA expression increases in Epstein-Barr virus-associated infectious mononucleosis (EBV-IM) and provides significant diagnostic value for EBV-IM [30]. LaMontagne et al. [31] demonstrated that the EBV-encoded EBNA1 protein binds to the promoter region upstream of the ADA gene, thereby promoting intracellular ADA expression. Furthermore, ADA is a direct and critical target in the EBV-mediated immortalization process.

EBV infection in SFTS patients may alter calcium flux. The latent membrane protein 1(LMP1) increase calcium influx into B cells by enhancing the amplitude of store-operated calcium entry (SOCE). [32] The elevated cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations are necessary to suppress EBV lytic reactivation. Ca2+-dependent signaling pathways are integral to the EBV life cycle and can be modulated by EBV genome expression. [33] Collectively, these mechanisms may contribute to reduced serum calcium levels in SFTS patients with EBV infection.

SFTS patients with EBV infection exhibit significantly elevated urine protein levels. Previous research indicates that EBV infection can directly damage the glomerular mesangium resulting in proteinuria. [34] Additionally, proteinuria arises from the cross-reactivity of antibodies to EBNA-1 with double-stranded DNA (dsDNA). Divya et al. observed that antibodies to EBNA-1 cross-react with dsDNA and may induce proteinuria by depositing in the glomeruli leading to increased mesangial matrix and hypercellularity.[35]

The 28-day mortality rate (10.6%) among 90 SFTS patients was consistent with previous findings [36]. In this study, we found that SFTS patients with EBV infection exhibited a higher mortality rate and were more likely to require invasive mechanical ventilation and ICU treatment than those without EBV infection. However, the difference was not significant (all p > 0.05). Consequently, careful monitoring of the physical condition and laboratory indicators in SFTS patients with EBV infection is warranted.

Given that glucocorticoids and immunoglobulins influence the human immune response, glucocorticoid and immunoglobulin administration were included as variables in the analysis to determine their potential role in facilitating EBV infection among SFTS patients. Univariate logistic regression indicated that glucocorticoid treatment was associated with an increased risk of EBV infection in SFTS. However, multivariate logistic regression did not identify glucocorticoid use as an independent risk factor. Glucocorticoid administration should therefore be approached with caution in SFTS, as it may exacerbate organ damage and worsen outcomes in the presence of EBV infection. Additionally, univariate analysis identified increased ALT levels, decreased white blood cell (WBC) count, and reduced neutrophil count as risk factors for EBV infection. Multivariable analysis confirmed that decreased WBC count and elevated ALT levels were independent risk factors for EBV infection in SFTS patients. The reduction in WBC count observed in SFTS reflects impaired host defense against viral infections, potentially increasing susceptibility to EBV infection in patients with SFTSV.

Although the present study suggests that SFTS patients with EBV infection experience more severe organ damage, it remains uncertain whether EBV infection requires targeted treatment. This question is critical for clinicians seeking to develop personalized, targeted treatment plans. Active EBV infection in patients with coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has been associated with poor prognoses, including disease deterioration and increased mortality [37]. Some studies have reported that COVID-19 patients with EBV infection showed significant improvement after receiving ganciclovir therapy for herpesvirus infection [37, 38]. Additionally, one in vitro study and clinical observations demonstrated that acyclovir inhibits EBV replication and eliminates its enhancement of Dengue virus replication [39]. These findings suggest that anti-herpesvirus therapy may improve prognosis and reduce organ damage in SFTS patients.

This analysis has several limitations, including its retrospective, single-center design, limited patient numbers, and selection bias due to the inclusion of only patients who underwent EBV quantification testing. Nearly half of SFTS patients from 2011 to 2025 were excluded from the cohort due to the absence of EBV DNA testing, which may have contributed to the lack of significant mortality differences between the two groups. Furthermore, the analysis did not incorporate more detailed data, such as longitudinal changes in EBV serological antibodies and EBV DNA loads. Only a small proportion of patients received serological antibody testing, and changes in EBV DNA load and serological antibodies during hospitalization and after discharge were not observed. Consequently, larger, multi-center, prospective studies are required to further investigate the relationship between EBV and SFTSV infections and to comprehensively evaluate the need for targeted treatment.

Conclusions

In summary, this study found that patients with severe fever with thrombocytopenia syndrome (SFTS) are at elevated risk for Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection. Patients with EBV infection demonstrate more severe clinical features, such as leukopenia, liver injury, and electrolyte disturbances, although no significant difference in short-term survival was observed. Decreased white blood cell count and increased alanine aminotransferase levels were identified as independent risk factors for EBV infection in SFTS. These findings indicate that early recognition of leukopenia and liver dysfunction is essential, as they may signal the potential for more severe clinical manifestations and poorer outcomes. Nevertheless, the necessity of targeted treatment for EBV infection remains unclear, and further research is required to fully assess its clinical value.
