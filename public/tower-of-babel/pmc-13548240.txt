Socioeconomic predictors of diabetic foot ulcers: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Annel Sinkala, Eustarckio Kazonga, Monde Watae, Julius Mwanza, Richard Mutemwa, Esnart Nguni, John Phiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the association between socioeconomic factors and diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) among adults with diabetes mellitus through systematic review and meta-analysis.DesignSystematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines.Data sourcesPubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL and Google Scholar were systematically searched from database inception to 31 December 2024, with a final updated search performed in February 2025.Eligibility criteriaCross-sectional and case–control studies evaluating the association between socioeconomic factors and DFUs among adults with diabetes mellitus were included.Data extraction and synthesisStudy selection and data extraction were performed independently by two reviewers. Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for cross-sectional studies and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale for case–control studies. Random-effects meta-analysis was used to pool ORs and corresponding 95% CIs. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic.ResultsEight cross-sectional and case–control studies involving 3488 participants were included. Lower educational attainment was significantly associated with higher odds of DFUs (pooled OR=2.54, 95% CI 1.24 to 5.20; I² = 68.0%). Similarly, lack of formal employment was associated with higher odds of DFUs (pooled OR=1.71, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.77; I² = 21.2%). In contrast, although low income showed increased pooled odds of DFUs, the association did not reach statistical significance under the random-effects model (pooled OR=2.70, 95% CI 0.36 to 20.41; I² = 81.9%). Interpretation of the income analysis was limited by substantial heterogeneity and wide CIs. Sensitivity analyses excluding moderate-to-high risk studies demonstrated similar directions of association for education and occupational status.ConclusionsLower educational attainment and lack of formal employment were associated with higher odds of DFUs among individuals with diabetes mellitus. These findings suggest that socioeconomic factors should be considered in diabetic foot prevention and management strategies alongside conventional clinical risk factors. Further high-quality prospective studies using standardised socioeconomic measures are needed to strengthen the evidence base in this area.Keywords: Diabetes Mellitus; Confounding Factors, Epidemiologic; Social Medicine; Systematic Review

Introduction

Background

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of the most serious and prevalent complications of diabetes mellitus, significantly contributing to increased morbidity, healthcare costs and mortality worldwide. It is estimated that 15–25% of individuals with diabetes will develop DFUs at some point in their lives.1 These ulcers can lead to severe outcomes such as infections and amputations, which further complicate the management of diabetes. The global burden of diabetes is rising, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the prevalence of DFUs is exacerbated by limited healthcare resources and pronounced socioeconomic disparities.2 Despite advancements in diabetes care, DFUs remain a significant public health challenge, partly due to the influence of socioeconomic factors on disease management and outcomes.

Recent insights: Recent studies underscore the critical role of socioeconomic status (SES) in the development and progression of DFUs. A growing body of literature indicates that lower SES is associated with delayed diagnosis, inadequate treatment and poorer health outcomes in diabetes management.3,4 Factors such as income, education level and occupation play a pivotal role in determining an individual’s access to healthcare services, their ability to afford medications and adherence to recommended lifestyle modifications. For example, patients with lower income levels are less likely to receive regular foot examinations or timely interventions, thereby increasing the risk of complications related to DFUs.5

The COVID-19 pandemic has further intensified these disparities, severely impacting access to routine healthcare services for low-SES populations. Reports indicate a surge in DFU cases and related amputations during this period, highlighting the vulnerability of disadvantaged groups.6 This situation underscores the urgent need to understand and address socioeconomic determinants to improve care and outcomes for patients with DFUs.

SES and health outcomes: SES is a well-established determinant of health that influences both the risk of developing chronic conditions and the quality of healthcare received. Individuals with lower SES often experience higher rates of chronic diseases and poorer health outcomes overall.7 In the context of diabetes, SES significantly impacts self-care practices, access to specialist care and the ability to maintain glycaemic control—all critical factors that influence the risk and severity of DFUs.8

Objective

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify specific socioeconomic predictors of DFUs, including income, education and occupation, and assess their impact on the incidence, complications and overall outcomes of this condition. By synthesising recent evidence, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive understanding of how SES influences DFUs and to guide targeted interventions for at-risk populations

Research question

How do socioeconomic factors, such as education, income and employment status, predict the risk of developing DFUs among individuals with diabetes mellitus?

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.9

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined using the PECO (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcome) framework. The population comprised adults aged 18 years and older with a confirmed diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. The exposures of interest included socioeconomic factors, specifically educational attainment, occupational or employment status and income level. Comparators consisted of individuals within relatively higher socioeconomic categories, as defined by the original studies. The primary outcome was the occurrence of DFUs.

Observational studies, including cross-sectional, case–control and cohort studies, published in English between database inception and 31 December 2024 were eligible for inclusion. Studies were required to report adjusted ORs (aORs) with corresponding 95% CIs, or provide sufficient quantitative data to calculate effect estimates for at least one socioeconomic factor associated with DFUs. Review articles, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, case reports, duplicate publications and studies lacking sufficient quantitative data were excluded.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, CINAHL and Google Scholar from database inception to 31 December 2024 with a final updated search performed in February 2025. In addition, the reference lists of eligible studies and relevant reviews were manually screened to identify potentially relevant studies not captured through the electronic database search.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to DFUs and socioeconomic factors using Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”). Search terms included combinations of “diabetic foot ulcer”, “diabetic foot”, “DFU”, “socioeconomic status”, “socioeconomic factors”, “education”, “income”, “occupation”, “employment”, “poverty”, “risk factors” and “associated factors”.

The search was not extended to Embase because of institutional access limitations at the time of the review. However, PubMed, Scopus and CINAHL provided broad coverage of biomedical and public health literature relevant to the review topic.

All retrieved records were exported into Mendeley Desktop (V.1.19.8), where duplicate records were identified and removed prior to screening.

Study selection and data extraction

Titles and abstracts were independently screened by two reviewers using predefined eligibility criteria. Potentially eligible studies subsequently underwent full-text assessment by the same reviewers. Disagreements during screening and study selection were resolved through discussion and consensus or, where necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Initial disagreements occurred in approximately 10% of screened studies during title/abstract screening and full-text assessment, and all disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus. The study selection process was documented using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a standardised Microsoft Excel extraction template. Extracted data included the first author, year of publication, country of study, study design, sample size, participant characteristics, socioeconomic variables assessed, confounding variables adjusted for in the original analyses where reported and adjusted ORs with corresponding 95% CIs. Any discrepancies identified during data extraction were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Quality and risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools appropriate for cross-sectional and case–control studies. The appraisal process evaluated methodological domains including participant selection, validity and reliability of exposure and outcome measurement, identification and control of confounding factors and appropriateness of statistical analyses.

Studies were classified as having low, moderate or high risk of bias based on overall appraisal outcomes. Sensitivity analyses were subsequently conducted by excluding studies classified as high risk of bias to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates.

The overall certainty of evidence for each socioeconomic exposure was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework. Evidence was assessed across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias. The certainty of evidence was rated as high, moderate, low or very low, with observational studies starting at low certainty and being upgraded or downgraded based on the magnitude of effect, dose-response gradient and plausibility of confounding. GRADE ratings are summarised in Table 3.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software V.4.4.2 with the ‘meta’ and ‘metagen’ packages. Adjusted ORs and corresponding 95% CIs were used as the principal measures of association.

Pooled effect estimates were calculated using random-effects meta-analysis models because substantial clinical, methodological and geographical heterogeneity was anticipated across studies, including differences in study design, socioeconomic variable definitions, healthcare settings and confounding adjustment strategies.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic and the I² statistic. I² values of 25%, 50% and 75% were interpreted as representing low, moderate and high heterogeneity, respectively.10 Exploratory subgroup analyses by geographical region were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. These subgroup analyses were interpreted cautiously because several subgroups contained a limited number of studies, resulting in unstable pooled estimates and wide CIs.

Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies classified as having high risk of bias to assess the robustness of pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically using Egger’s regression test where appropriate. Because fewer than 10 studies were included in several pooled analyses, publication bias findings were interpreted cautiously due to the limited statistical power of asymmetry tests in small meta-analyses. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this systematic review and meta-analysis.

Results

Study selection

A total of 406 records were identified through electronic database searching and additional sources. After removal of 320 duplicate records, 86 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Following screening, 72 studies were excluded because they did not meet the eligibility criteria. The full texts of 14 studies were subsequently assessed for eligibility, of which 6 were excluded for reasons including insufficient outcome data, absence of socioeconomic variables of interest or ineligible study design. Ultimately, eight studies were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis. The study selection process is presented in figure 1.

The study selection process.

Characteristics of included studies

The eight included studies were published between 2013 and 2021 and were conducted across several LMICs, including Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Kenya, Egypt, Iran, Palestine (Gaza Strip) and Sri Lanka. Two studies were conducted in Ethiopia. Six studies employed cross-sectional designs, while two were case–control studies. Most studies were facility-based, although two were community-based investigations. The total sample size across all included studies was 3488 participants, with individual study sample sizes ranging from 161 to 1200 participants. The socioeconomic factors evaluated across the studies included educational attainment, income level and occupational status. Methodological quality was assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for cross-sectional studies and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for case–control studies. Overall, the methodological quality of the included studies ranged from moderate to high.

Among the six cross-sectional studies, four were classified as having low risk of bias, while one study11 was rated as moderate risk of bias because of limitations related to incomplete confounding adjustment, participant selection and inconsistent measurement of socioeconomic variables. One study12 was classified as having moderate-to-high risk of bias due to concerns regarding sample representativeness and insufficient adjustment for important confounding variables. Several facility-based studies also demonstrated limited external representativeness, which may have contributed to heterogeneity across pooled estimates.

Both case–control studies were assessed using the NOS and were rated as having acceptable methodological quality, with scores of five stars or higher. The detailed methodological quality appraisal of the included studies is presented in table 2.

Association between educational status and DFUs

Five studies5,12–15 evaluated the association between educational status and DFUs. The pooled analysis demonstrated that individuals with no formal education had significantly higher odds of developing DFUs compared with those who had received formal education (pooled OR=2.54, 95% CI 1.24 to 5.20; p=0.0005).

Moderate heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 68.0%, p=0.014), indicating variability in effect estimates among studies. Differences in study design, socioeconomic measurements and confounding adjustment strategies across the included studies may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. The corresponding forest plot is presented in figure 2.

Forest plot for education.

The funnel plot assessing publication bias for studies included in the education analysis is presented in online supplemental figure 1.

A subgroup analysis by geographical region demonstrated variability in pooled estimates across regions. In Africa, the pooled OR was 2.14 (95% CI 0.00 to 966.56), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 58.2%). In Asia, the pooled OR was 1.41 (95% CI 0.67 to 2.98), with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). In the Middle East, the pooled OR was 4.12 (95% CI 1.31 to 12.89), also with no observed heterogeneity (I² = 0%). The test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (χ² = 8.97, df=2, p=0.0113), suggesting variability in the association across geographical regions (online supplemental figure 2). However, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because several subgroup analyses included a limited number of studies and wide CIs, indicating substantial statistical imprecision.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the study classified as having moderate-to-high risk of bias.12 Following exclusion of this study, the pooled OR for the association between educational status and DFUs remained statistically significant (OR=2.12, 95% CI 1.25 to 3.61), with moderately reduced heterogeneity (I² = 53.6%, p=0.0910), suggesting that the overall findings were relatively robust (online supplemental figure 3).

Association between income level and DFUs

Four studies5,12,13,16 evaluated the association between income level and DFUs. Under the fixed-effect model, individuals with low income had 1.53 times higher odds of developing DFUs (95% CI 1.15 to 2.05). However, under the random-effects model, the pooled association between low income and DFUs was not statistically significant (pooled OR=2.70, 95% CI 0.36 to 20.41; p=0.2164).

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 81.9%, τ² = 1.1976, p=0.0008), indicating considerable variability in effect estimates among studies. Differences in socioeconomic classifications, healthcare access, study populations and confounding adjustment strategies across the included studies may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. In addition, one study reported an exceptionally large effect estimate with a wide CI, which may have influenced the pooled estimate and overall heterogeneity. The corresponding forest plot is presented in figure 3.

Forest plot for income.

Online supplemental figure 4 presents a funnel plot assessing publication bias among the included studies. Although the funnel plot suggested possible asymmetry, interpretation should be approached cautiously because fewer than 10 studies were included in the analysis, limiting the reliability of publication bias assessment.

A subgroup analysis by geographical region (online supplemental figure 5) demonstrated variability in pooled estimates across regions. In Asia,5,16 the pooled OR was 5.70 (95% CI 0.00 to 77 241 964.10), with high heterogeneity (I² = 89.7%, τ² = 3.0207, p=0.0018). In the Middle East,12,13 the pooled OR was 1.45 (95% CI 0.01 to 404.44), with moderate heterogeneity (I² = 66.6%, τ² = 0.2748, p=0.0836). The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (χ² = 1.00, df=1, p=0.3174). However, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because the analyses included very small numbers of studies and extremely wide CIs, indicating substantial statistical instability and imprecision.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the studies classified as having moderate-to-high risk of bias. Following exclusion of these studies, the pooled OR for the association between low income and DFUs was 1.32 (95% CI 0.04 to 39.22), with moderately reduced heterogeneity (I² = 63.9%, τ² = 0.0912, p=0.0959). Despite the reduction in heterogeneity, the wide CI indicates substantial uncertainty in the pooled estimate (online supplemental figure 6).

Association between occupational status and DFUs

Three studies11,13,17 evaluated the association between occupational status and DFUs. The pooled analysis demonstrated that individuals with no formal employment had significantly higher odds of developing DFUs compared with those who were formally employed (pooled OR=1.71, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.77; p=0.0413).

Low heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 21.2%, τ² <0.0001, p=0.2810), indicating relatively consistent effect estimates among studies. The corresponding forest plot is presented in figure 4.

Visual inspection of the funnel plot assessing publication bias among the included studies did not suggest marked asymmetry (online supplemental figure 7); however, interpretation should be approached cautiously because fewer than 10 studies were included in the analysis, limiting the reliability of publication bias assessment.

A subgroup analysis by geographical region (online supplemental figure 8) demonstrated variability in pooled estimates across regions. In Africa,11,17 the pooled OR was 1.81 (95% CI 0.25 to 12.89), with low heterogeneity (I² = 11.9%, τ² = 0.0154, p=0.2867). In the Middle East,13 the pooled OR was 1.10 (95% CI 0.52 to 2.34), with no observed heterogeneity. The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant (χ² = 1.45, df=1, p=0.2285), suggesting no statistically significant regional differences in the association between occupational status and DFUs. However, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of included studies and wide CIs.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding studies classified as having moderate-to-high risk of bias. Following exclusion of these studies, the pooled OR for the association between occupational status and DFUs was 1.60 (95% CI 0.20 to 12.70), with low heterogeneity (I² = 20.3%, τ² = 0.0203, p=0.2627). Although the direction of association remained consistent, the wide CI indicates substantial uncertainty in the pooled estimate (online supplemental figure 9).

Discussion

Principal findings

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised evidence from eight observational studies to evaluate the association between socioeconomic factors and DFUs. The findings demonstrated that lower educational attainment and lack of formal employment were associated with higher odds of DFUs. Specifically, individuals with no formal education had more than twofold higher odds of DFUs compared with those who had received formal education, while unemployment or informal occupational status was associated with increased odds of DFUs. In contrast, although low income demonstrated a directional association with increased odds of DFUs, the pooled association did not reach statistical significance under the random-effects model because of substantial heterogeneity across studies. These findings suggest that some socioeconomic indicators may represent relatively consistent correlates of DFU occurrence, whereas the relationship between income and DFUs appears to vary considerably across healthcare and socioeconomic contexts.

Educational status and DFUs

The pooled finding demonstrating increased odds of DFUs among individuals with no formal education highlights the important role of health literacy and diabetes self-management in the prevention of diabetic complications. Educational attainment may influence an individual’s ability to understand diabetes-related information, adhere to preventive foot-care recommendations, recognise early symptoms of foot complications and seek timely healthcare. These findings are consistent with previous evidence demonstrating that lower educational attainment and limited health literacy are associated with poorer diabetes outcomes and reduced self-management capacity.8,18

Several behavioural and healthcare-access mechanisms may plausibly explain this association. Individuals with limited education may experience reduced access to structured diabetes education programmes, lower awareness of preventive foot-care practices and difficulties navigating healthcare systems. Schillinger et al8 demonstrated that limited health literacy among individuals with diabetes was associated with poorer glycaemic control and reduced understanding of diabetes self-management recommendations, which may subsequently increase vulnerability to diabetic complications, including DFUs.

Moderate heterogeneity was observed across the included studies (I² = 68%), suggesting variability in the strength of association across settings. This heterogeneity may reflect differences in educational systems, healthcare infrastructure, socioeconomic classifications and confounding-adjustment strategies across studies. Regional subgroup analyses also demonstrated variability in pooled estimates, with stronger associations observed in studies conducted in the Middle East compared with studies from Asia and Africa. However, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously because several subgroup analyses included small numbers of studies and wide CIs, indicating substantial statistical imprecision.

Importantly, the association between lower educational attainment and DFUs remained statistically significant following sensitivity analysis after exclusion of the study classified as having moderate-to-high risk of bias. This finding suggests that the observed association was relatively robust and not solely driven by lower-quality evidence.

Occupational status and DFUs

Occupational status was also associated with higher odds of DFUs, with individuals lacking formal employment demonstrating increased odds of DFUs compared with formally employed individuals. The relatively low heterogeneity observed in this analysis (I² = 21.2%) suggests comparatively consistent findings across different socioeconomic and healthcare settings.

This association is likely influenced by healthcare-access mechanisms and the broader ‘precarious work’ framework. Individuals employed in informal sectors frequently lack the social protections associated with formal employment, including employer-sponsored health insurance, paid medical leave and continuity of healthcare access, all of which are important for long-term diabetes management.19 In many low- and middle-income settings, informal employment also involves prolonged standing, physically demanding labour, inadequate footwear and increased exposure to repetitive minor trauma, factors that may contribute to the development of DFUs among individuals with diabetes.20

In addition, individuals engaged in unstable or informal employment may rely more heavily on episodic rather than preventive healthcare utilisation, whereby medical attention is sought primarily for acute complications rather than routine diabetes monitoring and foot screening. Similar patterns have been described within occupational-health literature examining precarious employment and chronic disease management.21 Sensitivity analysis demonstrated that the direction of association remained consistent after exclusion of studies classified as having moderate to high risk of bias, suggesting that occupational instability may represent an important socioeconomic factor associated with DFUs across diverse settings.

Income level and DFUs: a nuanced interpretation

The relationship between low income and DFUs requires cautious interpretation. Although the pooled estimate under the random-effects model suggested increased odds of DFUs among low-income individuals, the association did not reach statistical significance and demonstrated substantial heterogeneity (I² = 81.9%). The divergence observed between the fixed-effect model and random-effects model further highlights the instability of the pooled estimate and suggests that considerable between-study variability influenced the overall findings.

Several factors may explain the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies assessing income. Income classifications differed considerably between studies, and the socioeconomic implications of ‘low income’ likely varied across healthcare systems and regional economic contexts. Differences in healthcare financing structures, access to subsidised diabetes care, social welfare systems and out-of-pocket healthcare expenditures may also have modified the relationship between income and DFUs across settings.

One study from Sri Lanka reported an exceptionally large effect estimate with wide CIs, which substantially influenced the pooled estimate and overall heterogeneity. The extremely wide CIs observed in regional subgroup analyses further indicate substantial statistical instability and imprecision, particularly within analyses containing small numbers of studies. Consequently, the pooled estimate for income should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of absence of association but rather as evidence of inconsistent and context-dependent associations across study settings.

These findings partially align with previous literature examining socioeconomic deprivation and diabetes-related complications.2,22 However, unlike educational attainment, income may function as a more distal socioeconomic factor whose effects are mediated through healthcare access, health literacy, occupational stability and broader structural determinants of health. Broader socioeconomic disruptions, including those associated with the COVID-19 pandemic, may also have amplified income-related healthcare inequalities in some settings, although direct evidence within the included studies was limited.6

Comparison with existing literature

The findings of this review align closely with the social determinants of health (SDOH) framework proposed by Marmot,7 which emphasises that social and economic conditions strongly shape patterns of disease exposure, healthcare access and long-term health outcomes. The observed associations between lower educational attainment, occupational instability and DFUs support the broader evidence demonstrating that chronic disease complications are influenced not only by biological factors but also by structural socioeconomic inequalities.

The finding that educational attainment demonstrated the most robust association with DFUs is consistent with previous evidence linking limited health literacy to poorer diabetes outcomes. Al Sayah et al18 previously reported that inadequate health literacy was strongly associated with poorer glycaemic control and reduced diabetes self-management capacity, findings that closely align with the mechanisms proposed in the present review. These observations suggest that educational attainment may exert a stronger and more stable influence on diabetes-related complications than material wealth alone because education directly affects health knowledge, self-care behaviour and healthcare navigation.

The findings relating to income demonstrated greater inconsistency when compared with previous literature. Zhang et al2 reported stronger associations between socioeconomic deprivation and DFU outcomes in predominantly high-income settings, whereas the present review demonstrated substantial variability across studies conducted largely in LMICs. This divergence may reflect contextual differences in healthcare financing systems, social welfare structures and accessibility of subsidised diabetes care. In some settings, the material advantages associated with higher income may be attenuated by broader limitations in healthcare infrastructure, whereas educational attainment may remain more consistently associated with self-management behaviour and healthcare utilisation.

Furthermore, by prioritising adjusted ORs rather than crude estimates, this review provides an assessment less influenced by major measured confounding variables such as diabetes duration and glycaemic control. Previous epidemiological reviews have highlighted inadequate confounding adjustment as a major source of bias in socioeconomic health research.22 Nevertheless, residual confounding remains possible because adjustment strategies varied considerably across the included studies.

Strengths and limitations

This review has several important strengths. A systematic and comprehensive search strategy was conducted across multiple databases, and study selection, data extraction and methodological appraisal were performed using structured approaches. The use of random-effects meta-analysis models was appropriate given the anticipated heterogeneity across observational studies, and sensitivity analyses strengthened interpretation of the education and occupational status findings. In addition, subgroup analyses provided contextual insight into potential geographical variability in the association between socioeconomic factors and DFUs. Methodological quality assessment using design-appropriate appraisal tools (JBI and NOS) also improved transparency in the evaluation of included studies.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. The evidence base was predominantly derived from LMICs, with limited representation from high-income settings, potentially limiting broader global generalisability. Two of the eight included studies originated from Ethiopia, further contributing to regional concentration within the available evidence base. The relatively small number of included studies limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and publication-bias assessments.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed in the education and income analyses, likely reflecting differences in socioeconomic classifications, healthcare systems, study populations and confounding-adjustment strategies across studies. The extremely wide CIs observed in several regional subgroup analyses indicate substantial statistical imprecision and suggest that these subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Furthermore, most included studies employed cross-sectional designs, limiting the ability to infer temporal or causal relationships between socioeconomic factors and DFUs. Residual confounding also remains possible because adjusted variables differed substantially across studies. Because the adjusted covariates were not consistently reported or standardised across the included studies, differences in confounding adjustment may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity, particularly in the education and income analyses. Although publication bias assessment suggested possible asymmetry in the income analysis, funnel plot interpretation was limited by the small number of included studies.

Public health implications and recommendations

The findings of this review suggest that prevention strategies for DFUs should extend beyond biomedical management alone and incorporate broader socioeconomic determinants of health. Targeted health-literacy interventions and diabetes self-management education programmes may be particularly beneficial for individuals with limited formal education. Similarly, individuals lacking formal employment may require additional support to improve access to preventive diabetes care and routine foot-screening services.

These findings also support consideration of subsidised or universally accessible diabetic foot-care programmes, particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations in low-resource settings. Addressing structural barriers to healthcare access may contribute to earlier identification and management of diabetic foot complications and potentially reduce DFU-related morbidity.

Future research should prioritise standardised measurement of SES to improve comparability across studies. Broader geographical representation, particularly from high-income countries and under-represented low- and middle-income regions, is also needed to strengthen the global applicability of future evidence. Improved multicentre DFU surveillance systems and prospective observational studies may further enhance understanding of the complex relationship between socioeconomic factors and diabetic foot complications.

Recommendations

Clinical practice and patient education

Based on the consistent association observed between lower educational attainment and DFUs, clinical practice should incorporate health literacy-sensitive approaches to diabetes education and foot-care counselling. Healthcare providers may benefit from using simplified educational materials, visual foot-care instructions and ‘teach-back’ communication methods to improve patient understanding and adherence to preventive foot-care practices among individuals with limited formal education.

In addition, routine diabetic consultations may benefit from incorporating broader socioeconomic assessments to help identify patients who experience barriers to healthcare access and continuity of care. Individuals who are informally employed or socioeconomically disadvantaged may require additional preventive support, including more frequent foot screening, reinforced self-management education and earlier referral for podiatric evaluation where available.

Policy and structural interventions

The findings of this review support consideration of expanded access to diabetic foot-screening programmes and preventive foot-care services for socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, particularly within low- and middle-income settings. Reducing financial and structural barriers to preventive diabetes care may improve early identification and management of diabetic foot complications among vulnerable populations who lack stable occupational or healthcare protections.

Broader public-health strategies aimed at improving educational access, strengthening primary diabetes care and reducing healthcare inequalities may also contribute to reducing the long-term burden of DFUs. Given the observed association between socioeconomic disadvantage and DFU occurrence, policies addressing SDOH may complement traditional biomedical approaches to diabetic complication prevention.

Future research

Future studies should prioritise standardised measurement of SES to improve comparability across studies and reduce methodological heterogeneity in pooled analyses. Additional prospective studies with larger and more geographically diverse populations are also needed to improve the precision and generalisability of current evidence.

There is continued need for improved multicentre DFU surveillance systems, particularly within under-represented regions in Africa and Asia where evidence remains limited. Future observational studies should also incorporate more consistent adjustment for major clinical confounders, including diabetes duration, glycaemic control and comorbidities, to strengthen interpretation of socioeconomic associations.

Furthermore, future systematic reviews should continue to incorporate prospective protocol registration, rigorous methodological appraisal and transparent reporting standards to improve reproducibility and reduce the risk of bias within the evidence base.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that lower educational attainment and lack of formal employment were associated with higher odds of DFUs among individuals with diabetes mellitus. Educational status demonstrated the most consistent association across studies, while occupational instability also showed relatively stable associations with DFU occurrence. In contrast, the association between low income and DFUs remained statistically inconclusive because of substantial heterogeneity and instability across studies.

These findings suggest that DFUs are influenced not only by biological factors but also by broader social and structural determinants of health. Interventions aimed at improving health literacy, strengthening access to preventive foot care and reducing barriers to continuity of diabetes management among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations may contribute to reducing the burden of diabetic foot complications. However, the predominance of observational study designs, substantial heterogeneity in some analyses and limited geographical representation warrant cautious interpretation of the findings and highlight the need for further high-quality research in this area.
