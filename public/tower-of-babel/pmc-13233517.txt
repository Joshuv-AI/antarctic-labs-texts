Middle-Term Follow-Up Outcomes of the Customised Temporomandibular Joint-Skull Base Combined Prosthesis by 3D Printing
Authors: Qingyu Xu, Yuxin Zhang, Xiang Wei, Liang Huo, Zixiang Han, Lingtong Bu, Minjie Chen, Jisi Zheng, Chi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe combined lesions of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and skull base pose a considerable clinical challenge, frequently necessitating complex reconstructive procedures, yet established treatment protocols remain insufficient. This case series aims to assess the middle-term clinical safety, accuracy, and efficacy of our own customised temporomandibular joint-skull base combined prosthesis by 3D printing.MethodsThis is a prospective single-centre consecutive case series study and planned to enrol 12 patients. Patients with TMJ-skull base combined lesions who visited the Department of Oral Surgery of our hospital from September 2016 to April 2024 were recruited. General conditions, clinical examinations, and radiological evaluations were conducted preoperatively and at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and possibly beyond 3 years postoperatively. Computed tomography scans were used to assess prosthesis position and bone contact. The 10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS) was used to assess pain, diet, and mandibular function. Objective evaluations included maximum internal opening, deviation of opening, and maximum mandibular forward and lateral movement. SPSS software was employed for data analysis.ResultsA total of 12 patients (6 females and 6 males, with an average age of 46.58 ± 12.17 years) were included; all patients underwent prosthesis implantation. After a follow-up period of 3.85 ± 2.63 years, patients showed no adverse symptoms such as infection and cerebrospinal fluid leakage. The occlusion and face type were stable. The prosthesis did not exhibit significant loosening, displacement, or fracture, and all had good bone integration. Postoperative pain, diet, mandibular function, and maximum internal opening underwent significant enhancement, whereas deviation of opening and mandibular movement towards the non-diseased side remained unchanged.ConclusionThe customised TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis developed in this study exhibited favourable mid-term clinical safety, accuracy, and efficacy in its application.Clinical RelevanceOur findings offer promising prospects in and clinical translational value in terms of development of TMJ and skull base reconstruction technology in future.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint, Skull base, Customised prosthesis, Clinical application, Case series, Oral and maxillofacial surgery

Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a complex and crucial anatomical structure, situated anterior to the external auditory canal, posterior to the zygomatic arch, and lateral to the inferior temporal fossa. It is composed of the articular fossa, condyle, and articular disc. Anatomically, the articular fossa, which separates the condyle from the lateral cranial fossa, is a thin and translucent area of the temporal bone.1 Therefore, pathologies affecting the joint and the skull base often interact, leading to destruction of the joint structure and erosion of the skull base.2, 3, 4 Defects in the TMJ and skull base are typically caused by tumours or tumour-like lesions such as synovial chondromatosis, giant cell lesion, osteochondroma, and synovial chondrosarcoma.1,5, 6, 7 Patients may also suffer from a range of complications, including but not limited to preauricular swelling, hearing loss, severe headaches, neurological numbness and paralysis, varying degrees of limited mouth opening, and even cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, secondary meningitis, and brain injury.3,8,9

Therefore, the management of combined TMJ and skull base defects poses a great challenge for neurosurgeons, otolaryngologists, and oral and maxillofacial surgeons. Traditionally, early treatment approaches for such lesions tended to involve simple excision of the lesion and adjacent involved structures, with no or only minimal reconstruction, such as using fat or soft tissue flaps for filling.2,9,10 Such methods obviously fail to restore patients' normal bone structure and function. With the advancement of reconstruction techniques, autologous bone and artificial joint prosthesis can effectively reconstruct the TMJ, and artificial joint prosthesis reconstruction has gained popularity due to its minimal trauma and stable therapeutic effects.3,11 However, currently available mature TMJ prostheses on the market primarily focus on the reconstruction of the joint itself, and their ability to address complex defects that span the TMJ and extend to the skull base is limited.11,12 This has led to a significant neglect of skull base reconstruction. Some scholars address combined defects by only reconstructing the condyle while using soft tissue to fill the skull base, an approach that struggles to restore the biomechanical transmission and joint movement of the craniomandibular joint.8 Some scholars, including our team, have proposed using autologous free bone grafts to reconstruct the skull base simultaneously with joint reconstruction, achieving good therapeutic outcomes.2 However, this method is limited by factors such as significant surgical trauma, donor site morbidity, surgical complexity, and high risk, which restrict the widespread clinical application of autologous bone reconstruction.

Since 2010, advancements in computer-aided design and 3D printing manufacturing technology have facilitated the emergence of expanded custom prosthesis based on the principle of TMJ prosthesis as a new trend, offering a reliable concurrent reconstruction method for cranio-articular combined defects.11,12 However, compared to traditional total joint prosthesis, relevant research in this field is limited, primarily confined to sporadic case reports or small-sample case series studies, lacking large-scale clinical validation and follow-up data. Consensus on its design and application has yet to be reached.13, 14, 15 Our research team successfully developed a 3D-printed customised temporomandibular joint-skull base combined prosthesis in 2016 and conducted some small-scale clinical exploratory applications, with preliminary results confirming the combined prosthesis's effectiveness and safety.4,12

Based on previous studies, this study aims to further optimise the design, expand its clinical application scope, increase the number of cases, extend the follow-up period, and comprehensively evaluate its clinical safety, effectiveness and accuracy. It is hoped to provide valuable insights into the development prospect of the TMJ and skull base reconstruction.

Methods

Participants

This is a prospective, single-centre, consecutive case series study. All procedures performed in the study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and national research committee and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study has received ethical approval from our hospital and is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry. Each patient was informed in detail about the purpose of the surgery, the planned process, the expected recovery period and the possible complications before participating in the study, and signed an informed consent form on the basis of full understanding.

Patient recruitment was conducted from September 2016 to April 2024 in the Department of Oral Surgery of our hospital. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are as follows:

Inclusion criteria

1.Transcranial and maxillofacial enhanced computed tomography (CT) images confirmed the presence of TMJ-skull base lesions and skull base perforation, requiring TMJ-skull base combined radical treatment.

3.Agreed to accept the customised temporomandibular joint-skull base combined prosthesis implantation surgery.

1.Patients with allergic reactions to the component materials of the prosthesis.

2.There is an infection or suspected infection in the implant site to avoid increasing the surgical risk and affecting the stability of the prosthesis.

Preoperative preparation and surgical procedure

Enhanced pre-processing of CT data

All patients underwent enhanced comprehensive craniomaxillofacial CT scan (GE Healthcare, Buckinghamshire, England), with a slice thickness of 0.625 mm, slice spacing of 0.625 mm, tube voltage of 120 KV, tube current of 350 mAs, and pixel size of 512 * 512. The acquired CT data, in DICOM format, were imported into Mimics software version 18.0 (Materialize Co., Leuven, Belgium) for meticulous calculation and 3D models, including the entire skull, mandible, key blood vessels, nerves and lesions, were reconstructed to identify the extent of lesions.

Preoperative preparation and prosthesis design

As detailed in our prior research,4,16 a simulated surgery was conducted in Mimics software prior to the actual procedure, adhering to the fundamental principles of surgical resection. This simulation aimed to delineate the fenestration range of the temporal bone, the temporary zygomatic arch disarticulation, the incisal margin of hard tissue for complete resection of the tumour, and design the osteotomy guide plate.

Our prosthesis is the first attempt in this field. The skull base, glenoid fossa, condylar head, and ramus components of the combined prosthesis were designed according to the principles and experience of our total TMJ prosthesis design. The skull base component is used for repairing cranial base bone defects. A certain gap was designed between the inner side of the prosthesis and the skull bone to avoid bone resistance during the implantation of the prosthesis and to facilitate drainage. Four to 5 wing plates are individually designed to closely adhere to the bone surface of the residual zygomatic arch and the squamous part of the temporal bone, serving the purposes of stress conduction and prosthesis fixation; the fossa and condylar head component form the articular motion surface, which is used to restore joint motion function; the mandibular ramus component is individually fitted to the posterior bone surface of the mandibular ramus, with 6 to 8 screw holes designed on it for fixation. The prosthesis was made using five-axis milling and 3D printing technology. It is then polished, sandblasted, and examined on a 3D printed model of the patient's whole skull. Finally, the prosthesis was sterilised and packaged in preparation for implantation.

Collection of patient information

Demographic information, such as a patient's age, gender, diagnosis, and medical history, is obtained and recorded through an electronic medical record system. Surgical information was recorded, including lesion extent and prosthesis type, surgical site, surgical time, amount of blood loss, and facial nerve injury.

Evaluation of clinical outcomes

Evaluation of clinical safety

General clinical examination was carried out at 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, and over 3 years postoperative follow-up to evaluate and record postoperative wound healing, pain, CSF leakage, infection, bleeding and other common complications; a standardised photography recording method was taken to record facial morphology and occlusal relationship, and the facial nerve function was evaluated using the House-Brackmann facial nerve function scale.17 CT and panoramic radiographs are conducted immediately, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and over 3 years after surgery (if possible). All scans are completed in the patient's natural and stable centric occlusion state (habitual posterior teeth occlusion, with the mandible in the resting neutral position). On the sagittal and coronal CT images, the positional relationship between the condyle head component and the skull base component was evaluated.

Evaluation of clinical efficacy

Joint function assessment was performed using previously reported methods,12,18 including subjective and objective measures. Follow-up was performed before surgery, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, 2 years, and over 3 years (if possible) after surgery.

A 10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS) is employed to assess 1) pain, (2) mandibular function, and (3) diet. Objective evaluation indicators include (1) maximal interincisal opening (MIO); (2) maximum forward movement (MFM); (3) lateral movement, including moving towards the diseased side (MDS) and moving towards the non-diseased side (MNS); (4) mouth opening deviation (MOD). All indicators are in millimetres.

Statistical analysis

Using SPSS 21.0 software, all data were measured twice. For demography, frequency was used for categorical variables and mean ± SD was used for continuous variables. The paired t-test in one-way analysis of variance was used to compare the differences of subjective and objective evaluation indicators before and after surgery and between follow-up time points. A P-value <.05 was statistically significant (*P < .05, **P < .01, ***P < .001).

This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS Guideline.19

Results

General patient information

Our study encompassed a comprehensive cohort of 12 patients, consisting of 6 females and 6 males, with an average age of 46.58 years (range: 24-68 years). At baseline, the mean duration of disease was 3.34 ± 5.16 years (range: 5 months to 20 years), and 3 patients had previously undergone joint surgery. Based on postoperative pathologic findings, 8 patients were diagnosed with giant cell lesions, 2 patients with osteochondroma, 1 patient with synovial chondroma, and 1 patient with atypical cartilaginous tumours. During the operation, due to the close anatomical relationship between the tumour and the facial nerve, the facial nerve was cut temporally in 2 patients, and subsequently underwent suture repair. The average operation time was 265.38 ± 63.95 minutes, and the average intraoperative blood loss was 845.83 ± 348.48 mL. The average length of hospitalisation was 13.92 ± 4.11 days. The mean follow-up time of the patients was 3.85 ± 2.63 years (range: 6-96 months), as shown in the Table.

Clinical safety evaluation

All patients had good wound healing, no CSF leakage, intracranial and skull base hematoma, secondary meningitis, wound infection and other serious complications. Postoperative CT examination showed no tumour recurrence. The relationship between facial shape and occlusal was stable and consistent with that before the operation in all patients. Immediately after surgery, 2 patients who experienced temporary facial nerve cutting were classified as House-Brackmann (HB) grade III (moderate dysfunction) and HB grade IV (moderate to severe dysfunction), respectively. Although neurological function improved during follow-up, residual permanent damage was noted. In contrast, among the 10 patients without facial nerve dissection, 8 exhibited signs of facial nerve injury (80%), all at HB grade II (mild dysfunction). These 8 patients fully recovered within 3 to 12 months post-surgery, with a mean recovery time of 6.50 ± 2.58 months.

Follow-up CT scans revealed no signs of prosthesis displacement, fracture, loosening, rejection, or the need for revision. Additionally, there was no low-density imaging between the prosthesis and the host bone, indicating good osseointegration (Figure 2).

Clinical efficacy evaluation

Follow-up visits were conducted for all patients at 1 month, 3 months and 6 months after the surgery. At 1 year and 2 years after the surgery, follow-up was completed for 10 patients, but 2 patients were lost to follow-up. At the longest postoperative observation time point, a total of 8 patients participated in the follow-up. The mean pain VAS score prior to surgery was 4.75 ± 2.68, subsequently decreasing to 2.00 ± 1.20 at 1 month and further to 1.60 ± 0.49 at 3 months post-surgery. Notably, pain scores demonstrated a significant reduction to 0.71 ± 0.45 at 6 months (P < .05), then remaining stable during subsequent follow-ups of 0.50 ± 0.50, 0.60 ± 0.80, and 0.86 ± 0.83, respectively. This indicates that the operation is effective and stable in relieving the pain of patients (Figure 3A).

Regarding the diet VAS, patients exhibited a score of 5.41 ± 2.28, which improved postoperatively to 3.14 ± 2.42 at 1 month. By 3 months postoperatively, the diet score had significantly declined to 2.00 ± 0.63 (P < .05). It further decreased to 1.25 ± 1.03 at 6 months after surgery. And then stabilised at 0.75 ± 0.82, 0.80 ± 0.98, and 0.57 ± 1.05 (Figure 3B).

Mandibular function showed a similar trend. Before surgery, patients had an average mandibular function score of 5.17 ± 2.41. One month after surgery, this score was 2.86 ± 1.36, which then significantly declined to 1.80 ± 0.40 at 3 months post-surgery (P < 0.05). The score further decreased to 1.14 ± 1.12 at 6 months postoperatively. And stabilised at 1.00 ± 0.71, 1.40 ± 0.49, 1.28 ± 0.88 during follow-up periods (Figure 3C).

Preoperative MIO measured 21.42 ± 10.37 mm. 1 month postoperatively, MIO was slightly decreased to 21.29 ± 5.85 mm, then increased to 27.80 ± 2.71 mm at 3 months, and significantly enhanced to 29.71 ± 4.46 mm at 6 months postoperatively (P < .05). Then MIO stabilised at 1, 2, and over 3 years follow-up of 32.75 ± 2.86 mm, 33.40 ± 2.73 mm, and 32.88 ± 4.17 mm, respectively (Figure 4A).

Both preoperative and postoperative mouth opening exhibited deviation towards the diseased side. The mean mouth opening deviation (MOD) preoperatively was 2.42 ± 1.75 mm. The data remained unchanged at each follow-up time point. To be specific, across follow-up time points, MOD averaged 2.29 ± 2.66 mm, 3.00 ± 2.45 mm, 2.14 ± 1.81 mm, 1.75 ± 1.79 mm, 2.40 ± 2.33 mm and 1.62 ± 2.18 mm, respectively (Figure 4B).

Preoperative mean MFM was 4.50 ± 2.29 mm, at follow-up points it fluctuated between 3.00 and 4.88 mm, to be exact, the MFM average values are 3.57 ± 2.06 mm, 3.00 ± 1.10 mm, 3.14 ± 0.99 mm, 3.25 ± 0.43 mm, 4.20 ± 1.17 mm, and 4.88 ± 0.93 mm, respectively, but was not significantly different from the preoperative value (Figure 4C). This indicated that there was no significant improvement or deterioration.

Preoperative mean mandible movement towards the diseased side (MDS) was 4.33 ± 2.17, 1 month postoperatively slightly decreased to 3.14 ± 1.36 mm, but recovered to 3.60 ± 1.02 mm 6 months after surgery. Subsequently, MDS continued to recover, reaching 5.43 ± 2.77 mm at 6 months, 4.75 ± 1.09 mm at 1 year, 4.80 ± 0.98 mm at 2 years, and 5.50 ± 1.32 mm over 3 years after surgery. Compared with preoperative MDS, these values fluctuated but did not differ significantly overall (Figure 4D).

Preoperative mean mandible movement towards the non-diseased side (MNS) was 2.88 ± 1.56 mm, fluctuating slightly postoperatively to 1.71 ± 1.28 mm, 2.20 ± 1.72 mm, 2.00 ± 1.93 mm at 1, 3, 6 months postoperatively. Subsequently, MNS recovered somewhat, reaching 3.50 ± 1.12 mm, 2.80 ± 1.33 mm, 3.50 ± 1.73 mm at 1, 2 and over 3 years postoperatively (Figure 4E).

Discussion

The close proximity of the temporomandibular joint to the skull base means that primary tumours in either the TMJ or the skull base can lead to combined destruction in the temporomandibular joint-skull base (TMJ-SB) region.1 The large defects left after lesion resection pose a significant challenge for surgical reconstruction. In recent years, scholars have begun to explore the development of TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis, which has gradually become a trend in the field of craniofacial bone and joint reconstruction.14 Since 2016, our research group has been dedicated to the development and optimisation of customised TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis. This study included 12 consecutive cases from 2016 to 2024, with an average follow-up period of 3.85 years. The aim was to evaluate the mid-term effectiveness and safety of its clinical application.

The success of prosthesis replacement depends primarily on several crucial factors: the rationality of design principles, the choice of manufacturing materials and methods, and the accuracy of surgical procedures. In designing our prosthesis, the mandibular and condylar components were based on our previous design concepts and experience of total TMJ prostheses.12,20, 21, 22 The skull base component uses a solid plate structure to enhance the supporting force and effectively isolate the intracranial and external environment.16 As the temporal bone approach was used to remove the tumour, we designed a wing plate to assist fixation and reduction of the bone plate and/or a temporal bone plate to repair the defect on the skull base component, and designed the direction of the wing plate according to the stress distribution of the craniomaxillofacial bone and joint.16 These designs have been preliminarily verified for their rationality and safety through our early clinical studies. As far as we know, the research field of TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis is still in its nascent stages of exploration, and there is no unified consensus on the design principles.13,23 In 2018, Elledge et al. introduced the concept of total TMJ replacement (eTMJR) based on clinical practice, extending the joint prosthesis to the skull base and mandible. They proposed a classification system,24 which was later refined by Higginson et al.25,26 Among the classifications proposed, the FT type shares certain similarities with our combined prosthesis in design and function,13,14 but the specific design schemes were not mentioned in the relevant literature. Current reports primarily consist of case reports and small case series, with significant variations in the specific designs, manufacturing processes, and surgical protocols employed. Therefore, our research will provide some useful references and contributions to the development of this field.

In terms of material selection and processing, we employ titanium alloy powder (TC4), cobalt-chromium-molybdenum alloy (CoCrMo), and ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE). The joint socket made of UHMWPE and the joint head made of CoCrMo are precision-crafted using five-axis machining technology. The titanium alloy mandibular handle and skull base are manufactured through metal 3D printing. The joint head and mandibular stem are connected using Morse taper, while the skull base-joint fossa prosthesis, composed of metal and non-metallic materials, is joined through snap-fit or friction stir welding. In clinical applications, we adopt guide plate-assisted precise osteotomy and endoscope-assisted prosthesis fixation techniques, along with digital medical preoperative virtual analysis and surgical simulation technologies. These materials and methods have undergone extensive middle or long-term validation by our team during the early-stage research and development of total joint prosthesis, contributing to enhanced prosthesis performance and improved surgical precision and minimally invasiveness.4,12,16,21,27

To thoroughly evaluate the clinical applicability of our prosthesis and the rationality of our surgical strategies, we collected data on operation duration, blood loss, and other relevant indicators, and investigated complications as well as postoperative facial contour, occlusion, and prosthesis position. Our results indicated that patients had an average intraoperative blood loss of 846 mL, an average operation duration of 265 minutes, and an average hospital stay of 14 days. The effective control of blood loss, reduced operation time, and shortened hospital stay were attributed to digital design, accurate bone resection assisted by guiding plates, and the rapid installation of customised prostheses. During an average follow-up period of 3 years, no cases of infection or CSF leakage were reported in our patient population. In comparison to previous research that reported CSF leakage rates ranging from 0% to 12.5% after simple skull base reconstruction using traditional materials such as soft tissue flaps and titanium meshes, our prosthesis demonstrated its advantages in skull base sealing and enhancing surgical success rates.28, 29, 30, 31 Although 87.5% of the patients had symptoms of facial nerve injury of varying degrees immediately after surgery, most of the patients had mild injuries and recovered within 3 to 12 months after surgery, which was consistent with our previous short-term observation results, indicating the safety of our surgical strategy.16 The patient's postoperative facial shape and occlusal relationship were effectively maintained, and no prosthesis repair occurred during follow-up. CT examination showed that the position of the prosthesis was stable, and bone integration was good, which further confirmed the safety and effectiveness of customised TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis in mid-term clinical application.

The subjective and objective evaluations of postoperative clinical symptoms and joint function in patients have demonstrated the remarkable effectiveness of our prosthesis. In terms of subjective evaluation, our data indicates that within 3 years postoperatively, patients experienced an 82.46% reduction in pain, an 80.62% improvement in dietary function, and an 82.96% enhancement in mandibular function. These findings align with our previous short-term research, further validating the mid-term efficacy of the surgery in alleviating related symptoms.16 In terms of objective measurement, patients' maximum interincisal opening (MIO) increased by 11.46 mm, from 21.42 mm preoperatively to 32.88 mm. It is important to note that significant changes in pain, diet, jaw function, and MIO were observed only 3 to 6 months after surgery. This may be due to the relatively extensive tissue damage during surgery, as well as postoperative pain, oedema, and scarring, which together affect patient recovery.32,33 At the same time, these data also provide an important reference for the formulation and adjustment of postoperative rehabilitation plans. We recommend that postoperative mouth opening exercises be maintained for at least 6 months to promote better recovery. Furthermore, as the patient's MIO gradually enhances, we have observed that the mandibular forward and lateral movement (MFM, MDS, MNS) maintained a stable level without exhibiting a marked trend of improvement, while MOD persists, which is also consistent with our previous research results. The primary reason for these observations may lie in the extensive resection area during the cranio-maxillofacial joint reconstruction surgery. This procedure often involves the removal of not only the joint itself but also parts of the skull base, the lateral pterygoid muscle, sections of the masseter and temporalis muscles, as well as the ligaments surrounding the joint. The absence of the lateral pterygoid muscle, in particular, leads to a loss of the force required for the joint to move in the contralateral direction, as supported by previous studies.34,35 This muscle plays a crucial role in facilitating lateral movements of the mandible, and its removal can significantly impact the joint's mobility and stability. Despite the absence of any further detrimental effects on patients' daily routines, future research and improvements should focus on how to promote muscle attachment to the implanted prosthesis and how to further restore muscle power function to develop more efficacious treatment strategies to further elevate patients’ quality of life.

Although this study has achieved certain results, there are still some shortcomings which need to be further perfected and improved in future research. First, the sample size of this study is relatively small, including only 12 patients, which may affect the universality and reliability of the study results. In future studies, larger sample sizes and multi-centre studies are needed to more fully evaluate the clinical efficacy and safety of this prosthesis. Secondly, although the follow-up time of this study was 3 years, and the longest follow-up was about 8 years, compared with the prosthesis life expectancy, this follow-up time was still short, and a longer follow-up time was required. Finally, this study focused on the effect of customised TMJ-skull base prosthesis, but did not set up a control group for comparison. In future studies, it is possible to consider using autogenous bone grafting as a control group to more accurately assess the advantages and disadvantages of this prosthesis.

Conclusion

Overall, this study shows that 3D printed customised TMJ-skull base combined prosthesis has shown good safety and stability in mid-term follow-up clinical applications, providing a new solution for TMJ-skull base lesions.
