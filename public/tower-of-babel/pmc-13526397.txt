Identification and Comparative Evaluation of Analgesic Components in Toad Venom on Inflammatory Pain
Authors: Yuting Wen, Ruixiang Li, Xialian Cui, Fang Zhang, Jiange Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAlthough toad venom has been utilized in traditional Chinese medicine for centuries to manage pain, a systematic evaluation of its material basis against inflammatory pain remains lacking. This study sought to rigorously assess the analgesic efficacy of toad venom extracts and their active constituents, identify the principal bioactive components, and explore their potential underlying mechanisms through the integration of network pharmacology and experimental observation, thereby providing a scientific foundation for its potential therapeutic application.Materials and MethodsActivity-guided fractionation and LC-Q-TOF-MS were employed to identify the key active compounds of toad venom. Analgesic activities of the extracts, fractions, and selected monomers were evaluated using a carrageenan-induced mouse pain model. Network pharmacology was utilized to predict potential therapeutic targets. The predicted mechanisms were subsequently validated in vitro using lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated RAW 264.7 macrophages and in vivo across distinct murine inflammatory models. Finally, a preliminary toxicological evaluation was conducted to assess in vivo safety.ResultsEthyl acetate and methanol fractions were identified as the primary active extracts. Subsequent screening revealed that Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin exhibited the most prominent analgesic properties among the tested monomers. Focusing on an examined subset of the network pharmacology predictions, in vitro assays indicated that both compounds effectively downregulated the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis. Notably, Resibufogenin demonstrated superior anti-inflammatory efficacy and significantly mitigated oxidative stress by restoring cellular antioxidant defenses (GSH and TEAC). In vivo evaluations further showed that Resibufogenin robustly attenuated carrageenan-induced paw edema, xylene-induced ear swelling, and LPS-induced systemic inflammation. Furthermore, therapeutic dosages of Resibufogenin (3 and 10 mg/kg) exhibited no detectable toxicity under the tested conditions without inducing observable pathological lesions in major organs.ConclusionThrough origin screening, bioassay-guided fractionation, and LC-Q-TOF-MS analysis, Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin were identified as the principal analgesic constituents of toad venom. Integrated bioinformatic and experimental analyses suggest that their efficacy is associated with anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Notably, Resibufogenin, exhibiting no detectable toxicity under the tested conditions and alleviated inflammatory pain, which may involve the suppression of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB cascade and alleviating oxidative stress, highlighting its potential as a promising lead compound for novel analgesic therapeutics.

Introduction

Pain is a pathological symptom related to tissue damage that adversely impacts quality of life and adds to elevated healthcare costs. It frequently coexists with various diseases, such as inflammatory disorders, cancer, and gout.1 Inflammation is regarded as the primary cause of pain. Inflammatory factors could trigger specific nerve fibers, transmit signals to the brain, and thereby induce pain.2 Currently, the mainstay of clinical pharmacotherapy for inflammatory pain is nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). However, their analgesic efficacy is limited and frequently accompanied with adverse effects such as gastrointestinal and renal injury. Furthermore, NSAIDs exhibit a “ceiling effect” for pain management. Beyond a certain dosage, any increase does not enhance analgesic efficacy but only elevates the risk of adverse events. As a result, physicians and patients are often faced with challenging trade-offs between effective pain control and the tolerability of side effects.3 Hence, it is imperative to develop novel analgesic drugs with improved safety profiles.

Traditional Chinese medicines exhibit considerable therapeutic potential for managing various diseases, which is largely attributed to their multiple bioactivities, including immunomodulatory, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and antitumor activities. Chansu is a traditional medicinal substance primarily derived from the skin secretions of B. melanostictus and Bufo gargarizans, which has been used in China for over two centuries to alleviate pain caused by tumor or skin infection.4,5 It has been widely incorporated into various formulations due to its outstanding biological activities.6–8 The anti-inflammatory and antinociceptive effects of toad venom and its purified bioactive ingredients have been extensively investigated in recent years. Xu et al employed the hot plate and the acetic acid-induced writhing test in mice to determine the analgesic effects of toad venom. The results showed that a significant reduction in writhing frequency and a marked increase in pain threshold in treated mice.9 A toad venom ointment was discovered to alleviate adjuvant-induced arthritis in rats by regulating key immune mediators including interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), while concurrently exerting significant analgesic effects.10 The activation of the central serotonin nervous system by toad venom has demonstrated efficacy in reducing acetic acid-induced writhing responses and neuropathic pain resulting from sciatic nerve ligation in mice.11 Huachansu Injection, which contains the primary active ingredient sourced from the extract of toad venom, has been shown to reduce serum levels of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) in a mouse colitis mode, and it also alleviated chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy through the mitigation of inflammatory responses.12,13 The indolealkylamines obtained from toad venom had significant and dose-dependent analgesic effects in models of hyperalgesia caused by mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli.14 Furthermore, both ethanolic and aqueous extracts of toad venom secretions effectively inhibited the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells p65 (NF-κB p65) and suppressed the production of inflammatory cytokines in U937 cells stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and phorbol ester.15 Thus, toad venom represents a promising candidate for the development of novel analgesic agents. However, its therapeutic development is hindered by the limited animal sources and complex chemical composition. Chemically, toad venom is a complex mixture composed of bufadienolides, sterols, alkaloids, peptides, proteins, organic acids and amino acids.16,17 Given this complexity, pinning down the key bioactive compounds poses a central challenge in its research. Therefore, elucidating its active constituents and their mechanisms of action is of paramount importance.

Bufadienolides are recognized as the primary bioactive constituents responsible for the pharmacological effects of toad venom.18,19 Among these, Cinobufagin has been extensively characterized for its analgesic properties. Preclinical evaluations, including the acetic acid-induced writhing and hot plate assays, have confirmed its ability to alleviate somatic and visceral pain. Moreover, studies have indicated its role in relieving cancer-related pain, likely through the regulation of endorphin-mediated signaling, thereby highlighting its potential as a lead compound for pain management.20–22 Additionally, Cinobufagin has been shown to reduce inflammatory response in a bleomycin-treated mouse model and modulate immune responses in macrophages.23 Gao et al demonstrated that Resibufogenin exerts anti-inflammatory activities by inhibiting the NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/activator protein 1 (AP-1) signaling pathways, thereby reducing the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators in macrophages.24 Studies have indicated that bufalin alleviates neuropathic pain by directly targeting the P2X purinoceptor 7 (P2X7) receptor, leading to the inhibition of transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) activation and a reduction in the release of inflammatory cytokines.25 Bufalin has demonstrated potent analgesic efficacy across multiple pain models, including acetic acid-induced writhing, hot plate, and formalin tests, potentially through suppressing NF-κB activation and downstream expression of inflammatory mediators such as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2).26 However, current research has predominantly focused on some isolated individual compounds, with limited comprehensive assessment of active fractions for comparative analysis and identification of the most potent analgesic component.

Consequently, elucidating the pharmacodynamic material basis of toad venom is imperative for establishing rigorous quality control standards, thereby ensuring the stability, reliability, and reproducibility of its clinical analgesic efficacy. Therefore, in the present study, a systematic investigation was designed. We first conducted a geographical origin screening, followed by an activity-guided evaluation of fractionated extracts using a mouse model of inflammatory pain. Subsequently, LC-Q-TOF-MS was employed to characterize the chemical profiles of the most potent fractions, leading to the identification of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin as the principal active constituents. Furthermore, by integrating network pharmacology with comprehensive in vitro and in vivo experiments, we systematically explored their anti-inflammatory and analgesic mechanisms, particularly focusing on their association with the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis and oxidative stress. Coupled with a preliminary toxicological assessment, this work provides a robust scientific foundation for the traditional application of toad venom and highlights its key active monomers as promising lead compounds with no detectable toxicity under the tested conditions for novel analgesic drug discovery.

Material and Methods

Animals

KunMing (KM) mice (aged 4 weeks, 20–22 g) were purchased from the university’s Experimental Animal Center. The mice were maintained in comfortable conditions in a 12-hour light/dark cycle environment, with free access to food and water throughout the study. All animal experiments were approved by the ethical committee of the Shanghai University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (Approval No. PZSHUTCM2310080001). This investigation adhered to the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 for preclinical animal studies.

Chemicals and Reagents

Toad venom, including samples CS-1 (Batch no.170714-1), CS-2 (Batch no.180702-1, Huaren), CS-3 (Batch no.180423-1, Liaoning), and CS-4 (Batch no.180705-1, Shangqiu) were provided by Shanghai Hutchison Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd, and all of them was dissolved in a 0.05% (w/v) sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) solution. Cinobufagin (Cat. No. B20542), Resibufogenin (Cat. No. B21666), Bufalin (Cat. No. B20152), Gamabufotalin (Cat. No. B20542), Bufotaline (Cat. No. B B20153), Telocinobufagin (Cat. No. B21624), Cinobufotalin (Cat. No. B20541), Bufotalidin (Cat. No. B24688), Arenobufagin (Cat. No. B21113) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. 5-Hydroxytryptamine (Cat. No. DC0382), N-Methyl Serotonin (Cat. No. DJ0568), Bufotenin (Cat. No. DC0175), and Bufotenidine (Cat. No. DC0209) was purchased from Sichuan Lemeitian Pharmacy Co., Ltd. All the compounds were dissolved in 1:4:5:90 dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), ethanol, castor oil ethoxylated (Cat. No.61791-12-6, Adamas, China), and saline. Acetonitrile of mass spectrometry quality was procured from J.T. Baker (USA). Deionized water was produced with a Milli-Q purification equipment (Merck, USA). Carrageenan (Cat. No. 22049-5G-F, Merck, USA) was solubilized in saline to generate a 2% solution and maintained at ambient temperature for 24 hours before utilization.

Mechanical Allodynia Behavioral Tests

The mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT) of mice was evaluated using a dynamic plantar tactile apparatus (37450, Ugo Basile, Italy). Before testing, each animal was acclimatized for 2 hours in a separate plexiglass container (10 × 10×14 cm) with a metal mesh base. During the assessment, a calibrated probe was applied to the plantar surface of the mice hindpaw, with the force escalating from 0 g until a clear withdrawal reflex was observed. The force value at the moment of paw retraction was automatically recorded as the MWT (g). A baseline MWT was recorded before drug treatment. Subsequently, the MWT was re-tested at 30, 60, 120, 240 and 360 min post-administration. To prevent mice potential tissue injury, a cutoff force of 50 g was set. For every mouse, five measurements were taken at 5-minute intervals, and the final MWT was calculated as the mean of these five readings. The experimenters were blinded during the entire assessment process.

Carrageenan-Induced Inflammatory Pain Model

Carrageenan-induced inflammatory pain mouse model was established following previously reported.27 Briefly, the mice were given an intraplantar injection with 20 μL of 2% carrageenan. After 20 min, the drug treatment for the mice was conducted. The TWL or WMT was subsequently tested at 30, 60, 120, 240 and 360 min post-administration.

Xylene-Induced Mice Ear Edema

Xylene-induced mice ear edema model utilized for the in vivo anti-inflammatory assay. Mice were randomly divided into four groups and treated intragastrically for 4 days as follows: negative control group (Ctrl), positive control group (dexamethasone, Dex, 5 mg/kg), and two dose groups of Resibufogenin (3 and 10 mg/kg). 20 minutes after the last administration, xylene (50 μL) was coated to the left ear and induced for 15 min, whereas the right ear was not treated. Subsequently, mice were slaughtered via cervical dislocation, we used a hole punch to make ear disks (7.0 mm) and weighed. Auricular swelling was determined by the following formula: Mouse auricular swelling =weight of left ear weight of right ear.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-Induced Acute Inflammation Model Mouse

Mice were randomly divided into five groups and treated intragastrically for 4 days as follows: negative control group (Ctrl), model group (0.5 mg/kg LPS), positive control group (Dex, 5 mg/kg+0.5 mg/kg LPS), and two dose groups of Resibufogenin (3 and 10 mg/kg+0.5 mg/kg LPS). Following overnight fasting, the final dose was administered. Thirty minutes post-treatment, systemic inflammation was induced by intravenous in-jection of LPS (0.5 mg/kg), while the negative control received saline. Two hours later, the blood was collected. Serum pro-inflammatory cytokines were measured by ELISA.

Hot Plate Test

The hot-plate test was conducted following an established protocol.28 Female mice were first pre-screened by placing them on a heated surface (55 ± 0.5 °C); only those exhibiting a hind paw licking response within 30 seconds were selected for experiments. During the formal test, each mouse was confined to a transparent plexiglass cylinder (20 cm in diameter) on the hot plate. The thermal withdrawal latency (TWL), defined as the interval from stimulus application to the initiation of hind paw shaking, licking, or jumping, was documented as the pain threshold. A baseline TWL was recorded before drug treatment. Subsequently, the TWL was re-tested at 30, 60, 120, 240 and 360 min post-administration. The experimenters were blinded during the entire assessment process.

Preparation of Fractionated Extracts from Toad Venom

11 g toad venom was exhaustively extracted with anhydrous methanol (4 × 500 mL) using ultrasonication. The amalgamated extracts were concentrated via rotary evaporation, resulting in 5.5 g of crude material, further separated using silica gel column chromatography. Elution was conducted using petroleum ether (PE, 2 L), ethyl acetate (EA, 6 L), and methanol (MeOH, 8 L). The respective fractions, after solvent removal, yielded 13 mg (PE), 2.1 g (EA), and 2.9 g (MeOH).

Liquid Chromatography-Quadrupole-Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (LC–Q–TOF–MS) Analysis

MeOH fractions and EA fractions of toad venom: The LC-MS analysis was performed using an Agilent 1290 HPLC system and a 6545 UHD quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrum (Agilent Technologies, USA). A Thermo Fisher Scientific C18 column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 µm) was employed for separation. The mobile phase comprised (A) acetonitrile and (B) 0.1% formic acid in water (v/v). A gradient elution was applied at 0.3 mL/min as follows: 0–1 min, 1%A; 1–10 min, 1–15%A; 10–15 min, 15–35%A; 15–22 min, 35–45%A; 22–26 min, 45–95%A; 26–28 min, 95%A; 28–30 min, 95–100%A. The sample injection volume was 1 µL. Mass spectrometry detection was carried out in positive mode with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source. Data were acquired in a data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, where the five most abundant ions from the full MS scan (m/z 100–1200) were selected for MS/MS fragmentation. These DDA data were utilized for molecular networking (MN) establishment. The ESI parameters were set as follows: capillary voltage, 4 kV; fragmentor voltage, 120 V; nozzle voltage, 50 V. Sheath gas (nitrogen) flow and temperature were 10 L/min and 350 °C, respectively; nebulizer pressure was 50 psi; drying gas flow and temperature were 13 L/min and 320 °C, respectively. Prior to analysis, the Q-TOF-MS system was externally calibrated with fluorinated triazatriphosphorines (m/z 50–3200), ensuring a mass accuracy of < 2 ppm and a resolution of 20,000 (at m/z 322.0487).

Data Analysis of the Molecular Networking in the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking System (GNPS) Platform

The unprocessed MS/MS data files were initially converted into the “32-bit” mzXML format via MSConvert. These formatted files were then uploaded via WinSCP (version 6.5.3) to the MassIVE repository—a public platform for MS data maintained by the UCSD Center for Computational Mass Spectrometry—enabling subsequent manipulated MS data files on GNPS. Subsequent to the upload of data files as datasets into GNPS-MassIVE, they became accessible for analytical workflows within GNPS. The parameters for the molecular network were set with a “Precursor Ion Mass Tolerance” and a “Fragment Ion Mass Tolerance” of 0.02 Da each. A minimum cosine score threshold of 0.7 was established, along with the stipulation of a minimum of six matching fragment ions. The produced molecular networks were downloaded and then visualized utilizing Cytoscape (version 3.9.1).

Network Pharmacology Analysis

The SMILES of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin were acquired from the PubChem database. The SMILES were uploaded to three target prediction servers: PharmMapper (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), Super-PRED (https://prediction.charite.de/index.php) and Swiss Target Prediction (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), to obtain potential biological targets. Simultaneously, inflammatory pain-associated targets were collected from databases including DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com), DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org), OMIM (https://www.omim.org), and TTD (https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/).

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for shared targets of the two compounds and inflammatory pain was constructed using the database STRING with a minimum association confidence level of >0.7. The PPI network was subsequently constructed, key genes were identified, and visualized with Cytoscape (version 3.9.1). Functional enrichment analysis of Gene Ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways was conducted utilizing the Metascape database.

Western Blot

RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated until reaching approximately 80% confluence. Following the designated treatments with LPS and varying concentrations (20, 40, and 80 µM) of Resibufogenin or Cinobufagin, the cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1045; Beyotime, China) to preserve protein phosphorylation. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (MA0082; Meilunbio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal amounts of protein samples per were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently electro-transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk or 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 2 hours at room temperature to prevent non-specific binding. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with the following specific primary antibodies: anti-PI3K (1:1000, 4257T, CST), anti-p-PI3K (1:1000, 17366T, CST), anti-AKT (1:1000, 9272S, CST), anti-p-AKT (1:1000, 4060T, CST), anti-p65 (1:1000, ab32536, Abcam), anti-p-p65 (1:1000, ab76302, Abcam), anti-COX-2 (1:1000, 12282T, CST), and anti-GAPDH (1:1000, GB15002, Servicebio, China). After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000, Beyotime, China) for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using the Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (MA0186; Meilunbio, China) and captured with a C600 Multifunctional Molecular Imaging System (Azure Biosystems, USA). Quantitative densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software. Protein expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as internal controls.

Quantitative Real Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from Raw264.7 cells with TRIzol reagent (R401-01, Vazyme, China). The RNA concentration was measured using the Spectrophotometer system Lite (DS-11, DeNovix, USA). 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA following the directions of the HiScript® II Q RT SuperMix kit (R123-01, Vazyme, China). Following Quantitative PCR amplification was performed utilizing ChamQ Universal SYBR Master Mix (Q711-02/03, Vazyme, China) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with inflammatory factor expression normalized to β-actin by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Total Antioxidant Capacity (ABTS Assay)

The total antioxidant capacity of RAW 264.7 cells was evaluated using an ABTS assay kit (S0119, Beyotime, China). Briefly, cell lysates or Trolox standards were reacted with the ABTS working solution for 6 min at room temperature. The absorbance was recorded at 734 nm using a microplate reader. Results were calculated based on a Trolox standard curve and normalized to the total protein concentration determined by the BCA method. Data are expressed as Trolox equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC, µmol TE/mg protein).

Measurement of Intracellular GSH

The intracellular total glutathione levels in RAW 264.7 cells were measured using a Total Glutathione Assay Kit (S0052, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, after the designated treatments, cell lysates were collected, deproteinized, and centrifuged. The obtained supernatants were then mixed with the assay working solution containing DTNB. Following a 5-min incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 412 nm using a microplate reader. GSH concentrations were calculated using a standard curve and normalized to the total protein concentration (BCA assay). Data are expressed as nmol/mg protein.

Enzyme Linked Immune Sorbent Assays (ELISA)

IL-6 or TNF-α levels in supernatants were determined using specific ELISA kits (Neobioscience, China). Blood was collected from anesthetized mice through retro-orbital venous plexus puncture and clotted at room temperature for 30 min. Serum was isolated through centrifugation at 4°C, 2,000 × g, 15 min. Serum IL-6 or TNF-α levels were assessed following to the manufacturer’s protocols, and the optical density was recorded at 450 nm utilizing a Microplate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

In vivo Preliminary Safety Assessment

To evaluate the in vivo safety profile of Resibufogenin, mice were administered therapeutic doses (3 and 10 mg/kg). Following the treatment period, blood samples were collected for comprehensive hematological (eg, WBC, RBC, HGB, PLT) and serum biochemical (eg, ALT, AST, BUN) analyses using automated analyzers. Subsequently, major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were rapidly excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and examined under a light microscope to evaluate potential pathological alterations.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0). All quantitative data is presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Intergroup comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA with Newman-Keuls post-hoc analysis or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

The Analgesic Activity of Toad Venom and Its Extracts in a Mouse Model of Inflammatory Pain

The bioactivity of toad venom, which is determined by its chemical composition, varies according to geographic origin due to environmental and ecological factors that alter the synthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites. These variations potentially affect both the content and profile of active constituents, which may contribute to differences in pharmacological efficacy. Thus, identifying sources of toad venom with robust therapeutic potential is a crucial step before delving into the exploration of their specific active substances. To this end, we first assessed the analgesic activity of toad venom from four distinct geographic origins. The analgesic activity of toad venom from four distinct geographical sources was assessed following the behavioral testing workflow outlined in Figure 1A. Itraplantar (ipl) administration of carrageenan significantly induced paw hyperalgesia and caused a sustained reduction in thermal withdrawal latency (TWL) over an extended time period. Following intragastric (i.g) administration, CS-3 (P<0.01) and CS-4 (P<0.001) exhibited significant analgesic effects. At both 240 and 360 min post-administration, CS-4 showed a more pronounced effect than CS-3. In contrast, the CS-1 and CS-2 showed no appreciable effect on inflammatory pain (Figure 1B). The analgesic effects of CS-4 were further evaluated using thermal and mechanical allodynia assays. The results demonstrated that CS-4 significantly increased both TWL (P<0.05) and paw mechanical withdrawal threshold (MWT, P<0.01) in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 1C and D). To identify the bioactive monomers potentially contributing to the analgesic effects of toad venom, we performed extraction and fractionation following the workflow outlined in Figure 1E, yielding three distinct fractions. Subsequent analgesic activity screening revealed that the EA and MeOH fractions exhibited significant antinociceptive properties (Figure 1F, P<0.05), thereby providing a basis for exploring the specific active compounds. Chemical Composition Identification of MeOH and EA Fractions from Toad Venom Using GNPS Database

To further investigate the substances associated with the analgesic effects observed in the MeOH and EA extracts, LC-MS analysis was conducted. The acquired data from these fractions (Figure 2A and B) were processed using the GNPS web platform (Version 1.3.16-GNPS, https://gnps.ucsd.edu). By applying the MolNetEnhancer workflow on GNPS, the chemical classes of the molecular clusters were putatively annotated (Figure 3A and B). Subsequently, the major compound clusters were analyzed, facilitating the tentative characterization of 28 compounds based on comparisons of chromatographic retention times, MS fragmentation patterns, accurate molecular weights, and fragment ion data with literature reports. Among these, 13 compounds were tentatively identified from the MeOH fraction, and 15 were from the EA fraction (Table 1). Evaluation of Analgesic Activity of Chemical Constituents in Fractionated Extracts from Toad Venom

Based on criteria such as quantitative abundance, literature-reported bioactivity, and compound availability, 13 compounds were selected for analgesic evaluation: 9 bufadienolides (molecular structures shown in Figure 4A) and 4 indole alkaloids (structures depicted in Figure 4C). The results indicated that, compared to the model group, Bufalin significantly increased TWL at 120, 240 and 360 min (P<0.01); Bufotaline showed a significant increase at 120 min (P<0.001); Bufotalidin exhibited significant effects at 60, 120 and 360 min (P<0.05); Arenobufagin significantly increased TWL at 120, 240 and 360 min (P<0.01); and treatments with Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin were associated with sustained analgesic effects throughout the 30–360 min observation period (P<0.05). The positive control Ibuprofen (100 mg/kg) similarly exhibited significant effects at all time points (P<0.05) (Figure 4B). Among the indole alkaloids, only N-Methyl Serotonin displayed increased TWL at 120, 240, and 360 min (P<0.05, Figure 4D), whereas the other compounds did not exhibit significant activity. Collectively, these findings suggest that Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin exhibit more prominent and long-lasting analgesic profiles in the carrageenan-induced inflammatory pain model compared to other tested compounds. Network Pharmacology Approach to Elucidate the Analgesic Mechanisms of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin

Given that Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin emerged as prominent active components in the behavioral assays, these two compounds were selected for subsequent mechanistic exploration. A total of 216 compound-related targets were predicted using Swiss Target Prediction, Super-PRED, and Pharmmapper database (Figure 5A). Following the elimination of duplicate items, a total of 959 targets associated with inflammatory pain were extracted from the aforementioned databases. A total of 82 common targets were derived by Venn diagram analysis, and a PPI network was built with Cytoscape software (Figure 5B). The network comprised 188 nodes and 811 edges, as illustrated in Figure 5C. The targets were filtered according to Centiscape analysis and vetted based on degree value, betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC). 22 targets exhibited average betweenness centrality (0.007), closeness centrality (63.439), and degree values surpassing the average (23.610), as shown in Figure 5D, suggesting their potential central roles within the network. The predicted targets associated with Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin in the context of inflammatory pain included EGFR, STAT3, MTOR, SRC, HSP90AA1, and PPARG. A total of 455 biological processes were highlighted by GO enrichment analysis, encompassing peptidyl-tyrosine phosphorylation, transmembrane receptor protein tyrosine kinase signaling pathway, peptidyl-tyrosine autophosphorylation, reaction to lipid, and modulation of inflammatory response. The top 10 processes are presented in Figure 5E. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed 152 potential pathways, with the top ten most significantly enriched pathways (Figure 5F), including pathways in cancer, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, proteoglycans in cancer, Ras signaling pathway, and Rap1 signaling pathway. These data suggest that the potential therapeutic mechanisms of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin in inflammatory pain may be associated with anti-inflammatory signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT and MAPK.

Resibufogenin and Cinobufagin May Involve the Suppression of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB Signaling Axis and Alleviate Inflammation in LPS-Stimulated RAW 264.7 Cells

Based on our network pharmacology analysis, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway emerged as a potential key target for the analgesic effects of Resibufogenin and Cinobufagin. To experimentally investigate a selected subset of these predictions, we examined the expression levels of key proteins within this pathway, as well as downstream inflammatory mediators, using Western blot analysis. As shown in Figure 6, LPS stimulation significantly upregulated the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and p65, and increased the expression of COX-2 compared with the control group. Treatment with Resibufogenin dose-dependently attenuated these LPS-induced elevations. Specifically, Resibufogenin (20, 40, and 80 µM) significantly reduced the ratios of p-PI3K/PI3K, p-AKT/AKT, and p-p65/p65, and decreased COX-2 protein expression (Figure 6A–E, P<0.05). Similarly, Cinobufagin treatment at 40 and 80 µM significantly suppressed LPS-induced phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and p65 and downregulated COX-2 expression (P<0.05), whereas 20 µM showed no significant effect (Figure 6F–J). These results collectively suggest that the anti-inflammatory and analgesic effects of both Resibufogenin and Cinobufagin may involve the suppression of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis in activated macrophages. To further assess the anti-inflammatory profiles of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin, we examined the mRNA expression levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS, by RT-qPCR. LPS stimulation induced a robust increase in the mRNA expression of all tested cytokines compared to the control group. Cinobufagin exerted anti-inflammatory effects at higher concentrations (40 and 80 µM); however, at the lower concentration of 20 µM, it significantly reduced only IL-1β expression, with no statistically significant effects observed on IL-6, TNF-α, or iNOS (Figure 7A–D). Similarly, Resibufogenin treatment (20, 40, and 80 µM) significantly suppressed the LPS-induced upregulation of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7E–H, P<0.05). Combined with the Western blot results, which indicated that Resibufogenin was associated with a more pronounced inhibitory effect on the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis, these findings collectively suggest that Resibufogenin exhibits a more prominent anti-inflammatory activity compared to cinobufagin in this model. Therefore, Resibufogenin was selected as the lead compound for further investigation in our subsequent experiments. Evaluation of the Anti-Inflammatory and Antioxidant Activities of Resibufogenin in vivo and in vitro

Given the more prominent anti-inflammatory activity of Resibufogenin observed in our in vitro assays, we further investigated its potential pharmacological effects using multiple in vivo inflammatory models. In the carrageenan-induced paw inflammation model, H&E staining revealed extensive inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue damage in the Model group. Treatment with Resibufogenin (3 and 10 mg/kg) attenuated these pathological changes, displaying an effect comparable to the positive control, ibuprofen (Figure 7I).

To further assess the anti-inflammatory potential of Resibufogenin, we employed xylene-induced ear swelling and LPS-induced systemic inflammation models. In the ear swelling model, administration of Resibufogenin (3 and 10 mg/kg) significantly reduced ear thickness compared with the model group (Figure 7J). Moreover, in the LPS-induced murine inflammatory model was established according to the experimental timeline outlined in Figure 7K, Resibufogenin treatment dose-dependently decreased the serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and TNF-α (Figure 7L and M).

Given the involvement of oxidative stress in mediating inflammatory pain, we investigated the potential antioxidant effects of Resibufogenin in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. Our results showed that LPS stimulation significantly depleted intracellular total glutathione (GSH) levels and reduced trolox-equivalent antioxidant capacity (TEAC). Notably, Resibufogenin treatment dose-dependently restored both GSH levels and TEAC activity (Figure 7N, O, P<0.05), suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effects of Resibufogenin are associated with the enhancement of the cellular antioxidant defense system. Collectively, these in vivo and biochemical findings indicate that the anti-inflammatory and analgesic profiles of Resibufogenin are associated with the suppression of inflammation-associated oxidative stress.

Preliminary Safety Assessment of Resibufogenin in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo safety profile of Resibufogenin, we conducted a preliminary toxicological analysis following the administration of the therapeutic dosages utilized in our efficacy studies (3 and 10 mg/kgy). Histopathological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, was performed using H&E staining (Figure 8A). Compared with the control group, histological analysis revealed no observable pathological lesions, necrosis, or aberrant cellular architecture in any of the treated groups, suggesting an absence of apparent organ damage at the examined dosages. Furthermore, we assessed potential systemic toxicity by monitoring hematological and biochemical parameters. As presented in Figure 8B and C, the levels of hematological indices (eg, WBC, RBC, HGB, PLT) and markers of liver and kidney function (eg, ALT, AST, BUN) in the Resibufogenin-treated groups remained comparable to those observed in the control group. There were no statistically significant differences across these parameters, suggesting that short-term administration of Resibufogenin at the tested dosages was not associated with overt alterations in physiological homeostasis. Collectively, these findings provide preliminary evidence suggesting no detectable toxicity for Resibufogenin under the tested short-term conditions within the evaluated dosage range. Discussion

Inflammatory pain is initiated by diverse stimuli that trigger the release of inflammatory factors from macrophages, neutrophils, mast cells, and nerve endings, leading to localized tissue inflammation.35 Currently, the mainstay of clinical pharmacotherapy for inflammatory pain is anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which often provide insufficient efficacy or cause significant adverse effects. Therefore, the developing of novel therapeutics with enhanced safety and efficacy is urgently needed.36 Toad venom comprise numerous bioactive compounds with various pharmacological properties, including analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and anti-cancer activities.37 The development of toad venom as a therapeutic agent is hampered by the absence of standardized quality control protocols and a holistic understanding of its pharmacodynamic constituents. The existing research on its anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties is not comprehensive, primarily concentrated on a limited number of isolated compounds. A systematic investigation following a sequential paradigm—from evaluating the whole extract to identifying active fractions, isolating key monomers, and ultimately validating their synergistic effects—is critically needed to better understand the potential mechanisms of action. Therefore, based on the inflammatory pain mouse model, our study systematically evaluated the analgesic effects of toad venom, its extracts, and its monomeric compounds.9,21,38 The carrageenan-induced inflammatory model is widely utilized in pain research.39 Therefore, in this study, we first employed this model to evaluate the analgesic effects of toad venom. We evaluated the analgesic activity of toad venom samples collected from four distinct geographical origins. Among these, the sample designated as CS-4 exhibited notable analgesic properties, as it significantly attenuated carrageenan-induced thermal hyperalgesia and mechanical allodynia. These results suggest that the chemical composition of toad venom may vary depending on its geographical origin, manifesting as differences in the content or relative proportions of key constituents, which may ultimately contribute to variations in analgesic efficacy. These findings further underscore the importance of improving quality control standards of toad venom. Unfortunately, a limitation of this study is that the chemical variations of toad venom across the four different geographical origins were not thoroughly investigated, which remains a critical objective for our future research.

Current research on the material basis of efficacy in traditional Chinese medicine or natural products primarily relies on two methodological approaches.40 The first comprises the extraction, separation, and purification of individual monomeric compounds from the source material, followed by pharmacological evaluation of their biological activity. However, the process is often labor-intensive and time-consuming. The second, more targeted strategy involves fractionating the crude extraction into several chemical fractions based on polarity. Comparative pharmacodynamic studies are then conducted between these fractions and the crude extraction undergo comparative pharmacodynamic studies to identify the active fraction, which is subjected to further separation into its individual chemical constituents to determine the primary material basis for the efficacy.41 Compared to the original crude extraction, these purified active fractions offer the advantages in terms of reduced dosage requirements and enhanced therapeutic effects. For instance, Chen et al investigated the chemical constituents of active fractions derived from an Astragalus-based compound. Through activity-guided fractionation, they identified the n-butanol extract of the aqueous decoction as the active component associated with the hypoglycemic effect. Subsequent separation and analysis of this fraction preliminarily explored its mechanism action in reducing blood glucose.42 Therefore, in this study, we performed sequential extraction of toad venom using three solvents with increasing polarity: PE, EA, and MeOH. The analgesic efficacy of each fraction, categorized by polarity, was evaluated in a mouse model of inflammatory pain. These results indicate that the EA and MeOH fractions are likely the primary active fractions contributing to the analgesic effect of toad venom.

The identification of active monomeric compounds derived from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) is critical for transitioning from empirical applications to evidence-based pharmacological therapy. It contributes to elucidating the action mechanism and accurate targets of TCM, while also facilitating the establishment of a precise system for quality control standards. Furthermore, isolating key active constituents enables structural optimization for enhanced efficacy and reduced toxicity, thereby serving as a vital source of novel lead compounds in drug discovery.43 LC-Q-TOF-MS, recognized for its high sensitivity, selectivity, and specificity, is a premier technique for this purpose.44 Applying this technology, we successfully characterized the majority of chemical components in toad venom, identifying compounds such as Cinobufagin, Resibufogenin, Bufalin, Gamabufotalin, Bufotaline, Telocinobufagin, Cinobufotalin, Bufotalidin, Arenobufagin, 5-Hydroxytryptamine, N-Methyl Serotonin, Bufotenin, and Bufotenidine. Based on criteria including quantitative abundance, documented bioactivity, and compound availability, 13 candidate compounds were selected for analgesic evaluation. Among these, Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin exhibited more prominent analgesic efficacy in the carrageenan-induced inflammatory pain model. While these two compounds were identified as the key analgesic constituents, it is critical to acknowledge that the pharmacological complexity of toad venom likely involves multi-component synergy rather than the isolated action of individual compounds. In alignment with the “holistic” paradigm of traditional medicine—where multiple constituents engage distinct molecular targets or signaling pathways to elicit additive or synergistic outcomes—our study focused on elucidating the individual contributions of these monomers. Nevertheless, the potential for synergistic interactions between Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin cannot be disregarded. As both compounds are bufadienolides, they may modulate the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB pathway via distinct binding affinities and kinetics, or influence complementary inflammatory cascades. Future research employing checkerboard assays or isobologram analysis is warranted to characterize the nature of these interactions, specifically whether they act synergistically, additively, or antagonistically. Elucidating these complexities is essential for a comprehensive understanding of the pharmacodynamic basis of toad venom and for optimizing its therapeutic potential in future drug development.

In recent years, network pharmacology has emerged as a valuable approach for elucidating the action mechanisms of natural products.45 To clarify the potential mechanisms underlying the alleviation of inflammatory pain by Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin, we employed network pharmacology to predict their targets as well as those associated with inflammatory pain. Intersection analysis identified key shared targets, including MTOR and HSP90AA1. Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis indicated that the biological functions of these targets are closely associated with the regulation of inflammatory responses and protein kinase signaling. Importantly, KEGG pathway analysis revealed considerable enrichment in multiple cascades; notably, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway ranked second among the significantly enriched pathways. These bioinformatic findings strongly suggested the PI3K-AKT axis as a highly probable candidate pathway mediating the pharmacological effects of Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin, which provided a scientific rationale for our subsequent in vitro and in vivo experimental validations. However, while network pharmacology predicted a broad spectrum of potential targets and pathways, only one specific pathway focusing on the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis and representative inflammatory cytokines was experimentally examined in the current study, leaving the remaining network predictions to be verified in further investigations.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a critical mediator in inflammatory pain, primarily through its ability to activate the downstream transcription factor NF-κB, which promotes the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α.46 Our findings showed that both Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin were observed to downregulate the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, and p65. This aligns with previous studies indicating that bufadienolides can interfere with intracellular signaling cascades to dampen inflammatory responses.47 Notably, Resibufogenin displayed a more pronounced inhibitory effect on this axis, which directly correlated with its prominent efficacy in suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression in LPS-stimulated RAW 264.7 cells. Given its notable anti-inflammatory profile compared to Cinobufagin, we therefore prioritized Resibufogenin to further explore its anti-inflammatory and analgesic mechanisms in our subsequent investigations. In addition to the PI3K/AKT axis, oxidative stress remains a well-recognized culprit in the exacerbation of inflammatory pain.48 In this study, we observed that LPS-induced oxidative injury, characterized by depleted GSH and reduced TEAC, was significantly attenuated by Resibufogenin treatment. This suggests that Resibufogenin may function not only as a signaling pathway inhibitor but also as an enhancer of cellular antioxidant defense. The restoration of redox homeostasis is essential for resolving inflammation, as oxidative stress often creates a positive feedback loop that sustains cytokine production. By breaking this oxidative-inflammatory loop, Resibufogenin appears to mitigate the pathological microenvironment at the cellular level. Building upon these in vitro mechanistic insights, we further explored the pharmacological efficacy of Resibufogenin using distinct inflammatory animal models. Notably, Resibufogenin showed robust and consistent anti-inflammatory activity across multiple in vivo paradigms, successfully attenuating carrageenan-induced paw edema, suppressing xylene-induced ear swelling, and reducing circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines in an LPS-induced systemic inflammation model. In this study, IL-6 and TNF-α were selected as representative proinflammatory cytokines to evaluate the anti-inflammatory activity of Resibufogenin. These cytokines are well-established key mediators of inflammatory pain and are recognized as classical downstream target genes regulated by the NF-κB signaling pathway.49 Previous research has consistently demonstrated that the transcriptional activation of the NF-κB axis leads to the robust expression of IL-6 and TNF-α in LPS-stimulated macrophages, thereby propagating the inflammatory cascade.35 Therefore, the concurrent inhibition of these cytokines, as observed in our results, suggests that Resibufogenin alleviated inflammatory pain, which was associated with the suppression of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB-mediated transcriptional program.

A critical aspect of transitioning natural products like toad venom components into clinical application is addressing their safety profile. While crude toad venom has historically been associated with dose-dependent cardiotoxicity, recent studies evaluating isolated bufadienolides have reported wide therapeutic windows.50,51 For instance, previous in vivo studies demonstrated that prolonged administration of Resibufogenin at doses ranging from 10 to 20 mg/kg did not induce systemic toxicity or adverse effects on body weight in murine models.52 Consistent with these reports, our preliminary safety assessment indicated that the anti-inflammatory dosages used in this study (3 and 10 mg/kg) caused no observable pathological lesions in major organs and maintained normal hematological and biochemical parameters. These findings support the notion that Resibufogenin exhibits notable pharmacological effects in vivo with no detectable toxicity under the tested short-term conditions, although studies with larger sample sizes and long-term evaluations are still required.

Conclusion

In summary, this study utilized an integrated strategy to explore the material basis and potential pharmacological mechanisms of toad venom. By comparing samples from four distinct geographical origins, we identified the CS-4 sample (sourced from Shangqiu) as a highly promising raw material for analgesic research. Through bioassay-guided fractionation and LC–Q–TOF–MS analysis, Cinobufagin and Resibufogenin were tentatively identified as the principal bioactive constituents associated with the analgesic activity of toad venom. In vitro and in vivo evaluations examining one specific pathway from the bioinformatic predictions indicated that these compounds, particularly Resibufogenin, alleviated inflammatory pain, which may involve the suppression of the PI3K/AKT/NF-κB signaling axis and enhancement of cellular antioxidant defenses. Furthermore, our preliminary toxicological assessment indicated that Resibufogenin exhibits no detectable toxicity under the tested conditions. Collectively, these findings provide a scientific basis for the potential clinical application of toad venom and highlight Resibufogenin as a promising lead compound with no detectable toxicity under the tested conditions for the future development of inflammatory pain therapeutics.
